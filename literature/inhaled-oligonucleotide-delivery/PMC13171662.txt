## Abstract
Doxorubicin (DOX) is a cytotoxic chemotherapeutic drug, the clinical value of which is limited by its cardiotoxicity. Krüppel-like factor 9 (KLF9) is known to modulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis and plays critical roles in cardiovascular diseases. Here, we aimed to explore the potential effect of KLF9 on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. C57BL/6J mice with cardiac-specific overexpression or silencing of KLF9 received single intraperitoneal injections of DOX to establish a DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model. The cardiac function of the mice was monitored by echocardiography, cardiac morphology was evaluated by histopathological staining, biomarkers of myocardial injury were detected using ELISA, and TUNEL staining and Western blotting were performed to evaluate apoptosis. In addition, H9c2 cells were used to validate the function of KLF9 in vitro. To test the involvement of thioredoxin reductase 2 (Txnrd2), ROS and p53 in the observed effects, siRNAs directed against p53 and Txnrd2 and the ROS inhibitor N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) were used. KLF9 expression was upregulated in the hearts of DOX-treated mice and H9c2 cells. Cardiac-specific KLF9 overexpression exacerbated, while cardiac-specific KLF9 silencing alleviated, DOX-induced apoptosis, acute myocardial injury and dysfunction. Mechanistically, KLF9 deficiency resulted in upregulation of Txnrd2 expression and subsequent suppression of apoptosis through modulation by ROS/p53 signalling. KLF9 exerts a pro-apoptotic effect on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity by inhibiting Txnrd2 and regulating the ROS/p53 signalling pathway. KLF9 deficiency may be a promising target for mitigating DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

## Introduction
 The clinical application of chemotherapy significantly improves survival in cancer patients, but the side effects of chemotherapeutic agents affect long-term prognosis. Doxorubicin (DOX), a broad-spectrum chemotherapeutic agent, has been used in the treatment of various cancers because of its high potency, efficacy and value [ – ]. However, its clinical use is impeded by its cardiotoxicity, which eventually leads to serious cardiomyopathy, cardiac dysfunction and congestive heart failure [ , ]. The mechanism through which DOX induces cardiotoxicity is complex and considered to be comprehensive, involving many factors, including oxidative damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, the inflammatory response and apoptosis [ , , ]. Furthermore, an imbalance in reactive oxygen species (ROS) has recently been identified as the leading cause of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [ , ]. This effect is closely related to the quinone structure of DOX, which can be reduced to semiquinone radicals in cardiomyocytes. Increased oxygen consumption promotes the transformation of semiquinone, leading to the generation of large amounts of ROS [ ]. Excessive ROS accumulation triggers oxidative damage to lipids, proteins and nucleic acids, ultimately contributing to the destruction of cell membrane integrity and permeability, mitochondrial dysfunction and activation of downstream proapoptotic pathways [ , ]. Therefore, intracellular ROS homeostasis is considered a critical target for attenuating DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.
Previous studies have shown that ROS activate the apoptosis pathway and trigger the synthesis and release of multiple apoptosis-related factors via a series of mechanisms in which p53-mediated apoptosis plays a critical role [ , ]. p53 is well known as a key regulator of apoptosis [ ]. Studies have shown that DOX-induced cardiotoxicity depends in part on p53-mediated apoptosis and that inhibition of the p53-related pathway can alleviate the apoptosis induced by DOX [ , ]. ROS production can also be regulated by the endogenous antioxidant defence system. Strengthening the endogenous cardiac antioxidant defence system and reducing ROS production can ameliorate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [ – ]. These findings provide a basis for targeting inflammation and oxidative stress as an effective therapeutic strategy for ameliorating DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Thus, identifying potential intervention targets that suppress ROS production and cardiomyocyte apoptosis may lead to breakthroughs in the treatment and prevention of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.
Krüppel-like factors (KLFs) are transcription factors that contain zinc finger structural motifs. Seventeen members of the KLF family have been identified in mammals; these are designated KLF1–KLF17 [ ]. KLFs regulate the transcription of their target genes by binding to GC-rich regions of target DNA sequences via three Cys2/His2 zinc finger domains [ ]. The members of this gene family are expressed at different levels in different tissues. KLF9 is abundantly expressed in the heart, where it participates mainly in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis and energy metabolism [ , ]. It has been reported that the loss of KLF9 suppresses excessive inflammatory reactions in myocardial infarction by inhibiting the activation of nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [ ]. In a previous study, we showed that KLF9 overexpression aggravates cardiac dysfunction, the inflammatory response and oxidative stress in mice with diabetic cardiomyopathy [ ]. Notably, recent studies have shown that KLF9 plays a critical role in controlling ROS homeostasis by decreasing the expression of thioredoxin reductase 2 (Txnrd2) in a variety of disease models [ , ]. In the study reported here, we aimed to explore the function of KLF9 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved.

## Methods

## Animals and Treatment
All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (protocol code 202303T012). Healthy male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice weighed 23.5–27.5 g and were 8 to 10 weeks old. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free barrier conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University. To achieve cardiac-specific overexpression or silencing of KLF9 in the heart, the mice were injected via the tail vein with adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9; Vigene Bioscience Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) carrying KLF9 or a short hairpin RNA directed against KLF9 (shKLF9) under the cTnT promoter. Four weeks later, each mouse received a single intraperitoneal injection of DOX (15 mg/kg) to establish a model of DOX-induced acute cardiotoxicity [ , ]. Five days after DOX stimulation, serum samples were collected, the mice were sacrificed, and samples of their hearts were collected and stored.

## Cell Culture and Treatment
H9c2 cells were purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; HyClone, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone) in a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). The H9c2 cells were treated with DOX (1 µM, 24 h) to establish a model of DOX-induced acute cardiotoxicity in vitro. To achieve overexpression or silencing of KLF9 in vitro, adenovirus (Ad; Vigene Bioscience Co., Ltd.) carrying KLF9 or shRNA directed against KLF9 (shKLF9) was used in this study. In our earlier research, we tested multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 30, 50, 100, 150, and 200 and determined that the optimal MOI was 100. In addition, to validate whether p53 and Txnrd2 are involved in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, H9c2 cells were transfected with small interfering RNA against p53 (sip53; Vigene Bioscience Co., Ltd.) and small interfering RNA against Txnrd2 (siTxnrd2; Vigene Bioscience Co., Ltd.) using Lipo8000 (Thermo Scientific, USA).

## Cell Viability
H9c2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5000 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then treated with DOX (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, or 10 µM) for 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, or 48 h. The viability of the cells was then measured using a CCK-8 kit (Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China).

## Echocardiography
Inhalation of 1.5% isoflurane was used to induce mild anaesthesia, and echocardiography was then performed. The data recorded and collected included left ventricular (LV) end-systolic diameter (LVESd), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDd), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS).

## Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Detection of Biomarkers of Myocardial Injury
Serum levels of biomarkers of myocardial injury, including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase isoenzymes (CK-MB), and cardiac troponin T (cTnT), were detected using the corresponding ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China).

## Histopathology
Heart tissue samples were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, embedded in paraffin and cut into 4-µm sections. To assess the histological morphology of the heart, these samples were subjected to haematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining and wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining and Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick-End Labelling (TUNEL) Staining
The heart samples were dehydrated and subjected to antigen retrieval via a heat-mediated approach. They were then blocked with 10% goat serum, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% H2O2. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and then with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. After three washes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Finally, fluorescence images were collected and quantitatively analysed. To detect apoptosis of mouse cardiomyocytes, TUNEL kits (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were used. The ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei to DAPI-stained nuclei was subsequently calculated.

## Measurement of Intracellular ROS Production
Frozen mouse heart tissue samples were fixed and incubated with dihydroethidium (DHE) at 37 °C for 30 min. The nuclei were then counterstained with DAPI for 10 min, the fluorescence images were captured, and the mean fluorescence intensity was measured. In vitro intracellular ROS production was assessed through the use of the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA; the resulting fluorescence was detected using flow cytometry.

## Flow Cytometry Analysis of Apoptosis
Apoptosis of H9c2 cells was measured by flow cytometry using an Annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (Jiangsu Keygen Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). After the binding buffer was added, H9c2 cells prepared in advance were mixed with 5 µL of annexin V-APC and 5 µL of 7-AAD at room temperature and incubated for 10 min in the dark; the cells were then analysed by flow cytometry.

## Western Blotting
The expression of proteins encoded by specific target genes was quantitatively analysed by Western blotting. Total protein was extracted from mouse heart tissue and H9c2 cells through RIPA lysis. The protein concentration in the extracts was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Wuhan Abbkine Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). The proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, which were then blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were then incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, washed, and incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The resulting signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents. The following antibodies were used: anti-KLF9 (A7196; ABclonal, Wuhan, China), anti-β-actin (66009-1-Ig; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-Bax (50599-2-Ig; Proteintech), anti-Bcl-2 (26593-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (25128-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-p53 (GB15627-100; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and anti-Txnrd2 (A5490; ABclonal).

## Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT‒PCR)
Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using a FastKing First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Beijing Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). A SuperReal PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) kit purchased from Beijing Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd. was used for PCR amplification and analysis. The following primers were used: mouse KLF9, forward: 5′-ACCTTCGAGGGGTCACGATA-3′, reverse: 5′-GTGCACCATAGCTCCCTGTT-3′; mouse GAPDH, forward: 5′-ACTCCACTCACGGCAAATTC-3′, reverse: 5′-TCTCCATGGTGGTGAAGACA-3′; rat KLF9, forward: 5′-TACATGGACTTCGTGGCTGC-3′, reverse: 5′-AAGGGCCGTTCACCTGTATG-3′; and rat GAPDH, forward: 5′-GACATGCCGCCTGGAGAAAC-3′, reverse: 5′-AGCCCAGGATGCCCTTTAGT-3′.

## Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay
For chromatin immunoprecipitation, H9c2 cells were incubated in 1% formaldehyde at 37 °C for 15 min and then in glycine at room temperature for 5 min. The cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer for 30 min. The lysates were sonicated to generate DNA fragments and mixed with ChIP dilution buffer and proteinase inhibitor. Protein A + G agarose/salmon sperm DNA was then added, and the mixture was incubated at 4 °C with rotation for 30 min, followed by incubation with specific antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Before centrifugation, additional protein A + G agarose/salmon sperm DNA was added. The precipitate was washed in a series of buffers, dissolved in elution buffer, and incubated in the presence of NaCl and proteinase K at 60 °C overnight. Finally, the DNA was extracted with phenol‒chloroform, washed with ethanol, and visualized by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels. The data obtained by qPCR are presented as histograms. The following primers were used: rat Txnrd2, forward: 5′-CCTCACCTCACCACAAACC-3′, reverse: 5′-GGCAGGAGCACTAGGACAG-3′.

## Statistical Analysis
The data are presented as the mean ± standard error (SEM) and were analysed using SPSS 22.0 software. Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed to compare differences among three or more groups. The repeated measures data were assessed by repeated-measures ANOVA. The survival data were analysed by Kaplan‒Meier analysis and the Mantel‒Cox log-rank test. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

## Results

## Increased KLF9 Expression in the Heart Tissue of DOX-Treated Mice
A DOX-induced acute cardiotoxicity mouse model was established by giving mice a single intraperitoneal injection of DOX (15 mg/kg). Five days later, comparison with the animals in the control group showed that the cardiomyocyte structure, cardiac function and survival rate of the animals in the DOX-treated group were severely damaged (Figure ). To explore whether KLF9 is involved in the observed DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, we measured KLF9 levels in the heart tissue of the mice. The results revealed that KLF9 protein expression and mRNA levels were elevated in mouse heart tissue after DOX treatment (Fig.  A, B). To investigate the role of KLF9 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, mice were subsequently transfected with AAV9-KLF9 or AAV9-shKLF9 to cause overexpression or silencing, respectively, of KLF9. Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining revealed that the protein expression and fluorescence intensity of KLF9 increased significantly in mice injected with AAV9-KLF9. The immunofluorescence results also revealed that KLF9 was expressed mainly in cardiomyocytes (Fig.  C, D). In contrast, the KLF9 level in AAV9-shKLF9-injected mice was lower than that in the control group (Fig.  E, F).
 

## Increased KLF9 Expression in DOX-Treated H9c2 Cells
Cultured H9c2 cells were incubated with various concentrations of DOX (0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10 µM) for 6, 12, 24, or 48 h. The viability of the cells was then evaluated using CCK-8 assays. As shown in Figure S2, the higher the concentration of DOX used and the longer the duration of DOX treatment, the lower the H9c2 cell viability. Because the condition and quantity of cells treated with 1 µM DOX for 24 h made them suitable for further analysis, stimulation for 24 h with 1 µM DOX was used to establish a DOX-induced acute cardiotoxicity model in vitro. We found that DOX treatment induced KLF9 expression, as evidenced by the presence of increased KLF9 mRNA and protein levels in H9c2 cells (Fig.  A, B). Similar to the in vivo method, KLF9 was overexpressed or silenced in H9c2 cells through treatment with adenovirus carrying KLF9 or shKLF9. To estimate the efficiency of infection by the adenovirus, we measured the fluorescence intensity at different time points after infection of the cells at different MOIs (Figure ). We determined that the optimal infection time was 24 h and that the optimal MOI was 100 (Fig.  C, D; Tables  and ).
 

## KLF9 Overexpression Aggravates DOX-Induced Cardiac Injury and Dysfunction in Mice
Four weeks after transfection with AAV9-KLF9, the mice were injected intraperitoneally with DOX (15 mg/kg), and sampling and experiments were performed 5 days later (Fig.  A). Transfection with AAV9-KLF9 obviously increased DOX-induced endogenous KLF9 expression and mRNA levels in mouse heart tissue (Fig.  B, C). Compared with the control treatment, DOX treatment resulted in a lower survival rate and lower body weight gain in mice, and these parameters decreased more obviously in mice with KLF9 overexpression (Fig.  D, E). The decrease in the heart weight/tibia length (HW/TL) ratio was promoted by KLF9 (Fig.  F). H&E and WGA staining confirmed that the mice in the DOX group exhibited vacuolar degeneration, myofiber disarray and cardiomyocyte atrophy, and the phenotype was more evident in mice with KLF9 overexpression (Fig.  G). Induction of acute myocardial injury by DOX was confirmed by the increased serum levels of LDH, CK-MB and cTnT, which increased more in mice with KLF9 overexpression (Fig.  H). Similarly, KLF9 overexpression aggravated cardiac dysfunction in mice after DOX pretreatment; this was indicated by decreases in LVEF and LVFS and increased LVESd. However, no change in LVEDd was observed in mice with KLF9 overexpression (Fig.  I).
 

## KLF9 Deficiency Alleviates DOX-Induced Cardiotoxicity in Mice
Next, to verify whether KLF9 deficiency plays a cardioprotective role, we evaluated the effect of silencing KLF9 on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity in mice. In mice transfected with AAV9-shKLF9, the DOX-induced increase in KLF9 protein expression and mRNA expression was significantly inhibited (Fig.  A, B). As expected, compared with the mice in the DOX group, the mice in the KLF9-deficient group exhibited slight increases in survival rate, body weight gain and HW/TL ratio (Fig.  C‒E). DOX-induced vacuolar cellular degeneration and cardiomyocyte atrophy were also prevented by KLF9 silencing (Fig.  F). More importantly, the serum myocardial injury biomarker data indicated that KLF9 deficiency significantly attenuated the cardiac impairment associated with DOX (Fig.  G). DOX-associated cardiac dysfunction was also ameliorated in KLF9-deficient mice, as reflected by increases in LVEF and LVFS. There was no significant difference in LVEDd (Fig.  H).
 

## KLF9 Regulates DOX-Induced Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis In Vivo and In Vitro
Apoptosis of cardiomyocytes contributes to the development of DOX-associated cardiotoxicity [ ]. To investigate the role of KLF9 in DOX-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, both gain- and loss-of-function approaches were employed in vivo and in vitro.
Consistent with previous reports, DOX treatment markedly increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, as evidenced by an elevated number of TUNEL-positive cells in mouse heart tissue. Notably, KLF9 overexpression further exacerbated DOX-induced apoptosis, as demonstrated by a significant increase in TUNEL-positive cardiomyocytes (Fig.  A). In parallel, Western blotting analysis revealed that DOX stimulation increased the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3, while decreasing the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. These effects were further amplified by KLF9 overexpression in vivo (Fig.  B).
 To validate these findings in vitro, KLF9 was overexpressed in H9c2 cells via adenovirus infection. As expected, DOX-induced upregulation of KLF9 at both protein and mRNA levels was further enhanced in Ad-KLF9-infected cells (Fig.  C, D). Consistently, KLF9 overexpression significantly increased Bax and cleaved caspase-3 expression, while reducing Bcl-2 levels following DOX treatment (Fig.  E). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that KLF9 overexpression markedly aggravated DOX-induced apoptosis in H9c2 cells (Fig.  F).
Conversely, KLF9 deficiency exerted a protective effect against DOX-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis. In vivo, KLF9 deficiency significantly reduced the proportion of TUNEL-positive cardiomyocytes in DOX-treated mice (Fig.  A). Correspondingly, the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 was suppressed, whereas Bcl-2 expression was increased in the absence of KLF9 (Fig.  B).
 Similarly, in vitro silencing of KLF9 in H9c2 cells effectively attenuated DOX-induced KLF9 upregulation at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig.  C, D). Moreover, KLF9 deficiency reversed the DOX-induced increase in Bax and cleaved caspase-3 expression and restored Bcl-2 levels (Fig.  E). Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that KLF9 deficiency significantly decreased the percentage of apoptotic cells following DOX exposure (Fig.  F).
Taken together, these results from complementary gain- and loss-of-function experiments consistently demonstrate that KLF9 plays a critical role in promoting DOX-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis in vivo and in vitro.

## DOX Activates p53-Associated Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis via a ROS-Dependent Mechanism In Vitro
p53 is a well-known modulator of apoptosis, and previous studies have shown that DOX-induced cardiotoxicity is closely related to p53-mediated apoptosis [ , ]. Therefore, we first validated the role of p53 in DOX-related cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Western blotting indicated that p53 protein expression was markedly elevated in H9c2 cells after DOX treatment (Fig. A). H2c2 cells were subsequently transfected with siRNAs against p53 to determine the impact of p53 silencing. As expected, DOX-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis was partially prevented, as evidenced by decreased expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 and increased expression of Bcl-2 (Fig. B). Consistent with this, flow cytometric analysis also revealed a decreased percentage of apoptotic H9c2 cells (Fig. C). p53 is a classical apoptosis-related factor that can be regulated by ROS, and previous studies have shown that ROS contribute to the activation of p53 during the process of DOX-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ , ]. This study revealed that DOX stimulation markedly increased the accumulation of ROS in H9c2 cells, which is consistent with previous reports (Fig. F). To validate the ROS-dependent p53 activation, we examined the effect of obliterating ROS in DOX-treated H9c2 cells. After the addition of N-acetyl cysteine (NAC), an inhibitor of ROS, ROS levels were notably reduced in DOX-treated H9c2 cells (Fig. F). More importantly, addition of NAC inhibited the activation of p53, and this was accompanied by the prevention of alterations in the protein expression of Bax, cleaved caspase-3 and Bcl-2 (Fig. D). These findings prove that NAC can counteract the effect of DOX on the activation of p53 and apoptosis-related proteins. Flow cytometric analysis also confirmed that decreased generation of ROS obviously ameliorated DOX-induced apoptosis in vitro (Fig. E). Moreover, no alteration in ROS levels was detected in DOX-treated H9c2 cells after p53 was silenced, indicating that p53 is a downstream effector of the DOX-induced ROS pathway (Fig. G).
 

## Txnrd2 is Involved in the Activation of the ROS/p53 Axis Mediated by KLF9 In Vitro
In the above experiments, we showed that KLF9 affects DOX-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and that the apoptosis-related ROS/p53 signalling pathway participates in these events. Recent research has confirmed that endogenous ROS are closely related to KLF9 [ ]. Thus, we hypothesized that KLF9 activates the ROS/p53 pathway during DOX-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis. To validate this hypothesis, we measured ROS levels and p53 expression in DOX-treated H9c2 cells with KLF9 overexpression or silencing. DOX-induced upregulation of ROS production and p53 expression was exacerbated in H9c2 cells with KLF9 overexpression (Fig. A, C, D) but attenuated in H9c2 cells with KLF9 deficiency (Fig. E, G, H). These findings indicate that apoptosis caused by the ROS/p53 signalling axis is triggered by KLF9. The accumulation of ROS within cells is strictly controlled by the intracellular antioxidant defence system. Interestingly, Txnrd2 is not only a well-known antioxidant whose products have been implicated in the regulation of ROS in various cell types but also a downstream target of KLF9 [ , , ]. Therefore, we explored whether KLF9 contributes to the alteration in Txnrd2 expression that is observed in DOX-treated H9c2 cells. Western blotting revealed that the expression of Txnrd2 was suppressed by DOX and that it decreased further in DOX-treated H9c2 cells with KLF9 overexpression (Fig. A, B) but increased in H9c2 cells with KLF9 deficiency (Fig. E, F). To further confirm the involvement of Txnrd2 in the process of KLF9-dependent regulation of the ROS/p53 pathway, H9c2 cells were transfected with siTxnrd2 to knockdown Txnrd2. As expected, the inhibitory effects of KLF9 silencing on abnormal ROS accumulation and p53 activation were blocked by Txnrd2 deficiency, indicating that Txnrd2 is an upstream regulator of the ROS/p53 pathway (Fig. I‒L). Notably, the Txnrd2 promoter sequence contains several conserved KLF9 binding sites [ ]. In agreement with the results of previous studies, a direct interaction between the region of the Txnrd2 promoter and KLF9 was confirmed by ChIP assays (Fig. M, N). Moreover, the number of PCR-amplified fragments of the KLF9-bound Txnrd2 promoter significantly increased in cells subjected to DOX stimulation (Fig. O).
 

## KLF9 Affects the ROS/p53 Signalling Pathway by Inhibiting Txnrd2 In Vivo
To further validate this mechanism in vivo, we measured the levels of Txnrd2, ROS and p53 in mouse heart tissue with KLF9 overexpression or silencing. Consistent with the in vitro results, DOX stimulation markedly decreased Txnrd2 expression but increased ROS levels and p53 expression. These alterations were further enhanced by overexpression of KLF9 (Fig.  A, B). Conversely, DOX-induced Txnrd2 downregulation, ROS accumulation and p53 upregulation were blunted in the heart tissue of KLF9-deficient mice (Fig.  C, D).Collectively, these in vivo findings further support the mechanistic link between KLF9 and the Txnrd2/ROS/p53 signalling axis. 
 

## Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated that KLF9 expression is upregulated in DOX-treated mouse hearts and H9c2 cells. Cardiac-specific KLF9 overexpression exacerbated, while cardiac-specific KLF9 silencing alleviated, DOX-associated cardiomyocyte apoptosis, acute myocardial injury and dysfunction. Mechanistically, we found that KLF9 aggravated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity by inhibiting Txnrd2 expression and thereby activating the ROS/p53 pathway both in vivo and in vitro.
DOX is regarded as the cornerstone of various antitumour treatments, but its severe cardiotoxicity limits its clinical application. The cardiotoxicity caused by DOX includes both acute and chronic effects. Approximately 10% of cancer patients suffer acute cardiotoxicity within a few days after DOX treatment [ , ]. In this study, we found that mice treated with DOX experienced signs of cardiac dysfunction after 5 days. Serum biomarkers of myocardial injury also increased markedly, and the myocardial structure became abnormal. Recently, more studies have focused on the ROS-initiated intrinsic pathway, and those studies have reported that ROS are responsible for DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Intracellular ROS production increases when the balance between oxidative metabolism and antioxidation is disrupted, and this is followed by subsequent cascades [ , , ].
Mechanistically, DOX can be anchored to the inner membrane of mitochondria, disrupting the electron transport chain and promoting the formation of superoxide anions [ ]. It has been reported that resveratrol can ameliorate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and fibrosis in vivo by decreasing ROS levels [ , ]. Another study revealed that pretreatment with berberine can circumvent DOX-induced cardiotoxicity by altering the concentration of ROS. These effects are intensified when SIRT1 expression is increased; this inhibits the activity of 66 kDa Src homology 2 domain-containing protein (p66Shc), an intracellular mediator that converts oxidative stress into ROS [ ].
Beyond its direct role in oxidative damage, accumulating evidence suggests that excessive ROS also serves as a critical upstream trigger for multiple forms of regulated cell death. Among these, ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by excessive lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation, has emerged as a key contributor to redox imbalance. Recent studies have further demonstrated that ferroptosis is critically involved in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Notably, dihydroartemisinin has been reported to alleviate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by activating Nrf2 and regulating autophagy, while sarmentosin exerts similar protective effects via the p62-Keap1-Nrf2 signalling pathway, highlighting the central role of ROS regulation in this process [ , ]. These findings further underscore the importance of ROS as a converging mechanism in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.
A recent study demonstrated that KLF9 is the major regulator that promotes excessive accumulation of ROS during the pathological processes associated with cardiovascular diseases [ ]. KLF9 is activated in response to the intracellular accumulation of ROS. Interestingly, under conditions of mild oxidative injury, the increase in ROS is inhibited by antioxidant defence systems, whereas during severe oxidative injury it leads to KLF9-mediated increases in ROS and subsequent apoptosis [ ]. Our study revealed that KLF9 overexpression upregulates ROS levels and subsequently aggravates DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, consistent with the findings of previous studies.
As a transcription factor containing a zinc finger structural motif, KLF9 can act either as a repressor or as an activator of gene transcription; thus, it regulates diverse pathophysiological processes that include proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, survival and responses to external stress. The three conserved zinc finger motifs at the carboxyl terminus of KLF9 bind to GC-rich DNA sequences and to 5’-CACCC-3’ elements [ ]. KLF9, as a binding partner of c-Jun N-terminal kinase 3 (JNK 3), interacts with JNK3 to inhibit axonal regeneration in the central nervous system of rats [ ]. Another study revealed that KLF9 is related to obesity and that overexpression of microRNA-142-3p inhibits adipogenic differentiation and autophagy in obese mice by targeting KLF9 [ ]. In addition, KLF9 deficiency reduces ischaemic injury to cardiomyocytes through the inhibition of oxidative stress [ ]. Consistent with our findings, it has recently been proposed that KLF9 knockdown ameliorates myocardial infarction through an NF-κB and MAPK-mediated inflammatory mechanism [ ]. In this study, we observed that KLF9 expression increased significantly in DOX-treated mouse hearts and in DOX-treated H9c2 cells, indicating that KLF9 is involved in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. The results also revealed that KLF9 overexpression exacerbates, while KLF9 silencing alleviates, DOX-associated apoptosis, acute myocardial injury and dysfunction. Accordingly, we conclude that KLF9 has an adverse effect during the pathological process of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.
Among the intracellular signalling pathways involved in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, the ROS/p53 pathway has been reported to be among the major mechanisms through which such cardiotoxicity is triggered. p53 plays a crucial role in many pathways that are activated by ROS; these pathways branch to form a signalling network that modulates apoptosis [ , ]. Previous studies have revealed that p53-mediated apoptosis is induced by high levels of ROS in a variety of pathologies [ , , ]. The activity of p53 induced by elevated ROS is related not only to mechanisms associated with redox modulation but also to cellular responses to DNA damage [ ]. In this study, we found that activated p53 triggered apoptosis by altering the expression of Bax, Bcl-2, and cleaved caspase-3 in the heart tissue of DOX-treated mice and in H9c2 cells, a finding that is consistent with the findings of a previous study [ , ]. Moreover, we revealed that inhibition of ROS production by pretreatment with NAC protected DOX-treated heart tissue and H9c2 cells against p53-mediated apoptosis. Notably, we confirmed that KLF9 overexpression induced p53-mediated apoptosis through the activation of ROS/p53. Mechanistically, we found that KLF9 exacerbated the accumulation of ROS in part through inhibition of the transcription of Txnrd2. Many studies have indicated that Txnrd2, an important member of the thioredoxin reductase family, is involved in ROS clearance in mitochondria [ ]. In some circumstances, Txnrd2 expression can be partially suppressed by KLF9, and Txnrd2 participates functionally in the regulation of KLF9-mediated oxidative stress [ ]. In agreement with these findings, our experimental results revealed that Txnrd2 transcription decreased upon binding of its promoter region to the transcription factor KLF9 in vitro. In addition, we found that the antiapoptotic effect of KLF9 deficiency was suppressed by silencing Txnrd2. These findings suggest that the accumulation and clearance of ROS are regulated by a Txnrd2-dependent mechanism controlled by KLF9.
In summary, KLF9 overexpression was found to downregulate Txnrd2 expression and activate extrinsic apoptosis mediated by the ROS/p53 signalling pathway in vivo and in vitro. This provides preliminary insight into the effects of KLF9 on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and the molecular mechanism underlying these effects. From a therapeutic standpoint, KLF9 deficiency may be a promising target for mitigating DOX-induced acute myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction. Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. First, the mechanism that underlies the upregulation of KLF9 expression after treatment with DOX is unknown. Second, although apoptosis was the primary focus of this study, other forms of regulated cell death, such as ferroptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, may also contribute to DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Future studies are needed to explore whether KLF9 is involved in the regulation of these alternative cell death pathways. Third, DOX is frequently administered in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents in clinical oncology. Therefore, while our study identified KLF9 as a key mediator in a controlled DOX-induced model, determining the specific contribution of DOX to myocardial injury in patients receiving polychemotherapy remains challenging. Future studies are warranted to determine whether the KLF9/ROS/p53 signalling pathway also contributes to cardiotoxicity under combination chemotherapy regimens. Finally, we acknowledge that the acute high-dose DOX model used in this study has limitations in mimicking the clinical scenario, which does not completely recapitulate the chronic and cumulative cardiotoxicity observed in clinical settings. Therefore, future studies using chronic low-dose models will be necessary to further validate the clinical relevance of our findings.

## Conclusions
KLF9 expression in the mouse heart and H9c2 cells increased significantly after DOX stimulation. Moreover, KLF9, as a partner, exerted a pro-apoptotic effect in the context of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity by inhibiting Txnrd2 expression and regulating the ROS/p53 signalling pathway. Gene therapy targeting KLF9 may provide a promising therapeutic approach to prevent DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.
