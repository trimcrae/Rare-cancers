## Abstract
The current array of traditional antibacterial agents targeting Gram-negative infections are failing to meet the clinical need. Here we present a novel, bifunctional immunotherapy (CTX-09) with the ability to harness endogenous anti-galactose-alpha-1,3-galactosyl-beta-1,4-N-acetyl-glucosamine (anti-αGal) antibodies to drive immune-mediated clearance of Gram-negative bacteria. In addition, CTX-09 has direct-acting broad-spectrum bactericidal activity equivalent to colistin and meropenem against 1952 Gram-negative clinical isolates. In vitro, CTX-09 demonstrated immune-mediated efficacy through recruitment of anti-αGal antibodies and engagement of antibody effector mechanisms that enhanced bacterial clearance at sub-bactericidal concentrations. In vivo, at sub-bactericidal doses, CTX-09 demonstrated anti-αGal antibody driven clearance of susceptible and multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains. In the presence of anti-αGal antibody, bacterial burden was reduced by >99.9% (3-log10) in neutropenic mouse thigh and pneumonia infection models. This data suggest that CTX-09 or other antibody-recruiting molecules have potential to address the urgent clinical need of patients with gram-negative infections using a novel immunotherapeutic mechanism.
Keywords: antibodies, complement, gram-negative, immunotherapy, phagocytosis

## Introduction
Many available treatments for Gram-negative bacterial infections are falling short of expected outcomes in the clinic. Recent Phase 3 studies of new antibacterial agents for both uncomplicated and complicated infections show a 35%–50% failure rate for both standard-of-care, and novel agent treatment groups, suggesting that traditional antibacterial approaches to infection are no longer sufficient for many patients. These reports indicate an urgent need for the development of next generation antibacterials, including non-traditional approaches to treat bacterial infections. Furthermore, in therapeutic development, a more complex model of infection is now being recognized in which not only the susceptibility of the bacterial pathogen is considered, but also patient factors such as age, comorbidities, and immune function. The importance of a patient-focused approach could indicate why novel, traditional therapeutics focusing fundamentally on minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) are insufficient in meeting the needs of current patient populations.
The ever-increasing spread of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria also poses significant challenges in healthcare settings. These pathogens can cause severe infections in vulnerable populations and threaten our ability to perform life-saving medical procedures. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is already a global threat with a mortality rate greater than human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/AIDS and malaria. Furthermore, an estimated 10-million annual deaths will be attributed to AMR infections by 2050 if antimicrobial development efforts do not become more productive soon. 
As the current range of traditional therapeutics are at risk of becoming increasingly ineffective, novel approaches capable of leveraging the immune system, and thereby bypassing resistance mechanisms, offer new strategies for antibiotic development. Antibody recruiting molecules (ARMs) re-direct endogenous antibodies towards disease-specific targets, including pathogenic bacteria and viruses. The ARM-mediated opsonization of pathogens via endogenous antibodies results in the elimination of the target by innate immune processes such as complement dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and antibody dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP). Initial proof-of-concept studies suggest that targeting bacteria with ARMs that engage endogenous anti-glycan antibodies could be a novel immunotherapeutic strategy to combat infections. 
Endogenous antibodies targeting αGal are among the most abundant anti-glycan antibodies present in humans. , αGal is a natural component of tissue in all mammalian species except for humans, apes, and old-world monkeys, who lack a functional alpha-1,3-galactosyltransferase (GGTA1) enzyme resulting in a lack of αGal synthesis. , Humans are exposed to αGal through epitopes expressed on the surface of commensal bacteria and ingestion of animal products, leading to the production of anti-αGal antibodies. , Anti-αGal antibodies are abundant in the sera of human population. In >95% of sera from 300 normal individuals, anti-αGal antibodies titers ranged between 1:800 and 1:1600. The anti-αGal antibody titer is at its lowest in babies 3–6 mo of age. By 2–4 yr of age, anti-αGal antibody titers have reached adult levels. The anti-αGal antibodies titer in elderly individuals (70–90 yr old) was found to be within the same range as that of young adults. Harnessing these anti-αGal antibodies via an ARM could be utilized to enhance the host response to infection, allowing for a more complete clearance of bacterial load.
We and others have demonstrated that ARMs comprising an oligonucleotide aptamer targeting domain could be used to facilitate anti-αGal antibody recruitment resulting in immune-mediated clearance of gram-positive bacteria in vitro and in vivo. , However, the use of aptamer targeting domains is highly pathogen-specific, limiting their clinical use to narrow-spectrum infections. An in vitro study showed promise for broader spectrum targeting of gram-negative bacteria by using a derivative of polymyxin B (PMB) as the targeting domain linked to a 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) effector moiety. This ARM was shown to recruit anti-DNP antibodies and induce effector functions to target Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa in vitro but no in vivo data were reported.
Here we present the application of ARM technology to target a broad-spectrum of Gram-negative bacteria and recruit endogenous anti-αGal antibodies using a novel antibacterial immunotherapy, CTX-09. CTX-09 is a synthetically designed compound comprised of an antimicrobial peptide (targeting domain), which is derived from polymyxin B (PMB) and an αGal trisaccharide (effector moiety) joined by a stable, non-cleavable chemical linker ( ). We hypothesized that linking αGal to PMB would maintain the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of the targeting domain and allow recruitment of anti-αGal antibodies to induce antibody effector mechanisms. This report confirms our hypothesis by demonstrating in vitro immune-mediated efficacy driving clearance of Gram-negative bacteria at sub-bactericidal concentrations of CTX-09, coupled with CTX-09-dependent anti-αGal enhanced bactericidal activity in vivo.
 

## Materials and methods

## Bacteria

E. coli ATCC 700973, E. coli ATCC 25922–GFP, and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were purchased from LGC standards; the remaining strains for in vitro and in vivo testing were purchased from UK Health Security Agency culture collections (see for more details on the above strains). The strains used in the 1952-strain panel for minimum inhibitory concentration testing were held by International Health Management Associates (IHMA) within the IHMA bacterial repository. Bacteria were stored as frozen glycerol stocks, with working stocks available as frozen aliquots and on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) (for up to 7 d). Strains containing GFP plasmids were stored on MHA supplemented with 100 µg/ml Ampicillin (Merck Life Sciences UK, A9518) for plasmid retention. All bacteria were grown to exponential phase in LB broth unless otherwise stated.

## Compound preparation
Both CTX-09 and Polymyxin B (Merck Life Sciences UK, P4932) were stored as lyophilized powder at 4°C. Working stocks of each compound were produced at 1 mM in sterile, nuclease-free water (Life Technologies LTD, AM9938) and aliquots maintained at −20°C for long term storage. On the day of experimental procedure, individual aliquots of compound were thawed at room temperature and diluted to working concentration in sterile, nuclease-free water.

## Susceptibility testing
Susceptibility profiles were established by determining minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). MIC testing was performed following Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Modal values of all repeats were reported. Results were converted from µg/ml to µM to allow for comparison between compounds with different molecular weights.

## Cytotoxicity
Human renal proximal tubule endothelial cells (Lonza, CC-2553) were seeded in appropriate media on 96-well tissue culture treated black-walled, clear-bottomed, collagen coated microplates and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 72 h. The cells were incubated as above for a further 24 h in serum free media. Cells were dosed with test compound at increasing concentrations up to and including 500 µM. After a 24-h incubation at 37°C, 5% CO2, cellular ATP dye (Promega, GA5010) was added as per manufacturer’s instructions, and wells scanned using an automated cellular imager (Agilent, BioTek Cytation).

## Anti-αGal antibody recruitment
Mid-exponential phase bacteria were washed once and photometrically adjusted to 2 × 109 CFU/ml in HBSS+/+ (Life Technologies LTD, 14025-050). 45 µL bacteria were combined with 5 µl CTX-09, Polymyxin B, or vehicle to a final compound concentration of 0.625 to 20 µM. After a 45-min incubation at 25°C with shaking at 450 rpm, bacteria were washed 3 times with HBSS+/+ and incubated with 25 µg/ml anti-αGal human IgM M86 antibody (Absolute Antibody, Ab00532) or 100 µg/ml anti-αGal IgG (affinity purified from human IVIG using an alpha-galactosyl-HSA sepharose column, Rockland Immunochemicals Inc) in HBSS+/+ for 60 min at 25°C, 450 rpm. Then, bacteria were washed as above and then incubated with either anti-IgM-FITC (Abcam, ab8497, 1:80 dilution) or anti-IgG-FITC (ThermoFisher Scientific, 31529, 1:40 dilution) for 60 minutes at 25°C, shaking at 450 rpm. After 3 washes with HBSS+/+, antibody recruitment was determined via flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX).

## Complement deposition and complement dependent cytotoxicity
Bacteria from MHA plate were grown overnight at 37°C in RPMI 1640 (Life Technologies LTD, 11835-030) + 10% LB broth. On the day of the experiment, the culture was diluted 1:100 in RPMI 1640 + 10% LB broth and incubated at 37°C to mid-exponential phase. Bacteria were washed in HBSS+/+ and photometrically adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/ml. 225 µl bacteria were combined with 25 µl CTX-09, Polymyxin B, or vehicle to a final concentration of 1.25–20 µM. The combination of bacteria and compound were added 1:3 to human serum (Merck Life Sciences UK, H4522) diluted in 1% (w/v) BSA (Merck Life Sciences UK, A2153) in DPBS+/+ (Life Technologies LTD, 14040-117) and incubated without shaking at 37°C for 20 minutes. Bacteria were pelleted and then resuspended in anti-C3b-PE (Biolegend, 846104, 1:25 dilution) or a 1:1 mix of anti-C3b-PE (1:25 dilution) and anti-C1q-APC (US Biological Life Sciences, C0010-02E-APC, 1:225 dilution). Following a 45-min incubation at 4°C, cells were washed twice with HBSS+/+ and then analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX) and fluorescence microscopy imaging (ThermoFisher Scientific, EVOS M5000); 100 µl of cell suspension was serially diluted 1:10 in HBSS+/+ and cultured on Mueller-Hinton agar for 16 to 18 h. Cell counts were collected using Colony Count software (Analytik Jena UK) and converted to CFU/ml values.

## Phagocytosis assay
Bacteria from MHA plates were grown overnight at 37°C in RPMI 1640 (Life Technologies LTD, 11835-030) + 10% LB broth. On the day of the experiment, the culture was diluted 1:100 in RPMI 1640 + 10% LB broth and incubated at 37°C to mid-exponential phase. Bacteria were washed in HBSS+/+ and photometrically adjusted to 1x107 CFU/ml. Also, 10 µl bacteria was added to 10 µl CTX-09 or vehicle to a final concentration between 0.2 and 0.5 µM. 80 µl human serum diluted in 1% (w/v) BSA (Merck Life Sciences UK, A2153) in DPBS+/+ (Life Technologies LTD, 14040-117) was added to the bacteria and compound suspension and incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C, 250 rpm. HL-60 cells (LGC standards, CCL-240) were differentiated to neutrophil-like cells as described by Nahm and Burton (2014). Differentiated HL-60 cells were added 1:1 to a concentration of 1 × 105/ml and the suspension incubated for a further 60 minutes at 37°C, shaking at 250 rpm. Anti-CD11b-PE (BD Bioscience, 561001) and NucBlue dye (ThermoFisher Scientific, R37605) were added as phagocytic cell markers for imaging and incubated for 30 min at 4°C. 50 µl cell suspension was removed and used for analysis by fluorescence microscopy imaging (ThermoFisher Scientific, EVOS M5000). A further 100 µl cell suspension was removed and serially diluted 1:10 in DPBS+/+ and cultured on Mueller-Hinton agar for 16–18 h at 37°C. CFU/ml values were determined as above.

## Animal experiments
All animal experiments were performed under UK Home Office Licenses and with local ethical committee clearance. All experiments were performed by technicians who had completed parts A, B, and C of the UK Home Office Personal License course and held a current personal license. The Evotec (UK) animal facility holds a UK Home Office Establishment License, is fully AAALAC-I accredited and is a dedicated Containment Level 2 facility.

## Pharmacokinetics
In sum, 8- to 10-week-old, female, GGTA1−/− C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories, UK) were treated with 150 mg/kg cyclophosphamide (Baxter Healthcare, 2638B3847) subcutaneously (s.c.) 4 d prior to study. A second dose of 100 mg/kg cyclophosphamide was administered s.c. 1 d prior to study. At 1 d prior to study, mice were passively immunized with 8.3 mg/kg anti-αGal human IgG (affinity purified human IVIG using an alpha-galactosyl-HSA sepharose column, Rockland Immunochemicals, Inc.) or PBS. On the day of the study, mice were injected s.c. with 11.7 mg/kg CTX-09 or PBS. 0.25–12 h after CTX-09 treatment, terminal blood samples from 3 mice per group were collected by posterior vena cava bleed. Blood was centrifuged at 2500×g for 10 min to collect plasma. CTX-09 concentration in the plasma was measured by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Anti-αGal human IgG concentration in the plasma was measured by ELISA.

## Neutropenic thigh infection model
The 8- to 10-week-old, female, GGTA1−/− C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories, UK) were rendered neutropenic with cyclophosphamide and passively immunized with 8.3 mg/kg anti-αGal human IgG or PBS as described above. On the day of infection, mice were infected with 2.2 × 104 CFU/thigh E. coli ATCC 700973 intra-muscularly, prepared from a frozen aliquot and grown to mid-exponential phase at 37°C. 11.5 mg/kg CTX-09 or PBS (vehicle) was administered s.c. q6h starting 6 h post infection (group size n = 7). A pre-treatment group (n = 4) was humanely euthanized 6 hours post infection. The remaining cohorts of mice were humanely euthanized 30 h post infection. Both thighs were collected individually and homogenized. Homogenate was serially diluted in PBS and cultured on Mueller-Hinton agar for 16 to 18 h at 37°C. Bacterial cell counts were collected and converted to CFU/g of tissue values.

## Neutropenic pneumonia model
Mice were prepared as above. Bacterial strains were grown to mid-exponential phase in Mueller-Hinton broth and then washed in PBS. In study 1, on the day of infection, mice were infected intranasally with 6.1 × 104 CFU P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853. 35 mg/kg CTX-09, or PBS was administered s.c. q6h starting 2 h post infection (group size n = 7). A pre-treatment group (n = 4) was humanely euthanized 2 h post infection. The remaining mice were humanely euthanized 26 h post infection. In study 2, on the day of infection, mice were infected intranasally with 3.6 × 104 CFU P. aeruginosa AR0064. 28.5 mg/kg or 35 mg/kg CTX-09 or PBS was administered s.c. q6h starting 2 h post infection (group size n = 7–8). A pre-treatment group (n = 5) was humanely euthanized 6 h post infection. The remaining mice were humanely euthanized 30 h post infection. The entire lungs were individually collected and homogenized. Bacterial burden per gram of lung tissue were determined as described above.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1 software. Where appropriate, Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality were performed prior to further statistical analysis. Unpaired t-test or Mann-Whitney U test were used to compare 2 groups; 1-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for comparison of 3 or more groups. Where necessary, two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons within 2 or more groups. Appropriate post hoc tests were performed to adjust for multiple comparisons. Likewise, where appropriate Holm-Šídák multiple unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction or Holm-Šídák multiple Mann-Whitney test was used with a significance threshold of α < 0.05. The significance threshold was defined as P< 0.05 in all analyses unless otherwise specified.

## Results

## CTX-09 displays antibacterial activity against an array of gram-negative strains at Sub-cytotoxic concentrations
To assess the direct antibacterial activity of CTX-09, susceptibility testing was performed by MIC on a panel of 1952 gram-negative clinical isolates, including MDR and extensively drug resistant (XDR) strains. The standard-of-care antibiotics colistin and meropenem, were included as control agents, and results are presented as micromolar (µM) concentrations to allow direct comparison of the compounds despite their different molecular weights. The concentration required to inhibit growth in 90% of the tested strains (MIC90) was determined for each bacterial genus.
CTX-09 exhibited broad-spectrum Gram-negative antibacterial activity with a susceptibility profile similar to colistin and meropenem ( ). The data confirms that CTX-09 possesses clinically relevant levels of inherent antibacterial activity against gram-negative pathogens, independent of any enhanced activity through engagement of anti-αGal antibodies and their subsequent effector functions.
 Cytotoxicity was estimated in vitro by measuring the decrease in cellular ATP generation from human renal proximal tubule epithelial cells treated with increasing concentrations of CTX-09. The mean concentration of CTX-09 required to reduce cellular ATP generation by 50% was 46.6 µM (± 12.45 µM, n = 12) (data not shown), >29-fold higher than the MIC90 measured against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter baumannii.

## CTX-09 recruits anti-αGal antibodies to gram-negative bacteria
Next, we aimed to evaluate the effector moiety of CTX-09 by measuring anti-αGal antibody recruitment to the surface of gram-negative bacteria. PMB was utilized as a negative control as CTX-09 is a PMB derivative conjugated to a linker with a terminal αGal moiety ( ). While both PMB and CTX-09 (data not shown) exert direct antimicrobial activity via bacterial membrane disruption, PMB exposure should not lead to anti-αGal recruitment or anti-αGal mediated effects in other immune assays ( ). Immunoglobulin (Ig) M are typically the first antibodies produced during infection and are highly effective at mediating complement deposition. To measure IgM recruitment, various gram-negative strains were incubated with compound, and anti-αGal IgM. A fold change in antibody recruitment over vehicle was calculated for both CTX-09 and PMB. CTX-09 demonstrated significant recruitment of anti-αGal IgM to non-MDR strains E. coli ATCC 700973, P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and ATCC 15692 (PAO1), K. pneumoniae NCTC 13465, as well as the MDR strain E. coli NCTC 13441, while PMB did not lead to significant anti-αGal binding as expected ( ).
IgG antibodies are the principal isotype in human blood, they are produced later in an infection by plasma cells and have a higher affinity for their specific antigen than IgM. Like IgM, IgG induces effector mechanisms such as opsonophagocytosis and complement deposition. Anti-αGal IgG was affinity-purified from human serum (HS) and its specificity for αGal confirmed by ELISA (data not shown). E. coli ATCC 700973 was incubated with increasing concentrations of compound and affinity purified anti-αGal IgG. A fold change over vehicle was calculated at each concentration for both CTX-09 and PMB. As shown in , CTX-09 demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in IgG recruitment that was significantly greater than PMB at concentrations ≥2.5 µM.
Recruitment of anti-αGal antibodies to a range of Gram-negative bacterial strains, including equivalent activity against an MDR strain, confirms the potential of CTX-09 to act as a bifunctional compound. To determine whether CTX-09 recruits anti-αGal antibodies from HS and subsequently induces an enhanced immune response, we sought to demonstrate enhancement of antibody-dependent effector mechanisms that increase bacterial killing in vitro.

## Antibodies present in human sera are recruited by CTX-09 and induce effector functions
We next elucidated whether anti-αGal antibodies recruited by CTX-09 could induce effector functions such as complement deposition leading to CDC, and ADCP. To measure complement deposition, E. coli ATCC 25922 engineered to express green fluorescent protein (GFP) was incubated with HS and CTX-09. The relative fold increase in C3b deposition was calculated at each CTX-09 concentration compared to HS alone. C3b deposition was increased in HS supplemented with CTX-09 in a dose-dependent manner ( ). A peak increase of 7384-fold in C3b deposition over HS alone was observed at 20 µM, with a > 6000-fold increase observed at concentrations ≥5µM. This suggests that CTX-09 can significantly enhance complement pathway activation beyond the normal levels seen during bacterial infection. This was visually confirmed using fluorescence microscopy ( ) with a clear increase in co-localization of GFP-E. coli and PE-labelled C3b at 2.5 µM CTX-09 compared to HS alone.
 As the C3b protein is conserved across all complement pathways, we next established if the increase in C3b deposition resulted from induction of the antibody-mediated, classical complement pathway. C1q is a complement protein exclusive to the classical pathway and its presence would, therefore, indicate antibody recruitment leading to classical complement pathway activation and downstream C3b deposition. We measured both C1q and C3b deposition to E. coli ATCC 700973 incubated in HS. CTX-09 was compared to PMB, a compound that should not increase classical pathway-associated complement deposition. The percentage of C1q+C3b+ E. coli increased in a dose-dependent manner from 5 µM CTX-09 yet did not increase in the presence of increasing PMB concentrations ( ). At 20 µM CTX-09, a mean of 74.4% of E. coli cells were C1q+C3b+, compared to only 16.3% of E. coli cells at 20 µM PMB, demonstrating induction of antibody-mediated complement deposition by CTX-09. further highlights the enhanced classical pathway activation in the presence of 20 µM CTX-09 compared to HS alone.
Next, we looked to establish if CDC was an outcome of the observed complement deposition induced by CTX-09. We compared the CDC of E. coli ATCC 700973 in the presence of HS and increasing CTX-09 concentrations. CDC was increased in a CTX-09 dose-dependent manner, reaching the limit of detection by 20 µM ( ). All concentrations of CTX-09 tested demonstrated significant reductions in colony forming units (CFU) compared to HS alone. The reduction in bacterial burden was compared between HS alone, CTX-09 alone, and HS supplemented with CTX-09 to assess the additional effect of HS on CTX-09 driven bactericidal activity ( ). In sum, 20 µM CTX-09 in the absence of HS kills approximately 99% (2-log10) of E. coli ATCC 700973, which increases to >99.99% (>4-log10) in the presence of HS. As HS alone did not reduce CFU compared to stasis, these data indicate CTX-09 increases the sensitivity of E. coli ATCC 700973 to CDC through direct membrane destabilization and activation of the classical complement pathway.
ADCP is another effector mechanism induced by antibodies that leads to the uptake and clearance of bacterial cells ( ). We sought to demonstrate enhancement in ADCP in the presence of CTX-09 through the recruitment of anti-αGal antibodies from HS. HL-60 cells were differentiated to neutrophil-like cells with phagocytic capacity (dHL-60 cells) to facilitate phagocytosis. Initially, bacteria were incubated with dHL-60 cells and increasing concentrations of CTX-09 in the presence or absence of HS ( ). In the presence of 5% HS, CTX-09 concentrations ≥0.3 µM significantly increased clearance of bacterial burden, whereas in the absence of HS, clearance was not significantly increased until 0.5 µM. To highlight the additional effect of phagocytosis, dHL-60 cell addition was compared at each CTX-09 concentration in the presence of HS ( ) and the effects of each combination of components compared at a sub-bactericidal concentration of CTX-09 ( ). Clearance of bacterial burden was significantly greater in the presence of dHL-60 cells compared to HS supplemented with 0.5 µM CTX-09. Furthermore, a significant reduction in burden below stasis was achieved in the presence of a combination of CTX-09, HS, and dHL-60 cells, with this condition demonstrating a > 99% (>2-log10) reduction. CTX-09 was compared to PMB to confirm ADCP ( ). CTX-09 exhibited serum-enhanced bactericidal activity at 0.4 μM demonstrating an immune mediated phagocytic response. PMB demonstrated bacterial clearance at 0.125 μM irrespective of the presence of human sera, indicating that PMB anti-bacterial activity was not derived from an immune mediated phagocytic response. Fluorescence microscopy showed increased colocalization of GFP-labeled E. coli ATCC 25922 and dHL-60 cells in the presence of 0.2 µM CTX-09 in HS compared to HS alone and 0.2 µM CTX-09 in the absence of HS ( ).
 From these data, we have confirmed CTX-09 dependent enhancement of antibody effector functions, specifically CDC and ADCP, at sub-bactericidal concentrations of CTX-09. This presents an in vitro proof-of-concept for immune-enhanced efficacy.

## CTX-09 exhibits anti-αGal enhanced efficacy in an E. coli thigh infection model
To illustrate the in vivo capabilities of CTX-09, we utilized a neutropenic GGTA1-/- mouse model to assess the reduction in E. coli burden in the thigh. Wild-type mice could not be used as they express αGal while GGTA1−/− mice lack the GGTA1 enzyme required for αGal synthesis. We passively immunized the animals with anti-αGal IgG to assess enhanced CTX-09 activity mediated by anti-αGal antibodies.
Prior to the efficacy studies, plasma pharmacokinetics (PKplasma) of CTX-09 and anti-αGal IgG were measured over 12 h in uninfected neutropenic GGTA1−/− mice ( ). Mice were passively immunized with human anti-αGal IgG 24 h prior to sub-cutaneous dosing of CTX-09. PKplasma data indicated a CTX-09 half-life of 0.9 h. CTX-09 remained quantifiable in the blood until 6 h post-injection. This defined the dosing strategy for the efficacy studies as every 6 h. The anti-αGal IgG demonstrated no reduction in plasma concentration at 12 h post-CTX-09 injection (36 h post-immunization). This indicated that booster immunizations would not be required in the study. The model design is outlined in . In brief, female GGTA1−/− mice were treated with cyclophosphamide at 4 d and 1 d pre-infection, to induce neutropenia. At 24 h pre-infection the mice were passively immunized with human anti-αGal IgG. Animals were infected with E. coli ATCC 700973 intra-muscularly and CTX-09 or mock dosing began 6 h post-infection.
 Treatment with vehicle following passive immunization with anti-αGal IgG did not reduce bacterial burden in the thigh relative to mock immunized and vehicle treated mice, suggesting the antibody alone had no significant effect on the bacterial burden ( ). Likewise, treatment with a sub-bactericidal concentration of CTX-09 alone did not significantly reduce burden in the thigh compared to vehicle. However, CTX-09 in combination with anti-αGal IgG significantly reduced the bacterial burden compared to vehicle, and compared to CTX-09 treatment alone, to >1-log10 below the level of stasis. This suggests the αGal effector moiety is significantly enhancing in vivo efficacy through the recruitment of anti-αGal antibodies, allowing bacterial clearance at sub-bactericidal concentrations of CTX-09.

## CTX-09 demonstrates anti-αGal enhanced clearance of two P. aeruginosa strains in the lung

P. aeruginosa is an opportunistic pathogen associated with hospital- and ventilator-acquired pneumonia (HAP/VAP). In 2017, an estimated 32,600 cases of MDR P. aeruginosa were reported in the United States alone, with an 8.3% mortality rate. To determine in vivo efficacy against one susceptible strain and one MDR strain of P. aeruginosa, we investigated a pneumonia model in neutropenic mice. As described above, GGTA1−/− mice were treated with cyclophosphamide to induce neutropenia and passively immunized with human anti-αGal IgG. Mice were infected with P. aeruginosa intra-nasally prior to CTX-09 or mock dosing.
We were, once again, able to demonstrate an anti-αGal enhanced clearance of bacterial burden in vivo using CTX-09. Treatment with 35 mg/kg (q6h) CTX-09 significantly reduced the P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 burden compared to vehicle, demonstrating direct anti-bacterial activity in the lung ( ). The reduction in bacterial burden was further reduced to within 1-log10 of stasis upon addition of anti-αGal IgG. Treatment with 28.5 mg/kg (q6h) CTX-09 alone did not reduce the burden of MDR strain P. aeruginosa AR0064 compared to vehicle ( ). However, the addition of anti-αGal IgG in combination with the sub-bactericidal dose of CTX-09, reduced bacterial burden below the level of stasis, a significant reduction compared to vehicle. This suggests that at sub-bactericidal concentrations of CTX-09, clearance of both susceptible and MDR P. aeruginosa strains is enhanced because of the immune-effector moiety.
 Lower bioavailability of parenterally administered polymyxins in pleural tissues (epithelial lining fluid) vs. soft tissues (including muscle) and the systemic circulation is well described. Thus, efficacy in the lung model required higher doses of CTX-09 (28.5–35 mg/kg q6h) compared to the thigh infection model (11.5 mg/kg q 6 h).
These data demonstrate the capacity for CTX-09 lung penetration and enhanced efficacy in the presence of anti-αGal antibody at drug concentrations that exhibit direct anti-bacterial activity and at drug concentrations that are sub-bactericidal.

## Discussion
The unmet need of patients with bacterial infections has driven the requirement for advancements in non-traditional therapeutic development. This study outlines a proof-of-concept for CTX-09, a novel immunotherapy that illustrates direct antibacterial activity coupled with enhanced efficacy induced by anti-αGal antibody recruitment.
The broad-spectrum bactericidal activity of CTX-09 is an important measure of its suitability as a next-generation antibacterial therapy. Many novel therapeutics are pathogen specific and thus have limited usage in hospital environments due to the time delays in identifying organisms and their susceptibility profiles to the standard-of-care. For life-threatening infections, this process may not be possible, leading to a dependency on the trusted last-resort drugs available. Demonstrating an equivalent susceptibility profile to colistin and meropenem, independent of the action of the effector moiety, is indicative of the inherent potential of CTX-09 for use in the clinic as a broad-spectrum antibacterial agent for Gram-negative infections. However, susceptibility testing is not always representative of efficacy in dynamic systems due to the controlled testing conditions of MIC assays. , Variabilities in inoculum level and incubation times can have notable consequences on apparent activity. Likewise, traditional MIC testing does not account for the mechanism of action (MOA) of non-traditional therapeutic agents, such as ARMs. For example, the bifunctional activity of CTX-09 allows clearance of less-susceptible organisms which could improve the susceptibility profile and reduce the risk of resistance. Therefore, it was necessary for us to further characterize CTX-09 in vitro to display the potential superiority of the mechanism compared to traditional antibacterials.
In this study, we showed that engagement of CDC and ADCP drives additional bacterial killing in vitro. Anti-αGal antibody recruitment therefore is fundamental to the immune-mediated activity of CTX-09. CTX-09 showed significant levels of both IgG and IgM recruitment, the latter to a range of Gram-negative strains. Due to the antibody-mediated complement deposition, we can also infer that anti-αGal antibodies are recruited from HS. CDC induces cell lysis via recruitment of a series of complement proteins leading to insertion of the membrane attack complex (MAC) in the target cell. , As all the described complement pathways can result in MAC formation, this is a rapid and effective mechanism of clearance. Like PMB, CTX-09 may initiate the alternative and lectin complement pathways via membrane destabilization through the targeting domain; however, CTX-09 has also demonstrated classical pathway activation that significantly increases complement deposition compared to PMB and induces CDC at sub-bactericidal concentrations. Besides CDC, complement pathway activation plays a critical role in ADCP, with the C5a protein acting as a chemoattractant for phagocytic cells. , Upon infiltration of the site of infection, the Fc-gamma receptor on phagocytic cells recognizes the Fc portion of bound antibodies to induce phagocytosis of target cells. The significant anti-αGal recruitment exhibited by CTX-09 indicates the potential for ADCP-mediated clearance of a broad spectrum of gram-negative pathogens in vivo. We demonstrated CTX-09 enhanced ADCP of E. coli, in vitro, that significantly reduced bacterial burden below the level of stasis and anticipate this mechanism to transfer to further strains in future studies.
Neutropenic murine infection models are commonly used for predicting efficacy of traditional antibacterial agents during development. , , Induction of neutropenia via cyclophosphamide treatment reduces leukocyte counts and suppresses antibody formation, aside other systemic effects. , This is useful for traditional therapies as the immune response does not begin to clear the infection prior to compound dosing, thus providing a clearer understanding of efficacy. It also mimics immunosuppression in vulnerable patients. However, for characterization of CTX-09, the neutropenic model fails to fully represent the immune-mediated efficacy of the compound as ADCP is inhibited. Testing the in vivo activity of CTX-09 in immunocompetent mice would capture more fully the immune-mediated potency of CTX-09. However, establishing reliable lung or thigh infection models with most gram-negative bacterial strains in immunocompetent mice is technically challenging, with variable bacterial yields necessitating the use of large numbers of animals. Bacteria are naturally cleared in many animals even with high CFU inoculums, complicating the interpretation of any study with therapeutic test articles.
Despite this, we demonstrated promising results in an E. coli thigh infection model. We showed significant anti-αGal-mediated clearance at a sub-bactericidal concentration of CTX-09, outlining an in vivo proof-of-concept for the technology. CDC is anticipated to be a leading MOA for this enhanced efficacy due to the previously mentioned reduction in leukocyte counts in neutropenic animals. Induction of αGal-mediated activity to enhance the host response at sub-bactericidal concentrations could allow for lower dosing in humans and subsequently lower the risks of nephrotoxicity often associated with AMPs, particularly polymyxins. , This is also promising for the treatment of immunosuppressed patients who may be deficient in phagocytic cells, offering an alternative mechanism for leveraging their available immune system to help clear infections.
Polymyxin class antibiotics are known to have poor lung exposure upon systemic administration. Despite nebulized polymyxin treatment indicating reduced nephrotoxicity and increased lung exposure compared to intravenous administration in humans, there remains controversy around its inclusion in the clinic due to limited evidence of superiority. , , This suggests a class of standard-of-care agents contain notable inherent risk to the patient, with one study indicating a 50% chance of renal failure. The activity of CTX-09 in our P. aeruginosa pneumonia model outlined successful penetration of compound and human anti-αGal antibody into the lung tissue through non-aerosolized administration. Furthermore, the observed reduction in bacterial burden to the level of stasis, in the presence of anti-αGal is significantly improved compared to vehicle. This was observed against a non-MDR strain and a MDR strain of P. aeruginosa. The reduction in lung burden compared to CTX-09 treatment alone is >90% (>1-log10) against both strains, indicating that this promising immune mechanism is also effective in the lung. A limitation of the study is that we were not able to directly demonstrate that CTX-09 is superior to PMB in an in vivo infection model. PMB is known to be poorly tolerated at efficacious doses in murine pneumonia models, and an in-house study demonstrated that PMB was only tolerated in a murine P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 pneumonia model when dosed at or below 15 mg/kg when administered s.c. every 6 h as per the CTX-09 regimen (data not shown). In mice treated with this dose of PMB, mean bacterial lung burdens of P. aeruginosa did not reach stasis and were inferior to the mean lung burdens seen in later CTX-09 efficacy studies.
Using αGal as an effector moiety has proven to be effective at recruiting endogenous anti-αGal antibodies from HS and demonstrated efficacy in vivo but it is not without limitation. The αGal trisaccharide is arduous to produce in large quantities, increasing the cost of goods and reducing scalability. Also, the emergence of αGal syndrome (AGS), a rare allergy to red meat caused by a tick bite, has implications for CTX-09. Individuals with AGS produce an IgE response to αGal that could induce anaphylaxis upon CTX-09 administration. However, other αGal-containing therapies have safely been administered to humans due to the management of AGS in clinical settings. The epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitor, Cetuximab, had issues with AGS responses upon release , but has since proven successful in the clinic. The anti-tumor vaccine AGI-134 also recently met its primary endpoint of safety and tolerability in healthy human volunteers. 
In summary, we have established the proof of concept that an antibody recruiting molecule based on an AMP binding moiety may have therapeutic potential via direct, broad-spectrum antibacterial activity coupled with engagement of endogenous anti-αGal antibodies. We have outlined the MOA in vitro, showing CTX-09 has the capacity to induce antibody-mediated effector mechanisms, including CDC and ADCP, to enhance bacterial clearance. Furthermore, we have established efficacy in vivo in murine thigh infection and pneumonia models, demonstrating effective penetration of the compound into the tissue and anti-αGal-mediated enhancement of activity. The significant anti-αGal antibody recruitment to an MDR E. coli strain and activity in the MDR pneumonia model indicates that CTX-09 may also offer an approach for reducing the risk of untreatable infections in humans caused by MDR organisms.
In conclusion, novel antibacterial therapies are crucial to overcome the limitations of traditional antibiotics, particularly against multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria. While passive immunotherapies such as neutralizing monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have been successfully developed for preventing or treating viral diseases or bacterial toxin-mediated illnesses, no FDA-approved opsonizing mAbs against Gram-negative bacteria exist, and the spectrum of clinical candidate molecules is typically narrow as they are often bacterial species- or strain-specific. Our proof-of-concept study with CTX-09 demonstrates that ARMs with broad-spectrum binding to clinically relevant gram-negative bacteria that can recruit pre-existing endogenous antibodies, may offer a promising strategy for treating Gram-negative infections, particularly those caused by MDR strains. CTX-09 stands out as a unique ARM as it combines broad-spectrum binding and antibody-recruitment activity with inherent antibiotic properties. Further investigation into the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and safety (including in vivo toxicity studies) for CTX-09 and similar ARMs to investigate their clinical potential are warranted.
