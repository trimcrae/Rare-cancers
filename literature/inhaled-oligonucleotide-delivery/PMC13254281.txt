Using DNA repair-deficient (Aag-/-;Mgmt-/-) and wild-type mice, we systematically compare the effects of NDMA exposure in juveniles and adults. Juvenile Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice are profoundly more vulnerable, exhibiting persistent DNA damage, inflammation, and mutations that lead to liver pathology and tumorigenesis, particularly in males. ## Introduction
While children and adolescents account for a quarter of the population, our understanding of their potential heightened risk from environmental carcinogens remains limited . In this study, we investigated the role of age as a susceptibility factor to N-nitrosamine exposure. N-Nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA), a probable human carcinogen, exemplifies this concern . NDMA has been detected in various sources, including food, drinking water, cigarette smoke, and pharmaceuticals. Although epidemiological evidence in humans remains limited, there are studies that link NDMA exposure to elevated risks of bladder, stomach, prostate, and liver cancers, as well as leukemia and multiple myeloma , . An epidemiological study in Wilmington, Massachusetts identified a statistically significant association between in utero NDMA exposure and childhood cancer, emphasizing the potential vulnerability of younger populations . Understanding the mechanisms underlying age-dependent responses to NDMA in experimental models is therefore critical.
NDMA exposure through inhalation, ingestion, or injection increases the incidence of liver, lung, and kidney tumors in rodents . The adverse health effects of NDMA exposure in animals are most severe in the liver and include centrilobular necrosis, hemorrhage, fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and cancer. NDMA’s mutagenicity arises from its metabolism by cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1), which generates the highly reactive methyldiazonium ion. This intermediate methylates DNA to form mutagenic adducts such as O6-methylguanine (O6MeG) and 3-methyladenine (3MeA). If unrepaired, these adducts can induce mutations, toxicity, and inflammation, thus promoting carcinogenesis and aging , – . O6MeG is restored to guanine through direct reversal by the suicide protein O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT), while the remediation of 3MeA via base excision repair is initiated by 3-alkyladenine DNA glycosylase (AAG/MPG).
Mice lacking MGMT or AAG exhibit heightened susceptibility to NDMA-induced genotoxicity and tumorigenesis , – . In MGMT knockout mice, O6MeG mispairs with thymine during replication, while in AAG knockouts, 3MeA is bypassed with errors, both increasing point mutations – . Mismatch repair of O6MeG/T can generate single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) gaps and double-strand breaks (DSBs), and replication-blocking adducts like 3MeA can lead to replication fork stress , , – . These lesions may be resolved via homologous recombination, which can cause large-scale sequence rearrangements including deletions, translocations, or loss of heterozygosity, all of which are commonly associated with cancer , . Collectively, these mechanisms illustrate the mutagenic potential of unrepaired NDMA-induced adducts.
The downstream consequences of NDMA-induced DNA adduct formation extend beyond mutagenesis. Persistent mismatches, ssDNA gaps, stalled forks, and DSBs activate DNA damage response (DDR) pathways, triggering both cell death and compensatory tissue regeneration – . These DDR pathways concurrently stimulate inflammatory responses and interferon signaling, driving the activation of hepatic progenitor cells and resident macrophages, as well as the recruitment of circulating immune cells to injury sites. While these processes initially function to promote repair, chronic activation fosters tumorigenesis by enhancing cellular proliferation and survival signaling, thus creating a microenvironment conducive to mutation accumulation – .
Although NDMA toxicity has been widely studied in animal models, the influence of age on the severity of NDMA-induced DNA damage, inflammation, mutagenesis, and long-term liver pathology remains largely unexplored . To address this knowledge gap, we exposed both juvenile and adult mice to NDMA via drinking water and systematically evaluated molecular, cellular, and tissue-level responses. We utilized mice lacking both AAG and MGMT genes (Aag-/-;Mgmt-/-) as a mechanistic model that serves several critical purposes: (1) both MGMT and AAG exhibit substantial interindividual variation in humans , , , making the model relevant to studies of gene-environment interactions; (2) the model amplifies NDMA-induced responses, thus requiring fewer animals; and (3) it provides mechanistic insights into how DNA repair capacity and age interact to influence carcinogenic outcomes. We selected a relatively high drinking water dose of 5 ppm NDMA to ensure measurable biological effects within the two-week exposure period and to align with established toxicological studies .
Using this approach, we show that age is a critical determinant of the NDMA response, with younger mice exhibiting substantially greater DNA damage, interferon-mediated inflammation, mutations, and liver pathology. While this age-dependent susceptibility was most pronounced in DNA repair-deficient mice, a similar effect was also observed in WT animals, demonstrating developmental stage as a key risk factor even when repair pathways are intact. Importantly, neither CYP2E1 expression nor DNA adduct levels varied significantly by age, suggesting that age-related risk reflects the proliferation-dependent processing of adducts rather than differences in metabolism. This conclusion is supported by the increased susceptibility to NDMA-induced genotoxicity in adult mice treated with proliferation-stimulating triiodothyronine (T3). We also observed sex-dependent differences, consistent with known sexual dimorphism in liver biology. By revealing how age, DNA repair capacity, and sex interact to shape the response to a relevant environmental carcinogen, our study provides new insight into the mechanisms underlying early life vulnerability and highlights the importance of considering developmental stage in toxicological assessment.

## Results

## Enhanced juvenile genotoxicity is independent of DNA adduct levels
To investigate how age modulates the genotoxicity of NDMA, we exposed DNA repair-deficient (Aag-/-;Mgmt-/-) mice to 5 ppm NDMA in drinking water for two weeks, starting at postnatal day 21 (juveniles) or day 90 (adults) (Fig.  ). Formalin-fixed liver sections were then analyzed for H2AX phosphorylated at Ser139 (γH2AX), a marker of replication stress and DNA DSBs . Juvenile mice exhibited a striking increase in γH2AX levels immediately following the 14-day NDMA exposure period (14 + 0 d), an effect entirely absent in adults (Fig.  ). Importantly, γH2AX persisted in juvenile livers 10 days post-exposure but remained undetectable in adults (Fig.  ). Western blot analysis reinforced these age-specific disparities, revealing significantly elevated γH2AX only in younger animals after NDMA treatment (Fig.  ). To confirm that this age-dependent response was independent of exposure route, we also administered NDMA via intraperitoneal injection (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistently, juvenile mice showed a stronger γH2AX response by both histology and western blot following direct exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ). This robust, age-dependent induction of γH2AX across exposure routes highlights the particular vulnerability of juvenile mice to unresolved replication stress and DSBs. 
Stressed replication forks and DNA strand breaks can trigger DDRs . To further explore, we analyzed p53 phosphorylated at Ser15 (p-p53), a central effector in DDR pathways, which was significantly increased in juveniles treated with 5 ppm NDMA but not in adults (Fig.  ). Furthermore, NDMA exposure in younger mice resulted in increased total p53 (Supplementary Fig.  ). p53 activation can lead to increased transcription of p21, promoting cell cycle arrest . Western blot results showed p21 induction exclusively in juveniles (Fig.  ). This p21-mediated cell cycle arrest in response to NDMA exposure provides extended time for DNA repair processes, such as homologous recombination. RAD51, critical for homologous recombination repair of DNA DSBs and replication fork processing, was upregulated only in juveniles (Fig.  ) . Collectively, these findings demonstrate that younger mice mount a heightened, age-dependent DDR to NDMA.
Disparities in DDRs may stem from differences in adduct levels. To explore this, we quantified the levels of O6MeG and 3MeA by mass spectrometry. Both adducts were substantially increased in juveniles and adults after NDMA exposure (Fig.  ), but no significant differences were detected between age groups. This suggests that upstream processes such as NDMA metabolism and adduct formation are comparable across ages. Supporting this view, CYP2E1 protein levels were similar in untreated juvenile and adult control mice before and after the exposure timeline (Fig.  ). Thus, the pronounced sensitivity of juveniles to NDMA-induced DDRs occurs independently of adduct levels. Having established that age strikingly influences the cellular response to NDMA-induced DNA damage, we next examined how these genotoxic effects shape inflammatory responses and tissue regeneration.

## Prolonged inflammation and compensatory proliferation in juveniles
DNA damage and responses activate interferon signaling and downstream immune pathways, which, under chronic conditions, can establish a self-perpetuating cycle of tissue injury , . As such, we assessed STAT1 phosphorylated at Tyr701 (pSTAT1), a central marker of the interferon response . A significant increase in pSTAT1 was observed only in juvenile Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice exposed to NDMA (Fig.  ), along with elevated total STAT1 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Adults, by contrast, exhibited no significant changes in pSTAT1 or STAT1. Sustained interferon activation through STAT1 in response to DNA damage can lead to STAT3 activation (pSTAT3; Tyr705), forming a negative feedback loop that regulates inflammation and DDRs , , . pSTAT3 was upregulated only in juvenile mice (Fig.  ), reflecting unresolved inflammation. 
Induction of proinflammatory interferon-stimulated genes such as STAT1 can enhance immune activation, driving pathological outcomes. In line with this expectation, juvenile mice displayed moderately increased histiocytic and lymphocytic cell foci following NDMA exposure, an effect that was not as prominent in adults (Fig.  ). Although inflammation in juveniles largely resolved by 10 days post-exposure, a trend persisted (Supplementary Fig.  ). To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the observed pathology, we examined molecular markers of immune cells. NDMA exposure in juvenile mice led to a significant upregulation of immune cell markers CD3e (Fig.  ) and CD11C (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating stimulation and recruitment of immune cells to the liver . These immune responses were absent in adult mice, highlighting the unique vulnerability of younger animals.
The recruitment and activation of immune cells drive the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), a response particularly evident in juveniles, as shown by the significant upregulation of antioxidant enzyme HO-1 (Fig.  ) . This effect was also observed following intraperitoneal injection exclusively in juveniles, supporting the pronounced redox imbalance induced by NDMA in younger mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Excessive RONS levels can lead to hepatocellular degeneration, which was significantly more severe in juveniles treated with 5 ppm NDMA than in adults, as evidenced by the distinct multifocal, centrilobular hepatocellular degeneration (Fig.  ) . This injury persisted at 10 days and even 3 months post-exposure in juveniles (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating long-term hepatic damage in younger mice.
To further dissect the consequences of NDMA-induced DNA adducts and associated inflammation, we analyzed markers of cell death across age groups. Western blot analysis demonstrated that a key apoptosis marker, cleaved caspase-3, was increased only in juvenile mice following NDMA exposure, but not adults (Fig.  ) . While single-cell necrosis was not immediately apparent (Fig.  ), it became significantly elevated only in juveniles by 10 days post treatment (Fig.  ) . These results indicate that younger mice sustain prolonged and multifaceted hepatocellular damage after NDMA exposure, with apoptosis dominating acutely and necrosis emerging during recovery.
Following organ damage and inflammation, tissues undergo regenerative cell proliferation – . Liver regeneration, as measured by Ki67 staining, was heightened in juveniles (Fig.  ) . Western blot analysis confirmed increased expression of proliferation markers MCM2 and PCNA specifically in juvenile mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). This proliferative response remained elevated for at least 10 days post-exposure (Fig.  ), indicating ongoing regeneration in younger mice. In adults, Ki67 levels remained unchanged, emphasizing a lack of compensatory proliferation. When NDMA was administered via intraperitoneal injection, juveniles consistently exhibited cell death (cleaved caspase-3; Supplementary Fig.  ) and proliferation (Ki67 and PCNA; Supplementary Fig.  ), mirroring responses seen with drinking water exposure. These findings reveal that interferon-driven immune activation and oxidative stress promotes age-dependent liver injury and regenerative proliferation in juveniles.

## Younger mice display persistent transcriptomic dysregulation
The liver’s capacity for regeneration and detoxification is governed by a complex network of DNA repair, cell cycle, and immune pathways, which is largely shaped by changes in gene expression – . To uncover how developmental stage shapes the hepatic response to NDMA, we analyzed the transcriptome in juvenile and adult Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice immediately after exposure and during recovery (Supplementary Data  ). Juvenile mice demonstrated an 11-fold increase in the number of significantly upregulated or downregulated genes compared to adults (Fig.  ), highlighting a much broader transcriptional disruption in younger animals. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of juveniles revealed clear segregation of NDMA-treated and untreated samples (Fig.  ), with global gene expression patterns primarily defined by NDMA exposure rather than sex. Even after a 10-day recovery, juveniles retained substantial gene expression changes, whereas adults exhibited only minor alterations (Fig.  ). These findings reveal the magnitude of age-dependent transcriptomic reprogramming in response to NDMA. 
Consistent with protein-level data (Fig.  ), juveniles exhibited robust upregulation of DDR genes, including Cdkn1a (p21), Ccng1, Ddit4l, and Pttg1, all persisting through the 10-day recovery (Fig.  ) , – . In contrast, adults showed induction of only Cdkn1a (p21) immediately following exposure, albeit 2-fold less than juveniles (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ), with no corresponding, statistically significant increase in protein levels (Fig.  ). Pathway enrichment further revealed the exclusive activation of DDR-related pathways (p53, UV Response Up) in juveniles, with the p53 pathway continuing 10 days post-exposure (Fig.  ). Adults showed no comparable pathway activation, underscoring their resilience. DDR orchestrates cell fate decisions, and in juveniles, we observed concurrent activation of mTORC1 signaling, a key regulator of growth and metabolism , and apoptotic pathways (Fig.  ). This simultaneous engagement of proliferative and cell death mechanisms in response to unresolved DNA damage uniquely characterizes the juvenile hepatic response, and is further supported by protein expression and histological analyses (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ).
DNA damage and response pathways are well-established activators of innate immune signaling and subsequent inflammation . In juveniles, NDMA exposure led to a significant upregulation of immune modulators, including Stat1, Cd63, Ifi27, Igtp, Iigp1, Serpina3n, Ly6e, and Mbl2 (Fig.  ) , , . While expression of all of these immune-related genes remained elevated in juveniles for at least 10 days post-exposure, only one gene was induced at both timepoints in adults. Inflammatory processes are also known to transcriptionally suppress Cyp2e1 , the primary enzyme mediating NDMA bioactivation, which was notably downregulated exclusively in younger mice even at 10 days post-exposure (Fig.  ). This suppression likely reflects robust inflammation and stress signaling following NDMA exposure, consistent with established mechanisms in which activation of pathways such as NF-κB leads to decreased hepatic Cyp2e1 expression during liver injury and immune activation . Enrichment analysis also revealed a strong and sustained activation of interferon-α/γ and inflammatory pathways only in juveniles, as well as TNF-α signaling via NF-κB by 10 days post-exposure (Fig.  ) . These findings, along with the inflammation observed via immunoblotting and histopathology (Fig.  ), demonstrate a persistent, pro-inflammatory hepatic environment in younger mice.
The liver activates detoxification genes as a protective mechanism against injury, mitigating cellular damage and facilitating recovery . In juvenile mice, key detoxification genes including Gsta2, Gstm1, and Ephx1 showed sustained upregulation post-injury, a response absent in adults (Fig.  ) , . These genes, classically regulated by the Nrf2 signaling axis, are activated in response to oxidative and electrophilic stress, indicating ongoing oxidative injury during recovery . Functional enrichment highlighted xenobiotic metabolism pathways exclusively in juveniles (Fig.  ), consistent with histological and biochemical evidence of prolonged hepatic injury (Fig.  ). Paradoxically, NDMA exposure also suppressed several detoxification genes (Fabp1, Cat, Hacl1, Oat) in juveniles, potentially exacerbating hepatotoxicity by impairing clearance mechanisms , – . The concurrent upregulation of stress-responsive genes and downregulation of constitutive metabolic genes suggests unresolved hepatic injury, wherein protective mechanisms are engaged but normal detoxification capacity remains compromised – , – .
To determine whether these effects persist at lower exposure levels, we examined transcriptomic responses to a more environmentally relevant NDMA dose (5 ppb). Juveniles exhibited upregulation of cellular stress-related genes such as Cyb5r3, Cox8a, Fth1, Ftl1, Calr, Pdia3, and Exosc5 (Supplementary Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ) – , but lacked robust activation of detoxification or inflammatory genes , . Altogether, these findings reveal a clear dose-dependent response: higher NDMA concentrations in juveniles drive persistent DNA damage, inflammation, compensatory proliferation, and metabolic stress, fostering an environment permissive for mutation accumulation and tumor initiation. However, even brief juvenile exposure to as little as 5 ppb NDMA elicits detectable cellular stress responses, emphasizing the sensitivity of developing liver tissue to nitrosamines.

## Age-dependent mutagenesis and clonal expansion in younger mice
NDMA-induced DNA adducts can generate both point and large-scale mutations, which are replication-dependent and may be influenced by age – . To assess age-related effects on NDMA mutagenesis in Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice, we employed two complementary transgenic reporter systems: the gptΔ transgene, which detects point mutations via 6-thioguanine resistance in E. coli (Fig.  ), and the RaDR transgene, which identifies large-scale sequence rearrangements through restoration of EGFP expression following erroneous homologous recombination (Fig.  ) , . These assays together enable precise discrimination between base-level mutations and extensive genomic alterations induced by NDMA, providing a comprehensive assessment of mutagenic mechanisms and their modulation by age. 
Replication-dependent misinsertions are well-established drivers of point mutations caused by NDMA exposure – . Using the gptΔ assay, we observed that juvenile mice exhibited substantially higher point mutation frequencies compared to adults when analyzed 3 months post-exposure (Fig.  ). While adult mice showed no statistically significant increase relative to controls, an upward trend suggested residual mutagenic activity. Given the severe mutational burden in the highly proliferative livers of younger mice, these findings support DNA replication as a central mechanism facilitating misinsertions and subsequent point mutations.
Genotoxic ssDNA gaps, replication stress, and DSBs can induce large-scale mutations via misaligned homologous recombination events, which we evaluated using our RaDR-GFP mouse model , , . Fresh liver tissue was imaged for levels of RaDR foci, indicative of recombinant mutant cells, at multiple timepoints up to 10-11 months post-exposure (Fig.  ). Strikingly, age had a profound influence on NDMA-induced large-scale mutations, with juvenile mice displaying a significant increase in RaDR foci across all timepoints, peaking at 3 months post-exposure (Fig.  ). The decline in RaDR foci from 3 to 10 months likely reflects turnover of mutant cells or expansion of RaDR-positive regions. Even at 5 ppb NDMA, a small but significant increase in RaDR foci was detected at 10 days post exposure in juvenile mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, adult mice displayed only a modest increase in recombination events, first detectable at 3 months post-exposure and slightly elevated in long-term mice at 10-11 months post-exposure (Fig.  ), potentially due to persistent low-level proliferation. The age-dependent discrepancy in NDMA-induced RaDR foci was also evident after intraperitoneal injection, with juveniles displaying an 11-fold increase compared to adults (Supplementary Fig.  ), further reinforcing the heightened susceptibility of younger animals to NDMA-driven large-scale genomic alterations.
Clonal expansion of mutant cells is a crucial step in tumorigenesis, where cells acquiring oncogenic mutations gain a selective advantage and proliferate . This process allows the mutant cells to accumulate additional genetic and epigenetic alterations, ultimately contributing to the development and progression of cancer. We analyzed liver images from each timepoint for large RaDR foci representative of clonal expansion events , . Analysis of long-term mice at 10-11 months post exposure revealed a 5-fold increase in clonal expansion events in juveniles treated with NDMA compared to controls (Fig.  ), an effect not observed in adults. The substantial increase in de novo mutagenesis and clonal expansion are consistent with early stages of tumor development.
Duplex sequencing across a small fraction of the genome (48 kb of 20 endogenous genic and intergenic loci) provided a highly sensitive, quantitative mutagenicity readout and revealed pronounced, age-dependent differences in NDMA-induced mutagenesis. Juvenile mice exhibited a dramatic ~186-fold increase in per-base mutation frequency (MFmin) compared to controls (Fig.  ), while adults showed substantially lower levels (~51-fold, ~3.6-fold less than juveniles), reflecting the combined amplifying effects of juvenile proliferation and DNA repair deficiency , , , . NDMA-induced mutation spectra were dominated by GC → AT substitutions, with greater elevation in juveniles (Fig.  ), and the overall signature primarily matched alkylation-associated COSMIC (Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer) SBS11 (Fig.  ), confirmed by high similarity to reconstructed profiles (Supplementary Fig.  ) . Duplex sequencing also defined clonal expansion quantitatively by calculating the ratio of MFmax (total mutations) to MFmin (unique mutations), which was markedly increased only in NDMA-treated juveniles (Fig.  ), directly demonstrating clonal outgrowth of mutant hepatocytes. These mutational findings are consistent with the observed heightened gptΔ point mutations and clonal expansion of RaDR foci. The concordance across these methods emphasizes the mutational burden in juveniles, providing a mechanistic bridge between early mutagenesis and the later development of liver pathology and tumorigenesis after NDMA exposure.

## Exposure age determines severity of liver pathology and cancer risk
Results thus far demonstrate that DNA damage, inflammation, proliferation, and mutagenesis are more prominent in younger mice exposed to NDMA. For several endpoints, juvenile mice exhibited acute susceptibility, whereas adults appeared completely resistant. To investigate the long-term consequences of NDMA, we evaluated liver pathology 10-11 months post-exposure in Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice (Fig.  ). Younger mice displayed significant increases in hepatocellular hypertrophy, hyperplasia, and single-cell necrosis, all features less pronounced in adults (Fig.  ) – , , . Sustained inflammation, characterized by persistent immune cell infiltration, was observed exclusively in juvenile mice exposed to NDMA. These histopathological changes reflect persistent, low-grade hepatic inflammation and cellular stress, accompanied by recurrent cycles of cell death and regeneration, processes known to drive liver cancer development – , . 
In addition to local tissue damage, NDMA-induced hepatic pathology in juveniles manifested systemically. Multiple factors can contribute to weight loss in mice, including hepatic toxicity and tumorigenesis . Juvenile mice exposed to NDMA exhibited a 20% decrease in long-term body weight compared to controls (Fig.  ). Notably, this weight loss developed later in life rather than acutely, likely reflecting progressive hepatic pathology rather than acute NDMA toxicity (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistent with hepatocellular hypertrophy, liver weight was significantly increased only in NDMA-treated juvenile mice (Fig.  ) . In addition, lung weight was elevated in these animals, in line with the known metabolism of NDMA in pulmonary tissues (Supplementary Fig.  ) . Adult mice, however, did not display significant changes in body or organ weights.
The pronounced separation of these phenotypes between age groups likely underlies differences in cancer susceptibility. Pathological assessment revealed NDMA-treated juvenile mice harbored a significantly higher incidence of tumors compared to both untreated controls and adults (Fig.  ), with neoplasms identified in the liver, lungs, and lymph nodes (Supplementary Table  ). Liver tumors, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma, were most prevalent in younger mice exposed to NDMA, while none were observed in adults. The severity of liver tumorigenesis in juveniles is further illustrated by representative liver images and histopathology (Fig.  ). Juvenile mice also displayed the highest frequency of NDMA-induced pulmonary adenomas (Supplementary Table  ). Remarkably, submandibular lymphoma was detected exclusively in NDMA-treated juveniles, representing a unique finding in this study . These results demonstrate that the heightened susceptibility of younger mice to early genomic instability ultimately manifests as exacerbated liver pathology and increased cancer risk, persisting many months after NDMA exposure.

## Sex modulates NDMA-induced DDR and mutagenesis in juvenile mice
During the course of analyzing the susceptibility of juvenile Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice to NDMA, we observed notable sex-specific differences. Male mice exhibited a higher induction of NDMA-induced γH2AX compared to females (Supplementary Fig.  ), despite equivalent levels of O6MeG and 3MeA DNA adducts (Supplementary Fig.  ). As expected with hepatic injury, males showed a pronounced induction of proliferation markers (Ki67, PCNA, MCM2; Supplementary Fig.  ). Conversely, females demonstrated a more robust p21 (Cdkn1a) induction, as measured via RNA-seq (Fig.  ) and immunoblotting (Fig.  ), indicating enhanced cell cycle arrest. Consistent with their strong p21 induction, females did not upregulate proliferation markers following NDMA exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ), reinforcing their reliance on cell cycle arrest over regenerative proliferation and potentially mitigating genotoxic stress. 
Sex markedly influenced NDMA’s carcinogenic potential in juvenile mice. Male mice exhibited elevated large-scale mutation (RaDR foci) burdens compared to females at all timepoints up to 10-11 months post-exposure, culminating in a 2-fold difference in long-term mutants (Fig.  ). This sex disparity extended to the expansion of mutant cells, with clonal expansion events demonstrated only in males (Fig.  ). Interestingly, point mutations were not significantly altered by sex at 3 months post-exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ). Pathological analysis aligned with RaDR data, showing that males developed more NDMA-induced hepatocellular hyperplasia (Fig.  ) and sustained greater weight loss than females (Fig.  ). These findings underscore sex as a critical variable in NDMA risk assessment, with male predisposition linked to enhanced and more persistent liver pathologies.

## Repair-proficient mice display attenuated juvenile susceptibility
While repair-deficient Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice serve as a highly sensitive model, studies in WT mice revealed attenuated but mechanistically similar responses, highlighting both the relevance of NDMA exposure to the general population and the protective effects of functional repair pathways. As expected, the levels of NDMA-induced O6MeG and 3MeA in WT mice were greatly reduced compared to repair-deficient counterparts (Fig.  vs. Fig.  ). Nevertheless, unrepaired O6MeG adducts were significantly elevated in both juvenile and adult WT mice, with levels comparable across age groups. Despite low adduct burdens, WT juveniles displayed a statistically significant increase in γH2AX suggestive of residual replication stress and DSBs, an effect absent in adults (Fig.  ). 
Transitioning to downstream effects, WT mice demonstrated mild transcriptomic changes in response to NDMA treatment; however, these changes closely resembled those in Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice, including upregulation of Mbl2 (inflammation) and Ephx1 (detoxification), as well as a juvenile‑specific downregulation of Cyp2e1 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Despite these subtle molecular changes, immunofluorescence revealed an NDMA-induced proliferation (Ki67) increase in WT juveniles immediately post-exposure (Fig.  ), which may promote replication-dependent mutagenesis. Indeed, RaDR foci were significantly elevated in WT juveniles across all timepoints (Fig.  ) but resolved faster than in repair-deficient mice (Fig.  ), with no clonal expansion events detected (Supplementary Fig.  ). This demonstrates that WT levels of AAG and MGMT proteins limit mutant cell outgrowth despite initial mutation induction. In stark contrast to juveniles, WT adult mice showed no increase in NDMA-induced proliferation (Fig.  ) or RaDR foci (Fig.  ), further substantiating the role of age in mutagenic susceptibility.
Long-term analysis revealed no histopathological changes in WT mice at 10-11 months post NDMA exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ). Although NDMA-induced tumors were detected in long-term WT juveniles, the increase was not statistically significant compared to adults, which exhibited no tumors (Supplementary Table  ). As expected, detecting subtle NDMA effects in WT mice requires larger cohorts compared to repair-deficient models, reinforcing the utility of Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice for mechanistic studies. Collectively, these results demonstrate that NDMA inflicts measurable genomic stress, even in repair competent systems, so long as animals are young.

## Developmental and induced proliferation enhance NDMA genotoxicity
Despite comparable NDMA-induced DNA adducts, numerous phenotypes were observed in younger mice that are consistent with the expected consequences of DNA replication forks encountering damage. We therefore queried whether cell proliferation modulates susceptibility using our sensitive Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mouse model. We first examined baseline cell proliferation in untreated mice and found that juveniles exhibited over a 5-fold increase in proliferation markers Ki67, MCM2, and PCNA compared to adults (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). BrdU incorporation directly confirmed the presence of actively proliferating hepatocytes in juvenile mice, whereas no BrdU-positive cells were detected in adults (Supplementary Fig.  ). This proliferative surge coincided with significantly higher levels of cleaved caspase-3 (Supplementary Fig.  ), reflecting intense developmental tissue remodeling. In line with increased cell proliferation, juvenile body weight nearly doubled during the two-week NDMA exposure period, whereas adults maintained a stable weight (Fig.  ). The combination of rapid cell division and NDMA-induced adducts created a detrimental convergence, as evidenced by the drastic increase in colocalization of γH2AX and Ki67 in juveniles (Fig.  ), and can be observed throughout the tissue (Supplementary Fig.  ). Thus, cell proliferation in younger animals converts transient adducts into permanent mutations, a risk that is mitigated in quiescent adult hepatocytes. 
To directly test whether proliferative activity drives age-dependent NDMA susceptibility, we treated adult Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice with thyroid hormone T3, a known inducer of hepatocyte replication (Fig.  ) . Indeed, pretreatment with T3 significantly increased adult hepatocyte proliferation prior to NDMA exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ). Post NDMA treatment, T3 elevated proliferative marker Ki67 by ~5-fold in both control and NDMA-treated mice (Fig.  ). While T3 or NDMA alone had no effect on γH2AX levels, the addition of T3 to NDMA-treated mice resulted in a significant 3-fold increase in γH2AX (Fig.  ), as well as enhanced γH2AX-Ki67 colocalization (Fig.  ). Most strikingly, NDMA and T3 co-treatment elevated RaDR foci by 3.5-fold at 10 days post-exposure (Fig.  ). Although these levels remained below those observed in juvenile animals, T3-induced proliferation recapitulated developmental susceptibility, definitively linking replication stress, not mere adduct accumulation, to downstream DNA damage and mutagenesis.

## Discussion
While prior studies have investigated the effects of NDMA on young animals, our study provides the first in-depth, direct comparison with adults, thereby revealing the biological consequences of NDMA at an early stage of life . The striking age-dependent differences in response to NDMA exposure highlight a synergistic interaction between developmental stage and environmental exposure. Juvenile Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice, deficient in the removal of NDMA-induced 3MeA and O6MeG, displayed markedly elevated levels of downstream DNA damage, inflammation, mutagenesis, and liver injury compared to their adult counterparts. These findings demonstrate the importance of age as a key factor when evaluating the toxicological impact of environmental contaminants, particularly nitrosamines.
The dramatic differences between juvenile and adult responses challenge the adequacy of standard adult-centric toxicology testing. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Test Guideline 488 for mutagenicity evaluation permits the use of younger animals (4-6 weeks old at the start of treatment) for somatic tissue assessments when scientifically justified . Our data provide clear scientific justification: even in DNA repair-deficient adults exposed to NDMA, downstream effects were substantially attenuated compared to juveniles, despite comparable DNA adduct levels across age groups in both WT and Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mice. This clearly demonstrates that juveniles and adults differ fundamentally in their responses to DNA adducts, rather than in adduct formation or removal , , . Given the pronounced differences between juvenile and adult outcomes extending beyond mutations to clonal expansion and tissue pathology, our findings from mice younger than the standard guideline recommendations (3 weeks old at the start of treatment) suggest that even earlier exposure windows warrant investigation, and that standard uncertainty factors may underestimate risk for children exposed to NDMA and related nitrosamines .
To directly test the role of proliferation in NDMA susceptibility, we treated adult mice with T3 thyroid hormone, a known inducer of hepatocyte proliferation . T3 treatment significantly increased NDMA-induced γH2AX levels and γH2AX-Ki67 colocalization, demonstrating elevated genotoxicity in dividing cells. Furthermore, T3 exposure amplified the frequency of sequence rearrangement mutations in NDMA-treated adults, recapitulating juvenile vulnerability. The data support a replication-dependent model, wherein replication forks encountering DNA adducts generate genomic gaps and strand breaks. Therefore, the enhanced susceptibility of juvenile mice stems largely from their elevated hepatocyte proliferation rates, as compared to a disparity originating from traditional toxicokinetic or toxicodynamic differences. This proliferative state exacerbates mutagenesis through replication-driven mutation incorporation and hyperrecombination, effects further amplified by injury-induced regenerative responses unique to the juvenile liver – , , .
While previous in vivo studies have demonstrated that adult rodents can be susceptible to NDMA-induced mutagenesis, substantial variability remains due to differences in study design and animal models , , , . In the present work, stringent multiple comparison corrections limited the number of endpoints reaching statistical significance in adults. By prioritizing a conservative statistical analysis approach, the reliability of conclusions is strengthened, emphasizing that age is a key determinant of NDMA susceptibility.
For these studies, we focused primarily on DNA repair-deficient animals exposed to a relatively high dose of NDMA to minimize animal use while maximizing detection sensitivity. Although this approach limits direct environmental relevance, both MGMT and AAG exhibit wide interindividual variability, including naturally occurring variants with reduced repair capacity documented in human cancers, highlighting the importance of genetic diversity in toxicological studies and supporting the Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- model as a physiologically relevant system , , . Comparable biological responses were observed in DNA repair-deficient juveniles following both drinking water and intraperitoneal NDMA administration, showing that the route of exposure does not markedly influence hepatic outcomes. Even at a much lower, more environmentally relevant dose, we still detected a cellular stress response, demonstrating the heightened sensitivity of younger animals.
In repair-proficient WT mice, juveniles also exhibited NDMA-induced DNA damage, proliferation, and large-scale mutations, though at reduced levels, emphasizing the need for expanded WT studies with larger cohorts. Future dose-response studies incorporating long-term, low-dose NDMA exposures and biologically relevant co-exposures across multiple life stages will clarify whether the observed molecular changes progress to tumorigenesis and inform age-specific risk assessment.
Although our study primarily utilized DNA repair-deficient mice to enhance detection sensitivity, the core DNA repair systems (MGMT, base excision repair, mismatch repair), metabolic pathways, and age-related changes are evolutionarily conserved across mammals . In NDMA-treated mice, mutational signatures closely resemble COSMIC signature SBS11 observed in human alkylation-associated cancers, supporting alkylation rather than oxidative damage as the primary mutagenic mechanism . Thus, the Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- model reveals conserved biological responses, which may have implications for vulnerable human populations, particularly children and individuals with compromised DNA repair capacity.
Using the Aag-/-;Mgmt-/- mouse model, we discovered that NDMA induces a potent, sustained interferon response in repair-deficient juveniles, which may synergize with replication-dependent genotoxicity to promote clonal expansion and tumorigenesis. Indeed, findings from this study align with recent work in our laboratory demonstrating that NDMA can trigger irreversible interferon induction, providing a mechanistic link between DNA damage and immune activation . We predict that targeting this interferon response using immunosuppressants or antioxidants may disrupt NDMA carcinogenicity, particularly in repair-deficient populations. Future studies elucidating the roles of O6MeG (MGMT-dependent) and 3MeA (AAG-dependent) adducts in interferon induction will further reveal how DNA repair capacity modulates immune dynamics during NDMA exposure.
The observed sex differences in NDMA susceptibility emphasize the importance of considering sex as a variable in genetic toxicology , . We found a substantial decrease in NDMA-induced large-scale mutations and long-term hepatocellular hyperplasia in female mice compared to males, supporting previous literature indicating females may possess more robust DDR and repair mechanisms, potentially due to the protective effects of estrogen . Consistent with this model, female mice exhibited a stronger induction of p21, which may confer protection through cell cycle arrest, as evidenced by the absence of regenerative proliferation following NDMA exposure.
Our finding that T3-induced proliferation sensitized adult mice to NDMA highlights the need to evaluate co-exposures in the toxicity of NDMA. Adults encounter numerous proliferation-stimulating factors, including high-fat diets, viral infections, and other environmental toxicants . Evaluating these interactions would provide a more thorough understanding of NDMA’s impact on diverse human populations and help identify additional risk factors that may exacerbate NDMA-induced health effects. Such co-exposures may lower NDMA’s effective mutagenic dose by amplifying replication stress, driving responses at substantially lower doses.
While our study focused primarily on the liver, epidemiological evidence suggests possible links between prenatal NDMA exposure and childhood leukemia and lymphoma incidence in Wilmington, MA . Intriguingly, we observed lymphoma in NDMA-treated juvenile mice, a surprising result given the historically weak and inconsistent mutagenicity findings in rodent bone marrow following NDMA exposure . These discrepancies likely stem from the reliance on adult animals in past studies, as our data indicate that early life exposures markedly increase susceptibility to NDMA-induced mutational and neoplastic outcomes , . Collectively, these findings indicate that hematopoietic tissues are potential targets during development and warrant further mechanistic evaluation. Beyond cancer, emerging evidence implicates NDMA in metabolic dysregulation and cognitive decline , indicating broader health impacts requiring investigation across organ systems, exposure routes, and lifespans.
Beyond environmental exposure, results from this study have potential implications for pediatric oncology. SN1-type methylating agents such as procarbazine, widely used in the treatment of childhood hematologic malignancies, form the same DNA adducts as NDMA, including O6MeG . Clinical studies have shown that children treated with procarbazine face a significantly increased risk of secondary malignancies, which may be due to replication-driven mutagenesis and clonal expansion as demonstrated in our study . In this context, our work suggests a need for ongoing monitoring and preventive measures to help reduce secondary tumor risk in children exposed to methylating agents.
In this study, we aimed to determine how age influences NDMA-induced genotoxicity during periods of rapid developmental cell proliferation. We examined age, sex, and DNA repair capacity as key factors shaping susceptibility, thus providing a more nuanced framework for chemical hazard and risk assessment. By accounting for these factors, we can better predict population-wide risks and tailor protections for vulnerable subgroups, extending beyond nitrosamines to environmental toxicants with age-dependent metabolic or proliferative interactions. Crucially, adults are not universally resilient, as co-exposures that stimulate proliferation may recapitulate juvenile-like susceptibility. Future studies must explore these interactions to address escalating global chemical exposures , . Protecting young populations, while identifying latent risks in adults, will be essential for advancing evidence-based public health strategies in an era of pervasive environmental carcinogens.

## Methods

## Animals
The WT mice (Mus musculus) were generated on a C57BL/6 genetic background and contain a Rosa26 direct repeat GFP cassette (RaDRR/R) and gpt delta cassette (gptg/g) for the detection of sequence rearrangements and point mutations, respectively. Aag and Mgmt knockout mice (RaDRR/R;gptg/g;Aag-/-;Mgmt-/-) were created in-house by crossing RaDRR/R;gptg/g;Aag-/- mice and RaDRR/R;gptg/g;Mgmt-/- mice , and verified via PCR-based genotyping (Supplementary Fig.  ). All animals were bred and housed in an AAALAC-certified animal care facility at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 20–24 °C and relative humidity of 30–70%, in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Normal chow (5P76 - Prolab® IsoPro® RMH 3000) and filtered drinking water were provided ad libitum. For T3 treatment, normal chow containing 4 ppm T3 was formulated by Research Diets, Inc., and provided ad libitum for one week prior to NDMA treatment and continued until necropsy. All procedures were approved by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Committee on Animal Care (Protocols #0821-075-24 and #2404000664). Mice of both sexes were exposed to NDMA as juveniles (P21) or adults (P90). Details on animal numbers (n) and sex distribution are provided in the Statistics and Reproducibility section, figure legends, and Source Data file.

## NDMA treatment
Pure NDMA was produced in-house and verified through NMR and mass spectrometry and stored at -20 °C . Diluted stocks of 2 mg/mL NDMA were prepared in MQ water, aliquoted into sealed tubes, wrapped in aluminum foil, and stored at -20 °C. NDMA was added to drinking water at a final concentration of 5 ppm as confirmed by mass spectrometry (Fig.  ), then provided to mice at weaning (juvenile; P21) or at 3 months of age (adult; P90) ad libitum for two weeks. Bottles were changed weekly, with mass spectrometry confirming stable dosing over this period (Supplementary Fig.  ). Untreated mice were included as controls. Using a semi-quantitative cage-level measurement of water consumption, we observed higher water intake in juveniles compared to adults (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, these measurements are an estimate, and DNA adduct levels did not significantly differ between age groups (Fig.  ). No significant differences in drinking rate were detected with regard to sex (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## NDMA quantification in drinking water
To test the concentration of NDMA in drinking water, frozen NDMA stock solutions (2 mg/mL) were thawed at room temperature and added to fresh, filtered drinking water to yield a final concentration of 5 mg/L NDMA. Water bottles were inverted 10 times and samples (1 mL) were taken for analysis. Samples were stored at 4 °C for a maximum of one week before analysis.
NDMA concentrations in drinking water were analyzed using an Agilent 1290 liquid chromatograph coupled to a 6495B Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (Agilent) with atmospheric pressure chemical ionization operated in positive mode. All reagents were LC/MS grade including methanol, formic acid, and water from the Milli-Q® IQ 7000 ultrapure water purification system, in combination with a Millipore Sigma LC-Pak (#LCPAK0041) and Millipak 0.2 micron filter (#MPGP02001). Chromatographic analysis of NDMA from the matrix was performed on a Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 mm x 50 mm) at a flow of 0.3 mL/min. Chromatographic separation utilized 100% Solvent A (LC/MS grade water + 0.1% formic acid) held constant for 2.4 min followed by a gradient elution 100% Solvent B (methanol + 0.1% formic acid) until 6 min. Characteristic retention time (1.11 min) was compared to an authentic standard (5000 ppm NDMA; Sigma Aldrich, #CRM40059). This study utilized multiple reaction monitoring with two characteristic mass transitions (m/z 75 → 43.1 and 75 → 58.3) for the identification and quantification of NDMA. Calibration standards ranged from 1 to 6 ppm. All samples and calibration standards were fortified with deuterated internal standard (d6-NDMA; #33910) obtained by Restek Corporation at a concentration of 1 ppm for quantification. Daily laboratory blanks (LC/MS grade water) were analyzed in parallel for quality control.

## Necropsy and Histopathology
Mice were sacrificed at designated timepoints: pretreatment (day 0), immediately posttreatment (14 + 0 d), 10 days (14 + 10 d), 3 months (14 + 90 d), and 10-11 months (14 + 300 d) post-NDMA exposure as demonstrated in Fig.  . Mice were humanely euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation and liver collected as described in methods below. For long-term necropsies, mouse weight and organ weights for the liver and lung were recorded. Posterior and anterior photographs of the liver and lung were taken, and any macroscopic abnormalities noted on the liver or lung were photographed and submitted for histopathological evaluation. Enlarged lymph nodes were weighed, imaged, and collected for pathological analysis. A subset of 10 mice (5 males and 5 females) were randomly selected for long-term histopathological and tumor assessments.
Representative sections from the liver, lung, kidneys, and spleen were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, processed in alcohol and xylene, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (4-μm thick) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). H&E-stained sections of liver were scored by a board-certified veterinary pathologist blinded to sample identity. Sections from each hepatic lobe were evaluated for the presence of hepatocellular degeneration (cell swelling with cytoplasmic alteration with or without karyomegaly), hepatocellular hyperplasia, and hepatocellular hypertrophy using distribution-based ordinal scoring: 0 = normal; 1 = uncommon detection in <5% of hepatic lobes (10x high-power fields); 2 = detectable in up to 5–20% of hepatic lobes; 3 = detectable in up to 20–65% of hepatic lobes; 4 = detectable in > 65% of hepatic lobes. The following criteria were used for scoring hepatic inflammatory foci and single-cell necrosis: 0 = normal; 1 = minimal (1-5 foci), 2 = mild (6-12 foci), 3 = moderate (13-18 foci), 4 = severe ( > 18 foci) . Representative images of tissues were captured using a brightfield Olympus BX45 microscope with a DP26 camera and Olympus cellSens Dimension Software (v1.18).

## Histology: immunofluorescence
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded liver sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval with Dako Target Retrieval Solution (Agilent, #S1699). After washing with PBS and PBST, hydrophobic barriers were drawn around tissue sections using Diagnostic Biosystems Elite PAP pen (#K039). Sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in blocking buffer (PBS with 1% BSA, 0.4% Triton X-100, 5% goat serum), then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against γH2AX and Ki67 at 1:200. After PBS washes, Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies at 1:400 were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were washed with PBS then mounted with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant with DNA Stain DAPI (Invitrogen, #P36931).
Images were acquired on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope (20x objective) with an Andor Zyla VSC-03269 camera using NIS-Elements AR software (v5.11.02), with fields of view selected based on DAPI staining to minimize selection bias. Three-channel images (DAPI: nuclei; FITC: γH2AX; Texas Red: Ki67) were captured under identical exposure settings across samples, and positive and secondary-only controls were included to verify antibody specificity and background signal. A custom ImageJ (v1.54p) macro performed background subtraction, channel-specific thresholding (γH2AX/Ki67), and nuclear region-of-interest (ROI) generation via DAPI segmentation. Cells with ≥ 10% nuclear γH2AX staining or ≥ 30% nuclear Ki67 staining were classified as positive cells. Results were averaged across two images per mouse.
Li’s Intensity Correlation Quotient (ICQ) was calculated using a custom ImageJ (v1.54p) macro employing the Coloc2 plugin with Costes’ automatic thresholding to assess γH2AX-Ki67 spatial coordination within nuclear ROIs. ICQ values (-0.5 to +0.5) reflect proportional pixel intensity deviations between channels, with positive values indicating colocalization. Values were averaged across two images per mouse.

## Western blot
Flash-frozen liver samples were homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100) supplemented with Thermo Scientific Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (#78440). Lysates were sonicated, centrifuged (14,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), and protein concentrations determined using Thermo Scientific Pierce Rapid Gold BCA Protein Assay Kit (#A53225). Equal protein amounts (40–50 µg) were resolved on 12% SDS-PAGE gels alongside Precision Plus Protein All Blue Prestained Protein Standards (Bio-Rad, #1610373) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (LI-COR, #926-31092) overnight at 30 V (4 °C). Membranes were stained with LI-COR Revert 700 Total Protein Stain Kit (#926-11010) or Revert 520 Total Protein Stain Kit (#926-10010) for normalization and imaged on a LI-COR CLx or Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System.
For target detection, membranes were blocked in LI-COR Intercept Blocking Buffer (#927-60001) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer containing 0.2% Tween-20 (LI-COR, #927-65001) at 1:500 for γH2AX and p21, 1:2000 for CYP2E1, and 1:1000 for all other targets. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with LI-COR IRDye secondary antibodies at 1:10000 for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized on a LI-COR CLx Imager with LI-COR Acquisition Software (v1.1.0.61) and quantified using Image Studio Lite (v5.2.5) and normalized to Total Protein Stain. All biological replicates and group comparisons were performed on the same blot. Stripping and reprobing were performed as needed using LI-COR NewBlot Nitro (#928-40030) and/or IR (#928-40028) Stripping Buffers. Stripping efficiency was validated before proceeding with subsequent targets. All blot images are provided in Supplementary Fig.  . Full blot images including Total Protein Stain are provided in the Source Data file.

## DNA adduct analysis
DNA was isolated from 100 mg of frozen liver tissue using the Qiagen 100/G genomic tip following the procedure recommended for genomic DNA preparation. Briefly, liver tissue was homogenized in buffer QBT in the presence of RNase and proteinase K and incubated overnight at room temperature. The solution was loaded onto a Qiagen 100/G tip, washed with buffer QC, and retained DNA was eluted with buffer QF. DNA was precipitated with isopropanol and recovered by spooling onto a glass rod which was washed with 70% ethanol, briefly dried and then dissolved in 500 µL of purified water. The amount of recovered DNA was determined via UV absorption using a NanoDrop One C spectrometer (Thermo Scientific).
DNA (100-125 µg) containing 10 µg of methylated 15N E. coli DNA, which served as an internal standard, was hydrolyzed by addition of 50 µL formic acid and heating at 95 oC for 1 h. Hydrolyzed DNA samples containing isotopically labeled standards were loaded onto Strata X SC cartridges (100 mg; Phenomenex), which had been prewashed with acetonitrile followed by water. The cartridges were then washed with 15 mL of 0.1 N hydrochloric acid. The retained compounds were eluted with a solution containing 1.5 mL 5% ammonium hydroxide, 10% EtOH, H2O, and vacuum centrifuged to dryness at room temperature. Samples were reconstituted in 100 µL of 0.1 N HCl and transferred to glass vials for LC/QQQ MS analysis.
LC/ESI-MS/MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 6410 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer interfaced with an Agilent 1100 series HPLC. Samples (25 µL injected) were resolved on a 4.6 × 150 mm, 3.5 µm Eclipse Plus C18 column (Agilent Technologies) eluted at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The column was eluted with a step gradient of 4 mM ammonium acetate containing 2% acetonitrile (solvent A) to 100% acetonitrile/0.0001% acetic acid (solvent B) over a period of 30 min followed by column re-equilibration. QQQ MS analysis was performed in the positive ion mode using N2 as the nebulizing/drying gas. Capillary voltage was 4.0 kV, and the temperature was set at 350 °C. Selective reaction monitoring (SRM) was used for quantitative analysis of methylated purines with the collision energy set at 25 for methylated guanines and 30 for the methylated adenine. Nitrogen was used as the collision gas. The following transitions were monitored: m/z 166 [M + H]+ → m/z 149 for O6MeG; m/z 171 [M + H]+ → m/z 153 for O6-methyl-15N-guanine; m/z 150 [M + H]+ → m/z 108 for 3MeA; and m/z 155 [M + H]+ → m/z 112 for 3-methyl-15N-adenine.

## RNAseq
Total RNA was extracted from ≤ 30 mg mouse liver tissue stored in RNAlater at −20 °C using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, #74134). Tissue was homogenized in Buffer RLT Plus with β-mercaptoethanol in BeadBug prefilled tubes (Benchmark Scientific, #D1032-15) using a BeadBug homogenizer at 400 rpm for two 30-sec intervals. Lysates were centrifuged, and supernatant was processed through gDNA Eliminator and RNeasy spin columns according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was eluted in 30–50 μL RNase-free water and quantified by NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Scientific).
3’ Digital gene expression (3’DGE) libraries were prepared by capturing the 3’ ends of polyadenylated transcripts using barcoded oligo-dT primers followed by template switching . RNA samples were cleaned using SPRI beads (1.8X) and quantified and quality assessed using an Advanced Analytical Fragment Analyzer. A total of 10 ng of RNA was used for library preparation. 3’DGE custom primers (3V6NEXT-bmc#1-24) were added to a final concentration of 1 μM (Integrated DNA technologies, 5’-/5Biosg/ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT[BC6]N10T30VN-3’, where 5Biosg = 5’ biotin, [BC6] = 6 bp barcode specific to each sample/well, N10 = unique molecular identifiers) to generate two subpools of 24 samples each. After addition of the oligonucleotides, Maxima H Minus RT (Thermo Scientific) was added per manufacturer’s recommendations with template-switching oligo 5V6NEXT (10 μM, 5V6NEXT: 5’-iCiGiCACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCrGrGrG-3’, where iC = iso-dC, iG = iso-dG, rG = RNA G) followed by incubation at 42 °C for 90 min and inactivation at 80 °C for 10 min. Following the template switching reaction, cDNA from 24 wells containing unique well identifiers were pooled together and cleaned using RNA Ampure beads at 1.0X. cDNA was eluted with 17 µL of water, followed by digestion with Exonuclease I at 37 °C for 30 min, and inactivated at 80 °C for 20 min.
Second strand synthesis and PCR amplification was done by adding the Advantage 2 Polymerase Mix (Clontech) and the SINGV6 primer (10 pmol, Integrated DNA Technologies, 5’-/5Biosg/ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGC-3’) directly to the exonuclease reaction. Eight cycles of PCR were performed, followed by clean up using regular SPRI beads at 0.6X, and eluted with 20 µL of EB. Successful amplification of cDNA was confirmed using the Fragment Analyzer. Illumina libraries were then produced using standard Nextera tagmentation, substituting P5NEXTPT5-bmc primer (25 μM, Integrated DNA Technologies, 5’-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCG*A*T*C*T*-3’, where * = phosphorothioate bonds) in place of the normal N500 primer. Final libraries were cleaned using SPRI beads at 0.7X and quantified using the Fragment Analyzer and qPCR before being loaded for paired-end sequencing using an Illumina NextSeq500 in paired-end mode (28 F, 8 I, 50 R nt reads).
RNA-seq reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) using STAR aligner (v2.7.1b), and gene expression was quantified using the ESAT pipeline (v1). Differential expression was assessed using the two-sided Wald test in DESeq2 (v1.49.1), and log2 fold-change estimates were calculated using lfcShrink with the apeglm method , . Sample metadata, including sex, treatment, genotype, and timepoint, were incorporated into the design matrix (~ comparison_group) using custom R scripts. DESeq2 normalized count data, generated using the median-of-ratios normalization method, were used for downstream analyses and visualizations. Volcano plots were constructed to display -log10 p-values versus log2 fold changes, highlighting genes with raw p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change | > 0.58. Heatmaps included only these significant genes, with unsupervised hierarchical clustering and color gradients based on row-scaled z-scores of log2-transformed expression values. All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.1) using packages from Bioconductor (v3.19). To identify biological pathways, genes reaching a significance threshold of Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change | > 0.58 were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis using a one-sided Fisher’s exact test implemented in Enrichr with the MSigDB Hallmark 2020 mouse gene set collection , . Pathways were considered significantly enriched at an adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate) and a minimum of 3 genes per pathway. RNA-seq data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession number PRJNA1422080.

## GptΔ assay
The gpt mutation assay was used to quantify mutation frequency by rescuing λ-EG10 transgenes from genomic DNA and selecting for 6-thioguanine–resistant mutants , . Genomic DNA was extracted from approximately 30 mg of liver tissue using lysis buffer followed by overnight dialysis in TE buffer. λ-EG10 transgenes were rescued via in vitro packaging (Transpack; Agilent Technologies, #200221) and used to infect E. coli YG6020 (Cre recombinase-expressing strain), releasing the 6.4-kb plasmid containing gpt and chloramphenicol acetyltransferase genes. Infected bacteria were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min without shaking, followed by 30 min with shaking to allow plasmid formation. Mutant selection was performed using soft agar overlays on LB plates containing chloramphenicol (Cm, total plasmid count) or Cm + 6-thioguanine (6-TG, gpt mutant selection), blinded to sample identity. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72–96 h. 6-TG-resistant colonies were re-streaked on Cm + 6-TG plates to confirm resistance. Mutant frequency was calculated as confirmed 6-TG-resistant colonies divided by total rescued plasmids.

## RaDR
Immediately after excision, the left liver lobe was preserved in PBS containing 0.01% trypsin inhibitor on ice. The tissue was placed between a glass slide and coverslip, then imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope (2x objective) with an Andor Zyla VSC-03269 camera using NIS-Elements AR software (v5.11.02). One-channel images (FITC: EGFP) were captured under identical exposure settings across samples. RaDR foci (EGFP positive cells) were identified and quantified using a custom two-stage machine learning algorithm . This algorithm integrates intensity and gradient-based pipelines with a pre-trained support vector machine (SVM) to distinguish true foci from noise, enhancing detection accuracy for both shallow and deep tissue events. Foci counts were normalized to the visible liver area in each image.
For clonal expansion analysis, a custom ImageJ (v1.54p) macro was used to quantify the size of fluorescent foci. Images were processed with Gaussian blur, thresholding, and watershed segmentation to define regions of interest. Foci exceeding 2000 pixels were classified as clonal expansion events based on visual confirmation. The number of clonal expansion events per liver lobe was quantified.

## Duplex sequencing
Liver genomic DNA was analyzed by targeted duplex sequencing to quantify mutation frequency and characterize mutation spectra . Genomic DNA was isolated from ~20 mg of liver tissue using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, #69504). DNA concentration was measured with the Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #Q33266), and DNA integrity was assessed on a TapeStation using Genomic DNA ScreenTape (Agilent, #5067-5365/5067-5366). Samples with a DNA integrity number (DIN) > 7 were selected for library preparation.
Libraries were prepared for duplex sequencing using the Mouse Mutagenesis Panel v2.0 (TwinStrand Biosciences). The panel targets 20 genomic regions (~48 kb total) distributed across the mouse autosomes and is designed to approximate genome-wide diversity in sequence context, GC content, chromatin state, and coding status . For each sample, 500 ng of genomic DNA was enzymatically fragmented, end-polished, A-tailed, and ligated to duplex sequencing adapters with unique molecular identifiers. Final libraries were quantified with the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #Q33231), and average fragment size was assessed on a TapeStation using High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent, #5067-5584/5067-5585). Libraries were pooled and submitted to Psomagen (Rockville, MD) for sequencing.
Libraries were sequenced on two lanes of a NovaSeq X 25B flow cell (Illumina, #20104706), targeting ~400 million reads passing-filter per sample and at least 10,000-fold target locus coverage. Sequencing yielded a mean of ~489 million passing-filter reads per sample, a mean on-target duplex depth >15,000x, and a mean peak tag-family size of 31.
FASTQ files were processed with the TwinStrand duplex sequencing Mutagenesis App v4.6.1 (TwinStrand Biosciences) to generate per-site mutation calls. Mutation files were then imported into R and summarized with MutSeqR to calculate mutation frequencies (MF). Variant calls were filtered using a variant allele frequency (VAF) threshold of 0.01 to remove likely mosaic or germline variants, and single-nucleotide variants overlapping germline multinucleotide variants were excluded. We computed MF under two assumptions: MFmin, which collapses identical within-sample mutations to a single event, and MFmax, which counts all mutations, including identical occurrences.
To assess mutagenesis, we modeled MFmin as a conservative estimate of MF that collapses clonal copies. MFmin was analyzed as a binomial outcome using a generalized linear mixed model in glmmTMB with a logit link. Fixed effects were treatment (NT vs NDMA), treatment age (juvenile vs adult), and their interaction. A random intercept for sample accounted for between-sample variability. Model adequacy was evaluated, and no overdispersion was detected. Adjusted means and contrasts were obtained with emmeans .

## Statistics and reproducibility
An equal number of male and female mice from each cohort were used for analysis. For endpoints with excess mice of one sex, individuals were randomly excluded from analysis to balance group sizes using a cryptographically secure random number generator. Mice from separate litters were included to the maximum extent for heterogeneity. Due to lack of litter and cage treatment variability as demonstrated by the sensitive RaDR mutational assay (Supplementary Fig.  ), each mouse represents a biological replicate (n = 1), and all measurements were obtained from independent samples. Data analysis and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.4.2). Statistical analyses included two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparison test, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison test, unpaired two-sided t-test, two-sided chi-squared test, and generalized linear mixed model as appropriate. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to applying parametric tests and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (adjusted for multiple comparisons where applicable). Details regarding the specific statistical analyses, treatment timepoints, exact significant p-values, and sample sizes (n) are described in the figures and legends, with individual biological replicates displayed as discrete data points where applicable. Comprehensive statistical outputs, including all non-significant p-values and the exact n for every group, are provided in the Source Data file.
