## Significance
Alveolar macrophage (AM) development and function depends on signature transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ). The regulation of PPARγ stability during AM development and maintenance remains unexplored. Here, we identify that myeloid deficiency of adaptor protein receptor for activated C kinase 1 (RACK1) leads to the scarcity of mature AMs, which is associated with pulmonary alveolar proteinosis and impaired ability to control Streptococcus pneumoniae infection. Mechanistically, RACK1 controls AM identity and key molecular pathways. PPARγ signaling is blocked upon RACK1 deficiency. RACK1 prevents PPARγ ubiquitination and degradation by directly binding to it. These results uncover RACK1 as a key regulator of AM cell fate and provide insight into the posttranslational regulation of PPARγ in AMs.
Keywords: alveolar macrophages, PPARγ, RACK1, stability, interaction

## Abstract
Alveolar macrophages (AMs) are indispensable to prevent pulmonary alveolar proteinosis and clear inhaled pathogens. Receptor for activated C kinase 1 (RACK1) is a versatile adaptor protein that regulates multiple signaling pathways. Whether RACK1 is implicated in AM alterations remains elusive. Alveolar type 2 cells-derived granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and autocrine transforming growth factor-β1 drive the transcription of Pparg, the gene encoding AM signature transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ). The regulation of PPARγ stability during AM development and maintenance remains unexplored. Here, we report that myeloid RACK1 deficiency results in the scarcity of mature AMs and pulmonary alveolar proteinosis. A mixed bone marrow chimera approach reveals a cell-intrinsic role of RACK1 in AM differentiation. Bulk RNA-sequencing indicates a considerable loss of AM identity, impaired PPAR signaling, but a largely unchanged Pparg messenger RNA (mRNA) level in the absence of RACK1. Indeed, myeloid deletion of Rack1 halts AM differentiation in vivo and blocks the ability of PPARγ agonist to induce AM-like cells in vitro. Mechanistically, RACK1 directly binds to and stabilizes PPARγ by preventing its ubiquitination and degradation. Moreover, myeloid RACK1 deficiency renders mice susceptible to Streptococcus pneumoniae infection.

Alveolar macrophages (AMs), located on the luminal surface of alveoli, clear inhaled pathogens, cell debris, and surfactants secreted constantly by alveolar type 2 cells ( , ). The impaired function or significant deficit of AMs leads to pulmonary alveolar proteinosis ( – ). AMs also control inflammation in the lung while facilitating the clearance of pathogens and contribute to postinfection recovery ( – ). Similar to most other tissue-resident macrophages, AMs self-renew in homeostasis ( , ). Fetal liver monocytes seed the lung during the late stage of embryogenesis. Through a stage of intermediate, terminal differentiation into AMs occurs after birth ( , ). Alveolar type 2 cells produce abundant granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, gene name Csf2), which induces the differentiation of fetal liver monocytes into intermediate pre-AMs before birth and the full maturation of AMs after birth ( – , , ). Furthermore, alveolar type 2 cells express C-type lectin Clr-g, which interacts with NK cell-associated receptor, NKR-P1B (gene name Klrb1), expressed on AMs to promote surfactant metabolism and cell survival ( ). The alveolar niche also contains abundant transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1, gene name Tgfb1). Pre-AMs and AMs are the main origin of TGF-β1 that collaborates with GM-CSF to boost the development and local maintenance of AMs in an autocrine manner ( ). Besides, neonatal stimulation of AMs with neutrophil-derived 12-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid limits prostaglandin E2 production to prevent AM senescence ( ).
GM-CSF exerts its function through its receptor, the heterodimer of α chain (gene name Csfr2a) and β chain (gene name Csfr2b), which activates several signaling pathways including Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) and extracellular signal regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) ( , ). These events lead to the expression of lineage-defining factors including PU.1 (gene name Spi1) and tissue-specific transcriptional regulators including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ, gene name Pparg) ( , ). On the other hand, active TGF-β1 binds to transmembrane receptor serine/threonine kinase TGF-βR2 (gene name Tgfbr2), which initiates the formation of the heterodimeric receptor complex with TGF-βR1 (gene name Tgfbr1). Then, several signaling pathways are activated including Smad2/3 ( , ). These events collaborate with GM-CSF signaling to augment the expression of PPARγ, the master transcription factor for AM differentiation and local maintenance ( , ). Other transcription factors such as BTB domain and CNC homolog 2, basic helix–loop–helix family member e40/41, early growth response 2, CCAAT/enhancer binding protein β, and hypoxia-inducible factors have also been implicated in the imprinting of AM unique signatures ( , – ). In addition, mitochondrial fitness seems to be essential for AM homeostasis since deletion of mitochondrial transcription factor A or regulatory associated protein of mammalian/mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 results in reduced number and impaired function of AMs ( – ).
Nuclear receptor PPARγ transcriptionally controls the expression of hundreds of genes involved in lipid, energy, and carbohydrate metabolism. PPARγ-deficient AMs show reduced expression of genes involved in lipid transport, intracellular storage, degradation, and fatty acid β-oxidation ( ). The impaired catabolic lipid metabolism is associated with augmented expression of genes involved in lipid uptake, cholesterol esterification, and glycolysis ( ). There are two main PPARγ isoforms: PPARγ1 is ubiquitously expressed whereas PPARγ2 is specifically expressed in adipocytes and AMs ( ). Despite that, the two isoforms are identical except for an additional N-terminal 30 amino acids of PPARγ2 ( ). In AMs and their precursors, CCAAT/enhancer binding protein β has been reported to mediate the up-regulation of PPARγ2 expression by GM-CSF ( ), and early growth response 2 seems to be downstream of PPARγ ( ). The expression of PPARγ is also regulated by posttranslational modifications in a cell type–dependent manner. For example, E3 ligase murine double minute 2 (MDM2) has been shown to catalyze PPARγ ubiquitination and degradation in malignant cells but induce PPARγ neddylation and stabilization in preadipocytes ( , ). How the stability of PPARγ is regulated during AM development and homeostasis remains unknown.
Receptor for activated C kinase 1 (RACK1) is an adaptor protein. Intriguingly, RACK1 modulates multiple signaling pathways in a cell context-dependent manner. RACK1 bridges STAT1 to the β subunit of type 1 interferon receptor and STAT3 to insulin and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptors and thereby facilitates their activation ( , ). RACK1 binds to ERK1/2 and upstream core kinases to promote ERK1/2 activation or degradation, depending on the specific extracellular stimulus ( , ). RACK1 also binds to Smad3 to modulate TGF-β1 signaling in renal tubular epithelial cells and neural stem cells ( , ). Furthermore, RACK1 induces oxygen-independent degradation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α through directly binding to it in various human malignant cells and human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cell line ( , ). In addition, RACK1 promotes autophagy and thereby contributes to the fitness of mitochondria ( ). Despite these findings, a role of RACK1 in AM homeostasis has not been reported.
In this study, we report that RACK1 promotes the development and function of AMs through directly binding to and stabilizing PPARγ.

## Results

## Myeloid RACK1 Is Indispensable for the Homeostasis of Mature AMs (mAMs).
To explore the possible involvement of RACK1 in AM number and function, we first checked the protein level of RACK1 in macrophages from different tissues. Immunoblotting (IB) analysis showed that AMs in the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid exhibited a higher protein level of RACK1 than bone marrow (BM)-derived macrophages and peritoneal macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), suggesting a key role of RACK1 in AM homeostasis. For further investigation, we employed Lyz2-Cre+/+:Rack1F/F (Rack1ΔMye) mice, with Lyz2-Cre+/+:Rack1F/− (Rack1F/−) mice as controls ( ). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that the frequencies of myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs), monocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages in the BM (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B) were comparable between the two genotypes. Rack1ΔMye mice and control littermates also showed comparable frequencies of macrophages in the peritoneum, heart, liver, small intestine lamina propria, and white adipose tissue despite that there were fewer spleen red pulp macrophages in the absence of myeloid RACK1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–L). Strikingly, the frequency and number of CD11c+Siglec-Fhi mAMs either in the BAL fluid ( ) or in the lung tissue ( ) from Rack1ΔMye mice were significantly reduced relative to those from Rack1F/− control littermates at 6 to 8 wk of age. Meanwhile, the number of CD11c+Siglec-Flo immature AMs (imAMs) in the BAL fluid from Rack1ΔMye mice remained largely unchanged although their frequency increased ( ). The remaining BAL and lung mAMs from Rack1ΔMye mice displayed diminished expression of AM marker Siglec-F while CD11c expression was up-regulated in BAL mAMs and remained unchanged in lung mAMs from Rack1ΔMye mice ( ).
 To analyze the proliferation capacity of RACK1-deficient mAMs, we employed in vivo 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a lower proportion of the remaining lung mAMs in Rack1ΔMye mice were EdU+, as compared with their counterparts in Rack1F/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). We further characterized the phagocytic ability of the remaining mAMs in Rack1ΔMye mice. Intriguingly, RACK1-deficient BAL mAMs showed impaired ability of apoptotic cell clearance, as compared with their RACK1-sufficient counterparts ( ). Consequently, more granulocytes were detected in the BAL fluid from Rack1ΔMye mice ( ). Furthermore, the remaining BAL AMs from Rack1ΔMye mice exhibited enlarged size and lipid accumulation as assessed with Oil Red O staining ( ). Verhoeff Van-Gieson staining revealed the replacement of elastic fiber (as black) by collagen fiber (as pink) in the lung tissue of Rack1ΔMye mice, which was associated with the appearance of foam macrophages ( ). Accordingly, the BAL fluid from Rack1ΔMye mice showed augmented turbidity ( ), indicating pulmonary alveolar proteinosis. Together, these data suggest that myeloid RACK1 is indispensable for AM development and/or maintenance.

## RACK1 Deficiency Confers Cell-Intrinsic Defects on AM Differentiation.
AM depletion after irradiation enables monocytes to adapt to vacant alveolar niche and differentiate into functional mAMs ( , ). To examine whether RACK1 affects the homeostasis of mAMs in a cell-intrinsic manner, we set up BM transplantation experiments in which CD45.1CD45.2 Rack1F/− BM cells were mixed with CD45.2 Rack1ΔMye BM cells at a ratio of 1:1 and injected into lethally irradiated CD45.1 recipients ( ). Eight weeks after BM transplantation, the CD45.1−CD45.2+ to CD45.1+CD45.2+ ratios in BM and blood monocytes were around 1, ruling out the possibility that myeloid RACK1 deficiency affects the differentiation of monocytes ( ). Despite that spleen red pulp macrophages were almost exclusively CD45.1+CD45.2+, macrophages in the heart and small intestine lamina propria were around half CD45.1+CD45.2+ and half CD45.1−CD45.2+, indicating that RACK1 does not have a ubiquitous role in macrophage differentiation ( ). Notably, CD45.1−CD45.2+
Rack1ΔMye mAMs accounted for only a small fraction of the mAM pool either in the BAL fluid (3.83% ± 2.72%) or in the lung tissue (3.81% ± 1.88%) and were completely outcompeted by their CD45.1+CD45.2+ counterparts ( ). As for the CD11bhi population in the lung, the CD45.1−CD45.2+ to CD45.1+CD45.2+ ratio was around 1 ( ), indicating a functional recruitment of Rack1F/− and Rack1ΔMye myeloid cells to the lung. These data suggest that the scarcity of RACK1-deficient mAMs can be attributed to developmental arrest at terminal differentiation stage and/or defective maintenance. The remaining RACK1-deficient mAMs either in the BAL fluid ( ) or in the lung tissue ( ) of chimeric mice displayed diminished expression of AM marker Siglec-F and up-regulated expression of CD11c, as compared to their RACK1-sufficient counterparts. These data suggest that the phenotypic defects of mAMs from Rack1ΔMye mice are cell-intrinsic, rather than effects of RACK1 myeloid deficiency on the lung environment.
 

## RACK1 Controls AM Identity and Key Molecular Pathways Including PPAR Signaling.
To investigate the role of RACK1 in AMs in more detail, lung mAMs from adult Rack1F/− and Rack1ΔMye mice were sorted and subjected to bulk RNA-sequencing. Because differences in sex and/or housing conditions might affect the function and transcriptome of macrophages ( ), both male and female mice housed in different facilities were used. Principal component (PC) analysis revealed close similarity between Rack1ΔMye samples, whereas they were clearly different from Rack1F/− mAMs ( ). Comparison of the two genotypes revealed 992 significant differentially expressed genes (false discovery rate Q < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1; ). As expected, one of the down-regulated genes in Rack1ΔMye mAMs was Rack1 although the down-regulation of Mki67 failed to reach statistical significance ( ). Among the 21 transcription factors whose expression has been described to be selectively up-regulated in lung AMs ( ), 5 was down-regulated more than twofolds and 2 was down-regulated between 1.5 and 2 folds in Rack1ΔMye mAMs relative to their expression in Rack1F/− counterparts (Q < 0.05; ). Beyond the 21 transcription factors, the AM signature is composed of another 110 genes whose expression is selectively higher in AMs than in other tissue-resident macrophages ( ). Myeloid RACK1 deficiency led to the down-regulation of 27 “signature-up” genes albeit the further up-regulation of 3 “signature-up” genes in mAMs (Q < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.69; ). In line with the observations that RACK1-deficient mAMs are functionally impaired ( ), KEGG enrichment analysis indicated defective “phagosome,” signs of an inflammatory response (“cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction,” “chemokine signaling pathway,” “hematopoietic cell lineage,” and “rheumatoid arthritis”), and aberrant lipid metabolism (“arachidonic acid metabolism,” “fat digestion and absorption,” “fatty acid degradation,” and “ether lipid metabolism”) in the absence of RACK1 ( ). Furthermore, various pathways involved in AM development and/or maintenance were disturbed in RACK1-deficient mAMs, such as “cell cycle,” “p53 signaling pathway,” “cell adhesion molecules,” “ECM–receptor interaction,” and “PPAR signaling pathway” ( ). Defects in immune response and lipid metabolism are the inherent characteristics of PPARγ deficiency in AMs ( ). Therefore, most of the disturbed pathways can be partially explained by impaired PPAR signaling. Despite that, the down-regulation of Pparg in RACK1-deficient mAMs failed to reach statistical difference (Q = 0.0650; ). Specifically, three variants of Pparg were readily detected with a FPKM (fragment/kb of transcript/million mapped reads) value > 0.5. Among them, isoform 1 variant 4, but not isoform 1 variant 1 and isoform 2 variant 2, showed the tendency of down-regulation in the absence of RACK1 (Q = 0.2134, 0.7448, and 0.9278, respectively; ). The transcription of isoform 2 in AMs is primarily driven by alveolar type 2 cells-derived GM-CSF and autocrine TGF-β1 ( – , , , ). Accordingly, the expression of Csf2, Csf2ra, Csf2rb, Csf2rb2, Tgfb1, Tgfbr1, and Tgfbr2 in mAMs were not significantly down-regulated in the absence of RACK1 ( ). And there was no significant enrichment of JAK-STAT (Q = 0.4565) and TGF-β (Q = 0.4945) signaling pathways between RACK1-sufficient and -deficient mAMs. Even though aberrant adhesion of RACK1-deficient mAMs was suggested by the enrichment analysis ( ), the expression of Klrb1, which encodes the receptor of niche molecule C-type lectin Clr-g ( ), was not affected upon RACK1 deficiency ( ).
 

## PPARγ Signaling Is Blocked in the Absence of RACK1.
Next, we tried to clarify the role of PPARγ in AM alterations upon RACK1 deficiency. PPARγ-deficient AMs show up-regulated Itgam (encoding CD11b), down-regulated Siglecf (encoding Siglec-F), and unaltered Itgax (encoding CD11c) and Adgre1 (encoding F4/80) ( ). Indeed, such phenomena existed in Rack1ΔMye lung AMs, as revealed by bulk RNA-sequencing ( ). Furthermore, PPARγ deficiency leads to the scarcity of CD11c+F4/80+CD11bintSiglec-Fhi mAMs and the simultaneous accumulation of CD11c+F4/80+CD11bhiSiglec-Fint arrested AMs (aAMs) in adult mice ( ). Our aforementioned data have shown the reduction of mAMs. To delineate the role of PPARγ, we analyzed the two subsets simultaneously in this more accurate manner. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that adult Rack1ΔMye mice showed reduced frequency and number of both BAL and lung mAMs, as compared with their Rack1F/− control littermates ( ). On the other hand, the frequency of BAL aAMs increased in adult Rack1ΔMye mice although their number remained unaltered ( ). Meanwhile, the frequency and number of lung aAMs were comparable between the two genotypes ( ). Murine mAMs first appear on postnatal day 11 ( ). As expected, postnatal day 11 Rack1ΔMye mice also showed reduced number of lung mAMs, but unaltered number of lung aAMs, as compared with their Rack1F/− control littermates ( ). Because the frequencies and numbers of monocytes, neutrophils, CD103+ DCs, CD11b+ DCs, and interstitial macrophages were largely unaltered in the lung of Rack1ΔMye mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B) around this time point, the aberrant frequency of aAMs in the lung of Rack1ΔMye mice does not result from significantly disturbed lung environment.
 Fetal monocytes differentiate into pre-AMs perinatally ( – , ). Next, we set out to explore in which stage myeloid RACK1 affects AM development. As shown in , CD11cloF4/80+CD11bint fetal macrophages and CD11cloF4/80intCD11bintLy-6Chi fetal monocytes in the developing lung were unaffected in postnatal day 1 Rack1ΔMye mice compared to their counterparts in control littermates. However, a drastic decrease in CD11cintF4/80+CD11bintSiglec-Fint pre-AMs was noted. Thus, the AM defects in Rack1ΔMye mice partially phenocopy those in mice with PPARγ deficiency in hematopoietic cells.
Our chimeric mice data suggested impaired ability of RACK1-deficient BM monocytes to differentiate into mAMs ( ). However, the defects might be exaggerated due to the competitive disadvantage. Therefore, we performed nonchimeric transplantation by separately transferring CD45.2 Rack1F/− and Rack1ΔMye BM cells into lethally irradiated CD45.1 recipients (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Eight weeks after BM transplantation, similar frequency and number of CD45.2+ monocytes were detected in the lung of recipients regardless of donor origin (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C), indicating a functional recruitment of RACK1-sufficient and -deficient monocytes into the lung. However, there was a lower frequency and number of Rack1ΔMye BM-derived CD11c+F4/80+ total AMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E). In more details, the proportion and number of Rack1ΔMye BM-derived CD11c+F4/80+CD11bintSiglec-Fhi mAMs were much lower than those of their RACK1-sufficient counterparts whereas the proportion and number of Rack1ΔMye BM-derived CD11c+F4/80+CD11bhiSiglec-Fint aAMs were higher (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and F), suggesting a more profound developmental arrest at the aAM stage during the differentiation of RACK1-deficient BM monocytes into mAMs than RACK1-deficient fetal monocytes.
To further address this issue, we employed an optimized in vitro culture system for generating AM-like cells ( ). BM cells from adult Rack1F/− and Rack1ΔMye mice were cultured in the presence of 20 ng/mL GM-CSF and 2 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 9 d, with or without 0.1 μM PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone added on day 6. Flow cytometry analysis of the resultant adherent cells revealed that GM-CSF and TGF-β1 induced the generation of CD11c+Siglec-F+AM-like cells from adult Rack1F/− BM cells, which was augmented by the addition of rosiglitazone on day 6 ( ). However, in BM cells from adult Rack1ΔMye mice, the role of GM-CSF and TGF-β1 was half-diminished ( ). Furthermore, the addition of rosiglitazone on day 6 slightly hindered, but not augmented, the induction of AM-like cells from adult Rack1ΔMye BM cells ( ). In addition, AM-like cells generated in vitro from adult Rack1ΔMye BM cells displayed diminished expression of AM markers Siglec-F and CD11c ( ). In line with the in vivo EdU incorporation data (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B), a lower proportion of RACK1-deficient AM-like cells were labeled with EdU (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), indicating impaired proliferation capability. Together, these data suggest that AM alterations in the absence of RACK1 result, at least partially, from impaired PPARγ signaling.
 

## RACK1 Maintains PPARγ Protein Level.
Our aforementioned data suggest that PPARγ signaling is blocked in the absence of RACK1 although Pparg mRNA level is only marginally affected in mAMs. In this scenario, we tried to explore whether RACK1 affects PPARγ protein level. IB revealed a reduced protein level of PPARγ including both PPARγ1 (53 kDa) and PPARγ2 (57 kDa) in RACK1-deficient BAL AMs ( ) and AM-like cells ( ). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed the reduced protein level of PPARγ in lung mAMs from both adult (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B) and postnatal day 11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D) Rack1ΔMye mice. To further address this issue, HEK-293T cells were transfected with the same amount of Myc-tagged PPARγ2 expression construct and various amounts of FLAG-tagged RACK1 expression construct. IB indicated that RACK1 overexpression led to an augmented protein level of Myc-PPARγ2 in a dose-dependent manner ( ). In line with these data, RACK1 knockdown resulted in a lower level of Myc-PPARγ2 protein ( ). Furthermore, a chase assay with cycloheximide (CHX), which blocks de novo protein synthesis ( ), shortened the half-life of Myc-PPARγ2 protein ( ). These data suggest that RACK1 stabilizes PPARγ.
 Because RACK1 usually affects the stability of its binding proteins through the ubiquitin–proteasome system ( , , ) and PPARγ undergoes ubiquitination under certain circumstances ( , ), we next examined whether RACK1 affects the turnover of PPARγ in a proteasome-dependent manner. As shown in , the proteasome inhibitor MG132 reversed the reduced protein level of PPARγ in RACK1-deficient AM-like cells. Various residues within ubiquitin can be utilized to form poly-ubiquitin chains. Among them, K48-linked chains target proteins for degradation by the proteasome ( ). As expected, the K48only ubiquitin, in which lysines other than K48 have been mutated to arginines ( ), could confer Myc-PPARγ2 ubiquitination to a similar extent to the wild type (WT) ubiquitin in HEK-293T cells, whereas the K48R ubiquitin only partially supported Myc-PPARγ2 ubiquitination under the same conditions ( ). Ectopic expression of the longest isoform of MDM2 (95 kDa), a reported ubiquitin E3 ligase for PPARγ ( ), augmented the K48only ubiquitin-conferred Myc-PPARγ2 ubiquitination but simultaneously hindered the K48R ubiquitin-supported Myc-PPARγ2 ubiquitination in HEK-293T cells ( ). Myc-PPARγ2 ubiquitination in HEK-293T cells was suppressed upon RACK1 overexpression ( ) and augmented upon RACK1 knockdown ( ). Notably, enhanced ubiquitination of endogenous PPARγ was observed in RACK1-deficient AM-like cells ( ). Together, these data suggest that RACK1 stabilizes PPARγ by hindering its ubiquitination.

## RACK1 Prevents PPARγ Ubiquitination by Directly Binding to It.
Our aforementioned data indicate that PPARγ primarily undergoes K48-linked ubiquitination and RACK1 prevents its ubiquitination. In this scenario, we tried to explore whether RACK1 affects the ability of PPARγ to anchor its ubiquitin E3 ligases. Indeed, coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) analysis indicated that the interaction between Myc-PPARγ2 and HA-MDM2 in HEK-293T cells was blocked upon RACK1 overexpression ( ) but augmented upon RACK1 knockdown ( ). Furthermore, enhanced interaction between endogenous PPARγ and endogenous MDM2 (around 57/68 kDa) was observed in RACK1-deficient AM-like cells ( ).
 As RACK1 is a multifunctional scaffold protein, we then tested the notion that RACK1 directly binds to PPARγ. For this purpose, we first examined the possible colocalization of RACK1 and PPARγ. Nuclear cytoplasmic fractionation and subsequent IB revealed that both PPARγ1 and PPARγ2 showed nuclear localization in AM-like cells, whereas RACK1 was predominantly cytoplasmic with a small portion distributed in the nucleus ( ). Similar phenomena were also observed in BAL AMs with indirect immunofluorescence analysis ( ). Thus, the colocalization of RACK1 and PPARγ can occur in the nucleus. The physiological interaction of RACK1 and PPARγ was confirmed by co-IP. RACK1 was present in immuno-precipitates obtained from AM-like cells with an antibody against PPARγ, whereas no RACK1 coprecipitated with a control antibody ( ). Moreover, endogenous PPARγ1 and PPARγ2 in AM-like cells coprecipitated with endogenous RACK1 ( ). The interaction between RACK1 and PPARγ is direct because in vitro Glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay revealed that endogenous PPARγ1 and PPARγ2 in lysates of AM-like cells precipitated specifically by GST-RACK1 but not by GST alone ( ).
We further set out to analyze RACK1/PPARγ interacting regions. RACK1 contains seven Trp-Asp (WD) repeats ( ), whereas PPARγ is composed of five domains: activation function 1 (AF1), DNA-binding domain (DBD), Hinge, ligand-binding domain (LDB), and activation function 2 (AF2) ( ). With expression constructs encoding RACK1 truncated mutants without WD repeats 1 ~ 4 (ΔWD1-4) or 5 ~ 7 (ΔWD5-7) ( ), co-IP analysis in HEK-293T cells demonstrated that Myc-PPARγ2 coprecipitated with either deletion mutant of RACK1 ( ). Thus, it is possible that RACK1 binds to PPARγ through multiple regions. Then we generated expression constructs encoding PPARγ2 truncated mutants lacking each of its five domains ( ). Co-IP analysis in HEK-293T cells revealed that AF1-truncated mutant (ΔAF1), but not other truncated mutants, of PPARγ2 lost the ability to interact with coexpressed FLAG-RACK1 ( ). Thus, RACK1 binds to the AF1 domain of PPARγ. GFP-RACK1 only marginally affected the ubiquitination of Myc-PPARγ2 ΔAF1 in HEK-293T cells under the conditions that it potently inhibited that of Myc-PPARγ2 WT ( ). Together, these data indicate that RACK1 prevents PPARγ ubiquitination through directly binding to it.

## RACK1 Enables AMs to Destroy Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pn).
AMs not only clear cell debris and surfactants, they also clear inhaled pathogens. For example, they can efficiently clear the infection of a low dose of S. pn, a major respiratory pathogen, without significant recruitment of neutrophils ( , ). A downstream transcription factor of PPARγ in AMs, early growth response 2, is indispensable for the effective handling of S. pn at a low dose ( ). Therefore, adult Rack1F/− and Rack1ΔMye mice were intratracheally infected with 1 × 103 colony-forming units (CFU) S. pn (serotype 3 strain 6303, ATCC) and harvested the BAL fluid at 12 h post infection to examine the bacterial load, the number of mAMs, and the infiltration of neutrophils ( ). As expected, Rack1F/− mice almost cleared infection at this time point, whereas Rack1ΔMye mice had more bacteria in the airways ( ). Flow cytometry revealed the BAL fluid from infected Rack1F/− mice contained much more mAMs than that from infected Rack1ΔMye mice ( ). Low levels of neutrophil recruitment occurred in the airways of infected Rack1F/− mice but significant infiltration of neutrophils was detected in the airways of infected Rack1ΔMye mice ( ). Thus, the stabilization of PPARγ by RACK1 is not only essential for AM development and function in homeostasis, but also crucial for the control of S. pn infection ( ).
 

## Discussion
Our study indicates that Lyz2-Cre-driven deletion of Rack1 leads to AM scarcity. Besides myeloid cells, Lyz2-Cre also targets alveolar type 2 cells ( ). As RACK1 regulates multiple signaling pathways, it is possible that the lung environment is negatively affected in this model. Notably, myeloid subsets other than AMs were largely unaltered in the lung of 2-wk-old Rack1ΔMye mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B), suggesting that the aberrant AM development does not result from significantly disturbed lung environment. Moreover, CD45.1−CD45.2+
Rack1ΔMye mAMs were completely outcompeted by CD45.1+CD45.2+
Rack1F/− mAMs in chimeric mice ( ), indicating that RACK1-deficient BM monocytes lose the ability to differentiate into mAMs even in the same AM niche as their RACK1-sufficient counterparts. Profound developmental arrest at the aAM stage during the differentiation of RACK1-deficient BM monocytes into mAMs also occurred after nonchimeric transplantation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–F). Accordingly, the in vitro generation of AM-like cells from Rack1ΔMye BM cells was impaired ( ). Thus, the role of RACK1 in AM development is cell autonomous.
Bulk RNA-sequencing of lung mAMs from adult Rack1F/− and Rack1ΔMye mice was employed to explore the underlying mechanism(s). Because differences in sex and/or housing conditions might affect the function and transcriptome of macrophages ( ), both male and female mice housed in different facilities were used. Housing- and sex-specific differences may cause significant variations. Yet 992 significant differentially expressed genes (Q < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1) were identified between the two genotypes, which demonstrated the loss of AM identity in the absence of RACK1 ( ). Importantly, PPAR signaling pathway, which controls the transcriptional program intrinsically required for the differentiation and function of AMs and spleen red pulp macrophages ( ), was significantly enriched among differentially expressed genes ( ). In line with this, the defects of AMs and spleen red pulp macrophages in Rack1ΔMye mice partially phenocopied those in mice with PPARγ deficiency in hematopoietic cells ( and and SI Appendix, Figs. S2–S4) and the ability of PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone to augment the in vitro generation of AM-like cells diminished in the absence of RACK1 ( ).
Intriguingly, Pparg mRNA level in lung mAMs was not significantly reduced in the absence of RACK1. We have provided data to show that RACK1 maintains PPARγ protein level by preventing its ubiquitination. Although RACK1 is predominantly cytoplasmic, its basal or inducible nuclear localization has been reported in various cell types ( – ). In AM-like cells and BAL AMs, PPARγ showed nuclear localization and a small portion of RACK1 was detected in the nucleus ( ). Thus, the colocalization of RACK1 and PPARγ can occur in the nucleus. Indeed, their physiological interaction in AM-like cells was confirmed by co-IP ( ). Accordingly, the ability of PPARγ to anchor its ubiquitin E3 ligase MDM2 was affected by the protein level of RACK1 ( ). After mapping RACK1/PPARγ interaction regions ( ), we have shown that RACK1 prevents PPARγ ubiquitination by directly binding to it ( ). Protein stability initially refers to three-dimensional structure-based protein alteration ( , ). Later on, it has been frequently used to describe the regulation of protein degradation by posttranslational modifications ( , ). Here, we show that PPARγ primarily undergoes K48-linked ubiquitination, which can be enhanced by MDM2 ( ). K48-linked chains target proteins for degradation by the proteasome ( ). Therefore, RACK1 stabilizes PPARγ, possibly because their direct interaction changes the conformation of PPARγ and thereby prevents the anchoring of its ubiquitin E3 ligases.
Various other biological pathways significantly enriched among genes differentially expressed between RACK1-sufficient and -deficient lung mAMs ( ) can be partially attributed to impaired PPAR signaling. Besides, RACK1 promotes autophagy ( ), which also regulates phagocytosis, inflammatory response, and lipid metabolism ( , ). Indeed, RACK1-deficient AMs showed reduced apoptotic cell phagocytic ability ( ). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated defective “phagosome” in the absence of RACK1 ( ). Phagocytosis is pivotal for AMs to remove surfactants and apoptotic epithelial cells as well as inhaled pathogens and particles, which prevents the influx of granulocytes and the initiation of inflammation ( , ). Accordingly, more granulocytes were detected in the BAL fluid from Rack1ΔMye mice ( ). To keep lung integrity, AMs metabolize ingested surfactant lipids ( , ). The aberrant lipid metabolism (“arachidonic acid metabolism,” “fat digestion and absorption,” “fatty acid degradation,” and “ether lipid metabolism”) ( ) must worsen the lipid accumulation in RACK1-deficient AMs due to a small AM pool. Significantly reduced AMs with defects in phagocytosis and lipid metabolism collectively lead to spontaneous pulmonary alveolar proteinosis ( ). With impaired phagocytic function, AMs produce more chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines that recruit and activate various pro-inflammatory immune cells ( , ). On the other hand, lipid metabolism leads to the generation of either pro-inflammatory or pro-resolving bioactive lipid mediators ( , ). The aberrant lipid metabolism in the absence of RACK1 should also disturb the pro-inflammatory/pro-resolving balance in AMs and thereby augment the innate immune response. Indeed, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated signs of an inflammatory response (“cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction,” “chemokine signaling pathway,” “hematopoietic cell lineage,” and “rheumatoid arthritis”) in the absence of RACK1 ( ). Persistent low-grade inflammation may further damage lung integrity.
Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated aberrant “cell adhesion molecules” and “ECM–receptor interaction” in the absence of RACK1 ( ), in line with a previous report that RACK1 modulates adhesion-induced activation of ERK1/2 via directly binding to focal adhesion kinase ( ). Recently, it has been revealed that proper adhesion of AMs contributes to lung integrity. Besides the above mentioned Clr-g/NKR-P1B pathway ( ), AMs utilize β2-integrin CD11a to crawl on the alveolar epithelium, which is essential to sense inhaled bacteria ( ). Moreover, a subset of AMs form connexin 43-containing gap junction channels with the epithelium, which leads to Ca2+-dependent Akt activation and immunosuppression during lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation ( ). Thus, it is highly possible that the aberrant adhesion in the absence of RACK1 also contributes to the defective function of AMs. In addition, RACK1 has been demonstrated to bind to p53 family members and modulate their function and/or stability ( , ). Indeed, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated aberrant “p53 signaling pathway” in the absence of RACK1 ( ), which might dampen the survival of RACK1-deficient AMs and thereby worsen the AM scarcity caused by impaired PPAR signaling.
In addition, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated aberrant “cell cycle” in the absence of RACK1 ( ). Even though the down-regulation of Mki67 in RACK1-deficient mAMs failed to reach statistical significance ( ), a lower proportion of RACK1-deficient lung mAMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B) and AM-like cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B) incorporated EdU than their RACK1-sufficient counterparts. PPARγ-deficient AMs show augmented expression of COX1 (gene name Ptgs1) which generates prostaglandin E2 ( ). As prostaglandin E2 induces AM senescence ( ). Thus, the mechanism(s) underlying the impaired proliferation capacity of RACK1-deficient AMs can also be explained, at least partially, by impaired PPAR signaling.
Our bulk RNA-sequencing only revealed the transcriptome of AMs in homeostasis. We have previously shown that Lyz2-Cre-driven Rack1 deletion renders mice more resistant to influenza virus because RACK1 deficiency augments viral infection-induced activation of both interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and IRF7 ( ). On the other hand, it has been demonstrated that the loss of AMs results in higher viral load and more severe morbidity and mortality following influenza virus infection ( , ). This paradox is possibly because IRF7 is indispensable for the control of life-threatening influenza virus pneumonia by mediating the production of all type I and III interferons, and IRF3 assists IRF7 to accomplish this role ( , ). Type I and type III interferons play nonredundant roles to control influenza virus infection ( , ). The fewer number of AMs can be compensated by their enhanced antiviral innate immune response in the absence of RACK1. Meanwhile, the numbers of myeloid subsets other than AMs are largely unaltered in the lung of Rack1ΔMye mice and can provide enhanced antiviral innate immunity. Type III interferons are abundantly expressed by respiratory epithelial cells including alveolar type 2 cells at the early phase of influenza virus infection, which is essential to suppress initial viral spread without activating inflammation ( ). Now that Lyz2-Cre also targets alveolar type 2 cells ( ), the better control of influenza virus infection in Rack1ΔMye mice could be partially attributed to enhanced production of type III interferons by RACK1-deficient alveolar type 2 cells. These possibilities remain to be confirmed. Nevertheless, our data indicate significantly reduced AMs hinder the ability of Rack1ΔMye mice to clear bacterial infections. It is highly possible that defects in phagocytosis, adhesion, and survival also contribute to the impaired ability to fight against bacterial infections.
