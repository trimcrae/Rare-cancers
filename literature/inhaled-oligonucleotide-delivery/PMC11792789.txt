KEYWORDS: RNA, lungs, inhalable, immune system, barriers, nanoparticle, lipid, clinical

The enormous clinical success of mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines, despite their accelerated development and regulatory approval, has solidified RNA-based therapeutics as ‘the next big thing’ in medical advancements. During 2023 alone approximately 250 new RNA therapeutics or ‘nanomedicines’ entered development pipelines [ ]. This growth has, in part, been driven by recent high-level investment in the field by major government funding bodies and industry. It is important to note though, that this has only been made possible by over 30 years’ worth of preliminary RNA research that established a solid foundation for the field [ ]. While most R&D focus has been on injectable therapeutics since they represent, as nicely described by Witten [ ], the ‘lowest hanging fruit,’ there is now increasing interest in inhalable RNA therapeutics that can directly target the lungs. Despite their relative infancy, significant progress has been made with five inhalable products introduced into Phase I clinical trials in the last 7 years, with cystic fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and asthma as primary disease targets. The most significant advantage of RNA therapeutics over more conventional drug therapies is the potential to correct protein dysfunction seen with genetic respiratory conditions like cystic fibrosis and primary ciliary dyskinesia, and the capacity to treat conditions with ‘un-druggable’ targets. As a delivery route, however, the lungs represent a more significant challenge compared to injectables. This has been demonstrated by 1) failure of the first RNA therapeutic introduced by Translate Bio into clinical trials, 2) difficulties experienced in obtaining regulatory approval for inhalable liposomal amikacin (Arikayce®) and 3) insurance hurdles encountered by Mankind for Afrezza®, an inhalable insulin formulation [ ]. While clinical trials on inhalable RNA therapeutics (summarized recently by Huayamares) have generally shown reasonable tolerability, a general lack of consistent therapeutic efficacy (including improvements in lung function) has been observed [ , ]. Interestingly, one of the most successful constructs was ALN-RSV01, a naked siRNA targeting the RSV virus. This product, however, was not advanced into Phase III clinical trials [ , ]. To this end, it is important to consider that the clinical success of gene therapy is not just about the nucleic acid being delivered, but also the intricate construction of the delivery system and in vivo barriers to uptake by the target cells – each of which present their own unique challenges.
Lipid-based nanoparticle formulations (LNP) are currently leading the inhalable RNA delivery field since they offer the quickest route to clinical translation and have good lung biocompatibility [ ]. However, extensive research is still required to successfully develop aerosol or spray-dried formulations of LNPs that exhibit appropriate physicochemical and aerodynamic properties and preserve the biological function of the RNA cargo. In contrast, polymer-based systems have demonstrated better robustness to nebulization and spray drying processes than LNPs and may exhibit better stability in the lungs [ ]. Lipid-polymer hybrid delivery systems may, therefore, present a superior alternative to LNP-based delivery systems, since they can leverage the beneficial attributes of both materials for inhaled RNA delivery [ ]. Notably, the synergy of delivery strategies such as the use of lung surfactant coatings, mucus penetrating agents and cell targeting ligands can usefully increase the efficiency and selectivity of inhaled RNA formulations, but at the expense of added synthetic complexity and market cost [ ]. At this point, however, it is difficult to compare the clinical translatability, manufacturing and developmental advantages of lipid, polymer or lipid-polymer hybrid materials for the inhaled delivery of RNA because of a lack of sufficient comparison in the literature.
By far the biggest obstacle in developing inhalable RNA therapeutics, however, is predicting their disposition and safety within the lungs [ ]. Importantly, since underlying respiratory dysfunction can affect the lung tolerability and pharmacokinetics of nanomaterials, it is essential to evaluate the behavior of novel RNA therapeutics in diseased lung models early in the development pipeline. Unfortunately, while in silico tools are routinely used to predict the systemic biological behavior of small molecules, no such tools exist to predict drug or nanomaterial behavior in the lungs due to the complexity of both the lungs and the nano-biological behavior of nanomaterials. The biocompatibility of inhaled RNA formulations within the lungs can, however, be somewhat inferred by understanding their lung residence and biopharmaceutical behavior after pulmonary administration [ , ]. Unfortunately, there is a notable lack of pulmonary pharmacokinetic data for inhaled RNA formulations. Further, RNA therapeutics that are intended for cell transfection will fundamentally require repeat dosing at defined intervals, rather than a once-off ‘magical wand’ which may be applicable for vaccinations. This reliance on repeated dosing can, depending on the dose interval, increase nanomaterial lung burden over time and increase the risk of adverse effects, further highlighting the importance of understanding the kinetics of lung clearance in diseased lungs [ ]. To add an additional level of complexity, polyethylene glycol (PEG) is commonly used to formulate RNA therapeutics. PEG, however, is inherently immunogenic. With a reasonable proportion of the population now exhibiting anti-PEG antibodies, coupled with the significant immune surveillance function and reactogenicity of the lungs, the inhaled delivery of PEG-containing RNA therapeutics is likely to trigger unwanted immune responses [ , ]. There is, therefore, a critical need at this stage to identify a cost-effective proprietary technology platform that offers precision RNA delivery to the lungs, but also exhibits good aerosol stability, cell penetration, efficacy and safety.
Beyond the discovery and initial development phase inhaled RNA therapies face additional hurdles. The development pipeline for these systems must include optimization of the nanoparticle construction and formulation to enhance stability, transfection, endosomal escape and aerosol performance. Subsequently, characterization of transfection capability and safety in cell systems and preliminary testing of safety and transfection in live animals, with initial screening in small animals ahead of mandatory scale-up to larger more complex models are required. Currently, many novel formulations exhibit excellent aerosol properties and stability to nebulization as well as the ability to transfect lung cells in vitro. Convincing evidence of their ability to transfect cells in human lungs in vivo, however, is lacking. Gene delivery systems, irrespective of the nature of the nucleic acid or carrier, inherently show inconsistencies in transfection efficiency between the various in vitro assays and in vivo models. While air-liquid interface models using primary lung cells are currently considered the ‘gold standard’ for the in vitro evaluation of transfection capability in humans, they in no way guarantee such performance [ ]. On the other hand, while testing in rodents is important for preclinical safety evaluations, insights gained from inhaled transfection studies in rodents are of limited use given the substantial anatomical, physiological and biochemical differences between rodent and human lungs. This is notwithstanding the fact that rodent models poorly recapitulate human respiratory disease [ ]. Large animals, particularly non-human primates, and to a lesser extent pigs and sheep (depending on the target disease) are more reliable models for the pre-clinical evaluation of inhaled RNA therapies [ , ]. Testing in these more expensive models is also required to demonstrate the likely pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of inhaled therapeutic candidates in humans before clinical testing can be undertaken.
In the current absence of in vitro and small animal models that suitably recapitulate RNA delivery and transfection in the human lungs, lead constructs should ultimately be advanced into non-human primate models and humans as soon as possible to weed out candidates that will ultimately fail. Failure to do so may result in inhalable RNA therapeutics falling into the ‘nanomedicine trap’ seen after several decades of R&D into chemotherapeutic nanomedicines. Specifically, the appeal of chemotherapeutic nanomedicines gradually waned over the last 30 years as a result of the limited number of approved constructs, despite outstanding results in small animal models and expenditure of billions of dollars on nanomedicine R&D in the US alone [ ]. The downsides, of course, are the ethical and financial costs of testing in non-human primates which present significant limitations for academic researchers. Investment by large pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies will therefore be important to ensure promising constructs are progressed into research pipelines and pushed toward clinical trials early on.
With significant pre-clinical progress having already been made, coupled with a plethora of research still needed to find ways to optimally overcome existing barriers to clinical translation, inhalable RNA technology will remain a hot field for at least the next decade. Given the complexity of successful RNA delivery and transfection in the lungs, we must focus on identifying universal solutions to overcoming these barriers to make safe and effective inhalable therapeutics right the first time. In reality, however, first-generation inhalable RNA therapeutics will likely enter the market in the next 5–10 years, with better, more optimized therapeutics appearing later as we learn more about how inhaled RNA nanomedicines should ideally be made and delivered.
Finally, it is important to consider the cost of manufacturing these systems and patient equality. While optimized inhalable RNA medicines should be the ultimate goal, this should not come at the expense of synthetic complexity and cost so that these medicines may be accessible to all patients who could benefit from them. Going forward, powerful and widely accessible high throughput in vitro and in silico methods for identifying RNA formulations and delivery systems that are effective in humans must be developed. Notably, the improved development and standardization of in vitro models that more accurately mimic human outcomes are required, and these are summarized in a recent review by Petpiroon [ ]. Increased investment by big pharma will also be needed to fund appropriate early preclinical testing in large animal and non-human primate models to reach the full market and therapeutic potential of these systems.
