## Abstract

## Background
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by high invasiveness, high potential for metastasis, easy recurrence, and poor prognosis. There is an urgent need to develop new clinical treatments.

## Methods
This study utilized TNBC tissue microarrays to detect Peroxiredoxin 2 (PRDX2) expression levels and analyzed the correlation between PRDX2 and tumor invasion as well as invasion-related gene expression through the TCGA database. A stable PRDX2-knockdown triple-negative breast cancer cell line was established using lentiviral technology. The effects of PRDX2 on triple-negative breast cancer cell migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) were investigated via wound healing assays, Transwell assays, qPCR, and Western blotting. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), Western blotting, and dual luciferase reporter assays were performed to confirm that PRDX2 regulates FN1 expression through SP1. Furthermore, subcutaneous tumor xenograft models in nude mice were constructed to assess the effects of PRDX2 knockdown and the PRDX2 inhibitor Conoidin A on tumor growth in vivo.

## Results
Tissue microarray detection and correlative analysis revealed that PRDX2 is significantly upregulated in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) tumor tissues and positively correlated with genes associated with cell migration and invasion. Functional experiments demonstrated that in vitro knockdown of PRDX2 suppresses migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in TNBC cells. Furthermore, in vivo knockdown of PRDX2 or treatment with the PRDX2 inhibitor Conoidin A effectively reduced tumor burden. Mechanistic investigations utilizing RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) identified FN1 as a critical gene promoting TNBC cell migration and invasion. PRDX2 facilitates TNBC progression by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which enhances SP1 binding to the FN1 gene promoter. This regulatory cascade ultimately drives tumor advancement in TNBC.

## Conclusions
This study elucidates the role of the PRDX2/SP1/FN1 axis in TNBC migration and invasion, and highlights PRDX2 as a promising therapeutic target for triple-negative breast cancer.

## Introduction
Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignant tumor among women worldwide, accounting for 24.2% of all cancer cases. The mortality rate associated with breast cancer represents 15% of total cancer-related deaths each year [ ]. Metastasis of breast cancer is the primary cause of patient mortality [ ]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a subtype characterized by a lack of expression in estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). It has the highest rate of metastasis within breast cancers and accounts for 12-17% of all cases [ ]. Patients with TNBC generally have shorter survival times, with a mortality rate reaching 40% within five years after diagnosis [ ]. This high mortality can be attributed to the highly invasive nature of this subtype, as approximately 46% of patients experience distant metastasis and have a median survival period as short as 13.3 months. The postoperative recurrence rate can also be as high as 25% [ ]. Metastases in TNBC typically involve the brain and visceral organs, most occurring during the third year after diagnosis [ ]. Compared to non-TNBC patients, TNBC patients have shorter time intervals before recurrence, ranging from 19 to 40 months, and a death rate as high as 75% within three months after recurrence [ ]. Due to its unique molecular phenotype, TNBC is insensitive to endocrine or molecular targeted therapy; thus, chemotherapy becomes the primary systemic treatment method [ ]. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) plays an essential role in abnormal activation associated with TNBC and allows breast cancer cells to invade and spread distantly [ ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new treatment strategies and targets for TNBC, considering its aggressive behavior.
Peroxiredoxin 2 (PRDX2), an antioxidant enzyme, primarily functions within cells to eliminate hydrogen peroxide and safeguard against oxidative stress-induced damage [ ]. In neoplastic cells, PRDX2 expression is frequently elevated, potentially reflecting the heightened requirement for defense against oxidative stress [ ]. Nevertheless, elevated PRDX2 levels in tumors are detrimental [ ]. Research indicates that increased PRDX2 expression correlates with adverse tumor prognosis and may facilitate tumor epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via multiple pathways [ ]. For instance, PRDX2 can enhance the expression of EMT-associated genes by binding to transcription factors like Snail or Slug, thereby amplifying their transcriptional activity. Such genes encompass repressors of E-cadherin and inducers of mesenchymal markers, including N-cadherin and Vimentin, alterations characteristic of EMT [ ]. Additionally, PRDX2 might influence EMT by modulating cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [ ]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are crucial intracellular signaling molecules that regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration. By scavenging ROS, peroxiredoxin 2 (PRDX2) may influence intracellular ROS-dependent signaling pathways, such as mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MAPK/ERK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT), which are involved in the regulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. Furthermore, PRDX2 may facilitate EMT by modulating the tumor microenvironment. The tumor microenvironment comprises tumor cells, stromal cells, extracellular matrix, and various cytokines and growth factors [ ]. PRDX2 may alter the redox state within the tumor microenvironment, thereby influencing the expression and activity of cytokines and promoting EMT [ ]. Although the mechanisms underlying PRDX2’s role in tumor EMT have not been fully elucidated, the current findings provide a theoretical foundation for PRDX2 as a potential therapeutic target in cancer. Future research could further investigate the role of PRDX2 in diverse tumor types to support the development of novel cancer treatment strategies.
This study utilized an online database and transcriptome sequencing to elucidate the expression patterns and potential functions of PRDX2 in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Cellular experiments were conducted to investigate the impact of PRDX2 on the migratory and invasive capabilities of TNBC cells and dissect the underlying mechanisms. Despite PRDX2’s known role in oxidative stress, its specific mechanisms in TNBC metastasis remain unexplored. This study bridges this gap by elucidating PRDX2’s regulation of FN1 via the PI3K/AKT/SP1 axis. Overall, this research provides a scientific foundation for both understanding the role of PRDX2 in TNBC progression and developing potential novel therapeutic strategies.

## Materials and methods

## Acquisition of tissue microarray in TNBC patients
The tissue microarray of TNBC patients was procured from Shanghai Xin chao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Catalog No.: HBreD180Bc01-1, https://www.superchip.com.cn/). This microarray comprised 150 breast tumor tissues from TNBC patients and 30 adjacent non-tumor tissues. The inclusion criteria for TNBC patients were as follows: estrogen receptor (ER) negative, progesterone receptor (PR) negative, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2) negative. Additional details, including pathological grade, EGFR status, and Ki67 expression, are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

## Database download and analysis
RNA-seq data for TNBC were obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.com). The gene expression levels of PRDX2 in breast cancer tumors and the correlation heatmap of the PRDX family with genes associated with invasion and metastasis were analyzed. By using “invasion” and “metastasis” as keywords, 104 genes related to migration and invasion were retrieved from the GSEA database (gsea-msigdb.org).

## Download and analysis of core transcription factors
The TRRUST database (www.grnpedia.org) was utilized to identify transcription factors (TFs) that regulate invasion and migration genes [ ]. Using the online tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html), a Venn diagram was generated to illustrate the overlap between the RNA sequencing changes in breast cancer invasion and migration genes.

## Cell culture
The breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 (ATCC, #HTB-26) and MDA-MB-468 (ATCC, #HTB-132) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The HEK-293 cells were acquired from Pricella (CL-0001). For the cultivation of MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells, a complete DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin was utilized. In contrast, HEK-293 cells were maintained in a MEM medium containing 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

## RT-PCR
Total RNA from the cellular components was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, #15596026). cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription according to the previously described method [ ] and stored at -20 °C until further use. RT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Green (ABclonal, #RK21203) on a fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. GAPDH served as the reference gene. The experiment was replicated three times. The Ct values of the target genes were normalized to the Ct values of GAPDH, calculated as ΔCt = Ct target - Ct GAPDH. The relative mRNA abundance of the target genes was expressed as 2^(−ΔΔCt). The primer sequences are listed below:

H_N-cadherin:5’-AGCCAACCTTAACTGAGGAGT-3’, 5’-GGCAAGTTGATTGGAGGGATG-3’.
H_VIM:5’-AGTCCACTGAGTACCGGAGAC-3’, 5’-CATTTCACGCATCTGGCGTTC-3’
H_PRDX2:5’-GAAGCTGTCGGACTACAAAGG-3’, 5’-TCGGTGGGGCACACAAAAG-3’
H_FN1: 5’-CGGTGGCTGTCAGTCAAAG-3’, 5’-AAACCTCGGCTTCCTCCATAA-3’
H_COL6A2: 5’-GACTCCACCGAGATCGACCA-3’, 5’-CTTGTAGCACTCTCCGTAGGC-3’.
H_ITGB3: 5’-GTGACCTGAAGGAGAATCTGC-3’, 5’-CCGGAGTGCAATCCTCTGG-3

## Western blot analysis
In summary, total proteins were extracted from cell lysates using RIPA buffer (Biyuntian, #P0013C) using a previously published method [ ]. Equal total cellular proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, #IPVH00010). Following protein transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Shanghai Shengong, #A600669) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PRDX2 (CST, #46855), FN1 (CST, #63779), COL6A2 (Abclonal, #A3796), ITGB3 (Abclonal, #A19073), E-Cadherin (CST, #4065), N-Cadherin (CST, #4061), Vimentin (Abclonal, #A19607), MMP3 (PTG, #17873-1-AP), MMP9 (PTG, #10375-2-AP), Phospho-PI3 Kinase p85 (Tyr458)/p55 (Tyr199) (CST, #4228), Phospho-Akt (Ser473) (CST, #9271), Phospho-SP1-T739 (Abclonal, #AP0231), Phospho-NF-kB p65/RelA-S536 (Abclonal, #AP1294), Phospho-c-Jun-S63 (Abclonal, #AP0105), β-actin (Abclonal, #AC026), GAPDH (Abclonal, #AC033), and Histone (Abclonal, #A2348). After washing the membranes, they were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated sheep anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, #31466) or sheep anti-mouse secondary antibody (PTG, #SA00001-1) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were rewashed, and the proteins were detected using an ECL luminescence solution (Millipore, USA, #WBKLS0100) and imaged using an imaging system. Image analysis was conducted using Image J software.

## Scratch healing experiment
Cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, resuspended, and counted. The cell suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 3 × 105 cells/mL and seeded into 6-well plates, with three replicates for each group. Once the cells reached over 90% confluence, a 10 µL pipette tip was used to create a parallel scratch in each well. The wells were then washed with PBS to remove the old medium and floating cells. Cell cultures were maintained in serum-free DMEM medium, and the scratch widths were observed and photographed microscopically at 0 and 24 h. The cell scratch distances were measured using ImageJ (version 1.8.0) software. The cell migration rate (%) was calculated as follows: (0 h width − 24 h width) / 0 h width × 100%.

## Transwell cell invasion assay
In the upper chamber of a Transwell insert with an 8.0 μm pore size (Corning, #3422), 1:9 dilution of Matrigel (Corning Matrigel, #356234) was pre-coated to simulate the extracellular matrix environment encountered by cells during invasion in the body. Prepare a cell suspension with a concentration of 1 × 10^5 cells/mL using serum-free DMEM medium, and inoculate 0.2 mL of the suspension into the upper chamber of each Transwell insert. The lower chamber was supplemented with DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 24 h. Upon completion of the incubation period, the Transwell inserts were carefully removed and rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate non-invading cells and residual medium. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min to preserve cell morphology. Following fixation, cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog #32675), and the number of invading cells in each field was quantified by counting five randomly selected fields under a microscope.

## SiRNA interference assay
Target cells were seeded into 35 mm cell culture dishes and allowed to reach a confluence of 70–80% before transfection. The Lipofectamine™ 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Inc., Cat. No. #11668030) was pre-warmed to room temperature and gently mixed before use. One hour prior to transfection, the medium of the target cells was replaced with 1.6 mL of serum-free medium. Two 1.5 mL sterile centrifuge tubes were each filled with 200 µL of serum-free medium; one tube received 5 µL of siRNA, while the other received 3.5 µL of Lipofectamine™ 2000 transfection reagent. Both mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, the Lipofectamine™ 2000 transfection reagent solution was slowly added to the siRNA solution, mixed gently using a pipette, and incubated at room temperature for 15 to 20 min. The cells were then transfected and incubated at 37 °C. After 4 to 6 h, the medium was replaced with a complete medium. mRNA expression levels were assessed 48 h post-transfection, and protein expression levels were evaluated 72 h post-transfection. The siRNA sequences used are listed below:
si_NC: 5’-AGTAATATGTACTGAAGAT-3’.
si_FN1: 5’-TTGTTATGACAATGGAAAA-3’ #1,5’-TTGGTTTGTACTTGTTATG-3’ #2,5’-CTGTCTACTTCACAAACAA-3’ #3.
si_COL6A2: 5’-CTGTTTTGTGCTGAAAGGT-3’ #1,5’-GGGACAUGUUCCACGAGAA-3’ #2,5’-GGAGAGUGCUACAAGGUGA-3’ #3.
si_ITGB3: 5’-GCCAGATGATTCGAAGAAT-3’ #1,5’-GTCAATCTCTATCAGAACT-3’ #2,5’-GGGAAAATCCGTTCTAAAG-3’ #3.

## Generation of a stable PRDX2 knockdown HEK-293 cell line
Before transfection, HEK-293 cells were cultured in 2 mL of serum-free DMEM medium (Gibco, #1995500bt) for 2 h. The DNA mixture, consisting of pLVX-h-PRDX2-shRNA, psPAX2, and pMD2G plasmids at a ratio of 4 µg:2 µg:2 µg (Hunan Fenghuei Bio, #BR005), was prepared by adding it to 500 µL of serum-free DMEM. In a separate tube, 20 µL of Lipofectamine™ 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, #11668030) was mixed with 500 µL of serum-free DMEM. Both mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 5 min before being combined and added to the cells. The cells were then incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for 4–8 h, after which the medium was replaced with a fresh complete medium for further culture.
Viral supernatants were collected at 48、72 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and aliquoted into 1.5 mL EP tubes, which were stored at -80 °C. Target cells were seeded in 35 mm cell culture dishes (LabServ, #310109009). Once the cells reached approximately 70% confluence, the old medium was removed and replaced with 500 µL of fresh complete medium and 500 µL of virus solution. After 18–24 h of infection, the cells were maintained in a fresh complete medium supplemented with 1 µg/mL puromycin (Biomiky) until 90% of the cells exhibited green fluorescence under microscopic observation. The sequence as follows:
sh-PRDX2#1: 5’-GATCGTGAAGCTGTCGGACTACAAATTCAAGAGATTTGTAGTCCGACAGCTTCACTTTTTGGTACC-3’,
sh-PRDX2#2: 5’- GATCGCGGACTACAAAGGGAAGTATTCAAGAGATACTTCCCTTTGTAGTCCGCTTTTTGGTACC-3’,
sh-NC: 5’- AATTGCCCTTAGGGAACATTACTAGTTCAAGAGACTAGTAATGTTCCCTAAGGGCTTTTTTGCTAGC-3’.

## Transcriptome sequencing
RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher, #15596018). RNA quality was assessed via Nano Drop ND-1000 (concentration > 50 ng/µL) and Bioanalyzer 2100 (RIN > 7.0, total RNA > 1 µg). Poly(A) mRNA was enriched using Dynabeads Oligo(dT) (#25-61005, Thermo Fisher) in two rounds. Fragmentation was performed with NEB Next® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (#E6150S) at 94 °C for 5–7 min. cDNA synthesis used Super Script™ II Reverse Transcriptase (#1896649, Thermo Fisher), followed by double-stranded DNA synthesis with E. coli DNA Polymerase I (NEB #M0209) and RNase H (NEB #M0297), incorporating dUTP (#R0133, Thermo Fisher). Ends were blunted, A-tailed, and size-selected (~ 300 ± 50 bp) using magnetic beads. Second-strand digestion used UDG enzyme (NEB #m0280). PCR conditions: 95 °C for 3 min; 8 cycles (98 °C 15 s; 60 °C 15 s; 72 °C 30 s); final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Strand-specific libraries were sequenced on Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 (PE150; LC Bio, Hangzhou, China).

## Mouse models
Forty female BALB/c-nu mice (18–20 g), aged 6–8 weeks, were procured from Henan Skobes Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of the Medical College of Anhui University of Science and Technology under controlled conditions (temperature: 20 ± 2 °C, humidity: 55 ± 5%, 12-hour light/dark cycle). They were provided with continuous access to drinking water and fed three times a week. The mice were weighed daily. Following a 3-day acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups to establish the animal models: PBS, sh_NC, Con A (Conoidin A) treatment, and sh_PRDX2.
In the PBS group, mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 10^6 MDA-MB-231 cells. In the sh_NC group, mice received subcutaneous injections of 2 × 10^6 MDA-MB-231 (Plvx-sh_NC) cells. For the Con A treatment group, mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 10^6 MDA-MB-231 cells, and Con A (5 mg/kg) was administered every three days post-tumorigenesis. The PRDX2 inhibitor Conoidin A was synthesized by MCE (#HY-116090), and a stock solution was prepared using DMSO (Sigma, #D4540-100 ml). In the sh_PRDX2 group, mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 10^6 MDA-MB-231 (Plvx-sh_PRDX2) cells. Tumor volume was measured every three days starting from the day of tumor formation, and the experiment was terminated 27 days later, at which point the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following inhalation of 1% isoflurane (RWD, #R510-22-10). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Anhui University of Science and Technology (NO. HX-002).

## Immunohistochemical analysis
Breast cancer tissues from TNBC patients and tumor tissues from mice were sectioned into 5 μm thick slices and baked in an oven at 60 °C for 30 min. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene (three changes, 10 min each) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (from high to low concentration, 2 min each), followed by washing with distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Thermo Fisher, #005000) under high-pressure heating conditions. Tissues were circled with an immunohistochemical pen to prevent antibody loss. After a 30-minute incubation with blocking serum, primary antibodies were applied, and the sections were incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by three washes with PBS buffer. A horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by three additional PBS washes (5 min each). Sections were then developed with DAB chromogen, counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 30 s, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopic examination. The primary antibodies used were PRDX2 (CST, #46855, 1:500), MMP3 (CST, #13667, 1:500), and E-Cadherin (CST, #3195, 1:500). This study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Anhui University of Science and Technology (Ethics batch number: 202001006), and all patients provided written informed consent.

## Immunofluorescence assay
Mouse tumor tissues were sectioned into 5 μm thick slices, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in pH 8.0 EDTA antigen retrieval solution (Servicebio, #G1207) for 8 min. Following washing, the sections were air-dried, outlined with a histochemical pen (Servicebio, #G6100) to prevent antibody loss, and blocked with BSA (Servicebio, #GC305010) for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After two washes with PBS, the corresponding secondary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The sections were subsequently stained with DAPI (Servicebio, #G1012) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. An autofluorescence quenching agent (Servicebio, #G1221) was applied and incubated for 5 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by rinsing with running water for 10 min. After drying, the sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, #DMI3000 B) for imaging. The primary antibodies used were PRDX2 (CST, #46855, 1:500), FN1 (CST, #63779, 1:500), and Phospho-SP1-T739 (Abclonal, #AP0231, 1:200). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ (1.8.0) software.

## Promoter transfection and luciferase assay
When the confluence of HEK-293 cells reached 60–70%, a dual-luciferase reporter gene assay was conducted to investigate the binding relationship between SP1 and the FN1 promoter. Initially, the Human Transcription Factor Database (Human TFDB, http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/HumanTFDB/) was utilized to predict three binding sites for SP1 within the − 2000 to -200 bp region of the FN1 promoter. Subsequently, the FN1 promoter region fragments (Full, Mut1, Mut2, and Mut3) were cloned into the pGL3-basic vector, while SP1 was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-3xFLAG vector (Hanbio, China). The constructs pGL3-Basic-h-FN1 (Full, Mut1, Mut2, and Mut3) or pGL3-Basic (NC) were co-transfected into HEK-293 cells along with pcDNA3.1-3xFLAG-H-Sp1 or pcDNA3.1-3xFLAG and the pRL-TK Renilla luciferase vector. After 48 h, cell lysates were harvested, and the activities of firefly and Renilla luciferases were quantified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (HB-DLR-100, Hanbio, China). Luminescence signals were detected using a multifunctional microplate reader (SuPerMax3100, Shanghai Flash Spectrum Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). The experiment was replicated three times.

## Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com). Statistical significance was assessed via a two-tailed Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA; with the levels of significance denoted by asterisks as follows: ns, not significant; *P ≤ 0.05; **P ≤ 0.01; ***P ≤ 0.001; ****P ≤ 0.0001.

## Results

## PRDX2 was highly expressed in tumor tissues of TNBC patients
To analyze the expression of PRDX2 in TNBC, the protein expression levels of PRDX2 were assessed using microarrays of tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues from TNBC patients (Fig.  A). The results demonstrated that PRDX2 was significantly overexpressed in tumor cells (indicated by red dashed lines) within the tumor tissues of TNBC patients (Fig.  B-C). Consistent with these findings, the PRDX2 gene was also significantly upregulated in the tumor tissues of TNBC patients, as evidenced by data from the TCGA database (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Given that TNBC is characterized by high invasiveness and metastatic potential [ ], we further analyzed the tissue sequencing data of TNBC (ER-, PR-, and HER2-) from the TCGA database. The analysis revealed that 1,003 genes were upregulated in tumor tissues (Fig.  D). By intersecting these upregulated genes with 104 invasion and metastasis-related genes from the GSEA database, we identified 23 key genes involved in migration and invasion in TNBC (Fig.  E). Additionally, considering that PRDX2 is a member of the peroxiredoxin (PRDX) family [ ], we investigated the correlation between PRDX family members and the expression of migration and invasion-related genes. The results indicated that PRDX2 was significantly positively correlated with the expression of numerous migration and invasion-related genes (Fig.  F and Supplementary Fig. 1B).
 

## PRDX2 facilitated the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells
To investigate the impact of PRDX2 on the functionality of breast cancer cells, we initially constructed a lentiviral plasmid, pLVX-sh-hPRDX2, designed to knock down PRDX2 expression stably. This construct successfully reduced PRDX2 levels in the breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 (Fig.  A-B). Subsequent scratch assays and Transwell invasion assays revealed that PRDX2 knockdown significantly inhibited cell migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), with a more pronounced effect observed in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig.  C-D and Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). These findings suggest that PRDX2 might play a crucial role in the invasion and metastasis of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Further analysis via transcriptome sequencing indicated that the expression of genes such as CXCL3, MMPs (MMP3 and MMP9), and FN1 was markedly decreased following PRDX2 knockdown (Fig.  E). Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the PI3K-AKT, extracellular matrix (ECM), and Jak-STAT signaling pathways as potentially key regulatory mechanisms influenced by PRDX2 (Fig.  F). Given that invasion and metastasis are defining characteristics of TNBC, we intersected a set of 104 known invasion and metastasis-related genes with those downregulated upon PRDX2 knockdown. This analysis identified FN1, COL6A2, and ITGB3 as core intersection genes (Fig.  G).
 

## PRDX2 facilitates breast cancer cell migration and EMT by regulating FN1
Based on identifying three key genes (FN1, COL6A2, and ITGB3) that may influence the invasion and metastasis of breast cancer cells (Fig.  G), we aimed to determine whether these genes are the core regulators. We examined the mRNA and protein expression levels of FN1, COL6A2, and ITGB3 in breast cancer cells with PRDX2 knockdown. The results demonstrated that only FN1 was significantly downregulated in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig.  A, B).
 To elucidate the regulatory relationships between PRDX2 and the aforementioned proteins, we employed siRNA to knock down the expression of FN1, COL6A2, and ITGB3 in MDA-MB-231 cells, following PRDX2 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3A). The findings indicated that individual knockdown of PRDX2, FN1, COL6A2, and ITGB3 inhibited breast cancer cell migration and the expression of EMT-related and MMP proteins (Fig.  C-F and Supplementary Fig. 3B-D). However, compared to the groups with knockdown of PRDX2 or FN1 alone, the combination of PRDX2 and FN1 knockdown did not further suppress cell migration (Fig.  C and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Conversely, in PRDX2-knockdown cells, additional knockdown of COL6A2 and ITGB3 resulted in approximately 29% and 16% reductions in cell migration rate, respectively, compared to groups with knockdown of COL6A2 or ITGB3 alone (Fig.  D-E and Supplementary Fig. 3C-D). Moreover, the knockdown of FN1, COL6A2, and ITGB3 did not significantly affect PRDX2 expression (Fig.  F). These results suggest that FN1 is a critical downstream target directly regulated by PRDX2, and PRDX2 promotes tumor cell migration and EMT through the regulation of FN1 (Supplementary Fig. 3E).

## Effect of PRDX2 Inhibition on breast cancer growth in vivo
To further elucidate the role of PRDX2 in breast cancer progression in vivo, a murine breast cancer model with PRDX2 inhibition was established. The model was divided into four groups: control group (PBS), negative control group (sh_NC), PRDX2 enzyme activity inhibitor group (Conoidin A, ConA), and PRDX2 expression knockdown group (sh_PRDX2) (Fig.  A). After a 27-day observation period, a significant reduction in the volume and weight of subcutaneous tumors was observed in the ConA-treated group compared to the control group (Fig.  B-C). Similarly, the subcutaneous tumor volume and weight in the PRDX2 knockdown group were significantly reduced compared to the negative control group (Fig.  B-C). These findings indicate that both inhibiting PRDX2 activity and reducing its expression levels suppress breast cancer cell growth in vivo. Furthermore, when comparing the ConA group with the sh_PRDX2 group, it was noted that the tumor volume in the ConA group was smaller, suggesting that pharmacological inhibition may be more efficacious (Fig.  B-C). Additionally, the expression of PRDX2 and FN1 proteins in the tumor tissues of the four groups was examined, revealing that inhibition or knockdown of PRDX2 led to a reduction in FN1 protein expression in vivo (Fig.  D, PRDX2 in yellow dashed box and FN1 in white dashed box). Similarly, suppression of MMP3 and E-Cadherin expression was observed in the tumor tissues (Fig.  E-F, indicated by the black arrow).
 

## PRDX2 activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to promote the binding of SP1 to the FN1 promoter
To elucidate the mechanism by which PRDX2 regulates breast cancer cell invasion and metastasis, we initially screened 17 genes associated with these processes using the TRRUST database. This screening identified SP1, RELA (P65), and JUN as potential key transcription factors (Fig.  A). Consistent with these findings, we observed that the protein levels of both phosphorylated SP1 (p-SP1) and phosphorylated P65 (p-P65) were reduced in PRDX2 knockdown breast cancer cells, with the most significant reduction in p-SP1 (Fig.  B-C). Given the critical changes in PI3K-AKT signaling observed in PRDX2 knockdown breast cancer cells (Fig.  D), we further investigated the regulation of this pathway by PRDX2. Our results demonstrated that PRDX2 knockdown inhibited the activation of the PI3K/AKT/SP1 signaling pathway (Fig.  E-F).
 Additionally, based on the three potential binding sites of SP1 to the FN1 promoter, we constructed the corresponding mutants and human SP1 overexpression plasmids. The results indicated that the promoter regions from − 1456 to -1446 and from − 854 to -845 are crucial for SP1-mediated FN1 activation (Fig.  G). By examining PRDX2 and SP1 protein expression in tumor tissues from four groups of mice, we found that both the inhibition of PRDX2 activity (via Conoidin A treatment) and the reduction of PRDX2 expression (through PRDX2 knockdown) significantly inhibited the intranuclear translocation of SP1 (Fig.  J). These findings suggest that PRDX2 plays a pivotal role in breast cancer invasion and metastasis by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, subsequently promoting the binding of SP1 to the FN1 promoter (Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
Breast cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignant tumors globally [ ], and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents a highly invasive and poorly prognosed subtype [ ]. Currently, chemotherapy serves as the primary systemic therapy for TNBC; however, the issue of drug resistance remains a significant challenge [ ]. Peroxiredoxin 2 (PRDX2), a member of the catalase family, primarily functions to safeguard cells against reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced damage [ ]. ROS are detrimental molecules generated during cellular metabolism, and their excessive accumulation can induce oxidative stress, thereby damaging cellular components such as lipids, proteins, and DNA, contributing to various diseases, including cancer [ ]. This study’s findings demonstrate a positive correlation between PRDX2 overexpression and enhanced tumor cell migration and invasion in TNBC, suggesting potential molecular targets for novel therapeutic approaches in TNBC. Our data indicate that PRDX2 expression levels are markedly elevated in TNBC tumor tissues compared to normal breast tissues, corroborating prior research and underscoring the critical role of PRDX2 in TNBC.
In vitro, we observed that the stable knockdown of PRDX2 expression significantly inhibited the migration and invasion of TNBC cells. Additionally, PRDX2 silencing led to an increase in the expression of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related protein E-cadherin, while simultaneously decreasing the expression of N-cadherin and Vimentin. These findings suggest that PRDX2 may play a crucial role in the invasion and metastasis of TNBC cells.
To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which PRDX2 regulates breast cancer cell growth, RNA sequencing was conducted on PRDX2-silenced MDA-MB-231 cells. Through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and three core genes—Fibronectin 1 (FN1), Collagen type VI alpha 2 chain (COL6A2), and Integrin beta 3 (ITGB3)—were identified as key regulators. Fibronectin 1 (FN1) is a multifunctional extracellular matrix protein; its overexpression has enhanced cancer cell metastasis, angiogenesis, and proliferation [ ]. The COL6A2 gene encodes the Collagen type VI alpha 2 chain, a crucial component of the extracellular matrix that interacts with other collagens and non-collagenous molecules to preserve tissue structure and function [ ]. The ITGB3 gene encodes the integrin β3 subunit, which primarily mediates interactions between cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM) and cell-to-cell interactions [ ]. Subsequent experiments revealed that PRDX2 silencing markedly suppressed FN1 expression in MDA-MB-231 cells. Moreover, the knockdown of FN1 following PRDX2 silencing did not significantly affect breast cancer cells’ migratory and invasive capabilities. These findings indicate that PRDX2 modulates the invasion and metastasis of breast cancer cells via the regulation of FN1.
Regarding the regulatory mechanism of PRDX2 on FN1, our findings suggest that SP1, P65, and C-JUN are likely the key transcription factors through which PRDX2 regulates FN1, as determined by bioinformatics analysis. SP1 (Specificity Protein 1), a member of the CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein family, is ubiquitously expressed across various cell types. It modulates the transcription of numerous genes by binding to specific DNA sequences, typically GC boxes, within gene promoter regions [ ]. P65, also called RelA, is a transcription factor belonging to the family of NF-κB (Nuclear Factor-κB) and plays a crucial role in controlling a wide array of cellular responses [ ]. C-Jun, a constituent of the AP-1 (Activator Protein-1) complex, can regulate the expression of multiple genes [ ]. Experimental evidence indicates that PRDX2 enhances the binding of SP1 to the FN1 gene promoter by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting the progression of TNBC. The promoter regions − 1456 to -1446 and − 854 to -845 have been identified as critical sites for SP1 binding, which activates FN1 activity. This discovery elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying PRDX2’s role in TNBC development and offers a theoretical foundation for therapeutic approaches targeting PRDX2.
Our research has also demonstrated that both the knockdown of PRDX2 expression and the inhibition of its activity can effectively suppress the growth of TNBC tumors in vivo. These findings suggest that PRDX2 might be a promising therapeutic target for TNBC. Given the aggressive nature and high metastatic potential of TNBC, coupled with the current paucity of effective treatment options, a therapeutic approach targeting PRDX2 could offer a novel treatment alternative for TNBC patients.
While our study offers compelling evidence that PRDX2 represents a novel therapeutic target for TNBC, additional research is necessary to confirm the clinical significance of PRDX2 in this context. Future investigations should concentrate on several key areas: (1) the differential expression of PRDX2 across various TNBC subtypes; (2) PRDX2’s role across TNBC subtypes and assess combinatorial therapies with existing chemotherapeutics;3) the interaction between PRDX2 and established TNBC markers; 4) preclinical and clinical trials of PRDX2-targeted therapies. 5) The Potential Clinical Application of PRDX2 as a Diagnostic Marker for TNBC.
Overall, our results indicate that PRDX2 significantly influences the invasive and metastatic potential of TNBC and could serve as a promising therapeutic approach. Targeting PRDX2 might lead to the development of innovative treatments aimed at enhancing the prognosis of TNBC patients.

## Conclusions
In summary, this study reveals that PRDX2 is highly expressed in TNBC and promotes tumor invasion and metastasis. Silencing PRDX2 suppresses cell migration, invasion, and the EMT process, mechanistically linked to the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway and regulation of FN1 expression. PRDX2 drives FN1 transcription by enhancing the binding of transcription factor SP1 to specific promoter regions (-1456/-1446 and − 854/-845 positions). In vivo experiments confirm that targeting PRDX2 inhibits tumor growth, highlighting its potential as a novel therapeutic target for TNBC.
