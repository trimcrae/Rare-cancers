## Abstract
Despite optimal glycemic control, individuals with diabetes remain susceptible to diabetic mellitus erectile dysfunction (DMED), primarily due to metabolic memory—where the deleterious effects of previous hyperglycemia persist even after normoglycemia is achieved. Circular RNAs (circRNAs), representing an endogenous category of non-coding RNA that modulates eukaryotic gene expression regulation, demonstrate growing significance in this biological process. This investigation examines the influence of circRNAs in glucose metabolism memory-related DMED. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was executed to detect circPBX1, miR-195-5p, and YAP1 levels in rat penile cavernous endothelial cells under glucose metabolism memory conditions. Functional assays demonstrated that manipulating circPBX1 levels in vitro markedly influenced endothelial cell viability, proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenic capacity, while in vivo delivery of circPBX1 ameliorated DMED by restoring cavernous structure and improving erectile function. Mechanistically, circPBX1 functions as a sponge for miR-195-5p, thus relieving its inhibition on YAP1 and promoting VEGF expression. Introduction of a miR-195-5p mimic recapitulated the phenotypic consequences of circPBX1 suppression in both cellular and animal models. Our findings identify that circPBX1 mitigates glucose metabolism memory-induced erectile dysfunction by antagonizing endothelial dysfunction and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target in DMED.

## Introduction
Diabetic mellitus erectile dysfunction (DMED) represents a common and debilitating complication of diabetes, markedly diminishing quality of life. Global projections estimate that the diabetic population will rise to 578 million by 2030 , . The pathophysiology of DMED is multifactorial, encompassing endothelial impairment, neurogenic deficits, smooth muscle dysfunction, and metabolic derangements . Among these, endothelial dysfunction within the corpus cavernosum serves as a principal contributor, characterized by reduced endothelium-dependent vasodilation, attenuated angiogenic capacity, and elevated oxidative stress, collectively resulting in compromised penile perfusion and erectile failure , . Previous studies have demonstrated that in mouse arteries, high glucose (HG) and methylglyoxal (MGO) preferentially impair endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factor (EDHF)-mediated vasodilation compared to nitric oxide (NO)-dependent relaxation. In the corpus cavernosum, HG and MGO interfere with NO release, thereby compromising endothelial-dependent vasodilatory function .
Metabolic memory—where transient hyperglycemia exerts lasting deleterious effects despite subsequent glycemic normalization—has emerged as a pivotal mechanism underlying the onset and persistence of DMED – . In diabetic cardiomyopathy, for example, hyperglycemia-induced O-GlcNAcylation and CaMK2A activation have been shown to establish endothelial metabolic memory, driving the sustained secretion of SEV miR-15-16 and promoting cardiac injury. Analogous mechanisms may perpetuate endothelial dysfunction in the corpus cavernosum, thereby maintaining erectile deficits over time.
Circular RNAs (circRNAs) represent essential regulatory elements in multiple biological functions, encompassing gene expression modulation, protein interaction, and translational control . Defined by their covalently closed circular structure, circRNAs exhibit exceptional stability and extended half-life within cells . Recent findings suggest that circRNAs serve crucial functions in disease development, notably diabetes mellitus and its complications, through their involvement in cellular homeostasis and signaling network regulation , . In the context of DMED, circRNAs are believed to contribute to endothelial dysfunction and impaired angiogenesis by interacting with microRNAs (miRNAs) and other molecular regulators . miRNAs, representing small non-coding RNA sequences that typically bind to the 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTRs) of target mRNAs, function to downregulate gene expression via mRNA degradation or translational inhibition . Among these, miR-195-5p is a key modulator of vascular homeostasis, influencing endothelial proliferation, migration, and tube formation. Aberrant expression of miR-195-5p contributes to several vascular pathologies, encompassing diabetic atherosclerosis and renal cell carcinoma, highlighting its relevance in DMED pathogenesis , .
The Hippo/YAP1 signaling pathway serves as a central regulator of cell proliferation, apoptosis, and tissue homeostasis. Within the vasculature, it serves a pivotal function in preserving endothelial integrity and facilitating angiogenesis . YAP1, operating as a fundamental downstream component of the Hippo pathway, modulates the transcription of genes implicated in cellular proliferation and survival . Emerging evidence indicates that miR-195-5p directly targets YAP1, thereby modulating the activity of the Hippo/YAP1 pathway. This regulatory interaction is essential for maintaining endothelial function and promoting angiogenic processes , . Aberrant regulation of this pathway contributes to endothelial dysfunction and impaired angiogenesis—pathological features commonly observed in diabetic kidney disease and ED – . Moreover, in the context of diabetic kidney disease, YAP1 inactivation exacerbates renal injury by impairing mitochondrial quality control in tubular epithelial cells through a CXCL1-dependent M1 macrophage polarization mechanism. Notably, YAP1 activation restores mitochondrial function, suggesting its therapeutic potential for diabetic kidney disease .
This investigation explored the role of circRNAs regarding metabolic memory and DMED, with particular emphasis on their interactions with miR-195-5p and the Hippo/YAP1 signaling cascade. circPBX1 functions as a molecular sponge for miR-195-5p, thereby modulating the expression of Hippo/YAP1 downstream target genes and influencing endothelial function and angiogenesis in the corpus cavernosum. Additionally, the therapeutic potential of targeting the circPBX1–miR-195-5p–YAP1/Hippo–VEGF axis to restore endothelial function and mitigate DMED driven by metabolic memory was explored. Future investigations may further reveal the pathways responsible for the persistent downregulation of circPBX1 induced by glucose metabolic memory.

## Results

## The phenomenon of glucose metabolism memory can lead to erectile dysfunction in diabetic rats
The experimental workflow is illustrated in Fig.  . Experimental animals were categorized into three groups: NG (normal glycemic state), HG (persistent hyperglycemic state), and HG-NG (glucose metabolism memory). Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats received intraperitoneal injections of STZ, and random blood glucose levels were measured on days 3 and 7 post-injection. At week 8, apomorphine (APO) testing was executed to detect rats with confirmed DMED. In the HG-NG group, subcutaneous administration of neutral protamine zinc insulin was initiated at week 12 post-DM modeling to restore normoglycemia and continued until week 24. At week 24, Intracavernous pressure (ICP) was measured in all groups. Compared with the NG group, both maximum intracavernous pressure (Max ICP) and the ΔICP/MAP ratio were significantly reduced in the HG and HG-NG groups (p < 0.01); ΔICP was defined as maximal ICP minus basal ICP, and MAP as mean arterial pressure. Notably, erectile function in the HG-NG group showed partial improvement relative to the HG group, with statistically significant differences noted between the NG and HG-NG groups. However, no substantial distinction was detected between the HG and HG-NG groups (Fig.  ). Histological evaluation was performed on penile tissues collected at the end of the experiment. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining revealed marked structural disruption of the corpus cavernosum in both the HG and HG-NG groups, characterized by reduced smooth muscle layer thickness compared to the NG group. Masson’s trichrome staining further demonstrated a significant reduction in smooth muscle fiber content and a concomitant increase in collagen deposition in the HG and HG-NG groups, resulting in a significantly decreased smooth muscle-to-collagen ratio (p < 0.01) (Fig.  ). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of penile paraffin sections was conducted to evaluate vascular morphology and endothelial markers. Distinct alterations in vascular sinus structure and the expression of endothelium-related molecules (VEGF/CD31) were observed under varying glycemic conditions. In both the HG and HG-NG groups, the cavernous sinus exhibited irregular luminal contours, reduced sinusoidal density, and disorganized architecture. Expression levels of endothelial-specific markers—VEGF (green fluorescence) and CD31 (red fluorescence)—were significantly decreased compared to the NG group. Although a modest recovery trend in VEGF/CD31 expression was observed in the HG-NG group relative to the HG group, the variation between the two groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).
 

## The phenomenon of glucose metabolism memory impairs the function of vascular endothelial cells of the penile cavernosa in rats in vitro
The experimental design for the cavernous endothelial cell injury model is illustrated in Fig.  . The study included four groups: NG (euglycemic), HG (persistent hyperglycemia), HG-NG (glucose metabolism memory), and MG (hyperosmotic mannitol control). Primary endothelial cells were successfully isolated from rat penile corpus cavernosum tissue, exhibiting a characteristic cobblestone-like morphology . Endothelial identity was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining for CD31, a specific endothelial cell marker (Fig.  ). VEGF level was notably diminished in the HG group versus the NG group (p < 0.01). Although VEGF levels in the HG-NG group showed partial restoration relative to the HG group, they remained significantly lower than those in the NG group (p < 0.01). No substantial distinction in VEGF expression was noted between the MG and NG groups (p > 0.05) (Fig.  ). In functional assays, endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation were significantly impaired in both the HG and HG-NG groups versus the NG group (p < 0.01). Conversely, the MG group displayed no statistically significant differences in these functions versus the NG group (p > 0.05) (Fig.  ). To determine whether neural components exhibited sustained impairment despite subsequent glycemic control following initial hyperglycemic injury, we evaluated the expression of nitrergic nerve markers . qPCR analysis revealed that mRNA levels of nNOS and β-III tubulin (Tubb3) were significantly reduced in the diabetic (HG) and glucose metabolic memory (HG-NG) groups compared to normal controls (p < 0.05). Glycemic control through insulin treatment alone did not fully restore their expression. Furthermore, circPBX1 overexpression did not reverse this downregulation at the transcriptional level (p > 0.05 vs. HG-NG group), a finding consistent with Western blot results showing no recovery of nNOS and neurofilament protein levels (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 

## Rno-circPBX1 is a key molecule in glucose metabolism memory phenomena in DMED rats
circRNAs constitute a category of non-coding RNAs distinguished by their covalently closed-loop configuration. These molecules have been linked to the development of numerous diseases through their function as miRNA sponges, modulating essential cellular processes encompassing proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and metabolism. In the initial phase of this study, transcriptomic analysis was conducted to profile circRNA expression in penile tissue from male Sprague-Dawley rats under normal glycemic conditions (n = 3) and from diabetic rats with ED (n = 3). Distinct circRNA expression patterns were identified between the two groups. Based on thresholds of |log2 fold change (FC)| > log25 and -log10 error discovery rate (FDR) > 2, 11 circRNAs were detected to be markedly elevated and 16 downregulated in the corpus cavernosum of diabetic ED rats (Fig.  ). The full list of differentially expressed circRNAs identified by transcriptomic analysis is provided in Supplementary Data  . Among the differentially expressed circRNAs, circPBX1 was notably downregulated in both the HG and HG-NG groups relative to the NG group, as confirmed by qPCR (Fig.  ). circPBX1 is a 436 bp circRNA derived from the PBX1 gene (NM_001134862.2), located on chromosome 13 (chr13:82844952–83121046). circPBX1 was more resistant to RNase R digestion than linear PBX1 transcripts in rat corpus cavernosum endothelial cells (RCECs) (Fig.  ). Furthermore, Stability assays demonstrated that circPBX1 exhibited a half-life exceeding 24 hours following actinomycin D treatment, whereas the linear PBX1 mRNA counterpart degraded within 6 hours (Fig.  ). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed predominant cytoplasmic localization of circPBX1 in RCECs (Fig.  ). Sanger sequencing of amplified circPBX1 products, commissioned from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., confirmed sequence consistency with the transcriptome data, verifying the accurate identification of the circular transcript (Fig.  ). To exclude potential genomic rearrangement or trans-splicing artifacts, divergent primers specific for circPBX1 and PBX1 mRNA were designed. Gel electrophoresis of cDNA and genomic DNA (gDNA) samples demonstrated successful amplification of circPBX1 exclusively in cDNA, with no detectable product in gDNA, thereby confirming the circular nature and exon origin of circPBX1 (Fig.  ). Further analysis of the subcellular localization of circPBX1 using FISH revealed predominant cytoplasmic distribution, consistent with findings from nucleocytoplasmic fractionation assays (Fig.  ).
 

## Overexpression of circPBX1 improves endothelial function in rat cavernosum
To elucidate the role of circPBX1 in glucose metabolism memory, three distinct small interfering RNAs (siRNAs)—siRNA1, siRNA2, and siRNA3—were designed to target circPBX1. Among them, only siRNA1 significantly reduced circPBX1 expression in RCECs following transfection (Fig.  ). Stable overexpression of circPBX1 in RCECs was achieved using the Fast-LV vector system (Fig.  ). Functional assays were conducted to assess the effects of circPBX1 modulation in RCECs under glucose metabolism memory conditions. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) analysis demonstrated a substantial reduction in cell viability after transfection with si-circPBX1-1 (Fig.  ). Consistently, EdU incorporation assays demonstrated reduced proliferative capacity in cells transfected with siRNA1 (Fig.  ). Transwell migration assays, scratch wound healing assays, and Matrigel-based tube formation assays collectively indicated that circPBX1 silencing significantly impaired RCECs migratory and angiogenic capacity (Fig.  ). Conversely, overexpression of circPBX1 in HG-NG group RCECs significantly enhanced cell viability, proliferation, migration, and tube-forming ability (Fig.  D, F, H–M). Western blotting analysis further supported these findings, showing that knockdown of circPBX1 resulted in decreased expression of VEGF, while circPBX1 overexpression led to its upregulation. Collectively, these results suggest that circPBX1 facilitates RCECs proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis following hyperglycemia-induced injury (Fig.  ).
 

## circPBX1 functions as miR-195-5p sponge adsorption in RCECs
Given that circRNAs predominantly function in the cytoplasm by sequestering miRNAs through sponge activity, and considering the high basal expression of circPBX1 in normal RCECs, it was hypothesized that circPBX1 mitigates endothelial dysfunction by sponging specific miRNAs and thereby modulating downstream gene expression. As functions as an essential element of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) that directs miRNAs toward their mRNA targets, candidate miRNAs potentially interacting with circPBX1 were predicted using TargetScan and miRanda algorithms, in combination with transcriptome-based predictions. Five rno-miRNA candidates were identified: miR-195-5p, miR-103-3p, miR-107-3p, miR-15a-5p, and miR-16-5p. Given the critical function of miR-195-5p in regulating key biological processes—particularly cell proliferation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis—this miRNA was selected for further investigation. As a core component of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), AGO2 mediates interactions between miRNAs and their targets. RIP assays using an anti-AGO2 antibody in RCECs showed significant enrichment of endogenous circPBX1 in AGO2 immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls. In addition, RNA pull-down using a biotinylated circPBX1 probe markedly enriched miR-195-5p relative to the negative control probe, supporting a direct interaction between circPBX1 and miR-195-5p (Fig.  ). FISH demonstrated that both circPBX1 and miR-195-5p were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of RCECs, with evident co-localization (Fig.  ). An RNA pull-down assay using a biotin-labeled circPBX1 probe further confirmed significant enrichment of miR-195-5p compared to control probes (Fig.  ), suggesting that circPBX1 may function as a miR-195-5p molecular sponge. To investigate expression changes under diabetic conditions, qRT-PCR analysis revealed a notable elevation of miR-195-5p in both HG and HG-NG groups compared to the NG group (Fig.  ). Subsequent dual-luciferase reporter experiments confirmed the specific binding interaction between circPBX1 and miR-195-5p. The wild-type circPBX1 sequence harboring the predicted miR-195-5p binding site along with its corresponding mutant version was inserted into the psiCHECK2 vector and introduced into HEK-293T cells. Co-transfection utilizing miR-195-5p mimics with the wild-type construct resulted in a marked decrease in luciferase activity relative to the mutant construct. Conversely, miR-195-5p inhibition enhanced luciferase activity in cells transfected with the wild-type reporter (Fig.  ). Previous studies suggest that miRNA upregulation commonly suppresses mRNA expression in post-transcriptional regulatory networks. Based on this mechanism, it is postulated that circPBX1 downregulation eliminates its protective effect, allowing miR-195-5p to inhibit target mRNA expression, thereby exacerbating endothelial dysfunction. Through miRanda database predictions, YAP1 emerged as a potential downstream target of miR-195-5p. Dual-luciferase reporter assays provided additional confirmation of this interaction. In HEK-293T cells transfected with wild-type YAP1 3′UTR constructs, luciferase activity demonstrated significant suppression relative to mutant constructs (Fig.  ). Additionally, YAP1 protein levels exhibited marked reduction upon circPBX1 knockdown or miR-195-5p overexpression, while circPBX1 overexpression resulted in notable elevation of YAP1 expression, similar results were obtained by qPCR analysis (Fig.  ). Collectively, these results demonstrate that circPBX1 alleviates vascular endothelial dysfunction by protecting YAP1 from miR-195-5p–mediated suppression. Mechanistically, circPBX1 acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), enhancing YAP1 expression via direct binding and sequestration of miR-195-5p in RCECs.
 

## Inhibition of miR-195-5p level can improve glucose metabolism memory and cause damage to penile cavernous endothelial cell function
To additionally confirm the regulatory interactions within the circPBX1/miR-195-5p/YAP1 axis, rescue experiments were performed. RCECs were pretreated to establish a glucose metabolism memory model and subsequently transfected with either miR-195-5p mimics or inhibitors. Functional alterations in endothelial cells were assessed using a panel of assays, including EdU proliferation, Transwell migration, scratch wound healing, tube formation, and CCK-8 viability assays. Compared to the HG-NG + miR-NC group, HG-NG + miR-195-5p inhibition notably enhanced cell viability, proliferative capacity, migration, and tube formation in RCECs subjected to glucose metabolic memory conditions (Fig.  ). These findings demonstrate that silencing miR-195-5p partially recovers endothelial function compromised by prior hyperglycemic exposure. YAP1, a key downstream mediator of the Hippo signaling pathway, regulates transcription by forming a complex with TEAD4 after nuclear translocation . Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that inhibition of miR-195-5p markedly enhanced YAP1 nuclear accumulation in endothelial cells (Fig.  ), suggesting enhanced transcriptional activity. Collectively, these results support the existence of a functional regulatory axis involving circPBX1, miR-195-5p, and YAP1. Inhibition of miR-195-5p relieves its inhibitory effect on YAP1, thereby improving endothelial function in the corpus cavernosum under conditions of glucose metabolic memory.
 

## Local injection of overexpressing circPBX1 and miR-195-5p inhibitors significantly ameliorates HG-NG-induced erectile dysfunction in rats
In a rat model of DMED, this investigation additionally examined the function of circPBX1 in mediating persistent endothelial injury in cavernous vasculature associated with glucose metabolic memory. To assess the functional impact of circPBX1 overexpression, localized intracavernosal injections of adenovirus vectors carrying circPBX1 or miR-195-5p inhibitors were administered to DMED rats exhibiting glucose metabolism memory. To further delineate the mechanistic involvement of miR-195-5p in circPBX1-mediated effects, an additional group was treated with both circPBX1 overexpression and miR-195-5p mimics. One week following treatment, erectile function was evaluated, and penile tissue samples were harvested at the study endpoint for histological analyses, encompassing H&E staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and TEM. Compared to the HG-NG group, rats treated with either circPBX1 overexpression or miR-195-5p inhibition exhibited significantly improved erectile function (Fig.  ). In contrast, the group receiving both circPBX1 and miR-195-5p mimics showed no significant functional recovery, with erectile parameters comparable to the HG-NG group. These observations suggest that elevated miR-195-5p levels counteract the pro-angiogenic effects of circPBX1. Histological analysis revealed that circPBX1 overexpression or miR-195-5p inhibition markedly improved cavernous sinus architecture, restored smooth muscle thickness, and increased the smooth muscle-to-collagen ratio. Conversely, the miR-195-5p mimic group displayed disrupted sinusoidal structure, reduced smooth muscle thickness, and a decreased smooth muscle/collagen ratio, with no significant difference from the HG-NG group (Fig.  ). TEM analysis further demonstrated that diabetic rats in the HG-NG group exhibited severe ultrastructural abnormalities, including mitochondrial swelling, cristae fragmentation, and reduced mitochondrial numbers, thickened vascular walls, widened intercellular spaces, irregular endothelial morphology. These ultrastructural abnormalities were markedly attenuated following adenovirus-circPBX1 or miR-195-5p antagonist administration (Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
Recent investigations have increasingly focused on the pivotal regulatory role of circRNAs in gene expression and their involvement in various human pathologies . This study examined the function of circPBX1 in preserving endothelial integrity within the corpus cavernosum. Findings demonstrated that circPBX1 modulates endothelial dysfunction of cavernous vasculature both in vivo and in vitro, thereby ameliorating ED in diabetic rats. Mechanistically, circPBX1 functions as an endogenous sponge for miR-195-5p, suppressing its activity, which in turn facilitates the assembly of the YAP1/TEAD transcriptional complex and enhances the expression of VEGF, a downstream effector of the Hippo signaling pathway .
Transient hyperglycemia can imprint a long-lasting “metabolic memory” that persists even after glycemic normalization, predisposing patients with diabetes to long-term complications such as heart failure. Notably, individuals who achieve early glycemic control may still experience elevated cardiovascular risk, likely attributable to early hyperglycemia-induced endothelial injury. Previous research has identified sustained pathological effects of long-term diabetic plasma-derived small extracellular vesicles (SEVs) enriched in miR-15/16 on cardiomyocytes, capable of inducing cardiac dysfunction in otherwise healthy models. These SEVs predominantly originate from arterial endothelial cells. The prolonged release of SEV miR-15/16 has been mechanistically linked to persistent CAMK2A/O-GlcNAcylation pathway activation in endothelial cells .
The observed downregulation of circPBX1 in diabetic cavernosal endothelial cells may be attributed to the sustained hyperglycemic milieu. One plausible explanation is the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which are known to exert long-lasting regulatory effects due to their slow tissue clearance. AGEs may suppress circRNA biogenesis by disrupting the back-splicing machinery, potentially through altering the expression or activity of splicing factors such as QKI. Alternatively, the hyperglycemia- and AGEs-enriched environment might upregulate specific RNA-binding proteins (e.g., DDX5) that promote circRNA decay , . These interconnected mechanisms could collectively lead to the sustained reduction of circPBX1, contributing to the persistent vascular dysfunction seen in diabetes. Further investigations are warranted to delineate the precise regulatory network through which hyperglycemia modulates circPBX1 expression, thereby offering novel insights into the molecular basis of diabetic endothelial injury.
In the present study, persistent endothelial injury driven by glucose metabolism memory was identified as a key contributor to ED, characterized by compromised endothelial function, attenuated angiogenesis, disrupted cavernous sinus architecture, and reduced penile blood perfusion – . Early hyperglycemia markedly suppressed circPBX1 expression in RCECs, a downregulation that persisted despite subsequent glycemic normalization. Exogenous circPBX1 overexpression restored endothelial cell function and enhanced RCECs proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenic potential. Prior findings have established that miR-195-5p-mediated repression of YAP1 impairs tumor angiogenesis, thereby decelerating tumor progression . A comparable anti-angiogenic effect of miR-195-5p was observed in RCECs; however, YAP1 mimic transfection attenuated this suppression. These results support the hypothesis that either overexpression of circPBX1 in the corpus cavernosum or administration of miR-195-5p antagonists in rats with glucose metabolism memory-induced ED could effectively restore erectile function. Importantly, the negligible ectopic expression, coupled with the low baseline of its host gene in non-target organs, suggests a reduced potential for off-target effects, which supports the preliminary safety profile of the localized therapy (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1), a central effector of the Hippo signaling cascade, serves as an essential regulator in controlling cell proliferation, differentiation, and tissue homeostasis . When the Hippo pathway becomes activated, it initiates a kinase cascade (MST1/2 → LATS1/2), which causes phosphorylation and cytoplasmic sequestration of YAP1, ultimately leading to its degradation and inhibition of transcriptional activity. Conversely, when this pathway becomes inactive, YAP1 undergoes dephosphorylation and moves into the nucleus, subsequently forming complexes with TEAD transcription factors to activate genes essential for cellular growth and viability, encompassing CTGF and CYR61 , , . YAP1 activity is modulated by mechanical stimuli, metabolic cues (e.g., glucose), and cross-talk with signaling pathways including Wnt and TGF-β, contributing to tissue repair and regeneration , – . In vascular endothelial cells, YAP1 influences proliferation, barrier integrity, and inflammatory responses, thereby modulating the development of diabetic vascular complications. Under physiological conditions, YAP1 facilitates angiogenesis by upregulating pro-angiogenic factors encompassing VEGF and supports vascular repair. However, chronic hyperglycemia can induce aberrant YAP1 activation, leading to excessive angiogenesis observed in conditions such as diabetic retinopathy , , . Under glucose metabolism memory conditions, YAP1 expression in RCECs was downregulated, a process modulated by miR-195-5p and circPBX1. circPBX1 overexpression effectively reversed the suppression of YAP1 expression induced by transient hyperglycemia (HG-NG), while YAP1 inhibition exacerbated HG-NG-induced impairment of RCECs migration, invasion, proliferation, and tube formation. Western blotting analysis confirmed that miR-195-5p markedly diminished YAP1 level, consistent with dual-luciferase reporter assay results indicating direct binding between miR-195-5p and the YAP1 3’UTR. Beyond angiogenesis, YAP1 activity contributes to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. The YAP1–TEAD4 transcriptional complex regulates ECM-related gene expression—upregulating fibrogenic mediators like TGF-β and downregulating anti-fibrotic markers such as CYR61—thus promoting collagen synthesis and deposition in hepatic tissues. This activation drives the transdifferentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, which secrete large quantities of ECM proteins, encompassing type I and III collagens and fibronectin, ultimately aggravating tissue fibrosis , . YAP1 function exhibits strong context dependency, influenced by cell type, microenvironment, and pathological state. In penile cavernosal tissue, YAP1 may simultaneously contribute to antifibrotic endothelial protection and profibrotic signaling. Our study identifies YAP1 as a key downstream effector in the circPBX1/miR-195-5p-mediated protective pathway. It is important to note that YAP1 is often described as a ‘double-edged sword,’ capable of promoting processes such as tissue regeneration and angiogenesis but also pathological fibrosis and cancer , . In our DMED model, the restoration of YAP1 via circPBX1 sponging of miR-195-5p primarily enhanced endothelial function and angiogenesis without inducing noticeable penile fibrosis. Western blot analysis indicated YAP1 activation (reduced p-YAP1 Ser127) without upstream LATS1 upregulation. Notably, expression of the pro-fibrotic YAP1/TEAD target CTGF was not elevated upon circPBX1 treatment (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting YAP1-TEAD activity in this context promotes angiogenesis over fibrosis. This was corroborated histologically: Masson’s trichrome staining revealed no increase in collagen deposition, and an improved smooth muscle-to-collagen ratio, confirming that fibrosis was not exacerbated (Fig.  ). These suggests that the YAP1-driven transcriptional program in the diabetic penile microenvironment may favor genes governing vascular homeostasis over those driving extracellular matrix deposition. Future studies exploring the full spectrum of YAP1 target genes and its interaction with other transcriptional co-regulators in the corpus cavernosum will be crucial to fully harness its therapeutic potential while mitigating risks. The vascular endothelium serves as a critical regulator of diverse physiological processes, including maintenance of vascular tone, laminar blood flow, inhibition of thrombosis, promotion of fibrinolysis, modulation of inflammation and platelet aggregation, and regulation of vascular smooth muscle cell function . Under normal physiological states, it produces and releases numerous bioactive substances, encompassing vasoconstrictive and vasodilatory agents, pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, as well as fibrinolytic and antifibrinolytic factors. Endothelial dysfunction disrupts this balance, leading to impaired perfusion of the cavernous vascular sinus, inadequate corporal filling, and subsequent ED , , . Subsequent experimental validation demonstrated that the protective effect of circPBX1 is mediated, at least in part, through the restoration of endothelial junctional complexes, as evidenced by the rescued expression and proper localization of VE-cadherin and ZO-1. Notably, circPBX1 overexpression promoted endothelial marker expression without causing abnormal vascular morphology or disorganized CD31+ cell clusters, suggesting its pro-angiogenic effect is structurally ordered and distinct from pathological neovascularization (Supplementary Fig.  ).
The present study demonstrated the involvement of the circPBX1/miR-195-5p/YAP1 axis in mediating pathological alterations in RCECs under glucose metabolism memory conditions. Transfection with miR-195-5p mimics suppressed RCECs proliferation, migration, and tube formation under normoglycemic conditions, whereas miR-195-5p antagonists significantly attenuated the endothelial injury induced by prior hyperglycemia (HG-NG). In vivo data further confirmed that circPBX1 overexpression ameliorated ED in rats subjected to glucose metabolism memory, characterized by elevated miR-195-5p expression. These findings suggest that circPBX1 modulates the endothelial function of the cavernous sinus both in vitro and in vivo within the framework of metabolic memory. Dysregulated expression of circPBX1 and miR-195-5p in the cavernous endothelium of DMED rats implicates their potential role in the clinical pathogenesis of diabetic ED, warranting further investigation through human studies. In summary, the data provide compelling evidence that circPBX1 downregulation leads to miR-195-5p overexpression, triggering Hippo pathway activation, inhibiting YAP1 nuclear translocation, and suppressing VEGF expression. These molecular events disrupt endothelial function and reduce penile tissue perfusion, ultimately contributing to glucose metabolism memory-induced ED. Targeting the circPBX1/miR-195-5p/YAP1 regulatory axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for diabetic patients experiencing ED driven by metabolic memory.
This study demonstrates that high-glucose exposure markedly suppresses circPBX1 expression in RCECs, leading to endothelial dysfunction and contributing to microvascular impairment. Mechanistically, circPBX1 modulates endothelial homeostasis by functioning as an endogenous sponge for miR-195-5p, thereby regulating downstream signaling pathways (Fig.  ). These findings underscore a novel therapeutic approach targeting non-coding RNAs to counteract vascular complications associated with glucose metabolic memory.
 

## Materials and methods

## Experimental animals

## Animals and experimental design
All procedures received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Laboratory Animal Research Center at Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China), and were executed in compliance with Directive 2010/63/EU established by the European Parliament regarding the safeguarding of animals utilized for scientific research purposes. A total of 70 specific-pathogen-free (SPF) male Sprague-Dawley rats (aged 6–8 weeks) were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). All rats were acclimatized for one week under standard conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2°C, 50 ± 10% humidity) with free access to standard chow and water.

## Induction of type 1 diabetes and grouping
After acclimatization and an overnight fast, diabetes was induced in 60 rats by a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 60 mg/kg in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 4.5; Sigma-Aldrich). The remaining 10 rats (Normal Control, NG) received an equivalent volume of citrate-phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Random blood glucose levels were measured on days 3 and 7 post-injection. Rats exhibiting glucose concentrations above 16.7 mmol/L, along with signs of polyuria, polydipsia, and polyphagia, were classified as having Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM). Eight weeks after STZ injection, diabetic rats were subjected to an apomorphine (APO, 80 μg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) test to confirm the presence of diabetic erectile dysfunction (DMED). A total of 44 rats that successfully met both the hyperglycemia and DMED criteria were selected for the subsequent “glucose metabolic memory” study. These 44 rats were randomly divided into four experimental groups (n = 11 per group) using a random number table: glucose metabolic memory (HG-NG), glucose metabolic memory therapy (HG-NG circPBX1 / HG-NG miR-195-5p inhibitor, treated with AAV-circPBX1/ AAV-miR-195-5p inhibitor), glucose metabolic memory therapy control (HG-NG + miR-195-5p mimic, treated with AAV-miR-195-5p mimic). The 10 non-diabetic rats constituted the NG Group. An additional HG Group (n = 10) consisted of diabetic rats confirmed by STZ injection and APO test but sacrificed at the 12-week time point to establish the baseline diabetic injury state, prior to any intervention.

## Experimental protocol and interventions
Random blood glucose was monitored weekly to ensure treatment efficacy. To investigate therapeutic intervention, beginning at week 20 of the experiment, rats in the treatment groups received biweekly intracavernosal injections of the respective AAV constructs (AAV-circPBX1, AAV-miR-195-5p inhibitor, or AAV-miR-195-5p mimic) or vehicle (for the HG-NG group), continuing until week 24.

## Sample collection and safety assessment
Upon completion of the protocol at week 24, all rats were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane (RWD, China). To evaluate the potential systemic toxicity of long-term AAV-mediated gene therapy, key hepatic and renal function biomarkers were quantified. Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were measured using specific commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, strictly following the manufacturers’ protocols(Beyotime, Rat AST ELISA Kit, U96-3235E-48T; Rat ALT ELISA Kit, U96-3226E-48T; Urea Nitrogen Assay Kit, S0574S). All assays were performed in duplicate. Following humane euthanasia by cervical dislocation, penile and other relevant tissues were harvested for subsequent analysis.

## Cell extraction, culture, and transfection
The isolation and culture of Rat corpus cavernosum endothelial cell were adapted from an established protocol, with minor modifications – . RCECs were isolated from 6-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats procured from the Animal Experiment Center of Southern Medical University. Following anesthesia with Inhalation of 2-3% isoflurane, the penis and glans were exposed. Penile tissue was dissected along the foreskin and excised near the base. Cavernosal tissue was then isolated, sectioned into ~1 mm³ fragments, and subjected to enzymatic digestion using a mixture of type I collagenase (2 mg/L) and elastase (2 mg/L). Subsequently, the digested tissue was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in sorting buffer. RCECs were purified via positive selection using an immunomagnetic bead-based strategy. Briefly, streptavidin-coated magnetic beads were conjugated with a biotinylated mouse anti-rat CD31 antibody. The cell suspension was incubated with the antibody-bound beads at 4°C for 30 minutes. Unbound cells were removed by washing, and CD31-positive cells were isolated by applying the cell-bead complex to a magnetic stand. The captured cells were washed, resuspended in complete DMEM low-glucose medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and seeded into 25 cm² culture flasks for subsequent culture in a 37°C, 5% CO₂ incubator. The extracted primary cells underwent cultivation in a moisture-controlled chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 hours to achieve stable cellular conditions. The culture medium consisted of low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) comprising 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin . Cell morphology was regularly monitored, with short spindle-shaped or cobblestone-like (“paving pebble”) appearances indicating endothelial identity. RCECs were further analyzed by flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX N31376 instrument. The cells were confirmed to be CD31⁺/vWF⁺ based on staining with anti-CD31 (BD Biosciences, USA, cat. no. 774045) and anti-VWF antibodies (Abcam, UK, cat. no. AB6994) (Supplementary Fig.  ). CD31 immunofluorescence staining was employed to verify endothelial cell purity. Cells were passaged upon reaching 80% confluence, with the P3–P4 generations demonstrating the most stable morphology. To establish cell culture models simulating various glucose conditions, the following treatments were applied: 5.5 mM glucose for normal glucose (NG), 5.5 mM glucose with 24.5 mM mannitol as an osmotic control (MG), and 30 mM glucose for high-glucose (HG) conditions. For the glucose metabolism memory (HG-NG) model, cells were cultured in 30 mM glucose for four days, followed by 5.5 mM glucose for the subsequent four days.
Transfection of RCECs was executed utilizing Lipofectamine 3000 (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with the following constructs or oligonucleotides from D-nano (Beijing, China): synthetic small interfering RNA targeting circPBX1 (si-circPBX1), miR-195-5p inhibitor, corresponding negative controls (NCs), and miR-195-5p mimic. Full-length circPBX1 underwent PCR amplification and subsequent insertion into a D-nano expression vector, which was also introduced into RCECs via Lipofectamine 3000.

## RNA extraction and qRT-PCR
RNA isolation was executed utilizing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), while cytoplasmic and nuclear components were isolated through the NE-PER Cytoplasmic and Nuclear Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific). In the miRNA examination process, cDNA synthesis occurred via the miRNA cDNA First Strand Synthesis Kit (AgBio, Shanghai, China), with subsequent qRT-PCR analysis employing SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (AgBio, Shanghai, China). For circRNA and mRNA analysis, cDNA was synthesized using the Evo M-MLV RT Premix Kit (AgBio, Shanghai, China). The primer sequences utilized are depicted in Supplementary Table  . Gene expression quantification was computed through the 2–ΔΔCt methodology.

## Actinomycin D treatment and ribonuclease R (RNase R) treatment
For transcriptional stability analysis, cells were treated with 2 μg/mL actinomycin D or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) as a vehicle control. In RNA stability evaluations, RNA samples (approximately 2 μg) were maintained at 37 °C for 20–30 minutes with or without RNase R (3 U/μg) per the supplier’s protocols. The processed RNA was then purified utilizing the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit (Qiagen, Düsseldorf, Germany) prior to subsequent analyses.

## Western blotting analysis
The proteins were procured from rat corpus cavernosum tissues or cultured cells through a Total Protein Extraction Kit (Vazyme). After SDS-PAGE separation, the proteins underwent transfer to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). The membranes received blocking treatment with 5% non-fat milk for 2 hours at ambient conditions, succeeded by overnight exposure at 4 °C to primary antibodies: anti-β-tubulin (1:2000, 10094-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-VEGF (1:2000, 19003-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-YAP1 (1:2000, 13584-1-AP, Proteintech), as well as the other indicated proteins at the corresponding dilutions according to the manufacturers’ instructions or prior optimization. Subsequently, the membranes underwent washing and incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Proteintech) for 2 hours at ambient conditions. The BeyoECL Plus detection system (Beyotime) enabled the visualization of protein bands. β-tubulin, β-actin, or GAPDH was used as the loading control, as indicated in the corresponding figures.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
The circPBX1 sequence harboring either wild-type or mutated miR-195-5p binding sites was cloned into the SgfI and NotI sites of the psiCHECK2 vector (Geneseed). Likewise, the 3′-UTR of YAP1 containing either wild-type or mutant miR-195-5p binding sites was cloned into the XhoI and NotI sites of the pmiR-RB-REPORT vector (RiboBio). HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with luciferase reporter plasmids along with either miR-195-5p mimics or negative control (miR-NC) utilizing Lipofectamine 3000 (Life Technologies). After 48 hours, relative luciferase activities were determined using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Co-transfection with either pmir-RB-REPORT-YAP1-WT or pmir-RB-REPORT-YAP1-MUT and miR-195-5p mimics or NC was also conducted. Binding of miR-195-5p to the target RNA sequence led to the inhibition of Renilla luciferase (Rluc) expression, thereby decreasing relative luciferase activity.

## RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)
RNA-FISH was conducted utilizing a Cy3-labeled full-length RNA probe for circPBX1 and a FITC-labeled probe for miR-195-5p (5′–3′ sequence: UAGCAGCACAGAAAUAUUGGC). Hybridization and signal detection were performed using a fluorescence in situ hybridization kit (RiboBio) per the supplier’s protocols. Cell nuclei underwent counterstaining with DAPI, and fluorescent images were acquired with a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope.

## Cell proliferation assay, EdU staining, cell migration assays, and wound healing migration
Cell viability assessment utilized the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). RCECs undergoing experimental treatments were placed into 96-well plates, succeeded by the addition of 10 μL CCK-8 solution per well. After incubation for 4 hours at 37 °C, a microplate reader measured the absorbance values at 450 nm.
For DNA synthesis-based proliferation analysis, the EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was employed. RCECs underwent cultivation in 6-well plates, succeeded by exposure to 50 μM EdU over a 2-hour period. Cells were subsequently washed, fixed, permeabilized, and stained with a Click reaction solution for 30 minutes. Nuclear counterstaining was executed utilizing DAPI (1:1000, Beyotime). Fluorescence images were procured from six randomly selected fields using a Nikon N31373 fluorescence microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).
The migration ability of cells was assessed utilizing Transwell chambers with Costar Transwell inserts (Corning, Bedford, MA, USA), following established protocols . RCECs were placed in the upper compartment of 24-well plates, while 650 μL of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 hours of incubation, the cells that traversed to the lower surface of the membrane underwent methanol fixation and crystal violet (0.1%) staining. A Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to visualize and enumerate the migrated cells. Quantification involved averaging cell numbers from a minimum of six randomly chosen microscopic fields for each well.
A wound healing assay was executed to further examine cell motility. Cells were grown in 6-well plates, and a scratch was generated using a sterile pipette tip. Following PBS washing, a serum-free medium was introduced. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 12 hours from at least six randomly selected fields to assess migration.

## Tube formation assay
Endothelial tube formation was examined on Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel (Corning), as previously described . Matrigel (30 μL) was pre-coated onto 24-well plates, followed by seeding of RCECs. After 12 hours, capillary-like structures were imaged utilizing an Olympus IX73 motorized inverted microscope (Olympus). Tube lengths were quantified from at least six randomly selected fields per well using ImageJ software (version 1.49p; NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Evaluation of erectile function
Intracavernous pressure (ICP) was measured four weeks after treatment, as previously described . Anesthesia was achieved through inhalation of 2-3% isoflurane. A 25-gauge needle was placed into the left crus of the penis for ICP measurement. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was continuously recorded through a heparinized polyethylene (PE-50) catheter (200 IU/mL) inserted into the carotid artery, secured in place after a V-shaped incision. Hemodynamic parameters were monitored using the Biopac MP150 physiological recording system (BIOPAC Systems Inc., USA). The cavernous nerve was stimulated with bipolar electrodes at 50 Hz, 5 V for 50 seconds. Erectile function was assessed using two ratios: Max ICP/MAP and ΔICP/MAP, representing maximal erectile response and dynamic pressure change during stimulation, respectively.
Following functional evaluation, rats were euthanized, and penile tissues were harvested. One-third of each penis was fixed in paraffin for histological analysis, while the remaining tissue was stored at −80 °C for further molecular or biochemical studies.

## Immunofluorescence
For IF analysis, paraffin-embedded penile tissue sections and RCECs slides were prepared following protocols reported previously . Primary antibodies included anti-VEGF (1:400, rabbit, Cat#19003-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-CD31/PECAM1 (1:200, rabbit, Cat#A19014, Abclonal). After incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies, fluorescence images were captured from six randomly selected microscopic fields. Semiquantitative analysis of relative fluorescence intensity was conducted utilizing Image-Pro Plus software (Version 6.0).

## Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining and Masson trichrome staining
For H&E staining, the tissue specimens underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China) for 24 h before being embedded in paraffin. Sequential 4 μm sections were subjected to xylene dewaxing (two cycles, 10 min per cycle), succeeded by gradual rehydration utilizing descending ethanol concentrations (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, and 70%, 5 min each concentration), and three washes in distilled water (5 min per wash). Staining was executed utilizing an H&E solution (Servicebio) per the supplier’s protocols.
Masson’s trichrome staining was executed on 5 μm paraffin sections of rat penile tissue fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The collagen-to-smooth muscle ratio in the corpus cavernosum was quantitatively examined by employing Image-Pro Plus software (Version 6.0), following standard procedures.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
For TEM analysis, fresh penile tissue was sectioned into fragments no thicker than 1 mm and preserved in 0.1 M PBS comprising 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 4 hours. Secondary fixation was executed in 1% osmium tetroxide (in 0.1 M PBS) for 2 hours under ambient conditions. Samples were subsequently processed through a graded ethanol series, exposed to propylene oxide, and prepared for ultrathin sectioning. Sections of 60–80 nm thickness were generated and treated with uranyl acetate and subsequently with lead citrate. Examination of the ultrastructural characteristics was executed utilizing a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7700, Tokyo, Japan).

## RNA immunoprecipitation assay (RIP) and RNA pull-down
RIP experiments were executed utilizing the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore) per the supplier’s protocols. The cells underwent PBS washing, lysis in co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) buffer, and incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with an anti-AGO2 antibody or control IgG at 4 °C. After immunoprecipitation, RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and analyzed by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). For circRNA pull-down experiments, biotin-labeled circPBX1 or control probes (Bersinbio, Guangzhou, China) were incubated with RCECs lysates. After lysis and centrifugation-based pre-clearing, the obtained supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with M-280 streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Invitrogen, USA). Bound RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, and miR-195-5p enrichment in the pull-down fractions was quantified by qRT-PCR.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Rat corpus cavernosum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Immunohistochemistry was performed using the PV-8000 polymer detection system. After deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, immunohistochemistry was performed using the PV-8000 polymer detection system. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-CD31 primary antibody (rabbit, 1:2000, Abcam, ab182981), followed by DAB visualization. Staining was quantified in terms of endothelial staining intensity and microvessel density. All slides were imaged using an Olympus BX63 microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software. The stained sections were also assessed independently by three blinded pathologists.

## Statistics and reproducibility
For in vivo studies, sample sizes were determined based on preliminary experiments and prior publications. A total of 10 rats were included in the NG group and 11 rats per group for all other groups in the main physiological analyses (Figs.  , ). For histological and transmission electron microscopy analyses, a subset of animals (n = 3 biological replicates per group) was used (Fig.  ). For in vitro experiments, all assays were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, each with three technical replicates unless otherwise specified in the figure legends. Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry experiments were repeated at least three times with similar results; representative images are shown throughout. For fluorescence quantification, the nucleus/cytoplasmic ratio of YAP1 was calculated and analyzed as described (Fig.  ). Rats were randomly assigned to experimental groups. For in vivo treatments, investigators performing drug administrations and subsequent functional evaluations were blinded to group allocation. For in vitro studies, cell culture and treatment conditions were coded to ensure blinding during data acquisition and analysis.
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test or D’Agostino–Pearson omnibus test, as appropriate, prior to statistical analysis. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, while Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by appropriate post hoc multiple comparisons tests as specified: Tukey’s test (Figs.  , , ), Bonferroni’s test (Fig.  ), or other tests as indicated in the respective figure legends. All statistical tests were two-sided. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01. Statistical tests and sample sizes (n) for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.
