## Abstract

## Background
Acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are severe respiratory ailments globally, with the NLRP3 inflammasome playing a crucial role in the progression of ALI. G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43) plays a role in regulating the immune system and maintaining homeostasis; however, the relationship between this receptor and the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway in ALI is still not well understood.

## Methods
Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in combination with nigericin (Nig) was utilized to stimulate the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages from mouse peritoneum in vitro, after which the GPR43 selective agonist 4-CMTB was administered. For the in vivo component, an ALI model was created through intratracheal LPS injection, with the experimental group of mice receiving intraperitoneal administration of 4-CMTB at a dosage of 20 mg/kg. Lung damage was observed as well as the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis.

## Results
According to in vitro studies, 4-CMTB inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation, achieved by decreasing the activity of caspase-1. Additionally, it resulted in a reduction of IL-1β and IL-18 secretion, as well as decreased formation of ASC specks under LPS + Nig conditions. It also significantly inhibited the production of GSDMD-N terminus, the released lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and the comparable ratio of propidium iodide (PI). In vivo studies showed 4-CMTB significantly alleviated the pathological damage caused by LPS in the lungs, including the infiltration of neutrophils, formation of hyaline membranes, and thickening of alveolar septa. It also lowered the lung wet/dry weight ratio and extravasation of Evans blue (EB) dye, as well as protein content of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β). Based on the findings of the mechanistic investigation, β-arrestin 2 binding to NLRP3 was promoted by GPR43, thereby preventing the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

## Conclusion
GPR43 prevents NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis by the β-arrestin 2 pathway in LPS-induced ALI. The results of the research show that we can target GPR43 to treat and cure ALI and other inflammatory diseases.

## Introduction
Acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are severe respiratory diseases that pose a serious risk to human health. These diseases progress rapidly, characterized by dyspnea and hypoxemia [ ]. The emergence of ALI involves interplay of many cells and pathways. This multitude of causes that can trigger acute respiratory distress disease include pneumonia, trauma, sepsis, acute pancreatitis, aspiration of gastric contents inhalation of corrosive fumes, and mechanical ventilation [ , ]. When these triggers are encountered, there is uncontrolled inflammation of the lung causing damage to the lung tissue. As a result of ALI, inflammatory processes dysregulate at an important phase. Among these regulatory disruptions, the activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and its associated pyroptosis serve as significant mediators of the inflammatory response and contribute to tissue damage in ALI [ , ].
NLRP3 inflammasomes play a pivotal role in the innate immune system, regulating inflammatory responses. The multi-protein complex facilitates the processing and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) along with cleavage of Gasdermin D (GSDMD) which initiate strong inflammatory responses and pyroptosis [ ]. Pyroptosis is a type of programmed cell death (PCD) with burst of plasma membrane and spilling of cell contents into the microenvironment. The factors that are released activate adjoining immune cells, resulting in a vicious cycle that enhances inflammation [ ]. Consequently, achieving a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying NLRP3 inflammasome activation and subsequent pyroptosis induction during ALI is of critical significance for the development of effective therapeutic strategies.
G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43), also referred to as short-chain fatty acid receptor 2, is present in immune cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. This receptor is a significant regulator of the immune system [ , ]. GPR43 activity regulates the inflammatory reaction and plays a role in sustaining immune homeostasis. GPR43 stimulation, for example, can promote the M2 phenotype differentiation of macrophages and regulate the synthesis of inflammatory factors [ ]. GPR43 activation stimulates neutrophil chemotaxis, respiratory burst, and cytokine release to enhance resistance against sepsis due to methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection [ ]. In addition, GPR43 exerts anti-inflammatory activity in rheumatoid arthritis [ ], colitis [ ], atherosclerosis [ ] and diabetic nephropathy [ ] conditions. Earlier studies of our team have shown that acetate (a ligand of GPR43) can help ameliorate LPS-induced ALI although the mechanisms require further investigation [ ]. A deeper understanding of the immune regulatory mechanism of GPR43 would increase our knowledge on the role of short-chain fatty acid receptor in the immune system and their regulation of inflammatory diseases. Recent study found that the stimulation of GPR43 by acetate can modulate lung injury and inflammasome activation in neonatal mice with bronchopulmonary dysplasia [ ]. Still, the precise function of GPR43 activation in the inflammatory response associated with ALI and the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome requires additional research.
In this study, we developed an ALI model via LPS intratracheal instillation and investigated it in vitro cultured macrophages. We systematically studied the effect of GPR43 activation on NLRP3 inflammasome signal and pyroptosis, and its therapeutic potential for ALI. The goal of this study is to provide a new theoretical basis for the anti-inflammatory treatment of the GPR43 target.

## Materials and methods

## Animals
Healthy male C57BL/6 mice, aged between 6 and 8 weeks and weighing 20 to 25 g, were obtained from Jiangsu SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animals used in the experiments were kept in an environment with a 12-hour cycle of light and darkness, allowing them unrestricted access to both food and water. The Research Ethics Committee of Naval Medical University granted approval for all procedures conducted on the mice involved in the study. To create the ALI model, the experimental mice were randomly separated into three equal groups (n = 6 for each group): the control group, the group treated with LPS, and the group receiving both LPS and 4-CMTB. Under sevoflurane anesthesia, a small neck incision (5 mm along the midline) was performed to expose the trachea. To induce ALI, a measured dose of LPS (from E. coli O111:B4; Sigma; 5 mg/kg) was slowly administered intratracheally using a 1 mL syringe [ ]. Mice in the control group were administered an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After the injection, the surgical site was sterilized and sutured. All mice were housed in cages with unrestricted respiration and maintained at a physiological temperature. 30 min after LPS or PBS administration, mice in the LPS and control groups received intraperitoneal injections of corn oil, while those in the LPS + 4-CMTB group received an intraperitoneal injection of 4-CMTB (20 mg/kg dissolved in corn oil) [ ]. Twenty-four hours later, all the animals were anesthetized with sevoflurane followed by euthanasia. The lung tissues and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected for further experiments.

## Reagents
4-CMTB (HY-P1125), Nigericin (HY-127019), Forskolin (HY-15371), YM-254890 (HY-111557), and Go 6983 (HY-13689) were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE). LPS (437627), ATP (A6559), MSU (U2875), and PTX (P7208) were procured from the Sigma-Aldrich.

## Histological evaluation
Prior to sacrifice, experimental animals were anesthetized with sevoflurane. The tissue of the lung was taken right away and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding. The creation of sections and their staining were performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for evaluation. The degree of lung injury was evaluated based on several parameters, namely, neutrophil infiltration in the alveoli and interstitium, hyaline membrane formation, accumulation of proteinaceous debris within the airspace, and thickening of alveolar septa. The mean histopathology score was obtained from ten randomly selected fields for every mouse at high power [ ].

## Lung wet-to-dry ratio
To evaluate pulmonary edema, the ratio of wet-to-dry (W/D) weight of the lungs was analyzed. The wet weight of the lung samples was measured immediately. Subsequently, the tissues were subjected to drying at 80 °C for 72 h in order to determine their dry weight. The researchers then calculated the W/D ratio in relation to pulmonary edema.

## Assessment of pulmonary vascular leakage
The Evans blue (EB) dye extravasation assay was utilized to evaluate pulmonary alveolar-capillary permeability [ ]. To briefly summarize, mice were injected with 0.5% EB dye via the tail vein at the dosage of 0.01 mL/g body weight. Thirty minutes after injection, the mice were euthanized, and transcardially perfused with 100 mL of PBS to wash out the intravascular dye. Left lung tissue was harvested, homogenized, and incubated with formamide at 55 °C for 48 h. The supernatant’s absorbance was measured at 610 nm after centrifugation at 2000×g for 10 min. The absorbance values were used to calculate extravasated EB lung tissue concentrations.

## BALF collection
After the mice were killed, their chest cavities and respiratory tracts were exposed, and a catheter was placed in the airway. Using a 1-mL syringe, 1 mL of PBS was slowly instilled into the airway, allowed to dwell for 30 s, and then aspirated; this process was repeated four times. BALF was pooled and subsequently subjected to centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the liquid phase (supernatant) and cellular pellet were separated for analysis.

## Peritoneal macrophage cultures and treatments
Thioglycolate (BD, USA) was given intraperitoneally to mice and peritoneal macrophages were harvested. Three days after injection, the mice were euthanized and disinfected with 75% ethanol. The peritoneal cavity was rinsed three times with PBS in a 5 mL syringe while maintaining aseptic conditions. The harvested cells were then resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS, to reach a concentration of 2–4 × 10⁶/mL and subsequently incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment. Macrophages in the peritoneal cavity were initially treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 3 h and then stimulated with either adenosine triphosphate (ATP; 5 mM) for 30 min, Nig (20 µM) for 30 min or monosodium urate (MSU; 200 µg/mL) for 6 h. The cell cultures were stimulated and their supernatants and lysates were collected. Administration of 4-CMTB occurred 30 min before Nig, ATP or MSU stimulation. When necessary, pertussis toxin (PTX; 100 ng/mL), Forskolin (100 µM), YM-254890 (1 µM), or Go 6983 (10 µM) were added 30 min before 4-CMTB treatment. Peritoneal macrophages were incubated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 4 h. The cells were subsequently transfected with LPS (2 µg/mL) into the cytoplasm and incubated for 16 h. During LPS transfection, 4-CMTB (20 µM) was added. We collected cell lysates and culture supernatants to evaluate the activation of the non-canonical NLRP3 inflammasome.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
The concentration of IL-1β, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) was analyzed through ELISA assay kits procured from Invitrogen. An ELISA kit by R&D Systems was used for measuring IL-18.

## Cell death assays
Cell viability was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release and propidium iodide (PI) uptake. LDH release was quantified using the Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). For evaluation of pyroptosis, cells were stained with PI (1 µg/mL) for 5 min and images were acquired with an Olympus CKX53 inverted fluorescence microscope.

## Cell transfection
For transfection of mouse peritoneal macrophages, GPR43-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) and control siRNA (Sangon Biotech, China) were utilized. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) in Opti-MEM reduced-serum medium (Gibco, USA) following the manufacturer’s instruction. After an 8-hour interval, the transfection medium was replaced with the standard culture medium, and the cells were subsequently cultured for an additional 72 h.
The siRNA sequences (5’ to 3’) used were as follows:
GPR43 #1: UGGAUCCAUUAUUGUUCUADTDT
GPR43 #2: UAGAACAAUAAUGGAUCCADTDT
GPR43 #3: GGGUGGAAGUCAAGCAGAADTDT
To decrease the expression of β-arrestin 2, cells were transfected with siRNA specifically targeting β-arrestin 2 (sc-29743; Santa Cruz Inc.) or with a control non-targeting siRNA (sc-37007; Santa Cruz Inc.), adhering to the transfection protocol suggested by the manufacturer.

## Western blotting analysis
Lung tissues and cell lysates were mixed in cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) that contained a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The concentrations of proteins were assessed using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). SDS-PAGE was utilized to separate proteins, which were then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck, Germany). Following this, the membranes underwent blocking using a 5% non-fat milk solution before being incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies utilized in this process were: Anti-NLRP3 (AG-20B-0014-C100, AdipoGen, 1:2000), Anti-Caspase-1 (AG-20B-0042-C100, AdipoGen, 1:1000), Anti-IL-1β (#12426, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), Anti-Cleaved-IL-1β (#63124, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), Anti-GSDMD (ab219800, Abcam, 1:2000), Anti-Caspase-11 (ab180673, Abcam, 1:1000), Anti-β-arrestin 2 (sc-13140, Santa Cruz, 1:500), Anti-GPR43 (NBP3-12190, Novus Biologicals, 1:500), and Anti-β-actin (AC026, ABclonal, 1:50000). Following membrane washing, the samples were exposed to secondary antibodies conjugated with HRP (AT0097 or AT0098, Engibody) at a 1:3000 dilution. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence with a kit provided by Pierce (USA).

## Flow cytometry
Following BALF centrifugation, the cellular component was suspended in PBS (200 µL) and exposed to targeted antibodies, maintaining darkness at an ambient temperature for a 15-minute period. Before the final cell suspension in buffer solution, the washing process was carried out once with PBS of 2 mL. Data collection was carried out on a BD Biosciences FACS LSR II Flow Cytometer (USA) which was subsequently analysed using FlowJo analytical software (Tomy Digital Biology Co., Ltd. Japan). We identified neutrophils using anti-mouse CD11b and anti-mouse Gr-1 (eBioscience, USA) antibodies.

## Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence
The sections of lung tissue (4 μm) were subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration, and then underwent antigen retrieval using a citric acid buffer at pH 6.0. The specimens were treated with H₂O₂ to block endogenous peroxidase activity, and goat serum was applied to diminish non-specific binding. We incubated sections with the primary antibodies for F4/80 and MPO. To detect NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-N) in pulmonary tissues, standard immunofluorescence protocols were used. An Olympus fluorescence microscope was used to capture images.

## Immunofluorescence staining of cell monolayers
Tissue fixative fixed cells grown on slides. Cells were permeabilized subsequently and serum blocked. After adding primary antibodies, a night’s incubation occurred at 4 °C. This was followed up with a 1-hour room temperature incubation with secondary antibodies. DAPI counterstained the nuclei, and the slides were mounted with an anti-fade medium. We used an Olympus fluorescence microscope to take images.

## Co-immunoprecipitation
The NLRP3 and β-arrestin 2 interaction was assessed with an Immunoprecipitation Assay Kit (IF9055, Engibody Biotechnology). To begin with, cells in chilled extraction buffer were lysed, and the supernatant was taken. An aliquot of the cell extract was kept aside for input, and the rest of the solution was precleared on Protein A/G agarose beads. Following an initial clearing step, the cell lysates were left to incubate overnight at 4 °C with either a 1:100 proportion of Anti-NLRP3 antibody AG-20B-0014-C100 (AdipoGen), Anti β-arrestin 2 antibody sc-13140 (Santa Cruz), or control IgG. The mixtures were treated with Protein A/G beads and incubated for 2 h. The beads were washed and resuspended in loading buffer. They were boiled for 5 min and then run on a Western blot.

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). An unpaired Student’s t-test was used to perform statistical comparisons between groups. For three or more comparisons, we performed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test. Statistical analysis for the current study was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9; La Jolla, CA, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was a sign of statistical significance.

## Results

## Activation of GPR43 alleviates LPS-induced ALI in mice
In order to clarify the physiological status of endogenous GPR43 in LPS-induced ALI, we first detected its “expression level” and “functional activation (natural ligand contents)”. In in vitro experiments, LPS (100 ng/mL) was administered to mouse peritoneal macrophages for time periods of 1, 3, and 6 h, and the expression levels of GPR43 were assessed using Western Blot analysis. The findings indicated that no statistically significant differences in GPR43 expression levels were observed at any time point when compared to the control group (Fig. A, B). To create an ALI model, LPS (5 mg/kg) was administered intratracheally to C57BL/6 mice. Lung tissues were collected 24 h later, and GPR43 protein expression was detected. The findings indicated that there was no significant difference in the relative expression of the GPR43 protein in the lung tissues of mice from the group treated with LPS when compared to the control group (Fig. C, D). Considering that the functional activation of GPR43 is dependent on ligand binding, we detected the content of acetate, the major natural ligand of GPR43, in the lung tissues of ALI mice. The acetate levels in the lung tissues of the group treated with LPS were notably greater compared to those in the control group (Fig. E). In conjunction with our earlier discoveries indicating that “acetate has the potential to reduce lung injury induced by LPS and to suppress the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, these effects could be strongly linked to the activation of GPR43” [ , ], the above results suggest that although GPR43 expression does not undergo significant changes during ALI, the accumulation of its ligand may enhance the activation of endogenous GPR43, providing a physiological basis for subsequent functional studies focusing on “GPR43 activation”. Nevertheless, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including acetate, which naturally binds to GPR43, possess the drawback of having a wide range of action targets. To specifically target GPR43, 4-CMTB (a selective agonist of GPR43) was used as an intervention tool in this study.
Thirty minutes following the intratracheal injection of LPS to create an ALI model, 4-CMTB (20 mg/kg) was given via intraperitoneal injection to stimulate GPR43. 24 h following the administration of LPS, the mice were euthanized, and samples of pulmonary tissues, along with BALF, were obtained. Compared with the control group, LPS administration induced severe pulmonary tissue damage. Through H&E staining, structural alterations were observed, revealing the infiltration of neutrophils, the development of hyaline membranes, and the thickening of alveolar septa. However, these pathological changes were significantly alleviated following 4-CMTB treatment (Fig. A, B). Immunohistochemical analysis showed that 4-CMTB treatment significantly reduced the proportions of F4/80-positive cells (a macrophage marker) and myeloperoxidase (MPO)-positive cells (reflecting neutrophil infiltration) (Fig. A, C, D). The presence of neutrophils is a significant marker of inflammation in cases of ALI [ ]. Hence, we assessed the neutrophil levels in BALF through flow cytometry. The findings indicated that LPS elevated total cell counts and neutrophil levels in BALF, while 4-CMTB significantly diminished neutrophil infiltration and overall cellular composition (Fig. E-G). Furthermore, in LPS-induced ALI, the alveolar-capillary barrier, composed of pulmonary vascular endothelium and alveolar epithelium, exhibits impaired function, which further leads to pulmonary edema and increased vascular permeability. Findings from our research indicated that in mice administered LPS, there was a notable increase in the leakage of EB dye into lung tissues, a significant rise in the lung W/D ratio, and a marked elevation in protein levels within the BALF. Thus, the conclusion drawn is that LPS damages the alveolar-capillary barrier. After treatment with 4-CMTB, lung tissue EB dye leakage, lung W/D ratio, and BALF protein in ALI mice were all considerably lower than in the LPS group. 4-CMTB effectively ameliorates LPS-induced injury of the alveolar-capillary barrier and lung pulmonary edema (Fig. H-K). Furthermore, we measured the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in the BALF using ELISA to assess the effect of 4-CMTB on the inflammatory cascade during ALI. LPS greatly elevated the levels of these cytokines in BALF, while 4-CMTB treatment substantially inhibited their production, indicating that 4-CMTB inhibits the release of inflammatory mediators and exerts anti-inflammatory activity in LPS-induced ALI (Fig. L-N). Collectively, these in vivo studies indicate that GPR43 activation may attenuate LPS-induced ALI in mice, suggesting the possibility of developing therapeutics targeting GPR43.
 

## GPR43 activation alleviates LPS-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis in mouse lung tissues
According to multiple studies, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis is crucial in ALI [ ]. A new way to treat the ALI may be to target the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and associated signaling cascades. Importantly, apart from the canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation pathway, the caspase 11 (caspase 4/5 in humans)-focused non-canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation pathway also significantly contributes to LPS-triggered ALI. As a result, this study also ascertained whether 4-CMTB exhibited a regulatory effect on non-canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation as a part of an investigation into whether the protective effect of 4-CMTB was linked to NLRP3. To elucidate the corresponding mechanisms, we first conducted an immunofluorescence assay of NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD-N expression in lung tissues. The findings revealed that exposure to LPS significantly increased the fluorescence signals of NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD-N in lung tissues, which were reduced following treatment with 4-CMTB (Fig. A-F). At the same time, we detected the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-related proteins, pyroptosis-related proteins, and caspase 11 (a key molecule of the non-canonical inflammasome pathway) in lung tissues. As per our data, the LPS challenge significantly promoted the levels of NLRP3, cleaved caspase-1 (p20 form), and GSDMD-N in mouse pulmonary tissues compared to no challenge. Moreover, these upregulations were notably inhibited by 4-CMTB treatment (Fig. G-K). Moreover, after stimulation with LPS, the expression of pro-caspase-11 with two isoforms of 43 kDa and 38 kDa in tissues was increased; the expression of the caspase-11 active fragment was also significantly upregulated. Treatment with 4-CMTB significantly reversed these increasing effects (Fig. L-N). These results show that the activation of GPR43 reduces the activity of inflammasomes and pyroptosis in the lung tissues during LPS-induced ALI. This adds more evidence to the protective effect of GPR43 against LPS-induced ALI with its regulatory mechanisms.
 

## Activation of GPR43 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation in mouse peritoneal macrophages
To study how GPR43 activation affects NLRP3 inflammasome activation, peritoneal macrophages were pretreated with LPS for 3 h, and then Nig was added for 30 min to induce NLRP3 inflammasome activation. The GPR43 agonist 4-CMTB was administered 30 min prior to nigericin treatment. The findings exhibited that 4-CMTB inhibited the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome in a concentration-dependent manner at 5, 10, and 20 µM in LPS + Nig treated peritoneal macrophages. As shown in Fig.  A-F, 4-CMTB both reduced caspase-1 cleavage and IL-1β maturation in cell lysates and supernatants, and did not significantly affect the expression of NLRP3. In addition, the levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in the culture medium showed that 4-CMTB inhibited the secretion of these cytokines in a concentration-dependent manner, which also indicates 4-CMTB inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Fig.  G, H). To determine how GPR43 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation, peritoneal macrophages underwent transfection with mouse GPR43-specific siRNA. All three siRNA sequences effectively knocked down GPR43 expression (Fig.  I). At 72 h after the transfection, the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome was triggered by the combination of LPS and Nig, with the administration of 4-CMTB starting 30 min prior to the exposure to Nig. Experimental results indicated that the inhibitory impact of 4-CMTB on the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome was somewhat diminished after the knockdown of GPR43. Western blot analysis indicated a partial recovery of caspase-1 cleavage and maturation of IL-1β in both cell extracts and supernatants (Fig.  J-N). Consistently, the findings from the ELISA indicated that the GPR43 knockdown partially reversed the inhibition of IL-1β and IL-18 secretion mediated by 4-CMTB (Fig.  O, P). To confirm the wide-ranging inhibitory impact of the GPR43 agonist on the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, two other secondary signal activators, namely ATP and MSU, were employed. The findings indicated that 4-CMTB significantly blocked the cleavage of caspase-1 and the maturation of IL-1β induced by ATP and MSU in peritoneal macrophages, concurrently leading to a notable decrease in the release of IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig.  A-M). Furthermore, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) plays a vital role in the assembly of the inflammasome supramolecular complex. Upon NLRP3 activation, ASC aggregates into large spherical structures (“ASC specks”) in the cytoplasm via self-association. Consequently, we conducted additional research on how the GPR43 agonist influences the formation of ASC specks after the treatment with LPS and Nig. The findings from the immunofluorescence assay revealed that stimulation with LPS + Nig notably augmented the quantity of ASC specks. In contrast, the formation of these specks was significantly reduced by 4-CMTB, as illustrated by the yellow arrows in Fig.  N. Figure  O illustrates the percentage of cells that exhibit ASC specks, revealing that treatment with LPS + Nig significantly elevates this percentage, while the administration of 4-CMTB leads to a decrease in it.
 In addition to the canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation pathway, we further explored the influence of 4-CMTB on the non-canonical inflammasome activation mediated by caspase-11. Initially, mouse peritoneal macrophages underwent pretreatment with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 4 h to promote the expression of pro-caspase-11. Following this, LPS (2 µg/mL) was transfected into the cytoplasm of the cells and incubated for 16 h to trigger the activation of caspase-11. Meanwhile, the GPR43-specific agonist 4-CMTB (20 µM) was added at the time of LPS transfection to achieve simultaneous activation of GPR43. The findings indicated that in macrophages primed with LPS, there was an increased expression of pro-caspase-11, which includes two isoforms with molecular weights of 43 kDa and 38 kDa. After LPS transfection, the expression and secretion of the caspase-11 active fragment were significantly increased, indicating successful activation of caspase-11. Nevertheless, the administration of 4-CMTB did not lead to notable alterations in the expression or release of caspase-11 (Fig. A, B, D). We subsequently detected the expression of GSDMD (a hallmark of pyroptosis) and found that LPS transfection significantly increased the expression of the GSDMD-N. In contrast, 4-CMTB treatment partially reduced the expression of the GSDMD-N, implying that it can inhibit pyroptosis (Fig. A, F). Significantly, activated caspase-11 has the ability to facilitate the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which in turn enhances both the activation of caspase-1 and the maturation of IL-1β. Consequently, we proceeded to assess the expression and activation levels of caspase-1. Results reveal the promotion of caspase-1 activation by the activation of caspase-11, as evidenced by increased expression and secretion of caspase-1 p20 fragment. The enhanced effect was prevented with the treatment of 4-CMTB (Fig. A, C, E). Consistently, the activation of caspase-11 also promotes the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, while 4-CMTB partially reduces the secretion of these two cytokines (Fig. G, H).
Overall, these results suggest that the activation of GPR43 successfully inhibits the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in mouse peritoneal macrophages. This suppressive effect mainly occurs by influencing the canonical activation pathway of the NLRP3 inflammasome, rather than directly modulating the non-canonical activation mediated by caspase-11.

## Activation of GPR43 inhibits pyroptosis of mouse peritoneal macrophages
NLRP3 inflammasome activation not only mediates caspase-1-dependent secretion of inflammatory cytokines but also triggers GSDMD-driven pyroptosis [ ]. We therefore analyzed GSDMD protein levels in cell lysates via Western blotting. The results indicated that treatment with increasing concentrations of 4-CMTB (5, 10, 20 µM) had a significant inhibitory effect on the expression of GSDMD-N under LPS + Nig activation. In other words, 4-CMTB inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. A, B). To establish the occurrence of pyroptosis, LDH release into the culture medium was measured, which indicates cell membrane damage and increased permeability. Higher doses of 4-CMTB lowered the release of LDH induced by LPS + Nig (Fig. C). Pores forming in the cell membrane during pyroptosis allow the entry of extracellular dyes, like PI, which labels the nucleus [ ]. According to the results of confocal microscopy, 4-CMTB dose-dependently decreased the percentage of PI positive cells induced by LPS + Nig (Fig. D, E). Moreover, transmission electron microscopy, which is capable of directly visualizing pyroptotic morphological features, showed that LPS + Nig stimulation causes typical pyroptotic features: cell swelling, irregular shapes, widened spaces around the nucleus, organelles with swelling and partial vacuolization, membrane pore formation, and extrusion of the cytoplasm. GPR43 activation by 4-CMTB diminished the pyroptotic events (Fig. F). To verify the involvement of GPR43 in pyroptosis induced by LPS + Nig, we conducted GPR43 knockdown and GPR43 4-CMTB treatment. The results showed that reduced levels of GPR43 partially reversed the inhibitory impact of 4-CMTB on GSDMD-N (Fig. G, H). The experimental results demonstrate that the initiation of GPR43 can inhibit the pyroptosis induced by LPS + Nig.
 

## GPR43 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis via the β-arrestin 2 mediated signaling pathway
GPR43 is capable of activating classical G protein signaling pathways (particularly Gαi and Gαq) as well as the pathway dependent on β-arrestin 2, which in turn initiates various downstream responses [ ]. Therefore, we investigated the contribution of these signaling mechanisms to GPR43-mediated suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. Initially, we investigated the involvement of classical G protein signaling pathways in the suppressive impact of 4-CMTB on NLRP3 inflammasome activation. GPR43 lowers levels of intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) via its interaction with Gαi proteins [ ]. Previous research has indicated that a rise in cAMP can impede the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [ ]. To clarify whether this signaling pathway is involved in the GPR43-mediated regulatory effect on the NLRP3 inflammasome, we treated cells with Forskolin, an adenylate cyclase (AC) agonist, to elevate intracellular cAMP levels. A Forskolin-only treatment group was set up as a positive control in the experiment. The results showed that, under the background of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, administration of Forskolin alone could inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation to a certain extent. Furthermore, this intervention not only did not succeed in reversing the inhibitory effect of 4-CMTB on the NLRP3 inflammasome, but it also led to a further decrease in the cleavage level of caspase-1, as well as in the maturation level of IL-1β found in cell lysates and culture supernatants (Fig. A-E). The results from ELISA additionally validated that treatment with Forskolin not only failed to counteract the inhibitory influence of 4-CMTB on the release of IL-1β but also resulted in a further reduction in the levels of IL-1β released (Fig. G). In addition, PTX, a Gαi protein inhibitor, also exhibited similar control characteristics: PTX by itself also inhibited NLRP3 activation. However, it did not mitigate the suppressive impact of 4-CMTB on the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome (Fig. F, H-J, L, M). It is known that GPR43 can also activate another classical G protein pathway, namely the Gαq-PLC-PKC signaling pathway [ ]. Therefore, in subsequent experiments, we selected YM-254890 (a Gαq inhibitor) and Go 6983 (a PKC inhibitor) for intervention. The experimental data showed that when these two inhibitors were used alone, both could inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation to a certain extent. When co-administered with 4-CMTB, neither had any effect on the inhibitory action of 4-CMTB regarding the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome (Fig. K, N-X).
 Based on these findings, we suggest that the signaling pathway that includes β-arrestin 2 may be involved in facilitating the inhibitory effect of GPR43 on the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. To investigate this, we introduced β-arrestin 2-specific siRNA into mouse peritoneal macrophages (Fig.  A). The experiments revealed that the knockdown of β-arrestin 2 led to a partial restoration of the suppressive action exerted by GPR43 activation on the NLRP3 inflammasome. The data illustrated in Fig.  B-G indicate that β-arrestin 2 knockdown partially abrogated the inhibitory effect of 4-CMTB on caspase-1 and IL-1β expression, accompanied by increased IL-1β secretion in the culture supernatant. Moreover, β-arrestin 2 knockdown affected LPS + Nig-induced pyroptosis. In the control condition, 4-CMTB reduced the expression of GSDMD-N in macrophages. However, this inhibitory effect was alleviated after β-arrestin 2 knockdown (Fig.  H, I). Additionally, alterations in markers of pyroptosis (such as the release of LDH and the proportion of PI-positive cells noted through fluorescence microscopy) were observed after the knockdown of β-arrestin 2. Our research indicated that the knockdown of β-arrestin 2 significantly diminished the protective role of GPR43 in counteracting pyroptosis induced by LPS + Nig (Fig.  J-L). The results suggest that β-arrestin 2 probably serves as a mediator in the inhibitory impacts of GPR43 on the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis.
 

## Activation of GPR43 promotes the interaction between β-arrestin 2 and NLRP3
As a crucial mediator in the signaling of GPCR, β-arrestin 2 engages in various cellular processes by interacting with several intracellular molecules. For example, β-arrestin 2 binds the coiled-coil domain of TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and Beclin 1 to inhibit the ubiquitination of TRAF6 to stop NF-κB activation. Simultaneously, it inhibits the ubiquitination of the TRAF6-Beclin 1 complex to block autophagy [ ]. Through its interaction with NLRP3, β-arrestin 2 mediates the inhibition of inflammasome activity by omega-3 fatty acids, which prevents inflammatory and metabolic diseases [ ]. Given these observations, we hypothesize that GPR43 activation may modulate the β-arrestin 2 and NLRP3 interaction during NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages. We first performed immunofluorescence staining to assess β-arrestin 2 and NLRP3 intracellular co-localization. The treatment with 4-CMTB promotes the co-localization of NLRP3 (green fluorescence) and β-arrestin 2 (red fluorescence), as revealed in Fig. A. To further corroborate this interaction, a co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay using antibodies against β-arrestin 2 and NLRP3 was carried out. The findings showed that 4-CMTB significantly increased the NLRP3 and β-arrestin 2 binding (Fig. B-E). It is noteworthy that silencing of GPR43 abrogated the ability of 4-CMTB to strengthen the β-arrestin 2-NLRP3 association (Fig. F-I). All things considered, it indicates that GPR43 activation promotes the interaction of β-arrestin 2 and NLRP3, which may trigger the inhibitory effect of GPR43 on NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This offers a new idea about how GPR43 affects inflammatory responses.
 

## Discussion
ALI/ARDS are significant ailments that threaten humans. The burden that patients and their families experience as a result of these conditions has a major impact on the overall healthcare system globally [ ]. In recent years, due to the advancing research related to the pathogenesis of ALI, the importance of the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis in the development of ALI/ARDS has gained much attention [ ]. Upon activation, the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers the secretion of key inflammatory mediators including IL-1β and IL-18, which recruit and activate various immune cells, initiating an inflammatory cascade that ultimately leads to lung tissue inflammation, damage, and edema [ ]. In addition, in the development of ALI, the activated form of caspase-1, produced through NLRP3 inflammasome stimulation, cuts GSDMD into segments, creating its N-terminal portion that possesses membrane-perforating capabilities. This specific region creates holes in the cellular membrane, triggering the discharge of internal cellular components and intensifying the inflammatory cascade, thereby worsening ALI severity [ ]. An important regulatory node in ALI is the non-canonical pyroptosis pathway mediated by caspase-11 that acts beyond the canonical NLRP3 inflammasome [ ]. Caspase-11 is a molecule in the cytoplasm that can recognize intracellular LPS, allowing it to cleave the protein GSDMD to form an active N-terminal fragment, which perforates the plasma membrane, resulting in the release of cytoplasmic contents [ ]. This process not only directly causes pyroptotic damage to alveolar macrophages and epithelial cells but also further recruits inflammatory cells such as neutrophils, amplifying the pulmonary inflammatory cascade.
Therefore, thorough investigation of inflammasome signaling pathways can provide insight into the mechanisms of ALI and help identify new therapeutic targets for the disease. The findings in this study corroborate earlier studies showing that both the canonical and caspase-11-mediated non-canonical NLRP3 inflammasome pathways are activated in pulmonary tissues during LPS-mediated ALI, indicating their relevance in ALI pathology. Our studies showed that 4-CMTB, an agonist of GPR43, mainly inhibits canonically activated NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis through the signaling pathway of β-arrestin 2, thereby ameliorating the intensity of ALI triggered by LPS (Fig.  ). The above data show that GPR43 can be a possible target of therapy, which is significantly important in the modulation of activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.
 The immunoregulatory actions of GPR43 are complex and diverse in their nature and mechanism of action. Evidence suggests that GPR43 has protective effects in ALI. For example, propionic acid, a metabolite of gut microbiota, reduces ALI by activating GPR43 to inhibit zinc oxide nanoparticle-induced inflammatory response and oxidative stress in alveolar macrophages [ ]. GPR43 inhibits the JNK/ELK1 signaling pathway to reduce pulmonary inflammation, alleviating alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis [ ]. We previously showed that acetate can inhibit LPS-induced ALI [ ] and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [ ], which may be associated with the activation of GPR43.
SCFAs serve as natural ligands for GPR43, thereby activating this receptor while also exerting effects on multiple other receptors, including GPR41 and GPR109A. Additionally, SCFAs are involved in diverse biological processes such as histone acetylation and energy metabolism [ ]. Selective agonists, in contrast to natural ligands, have simple structures and a strong action specificity. Accordingly, we selected GPR43-specific agonist 4-CMTB as a research tool to investigate the mechanism of GPR43 in ALI and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This strategy reduces interference from other receptors and thereby allows for a more precise comprehension of the unique regulatory function of GPR43 as it relates to the pathology of ALI. As a selective GPR43 agonist, 4-CMTB can mimic the actions of GPR43 agonists to activate GPR43 and initiate cellular signaling pathways. Earlier research indicated that ovalbumin-induced allergic asthma was significantly alleviated with 4-CMTB. Moreover, it reduced the number of immune cells in BALF and inhibited IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 expression [ ]. 4-CMTB is effective in controlling atopic dermatitis, as mice injected with 4-CMTB showed a reduction in IgE production and mast cell clustering as well as lowered levels of Th2 cytokines (IL-4 and IL-13) and swelling in their lymph nodes [ ]. Currently, there are no studies that explore the therapeutic potential of 4-CMTB in ALI. According to our study, 4-CMTB can exert a suppressive effect on NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis after GPR43 knockdown. Pulmonary tissue ALI, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, and pyroptosis are reduced by 4-CMTB. This study gives experimental proof that GPR43 can be employed therapeutically in ALI. It also gives new insight into the way GPR43 regulates inflammatory pathways and inflammasomes.
GPR43, upon binding to its ligand, conformationally activates downstream signaling pathways. The study involves multi-signal molecules; GTP-based arrestors and β-arrestin 2 are the two major signaling tasks [ ]. In the G protein-mediated signaling pathway, GPR43 inhibits AC via activation of the Gαi subunit, leading to reduced intracellular levels of the second messenger cAMP. Notably, cAMP plays a pivotal role in preserving pulmonary vascular integrity and mitigating inflammatory injury [ ]. Furthermore, GPR43 may activate phospholipase C (PLC) with Gαq. PLC hydrolyzes the membrane lipid phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to produce inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG), which play important roles in cellular signaling pathways. DAG activates protein kinase C (PKC) [ , ]. However, our data showed that 4-CMTB suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation is not through classical G protein signaling. Notwithstanding Forskolin-induced cAMP accumulation or the administration of the Gαi inactivator (PTX), the Gαq antagonist (YM-254890), or the PKC inhibitor (Go 6983), the inhibitory effects of 4-CMTB were unaffected on NLRP3 inflammasome activation. These findings suggested that 4-CMTB-mediated suppression of NLRP3 via GPR43 represents a non-classic G protein signaling pathway, which may involve other signaling pathways or molecular mechanisms for the regulation of NLRP3.
β-arrestin 2 is a key mediator of GPCR signaling cascades and plays important roles in various cellular functions. As a multifunctional scaffold/adaptor protein, β-arrestin 2 is implicated in regulating a variety of biological mechanisms such as cell migration, PCD, metastasis, and inflammatory responses [ ]. On activation, GPR43 causes recruitment of β-arrestin 2, whose conformational change activates downstream signaling cascades [ ]. Research increasingly notes the involvement of β-arrestin 2 in inflammatory diseases. Studies suggest that β-arrestin 2 activity inhibits inflammation in sepsis and other bacterial infections [ ]. β-arrestin 2 typically regulates the immune response and inflammatory signals through interaction with specific signaling pathways and proteins. For example, β-arrestin 2 is capable of binding with cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and enhances the innate immunity [ ]. In addition, β-arrestin 2 can closely interact with NLRP3, inhibiting assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [ ]. Our study shows GPR43’s specific biased signaling towards β-arrestin 2 modulates ALI through enhancement of β-arrestin 2 and NLRP3 interaction. As this study demonstrates, it is viewed that β-arrestin 2 plays an extra-cautionary role in providing an inhibitory effect during inflammation. This has a promising impact in establishing targeted approaches and fundamental concepts in the treatment of inflammatory disease, which includes ALI.
According to this study, GPR43-mediated inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome is independent of the classical Gαi and Gαq signaling cascades. This suggests that the biased signaling properties of GPCR may underlie this phenomenon. GPCR biased signaling is defined as the selective activation of a subset of a receptor’s downstream signaling pathways, which is referred to as “G protein bias” or “β-arrestin bias” and triggered by specific stimuli such as ligands or therapeutic agents [ ]. In recent times, these biased GPCR ligands have been part of the GPCR for the study of signaling functions [ ]. In the present study, 4-CMTB was found to mainly function through the β-arrestin 2 signaling pathway. These observations differ from those made in other cell types and disease models where GPR43 activation predominantly triggers a downstream Gαq or Gαi pathway [ , ]. This study will not only enrich the theoretical framework of GPCR-biased signaling but also offer new insights into developing cell-selective GPR43-targeting anti-inflammatory drugs. Future research can help in studying the molecular basis of GPR43 signaling bias and its dynamic changes during different pathophysiological conditions, which can help in giving a solid theoretical basis to develop precise anti-inflammatory therapeutic strategies.
Importantly, recent studies have demonstrated that pyroptosis mediated by NLRP3 activation interacts with and cross-regulates other PCD pathways, which collectively form a complex cell death network that participates in the development and progression of lung injury [ ]. An example of this is that the NLRP3 Inflammasome promotes apoptosis by ASC-mediated cleavage of caspase-8 [ ]. In addition, the NLRP3 can regulate the expression of Bcl-2 family proteins, thereby affecting mitochondrial membrane stability and modulating other PCD pathways like apoptosis [ ]. The recently suggested mechanism of PANoptosis focuses on the interactions and co-regulation of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis [ ]. The understanding of the role of NLRP3 in diseases could be enhanced through this integrated mode of programmed cell death. The study at hand mainly focuses on GPR43 activation’s inhibitory effect on pyroptosis induced by NLRP3 inflammasome. Given the close association between NLRP3 and multiple forms of PCD, further investigation is warranted to explore whether GPR43 activation can modulate other PCD types via regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. We hypothesize that GPR43 may indirectly regulate downstream signaling pathways of multiple PCD types by inhibiting the overall activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, though this hypothesis requires further experimental verification. Such extended research will help to more comprehensively uncover the protective mechanism of GPR43 in ALI, achieve coordinated regulation of multiple types of cell death, and provide a more abundant theoretical basis for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies for ALI.
As our research showed, GPR43 significantly inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation, pyroptosis, and ALI, but certain limitations apply. A major limitation is the use of intratracheal LPS administration to establish the mouse ALI model, which does not mimic the natural pathogenesis of human ALI. Human ALI occurs due to multiple causes, usually with a progressive onset. As a result of the experimental model utilized in this study, the generalizability of our results may be limited. Future research should investigate the effects of GPR43 and its agonists on ALI in human clinical specimens. Secondly, in terms of cell models, this study primarily utilized in vitro-cultured macrophages. These in vitro models exhibit inherent differences from macrophages in the in vivo microenvironment, as they lack complex intercellular interactions and extracellular matrix support. Such disparities may lead to inconsistencies in cellular functions and signal transduction pathways compared to in vivo conditions. In the future, more advanced animal models, organoid models, and organ-on-a-chip technologies could be useful for research again. The use of these advanced models will permit more accurate insights into the role of GPR43 in ALI, furthering the basis of therapeutic interventions. Additionally, although our findings indicate the important role of β-arrestin 2 in GPR43 signaling, further investigations will need to be conducted to reveal the exact molecular mechanism, including amino acids and domains involved in the interaction with the NLRP3 inflammasome. It is important to study GPR43 and β-arrestin 2 interaction with β-arrestin 2.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, the present study revealed that GPR43 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis via β-arrestin 2-dependent signaling, thereby protects against LPS-induced ALI. The research suggests that GPR43 could be a significant therapeutic target for ALI. Additional studies should be pursued to investigate GPR43’s involvement in different inflammatory diseases and its potential clinical value as a therapeutic target. A deeper understanding of GPR43 and its associated signaling pathways is expected to provide new strategies and approaches for the prevention and treatment of inflammatory diseases.
