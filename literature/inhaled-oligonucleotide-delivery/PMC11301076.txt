## Summary
Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection, with the highest single-cause mortality. Monocarboxylate transporter 4 (Mct4) transports intracellular lactate outside, but its role in regulating host immune response against Mtb infection remains unknown. Mct4 expression was upregulated in Mtb-infected macrophages and in patients with TB. Mct4 silencing/deficiency significantly decreased Mtb survival in macrophages and in lungs and spleens of mice, while Mct4 overexpression facilitated Mtb survival in macrophages. Furthermore, Mct4 promoted intracellular lactate transport, nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) p65 activation, and interleukin-10 (IL-10) production upon Mtb infection. Mechanistically, IL-10 silencing and IL-10-neutralizing antibody blocked Mct4 overexpressing increased Mtb survival. Replenishing lactate and NF-κB p65 inhibitor JSH23 treatment could inhibit Mct4 overexpressing increased NF-κB p65 activation, IL-10 production, and Mtb survival in macrophages. This study demonstrates that Mct4 promotes Mtb survival through restricting intracellular lactate accumulation to promote NF-κB p65-mediated IL-10 production and suggests Mct4-NF-κB p65-IL-10 axis a potential target for TB treatment.

## Introduction
Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection. According to the Global Tuberculosis Report 2023, TB caused an estimated 1.30 million deaths in 2022, and China is the third largest country of TB incidence in the worldwide. In addition, the incidence of multidrug/rifampicin-resistant TB reached 410,000 in 2022, but only two in five of those diagnosed and started on treatment. TB continues to pose a significant threat as one of the leading causes of infectious mortality worldwide, urging to identify novel therapeutic targets for TB treatment. Mtb is an intracellular pathogen that resides within macrophages. Macrophages serve as the primary line of host defense system against Mtb infection, and they possess multiple antimicrobial mechanisms, such as releasing cytokines, phagocytosing, and interacting with other immune cells. However, the specific actions of different molecules within macrophages against Mtb infection remain unclear.
Solute carriers (SLCs) are the second largest family of membrane proteins comprising over 400 members, most of which are located in cell membranes. They primarily function on transporting various substrates across biological membranes, such as the uptake of small molecules into cells. Human genetic studies have provided valuable insights into the roles of recently characterized SLC transporters in various diseases, , revealing numerous new therapeutic opportunities. The monocarboxylate transporter 4 (Mct4) encoded by the Slc16a3 gene is a protein responsible for the extracellular transport of lactate, a crucial component of glycolysis.
Aerobic glycolysis and their mechanistic effectors play critical roles in macrophages to eliminate Mtb. Lactate, a byproduct of glycolysis, accumulates in the extracellular environment. Lactate significantly enhances bacillary clearance in Mtb-infected human macrophages by modulating cellular metabolism. Lactate has been demonstrated to signal through its specific receptor, G protein-coupled receptor 81 (GPR81), or to be transported into cells via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs). Lactate suppressed lipopolysaccharide-stimulated nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation via GPR81 in macrophages. MCTs belong to the solute carrier 16 (SLC16) family, which comprises a class of transmembrane lactate transporters. L-lactate is exported by Mct4 (Slc16a3), while it is imported by Mct1 (Slc16a1). 
Lactate is a critical factor to support immune cell function via modulating cytokine production. Lactate plays an important role in regulating macrophage polarization, tumor immunity, and antiviral responses through signaling pathway activation and histone modification. The ability of Mtb to persist in its human host relies on numerous immune evasion strategies, especially in promoting anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) production. IL-10 deficiency could prevent the induction of foamy macrophages by deregulating lipid metabolism and suppressing Mtb survival. Rv0580c-expressing Mycobacterium smegmatis strain (Ms_Rv0580c) downregulated IL-10 expression via NF-κB signaling in human THP-1-derived macrophages (THP-1-Mφs). However, whether Mct4 plays a key role in anti-Mtb immune response through lactate transport and IL-10 production in macrophages needs further investigation.
In this study, we screened SLCs family and found that Mct4 is highly expressed in Mtb-infected macrophages and lymphocytes of patients with TB. Mct4 deficiency results in reduced Mtb survival in macrophages and in mice. Mechanistically, Mct4 facilitates the transport of lactate outside of macrophages to promote IL-10 production via NF-κB p65 signaling. This study explores the novel function of Mct4 and provides a potential target for TB treatment.

## Results

## Mct4 expression is promoted by Mtb infection and exerts pro-Mtb effect in macrophages
Firstly, we identified the gene expression of SLC family in bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) infected with H37Rv for 24 h by RNA sequencing. The results showed that Slc46a3, Slc5a3, Slc9a9, Slc40a1, Slc18a1, Slc39a8, Slc26a11, Slc2a3, Slc25a13, Slc18b1, Slc4a11, Slc16a14, Slc25a53, Slc8a2, and Slc25a27 mRNA expression levels were significantly decreased upon Mtb infection. Conversely, Slc28a2, Slc1a2, Slc6a9, Slc16a3, Slc25a37, Slc7a11, Slc39a4, and Slc7a2 mRNA expression levels significantly increased upon Mtb infection ( A). We further verified Mtb-promoted gene expression of eight SLCs and found that their gene expressions peaked at different time points upon Mtb infection at 6, 24, 48, and 72 h ( B). Next, we used small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to knockdown these genes, and all of them are silenced successfully in Mtb-infected BMDMs ( C). Next, we demonstrated that Slc7a2 and Slc39a4 showed anti-Mtb, while Slc16a3 (Mct4) exerted pro-Mtb effects in BMDMs upon Mtb infection for 48 h ( D). As reported, anti-Mtb effect of Slc7a2 was consistent with our result. Slc39a4 plays a crucial role in maintaining intracellular zinc ion level to affect diverse biological processes, and associates with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and gallbladder cancer. We further investigate the effects and mechanisms of immune escape of Mct4 against Mtb infection. We assessed Slc16a3 mRNA and Mct4 protein expression and found that they were upregulated time-dependently of Mtb infection in BMDMs, Raw 264.7, and human THP-1-Mφs ( E–1J). Additionally, Mct4 expression was elevated in the lymph node of patients with lymphatic TB compared to that of patients with chronic inflammation by immunohistochemistry assay ( K). Therefore, our findings suggested that Mct4 negatively regulated host immune response against Mtb infection.
 

## Mct4 facilitates Mtb survival in human and mouse macrophages
We further validate the role of Mct4 on Mtb survival of mouse/human macrophages with loss- and gain of functions. We utilized siRNA to knockdown Mct4 expression in BMDMs. We found that Slc16a3 gene expression and Mct4 protein expression were significantly reduced after Mct4 silencing upon Mtb infection for 6, 24, and 48 h ( A and S1B). Mct4 silencing resulted in a decrease in Mtb load at 48 h in BMDMs ( C). Consistently, Mct4 has been knocked out successfully in Slc16a3−/− mice ( A and 2B). Mct4 deficiency significantly decreased intracellular Mtb survival upon Mtb infection at 48 h ( C). Then, we constructed Raw 264.7 cells with Mct4 overexpression for reverse verification. We found Mct4 overexpression significantly increased Slc16a3 gene and Mct4 protein expressions ( D and 2E) and promoted Mtb infection in macrophages ( F). Finally, human THP-1-Mφ cells with Mct4 knockdown showed decreased expressions of Slc16a3 gene and Mct4 protein upon Mtb infection at 6, 24, and 48 h ( G and 2H). Consistently, Mct4 silencing suppressed Mtb survival in THP-1-Mφs ( I). These results indicated that Mct4 promoted Mtb survival in mouse and human macrophages.
 

## Mct4 deficiency suppresses Mtb survival in mice
Next, we established a mouse model of TB by aerosol inhalation of 1 × 107 colony-forming units of Mtb. Slc16a3−/− mice exhibited a lower burden of Mtb in lungs and spleens at 1 week and 4 weeks of Mtb infection compared with Slc16a3+/+ mice ( A). Then, paraffin sections of lungs and spleens from infected mice upon 4 weeks of Mtb infection were stained by hematoxylin-eosin. Langhans giant cells are a kind of multinucleated giant cells (MGCs) and not conducive to the removal of pathogens of long-term infection with matrix metalloproteinase production for tissue destruction. We found that the lungs of Slc16a3−/− mice displayed intact structure, while the lungs in Slc16a3+/+ mice were swollen. The spleens of Slc16a3−/− mice minimized fewer MGC frequencies compared to the spleens of Slc16a3+/+ mice ( B). Additionally, Mct4 deficiency did not affect the numbers of neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages in lungs ( A and S2B). However, the expression of CD80, CD86, and major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-II on macrophage surface was increased in the lungs of Slc16a3−/− mice, indicating enhanced macrophage activation ( C). Similarly, Mct4 deficiency in BMDMs enhanced the expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC-II ( D). Conversely, Raw 264.7 cells overexpressing Mct4 decreased the expressions of CD80, CD86, and MHC-II ( E). These results indicated that Mct4 deficiency minimized tissue damage, enhanced macrophage activity, and reduced Mtb survival in mice.
 

## Mct4 promotes Mtb survival through IL-10 production in macrophages
Initiating and maintaining inflammation are critical aspects of the innate immune response of macrophages against Mtb infection. To further investigate whether Mct4 affects inflammatory cytokine production in macrophages, we detected IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), IL-6, and IL-10 production. Mct4 deficiency resulted in a significant decrease in the cellular expression and production of IL-1β and IL-10 ( A), while IL-6 and TNF-α expression and production remained unchanged ( A). Conversely, Mct4 overexpression significantly increased IL-1β and IL-10 expression ( B), without affecting IL-6 and TNF-α production ( B). Mct4 silencing in BMDMs and THP-1-Mφs significantly inhibited IL-1β and IL-10 production ( C and C) but did not affect IL-6 and TNF-α production ( D and S3E). Then, we used anti-IL-1β antibody and anti-IL-10 antibody to further confirm whether Mct4 functions by regulating IL-1β or IL-10. We validated that these antibodies did not affect the viability of macrophages and did not kill Mtb directly ( A and S4B). IL-1β-neutralizing antibody did not block the intracellular mRNA expression and protein level of IL-1β increased by Mct4 overexpression ( C), as well as the increased Mtb load ( D). These results suggested that Mct4 deficiency reduced inflammation damage, which may be attributed to IL-1β production. Additionally, Mct4 knockdown and overexpression did not affect LC3 expression and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression in macrophages, indicating Mct4 might not influence autophagy and iNOS upon Mtb infection ( A and S5B). IL-10-neutralizing antibody totally blocked IL-10 production increased by Mct4 overexpression, without affecting IL-10 mRNA expression ( D). Additionally, anti-IL-10 antibody blocked Mtb load facilitated by Mct4 overexpression ( E). To further investigate whether Mct4 promotes Mtb survival through IL-10 production, we knocked down IL-10 in Mct4-overexpressing Raw 264.7 cells. We demonstrated that IL-10 silencing totally blocked Mct4-induced IL-10 mRNA expression and production ( F), as well as Mct4-promoted Mtb load ( G). These results suggested that Mct4 promoted Mtb survival through IL-10 production in macrophages.
 

## Mct4 enhances IL-10 production by NF-κB p65 activation in Mtb-infected macrophages
As reported, IL-10 production has been involved in NF-κB p65, p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), Erk1/2, and Stat1/3 signaling pathways. , , All these host factors were activated by Mtb infection as indicated by their phosphorylation levels. The phosphorylation levels of NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK, but not Erk1/2 and Stat1/3, were decreased in BMDMs of Slc16a3−/− mice upon Mtb infection at 1 and 2 h, compared with Slc16a3+/+ mice ( A). Overexpression of Mct4 in Raw 264.7 cells promoted NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK, but not Erk1/2 and Stat1/3 phosphorylation upon Mtb infection at 1 and 2 h ( B). To further investigate whether Mct4 regulates IL-10 production through these two transcription factors, we exploited the NF-κB p65 inhibitor JSH23 and the p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580 to treat Raw 264.7 cells with Mct4 overexpression. We validated that these inhibitors did not affect the viability of macrophages and did not kill Mtb directly ( A). Furthermore, these inhibitors effectively suppressed phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK ( C and 5D). Then, we found that SB203580 treatment failed to block Mct4 increased IL-10 production and Mtb load ( E and 5F). However, JSH23 totally blocked the increased IL-10 production and Mtb load in Mct4-overexpressing Raw 264.7 cells ( G and 5H) Additionally, the co-localization of F4/80 protein (as a key marker for mature macrophage cells) with phospho-p65 in lungs of Slc16a3−/− mice was reduced in contrast with Slc16a3+/+ mice ( I). These results indicated that Mct4 enhanced IL-10 production by activating NF-κB p65 to promote Mtb survival in macrophages.
 

## Mct4 activates p65-IL-10 axis to promote Mtb survival by reducing intracellular lactate of macrophages
Lactate significantly enhances bacillary clearance in Mtb-infected human macrophages by modulating cellular metabolism. Mct4 could transport the intracellular lactate out of cells. Indeed, we found that Mct4 deficiency led to a significant increase of intracellular lactate and a decrease of extracellular lactate in BMDMs ( A). On the contrary, Mct4 overexpression significantly decreased intracellular lactate and increased extracellular lactate ( B). Then, we excluded the possibility that the extracellular lactate was induced by IL-10 production ( B). We confirmed that lactate has no toxicity on macrophages and could not directly kill Mtb ( C). Lactate treatment totally blocked the increased Mct4-promoted NF-κB p65 phosphorylation ( C). As expected, Mct4 increased IL-10 production, and Mtb survival was totally blocked by lactate treatment ( D and 6E). Therefore, our results suggested that Mct4 transferred the lactate out of macrophages to activate the NF-κB p65 and IL-10 production, thereby promoting Mtb survival.
 

## Discussion
TB remains a leading cause among infectious diseases and death globally. Although a robust immune defense system is usually activated to kill Mtb, the immune escape mechanisms often help Mtb to escape from immune surveillance. SLCs are the second largest family of transmembrane transporters in humans and are major determinants of cellular metabolism and involves in various physiological processes. In this study, we profiled the gene expression of SLC family in macrophages upon Mtb infection, and further investigated the effect and mechanisms of Mct4 on Mtb survival. We found that Mct4 expression was upregulated upon Mtb infection in macrophages and was increased in the lymph node of patients with lymphatic TB in contrast with patients of chronic inflammation. Mechanistically, Mct4 deficiency contributed to lactate accumulation in macrophages, inhibited NF-κB p65 activation to suppress IL-10 production, and eventually reduced Mtb survival ( ). This study sheds new light on treating TB based on targeting Mct4 as an immune escape target.
 SLCs play an important role in tumors and infections with the regulation of immune system coordinating complex signals to support proliferation, differentiation, and effector function. In our study, Slc7a2 and Slc39a4 exert anti-Mtb effects and Slc16a3 exerts pro-Mtb effect in macrophages among all the screened SLCs affected by Mtb infection. Slc7a2, a member of the cationic amino acid transporter family, plays a crucial role in transporting arginine, which is essential for nitric oxide (NO) production, which is a potent host defense factor against Mtb infection. Previous study has reported that knocking out of Slc7a2 may potentially inhibit M1 polarization, consequently suppressing the production of TNF-α and NO, which in turn promotes Mtb survival. SLC39A4 (ZIP4) promoted epithelial-mesenchymal transition plasticity in human pancreatic cancer. How Slc39a4 regulates Mtb infectious immunity needs further investigation. Disruption of xCT (Slc7a11) has been shown to enhance the bacterial-killing ability of macrophages by increasing the oxidation of bacterial mycothiol. On the other hand, a functional SNP (rs13120371) located in the 3′ UTR region of xCT has been identified to increase susceptibility to TB by interacting with miR-142-3p. Mct4-dependent lactate secretion enhanced M2 macrophage polarization and resisted to PD-1 blockade in LKB1-deficient lung adenocarcinoma. Mct4 expression is upregulated from 6 to 48 h in both mRNA and protein level upon Mtb infection, but decreased in 72 h. As we concerned, gene expression in mRNA and protein level is a dynamic progress. When it reaches its peak, it will be regulated by feedback regulation and reduction. In this study, we demonstrated that Mct4 deficiency suppressed Mtb survival in macrophages and in mice, suggesting Mct4 to be a potential therapeutic target for TB treatment.
Macrophages are the primary targets of Mtb infection. Macrophages can induce autophagy, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and apoptosis to limit the intracellular growth of Mtb. Additionally, macrophages become activated and create a positive immune response to control infection, for example, by promoting phagocytosis and releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. , Phagocytosis of macrophages is also the first step in tumor-specific antigen processing. The antigens that combine with the co-stimulatory molecules CD80/CD86 effectively activate CD4+ and CD8+ T cells with the exception of the MHC molecules. Our results suggested that Mct4 deficiency enhanced the expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC-II in Mtb-infected lungs and macrophages; this might boost their ability to kill intracellular bacteria. In this study, Mct4 did not affect autophagy and ROS production; however, it promoted IL-1β and IL-10 expression, but had no effect on IL-6 and TNF-α expression. The cytokine release results were inconsistent in different cells after simulated with H37Rv in 24 and 48 h. We have concerned cells from knockout mice and Raw 264.7 cells as a stable system, but gene silencing as an instantaneous system. Therefore, the cytokine changes in gene silencing could not sustain as long as in cells from knockout mice and Raw 264.7 cells. Genetic ablation of Nlrp3 or Il1b ameliorated spreading depolarization-induced neuronal inflammation. Mct4-enhanced inflammation damage in the lungs may be attributed to IL-1β production. Furthermore, we figured out that Mct4 increased IL-10 production to promote Mtb survival. This study lays the theoretical foundation for the role of Mct4 in macrophage-mediated innate immunity.
IL-10 is an important factor to Mtb infection in different cell subset. CD4+ T cells respond rapidly to Mtb-infected lungs for TB control. IL-10 acts intrinsically in CD4+ T cells and impairs their parenchymal migratory capacity. Clinically, BCG vaccination was preventing the immunosuppressive effects of IL-10 in the fast progression of Mtb infection. Besides, IL-35-producing B cells may play a regulatory role during Mtb infection by producing IL-10. Foam macrophage formation reduced the phagocytosis of Mtb to protect apoptosis, while IL-10 deficiency suppressed foam macrophage formation. Therefore, IL-10 is significant to Mtb survival. Fc receptor-like 3 can activate p38 MAPK pathways to promote the secretion of IL-10 in B cells, thus inhibiting the secretion of inflammatory factors. Inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway rescued T cell functionality caused by IL-10 release, suggesting IL-10 is an important driving force of tumor immune escape. Casitas B-lineage lymphoma deficiency in dendritic cells suppressed p65-mediated transcription of IL-10, thereby aggravating dextran sodium sulfate-induced colitis. Our results showed Mct4 promoted IL-10 production through NF-κB p65 activation to promote Mtb survival, instead of p38 MAPK or JAK/STAT pathway.
Lactate is involved in various immune responses. The presence of exogenous lactate caused a decrease in extracellular acidification rate and an increase in the oxygen consumption rate, resulting in killing Mtb. Lactate reduced by lactate dehydrogenase A inactivation heightens type I interferon production upon retinoic acid-inducible gene I-like receptor) activation to protect mice from viral infection. Lactate exposure caused excessive intracellular ROS generation and blocked autophagy in diabetes. Lactate inhibited NF-κB activation and promoted IL-10 production in BV2 cells. Lactate suppressed NF-κB and Yes-associated protein activation via activation of adenosine 5′-monophosphate-activated protein kinase and large tumor suppressor in a GPR81-mediated signaling. In this study, we showed that Mct4 deficiency accumulated lactate in macrophages, attenuated NF-κB p65 activation, and decreased IL-10 production. However, the mechanism of how lactate affects NF-κB p65 activation remains further investigation.
In this study, we confirmed the ability of Mct4 to transport lactate outside of macrophages during Mtb infection. Mct4 promoted NF-κB p65 activation and induced IL-10 production by reducing intracellular lactate to facilitate Mtb survival. This study helps with understanding the interactions between metabolites and cytokines in Mtb infectious immune response and provides theoretical support and possible opportunities for TB treatment.

## Limitations of the study
We show that Mct4 promotes NF-κB p65 activation and induces IL-10 production by reducing intracellular lactate to facilitate Mtb survival. However, the molecular mechanism by which lactate affects NF-κB p65 activation has been unclear. Further research is needed to fully understand the molecular mechanism between lactate and NF-κB p65.

## Mice
C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). Slc16a3 deficient (Slc16a3−/−) mice on a C57BL/6J background were generated by GemPharmatech (Strain NO. T015436, Nanjing, China). All mice were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of the Southern Medical University under specific pathogen-free conditions. 6 to 8-week-old female Slc16a3+/+ and Slc16a3−/− mice were used in the study.

## Differentiation of mouse and human Mφs in vitro
Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were obtained from 6 to 8-week-old female mice, either Slc16a3+/+ or Slc16a3−/− mice, as specified. The cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, New York, USA) supplemented with 100 ng/mL mGM-CSF (PeproTech, Cranbury, USA) and at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The half of medium mentioned above was replaced with fresh medium on day 3 and day 5. After seven days of culture, BMDMs were detached by trypsin and transferred to 12-well cell culture plates (Cellter, Shanghai, China). Following 24 h of incubation at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator, subsequent experiments were conducted. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, New York, USA) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. THP-1 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, AdipoGen, San Diego, USA) to derive macrophages (THP-1-Mφ). Following 24 h of incubation at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator, subsequent experiments were conducted.

## Mycobacterial culture and infection
Mtb strain of H37Rv (American Type Culture Collection, NO.7294) were cultured in 7H9 broth medium (Becton Dickinson, New Jersey, USA) supplemented with 10% OADC (0.06% (v/v) oleic acid (SIGMA, St. Louis, USA), 5% albumin (SIGMA, St. Louis, USA), 100 mM glucose (GHTECH, Guangzhou, China), 0.003% catalase (SIGMA, St. Louis, USA), and 145 mM NaCl (GHTECH, Guangzhou, China) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Regular passaging is necessary to maintain the viability and virulence of Mtb. For infection experiments, a single bacterial suspension was prepared, and all procedures were performed aseptically in the biosafety cabinet within the BSL-3 laboratory at Southern Medical University. The concentration of the bacterial suspension was determined by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 600nm (OD600), and macrophages were infected based on the desired multiplicity of infection (MOI) for each experiment. 6-8-week-old female mice, both Slc16a3+/+ and Slc16a3−/− mice, with similar weights, were exposed to 1 × 107 colony-forming units (CFUs) of Mtb H37Rv using an Inhalation Exposure System (Glas-Col, Terre Haute, USA). , 

## RNA sequencing analysis
BMDMs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells per well and infected with H37Rv at a MOI of 1 for 24 h. Then the total cellular RNA of BMDMs was extracted by Trizol method. After RNA sequencing, we screened for genes with log2 (Fold change) ≥ 1 or ≤ −1 in solute carrier family.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR analysis
The total cellular RNA of BMDMs, Raw 264.7, and THP-1-Mφ was extracted by Trizol method, and RNA concentration was measured by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA). Then cDNA synthesis and reverse transcription experiments were carried out using cDNA synthesis kit Honor II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Novogene, Beijing, China). The abundance of mRNA of individual genes was quantified by real-time PCR using an SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Novogene, Beijing, China) on a LightCycler 480 thermocycler (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The reaction procedure of qPCR was adjusted according to the three-step method, which was predenaturation at 95°C for 2 min, 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, 40 cycles at 60°C for 15 s, 40 cycles at 68°C for 20 s, and finally stored at 4°C. All PCR products were normalized to β-actin transcript, then relative gene expressions abundance was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method and expressed as fold change. The complete primers are listed in .

## Western blot analysis
After removing the medium, BMDMs, Raw 264.7, and THP-1-Mφ were washed by 1×PBS and then lysed by protein lysis buffer. The protein lysate (composed of 20mM HEPES (SIGMA, St. Louis, USA), 10 mM KCl (GHTech, Guangzhou, China), 1 mM EDTA (Solarbio, Beijing, China), 400 mM NaCl (GHTech, Guangzhou, China), 0.5% NP-40 (Solarbio, Beijing, China), 5% (v/v) glycerol (GHTech, Guangzhou, China), 0.1% protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), and 1 mM dL-dithiothreitol (DTT, SIGMA, St. Louis, USA) was added 80 μL per well of the 12-well plate to lysis cells. The samples were then thoroughly disrupted with an ultrasonic disruptor for 5 min. After disruption, the samples were centrifuged at 4°C, 12,000g for 10 min. Protein quantification was performed, and the samples were denatured by heating at 95°C for 10 min in a metal dry heater. Different concentrations of separating gel (6%, 8%, 10%, 12%, and 15%) and 5% stacking gel were prepared according to experimental requirements. After electrophoresis and transfer, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck, KGaA, Germany). The proteins were blocked in 5% (w/v) BSA (SIGMA, St. Louis, USA) in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies for the target proteins were diluted according to the instructions. The proteins with primary antibodies were gently shaken overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBST for 10 min at three times, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Then repeated washing with PBST for 10 min at three times. The target protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Fude Biological Technology, Hangzhou, China) on a FluorChem System (ProteinSimple, Silicon Valley, USA). All immunoblotting bands were scanned using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) for quantitative density values, and normalized with β-actin as a control. The antibodies used in this study are listed in .

## Immunohistochemistry analysis
Active lymphatic TB patients and chronic inflammation (CI) subjects were diagnosed in and recruited from the Guangzhou Chest Hospital (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). 9 patients with active lymphatic TB (5 males and 4 females) aging from 16 to 71 years (median 40) were recruited between January 2019 to January 2021. CI subjects were chosen from consenting adults drawn from the same background and locality as the TB patients. 7 CI subjects (4 Males and 3 females) ranged in age from 18 to 78 years (median 45). Immunohistochemical staining of lymph node paraffin sections by Mct4 antibody. Set the edge of the tissue contour by the TMA plug-in in Quant Center 2.3 software to automatically identify the tissue. The H-Score of the region of interest at each point of each chip was quantified by the Densito quant in the Quant Center 2.3 software. (H-SCORE = ∑(PI×I)=(percentage of cells of weak intensity ×1)+(percentage of cells of moderate intensity ×2)+percentage of cells of strong intensity ×3). The H-Score of each sample was shown in .

## Small interfering RNA transfection and lentivirus infection
Transient small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting mouse Slc16a3 (NM_001038653.1), human SLC16A3 (NM_001042422.3), mouse IL-10 (NM_010548.2) were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) according to NCBI gene database. The siRNA target sequences involved in this study were listed in . Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA) and optiMEM (Gibco, New York, USA) as transfection reagent were mixed with siRNAs at final concentration of 100 nM for BMDMs and Raw 264.7 cells (250 nM for THP-1-Mφ). The mixed transfection reagents were incubated at room temperature for 20 min and added into macrophages. After 4–6 h incubation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. BMDMs were incubated for 48 h for subsequent experiments, while Raw 264.7 cells and THP-1-Mφ were incubated for 24 h.
293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1×106 cells per well overnight. For each well, 1.5 μg of plasmid containing the vector of Slc16a3 mRNA targeting in mouse, 1.5 μg of psPAX2, and 0.5 μg of pMD2.G were transfected using 7 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 and 400 μL of Opti-MEM. Media was changed to DMEM with 10% FBS 6 h after transfection. Virus supernatant was harvested 48 h post-transfection, filtered with a 0.45 μm PVDF filter. Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1×106 cells per well overnight and then infected with lentivirus. After 24 h infection, media was changed to DMEM with 10% FBS for cells to passaging. When cells reached appropriate density, media was changed with 1 μg/mL puromycin. After puromycin selection, Raw 264.7 cells with Mct4 overexpression were harvested for subsequent experiments.

## Colony-forming unit assays
BMDMs or THP-1-Mφ were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells per well and infected with H37Rv at a MOI of 5 for 1 h. Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells per well and infected with H37Rv at a MOI of 5 for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS at three times to remove excess extracellular bacteria, and complete medium was added for culture at different time points. DdH2O containing 0.01% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used to lysing macrophages. After appropriate dilution of 0.01% Triton X-100 (100 times at 0 h, 1000 times at 48h), 50 μL of the dilution was thoroughly mixed and inoculated onto 7H10 agar plates (Becton Dickinson, New Jersey, USA). The bacterial solution was evenly spread on the plate by coating stick, repeating the process 8 to 10 times per set. After coating, the plates were sealed and placed upside down at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After 21 days incubation, the number of colonies was counted.

## Flow cytometry analysis
Cells from lungs of Mtb-infected mice, BMDMs and Raw 264.7 cells were harvested and incubated with a fluorescently labeled antibody against the surface marker for 30 min. For intracellular markers, a fixation buffer was applied for 30 min, followed by incubation with a fluorescently labeled antibody against the intracellular marker for 60 min. After staining, the cells were washed with 1% BSA in PBS on ice, and the cell phenotype was analyzed using flow cytometry on a flow cytometer Attune NxT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA). Data were acquired as the fraction of labeled cells within a live-cell gate and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star). All gating strategies were established based on isotype-matched control antibodies. The antibodies used in this study are listed in .

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The cell supernatant was collected into a 1.5 mL EP tube, and any remaining H37Rv was filtered using a 0.22 μm microporous filter. The extracellular secretion of IL-10 was measured by the appropriate ELISA kit (MultiSciences, Hangzhou, China). All experimental procedures strictly followed the instructions provided by the manufacturer of the reagents. The OD values were read at wavelengths of 450 nm and 630 nm using a Microplate Reader (TECAN SPARK, Salzburg, Austria), and the corresponding IL-10 concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

## Treatment of reagents in Mφs
Raw 264.7 cells were pretreated with specific antibodies: 6.6 μg/mL IL-1β antibody (Selleckchem, Houston, USA) or 1 μg/mL IL-10 antibody (eBioscience, California, USA) for 2 h, and isotype control antibody as the solvent control, for the corresponding duration before H37Rv infection. Raw 264.7 cells were pretreated with specific inhibitors: 20 μM JSH23 for NF-κB p65 (Selleckchem, Houston, USA) or 10 μM SB203580 for p38 MAPK (Selleckchem, Houston, USA) for 1 h, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as the solvent control. Raw 264.7 cells were pretreated with 25mM Lactate (Selleckchem, Houston, USA) for 3 h, and DMEM with 10% FBS as the solvent control. The reagents used in this study are listed in .

## Cell viability assays
Cell viability was assessed using the TransDetect CCK8 kit (TransGen Biotech, China). Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well. After different treatments at various time points, the medium was replaced with CCK-8 solution and incubated at 37°C for approximately 20–40 min. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by the Varioskan Flash (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA) within 30 min. This allows for the evaluation of potential toxicity of the experimental treatments and their impact on cell survival.
Drug toxicity assessment was conducted as follows: According to the drug dosage specified in the experimental protocol, 100 μL of drug diluent and 100 μL of bacterial solution (containing 2 × 105 bacteria) were thoroughly mixed and inoculated for 48 h. The bacterial solution was evenly spread onto the 7H10 agar plate by coating stick, repeating the process 8 to 10 times per set. After coating, the plates were sealed and placed upside down at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After 21 days incubation, the number of colonies could be counted.

## Immunofluorescence analysis
After four-week infection, lungs and spleens from Slc16a3+/+ and Slc16a3−/− mice were dissected and fixed in formaldehyde. The lungs and spleens were then processed into paraffin sections with the following steps: xylene 1 (15 min) (Sinopharm, Beijing, China), xylene 2 (15 min), anhydrous ethanol 1 (5 min) (Sinopharm, Beijing, China), anhydrous ethanol 2 (5 min), 95% ethanol (5 min), 85% ethanol (5 min), 75% ethanol (5 min), and finally rinsed with water. The tissues were fixed on ice after drying. For antigen retrieval, 1× citric acid (pH 6.0) (Sinopharm, Beijing, China) was used as the repair solution. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the tissues with 3% H2O2 (Sinopharm, Beijing, China) in water for 20 min at room temperature. The tissues were then incubated with serum from the same source as the secondary antibody at 37°C for 30 min for blocking. The primary antibody was diluted in antibody diluent to a specific concentration and incubated with the tissues at 4°C overnight. The HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Pinuofei, Wuhan, China) was incubated with the tissues using PBST (Pinuofei, Wuhan, China) as the diluent at 37°C for 1 h. TSA reagent (Pinuofei, Wuhan, China) was incubated with the tissues at 37°C for 30 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Antigen retrieval was repeated until completion. DAPI working solution (Pinuofei, Wuhan, China) was added to the tissues, incubated at room temperature for 5 min, and then washed three times with PBS (Pinuofei, Wuhan, China) for 5 min each time. After drying, an anti-fluorescence tablet was added to the tissues, and a cover glass was placed. The fluorescence slides were stored at 4°C in the dark or observed under a fluorescence microscope. The obtained images were scanned and analyzed using ImageJ Software, and the results were analyzed using Pearson correlation coefficient.

## Lactate assay
The cell supernatant from the culture wells was collected into a 1.5 mL EP tube, and the residual H37Rv was filtered using a 0.22 μm microporous filter. The intracellular and extracellular lactate levels were measured using the Micro Lactate Assay kit (Chekine, Wuhan, China). All experimental procedures strictly followed the instructions provided by the manufacturer of the reagents. The Varioskan Flash (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA) was set to a wavelength of 450nm to measure the corresponding OD values, and the lactate concentration was calculated based on the standard curve.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Results are presented as mean values ±standard deviation (SD) of at least triplicate experiments. T-test was used to assess the differences between two parameters, usage of unpaired t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test depended on the normal distribution of the data. One-way ANOVA was used to analyze the differences in a one-way multilevel design. two-way ANOVA was utilized to assess the impact of two independent factors on the response variable and determine whether there was an interaction between the two factors. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures as follows: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; ns, not significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad).
