## Abstract
Chronic Obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a global health concern affecting over 300 million individuals worldwide, with airway fibrosis and subsequent small airway narrowing identified as the primary mechanism driving disease progression. This pathological process is characterized by accelerating lung aging, manifesting as reduced cellular growth and increased expression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors in fibroblasts, which we have demonstrated in our previous study utilizing a cadmium-induced COPD mouse model. Cadmium, a toxic heavy metal, penetrates deep into the lung airways inducing oxidative stress, promoting extracellular matrix deposition, and triggering inflammatory reactions. The present study elucidates that cadmium exposure results in the inhibition of sirtuin-6 (SIRT6) which promotes phosphorylation of the AKT/PI3K signaling pathway, which in turn leads to phosphorylation of vimentin, an intermediate filament protein. Cadmium increases extracellular vimentin which is phosphorylated through AKT/PI3K signaling due to SIRT6 inhibition. Vimentin inhibition using siRNA transfection study suggested no effect on SIRT6, phosphorylated AKT and PI3K expression. SIRT6 induction (UBCS039) in fibroblasts leads to a decrease in phosphorylated AKT/PI3K inhibiting extracellular vimentin levels (P = 0.0002) in cadmium-exposed cells. The present work proposes role of SIRT6 in regulation of vimentin, an important intermediate filament in pathogenesis of COPD.
Keywords: Intermediate filaments, COPD, Sirtuins, Cadmium, Airway fibrosis, Pulmospheres

## Introduction
COPD is a progressive irreversible airflow limitation into the lung, causing significant morbidity and mortality worldwide ( ; ; ; ). Airway remodeling in COPD is a complex process characterized by structural changes in the airways and lung parenchyma, leading to irreversible airflow limitation ( ). This process involves airway wall thickening and fibrosis with increased deposition of extracellular matrix proteins like vimentin, collagens and fibronectin ( ). While smoking remains the primary risk factor, environmental exposure plays a crucial role in COPD development and progression ( ; ; ). Cadmium (Cd) is a toxic heavy metal found in cigarette smoke and environmental pollution, has been extensively studied for its role in COPD pathogenesis ( ; ; ; ). Increased Cd levels in lung tissue correlate with smoking history and disease severity. Cd exposure enhances fibrinogen secretion leading to macrophage activation and fibrotic signaling ( ; ). When combined with carbon black, another component of cigarette smoke, Cd induces the production of citrullinated vimentin and activates lung fibroblasts through TLR4-dependent signaling ( ; , ; ).
Intermediate filaments, particularly vimentin ( ), play a critical role in Cd-induced lung pathology and the development of COPD. Cd exposure triggers vimentin phosphorylation at specific sites (Ser39 and Ser55) ( ; ), leading to myofibroblast differentiation and increased extracellular matrix deposition around small airways ( ). This phosphorylation mediates Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) and Smad3 signaling, contributing to peribronchiolar fibrosis and airway remodeling ( ; ). The importance of vimentin in this process is emphasized by studies showing that vimentin-deficient mice are protected from Cd-induced peribronchiolar fibrosis ( ). Cd exposure also alters vimentin filament morphology, affecting cell attachment and subcellular organization ( ). Moreover, vimentin regulates fibroblast invasiveness, which is relevant to lung fibrosis progression ( ). Even at low exposure levels, Cd stimulates pulmonary fibrotic signaling and myofibroblast differentiation.
The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating vimentin phosphorylation, with far-reaching implications for cellular processes such as motility, invasion, and fibrosis ( ). AKT1 kinase specifically phosphorylates vimentin at serine 39, which protects it from caspase-induced proteolysis and enhances cell motility and invasiveness ( ; ). Phosphorylated vimentin acts as a scaffold, sequestering 14–3–3 protein complexes and amplifying the effects of activated AKT ( ). In the context of autophagy regulation, AKT-phosphorylated vimentin forms a complex with beclin 1 and 14–3–3, promoting tumorigenesis by reducing autophagy. In pulmonary fibrosis, Cd exposure activates AKT, leading to vimentin phosphorylation at Ser39, which is critical for extracellular matrix deposition and peribronchiolar fibrosis ( ). PI3K/AKT signaling-induced vimentin phosphorylation is also essential for efficient cellular migration and contributes to cytoskeletal reorganization and cell shape changes ( ; ; ; ; ). These findings underscore the significance of PI3K/AKT signaling in modulating vimentin phosphorylation and its subsequent effects on cellular processes involved in fibrotic diseases.
SIRT6, a member of the sirtuin family of NAD-dependent enzymes, plays a complex role in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and other respiratory conditions ( ; ). While SIRT6 expression is decreased in lung homogenates from COPD patients and correlates with lung function, it exhibits both protective and potentially harmful effects depending on the context ( ). Decreased expression of SIRT6 has been associated with reduced lung function (FEV1) in COPD patients, suggesting its role in maintaining pulmonary function ( ). It offers protection against DNA damage, which is notably increased in patients with COPD, and inhibits fibrosis, a process involved in airway remodeling ( ; ; ). SIRT6 inhibits fibrosis in COPD, attenuates cigarette smoke-induced cellular senescence, and induces autophagy ( ). SIRT6 also helps to downregulate inflammation through modulation of NF-κB signaling and promotes Nrf2/ARE signaling, thereby protecting cells against oxidative stress, a major contributor to COPD pathogenesis ( ).
Cd exposure significantly alters SIRT6 activity and localization in cells It causes SIRT6 to move from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, mediated by poly-ubiquitinated p62 ( ). The effects vary with exposure time: short-term exposure reduces apoptotic markers, while long-term exposure increases apoptosis. This redistribution of SIRT6 is crucial in Cd-induced cell death ( ). Cigarette smoke extract (CSE) suppresses SIRT6 expression in human bronchial epithelial cells (HBECs)( ). Importantly, SIRT6 overexpression induces autophagy by attenuating the IGF-Akt-mTOR signaling pathway ( ). However, the role of SIRT6 in regulating expression of vimentin is not known in context with Cd exposure in lung tissue.

## Methods
2.1.

## Lung tissue biospecimens
Human lung tissues were obtained from tissue repository at University of Alabama at Birmingham as per the IRB protocol and guidelines. For control group, n = 5, non-COPD samples (smokers, n = 2; never-smokers, n = 3) were processed. n = 6, COPD lung tissue samples (smokers, n = 3; never-smokers, n = 3) were processed as test samples. All experiments were performed with relevant guidelines of the protocol approved by the institutional Review Board from The University of Alabama at Birmingham, affiliated hospital.

2.2.

## Primary fibroblast isolation
Primary human lung fibroblasts were isolated as described previously ( ). lung tissue fragments were enzymatically digested using a 1 mg/ml collagenase solution in HBSS for 30 min at 37°C. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer to remove larger tissue pieces, followed by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min. To enhance fibroblast purity, the cell population underwent negative selection by culturing in dishes coated with CD16/32 and CD45 antibodies, effectively removing lymphocytes and macrophages. The primary fibroblasts were then cultured in Eagle’s MEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and passaged twice to further increase purity. Cells were allowed to reach 80–90 % confluency before trypsinization and subsequent plating. For comparison, the IMR-90 human fetal lung fibroblast cell line, obtained from ATCC, was cultured in MEM containing 2 mM glutamine and 10 % fetal bovine serum.

2.3.

## In-vitro studies
Fibroblasts were treated with Cd for 3 h and rested for 48 h to allow for recovery time. For experiments involving AKT inhibitor (LY294002, Sigma-Aldrich), Rapamycin (Sigma-Aldrich) or SIRT6 activator (UABCS039), cells were pretreated for 3 h before Cd exposure. For experiments with SIRT6 activator (UABCS039, 80 μM, Selleckem) cells were pretreated for 24 h. Then cells were treated with Cd for 3 h followed by a resting time of 48 h. For experiments with SIRT6 inhibitor (SIRT6-IN-1, 100 μM, Selleckem), cells were treated for 24 h.

2.4.

## Transfection studies
Human lung fibroblast, IMR90 cells, were used for siRNA transfection experiments ( ). The cells were seeded at 70 % confluence and then exposed to either vimentin siRNA or a nontargeting control siRNA (NT-siRNA). These siRNAs were combined with a diluted transfection reagent obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. The transfection process was carried out following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Following a 24 h transfection period, the cells were subjected to a low dose CdCl2 treatment (10 μM). Subsequently, the cells were collected for protein analysis using Western blotting techniques. This experimental setup allowed for the investigation of vimentin knockdown effects in the context of Cd exposure ( ).

2.5.

## Cadmium exposed mouse model
Animals were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and experiments were approved by the Institutional Committee of Animal Care of the UAB. Randomly assigned 8-wk-old mice weighing 25–30 g to experimental group ( , ). Mice were treated by intratracheal instillation (IT) once with cadmium chloride (CdCl2, Sigma-Aldrich, CAS 10108–64–2, 0.458 mg/kg) as described before. The dosage was selected for mice because they resulted in tissue levels of Cd that were comparable to those found in the lung tissue of COPD patients. After the mouse’s tongue was carefully moved out of the way, nonsurgical intratracheal instillation is noninvasive intratracheal inoculation through the intra-tracheal channel. Mice were sedated with oxygen and isoflurane, and their weights were used to determine the CdCl2 dosage needed for each mouse. At week 8, mice were sacrificed, and lungs were removed for histology. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected for vimentin estimation. Lung tissues from mice were dried, acid hydrolyzed and hydroxyproline concentration was examined as manufacture’s protocols (Chondrex, 6017).

2.6.

## Airways resistance measurements
Airway resistance alterations in Cd-exposed mice were evaluated. Mice were first anesthetized using a combination of ketamine and xylazine. After anesthesia induction, the animals underwent tracheal cannulation to establish a direct airway access. Respiratory mechanics were then assessed using the FlexiVent system (SCIREQ). The ventilation parameters were set to 160 breaths per minute, with a tidal volume of 6 ml/kg body weight, and a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 2.5 cm H2O. To evaluate airway responsiveness, methacholine was administered via inhalation at 5-minute intervals. This setup allows for detailed analysis of lung mechanics and airway hyperresponsiveness, which are crucial parameters in respiratory research ( , ).

2.7.

## Immunohistochemistry and subepithelial collagen thickness measurement
Lung specimens were fixed with formalin and embedded in paraffin, and immunohistochemistry was performed using Dako Envision visualization kit following the standard protocol. Slides were stained with hematoxylin-eosin or incubated with primary antibodies against α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; 1:400; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA), vimentin (1:400, Invitrogen, US) or collagen-1 (1:200; Rockland, Limerick, PA). The mean subepithelial collagen thickness was traced and determined as previously described ( ).

2.8.

## Measurement of collagen
Using the Sircol collagen assay, collagen levels in mice lung tissue and the supernatant of cells treated with CdCl2 were determined ( ). This assay was performed as per the protocol provided in a commercial kit produced by Biocolor (Northern Ireland, UK), ensuring standardized and reliable results.

2.9.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay analysis
Extracellular vimentin (eVim) concentrations in mice BALF were quantified using a commercially available mouse vimentin enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) kit (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendation ( ). The assay was also performed to estimate eVim levels in supernatants from cells treated with Cd and kinase inhibitors and SIRT6 activator.

2.10.

## Immunoblot analyses
Lysate was prepared from human lung tissue, mice lung tissue, primary lung fibroblasts or IMR-90 cells. To examine vimentin secretion, supernatants from treated and untreated cells were collected. Supernatants were concentrated by ultracentrifugation using Millipore centrifugal concentrator (Labsupplyoutlaws, Cleveland, OH). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membranes for immunoblot analysis. The membranes were blocked with TBS-T and skimmed milk, then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C. Primary antibodies-entin (1:500, Invitrogen), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, 1:1000, CST), AKT (1:1000, CST), p-AKT (1:1000, CST), PI3K (1:1000, CST), p-PI3K (1:1000, CST), p-mTOR (1:1000, CST) and β-actin (1:1000, CST) were used. After washing, blots were treated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were detected using DAB substrate, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

2.11.

## Intracellular NAD+ /NADPH assay
IMR-90 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well. The next day, cells were treated with different concentrations of Cd (0,1, 5, 10 and 20 μM) for 3 h. After treatment, intracellular NADPH content was measured using the NAD+ /NADPH Assay Kit-WST (Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

2.12.

## Intracellular ATP assay
IMR-90 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well. The next day, cells were treated with different concentrations of Cd (0,1, 5, 10 and 20 μM) for 3 h. After treatment, intracellular ATP content was measured using the ATP Assay Kit (Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

2.13.

## 3D Pulmospheres preparation
Lung tissue samples were washed with cold PBS, digested with collagenase A for 30 min at 37°C with gentle agitation, and dissociated into a single-cell suspension by adding PBS and vigorous shaking. The suspension was filtered through a 100-μm filter, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, washed with complete DMEM (10 % FCS, penicillin-streptomycin, and Glutamax), and treated with ACK lysis buffer to remove red blood cells. After stopping the reaction with PBS and centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in growth media, plated in T-25 flasks, and used to generate passage 0–1 cells for spheroid preparation. Poly-HEMA-coated plates were prepared by adding a 5 mg/ml poly-HEMA solution to wells of a U-bottom 96-well plate and allowing evaporation for 24 h. For spheroid formation, primary lung cells were trypsinized, neutralized, centrifuged, resuspended in DMEM, and seeded at 8000–10,000 cells per well in coated plates( ; ). After centrifugation at 200 g for 1 min and incubation at 37°C in a CO2 (5 %) incubator for 16 h, spheroids were pretreated with UBCS039 (80 μM) for 24 h ( ). Pulmospheres were then treated with cadmium chloride (CdCl2, 10 μM) for 3 h and then rested. Following treatment, pulmospheres were processed for invasion assay or stained for the presence of vimentin.

2.14.

## Invasion assay
Invasiveness of pulmospheres was assessed using previously established and described protocol ( ; ). A bottom layer of collagen solution containing collagen type I solution (3.98 mg/ml) (Corning) with DMEM (Invitrogen) at a ratio of 2.1 ml of collagen solution and 6.2 ml of DMEM were layered to bottom of the well. Pulmospheres were seeded onto the collagen-coated well with 100 μl of the collagen-DMEM solution containing CdCl2 (10 μM) or UBCS039 (80 μM) or media alone without a test drug in a single well. In total, 5 pulmospheres were seeded for each treatment. The plates were incubated at 37°C in a CO2 incubator overnight. Images were captured with a Carl Zeiss AxioCam color camera and analyzed using AxioVision LE Imaging System software. In the AxioVision LE Imaging System software, the invasive cells’ area was delineated and calculated (H). Additionally, the center of the pulmospheres was outlined and measured (R). The percentage of the total invaded area (ZOI%) was determined by (H - R)/R × 100. The percentage invasion area was calculated. The ZOI fold change was measured as the ratio of the ZOI% with treatment to the ZOI% without treatment in vitro with an antifibrotic drug.

## Results
3.1.

## Extracellular vimentin level increases in lungs of Cd-exposed mice model of COPD
Cd-exposure was investigated for its potential to induce vimentin secretion in an established mouse model of COPD. Based on our previous studies, a dose of 0.458 mg/kg CdCl2 was administered to mice ( , ). At 4-week post intra-tracheal instillation of CdCl2 in mice, the Cd concentrations in the lungs (0.85 ± 0.3 ng/mg protein) closely approximated levels observed in lung tissue from human COPD subjects ( , ). This finding demonstrates that the mouse model successfully achieved Cd concentrations in lung parenchyma comparable to those found in COPD patients, thus providing a clinically relevant experimental system for studying Cd-induced pulmonary pathology. Histological analysis of mice exposed to CdCl2 revealed small airway wall thickening and ECM deposition ( ). Quantitative assessment demonstrated a statistically significant increase in subepithelial thickness in Cd-exposed mice compared to saline controls (p < 0.0001, ). Furthermore, pulmonary function tests showed elevated airway resistance in Cd-exposed mice ( ). Increased staining was observed for α-SMA, which is a marker for myofibroblasts, along with increased expression of collagen I ( ). Collagen deposition in lung is also shown with picrosirus staining ( ) and hydroxyproline concentration ( ). Increase in vimentin expression due to Cd exposure was observed in lung tissue sections ( ). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) showed a significant increase in vimentin levels in Cd-exposed mice compared to saline controls (p < 0.0001, ). The secretion of vimentin was further confirmed by treating mice lung tissue isolated fibroblasts with increasing concentration of CdCl2 for 3 h and rested for 48 h. Immunoblotting of concentrated supernatants revealed the presence of both full-length vimentin (57 kDa) and novel vimentin fragments (49 kDa or less) in supernatants from Cd exposure ( ). This observation supports our previous demonstration of Cd-induced dose-dependent increase in vimentin fragmentation ( ). These experiments provided evidence that Cd induces extracellular matrix material release and deposition in lung tissue. Also, an increase in extracellular fragmented vimentin levels predicts the activation of caspase substrates and cleavage of vimentin protein ( ; ).

## Extracellular vimentin is phosphorylated by Cd-induced AKT/PI3K signaling
The release of vimentin into the extracellular space is associated with its post-translational modification ( ; ; ). demonstrates vimentin arrangement and release in CdCl2 exposed (3 h exposure) human primary fibroblasts after 48 h. CdCl2 induces phosphorylation of vimentin and kinase-AKT ( ). To investigate the role of AKT/PI3K signaling in phosphorylated eVim we used human primary fibroblasts (n = 5). We used inhibitors for AKT (LY294002) alone and for AKT/PI3K/mTOR (rapamycin) signaling. Cells were treated with rapamycin and LY294002 for 1 h prior Cd exposure for 3 h. 48 h later these cells were observed to demonstrate a change in expression of phosphorylated-AKT and -PI3K. Cd treatment significantly increased soluble collagen content (p < 0.0001, ). Cells pretreated with AKT/PI3K/mTOR inhibitor (rapamycin) and AKT inhibitor (LY294002) demonstrated significant (p < 0.0001) decrease in collagen content post Cd treatment ( ). Immunoblot analysis also revealed AKT, PI3K and mTOR phosphorylation 48 h post Cd treatment ( ). α-SMA activation in the presence of Cd alone and inhibition in the presence of inhibitors further evaluates the potential effects of AKT/PI3K mediated inhibition of myofibroblast differentiation ( ). Cell supernatants from different treatment sets were analyzed for extracellular vimentin (eVim) as well as phosphorylation. In we demonstrated increased eVim release due to Cd treatment. LY294002 and rapamycin (p < 0.0001) treatment decreased eVim levels in Cd exposed cells ( ). Analyzing supernatant and cell lysate on immunoblots demonstrated that Cd stimulation led to vimentin phosphorylation and release increased release in supernatant ( ). AKT inhibitor and rapamycin displayed an inhibition of phosphorylated vimentin (p-Vim) induced by Cd ( ). These observations suggest collective inhibition of AKT/PI3K/mTOR may be effective towards inhibition of p-Vim.

## SIRT6 expression in COPD lung tissue
Smoking is one the leading causes of COPD and Cd is one of the major components of cigarettes. 2–3 μg concentration of Cd is present per cigarette and has half-life of 26 years in the human body. The lack of dedicated mechanism for Cd secretion leads to its bioaccumulation and increases oxidative stress. SIRT6 expression protects against oxidative stress and therefore we analyzed expression of SIRT6 in non-COPD and COPD lung tissue. Overall SIRT6 expression levels were lower in COPD compared to non-COPD ( & ). Further, SIRT6 expression was observed to decrease specifically in smokers from COPD and non-COPD group. Lung tissue section staining also suggest enhanced vimentin in COPD compared to control (non-COPD) ( ). BALF samples were analyzed for the release of vimentin in extracellular space. Extra cellular vimentin was increased in the COPD BALF samples ( ).

## Cd exposure inhibits SIRT6 expression in lung fibroblasts
We evaluated the effect of Cd on SIRT6 expression using human primary fibroblast cell line, IMR-90. Cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of Cd (0, 5, 10 and 20 μM) for 3 h and rested for 48 h before testing the target protein expression. Increasing Cd concentration significantly decreased NAD+/NADH ratio ( ) and ATP levels ( ) which is seen upon inhibition of SIRT6 expression and increased. Cd treatment decreased expression of SIRT6 in dose dependent manner ( ). 10 μM CdCl2 significantly (p < 0.0001) decrease SIRT6 expression in fibroblasts ( & ). Further, cells were transfected with vimentin siRNA or NT-siRNA and treated with 10 μM of CdCl2 for 3 h and then rested for 48 h. Inhibition of vimentin using siRNA resulted in a significant reduction of vimentin expression ( ). However, this depletion did not alter the phosphorylation status of AKT or PI3K. These observations suggest that vimentin expression is not a prerequisite for activation of AKT/PI3K/mTOR signaling pathway. SIRT6 inhibits AKT/PI3K/mTOR signaling ( ). The vimentin inhibition had no effect on SIRT6 expression which was reduced due to Cd exposure ( ) demonstrating role of SIRT6 as an upstream signaling protein.

## Discussion
Intermediate filaments (IFs) exhibit cell-type-specific expression patterns, conferring unique viscoelastic properties to the cytoskeleton ( ). In humans, 70 genes encode IF proteins, with vimentin being a type III IF protein predominantly found in mesenchymal cells ( ; ; ). Vimentin intermediate filaments (VIFs) form extensive, dynamic, and hyper elastic networks ( ). Recent studies have elucidated the complex process of vimentin secretion, revealing that cytoskeletal vimentin must undergo disassembly before it can be released extracellularly ( ). This disassembly is facilitated by post-translational modifications, primarily citrullination in neutrophils ( ) and phosphorylation in macrophages ( ) and fibroblasts ( ; ). Despite lacking conventional secretory signals, vimentin has been shown to be actively secreted into the extracellular space rather than merely appearing due to cell membrane damage ( ; ). Increased expression of vimentin in the airway epithelium of COPD patients has been correlated with airflow limitation and basement membrane thickening ( ). Here we demonstrate increased deposition of vimentin in lung tissue sections of COPD patients ( ). This increased vimentin expression is associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related de-differentiation, which contributes to airway remodeling in COPD ( ). We also demonstrate an increase in phosphorylated vimentin in smokers with COPD ( ). Cd-exposure induces significant post-translational modifications (PTMs) of vimentin including phosphorylation, citrullination, and potentially oxidative modifications, which play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of peribronchiolar fibrosis and airway remodeling ( ; , ). In the study we demonstrate phosphorylated vimentin in BALF from mice lung exposed to Cd ( ). Increased collagen deposition and vimentin release in extracellular spaces and airway resistance suggest presence of fibrotic tissue due to Cd exposure ( ). Specifically, Cd triggers phosphorylation of vimentin at Ser39 and Ser55, mediated by AKT and cdc2 kinases respectively, leading to vimentin fragmentation and secretion ( ). AKT1-mediated phosphorylation at Ser39 enhances vimentin’s ability to induce motility and invasion while protecting it from caspase-induced proteolysis.
The extracellular expression of vimentin involves at least two critical steps ( ). First, PTM of key residues essential for vimentin filament assembly ( , , ). These modifications result in the solubilization of vimentin into smaller oligomeric units. The second step involves the unconventional type III secretion pathway, which facilitates the release of vimentin into the extracellular space or its localization to the exterior cell membrane surface. The secreted vimentin often appears in a fragmented, non-filamentous form. Microscopy images in shows this confirmational change in vimentin secreted from the cells in the presence of Cd. ( ). This eVim subsequently promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), through the activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and NF-κB signaling pathways. Importantly, eVim ( ; ) acts as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) in modulating the immune response, particularly through neutrophil activation ( ; ; ). These modifications significantly alter vimentin’s functional properties, promoting myofibroblast differentiation, enhancing extracellular matrix production, and activating fibrosis-related signaling pathways such as YAP1 and Smad2/3 ( ).
Extracellular release of p-Vim was higher in Cd exposed lung fibroblast which inhibited in the presence of AKT and mTOR inhibitors, individually ( ). Release of eVim is an outcome of EMT ( ) due to Cd exposure thus inhibiting vimentin may not have an overall regulatory effect towards the toxicity ( ). In this study we investigated the regulation of eVim in Cd exposure to lungs. SIRT6 deficiency leads to increased AKT phosphorylation ( ) at Ser-473 and Thr-308, resulting in hyperactivation of IGF-AKT signaling ( ; ). This hyperactivation has been associated with the development of cardiac hypertrophy, heart failure, and chronic inflammation in SIRT6-deficient mice ( ; ). SIRT6, a member of the sirtuin family of NAD-dependent enzymes, plays a multifaceted role in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) pathogenesis ( ). We observed decreased SIRT6 expression in smokers with COPD ( ). SIRT6 exhibits protective effects against COPD by inhibiting cellular senescence and fibrosis ( ). It regulates redox balance and reduces inflammation by modulating NF-κB signaling and other pathways involved in oxidative stress responses ( ). SIRT6, along with SIRT1, protects cells from senescence by deacetylating the transcription factor FOXO3 and inhibiting the PI3K-mTOR pathway ( ; ). Additionally, SIRT6 has been shown to improve genomic stability and DNA damage response. UBCS039, the first synthetic and specific activator of Sirtuin 6 (SIRT6) ( ), demonstrates potential therapeutic benefits for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and lung fibrosis ( ). Pretreatment of fibroblasts and pulmospheres with UBCS039 reduced p-Vim levels and inhibited invasiveness of pulmospheres even after the exposure with Cd ( ). SIRT6 upregulation inhibited p-Vim in cellular as well as in extracellular component. UBCS039 shows selective binding to SIRT6 without significantly affecting the deacetylation activities of other closely related sirtuin family members, namely SIRT1, SIRT2, and SIRT3 ( ). Additionally, UBCS039 demonstrates antioxidant properties by regulating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway ( ), which could help alleviate oxidative stress damage in lung tissues ( ). In the present study we also observed increased levels of NAD+ /NADPH ratio and ATP levels in SIRT6 agonist treated cells ( ). While direct studies of UBCS039 in COPD are limited, its ability to attenuate inflammation and oxidative stress suggests potential benefits for lung fibrosis and COPD management. Thus, we used SIRT6 inhibitor for its role in regulating AKT/PI3K signaling and vimentin expression. SIRT6 inhibitor further supported our findings with Cd exposure and suggested inhibition of SIRT6 upregulated phosphorylation of AKT and PI3K which further phosphorylate vimentin as estimated extracellularly.
Vimentin is a pleiotropic intermediate filament protein that not only maintains cellular structure and mechanical stability but also regulates key processes such as cell migration, signal transduction, and immune responses. Its dynamic post-translational modifications enable vimentin to influence fibrosis and tissue repair underscoring its significance as a multifunctional regulator and potential therapeutic target in diverse disease contexts. Our study identifies a novel SIRT6-vimentin interaction axis that elucidates the functional mechanisms of vimentin in the context of pulmonary fibrosis. By uncovering this regulatory relationship, we provide new insight into how SIRT6 may modulate vimentin’s downstream effects, which are central to the development and progression of fibrosis in the distal airways. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the molecular underpinnings of COPD but also highlight potential strategies for manipulating vimentin upstream regulators with greater precision. Ultimately, targeting these pathways offers a promising approach to inhibit or mitigate distal airway fibrosis in COPD, opening avenues for the development of more effective therapeutic interventions.
