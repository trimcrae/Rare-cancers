## Background
Periodontitis is a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive destruction of periodontal supporting tissues. Although DNA vaccines targeting periodontal pathogens have shown potential, insufficient immunogenicity remains a major limitation.

## Methods
A MyD88-adjuvanted DNA vaccine targeting Porphyromonas gingivalis antigens was developed and administered intranasally in a rat model of periodontitis. Rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 3–7 per group). Vaccine‑induced immune responses, alveolar bone loss, and safety were systematically evaluated.

## Results
Intranasal immunization with the MyD88-adjuvanted vaccine elicited significantly enhanced mucosal and systemic antibody responses, including elevated salivary secretory IgA and serum antigen‑specific IgG and IgA. The vaccine promoted coordinated activation of B cells and follicular helper T cells in mucosal and systemic lymphoid tissues. Importantly, vaccinated rats exhibited markedly reduced alveolar bone loss compared with disease controls and the non‑adjuvanted vaccine group. Quantitatively, intranasal MyD88-adjuvanted vaccination significantly decreased alveolar bone loss (one-way ANOVA, F(4,10) = 137.1, P < 0.05). The mean bone loss was reduced from approximately 0.78 mm in the periodontitis group to approximately 0.56 mm in the MyD88-adjuvanted group. No significant adverse effects or pathological changes were observed.

## Conclusions
These findings indicate that MyD88 effectively enhances the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of a periodontal DNA vaccine. This intranasal MyD88-adjuvanted DNA vaccine may be useful as a preventive and adjunctive immunotherapeutic strategy and warrants further translational studies in oral health.

## Background
Periodontitis, a chronic infectious disease initiated by bacterial biofilms and modulated by host immune-inflammatory responses and environmental factors, is characterized by inflammation of periodontal soft tissues, progressive destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, and eventual tooth loss [ – ]. It affects 45%–50% of the global population and is associated with systemic conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, significantly compromising patients’ quality of life.
Accumulating evidence links chronic oral infections to systemic low-grade inflammation and immune-mediated diseases [ ], with the “keystone pathogen” hypothesis indicating that Porphyromonas gingivalis disrupts immune homeostasis and triggers uncontrolled inflammation [ ]. Consequently, effective prevention and control are crucial. Current clinical strategies rely primarily on mechanical debridement combined with antimicrobial therapy [ ], and persistent challenges in tissue regeneration make vaccination a promising strategy for long-term disease control.
DNA vaccines offer advantages such as ease of production, high stability, and a favorable safety profile. They also have the ability to induce both humoral and cellular immune responses [ ]. The 2021 approval of the first DNA vaccine for human use, ZyCoV-D, underscores their translational potential [ ], and DNA vaccines against various infectious diseases are currently under clinical evaluation, providing a novel approach for periodontitis prevention [ – ].
As the keystone pathogen of periodontitis, P. gingivalis relies on virulence factors such as HA2 and FimA, which play core roles in disease progression, making them rational targets for DNA vaccines [ – ]. Based on this, our group previously constructed a dual-antigen DNA vaccine targeting both factors (pVAX1-HA2-IRES-fimA) [ ]; however, its immunogenicity requires optimization, and the use of molecular adjuvants represents a key direction for enhancing vaccine efficacy [ – ]. MyD88, an intracellular adaptor in the Toll-like receptor pathway, offers superior safety and can activate downstream transcription factors to boost both innate and adaptive immunity [ – ], with preclinical studies confirming its ability to enhance vaccine-induced immune responses [ , , ].
Therefore, in this study, we applied MyD88 as a molecular adjuvant in a periodontal DNA vaccine for the first time. We constructed the pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 DNA vaccine, verified its expression and biosafety in vitro, and evaluated its immunogenicity in a periodontitis rat model via intranasal immunization. Furthermore, we explored the molecular mechanisms underlying its immune enhancement using RNA-seq. In summary, this study provides preclinical evidence for the use of MyD88-adjuvanted DNA vaccines in the prevention and treatment of periodontitis.

## Methods

## Animals and cells
Thirty-five 4-week-old male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (weight 117 ± 21 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Zunyi Medical University (license SYXK-Qian-2021-0004). All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Rats were housed under standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells were purchased from Sivier Biological Co. (China), cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, and stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

## Main Experimental Reagents
The main reagents used in this study included DMEM high-glucose medium; erythrocyte lysis buffer (Sivier, China); premium-grade fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA); CCK-8 reagent (Abbkine, China); PrimeScript RT reagent kit, TRIzol reagent, and TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Japan); 2× SanTaq PCR Mix (Sigma, USA); anti-HA, anti-FLAG, and anti-His antibodies; anti-Tubulin (Bioworld, USA); HRP-conjugated anti-rat IgA and anti-CXCR5 (Abcam, USA); pilocarpine nitrate (Bausch + Lomb, USA); and isoflurane anesthetic (RWD, China).
Recombinant HA2 and FimA proteins were prepared in-house prior to the experiments. Additional reagents included Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent, Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, PageRuler™ Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Fisher, USA); IL-4 monoclonal antibody (Ox81) eFluor™ 660 (eBioscience™, Thermo Fisher, USA); CoraLite594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, USA); purified mouse anti-rat CD32 and FITC mouse anti-rat IFN-γ (DB-1) (BD Pharmingen, USA); APC anti-rat CD45RA [OX-33], FITC anti-rat CD3 [G4.18], PE anti-rat CD8a [OX-8], PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-rat CD3 [G4.18], FITC anti-rat CD4 (domain 1) [OX-38]; Cell Stimulation and Protein Transport Inhibitor Kit (Elabscience, China); and PE anti-ICOS (CD278) and FITC anti-rat CD4 antibodies (BioLegend, USA). All reagents were prepared and stored according to the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure activity and consistency.

## Main Experimental Methods

## Construction and Identification of Periodontitis DNA Vaccines
The pVAX1-HA2-fimA plasmid was constructed by sequentially inserting coding sequences for 6×His-tagged HA2 and 3×FLAG-tagged fimA into the pVAX1 backbone using standard restriction enzyme digestion and ligation protocols. Subsequently, the HA-tagged MyD88 gene was inserted to generate the pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 construct. Each recombinant plasmid was transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells, and three independent bacterial colonies per construct were selected for colony PCR to verify correct insertion. Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was performed with two technical replicates for each plasmid to confirm sequence accuracy and minimize potential errors. Plasmid DNA for in vitro and in vivo experiments was purified using endotoxin-free maxi prep kits and quantified by NanoDrop spectrophotometry.

## In Vitro Expression Analysis of Periodontitis DNA Vaccines
HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and seeded into 6-well plates at 1 × 10^6 cells/mL. Cells were divided into three groups: blank control, pVAX1-HA2-fimA, and pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88, with three technical replicates per group. When cells reached 70–90% confluence, they were transfected with the respective plasmids using Lipofectamine™ 3000 and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Total RNA was extracted from transfected cells and analyzed by RT-PCR to confirm the transcription of HA2, fimA, and MyD88, with PCR products verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Total protein was extracted and assessed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blot to detect HA2, fimA, and MyD88 protein expression. Cell proliferation effects were evaluated using the CCK-8 assay in 96-well plates at 1 × 10^4 cells/well, with plasmid doses of 50, 100, or 200 ng/well, and absorbance measured at 450 nm after 24, 48, and 72 h.

## In Vivo Immunogenicity Evaluation of pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 in SD Rats

## Optimal dosage screening
Fifteen male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 3 per group) using a computer-generated random number table. This sample size (n = 3 per group) was chosen in accordance with the 3R principles for animal research, as preliminary data indicated it was sufficient to detect clear trends in mucosal immune responses while minimizing animal use. Group allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by personnel not involved in subsequent procedures. 
The groups were as follows: blank control (sterile saline), vector control (pVAX1-HA2-fimA, 100 µg), and three dose groups of the test vaccine (pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 at 50, 100, or 200 µg). All rats received bilateral intranasal immunizations under isoflurane anesthesia at 5, 6, and 8 weeks of age. Immediately prior to administration, saline and plasmid preparations were diluted in sterile distilled water to a total delivery volume of 100 µL per rat (50 µL per nostril).
Saliva samples were collected weekly for nine consecutive weeks. Salivation was induced by intraperitoneal injection of pilocarpine nitrate (0.5 mg/kg) under isoflurane anesthesia. Salivary secretory IgA (SIgA) antibody levels specific to the vaccine antigens were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), with n = 3 biological replicates per group and each sample measured in technical triplicate.

## Experimental groups and periodontitis model establishment
A total of 37 four-week-old male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (117 ± 21 g) were used. Eight rats were assigned to the blank control cohort (no ligature), and the remaining 29 rats were allocated to the periodontitis modeling cohort. In the modeling cohort, experimental periodontitis was induced by ligating the maxillary second molars with 3 − 0 silk sutures under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. The periodontal status of the maxillary second molars, including gingival redness, bleeding, and tooth mobility, was monitored weekly by gross examination. Ligatures were checked regularly, and any dislodged sutures were promptly replaced to maintain continuous local irritation.
At 4 weeks after the initial ligation, one rat from the blank cohort and one rat from the modeling cohort were euthanized. Maxillae were collected for hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining and micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) to evaluate alveolar bone resorption and inflammation, thereby confirming successful establishment of the periodontitis model.
After confirmation of model success, the remaining 7 rats in the blank cohort were designated as group A (blank control, no ligature). The remaining 28 rats from the successfully modeled cohort were randomly assigned to four experimental groups, with 7 rats per group (n = 7): group B, periodontitis model; group C, periodontitis + pVAX1 vector; group D, periodontitis + pVAX1-HA2-fimA; and group E, periodontitis + pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88. Group allocation for groups B–E was performed using a computer-generated random table by personnel not involved in subsequent experiments. All procedures and assessments were conducted under blinded conditions.

## Vaccination and Efficacy Assessment
Following confirmation of successful periodontitis model establishment and group allocation, rats in groups A–E received intranasal immunization under isoflurane anesthesia at weeks 9, 10, and 12. A total volume of 100 µL per rat (50 µL per nostril) was administered at each time point. Group-specific treatments were as follows: groups A and B received sterile saline, group C received the empty pVAX1 vector, group D received pVAX1‑HA2‑fimA, and group E received pVAX1‑HA2‑fimA‑MyD88. All plasmid-treated groups (C–E) received 100 µg plasmid DNA per rat per immunization, diluted in sterile water to the final administration volume.
Peripheral blood was collected at weeks 9, 13, and 17. Serum levels of anti‑FimA and anti‑HA2 IgA and IgG antibodies, as well as IL‑4 and IL‑21 cytokines, were quantified by ELISA (n = 3 biological replicates per group, each measured in technical triplicate; these animals were selected from the 7 rats per group for detailed immunological analysis).
At week 13, a subset of rats (n = 3 per group) was euthanized for cellular immune profiling. Spleens and mesenteric lymph nodes were harvested for flow cytometric analysis of B cells, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, and Th1/Th2 subsets. For flow cytometry, lymphocytes were gated by forward and side scatter to exclude debris and doublets, followed by marker-based gating to define major T- and B-cell subsets, Tfh cells, and Th1/Th2 populations. Detailed staining panels, gating strategy, and representative plots are provided in the Supplementary Materials. All procedures and assessments were conducted under blinded conditions.

## Quantification of Alveolar Bone Loss
To quantify alveolar bone resorption, we performed micro-CT scanning on maxillae collected at week 17 (Hiscan XM Micro-CT). The 3D-reconstructed images were used to measure the distance between the cementoenamel junction and the alveolar bone crest at six predetermined sites per maxillary second molar.

## General Safety Assessment of Experimental Animals
Body weight was recorded weekly throughout the study to monitor general health. At the endpoint, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected for histopathological examination to assess vaccine safety.

## Bioinformatics Analysis and Validation
Splenic tissue samples were collected from 13-week-old rats in Group B (periodontitis alone) and Group E (periodontitis + adjuvanted vaccine), with 4 rats per group. Total RNA was extracted from spleen tissues, and its concentration, purity, and integrity were assessed. Eukaryotic mRNA was enriched, reverse-transcribed to construct cDNA libraries, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150 mode).
After quality control and filtering of raw reads, gene-level count matrices were generated. These data were analyzed using the edgeR package in R 4.0.5 for normalization and differential expression analysis. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Genes with FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Volcano plots of DEGs were generated using the ggplot2 package. Functional enrichment of DEGs was analyzed with the clusterProfiler package for GO, BP, CC, and MF categories. GSEA was performed based on all expressed genes to identify pathways significantly enriched by the DEGs (Fig. 25). Pathways with |NES| > 1 and nominal P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.
Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database. The networks were visualized and analyzed in Cytoscape 3.8.0 to identify putative hub genes. Key DEGs between the pVAX1-HA2-fimA and pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 groups were further validated by RT-qPCR, with four technical replicates for each sample.

## Statistics
All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, Version 29.0). For each dataset, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variance was evaluated with Levene’s test. Data with normal distribution and homogeneous variance are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD); multiple groups were compared using one-way ANOVA, and two-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-test. For data with non-normal distribution or unequal variance, results are presented as median and interquartile range (median, IQR); multiple groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and two-group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test. For multiple comparisons following ANOVA, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied.
Experimental timeline.Periodontitis model established at 5–9 weeks; immunizations at 9, 10, 12 weeks; blood collection and analyses at 9, 13, 17 weeks.
Although seven animals were allocated to each experimental group, a subset of three biological replicates per group (n = 3) was randomly selected for immunological assays (ELISA, flow cytometry, cytokine measurements) and micro-CT analysis. This approach was adopted to ensure consistency in sample processing and to minimize batch effects. It also allowed sufficient tissue availability for parallel analyses, including histology and transcriptomic profiling. The animals selected for these analyses were chosen using a random number table by investigators blinded to group allocation. The remaining samples were preserved for confirmatory experiments, histopathological evaluation, and potential future validation studies. We acknowledge that the relatively small number of biological replicates used for certain assays may limit statistical power and the detection of subtle effects. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes will be required to further validate these findings.

## Results

## Construction and Identification of Periodontitis DNA Vaccines
The PCR amplicons of the periodontitis DNA vaccines were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized using a gel imaging system. For pVAX1-HA2-fimA, target bands were observed at 405 bp (HA2) and 1164 bp (fimA). In pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88, an additional band corresponding to MyD88 (888 bp) was detected alongside HA2 and fimA, consistent with expected sizes.
Both constructs were sequenced by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sequence analysis confirmed that pVAX1-HA2-fimA and pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 were 5236 bp and 6217 bp in length, respectively. BLAST alignment against NCBI databases showed 100% sequence identity for HA2, fimA, and MyD88, with no mutations or deletions detected. Successful construction and sequence verification of recombinant plasmids are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.
These results confirm the successful construction of pVAX1-HA2-fimA vaccine and its MyD88-adjuvanted derivative. Colony PCR and sequencing validated the correct molecular sizes and nucleotide sequences of all target genes, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent functional experiments.

## In Vitro Study on Periodontitis DNA Vaccine pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88​
Twenty-four hours after transfecting 293T cells with pVAX1-HA2-fimA or pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88, total RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed, and subjected to PCR amplification using gene-specific primers. Agarose gel electrophoresis demonstrated successful transcription of all target genes in both groups (Fig.  A).
Protein expression was further confirmed by Western blotting. The results showed robust expression of HA2-6×His (15 kDa), FimA-3×FLAG (45 kDa), and MyD88-HA (33 kDa), consistent with their predicted molecular masses (Fig.  B).
To assess cytotoxicity, a CCK-8 assay was performed at 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection. Neither pVAX1-HA2-fimA nor pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 significantly affected 293T cell proliferation compared with the empty vector control (all P > 0.05) ( Supplementary Figure S2), indicating an excellent in vitro safety profile.
Together, these results indicate that both recombinant plasmids are efficiently transcribed and translated in mammalian cells without inducing cytotoxicity. This confirms their suitability for subsequent in vivo immunogenicity studies.

 

## Observation of in vivo immune efficacy of periodontitis DNA vaccine pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 in SD rats

## Analysis of Specific SIgA Antibody Levels in the Saliva of Experimental Rats​
Analysis of salivary SIgA revealed that the 100 µg dose of pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 induced the strongest mucosal immune response. This group produced markedly elevated levels of anti-HA2 (peak at week 4) and anti-FimA (plateau at week 5) antibodies than all other groups throughout the study (P < 0.05) (Fig.  A and B). Consequently, 100 µg was selected as the optimal dose, with the week 4–5 period serving as the peak response window for follow-up investigations.

 

## Establishment of Periodontitis Model in SD Rats
A rat periodontitis model was established by placing 3 − 0 silk ligatures around the maxillary second molars, followed by dynamic monitoring of periodontal status. At 4 weeks post-ligation, rats in the periodontitis group exhibited typical clinical manifestations, including gingival erythema and edema, bleeding on probing, increased tooth mobility, and the formation of periodontal pockets. By 8 weeks, the gingival tissues showed persistent bleeding upon gentle probing, accompanied by progressively increased tooth mobility (Fig.  A).
At week 4, one rat each from the blank control group and the periodontitis group was randomly selected and euthanized via lethal isoflurane anesthesia for specimen collection. Histopathological examination after decalcification, paraffin embedding, and staining revealed pronounced pathological changes in the periodontitis group compared with the blank controls. Specifically, junctional epithelial hyperplasia with apical migration was observed, resulting in detachment of the junctional epithelium from the tooth surface and the formation of deep periodontal pockets, accompanied by marked inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig.  B).
Furthermore, three-dimensional reconstruction performed using the Hiscan Analyzer system demonstrated substantial alveolar bone resorption in the maxillary second molar region of the periodontitis group compared with the blank control group (Fig.  C).
These findings indicate the successful establishment of the rat periodontitis model. The ligatures around the maxillary second molars were subsequently removed to facilitate further experimental procedures.

 

## Effect of Molecular Adjuvant MyD88 on Immune Responses Induced by Periodontitis DNA Vaccine
To assess the immunomodulatory role of MyD88, we longitudinally monitored vaccine-induced humoral responses. The pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 construct (Group E) elicited a markedly stronger and more persistent systemic antibody response compared with all non-adjuvanted groups. For IgG, Group E exhibited significantly higher anti-HA2 IgG titers than Groups A, B, C, and D (P < 0.05), and higher anti-FimA IgG titers than Groups A, B, and C (P < 0.05) at week 13, with this advantage maintained through week 17. For IgA, Group E generated substantially elevated anti-HA2 IgA levels relative to Groups A, B, and C (P < 0.05), and higher anti-FimA IgA levels than all groups (P < 0.05) at week 13. Although IgA levels declined by week 17 (P > 0.05), the pronounced early response likely contributed to enhanced mucosal protection (Fig.  ). These findings indicate that MyD88 significantly amplifies the magnitude and persistence of antigen-specific IgG and IgA responses.
To elucidate the cellular basis of this efficient humoral immunity, we analyzed key lymphocyte subsets at the peak response time point (week 13). In mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs), the major mucosal immune site (Fig.  ), Group E displayed markedly higher frequencies of CD4⁺ T cells (vs. Group B), CD8⁺ T cells (vs. Group A), B cells (vs. all groups), T follicular helper (Tfh) cells (vs. Groups A, B, C), and Th2 cells (vs. all groups) (all P < 0.05). In the spleen (Fig.  ), Group E also showed significantly increased frequencies of CD4⁺ T cells (vs. all groups), B cells (vs. Groups A, C, D), Tfh cells (vs. Groups A, B, C), and Th2 cells (vs. all groups) (all P < 0.05). Flow cytometric analysis consistently demonstrated that the MyD88-adjuvanted group exhibited the highest frequencies of B cells, Tfh cells, and Th2 cells in both MLNs and spleen.
In line with these cellular changes, serum levels of IL-4 and IL-21 were significantly upregulated in Group E at week 13 (vs. all groups, P < 0.05). These two cytokines act synergistically to promote B-cell activation, class-switch recombination, and Tfh differentiation, thereby forming the core molecular mechanism by which the MyD88-adjuvanted enhances the immunogenicity of the periodontitis DNA vaccine.

 

 

 

## Alveolar Bone Resorption of Maxillary Second Molars in Rats
Alveolar bone resorption was evaluated by three-dimensional reconstruction of maxillary samples using the Hiscan Analyzer software. The distance from the cementoenamel junction to the alveolar bone crest (CEJ–ABC) was measured at six sites (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiopalatal, midpalatal, distopalatal) of the maxillary second molars (Fig.  A).One-way ANOVA showed significant differences in CEJ–ABC distances among groups (F(4,10) = 137.1, P < 0.05). The blank control group exhibited minimal bone loss, with a mean CEJ–ABC distance of approximately 0.29 mm. In contrast, the periodontitis group showed marked bone resorption, with a mean CEJ–ABC distance of approximately 0.78 mm, which was significantly greater than that of the blank control. The periodontitis + pVAX1 empty vector group displayed a similar extent of bone loss to the periodontitis group.
Both vaccine-treated groups had significantly reduced bone loss compared with the periodontitis and empty vector groups. The pVAX1-HA2-fimA vaccine group showed an intermediate reduction, whereas the MyD88-adjuvanted vaccine group had a lower mean CEJ–ABC distance of approximately 0.56 mm. Thus, intranasal MyD88-adjuvanted vaccination reduced mean alveolar bone loss from about 0.78 mm in the periodontitis group to 0.56 mm (P < 0.05) (Fig.  B).

 

## General safety testing
Throughout the study, all animals remained healthy and displayed normal activity. Body weight increased steadily in all groups, and no significant differences were observed between the experimental groups (B, C, D, and E) and the blank control group (A) at any time point (one-way ANOVA, P > 0.05; Fig.  A). Gross examination at necropsy revealed no abnormalities in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys. Consistently, histopathological assessment using H&E staining showed no evidence of vaccine-related lesions such as inflammation, necrosis, or tissue degeneration in any group (Fig.  B).

 

In summary, these results indicate that the molecular adjuvant MyD88 enhances the immunogenicity of the periodontitis DNA vaccine by increasing the production of antigen-specific antibodies, promoting the activation of B cells and Tfh cells, and upregulating Th2-associated cytokines and T cell subsets. These findings indicate that MyD88 effectively augments both humoral and cellular immune responses induced by the vaccine in rats, while maintaining a favorable safety profile.

## Mechanistic Exploration of the Immunopotentiating Effect of Molecular Adjuvant MyD88 on Periodontitis Genetic Vaccine
To explore how the MyD88-adjuvanted enhances vaccine immunogenicity, we performed RNA-Seq transcriptomic profiling of spleen tissues from SD rats in the control (periodontitis + pVAX1-HA2-fimA) and experimental (periodontitis + pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88) groups. All RNA samples met the quality requirements for cDNA library construction and high-throughput sequencing (Supplementary Table S1). RNA-seq data are inherently correlative rather than causal. In this study, the observed transcriptional changes are interpreted as reflecting global immune and inflammatory states, rather than direct molecular targets of the vaccine.
High-throughput sequencing generated high-quality and highly reproducible datasets, with strong correlations both within and between groups (Spearman r > 0.9; Fig.  A). Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed clear separation between the control and experimental groups, indicating extensive transcriptional reprogramming in response to the MyD88-adjuvanted vaccine (Fig.  B). Consistently, volcano plot analysis revealed a large number of DEGs between groups, confirming robust transcriptomic differences (Fig.  C; detailed RNA-seq quality metrics and sequencing statistics are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).
Functional enrichment analyses demonstrated that these DEGs were predominantly involved in immune- and inflammation-related biological processes and pathways, including innate and adaptive immune responses, leukocyte migration, and regulation of the inflammatory response (Supplementary Figure S3). These pathway-level changes are more likely to reflect altered systemic immune and inflammatory states following vaccination. They are less likely to represent direct inhibition or activation of individual signaling pathways by the vaccine components. Protein–protein interaction network analysis further highlighted several highly connected genes associated with cell-cycle and inflammatory regulation, suggesting secondary effects linked to immune activation and subsequent resolution phases (Supplementary Figure S3).To validate the RNA-Seq results, we selected six representative DEGs (Cdc20, Ccna2, Kif11, Cdk1, Col4a1, Col1a2) for RT-qPCR analysis. All six genes showed significant downregulation in the experimental group compared with the control group, consistent with the transcriptomic trends (Fig.  D). Primer sequences and additional validation details are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

 

## Discussion
In this study, we successfully constructed a novel intranasal DNA vaccine, pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88, and evaluated its immunogenicity and regulatory effects in a rat model of periodontitis. This study yielded three core findings: First, the successful transcription and translation of HA2 and fimA genes in 293T cells confirmed the structural integrity and functional expression capabilities of both pVAX1-HA2-fimA and pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 vectors, laying a solid foundation for subsequent in vivo evaluation. Second, the pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 vaccine induced synergistic mucosal and systemic humoral immune responses, including the production of antigen-specific secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA) and immunoglobulin G (IgG), which were closely associated with a regulated CD4⁺ T cell phenotype. Third, transcriptomic analysis of spleen tissue revealed that vaccination downregulated the expression of genes related to the cell cycle and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, suggesting a remodeling of the systemic immune microenvironment conducive to inflammation resolution.
A core advantage of DNA vaccines lies in their ability to induce mucosal-specific antibody responses via the common mucosal immune system. As a key immune effector molecule, SIgA can block the adhesion, colonization, and proliferation of pathogenic bacteria [ , ]. To leverage this mechanism, this study employed an intranasal immunization strategy: the nasal mucosa is rich in nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT), which can efficiently capture antigens and initiate local immune responses. Consistent with previous reports [ , ], our results confirm that intranasal administration is a more suitable route for activating mucosal immune defenses relevant to periodontal infection.
Beyond eliciting mucosal and systemic antibody responses, the pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 vaccine promoted a coordinated humoral immune program centered on the Tfh-B cell axis. This axis is a critical determinant of antibody magnitude, quality, and durability [ , ]. Our findings indicate that the MyD88-adjuvanted effect preferentially enhances this Tfh-oriented axis rather than broadly amplifying effector T cell responses. This process is characterized by the regulated expression of IL-21 and IL-4, cytokines essential for B cell activation, maturation, and differentiation into antibody-secreting cells. Although Th2-dominated responses are traditionally associated with periodontal tissue destruction [ ], the immune profile observed here does not reflect excessive Th2 polarization; instead, the concurrent expression of IFN-γ and IL-4 suggests a balanced, non-pathological microenvironment. Notably, IL-4 is typically under-expressed in untreated periodontitis but increases following therapeutic intervention [ ], supporting the interpretation that its regulatory induction here represents a supportive signal for humoral immunity. Furthermore, this cytokine profile aligns with the function of NALT as an inductive site that preferentially supports Tfh and Th2-dependent antibody responses [ ]. These features suggest that the vaccine induces a controlled immune environment where Tfh–B cell interactions, aided by IL-21 and IL-4, facilitate robust antigen-specific SIgA and IgG production. Importantly, this occurs without driving inflammatory amplification.
To characterize the transcriptomic profile associated with vaccination, we performed RNA-seq analysis on spleen tissue. We identified a distinct downregulation of gene expression related to the cell cycle and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in the vaccination group. Specifically, six key differentially expressed genes were identified: Cdc20, Ccna2, Kif11, Cdk1, Col4a1, and Col1a2. While these expression patterns are consistent with previous reports regarding the involvement of these genes in inflammatory responses [ – ], the RNA-seq data reflect correlational associations rather than direct causal mechanisms. Therefore, a detailed interpretation of these findings, including the potential mechanistic links between MyD88 signaling, PI3K/AKT pathway regulation, and inflammation resolution, is provided in the Supplementary Discussion. These observations are consistent with previous reports on the roles of PI3K/AKT signaling, cell-cycle regulation, and extracellular matrix remodeling in inflammatory responses and tissue homeostasis [ – ].
In summary, the intranasally administered DNA vaccine pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 effectively enhances the host immune response at multiple levels: at the cellular level, by inducing a regulated, Th2-associated humoral response that supports antibody production at the humoral level, by reinforcing the Tfh-B cell axis to promote potent antigen-specific IgA and IgG generation; and at the molecular level, via transcriptomic evidence suggesting the involvement of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and related genes in inflammation regulation and tissue homeostasis. These results provide an integrative theoretical framework that combines the cellular, humoral, and molecular effects triggered by the vaccine. This framework lays a theoretical basis for further optimization and potential clinical translation.

## Limitations
First, the sample size in several of our experimental readouts, including the transcriptomic analysis, was relatively small (typically n = 3 per group). This increases the risk of type II error and may limit the robustness and generalizability of the present findings. In particular, the transcriptomic results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and they require validation in larger, independently powered cohorts using targeted assays (e.g., qPCR or flow cytometry). Second, although the ligation-induced periodontitis model in rats is a well-established model of inflammatory bone loss, it represents an acute, mechanically triggered and bacteria-accelerated disease process that differs from the slow, chronic and polymicrobial nature of human periodontitis. Thus, the efficacy of the pVAX1-HA2-fimA-MyD88 vaccine in chronic, long-standing inflammation and in the context of a complex oral microbiome remains to be determined, and future studies should evaluate this vaccine in more physiologically relevant models and larger animals. Third, this study did not include a classic adjuvant comparator, such as CpG oligodeoxynucleotides or alum, which prevents a direct head-to-head assessment of the adjuvant potency and safety profile of MyD88 relative to established adjuvants. Subsequent work should incorporate standardized adjuvant controls to clarify the added value and risk–benefit balance of MyD88-based constructs. Finally, our immunological readouts focused mainly on systemic and secondary lymphoid organs. Direct analyses of inductive or effector mucosal sites (e.g., NALT, gingival tissue) were not performed. Therefore, enhancement of mucosal immunity is inferred primarily from indirect markers such as salivary SIgA and splenic immune phenotypes, and future studies should include histological and immunohistochemical characterization of local mucosal tissues to delineate site-specific mechanisms of vaccine action.
Future research directions will also focus on comprehensive safety evaluations (such as biodistribution analysis and genomic integration risk assessment) and efficacy validation in large animal models (such as beagles or non-human primates). These steps are crucial for determining optimal dosing regimens and evaluating long-term immunogenicity in systems more closely resembling human disease.
