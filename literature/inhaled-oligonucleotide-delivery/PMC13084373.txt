## Abstract
The formation of a hardened fibrous membrane through in situ degradation of polycaprolactone (PCL) in soft tissues has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional rigid bone implants for chest wall reconstruction. However, strategies to enhance the mechanical integrity and biological performance of such fibrous constructs remain limited. While nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) is known to promote osteoblast proliferation and mineralization, its role in regulating fibroblast behavior remains unclear, particularly within a dynamically strained environment mimicking respiratory motion. In this study, we developed PCL scaffolds incorporating various concentrations of nHA using fused deposition modeling (FDM). The PCL/10 wt% nHA scaffold exhibited optimal mechanical properties and significantly enhanced fibroblast proliferation, adhesion, and extracellular matrix deposition in vitro. Notably, higher nHA content led to excessive Piezo1 activation, resulting in Ca2+ overload and increased fibroblast apoptosis. Under dynamic mechanical stimulation, the PCL/10 wt% nHA scaffold markedly promoted fibroblast functionality and tissue fibrosis, facilitating robust soft tissue hardening in vivo. Mechanistic investigations revealed that the Piezo1/TGF-β1 signaling axis plays a central role in mediating fibroblast responses to cyclic shear stress. These findings demonstrate that the PCL/10 wt% nHA scaffold effectively supports tissue-engineered structural reinforcement through fibroblast-driven fibrosis, presenting a biodegradable and mechanically adaptive approach with potential for future chest wall repair applications.
Keywords: PCL/nHA scaffold, Respiratory dynamic environment, Piezo1/Ca2+ signaling axis, Soft tissue fibrosis, Chest wall reconstruction

## Graphical abstract
In this study, PCL/nHA composite scaffolds were designed and fabricated using fused deposition modeling technology. These scaffolds not only exhibit excellent mechanical properties but also significantly promote the proliferation and fibrosis of fibroblasts. As a biodegradable implant, it supports the chest wall structure by stimulating the proliferation and fibrosis of soft tissues, providing a promising strategy for future chest wall reconstruction.

## Introduction
Chest wall defects, frequently resulting from trauma, extensive tumor resection, or severe infection, compromise the structural and mechanical integrity of the thoracic cavity that often leads to paradoxical respiration and respiratory failure [ , ]. The traditional clinical view holds that bony reconstructions typically require implants that offer adequate mechanical support and biocompatibility to prevent chest wall softening [ , ]. Titanium and its alloys are most common used in repairing chest wall defects in clinical practice, but the excessively mechanical strength of the material is prone to cause stress shielding effects [ , ]. Other implants, including bone cement, autologous ribs and bioengineered ribs have also been applied in clinical settings [ , ]. In order to achieve better anatomical and mechanical compatibility of the implant, we have developed three dimensional (3D)-printed polyetheretherketone (PEEK) implants to carry out chest wall reconstruction for more than 200 patients in clinical practice [ , ]. However, all of the above permanent serviceable implants may limit the development of thorax for adolescent patients. Moreover, the displacement and infection of implants are also common causes of surgical failure during the follow-up period [ ]. Therefore, in order to benefit the youth population and reduce complications from such surgeries, there is an urgent clinical need to fabricate novel implants.
Polycaprolactone (PCL) has been widely explored in bone tissue engineering due to its favorable mechanical strength, tunable degradation profile, and biocompatibility [ , , ]. Despite these advantages, the application of PCL in the repair of long-segment defects in load-bearing bones remains controversial [ , , , ]. A major concern centers on whether the regenerated bone tissue can achieve sufficient mechanical integrity to withstand physiological loads after PCL degradation. In contrast, the sternum and ribs, while critical for thoracic stability, are classified as non-load-bearing skeletal elements that primarily facilitate respiratory dynamics rather than weight support. This functional distinction creates a unique opportunity for the use of degradable materials in chest wall reconstruction, where structural reinforcement can be achieved without the need for high-load resistance. Building on this premise, we have previously demonstrated the feasibility of using PCL-based implants to repair chest wall defects [ ]. During implant degradation, PCL stimulates the formation of a dense fibrous tissue layer termed “tissue-derived fiberboard”. This newly formed, hardened soft tissue effectively supports thoracic integrity, offering a promising alternative to rigid, degradable bone grafts. However, to optimize clinical outcomes and ensure long-term structural stability, novel strategies are needed to enhance the degree and quality of soft tissue hardening following PCL degradation.
The incorporation of nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) into PCL matrices has been shown to improve hydrophilicity, surface roughness, and mechanical stability, thereby promoting osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and mineralization. These enhancements make PCL/nHA composites attractive candidates for bone regeneration [ , , , , ]. However, in the context of chest wall repair, the primary cellular responders are not osteoblasts but fibroblasts, which are the key effectors of connective tissue remodeling and fibrosis. [ , ]. Upon PCL implantation, fibroblasts are activated and differentiate into myofibroblasts, acquiring enhanced migratory, proliferative, and extracellular matrix (ECM)-secretory capabilities that drive tissue fibrosis and mechanical stiffening [ , , ]. However, it is unknown whether PCL/nHA can regulate fibroblast proliferation and fibrosis, and the regulatory mechanism is also unclear. Adding further complexity, the chest wall is subject to continuous, rhythmical mechanical deformation due to respiratory motion—a dynamic biomechanical environment that profoundly influences cell-scaffold interactions. Unlike static implant sites, the implant-tissue interface in the chest wall is exposed to cyclic shear and tensile forces with each breath. [ , ]. Although mechanical cues are recognized as critical regulators of tissue integration and fibrotic remodeling, few studies have directly investigated cellular responses under such physiologically relevant dynamic conditions [ , , ]. To address this gap, we constructed a biomimetic dynamic cell culture system to precisely simulate the alternating shear forces characteristic of respiration. Using this platform, we investigated how the PCL/nHA composite material modulates fibroblast behavior within this dynamic mechanical environment.
In this study, we fabricated PCL scaffolds containing 0, 10, 20, and 30 wt% nHA, respectively, to systematically investigate the relationship between biomechanical properties and fibroblast biological responses under dynamic fluid shear force to simulate respiration movement ( ). By evaluating scaffolds with different nHA loadings, we identifiied the optimal nHA composition that balanced mechanical integrity with enhanced bioactivity. Notably, we discovered that the 10 wt% nHA composite exhibits superior performance in promoting fibroblast proliferation, adhesion, and fibrotic differentiation under dynamic strain, which is mediated by the Piezo1/TGF-β1 mechanotransduction pathway. This work provides a promising strategy for chest wall reconstruction by enhancing implant–soft tissue integration, thereby advancing the clinical translation of biomaterial-based regenerative solutions.

## Experimental section
2.1.

## Materials and regents
Medical level PCL powders were purchased from Evonik Industries AG (Germany). Medical level Nano-Hydroxyapatite powders were purchased from Emperor Nano Co. (China). Cell culture medium, Penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), 0.25% trypsin solution, and Serum-free cell freezing medium were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. (China). CCK-8 assay kit, Fluo-4AM, Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit, Mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit and ROS Assay Kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Bovine Primary antibodies targeting Piezo1, ITGB1, and Fibronectin were acquired from Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. (USA). TGF-β1, α-SMA, ERK1/2, p-ERK and YAP1 were acquired from Proteintech Group, Inc. (China). Fluorescent secondary antibody was purchased from Xi'an Zhuangzhi Biotechnology Co. (China). The agonists (Yoda1) and inhibitors (GsMTx4) of PIEZO1 and EIPA were purchased from MedChemExpress (China).

2.2.

## Preparation of PCL and PCL/nHA samples
Weighed nHA accurately was dispersed in hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) under stirring to achieve a homogeneous suspension. Subsequently, a precisely measured quantity of PCL was added to the mixture, which was then heated to 60 °C under continuous stirring until complete dissolution was attained. The resulting homogeneous solution was cast onto a glass Petri dish, evaporated in a fume hood to ensure complete solvent removal, and subsequently desiccated in a drying oven to yield a solid PCL/nHA composite mixture. This solid mixture was subsequently cut into small pellets, loaded into a printing syringe, and processed via FDM to fabricate composite discs with nHA mass fractions of 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30%. Printing parameters were set as follows: nozzle temperature, 90 °C; print speed, 4 mm/s; layer height, 0.2 mm; nozzle diameter, 0.4 mm; and pneumatic extrusion pressure, 5000 Pa.

2.3.

## Characterization of PCL and PCL/nHA samples
The microstructure of the PCL sample surface was observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, Quattro S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The elemental composition of different PCL samples was analyzed using an X-ray Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS, Ultim Max, Oxford Instruments, UK). The surface morphology parameters of the samples with varying nHA contents were measured using an atomic force microscope (AFM, Cypher ES, USA) and the arithmetic mean height (Sa) was measured using Gwyddion software (Oxford Instruments, UK). The hydrophilicity of the sample surface was evaluated using an optical contact angle meter (OCA20, Data Physics Corporation, Germany). For each type of nanoscale sample, three parallel samples were prepared, and replicate measurements were conducted in different regions of each sample to ensure data reliability and reproducibility. For tensile test and three-point bending test, the PCL and PCL-nHA specimens were designed to have a uniform size of approximately 50 mm in length, 10 mm in width, and 0.45 mm in thickness. To evaluate the flexural fatigue properties of the 3D-printed rib implants, rib implants with a length of 6 cm and a diameter of 9 mm were fabricated. A periodic vertical displacement of 2 mm at a loading speed of 40 mm/min was applied to the implants for 200 times in accordance with our previously established protocol. The evolution of the load-displacement relationships of the PCL and PCL-nHA rib implants was recorded.

2.4.

## Cell culture
The murine fibroblast cell line L929 and Human Skin Fibroblasts (HSFs) were obtained from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Shanghai Institute of Life Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The PCL samples were positioned at the bottom of 6-well/48-well plates with sterile forceps and sterilized using ethylene oxide. Subsequently, L929 cells and HSFs were cultured directly on the sample surfaces in low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO2, with complete medium replacement performed every 48 h. The human respiratory rate typically ranges from 12 to 20 breaths per minute. To replicate this dynamic physiological condition in vitro, cells cultured under dynamic conditions were maintained on an orbital shaker operating at 40 rpm. According to relevant studies, the fluid shear stress at the bottom of the well is estimated as:
τ=Rρη(2πf)3where R is the orbital radius (11 mm in our system), ρ is the density of the culture medium (1.0 g/mL), η is the medium viscosity (taken as 0.0075 P), and f is the frequency of rotation (rps). At 40 rpm, the calculated shear stress value is approximately 0.82 dyn/cm2.

2.5.

## Assessment of cell proliferation capacity
We used the CCK-8 and live/dead staining assays to evaluate the proliferation capacity of L929 cells on PCL samples. L929 cells were seeded at a density of 6 × 103 cells per well onto 48-well plates pre-coated with PCL samples. Each group had 5 parallel samples and were cultured for 1 to 3 days. At each time point, CCK-8 working solution was added and incubated in a cell culture incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) for 1 h. Absorbance was then measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader. The live/dead staining (C2015S, Beyotime, China) was carried out after 24 h of cell culture. A suitable volume of Calcein AM/PI working solution was added to each sample and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The samples were then transferred to a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan) for fluorescence imaging. Cell proliferation on PCL samples under different culture conditions was assessed by quantifying the average fluorescence intensity of green fluorescence using ImageJ software.

2.6.

## Detection of cell apoptosis
L929 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates pre-coated with PCL samples. Cell apoptosis on different PCL samples was analyzed using a cell apoptosis detection kit (C1062S, Beyotime, China). After 24 h of cell culture, the Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide staining solutions were added and gently mixed. After incubated for 20 min. The fluorescence intensity of each cell population was measured using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, USA), and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software to generate flow cytometry analysis charts. Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed using a ROS detection kit (S0033S, Beyotime, China). Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using the JC-1 kit (C2006, Beyotime, China). The decline in mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed by calculating the ratio of green to red fluorescence intensity.

2.7.

## Assessment of calcium influx
Fluo-4 AM is a widely used fluorescent probe for labeling intracellular calcium ions. After washing the L929 cells and HSFs on the sample three times with PBS, the cells were incubated with 1 × Fluo-4 AM working solution for 30 min. Subsequently, the intracellular calcium ion (Ca2+) influx was evaluated with the confocal laser scanning microscope and flow cytometry.

2.8.

## Observation of cell morphology
For electron microscopy observation and to maintain cell morphology, the cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, gradient dehydration was performed with ethanol. Hexamethyl diphenylamine was dropped onto the sample surface and air-dried at room temperature for 24 h. After sputter-coating the samples with gold, the cell morphology under different culture conditions was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM).
Another batch of L929 cells was seeded on PCL samples and cultured under dynamic and static conditions for 48 h, respectively. After culture, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature and rinsed with PBS. To enhance membrane permeability, the samples were treated with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. Subsequently, they were incubated with iFluor™555 staining solution supplemented with 1% BSA at room temperature in the dark, followed by DAPI staining and observed and photographed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. The ratio of average fluorescence intensity of blue to green fluorescence was measured using ImageJ software to quantify the nucleocytoplasmic ratio, so as to evaluate the cell spreading area on PCL samples.

2.9.

## Wound healing and migration assay
For the wound healing and migration assay, PCL samples were placed into 24-well plates, followed by attaching culture inserts to the surface of each PCL sample. Subsequently, L929 cells were seeded into each well. After incubation for 24 h, the culture inserts were carefully removed to create a 500-μm cell-free scratch zone in the central region. The samples were then washed and further incubated in low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum for 24 or 48 h. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, and nuclear staining with DAPI, cell migration into the scratch area was visualized and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Quantitative analysis of cell migration was performed by counting the cells within the scratch zone using ImageJ software. Select an area within the scratch wound with a width of 500 μm on the interface for cell counting. Each group is repeated three times. Then, evaluate the wound healing and migration behavior based on the increase in the number of cells within the wound width over time.

2.10.

## Western blotting assay
The expression levels of target proteins were analyzed by Western blotting. L929 cells were seeded onto PCL samples at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. For protein extraction, cells were lysed by incubation with RIPA cell lysis bufferfor 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with absorbance measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader. Western blotting was performed following established protocols.

2.11.

## RNA-sequencing
Following a 24-h incubation of L929 cells on PCL samples, the cells were gently washed with 1 mL of Sparkzol reagent and collected. The resulting cell suspension was transferred to sterile, nuclease-free EP tubes and stored at −80 °C until further processing. Subsequently, the samples were sent to Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for whole transcriptome sequencing analysis. Differentially expressed genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis to elucidate the potential biological functions and signaling pathways involved.

2.12.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
The concentration of secreted cytokines in the cell culture supernatant was quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Supernatants were collected from L929 cells after 24 h of culture. A TGF-β1 ELISA kit (Westang Ltd., Shanghai, China) was employed to measure cytokine levels in the supernatants. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated from known concentrations.

2.13.

## Immunofluorescence staining and imaging
L929 cells were seeded onto PCL samples at a density of 6 × 103 cells per well. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocking with PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature. Then, the diluted primary antibodies (Piezo1, ITGB1, Yap1 TGF-β1) according to the manufacturer's instructions were applied to the sample and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Afterwards, add the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody, incubate the sample in the dark at room temperature for 1 h, and then perform nuclear staining with DAPI. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope and processed with Olympus Fluoview software Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

2.14.

## Piezo1 knockdown experiment
L929 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. When the cell confluence reached approximately 60% - 70%, diluted Piezo1 siRNA and transfection reagent (Lipofectamine, Thermo Fisher, USA) were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form the transfection complex. The complex was then added dropwise to the culture medium to achieve a final siRNA concentration of 50 nM. After 48 h of transfection, the cells were harvested for Western blotting.

2.15.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay
Collect the cultured cells, perform lysis, centrifugation and protein quantification to obtain the protein concentration. For immunoprecipitation, approximately 500–1000 μg of total protein lysate was incubated with 1–2 μg of the specific antibody targeting Piezo1/ITGB1 or an isotype-matched IgG control overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Subsequently, 30 μL of pre-washed Protein A/G Magnetic Beads were added to the mixture and incubated for an additional 2–4 h at 4 °C. The beads were collected using a magnetic rack and washed five times with ice-cold IP lysis buffer to remove non-specific binding. The bound protein complexes were eluted by boiling in 2 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 10 min at 98 °C. The immunoprecipitates and 5% of the total cell lysate (Input) were subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting analysis using antibodies against Piezo1and ITGB1.

2.16.

## In vivo experiments
In vivo experiments were conducted using SD rats and BALB/c mice, which were randomly divided into four experimental groups (five rats per group, three mice per group). All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics and Welfare Committee of the Experimental Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University (approval number: 20241363). Six-week-old rats and mice were acclimated for 7 days before surgery and then anesthetized using an inhalation anesthesia system. Subsequently, PCL or 10% (mass fraction) nHA implants were respectively implanted subcutaneously in the chest (dynamic environment) and the cranium (static environment) of each mouse. On the 7th and 14th days after surgery, the mice were euthanized, and the skin tissues around the implantation sites were collected. On the 10th and 20th days after surgery, the rats were euthanized, and the soft tissues of the chest wall and the soft tissues around the cranium were collected. The collected tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemical analysis.
To evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility and safety of the implanted materials, histopathological examinations and serum biochemical analyses were performed on the experimental mice. After euthanasia, the major organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Tissue sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE). Histological changes such as inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, cellular degeneration, and structural damage were evaluated under an optical microscope. Blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital vein for complete blood cell count, liver and kidney function tests, and myocardial enzyme spectrum analysis to assess the potential hematotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity of the implanted materials.

2.17.

## Statistical analysis
All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent samples t-test, multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while multiple time points and multiple groups comparisons were analyzed by two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001). Statistical graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 10.3 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).

## Results and discussion
3.1.

## Fabrication and characterization of PCL/nHA composite materials
The PCL samples containing varying amounts of nHA were fabricated using FDM and were designated as PCL, 10 wt% nHA, 20 wt% nHA, and 30 wt% nHA, respectively. The surface morphology of PCL based samples with different nHA contents was clearly characterized by SEM ( A, ). It was observed that the number of nHA particles exposed on the sample surface increased with the increasing nHA loading. Surface roughness (Sa) differences among the PCL sample groups were clearly revealed by AFM ( B and C). The Sa values of PCL, 10 wt% nHA and 20 wt% nHA showed no statistically differences among the other groups except for a significant increase in the 30 wt% nHA group (P < 0.05). The surface elemental composition of different PCL samples was systematically analyzed using EDS ( D– ). The calcium content in the 10 wt% nHA, 20 wt% nHA, and 30 wt% nHA samples was quantified as 3.71 ± 0.12%, 5.21 ± 0.99%, and 7.80 ± 0.13%, respectively ( E). As shown in , quantitative analysis of key elements including C, O, Ca, and P was performed for PCL and nHA-doped samples, and the corresponding elemental distribution profiles were plotted. The calcium elemental distribution across the scaffold cross-section confirms the uniform internal dispersion of nHA ( ). Water contact angle measurements ( F and G) showed the 10 wt% nHA group had the lowest angle (72.00 ± 1.34°), significantly lower than PCL and higher nHA groups (P < 0.01), indicating optimal hydrophilicity. Young's modulus increased progressively with nHA content (P < 0.05), reflecting enhanced interfacial hardness ( H and I).
 Under cyclic bending, both PCL and PCL/nHA rib implants exhibited rapid stabilization after initial hysteresis, with reloading/unloading paths converging by 150 cycles ( J– ). These findings align with the deformation responses of 3D-printed lattice PCL scaffolds and rib implants under periodic compression or bending, as reported in our previous study [ ]. Further tensile testing ( K) revealed typical semi-crystalline polymer behavior. PCL/nHA composites showed significantly improved mechanical properties: tensile modulus increased from 360.8 ± 130.0 MPa (PCL) to 627.7 ± 104.3 MPa (PCL/nHA), and tensile strength from 20.4 ± 1.7 MPa to 39.3 ± 5.1 MPa. Similar trends were observed in three-point bending ( L): flexural modulus rose from 720.8 ± 4.7 MPa to 1694.6 ± 94.5 MPa, and flexural strength from 14.4 ± 1.6 MPa to 34.5 ± 6.9 MPa. EDS spectra confirmed the absence of Ca and P in pure PCL, while their presence and increasing intensity with nHA loading validated successful composite formation. The 3D reconstruction reveals that the printed discs possess a structurally dense morphology. Due to the specific printing parameters employed, the extruded filaments were fused and densely packed, resulting in a continuous material phase with negligible internal porosity or interconnecting pore networks. Collectively, these results demonstrate that nHA incorporation enhances surface roughness, hydrophilicity, and mechanical performance in a dose-dependent manner, while maintaining uniform dispersion and structural stability under dynamic loading.

3.2.

## 10 wt% PCL/nHA enhances cell proliferation and reduces apoptosis via calcium ion regulation
To investigate cellular responses at the interface of PCL materials with varying nHA concentrations, the proliferation behaviors of mouse fibroblast L929 cells and Human Skin Fibroblasts (HSFs) were first evaluated. Live/dead cell staining ( A–D) revealed that the 10 wt% nHA group exhibited the highest cell viability. Consistently, the CCK-8 assay confirmed that the 10 wt% nHA group demonstrated the most favorable cell proliferation capacity ( E and F). Wang et al. found that integrating nHA into PCL matrices can improve cell adhesion through increased hydrophilicity and enhanced mechanical properties [ , , ]. Moreover, apoptosis analysis was also conducted on L929 cells cultured on PCL samples with different nHA contents ( G), revealing that the 10 wt% nHA group had the lowest apoptosis rate (P < 0.01) ( H). To systematically investigate the regulatory effects of calcium ions on the proliferation of L929 cells, we cultured the cells in media supplemented with varying concentrations of calcium ions for 24 h and assessed the apoptotic response using standardized detection methods ( I). The experimental results demonstrated that as the extracellular calcium ion concentration increased, the level of cell apoptosis initially decreased and subsequently increased ( J).
 To rule out nHA particle-specific cytotoxicity as the cause of apoptosis, L929 cells were cultured under three conditions: standard DMEM, CaCl2-supplemented DMEM, and nHA-supplemented DMEM (each adjusted to provide equivalent extracellular calcium ion concentrations). CCK-8 assays revealed that L929 cells in the control group exhibited significantly higher proliferation, whereas proliferation was significantly suppressed in both the CaCl2 and nHA groups ( ). Similarly, live/dead staining showed significantly reduced cell viability in both the CaCl2 and nHA groups compared to the control (p < 0.01), with no statistically significant difference observed between these two groups (p > 0.05) ( K– ). These findings indicate that 10 wt% nHA group significantly promoted L929 cells and HSFs proliferation while minimizing apoptosis, attributed to improved biocompatibility and optimal calcium ion release. In contrast, higher nHA content induced calcium overload, leading to increased apoptosis, indicating a calcium-dependent regulatory mechanism rather than nHA particle toxicity.

3.3.

## Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ signaling in PCL/nHA-driven cell behaviors
Next, we investigated the calcium ion influx in L929 cells and HSFs on PCL/nHA samples. Fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry showed that Ca2+ influx in L929 cells and HSFs was concentration-dependent on nHA, with the 30 wt% nHA group exhibiting the highest influx ( A–D, A, B). To further elucidate the impact of PCL and its composite material PCL/nHA on the oxidative stress status of L929 cells, we performed fluorescence staining to quantify intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels ( E). Intracellular ROS levels were lowest in the 10 wt% nHA group but markedly elevated in the 30 wt% group ( E– ), paralleling a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), most pronounced in the 30 wt% nHA group ( F– ).
 Previous research has demonstrated that nanoscale surface roughness enhances cellular mechanotransduction by increasing the available surface area for cell-substrate interactions [ ]. Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, exhibits high sensitivity to mechanical cues derived from the extracellular matrix (ECM) and substrate stiffness. Upon activation, Piezo1 rapidly transitions to an open conformation, allowing calcium ions influx into the cell and activating Integrin β1(ITGB1) [ , , , , ], promotes cell proliferation. However, excessive activation of Piezo1 results in a massive influx of calcium ions, leading to intracellular calcium overload. This condition subsequently promotes excessive production of mitochondrial ROS, depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential, and the release of pro-apoptotic factors, ultimately leading to irreversible cellular damage involving Calpain 1, cytochrome c release and caspase-3 activation [ , , , ]. This process is further characterized by the suppression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins and the activation of pro-apoptotic BCL-2 family members BAX and BAK, ultimately inducing programmed cell death [ , , ].
Therefore, we assessed the protein expression levels of L929 cells and HSFs cultured on PCL/nHA. As illustrated in G–I, Western blot analysis demonstrated that the 30 wt% nHA sample strongly upregulated Piezo1 expression, correlating with excessive Ca2+ entry. This overactivation led to calcium overload, triggering downstream pro-apoptotic events: Calpain1, Bax, and Caspase-3 expression peaked, while Bcl-2 expression was highest in the 10 wt% nHA group. Beyond mechanical signaling, calcium overload and subsequent caspase-3 activation result from the synergy between Piezo1-mediated influx and chemical dissolution of Ca2+ from the PCL/nHA scaffolds. CO-IP confirmed no direct interaction between Piezo1 and ITGB1 ( ), suggesting Piezo1 functions independently of integrin β1 in this context. Despite distinct sensing modalities, Piezo1 and Integrin β1 signals converge via Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx, which activates Calpain to cleave Talin1 into active form, triggering ITGB1 activation and clustering for ECM deposition. Excessive ECM stiffening then provides an outside-in signal that amplifies Piezo1's mechanosensory function. Thus, Ca2+-dependent cytoskeletal signaling serves as the intracellular nexus integrating fluid shear stress and matrix cues [ , ]. In contrast, the 10 wt% nHA condition promoted optimal Ca2+ influx, activating pro-proliferative pathways: Piezo1 activation facilitated Yap1 nuclear translocation and enhanced TGF-β1 signaling via Smad2/3 phosphorylation, both of which drive cell proliferation. Thus, while moderate nHA (10 wt%) promotes proliferation through balanced Piezo1/Ca2+/Yap and TGF-β1 signaling, excessive nHA (30 wt%) induces apoptosis via calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction ( J). These results establish 10 wt% nHA as the optimal formulation for tissue engineering applications and were selected for subsequent studies.

3.4.

## Dynamic shear forces enhance fibroblast function on PCL/10 wt% nHA
The human chest wall undergoes continuous respiratory movements [ , ], subjecting the soft tissue cells to persistent alternating shear forces in interaction with implanted materials( A). However, the effects of alternating shear forces induced by the implants within respiratory motion on fibroblast behavior remain largely unclear. To better mimic the continuous respiratory movements, we developed a dynamic cell culture model ( ). This model enables real-time investigation of fibroblast behavior under conditions simulating in vivo mechanical stimuli.
 Under dynamic culture, we evaluated the proliferation, migration, and adhesion behavior of L929 cells and HSFs on PCL and PCL/10 wt% nHA samples. Both cells exhibited significantly enhanced viability compared to static controls, with the 10 wt% PCL/nHA group showing the most pronounced improvement, as confirmed by live-dead staining ( B–E). CCK-8 assays further demonstrated that L929 cells on PCL/10 wt% nHA displayed the highest proliferation capacity under dynamic conditions ( F). In scratch assay analysis, the number of migrating L929 cells within a 500 μm cell-free zone was greatest in the dynamic PCL/10 wt% nHA group, indicating superior migratory potential ( G and H). To evaluate the adhesion ability of L929 cells on PCL and PCL/10 wt% nHA samples under different culture conditions, the nuclear to cytoplasmic ratio was analyzed using fluorescent labeling ( I). The results showed that compared with static conditions, the nuclear cytoplasmic ratio of L929 cells was significantly reduced under dynamic culture conditions ( ). Morphologically, L929 cells developed flatter pseudopodia under dynamic culture, facilitating tighter attachment to the material surface ( J). Quantitative results show that, compared with static culture conditions, L929 cells exhibit significantly larger cell spreading area under dynamic culture conditions ( ).
Collectively, these results demonstrate that alternating shear forces simulate respiratory mechanics to promote fibroblast proliferation, migration, and adhesion, with PCL/10 wt% nHA exhibiting optimal bioactivity under dynamic conditions. However, the underlying mechanism still requires further investigation.

3.5.

## Piezo1/Ca2+ activates fibrotic responses under dynamic shear stress
To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying fibroblast activation on PCL/10 wt% nHA under physiological mechanical cues, we performed high-throughput transcriptome sequencing of L929 cells cultured under dynamic versus static conditions. Differential gene expression analysis revealed significant transcriptional reprogramming induced by dynamic fluid shear force ( A, ). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment highlighted key biological processes including extracellular matrix remodeling, cell adhesion molecule binding, and positive regulation of cation channel activity ( B). KEGG pathway analysis identified modulation of critical signaling pathways involved in cell proliferation and adhesion, notably the cAMP and Cell Adhesion Molecules pathways ( C), with both GO and KEGG analyses indicating perturbations in the calcium signaling pathway. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) independently confirmed these findings ( ).
 Given Ca2+ as a central second messenger in mechanotransduction [ ], we assessed intracellular Ca2+ influx using Fluo-4 AM staining. Dynamic culture significantly enhanced Ca2+ influx in L929 cells ( D– ), a finding further validated in human skin fibroblasts (HSFs) ( E– ), and quantitatively supported by flow cytometry ( ). Notably, real-time calcium imaging revealed characteristic “calcium flickers” in L929 cells on PCL/10 wt% nHA under shear stress: fluorescence signals emerged within 30 s of Fluo-4 AM loading and intensified by 150 s ( F), indicative of Piezo1 channel activation. Concomitantly, dynamic culture upregulated key components of the cAMP pathway. PKA expression was enhanced, while downstream effectors CREB and ERK1/2 were significantly activated ( G). This supports a model in which mechanical stimulation activates Piezo1, triggering Ca2+ influx and initiating cAMP signaling to drive cell proliferation and migration. What's more, Western blot analysis also demonstrated that Piezo1/ITGB1/YAP/TGF-β1 activation occurred earlier and more robustly in the dynamic group compared to the static group, exhibiting initial upregulation followed by gradual decline and potentially reflecting channel desensitization or recovery ( H). Immunofluorescence also confirmed strong Piezo1/ITGB1/YAP/TGF-β1 signals in the PCL/10 wt% nHA-dynamic group, significantly exceeding those in static cultures ( I and J), and YAP1—a key mechanotransduction effector was markedly upregulated and showed increased nuclear localization in dynamic cultures. ELISA also revealed elevated TGF-β1 secretion ( ). Functionally, dynamic stimulation promoted Fibronectin1 (FN1) expression and α-SMA, indicating that dynamic culture on PCL/10 wt% nHA promotes a pro-fibrotic phenotype characterized by enhanced matrix remodeling, cell-matrix adhesion, and contractile machinery assembly.
These results illustrates that dynamic shear stress on PCL/10 wt% nHA activates Piezo1/ITGB1 signaling, triggering Ca2+ influx and cAMP pathway activation, which together promote TGF-β1 secretion and YAP nuclear translocation, leading to enhanced fibrotic responses in fibroblasts.

3.6.

## Piezo1/TGF-β1 drives fibroblast activation signaling under dynamic stimulation
To validate that mechanical stimulation transduces biomechanical signals into L929 cells through the Piezo1 channel, cells cultured on PCL/10 wt% nHA under dynamic conditions were treated with Yoda1 (a specific Piezo1 activator) or GsMTx4 (a mechanosensitive cation channel inhibitor). Fluo-4AM fluorescence staining showed that Yoda1 significantly increased intracellular Ca2+ influx, while GsMTx4 markedly reduced it ( A and B), a finding confirmed by flow cytometry ( C and D). ELISA revealed that Yoda1 treatment strongly enhanced TGF-β1 secretion ( E). Western blotting analysis further demonstrated that Yoda1 activated Piezo1, ITGB1 and TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway, an effect further amplified by alternating shear stress ( ). To examine Piezo1's essential role in mechanosensing and fibrotic transdifferentiation in L929 cells and HSFs, we performed siRNA-mediated Piezo1 knockdown. Efficient depletion of Piezo1 was confirmed by Western blotting. Critically, Piezo1 deficiency severely impaired cellular responsiveness to mechanical stimulation: mechanically induced activation of ITGB1, YAP1, and the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 axis was abolished. Moreover, the upregulation of α-SMA, COL1A1, and FN1 was significantly suppressed in Piezo1-knockdown cells ( F and G).
 Collectively, Piezo1 acts as a central mechanotransducer that coordinates with ITGB1 to activate the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis, forming a critical regulatory network that links biomechanical cues to cellular responses in fibroblasts ( H). Upon stimulation by external mechanical forces, Piezo1 undergoes a conformational change and triggers Ca2+ influx [ , ], which activates the cAMP signaling pathway and its downstream effectors, including PKA and EPAC, thereby promoting essential cellular processes such as proliferation and migration [ ]. Concurrently, ITGB1, which physically links the extracellular matrix to the intracellular cytoskeleton, exhibits a coordinated expression pattern with Piezo1 [ ]. Together, Piezo1/ITGB1 cooperatively regulate the expression of TGF-β1 and nuclear translocation of YAP1 [ ], which ultimately actives Fibronectin1 [ ] and α-SMA, thereby modulating adhesion, cytoskeletal remodeling, and fibroblast activation in response to mechanical cues. This signaling network drives proliferative and fibrotic responses essential for soft tissue repair and implant integration.

3.7.

## Dynamic mechanical force enhance fibrotic integration of soft tissues on PCL/10 wt% nHA implants In vivo
To evaluate soft tissue response to PCL/10 wt% nHA implants under static versus dynamic mechanical environments, a rat chest wall defect model was established, with implants placed in either the static calvarial subcutaneous space or the dynamically loaded chest wall region ( A). The study included four experimental groups: PCL-cranial, PCL-chest wall, PCL/10 wt% nHA-cranial, and PCL/10 wt% nHA-chest wall. Soft tissues in direct contact with the implants were harvested on days 10 and 20 post-implantation for histological analysis. B shows the process of rib implantation and the Micro-CT result after the operation. H&E and Masson staining revealed more organized cellular architecture and significantly higher collagen deposition in the chest wall group, particularly in the PCL/10 wt% nHA-chest wall group at both day 10 and day 20 ( C and D). Picrosirius Red polarization further confirmed a higher proportion of mature Type I collagen in dynamic sites, with a time-dependent increase in the Type I/III collagen ratio, indicating progressive matrix maturation. Immunohistochemistry showed marked upregulation of Piezo1, ITGB1, and TGF-β1 in tissues surrounding chest wall implants, most prominently in the PCL/10 wt% nHA-chest wall group, correlating with elevated COL1A1 expression, thus linking mechanical activation to fibrotic remodeling.
 To evaluate the mechanical properties of the fibrous plate formed after chest wall defect repair, we conducted cyclic compression tests. The results demonstrated that fibrotic tissues formed around PCL/10 wt% nHA implants exhibited superior fatigue resistance and achieved a stable mechanical state within 200 cycles ( E), while displaying significantly higher maximum bending force than the PCL group ( F), indicating enhanced structural integrity. These findings demonstrate that dynamic mechanical force induced by respiration promotes Piezo1/ITGB1-mediated fibrotic integration and functional tissue maturation on PCL/10 wt% nHA implants in vivo, possessing higher bending strength and offering more effective resistance against thoracic deformation. In addition, SEM analysis of material surface morphology was performed at baseline and at 10 and 20 days post-implantation. With increasing in vivo residence time, the surface topography became progressively irregular—consistent with active tissue–material interfacial remodeling ( ). Quantitative mass assessment after freeze-drying revealed only minimal weight loss over time, indicating negligible bulk degradation under physiological conditions ( ).
We also established subcutaneous implant models in the cranial and thoracic walls of BALB/c mice, with the experimental groups being the same as those in the rat experiments ( ). The soft tissues in direct contact with the implants were removed at the first and second weeks after implantation for histological analysis. At the first and second weeks, the collagen fiber content in the PCL/10 wt% nHA thoracic wall group was significantly higher than that in the other groups ( ). IHC staining indicated that the expression levels of Piezo1, ITGB1, and TGF-β1 in the subcutaneous soft tissues of the thoracic wall were significantly higher than those in the subcutaneous tissues of the cranial region at the first and second weeks ( ). This result was consistent with that of the rat experiments.
To further assess the biocompatibility of PCL/10 wt% nHA implants, histopathological and biochemical evaluations were performed in SD rats and BALB/c mice. Histological analysis using H&E staining of major organs, including the lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, and heart, revealed no evident pathological changes such as inflammatory infiltration or tissue necrosis ( ). Furthermore, blood samples were collected from the control group, as well as at 1st and 2 nd weeks post-surgery, and subjected to complete blood count (CBC) and biochemical analysis ( ). The results showed no statistically significant differences between the PCL and PCL/10 wt% nHA groups in parameters including three-part differential blood cell counts, liver and kidney function markers, and myocardial enzyme levels compared to the control group (P > 0.05). These findings indicate that both PCL and PCL/10 wt% nHA implants exhibit favorable biocompatibility in vivo.
This study reveals that the novel PCL/nHA rib implant promotes fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix remodeling under respiratory alternating force, thereby enhancing soft tissue integration and implant fixation, offering a promising strategy for advancing chest wall reconstruction. The patient-specific 3D printing with biodegradability, enabling precise anatomical fit and gradual load transfer during chest wall repair. Unlike rigid metal implants like titanium and PEEK, it avoids stress shielding and chronic complications, while promoting vascularized tissue ingrowth and minimizing inflammation. Compared to rapidly degrading collagen scaffolds or brittle PCL/nHA composites, our optimized nHA dispersion yields improved mechanical resilience and controlled degradation. Preliminary in vivo results show no infection, though further antimicrobial evaluation is needed. This study highlights its balanced performance in mechanics, biocompatibility, and clinical adaptability, representing a promising step toward personalized, biologically integrated chest wall reconstruction.
However, it is critical to distinguish between pathological fibrosis and functional fibrosis. Pathological fibrosis is commonly observed in chronic diseases such as pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, characterized by uncontrolled extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and tissue architectural disruption, but functional fibrosis described in this study refers to a spatially confined, temporally regulated, and mechanically instructive fibrotic response that is guided by the degradable PCL/nHA scaffold. This process results in the formation of a highly organized, collagen-rich fibrous tissue that integrates with host tissue, provides structural support rather than progressing to irreversible scarring. And we have to acknowledge that persistent foreign body reactions or chronic inflammation could drive maladaptive fibrosis. Our histological evaluations show no evidence of granuloma formation or lymphocytic infiltration, but long-term animal studies should be designed to monitor tissue remodeling, respiratory function, and potential late complications. Apart from the activation of intracellular biological pathways, the chemical dissolution of nHA originating from the PCL/nHA scaffolds may act as an initial upstream event. As evidenced by previous studies, nHA-incorporated PCL scaffolds release calcium ions into the surrounding medium in a concentration-dependent manner. This localized elevation in extracellular Ca2+ could prompt excessive calcium influx into cells, thus contributing to the observed intracellular calcium overload and subsequent caspase-3 activation.
Besides, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the current evaluation of soft tissue regeneration was primarily conducted in small animals. This model lacks the mechanical loading and anatomical complexity of a clinical rib defect, which may influence the translation of findings to human applications. Second, the long-term degradation behavior of the PCL/nHA composite scaffolds and the quality of hardening tissue formation require further investigation over extended implantation periods. Third, the horizontal shaker provides a simplified and reproducible means of applying mechanical stimulation, it does not fully replicate the complex 3D mechanical environment of the thoracic cavity. Future studies will employ the newly developed bioreactor with physiological strain profiles to validate and extend these findings toward more clinically predictive models. Last, while L929 cells and HSFs provide a reproducible and accessible model for initial screening, they do not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity and physiological behavior of primary human chest wall or pleural fibroblasts. Future work will prioritize the isolation and characterization of primary human fibroblasts from thoracic tissues. These limitations highlight important directions for future research, which will be critical for translating our findings into clinically relevant strategies for modulating fibrotic remodeling in human patients.

4.

## Conclusion
In this study, PCL-based composite scaffolds incorporating varying mass fractions of nHA were successfully fabricated using fused deposition modeling technology. Among the formulations tested, the PCL/10 wt% nHA composite demonstrated the highest level of biological activity, exhibiting superior enveriment for fibroblast proliferation, adhesion, and fibrotic differentiation under dynamic mechanical stimulation. Mechanistic investigations revealed that these effects were predominantly mediated through the Piezo1/Ca2+ signaling axis. Piezo1 acts as a central mechanotransducer that coordinates with ITGB1 to activate Ca2+ influx and the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling axis, forming a critical regulatory network that links biomechanical cues to cellular responses in fibroblasts. In vivo evaluations further confirmed that the degradable rib implant, under the physiological cyclic mechanical forces of respiration, significantly enhanced functional fibrosis and fixation with surrounding chest wall soft tissues. These findings collectively demonstrate that the PCL/10 wt% nHA degradable rib implant represents a promising therapeutic strategy for chest wall defect repair, which allows for the simultaneous restoration of both anatomical integrity and physiological function, addressing structural and biomechanical demands of the thoracic cavity. We expect that combining this biomaterial with 3D printing technology holds significant potential to enable patient-specific, precision-guided chest wall reconstruction, thereby improving patients’ quality of life.
