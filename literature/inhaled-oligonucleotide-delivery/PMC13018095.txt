RNA-binding motif single-stranded interacting protein 1 (RBMS1) is a well-conserved RNA-binding protein that orchestrates pivotal post-transcriptional processes, including alternative splicing, mRNA stability, and translation [ , ]. Through these mechanisms, RBMS1 fine-tunes the expression of a wide array of target genes, thereby exerting profound influence over critical cellular functions such as proliferation, differentiation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and programmed cell death [ – ]. Its dysregulation has been extensively implicated in the progression of various cancers, underscoring its role as a key regulator of cellular homeostasis and disease pathogenesis. For instance, RBMS1 has been shown to sustain PD-L1 expression in triple-negative breast cancer by stabilizing B4GALT1 mRNA, thereby promoting PD-L1 glycosylation and impairing antitumor immunity [ ]. In contrast, RBMS1 acts as a metastasis suppressor in colon cancer, where it binds and stabilizes a distinct set of target mRNAs; its downregulation is associated with poor patient survival [ ]. Furthermore, in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, RBMS1 promotes tumor progression by upregulating HSPA8 [ ]. However, the expression pattern, functional significance, and regulatory mechanisms of RBMS1 in the context of intestinal inflammation and UC remain largely unexplored. Elucidating its potential role, particularly its impact on epithelial integrity and inflammatory cell death pathways, could provide critical insights into the post-transcriptional regulatory networks underlying colonic inflammation.
In recent years, ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lethal lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a critical player in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases, including UC [ – ]. Evidence suggests that the inflamed colonic mucosa in UC patients and experimental models exhibits hallmarks of ferroptosis, such as depletion of the antioxidant glutathione (GSH), accumulation of lipid peroxides, and dysregulation of key ferroptosis regulators like Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [ , ]. Pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis has been shown to ameliorate colitis in preclinical models, highlighting its therapeutic potential [ , ]. However, the upstream molecular triggers and the specific regulatory networks that initiate and propagate ferroptosis within the colonic epithelium in UC remain incompletely defined, representing a significant knowledge gap that this study seeks to address.
N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent internal modification in eukaryotic mRNA, and its dynamics—mediated by writers, erasers, and readers—play crucial roles in regulating RNA metabolism, including stability, decay, and translation efficiency [ , ]. YTH domain-containing 2 (YTHDC2) is an m6A reader protein known to promote the degradation of its target transcripts, with emerging roles in cellular differentiation and disease [ , ]. While m6A methylation has been increasingly implicated in immune regulation and inflammatory pathologies, its functional consequences are mediated by distinct reader proteins [ ]. However, the specific role of the reader YTHDC2 in modulating key genes like RBMS1 and influencing ferroptosis in UC remains an open and compelling question.
Therefore, this study was designed to bridge these gaps by integrating multi-omics analyses with rigorous experimental validation. We first employed a comprehensive bioinformatics approach to identify robust ferroptosis-related hub genes from UC transcriptomic datasets. Subsequently, we focused on RBMS1, a candidate hub gene, to delineate its functional role and upstream regulation. Given the potential link between RNA metabolism and cell death, we proposed that RBMS1 might be regulated post-transcriptionally by the m6A machinery, specifically by YTHDC2. Through a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we aimed to elucidate the functional contribution of the YTHDC2-RBMS1 axis to colonic epithelial injury, inflammation, and ferroptosis in UC, ultimately proposing a novel regulatory mechanism and potential therapeutic strategy for this debilitating disease.

## Materials and Methods

## Data Acquisition and Preprocessing
Publicly available transcriptomic datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the search keywords “Ulcerative colitis” and “Homo sapiens”. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies containing at least 20 samples; (2) datasets comprising colonic or mucosal tissues from both UC patients and healthy controls. Five datasets meeting these criteria were selected: GSE36807, GSE87466, GSE38713, GSE75214, and GSE92415. The training set included GSE36807 (GPL570 platform, 15 UC and 7 normal samples), GSE87466 (GPL13158 platform, 87 UC and 21 normal samples), GSE38713 (GPL570 platform, 30 UC and 13 normal samples), and GSE75214 (GPL6244 platform, 97 UC and 11 normal samples). The independent validation set was GSE92415 (GPL13158 platform, 87 UC and 21 normal samples).
Raw gene expression data were normalized using the limma (Linear Models for Microarray Data) package in R. To address batch effects arising from different platforms and experimental conditions, we applied the ComBat algorithm from the sva package, based on an empirical Bayes framework. The efficacy of batch correction was assessed via principal component analysis (PCA) before and after adjustment.

## Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)
DEGs between UC and control samples in the training cohort were identified using the limma R package. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) ≥ 1 and an adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg procedure) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The resulting DEGs were visualized using a volcano plot and a heatmap, generated with the ggplot2 and pheatmap R packages, respectively, to provide an intuitive summary of the expression changes.

## Functional Enrichment Analysis
To elucidate the biological functions and pathways associated with the identified DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. These analyses were executed using the clusterProfiler R package. The GO analysis encompassed three domains: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). Terms with a p-value < 0.05 were deemed significantly enriched. The top 10 most significant terms from each category were selected for visualization to succinctly represent the key enriched biological themes. The most significantly enriched pathways in KEGG were displayed in an enrichment bubble plot.

## Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and Hub Module Enrichment
To identify clusters of highly correlated genes and their association with UC, we performed a WGCNA using the respective R package on the normalized expression data from the combined training cohort. A weighted adjacency matrix was constructed using a soft-thresholding power (β) selected to achieve a scale-free topology fit index > 0.85. The adjacency matrix was then transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), and the corresponding dissimilarity (1-TOM) was used for hierarchical clustering to group genes with highly synergistic expression patterns into distinct modules. For each derived module, we calculated the module eigengene (ME), defined as the first principal component of the module, which represents the overall expression pattern of all genes within that module. The correlation between each module’s ME and the clinical trait (UC vs. control) was computed to identify modules significantly associated with the disease phenotype. These were designated as key modules of interest. Within the key modules, we calculated two critical metrics for each gene: module membership (MM), defined as the correlation between the gene’s expression and the ME, and gene significance (GS), defined as the absolute correlation between the gene and the clinical trait. Hub genes were rigorously selected as those exhibiting high connectivity within their module (high MM, |MM| > 0.85) and a strong association with the disease phenotype (high GS, |GS| > 0.3).
Subsequently, all genes from the hub module(s) were subjected to functional enrichment analysis to interpret their biological relevance. GO and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package, with a significance threshold of p-value < 0.05. The top 10 significantly enriched terms from each category were visualized to summarize the principal biological functions and pathways implicated in UC pathogenesis.

## External Validation and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Analysis
To independently assess the robustness and generalizability of the identified hub genes, we performed an external validation using the independent dataset GSE92415. The expression profiles of the candidate genes were extracted from the validation dataset. Their diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing UC samples from healthy controls was then evaluated by performing ROC curve analysis for each gene. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify the discriminatory power of each biomarker. All ROC analyses were conducted using the pROC package in R.

## Correlation Analysis Between Key Gene Expression and Disease Severity
To evaluate the association between the expression levels of key candidate genes and the clinical severity of colitis, a correlation analysis was performed using the independent validation cohort GSE92415 obtained from the GEO database. Sample preprocessing was first conducted. Specifically, samples from the colitis group within the dataset that had missing values in either gene expression data or the Mayo endoscopic subscore (a validated index for assessing mucosal disease activity in ulcerative colitis) were excluded to ensure data completeness and analytic robustness. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was selected as the appropriate measure to assess linear relationships. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Immune Cell Infiltration Analysis
To investigate the relationship between the expression of candidate hub genes and immune cell composition in UC, we performed an immune infiltration analysis on the merged training cohort. The relative proportions of 22 immune cell types were estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm. To assess associations between hub gene expression and immune infiltration, samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups for each candidate gene based on median expression values. Differences in immune cell fractions between these groups were visualized using boxplots generated with the ggplot2 package in R. Statistical comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

## Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)
GSEA was performed on key candidate genes to investigate their potential associated pathways. For each gene, samples were stratified into high and low-expression groups based on the median expression value. This analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package.

## Establishment of Dextran Sulfate Sodium (DSS)-Induced Colitis Mouse Model
All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of our hospital. Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g) were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle at 25 °C, provided with free access to food and water. Following a 7-day acclimatization period, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n ≥ 6): Control, DSS, LV-sh-NC, LV-sh-RBMS1, LV-NC, and LV-YTHDC2. To induce colitis, the DSS, LV-sh-NC, LV-sh-RBMS1, LV-NC, and LV-YTHDC2 groups received 3% (w/v) DSS in their drinking water for 7 consecutive days, while the Control group received normal drinking water [ ]. Concurrently, lentiviral interventions were performed via colonoscopic injection on days 1, 3, and 5 of DSS administration. The LV-sh-NC and LV-sh-RBMS1 groups received a non-targeting control short hairpin RNA (shRNA) and an RBMS1-targeting shRNA lentivirus, respectively. The LV-NC and LV-YTHDC2 groups received an empty vector lentivirus and a YTHDC2-overexpression lentivirus, respectively (50 µL, 1 × 10⁸ TU per injection). The DSS group received an equivalent volume of saline injections at the same time points. Throughout the experimental period, body weight, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding were monitored daily to assess the disease activity. On day 7, all mice were euthanized by inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane (5%), and colon tissues were collected for subsequent analysis.

## Cell Culture and Treatment
The mouse intestinal epithelial cell line MODE-K, obtained from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China), was cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified atmosphere. Cells were subjected to various interventions as follows: the Control group received no treatment; the lipopolysaccharide(LPS) group was exposed to 200 ng/mL LPS for 48 h; the LPS + si-NC and LPS + si-RBMS1 groups were first transfected with negative control siRNA or RBMS1-targeting siRNA (siRNA sequences were provided in Supplementary Table ), respectively, for 24 h before LPS challenge; similarly, the LPS + oe-NC and LPS + oe-YTHDC2 groups underwent transfection with empty vector or YTHDC2 overexpression plasmid prior to LPS treatment. To investigate potential rescue effects, additional groups included LPS + oe-YTHDC2 + oe-NC (co-transfected with YTHDC2 overexpression plasmid and empty vector) and LPS + oe-YTHDC2 + oe-RBMS1 (co-transfected with both YTHDC2 and RBMS1 overexpression plasmids). All transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with LPS treatment commencing 24 h post-transfection and maintained for 48 h to establish the inflammatory model [ ].

## Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay
Cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Cells from respective experimental groups were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well in 100 µL complete medium. Following 24 h of incubation under standard culture conditions, the original medium was aspirated and replaced with 90 µL fresh maintenance medium containing 10 µL CCK-8 reagent. After an additional 2-hour incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

## Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
The concentrations of inflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), along with ferroptosis-related parameters comprising iron (Fe²⁺), malondialdehyde (MDA), and GSH, as well as m6A levels in both serum samples and cell culture supernatants were quantified using respective commercial ELISA kits (Esebio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Following the appropriate incubation and development steps, the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Sample concentrations were determined by interpolation from standard curves generated with reference standards provided in each kit.

## Histopathological Examination by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining
Colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series, clearing in xylene, and embedding in paraffin blocks. Tissue sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm using a microtome and mounted onto glass slides. For H&E staining, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin according to standard protocols. After dehydration through a graded alcohol series and clearing in xylene, sections were mounted with neutral balsam. Histopathological evaluation was performed under a light microscope.

## Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL) Assay
Apoptosis in colon tissues was evaluated using the TUNEL assay. Deparaffinized sections were treated with proteinase K and incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, the sections were incubated with peroxidase converter and developed with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrates. Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin before dehydration and mounting. TUNEL-positive cells were quantified under a fluorescence microscope.

## Real-Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was isolated from tissues and cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA concentration and purity were determined using the Nano-100 spectrophotometer. cDNA synthesis was performed using the SweScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Supermix for qPCR (Servicebio Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China). Quantitative PCR was carried out on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with SYBR Green I under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the internal control. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table .

## Measurement of Lipid Peroxidation
Cellular lipid peroxidation was assessed using the fluorescent probe 4,4‑difluoro‑5‑(4‑phenyl‑1,3‑butadienyl)‑4‑bora‑3a,4a‑diaza‑s‑indacene‑3‑undecanoic acid (C11‑BODIPY 581/591; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, after the respective treatments, cells were washed twice with phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 5 µM C11‑BODIPY in serum‑free medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed again with PBS to remove excess probe. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal microscope. Image acquisition parameters were kept consistent across all experimental groups. The fluorescence intensity of green and red channels was quantified using ImageJ software. The extent of lipid peroxidation was expressed as the ratio of green (oxidized) to red (non‑oxidized) fluorescence intensity.

## Measurement of Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)
Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated in serum-free medium containing 10 µM 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‑DA) and 5 µg/mL Hoechst 33,342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove residual probes. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal fluorescence microscope. The same imaging parameters were applied to all samples. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

## Western Blot Analysis
Protein expression levels of RBMS1, YTHDC2, Cyclooxygenase-2 (Cox2), GPX4, Ferritin Heavy Chain (FTH), and Ferritin Light Chain (FTL) were analyzed by western blot, with GAPDH serving as the loading control. Protein electrophoresis was performed using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels, followed by electroblotting onto methanol-activated polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were subsequently blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at ambient temperature. Primary antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C using the following specific antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK): RBMS1 (ab150353, 1:2000), YTHDC2 (ab220160, 1:2000), Cox2 (ab179800, 1:2000), GPX4 (ab125066, 1:2000), GAPDH (ab8245, 1:1000), FTH (ab75972, 1:2000), and FTL (ab69090, 1:2000). After thorough washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam; rabbit: ab6721; mouse: ab6789; 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and detected with the Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

## Bioinformatic Prediction of m6A Modification Sites
Based on emerging evidence implicating m6A methylation in inflammatory bowel disease pathogenesis, we performed computational analysis to identify potential m6A modification sites within RBMS1 transcripts. The reference sequence of RBMS1 was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Putative m6A modification sites were subsequently predicted using the Sequence-based RNA Adenosine Methylation Site Predictor (SRAMP) online tool (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp/).

## Quantification of Global m⁶A RNA Methylation Levels
The m⁶A levels were quantified using the m⁶A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and data were normalized to the mean value of the Control group to obtain relative m⁶A levels.

## RNA Immunoprecipitation Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RIP-qPCR)
RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were performed to investigate the potential interaction between YTHDC2 and RBMS1 using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, magnetic beads were pre-coated with 5 µg of anti-YTHDC2 antibody or normal immunoglobulin G (IgG, negative control). Cell lysates containing approximately 2 × 10⁷ cells were incubated with the antibody-bound beads overnight at 4 °C. Following extensive washing, the immunoprecipitated complexes were treated with proteinase K. RNA was subsequently extracted from the complexes and purified for quantitative PCR analysis. The relative enrichment of target RNAs was calculated using the input normalization method: % input = 1/10 × 2 (Ct [IP] − Ct[input]).

## mRNA Stability Assay
To assess mRNA stability, cells from different experimental groups were treated with actinomycin D (5 mg/mL) to inhibit de novo RNA synthesis. Total RNA was isolated at designated time points (0, 3, and 6 h post-treatment) and subjected to quantitative RT-PCR analysis.

## Statistical Analysis
All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was employed. Multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

## Results

## Identification of DEGs in UC and Functional Enrichment Analysis
This search yielded five eligible datasets, which were allocated as follows: GSE36807, GSE75214, GSE87466, and GSE38713 constituted the training cohort, while GSE92415 was reserved as an independent validation set. The raw data from the training datasets were integrated and subjected to a rigorous batch effect correction using the ComBat algorithm, which demonstrated a marked improvement in data distribution homogeneity across the different studies after adjustment (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Following batch correction and merging, the combined training cohort comprised 229 UC samples and 52 normal control (NC) samples. We performed a differential gene expression analysis between the UC and NC groups using the limma R package. This analysis identified a total of 529 DEGs meeting our significance criteria, with 348 genes being significantly upregulated and 181 downregulated in UC tissues compared to controls (Supplementary Material ). The overall pattern of differential expression was depicted in a volcano plot (Fig.  A), and an unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis based on these DEGs effectively segregated the UC samples from the NC samples, as illustrated in the heatmap (Fig.  B).

 

To elucidate the biological implications of the 529 identified DEGs, we conducted a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package. GO enrichment analysis was performed across the three main categories: BP, CC, and MF. In BP, the predominant enrichments were leukocyte migration, neutrophil migration, and granulocyte migration. For CC, the genes were primarily localized to the collagen-containing extracellular matrix, the external side of plasma membrane, and apical part of cell. Meanwhile, the MF were dominated by chemokine activity, chemokine receptor binding, and glycosaminoglycan binding (Fig.  C). Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis was employed to identify key signaling pathways perturbed in UC. The DEGs were significantly enriched in several immune and inflammatory pathways, most notably the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and the Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells signaling pathway (Fig.  D).

## WGCNA Identifies Key Modules Associated with UC
To decipher the system-level functional organization of the transcriptome in UC, we performed a WGCNA on the merged training cohort expression profile. The analysis commenced with data pre-processing to ensure robustness, wherein genes with the lowest 50% median absolute deviation (MAD) were filtered out, and the goodSamplesGenes function was employed to remove outlier genes and samples. A critical step in constructing a biologically meaningful co-expression network is the selection of an appropriate soft-thresholding power (β) that approximates a scale-free topology. The analysis of scale-free topology fit and mean connectivity for a range of soft-thresholding powers led to the selection of β = 20 (Fig.  A and B). Using this parameter, an unsigned co-expression network was built, and a TOM was calculated to quantify gene-gene interconnectedness. To identify coherent gene modules, average linkage hierarchical clustering was performed based on the TOM-based dissimilarity. A dynamic tree-cutting algorithm with a minimum module size of 30 genes was applied to the resulting cluster dendrogram, yielding an initial set of gene modules (Fig.  C). To refine these modules, we calculated module eigengenes and merged those with highly correlated expression profiles (dissimilarity threshold < 0.25), ultimately consolidating the initial clusters into five distinct and robust co-expression modules. Subsequently, we investigated the relationship between the identified modules and the clinical trait (UC vs. NC) by correlating each module’s eigengene with the sample groups. This module-trait association analysis revealed that the grey60 module exhibited the strongest and most statistically significant positive correlation with the UC phenotype, identifying it as the key hub module for the disease (Fig.  D). To pinpoint the core genes within the grey60 hub module, we calculated MM, defined as the correlation of a gene’s expression with the ME, and GS, defined as the correlation with the clinical trait. Applying stringent criteria (|MM| > 0.85 and |GS| > 0.3), we identified 277 high-confidence hub genes that were centrally positioned within the module and strongly associated with disease status (Fig.  F).

 

## Functional Enrichment Analysis of Hub Genes
To delineate the biological functions and pathways underpinning the 277 hub genes identified from the key co-expression module (grey60), we performed comprehensive functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package. GO and KEGG analyses were conducted, with terms possessing a p-value < 0.05 deemed statistically significant. The top 10 significantly enriched terms in each category were visualized. GO enrichment analysis revealed highly specific roles for these hub genes. For BP, the most significantly enriched terms were leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, regulation of cell-cell adhesion, and leukocyte migration (Supplementary Fig.  A). In CC, these genes were predominantly localized to the collagen-containing extracellular matrix, external side of plasma membrane, and secretory granule membrane (Supplementary Fig.  B). Regarding MF, the analysis showed prominent enrichment in GTPase regulator activity, nucleoside-triphosphatase regulator activity, and GTPase activator activity (Supplementary Fig.  C). Complementing these findings, KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the hub genes were significantly overrepresented in several pivotal signaling and trafficking pathways. The most prominent pathways included Leukocyte transendothelal migration, Cell adhesion molecules, and Rheumatoid arthritis (Supplementary Fig.  D).

## Identification and Validation of Ferroptosis-Related Hub Genes
To pinpoint central regulators linking ferroptosis to UC, we integrated our transcriptomic findings with a curated set of ferroptosis-related genes. Specifically, 484 ferroptosis-related genes were retrieved from the FerrDb database. Specifically, 484 ferroptosis-related genes were retrieved from the FerrDb database. An integrative analysis, visualized via a Venn diagram, was performed to identify the intersection among these ferroptosis genes, the DEGs, and the hub genes from the WGCNA grey60 module. This approach identified four overlapping hub genes: Acyl-CoA Synthetase Long Chain Family Member 4 (ACSL4), RBMS1, TIMP Metallopeptidase Inhibitor 1 (TIMP1), and Tribbles Pseudokinase 2 (TRIB2) (Supplementary Fig.  A). We subsequently validated the expression patterns and diagnostic potential of these four candidate genes. Initially, their expression levels were assessed in the merged training cohort, which confirmed significant differential expression between UC and NC samples (Supplementary Fig.  B). Crucially, this differential expression profile was consistently recapitulated in the independent validation dataset, GSE92415, where all four genes maintained their significant dysregulation in UC tissues (Supplementary Fig.  C). To evaluate the diagnostic utility of ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, and TRIB2, we performed ROC curve analysis. In both the merged training set and the independent validation set GSE92415, each gene exhibited an AUC value exceeding 0.85 (Supplementary Figs.  D, E).

## GSEA of Core Ferroptosis-Related Genes
To gain deeper insight into the global biological pathways influenced by the four core ferroptosis-related genes (ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, TRIB2), we performed GSEA. The GSEA revealed distinct KEGG pathways significantly associated with the expression profiles of each key gene (Supplementary Figs.  A–D). Notably, these ferroptosis-related genes were predominantly enriched in critical pathways, including allograft rejection, butanoate metabolism, and the citrate cycle (tricarboxylic acid cycle), suggesting their concurrent involvement in immune-inflammatory responses, metabolic reprogramming, and cellular energy homeostasis in UC.

## Correlation between Key Candidate Gene Expression and Clinical Disease Activity in Colitis
To investigate the potential clinical relevance of the identified key genes (ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, TRIB2), we evaluated their relationship with disease severity in an independent validation cohort (GSE92415). Gene expression data from colitic tissue samples were correlated with their corresponding Mayo endoscopic subscores, a gold-standard metric for assessing mucosal inflammation and damage severity in UC. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig.  , Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant positive associations between the expression levels of three genes and the Mayo score. Specifically, the expression of ACSL4 showed a strong positive correlation (r = 0.1211, p = 0.001; Supplementary Fig.  A). Similarly, the expression of RBMS1 (r = 0.05264, p = 0.0325; Supplementary Fig.  B) and TIMP1 (r = 0.1463, p = 0.0003; Supplementary Fig.  C) were also significantly positively correlated with disease severity. In contrast, the expression level of TRIB2 demonstrated no statistically significant correlation with the Mayo score (r = 0.03546, p = 0.0807; Supplementary Figure D). These results suggest that elevated expression of ACSL4, RBMS1, and TIMP1, but not TRIB2, is closely associated with worsening pathological damage in colitis, implicating their potential roles in disease progression.

## Immune Infiltration Patterns Associated with Ferroptosis-Related Hub Genes
To further characterize the immunomodulatory potential of the identified ferroptosis-related hub genes, we investigated their association with immune cell infiltration within the colonic mucosa. The analysis revealed a consistent pattern wherein the expression levels of these hub genes were significantly associated with distinct immune infiltration profiles (Supplementary Fig.  ). Specifically, higher levels of ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, and TRIB2 were correlated with enriched proportions of both innate and adaptive immune cells, including memory B cells, CD8⁺ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), activated natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, macrophages (M0, M1, and M2 subtypes), and neutrophils.

## Validation of Hub Gene Expression in a Murine Model of UC
To further validate the pathological relevance of the identified hub genes in vivo, we employed a DSS-induced colitis model in C57BL/6 mice. Successful induction of colitis in the DSS group was confirmed by a significant and progressive reduction in body weight compared to the Control group (Fig.  A). The macroscopic examination of colon tissue revealed severe inflammation and a significant shortening of colon length in DSS-treated mice relative to controls (Figs.  B, C). Histopathological analysis by H&E staining further corroborated the disease phenotype. While colon architecture from Control mice remained intact, tissues from the DSS group exhibited substantial crypt distortion, depletion of goblet cells, and massive inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig.  D). Consistent with this inflammatory status, ELISA measurements demonstrated that the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, were markedly elevated in the colons of DSS-treated mice (Fig.  E). Concomitantly, key ferroptosis‑related indicators were significantly altered: Fe²⁺ and MDA levels were notably increased, while GSH content was substantially decreased in the DSS group compared with the Control group (Fig.  E). Moreover, western blot analysis of ferroptosis‑associated proteins revealed that the expression of FTH, FTL, and GPX4 was significantly downregulated, whereas COX2 was upregulated in DSS‑exposed colon tissues (Fig.  F).

 

Subsequently, we assessed the colonic mRNA expression of the four hub genes (ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, TRIB2) using RT-qPCR. In line with our bioinformatic predictions and dataset validation, the expression levels of all four genes (ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, TRIB2) were significantly upregulated in the DSS-induced colitis group compared to the Control group (Fig.  G).

## RBMS1 Knockdown Attenuates LPS-Induced Inflammation and Ferroptosis in Colonic Epithelial Cells
Among the four candidate ferroptosis-related hub genes (ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, and TRIB2), subsequent mechanistic investigation was directed toward RBMS1. This decision was informed by its distinctive profile as an RNA-binding protein, a class of regulators whose role in ferroptosis during colitis remains particularly underexplored compared to the more extensively characterized metabolic and inflammatory functions associated with ACSL4, TIMP1, and TRIB2. Therefore, RBMS1 was selected for in-depth study to explore its novel, post-transcriptional regulatory function in UC pathogenesis.
To investigate the functional role of RBMS1 in colonic epithelial cells, we employed an in vitro model of inflammation using LPS-stimulated MODE-K cells. Successful transfection with siRNA targeting RBMS1 was confirmed by RT-qPCR, which showed a significant reduction in its basal expression (Fig.  A). Following the establishment of four experimental groups (Control, LPS, LPS + si-NC, LPS + si-RBMS1), we first verified that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated RBMS1 expression compared to the Control, and this induction was effectively reversed by RBMS1 knockdown (LPS + si-RBMS1) relative to the LPS + si-NC group (Fig.  B). Functionally, LPS challenge led to a significant decrease in cell viability, as assessed by CCK-8 assay. Importantly, the silencing of RBMS1 markedly rescued this LPS-induced cytotoxic effect (Fig.  C). To dissect the underlying mechanisms, we measured markers of inflammation and ferroptosis. ELISA results demonstrated that LPS stimulation significantly elevated the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and key ferroptosis hallmarks (Fe²⁺, MDA), while depleting the antioxidant GSH. Notably, all these pathological changes were significantly mitigated in the LPS + si-RBMS1 group compared to the LPS + si-NC group (Fig.  D).

 

Furthermore, western blot analysis corroborated these findings at the protein level. LPS exposure resulted in the upregulation of RBMS1 and the pro-ferroptotic protein COX2, concomitant with the downregulation of the ferroptosis suppressor proteins FTH, FTL, and GPX4. As anticipated, knockdown of RBMS1 effectively counteracted these alterations (Fig.  E). To evaluate the extent of oxidative stress and ROS generation under inflammatory challenge, we performed fluorescence‑based assays using C11‑BODIPY 581/591 and DCFH‑DA. In LPS‑stimulated cells, lipid peroxidation was markedly increased, as indicated by a significantly elevated green/red fluorescence ratio in the C11‑BODIPY assay compared to the Control group (Fig.  F). Concurrently, intracellular ROS levels were also substantially enhanced upon LPS treatment (Fig.  G). Importantly, knockdown of RBMS1 effectively attenuated these oxidative changes: cells in the LPS + si‑RBMS1 group exhibited significantly lower lipid peroxidation and ROS levels relative to those transfected with control siRNA (LPS + si‑NC group).

## RBMS1 Knockdown Ameliorates Colitis and Suppresses Ferroptosis in a Murine UC Model
To definitively establish the pathogenic role of RBMS1 in vivo, we employed a DSS-induced colitis model in C57BL/6 mice with lentivirus-mediated knockdown of RBMS1. The experimental groups included Control, DSS, LV-sh-NC, and LV-sh-RBMS1. Consistent with our previous findings, DSS administration induced a progressive loss of body weight, which was significantly restored in the LV-sh-RBMS1 group compared to the LV-sh-NC group (Fig.  A). Macroscopic evaluation at the endpoint revealed that the pronounced DSS-induced colon shortening was markedly alleviated by RBMS1 knockdown (Figs.  B, C).

 

Histopathological analysis by H&E staining provided further evidence of protection. While DSS treatment caused severe mucosal damage, including crypt destruction, ulceration, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, these pathological features were substantially improved in mice receiving LV-sh-RBMS1 (Fig.  D). To assess cell death, we performed TUNEL staining, which showed a significant increase in TUNEL-positive cells in the DSS group. This indicator of cellular apoptosis was significantly reduced upon RBMS1 silencing (Fig.  E). ELISA results confirmed that the DSS-induced elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and key ferroptosis drivers (Fe²⁺, MDA), as well as the reduction of the anti-ferroptotic factor GSH, were all significantly reversed in the LV-sh-RBMS1 group compared to the LV-sh-NC group (Fig.  F). Finally, western blot analysis of colonic tissues demonstrated that RBMS1 knockdown effectively counteracted the DSS-induced upregulation of RBMS1 and the pro-ferroptotic protein COX-2, while restoring the expression of ferroptosis suppressors, including FTH1, FTL, and GPX4 (Fig.  G).

## The m6A Reader YTHDC2 Negatively Regulates RBMS1 mRNA Stability
Interrogation of the RBMS1 mRNA sequence using the SRAMP algorithm revealed multiple high-confidence m6A modification sites (Fig.  A). We then quantitatively assessed the global m6A methylation level in colonic tissues from our murine model. The results demonstrated a significant increase in m6A modification in the DSS group compared to the Control group. Notably, this increase was markedly attenuated in the LV-sh-RBMS1 group (Fig.  B). Subsequently, we focused on identifying the specific m6A “reader” protein responsible for regulating RBMS1. Notably, among the m6A reader family, YTHDC2 remained relatively understudied in the context of intestinal inflammation, with its functional significance in colitis being largely unknown. We therefore investigated YTHDC2’s role in colitis. Strikingly, both RT-qPCR and western blot analyses revealed a significant downregulation of YTHDC2 at the mRNA and protein levels in colonic epithelial cells upon LPS stimulation (Figs.  C, D). To test the direct interaction, we performed RIP-qPCR, which confirmed the significant enrichment of RBMS1 mRNA in the YTHDC2 immunoprecipitate compared to the control IgG (Fig.  E), demonstrating a specific binding between YTHDC2 and RBMS1 transcripts. Finally, to determine the functional consequence of this interaction, we assessed RBMS1 mRNA stability using an actinomycin D assay. LPS treatment significantly enhanced the stability of RBMS1 mRNA. Conversely, overexpression of YTHDC2 substantially promoted the decay of RBMS1 mRNA (Fig.  F).

 

## YTHDC2 Overexpression Attenuates Colitis and Ferroptosis in a Murine UC Model
To confirm the therapeutic potential of targeting the YTHDC2-RBMS1 axis, we investigated the effect of YTHDC2 overexpression in the DSS-induced colitis model. DSS administration induced a characteristic progressive loss of body weight, which was significantly ameliorated in mice overexpressing YTHDC2 (LV-YTHDC2) compared to the LV-NC group (Fig.  A). Post-mortem analysis showed that the pronounced DSS-induced colon shortening was notably rescued by YTHDC2 overexpression (Figs.  B, C). Histopathological evaluation by H&E staining revealed that YTHDC2 overexpression markedly alleviated the architectural damage characteristic of colitis. While DSS-treated mice exhibited severe crypt destruction, mucosal ulceration, and inflammatory cell infiltration, these pathological features were substantially improved in the LV-YTHDC2 group (Fig.  D). Consistent with this, TUNEL immunofluorescence showed a significant increase in apoptotic cells in the DSS group, which was effectively suppressed by YTHDC2 overexpression (Fig.  E). ELISA results confirmed that the DSS-induced elevations in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and key ferroptosis drivers (Fe²⁺, MDA), as well as the reduction of the anti-ferroptotic factor GSH, were all significantly reversed in the LV-YTHDC2 group (Fig.  F). Western blot analysis of colonic tissues demonstrated that YTHDC2 overexpression effectively restored YTHDC2 protein levels and counteracted the DSS-induced expression changes of critical ferroptosis regulators: it decreased the elevated levels of RBMS1 and COX-2, while increasing the suppressed levels of FTH, FTL, and GPX4 (Fig.  G).

 

## YTHDC2 Ameliorates Colitis Through Regulation of RBMS1 mRNA Stability
To establish the functional hierarchy between YTHDC2 and RBMS1 in the regulation of colonic epithelial cell injury, we conducted a series of rescue experiments in MODE-K cells. Successful overexpression of RBMS1 was first confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig.  A). Assessment of cell viability by CCK-8 assay demonstrated that LPS challenge significantly reduced cell survival. This effect was markedly rescued by the overexpression of YTHDC2 (LPS + oe-YTHDC2). Crucially, the concurrent overexpression of RBMS1 (LPS + oe-YTHDC2 + oe-RBMS1) largely abolished the protective effect conferred by YTHDC2 overexpression (Fig.  B). Parallel results were observed in the analysis of inflammatory and ferroptotic markers. ELISA measurements confirmed that YTHDC2 overexpression significantly attenuated the LPS-induced elevations in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and ferroptosis drivers (Fe²⁺, MDA), while restoring the levels of the anti-ferroptotic factor GSH. Again, the beneficial effects of YTHDC2 overexpression were effectively reversed by the co-overexpression of RBMS1 (Fig.  C). Western blot analysis provided mechanistic insight into this regulatory cascade. As expected, LPS stimulation downregulated YTHDC2 and the ferroptosis inhibitors (FTH, FTL, GPX4), while upregulating RBMS1 and the pro-ferroptotic protein COX-2. Overexpression of YTHDC2 rescued the expression of all these proteins. Importantly, the co-overexpression of RBMS1 specifically counteracted the effect of YTHDC2 on the ferroptosis-related proteins (FTH, FTL, GPX4, COX-2) without affecting the elevated YTHDC2 level itself (Fig.  D).

 

## Discussion
In the present study, we delineated a novel post-transcriptional regulatory axis that plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of UC. Through integrated bioinformatics and experimental validation, we identified the RNA-binding protein RBMS1 as a significantly upregulated ferroptosis-related hub gene in UC. We further demonstrated that its suppression alleviated inflammatory injury and ferroptosis in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, we discovered that the m6A reader protein YTHDC2, which was downregulated in colitis, directly bound to RBMS1 transcripts and promoted their degradation. The protective effect of YTHDC2 overexpression against colitis and ferroptosis was demonstrated to be largely dependent on its negative regulation of RBMS1.
Through an integrative bioinformatics approach, we successfully identified ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, and TRIB2 as core ferroptosis-related hub genes in UC, whose significant dysregulation and diagnostic potential (AUC > 0.85) were further validated in an independent cohort. Notably, independent studies consistently support the relevance of these genes in related pathologies. For instance, ACSL4 is recognized as a key ferroptosis regulator in UC through Protein-Protein Interaction network analysis of GEO and FerrDb datasets [ ]. Similarly, RBMS1 emerges as a hub gene linking inflammation and iron overload in UV-damaged skin via multi-omics integration [ ]. In the shared pathogenesis of UC and acute myeloid leukemia, TIMP1 is pinpointed as a central gene through combined differential expression and WGCNA [ ]. Furthermore, through the integration of WGCNA with machine learning approaches, including Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator and Support Vector Machine algorithms, TRIB2 is identified as a key ferroptosis-related hub gene in UC [ ]. These findings collectively underscore the potential of ACSL4, RBMS1, TIMP1, and TRIB2 as pivotal hub genes orchestrating ferroptosis and inflammatory processes across multiple disease contexts.
In UC, the pathways enriched in our bioinformatics analysis have been well‑characterized in terms of their pathogenic mechanisms. The IL‑17 signaling pathway, for example, drives sustained mucosal inflammation by promoting neutrophil recruitment and activation, as well as exacerbating epithelial barrier injury [ ]. TNF‑α signaling not only amplifies the inflammatory response by inducing epithelial cell apoptosis and pro‑inflammatory cytokine release, but also forms a positive feedback loop with the NF‑κB pathway, collectively maintaining the inflammatory microenvironment and impairing epithelial repair [ , ]. The NF‑κB pathway, a central regulator of inflammation, is persistently activated in UC, controlling the expression of multiple cytokines, including TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and IL‑6, and is closely associated with upregulation of cell adhesion molecules, thereby promoting leukocyte migration and infiltration into the intestinal mucosa [ , ]. These pathways interconnect to form a core immune‑inflammatory network in UC. The accumulation of lipid peroxides and membrane rupture during ferroptosis can release damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which directly activate NF‑κB and stimulate the expression of cytokines [ ]. This creates a link between ferroptosis and the classical inflammatory pathways mentioned above. Thus, ferroptosis may serve as a pivotal link between epithelial injury and the dysregulated immune-inflammatory network in UC, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for modulating disease progression.
We demonstrated that RBMS1 knockdown significantly ameliorated disease severity in a DSS-induced colitis model, attenuating both inflammatory injury and ferroptotic cell death. While direct evidence of RBMS1’s role in UC pathogenesis remains limited, emerging studies across different disease contexts consistently highlight its crucial regulatory functions. In pulmonary epithelium, circRBMS1 promotes inflammation and oxidative stress through molecular sponge mechanisms [ ], while in myocardial tissue, it exacerbates inflammatory responses via the circRBMS1/microRNA-2355-3p (miR-2355-3p)/Mammalian Ste20-like kinase 1 (MST1) axis [ ]. Meanwhile, RBMS1’s involvement in ferroptosis regulation exhibits tissue-specific duality: it facilitates ferroptosis evasion in lung cancer by enhancing Solute Carrier Family 7 Member 11 (SLC7A11) translation through eukaryotic Translation Initiation Factor 3 Subunit D (eIF3d) interaction [ ], yet promotes ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma by suppressing GPX4 expression [ ]. In the present study, we observed that RBMS1 knockdown restored the expression of key ferroptosis-suppressor proteins that were downregulated in colitis. Specifically, the recovery of FTH and FTL— which sequester intracellular free iron to inhibit iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and are established suppressors of ferroptosis [ , ]— was evident upon RBMS1 silencing. Concurrently, RBMS1 knockdown also upregulated GPX4, a central antioxidant enzyme that reduces lipid peroxides and serves as a core negative regulator of ferroptosis [ ]. The restoration of these proteins aligns with the attenuation of lipid peroxidation, decreased ROS levels, and reduced cell death observed following RBMS1 depletion, thereby supporting the conclusion that RBMS1 promotes ferroptosis in UC by downregulating these protective factors.
We demonstrated that YTHDC2, which was downregulated in colitis, directly bound to RBMS1 transcripts and promoted their degradation, establishing an m6A-dependent regulatory relationship. Our rescue experiments found that the protection conferred by YTHDC2 overexpression against colitis and ferroptosis was critically dependent on its suppression of RBMS1. These findings align with growing evidence underscoring the importance of m6A modification in colitis pathogenesis, which is executed by distinct reader proteins that dictate functional outcomes [ ]. In addition to YTHDC2, other m6A readers have been implicated in inflammatory bowel disease through diverse mechanisms. For instance, YTHDF1 promotes inflammatory responses in a dextran sulfate sodium‑induced colitis model by enhancing the stability of forkhead box O1 (FOXO1) mRNA in an m6A‑dependent manner, thereby driving epithelial inflammation [ ]. In macrophages, YTHDC1 maintains intestinal homeostasis by suppressing inflammatory responses through regulation of Ras homolog family member H (RHOH) and by enhancing epithelial barrier integrity via fine‑tuning of NME nucleoside diphosphate kinase 1 (NME1) [ ]. Furthermore, YTHDF1 also contributes to the immunomodulatory effects of mesenchymal stem cell‑derived exosomes by facilitating M2 macrophage polarization through post‑transcriptional regulation of solute carrier family 37 member 2 (SLC37A2) [ ]. Taken together, our work identifies the YTHDC2-RBMS1 axis as a novel m6A-dependent regulatory pathway in UC, working alongside other established m6A-mediated mechanisms to control epithelial integrity, immune cell function, and inflammatory responses.
In conclusion, our study unveils the YTHDC2-RBMS1 axis as a crucial regulatory mechanism in UC pathogenesis, which links m6A-dependent RNA metabolism to ferroptotic cell death and intestinal inflammation. We systematically demonstrate that RBMS1 drives ferroptosis in the colonic epithelium and that its expression is controlled by YTHDC2 through an m6A-dependent mechanism. These findings not only advance our understanding of the molecular underpinnings of UC but also identify promising therapeutic targets. Strategies aimed at restoring YTHDC2 function or inhibiting RBMS1-mediated pathways may offer novel approaches to protect epithelial integrity and mitigate disease progression in UC.
