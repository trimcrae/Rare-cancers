## Abstract
Endogenous reprogramming of pancreas-derived non-beta cells into insulin-producing cells is a promising approach to treat type 1 diabetes (T1D). One strategy that has yet to be explored is the specific delivery of insulin-producing essential genes, Pdx1 and MafA, to pancreatic alpha cells to reprogram the cells into insulin-producing cells in an adult pancreas. In this study, we used an alpha cell-specific glucagon (GCG) promoter to drive Pdx1 and MafA transcription factors to reprogram alpha cells to insulin-producing cells in chemically induced and autoimmune diabetic mice. Our results showed that a combination of a short glucagon-specific promoter with AAV serotype 8 (AAV8) can be used to successfully deliver Pdx1 and MafA to pancreatic alpha cells in the mouse pancreas. Pdx1 and MafA expression specifically in alpha cells were also able to correct hyperglycemia in both induced and autoimmune diabetic mice. With this technology, targeted gene specificity and reprogramming were accomplished with an alpha-specific promotor combined with an AAV-specific serotype and provide an initial basis to develop a novel therapy for the treatment of T1D.
Keywords: type 1 diabetes (T1D), glucagon (GCG) promoter, pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1 (Pdx1), v-maf musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene family, protein a (MafA), adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector

## Introduction
Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an autoimmune disease caused by an immunological response against pancreatic beta cells that leads to severe insulin deficiency. In the developing and mature pancreas, the pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1 (Pdx1) transcription factor is essential for beta cell maturation, proliferation, and function, whereas the v-maf musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene family protein A (MafA) transcription factor binds to the insulin promoter to regulate insulin expression and beta cell metabolism. In T1D, reduced Pdx1 and MafA expression in dysfunctional beta cells results in permanent glucose intolerance and eventual beta cell apoptosis. An alternative to the gold standard of daily insulin injections that targets insulin-deficient beta cells is pancreatic islet cell transplantation ; however, due to the lack of donor organs, this procedure is highly selective and only a small number of patients with T1D are treated with islet cell transplantation. Stem cell therapy is also currently practiced for the treatment of T1D but also faces many challenges. Although stem cell and islet cell transplantation modalities are current options to treat T1D, there is a significant appeal to use mature alpha cells that are native to the pancreas to re-establish insulin production in the pancreas.
Alpha cells are a primary source of endocrine cells of pancreatic islets that are of the same origin as beta cells in the pancreas. Our group and others in the field have previously found that the islet centers are predominately occupied by proliferative alpha cells in the absence of insulin-producing beta cells and following the loss of lymphocytic infiltration. , , Thus, generating new insulin-producing beta-like cells by reprogramming other native islet cell types in an adult pancreas through genetic modification may be a viable approach. Ectopic expression of a combination of three key pancreatic beta cell transcription factors, Pdx1, neurogenin 3 (Ngn3), and MafA, have been shown to reprogram adult mouse pancreatic acinar cells into insulin-producing beta-like cells. , MafA was found to potentiate the ability of Pdx1 to induce β-cell formation from Ngn3-positive endocrine precursors and enable Pdx1 to produce beta cells from alpha cells. Moreover, co-overexpression of these three genes has been shown to convert Sox9-positive liver cells into insulin-producing cells. Adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors can deliver long-term expression of transgenes up to 4.5 kb in length and have been found to be more efficient than adenoviral and lentiviral vectors in transducing pancreatic cells. , , Among the AAV vectors, serotype 8 (AAV8) has the highest transduction efficiency in mouse islet endocrine cells. , , , In fact, AAV8 with cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter can drive Pdx1 and MafA expression to enable alpha cell to beta cell transdifferentiation. These results further support the use of AAV8 for targeted delivery of pancreatic beta cell transcription factors, Pdx1 and MafA.
In this study, our primary objective was to human GCG promotor to drive the transcription factors Pd×1 and MafA via an AAV8 vector to reprogram alpha cells into insulin-producing beta-like cells. Our secondary objective was to use this reprogramming technique to reverse beta cell dysfunction in autoimmune T1D. Pancreatic alpha cell expression of Pdx1 (GenBank NM_008814.4) and MafA (GenBank NM_194350.2) genes by AAV8 mediation were evaluated to determine whether reprogrammed alpha cells via gene transduction can normalize blood glucose levels and resist immune cell attack in mice with induced or autoimmune diabetes. Considering the potential clinical applications of this technology, the human GCG promoter, that drives Pdx1 and MafA genes, was evaluated in this study for future translation.

## Results

## Alpha cell number coincides with disease advancements in T1D mouse islets
We examined the pancreas of NOD/ShiLtJ mice based on blood glucose levels at the early onset of disease (between 200 and 250 mg/dL) to the advanced stage (600 mg/dL, glucometer highest point). Compared with wild-type (WT) mice islets ( A), the alpha cells were located at the periphery of the islets in the NOD/ShiLtJ mouse when blood glucose levels were lower than 250 mg/dL before disease onset (normal level of blood glucose <200 mg/dL) ( B). Interestingly, the alpha cells began to concentrate at the center of the islet when blood glucose levels increased ( C–1F). These results confirmed a previous finding that alpha cells predominantly occupy the islet center where beta cells recede. , , , , This observation also validated that the alpha cell population is preserved at the time of early-onset diabetes and in the late stage of autoimmune diabetes. Alpha cell proliferation was observed in NOD/ShiLtJ mice islets ( H and 1I). The proliferating alpha cells, Ki67 and glucagon double-positive cells, were observed in 300 mg/dL islets of NOD/ShiLtJ mice ( H), but more non-glucagon-positive cells (Ki67 positive and glucagon negative) were observed in hyperglycemic environment 600 mg/dL islets of NOD/ShiLtJ mice ( J). Alpha cell proliferation rate (the Ki67 labeling index) was higher at the diabetes pathological initiate period when blood glucose levels are elevated and beta cells disappear in the islets of NOD/ShiLtJ mice ( K).
 

## AAV8-GCG-Pdx1-MafA vector construction and GCG promoter drive gene expression specificity
To deliver Pdx1 and MafA transcription factors to alpha cells in the early onset of diabetes, human GCG promoters were used to first determine which size GCG promoter combination with AAV8 vector measured and compared to effectively facilitate transduction in specific alpha cells. To assemble and purify the viruses, the AAV-GCG-GFP vector was constructed by modifying the pAAV-CMV-GFP vector with the GCG promoters and replacing the CMV promoter ( A), as previously described. , These vectors were used for in vitro and in vivo transduction experiments. First, gene expression in the GCG-activated alpha cell line, alpha TC1 clone 9 cells, was measured to determine the activation of the GCG promoter in these cell populations. Briefly, alpha TC cells that were infected with purified AAV8-GCG-GFP and AAV8-GCG-Pdx1-MafA-GFP were used as a confirmatory experiment. Three days after viral infection, the alpha TC cells were up to 95% GFP positive. In addition, Pdx1 and MafA genes measured by western blotting were present in the infected alpha TC cells. The virus generated from clone 2 plasmid was used for the experiments. We also observed elevated Pdx1 and MafA genes were expressed in alpha TC1 clone 9 cells 7 days following infection. More than 80% of alpha cells were GFP positive under fluorescence microscopy ( B). Pdx1 and MafA proteins were measured by western blot ( C). MafA expression (lane 2 in C) is similar compared with other clones (lane 1 and 3). To note, Pdx1 is highly expressed from the clone 2 virus that infected alpha TC1 clone 9 cells. The clone 2 virus was used in this study.
 

## AAV8-GCG viral gene delivery leads to specific targeting in pancreatic alpha cells in vivo
With confirmed specificity and gene expression in vitro, we then performed pancreatic ductal infusion with the AAV8-GCG-PM virus. Ten days after pancreatic AAV8-GCG-PM viral infusion of C57BL/6 WT mice, we observed more than 96% GFP expression in glucagon-positive alpha cells ( A and 3B). To note, there were approximately 7% uncertain insulin and glucagon positive that presented as beta-like cells under a confocal microscope. In the pancreas infused with AAV8-GCG-PM virus, very few GFP-positive cells (p < 0.05) were outside of the islets compared with the AAV8-CMV-GFP virus-infused pancreas ( A). mRNA expression of Pdx1 and MafA from two AAV8-GCG-PM viral-infused mice were confirmed by Real-Time PCR (RT-PCR) and western blot ( C and 3D). To further confirm the specificity, we then performed pancreatic ductal infusion with the AAV8-GCG-Cre viral infusion in the pancreas of ROSA26 tomato reporter mice, and 94% tomato red are glucagon-positive cells ( A, p < 0.05). We found that alpha cells transduced with AAV8-GCG-Cre virus proliferated (data not shown). We also observed insulin-positive and glucagon-positive cells (double-positive cells) indicating that the reprogrammed pancreatic alpha cells transdifferentiated to insulin-producing cells in ALX-induced diabetic mice 10 days after viral transfusion ( B). Targeting specificity of pancreatic alpha cells was further confirmed by confocal microscope scanning ( ).
 

## Delivery of the Pdx1 and MafA genes into pancreatic alpha cells by AAV viral infusion in the pancreas of ALX-induced diabetic mice
To address whether the human GCG promoter drives Pdx1 and MafA to normalize autoimmune NOD/ShiLtJ diabetic mice, we first tested beta cell toxin alloxan (ALX)-injected female C57BL/6J mice that developed diabetes at 1 week. We observed that blood glucose levels were normalized 18 days after AAV8-GCG-PM infusion ( A). The mice were killed at 7, 21, 35, and 45 days after AAV8-GCG-PM delivery. After sectioning and staining with anti-insulin and glucagon antibodies, pancreatic tissue in the ALX-induced diabetic mice contained very few GFP-positive cells outside of islets 7 days following viral infusion ( C i–l). Pdx1 and MafA expression was confirmed by RT-PCR after fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) of GFP-positive cells from AAV8-GCG-PM 7 days post viral infusion (data not shown).
 

## Blood glucose normalizes by AAV8-GCG-PM viral infusion in ALX-induced diabetic mice
After viral infusion, the blood glucose and intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) were tested at 4 weeks, and the blood glucose levels were monitored for more than 6 weeks. As we had anticipated, the blood glucose levels normalized in the AAV8-GCG-PM viral-infused group compared with the AAV8-GCG-GFP group ( A). Glucose tolerance in the AAV8-GCG-PM viral-infused ALX-induced mice dramatically improved 4 weeks after transfusion compared with that in the AAV8-GCG-GFP viral-infused group ( B). Immunohistochemistry of glucagon and insulin showed that beta cell mass significantly increased in ALX-induced mice receiving AAV8-GCG-PM compared with mice that received AAV8-GCG-GFP at 4 weeks after viral infusion ( D). In addition, the beta cell mass reached more than 65% of that of WT mice (no ALX, no virus) ( D). A statistically significant change in alpha cell mass compared with AAV8-GCG-GFP viral-infused mice was observed (∗p < 0.01, D). Immunohistochemistry showed a lower alpha cell mass in 6-week-old ALX + AAV8-GCG-PM mice ( C i–l) than in ALX + AAV8-GCG-GFP mice ( C e–h), similar to normal mice (non-ALX treated) targeted with GFP ( C a–i). These results demonstrated that AAV8-GCG-PM rescued blood glucose levels 3 weeks after viral infusion in ALX-induced mice ( ). Thus, these results demonstrated that intraductal infusion of the AAV-GCG-PM virus normalized blood glucose levels in ALX-induced diabetic mice.

## Blood glucose normalizes by AAV8-GCG-PM viral infusion in autoimmune (NOD/ShiLtJ) diabetic mice
Next, we examined whether AAV8-GCG-PM-transformed alpha cells would be recognized by an autoimmune diabetic immune system in NOD/ShiLt mice. The NOD/ShiLt mice were treated with a single dose of ductal infusion with the AAV8-GCG-PM virus early after the onset of hyperglycemia, when the blood glucose of the mice had surpassed 200 mg/dL. With 150 μL virus (1012 genome copy particle [GCP]/mL) infusion, blood glucose levels were monitored, and IPGTT was performed following the same procedures as the ALX-induced diabetic mice described above. After monitoring blood glucose levels for more than 7 months, blood glucose levels normalized in the AAV8-GCG-PM viral-infused group compared with the AAV8-GCG-GFP viral-infused group in NOD/ShiLtJ female mice ( A). Immunohistochemistry of glucagon and insulin demonstrated that AAV-GCG-PM normalized blood glucose levels at 5 weeks after viral infusion ( B), but AAV-GCG-GFP viral infusion did not result in normalized blood glucose levels ( B a–f). Pdx1 and MafA expression was detected by RT-PCR from FACS sorted GFP + cells harvested from the islets of AAV8-GCG-PM viral-infused NOD mice, but expression was very low in control mice (data not shown). These results demonstrate that AAV8 combined with a human GCG promoter can drive Pdx1 and MafA in alpha cells more efficiently and greater targeted specifically than the AAV8-CMV promoter approach. Furthermore, the mice receiving control AAV-GCG-GFP viral infusion showed a continuous increase in blood glucose levels and died within 6 weeks ( A). Immunohistochemical staining showed that the NOD/ShiLtJ mice that received AAV-GCG-PM had a significantly greater insulin-positive cell mass ( B e–l) as a basis for their normalized blood glucose. The increased beta cell mass was maintained at approximately 70%, which is comparable to the normal glycemia (NG) pancreas and maintained until the end of the experiment ( D). IPGTT showed that glucose tolerance was better than that of the pancreas infused with AAV-GCG-GFP virus ( C). We observed that beta cell mass was increased at 4 weeks (∗p < 0.01, D) and maintained until the end of the 30-week experiment. None of the AAV8-GCG-GFP infused NOD/ShiLt mice survived after 6 weeks ( D). We also observed concentrations of triglycerides, free fatty acids, and β-hydroxybutyrate in the serum of AAV8-GCG-PM virus-infused mice with normalized insulinemia ( E). To this end, insulitis (based on CD45 staining for immune cells) also decreased ( ). Normalized blood glucose levels were maintained for 7 months, with very few immune cells found in the islet centers ( ). ## Discussion
Interestingly, we found that the alpha cell population is preserved in the early and late stages of autoimmune diabetes. More evidence has demonstrated that the role of glucagon expression in alpha cells is vital for hyperglycemia to arise in T1D. , , 
We and other groups have reported that alpha cell mass increases through proliferation when beta cell numbers diminish in islet centers and blood glucose levels begin to surge during the development of diabetes. , , , , Thus, a more efficient approach to treat early onset of T1D is to reprogram alpha cells to insulin-producing cells and transforming alpha cell number via proliferation. We observed that T1D diabetic islets dramatically decrease functional beta cells that coincide with the remaining alpha cells occupying the islet centers ( ). Based on this finding, we designed a therapeutic strategy to infect prediabetic pancreatic alpha cells using an AAV8-GCG-PM viral delivery method that requires beta cell essential genes, Pdx1 and MafA, to reprogram alpha cells into insulin-producing cells. Following disease development, alpha cells proliferate, and virally infected alpha cells carrying the Pdx1 and MafA genes coincide to proliferate, which makes reprogramming more effective and prevents the development of severe disease. A common approach is to target glucagon-secreting alpha cells in vivo by crossing mice expressing Cre recombinase under the glucagon promoter (Gcg-Cre mice) with loxP reporter mice. , However, Cre-loxP methods are time consuming due to breeding and mouse strain limitations. We previously demonstrated that a combination of the sox9 promoter and AAV serotype 6 can deliver the GFP gene in pancreatic ductal cells. Based on this finding, we used GCG promoter-driven reporter gene with Pdx1 and MafA genes specific to alpha cells. With AAV8-GCG-PM infusion into murine pancreases, we observed specific GFP expression in mouse alpha cells with little overlap in insulin-positive cells but almost no GFP expression in acinar cells. In the AAV-GCG promoter-driven Cre virus used to infuse into the pancreas of ROSA26 tomato reporter mice, we observed 85%–90% Cre cleaved red fluorescence in the nuclei of alpha cells. Even though the human promoter sequence, GCG, is only 648 base pairs (bp), we found that the promoter sequence is 83% and 82% similar to the mouse and rat promoters, respectively, in GenBank. Our experimental results suggest that the short GCG promoter is sufficient to drive gene expression and with specificity for identifying alpha cells in the mouse pancreas. We previously demonstrated that using AAV serotype 8 with CMV promoter driving Pdx1 and MafA success reprogrammed alpha cells into insulin-producing cells and avoided immune cell attack for 4 months following treatment. As the CMV promoter is a broad activation strong promoter, although it has serotype 8 tropism tight controlled expression in islet cells when beta cells disappeared in diabetic islets, it still has leaky expression in other islet cells and acinar cells. We used the GCG promoter to replace the CMV promoter to drive Pdx1 and MafA expression in diabetic mice islets that were seen specifically in alpha cells. First, we tested beta cell toxin alloxan (ALX)-injected C57BL/6J mice, which developed diabetes 1 week after degradation of the beta cells of pancreatic islets. ALX-induced diabetes has a modest blood glucose increase with a significant increase in alpha cell mass, which is consistent with our previous observations. Among all AAV serotypes, we found that 8 and 6 were the best for infecting mouse pancreatic cells. , We chose the AAV serotype 8 vector because we found that serotype 8 had a better infection efficiency in mouse islet cells than serotype 6.27 Combining the AAV serotype 8 with the human GCG promoter increases tighter control of the tropism of AAV8, which could specifically infect alpha cells. The AAV8 virus carrying Pdx1-MafA genes was successfully delivered into mouse pancreatic ducts of ALX-induced diabetic C57BL/6J mice by retrograde infusion of AAV8-GCG-PM. , , , , , The euglycemia was seen 2 weeks post viral delivery. The time to trigger alpha cells to reprogram into insulin-producing cells is critical for the treatment of diabetes, especially for NOD/ShiLtJ mice, as they give impetus to diabetes very quickly during the onset of T1D. Our result demonstrated that the overexpression of Pdx1 and MafA in NOD/ShiLtJ islets completely protected against the development of spontaneous autoimmune diabetes. With AAV8-GCG-PM treatment, we observed longer euglycemia in NOD/ShiLtJ mice without immune cells attacked in 7 months. In addition, in contrast to their diabetic NOD/ShiLtJ with AAV8-GCG-GFP control, NOD-AAV8-GCG Pdx1 and MafA animals had normal levels of serum triglycerides, free fatty acids, and beta-hydroxybutyrate, indicating that they have normal energy metabolism. AAV8-GCG-PM-treated mice also showed preservation of beta cell mass and normal levels of circulating insulin. Our results demonstrated that Pdx1 and MafA expression under the GCG promoter driven in vivo was able to correct hyperglycemia in both ALX-induced diabetes and in autoimmune diabetic NOD/ShiLtJ mice, signifying that beta cell-like reprogramming was occurring. This finding is not due to the suppression of the GCG promoter in alpha cells, as we observed normalized glucagon expression and AAV is not a genome integration virus. We noticed that few beta cells (less than 7%) are infected with the virus or insulin and glucagon double-positive in WT islets; however, beta cells no longer express insulin in a diabetic environment. Thus, viral infection of beta cells in WT islet may not occur in the diabetes islets. In the diabetes islets, double-positive cell observations further corroborate with the findings of Lam et al. that double-positive, beta-like cells exist in a normal functioning pancreas and in T1D islets in humans. Whether Pdx1-MafA expression in T1D beta cells can alleviate diabetes development requires further study that specifically focuses on the design of an insulin promoter to drive Pdx-MafA. With this cell reprogramming technique, we did not observe a significant number of immune cells in the AAV-GCG-Pdx1-MafA infused islets of NOD/ShiLtJ mice. This finding was unexpected and our best prediction is that ductal viral infusion with AAV-GCG promoter-driven Pdx1 and MafA altered the autoimmunity of NOD/ShiLtJ mice that led to extended survival of neogenic insulin cells. It is also possible that AAV8-GCG-PM-infected alpha cells are not recognized by immune cells or alpha cells express higher amounts of survival factors than beta cells, as they do not produce mature insulin at the preprogramming stage. To this end, reprogrammed alpha cells tend to maintain glucagon production, which may confuse the immune system of NOD/ShiLtJ mice. In addition, the GCG promoter is significantly different from the insulin promoter. For example, the transcription factor ATF4, regulator of cell immunity upon activation, binds to the insulin promoter causing beta cell dysfunction and apoptosis, , which may play a role in immune cell homing to islets. Promoter regions may also factor into immune cell sensing to beta and beta-like cells; however, the mechanisms by which Pdx1 and MafA reprogram alpha cells into insulin-producing cells with protective action on transgenic islets or after AAV8-GCG-Pdx1 and MafA-mediated gene delivery have not been fully elucidated.
In summary, we report here that the short human GCG promoter combined with the AAV8 vector was effective in driving the expression of two transcription factors, Pdx1 and MafA, and normalizing blood glucose levels in a chemically induced and autoimmune diabetic mouse model. Our data corroborate with previously reported data on the restoration of blood glucose and beta cell survival for 4 months in NOD/ShiLtJ mice following viral infusion of AAV8-CMV that drove Pdx1 and MafA expression. In this study, the GCG promoter that drove Pdx1 and MafA caused euglycemia for 7 months without immune cell invasion in the neogenic islets of NOD mice. This motivated us to use glucagon promoter (human GCG promoter) to drive Pdx1 and MafA to improve the reprogramming technique of alpha cells into insulin-producing beta-like cells for T1D. With this technology, targeted gene specificity and reprogramming were accomplished with an alpha-specific promotor (GCG) combined with an AAV8- serotype, providing an initial basis to develop a novel therapy for the treatment of T1D.

All murine procedures were approved by the Animal Research and Care Committee at the Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh and the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and University of Texas Science Center IACUC at Houston. The methods were performed in accordance with the approved guidelines and regulations. The beta cell toxin ALX was created to induce diabetes by injection of ALX via the dorsal tail vein at a dosage of 65 mg/kg. The study was carried out in compliance with the Animal Research-Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

## Autoimmune diabetic mice
With a diabetic incidence above 90% by 30 weeks of age, NOD/ShiLtJ female mice are more widely used than males due to an early onset of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus symptoms (Jackson Lab NOD/ShiLt mice). Female NOD/ShiLtJ mice were used for our autoimmune diabetes therapy with AAV8-GCG-PM viral infusion at 16 weeks of age or older at early onset of hyperglycemia, as previously described. , , , , , AAV8-GCG-GFP viral infusion used as control.

## Tissue harvest and immunohistochemistry
Harvesting of murine pancreases was carried out as described previously. , Briefly, for excision of the pancreas, to dismiss the autofluorescence in the pancreas, mice were perfused with 10 mL 1× PBS through the heart to remove blood cells, followed by 10 mL 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The pancreatic tissues were then harvested and fixed in 4% PFA for 2 h at 4 °C and then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose overnight before freezing. The pancreas was embedded in Tissue Tek OCT (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA) and immersed in liquid nitrogen for freezing. Frozen blocks were stored at −80 °C until batch sectioning. Pancreatic tissue was cryosectioned (5 μm), blocked with 10% normal donkey serum (NDS) in 0.1% PBST for 45 min at room temperature (RT), incubated with primary antibody in 5% NDS in 0.1% PBST at 4 °C overnight, the anti-insulin antibody was 1:50 dilution, anti-glucagon-specific antibody was 1:300 dilution, and Ki67 antibody was used at 1:200 dilution in 5% NDS. The tissues were washed with 0.1% PBST at RT three times for 15 min. The tissues were then incubated with a secondary antibody in 1% NDS in PBST at RT for 30 min, washed three times with 0.1% PBST at RT for 10 min, and counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Tissues were then washed three times with 0.1% PBS and mounted on slides with aqua mounting media and a coverslip. For confocal microscopy, pancreatic tissue was sectioned and stained with insulin and glucagon antibodies, followed by treatment with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies. The fluorescence was visualized with a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope by sequentially scanning at (excitation/emission) 488/505–530 nm (Cy2), 543/570(Cy3), and 633/650 nm (Cy5) ( ).

## Human GCG promoter
The human glucagon gene is over 85% similar to the mouse, rat, bovine, and hamster glucagon genes. , , , , In fact, the TATA box and CAAT box are located at the 577 to 581 and 535 to 538 promoter regions, respectively, and have the ability to drive gene expression. Length variations of human GCG promoters (US patent US20170087254A1) were screened, and the 648-bp human GCG promoter was selected to drive gene expression for the in vivo experiments. We and other groups have reported that AAV stereotypes 6 and 8 are similar in function and able to facilitate transduction of pancreatic cells. , , Our group has previously reported that combining the sox9 promoter and AAV serotype 6 was able to deliver the green fluorescent protein (GFP) and Cre genes specifically to pancreatic ductal cells and was used for pancreatic ductal cell lineage tracing. Combining the AAV serotype 8 tissue tropism with a specific GCG promoter, using viral pancreatic ductal infusion, is an achievable in vivo gene delivery method of Pdx1 and MafA and labeling method of GFP+ pancreatic cells. In this experiment, we first designed and constructed a double-stranded AAV vector containing a human proglucagon short promoter driving GFP, AAV-GCG-GFP, and AAV-GCG-Cre to specifically target pancreatic alpha cells in WT mice and tomato reporter in ROSA26 mice, respectively. Then, the GCG promoter with Pdx1 and MafA was constructed to generate a recombinant viral vector with a GFP reporter gene, labeled as AAV8-GCG-PM (AAV8-GCG-Pdx1-MafA-GFP). This recombinant virus construct was then infused in the pancreatic ducts of mice through the common bile/pancreatic duct to tag alpha cells. After confirming AAV8 and GCG promoter specificity, we then infused the virus into the pancreatic ducts of beta cell toxic chemical, Alloxan (ALX)-induced diabetic mice and autoimmune diabetic mice (NOD/ShiLt mice) at the early onset of diabetes based on blood glucose levels (blood glucose at 200 mg/dL, lower than 250 mg/dL).

## Design of the AAV8-GCG-pdx1-MafA gene expression vector
The pAAV-CMV-GFP plasmid was modified with the human 648-bp GCG promoter to replace the CMV promoter. The Pdx1 and MafA gene cassettes were added to the front of the GFP gene ( A). The GCG promoter drives Pdx1 and MafA open reading frames (ORFs), and the GFP tag was connected with 2A peptide sequences, pAAV-GCG-Pdx1-P2A-MafA-T2A-GFP (short form: pAAV-GCG-PM). For cleavage of the stop codon flanking the tomato red reporter gene, the GFP sequence in the pAAV-GCG-GFP vector was replaced with iCre ORFs. , 

## Purification of AAV serotype 8 vector and determination of transgene expression
The generation and purification of AAV was performed with triple plasmid PEI transfection and PEG/(NH4)2SO4 aqueous two-phase partitioning, as previously described. , Determination of transgene expression was also performed using an established protocol, as previously described. Briefly, virus-mediated GFP or Pdx1-MafA cassette gene transfer assays were performed in vitro in a 12-well culture plate with alpha-TC1 clone 9 (ATCC CRL-2350) cells. Approximately 10 μL of the purified AAV-GCG-GFP (titer was 3 × 10ˆ12 genome copy particle GCP/mL) or AAV-GCG-Pdx1-MafA (titer 3 × 1012 GCP/mL) virus was added to each well. At 72 h post infection, GFP cell fluorescence was visualized under a microscope and counted for quantification (Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany) with a 525-nm filter. For gene expression, the alpha-TC1 clone 9 cells were infected once with AAV-GCG-GFP or AAV-GCG-PM for 72 h. The cells were then harvested at 4 °C and lysed for RNA or protein extraction. Approximately 10 μg of protein lysates were separated on a 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% milk-PBS for 1 h at room temperature and probed with anti-GAPDH (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and anti-Pdx1 and MafA antibodies were applied to probe the protein for 4 h at RT. Samples were washed three times with PBST and treated with anti-rabbit conjugated horseradish peroxidase secondary antibody. GAPDH, Pdx1, and MafA proteins in the blot were visualized by chemiluminescence and imaged with ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Total RNA was isolated from GFP-positive cells after FACS sorting using an RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Inc., Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sample quality was analyzed using Genesys 10 UV (Thermo Electron Co., Waltham, MA, USA). RNA (1 μg) was treated with 0.5 units DNase I (amplification grade; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) for 5 min at 37°C and 1 μL of 0.5 M EDTA was added to stop the DNase reaction and then annealed with 50 ng random hexamer oligonucleotide. cDNA was synthesized using the superscript first strand synthesis system for real-time PCR (RT-PCR) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA products were diluted 20-fold before PCR amplification. The PCR primers that were used are described in .

## AAV-mediated gene transfection and pancreatic alpha cell specific targeting in vivo
In vivo viral gene transfer into adult C57BL/6 mice was performed at 10 weeks of age, as previously described. , , Briefly, for pancreatic ductal infusion the mouse was anesthetized via inhalation with a fitted isoflurane anesthetic machine. For GFP gene pancreatic ductal transformation, anesthetized 10-week-old C57BL/6 mice (n = 8) were infused with 120 μL of AAV8-GCG-GFP (titer 3.31 × 1012 GCP/mL) or 150 μL AAV8-GCG-P-M (titer 3.01 × 1012 GCP/mL) through the common pancreatic/biliary duct at a rate of 10 μL/min. A clip was placed on the bile duct near the hilum of the liver to temporarily occlude the common hepatic duct, leading to specific perfusion of the pancreatic ducts. Once blood glucose levels reached 200 mg/dL, C57BL/6 or ALX-induced diabetic C57BL/6 and 16-week-old NOD/ShiLtJ female mice were used for AAV8-GCG-PM pancreatic ductal transformation and treatment for diabetes in vivo. The same infusion technique was applied to ROSA26 tomato reporter mice using the AAV8-GCG-Cre virus to confirm GCG promoter specificity and lineage tracing of transdifferentiating alpha cells in ALX-induced diabetic mice.

## Flow cytometry and cell sorting
All samples were analyzed on a FACSAria cell sorter (BD Biosciences) with FlowJo software (Tree Star), as previously described. , Briefly, single-cell populations were needed for FACS, the digestion of islets or pancreatic digests was performed with 10 μg/mL trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 μg/mL DNase (Roche) after collagenase treatment. After filtration at 30 μm, the single-cell populations from AAV-GCG-PM mice were sorted on a FACSAria cell sorter to isolate red (glucagon antibody-stained alpha cells), green, and yellow fluorescent cells, and those from AAV-GCG-GFP mice were sorted to isolate GFP+ and GFP– cells, respectively, based on fluorescence.

## Western blot
FACS sorted GFP-positive cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl with pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1% v/v Triton 100X, 0.5% w/v sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% w/v sodium azide, 50 mM NaF, 0.1% sodium dodecylsulfate, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, protease and phosphatase inhibitor) and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The 5-μg sample from each group was mixed with an equal volume of loading buffer (0.125 mM Tris-HCl with pH 6.8, 4% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 20% glycine, and 10% 2-mercaptoethanol) and boiled for 5 min at 98°C. Denatured proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and were transferred to PVDF membrane by the Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system. Then, membranes were placed in a blocking solution with 10% milk for 1 h. The membranes were washed three times in TBS-Tween buffer (containing 10 mM Tris with pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.1 mM Tween 20) for 10 min, then incubated with primary Pdx1, MafA, and ZsGreen antibodies according to the manufacturer’s protocols at 4°C overnight. Then the membranes were washed with TBS-Tween buffer three times for 10 min and they were incubated with secondary antibody at RT for 2 h. After washing the membrane three times for 10 min in TBS-Tween buffer, bands were revealed by enhanced chemiluminescence and scanned with ChemiDoc MP Imaging System and the bands were quantified use the ImageLab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

## Murine hormone and metabolic assays
Murine blood glucose measurements were performed after a 2-h fasting period using the tail-tip method to collect sufficient blood samples. The effects of the interventions on blood glucose levels were determined by the Optium EZ monitoring system (Abbott, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) and glucose tolerance testing (IPGTT), as previously described. Briefly, ALX-induced or NOD/ShiLtJ mice that underwent overnight fasting for 16 h were administered an intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g/kg body weight). Blood glucose levels were measured at 10-, 15-, 30- and 120-min timepoints. Serum triglycerides and total cholesterol were measured spectrophotometrically using an enzymatic assay kit (ABX Pentra A11A01640, Horiba-ABX, Montpellier, FRA). Serum free fatty acids were measured by the acyl-CoA synthase and acyl-CoA oxidase methods (NEFA-HR 434-91795, Wako Chemicals, Richmond, VA, USA). Serum β-hydroxybutyrate was quantified by an enzymatic assay (NEFA FA115, Randox Lab, Crumlin, UK). The biochemical parameters were determined using a Pentra 400 analyzer (Horiba-ABX, Montpellier, FRA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma insulin content was measured in duplicate without dilution by using a standard fluorescence direct sandwich ELISA system (catalog no. 10-1247-01, Mouse Insulin ELISA, Mercodia Inc, NC, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Sample size was determined based on published literature. Immunohistochemistry quantification was performed on at least five sections that were 100 μm apart. At least 2,000 cells were counted for each experimental condition. If the percentage of GFP+ or red fluorescent cells was low, counting continued beyond 2,000 cells until at least 50 positive cells were counted. Each experiment was repeated twice. All values are depicted as means ± SE. In each condition, at least five mice were analyzed in all groups. RT-PCR data were analyzed from five samples from each condition. All data were statistically analyzed by 2-tailed Student’s t test. Statistical significance was considered when p < 0.05.
For in vitro experiments, each experimental condition entailed at least five repeats or three repeats. For in vivo experiments, at least five mice were used for each group. Alpha cell mass, beta cell mass, beta cell number, and percentage of beta cells were quantified, as previously described. All data were statistically analyzed by one-way ANOVA with a Bonferroni correction, followed by Fisher’s exact test. The χ2 test with 1 degree of freedom was applied to compare observed and estimated data. All error bars represent SD. Significance is presented as ∗ when p < 0.05 and # when p < 0.01. No significance was presented as NS. P and n values as well as statistical methods are described in the figure legends.
