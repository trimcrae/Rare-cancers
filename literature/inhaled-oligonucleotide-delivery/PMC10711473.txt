## Summary
Influenza B circulates annually and causes substantial disease burden in humans. However, little is known about the infection mechanisms of influenza B virus (IBV). Here, we find that the host factor cyclophilin A (CypA) facilitates IBV replication by targeting IBV non-structural protein 1 (BNS1) and nucleoprotein (BNP). CypA promotes OTUD4-mediated K48-linked BNS1 deubiquitination to stabilize BNS1 by upregulating OTUD4 expression. Meanwhile, CypA and the E3 ligase MIB1 competitively interact with BNP to inhibit its proteasomal degradation. Moreover, cyclosporine A treatment or CypA R55A mutation results in an impaired function of CypA in IBV replication. Notably, BNP hijacks CypA into the nucleus to enhance the activity of viral ribonucleoprotein complexes by enhancing the interaction between BNP and IBV polymerase basic protein 1. Taken together, this study unveils the critical role of CypA in facilitating IBV replication, suggesting that CypA is a promising target for anti-IBV drug.

## Introduction
Two antigenically distinct phylogenetic lineages (B/Victoria and B/Yamagata) of influenza B virus (IBV) and two subtypes (A/H1N1 and A/H3N2) of influenza A virus (IAV) co-circulate worldwide, causing annual seasonal epidemics in humans. Influenza B accounts for an average of 25% of influenza cases each year, and even as much as 80% or more in some seasons. IBV infection was the leading cause of seasonal influenza-associated mortality in four out of 12 seasons in the United States between 1997 and 2009 and dominated the 2010–2011 season in the United Kingdom. Before the COVID-19 outbreak, IAVs dominated in China, whereas the distribution of influenza subtypes changed from A-dominant to B/Victoria-dominant after the COVID-19 outbreak. It is therefore undeniable that IBV accounts for a significant proportion of the total influenza burden, and a deeper understanding of IBV is needed for the development of influenza B therapeutics.
Influenza viruses depend on host cellular machinery to complete their life cycle. Because many host factors are more conserved than viral proteins, host factor-directed antiviral therapy may be an alternative therapeutic strategy to reduce the emergence of viral resistance due to “escape” viral genetic mutations. Worldwide genome-wide screens have identified >1,000 human genes potentially involved in the life cycle of influenza viruses. However, the lack of mechanistic understanding of the effect of these host factors on influenza hinders the development of antiviral drugs and is even more limited for influenza B. Hence, there is an urgent need to elucidate the functional role of host factors in IBV replication to provide a theoretical basis for antiviral drug development.
Cyclophilin A (CypA), a key member of the immunophilins, has peptidyl-prolyl cis/trans isomerase (PPIase) activity and plays multifunctional roles in physiological processes and virus replication. It has been reported that CypA is widely involved in viral replication, exerting either facilitative or inhibitory effects. For example, CypA directly interacts with the M1 protein of IAV, inhibiting M1 entry into the nucleus in the early stage of infection, as well as leading to nuclear retention of M1 by suppressing AIP4-mediated M1 ubiquitination. , CypA can inhibit rotavirus (RV) replication by reducing viral protein expression and mediating interferon (IFN) β production. , Further, CypA limits respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) replication by binding to RSV-N protein via its PPIase activity. In contrast, CypA can also promote the replication of numerous viruses, such as human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), , , hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV). Therefore, we wondered if CypA could regulate the replication of IBV and be a potential target of anti-IBV drugs.
This study aimed to investigate the effect of CypA on IBV replication and to elucidate its mechanisms in detail, which may provide new insights into the development of antiviral drugs. By establishing IBV infection models using wild-type and CypA-knockdown A549 cells, a positive effect of CypA on IBV replication was discovered. CypA stabilized two viral proteins by inhibiting their proteasomal degradation, and it also increased the activity of the viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complex in the nucleus.

## Results

## CypA facilitates IBV replication in vitro
Our previous study shows that IAV can interact with M1 and restricts IAV replication. , , , Moreover, CypA level is increased during IAV or IBV infection. , To investigate whether CypA affects IBV replication, CypA-knockdown A549 (A549/CypA-) cells and wild-type A549 (A549/CypA+) cells were infected with B/Yamagata/16/1988 (hereafter referred to as B/Yamagata). We found that CypA knockdown significantly reduced viral titers in the cell supernatants at 8 and 12 h post-infection (p.i.) ( A). Meanwhile, the viral RNA (vRNA) levels of IBV matrix protein 1 (BM1) and IBV nucleoprotein (BNP) were significantly higher in A549/CypA+ cells than those in A549/CypA- cells upon IBV infection ( B). Consistently, CypA knockdown significantly reduced the BM1 and BNP protein levels ( C). 
Next, whether the PPIase activity of CypA was involved in its effect on IBV replication was investigated. A549/CypA+ cells were first treated with cyclosporine A (CsA, an inhibitor of CypA that inhibits the PPIase activity of CypA) or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 2 h and then infected with B/Yamagata. We found that CsA significantly decreased the viral titers at 8 and 12 h p.i. ( D). We also observed that CsA treatment substantially reduced the vRNA ( E) and protein levels ( F) of BM1 and BNP in A549/CypA+ cells infected with B/Yamagata. Notably, CsA did not influence the vRNA levels of BM1 and BNP in A549/CypA- cells ( ), suggesting that CsA exclusively inhibited IBV replication by targeting CypA. It has been reported that the R55 residue of CypA is crucial for its isomerase activity. , We observed that the CypA R55A mutation significantly restricted the promotive effect of CypA on the vRNA expression of BM1 and BNP ( G). Taken together, our data suggest that CypA facilitates IBV replication and that its PPIase activity is involved in this process.

## CypA interacts with BNS1 and BNP
To investigate the target viral proteins of CypA, LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using B/Yamagata-infected wild-type 293T (293T/CypA+) cell samples ( A). Among 11 viral proteins, only BNS1, BNP, and the polymerase basic protein 1 of IBV (BPB1) were most likely to interact with CypA ( ). These interactions were confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in 293T/CypA+ cells transfected with Myc-tagged viral proteins. We found that only BNS1 and BNP interacted with the endogenous CypA, whereas BPB1, the IBV polymerase basic protein 2 (BPB2), and the IBV polymerase acidic protein (BPA) did not ( B). At the same time, co-IP analysis was performed in 293T/CypA-cells transfected with Myc-CypA and then infected by B/Yamagata. Consistently, CypA was able to interact with BNS1 and BNP during virus infection ( C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence confocal microscopy confirmed the colocalization of CypA with BNS1 or BNP ( D). We further investigated whether the CypA R55A mutation affects the binding of CypA to BNS1 and BNP and found that the mutation significantly inhibited the interaction between CypA and BNS1 ( E) but minimally affected the CypA-BNP interaction ( F). 

## CypA maintains BNS1 and BNP stability by inhibiting proteasomal degradation
Interestingly, we observed that the protein levels of BNS1 and BNP were slightly higher when co-transfected with CypA ( E and 2F). Thus, we sought to examine whether CypA affects the stability of BNS1 and BNP. The 293T/CypA- cells were transfected with HA-BNS1 or FLAG-BNP, together with Myc-CypA or Myc-vector, and then treated with cycloheximide (CHX) for various time periods. The degradation rates of BNS1 and BNP were markedly slower with the presence of overexpressed CypA ( A and 3B). To further investigate which degradation pathways of BNS1 and BNP are affected by CypA, 293T/CypA- cells were transfected with HA-BNS1 or FLAG-BNP and Myc-CypA or Myc-vector, and then treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 or different concentrations of the lysosome inhibitor ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) as well as CHX. We found that MG132 rescued the protein levels of BNS1 and BNP, whereas NH4Cl did not, and the overexpression of CypA resulted in stronger effects of MG132 on the inhibition of BNS1 and BNP degradation ( C and 3D). We also investigated whether the PPIase activity of CypA is critical for its capability to stabilize BNS1 and BNP. Western blot analysis revealed that the R55A mutation overwhelmingly deprived CypA of its inhibitory effect on BNS1 degradation ( E). In contrast, it minimally affected the ability of CypA to stabilize BNP ( F), consistent with its failure to restrict the CypA-BNP interaction. We further determined the effect of CsA on the stability of BNS1 and BNP and found that CsA decreased the stability of BNS1 but not BNP in 293T/CypA+ cells ( ). 
We then investigated the effect of CypA on the ubiquitination of BNS1 and BNP. The results of the ubiquitination assays demonstrated that CypA significantly repressed the overall ubiquitination levels of BNS1 and BNP ( G and 3H). We further determined the effect of CypA on K48- or K63-linked ubiquitination of BNS1 and BNP. CypA overexpression decreased the K48-linked ubiquitination of BNS1 but increased its K63-linked ubiquitination ( I). For BNP, overexpression of CypA remarkedly reduced K48-linked ubiquitination but had no effect on K63-linked ubiquitination ( J). Altogether, these data suggest that CypA inhibits ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of BNS1 and BNP.

## CypA promotes OTUD4-mediated K48-linked BNS1 deubiquitination
We next explored the E3 ligases or deubiquitinases (DUBs) that mediate the ubiquitination or deubiquitination of BNS1. The DUB OTUD4 and the E3 ligases FEM1C, TRIM9, CUL9, TTC3, and FBXL17 ( ) were suggested to interact with BNS1 based on LC-MS/MS. Transient knockdown of OTUD4, FEM1C, CUL9, TTC3, and FBXL17 by siRNAs reduced BNS1 protein levels, and conversely, transient knockdown of TRIM9 by siTRIM9-1 upregulated the BNS1 protein level ( A). We then investigated which protein might be involved in CypA-regulated BNS1 degradation. These E3 ligases or DUBs in 293T/CypA- cells were separately knocked down with the corresponding siRNAs, and then the cells were co-transfected with Myc-BNS1 and FLAG-CypA or FLAG-vector. Western blot analysis showed that only after OTUD4 knockdown, CypA no longer affected BNS1 degradation, and the protein level of BNS1 decreased ( B), indicating that OTUD4 was involved in CypA-regulated BNS1 degradation. The interaction of OTUD4 and BNS1 was then confirmed using co-IPs ( C). 
Next, the effects of OTUD4 on BNS1 degradation and ubiquitination were evaluated. We observed that OTUD4 enhanced the protein levels of BNS1 in a dose-dependent manner ( D). Moreover, OTUD4 significantly inhibited the K48-linked ubiquitination of BNS1 but had little effect on its K63-linked ubiquitination ( E). CypA overexpression enhanced OTUD4-mediated BNS1 deubiquitination ( F). In addition, we found that CypA did not interact with OTUD4 ( A) or affect the interaction between BNS1 and OTUD4 ( B). Tianzi L et al. reported that RNA viruses (Sendai virus and VSV) can induce the expression of OTUD4 by activating IRF3/IRF4 which then translocated into the nucleus and bound to the promoter of OTUD4 and facilitated its expression. We then investigated whether CypA facilitates OTUD4 expression during IBV infection. qPCR ( G) and western blotting ( H) analysis demonstrated that knockdown of CypA significantly decreased both the mRNA and protein levels of OTUD4 in IBV-infected A549 cells, indicating that CypA was able to upregulate OTUD4 expression. Therefore, CypA might enhance OTUD4-mediated K48-linked deubiquitination of BNS1 by upregulating OTUD4 expression.

## CypA inhibits MIB1-mediated K48-linked BNP ubiquitination
Two E3 ligases (MIB1 and TRIM28) and two DUBs (USP10 and USP17L21) were suggested to interact with BNP by LC-MS/MS ( ). The protein levels of ectopically expressed BNP were increased by transient knockdown of MIB1 and decreased by transient knockdown of USP10 and USP17L21, whereas they were minimally influenced by TRIM28 knockdown ( A). We also observed that only after MIB1 knockdown, CypA no longer affected BNP degradation, and the protein level of BNP increased ( B), indicating that MIB1 was involved in CypA-regulated BNP degradation. The results of co-IP assays ( C) further confirmed the interaction between MIB1 and BNP. 
Further, the effects of MIB1 on BNP degradation and ubiquitination were evaluated. MIB1 was found to decrease the protein levels of BNP in a dose-dependent manner ( D). Additionally, MIB1 significantly enhanced K48-linked BNP ubiquitination but had little effect on K63-linked BNP ubiquitination ( E). We also observed that CypA overexpression inhibited MIB1-mediated BNP ubiquitination ( F).
To investigate whether CypA affects the interaction between MIB1 and BNP, 293T/CypA- cells were transfected with FLAG-BNP and HA-MIB1 with or without Myc-CypA. We found that CypA inhibited the interaction between MIB1 and BNP ( G). Meanwhile, the results of CypA (PDB: 3K0M)-BNP (PDB: 3TJ0) docking and MIB1 substrate-recognition domain (MZM-REP, PDB: 4XI6)-BNP docking using HADDOCK (v2.4) predict that CypA and MIB1 may interact with BNP on the same domain ( H and ), suggesting that CypA and MIB1 competitively interact with BNP, thereby inhibiting ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of BNP.

## BNP hijacks CypA into the nucleus to enhance the activity of vRNP complexes
CypA is mainly located in the cytoplasm and can be secreted by cells. Nevertheless, in spinal neurons, CypA can also be translocated to the nucleus and is functionally involved in the assembly of TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (known as TDP-43 or TARDBP) into heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein complexes. Interestingly, we noticed that CypA was mainly located in the cytoplasm but was generously translocated to the nucleus when co-transfected with FLAG-BNP ( F). We speculated that BNP might hijack CypA into the nucleus. To test this hypothesis, we first compared the subcellular location of CypA with or without BNP co-transfection at different time points and discovered that BNP promoted the translocation of CypA from the cytoplasm to the nucleus ( A and ), which was supported by the analysis of separated nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of the cells ( B). In addition, BNS1 was mainly localized in the nucleus at 12 h and relocalized to the cytoplasm at 24 h post-transfection, whereas BNP were mainly localized in the nucleus and CypA were mainly localized in the cytoplasm at both 12 h and 24 h post-transfection ( ). When CypA was co-transfected with BNS1, they had no effect on each other’s localization. However, when CypA was co-transfected with BNP, CypA was translocated into the nucleus from the cytoplasm ( ). These results suggested that BNP was able to affect the localization of CypA, but BNS1 was not. 
vRNPs, composed of viral genetic material, NP, and viral polymerases (PB1, PB2, and PA subunits), are the implementing units of viral transcription and replication. It was therefore necessary to investigate whether CypA could affect the activity of IBV vRNP complexes. Co-IP assays demonstrated that CypA enhances the interaction between BNP and BPB1 ( C), whereas it had little effect on the BNP-BPB2 interaction ( D) and BNP self-polymerization ( E). Thereafter, we examined the vRNP complex activity with or without MG132 treatment to remove the inhibitory effect of CypA on BNP degradation and found that CypA increased vRNP complex activity ( F). Collectively, these data illustrate that BNP hijacks CypA to the nucleus to promote vRNP complex activity by enhancing the BNP-BPB1 interaction.

## CsA suppresses IBV replication in vivo
We wondered whether CypA promotes IBV replication in vivo. To investigate this, mice were intraperitoneally injected with the CypA inhibitor CsA or placebo 12 h prior to intranasal infection with IBV. Additional injections of CsA or placebo were administered every 48 h. The results of plaque assays indicated that CsA inhibited IBV replication in mouse lungs at 1 and 3 days p.i. ( A). Similarly, the vRNA levels of BM1 and BNP ( B) and the protein levels of BNP ( ) in the lungs of CsA-treated mice were significantly lower than those of the placebo-treated mice. In addition, CsA treatment alleviated IBV-induced lung inflammation in mice, as reflected by lower pathological scores ( C) and lung indices ( D) of lung tissue from CsA-treated mice compared to placebo-treated mice. The less severe inflammation in the CsA-treated mice was also evidenced by the continuously increasing body weights, whereas the body weights of the placebo-treated mice significantly decreased from 0 to 7 days p.i. and then were gradually restored ( E). Taken together, these results confirm that CsA-mediated CypA PPIase inhibition can suppress IBV replication in vivo. 

## Discussion
Given that IBV is a non-negligible component of seasonal influenza epidemics, resulting in a significant disease burden and socio-economic impact, there is an urgent need to expand the current knowledge of IBV and to explore valuable antiviral therapeutic strategies. Here, we demonstrate that CypA maintains the stability of two IBV proteins, BNS1 and BNP, by facilitating K48-linked deubiquitination and inhibiting K48-linked ubiquitination, respectively. Our study extends current knowledge of the IBV-host interaction and reveals an important mechanism by which the host factor, CypA, promotes IBV replication, suggesting that CypA is an attractive therapeutic target for IBV infection.
It is well-known that host-virus interactions orchestrate the viral life cycle. , Many studies have been designed to effectively identify host factors that are utilized by virus. For example, Watanabe et al. provide a comprehensive mapping of IAV-host protein interactions and further find that some of these host factors affect viral genome replication and transcription, as well as the intracellular localization of viral proteins. Günther et al. find that the intracellular cysteine protease cathepsin W cooperates with its host target proteins to facilitate IAV entry. Patzina et al. identified 22 human proteins that interact with BNS1 by affinity-purification and mass spectrometry. They confirmed the interaction of BNS1 between DHX9, ILF3, YBX1, and HNRNPC by GST-pulldown analysis and revealed the dual role of ILF3 in IFN induction and IBV replication. However, the specific mechanisms by which these host factors affect the virus replication remain to be elucidated to aid in the development of anti-IBV drugs.
There are two approved classes of anti-influenza drugs, M2 ion-channel inhibitors (amantadine and rimantadine) , and neuraminidase inhibitors (oral oseltamivir, inhaled zanamivir, intravenous peramivir, and inhaled laninamivir ), of which only the latter have antiviral activity against IBV. These drugs mainly target viral proteins. However, genetic mutations grant influenza viruses drug resistance , and the ability to evade antibody-mediated viral neutralization, highlighting the need to explore conserved drug targets and design next-generation antivirals that avoid drug resistance. Relatively conserved host factors involved in viral replication may be promising alternatives. CypA is a ubiquitous host factor that acts as a pro-viral component during the infection of several viruses (e.g., HIV, HCV, HBV, and CoV). Here, we demonstrated that CypA is also critical for IBV replication through its interaction with and stabilization of viral proteins. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that CypA inhibitors are effective against infections with multiple viruses (e.g., alisporivir for HBV; CsA for HIV, VV, and HCMV; and Debio 025, NIM811, and SCY-635 for HCV , , , , ). In this study, our data demonstrate that the inhibition of CypA by its PPIase activity inhibitor CsA effectively prevented IBV replication both in vitro and in vivo ( D–1G and ). In conclusion, our study highlights the value of CypA as a promising therapeutic target for IBV infection.
Ubiquitin modifications are dynamic, versatile, and involved in many aspects of biological reactions, including protein degradation, activation, and localization. Further, ubiquitination and deubiquitination of proteins are essential for the regulation of virus replication. Ubiquitination of IAV NP by CNOT4 (Ccr4-Not transcription complex subunit 4) promotes vRNA replication, whereas its ubiquitination by tripartite motif containing 22 (TRIM22) suppresses IAV replication. What is more, James et al. demonstrate that all proteins in the IAV RNP complex can be ubiquitinated, which is critical for IAV polymerase function. In the present study, six and four candidates that may be directly involved in the regulation of BNS1 ( ) and BNP ( ) ubiquitination, respectively, were identified by LC-MS/MS. We validated that the DUB OTUD4 can mediate the K48-linked deubiquitination of BNS1 and the E3 ligase MIB1 can mediate the K48-linked ubiquitination of BNP. It is likely that the remaining candidates can also regulate the ubiquitination of these viral proteins, and thus, further studies are needed to explore whether they are essential in the viral life cycle.
Several studies have revealed the critical role of CypA-regulated ubiquitination in virus infection and host immune responses. For example, CypA regulates the ubiquitination of IAV M1, either leading to its nuclear retention or promoting its degradation. , CypA inhibits cCbl-mediated, K48-linked FAK ubiquitination, which facilitates IAV-triggered group A Streptococcus coinfection. In addition, CypA facilitates pro-IL-1β processing by increasing Smurf1-mediated K63-linked ubiquitination and also accelerates pro-IL-1β degradation through Smurf1-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination. In this study, we demonstrated that CypA enhanced OTUD4-mediated BNS1 deubiquitination and inhibited MIB1-mediated BNP ubiquitination, thereby maintaining the stability of BNS1 and BNP. In addition, we found that CypA both reduced BNS1 K48-linked ubiquitination and increased its K63-ubiquitination ( I), which is valuable to explore in future studies. These data highlight the importance of CypA in regulating protein ubiquitination and indicate the need for further investigation of the relationship between CypA and ubiquitin modifications.
CypA normally resides in the cytoplasm to carry out its physiological functions. However, it can be secreted outside the cell or translocated to the plasma membrane or nucleus in some cases, particularly in response to stimulation. For example, CypA can be secreted through a vesicular pathway in response to reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipopolysaccharide, promoting inflammation, cell growth, and apoptosis. , CypA translocation to the plasma membrane in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) can be detected under oxidative stress, which promotes the translocation of the NADPH subunit p47phox to caveolae to enhance angiotensin II-induced ROS generation. In addition, it is reported that CypA can interact with apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF), and the two proteins move together to the nucleus during the peripheral chromatin condensation stage accompanying apoptosis. , Moreover, Lauranzano et al. find that CypA co-immunoprecipitated with proteins that are components of several heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein complexes, and thus plays an important role in their assembly and may even be involved in the architecture of RNA-binding protein complexes. Here, we also find that CypA can translocate from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, together with BNP, and enhance the interaction between BNP and BPB1, both of which are key components of vRNP complexes. This finding reveals an important mechanism by which CypA affects IBV replication specifically in the nucleus.
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that CypA is a key host factor that actively regulates IBV replication by inhibiting the proteasomal degradation of BNS1 and BNP. Additionally, CypA can be hijacked by BNP to facilitate vRNP activity in nuclei. Thus, CypA is a promising drug target for antiviral drugs against IBV. Three alternative drug design strategies can be considered: (1) disruption of the interaction between CypA and BNP or BNS1; (2) targeted degradation of CypA; and (3) inhibition of CypA PPIase activity. What is more, we have investigated the potential of the CypA inhibitor, CsA, to suppress IBV infection both in vitro and in vivo. These observations highlight the potential broad therapeutic value of CypA as an antiviral drug target.

## Limitations of the study
We demonstrate that CypA promotes IBV replication by targeting BNS1 and BNP. In addition, CsA, an inhibitor of CypA PPIase activity, inhibits the binding of CypA to BNS1 but not BNP. In this study, we only investigated the effect of CsA on IBV replication. The antiviral effects of other drugs targeting CypA-BNP interaction are worthy of further study.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Mice
All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and complied with the Beijing Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethical Guidelines of the Beijing Administration Committee of Laboratory Animals. Six-week-old female BALB/c mice were used and maintained in a barrier facility with free access to food and water.

## Cell lines and cell culture
The 293T cells (human embryonic kidney cells, ATCC CRL-3216) and A549 cells (human lung carcinoma cells, ATCC CCL-185) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and shRNA-based CypA knockdown in 293T (293T/CypA-) and A549 (A549/CypA-) cells was previously described. MDCK cells (Madin-Darby canine kidney cells, ATCC CCL-34) were used to determine IBV titers. Cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a MycoAlert mycoplasma detection kit (Lonza, Switzerland) and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, GIBCO) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, GIBCO), 100 U/mL penicillin (Sigma), and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Sigma) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

## Virus strains
The influenza B virus B/Yamagata/16/1988 strain was generated using an eight-plasmid reverse genetic system and propagated in the allantoic cavities of 10-day-old specific pathogen-free embryonated chicken eggs at 33°C.

## Method details

## Virus titration
Plaque assays were used to determine IBV titers. MDCK cells were seeded in 12-well plates for 16 h, and then cell monolayers were washed with PBS and inoculated with 10-fold serially diluted viruses with three replicates of each dilution for 1–2 h at 33°C. The supernatants were removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS and overlaid with phenol red-free DMEM containing 1% low-melting-temperature agarose and 1 mg/mL L-(tosylamido-2-phenyl) ethyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin. After incubation for 72–96 h at 33°C with 5% CO2, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet after removal of the agarose. IBV titers were determined based on visible plaques.

## RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega) and an oligo (dT) primer or the specific primers for BNP and BM1 cDNA. Relative RNA levels were analyzed by qPCR using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara). The primers are listed in . The Ct values generated by an ABI 7500 software were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The relative expression of the target gene was normalized to that of GAPDH.

## Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblot analysis
The 293T cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids and lysed in lysis buffer supplemented with a complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics). For immunoprecipitation, cell lysates were incubated with beads (the Bimake anti-Myc magnetic beads or the Thermo Scientific™Pierce™Anti-DYKDDDDK Magnetic Agarose) at 4°C. After incubation, the beads were washed with washing buffer, and the precipitates were resuspended with 1× loading buffer. The precipitates and whole-cell lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies.

## Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)
The 293T cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 24 hours, and were lysed in lysis buffer supplemented with a complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics). The target proteins were immunoprecipitated with anti-Myc magnetic beads (Bimake) and subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. The proteins on the lanes of SDS-PAGE gels were digested with trypsin and subjected to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis at the Institutional Center for Shared Technologies and Facilities of Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.
LC separation was conducted using a Thermo Scientific™ EASY-nLC™ 1000 HPLC system. Mobile phases were composed of [A] water (0.1% formic acid) and [B] acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid). Peptides were desalted online by an in-house packed trap column (C18, 5 m particles, 150μm ID, 2 cm length, Dr. Maisch GmbH, Ammerbuch-Entringen, Germany). The trapped peptides were loaded onto an in-house packed reversed-phase C18 column, 150 μm ID x 15 cm, 1.9 μm, 100 Å. The analytical gradient for the sample was from 3-10% B in 3 min, from10-22% B over 40 min, from 22-35% B over 8 min, from 35-90% B over 4 min and 90% B over 5 min. Flow rate for analytical gradients was 500 nL/min.
All samples were analyzed using a Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer. Data was acquired on the Orbitrap Fusion MS using a resolution of 120,000 (@ 200 m/z) for full MS scans (m/z range 350–1550, 1ⅹ106 AGC target, and 50 ms maximal injection time) and using a resolution of 150,000 for data-dependent MS/ MS (fragmentation HCD; 33% collision energy, detector type: Orbitrap; 1.0E+05AGC Target; 50 ms maximal injection time; 25sec dynamic Exclusion), respectively.
All MS/MS samples were confidently identified using Mascot (Matrix Science, London, UK; version 2.8.0). Mascot was set up to search the Influenza virus database (128457 entries) download from Uniprot assuming the digestion enzyme trypsin. For LC-MS/MS screen for E3 ligases and DUBs of BNP or BNS1, the Mascot search engine was applied to search for the matching peptides among sequences from the iUUCD 2.0 database (http://iuucd.biocuckoo.org), which contains protein sequences of key regulators in the modulation of ubiquitin and ubiquitin-like (UB/UBL) conjugations. Mascot was searched with a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da and a parent ion tolerance of 10.0 PPM respectively. Carbamidomethyl (C) of IAM was specified in Mascot as fixed modifications. Oxidation (M) of DTT and Ubiquitination (K) were specified in Mascot as variable modifications. False discovery rate (FDR) thresholds for protein, peptide, and modification site were adjusted to <1%. p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. p values were corrected for FDR in each dataset. All the other parameters in Mascot were set to default values.

## Immunofluorescence assay
The 293T cells grown in 24-well cell culture plates with cover slides were transfected with CypA-, BNS1-, or BNP-expressing plasmids. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at the indicated time points overnight at room temperature. Cells were then permeabilized with PBST (0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) at room temperature for 30 min, incubated with 0.4% BSA in PBST at 37°C for 1 h, and further incubated with anti-FLAG or anti-Myc antibodies diluted in PBST (containing 0.2% BSA) at 37°C for 1 h. After washing with PBST five times for 10 min each, the cells were incubated with TRITC-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit or mouse antibody or FITC-conjugated donkey anti-mouse or rabbit antibody diluted in PBST (containing 0.2% BSA) at 37°C for 1 h and washed with PBST five times for 10 min each. Finally, the cells were incubated with the nuclear stain DAPI for 10 min. The intracellular locations of CypA, BNS1, and BNP were visualized with a confocal laser scanning fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8).

## Protein-protein docking
E3 ligase/DUB-BNP docking was performed using the HADDOCK (v2.4) web server, and the intermolecular interactions were visualized in a two-dimensional (2D) diagram using LigPlot+ and a three-dimensional (3D) model using PyMOL (https://pymol.org, Schrödinger Inc.). The docking models are depicted by the 2D diagram generated by LigPlot+.

## Subcellular fractionation
Cells were harvested via scraping into 500 mL cell lysis buffer containing 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM EDTA with complete protease inhibitor cocktail. Then, subcellular fractionation was performed on ice according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit, Product No. P0027). The separated cytoplasmic protein samples and nuclear protein samples were analyzed by western blotting with rabbit anti-CypA, mouse anti-FLAG, mouse anti-GAPDH, and mouse anti-TBP antibodies, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or mouse IgG secondary antibody.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Data are presented as the mean values ± the standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-tailed Student’s t test, with p < 0.05 being considered significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001).
