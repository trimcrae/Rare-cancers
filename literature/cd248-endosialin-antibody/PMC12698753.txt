## Purpose
Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are major constituents of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and have been associated with chemotherapeutic failure via different mechanisms. However, the CAFs inhibit chemotherapy mechanism in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains undetermined.

## Methods
Fibroblasts were isolated from tumor and normal lung tissues from patients with poorly differentiated LUAD (pCAFs), moderately differentiated LUAD (mCAFs), and normal fibroblasts (NFs). Then, the influence of these fibroblasts on carboplatin’s cytotoxic effects on LUAD cell lines A549 and NCI-H1299 was assessed by measuring their IC50 values. Furthermore, CXCL12 secretion and its role in chemotherapeutics were also evaluated.

## Results
The data revealed that pCAFs significantly inhibited apoptosis in LUAD cells and increased carboplatin IC50 values. Furthermore, pCAFs secreted higher CXCL12 content than mCAFs and NFs. Moreover, in pCAFs, CXCL12 silencing enhanced carboplatin’s cytotoxic effects, while NFs overexpressing CXCL12 inhibited carboplatin’s efficacy. Mechanistically, pCAFs promote the secretion of CXCL12 by activating the NF-κB pathway, and CXCL12 binds to CXCR4 on LUAD cells, thereby promoting carboplatin resistance. Moreover, in the xenograft models, pCAFs were found to reduce carboplatin’s cytotoxicity by CXCL12 secretion. Moreover, the analysis of the LUAD patient’s tumor and peripheral blood sample indicated a correlation between lower differentiation and higher CXCL12 expression levels.

## Conclusion
This study revealed that LUAD-derived CAFs activate the NF-κB axis to secrete CXCL12, thereby weakening the carboplatin’s killing effect on LUAD. Furthermore, poorly differentiated LUAD secreted more CXCL12. These findings indicate a novel strategy to enhance carboplatin’s chemotherapeutic potential against LUAD.

## Introduction
Lung cancer is a malignancy with a high fatality rate. The most prevalent pathological type of lung cancer is lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [ ], accounting for about 40% of non-small cell lung cancer cases [ ]. The fifth edition of the “WHO Classification of Tumors of the Thoracic System” (published in May 2011) categorized invasive non-mucinous adenocarcinoma (INMA) into five types [ ]. The INMA classification was based on histological characteristics: lepidic growth pattern adenocarcinoma (LPA), acinar type adenocarcinoma (APA), papillary adenocarcinoma (PPA), solid type adenocarcinoma (SPA), and micropapillary adenocarcinoma (MPA). Of these, PPA or SPA is classified as poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, has a proportion of ≥ 20%, is prone to hematogenous and lymphatic metastasis, and has a poor treatment prognosis, which poses a serious threat to patient health [ ]. Immunotherapy and targeted therapy for LUAD have the limitations of postoperative recurrence and limited benefits, indicating the irreplaceable role of chemotherapy for LUAD treatment [ ]. In clinical practice, chemotherapy drug resistance is unavoidable and can severely affect patient prognosis and survival [ ].
The tumor microenvironment (TME) is the habitat for tumor cells [ ]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) have been observed to primarily originate from the activation of normal fibroblasts surrounding cancer cells [ ]. In TME, CAFs are the predominant stromal cells. Quiescent fibroblasts are typically spindle-shaped and, upon activation, express substantial amounts of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) [ ] and fibroblast activation protein-α (FAP) [ ]. Furthermore, CAFs can secrete various substances, such as extracellular matrix (ECM) degrading enzymes, growth factors, cytokines, chemokines [ ], and various metabolic products. These substances may promote tumor progression, occurrence, and metastasis [ ]. Moreover, the different cytokines secreted by these substances are markedly related to drug resistance in malignant tumors.
CXCL12 (stromal cell-derived factor-1; SDF-1) belongs to the family of small chemokines. Chemokines have been observed to modulate cell migration [ ]. Previous literature suggests that CXCL12 is substantially expressed in CAFs of bladder cancer, promoting tumor growth and significantly upregulating PDL-1 [ ]. Furthermore, CXCL12 is associated with poor prognosis and later clinical stages [ ]. In tumor tissues, CXCL12 expression is often reduced, whereas its expression is high in CAFs [ ], indicating the close relationship between CAFs and CXCL12. Studies suggest that CXCL12 can accelerate tumor growth and metastasis via the CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling pathway [ ]. Moreover, CXCL12 has been found to inhibit T-cell infiltration within tumors [ ]. Therefore, CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway inhibition can enhance the checkpoint inhibitor’s efficacy, making this combination therapy a promising cancer treatment strategy [ ]. Our previous study revealed that CD248-expressing CAFs can induce cisplatin resistance in NSCLC patients by secreting IL-8 [ ]. Similarly, CXCL12 derived from CD248-expressing CAFs induces M2 macrophage polarization, promoting the progression of NSCLC [ ].
In this research study, in vivo, and in vitro analyses were carried out to validate that CXCL12 overexpressing pCAFs and NFs can secrete higher levels of CXCL12, thereby inhibiting the carboplatin’s cytotoxicity against LUAD. CXCL12 silencing in pCAFs can reduce the cytotoxicity of carboplatin against LUAD compared to wild-type pCAFs. Furthermore, pCAFs secrete more CXCL12 via the NF-κB axis. Therefore, NF-κB inhibitor Bortezomib, PDTC, and the p65 nuclear translocation inhibitor SN50 reduced CAFs-secreted CXCL12 by inhibiting the NF-κB axis. The CXCR4 inhibitor AMD3100 (Plerixafor) can reduce the carboplatin cytotoxic effects on LUAD by inhibiting the binding of CXCL12 to CXCR4. Moreover, the analysis of LUAD patient’s peripheral blood, tumors, and adjacent tissues revealed that reduced differentiation in LUAD can secrete more CXCL12. The findings suggest that CXCL12 is highly expressed in poorly differentiated LUAD and modulates carboplatin resistance, providing new insights into strategies to reverse carboplatin resistance in LUAD patients.

## Materials and methods

## Study approval
NSCLC and matched healthy specimens collection was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University and conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations. Informed consent was obtained from each patient, and the study protocol (no. 2024LL-164) was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University and complied with all relevant ethical guidelines. Animal procedures were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Guizhou Medical University (no. 2400397). All experiments were performed according to the relevant guidelines, regulations, and ARRIVE guidelines.

## Human tumor specimens
The ethical approval and LUAD patient’s tissues and peripheral blood were obtained from The Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. All the subjects were first informed about the study and then their consents were acquired.The pathological information of patients was compiled into tables and placed in Supplementary Table 1.

## Mice
BALB/c nude mice (male, 5‒6 weeks old, 18–20 g) were procured from the Hunan Silek Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd and kept in a pathogen-free room, in individual cages, at 12-h dark/light cycle, 22 ± 1 °C, with ad libitum access to a standard laboratory diet, and in 55% ± 5% relative humidity. At the time of experimentation, the mice were aged 4–12 weeks. Guizhou Medical University authorized all animal protocols.

## Cell lineages and co-culture assay
The human LUAD cell lineages NCI-H1299-luciferase (NCI-H1299-L) and A549-luciferase (A549-L) were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling confirmed that the cells were not contaminated by Mycoplasma. The cells were propagated in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium augmented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10% FBS in a 5% CO2 at 37 °C.
For fibroblast extraction and cultivation, a previously defined protocol was followed [ , ]. pCAFs originated from a 58-year-old non-smoking female, with pathological results indicating invasive lung adenocarcinoma (Micropapillary pattern adenocarcinoma 40%; Acinar pattern adenocarcinoma 50%; Papillary pattern adenocarcinoma 10%). mCAFs were derived from a 57-year-old smoking male, with pathological results showing invasive lung adenocarcinoma (Acinar pattern adenocarcinoma 100%). NFs were sourced from lung tissues located over 3 cm from the tumor.
Furthermore, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12#1, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12#2, and pCAFs-sh-CXCL12#3 cells with stable CXCL12 suppression as well as NFs-CXCL12 cells with CXCL12 overexpression (NFs-CXCL12 OE) were generated by infecting cells with lentivirus. The human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK-293T) was acquired from Genewiz (Shanghai), the cells were propagated in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10% FBS in a 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

## Western blot
Whole cellular protein was harvested with the help of RIPA buffer, separated by SDS–PAGE, visualized via MultiImage, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked for 2 h at RT using 5% BSA in TBST, and treated with the following primary antibodies: anti-CXCL12 (Abcam, #ab155090), anti-α-smooth muscle actin(anti-α-SMA) (Servicebio, #GB111364), anti-IKK-α (CST, #11930), anti-IKK-β (CST, #8943), anti-pIKK-α/β (CST, #2697), anti-P65 (CST, #8242), anti-pP65 (CST, #3033), anti-β-actin (Servicebio, #GB15003),Anti-Vimentin (Servicebio, #11192), PDGRFβ (Thermo Fisher,#MA5-15143), FAP (CST, # 66562T), Rabbit anti-CXCR7 (Absin, #111743), CXCR4 (CST, #64837T), Caspase-3 (CST, #14220T). The proteins were then probed with associated HRP-linked secondary antibody (Servicebio, #GB23303; #GB23301). The enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) assay (Thermo Fisher) was employed to visualize the antigen-antibody reaction.

## Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
For whole RNA isolation, the MiniBEST Universal RNA Isolation Kit (Takara, #9767, San Jose, CA, USA) was employed. Then, the acquired RNA was transformed into cDNA via PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, #RR063A) amplified via qPCR using a TB Green Premix Ex Taq II Kit (Takara, #RR820). The Supplementary Table 2 enlists the primer sequences employed.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) stainings
For IF evaluations, the primary antibodies employed included anti-α-SMA (Abcam, #ab5694), PDGRFβ (ThermoFisher,#MA5-15143), FAP (CST, # 66562T), anti-p65 (CST, #8242), anti-CXCL12 (CST, #ab155090), TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, #C1089), and Ki-67 (Abcam, #ab16667).
Briefly, the immunofluorescent cells were preserved using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at RT, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, occluded at RT in 5% BSA for 2 h, and then treated overnight with antibodies against α-SMA (1:150) and CXCL12 (1:100) at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were probed for 1 h with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio) at RT. The nuclei were counterstained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), and the images were acquired via a laser scanning confocal microscope.
For TUNEL and Ki-67 analyses, cell slides were treated with Proteinase K at room temperature for 20 min, followed by 1 h incubation in TUNEL or Ki-67 assay buffer at 37 °C in the dark. For nuclear staining, diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was employed. Images were captured using laser scanning confocal microscopy from 6 random fields of view/group at 400× magnification to determine the total number of Ki-67 or TUNEL-positive cells.
The tyramide signal amplification (TSA) technique was employed to elucidate α-SMA, FAP, PDGRFβ and CXCL12 colocalization and expression in tumor sections via multiple IF labeling. Briefly, the slides were blocked in 5% BSA for 2 h at RT, reacted with 3% H2O2 for 15 min, and treated overnight with primary antibody for 1 h at 4 °C. Then, the slides were incubated for 50 with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at RT, washed in TBST, exposed to TSA fluorophores at RT for 10 min, and then boiled in EDTA antigen retrieval buffer to remove antibody-TSA complexes. These steps were conducted for each antibody. DAPI-counterstained nuclei were observed via a Laser-scanning confocal microscope.
The H-score is a semi-quantitative method for evaluating the intensity and distribution of protein expression in tumor cells, calculated using the following formula: H-score=(Percentage of weak-positive cells×1)+(Percentage of moderate-positive cells×2)+(Percentage of strong-positive cells×3).The theoretical range is from 0 to 300, where 0 indicates no staining and 300 represents 100% of the cells showing strong positive staining. The criteria for defining staining intensity are as follows: Weak positive (1+): Weak staining that is slightly discernible above background level and visible under high power (400x magnification). Moderate positive (2+): Clearly visible moderate intensity staining under low to medium power (100x-200x magnification).Strong positive (3+): Dark brown intense staining that is clearly visible even under low power (40x magnification).A blind evaluation procedure was implemented during the scoring phase to minimize observer bias. To quantify inter-rater reliability, all slides were evaluated by two pathologists who calculated the percentage areas of weak, moderate, and strong positivity and took the average value. If there was a significant discrepancy between their assessments, both judges jointly re-evaluated the slides.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The LUAD patient’s peripheral blood (5 mL) was treated with sodium citrate anticoagulant for 10–20 min and centrifuged for 20 min at 2500 rpm to collect the supernatant. Subsequently, the corresponding ELISA kit (Wuhan Saipei Biological, SP#16109) and related instructions were used to detect serum CXCL12 expression.

## Mice tumor experiments
The A549-L or NCI-H1299-L cells (8 × 106) were mixed with NFs, mCAFs, pCAFs, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12, or NFs-CXCL12 OE (8 × 106) to generate tumor cells and CAFs mixed models in BALB/c nude mice. After tumor development, the mice were injected with two 200 µL doses of carboplatin (30–50 µg/mL) via the tail vein. Then, the tumor-bearing mice were administered substrate (D-luciferin) and isoflurane anesthesia and evaluated via an in vivo fluorescence imaging system. Data were assessed using Living Image software. The animals were euthanized, and their tumors were dissected, measured, imaged, and then submerged in paraffin for IF analysis (CXCL12, TUNEL and Ki-67) and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

## Flow cytometry
Cells were treated with specified chemotherapeutic agents for 48 h and then harvested by centrifugation. To examine apoptosis, the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, #550889, Haryana, India) was employed. Briefly, cells were treated with binding buffer (BB; 100 µL) + FITC Annexin V (5 µL) and PI (5 µL) at RT without light for 15 min. Then, 400 µL BB was introduced, and apoptotic analysis was carried out by flow cytometry.

## Luciferase assay
HEK-293T cells were transfected with 200 ng pGL3-CXCL12 plasmid, or pcDNA3.1 plasmid with 200 ng p65 plasmid or negative control plasmid. An analysis of firefly luciferase activity versus Renilla luciferase activity was carried out by co-transfecting the cells with 50 ng Renilla plasmid. Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, #E2920) was used to measure Luciferase activity 48 h after the transfection.

## RNA sequencing and GO and KEGG pathway assessment
Total RNA was extracted from NFs, mCAFs, pCAFs cells using TRIzol reagent. RNA integrity was verified (RIN > 8.0, Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100). Libraries were prepared with poly(A) selection and sequenced (Illumina NovaSeq 6000; 150-bp paired-end) by Genechem Co. (Shanghai). Differentially expressed genes (|log₂FC| >1, FDR < 0.05) were analyzed via GO enrichment (Gene Ontology Consortium) and KEGG pathway mapping (KEGG PATHWAY Database). The expression level normalization for RNA-seq was performed using the FPKM algorithm, which stands for Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads. This method takes into account both the sequencing depth and the gene length when calculating read counts, and it is currently the most commonly used method for estimating gene expression levels. Differential analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 software (version 1.16.1) for differential expression analysis between two comparison groups (with two biological replicates for each group). DESeq2 provides a statistical framework that utilizes a negative binomial distribution model to identify differentially expressed genes in count data. The Benjamini and Hochberg method was used to adjust the resulting p-values to control the false discovery rate.

## Statistical analysis
All the data were elucidated via Excel 2016. Before the analysis, equality, and normality tests of variance were carried out for quantitative data. A two-tailed unpaired Student t-test was used to compare the variablesof two groups, a two-tailed paired Student t-test to compare dynamic variables of the same indicator at different stages in the same patient, and one-way or two-way ANOVA were performed for multi-group comparisons.Results are depicted as mean ± SEM or mean ± SD. p-values were assessed via two-sided t-tests, and the differences were deemed statistically significant at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

## Results

## CAFs derived from poorly differentiated LUAD can more effectively reduce carboplatin’s cytotoxic effect
The tumor-associated fibroblasts and normal fibroblasts were extracted from tumor tissues of moderately and poorly differentiated LUAD, as well as from normal lung tissues, named mCAFs, pCAFs, and NFs, respectively (Fig.  A). To elucidate whether pCAFs mediate carboplatin resistance in NSCLC, LUAD cells were treated with CM from NFs, mCAFs, and pCAFs before exposure to varying carboplatin doses. It was observed that pCAFs-CM treated A549 and NCI-H1299 cells had enhanced carboplatin IC50 relative to cells treated with NFs-CM, mCAFs-CM, and carboplatin alone (p < 0.01) (Fig.  B-C). Furthermore, the colony formation assay showed that pCAFs-CM-treated cells had stronger resistance to carboplatin than other groups (p < 0.05) (Fig.  D-E). Moreover, flow cytometry revealed that these pCAFs-CM treated cells had elevated cell apoptosis relative to the untreated group and the cells treated with CM from NFs and mCAFs (p < 0.05) (Fig.  F-G).
To elucidate whether pCAFs promote carboplatin chemoresistance in LUAD in vivo, 8 × 106 pCAFs, mCAFs, and NFs were combined with 8 × 106 A549 cells carrying lentiviruses and then administered to mice. The animals were then treated with 200 µL carboplatin (30 µg/mL) (Fig.  H). Tumors were measured using bioluminescence imaging (BLI), and tumor fluorescence intensity was also assessed, which revealed that relative to the combination of A549-L cells with mCAFs and NFs, the combination of A549-L and pCAFs cells had stronger fluorescence (p < 0.05) (Fig.  I-J). The tumor tissue samples showed the pCAF group tumors were more developed than those of other groups (Fig.  K-L). Altogether, these data suggest that pCAFs can weaken carboplatin’s cytotoxic effect on LUAD.

 

## pCAFs can secrete more CXCL12
To identify the differences among the three types of fibroblasts, RNA sequencing was performed on NFs, mCAFs, and pCAFs. It is well known that CAFs can secrete various cytokines, and pCAFs can promote carboplatin resistance in LUAD more effectively than NFs and mCAFs in vitro with their complete culture medium. Therefore, we hypothesized that a certain cytokine derived from pCAFs plays a significant role in the resistance process. Consequently, we analyzed cytokine-related genes from the RNA-seq data and generated a heatmap, which indicated that multiple genes, including CXCL12, were enriched in pCAFs (Fig.  A). Next, we used qPCR to detect the levels of 11 cytokines, including CXCL12. The results showed that the relative expression level of CXCL12 in pCAFs was significantly higher than that of other candidate factors (upregulated > 8.2 times compared to NFs, p < 0.001) (Fig.  B). Furthermore, CXCL12 expression enhanced with the degree of CAF infiltration (Fig.  C-D). The single-cell data retrieval from databases indicated that fibroblasts comprise a certain proportion of NSCLC, and CXCL12 is mainly derived from CAFs (Fig.  E-F). In NSCLC samples, CXCL12 was primarily expressed on fibroblasts and malignant tumor cells, as well as in endothelial cells to some extent, while other immune cell types had reduced expression (Fig.  G). Western blotting was conducted to determine CXCL12 protein levels in NFs, mCAFs, and pCAFs. It was observed that Vimentin, FAP, PDGFRβ and α-SMA were expressed in NFs, mCAFs, and pCAFs, whereas the expression level of CXCL12 was increased in pCAFs (Fig.  H). In addition, IF staining revealed that all the fibroblast types expressed FAP, PDGFRβ, α-SMA and CXCL12, with pCAFs exhibiting the highest CXCL12 expression (Fig.  I). These results indicated that CXCL12 originates from CAFs, and pCAFs can secrete more CXCL12.

 

## CXCL12 inhibited carboplatin killing in LUAD
This study also established CXCL12 overexpressing NFs cells, called NFs-CXCL12 OE. qPCR analysis of CXCL12 expression in these cells revealed substantially elevated levels of CXCL12 in NFs-CXCL12 OE than that in NFs (p < 0.0001) (Fig.  A). These data were also verified by Western blotting, indicating raised CXCL12 protein levels in the NFs-CXCL12 OE (Fig.  B). Moreover, CXCL12 was silenced in pCAFs using a lentivirus, which yielded pCAFs-sh-CXCL12#1, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12#2, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12#3. CXCL12 silencing was confirmed via qPCR, which showed that CXCL12 expression was decreased in the silenced cells relative to the pCAFs (p < 0.0001) (Fig.  C), which was also verified by Western blotting (Fig.  D). The IF analysis of the three CXCL12 silenced pCAFs groups, NFs-CXCL12 OE, pCAFs, and NFs cells showed that all three pCAFs-sh-CXCL12 groups had markedly reduced CXCL12 levels, while CXCL12 expression in NFs-CXCL12 OE not only far exceeded that in NFs but was also very close to that in pCAFs (Fig.  E). Therefore, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12#2 was chosen for further experiments.
To verify that CXCL12 promotes carboplatin resistance in LUAD, we first conducted CCK-8 assays in which A549 and NCI-H1299 cells were either treated with rhCXCL12 or not, and then exposed to various doses of carboplatin. The results showed that the carboplatin IC50 values for A549 and NCI-H1299 cells treated with rhCXCL12 were significantly increased (p < 0.01) (Fig.  F-G). Subsequently, we cultured A549 and NCI-H1299 cells either alone, or treated only with carboplatin, or treated with both carboplatin and rhCXCL12, and then detected cleaved/total caspase-3 levels using Western blot analysis. The results indicated that rhCXCL12 can reduce apoptosis in LUAD cells caused by carboplatin (Fig.  H).To verify that CXCL12 secreted by fibroblasts also plays a role in the carboplatin resistance mechanism of LUAD, in vitro analyses were carried out. Briefly, A549 and NCI-H1299 cells were conditioned with media from NFs, pCAFs, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12, or NFs-CXCL12 OE and then exposed to various carboplatin doses. It was observed that relative to NFs-stimulated A549 and NCI-H1299 cells, those exposed to NFs-CXCL12 OE CM had substantially increased carboplatin IC50 (p < 0.0001), surpassing those in the pCAFs group. Furthermore, the IC50 values of A549 and NCI-H1299 treated with pCAFs-sh-CXCL12 conditioned medium were markedly reduced relative to those in the pCAFs group (p < 0.001) and were closer to the levels in the NFs group (Fig.  I-J). Further, A549 and NCI-H1299 cells treated with CM from NFs, mCAFs, pCAFs, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12, and NFs-CXCL12 OE were exposed to carboplatin. The flow cytometry analysis of these cells revealed that compared to the untreated LUAD cells, the apoptosis of adenocarcinoma cells in the NFs-CXCL12 OE-CM group was reduced compared to NFs (p < 0.001). Whereas the apoptosis of pCAFs-sh-CXCL12-CM treatment cells was increased compared to the pCAFs cells (p < 0.001) (Fig.  K-M). The colony formation assay further validated that NFs-CXCL12 OE CM can reduce carboplatin’s apoptotic effect on adenocarcinoma cells, while the CM from CXCL12-silenced pCAFs had opposite effects (p < 0.0001) (Fig.  N-P). This study also assessed the levels of cleaved/total caspase-3 in carboplatin-treated A549 and NCI-H1299 cells, which were grown either alone or in combination with specific components, using western blot analysis. The data revealed that conditioned media from NFs-CXCL12 OE and pCAFs effectively protected cancer cells from chemotherapy-induced apoptosis, whereas the pCAFs-sh-CXCL12 and NFs media lacked this protective effect (Fig.  Q-R). These findings confirmed the important role of CXCL12 in carboplatin’s cytotoxicity mechanism against LUAD cells.

 

## LUAD-derived CAFs inhibited carboplatin killing by secreting CXCL12 via the NF-κB axis
To assess why CXCL12 is differentially expressed in NFs, mCAFs, and pCAFs, the RNA-seq data was subjected to gene enrichment analysis. The findings indicated that this difference may be modulated via the NF-κB pathway (Fig.  A), a classic proinflammatory network that stimulates proinflammatory genes, such as chemokines, adhesion molecules, and cytokines [ ]. To investigate if NF-κB is differentially expressed among NFs, mCAFs, and pCAFs, western blotting was carried out to assess the levels of their NF-κB axis-related proteins. It was observed that pCAFs had the highest levels of p-IKKα/β and p-P65, while NFs had the lowest levels (Fig.  B). Bortezomib and Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate ammonium (PDTC) are potent NF-κB inhibitors that prevent the phosphorylation of IκB and the nuclear translocation of NF-κB (SN50), is a specific NF-κB inhibitor that is a small molecule compound that can interfere with the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, thereby inhibiting its activity. In this study, mCAFs and pCAFs were treated with Bortezomib, PDTC, and SN50, respectively, and then subjected to IF staining. The results showed that pCAFs had significantly increased p65 nuclear translocation compared to mCAFs, and this effect was significantly inhibited by treatment with all three inhibitors (Fig.  C). Furthermore, western blot analysis indicated that Bortezomib, PDTC and SN50 markedly reduced the levels of p-IKKα/β and p-P65 in CAFs, and reduced CXCL12 protein levels (Fig.  D-F). Upon transfection of the transcription factor P65, a significant increase in the fluorescence signal from the CXCL12 promoter was observed, indicating that the P65 transcription factor promotes the expression of CXCL12. Furthermore, the application of the inhibitors SN50 and PDTC resulted in a decrease in the fluorescence signal, suggesting that both inhibitors exert inhibitory effects on the expression of CXCL12 (Fig.  G). Altogether, it was observed that NF-κB activation promote CXCL12 release from pCAFs.

 

## CXCL12 promotes carboplatin resistance in LUAD by binding to CXCR4
To explore the downstream mechanism by which CAFs secrete CXCL12 through NF-κB to promote carboplatin resistance in LUAD, we reviewed relevant literatureand found that CXCL2 can function through the CXCL12/CXCR4 or CXCL12/CXCR7 axis [ , ]. To verify this conclusion, we used Western blot analysis to detect the levels of CXCR4 and CXCR7 in BEAS-2B, A549, and NCI-H1299 cells. The results showed that compared to BEAS-2B cells, A549 and NCI-H1299 cells exhibited high expression of CXCR4 (Fig.  A) and low expression of CXCR7 (Fig.  B). Therefore, we inferred that CXCL12 promotes carboplatin resistance in LUAD by binding to CXCR4 on LUAD cells, which is consistent with the results reported in the literature [ ]. To validate this inference, A549 and NCI-H1299 cells were first cultured alone; simultaneously, we also cultured both cell types in the conditioned media from mCAFs and pCAFs, with and without the CXCR4 inhibitor AMD3100, the five groups of cells were then exposed to different doses of carboplatin. The results indicated that the carboplatin IC50 for cells cultured with both AMD3100 and mCAFs or pCAFs CM decreased compared to A549 and NCI-H1299 cells exposed only to mCAFs or pCAFs CM (p < 0.05) (Fig.  C-D). Subsequently, we assessed the levels of cleaved/total caspase-3 in carboplatin-treated A549 and NCI-H1299 cells, which were cultured either alone or in combination with specific components, using Western blot analysis. The data suggested that AMD3100 could reverse the protective effect of mCAFs and pCAFs conditioned media on LUAD cells against carboplatin-induced apoptosis (Fig.  E-F). The colony formation assay further confirmed that CM from CAFs could reduce the apoptotic effect of carboplatin on LUAD cells, while the use of AMD3100 could reverse this phenomenon (p < 0.05) (Fig.  G-I).
To verify the in vivo effects of these observations, we injected one group of 8 × 106 NCI-H1299 cells and two groups of 8 × 106 pCAFs combined with 8 × 106 NCI-H1299 cells into nude mice. Following this, we administered two injections of 200 µL of carboplatin (50 mg/mL), and one group of mice with pCAFs combined with NCI-H1299 tumors received additional biweekly intravenous injections of AMD3100 (200 µL, 2.5 mg/kg) [ , ] (Fig.  J). Tumor length and width were measured every three days to calculate tumor volume. The results showed that the tumor volume was reduced after the addition of AMD3100 compared to mice with NCI-H1299 combined with pCAFs cells (p < 0.05) (Fig.  K). Tumor samples indicated that the tumors in the pCAFs group developed more significantly than those in the NCI-H1299 group, and the addition of AMD3100 could reverse this phenomenon (Fig.  L-M). We also weighed all tumors, and the results showed that the addition of AMD3100 reduced the tumor weight in the pCAFs group (p < 0.01) (Fig.  N). H&E staining of tumor tissue confirmed that the established tumor was LUAD (Fig.  O). Furthermore, IF staining for Ki-67 and TUNEL demonstrated that the proliferation rate of pCAFs xenografts treated with AMD3100 was lower than that of the pCAFs group, while the apoptosis rate increased (Fig.  P). Overall, these data suggest that AMD3100 can enhance the cytotoxic ability of carboplatin against LUAD both in vitro and in vivo by blocking the binding of CXCL12 to CXCR4.

 

## CAFs inhibited the cytotoxic effects of carboplatin in LUAD by secreting CXCL12 in vivo
To elucidate whether CXCL12 promotes carboplatin chemoresistance in LUAD in vivo, 8 × 106 NFs, mCAFs, pCAFs, pCAFs-sh-CXCL12 or NFs-CXCL12 OE were combined with 8 × 106 A549-L cells. Then, nude mice were injected with these cells, followed by two injections of 200 µL of carboplatin (30 mg/mL) (Fig.  A). Tumor development was carefully monitored with BLI, and tumor fluorescence intensity was recorded. It was observed that the combination of A549-L and pCAFs-sh-CXCL12 cells markedly weakened tumor development compared to the combination of A549 and pCAFs cells (p < 0.0001). Whereas the combination of A549 and NFs-CXCL12 OE cells substantially enhanced tumor development compared to the NFs group (p < 0.05) (Fig.  B-E). This study employed the same grouping and experimental methods to verify that CXCL12 can weaken carboplatin’s cytotoxicity against NCI-H1299-L in vivo. A control group that only received NCI-H1299-L cell inoculation was also employed (Fig.  F). The findings showed that the combination of NCI-H1299-L cells with pCAFs or NFs-CXCL12 OE had a stronger fluorescence than the pCAFs-sh-CXCL12 or NFs group (p < 0.01) (Fig.  G-H). The tumor samples revealed that CXCL12 silencing in pCAFs markedly weakened tumor development, while CXCL12 overexpression in NFs substantially enhanced tumor development (Fig.  I-J). Moreover, the H&E staining of tumor tissue showed the established tumor was LUAD (Fig.  K). The IF staining of tumor tissue from each group was carried out via α-SMA and CXCL12, which revealed co-localization of α-SMA and CXCL12, confirming that CAFs secretes CXCL12. Furthermore, tumors from the NFs-CXCL12 OE and pCAFs groups secreted more CXCL12, while those from the pCAFs-sh-CXCL12 group secreted markedly reduced CXCL12 levels (Fig.  L). Furthermore, the IF Ki-67 and TUNEL staining revealed that the carboplatin-treated pCAFs or NFs-CXCL12 OE xenografts had increased proliferation and reduced apoptosis rates relative to other groups (Fig.  M). Altogether, these data indicate that CAFs derived from poorly differentiated LUAD can secrete more CXCL12, which in turn mediate carboplatin’s killing ability against LUAD in vivo.

 

## Patients with poorly differentiated LUAD can secrete more CXCL12
The pathological tissues, corresponding adjacent tissues, and peripheral blood were collected from LUAD patients admitted at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. In total, 51 LUAD tissues, including 18 highly differentiated LUAD, 15 moderately differentiated LUAD, and 18 poorly LUAD tissues, were collected (Fig.  A). The IHC analysis of CXCL12 levels in LUAD tissues and 14 adjacent tissue sections was carried out (Fig.  B). The statistical analysis revealed that high CXCL12 expression was closely related to a lower differentiation degree (p < 0.001), late TNM stages (p < 0.001), and the presence of airway dissemination in LUAD patients (p < 0.0001), while it was not linked with the smoking history or patients’ gender (Fig.  C-G). Moreover, peripheral serum was also collected from 54 patients to assess serum CXCL12 concentration via ELISA, which revealed that patients with poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma had higher CXCL12 levels (Fig.  H). We conducted follow-up on some of the aforementioned patients to examine changes in peripheral blood CXCL12 levels: Three poorly differentiated patients chose carboplatin treatment post-surgery due to the unavailability of molecular targeted therapy and immunotherapy. After treatment, they experienced recurrence and metastasis, with CXCL12 levels in their serum increasing compared to pre-surgery (p < 0.05) (Fig.  I). Two poorly differentiated patients received TKI therapy post-surgery, and no recurrence or metastasis was detected at the time of blood sampling, showing a downward trend in serum CXCL12 levels (Fig.  J). Two moderately differentiated patients did not receive any treatment post-surgery, and no recurrence or metastasis was detected during blood sampling, with a downward trend in serum CXCL12 levels (Fig.  K). Three highly differentiated patients also did not receive any treatment post-surgery, and no recurrence or metastasis was detected during blood sampling, with no significant changes in serum CXCL12 levels (Fig.  L). Overall, these findings indicate that CAFs derived from poorly differentiated LUAD can secrete more CXCL12 via the NF-κB axis, which in turn weakens carboplatin’s cytotoxic effect on LUAD (Fig.  M).

 

## Discussion
The TME is a complex environment surrounding tumor cells and comprises various cell types, fibroblasts, ECM, nerve cells, immune cells, blood vessels, cytokines, and chemical substances [ ]. TME influences the biological characteristics of tumors and modulates tumor treatment response and prognosis [ , ]. Studies have indicated that CAFs are important modulators of the TME [ , ] and induce tumor chemo-resistance by releasing cytokines and chemokines and enhancing ECM remodeling [ , ].
INMA is classified into the following five types: LPA, APA, PPA, SPA, and MPA [ ]. Among these, PPA or SPA has ≥ 20% proportion, is a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, and is characterized by high malignancy, low differentiation, rapid growth, and poor prognosis [ ]. It has been observed that poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma has reduced sensitivity to conventional chemotherapy agents (such as platinum-based drugs); however, the molecular features of the different subtypes and potential transitions are poorly understood [ ]. This might be related to a higher proportion of cancer stem cells (CSCs) and enhanced drug efflux pumps (such as ABC transporters) expression; however, the research evidence is scarce. CAFs are heterogeneous, with various roles in different cancer types, and the functions of CAFs can vary among different patients, even within the same tumor type. Whether CAFs derived from LUAD with varied differentiation degrees have different chemotherapy resistance remains elusive. Here, it was observed that poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma-derived CAFs (with MPA accounting for 40%) promote LUAD cell development and reduce the killing of carboplatin against adenocarcinoma cells more effectively than those derived from moderately differentiated LUAD and normal fibroblasts.
CXCL12 is an important chemokine that belongs to the CXC subfamily of the chemokine family [ ]. It is widely expressed in tumors and has been linked with cell proliferation, migration [ ], and tumor metastasis [ ]. Several studies have reported that CXCL12 can promote chemoresistance in various tumors [ , ]. Furthermore, its expression is reduced in tumor cells but highly expressed in CAFs [ , ]. The literature suggests that IL-8 expression is elevated in advanced primary gastric cancer patients, which in turn reduces the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy [ ]. However, whether CAFs affect LUAD chemoresistance by secreting CXCL12 needs further investigation. In this study, RNA-seq analysis and database searches revealed that CAFs derived from poorly differentiated LUAD express more CXCL12, validated by qPCR, western blot, and IF analyses. To better understand CXCL12’s role in carboplatin resistance, CXCL12 was knocked down in pCAFs and overexpressed in NFs. The colony formation assays, flow cytometry, and CCK8 assays confirmed that CXCL12 can reduce carboplatin’s cytotoxicity against LUAD cells.
Studies indicated that CXCL12 can exert its effects by binding to CXCR4 or CXCR7 on tumor cells [ , ]. In this study, Western blotting revealed that A549 and NCI-H1299 cells express high levels of CXCR4 and low levels of CXCR7 compared to the normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B. CXCR4 is the most prevalently expressed chemokine receptor in tumor cells, facilitating metastasis to the lungs, liver, and bone marrow, which are some of the most common sites of metastasis in many cancers. Therefore, the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis plays a crucial role in cancer metastasis, and these studies suggest it may serve as a primary target for therapeutic intervention [ ]. The CXCL12/CXCR4 axis has been found to promote and maintain CSCs [ ]. Studies have shown that CXCL12/CXCR4 axis inhibition may suppress cancer cell migration and metastasis, as well as induce cell apoptosis [ ]. There is also literature indicating that CXCL12 exerts its anti-apoptotic effect on A549 cells through CXCR4 [ ].We found through in vivo and in vitro experiments that the CXCR4 inhibitor AMD3100 can inhibit CXCL12-mediated carboplatin resistance in LUAD.
NF-κB signaling was discovered about 40 years ago as a pivotal pathway that mediates inflammatory responses. Later, it was found that NF-κB signaling also modulates immune regulation and the TME [ ]. Studies have also indicated that CXCR4 CAR-T cells can migrate more effectively to tumor sites and inhibit the SDF-1α secretion in CAFs via the STAT3/NF-κB/SDF-1α axis, thus enhancing the therapeutic efficacy against pancreatic cancer [ ]. Here, the gene enrichment and subsequent experiments show that CAFs secrete CXCL12 via the NF-κB axis and that inhibiting NF-κB axis activation and its nuclear translocation with Bortezomib, PDTC or SN50 can reduce CXCL12 secretion.
Our study also has several limitations. Firstly, although in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed the differing effects of NFs, mCAFs, and pCAFs CM on carboplatin resistance in LUAD, and in-depth validation focused on the most significantly different factor, CXCL12, through RNA sequencing and literature support, the roles of other potential cytokines that may influence resistance were not explored in this research and require further confirmation. Secondly, given that CXCL12 is known to shape the TME by recruiting immune suppressive cells such as Treg cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) [ ], our use of immunodeficient nude mice in the animal experiments overlooked the potential immunoregulatory function of CXCL12. This represents a limitation, and future studies should employ allogeneic or humanized models to evaluate this aspect more comprehensively. Thirdly, based on the research objective of investigating the resistance patterns caused by differentially differentiated CAFs and the differences in CXCL12 secretion, we selected a single chemotherapeutic agent, carboplatin, for testing, without examining its combination with pemetrexed or immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, literature suggests that high expression of CXCL12 may correlate with poor responses to PD-L1 therapy, making this a valuable direction for future exploration [ ].Finally, a significant proportion of LUAD patients are unable to benefit from targeted therapies and immunotherapies, and patients receiving targeted treatments frequently develop resistance, thus, chemotherapy remains an important treatment modality. In our experiments, we selected cell lines A549 and NCI-H1299, both of which do not harbor EGFR/ALK mutations (although NCI-H1299 has a positive KRAS mutation, there currently lacks a widely applicable and clinically effective targeted drug) [ ]. Our study aims to improve the prognosis of this subset of patients, so the conclusions drawn cannot be generalized to all LUAD patients.
Furthermore, the assessments were carried out to verify that CAFs mediate LUAD resistance to carboplatin by secreting CXCL12 in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, analysis of clinical specimens revealed that poorly differentiated LUAD patients secrete more CXCL12. Overall, this study highlights the relationship between the CXCL12 secretion level and LUAD differentiation degree, confirming that CAFs derived from poorly differentiated LUAD can secrete more CXCL12 via the NF-κB signaling pathway, thus weakening carboplatin’s cytotoxic effects on LUAD cells. These findings provide new insights addressing carboplatin resistance in LUAD.
