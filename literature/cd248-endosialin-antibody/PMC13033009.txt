A main challenge in treating gliomas lies in the limited permeability of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which restricts drug access to the tumor site. Conventional treatment protocols, including surgery, radiotherapy, and the Stupp regimen, offer only modest survival aids due to drug resistance and inefficient delivery. Recently, nanotechnology-based approaches have gained attention for enhancing drug transport across the BBB. Among them, exosomes endogenous extracellular vesicles have emerged as promising nanocarriers due to their innate biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and natural ability to cross the BBB. These vesicles can be planned to deliver therapeutic agents selectively to tumor cells and modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME), influencing processes as well as angiogenesis, immune evasion, and intercellular signaling. This review articulates the innovative contributions of exosome-based drug delivery systems in glioma management, highlighting recent advancements in bioengineering approaches and their dual role in treatment and diagnostics (theranostics). By speaking the biological barriers to drug delivery and enhancing targeted therapeutic effects, exosomes represent a significant leap forward in precision medicine for glioma therapy. Additional clinical and translational research is crucial to fully realize the potential of exosomes as effective delivery systems in glioma treatment.

## Introduction
Recognizing the broad spectrum of central nervous system (CNS) tumors, the World Health Organization (WHO) has established over 100 distinct categories. Their classification system is based on key biological factors: the tumor’s point of origin, its microscopic cellular characteristics, and its specific anatomical position [ ]. It’s increasingly evident that the unique molecular profiles of these tumors are now pivotal for classification. This development signals a highly promising trajectory for future advancements in precision molecular medicine. Within this landscape, gliomas stand out as the predominant category of primary intracranial tumors, accounting for 31% of all brain and central nervous system tumors, and an even more significant 81% of malignant tumors found in these regions [ ]. Gliomas are characterized by their propensity for swift proliferation, widespread infiltration of surrounding tissues, and a marked degree of genetic heterogeneity [ ].
Glioblastoma, representing 70% of all gliomas, has a median overall survival of merely 15 months [ ]. This cancer is characterized by significantly heightened mitotic activity, the presence of nuclear abnormalities (atypia), tissue necrosis, and the proliferation of microvessels [ ]. The prevailing international standard of care is the Stupp protocol, which encompasses the use of temozolomide, chemotherapy, and radiation following substantial surgical resection. Nonetheless, given the aggressive characteristics of glioblastoma multiforme, along with its histological diversity, challenges in surgical intervention, and limited drug penetration through the blood-brain barrier, the effectiveness of current treatments remains constrained [ ]. While combination therapies have brought about improvements in progression-free survival, these gains have not yet translated into substantial changes in overall survival rates [ ]. Current treatment approaches for gliomas primarily aim to prolong a patient’s life rather than achieving a lasting cure [ ].
The neurovascular unit (NVU) functions as a precisely controlled structure, with its central component, the BBB, acting as a specialized biological interface. This unit is essential for maintaining a stable internal environment within the central nervous system (CNS) [ ]. The neurovascular unit (NVU) consists of specialized endothelial cells (ECs) that are interconnected by tight junctions (TJs), along with a basal membrane supported by astrocytes and pericytes. A “metabolic barrier” collaborates with these physical barriers to swiftly modify unwanted hormones, peptides, metabolites, and other substances that penetrate the brain tissue [ ]. Additionally, various multi-substrate efflux transporters play a crucial role in returning therapeutic agents intended for the treatment of most neurological disorders back into the bloodstream [ ]. The complexity of the blood-brain barrier arises from various factors, such as alterations caused by pathological conditions, its considerable heterogeneity across different regions of the brain, and variations in delivery methods [ , ]. The BBB serves as a sophisticated and multi-faceted barrier, despite its seemingly straightforward designation. Temozolomide stands out as one of the limited chemotherapeutic agents available for the clinical treatment of gliomas, largely because of the challenges presented by the blood-brain barrier [ , ]. Nonetheless, the overall contribution of these agents to patient survival appears to be minimal. A variety of strategies have been devised to enhance the concentration of the drug within the brain. These include the application of biodegradable wafers, the selection of appropriate delivery vehicles, the injection of vasoactive substances, the utilization of ultrasound, and the implementation of convection-enhanced delivery (CED). The use of nanoparticles as delivery vectors offers a encouraging method for the effective and accurate targeting of medications to tumor sites [ , ]. Traditional chemotherapy approaches for brain tumors face challenges in delivering therapeutic agents directly to the tumor site due to the limiting nature of the BBB, which also increases the risk of systemic toxicity, see Fig.  [ , ]. To address these subjects, drug delivery systems utilizing NPs have shown promise by enabling the encapsulation, protection, and targeted transport of therapeutic agents through the brain to influence tumor cells [ , ]. While the precise mechanisms governing the transport of these agents into the brain remain unclear, studies show that factors such as particle size, material composition, structure, and nanoparticle design play significant roles. When nanoparticles are equipped with targeting ligands, such as antibodies or peptides, the efficiency of drug delivery to specific sites expands, resulting in enhanced therapeutic effectiveness and reduced side effects [ , ]. Accordingly, chemotherapy drugs can more effectively decrease tumor size and edema while aiding in the elimination of cancer cells. Figure  provides a schematic overview of representative nanocarrier platforms explored to improve drug delivery across the BBB in brain tumor therapy, highlighting exosomes alongside major synthetic nanoparticle systems (Fig.  ).

 

Their beneficial physicochemical properties, capacity for self-assembly, compatibility with biological systems, and adaptable surface modifications for targeting tumors suggest a significant potential for treating GBM [ ]. Moreover, by enabling a regulated release of medication, nanoparticles decrease the frequency at which dosages need to be administered. While synthetic nanocarrier systems present certain toxicity risks, there remains a pressing need for safe and highly effective delivery methods [ ]. The favorable outcomes observed in nanotechnology-driven drug delivery since its inception account for the ongoing demand in this field [ , ].
This requirement could be significantly addressed by the use of exosomes and other extracellular vehicles (EVs). As minute vesicles secreted by diverse cell types, exosomes are particularly well-suited for drug delivery, largely owing to their impressive capacity to traverse the BBB [ ]. Exosomes are essential in the progression of gliomas, facilitating communication between cells and the transport of biomolecules within the TME [ , ]. The characteristics discussed here hold promise for enhancing the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of glioma. This review seeks to elucidate the properties of exosomes, underscore recent advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic methodologies, discuss their involvement in the progression of glioma, and propose avenues for future research. The introduction offers an overview of gliomas and highlights the crucial need for innovative therapeutic approaches. As we investigate deeper into the mechanisms and approaches for glioma therapy, it becomes clear that considerate exosomes and their properties is essential for developing effective treatments.
Search strategy: To bearing a comprehensive review on “Engineered Exosomes for Targeted Glioma Therapy: Overcoming the Blood-Brain Barrier with Nature-Inspired Nanocarriers,” an organised search strategy was applied. Primary topics included engineered exosomes, the blood-brain barrier, targeted glioma therapy, and nanocarriers. Keywords such as exosome biocompatibility, glioblastoma multiforme, and drug delivery systems were utilized. Databases selected were PubMed, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and Scopus. Inclusion criteria focused on peer-reviewed studies from the past ten years discussing exosomes in glioma therapy. The primary search involved keyword combinations and Boolean operators. Titles and abstracts were screened, and relevant full-text articles were obtained. A standardized tool assessed the quality of included studies. Results were categorized based on mechanisms, progresses, and challenges in exosome engineering. The review interpreted these findings in the context of GBM treatment and discussed future prospects in the area. The data looks to be recorded annually from 2010 to 2025, showing a notable trend in reference usage.

 

## Exosomes and extracellular vesicles
Lipid and protein markers are necessary for characterizing the overall structure of extracellular vehicles (EVs), particularly as these properties are optimized. The choice of specific markers for EV description varies depending on their cellular origin whether from eukaryotic, non-mammalian, or mammalian sources. Additionally, some markers may be private to particular EV subtypes, while others are prevalent across all categories of EVs [ ]. The EV-TRACK information base, developed by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV), has significantly improved the precision, representation, and reproducibility of findings in EV research by adhering to the MISEV criteria. Notably, MISEV2018 is structured to encourage advancement in the field rather than hinder it [ ]. In addition, glioma cells (GMs) display an active increase in glucose consumption and lactate production. They accomplish this by elevating levels of cluster of differentiation 147 (CD147) and monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) on their plasma membranes. This critical mechanism facilitates the efficient export of lactate from the cells, fueling ongoing glycolysis and resulting in lactate accumulation within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ ]. Glioma-derived exosomes, typically measuring between 30 and 200 nanometers, are exclusively capable of crossing crucial physiological barriers like the BBB and the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB). This inherent ability permits them to then spread into systemic biofluids, as well as the bloodstream and cerebrospinal fluid. Importantly, despite their small size and widespread dissemination, these exosomes have emerged as highly valuable tools for identifying specific biomarkers linked to glioma progression. Distinctly, for diagnosing and treating gliomas, intracranial biopsies are now being put forward as a distinct method, offering an alternative to conventional imaging techniques such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans [ ]. The TiO2-CTFE-AuNIs real-time label-free plasmonic biosensor demonstrated a notably higher sensitivity, enabling the detection of prognostic biomarkers in GM-derived exosomes, which may be applicable in glioma liquid biopsy [ ]. Exosomes are a type of extracellular vesicle (EV) that are created by gliomas and are released in significant amounts into the TME. These vesicles typically range in diameter from 30 to 200 nm. These vesicles play an critical role in facilitating communication between cells and are present in biological fluids, and various extracellular environments including blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid [ ]. It is notable that exosomes derived from GM are often found in the peripheral circulation and have the ability to cross the blood-brain barrier [ ]. Accordingly, this feature renders them an invaluable source for pinpointing biomarkers that could be used to track glioma progression. Within this context, BIGH3 stands out as a highly compelling potential prognostic biomarker for gliomas, notably following its identification within exosomes derived from glioma cells (GMs) [ ] (Fig.  ).

 

The development of primary tumors and their spread within the tumor microenvironment (TME) are significantly influenced by mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). A clear instance of this influence is the hypothesis that exosomes released by differentiated adipocytes (which stem from MSCs) can trigger the Hippo signaling pathway, thereby encouraging epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in breast cancer cells [ ]. Research into lung cancer indicates that exosomes, which are generated by hypoxic bone-marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) within the tumor microenvironment (TME), actively encourage cancer cell invasion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). This process is mediated by the transfer of specific microRNAs (miRs), namely miR-5100, miR-193a-3p, and miR-210-3p, from these hypoxic BMSCs directly to the cancer cells [ ].
Electric vehicles can be separated through several techniques, such as chromatography, density gradient centrifugation, microfluidic devices, immunomagnetic bead-based extraction, and [ ]. Improving the effectiveness of treatment delivery to gliomas and ensuring that therapeutic levels are sustained within glioma tissue relies on enhancing the process by which anticancer agents are incorporated into exosomes. To boost the incorporation of therapeutic agents into exosomes, diverse techniques have been under investigation, such as electroporation, the use of chemical reagents, and incubation [ ]. Advancements in micro- and nanofabrication technologies have significantly facilitated research on the use of microfluidics for drug loading and subsequent delivery to cells [ ]. In the case of nanospheres, where the drug is consistently distributed, drug release occurs by erosion or diffusion of the matrix. If the diffusion of the drug is earlier than matrix erosion, then the mechanism of release is basically controlled by a diffusion process [ ]. Investigation indicates that utilizing microfluidics for the loading of therapeutic agents into exosomes has demonstrated superior performance compared to alternative methods. This technique’s capacity for precise and controllable setup facilitates the optimization process, leading to enhanced drug encapsulation [ ]. Microfluidics offers numerous advantages, including a straightforward and rapid setup process. Additionally, it allows for precise control over variables such as pressure and flow rate, ensuring optimal conditions for drug loading. For numerous years, scholars from various corners of the globe have been investigating the application of microfluidics to encapsulate therapeutic agents within liposomes, aiming to address diverse forms of cancer [ ]. Exosomes are a subtype of EVs that play fundamental roles in cell communication. By discovering their characteristics and utilities, we can better recognise how they contribute to glioma pathology and therapy.

## Exosome isolation and purification
Exosomes, small EVs playing serious roles in intercellular communication, are of great attention in various fields, as well as diagnostics and therapeutics [ ]. The isolation and purification of exosomes are important steps in studying their functions and possible uses [ ]. Below are the most applied isolation approaches, highlighting both conventional and emerging methods. Differential ultracentrifugation (DUC) is one of the most public methods for exosome isolation (Fig.  ) [ ].

 

This method involves subjecting biofluids to a series of centrifugation steps with incrementally increasing centripetal acceleration [ ]. Primarily, larger cellular debris and apoptotic bodies are removed through low-speed centrifugation. Following higher-speed spins pellet smaller vesicles, with exosomes. The process relies on the differences in size and density, making it a robust method for obtaining relatively pure exosome fractions. On the other hand, DUC can be time-consuming and may co-isolate certain contaminants, such as protein aggregates [ ]. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) is additional established method that exploits the physical characteristics of exosomes. This technique involves passing a sample through a column comprising a porous stationary phase, where the biofluid acts as the mobile phase [ ]. The separation occurs based on size: larger particles elute first, while smaller particles are temporarily trapped inside the pores of the stationary phase. As a result, SEC allows for the separation of exosomes from other molecules, providing high purity. This technique is relatively simple and can preserve exosome functionality, but it doesn’t concentrate exosomes as much as other methods [ ]. Precipitation is a more straightforward method that relies on the addition of specific solutions to stimulate the aggregation of exosomes into larger polymer-encapsulated vesicle aggregates. The process enables the separation of exosomes from the solution due to their enlarged size and density [ ]. It is a fairly quick and cost-effective method, proper for large-scale applications. However, this method may yield lower purity levels, leading to the co-isolation of other vesicles and proteins. Immunoaffinity capture engagements antibodies that specifically target exosomal surface proteins. By immobilizing these antibodies on a solid support, scientists can selectively isolate specific subpopulations of exosomes based on their protein profile [ ]. This method allows for high specificity and the opportunity of enriching exosome subsets that may have different biological activities. Regardless of its advantages, the dependence on antibodies may limit the versatility of this method [ ]. Microfluidics characterises a cutting-edge approach for exosome isolation. It uses specialized chips designed for precise and organised fluid flow, which can be tailored for specific antibody-mediated interactions [ ]. This technique allows for rapid processing and the ability to achieve high sensitivity and specificity in capturing exosomes. The microfluidic devices can handle small sample volumes, creation them suitable for applications where sample availability is limited [ ]. However, this equipment is still primarily in the research stage and may involve further development for widespread clinical use. Ultrafiltration is another effective technique that employs filters with defined pore sizes to separate exosomes from larger particles and pollutants [ ]. By applying pressure or centrifugal force, the filtrate obtained is rich in vesicles tailored to specific dimensions. Ultrafiltration is efficient and scalable, though it can also lead to the loss of some smaller exosomes or non-specific binding to filter membranes [ ] see Table  .

 

Individually of these methods has its advantages and restrictions, often leading scientists to utilize a combination of approaches for optimized results in exosome isolation and purification. Understanding these methods is crucial for advancing research in exosome-based diagnostics and therapeutics.

## The composition of exosomes
Exosomes are intricate structures, distinguished by their diverse cargo of lipids, various nucleic acids (such as DNA and multiple RNA types), and a broad spectrum of proteins. These proteins notably include cytoskeletal proteins, heat shock proteins (HSPs), and ESCRT proteins. Furthermore, their membranes feature crucial transmembrane proteins, encompassing adhesion molecules, glycoproteins, tetraspanins, and antigen-presenting molecules originating from both major histocompatibility complex (MHC) Class I and II [ ]. The makeup of this cargo is significantly influenced by the state of the donor cell, mirroring its inherent physiological condition, whether it is experiencing disease, stress, or activation. Exosomes serve as valuable indicators of their cellular origin, as the cell from which they originate dictates their lipid structure and the content found within their lumen [ , ]. Exosomes are characterized by a robust lipid bilayer membrane that encases an aqueous core, with ceramides, cholesterol, and sphingomyelin being notably prevalent within this membrane (Fig.  ). This lipid composition plays a crucial role in regulating cargo sorting, facilitating cell communication, promoting exosome release, and ensuring the stability and structural integrity of vesicles. Exosomes possess the ability to carry both hydrophilic and lipophilic agents due to their unique composition [ ].

 

Given that the ESCRT proteins Alix and TSG-101 play a huge role in the regulation of exosomal assembly and MVB trafficking, it is expected that all exosomes, irrespective of their cellular origin, will contain these proteins. In a similar vein, molecules that play a role in guiding exosome transport and aiding in their uptake by recipient cells including integrin, flotillin, actin, and heat shock proteins (HSPs), along with members of the tetraspanin family such as CD81, CD63, and CD9, are typically expected to be detectable. Conversely, T-cell-mediated immune responses are associated with MHC Class I and II proteins, which are particular to the type of donor cell involved [ , ].
Exosomes carry a comprehensive range of nucleic acids and specific proteins. Their genetic cargo is notably rich, comprising numerous RNA types like messenger RNA (mRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), microRNA (miRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), and piwi-interacting RNA (piRNA), alongside DNA [ ]. Exosomes contain a significant number of miRNAs, which play a crucial role in the intercellular communication that these vesicles enable. Additionally, recent findings indicate that exosomes are carriers of longer RNA species, including circular RNAs (circRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs). These molecules play a significant role in various biological processes, notably in the initiation of cancer [ ]. Consequently, studies have explored the potential of nucleic acids as non-invasive tools for diagnosis and prognosis. There are two forms of exosomes: engineered exosomes, and unmodified exosomes see Table  . Unmodified exosomes are naturally occurring extracellular vesicles released from cells and considered by a lipid bilayer that facilitates intercellular communication. They carry lipids, proteins, and RNAs, reflecting the molecular composition of their parent cells. These vesicles play critical roles in cell signaling, immune modulation, and waste managing. In contrast, engineered exosomes are adapted versions of these natural vesicles. They are formed through various techniques to increase their functional capabilities, which can include addition specific targeting ligands to their surface, optimizing their cargo to include therapeutic agents, or improving loading approaches. These modifications aim to increase their efficacy in targeted drug delivery and therapeutic uses.

 

The composition of exosomes can vary based on their origin, comprising diverse biomolecules that can mediate various biological progressions. Identifying these components is critical for attaching their potential in targeted glioma therapies.

## Signaling pathways in glioblastoma, along with genetic alterations and molecular targets, play a crucial role in understanding this complex disease
GBM represents one type of primary brain tumor within the broader category known as “gliomas.” These tumors share histological characteristics with normal glial cells, such as astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and ependymal cells. Despite their commonalities, all types of gliomas display a range of aggressive biological characteristics. The two main subtypes of diffuse gliomas, which are the most common form of glioma, are IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype [ ].
GBM, classified as a high-grade IDH-wildtype glioma, stands out as the most prevalent and aggressive form of malignant primary brain tumor found in adults [ , ]. The significant heterogeneity and invasiveness of GBMs pose serious challenges to effective treatment approaches. The biological profile of these entities is primarily characterized by three key features: proliferation, active invasiveness, and abundant angiogenesis. The characteristics observed can largely be attributed to the dysregulation of various signaling cascades, including the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, the RAS/RAF/MAPK pathway, the p53 pathway, and the DNA repair pathways, see Fig.  [ ].

 

The Cancer Genome Atlas network classifies GBM into four distinct molecular groups: neural, prone, classical, and mesenchymal, according to specific gene expression characteristics [ ]. Every subgroup exhibits unique patterns in disease progression and survival rates, influenced by particular molecular and genetic abnormalities that play a role in the onset of cancer. Scientists continue to engage in significant debate concerning the precise cellular origins of GBM, resulting in two prominent and credible theories: the astrocyte dedifferentiation theory and the stem cell theory. The stem cell hypothesis suggests that GBM originates from stem cells located in the subventricular zone. These cells maintain their capacity to differentiate into various brain cell types, even following malignant transformation [ , ]. Conversely, the astrocyte de-differentiation hypothesis suggests that GBM originates from mature astrocytes in adults. These cells experience mutations that enable them to revert to a more primitive, pluripotent state, allowing for extensive proliferation and the generation of diverse cell populations [ ].
GBM is characterized by a range of genetic alterations that facilitate the progression and dissemination of the disease. For instance, mutations in the EGFR gene are found in about 60% of cases of GBM [ ]. The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), encoded by the EGFR gene, plays a crucial role in various cellular processes such as cell division, migration, adhesion, differentiation, and apoptosis. The EGFR-phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway is persistently activated by EGFR, which acts as an oncoprotein. As a result, cells that have undergone mutations tend to have a greater chance of surviving and multiplying [ , ]. Almost 90% of glioblastomas demonstrate changes in the p53 pathway, a vital component for processes such as apoptosis, DNA repair, and the regulation of the cell cycle. The typical role of the traditional tumor suppressor protein p53 (TP53) is compromised due to mutations in the TP53 gene. This disruption allows GBM cells to evade death and fosters genomic instability [ , ]. Additionally, GBM are associated with rare genetic ailments linked to germline mutations. Mainly notable are Li-Fraumeni syndrome, which includes mutations in the TP53 gene, and neurofibromatosis types 1 and 2, both of which highlight the genetic predisposition to these tumors [ , ]. Astrocytomas (AS) frequently harbor mutations in several key genes, as well as isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1), alpha-thalassemia/mental retardation syndrome X-linked (ATRX), isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2), tumor protein p53 (TP53), and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A/B (CDKN2A/B) [ , ]. Oligodendrogliomas (ODG), categorised by a specific 1p/19q-codeletion and IDH mutations, typically carry mutations in IDH1, TERT promoter, IDH2, capicua (CIC), far upstream element-binding protein 1 (FUBP1), and Notch homolog 1 (NOTCH1), among others [ , ]. In contrast, glioblastomas (GBM), which are commonly IDH-wildtype, show mutations in the TERT promoter, chromosomal changes on chromosomes 7 and 10, and modifications in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene, see Fig.  [ , ].

 

Pediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomas (LGGs), mainly diffuse astrocytomas with changes in MYB or MYBL1, mainly involve mutations in the MYB and MYBL1 genes. Angiocentric glioma is also associated with mutations in MYB. The Polymorphous Low-Grade Neuroepithelial Tumor of the Young presents mutations inside the BRAF and FGFR gene families. Moreover, diffuse LGGs that display alterations in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) way often have mutations in BRAF and fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) .
The inactivation of the PTEN tumor suppressor gene, found on the long arm of chromosome 10, is associated with the malignant progression seen in GBM. In approximately 40% of cases of GBM, there are mutations or deletions associated with the PTEN gene [ ]. This cancer is often associated with various chromosomal abnormalities, with trisomy 7, which involves an increase in the number of chromosome 7, and monosomy 10, characterized by the absence of one copy of chromosome 10, being the most prevalent. The proximity of the EGFR gene on chromosome 7 indicates a significant relationship between its amplification and gain [ ].
One significant factor contributing to the unregulated growth of tumor cells is the elongation of telomeres. This phenomenon can be attributed to mutations in the promoter of the TERT gene, which plays a crucial role in maintaining telomere length and preventing cell death. These mutations result in heightened telomerase activity, a significant contributor to the progression of GBM. The retinoblastoma (RB) protein, encoded by the RB1 gene, plays a crucial role in inhibiting the progression of the cell cycle. Similarly, RB1 frequently undergoes inactivation or mutation in glioblastoma, leading to unregulated cell proliferation [ ].
Additionally, in approximately 50% of primary glioblastoma multiforme cases, the O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) locus is found to be either methylated or silenced. The DNA repair protein encoded by MGMT plays a crucial role in removing alkyl groups from guanine-rich areas, which are the main targets for alkylating agents. This function diminishes the therapeutic effectiveness of alkylating agents used in the treatment of GBMand contributes to the development of resistance to chemotherapy. Individuals with methylated MGMT promoters frequently experience extended survival durations and demonstrate increased sensitivity to alkylating agents, especially temozolomide (TMZ). Consequently, evaluating the methylation status of this locus can be a valuable indicator for predicting the likelihood of a response to chemotherapy [ , ].
Glioblastomas often show heightened activity in two essential signaling pathways: RAS/RAF/MAPK and PI3K/Akt/mTOR. Interestingly, studies indicate that these two pathways engage in a reciprocal inhibitory interaction with one another [ ]. The regulation of cell growth, survival, and proliferation is intricately managed by the PIK3/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), including EGFR and RAS, play a crucial role in activating this pathway. In contrast, the tumor suppressor protein PTEN serves to inhibit this pathway, effectively limiting the activity of PI3K. Additionally, the control of cellular growth is influenced by serine/threonine-specific protein kinases (STKs), such as serine/threonine-protein kinase B (Akt) and the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) [ ]. Consequently, it is understandable that 90% of GBMs display modifications in RTK/PI3K/Akt signaling. In a similar vein, the RAS/RAF/MAPK pathway, commonly known as the MAPK/ERK cascade, plays a crucial role in regulating essential cellular functions such as cell division, proliferation, and survival. Upon activation of the RAS protein, it engages the RAF STK family, including RAF1, initiating a series of consequential events. MAP2K, also known as MEK, undergoes phosphorylation by activated RAF, leading to its subsequent activation. This phosphorylation step ultimately stimulates key transcription factors that play a crucial role in cell proliferation and the expression of anti-apoptotic genes [ , ]. In GBM, there is a notable abundance of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and various other molecules that facilitate angiogenesis, reflecting the tumor’s highly vascularized environment [ ]. The BBB and the BBTB present substantial challenges to the effective delivery of drugs for GBM. Addressing these barriers is serious for optimizing exosome-based delivery systems.

## Detrimental consequences of genetic alterations in glioma
Genetic alterations in glioma are not merely diagnostic markers; they can directly worsen disease biology and treatment response. Key mutations and pathway dysregulations often increase intratumoral heterogeneity and clonal evolution, which fuels invasiveness, recurrence, and resistance to standard therapies [ , ]. For example, constitutive activation of RTK driven signaling (such as EGFR alterations) and loss of tumor suppressor control (such as PTEN and TP53 pathway disruption) can enhance proliferation, survival under stress, and adaptive resistance. In parallel, DNA repair related changes, particularly MGMT mediated repair capacity, reduce sensitivity to alkylating chemotherapy and contribute to treatment failure. Clinically, these molecular features are associated with aggressive growth, limited durability of responses, and frequent relapse, emphasizing why delivery strategies and combination approaches that address both BBB transport and molecular resistance programs are needed [ ].

## GSCs biology
Glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) are a exclusive subpopulation of cells within GBM tumors that possess the capability to recruit and sustain tumor growth [ ]. These cells were isolated from GBM and have verified a remarkable capacity to develop GBM when transplanted into mouse models, highlighting their critical role in tumor development and progression [ , ]. Recent studies have recognised several biomarkers that effectively separate GSCs from non-tumorigenic stromal cells. Notable markers include CD15+, CD56+, SOX2+, SOX9+, and CD133+, which are associated with the stem-like properties of these cells [ ]. Moreover, GSCs exhibit negative expression of specific markers such as CD248−, CD105−, and αSMA−, further aiding in their documentation, see Fig.  [ ]. These biomarkers are critical for understanding the biological features of GSCs and developing targeted therapies. Their presence or absence can serve as indicators of tumor aggressiveness and may guide treatment decisions. GSCs are classified into different groups based on various criteria. Recent analyses have categorized GSCs according to specific gene expression profiles that associate with different tumor characteristics and patient consequences [ ]. The metabolic features of GSCs also play a significant role in their behavior and treatment response. GSCs often exhibit distinct metabolic profiles that enable them to thrive in the harsh tumor microenvironment, which is critical for their survival and proliferation [ ]. Moreover, GSCs demonstrate varied biological behaviors, including increased self-renewal capability, improved resistance to therapies, and the capability to differentiate into various cell types within the tumor. These attributes contribute to the challenges of effectively treating GBM and underscore the importance of understanding GSC biology in emerging novel therapeutic strategies.

 

Generally, the complex nature of GSCs, categorised by exceptional biomarkers, gene signatures, and distinct metabolic and biological properties, emphasises their significance in GBM research and treatment. Understanding these features is crucial for developing targeted therapies that could improve patient outcomes.

## The blood-brain barrier and the blood-brain tumor barrier present significant challenges to the effective delivery of drugs for GBM
The therapeutic options for gliomas, which are a prevalent type of brain tumor, are primarily constrained by two key factors. The BBB and the blood-brain tumor barrier (BBTB) serve as the two main obstacles that inhibit the movement of numerous medications (Fig.  ) [ ]. By developing drug delivery systems, we may be able to mitigate these challenges. Gliomas account for approximately 80% of all brain tumors, and the treatment options available for these conditions generally encompass radiation therapy, surgical intervention, electric field therapy, and the use of temozolomide [ ].

 

Gliomas present significant treatment challenges due to their invasive characteristics, which hinder the possibility of complete surgical removal. This particular type of brain tumor integrates with surrounding healthy brain cells, demonstrating a pattern of growth that is infiltrative in nature. The process of healing is hindered by the coexistence of both malignant and healthy cells. To safeguard the brain’s sensitive environment, the blood-brain barrier inherently prevents certain drugs and proteins from accessing the brain. The complexity of the examination processes, which require the utilization of tools like fluorescent tracking and tumor treatment instruments, significantly contributes to this barrier [ ].
The BBB is formed by a continuous, non-porous layer of capillary endothelial cells, which are interconnected by tight intercellular junctions (TJs), adherens junctions, a basement membrane, perivascular astrocyte end-foot processes, and pericytes [ ]. The blood-brain barrier is composed of three distinct layers: the glycocalyx, the endothelium, and the extravascular component, each contributing to its complex structure. The arrangement progresses sequentially from the blood to the brain. The challenge of delivering drugs for brain tumors, particularly those hidden by the BBB, can potentially be addressed through a deeper understanding of the BBB’s composition and function. The upcoming sections will provide an explanation of the layers that constitute the blood-brain barrier [ , ]. The BBB is a discerning permeability barrier that protects the CNS, generating challenges for delivering radiopharmaceuticals. Several mechanisms facilitate the penetration of these agents across the BBB. Convection enhanced delivery (CED) uses controlled infusion to promote bulk flow, permitting for uniform distribution of radiopharmaceuticals in brain tissue while circumventing tight junctions [ ]. This technique is effective for larger molecules that struggle to cross the BBB through conventional means [ ]. Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) are short peptide sequences that can enable transport across cellular membranes through non-specific endocytosis or membrane translocation [ ]. By conjugating CPPs with radiopharmaceuticals, their brain uptake can be improved, leveraging the peptides’ ability to bypass the BBB. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) can be planned to target specific receptors on endothelial cells, enabling transcytosis. This targeted approach improves the delivery of radiopharmaceuticals while reducing off-target effects, optimizing therapeutic efficacy [ ]. P-glycoprotein (P-gp) is a transporter that plays a critical role in the efflux of compounds out of the brain, which can limit the efficacy of radiopharmaceuticals. Understanding P-gp is vital for developing strategies to inhibit its function, growing the retention of these agents in the CNS [ ]. Tight junctions (TJs) restrict the passage of large or hydrophilic molecules. Advanced approaches are being developed to modulate TJ integrity, allowing for the effective infiltration of radiopharmaceuticals into the CNS without compromising the BBB’s protective function, see Fig.  [ ].

 

These progresses characterise significant advancement in overcoming the barriers to BBB penetration for radiopharmaceuticals, with the potential to improve therapeutic strategies for neurological disorders. Exosomes are progressively recognized for their role in glioma expansion and development. By elucidating these mechanisms, we can open ways for potential therapeutic interventions.

## Exosomes and their contribution to glioma development
Exosomes play a fundamental role in mediating intercellular communication by transporting bioactive molecules from their originating (donor) cells to target (recipient) cells. Studies consistently show that cancer cells, including those prevalent in gliomas, heavily leverage this mechanism, releasing copious amounts of exosomes to establish communication with both adjacent and far-removed cells [ ]. A significant role for glioma-derived exosomes (GDEs) is evident in multiple facets of tumor development and progression. Their contributions span critical areas like immune evasion, angiogenesis, anti-apoptotic signaling, resistance to therapy, and the formation of pre-metastatic niches. Interestingly, findings show a reciprocal effect: exosomes from healthy cells, including T cells, B cells, and dendritic cells, demonstrate a strong potential to notably impede tumor growth [ ]. The proteins, mRNAs, and noncoding RNAs contained within these exosomes are linked to the effects observed in this context (Fig.  ).

 

Exosomes are progressively recognized for their role in glioma growth and development. By elucidating these mechanisms, we can open ways for potential therapeutic interventions.

## Importance for diagnosis
The limited availability of specific biomarkers for glioblastoma presents significant challenges for early detection. In recent years, exosomes have emerged as a promising source of marker molecules that can provide insights into the early stages of malignant tumor development. The identification of these molecules in patients holds significant promise for enhancing early cancer detection and improving the chances of achieving complete remission. Providing personalized therapy necessitates a thorough and accurate molecular characterization of a tumor. Previous research on glioblastoma markers has identified specific molecules present in tumor cell exosomes that may hold promise for diagnostic applications [ , ].
For example, GBM cells that express EGFRvIII often exhibit aggressive and highly proliferative characteristics. Their interference with intercellular communication pathways leads to effects that aren’t solely dependent on the cells themselves. Another marker, Syndecan-1, is considered valuable for distinguishing between high-grade and low-grade gliomas. Furthermore, the levels of microRNA (miRNA) hold substantial diagnostic significance; for instance, elevated miR-21 expression indicates a greater invasive potential, and increased concentrations of these molecules are frequently linked to cancer development. Specific miRNAs, including miR-222, miR-221, miR-92b, miR-30a, and miR-23a, actively promote cancer cell proliferation while simultaneously inhibiting apoptosis. Collectively, these various markers play a crucial role in assessing the tumor’s degree of malignancy, which is a vital factor in developing an appropriate treatment strategy [ , ].
One notable feature observed in the development of glioblastoma is the increased expression of various proteins, which has been identified in exosomes obtained from different glioma cell lines. In the proteomic profiles of both cancerous and normal glial cells, thirteen exosomal proteins—KPYM, TERA, ENO1, 1433E, NPM, HS90B, TPIS, ANXA1, PRDX1, G3P, COF1, ANXA2, and VIM—were identified as being overexpressed. Consequently, these represent additional protein markers identified within the exosomes of gliomas. It is important to note that exosomes do not contain p53 and PCNA, which are considered two of the key biomarkers for diagnosing glioma [ , ]. As previously noted, exosomal lncRNA molecules possess the potential to serve as valuable diagnostic tools. Exosomal lncRNAs serve as valuable prognostic indicators, enabling the monitoring of a patient’s response to chemotherapy. Additionally, they act as biomarkers for the early detection of malignant brain tumors, owing to their notable specificity and sensitivity [ , ] (Table  ).

 

The efficacy of exosomes in diagnosing gliomas offers valuable visions into tumor biology and patient organisation. This understanding is vital for integrating exosome-based strategies into clinical practice.

## Potential therapeutic benefits
A major hurdle in delivering chemotherapy drugs for brain tumor treatment stems from the formidable BBB. In this context, exosomes emerge as a highly promising solution, particularly for addressing challenges in glioblastoma therapy. The potential therapeutic applications of exosomes have expanded considerably, a development largely attributable to ongoing advancements in nanotechnology and refined techniques for isolating these vesicles from cells. Consequently, exosomes are now being recognized as viable delivery vehicles for a range of anticancer drugs or molecules specifically designed to induce apoptosis in cancer cells [ , ].
Studies indicate a relationship between the responsiveness of glioma cells to TMZ therapy and the transport of exosomal molecules, including miR-151a. This discovery stems from a study that utilized exosomes enriched with selumetinib, which were derived from the U87MG glioma cell line. Research indicates that exosomes carrying this compound exhibit a specific anti-cancer effect while preserving the integrity of healthy cells, suggesting a potential avenue for the effective treatment of malignant brain tumors [ ]. Furthermore, the upregulation of XRCC4.18 expression via exosomes impedes DNA repair mechanisms, thereby augmenting the sensitivity of TMZ-resistant tumor cells. Exosomes could potentially enhance the effectiveness of existing chemotherapy treatments when used together [ – ].
Exosomes play a significant role in personalized anticancer treatment strategies that involve transdifferentiation, a process that reprograms somatic cells. Exosomes derived from neural stem cells induced through transdifferentiation (Exo-iNSC) have demonstrated in later research to be a promising and effective therapeutic approach for various types of brain tumors. Research has shown that genetically modified iNSC cells secrete exosomes that contain the TRAIL protein, which is known to be lethal to cancer cells and possesses the ability to penetrate their outer membrane. Exo-iNSC-TRAIL demonstrates a more rapid cytotoxic effect on cancer cells compared to free TRAIL, along with a preference for tumor accumulation and a protective effect on healthy cells, as indicated by ex vivo studies. A significant development in the field of oncology is the application of exosomes for personalized therapies, potentially offering solutions for highly drug-resistant cancers like glioblastoma. This method has shown effectiveness in addressing primary malignant brain tumors and potential metastatic locations [ , ]. Exosomes offer numerous potential therapeutic benefits in glioma treatment. By leveraging their exceptional properties, we can improve drug delivery and progress therapeutic outcomes.

## The influence of glioma on its microenvironment
The TME associated with gliomas comprises a various array of components, including glioma stem-like cells, endothelial cells, immune cells, and non-cellular elements such as the extracellular matrix [ ]. Current understanding highlights that the TME plays a significant role in regulating tumor growth and is a crucial element in the advancement of gliomas [ ]. Exosomes are crucial mediators that facilitate intricate communication between tumors and their surrounding TME. A specific study, for instance, uncovered a feedback loop involving macrophages and miR-340-5p that significantly impacted the progression of both GBM and the TME itself [ ]. Numerous studies have shown that exosomes can transport the tumor-suppressive miR-3591-3p, which then specifically targets tumor-associated macrophages within glioma cells. This action generates a suppressive microenvironment that, in turn, facilitates the invasion and migration of gliomas [ ]. Gliomas influence their microenvironment in ways that promote tumor growth and survival. Understanding these interactions is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

## Exosome-mediated angiogenesis
The course of exosome biogenesis and release into the extracellular space recruits an endocytic pathway at the plasma membrane (PM) [ ]. While the full details of this mechanism are still being clarified, it begins with the formation and gathering of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) [ ]. These late vesicles accomplish to avoid degradation by the lysosomal digestive system. Finally, upon fusion with the plasma membrane, ILVs are secreted into the extracellular space, where they contribute to several physiological processes. Exosome creation and secretion are facilitated through pathways that can be classified as either Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT)-dependent or ESCRT-independent [ ]. The ESCRT-dependent way includes a series of protein complexes that play vital roles in the formation of ILVs and their subsequent release [ ]. Meanwhile, the ESCRT-independent pathway mainly includes mechanisms that rely on lipid microdomains and the action of specific proteins such as ceramides. Together, these trails underscore the difficulty of exosome biogenesis, highlighting its importance in cellular communication and environmental reaction, see Fig.  [ , ].

 

Exosomes released by gliomas are critical drivers of angiogenesis, a process essential to glioma progression. Research has revealed that glioma cells can influence angiogenesis by transferring specific molecular cargo to endothelial cells through exosomes [ , ]. For example, one study recommended that glioma cells might facilitate the transfer of Long Intergenic Non-Coding RNA (Linc-CCAT2) to endothelial cells, stimulating angiogenic processes [ , ]. This transfer of molecular information via exosomes significantly alters the tumor microenvironment, promotion conditions conducive to tumor growth and vascularization. Additional studies, have recognised the significance of exosome-derived circGLIS3 in high-grade gliomas [ ]. This molecule has been revealed to enhance both glioma invasion and angiogenesis by modifying the phosphorylation of Ezrin at T567 a critical phosphorylation event that affects cell surface dynamics and signaling pathways [ ]. The interplay of these mechanisms exemplifies how glioma cells exploit exosomal communication to manipulate their surroundings for progression [ ]. Conversely, not all exosomal interactions promote tumor growth. For instance, studies indicate that ECRG4-exosomes (ECRG4-EV), formed by brain endothelial cells, inhibit glioma growth. These exosomes are intricate in the regulation of angiogenesis and inflammation, providing a protective mechanism against glioma progression [ , ]. This dual role of exosomes both promoting and inhibiting angiogenesis shows their complex contribution in glioma biology and highlights the beneficial potential of targeting exosomal signaling trails. In summary, the biogenesis and release of exosomes characterise a critical aspect of cellular communication, with profound implications for glioma progression and angiogenesis. Understanding these processes may offer insights into potential therapeutic strategies for managing gliomas and other cancers. Exosomes significantly contribute to angiogenesis in gliomas. Discovering this process may help us recognise innovative targets for therapeutic intervention.

## Exosomes and their influence on glioma invasion and growth
Glioma-derived exosomes (GDEs) act as carriers for both coding and noncoding RNA molecules, thereby significantly contributing to tumor growth, invasion, and survival. These transported factors are critical facilitators of glioma cell recurrence and persistence. Notably, exosomal miR-148a has been implicated in the progression of GBM, specifically by targeting CAMD1 and enhancing the activity of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) [ ]. Research involving GBM cells indicates that the silencing of circZNF652 and the overexpression of miR-486-5p impede processes such as cell migration, invasion, proliferation, and the transition from epithelial to mesenchymal states (EMT). Specifically, exosomes originating from GBM cells demonstrated a notable expression of circZNF652. Furthermore, additional research indicates that GDEs associated with miR-9, circARID1A, and miR-3184-3p enhance the invasion, migration, and proliferation of glioma cells [ , ]. Exosomes play an essential role in promoting glioma invasion and development. Exploring these mechanisms will help us design more effective treatments.

## Exosomes and their impact on treatment resistance in gliomas
Exosomes are instrumental in the export of therapeutic compounds from tumor cells, thereby significantly contributing to the resistance of gliomas to treatment [ ]. By promoting the growth of fibroblasts and consequently triggering fibroblastic responses, they may also offer a degree of protection against anti-tumor agents. Moreover, exosomes have the capacity to utilize biomolecules such as miRNA to transform drug-sensitive tumor cells into those that exhibit resistance [ , ]. Hypoxia enhances the uptake of exosomes, which subsequently activates specific signaling pathways, thereby increasing the resistance of gliomas to treatment. Research conducted by Garnier et al. indicates that glioma stem cells (GSCs) release MGMT mRNA via exosomes, showcasing a mechanism of resistance to TMZ [ ]. A different study revealed that genomic rearrangements of MGMT play a role in repairing O6-Methylguanine damage induced by the drug, which subsequently contributes to resistance against TMZ [ ]. Additionally, studies have shown that exosome-mediated circWDR62 and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) could contribute to increased resistance to TMZ in gliomas, indicating their possible role as prognostic biomarkers. This highlights the role of tumor-associated macrophages in the recurrence of GBM [ ]. Treatment resistance is a main problem in glioma therapy, with exosomes contributing to this phenomenon. Addressing these challenges will be critical for improving patient outcomes.

## The role of exosomes in modulating the immune response
Exosomes that are released by tumors engage with various immune cells, including effector T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and naturally occurring regulatory T cells, among others, to facilitate tumor growth and suppress the immune response [ ]. In the serum exosomes of patients with GBM, researchers have identified various molecules such as TGF-b, immune intracellular adhesion molecules, tumor antigens, and antigen-presenting molecules [ ]. Research conducted by Reza et al. indicates that brain tumor-initiating cells generate TNC, an extracellular matrix protein linked to exosomes, which serves to inhibit T cell function. TNC interacts with T cell-associated α5β1 and αvβ6 integrins, leading to the inhibition of T cell proliferation by diminishing mTOR signaling [ ]. A separate study indicates that miR-1246 plays a role in the polarization of M2 macrophages in response to H-GDEs by targeting TERF2IP. The activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway, along with the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway, may contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [ , ]. Recent studies suggest that CircNEIL3 is delivered to infiltrated tumor-associated macrophages following its packaging into exosomes by hnRNPA2B1. This transfer enhances the stabilization of IGF2BP3, leading to immunosuppressive characteristics that subsequently accelerate the growth of gliomas [ ].
In summary, bioactive molecules transported by exosomes significantly contribute to the progression of gliomas. Understanding the interrelated nature of the various stages of tumor development is crucial, as they do not evolve independently but rather influence one another significantly. Further investigation is necessary to determine the types and mechanisms of action of bioactive molecules that affect the progression of gliomas, with the aim of utilizing them as diagnostic and therapeutic instruments. Exosomes can moderate the immune response in the tumor microenvironment. Understanding these connections could lead to novel immunotherapeutic approaches.

## Utilizing exosomes for drug delivery
The BBB presents a significant challenge in effectively delivering chemotherapy drugs to tumors located within the central nervous system (CNS). Exosomes offer intriguing possibilities for overcoming this challenge, particularly in the context of treating GBM. Exosome-based combination therapies may offer solutions to the limitations of existing GBM treatments [ , ]. Earlier research has shown that exosomes are capable of transporting a range of medications, encompassing both lipophilic substances such as curcumin and hydrophilic agents like doxorubicin, while avoiding immunological complications or the activation of the complement system [ , ].
The effectiveness of temozolomide (TMZ), a widely utilized chemotherapy agent for GBM, is constrained by various resistance mechanisms, including DNA repair facilitated by O6-alkylguanine-DNA alkyltransferase (AGT) [ ]. While O6-benzylguanine (BG) and TMZ have the potential to enhance the effectiveness of TMZ, the clinical application of BG is limited due to its associated side effects and its low permeability across the blood-brain barrier [ ]. Exosomes that target dual receptors and are loaded with both TMZ and BG have been created to tackle these challenges and improve drug delivery to the brain for the treatment of GBM [ ].
Various methods, such as sonication, electroporation, and incubation, can be employed to facilitate the loading of drugs into exosomes [ , ]. Research has demonstrated promising outcomes in the delivery of therapeutic agents via exosomes. For instance, administering the STAT3 inhibitor cucurbitacin I to mice with GBM resulted in an increase in their survival rates [ ]. Furthermore, research involving zebrafish cancer models has shown that exosomes originating from brain endothelial cells can effectively reduce markers associated with tumor growth [ ].
Research indicates that chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin (Dox) and paclitaxel (PTX) demonstrate improved anticancer effectiveness when they are encapsulated within exosomes [ , ]. While Dox demonstrates effectiveness, it is important to acknowledge its limitations, particularly concerning its biocompatibility and the potential for adverse effects [ ]. Nonetheless, exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells have shown potential in improving the uptake and efficacy of doxorubicin (Dox) in individuals diagnosed with osteosarcoma [ ]. Paclitaxel (PTX), a frequently utilized anti-cancer medication, presents challenges related to toxicity and its ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier [ , ]. Exosomes derived from cancer cells that contain Paclitaxel (PTX) could represent a promising approach to specifically target drug-resistant cancer stem cells and enhance the toxicity towards malignant cells [ ] (Table  ). Harnessing exosomes for targeted drug delivery characterizes a promising strategy in glioma therapy. This method may enable more effective treatment options for patients.

 

## Progress and obstacles in the production and characterization of exosomes for therapeutic purposes
The application of exosomes in clinical settings remains in the early stages, primarily due to several challenges that limit their therapeutic potential. A significant challenge lies in the necessity to produce exosomes on a large scale, all the while ensuring precise and high-quality characterization [ ]. Exosomes are usually obtained in limited quantities from biological fluids or cell cultures [ ]. Nonetheless, innovative production methods such as bioreactors, microfluidics, and 3D scaffolds have demonstrated potential in enhancing exosome yields. In comparison to traditional methods, it is possible to achieve yields that are up to ten times greater, particularly when utilizing bioreactors [ , ]. Despite these advancements, further progress is essential to create exosomes that are cost-effective, consistently reproducible, and sufficiently effective for clinical applications [ , ].
Enhancing process efficiency necessitates the establishment of standardized procedures for the separation of exosomes from various biological sources utilizing a unified technique. While techniques like fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) are widely recognized for their roles in characterizing and validating exosomes, they have limitations when it comes to independently evaluating the biochemical and biophysical properties of these vesicles. To address the existing gap in current methods, it is essential to employ sophisticated techniques for the precise characterization of exosomes in clinical settings [ , ].

## The physicochemical properties of pharmaceuticals and their effectiveness in exosome loading
In recent years, there has been considerable interest in the potential of exosomes to serve as carriers for therapeutic agents. Exosomes have the ability to encapsulate a diverse range of substances, including medications, which positions them as a promising alternative for targeted therapies. The way in which drug molecules are loaded into these vesicles is significantly affected by their physicochemical characteristics. Hydrophobicity stands out as an essential property that influences the efficiency of drug loading into exosomes. The favorable interactions that hydrophobic drugs have with the lipid bilayer of the exosome lead to enhanced loading of these substances. Conversely, hydrophilic drugs may encounter difficulties in traversing this lipid layer, which is essential for achieving effective encapsulation. For instance, the hydrophobic anticancer drug paclitaxel has been effectively integrated into exosomes, thanks to its beneficial interactions with the lipid membrane [ ]. The dimensions and configuration of exosomes play a crucial role in the efficiency of drug molecule loading within them. Smaller molecules, particularly those whose dimensions closely resemble the lipid bilayer of the exosome, are more likely to be successfully encapsulated. Additionally, medications that possess a spherical shape might align better with the curvature of the exosome membrane, potentially enhancing the efficiency of loading [ ]. For example, researchers have effectively incorporated curcumin, a small, spherical molecule known for its anti-inflammatory properties, into exosomes [ ]. Furthermore, the charge of drug molecules can play a crucial role in determining how effectively they are encapsulated within exosomes. Positively charged medications tend to show higher encapsulation rates due to their stronger interactions with the negatively charged phospholipids present in the exosome membrane. Conversely, the presence of repulsive forces interacting with the membrane can lead to a decrease in the loading efficiency of negatively charged compounds. For instance, exosomes have demonstrated effectiveness in encapsulating the positively charged chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin [ , ].
A variety of techniques have been established to enhance the effectiveness of drug incorporation into exosomes. A widely used approach involves employing membrane permeabilization techniques, such as extrusion or sonication, to facilitate the entry of drugs into the vesicles [ ]. To enhance the effectiveness of encapsulation, it is possible to chemically modify drugs to alter their charge or increase their hydrophobic properties. For example, research has shown that attaching hydrophobic components to hydrophilic drugs enhances their incorporation into exosomes [ , ]. Optimizing drug encapsulation into exosomes for targeted drug delivery involves a careful consideration of specific features and the application of appropriate techniques. Enhancing therapeutic outcomes for various diseases and progressing the development of exosome-based drug delivery systems necessitates further research in this field [ , ] (Table  ).

 

The physicochemical properties of pharmaceuticals considerably influence their loading efficacy in exosomes. Understanding these properties is essential for optimizing drug creations that can be successfully transported via exosomes to target glioma cells.

## Future prospects
Exosomes have demonstrated significant potential as drug delivery vehicles for glioma therapy, particularly because of their native biocompatibility, relatively low immunogenicity, and emerging evidence for brain delivery capabilities [ ]. In addition, their membrane and cargo can be engineered to enhance loading capacity, circulation stability, and targeting performance. Despite this promise, translation remains limited by incomplete understanding of exosome biology, heterogeneous subpopulations, and practical barriers related to scalable production, consistent characterization, and predictable in vivo behavior [ ].
A key priority for future work is the development of rational and testable engineering strategies that directly address BBB and blood brain tumor barrier constraints. Surface functionalization should be optimized to improve brain entry and glioma specificity, including receptor mediated transcytosis targeting for BBB transport combined with tumor selective ligands for glioma homing. Dual targeting designs may reduce off target uptake while increasing intratumoral exposure. In addition, stimulus responsive approaches could improve local release and functional delivery, for example via pH sensitive or enzyme responsive mechanisms that exploit the acidic and protease rich glioma microenvironment. These designs should be evaluated with quantitative readouts that connect formulation parameters to BBB penetration, intratumoral drug levels, and biological response [ ].
Another major direction is improving cargo loading efficiency and selecting functionally relevant payloads. Current loading methods often remain inefficient for clinical translation, particularly for large hydrophilic agents and nucleic acid cargos, and basic incubation provides limited and variable encapsulation [ , ]. Future platforms should prioritize scalable loading approaches that preserve vesicle integrity while enabling higher payloads and controlled release kinetics. Beyond single agent delivery, combination cargo concepts are likely to be increasingly important in glioma. Examples include co delivery of a cytotoxic drug with resistance modulators that address DNA repair related therapy failure, as well as payloads that reprogram immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment [ ].
From a translational perspective, standardization and manufacturing readiness are essential. Progress toward clinical adoption requires GMP compatible, scalable production workflows, such as controlled bioreactor based systems, alongside harmonized characterization panels that assess purity, identity, potency, stability, and batch to batch consistency [ ]. Establishing robust potency assays that reflect intended mechanisms of action is critical because performance cannot be inferred from size and marker profiling alone. In parallel, rigorous safety and biodistribution evaluation should be prioritized, including immunogenicity, off target organ accumulation, procoagulant potential, and long-term toxicity, especially under repeated dosing paradigms.
Finally, more predictive preclinical validation is needed to reduce the gap between early proof of concept and clinical outcomes. This includes orthotopic glioma models and patient derived systems combined with quantitative imaging and pharmacokinetic profiling to evaluate delivery efficiency and therapeutic benefit. Exosomes also remain promising as biomarkers for diagnosis and monitoring, and future research should integrate standardized isolation and analysis pipelines to strengthen clinical interpretability [ , ]. Overall, continued advances in exosome bioengineering, together with disciplined translational development and standardized outcome measures, may substantially expand the role of engineered exosomes in precision glioma therapy [ – ].

## Conclusion
Exosomes have been recognized as significant contributors to the underlying mechanisms of gliomas. Cancer cells produce and release these substances, facilitating interactions between the TME and malignant cells. Exosomes are involved in various processes that are crucial for the growth and survival of gliomas. These processes include immune tolerance, angiogenesis, cell invasion and migration, cancer cell stemness, cell proliferation, malignant transformation, and ultimately, treatment resistance. The field of exosome-targeted therapy is advancing rapidly through ongoing research efforts. Engineered exosomes, as instruments that have the potential to enhance or refine existing drug delivery systems, show significant promise. Their small size, flexibility, and the presence of adhesive proteins on their surface are believed to play a significant role in their capacity to traverse the BBB. Furthermore, the encapsulation within a lipid bilayer and the natural production processes contribute to a reduction in toxicity and immunogenicity, while simultaneously enhancing stability in peripheral blood. Exosomes are currently being explored for their potential roles as diagnostic and therapeutic agents due to their ability to facilitate communication between cells during the progression of brain tumors, particularly in the challenging context of glioma treatment. Exosomes have the capacity to transport a variety of medicinal substances. These markers serve as valuable indicators for monitoring how well therapies are working, allowing for the detection of treatment resistance through less invasive approaches. Exosomes can be administered either through intravenous or intranasal routes to specifically target tumor cells in clinical environments. Administering tumor-targeting membrane peptides either intravenously or intranasally has the potential to enhance exosome transport, which could be beneficial in the context of brain tumors. Future studies should prioritize the development of advanced methods for the accurate identification of exosomes and their contents. Additionally, further investigation is needed to assess the potential of exosomes as targeted delivery systems in the treatment of gliomas. In conclusion, engineered exosomes shows a promising way for addressing the challenges of glioblastoma therapy. By leveraging their exceptional properties, we can advance innovative approaches that enhance drug delivery and treatment efficacy, ultimately improving outcomes for patients with this aggressive form of brain cancer.
