## Abstract
 Diabetic wounds exhibit delayed and incomplete healing,
usually
due to vascular and nerve damage. Dysregulation of cellular Ca2+ homeostasis has recently been shown to be closely related
to insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes mellitus. However, the involvement
of this dysregulation in diabetic wound complications remains unknown.
In this study, we found calcium dysregulation in patients with diabetic
ulcers via tissue protein profiling. High glucose and glucometabolic
toxicant stimulation considerably impaired the function of TRPC6,
a pore subunit of transient receptor potential channels mediating
Ca2+ influx, and mitochondria, which regulate calcium cycling
and metabolism. Furthermore, we found that mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived
small extracellular vesicles (MSC-sEVs) could play a dual role in
restoring the function of TRPC6 and mitochondria by delivering transcription
factor SP2 and deubiquitinating enzyme USP9, respectively. MSC-sEVs
could transfer SP2 that activated TRPC6 expression by binding to its
specific promoter regions (−1519 to −1725 bp), thus
recovering Ca2+ influx and downstream pathways. MSC-sEVs
also promoted mitophagy to restore mitochondrial function by transporting
USP9 that stabilized the expression of Parkin, a major player in mitophagy,
thereby guaranteeing Ca2+ efflux and avoidance of Ca2+ overload. Targeting the regulation of calcium homeostasis
provides a perspective for understanding diabetic wound healing, and
the corresponding design of MSC-sEVs could be a potential therapeutic
strategy.
Keywords: hucMSC-sEVs, TRPC6, calcium homeostasis, diabetic wound healing, mitophagy

## Introduction
Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is one of the
most prevalent complications
of diabetes mellitus (DM), which gradually develops into foot circulation
and sensory disorders, resulting in amputation or death from severe
systemic infection. − Refractory DFU wounds are the major cause of nontraumatic
amputation and have become a critical problem that threatens the life
and health of patients with DM. The delayed
healing of wounds in diabetes is due to the lack of chemokines, abnormal
inflammatory response, and a lack of angiogenesis and epithelium formation.
Although the currently used treatments, such as wound debridement,
anti-inflammatory treatment, and local topical application of antibiotics
or growth factors and angiogenic factors, provide some relief, their
efficacy remains unsatisfactory. Therefore,
examining diabetic wounds from different perspectives and deciphering
hitherto unknown pathogenic mechanisms may help identify targets and
therapeutic strategies for refractory diabetic wounds.
Clinical
studies have shown that patients with DM often exhibit
electrolyte disorders. Ca2+ homeostasis was recently reported
to be closely related to metabolic diseases, such as obesity, insulin
resistance, and type 2 DM (T2DM). As a
second messenger that regulates cell fate through signal transduction,
Ca2+ plays a crucial role in maintaining cell function. , The influx and efflux of Ca2+ into and out of cells are
precisely regulated. Ca2+ channels are major carriers mediating
Ca2+ transport; these include voltage-gated calcium channels
(VGCCs), storage-operated calcium channels (SOCCs), and transient
receptor potential (TRP). VGCCs mainly
mediate Ca2+ transport in excitatory cells, such as myocardial
and nerve cells. A variety of Ca2+ channels are expressed in skin tissues. Ca2+ channel-mediated
inflow of Ca2+ and Ca2+-dependent signal transduction
facilitates skin cell development, differentiation, and barrier restoration. , Moreover, knocking down of TRP significantly affects the function
of the glucose transporter GLUT4 and the uptake and utilization of
glucose by peripheral tissues, which
indicates the role of calcium channels in regulating glucose metabolism
in the skin. Mitochondria are vital organelles for intracellular Ca2+ metabolism, which maintain physiological processes by responding
to and rectifying cytoplasmic Ca2+ signals. Mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) and Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCLX) on the inner membrane of
mitochondria can expel excess intracellular Ca2+, regulate
intracellular Ca2+ metabolism, and ensure Ca2+ homeostasis. Recent studies also revealed
that mitochondrial dysfunction is mandatorily linked with the occurrence
and progression of metabolic diseases, such as diabetes. , However, under the complex environment of diabetic wound complications,
the involvement of the Ca2+ homeostasis disruption remains
unknown. Targeting calcium homeostasis may provide other insights
into diabetic wound healing.
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have
attracted much attention in
the field of regenerative medicine for their pluripotency and paracrine
functions. Emerging evidence shows that MSC-secreted small extracellular
vesicles (sEVs) are promising cell-free therapeutic options for many
diseases. sEVs are involved in various
biological processes, such as cell survival, angiogenesis, and immune
regulation. − In previous studies, we found that human umbilical
cord MSC-derived sEVs (hucMSC-sEVs) could reduce blood glucose in
T2DM rats, which indicates natural advantages
of MSC-sEVs for treatment of DFU. sEVs are multifunctional vesicles
containing diverse bioactive components. Sluijter proposed that different protein contents of sEVs can be
related to their roles in different stages of tissue damage repair
or on different subcellular levels even at the same stage and that
sEVs often work together through multiple components rather than through
a single component. For example, in chronic
wound repair, miR-146a and TGF-β in sEVs could promote wound
healing by regulating inflammation and angiogenesis. − In previous studies, we found that sEVs transport Wnt4 and 14-3-3-ζ
to promote wound healing in the early stages of repair and prevent
excessive cell proliferation and scar formation in the later stages
of repair, suggesting the dynamic regulatory effects of sEVs as “accelerator”
and “brake” in different stages of the wound healing
process. We surmised that, compared to a single target treatment,
it would be more advantageous to make use of the multicontent and
multifunctional features of sEVs for diabetic wound healing.
In the present study, we explored a perspective related to the
involvement of calcium homeostasis in diabetic wound complications
and tried to decipher the underlying mechanism, focusing on how calcium
channel protein and mitophagy are involved in diabetic wound healing.
Our results show that targeting both the calcium channel and mitochondria
by loading specific bioactive components, such as SP2 and USP9, into
hucMSC-sEVs may be a promising therapeutic strategy for diabetic wound
healing.

## Results and Discussion

## TRPC6 Is Impaired in Diabetic Wounds
Dermal fibroblasts
(DFs) are the main cell source for the repair phase of proliferation
and matrix remodeling. Primary DFs were obtained from neonatal Sprague–Dawley
(SD) rats and cultured in vitro. The positive markers
α-SMA and vimentin and the negative marker CK10 were used to
identify DFs (Figure S1). DFs were treated
with bovine serum albumin modified with advanced glycation end products
(AGE-BSA) to mimic the high-glucose environment prevalent in diabetes in vitro. AGE-BSA caused apoptosis in DFs at 100 and 200
mg/L (Figure S2A). The lower AGE-BSA concentration
(100 mg/L) was selected for subsequent experiments. AGE-BSA treatment
diminished the ability of DFs to secrete collagen and promoted inflammation
(Figure S2B). These results imply that
the high-glucose environment or the toxic products of glucose metabolism
impaired the skin cells and their function.
The intracellular
Ca2+ levels decreased after AGE-BSA stimulation ( A,B). We focused
on evaluating the expression of Ca2+ channels that mediate
Ca2+ influx. Different Ca2+ channels were expressed
in skin tissue ( C). Given that DFs are nonexcitable, TRP channels and SOCCs were
prioritized for investigations (Table S1). Multiple independent qRT-PCR analyses (repeated three times) showed
that, among the candidate Ca2+ channels, the mRNA expression
level of TRPC6 was significantly and stably decreased in response
to AGE-BSA treatment (Figures S3 and D). The expression of TRPC6 was also significantly
decreased at the protein level, which was consistent with the trend
observed at the mRNA level ( E). Furthermore, targeted mass spectrometry of 13 samples
from patients with DFU and 8 control samples showed that TRPC6 expression
was decreased in the diabetic wound area ( F). Gene correlation analysis revealed that
TRPC6 was closely related to genes regulating the cell cycle, glucose
transporters, and collagen generation ( G). Subsequently, immunohistochemical (IHC)
detection using other clinical DFU samples revealed decreased TRPC6
expression in the dermal region of the wound ( H). Quantitative proteomic analysis of samples
from patients with DFU revealed that Ca2+ homeostasis and
mitochondrial inner membrane function were impaired in the wound tissue
(Figure S4). These results confirmed that
TRPC6 is impaired in diabetic wounds and may be involved in diabetic
wound healing.
 

## TRPC6 Knockdown Decreases Ca2+ Influx and Impairs
the Biological Function of DFs in Wound Healing
To determine
the function of TRPC6, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were designed
for silencing the TRPC6 gene (Table S3). The working efficiency of the three siRNAs was
verified; all of the siRNA fragments significantly reduced the expression
of TRPC6 at the mRNA level (Figure S5A).
However, only siRNA fragments 2 and 3 significantly reduced the level
of protein expression (Figure S5B). Therefore,
the siTRPC6 fragments 2 and 3 were used for further experiments. TRPC6
knockdown reduced Ca2+ influx and intracellular Ca2+ concentrations with or without exogenous calcium addition
( A–C).
TRPC6 knockdown attenuated DF-specific collagen secretion and extracellular
matrix (ECM) production ( D,E, and S6A). The proliferation
and migration abilities of DFs were weakened in conjunction with decreased
TRPC6 expression, whereas the number of apoptotic cells and the level
of inflammation were increased ( F and S6B–D). We also infected the rat skin tissue with shTRPC6-AAV9, which
effectively knocked down the expression of TRPC6 in skin tissue ( G,H). TRPC6 knockdown
damaged the skin integrity, collagen secretion and arrangement, and
angiogenesis ( I–K). These results show that TRPC6 knockdown in skin tissue
affects the biological processes critical to wound healing. SAR7334,
a TRPC6 channel-specific inhibitor, was introduced to block the TRPC6
channel activity. In line with the results obtained for TRPC6 knockdown,
Ca2+ influx was markedly attenuated after SAR7334 administration,
impairing the biological function of the target cells (Figure S7). To confirm the role of TRPC6, we
overexpressed TRPC6 in DFs by using plasmid transfection. In contrast
to TRPC6 knockdown, TRPC6 overexpression enhanced Ca2+ influx
and the biological functions of DFs (Figure S8). Furthermore, TRPC6 overexpression in a high-glucose environment
could restore the function of DFs under conditions of AGE production
(Figure S9). We also investigated the effects
of TRPC6 on the epidermis and angiogenesis. HaCaT and human umbilical
vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were transfected with siRNAs or overexpression
plasmids, respectively (Table S4). TRPC6
decreased Ca2+ influx and impaired the biological function
of HaCaT cells and HUVECs (Figures S10 and S12), whereas its overexpression partly enhanced the Ca2+ influx and biological function of these cells (Figures S11 and S13). These results indicate that TRPC6 plays
a non-negligible role as either a channel or a protein and contributes
to the skin architecture.
 

## HucMSC-sEVs Effectively Help in Repairing the Diabetic Wound
sEVs secreted by MSCs hold promise in regenerative medicine. In
this study, hucMSCs and hucMSC-sEVs were obtained and characterized
in Figures S14 and S15. HucMSC-sEVs were
effectively internalized ( A). HucMSC-sEV treatment reduced apoptosis and inflammatory
damage caused by AGE-BSA in DFs ( B,F) and enhanced the ability of DFs to secrete
collagen-like ECM ( C,D). HucMSC-sEVs also effectively accelerated wound healing
in diabetic rats ( G). An examination of the wound revealed a comparatively decent
re-epithelialization in the wound area in the diabetic rats treated
with hucMSC-sEVs; the skin tissue architecture was complete, and the
epidermis and dermis layers were clear. Other groups are set as follows:
the PBS treatment was used as a negative solvent control. sEVs-free
conditioned medium (sEVs-free CM) treatment was used to show whether
the function is mediated by sEVs or other components in the hucMSC
culture medium. HFL1-sEVs, which are the human fetal lung fibroblast
1 (HFL1) derived sEVs, were used to show whether other cell derived
sEVs have a similar function as hucMSC derived sEVs. The alginate
dressing treatment was used as a positive control, which is approved
for clinical use. The results showed that epidermal rebiochemistry
was not complete, and the wound tissue structure was disordered and
was accompanied by a large amount of blood callus and inflammation
in other groups ( E). The wound area was the smallest in the hucMSC-sEV-treated group
( E). Hematoxylin
and eosin (HE) staining showed abundant inflammatory cell infiltration
and epidermal coverage in the control group. In the hucMSC-sEV-treated
group, a complete epidermal architecture was established, and some
skin accessory glands and organs, such as the haptic body and lymphatic
vessels, were observed. CD31 was used as a marker to identify neoangiogenesis.
HucMSC-sEVs treatment significantly promoted angiogenesis with an
intact lumen structure, whereas the neovascularization in the control
group was incomplete ( H). Sirius Red staining was used to observe collagen distribution.
In the hucMSC-sEVs-treatment group, the collagen was stained dark
red and showed bundles, cords, and waves, which were closer to the
collagen distribution pattern observed in the normal skin tissue (Figure S16).
 

## HucMSC-sEVs Restored TRPC6 Expression in Vitro and in Vivo
As TRPC6 expression is reduced
in diabetic wounds, hucMSC-sEVs treatment can facilitate diabetic
wound healing. We investigated whether hucMSC-sEVs could repair diabetic
wounds by regulating TRPC6 expression. AGE-BSA-stimulated DFs were
treated with or without hucMSC-sEVs. This treatment rescued the reduction
in TRPC6 expression caused by AGE-BSA at both mRNA and protein levels,
which was verified in multiple repeats of the experiments ( A,B). The immunofluorescence
assay revealed an enhancement in the expression of TRPC6 caused by
hucMSC-sEVs treatment. Because TRPC6 is a transmembrane Ca2+ channel protein on the cell membrane, we labeled the cell membrane
with a membrane dye, DIO (green), at the same time and found that
hucMSC-sEVs promoted the expression of TRPC6 (red) on the cell membrane
(white arrowheads), thereby, mediating the intracellular Ca2+ influx and the increase in the cytosolic Ca2+ levels
( C). Furthermore,
the results of IHC and tissue immunofluorescence showed that, compared
with the control group, the TRPC6 levels in the hucMSC-sEVs-treatment
group were significantly increased, a finding consistent with the in vitro results ( D,E). HucMSC-sEVs increased the Ca2+ influx
( F–H)
and thereby affected the cell function. These results demonstrate
that hucMSC-sEVs promote diabetic wound healing, at least partly by
regulating the expression of TRPC6.
 

## HucMSC-sEVs Transfer the Transcription Factor SP2 to Activate TRPC6 Gene Expression
Given that hucMSC-sEVs could
increase the expression of TRPC6 at the transcriptional level, and
considering the interaction between the essential transcription factors
and promoters of genes, we hypothesized that hucMSC-sEVs may transfer
transcription factors into the target cells that bind to the promoter
of TRPC6 to initiate its transcription. We searched
for the promoter sequence of TRPC6 using the NCBI
network, predicted the transcription factors that could bind to it
using the UCSC database, and predicted five putative transcription
factors. These were SP2, KLF5, STAT1::STAT2, NEUROD1, and ZSCAN4.
The JASPAR database was used to score the five predicted and screened
transcription factors and to search for the binding sites. The transcription
factor SP2 had the highest score and was the most likely to bind to
the TRPC6 promoter region ( A). We repeatedly detected and verified the
expression of SP2 in hucMSC-sEVs and found that SP2 was present in
hucMSC-sEVs ( B and S17A,B). Western blot analysis showed
that the expression of SP2 in DFs increased after the hucMSC-sEVs
treatment, and the expression of TRPC6 was correspondingly elevated
(Figure S17C). Because SP2 needs to enter
the nucleus to perform its function, nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins
were extracted from hucMSC-sEVs-treated DFs using a nucleoplasmic
separation assay for detection using Western blotting ( C). With the hucMSC-sEVs treatment,
the intranuclear SP2 levels were increased, whereas SP2 was barely
detected in the cytoplasm of DFs, indicating that hucMSC-sEVs could
transport SP2 and increase its expression, particularly the intranuclear
expression in DFs.
 We further verified the specific regulatory relationship
between
SP2 and TRPC6 expression. The results of double luciferase reporter
gene and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays showed that SP2
could directly bind to the TRPC6 gene promoter and
positively regulated the target gene expression ( D,E). To further determine
the specific site of SP2 binding in the TRPC6 gene
promoter, we first segmented the TRPC6 gene promoter
into two fragments (TRPC6 promo 1: −2000 to
−1518 bp; TRPC6 promo 2: −1519 to +100
bp). The results of the double luciferase reporter gene assay showed
that SP2 bound to TRPC6 promo 2 ( F,G). We then cut TRPC6 promo 2 into three fragments (TRPC6 promo 2-1:
−1519 to −1725 bp; TRPC6 promo 2-2:
−1726 to 1932 bp; TRPC6 promo 2-3: −1933
to +100 bp), and the binding position was determined using the double
luciferase reporter assay ( F,H). These results indicated that the binding of SP2
to the TRPC6 gene promoter (−1519 to −1725
bp) positively regulated TRPC6 expression.
To demonstrate that
hucMSC-sEVs transfer SP2 into target cells,
hucMSCs were transfected with siRNA to knockdown SP2 genes; the supernatant was collected, and hucMSC-sEVs were isolated
and verified (Table S5). The efficiency
of hucMSC and hucMSC-sEVs knockdown was verified (Figure S18A,B). SP2 knockdown hucMSC-sEVs (SiSP2-sEVs) and
the control group (SiCtrl-sEVs) were characterized. The results of
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that SiCtrl-sEVs and
SiSP2-sEVs had a typical cuplike structure (Figure S18C,F). The particle sizes of the SiCtrl-sEVs and SiSP2-sEVs
were mainly in the 30–200 nm range with a peak at 152 nm (Figure S18D,G), and SiSP2-sEVs presented a negative
zeta potential (Figure S18E,H). We used
SiSP2-sEVs to treat DFs. As expected, compared with SiCtrl-sEVs, SiSP2-sEVs
exhibited a decreased ability to rescue the mRNA and protein expression
of TRPC6 ( I,J).
Similarly, the collagen secretion by DFs treated with SiSP2-sEVs did
not change significantly ( L) and SiSP2-sEVs did not promote the proliferation
and migration of DFs ( K and S18I). This further indicates
that hucMSC-sEVs regulate TRPC6 via SP2.

## HucMSC-sEVs Protect the Function of Mitochondria to Maintain
Calcium Balance
The overexpression of TRPC6 and hucMSC-sEVs
can upregulate intracellular Ca2+ levels to restore cell
function within a certain period. We found that TRPC6 overexpression
alone was not always an effective treatment when the time of action
was longer (72 or 96 h), as it led to continuous Ca2+ influx
without the efflux of this ion ( A,B). Ca2+ accumulation may lead to intracellular
calcium overload and cause cell damage, affecting the proliferation and migration of DFs ( C,D, S19, and S20). Mitochondria are crucial organelles for expulsion
of calcium from cells, and an increased local cytosolic Ca2+ concentration stimulates mitochondrial Ca2+ (mCa2+) uptake and drives changes in mCa2+. We found that hucMSC-sEVs treatment relieved
AGE-BSA-induced impairment of the mitochondrial membrane potential
and restored mitochondrial function ( E). Moreover, hucMSC-sEVs enhanced the expression
of MCU and NCLX, which mediate calcium movement in the mitochondria,
participate in mitochondrial calcium metabolism ( F), and increase mitochondrial Ca2+ levels ( G,H). These results demonstrated
that hucMSC-sEVs protected mitochondrial function, which guaranteed
that intracellular Ca2+ could flow out of the cells for
avoiding Ca2+ overload and thus help maintain homeostasis.
Therefore, treatment with hucMSC-sEVs is safer and more effective.
 

## HucMSC-sEVs Promote Parkin-Mediated Mitophagy by Delivering
USP9
Mitophagy is a critical process for removing damaged
mitochondria and for maintaining cellular homeostasis. , We observed that hucMSC-sEVs upregulated the expression of mitophagy-associated
proteins and activated the PINK1/parkin-mediated classical pathway
of mitophagy, whereas the expression of P62 had the opposite effect
( A). TEM showed
that hucMSC-sEVs facilitated the formation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes
( B). Moreover,
hucMSC-sEVs enhanced the expression of parkin and activated its recruitment
to the mitochondria ( C,D and S21). Given the fact that
a ubiquitin-involving regulatory system is the essence of mitophagy
mediated by parkin, we speculated whether a deubiquitination enzyme
is present in hucMSC-sEVs to stabilize the expression of parkin and
thereby promote the classical pathway-mediated mitophagy. Toward this
end and to explore the specific molecular mechanism, we screened the
deubiquitinating enzyme USP9 from hucMSC-sEVs by protein profile sequencing
and verified the results using secondary mass spectrometry and Western
blotting ( E and S22). After hucMSC-sEVs treatment,
the expression of USP9 in DFs was significantly increased ( F). To further explore
whether the increased expression of USP9 in DFs was due to hucMSC-sEVs
or whether the expression of USP9 in DFs was affected by hucMSC-sEVs
treatment, we transfected hucMSCs with sh-USP9 using lentiviral vectors
and collected the conditioned medium for the isolation and identification
of sEVs (Figure S23). Compared with the
control group (shGFP-sEVs), shUSP9-sEVs did not significantly promote
the expression of USP9 (Figure S24A) or
reduce the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Figure S24B), which indicates that USP9 could be transferred
by hucMSC-sEVs. The results of the coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay
further confirmed that USP9 and parkin could bind to each other ( G,I). USP9 is a deubiquitinating
enzyme that functions by deubiquitinating its substrates. After treating
DFs with MG-132, with or without AGE-BSA and hucMSC-sEVs, parkin was
immunoprecipitated with specific antibodies, and the degree of ubiquitination
of parkin was detected. AGE-BSA treatment promoted the ubiquitination
of parkin, whereas hucMSC-sEVs removed the ubiquitin molecules from
parkin ( H),
further stabilizing the protein.
 

## Conclusion
We report several major findings: (i) Ca2+ homeostasis,
particularly the functions of the Ca2+ channel protein,
TRPC6, and mitochondria, is impaired in the specific microenvironment
of diabetic wounds; (ii) TRPC6 can act as both a calcium channel and
a protein that affects the function of skin cells; (iii) HucMSC-sEVs
can restore the TRPC6 function by transferring the transcription factor
SP2 to activate the expression of the TRPC6 gene
by binding to a specific promoter region (−1519 to −1725
bp); (iv) HucMSC-sEVs can deliver USP9 to inhibit parkin ubiquitination
so as to promote mitophagy and restore mitochondria function. In conclusion,
the present study provides another perspective based on calcium homeostasis
to understand the dynamic mechanisms of diabetic wound complications.
Targeting both TRPC6 and mitochondria and loading specific bioactive
components, such as SP2 and USP9, into hucMSC-sEVs may be a promising,
effective, and safe method for diabetic wound healing.

## Materials and Methods

## Ethics
All of the experiments in this study were approved
by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangsu University (IRB approval
protocol number: 2020161).

## Animals
T2DM animal models were established as described
previously. Rats were purchased from
the Laboratory Animal Center of Jiangsu University (Zhenjiang, China).
All animal experiments complied with the standard guidelines for the
care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Institutional
Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu University. The fasting blood
glucose of the model rats was confirmed to be ≥16.7 mmol/L.
The rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10% chloral
hydrate solution (250 μL/100 g). Round, full-thick
cutaneous wounds (2 cm) were created on the back of each rat. Rats
were then randomly assigned to one of the five groups (n = 6): PBS control group (200 μL), hucMSC-sEV
group (200 μL of PBS containing 1.0 ×
1010 hucMSC-sEVs), sEV-free CM group (200 μL), HFL1-sEV group (200 μL of PBS containing
1.0 × 1010 HFL1-sEVs), and clinical alginate dressing
group. Clinical alginate dressings were purchased from the Affiliated
Hospital of Jiangsu University, China. The different solutions were
injected subcutaneously at four points on the edge of the wound by
using an insulin syringe. The injection was repeated 1 week later.
Wound area measurements were performed every 2–3 days starting
on day 0 of surgery. On the 20th day after surgery, the rats were
sacrificed and harvested skin specimens were analyzed using HE staining,
Sirius Red staining, and IHC. The percentage of wound closure was
calculated using the following formula: wound closure (%) = (A0 – At)/A0 × 100%, where A0 is the wound area on day 0 and At is the wound area on a particular day.

## Cell Culture
DFs were isolated by dissecting the skin
of newborn rats using a standard protocol. The removed skin was washed
in PBS supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptavidin (Bioind,
Israel) and cut into small pieces (1–2 mm). They were then
placed in a 10 cm well and cultured for 27 days in α-MEM medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum
(FBS, Excell) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Bioind, Israel).
The culture medium was changed every 3 days. DFs growing around the
skin pieces were dissociated 7 days later, and 3–5 passages
of DFs were used for the experiments. DFs were photographed using
a microscope (Olympus) and identified using α-SMA and vimentin via Western blotting. Primary hucMSCs were isolated
as described previously. HUVECs were
purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and
cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM,
Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% FBS. HaCaT cells were purchased
from Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and cultured in DMEM.

## Isolation and Characterization of sEVs from Cell Supernatant
FBS was ultracentrifuged at 100 000g at
4 °C for 16 h using an ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter) to prepare
sEVs-free FBS. The sEVs-free culture medium was used to replace the
conventional culture medium when the cell density reached 80%. After
the culture medium was gradient-centrifuged to remove cell debris
(300g, 10 min), dead cells (2000g, 10 min), and organelles (10 000g, 30 min),
the supernatant was centrifuged at 100 000g for 90 min, and the isolated sEVs were resuspended in PBS for further
use. The nanosight tracking analysis (NTA) assay was used to detect
the size of the sEVs, and TEM (FEI Tecnai 12, Philips) was used to
analyze their morphology. Western blotting was performed to determine
the expression of CD9 (Abcam), CD63 (Abcam), TSG101 (Abcam), HSP70
(Abcam), and calnexin (Abcam). A BCA protein assay kit (Vazyme) was
used to determine the concentrations of sEVs.

## siRNA and Plasmid Cell Transfection
All siRNAs and
plasmids were synthesized by Gene Pharma (Suzhou, China) and are listed
in Table S1. Cells were transfected with
100 pmol of siRNA or 2.5 μg of plasmid using
4 μL of the Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime,
China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The efficiency
of cell transfection with siRNAs and plasmids for each target was
validated by using real-time quantitative PCR and Western blotting.

## Cell-Proliferation Assay
DFs were harvested and plated
at an initial density of 1500 cells per well in 96-well plates (Cellstar)
and treated with 10 μL of the CCK-8 reagent
(Vazyme, China). The number of viable cells was determined every 24
h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an Absorbance Reader
(BioTek, America).

## Tube Formation Assay
HUVECs were first transfected
with the siRNA or plasmid in 6-well plates for 48 h. Thereafter, 50 μL of cold Matrigel (BD Biosciences, America) was
added to each well of a 96-well plate with a precooled tip and allowed
to polymerize at 37 °C for 30 min. A total of 3.0 × 104 cells from different groups was plated onto the Matrigel
in 100 μL of complete medium (n = 3). After incubation for 6 h at 37 °C, the tubular structures
were viewed using a Nikon TS100 microscope and random photomicrographs
were taken for each well.

## Wound-Healing Assay
DFs were seeded in 6-well plates
(Cellstar), allowed to reach subconfluence, and then transfected with
siRNAs or plasmids. After 48 h, a 200 μL pipet
tip was used to scratch across the dish surface and washed with PBS
to remove the floating cells. Bright-field images were captured along
the wound using a Nikon TS100 microscope (×40), and a series
of pictures were taken 0, 12, and 24 h after wounding. The rate of
migration was calculated as follows: migration area (%) = (A0 – At)/A0 × 100%, where A0 represents the area of the initial wound and At represents the remaining area of the wound
at a particular time point.

## Quantitative Real-Time PCR
Total RNA was extracted
using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme) according to standard protocols, and
RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop One instrument (Thermo
Fisher Scientific). A reverse transcription kit (Vazyme) was used
to synthesize cDNA from 1 μg of total RNA following
the manufacturer’s protocols. RT-PCR was performed using an
AceQ QPCR SYBR Green Master mix (Vazyme) on a thermal cycler (Applied
Biosystems QuantStudio 3, Invitrogen). The primer sequences are given
in Table S2. The β-actin gene was used as an endogenous control to normalize variations
in the RNA extraction, the degree of RNA degradation, and variability
in RT efficiency. To quantify the results, the comparative threshold
cycle method ΔΔCt was used.

## Western Blotting
Cells were treated with an ice-cold
lysis buffer (Pierce) containing proteinase inhibitors (Pierce). The
lysates were centrifuged at 12 000g and 4
°C for 15 min, mixed with a sample buffer (Life Technologies),
and boiled for 10 min at 100 °C. The total protein samples were
subjected to 12% SDS/PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore)
by semidry Western blotting. The membrane was blocked in 5% nonfat
milk for 2 h and then probed using a specific primary antibody at
4 °C overnight with β-actin as the internal
control. Membranes were washed with TBST and then incubated with an
HRP conjugated goat antirabbit/mouse IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen,
#31460/#31430, 1:2000) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently,
the membranes were washed using TBST three times. The bands on the
membrane were visualized using an ECL detection system (General Electric
Company) and analyzed using ImageJ software. The primary antibodies
are as follows: β-actin (Abclonal, #AC026,
1:8000), TRPC6 (Novus, #NBP1-77260, 1:500), α-SMA (HUABIO, #ET1607-53, 1:500), Collagen I (Bioss, #bs-10423R,
1:500), Vimentin (Bioworld, #BS1491, 1:500), SLC8B1 (HUABIO, #ER1901-93,
1:500), MICU1 (HUABIO, #ER1803-99, 1:500), MCU (HUABIO, #ER1803-57,
1:500), SP2 (abcam, #ab229468, 1:500), CD63 (abcam, #ab271286, 1:500),
TSG101 (abcam, #ab125011, 1:500), CD81 (abcam, #ab79559, 1:500), CD9
(abcam, #ab236630, 1:500), ALIX (abcam, #ab117600, 1:500), HSP70 (abcam,
#ab2787, 1:500), Calnexin (Abclonal, #A15631, 1:500), PCNA (CST, #13110,
1:500), and Histone H3 (CST, #4620).

## Immunohistochemistry
Paraffin-embedded skin sections
were subjected to conventional deparaffinization, followed by antigen
retrieval using citrate buffer at 95 °C in a water bath. After
blocking with a 5% BSA solution, the skin sections were incubated
overnight with a specific rabbit polyclonal anti-TRPC6 antibody (Novus,
#NBP1-77260, 1:500) at 4 °C. Goat polyclonal antirabbit and antimouse
peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (#SA1020; Boster) were
used. After incubation with diaminobenzidine (Boster, no. AR1022),
images were captured and analyzed using a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic
MIDI, 3D Histech).

## [Ca2+]cyto Measurement
Cytosolic
Ca2+ levels were recorded in Fura-2 AM (Beyotime, no. S1052)-
or Fura-4 AM (Beyotime, #S1060)-loaded cells. A total of 8 ×
103 cells were seeded onto coverslips and subjected to
various treatments. After 48 h, the cells were washed three times
with PBS and incubated with 5 μM Fura 2-AM
or Fura-4 AM for 30 min. The cells were then washed three times with
an extracellular solution without Ca2+. Images were acquired
using a cell imaging microplate detection system with alternating
excitations at 340 and 380 nm and emission at 510 nm every 60 s. Data
are presented as ΔF340/F380.

## [Ca2+]mito Measurement
Mitochondrial
Ca2+ uptake was measured using Rhod-2 AM, a fluorescent
Ca2+ indicator (Abcam, #ab142780-1 mg). A total of 8 ×
104 cells were seeded onto the coverslips and subjected
to different treatments. For loading with Rhod-2 AM, the cells were
incubated with 5 μM Rhod-2 AM for 30 min. The
cells were then washed three times with PBS. Images were acquired
using an orthotopic fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan)
with a 40× objective. The agonists (histamine or ATP) were applied,
and the cell imaging microplate detection system was continuously
monitored. Data for Rhod-2 AM are presented as RX/R0, where R is
the emission at 581 nm induced by excitation at 552 nm, captured at
60 s intervals, and R0 is the mean value
of R during the pretreatment period in each experiment.

## Double Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay
Luciferase plasmids
(pmirGLO; GenePharma Inc.) encoding the mutant or wild-type 5′-promoter
region of TRPC6 were treated with the transcription factor vector
pcDNA3.1 or pcDNA3.1-SP2. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine
3000 (Invitrogen). A Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, China)
was used to determine the luciferase activity after 48 h, according
to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
ChIP analysis
was performed using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic
Beads) from Cell Signaling Technology. Chromatin was immunoprecipitated
using normal rabbit IgG (IgG; negative control; no. 2729), anti-SP2
(Abcam, #ab229468), and antihistone H3 (positive control; no. 4620)
antibodies. Two percent of the supernatant fraction from the chromatin
not treated with the primary antibody was saved as the “input
sample”. Real-time RT-PCR was performed using a Maxima SYBR
Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) with specifically designed primers
to amplify the promoter regions of the selected genes. The primers
used were as follows: TRPC6 promoter-1: sense 5′-GGGAAGAAGGCGGGAGGCGA-3′,
antisense 5′-AGGCAGAGGGCACAGGCGGA-3′; TRPC6 promoter-2: sense 5′-GCAGGGTTTAGAGGTGAGAG-3′,
antisense 5′-GCAAAAGTATGGGACAGGAG-3′; TRPC6 promoter-3: sense 5′-GACTCCCAGCCATCTCCCTT-3′,
antisense 5′-TAGGAGGCGCTCAGTTGTGA-3. The
primers were purchased from Sangon Biotech.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
Each group of cells
was treated with a cell lysis buffer (#P0013; Beyotime), and the lysate
was collected after centrifugation. The lysate was shaken to form
a flocculent precipitate, and the supernatant from each group was
divided into an input group, an IgG group (negative control), and
an antibody group (USP9/Parkin). The IgG and antibody groups were
mixed with corresponding antibodies and incubated overnight with rotation
at 4 °C. Protein G Magnetic Beads (no. 9006; Cell Signaling Technology)
were subsequently added and washed after incubation. Proteins were
eluted from the magnetic beads for further analysis.

## Lentivirus Infection
P2 generation hucMSCs (8.0 ×
104 cells) were plated in 6-well plates. After cell adhesion,
the lentiviral vector shUSP was diluted and mixed with serum-free
α-MEM (MOI = 10) in the 6-well plates. GFP fluorescence was
observed at 24 and 48 h. Puromycin was diluted with complete medium
to 2 μg/mL for screening GFP-positive cells. The screening cycle
lasted 3–5 days, and subsequently, fluorescence was observed.
Screening was stopped when the positivity rate exceeded 90%. The sequence
of shUSP9 was 5′-CGATTCTTCAAAGCTGTGAAT-3′,
and shGFP: JV032 h U6-C8h-gc GFP-IRES-puromycin (Justscience, China)
was used.

## Adeno-Associated Virus 9 (AAV9) Infection
Male SD rats
(weighing approximately 180 g) were purchased from the Laboratory
Animal Center of Jiangsu University (Zhenjiang, China) and randomly
divided into two groups: shNC and shTRPC6 (n = 5).
All animal experiments complied with the standard guidelines for the
care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Institutional
Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu University. The rats were
anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10% chloral hydrate solution
(250 μL/100 g), and 50 μL of virus was injected into the dermis of the back skin of each
rat. The rats were fed for 4 weeks to maximize the efficiency of viral
interference. After 4 weeks, the rats were sacrificed and the back
skin was collected for determining the efficiency and for histopathological
analysis. The sequence of Si-rat-243 was GACCAGCAUACAUGUUUAAtt.
The titers of the target and control virus were 1.0 × 1012 vg/mL, and both were labeled with EGFP (HANBIO, China).

## Statistical Analysis
For each type of experiment, the
data were accumulated from at least three measurements and analyzed
using GraphPad Prism software (version 7.0/8.0). Results were expressed
as the mean ± SEM, where appropriate. t tests
were used to compare the means between two different groups, while
ANOVA was used for statistical comparison of the differences between
three or more groups. P < 0.05 was considered
significant. In the graphs, NS is not significant; *, **, and ***
denote statistically significant differences with P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P <
0.001, respectively.
