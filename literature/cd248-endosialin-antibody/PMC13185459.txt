## ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is characterized by synovitis and joint destruction, with macrophages playing a crucial role in pathogenesis. Macroautophagy/autophagy is essential for cellular homeostasis and has been implicated in RA, but its role in macrophage polarization remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the expression of autophagy-related molecules and macrophage phenotypes in RA synovium. Synovial tissues from patients with RA were analyzed and compared with samples of osteoarthritis (OA) and less-inflammatory synovium (LIS) obtained at immediate surgery for hip fracture. Synovitis severity was histologically assessed, and cellular ultrastructure was examined via electron microscopy. Immunohistochemistry, western blot, and flow cytometry were used to analyze autophagy-related molecules and macrophage phenotypes. RA synovium exhibited significant inflammation, with macrophage-like synovial cells and intimal macrophages showing abundant autophagy-related structures in comparison to those of OA and LIS. Autophagy markers, BECN1 (beclin 1), WIPI2, ATG5, ATG16L1, LC3B, ATG3, SQSTM1, and LAMP1, were highly expressed in CD68+ macrophages but less in CD248/TEM-1+ fibroblasts. Western blot confirmed higher levels of autophagy-related proteins in RA synovial tissue compared with OA and LIS. Macrophage polarization analysis identified M1-like (NOS2+/iNOS+, CD86+), M2-like (CD163+, MRC1/CD206+, MERTK+), and M1/M2-like (CD86+ MRC1+ NOS2+ CD163+) populations. M1/M2-like macrophages showed the highest autophagy-related molecule expression. Autophagy is strongly associated with macrophage polarization in RA synovium. M1/M2-like macrophages, highly enriched in autophagy markers, may play an anti-inflammatory role in modulating inflammation and tissue repair. These findings suggest a potential autophagy-mediated regulatory mechanism in RA macrophage function.

## Introduction
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), which affects approximately 1% of the population, is a chronic inflammatory systemic disease primarily characterized by synovitis and joint destruction [ , ]. An autoimmune response is observed in rheumatoid synovium, with persistent inflammation occurring mainly in the joints, due to a network of various inflammatory cells, cytokines, and chemokines. In the cellular responses, a diverse array of cells is observed, including increased synovial cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, T cells, B cells, neutrophils, osteoclasts, lymphocytes, mast cells, and vascular endothelial cells [ ]. It is well established that macrophage-like synovial cells and intimal macrophages are deeply involved in the pathogenesis of RA synovitis [ ].
Autophagy is one of the mechanisms for degrading intracellular proteins and other intracellular molecules, preventing the accumulation of abnormal proteins, recycling proteins during nutrient deprivation or excessive protein synthesis, and regenerating cellular organelles. It plays an important role to maintain cellular function, suppressing cell death, and allowing cell survival [ ]. Additionally, autophagy is involved in the elimination of pathogenic microorganisms and contributes to the maintenance of cell structure and function. The autophagy system is crucial for maintaining homeostasis and is associated with various pathological conditions, including neurological disorders [ , ], cancer [ , ], cardiovascular diseases [ , ], and liver diseases [ ]. This ubiquitous process and its functions have recently become the focus of intensive research [ ]. The autophagy process is intricate, involving the expansion and membrane fusion of the phagophore (PG), leading to the formation of autophagosomes [ ]. Currently, there is an increasing focus on macrophage autophagy and tissue fibrosis in relation to macrophage polarization [ ].
Since the early 1990s, macrophages have been classified into two subsets: classically activated macrophages (M1) and alternatively activated macrophages (M2) based on in-vitro analysis [ ]. Furthermore, these subtypes can acquire diverse phenotypes in response to various stimuli [ ]. Recent studies on macrophage diversity have advanced the concept of a spectrum of different phenotypes; it has become increasingly common to describe inflammatory macrophages as M1-like, and anti-inflammatory and/or tissue-repairing macrophages as M2-like [ , ]. In general, although clear infectious pathogens are not observed in the RA synovium, some form of autoimmune response is triggered along with inflammatory proliferation of the synovium. Dendritic cells in the synovial stroma present antigens to helper T cells and naïve T cells, leading to the activation of various cells, including macrophages [ , ]. M1-like macrophages are known to recognize and destroy pathogens and respond to local tissue trauma. In RA synovitis, M1-like macrophages are characterized by their inflammatory properties; they secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF/TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor), IL1 (interleukin 1), and IL6, contributing to synovial inflammation and joint destruction. In contrast, M2-like macrophages exhibit anti-inflammatory properties. They promote Th2-type immune response, secrete type II anti-inflammatory cytokines including IL1R1/IL-1ra, IL4, IL10 and IL13, and produce TGFB/TGF-β (transforming growth factor), which protect tissues and promote tissue repair and regeneration [ ].
Regarding the relationship between RA synovitis and autophagy, it has been reported that induction of autophagy in fibroblasts derived from rheumatoid synovium increased cellular resistance to endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced cell death in-vitro, thus leading to speculation of synovial proliferation and prolonged inflammation due to activation of autophagy system [ ]. Additionally, a decrease in apoptosis in the synovium has been linked to increased autophagy in synovial tissues [ ], and the elevated levels of autophagy in synovial tissues correlate with disease severity [ ]. It is also known that increased autophagy in fibroblast-like synoviocytes enhanced immune response in-vitro [ ]. Furthermore, autophagy is thought to promote the proliferation of fibroblast-like synoviocytes via IL17 [ ] and to increase the concentrations of IL6, IL8, IL10, and CCL2/MCP-1 when autophagy was enhanced in neutrophils [ ]. These studies mainly focused on fibroblast-like synovial cells and neutrophils. However, specific cell types responsible for autophagy in-vivo were not emphasized and clarified yet [ ]. Thus, the detailed role of autophagy in RA synovium needs to be more clearly understood, and distribution of autophagy-positive cells in synovitis should be examined precisely. In particular, functional role of autophagy within macrophages have to be clarified.
Therefore, we evaluated the degree of rheumatoid synovitis pathologically, observed the structure of autophagy by electron microscopy, and analyzed the localization of autophagy-related molecules alongside macrophage phenotypes using immunohistochemistry. We also examined tissue-level expression of autophagy-related molecules by western blot analysis. In addition, we analyzed not only phenotype of macrophages, but also expression patterns of these molecules from isolated synovial cells by flow cytometry. A comparative analysis of synovial tissue was also conducted between RA, OA and hip fracture without arthritis. Through these investigations, we aimed to characterize autophagy-related molecules expression and macrophage phenotypes in RA synovial tissues and to gain insight into the potential involvement of macrophages and autophagy in the pathogenesis of RA synovitis.

## Results

## Evaluation of synovitis by Krenn classification
When comparing synovial tissue samples of RA (n = 19), OA (n = 21) and LIS (n = 4), a marked inflammatory response was observed in RA, with the Krenn score for RA being significantly higher at 5.8 ± 2.0 compared to OA (2.5 ± 1.1) and LIS (0.25 ± 0.4) (p < 0.01) ( ). In RA synovial tissues, the synovial lining layer was hyperplastic, consisting of at least 3 to 4 layers of synovial cells. Numerous macrophages, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, and vascular endothelial cells were observed in the synovial sublayer. Follicular structures and lymphocyte clusters were also often noted. In contrast, the synovial lining in OA tissues showed mild stratification with fewer than three layers, and the presence of macrophages and fibroblasts in the intimal sub-synovial layer was also mild. In the samples of LIS, no signs of synovitis were observed. The synovial lining layer was composed of a thin, uniform single layer of synovial cells, without hyperplasia. The sub-lining area exhibited a sparse distribution of fibroblasts and resident macrophages, with minimal infiltration of inflammatory cells even after trauma, probably due to immediate sampling.

 

## Observation of ultrastructural morphology by electron microscopy
In the synovial lining of RA tissues, proliferating synovial cells were observed, including macrophage-like synovial A cells and fibroblast-like synovial B cells. There was an infiltration of inflammatory cells predominantly composed of macrophages, as well as fibroblasts and proliferating lymphocytes in follicular structures. Numerous dense bodies were observed within synovial A cells, and some structures displayed features consistent with endoplasmic reticulum morphology, corresponding to PGs. Signs of early autophagy, such as intracisternal sequestration ( ), double-membrane vesicles consistent with autophagosome-like structures containing cytoplasmic material and organelles, as well as swollen mitochondria and autophagosome-like vesicles containing mitochondria were observed ( ). In addition, large phagocytic vacuoles were present ( ), which may represent phagocytic or autophagy-related vesicular structures but cannot be definitively distinguished by morphology alone. In synovial B cells, well-developed endoplasmic reticulum was noted, but autophagosomes, autophagic vacuoles, or membrane protrusions were rarely observed within the cells.

 In OA tissues, the synovial lining and sub-lining stroma were predominantly composed of fibroblast-like synovial B cells with well-developed rough endoplasmic reticulum and fibroblasts, with few macrophage-like synovial A cells or macrophages. In macrophages, intracellular phagocytic vacuoles and dense bodies were rare, but autophagic structures were observed. Thus, compared to RA tissues, in which macrophage-like synovial A cells were highly active with abundant autophagy-related structures, OA synovial tissue was predominantly composed of fibroblast-like synovial B cells, with fewer inflammatory cells and less frequent autophagy structures.

## Immunohistochemical analysis
Immunohistochemical analysis was performed using RA synovial tissues with Krenn score of 5.6 ± 2.2 (n = 10) and OA synovial tissues with that of 2.6 ± 1.1 (n = 10), and LIS tissues with that of 0.25 ± 0.4 (n = 4). The degree of synovitis was significantly higher in RA synovial tissues (p < 0.01). To compare the intensity of autophagy-related molecule expression in the synovial lining, the ratio of the autophagy-positive signal area to the total synovial lining area was evaluated ( ). The intensity of autophagy-related molecule expression in the synovial sub-lining was also compared using the same method. In RA, the expression of all the autophagy-related molecules, ATG3, ATG5, LC3B, WIPI2, BECN1, ATG16L1, LAMP1, and SQSTM1, examined was significantly higher in both the synovial lining and sub-lining stroma (p < 0.01) ( ) ( ).

 Although LIS tissues were included as a control, macrophages were sparse and difficult to identify immunohistochemically, making quantitative assessment of autophagy-related molecule expression infeasible in these samples. Therefore, the comparison of autophagy-related marker expression was conducted primarily between RA and OA synovial tissues.
When autophagy-related molecules were observed in double staining with anti-CD68+ synovial A cells and CD248/TEM-1+ fibroblast-like synovial B cells, autophagy-related molecules, ATG3, ATG5, LC3B, WIPI2, BECN1, ATG16L1, LAMP1, and SQSTM1, were frequently observed as positive signals (red) in CD68+ macrophages (green). On the other hand, CD248+ fibroblast-like synovial B cells (green) and stromal fibroblasts showed less double staining with autophagy-related molecules ( ).

 The immunohistochemical electron microscopy analysis observed positive reactions for the autophagy-related molecule ATG5 in macrophage-like synovial A cells and macrophages in RA tissues. Granular positive signals and positive signals on the membrane of vesicles ( ) were also observed. In OA synovial macrophage-like A cells, some granules and vesicles showed positive signals. Still, in fibroblast-like synovial B cells, positive findings were only observed in some rough endoplasmic reticulum.

 The RA synovium (n = 5, Krenn score: 6.4 ± 1.9), OA synovium (n = 6, Krenn score: 2.2 ± 1.1) and LIS (n = 4, Krenn score: 0.25 ± 0.4) (p < 0.01) were immunoassays for CD68+ macrophage-like synovial A cells and intimal macrophages. The synovial lining and sub-lining stroma of synovium were observed under a light microscope. In the quantitative analysis of immunostaining for CD68+ macrophage-like synovial A cells and macrophages, the percentage of CD68+ cells and the proportion of fluorescent area in the synovial lining of RA synovium ( ) were significantly higher compared to OA and LIS ( ). In LIS tissues, although CD68+ macrophages were detected, the overall macrophage count was low, limiting the ability to perform detailed quantitative analysis for autophagy-related markers.

 Specifically, the percentage of CD68+ cells was significantly higher in RA synovium (57% ± 19%) compared to OA synovium (27% ± 4%, p < 0.01), and LIS (13% ± 9%), as well as that of the CD68+ cell area (RA: 22% ± 6%; OA: 12% ± 3%, LIS: 2% ± 2%, p < 0.01) ( ). Similarly, in the sub-lining stroma of RA synovium, both the number and percentage in fluorescent area of CD68+ were significantly higher than in OA and LIS: 17% ± 14% (percentage of CD68+ cells: RA: 20% ± 4%; OA: 12% ± 5%, LIS: 17% ± 14%, p < 0.01; percentage of CD68+ area: RA: 8% ± 4%; OA: 3% ± 2%, LIS: 1% ± 0%, p < 0.01) ( ). Although CD68+ macrophages could be detected in LIS tissues, the limited sample size (n = 4) may reduce statistical power, and results involving LIS should be interpreted with caution.

 Double immunofluorescence staining was performed using NOS2/iNOS (inflammatory macrophage/M1-like marker) and CD163 (anti-inflammatory, tissue repairing macrophage/M2-like marker) to analyze macrophage phenotypes. In the hyperplastic synovium of RA, NOS2+ macrophages and CD163+ macrophages were observed ( ). Besides, macrophages expressing both NOS2+ and CD163+ phenotypes were also confirmed in RA synovium ( ). Inflammatory RA synovial tissue showed that CD163+ macrophages were primarily distributed in the hyperplastic synovial lining and its vicinity, while NOS2+ macrophages tended to be distributed in the deeper layers ( ). In the synovial lining and sub-lining stroma of mildly inflamed OA tissue, NOS2+ synovial A cells and macrophages were mainly observed, with scattered CD163+ macrophages ( ). In LIS, the distribution pattern of CD163+ and NOS2+ cells was generally similar to that observed in OA tissues; however, the number of positive cells was markedly lower ( ). Macrophages with both NOS2+ and CD163+ phenotypes were more prevalent in the synovial lining than in the sub-lining stroma of both RA and OA synovial tissues. Compared to the samples of OA and LIS, RA synovial tissue had a significantly higher presence of these macrophages in the synovial lining and tended to have more in the sub-lining stroma as well (p < 0.05) ( ) ( ).

 Double immunofluorescence staining with CD86 (inflammatory macrophage/M1-like marker) and MRC1/CD206 (anti-inflammatory, tissue repairing macrophage/M2-like marker) also was done to analyze macrophage phenotypes. In the hyperplastic synovium of RA, CD86+ macrophages and MRC1+ macrophages were observed, besides, macrophages expressing both CD86 and MRC1 phenotypes were also confirmed in RA synovium. Inflammatory RA synovial tissue showed that CD86+ and MRC1+ macrophages were primarily distributed in the synovial sub-lining stroma ( ). In mildly inflamed OA tissue, MRC1+ synovial macrophage-like A cells and intimal macrophages were mainly observed, with scattered CD86+ macrophages ( ), but macrophages with both CD86+ and MRC1+ phenotypes were less observed. Compared to OA, RA synovial tissue had a significantly higher presence of CD86+ MRC1+ macrophages in the sub-lining stroma as well. In LIS, both CD86+ and MRC1+cells were occasionally detected, but their numbers were even lower than those observed in OA tissues, consistent with the less-inflammatory nature and sparse immune cell infiltration of non-inflamed synovial tissue.

 As for MERTK+ (anti-inflammatory, tissue-repairing macrophage marker/M2-like) macrophages, their distribution in the RA synovium is similar to that of CD163+, they are primarily distributed in the synovial lining layer, with sparse punctate distribution in the sub-lining layer ( ). In OA synovium, they are mainly punctate-distributed in the sub-lining layer ( ). In LIS, both MERTK+ and CD163+ macrophages were sparsely distributed, with only a small number of positive cells observed.

 

## Western blot analysis
Western blot analysis was performed using representative samples from RA (n = 3), OA (n = 3), and LIS (n = 3). Krenn scores of the RA, OA and LIS were 6 ± 0.82, 2.3 ± 0.47 and 0 respectively, with significantly higher inflammation in the RA synovial tissues (p < 0.05). The expression levels of ATG16L1, BECN1 and LC3B were highest in RA, followed by OA, and lowest in LIS. Although statistical analysis revealed significant differences (p < 0.05), the small sample size limits the statistical power, and these results should be interpreted as preliminary findings that support the overall trend observed in other analyses. ## Flow cytometry of cells isolated from synovial tissue
Single cells were successfully isolated from synovial tissues of RA (n = 6, Krenn score: 6.0 ± 1.9), OA (n = 8, Krenn score: 2.4 ± 1.1) and LIS (n = 4, Krenn score: 0.25 ± 0.4, p < 0.01). The isolated cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using anti-ITGAM/CD11b (pan-macrophage marker), anti-CD86 (inflammatory macrophage marker/M1-like macrophage marker), and anti-MRC1 (anti-inflammatory, tissue repairing macrophage/M2-like marker) antibodies, in comparison with isotype controls ( ). ITGAM+ cells were categorized into M0-like macrophages (ITGAM+ CD86− MRC1−), M1-like macrophages (ITGAM+ CD86+ MRC1−), M2-like macrophages (ITGAM+ CD86− MRC1+), and double-positive M1/M2-like macrophages (ITGAM+ CD86+ MRC1+) ( ). Synovial ITGAM+ myeloid cells, representing a macrophage-enriched population, showed phenotypes that could be classified as M0-like, M1-like, M2-like, and M1/M2-like. In RA synovial tissues, the distribution percentages were M0-like: 18.2% ± 6.7%, M1-like: 16.04% ± 10.4%, M2-like: 12.15% ± 9.29%, and M1/M2-like: 53.58% ± 15.01%, while in OA synovial tissues, they were M0-like: 38.9% ± 11.22%, M1-like: 26.83% ± 12.02%, M2-like: 10.30% ± 7.04%, and M1/M2-like: 23.98% ± 13.82%. In LIS samples, they were M0-like: 23.8% ± 11.4%, M1-like: 13.7% ± 6.9%, M2-like: 33.36% ± 20.89%, and M1/M2-like: 29.03% ± 7.79%.

 The proportions of macrophage subtypes in synovial tissues were visualized using donut charts for RA (n = 6), OA (n = 8), and LIS (n = 4) groups. In RA tissues, M1/M2-like macrophages constituted the largest proportion (mean 53.6%), whereas in OA tissues, M0-like macrophages were dominant (mean 38.9%). In LIS, M2-like macrophages were more prevalent than in RA or OA ( ).

 The subtype distribution was further analyzed statistically ( ). The proportion of M0-like macrophages was significantly higher in OA than in RA (p < 0.01), while M1/M2-like macrophages were significantly more abundant in RA than in OA (p < 0.01). No significant differences were observed in the proportions of M1-like and M2-like macrophages among the three groups.
Although macrophage subtypes could be identified in less-inflammatory synovial tissues, the total macrophage count was low, limiting further autophagy-related marker expression analysis.
Furthermore, the cells from RA and OA synovium tissue were labeled with antibodies against autophagy-related molecules LAMP1, BECN1, SQSTM1, WIPI2, and LC3B, and multicolor staining was performed ( ). Focusing on the intensity of autophagy-related marker expression in the M1-like, M2-like, and M1/M2-like macrophage phenotypes, the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of each autophagy marker (LAMP1, WIPI2, BECN1, LC3B, SQSTM1) was calculated. In RA, MFI for LAMP1 was significantly higher in the M1/M2-like macrophages compared to M1-like and M2-like macrophages ( ). Similarly, in OA, MFI was higher for LAMP1, WIPI2, BECN1, and SQSTM1 in M1/M2-like macrophages than in M1-like or M2-like macrophages ( ). Overall, M1/M2-like macrophages tended to have higher MFI than M1-like or M2-like macrophages.

 Considering the differences in autophagy-related molecule expression among different macrophage phenotypes and the different proportions of macrophage phenotypes in RA and OA synovial tissues, the cumulative fluorescence intensity values of the measured autophagy markers were also analyzed across all isolated cells. In macrophages derived from RA tissues, the cumulative MFI for M1/M2-like macrophages was significantly higher than that for M1-like or M2-like macrophages. Conversely, the cumulative MFI for M2-like macrophages was significantly lower in macrophages derived from OA tissues than for M1-like or M1/M2-like macrophages ( ). Comparing RA and OA, M1-like macrophages from RA synovium had lower autophagy-related molecule expression than M1-like macrophages from OA synovium. However, M1/M2-like macrophages from RA synovium exhibited higher autophagy-related molecule expression than M1/M2-like macrophages from OA synovium. There was no significant difference in autophagy-related molecule expression between M2-like macrophages from RA and OA ( ).

 Synovial tissues from four patients with hip fractures were included as a minimal-inflammatory control group. Although ITGAM+ macrophages were detectable in LIS, their overall frequency was low. While the cell yield was sufficient for general subtype classification, the number of cells was inadequate for reliable analysis of autophagy marker expression within individual macrophage subtypes. Therefore, only basic macrophage subset data were reported for this group, and autophagy-related molecule expression analysis was limited to the RA and OA groups.

## Discussion
Previous studies revealed involvement of autophagy in reducing apoptosis in synovial cells [ ], generating citrullinated peptides in-vitro study [ ], and enhancing immune responses in fibroblast-like synoviocytes [ ]. Elevated expression of autophagy-related molecules ATG5, BECN1, and LC3, was observed in RA synovial tissue compared to OA [ ], with autophagy reported to promote fibroblast-like synoviocytes proliferation via IL17 [ ] and increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL6, IL8, and CCL2 in neutrophils [ ]. However, little research has differentiated autophagy in specific synovial cell types, such as macrophages and fibroblasts, or studied intimal macrophages and fibroblast-like synoviocytes in RA [ , ].
This study uniquely examined macrophage phenotypes and/or autophagy-related marker expression in RA and OA synovium using immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy, western blot analysis and flow cytometry. We found that autophagy-related molecules were more prominently expressed in macrophages than fibroblasts in RA synovium. While fibroblast-like synoviocytes are known to exacerbate inflammation and tissue destruction through proliferation and anti-apoptotic functions [ ], macrophages displayed polarization into distinct phenotypes with inflammatory (M1-like) and anti-inflammatory (M2-like) roles, which was supported by in-vitro studies [ ]. After polarization, macrophages are known to exhibit various characteristics [ , ]. Autophagy has been proposed in prior studies to support macrophage housekeeping functions such as apoptotic-cell clearance, and pro-inflammatory substances, while contributing to the resolution of inflammation and tissue protection [ ]. Autophagy-related molecules were enriched, but functional involvement remains unclear. In our study, comparison of macrophage-like cells and fibroblast-like cells from the perspective of autophagy showed that macrophage-like A cells and intimal macrophages expressed higher levels of autophagy-related molecules than fibroblasts, suggesting a stronger association with autophagy-related pathways in RA synovium; however, the functional involvement of autophagy cannot be determined because autophagic flux was not assessed.
Previous studies have reported on the tissue distribution of CD86+ M1-like and MRC1+ M2-like macrophages in RA synovial tissue, focusing on the expression of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), an enzyme that plays a key role in cellular protection and stress responses [ ]. However, the degree of synovitis or distribution characteristics of macrophage phenotypes, or quantitative analysis, were not examined in detail.
Our study is the first report to compare macrophage phenotypes and the expression of autophagy-related molecules in inflammatory RA synovial tissue to OA synovial tissues. Firstly, in RA synovium, MERTK+ and/or CD163+ M2-like macrophages were abundant in the hyperplastic lining, consistent with their reported anti-inflammatory and tissue-repairing roles. In contrast, CD86+ and NOS2+ M1-like macrophages localized in the deeper sub-lining layer, signifying active inflammation. In OA synovium, M2-like macrophages were sparse, and NOS2+ macrophages were more prevalent, particularly in the mildly inflamed lining. The spatial proximity of these macrophage phenotypes in RA suggests attempted resolution and tissue repair, even in the areas of active inflammation. The anti-inflammatory roles of M2-like cells, particularly MERTK+ and CD163+ macrophages, include promoting efferocytosis, preventing secondary necrosis, and secreting growth factors to support synovial tissue regeneration [ , ]. These roles align with the preventative local host response against elevated inflammatory mediators (TNF, IL1B, IL6, IL17, and CSF2/GM-CSF) contained in RA synovial fluid [ ], which likely influence the polarization and function of macrophage phenotypes. The spatial distribution of these cells, particularly their proximity to inflammatory and tissue-repair zones, underscores their complex roles in the dynamic inflammatory environment of RA.
Furthermore, we also identified the presence of macrophages expressing both M1-like and M2-like phenotypes in both RA and OA synovium by immunostaining. These characteristic M1/M2-like double-positive macrophages were more prevalent in the synovial lining compared to the sub-lining stroma and, together with the MERTK+, and CD163+ M2-like macrophages in the outermost layer, also seems to support an anti-inflammatory, tissue-protective and reparative role against continued chronic inflammation, especially in RA synovium. The identification of M1/M2-like macrophages (CD86+ MRC1+) using flow cytometry, was more prevalent in RA than OA synovium. The presence of M1/M2-like macrophages suggests a spectrum of macrophage phenotypes, challenging the traditional binary classification of M1 and M2 [ , , ]. Notably, in LIS tissues, M2-like macrophages were relatively abundant, while the proportion of M1/M2-like macrophages was intermediate between RA and OA. These findings support the concept that M2-like macrophages may represent the steady-state or tissue-repairing phenotype in less-inflammatory conditions. The dominance of M0-like macrophages in OA may reflect a relatively quiescent macrophage population, while the increased M1/M2-like macrophages in RA indicate a dynamic polarization process in response to persistent inflammation. Meanwhile, higher expression of autophagy-related markers in M1/M2-like macrophages compared to M1- or M2-like macrophages was confirmed. This hybrid phenotype showed the highest expression of autophagy-related molecules among macrophage subsets, suggesting a potential association with both inflammatory and tissue-repair – related processes. This association raises the possibility that autophagy may be linked to macrophage functional states in RA, but causality cannot be inferred without flux-based or perturbation experiments. The reduced proportion of M0-like macrophages and increased M1/M2-like macrophages in RA further highlight the significance of this hybrid phenotype in chronic inflammation and tissue repair. Additionally, recent reports on the anti-inflammatory, tissue repair and regeneration roles of tissue-resident macrophages have garnered attention [ , , ]. Therefore, further examination of similarities and differences between the macrophage phenotypes, including tissue-resident macrophages, is also necessary in future [ ].
This study is the first to compare expression of autophagy-related molecule and macrophage phenotypes in inflammatory synovium of RA and OA, and LIS. In RA, persistent inflammation was associated with increased expression of autophagy-related molecules in macrophages, particularly in those with M1/M2-like phenotypes. These findings raise the possibility that autophagy-related pathways could be explored as therapeutic targets to modulate macrophage function, re-polarizing inflammatory macrophages into anti-inflammatory states to restore tissue homeostasis suggests their critical role in balancing inflammatory and anti-inflammatory processes, also provide insights into the interplay between macrophages and fibroblast-like synoviocytes in RA, with macrophages emerging as major cell types associated with autophagy-related pathways in synovial inflammation.
Future studies should incorporate autophagy flux – based assays, lysosomal inhibition, or genetic perturbation of autophagy-related genes to determine whether autophagy directly influences macrophage polarization and functional states. Further work is also needed to clarify how autophagy-related pathways vary across diverse macrophage phenotypes, delineate the molecular mechanisms linking autophagy to macrophage functional transitions, and examine the cross-talk between macrophages and fibroblast-like synoviocytes in RA. Additionally, comparing tissue-resident macrophages with the phenotypes identified in this study could provide deeper insights into their potential roles in synovial inflammation and tissue maintenance. By understanding these mechanisms, new therapeutic approaches targeting autophagy and macrophage polarization could be developed to treat RA more effectively. The plasticity of macrophage phenotypes suggests that future therapeutic strategies might aim to modulate these states; however, such approaches require extensive mechanistic validation.
This study has several methodological limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the ultrastructural observations obtained by transmission electron microscopy are inherently static. Morphology alone cannot reliably distinguish canonical autophagosomes from autolysosomes/AL or LC3-associated phagocytosis (LAP)-related vesicles; therefore, the EM findings should be interpreted as qualitative supportive evidence rather than definitive identification of specific autophagic structures. In addition, the immuno-EM analyses were descriptive and lacked blinded morphometric quantification, preventing precise assessment of autophagy-related vesicle abundance. Future studies should incorporate rigorous blinded morphometric analyses, such as stereological quantification of vesicle profiles per cell area, to validate these qualitative observations.
Second, while LIS tissue was included as a less-inflammation reference, it does not represent true normal synovium because of differences in anatomical site, patient age, and the acute trauma context in which these tissues were obtained. The markedly reduced macrophage presence limited further flow cytometric phenotyping and autophagy profiling. This limitation is consistent with previous reports [ , ] showing sparse immune cell infiltration in non-inflamed synovial tissue. Nonetheless, inclusion of histologically LIS and the baseline macrophage levels still strengthened the interpretability of RA versus OA findings, suggesting different natures of each synovitis. Future studies using tissue-resident macrophage enrichment or alternative healthy tissue sources may help further delineate basal autophagy levels in non-inflamed human synovium.
A third limitation concerns the definition of macrophage subsets in our flow cytometric analysis. Although ITGAM+ is widely used to identify synovial myeloid cells, ITGAM+ populations remain heterogeneous and include macrophages along with monocytes, dendritic cells, and occasional neutrophils. Previous studies using well-accepted macrophage markers (e.g., FCGR1A/CD64, MERTK, CD163) suggest that a substantial proportion of synovial ITGAM+ myeloid cells correspond to tissue macrophages, supporting the interpretation of ITGAM+ cells as a macrophage-enriched – but not macrophage-exclusive – compartment [ , ]. Because only limited viable cells could be recovered from fibrotic synovial tissue, we used a simplified panel (ITGAM, CD86, MRC1) commonly applied in prior RA studies. However, the absence of additional lineage markers (PTPRC/CD45, FCGR1A, CD64, MERTK) and viability dyes prevents definitive exclusion of non-macrophage subsets. Accordingly, the phenotypes identified here should be interpreted as relative differences within an ITGAM+ macrophage-enriched myeloid compartment rather than strictly purified macrophage subtypes.
Finally, RA synovium contains a markedly higher density of macrophages and a thicker lining layer than OA or tissues, thus differences in autophagy-positive area may partly reflect variation in cell populations rather than true per-cell upregulation. Although flow cytometric measurements helped address this limitation, they likewise relied on static expression of autophagy-associated markers and did not measure autophagic flux.
In conclusion, this study revealed macrophages, particularly unique M1/M2-like phenotype with high autophagy activity, are central to the inflammatory initially, but later toward anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair processes as a result of local host response in RA synovium, probably along with phenotype change. These findings highlight a strong association between macrophage phenotypes and autophagy-related marker expression in RA. Autophagy-related pathways may represent potential therapeutic targets for modulating macrophage states in RA [ , , ].

## Materials and methods

## Patients and sample preparation
In this study, synovial tissues were obtained from 19 patients with RA who underwent surgery for joint disorders of the limbs. The cohort consisted of 17 females and 2 males, with a mean age of 63 years (ranging from 29 to 78 years) at the time of surgery and an average disease duration of 10 years (ranging from 2 to 20 years). The mean C-reactive protein (CRP) level at the time of surgery was 9.2 mg/L (ranging from 1 to 48 mg/L). The mean disease activity score (DAS)-28 with CRP, 4 variables, was 3.55 (ranging from 2.2 to 7.38), and the clinical disease activity index (CDAI) was 15.6 (ranging from 3 to 30), both of which indicate disease severity respectively, reflecting moderate disease activity on average. All patients with RA met the criteria of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) from 1987 [ ] or the 2010 ACR/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) classification criteria [ ] for RA. The type of drugs used by the patients and surgical procedures are listed in . Synovial tissues were collected from 21 patients with OA and 4 elderly patients undergoing surgery for hip fracture, in order to compare pathological conditions. The OA cohort included 21 females, with a mean age of 68 (ranging from 53 to 86), the hip fracture cohort included 4 females, with a mean age of 93 (ranging from 91 to 95). For the hip fracture group, synovial tissues were obtained intraoperatively, which surgeries were immediately performed after the fracture. These patients had no known history of inflammatory or degenerative joint diseases, and the synovial tissues were also histologically confirmed without arthritis due to immediate sampling after trauma, and served as less-inflammatory synovium (LIS) as control.
 

## Evaluation of synovitis by Krenn classification
Synovial tissues were fixed in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (4-μm), deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin. The degree of synovitis was evaluated using the Krenn classification (0–9 points) [ ] with an optical microscope (DMD108; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) based on three criteria: (1) synovial lining layers (0–3 points), (2) sublayer cellular density (0–3 points), and (3) inflammatory cell infiltration (0–3 points). Scores were categorized as no synovitis (0–1), low-grade (2–4), and high-grade synovitis (5–9).

## Observation of ultrastructural morphology by electron microscopy
The collected tissues were finely cut into 2 mm ×2 mm pieces, fixed in 2.5% buffered glutaraldehyde, and post-fixed in a 1% osmium tetroxide solution. After washing with phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4), the tissues were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 95, 99.5, and 100%). The tissues were then embedded in Epon 812 (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 14,120), sectioned into ultrathin sections, stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate, and observed using a transmission electron microscope [ ].

## Immunohistochemical analysis
Paraffin-embedded sections (4-μm) from the samples of RA, OA, and LIS were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at 121°C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 30 min, followed by a 60-minute treatment with SuperBlock™ T20 Blocking Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K., 37,516) to prevent nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies against autophagy-related molecules were mixed and reacted with an anti-CD68 macrophage antibody (Abcam plc, Ab213363) [ ] and an anti-CD248/TEM1 fibroblast antibody (Abcam, Ab204914) [ ] to confirm the cellular localization of autophagy-related molecules. The sections were subsequently treated with secondary fluorescent antibodies (dilution 1:250; Life Technologies, A11003, A11008) and then with Vector TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching kit with DAPI (VEC, SP-8500–15) to stain the cell nuclei while removing nonspecific staining. Finally, the sections were observed using an immunofluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan).
Immunostaining was performed to evaluate synovial macrophages using the following antibodies: anti-CD68 (pan-macrophage; Dako Denmark A/S, Agilent Technologies, Denmark; 20,066,303; Abcam, Ab213363), anti-MERTK (anti-inflammatory macrophage/M2-like marker; Abcam, Ab52968), anti-NOS2/iNOS (M1-like marker; NOVUS, NBP2-22119SS) with anti-CD163 (M2-like marker; Abcam, Ab74604), and anti-CD86 (M1 marker; Abcam, Ab220188) with anti-MRC1/CD206 (M2-like marker; Abcam, Ab64693). Details of primary antibodies are listed in .
 Three random fields of view of synovial tissues were imaged using a BZ-X 710 digital microscope (KEYENCE). The synovium was divided into the lining and sublayer regions, and the percentage area of CD68+ and NOS2 and CD163 double-positive signals was quantified using KEYENCE image processing software [ ]. Immunohistochemically reactive area of fluorescent positive cells was analyzed with BZ H3C Hybrid Cell Count Software (KEYENCE).
All images were acquired using identical exposure settings, gain, and background subtraction parameters. Positive areas were quantified using unified threshold values across all samples, and measurements were normalized to total tissue area to minimize bias introduced by differences in synovial lining thickness among RA, OA, and LIS specimens.
Additionally, sections immunostained for ATG5 were post-fixed in 2% buffered glutaraldehyde after washing, dried, and sequentially dehydrated with ethanol (the method is described in section 4). These specimens were then embedded in Epon 812, ultrathin-sectioned, and stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate, followed by observation by a transmission electron microscope [ ].

## Western blot analysis
Synovial tissue samples of RA, OA and LIS were embedded in OCT compound and stored at −80°C. Thirty-micrometer sections were prepared, sonicated in sterile ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm, Smart2Pure 3 UV/UF system; Thermo Scientific, 50,129,688), and centrifuged at 250 g for 5 min at 4°C to remove water and OCT compound (Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound; Sakura Finetek USA, Inc., 4583). The residue was dissolved in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., 89,900) on ice for 30 min, then centrifuged again to remove tissue debris. The supernatant, adjusted to a protein concentration of 1 μg/μl, was boiled in SDS sample buffer with 2-mercaptoethanol for 3 min and used for western blot analysis. Proteins were separated on a 5–20% polyacrylamide gel, transferred to a PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore Ltd., IPVH00010), blocked with 4% skim milk in PBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10,010–023) containing 0.05% Tween-20, prepared using PBS-Tween tablets (Medicago AB, 09–9410-100), and incubated overnight with primary antibodies. After washing, the membrane was treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Dako, P0260) for 60 min, and the signal was visualized with ImmunoStar LD Chemiluminescence Reagent (WAKO Ltd., 290–69904). Images were captured using an Ez-Capture II camera (ATTO, Japan), and densitometric analysis was performed [ ]. Antibodies are detailed in .

## Flow cytometry of cells isolated from synovial tissues
Synovial tissue samples of RA (n = 6), OA (n = 8), and LIS (n = 4) were cut into 0.5 mm pieces, treated with dissolution buffer (Multi Tissue Dissociation Kit 1; Miltenyi Biotec GmbH, 130–110-201) at 37°C for 60 min, and filtered through a 100-μm strainer (Corning Inc., 431,752) to collect cell suspensions. Cells were counted with trypan blue, washed with Cell Staining Buffer (Biolegend, 420,201), and treated with an Fc blocker (Biolegend, 422,301). For macrophage subtype analysis, a simplified gating strategy focusing on ITGAM+/CD11b+ myeloid cells was adopted, consistent with previous studies of synovial macrophage – enriched populations [ , , ]. Cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies: anti-ITGAM/CD11b (pan-macrophage marker, PE; Biolegend, 393,111), anti-CD86 (M1-like marker, APC; Biolegend, 374,207), and anti-MRC1 (M2-like marker, Alexa Fluor 488; Biolegend, 321,113). Within the ITGAM+ gate, phenotypic subtypes were defined as M0-like (ITGAM+ CD86− MRC1−), M1-like (ITGAM+ CD86+ MRC1−), and M2-like (ITGAM+ CD86− MRC1+) and CD86+ MRC1+ double-positive cells were analyzed as macrophage-enriched hybrid phenotypes. These subsets are interpreted as relative phenotypic distributions within the ITGAM+ synovial myeloid compartment rather than strictly purified macrophage populations. After antibody labeling, cells were permeabilized and incubated with a biotin-conjugated autophagy antibody followed by Streptavidin-Pacific Blue (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc., S11222).
Flow cytometry was performed using a BD FACSMelody™ cell sorter, analyzing 5 × 104 cells per sample. Dead cells and debris were excluded using forward and side scatter (FSC/SSC) gating before analysis, which is a widely used approach for synovial tissue samples. Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was calculated and processed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, OR, USA). MFIs were normalized by subtracting isotype-control background signals for each marker (ΔMFI). To reduce batch-to-batch variability, all samples were processed using the same antibody lots and acquired on the same cytometer with identical voltage settings. Antibodies are detailed in .

## Human subjects

Synovial tissues were obtained from patients with rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis, as well as from elderly patients undergoing surgery for hip fracture, which were included as less-inflammatory synovium (LIS). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, or waived with an opt-out approach in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

## Statistical analysis
The obtained data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons among three tissues were conducted using Prism software (GraphPad Prism, version 9, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), employing a Normality test and One-way ANOVA test, followed by post-hoc analysis using either a non-paired t-test or paired t-test.
