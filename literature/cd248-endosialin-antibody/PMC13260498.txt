Prognostically, patients with local lesions have a 5-year survival rate of up to 76%, but patients with metastases have a 5-year survival rate of only 24% (Kansara et al. , Miranda-Carboni et al., ). The comprehensive treatment plan of chemotherapy and surgery is currently mostly used in clinical practice; yet, patient survival rates have not improved considerably in recent decades, suggesting that the current treatment strategies still have serious flaws. Furthermore, osteosarcoma has a high degree of heterogeneity, and no distinct driver mutations have been identified inside the tumor or across instances (Corre et al. ). Finding new biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity is therefore crucial in this clinical setting for the early detection and accurate treatment of osteosarcoma.
Emerging evidence has established the gut microbiota as a critical modulator of cancer development and progression (Long et al. ). Pathogenic microbes directly shape the tumor microenvironment: Fusobacterium nucleatum promotes immunosuppression by recruiting M2 macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells while blocking T cell and NK cell activity (Kostic et al. ). Helicobacter pylori-induced chronic inflammation drives gastric carcinogenesis through accumulated genetic alterations in gastric epithelial cells (Marshall et al., ). The gut microbiome also impacts therapeutic efficacy and toxicity. Commensal bacteria like Bifidobacterium enhance responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors by modulating dendritic cell activity, while microbial β-glucuronidase reactivates irinotecan metabolites, causing severe diarrhea (Sivan et al. ; Bhatt et al. ). Dietary fiber and natural compounds can be converted by gut bacteria into bioactive metabolites with cancer-preventive properties (Hanus et al. ). Probiotics (Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium bifidum) reduce tumor growth and suppress potentially carcinogenic microorganisms (Carroll et al. ). Natural substances such as curcumin, genistein, and apigenin exert chemopreventive effects partly through microbiota modulation (Ohkawara et al. ). These discoveries collectively highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the “microbiota-host” axis in cancer prevention, treatment, and toxicity management.
Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships between exposures and disease outcomes (Ding et al. ). When integrated with transcriptomic data, MR has successfully identified therapeutic targets such as ARL4C and GAPDH in osteoarthritis, and TGS1 as a susceptibility gene in multiple cancers (Cheng et al. ; Qiu et al. ). However, no study has systematically investigated the causal relationship between gut microbiota, microbial metabolites, and osteosarcoma using MR approaches, warranting further investigation.
Therefore, this study adopts a microbiota-centered strategy to generate a working hypothesis regarding the potential role of gut microbiota and their metabolites in osteosarcoma. By integrating Mendelian randomization, transcriptomics, 16S rRNA sequencing, and functional validation, we aim to identify microbiota-related candidate genes and metabolic pathways that may contribute to osteosarcoma pathogenesis. This integrated approach may serve as a hypothesis-generating resource for exploring microbiota-based strategies and offer new perspectives on the complex interplay between intestinal microbiota and osteosarcoma.

## Materials and methods

## Data collection and preprocessing
The GEO database’s osteosarcoma-related dataset GSE99671, which contains 18 samples of normal tissue and 18 samples of osteosarcoma tissue, was used in this investigation. To find genes that are differently expressed between groups, use the limma software program to perform variance analysis with | log2FC |> 1 and p value < 0.05 as the standard. Based on these results, we focused on cis-eQTLs in conjunction with the 4463 drug-targeting genes identified by Chris Finan et al. The FinnGen database (code: C3_OSTEOSARCOMA_EXALLC) provided the findings of osteosarcoma-related genome-wide association studies (GWAS).

## Mendelian randomization
To assess potential causal associations between druggable target genes and osteosarcoma, we performed two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis. SNPs significantly associated with target gene expression were extracted as instrumental variables from eQTLGen and GTEx v8 databases, with selection criteria: P < 5 × 10−8, linkage disequilibrium r2 < 0.001, and F-statistic > 10. Inverse variance weighting was used as the primary analysis, supplemented by MR Egger regression and weighted median methods. Sensitivity analyses included MR Egger intercept test for horizontal pleiotropy, Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity, and leave-one-out analysis. All analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package in R, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Enrichment functional analysis of drug genes
We conducted bidirectional screening based on the consistency of gene expression direction and effect estimates (OR) to find important genes in osteosarcoma that are consistent with Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis results. Genes with upregulated expression and OR > 1 were identified as potential risk genes, while genes with downregulated expression and OR < 1 were identified as potential protective genes. We next used the “clusterProfiler” software to perform gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed genes in order to further investigate the biological roles of candidate genes in osteosarcoma. The P value and Q-value < 0.05 after correction were used as significance thresholds to methodically identify the major pathways and possible molecular mechanisms potentially associated with the development and incidence of osteosarcoma. All enrichment results were corrected by multiple testing.

## Analysis of gut microbiota target genes metabolites
The gutMGene database is a literature-curated resource that compiles experimentally validated or literature-supported associations between gut microbiota and host target genes. We utilize the gutMGene database (http://bio-computing.hrbmu.edu.cn/gutmgene) to obtain association information between gut microbiota and target genes. It is important to note that the associations retrieved from gutMGene are derived from previously published literature, rather than from datasets generated in this study or from computational/statistical integrations performed herein. This database integrates 298 experimentally validated or literature supported associations between 126 human target genes and 83 gut microbiota. We used the osteosarcoma related genes obtained through transcriptome analysis and Mendelian randomization screening in this study as query items to search for associated gut microbiota and metabolite information in the database. By performing intersection analysis between target genes and gut microbiota related target genes included in the database, candidate core genes are obtained, and the gut microbiota species associated with these genes are further traced. Based on the above analysis, we constructed a ternary association network containing microbiota, metabolites, and target genes, providing a hypothesis-generating resource for exploring the potential metabolic pathways that may be involved in the relationship between gut microbiota and osteosarcoma.

## 16S rRNA gene sequencing data
We retrieved the osteosarcoma gut microbiota dataset PRJNA1184406 from the SRA database and performed rigorous bioinformatics analysis to confirm the overall alterations of microorganisms in osteosarcoma. First, we created high-quality sequence labels by concatenating and quality-checking the original double-ended sequencing sequence using FLASH software (version 1.2.11). The amplified subsequence variants were then grouped into operational classification units at a 97% similarity level after the sequence was deduplicated and filtered for chimerism using the UPARSE procedure (version 11). To taxonomically annotate OTU representative sequences from phylum to genus using the SILVA 138 database, we used RDP Classifier (version 2.6). The Shannon index was computed using the flattened OTU abundance table to assess alpha diversity, and statistical analysis and visualization were finished in the R environment.

## Molecular docking and molecular dynamics
The PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) provides the two-dimensional and three-dimensional structural data of metabolites.The RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) is the source of the crystal structure of the key target protein JUP. We performed molecular docking analysis using the CB-Dock2 online platform to predict the possible binding interaction between candidate metabolites and JUP. The results were visualized using PyMOL software to methodically examine the binding conformation and intermolecular interaction patterns between receptors and ligands. Glycerol was chosen as the negative control in this investigation in order to assess the binding specificity. This molecule is a common polyol that, in theory, has little binding capacity and no known specific interaction with JUP. The AutoDock Vina platform was used to repeatedly validate all docking experiments in order to further guarantee the accuracy of the results.
We performed molecular dynamics simulations on complex systems with stable binding configurations based on the outcomes of molecular docking screening in order to thoroughly examine their dynamic binding properties. The GROMACS 2022.4 program was used for all simulations. The CGenFF tool was used to create ligand parameters, and the CHARMM36 force field was used to describe protein–ligand interactions. Ions at a physiological concentration (0.15 M NaCl) were added after the system was dissolved in the TIP3P water model. Each system was officially simulated for 100 ns (NPT ensemble, 310 K, 1 atm) with a time step of 2 fs following energy minimization and stepwise equilibrium. By computing the MM/PBSA binding free energy of the trajectory and examining the contribution of important residues, the binding stability and interaction mode of the complex under physiological settings were methodically assessed.

## Correlation analysis between target and immune infiltration
The CIBERSORT algorithm, which is based on support vector regression (SVR), was used to systematically assess the relative infiltration degree of different immune cell subgroups, including B cells, T cells, NK cells, myeloid cells, and granulocytes, and to quantitatively analyze the immune cell composition in osteosarcoma samples. We used correlation analysis to further investigate the statistical relationship between the percentage of particular immune cell infiltration and the expression levels of important target genes.

## Cells and cell culture
Human osteosarcoma cell lines SaOS-2 and 143B were purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM medium (containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin streptomycin) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum was anaerobically cultured at 37 °C for 24 h in MRS broth medium. The bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in antibiotic free medium. Osteosarcoma cells were co cultured with L. plantarum at a ratio of 100:1 infection multiplicity (MOI) for 24 h. DPV was purchased from Shanghai, China and dissolved in DMSO to prepare a storage solution. Before use, it was diluted with culture medium to the working concentration (50 μM). The final concentration of DMSO during cell processing is ≤ 0.1%, and an equal amount of DMSO is added to the control group.

## Cell transfection
ShRNA targeting JUP and negative control shNC were cloned into pLKO.1-puro vector. Co-transfect the recombinant plasmid with packaging plasmids (psPAX2, pMD2.G) into HEK293T cell packaging lentivirus. Collect virus supernatant and infect SaOS-2 and 143B cells (containing 8 μg/mL of polyamine). After 24 h of infection, stable transducing cells were screened using 2 μg/mL puromycin.

## RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was isolated from cells using the TRIzol method, and after being digested with DNase I to remove genomic DNA contamination, the first strand cDNA was produced using a reverse transcription kit. Using cDNA as a template, specific primers for the target and reference genes are employed. The forward primer for JUP is ATACTCAGGTGCGGGCTATC, while the reverse primer is ACTCCAGATACCTTGGCTG. The forward primer for GAPDH is TGACTTCAACAGCGACACCCA, while the reverse primer is CACCCTGCTGTAGCCAA. Real-time quantitative PCR amplification was carried out in a pre-mixed solution containing SYBR Green fluorescent dye. The 2−ΔCt technique was used to calculate the relative expression levels of the target gene compared to the reference gene and control groups. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure data reliability.

## Cell-counting-kit 8 (CCK-8) assay
In a 96-well plate, inoculate SaOS-2 and 143B cells at a density of 2 × 103 per well. After treatment, incubate with CCK-8 reagent for 1–4 h. Dehydrogenases in living cells convert the reagent to orange formamide products, which are then detected at 450 nm with an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader. The absorbance value is proportional to the number of living cells when calculating cell viability or inhibition rate.

## Western blot validation of knockdown efficiency
Collect three groups of cells (NC-Ctrl, NC-Lacto, and NC-DPV), extract total protein from RIPA lysate, and perform protein quantification using BCA method. After SDS-PAGE separation, membrane transfer, and blocking, equal amounts of protein were incubated overnight at 4 °C with JUP primary antibody (Abcam, 1:1000) and β—actin primary antibody (Proteintech, 1:5000). HRP labeled secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and protein bands were detected by ECL chemiluminescence method. Repeat the experiment three times.

## Colony formation assay
Using a 2 × 3 factorial design, six groups of cells (NC-Ctrl, NC-Lacto, NC-DPV, shJUP-Ctrl, shJUP-Lacto, shJUP-DPV) were seeded at a density of 500 cells/well into a 6-well plate, with 2 mL of complete culture medium added to each well and gently shaken. Cultivate continuously in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for 10–14 days, changing the culture medium every 3 days. Terminate cultivation when visible clone clusters appear. After washing with PBS, fix with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stain with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Wash away the dye with running water and dry at room temperature. Count the number of clones containing more than 50 cells under a microscope and calculate the clone formation rate (clones/seeded cells × 100%). The experiment was independently repeated three times.

## Transwell invasion assay
Cell migration detection was performed using an uncoated upper chamber (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA), while Transwell membranes were pre coated with 55 µl Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA). After culturing for 4–24 h, gently remove the cells attached to the top of the upper chamber with a cotton swab, and stain the cells attached to the lower chamber for 5 min. Subsequently, these cells were captured and quantified under a microscope.

## Statistical method
Mendelian randomization used inverse variance weighting as primary analysis, with weighted median and MR Egger for validation. Instrumental variables: P < 5 × 10−8, r2 < 0.001, F > 10. Heterogeneity assessed by Cochran’s Q, pleiotropy by MR Egger intercept. DEGs: |log2FC|> 1, P < 0.05. Enrichment: FDR < 0.05. Immune infiltration: CIBERSORT with Wilcoxon test. All analyses performed in R (significance: P < 0.05).

## Result

## Screening of gene targets for osteosarcoma related drugs
Using gene expression profiles from the GEO database, we performed differential gene expression analysis to identify candidate genes for further investigation in osteosarcoma. A total of 2415 downregulated genes and 3310 upregulated genes were identified from GSE99671 using threshold screening (Fig.  A). Among these, the dataset showed relatively high upregulation of EEF1A2, FNBP1L, KRT8, KRT81, and TPD52 and relatively low expression of CD248, CHN1, CEMIP, CTSK, and GREM2. The top 25 most significantly upregulated and downregulated genes in the dataset are shown in the heatmap (Fig.  B). 
Next, using a 0.05 inverse variance weighted (IVW) criterion, we performed MR analysis on 2893 drug-target genes and identified 160 genes showing potential associations with osteosarcoma. By intersecting these genes with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the previous analysis, we obtained 20 overlapping candidate genes (Fig.  A, B). Based on the MR estimates, nine of these genes showed a negative association with osteosarcoma risk (suggesting a potential protective association), and eleven showed a positive association (suggesting a potential positive association with risk). Figure  C displays the expression levels of these 20 candidate genes. Furthermore, Fig.  D displays the IVW-P values and the estimated associations between these genes and osteosarcoma. 

## Enrichment analysis of drug-target genes
We performed GO functional enrichment analysis on the identified druggable genes, covering three categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). The results showed that the enriched BP terms included protein targeting, glycosylation, macromolecule glycosylation, and neuron apoptosis. Enriched CC terms included contractile fibers, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, cohesin complex, gamma-tubulin complex, and desmosomes. Enriched MF terms included hexosyltransferase activity, actin filament binding, glycosyltransferase activity, cadherin binding, and actin binding (Fig.  A). KEGG pathway analysis indicated potential associations with protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum and muscle cell cytoskeleton (Fig.  B). 

## Construction of a metabolite-target network based on microbial metabolites
Based on pairing information from the gutMGene database, we integrated microbial target genes and druggable gene datasets and identified JUP as a potential hub gene for investigating possible relationships between genes, microbes, and metabolites (Fig.  A). A proposed gene-microbe-metabolite interaction network was constructed using the structures of four metabolites (4-hydroxy-(3′,4′-dihydroxyphenyl)-valeric acid, 5-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-valerolactone, 10-Oxo-trans-11-octadecenic acid, and L-ascorbic acid) obtained from the PubChem database (Fig.  B). 
The gutMGene database indicates a literature-derived association between Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and JUP. Based on this, we considered JUP as a candidate for further exploratory studies investigating potential links between gut microbiota and osteosarcoma. We analyzed 16S rRNA gene sequencing data to examine the overall alterations of gut microbiota in osteosarcoma. The gut microbiota’s Shannon diversity index decreased by almost 27% in the osteosarcoma group (OS) compared to the healthy control group (HC) (Fig.  A). We examined the genus Lactobacillus using our 16S rRNA data to explore potential associations with this shift, and we observed a relatively lower abundance of this genus in the OS group (Fig.  B). The majority of samples in the OS group had lower Lactobacillus abundance than the median level in the HC group, according to the cumulative distribution function (CDF) analysis (Fig.  C), suggesting that this pattern may be broadly observed in this cohort. Microbial abundance network analysis revealed that 45 bacterial genera, including Lactobacillus, formed a co-occurrence module that showed a trend toward lower abundance in the OS group (Fig.  D). Collectively, these observations suggest alterations in the gut microbiota composition of osteosarcoma patients, and their co-occurrence network features may reflect a synergistic dysbiosis state at the community level. 

## Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations
We selected four candidate metabolites for molecular docking analysis with JUP to explore the potential molecular interaction suggested by the network analysis (Fig.  A). These docking experiments are computational predictions and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The predicted binding energy between metabolites and JUP ranged from − 4 to − 6.7 kcal/mol in the docking simulations. Among these, 5-(3,4-Dihydroxyphenyl)-valerolactone showed the most favorable binding energy (− 6.7 kcal/mol) (Fig.  B, C) and was predicted to bind within the C1 pocket of JUP. To evaluate binding specificity, we used glycerol as a negative control (predicted binding energy − 3.2 kcal/mol) (Fig.  D, E). The docking results suggested that the candidate metabolite may have higher binding affinity than the negative control, with potential interactions involving residues in the C1 pocket. These computational predictions raise the possibility that the C1 pocket might recognize metabolites with specific structural features (benzene ring dihydroxy and lactone ring configurations), though this requires experimental validation. We performed a second independent molecular docking run to assess the reproducibility of the computational predictions. The second run yielded similar results: predicted binding energy of − 6.4 kcal/mol for 5-(3,4-Dihydroxyphenyl)-valerolactone and − 2.9 kcal/mol for glycerol (Figure A–D). The consistency between the two computational runs suggests reproducibility of the docking protocol, though the predicted interactions remain to be validated biochemically. 
In the 100 ns MD simulation, the system showed equilibrium and conformational stability under the simulation conditions, with temperature maintained at 300.02 K (Fig.  F) and potential energy converging smoothly (Fig.  G). The simulated structure was generally stable, as indicated by an RMSD plateau at approximately 1.080 nm (Fig.  H) and a consistent radius of gyration of around 4.091 nm (Fig.  I). Residue-level fluctuations were within a moderate range (average RMSF = 0.292 nm, Fig.  J), and the hydrogen‑bond network remained stable with an average of about 579 bonds (Fig.  K). Together, these in silico observations suggest conformational stability of the JUP-metabolite complex under the simulated conditions, providing a computational basis for further experimental investigation. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and await experimental validation.

## Mendelian randomization analysis of JUP and osteosarcoma
We performed two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to examine the potential association between JUP and osteosarcoma. Genetically predicted higher JUP expression levels showed a positive association with osteosarcoma risk in the inverse variance weighted analysis. Specifically, the estimated odds ratio for osteosarcoma was 1.008 (95% CI 1.002–1.014, P = 0.0047) per unit increase in genetically predicted JUP expression (Fig.  A). We performed sensitivity analysis using four methods, including weighted median and MR Egger, to assess the robustness of the findings. The scatter plot shows individual SNP effects and MR Egger regression lines, with no strong evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Fig.  B). Cochran’s Q test did not detect significant heterogeneity, and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis suggested that no single SNP was driving the observed association (Fig.  C). A colocalization analysis provided additional evidence consistent with a potential link, with a PP.H4 value of 0.843, suggesting that osteosarcoma and JUP may share a common genetic variant in the region (Fig.  D). 

## Correlation between JUP expression and immune cell infiltration in osteosarcoma
The estimated fraction of immune cell infiltration was compared between JUP high and low expression groups for major immune cell subtypes using the CIBERSORT algorithm (Fig.  A). The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) to illustrate the association between JUP expression and the estimated infiltration levels of different immune cells. In this analysis, the JUP high expression group showed relatively lower estimated infiltration levels for several cell types, including T cells CD8, T cells regulatory (Tregs), T cells gamma delta, Macrophages M1, Macrophages M2, T cells CD4 memory resting, T cells CD4 memory activated, NK cells resting, Macrophages M0, and Mast cells resting (Fig.  B). We performed correlation analysis to examine the expression relationships between JUP and marker genes of the cell subtypes identified as showing differences between JUP high and low expression groups (Macrophage M0, Macrophage M1, Gamma delta T, and NK resting) (Fig.  C). The results showed a positive correlation between JUP and M0 macrophage marker genes, suggesting a potential association between JUP expression and M0 macrophage abundance, which may warrant further functional investigation. 

## Knockdown of JUP inhibits in vitro proliferation of osteosarcoma cells
We created a stable JUP knockdown model in cell lines to explore the potential involvement of JUP in the malignant development of osteosarcoma. Both separately generated shRNAs targeting JUP can successfully downregulate the mRNA expression level of this gene, as confirmed by qRT-PCR technique (Fig.  A, B). Based on this, we used the CCK-8 cell proliferation test method to examine the effects of JUP deficiency on cell growth. The results showed that during the continuous monitoring period, the JUP knockdown group’s cell proliferation activity showed a significant and persistent decrease trend when compared to cells transfected with negative controls (Fig.  C, D). Collectively, these observations suggest that JUP expression levels may be associated with the in vitro proliferation ability of osteosarcoma cells, and that JUP may play a role in the growth process of osteosarcoma. 

## JUP mediates Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and DPV effects in osteosarcoma Cells
Based on the database-derived association between Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (L. plantarum) and JUP, we established stable JUP-knockdown SaOS-2 and 143B cell lines to explore whether JUP may be involved in the effects of L. plantarum and its metabolite DPV on osteosarcoma cells. First, we examined the impact of L. plantarum and DPV treatment on JUP expression; Western blot analysis revealed decreased JUP protein levels in the NC-Lacto and NC-DPV groups compared with the NC-Ctrl group (Fig.  A), suggesting that L. plantarum and its metabolite DPV may exert their effects in part through downregulating JUP expression. Subsequently, CCK-8 proliferation assays showed that both L. plantarum and DPV treatments significantly inhibited cell proliferation in negative control cells (P < 0.05); similarly, JUP knockdown alone (shJUP-Ctrl) also reduced proliferative capacity. However, in JUP-knockdown cells, the anti-proliferative effects of L. plantarum or DPV treatment were attenuated relative to the shJUP-Ctrl group (Fig.  B). Colony formation assays yielded consistent results: the number of colonies was significantly reduced in the NC-Lacto and NC-DPV groups compared with the NC-Ctrl group, while L. plantarum or DPV treatment-induced suppression of colony formation was diminished following JUP knockdown (Fig.  C, D). In Transwell invasion assays, L. plantarum and DPV treatments significantly decreased invasive capacity in negative control cells, but this effect was diminished in JUP-knockdown cells (Fig.  E, F). Collectively, L. plantarum and its metabolite DPV downregulate JUP expression and inhibit malignant phenotypes—including proliferation, colony formation, and invasion—of osteosarcoma cells, suggesting that JUP may play a role in mediating these effects. It should be noted that these observations are based on phenotypic assays and do not establish a direct regulatory mechanism between microbiota, metabolites, and JUP. 

## Discussion
Emerging evidence has highlighted the complex interplay between gut microbiota and cancer (Hanus, ). Pathogenic microbes such as Fusobacterium nucleatum can promote immunosuppression, while microbial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids influence tumor cell growth through HDAC regulation (Makkar et al., ; Wang et al. ). The anti-tumor activity of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum has also been documented (Fida et al. ). Building on this evidence, the present study adopted a microbiota-centered approach to investigate potential associations between gut microbiota and osteosarcoma. Through integration of Mendelian randomization, transcriptomics, 16S rRNA sequencing, and functional validation, we identified JUP as a candidate gene potentially involved in microbiota–host interactions. Multi-omics analyses revealed enrichment of osteosarcoma-related genes in host–microbe interaction pathways, including glycosylation and cell adhesion, suggesting their potential involvement in microbiota-associated biological processes.
In this study, we observed alterations in gut microbiota composition in patients with osteosarcoma. 16S rRNA sequencing revealed reduced alpha diversity and decreased relative abundance of Lactobacillus—findings that were consistently observed across the patient cohort. Co-occurrence network analysis identified 45 bacterial genera, including Lactobacillus, that formed a coordinated variation module, suggesting a potential association between microbial ecological patterns and osteosarcoma. These observations align with prior reports of reduced beneficial bacteria in cancer patients and associations between specific taxa and osteosarcoma risk (Ordóñez et al. ; Chen et al. ; Li et al. ). Although our 16S data could not be resolved at the species level, the gutMGene database indicates a literature-derived association between L. plantarum and JUP. This database-derived observation—together with our 16S findings—provides a rationale for further investigation into the potential role of this bacterial species and its metabolites in osteosarcoma, while acknowledging that direct evidence for L. plantarum in our patient cohort remains to be established.
A potential molecular basis for microbiota–host communication is suggested by the predicted interaction between the microbial metabolite DPV and JUP. Molecular docking predicted that DPV may bind to the C1 pocket of JUP, and molecular dynamics simulations supported the stability of the complex. DPV, a gut metabolite of flavan-3-ol, has been reported to possess antioxidant properties and modulate signaling pathways such as IκBα/NF-κB (Della Vedova et al. ). These computational observations suggest that the diet–microbiota–metabolite axis may be involved in osteosarcoma progression through potential interactions with JUP. However, this proposed axis remains a working hypothesis that requires direct experimental confirmation, including biophysical validation of DPV-JUP binding and functional studies to establish mechanistic links.
An interactive network involving microbiota, immunity, and tumors is suggested by the positive correlation between JUP expression and M0 macrophage infiltration. High JUP expression is positively associated with M0 macrophage infiltration, and under some circumstances, M0 macrophages can polarize toward a pro-tumor phenotype. Microbial metabolites have been shown to modulate macrophage function: short-chain fatty acids influence macrophage polarization, and L. plantarum metabolites can shift tumor-associated macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype (Sarkar et al. ; Garavaglia et al. ; Jeong et al. ; Naz et al. ). We hypothesized that JUP may serve as a bridging molecule in mediating the effects of microbial metabolites on the tumor immune microenvironment. Consistent with this working hypothesis, we observed that a DPV metabolism-related gene signature (PON1, NQO1, UGT1A1/8/9/10) positively correlated with both JUP expression (r = 0.252, P = 0.04) and M0 macrophage infiltration (r = 0.242, P = 0.05) in the GSE99671 dataset (Figure ), further suggesting a potential association between DPV metabolic activity and the JUP-M0 axis, though this correlation does not establish a causal or mechanistic link.
This study has a number of significant limitations. This study is exploratory in nature, as the multi-omics datasets were analyzed separately without formal integrative modeling. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. First, the 16S rRNA sequencing data can only be analyzed at the genus level and cannot verify the involvement of specific strains such as L. plantarum. Therefore, the species-level associations discussed in this study (e.g., L. plantarum) are derived from the gutMGene database rather than from our own sequencing data. Second, metabolite levels in patient samples were not directly measured; DPV levels in human biological samples were not assessed, and direct binding between DPV and JUP remains to be experimentally validated using biophysical techniques. Third, the strain associations reported in the gutMGene database are derived from literature summaries rather than empirical data generated in this study. Fourth, the DPV-JUP binding predicted by molecular docking and kinetic simulations represents a computational prediction, constrained by force field accuracy and solvent effect simplifications. Fifth, while our in vitro experiments suggest that JUP may play a role in mediating the effects of L. plantarum and DPV, these microbial factors may exert their effects through multiple pathways beyond JUP, and additional mechanistic studies are needed to delineate their relative contributions. Furthermore, the observed effects are based on phenotypic assays and do not establish a direct regulatory mechanism between microbiota, metabolites, and JUP. Sixth, the clinical significance of these findings requires confirmation in independent patient cohorts with matched metabolomics and transcriptomics data to further explore the relationships among DPV levels, JUP expression, and patient prognosis.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study proposes a hypothesis-generating framework centered on the potential interplay involving database-derived associations between Lactobacillus-related species and their metabolites and JUP, which requires further validation. By integrating Mendelian randomization analysis (performed to assess host gene expression in relation to osteosarcoma) with a gut microbiota–metabolite–target network derived from literature-based databases, we provide multi-omics clues regarding the associations between gut microbiota and osteosarcoma. These findings suggest that JUP and its associated microbe–host interaction axis may represent promising avenues for future research into osteosarcoma mechanisms and the development of intervention strategies. However, given the exploratory nature of this study, additional validation through clinical samples and animal studies is necessary to further investigate the functional role of JUP and to explore potential therapeutic implications for osteosarcoma.
