## Physical Properties of the Cytoplasm Regulate Single Enzyme
Activity
To study metabolic regulation, it helps to simplify
the complexity
of metabolic networks by focusing on individual enzymes. This allows
for a deeper exploration into another layer of complexity: the physical
nature of the cytoplasm. Unlike the dilute solutions typically used
in biochemical experiments, the cytoplasm is a crowded, viscous, and
spatially heterogeneous environment. While simplified in vitro systems
allow precise control, they overlook how the physical properties of
the intracellular space shape enzyme behavior. In the following paragraphs,
we focus on key physical contributors that tune enzymatic functions:
macromolecular crowding, viscosity, and diffusion.

The
physical and mechanical characteristics of the cytosol provide
a regulating scaffold for enzymatic processes in many ways. The phenomenon of cytosol-mediated enzymatic
processes is especially well-understood in terms of glucose metabolism.
This set of enzymes is subject to intracellular compartmentalization,
and this colocalization allows expediated funneling through the pathway.

 Early in vitro work demonstrated that cytosolic
filaments affect enzyme performance. The involvement of the cytoskeleton in regulating glycolysis has
been well-established in vivo and is thoroughly reviewed
elsewhere.

 The amelioration of in-cell techniques such
as fluorescence microscopy and other forms of imaging allowed further
exploration of the relationship between cytosol and enzyme. These
have been used to illustrate the heterogeneous environment within
the cell, and how this is used to the cell’s benefit to control
catalysis.
,
 This understanding of the complex
interplay between enzymes and the cellular milieu continues to grow
and evolve, incorporating both classical in vitro and newer in vivo methods.
A combination
of these in vitro and in
vivo techniques, alongside modeling, was used to demonstrate
a cytosol-mediated coordination of the carbon–nitrogen cycle. Two nonconsecutive enzymes from within the
TCA metabolic pathway formed a pairwise interaction in cytomimetic
phase-separated droplets. This association resulted in significant
rate enhancement of one of the enzymes, matching the molar ratio of
the enzymes. Additionally, the association of these enzymes resulted
in sequestration of a reaction product. These phenomena together increased
flux through the carbon–nitrogen metabolic pathway in vivo. The authors offer these results as an example of
metabolon formation to expediate metabolic channeling. The cytological
regulation of enzyme function is thereby shown to play a part in TCA
cycling.
The glycolytic process is subject to fluctuation, in
terms of reaction
rate and ATP synthesis. Glycolytic oscillations
can occur in response to environmental stimuli, and to fuel energetically
expensive processes. Historically, the control of glycolytic flux
has been attributed to the action of specific enzymes in the pathway. However, recent developments in fluorescence
spectroscopy have revealed glycolytic flux to be subject to the state
of intracellular water.
,
 A probe which responds
to water polarity was used in yeast cells and coupled to NADH measurements.
The polar state of water and oscillations in glycolytic rate were
tightly linked, even across different mutant strains and stress conditions.
The authors propose that this physical, dynamic property represents
a novel element of metabolic control.

## The Organization of Enzymes Is Subject to Selection
The impact of cellular organization on enzyme function is evident
especially when we consider its evolutionary impact. The ability of
enzymes to self-assemble has long been established as a method of
regulatory control. Oligomerization can regulate the activity of some
enzymes, and others polymerize into
fibrils to prevent activity in response to stress. It is now evident that the residues which make self-assembly
possible are locally buffered, indicating this is an evolutionarily
protected mechanism. Structural analysis
of more than 7000 symmetrical E. coli complexes revealed
geometric “hotspots”, which were implicated in self-assembly.
These hotspots were locally buffered by hydrophilic residues. This
allows a single point mutation to trigger self-assembly, suggesting
frequent sampling of these structures by evolution. Self-assembling
enzymes can evolve much more easily than previously thought, though
the extent of this feature across the enzyme landscape remains unexplored.
It may, therefore, be currently underestimated as a mechanism for
enzyme regulation. Currently, there is little literature exploring
the ways in which the physical properties of the cytoplasm impact
enzyme evolution. This overlooked property likely shapes the evolutionary
trajectories of certain enzymes, which further research in this area
may shed light on.

## Conclusions and Future Directions
For much of biochemical
history, enzymes have been assayed under
in vitro conditionsisolated in optimized buffers, flooded
with substrate, and free of crowding agents. These settings are far
removed from the complex cytoplasmic environments in which enzymes
naturally operate and, crucially, evolved.
Over the past two
decades, advances in in-cell methods have revealed
that the cytoplasm is anything but simple. It is a crowded, dynamic
environment, filled with macromolecules, limited free water, and heterogeneous
physical properties such as viscosity and diffusion. This has led
to a surge in cytomimetic studies, in which enzymes are examined under
more biologically relevant conditions: in artificial cytoplasms, in crowded in vitro solutions, and eventually in artificial compartments or
cells. These studies consistently show
that catalytic performance in such environments differs markedly from
canonical in vitro measurements. Moreover, these complex environments behave as feedback
systemsenzymes shape their environment, and in turn, the environment
shapes metabolic flux.
Enzyme control is traditionally viewed
through the lens of genetics
and molecular interactions. However, we propose that the physical
state of the cytoplasm itself acts as an additional layer of control,
as well as the regulatory layers of allostery or gene expression.
In fact, the cytoplasm engages the axes of regulation (time scale
and action) uniquely, enabling rapid, concerted responses that distinguish
it from slower mechanisms like genetic regulation. Its broad effects
(or actions) on enzymes, influencing multiple biochemical reactions
simultaneously, separates it from more targeted effects like allostery.
This makes the cytoplasm a unique regulator capable of up- and down-regulating
processes through different physical modes of action, offering adaptable
regulation. The self-regulating nature of the cytoplasm is vital for
cell survival, enabling dynamic adaptation to stress. This involves
the modulation of crowding, viscosity, and intracellular diffusionfactors
that influence enzyme organization and activity. Beyond catalysis,
enzymes help shape the physical state of the cytoplasm, creating a
feedback loop that couples metabolism with spatial organization.
One striking example is the glassy dynamics of the bacterial cytoplasm,
which can shift between fluid-like and glass-like states. These transitions are tightly linked to metabolic
activity and reflect a system evolved to balance stability with responsiveness.
Such self-organizing properties are likely foundational to core cellular
behaviors.
Recent in-cell techniques have deepened our understanding
of how
cytoplasmic material properties influence enzymatic catalysis. While
much of this research still relies on cell-mimetic environments, there
is a growing movement toward in vivo studies and
metabolomics to uncover the underlying mechanisms in physiological
contexts. Promising approaches, such as live-cell imaging techniques
like FRET, and mass spectrometry imaging (e.g., GCIB-SIMS), are paving
the way for more quantitative insights into pathway dynamics in live
cells. Understanding such dynamics is increasingly recognized as vital,
given that protein function often depends on conformational changes
that can be modulated by cytoplasmic properties. For example, FRET
studies on the multidomain enzyme AK3L1 show that crowding induces
LID-CORE domain compaction, reducing activity. Given this sensitivity in single enzyme dynamics, multienzyme
complexes – requiring coordinated conformational shifts –
are likely even more affected. Work by Vera et al. and Yin et al. demonstrates
the potential of real-time imaging by FRET and cryo-EM to capture
dynamic binding modes and conformational states in multienzyme complexes
under traditionally dilute conditions. Future efforts should extend
these approaches to physiologically relevant, crowded conditions to
elucidate how cytoplasmic physical properties modulate multienzyme
assembly, dynamics, and metabolic regulation.
This remains an
underappreciated layer of enzyme regulation. Expanding
quantitative in vivo approaches will be key to fully
understanding how this feedback system operatesand how it
evolved to support robust, adaptive life.
