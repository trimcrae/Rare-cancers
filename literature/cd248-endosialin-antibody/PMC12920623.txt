One of the major TME components is cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), of which several subtypes have been identified in tumor tissues. Although single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology is a powerful tool for investigating the proportions and composition of cells in tissues, large-scale datasets are needed to analyze small cell populations. Here, we constructed an integrated scRNA-seq dataset for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by compiling publicly available data and observed differences in TME heterogeneity and cellular composition between lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). In particular, we identified significant differences in CAF subtypes between LUAD and LUSC. Inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) were predominantly found in LUAD, whereas myofibroblastic CAFs (mCAFs) were more common in LUSC. A coculture analysis of lung fibroblasts confirmed that LUAD cells induced the iCAF phenotype and, in contrast, that LUSC cells promoted myofibroblastic differentiation. A correlation analysis with the prognosis identified mCAFs as poor prognostic factors in both LUAD and LUSC, but iCAFs were found to be a poor prognostic factor only in LUSC, with the opposite pattern observed in LUAD. This work highlights important considerations regarding the CAF subtype dominance in LUAD and LUSC, which may be related to the prognosis.

## Introduction
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of all lung cancer cases and consists of two main histological subtypes: lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). Although both LUAD and LUSC are malignant tumors that develop within the lung tissue, they differ significantly in their pathophysiology, treatment strategies, and clinical outcomes. Emerging evidence has shown that not only the properties of the cancer cells themselves but also the tumor microenvironment (TME) influence cancer progression and prognosis. Elucidating the TME is important for clarifying the differences in the characteristics of LUAD and LUSC, leading to improvements in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.
In the preceding decade, the pivotal function of the TME in the initiation and progression of primary de novo lung carcinoma has been acknowledged – . NSCLCs are highly infiltrated by stromal cells and immune cells in tumors . The TME, which includes endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and myeloid cells, among other components, has important roles in determining the characteristics of lung tumors. One of the major stromal cell types in the TME is cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). CAFs are involved primarily in tumor maintenance and promotion through a variety of mechanisms . In addition, various cell types, such as endothelial cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), are known to contribute to the progression of lung cancer – . However, the differences in heterogeneity and cellular composition between LUAD and LUSC and the extent to which these cellular compositions affect patient survival remain largely unexplored.
Technological advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have facilitated studies of cell state variations and the strength of cell plasticity – . However, scRNA-seq studies cannot easily analyze large sample numbers, mainly because of the high cost of preparation and sequencing. In addition, when some cell populations that are difficult to isolate from tissues (e.g., fibroblasts) are analyzed, a single dataset is often insufficient because statistically robust cell counts cannot be obtained . On the other hand, if a sufficient sample size is available, the characterization of cancer complexity can be facilitated by excluding individual variants and enriching for different components of the cell population, especially those common to different cancer types.
We developed a single-cell meta-analysis of lung cancer that integrates publicly available datasets to overcome these obstacles associated with scRNA-seq and clarify the heterogeneity and composition of the TME between LUAD and LUSC. We constructed large scRNA-seq datasets of 175 samples from LUAD and 74 samples from LUSC. By focusing on CAFs and other cell types in the TME, our data have revealed differences in cellular composition and heterogeneity between LUAD and LUSC. Additionally, we show that CAF predominance is associated with the prognosis.

## Results

## Establishment of integrated scRNA-seq datasets of LUAD and LUSC
We compiled ten independent scRNA-seq datasets comprising 121 primary and 54 metastatic tumors from LUAD and 73 primary and one metastatic tumor from LUSC to investigate the characteristics of the TMEs in LUAD and LUSC (Fig. ; Supplementary Fig. ). The datasets and clinical information are provided in Supplementary Data – and Supplementary Fig. . Datasets were separately generated for LUAD and LUSC to extract nontumor cells from the TME (Fig. ). After a series of data quality control processes, including data integration, normalization, and batch effect removal , a total of 132 patients, 175 samples, and 366,652 cells were included in the subsequent analysis of LUAD, and a total of 39 patients, 74 samples, and 125,238 cells were included for LUSC. In the LUAD dataset, we performed graph-based clustering and annotated five major cell populations using canonical markers, including stromal cells (n = 6848) positive for COL1A2, lymphoid cells (n = 140,083) positive for PTPRC and negative for AIF1, myeloid cells (n = 83,462) positive for AIF1, endothelial cells (n = 4794) positive for CLDN5, and epithelial cells (n = 72,166) positive for EPCAM (Fig. ). Similarly, in the LUSC datasets, we annotated five cell populations, including stromal cells (n = 4542), lymphoid cells (n = 38,820), myeloid cells (n = 18,264), endothelial cells (n = 715), and epithelial cells (n = 50,647). UMAP plots with cell type-specific markers showed the clear segregation of epithelial, stromal, endothelial, lymphoid, and myeloid lineages (Supplementary Fig. ). The utilization of the copy number assignment facilitated the differentiation of malignant (n = 104,079) and nonmalignant (n = 18,734) epithelial cells through the implementation of the “inferCNV” v1.20.0 package in R (Supplementary Fig. ). We then pooled both datasets to better understand the differences in the TMEs between LUAD and LUSC, and separately identified four major stromal and immune cell populations (Fig. , bottom panel). UMAP among the cell types revealed that most cell clusters appeared to overlap among the 9 datasets, although some of the endothelial clusters might be specific (Supplementary Fig. ). This comprehensive evaluation highlights that the integrated scRNA-seq datasets of LUAD and LUSC could be useful in analyzing the TME. 

## Distinct TMEs between LUAD and LUSC
We further dissected the differences within the stromal and immune cell populations between LUAD and LUSC by first reclustering the lymphoid population (n = 178,903 cells) and dividing it into eight cell types (Fig. ) according to the expression of the following canonical markers, as previously reported , : GNLY, NKG7, and KLRD1 for natural killer (NK) cells/NK T cells; CD3E, CD4, IL7R, and CCR7 for CD4+ T cells; FOXP3, TNFRSF4, and CTLA4 for Tregs; CD8 and GZMK for CD8+ T cells; HAVCR2, LAG3, TIGIT, and PDCD1 for exhausted CD8+ cells; CD79A and CD24 for B cells; JCHAIN for plasma cells; and MKI67 for proliferating lymphoid cells. We used a statistical method to assess disparities in the proportions of cell types between samples within a single-cell dataset and to investigate whether the composition of the above cell populations differs between LUAD and LUSC . Compared with LUAD samples, LUSC samples presented significantly higher proportions of plasma cells and proliferating lymphoid cells, whereas LUAD samples presented higher proportions of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B cells than LUSC samples (Fig. ). 
Next, in the endothelial UMAP, we obtained transcriptome data from 5509 endothelial cells (ECs), which was fewer than those from other cell types (Fig. ). A higher microvessel density has been reported in LUAD than in LUSC , , and our datasets confirmed that fewer ECs were extracted from LUSC (Figs. B, ). ECs were classified into nine cell types (Fig. ), including arterial ECs expressing GJA5 and BMX; venous ECs expressing ACKR1, POSTN, and CCL14; capillary ECs marked with CA4; lymphatic ECs expressing PDPN and PROX1; tip cells expressing CXCR4, ESM1, PLVAP, and ENG; CXCL9+ ECs expressing CXCL9; liver sinusoidal and immature ECs expressing ENG and CLEC4G; ECs that underwent the endothelial‒mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and expressed ACTA2 and COL1A1; and MKI67-positive proliferating ECs, according to previous reports , . We detected significant increases in the number of CXCL9+ ECs in LUAD and in the number of proliferating ECs in LUSC; however, almost no differences in the proportions of most endothelial cell types were detected between LUAD and LUSC (Fig. ).
Then, myeloid cells (n = 101,726) were classified into seven clusters (Fig. ), including dendritic cells (DCs)1 marked with XCR11 and CLEC9A; DC2s expressing CD1C and PLD4; mature DCs (mDCs) expressing LAMP3; neutrophils expressing FCGR3B, CSF3R, CXCR2, and G0S2; monocytes and macrophages expressing CD14, FCGR3A, MARCO, CD68, and CD163; mast cells expressing MS4A2 and CPA3; and proliferating myeloid cells expressing MKI67. We observed the significant enrichment of DC1s, mast cells, and proliferating myeloid cells in LUSC, whereas greater proportions of monocytes–macrophages and mDCs were detected in LUAD (Fig. ). Our data revealed significant differences in the macrophage population (Fig. ), and recent reports have shown that TAMs play critical roles in the TME . Single-cell omics technologies have significantly advanced our understanding of the molecular diversity of TAMs, and seven TAM subsets are known to be preserved in most cancer types . We performed further subclustering of the macrophage populations to assess TAM diversity in LUAD and LUSC (Supplementary Fig. ). We classified 7 subsets, including tumor-infiltrating monocytes (TIMs) marked with S100A8, S100A9, and LYZ; tissue-resident macrophages such as TAMs (RTM-TAMs) expressing MARCO, MRC1, and FABP4; lipid-associated TAMs (LA-TAMs) expressing APOC1, APOE, C1QA, and FOLR2; SPP1-positive TAMs (SPP1-TAMs) marked with SPP1, MMP9, MT1G, and IFI6; TAMs that upregulate C3, PLD4, and FCGBP, which are required for opsonization and phagocytosis (C3+ TAMs); heat shock protein (HSP)-positive TAMs expressing HSPA6 and HSPA1A; and IFN-regulated gene-positive TAMs (IFN-TAMs) expressing CXCL10, ISG15 and VAMP5 (Supplementary Fig. ). We revealed that RTM-TAMs and C3+ TAMs were significantly enriched in LUAD, whereas the population of HSP-TAMs was significantly higher in LUSC (Supplementary Fig. ).
Finally, we sought to analyze the stromal populations in LUAD and LUSC. After excluding potential low-quality cells and removing contaminating cell clusters with high expression of the epithelial markers EPCAM and KRT5; the immune cell markers CD3D, MARCO, JCHAIN, and PTPRC; the endothelial marker CLDN5; and the oligodendrocyte markers PLP1, S100B, and MAG, we obtained 11,390 stromal cells, which were classified into five subclusters: fibroblasts, pericytes, smooth muscle cells, mesothelial cells, and proliferating stroma cells (Fig. ). The proportions of the populations of fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells and proliferating stroma cells differed between LUAD and LUSC (Fig. ). In particular, as fibroblasts constitute the majority of stromal cells and recent findings have shown that they can be classified into various subtypes within cancer tissues , we were able to perform a more detailed analysis of the fibroblast population.

## Distinct CAF populations between LUAD and LUSC
The robust extracellular matrix structure and cell size make the efficient isolation of fibroblasts from tissues difficult. These properties limit the number of cells that can be obtained in a single study and may limit an in-depth analysis of fibroblast subpopulations , . By constructing a large-scale dataset of LUAD and LUSC scRNA-seq data, we sought to conduct a comprehensive analysis of CAF subtypes. For this purpose, we performed unsupervised clustering following the isolation of CAFs from stromal populations for a refined analysis. Ultimately, 8737 fibroblasts were divided into eight clusters (Supplementary Fig. ), and common fibroblast markers, such as COL1A1, S100A4, and DCN, and the mesenchymal cell marker VIM, were expressed in all CAF subclusters, confirming their fibroblast identity (Supplementary Fig. ). Recently, five CAF subtypes have been characterized by a single-cell analysis in various types of cancers . The utilization of selective CAF markers served to elucidate the distinguishing characteristics of each identified subtype (Supplementary Fig. ). Cluster 2 was enriched in the expression of IL6, CXCL2, CXCL12, CFD, and C3 and was subsequently identified as inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs). Clusters 0, 1, and 6 were identified as myofibroblastic CAFs (mCAFs) because they expressed high levels of ACTA2, MMP11, COL, and FAP. Vascular-associated CAFs (vCAFs) with a signature associated with angiogenesis and vascular development have been identified . Clusters 5 and 7, which expressed signature genes, such as VEGFA, NOTCH3, COL18A1, and EPAS1, were identified as vCAFs in this dataset. Cluster 4 was considered cycling CAFs (cCAFs) because of the high expression of a set of cell cycle-related genes. Genes involved in antigen presentation, such as HLA-DRA and CD74, were highly expressed in Cluster 3, and this cluster was designated antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs). These five CAF subtypes were visualized in a two-dimensional UMAP plot in Fig. , and the above CAF markers are shown in a dot plot in Fig. . Additionally, we confirmed the presence of all five CAF subtypes across all datasets (Supplementary Fig. ). 
We performed a differential gene expression analysis to comprehensively characterize these CAF subpopulations at the population level (Supplementary Data ). The selected differentially expressed genes in each of the CAF subtypes are shown (Fig. ) and reveal the diversity of CAFs. We further investigated subtype-specific gene signatures by performing a gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of Gene Ontology (GO) terms (Fig. ). GSEA revealed that mCAFs function in collagen fibril organization, proton motive force-driven mitochondrial ATP synthesis, and NADH dehydrogenase complex assembly. On the other hand, the phospholipase C-activating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, the catecholamine metabolic process, and the regulation of positive chemotaxis were enriched in iCAFs. Additionally, vCAFs presented unique GO terms related to the cellular response to decreased oxygen levels, vasculogenesis, and signal transduction by p53 class mediators.
To investigate the relationships and potential differentiation pathways among CAF subtypes, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle3 (Supplementary Fig. ). Although CAFs may arise from multiple cellular origins , PI16-expressing cells are thought to represent a common fibroblast precursor population , . Starting the trajectory from PI16⁺ cells, we observed that fibroblasts in LUAD predominantly converged into vCAF and apCAF lineages, whereas those in LUSC mainly differentiated toward the iCAF lineage. These findings suggest that the CAF differentiation trajectory differs fundamentally between LUAD and LUSC.
Although mCAFs and iCAFs are the major CAF subtypes , predominant CAF subtypes have been reported in certain cancer types . We compared the proportions of CAF subtypes to evaluate the predominant CAF subpopulations between LUAD and LUSC (Fig. ). We found that mCAFs and cCAFs were significantly enriched in LUSC, whereas iCAFs were more abundant in LUAD (Fig. ). Immunohistochemistry confirmed that FAP, an mCAF marker, was highly expressed in LUSC (Fig. ). As fibroblast types and their roles can vary due to the tissue composition , the proportions were examined separately for primary and metastatic sites. Similarly, mCAFs and cCAFs were significantly enriched in primary LUSC tumors, whereas iCAFs were significantly enriched in LUAD tumors (Supplementary Fig. ). As the tumor microenvironment changes with the cancer stage , we also tested whether the proportions of CAFs differed according to cancer stage and obtained similar results (Supplementary Fig. and D). The effects of smoking and sex were also considered, with similar results (Supplementary Fig. , and F). These data indicate that mCAFs are more abundant in LUSC, whereas iCAFs are more abundant in LUAD.

## Cancer subtypes may influence the CAF subtype predominance
We performed a modular score-based analysis based on CAF-specific markers to examine the CAF subtype predominance in a larger cohort. Except for apCAFs, for which we could not find a specific gene set, we established specific gene sets for the other four CAF subtypes (Supplementary Fig. ; Supplementary Data ). In LUAD and LUSC, we tested whether the composition of CAF subtypes differed from that of bulk transcriptome profiles from TCGA data using GSVA . This analysis also revealed the significant enrichment of mCAF markers in LUSC and iCAF markers in LUAD (Fig. ). Furthermore, we verified these findings by performing a deconvolution analysis using CIBERSORTx . Similarly, similar results were obtained in the analysis (Fig. ). These findings corroborate earlier research findings , . 
Next, we sought to test whether the properties of the cancer cells affected the differentiation of the surrounding CAFs. We hypothesized that different types of cancer cells induce different subtypes of CAFs and applied an indirect transwell coculture system between lung cancer cell lines, MRC-5 cells, and lung fibroblasts. For coculture, we used Calu-3 cells, a LUAD cell line, and LC-1 sq cells, a LUSC cell line. After coculture with each of the cancer cell lines, total RNA was extracted from MRC-5 cells, and a transcriptomic analysis was performed, indicating that the profile of MRC-5 cells cocultured with Calu-3 cells was different from that of MRC-5 cells cocultured with LC-1 sq cells (Fig. ). A heatmap with selected mCAF and iCAF marker genes revealed that iCAF markers such as IL6, CXCL1, CCL2, and C3 were highly expressed in MRC-5 cells compared with Calu-3 cells. In contrast, the expression of mCAF markers such as ACTA2, COL1A1, TAGLN, and THY1 was induced in MRC-5 cells cocultured with LC-1 sq cells (Fig. ). Immunofluorescence staining showed that the expression of IL6, a typical inflammatory marker, was highly induced in MRC-5 cells cocultured with Calu-3 cells (Fig. ). The levels of protein markers for mCAFs, such as COL12A1 and ACTA2, were higher in MRC-5 cells cocultured with LC-1 sq cells (Fig. ). We extracted differentially expressed genes (2-fold or greater and p < 0.05) and performed an enrichment analysis by IPA to examine the molecular basis of gene expression. Analyses of canonical pathways and upstream regulators revealed functionally distinct phenotypes of MRC-5 cells cocultured with either Calu-3 cells or LC-1 sq cells (Fig. ; Supplementary Fig. ). Pathogen-induced cytokine storm signaling, IL-10 signaling, and IL-17A signaling were significantly enriched in MRC-5 cells cocultured with Calu-3 cells, which is reminiscent of iCAF properties (Fig. , top; , left panel). On the other hand, the S100 family signaling pathway, elastic fiber formation, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, and TGFβ signaling were significantly enriched in MRC-5 cells cocultured with LC-1 sq cells (Fig. , bottom panel, , right panel), suggesting that mCAF differentiation occurs in MRC-5 cells. Using additional cancer cell lines, we confirmed by qPCR that iCAF markers were induced in MRC-5 cells co-cultured with A549 cells, a lung adenocarcinoma line, whereas mCAF markers were induced in MRC-5 cells co-cultured with EBC-1 cells, a lung squamous cell carcinoma cell line (Supplementary Fig. ). These data suggest that cancer cells with different properties, such as LUAD and LUSC, influence CAF fate determination and contribute to the formation of different TMEs.

## CAF diversity between LUAD and LUSC
As fibroblasts exhibit heterogeneity based on their anatomical origin, their subtypes and roles can vary due to the tissue composition . We recently developed a method to quantify and visualize cellular diversity based on gene expression profiles – . In this method, closeness centrality is calculated to show alterations in gene expression in an arbitrary population (see the “Methods” section). The inverse of closeness centrality is defined as diversity, with a higher value indicating high heterogeneity. No differences in diversity between LUAD and LUSC were observed in the endothelial populations, but significant differences were observed in the cancer cell, macrophage, and CAF populations (Fig. ). Among the macrophage populations, with the exception of RTM TAMs, almost all TAM subsets were highly heterogeneous in LUSC (Supplementary Fig. ). No difference in the heterogeneity of endothelial cells as a whole was observed; however, evaluating each subtype revealed that venous ECs, tip cells, and lymphatic ECs were considered more heterogeneous in LUSC (Supplementary Fig. ). Among the CAF populations, LUADs were more heterogeneous in terms of total CAFs (Fig. ), particularly mCAFs, vCAFs, and apCAFs (Fig. ), suggesting that CAFs in LUSC are relatively uniform populations. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of CAFs, macrophages, and endothelial cells was positively correlated with the heterogeneity of cancer cells in both LUAD and LUSC (Fig. ). As the cancer site may influence the fibroblast phenotype , the heterogeneity evaluation was limited to cases in the primary site and yielded similar results, with no effect observed (Fig. ). These data confirmed the diversity of cells in the TME, particularly within the CAF and macrophage lineages. 

## Interactions of CAFs and cancer cells
We examined the interactions of CAFs with other cellular entities within the TME to gain further insights into the functional dynamics of CAFs. First, as fibroblasts induced by cancer cells exhibit distinct characteristics, the cell‒cell interactions between cancer cells and CAFs were analyzed using CellChat (Fig. ) . The interaction intensity from cancer cells to CAF subtypes differed between LUAD and LUSC (Fig. ). The degree of interaction from cancer cells to mCAFs was slightly greater in LUSC, whereas the intensity of interactions from cancer cells to iCAFs was markedly greater in LUAD. 
While proinflammatory cytokines, including IL1, LIF, OSM, and TNF, appear to regulate immune functions , TGFβ production and matrix stiffness are essential for the induction of myofibroblast differentiation , . WNT signaling and noncanonical WNT (ncWNT) signaling have also been reported to induce myofibroblast induction , . A comprehensive analysis of the cell‒cell interactions between cancer cells and CAFs (cancer cells: ligands; CAFs: receptors) revealed that IL1, LIFR, OSM, WNT, and TGFβ signaling are strongly linked to the interaction between LUAD cells and CAFs. In contrast, ncWNT signaling was observed exclusively in LUSC (Fig. ). Further examination of individual ligand‒receptor pairs revealed that OSM-(OSMR + IL6ST), OSM-(LIFR + IL6ST), LIF-(LIFR + IL6ST), IL1B-IL1R2, IL1B-(IL1R1 + IL1RAP), and CLCF1–(CNTFR + LIFR) interacted more strongly between cancer cells and iCAFs in LUAD than LUSC (Fig. ). Given these results, iCAFs are likely induced by cytokines such as OSM, LIF, and IL1B secreted from LUAD cells. The effects of CAFs on cancer cells were also examined (CAFs: ligands; cancer cells: receptors), and the results suggested that mCAFs affect both LUADs and LUSCs, whereas iCAFs primarily affect LUADs (Fig. ). Interestingly, our analysis revealed that in LUAD, but not in LUSC, the interaction between cancer cells and mCAFs was stronger for TGFβ1 and TGFβ2 signaling, except for TGFb3-(TGFBR1 + TGFBR2) (Supplementary Fig. ). Recently, WNT5A was reported to regulate the activation of latent TGFβ and to stimulate the fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition . The interaction of WNT5A with cancer cells and CAFs has been observed only in LUSC (Supplementary Fig. ). To validate these findings, we investigated whether adding cytokines induced iCAF marker expression in MRC-5 cells. The results demonstrated that the addition of IL-1α, IL-1β, LIF, or OSM resulted in an increase in the expression of CCL2, IL-6, and CXCL1 in MRC-5 cells (Supplementary Fig. ). Furthermore, the addition of an anti-IL-1 receptor type I (IL-1R1) antibody (AMG108) to MRC-5 cells co-cultured with Calu-3 cells resulted in a reduction of iCAF marker expression induced by Calu-3 co-culture, while treatment with a TGF-β receptor inhibitor markedly decreased the expression of the mCAF marker ACTA2 in MRC-5 cells co-cultured with LC-1 sq cells (Supplementary Fig. ). The findings presented above suggest the possibility of different mechanisms by which cancer cells induce the differentiation of CAFs.

## The clinical implications of the different CAF subtypes as prognostic indicators
We assessed the prognostic value of the CAF classification system to determine its clinical significance. The patients were divided into two groups based on the expression levels of CAF subtype markers, and survival analyses were conducted using TCGA data. A consistent association was observed between increased mCAF marker gene expression and a poor prognosis of both LUAD and LUSC (Fig. ). Interestingly, iCAF markers were significantly correlated with a poor prognosis of LUSC but were correlated with a good prognosis of LUAD (Fig. ). Similarly, differences in the prognosis were observed between LUAD and LUSC in terms of the expression levels of cCAF and vCAF markers (Supplementary Fig. ). This finding underscores the promise of CAF markers as prognostic indicators and potential therapeutic targets in each cancer subtype. 
In light of the divergent outcomes exhibited by the iCAF markers, we performed a comprehensive analysis of differentially expressed genes in iCAFs in LUAD and LUSC to elucidate the underlying causes of this discrepancy using an aggregation-based (pseudobulk) workflow . A total of 125 genes were identified and further analyzed (Supplementary Fig. , Supplementary Data ). Some ECM genes (INHBA, COL5A1, and MMP14) have been identified to be positively associated with immunosuppressive cell types and negatively correlated with overall survival – . In LUSC, iCAFs presented high expression of INHBA, MMP14, COL5A1, and COL1A1. Additionally, LGALS1+ fibroblasts stimulate the proliferation and migration of tumor cells by increasing the expression levels of CCR2, ADAM15, and β-integrin . The expression of these genes has also been shown to be increased in the iCAFs of LUSC. Furthermore, LUSC iCAFs presented high expression of several cytokines, including CXCL1, 2, 3, 6, and 8. These cytokines are involved in cancer progression through various mechanisms – . The GO analysis of the DEGs (Supplementary Fig. ) revealed that the iCAFs of LUSC are characterized by neutrophil chemotaxis, neutrophil migration, collagen biosynthetic processes, and chemokine-mediated signaling pathways. High intratumoral neutrophil abundance has been associated with reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration , . Consistent with this, iCAFs in LUSC may hinder CD8⁺ T cell infiltration through the recruitment of neutrophils. Supporting this notion, a negative correlation between iCAFs and CD8⁺ T cells at the primary tumor site was observed exclusively in LUSC (Supplementary Fig. ). In parallel, the proportion of iCAFs showed a significant positive correlation with neutrophil abundance in LUSC (Supplementary Fig. ). Moreover, neutrophils in LUSC exhibited elevated expressions of CXCR4, CD274, and VEGFA (Supplementary Fig. )—markers typically associated with protumor neutrophil phenotype , . Finally, based on the TCGA Pan-Cancer RNA-seq dataset, GSVA analysis of neutrophil-related GO signatures revealed that their correlations with the iCAF signature were consistently stronger in LUSC than in LUAD (Supplementary Fig. ). These disparities in expression profiles imply that the distinct impacts of iCAF subtype on cancer progression in LUAD and LUSC might be attributable to these variations.

## Discussion
In this study, the integration of scRNA-seq datasets comprising 175 samples from LUAD and 74 samples from LUSC yielded novel insights into the heterogeneity of the TME between LUAD and LUSC, especially in cancer-associated fibroblasts. By identifying distinct features and altered pathways, highlighting the cellular sources of stromal signals, and cataloging marker genes, this comprehensive analysis enabled the identification of five robust CAF subtypes and facilitated an in-depth characterization of their molecular and functional phenotypes. This comprehensive view enhances our understanding of disease-associated fibroblasts and establishes a valuable resource for refined therapeutic strategies and patient categorization.
CAFs within the TME exert a pivotal influence on cancer progression. These cells fulfill functions that can either promote or impede tumor growth, contingent upon their specific role within the context of cancer , , . Recent advancements in single-cell transcriptomics have elucidated the heterogeneity of CAFs across diverse cancer types . Two major CAF subpopulations have been identified in various cancer types: myofibroblastic CAFs (mCAFs) and inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) , . mCAFs have been shown to play tumor-promoting and tumor-restraining roles, depending on the context of the tumor microenvironment . The myofibroblast subtype, characterized by the expression of ECM proteins and activation of TGF-β signaling, actively remodels the extracellular matrix and forms a fibrotic barrier around tumors that may impede immune cell infiltration . Conversely, the depletion of mCAFs in a targeted manner resulted in the identification of their tumor-restraining functions , , . However, the mechanisms that maintain the balance between tumor promotion and inhibition are not yet fully understood. iCAFs are defined by the expression of immunomodulatory molecules such as IL6 and CXCL12 . iCAFs are also plastic, change with tumor progression, and have also context-dependent prognostic roles, being associated with either poor or favorable outcomes depending on the type of cancer . iCAF biomarkers are enriched in immune-“hot” tumors but scarce in “cold” tumors, which may explain poor immunotherapy responses in the latter. Through interactions with T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells, iCAFs shape immune responses, yet their high cytokine output can also drive chemoresistance. Notably, iCAFs directly impair CD8⁺ T cells via chemokine–receptor interactions, leading to dysfunction and exhaustion . Despite mCAFs and iCAFs being the most extensively studied CAF subtypes, additional subtypes have also been described. Antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) are characterized by the expression of MHC class II on their surfaces, but they lack the co-stimulatory molecules that are expressed by professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and are needed to activate CD4+ T-cell signaling , . A lack of co-stimulatory molecules on APCs has been reported to induce T cell anergy and promote the development of regulatory T cells (Tregs) . Consistent with these reports, apCAFs have been shown to activate CD4⁺ T cells and drive their differentiation into Tregs, thereby directly contributing to tumor immunosuppression . However, unlike the previously reported immunosuppressive role, apCAFs in lung cancer are distinct in their ability to activate effector T cells and exert tumor-suppressive functions . Although apCAFs are rare, they have been detected across various cancer types. However, further research is required to determine their exact function, lineage, and origin. vCAFs are thought to originate from perivascular cells and are initially localized near blood vessels in early-stage tumors, later invading the tumor stroma as progression occurs . They are enriched in angiogenesis-associated genes such as COL18A1, NOTCH3, and EPAS1. The vCAF signature has been identified as an independent prognostic marker associated with an increased risk of metastatic disease . The extent to which these CAFs differ between LUAD and LUSC is not yet known, nor is it clear which CAF subtypes influence prognosis. In this study, we defined clusters highly expressing MMP11, COL1A1, ACTA2, and FAP as mCAFs and a group expressing IL6, CXCL12, CFD, and C3 as iCAFs. FAP+αSMA+ CAFs have been documented as a marker for areas with limited infiltration of T cells within tumor lesions and have been linked to high-density ECM deposition . High expression of mCAF markers was shown to be a poor prognostic factor for both LUAD and LUSC in this study. However, iCAF markers were identified as favorable prognostic factors in LUAD patients and poor prognostic factors in LUSC patients. A recent imaging mass cytometry analysis of CAF heterogeneity revealed that iCAF (CD34+/CD248+) and ifnCAF (IDO+) phenotypes were associated with longer survival of both LUAD and LUSC patients presenting with inflammatory tumor microenvironments . One possible explanation for the difference in the results between this study and the previous study is that the definitions of iCAFs were not identical. Differences in the definition of CAFs are a common problem. The nomenclature and markers used to define CAF subtypes vary from one study to another . The absence of discernible markers to differentiate between these distinct CAF subtypes impedes numerous analytical pursuits, encompassing imaging studies of these cell types, functional and lineage analyses in animal models, and the facilitation of productive comparisons among studies. Although some recent reports classify mCAFs and iCAFs in detail , , we have classified CAFs into broad categories to avoid the differences in the definitions mentioned above. In addition, since the lack of clinical information may lead to the overestimation of certain cell populations, we avoided a detailed classification. Another possible explanation is the differential expression profiles of iCAFs between LUAD and LUSC. Specifically, iCAFs in LUSC exhibited a gene expression pattern associated with a neutrophil-rich, immunosuppressive microenvironment that may suppress tumor immunity, which could account for the distinct prognostic impact of iCAFs between LUAD and LUSC.
We also found that when we cocultured cancer cell lines with fibroblasts, the adenocarcinoma cell line caused the fibroblasts to express inflammatory markers. On the other hand, the coculture of the squamous cell carcinoma cell line and fibroblasts caused the fibroblasts to express more mCAF markers. While the potential for further examination to elucidate the underlying mechanisms exists, a comprehensive analysis of cell‒cell interactions revealed a robust association between lung adenocarcinoma and cytokines such as IL1, OSM, and LIF, which could stimulate the proliferation of inflammatory fibroblasts . In contrast, lung squamous cell carcinoma may predominantly induce cancer cells and fibroblasts through the ncWNT pathway, particularly via the action of WNT5A . The variations in the interactions between these cancer cells and fibroblasts may have had an impact on the differences in the composition of CAFs. The growth characteristics of cancer may affect the composition of CAFs. The elastic modulus of solid tumors (20–30 kPa) is significantly higher than that of normal lung parenchyma (0.5–5 kPa) . LUSC tumors exhibit solid growth with no morphologic findings of alveolar space-filling characteristics within them . Additionally, the mean interstitial fluid pressure of LUSC is significantly higher than that of nonsquamous cell carcinoma of lung cancer . A solid organization has been shown to promote the induction of myofibroblasts . The stiffness of these cancer tissues may facilitate the transformation of fibroblasts into mCAFs, and collagen production by mCAFs may further increase the stiffness of the TME, forming a positive loop of mCAF differentiation. In this study, the proportion of mCAFs increased as LUAD progressed, suggesting that tissue pressure and architecture are also important for the induction of mCAFs. Other reports state that myofibroblasts or FAP+ CAFs are common in solid-type lung adenocarcinomas , . However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying mCAF induction in lung cancer by the tissue and stage remain to be fully elucidated.
Despite the efficacy of our study in characterizing fibroblast heterogeneity across LUAD and LUSC, it has several limitations. First, the dearth of comprehensive clinical data in public datasets impedes our capacity to investigate the impacts on fibroblast phenotypes and compositions. Second, while our primary focus has been on fibroblast–cancer cell interactions, future investigations must delve into fibroblast–immune cell interactions to obtain a comprehensive understanding. Third, spatial information is lost in the sampling process of scRNA-seq, and thus, an analysis that takes spatial information into account is desirable. However, our robust study provides an exhaustive investigation of fibroblast biology in both LUAD and LUSC. This investigation revealed distinct fibroblast subsets and functionally distinct multicellular modules. This comprehensive view enhances our comprehension of disease-associated fibroblasts and establishes a valuable resource for refined therapeutic strategies and patient categorization.

## Methods

## scRNA-seq data collection from public databases and data alignment
The scRNA-seq datasets were obtained from the public Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and European Genome–Phenome Archive (EGA) databases (see Supplemental Data for details). The raw sequencing data were processed with the CellRanger pipeline (version 6.1.2, 10x Genomics) and mapped to the human genome (GRCh38-2020-A) to generate matrices of gene counts by cell barcodes.

## Data quality control and normalization
scRNA-seq data analyses, including normalization, scaling, clustering of cells, and identification of cluster marker genes, were performed using the R software package Seurat (version 5.0.3) . Low-quality cells were removed from the merged dataset before batch effect removal according to the following criteria: nFeature_RNA > 200, nFeature_RNA ≤ 6000, nCount_RNA ≥ 1000, and percent.mt < 20. Log normalization was performed with the function “NormalizeData” in Seurat. The expression data were scaled using the function “ScaleData”. For data integration, “Harmony” (version 1.2.0) was applied to integrate the scRNA-seq data by sample and dataset .

## Data clustering and dimensionality reduction
We performed principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction in R with Seurat. Clustering of single cells was performed with the functions “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” in Seurat. Dimensionality-reduced cell clustering was shown in a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plot with the function “runUMAP”. Heatmaps were generated with Morpheus software from the Broad Institute (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/).

## InferCNV on scRNA-seq data
Copy number variations were inferred using the InferCNV R package (version 1.20.0) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV) to discriminate cancer cells from normal cells . For the scRNA-seq data, normal macrophages were used as a reference, and epithelial cells were used as a query. The analysis was run using cluster_by_groups set to True and analysis_mode set to subclusters. Otherwise, the default parameters were used. Genes with a mean count of 0.1 or less across cells were excluded from the 10x Genomics datasets, and those with a mean count of 1 were excluded from the Smart-Seq dataset. In addition, the hidden Markov method (HMM; “type i3”) was used, applying noise filters and the “subcluster” analysis. Given the regions with copy number alterations identified by the HMM, a Bayesian latent mixture model was employed to identify the posterior probabilities of the alteration status in each cell and the whole copy number alteration region.

## Testing for differences in the proportions of clusters between conditions
We used speckle (version 1.8.0) with the arcsin transformation to test for differences in the proportions of clusters between LUAD and LUSC.

## Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
Marker genes of the CAFs were calculated by “FindMarkers” with the MAST method. An adjusted p value < 0.05 was used for further analysis.

## Analysis of DEGs in CAFs between LUAD and LUSC
We conducted pseudobulk analyses to examine differences in the transcriptomic profiles of the same CAF group between LUAD and LUSC. The expression data were aggregated with the “AggregateExpression” function, thereby creating a pseudobulk dataset in which expression levels from the CAFs belonging to the same group and the same sample were summed. We then applied DESeq2 method to identify the DEGs in the same CAF group between LUAD and LUSC. The DEGs were defined as those with an adjusted p-value < 0.05.

## GO and gene set enrichment analyses
We performed enrichment analyses of the signature gene list from the cell clusters using the ‘ClusterProfiler’ package in R (version 4.10.0). For enrichment analyses, gene symbols were converted to ENTREZ IDs using the ‘org.Hs.eg.db’ package (Carison M, R package version 3.18.0, 2019). GO terms were defined as those with an adjusted p-value < 0.05.

## Pseudotime trajectory analysis
A pseudotime analysis was conducted using Monocle3 (version 1.3.7) , with the trajectory rooted in PI16-expressing cells. The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings and cell clusters obtained from Seurat were utilized as input for Monocle3, which performed trajectory graph learning and pseudo-time measurement through reversed graph embedding.

## Diversity analysis
We calculated the correlation coefficients to evaluate the heterogeneity in expression in the cell populations for each sample. The correlation network analysis was performed with the ‘igraph’ package. In each cluster, normalized closeness centrality was calculated in R, where r is the absolute value of Pearson’s correlation coefficient and n is the number of cells in the cluster. The correlation between cancer cells and CAFs, macrophages, and endothelial cells in the diversity analysis was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The detailed procedures were previously described , .

## Cell–cell interaction (CCI) analysis
We applied the R package CellChat (version 2.1.1) to different cell populations to predict cell–cell interactions . Briefly, ligand‒receptor interactions between cells were estimated using a manually curated database. Cell communication was inferred by identifying overexpressed secreted ligands and overexpressed receptors in different cell groups using the identifyOverExpressedGene and identifyOverExpressedInteraction functions. The probability of communication was then inferred using the computeCommunProb function. We calculated the aggregated cell‒cell communication using the aggregateNet function to represent the number of interactions, the strength of the interactions, and the ligand–receptor pairs.

## Calculation of the infiltration of specific cell types in different TME types
We characterized the infiltration of our defined CAF types from bulk RNA-seq data using the online tool CIBERSORTx to create a reference signature matrix from our collected scRNA-seq data and to estimate cell type proportions for bulk RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data. When constructing signature matrices, CIBERSORTx was run with quartile normalization disabled for RNA-seq datasets, the permutation parameter was set to 500 times, and batch correction was applied in “S-mode”. To calculate the correlation between iCAFs, neutrophils, and CD8⁺ T cells at the primary site in single-cell RNA-seq data, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was applied to samples in which each pair of cell populations (iCAFs–neutrophils or iCAFs–CD8⁺ T cells) was detected.

## CAF signature markers
We used the ‘AddModuleScore’ function of the R package Seurat to calculate the signature enrichment score for each cell cluster at the single-cell level. Pairwise comparisons were performed to assess signature gene expression across subgroups, and the resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

## Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)
Gene expression scores for all bulk RNA-sequencing cohorts were generated with the R package GSVA (version 1.52.3) using the default arguments. GSVA scores were generated for each CAF marker gene list and neutrophil-related GO terms.

## Survival analysis
The RNA-seq data from TCGA NSCLC cohorts were retrieved from the TCGA database, an open-access database that is available at http://www.cbioportal.org.

## Cell culture
MRC-5, Calu-3, and A549 were purchased from ATCC and tested to verify the absence of mycoplasma contamination when the cells were prepared. LC-1 sq and EBC-1 were kindly gifted by Dr. Toshikazu Ushijima from Hoshi University. The cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and 1x antibiotic and antimycotic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and grown at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The medium was changed every 3 days, and the cells were passaged when they reached 70–80% confluence. MRC-5 cells were used for experiments until passage 6.

## Transwell coculture
For transwell cocultures, 2.5 × 104 Calu-3 or LC-1 sq cells were seeded on top of the transwell membrane (0.4 μm pore size, Corning Life Science, Tewksbury, MA) with MRC-5 cells (5 × 104) growing in the lower compartment of a 12-well plate in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and a 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Whole RNA sequencing
RNA was isolated from each cell lysate using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). mRNA libraries were sequenced in paired-end 150 bp mode on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 by Azenta (Tokyo, Japan). The expression levels of each gene were quantified from the sequencing data by kallisto (version 0.46.0). Afterward, the data were summarized using the tximport package (version 1.30.0), and scaled TPM counts were used for further analysis.

## Immunoblot analysis
The cells were lysed using a mammalian protein extraction reagent (M-PER; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78501) and a sample buffer solution (Wako, Osaka, Japan, 198–13282). For each immunoblotting experiment, equal amounts of total protein were loaded onto 4–20% SDS‒PAGE gels (Mini‒Protean TGX Gel, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). After SDS‒PAGE, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). After being blocked with Blocking One reagent (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies (Supplementary Data ) at room temperature for 1 h. Anti-rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, 5127) and anti-mouse IgG (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA, NA931) were used as secondary antibodies at a 1:1000 dilution. Anti-goat IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA, 705-035-147) was used at a 1:10,000 dilution. The membranes were then exposed to ImmunoStar LD (Wako) using an LAS-4000 UV mini system (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

## Immunofluorescence staining
After being washed with PBS, the MRC-5 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Wako) and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Data ) in a solution containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies at a 1:1000 dilution in a solution containing 0.1% BSA. DAPI staining was performed immediately before imaging. The stained sections were observed and imaged using a BZ-X710 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

## qRT-PCR
For the qRT-PCR analysis, complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated from the total RNA using a High-Capacity cDNA RT kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Subsequently, real-time PCR was performed using the cDNA by using a THUNDERBIRD® Next SYBR™ qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Data were collected and analyzed using a StepOne Real-Time PCR system and StepOne software v2.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All mRNA quantification data obtained from the cultured cells were normalized to the expression of β-actin, which was used as a housekeeping gene. All primers used are listed in Supplementary Data .

## Drug and antibody treatments
Cells were treated with 1 ng/mL human IL1α (HZ-1320, Proteintech, Tokyo, Japan), 0.1 ng/mL human IL1β (HZ-1164, Proteintech), 10 ng/mL human LIF (HZ-1292, Proteintech), 0.1 ng/mL human OSM (HZ-1030, Proteintech), 1 μg/mL Anti-IL-1R1/CD121a (AMG 108) (A2445, Selleck Biotechnology) or IgG2SA isotype control (A3176, Selleck Biotechnology), 0.1 μM type 1 TGF-β receptor (ALK5) inhibitor (SB431542) (S1067, Selleck Biotechnology) or Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).

## Patient recruitment
Human lung tissues were obtained from patients undergoing lobectomy for local lung cancer. Informed consent was obtained from all surgical participants as part of an approved ongoing research protocol by the ethics committee of the Jikei University School of Medicine (#34-390 (11547)). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients. Approval for an opt-out consent method was provided by the Ethics Review Committee for this study. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Immunohistochemical staining
Human samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Wako) in PBS and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded samples were baked at 60 °C for 1 h before proceeding with the following steps. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated in xylene, a graded ethanol series ranging from 100% to 50%, and distilled water. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, antigen retrieval was performed by soaking the sections in ImmunoSaver (Wako) for 45 min at 98 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 in ultrapure water for 30 min. These sections were then incubated with 2.5% normal horse serum (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) for 20 min at room temperature. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Data ) overnight at 4 °C. The sections were then incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody solutions (PK-6101; Vector Laboratories) for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were stained with an ImmPACT® DAB EqV substrate kit (Vector Laboratories), followed by hematoxylin staining, dehydration, and mounting. Images were captured using a BZ-X700 microscope (Keyence Corporation) and analyzed using image analysis software.

## Statistical analysis
Welch’s t-test was used for comparisons of two datasets. The presence of the five CAF subtypes across all datasets was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. All t-tests were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p-value < 0.05.
