## Summary
Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the tumor microenvironment (TME) have garnered significant research attention in the last decade. As key stromal cells of the TME, studies have explored them as a potential target for controlling cancer. Using high-throughput technologies like single-cell RNA sequencing coupled with proteomics, the classification of different CAF subgroups reveals a complex system that varies by cancer type. Unraveling novel big data, potentially through AI platforms, will be key to identifying the role of CAFs in tumor progression and therapy escape mechanisms, enabling new therapies that manipulate CAFs to increase patients’ survival. We summarize and discuss new developments in an attempt to target CAFs for cancer control. Furthermore, we explore whether the complex heterogeneity of CAF origins can be targeted by single drugs in the future or require targeted, personalized therapies to combat cancer.

## Cancer-associated fibroblasts and their role in the TME
Curing cancer is among the most challenging endeavors of humankind in this century. A major challenge in improving cancer therapy and treatment outcomes is the complex biology of tumors and their microenvironment (tumor microenvironment [TME]), in which a heterogeneous cell group called cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) plays a critical role. , , , , The TME comprises extracellular matrix (ECM), vascular and endothelial cells, fibroblasts, pericytes, and immune cells, giving rise to a unique ecosystem that varies by tumor type. , , 
Fibroblasts are non-epithelial and non-immune cells, typically of mesenchymal origin. In the context of disease, fibroblasts are involved in tissue fibrosis and wound healing. , , The most common source of CAFs is activation of local fibroblasts by tumor-derived signals, after which they acquire functions such as ECM synthesis and organization, enhanced migration, and intercellular communication. The activation is induced by stress, transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), heat shock factor 1 (HSF1), yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1), hypoxia, notch signaling, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), interleukin 1 (IL-1), and reactive oxygen species, among other stimuli ( ). , , , 
 Evidence shows that certain CAF phenotypes are associated with poorer prognoses for cancer patients, which highlights the urgent need to develop novel therapies that target CAFs directly. , The propensity of CAFs to migrate from primary tumors into metastases further emphasizes this need. , , 
This review provides a comprehensive overview of clinical trials and associated pathways, introduces the CAF secretome as a new research focus, and summarizes CAF markers by cancer type, serving as both an atlas of the past decade of translational research and a guide for future directions.

## How CAFs interact with their surroundings

## CAF secretome
The secretome of CAFs has become an important subject of investigation, typically utilizing human primary tumors and in vitro cell cultures. While CAFs exert their protumorigenic effects through diverse mechanisms, including direct cell-cell contact, metabolic crosstalk, and ECM remodeling, their secretory activity represents a particularly dynamic mode of tumor-stroma communication. In addition to secreting soluble mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), CAFs also contribute to ECM deposition and remodeling, thereby influencing tissue architecture, stiffness, and signaling pathways that modulate tumor cell behavior. Unlike soluble factors, the ECM represents a structural component of the secretome, composed of collagens, fibronectin, and proteoglycans, whose composition and organization are actively reprogrammed in the TME. This dynamic remodeling not only facilitates cancer cell invasion and immune evasion but also confers resistance to therapy. Therapeutic strategies aimed at normalizing or disrupting the ECM, such as enzymatic degradation, integrin blockade, or inhibition of ECM-crosslinking enzymes, are being actively explored. In the present review, we focus primarily on the soluble fraction of the CAF-derived secretome to highlight its role in paracrine signaling, tumor progression, and treatment response, while acknowledging the integrated contribution of ECM components to the overall tumor-promoting CAF phenotype.
Such studies have characterized CAF-secreted cytokines, specifically ILs including IL-1, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10, and chemokine ligand (CCL/CXCL), such as CCL-2, CCL-5, and CCL-7, as well as CXCL-1, CXCL-12, and CXCL-14, which influence the infiltration of monocytes, memory T lymphocytes, and natural killer (NK) cells into the TME. , Another immunosuppressive influence can be displayed by leucine-rich repeat-containing 15 (LRRC15+) CAFs. They are capable of reducing the activity of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells by repressing interferon-gamma (IFN- ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF- ). In breast cancer, a subset of CAFs known as CAF-S1 fibroblasts secretes CXCL12, which attracts CD4+CD25+ T cells and contributes to an immunosuppressive environment. These CAF-S1 fibroblasts promote the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and suppress T effector cell proliferation. 
Furthermore, CAFs influence angiogenesis through secretion of pro-angiogenic factors like VEGF-A and VEGF/VEGFR, as modulated by proteins including hypoxia-induced angiogenesis regulator protein, or transform into a VEGF-A-secreting phenotype. , Due to these and other functions, CAFs can influence drug resistance and act as key communicators within the TME through recently discovered CAF-specific exosomes and their molecular signatures.

## Proteomics reveals CAF secretome, which influences drug resistance
The advancement of proteomic analysis tools has been a major achievement in studying the secretome of various cell types, including CAFs. Recent translational studies have begun to address the critical question which components of the CAF secretome contribute to drug resistance?
In a secretome analysis comparing CAFs and KRAS mutant cancer cell lines, 2,573 secreted proteins were identified via multiplexed mass spectrometry in lung, pancreatic, and colorectal tissue. Employing prediction software SignalP, SecretomeP, and TMHMM, proteins were cross-referenced to databases including Cell Surface Protein Atlas, FANTOM5, Surfaceome, and Vesiclepedia to distinguish secreted vs. transmembrane proteins. Syndecan 2, follistatin-like 1, and well-known collagens COL28A1 and COL1A1 were identified as having the highest fold differences, consistent with prior evidence of their genetic upregulation in CAFs. Additionally, Wnt family protein member 5B was distinctly enriched in the CAF secretome. A functional drug screen analysis using 97 anticancer drugs revealed that CAF-conditioned media (CM) conferred more frequent drug resistance, especially to methotrexate, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC H1792) cell lines. Sensitivity to the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitor erdafitinib was also observed. Overall, however, no single agent generated a detectable response universally across cell lines. These findings support the notion that personalized therapies may represent a promising direction for future cancer treatment.
In a different study, secretome and gene expression analyses were conducted in co-cultures of CAFs and normal lung-associated fibroblasts after unexpected drug sensitivity was observed. Abundant insulin-like growth factors (IGFs) and IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs) play opposing roles to promote or inhibit signaling by the receptor IGF1R and the focal adhesion kinase (FAK). After treatment with a recombinant IGFBP, drug sensitization was observed in gefitinib-resistant EGFR-mutant PC9GR lung cancer cells. In contrast, a decrease in drug sensitivity was observed following treatment with recombinant IGFs. Using phosphoproteomics and receptor tyrosine kinase array analyses, the EGFR inhibitor osimertinib was found to inhibit IGF1R and FAK signaling in PC9GR cells exposed to CAF-CM. By combining small-molecule inhibition of IGFR1 and FAK, the CAF-mediated effects in culture could be phenocopied, and sensitization to osimertinib was observed in mice, with an increase in antitumor effects. This study underscores the variable role of CAFs in supporting and restraining tumor growth, providing compelling rationale for combination treatment strategies.
In a separate investigation, proteome analyses revealed that the G-protein-coupled somatostatin receptor sst1 is highly expressed in pancreatic cancer CAFs. By blocking this receptor with an agonist called SOM230, CAFs and protumoral and immunoregulatory factors can be antagonized both in vitro and in vivo. A reduction in colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1), a macrophage growth factor, was observed in the plasma and tumors of pancreatic adenocarcinoma-harboring mice. In parallel, the intra-tumoral stroma appeared normalized with fewer metastases also observed. This study demonstrates the capacity of SOM230 to disrupt the production of CSF-1, and thus crosstalk to macrophages, which is associated with more aggressive forms of pancreatic cancer. Consequently, this provides further evidence for the importance of regulating immune cell infiltration into the TME.
In human mammary CAFs, a secretome analysis discovered an abundance of the chloride intracellular channel protein 3 (CLIC3). Secretion of CLIC3 supports the invasive behavior of endothelial cells, which induces angiogenesis and cancer invasiveness both in vivo and in 3D cell culture models. CLIC3, also commonly expressed in ovarian cancer tumors, was found to be associated with poor clinical outcomes, suggesting its clinical relevance and potential prognostic value.
In conclusion, we observe that across multiple cancer types, various secretome-derived factors come into play, offering novel therapeutic targets to help overcome drug resistance.

## CAF-derived exosomes as a novel and informative component of the secretome
Currently, CAF-derived exosomes are an active area of TME research. The fast-growing interest is due to the recent appreciation for the role of exosomes in mediating therapy response and tumorigenesis. , , , , 
For example, in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), TME CAF-derived EVs induce lung cancer progression. 
In vitro investigations showed that IPF lung fibroblast-derived EVs exhibit markedly altered microRNA (miRNA) compositions (mainly miR-19a), which exert pro-proliferative functions on NSCLC cells. Further downstream, ZMYND11-mediated c-Myc activation is caused by EVs and may contribute to the poor prognosis of NSCLC patients. 
Through novel, in-depth secretome analysis, intercellular communication pathways in the TME were observed between endothelial cells from blood vessels and CAFs in breast cancer patient samples. EVs from CAFs were shown to transfer the transmembrane glycoprotein THY1 to endothelial cells, which was confirmed via mass spectrometry-based proteomics. The uptake of THY1 resulted in increased monocyte to endothelial cell adhesion and influenced interaction with immune cells. 
Next, a new study investigated human pancreatic cancer tissues (n = 165) and revealed that EVs derived from tumor cells and TME cells promote increased Schwann cell (SC) migration. In particular, pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) have demonstrated the ability to selectively package EV cargo that significantly influences SC migration. Upon analyzing the EV cargo, the authors identified an enrichment of p75NTR in EVs derived from cancer cells, which they proposed as a potential biomarker. However, the study did not separately investigate the EV signature derived specifically from PSCs.
Other studies have focused on EV cargo in the form of miRNAs. These miRNAs have been summarized in recent literature, highlighting several, including miR-10a-5p, miR-21, miR-22, miR-106b, and miR-125b, that may modulate signaling pathways within the TME to promote the growth and dissemination of pancreatic cancer cells. 
A study that investigates multiple myeloma CAF-derived exosomes found microRNA-21 to promote angiogenesis by increasing proliferation, migration, and tubulogenesis. The microRNA-21 is abundant in CAF-derived exosomes and could be detected after uptake in multiple myeloma endothelial cells in co-culture. Furthermore, the miRNA increased the expression of α-SMA in normal fibroblasts when transfected.
These studies exemplify how modern proteomic tools open new avenues for the molecular investigation of CAF-tumor cell interactions that shape the TME and tumor growth and how these insights can be leveraged to improve patient outcomes.

## Cancer-specific roles of CAFs
To date, CAFs have been detected and interrogated in nearly all organs in the human body that develop malignancies. Nonetheless, the extent of studies and their findings vary tremendously based on the cancer type ( ). In some cancers such as breast and pancreatic, CAFs have been broadly studied and characterized; however, one commonly accepted nomenclature for all cancers to identify individual subtypes and their functions is lacking.
 The most widely used nomenclature classifies CAFs into three main subgroups: myofibroblast-like CAFs (myCAFs), immunomodulatory CAFs (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) ( ).
A critical limitation of these classifications is that they do not clearly distinguish between tumor-promoting and tumor-restraining CAF populations.
Other recent single-cell and spatial multi-omics investigations have introduced powerful strategies to characterize the spatial organization and classification of the TME. In one such study, CAFs were classified into four subgroups—s1-CAF to s4-CAF—which were extensively analyzed across multiple cancer types, including lung, breast, colon, prostate, ovarian, liver, and melanoma. s1- and s2-CAFs were predominantly located adjacent to tumor and stromal cells and were associated with stroma-related interactional functions. In contrast, s3- and s4-CAFs were primarily localized to regions involved in immune modulation and around immune cells. A key limitation of this study is the exclusion of several well-established CAF markers, such as ACTA2, FAP, PDPN, CD74, and human leukocyte antigen class II molecules.
In other cancers like gastric cancer (GC), CAF subtype investigations are still in their infancy. Thus, efforts to develop novel CAF-targeting therapies must first close existing gaps in our understanding of their basic biology and function in cancer-specific contexts and establish systematic use of terminology across the field. Toward this end, organ-specific CAF findings are summarized in the following sections focusing on tumor-promoting vs. tumor-restraining functions. An overview of the commonly used CAF classifications and their organ-specific markers is provided in . These sections provide the foundation for the most important translational aspect: the specific targeting of CAFs.

## Skin cancers
Starting in melanoma, CAFs have garnered interest due to their involvement in tumor-promoting inflammation. They can induce a tumor-promoting phenotype through myeloid cells via cyclooxygenase/indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase. Furthermore, they can secrete IL-6, IL-11, and LIF, which mechanistically suppress CD8+ T cells through the recruitment of Tregs and myeloid-derived-suppressor cells (MDSCs). A new study in melanoma investigates CAF expression of the matricellular protein CCN1, which is highly proangiogenic and coordinates neovascularization. Calponin 1 (CNN1) can also influence stroma deposition in melanoma metastases and was found to be associated with therapeutic resistance to programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1) checkpoint inhibitors as well as disease progression. Another study discovered the influence of CAFs on macrophages and CD8+ T cells, which were mediated by activin-A polarization, resulting in immune suppression and therapy resistance. 
Additionally, CAFs contribute to immunosuppression in melanoma through chromatin regulation within the tumor stroma, as demonstrated in a study using a macroH2A-deficient mouse model. Depletion of functional CD8+ T cells and an aggregation of immunosuppressive monocytes compromised anti-tumor immunity to facilitate tumor growth. The investigated CAFs displayed a hyperinducible expression of inflammatory genes.
These studies highlight that CAFs exert multiple tumor-promoting effects in skin cancers, particularly through immunomodulatory mechanisms. However, as these findings are currently limited to preclinical models, this must be carefully considered when evaluating clinical targeting strategies.

## Head and neck cancers
In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), CAFs were found to modulate the immunoregulatory milieu by reducing the TGF-β-dependent PD-1+TIM-3+ exhaustion of CD8+ T cells and increasing the cytolytic profile of T cells. Following treatment with nivolumab, a significant change in CAF abundance was detected in patient tumors, suggesting their utility as biomarkers for therapeutic response.
Another study applying spatial and single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed CAF subsets in HNSCC that restrict infiltration and antitumor activity of CD8+ T cells. Specifically, INF- induced MHC-I; Galectin9+ CAFs were found to ensnare and inhibit the function of cytotoxic T cells.
Both studies further underscore the immunomodulatory functions of CAFs as key contributors to tumor promotion.

## Glioblastoma
In glioblastoma (GBM), a spatial transcriptome analyses revealed co-location of CAFs with mesenchymal glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs), endothelial cells, and M2 macrophages in clinical samples from 16 GBM patients. After mapping the expression of receptors to their cognate ligands, TGF-β and PDGF were detected as mediators that activate CAFs, subsequently enriching GSCs. Moreover, CAFs cause an M2 macrophage polarization via production of the extra domain A fibronectin variant, which binds macrophage TLR4. Through supplementation of GSC-derived xenografts with CAFs, tumor growth was enhanced in vivo, underscoring the influence of CAFs in this devastating disease.
Other studies found that even low CAF abundance can influence tumor grades, poor clinical outcomes, and TME remodeling in GBM and revealed fibronectin 1 as a potential regulator of CAF function in GBM.
Overall, our understanding of CAFs in GBM remains limited. Only a few mechanistic studies have been conducted, and these have not yet translated into clinical targeting strategies.

## Breast cancer
The history of CAF research in breast cancers is long and well storied. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), CAFs can influence inflammation, immune trafficking, chemotaxis, and complement. A study applying single-cell analysis in breast cancer identified eight CAF subclusters, among which the FAP+/CAF-S1 subset was shown to influence immunosuppression. The authors identified additional distinct myCAF clusters that may contribute to primary resistance to immunotherapy. This detailed study serves as a strong example that specific myCAF populations can also be tumor promoting by facilitating therapy resistance. The main finding is a positive feedback loop between CAF-S1 clusters and Tregs, which contributes to immunotherapy resistance. 
Next it was shown that CAF composition in breast cancer changes over time, and podoplanin+ CAFs were detected to influence inflammation via CXCL1 and IL-6, which is correlated to impact clinical outcome. 
The ability of CAFs to migrate into metastatic lymph nodes has been demonstrated in breast cancer patients, where CAFs were classified into four subclusters (CAF-S1 to CAF-S4). Functionally, CAF-S1 cells promoted epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through CXCL12 and TGF-β signaling pathways. In contrast, CAF-S4 cells enhanced cancer cell invasion via NOTCH signaling. These findings highlight the functional versatility of CAFs.
The Notch ligand Dll1 was also recently discovered to influence the crosstalk between CAFs and tumor cells, contributing to radioresistance in breast cancer. These CAFs, which also co-express -SMA and FAP, could be targeted by pharmacologically blocking Dll1-mediated Notch signaling, which increased radiosensitivity.
Also in breast cancer, CD26 is involved in CAF activation, as is the IL-6/STAT3/AUF1 signaling pathway, which influences activation of stromal fibroblasts through the downregulation of decorin (DCN), promoting their carcinogenic effects. 
Taken together, these investigations highlight the heterogeneity of CAFs in breast cancer and support ongoing efforts to identify effective therapeutic targets.

## Lung cancer
In lung cancer, the important role of CAFs is well established. In a study analyzing 1,070 patient tissue samples, the authors identified prognostic patient groups based on 11 distinct CAF phenotypes. Tumor-like CAFs were associated with a worse patient prognosis. Interestingly, in this study, inflammatory CAFs and “IFN response” CAFs were related to high patient survival. In contrast, a high prevalence of matrix CAFs was linked to reduced immune infiltration and potentially decreased patient survival. 
Another single-cell RNA sequencing study coupled with multiplex imaging confirmed CAF subtypes that serve to obstruct T cells from entering lung cancer tumor cell nests. In early-stage tumors, they present as MYH11+/ -SMA+ CAFs, forming a single-cell-thickness barrier around tumor cell aggregates. In more advanced stages of lung cancer, they are FAP+/ -SMA+ CAFs, forming multiple layers around tumor nests.
In NSCLC, recent evidence suggests that CD248-expressing CAFs secrete IL-8, which contributes to cisplatin resistance. The inflammatory response in NSCLC also proceeds via the FAP/IL-6 axis, which promotes tumor growth and metastases via circNOX4. Additionally, GPX8+ CAFs promote an immunosuppressive TME with cancer-promoting effects. 
These studies suggest that iCAFs in lung cancer predominantly exhibit tumor-promoting functions.
Similarly, myofibroblast-like POSTN+ CAFs are correlated with immune suppression and tumor progression in NSCLC. Expression of PDL-1 in CAFs, induced through chemotherapy, promotes general chemoresistance in NSCLC. Mechanistically hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) secretion is observed after upregulation of PDL-1 CAFs, which stimulates cancer progression, cell invasion, and stemness of lung cancer cells.
The complex role of CAFs in driving immune escape and therapy resistance in lung cancer highlights the need to establish them as strategic therapeutic targets.

## Gastric cancer
The role of CAFs in GCs is still relatively unknown; however, their function in tumorigenesis, angiogenesis, immunosuppression, and metastasis has garnered recent interest. 
CNN1 is a newly identified marker for gastric CAFs that is correlated with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) resistance and poor clinical outcome in patients. CNN1 knockdown reduces matrix stiffness and increases 5-FU treatment efficacy in mouse models of GC employing single-cell analyses in GC patient tissues.
Next INHBA, a member of the TGF-β superfamily, was shown to be the dominant co-expressed marker with FAP in GC CAFs and is predictive of poor prognosis. 
GCs also express inflammatory CAFs and ECM CAFs, which influence M2 macrophages through periostin (POSTN). These CAF subtypes are highly invasive and thus correlated with decreased survival in patients.
A recent study investigates the axon guidance protein SLIT2, which is produced by CAFs and binds to the roundabout guidance receptor 1 (ROBO1). It further interacts with the serine/threonine kinase NEK9, promoting GC metastasis. Mechanistically SLIT2 could level up the phosphorylation and expression of CTT that initiates the reorganization of the cytoskeleton. These findings of high NEK9 and CTT expression were confirmed in metastatic GC lesions of patients via multiplex immunohistochemistry. High levels of NEK9 and CTT were aligned with a reduced overall survival of patients. That is why NEK9 and CTT were suggested as potential biomarkers.
Another analysis revealed an inverse correlation between CAF abundance and NK cells in human GCs. CAFs induce the cell death process ferroptosis in NK cells, which leads to an accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxidases/iron overload in the NK cells. In patient-derived organoid models that target CAFs by combining deferoxamine and follistatin-like protein 1, CAF-induced NK cell ferroptosis was decreased and NK cell cytotoxicity against GC cells was increased.
These highly valuable mechanistic studies underscore the importance of further investigating the role of CAFs in GC.

## Pancreatic cancer
CAFs have been studied extensively in pancreatic cancer and most frequently in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) ( ). As such, a standard nomenclature system now exists, which categorizes pancreatic CAFs into three main subtypes: myofibroblast-like CAFs (myCAFs), immunomodulatory CAFs (iCAFs), and apCAFs ( ). , , Additionally, myCAFs and iCAFs have not only unique functional features but also the ability to transition and overlap between subtypes and functions. 
While individual CAF subtypes are associated with a poorer prognosis or immune escape, CAF heterogeneity in PDAC, collectively, contributes to mechanistic complexity. For example, when -SMA+ CAFs are depleted, it leads to decreased survival in mice, whereas the depletion of FAP+ CAFs results in increased survival, in a manner relative to Treg accumulation. , Similarly, deletion of IL-6 from -SMA+ CAFs, but not from FAP+ CAFs, improves the efficacy of gemcitabine treatment. CAFs can polarize naive M1 macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype; however, in the context of treatment with FOLFIRINOX, CAFs limit the chemotherapy-induced cell death of macrophages. These phenomena illustrate the role of CAFs as both tumor promoting and restraining.
myCAFs can be induced by TGF-β through an autocrine process mediated by amphiregulin, which is initiated by the epidermal growth factor receptor/Erb2 receptor. 
In lineage tracing studies, PSCs were detected as a source of CAF subtypes that influence the production of ECM components and tissue stiffness. 
Separately, meflin is a marker of PSCs, which are also involved in fibrosis and EMT, as shown by their ability to store lipid droplets and later accelerate fibrosis. 
Through hypoxia, which is a characteristic in PDAC tumors, iCAFs can evolve, which is further stimulated by cell-derived cytokines. CAFs exhibit a certain inflammatory gene expression signature that is related to hypoxia-inducible factor-1-alpha (HIF-1 ). In PDAC organoid cocultures, the stabilization of HIF-1 induced the phenotype of iCAFs in stromal cells, promoting tumor growth. This study suggests that iCAFs in PDAC predominantly exhibit tumor-promoting characteristics.
Novel approaches have utilized human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived stromal cells to create pancreatic cancer organoids with heterogeneous CAFs for anticancer drug screenings. 
Another recent study applied machine learning approaches to integrate data from human PDAC single-cell analyses and organoid co-culture experiments, which identified fibroblast-mediated regulation of EMT and inflammation. Specifically, integrin beta 1 and VEGF-A interact with neuropilin-1 (NRP1) to mediate the CAF-epithelial crosstalk.
In PDAC, myCAFs and iCAFs have been the main focus so far, but despite extensive studies and numerous described markers, a clearer classification—especially distinguishing tumor-promoting from tumor-restraining CAFs—is still needed to advance therapeutic development.

## Cholangiocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma
In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), the central role of CAFs in remodeling ECM, cancer stemness, immune modulation, and therapeutic resistance is also well established.
In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) it was discovered using genetic tracing, single-cell RNA sequencing, and ligand receptor analysis that hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) provide the main source for CAFs. 
In vivo studies investigating the depletion of HSC-derived CAFs confirmed that these cells accelerate ICC progression. When pan-CAF gene signatures were applied to patient survival data, high signature scores were associated with reduced overall survival. Further analysis revealed a separation into myCAFs and iCAFs. Mechanistically, myCAFs expressed hyaluronan synthase 2, while iCAFs expressed HGF, both contributing to ICC growth. 
Additionally, another new study in CCA patients (n = 209) investigated the influence of iCAF-derived lysyl oxidase (LOX), which increases oxidative phosphorylation and stemness in the TME. Furthermore, iCAF-derived LOX can influence the mitochondrial function through transcription factor A. Thus, the fitness of cancer cells is supported and a decrease in patient survival is observed. These results once again identify iCAFs as tumor supporting.
In advanced HCC, spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed that resistance to neoadjuvant immunotherapy may be driven by a lack of CAF-mediated pro-inflammatory signaling. In tumors responding to cabozantinib and nivolumab, CAFs exhibited high activation of transcription factors FOS and JUN. Moreover, interactions between HCC cells and CAFs were enriched in these responding tumors, accompanied by increased matrix remodeling.
A study investigating CAFs in HCC patients found that CAF-derived signal peptide-CUB-EGF domain-containing protein 1 (SCUBE1) promotes cancer stemness and is associated with poor prognosis. SCUBE1 expression was linked to activation of the sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathway. Inhibition of this pathway using cyclopamine reduced SCUBE1-induced signaling, suggesting SCUBE1 as a potential target for novel therapeutic strategies.
Taken together in CCA and HCC, CAFs exhibit opposing tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting roles, underscoring their potential as therapeutic targets.

## Colorectal cancer
In colorectal cancer (CRC), the role of CAFs in tumorigenesis and progression is an active area of investigation. 
Lineage tracing studies in colon cancer show that ACTA2+ CAFs emerge through proliferation from intestinal pericryptal leptin receptor cells (Lepr). The Lepr+ CAFs, induced by TGF-β, express melanoma cell adhesion molecule (MCAM). MCAM+ CAFs contribute to a tumor-promoting immune TME, with high expression levels being inversely associated with patient survival.
In rectal cancer, iCAFs were found to mediate resistance to neoadjuvant therapy. IL-1a polarizes CAFs to become iCAFs and triggers oxidative DNA damage and p53-mediated therapy-induced senescence, which contributes to chemoradiotherapy resistance and disease progression.
Single-cell investigations in CRC show that IL-1R1+ CAFs also drive tumor development and immunosuppression alongside immune exhaustion, with a reduction in Th17 infiltration specifically observed. Thus, the administration of IL-1 antagonist anakinra in this context may reduce tumor growth.
Other studies demonstrate that neoadjuvant chemotherapy can influence CAFs to suppress rectal cancer by activating immunity crosstalk via secreted cytokines, including CXCL12, SLIT2, and DCN. 
Together, these four studies further support the notion that iCAFs in CRC may exhibit tumor-promoting functions.
Meanwhile, the upregulation of FAP+ CAFs is associated with poor clinical outcomes, too. In early-stage CRC, CAFs promote cancer cell invasion via direct cell-cell interactions with epithelial CRC cells and matrix remodeling. In later stages of CRC, cathepsin H is highly upregulated.
In recent years, new patient-derived organoid fibroblast models have been developed, which mimic the tumor cell heterogeneity in CRC, enabling studies to optimize treatment efficacy. 

## Ovarian cancer
Ovarian cancer, which remains one of the deadliest malignancies, has also been investigated with regard to the role of CAFs in its progression and therapeutic response. 
Different CAF subtypes in ovarian cancer were shown to play essential roles in regulating tumor progression through downstream mediators and pathways including IL-6, TGF-β, nuclear factor-κB, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and AKT/mTOR, among others. In ovarian cancer, CAFs have been subtyped and categorized as inflammatory CAFs, vascular CAFs (vCAFs), and matrix CAFs, and highly expressed subtype markers STAR, TSPAN8, and ALDH1A1 are enriched following chemotherapy treatment in ovarian cancer patients.
Other human ovarian cancer single-cell analyses revealed that TGF-β-induced reactive stroma is defined by multiple collagen markers, periostin, fibronectin, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which influence the tumor cell-T cell crosstalk via CXCL16-CXCR6 and stromal-immune cell crosstalk via CXCL12/14-CXCR4. 
CAFs in ovarian cancer can also influence complement regulation and chemotaxis. 
Recent studies found that the stromal discoidin domain receptor 2 (DDR2) drives ovarian cancer metastasis by regulating the metabolism and secretion of ECM proteins. DDR2 can also regulate an arginase activity in ovarian CAFs and promote collagen production, contributing to tumor progression. 
Moreover, the expression of the TGF-β-related LRRC superfamily in CAFs promotes an immunosuppressive TME, is correlated with clinical prognosis, and is predictive of platinum resistance of ovarian cancer patients. 
Once again, iCAFs emerge as key contributors to tumor-promoting conditions.

## Prostate cancer
Compared to other cancers, the function of CAFs in prostate cancer is understudied. However, recent studies have identified CAF-derived gene signatures predictive of biochemical recurrence-free survival and metastasis-free survival in patients receiving radiation therapy for prostate cancer, which found ECM- and immune-related genes to be specifically enriched. 
Separately, single-cell and bulk transcriptome analyses were conducted to identify prostate CAF subtypes and related prognostic indicators. As in many other disease sites, prostate myCAFs marked by -SMA and FAP play a dominant role. 
Metabolomic analyses show that CAFs in prostate cancer have the ability to secrete glucosamine, which alters androgen biosynthesis via upregulation of the 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-1. This process is mediated via the transcription factor Elk1, which can be suppressed in cancer cells, thus suppressing CAF-induced androgen biosynthesis in vivo. These preclinical findings point to the value of CAF-related therapeutic strategies in prostate cancer.
Further studies are critically needed, particularly those aimed at classifying CAFs into tumor-promoting and tumor-restraining subtypes.

## Bladder cancer
In bladder cancer, the role of CAFs remains poorly understood. , Recent investigations have identified myCAF marker gene signatures for immune infiltration and therapeutic response, which have prognostic utility. 
A risk signature involving COL6A1 and MAP1B genes demonstrates exceptional prognostic value, whereas MAP1B specifically correlates with immunotherapy efficacy.
Other studies have identified TGF-β as the predominant mediator of EMT in bladder cancer via the FAP/VCAN axis. The clinical relevance was shown in a study that discovered a signature of TGF-β CAFs that was associated with exclusion of CD8+ T cells from urothelial tumors. These patients exhibited resistance to PD-1 blockade. A therapeutic approach combined treatment with a TGF-β inhibitor and anti-PD-L1 therapy, which reduced the population of TGF-β-rich stromal CAFs and led to tumor decrease.
A study using single-cell analyses found that IFN-dependent SLC14A1+ is shown to promote cancer stemness via WNT5A. By targeting STING, SLC14A1+ CAFs are inhibited, sensitizing tumors to chemotherapy.
In bladder cancer, incorporating CAFs into therapeutic strategies holds promise, but further research is needed.

## Therapeutic opportunities and challenges
The primary challenge in targeting CAFs is related to their opposing tumor restraining- and tumor-promoting functions. , Tremendous growth in understanding the different mechanisms of crosstalk between tumor cells, immune cells, and CAFs has led to many clinical trials in recent years (see ). Trials have examined therapeutic mechanisms ranging from inhibiting CAF activation to reprogramming CAFs into a quiescent state. We summarize the therapeutic strategies and relevant clinical trials, by mechanism of action, in the following sections ( ; ).
 

## Targeting the inhibition of CAF activation and CAF-related pathways

## TGF-β pathway
The history of TGF-β in tumorigenesis and its influence on CAFs is long and well described. , In invasive lung adenocarcinoma, CAFs contribute to tumor heterogeneity through paracrine TGF-β mediation, which induces the remodeling of tumor tissues. Malignant cell solid-to-acinar transition is specifically provoked by -SMA+ CAFs, which gives rise to specific histological patterns. In PDAC, TGF-β is the primary driver of myCAFs. Through an autocrine process mediated by TGF-β and amphiregulin, myCAFs can induce epidermal growth factor receptor/Erb-B2 (EGFR/ERBB2) signaling. In mice, EGFR-activated myCAFs promote metastasis in PDAC, which underscores the importance of therapeutically targeting this pathway. A recent preclinical study investigated the combination of a TGF-β inhibitor with docetaxel micelles, a cytotoxic agent, which synergistically inhibited myCAF proliferation and increased p53 protein expression. Following treatment, a substantial reduction in collagenous fibers—primarily deposited by α-SMA+ and FAP+ myCAFs—was observed, leading to microvessel normalization and restoration of the TME. The authors concluded that depletion of myCAFs enhanced the intratumoral penetration of docetaxel-loaded micelles in vivo.
In a GC study, TGF-β1 was observed to increase the motility and invasiveness of CAFs through expression of rhomboid 5 homolog 2 (RHBDF2). A knockdown of RHBDF2 could reduce the motility.
Resistance to PD-L1 immunotherapy in patients with metastatic urothelial cancer correlates with TGF-β signatures in fibroblasts, specifically in tumors containing evidence of T cell exclusion. When combining a TGF-β inhibitor with PD-L1 antibodies in mice, a reduction of TGF-β signaling in stromal cells was observed, with enhanced T cell entry into tumors.
However, translation of these preclinical models into clinical trials remains challenging , and highlights the need for further characterization of this convoluted pathway and its interplay within the TME.
Multiple trials have investigated the TGF-β1 inhibitor galunisertib (LY2157299) (NCT01373164, NCT02734160, NCT01246986, NCT02423343, NCT02672475, NCT01682187, NCT02688712, NCT05700656, NCT04952753, NCT03724851, NCT02937272, NCT02452008, and NCT02947165) ( ). In advanced HCC, treatment with galunisertib lowered AFP levels, prolonged survival, and was found to be reasonably safe and well tolerated , (NCT01246986). In patients with advanced pancreatic cancer, treatment with galunisertib in combination with gemcitabine improved overall survival and revealed a biomarker subset of four proteins (IP-10, FSH, MIP-1a, PAI-1) , with predictive value (NCT01373164). Locally advanced rectal carcinoma patients also benefitted from the addition of galunisertib to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (response rate 32%) (NCT02688712), although further assessment in larger cohorts is warranted. In patients with NSCLC, galunisertib administration in combination with PD-1 therapy was well tolerated and met primary efficacy outcomes (NCT02423343). A clinical trial combining galunisertib with enzalutamide (a nonsteroid antiandrogen) in patients with prostate cancer remains ongoing (NCT02452008). The foundational preclinical studies utilizing in vivo models demonstrated increased apoptosis and decreased tumor burden, showing exceptional promise.
Other inhibitors under investigation include NIS793, vactosertib (TEW-7197), and LY3200882 (NCT02947165, NCT04952753, NCT0372485, and NCT02937272) ( ). In a phase 1/1b multicenter trial investigating the effect of a combination of the TGF-β antibody NIS793 and the checkpoint inhibitor spartalizumab in patients with NSCLC and microsatellite stable CRC (MSS-CRC), treatment successfully inhibited the TGF-β signaling pathway (NCT02947165). A follow-up phase 2 trial in patients with metastatic CRC has recently been completed (NCT04952753). Another TGF-β inhibitor, vactosertib (TEW-7197), showed anti-tumor activity and prolongs overall survival in patients with MSS-CRC (NCT03724851). These clinical trials raise hopes that TGF-β inhibitors may soon achieve approval for standard clinical use in certain cancers.

## FAK pathway
The influence of CAFs on FAK-regulated pathways is evidenced in malignant cell metabolism and may be exploited for therapeutic purposes. In breast and pancreatic cancer patients, low FAK expression in the TME is predictive of reduced overall survival. A functional mechanism was identified using a model of FAK depletion in CAFs, which led to an increased chemokine production through CCR1/CCR2 on cancer cells and activated protein kinase A, promoting glycolysis in malignant cells.
In other cancers, EMT mediated by CAF-derived LOX promotes tumor invasion through activation of the FAK phosphorylation pathway. 
In lung tumors, FAK inhibition, and thus the inhibition of CAF activation, remodeled the TME from an “immune cold”/low CD8+ T cell-containing state to a TME with increased CD8+ T cell-, dendritic cell (DC)-, and M1-like macrophages with low amounts of infiltrated collagen. In combination with a PD-1 agonist, FAK inhibition decreased primary tumor size and metastatic growth in lung cancers. This study suggests that combinatorial therapies targeting CAFs alongside other agents may hold significant therapeutic potential.
In PDAC in vitro and in vivo studies, FAK inhibition led to hyperactivation of the RAS/MAPK pathway. Through combined inhibition of FAK and RAF-MEK, tumor growth was reduced and survival was increased. Mechanistically, CAFs were reprogrammed and stopped the production of FGF1, which is a known resistance driver to RAF-MEK inhibitors. Treatment with chemotherapy in combination with FAK- and RAK-MEK inhibitors yields improved survival and a reduction of liver metastases in mouse models of PDAC.
On the basis of these findings, numerous clinical trials have been initiated (NCT01778803, NCT01951690, NCT02546531, NCT03875820, NCT04620330, NCT03727880, NCT05669482, NCT03287271, NCT05798507, NCT05074810, NCT06394804, NCT05512208, NCT06487221, NCT06630260, NCT06072781, NCT02428270, and NCT01335269) ( ).
In a phase 2 study of previously treated advanced KRAS-mutant NSCLC, the FAK inhibitor defactinib (VS6063) was given as monotherapy to 55 patients and demonstrated modest clinical activity with good tolerance (NCT01951690). Other trials combining defactinib with the RAF/MEK clamp VS-6766 in BRAF mutant NSCLC are ongoing (NCT04620330). FAK antibody GSK2256098 in combination with the MEK 1/2 inhibitor trametinib, showed no effect in advanced PDAC patients (NCT02428270). In contrast, FAK inhibitor defactinib, combined with the PD-1 blocker pembrolizumab as a neoadjuvant therapy (NCT03727880), followed by chemotherapy with nab-paclitaxel and gemcitabine for patients with resectable PDAC shows promising results. , Preliminary results indicate that lower FAP+ fibroblast density is significantly associated with higher CD8+ T cell infiltration in the combination therapy treatment arm, as confirmed by multiplex immunohistochemistry. Although all patients on trial were found to have elevated levels of CXCR4+ cells, an increase in M1 macrophage density was additionally observed. In advanced diffuse GC, an active, recruiting trial will test defacitinib in combination with avutometinib (NCT06487221). 
In BRAF V600E melanoma, the combination of a FAK inhibitor plus avutometinib (RAF-MEK clamp) overcomes resistance to targeted and immune therapies through synergistic effects. Mechanistically, a feedback loop is activated when MAPK-RhoE (RND3) is inhibited, which results in the adaptive activation of RhoA-FAK-AKT. When combining both antibodies, potent pro-apoptotic activity is observed in both mouse models and melanoma patients. 
Clinical trials combining stromal targeting with established inhibitors, such as RAF-MEK inhibitors, offer hope for more effective treatments and underscore the need to integrate stromal-targeting strategies into therapy.

## LIF pathway
In pancreatic cancer, activated PSCs can secret leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), thereby activating signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) in malignant cells. , Only few multicenter phase 1 and 2 trials have investigated treatment with LIF-targeting antibodies (MSC-1 and AZD0171) in advanced solid tumors and locally advanced or metastatic tumors ( ) (NCT03490669, NCT04999969). However, the results of the phase 2 trial (n = 126) have not yet been published.

## CXCR4 inhibitor
In many solid tumors, elevated C-X-C receptor 4 levels correlate with an increase in distant metastases, and thus unfavorable outcomes and poor prognosis. In human primary tumors and metastases, CXCR4 was detected to be highly expressed. In preclinical mouse models of breast cancer, blocking CXCR4 was found to decrease fibroblast-promoted fibrosis, reduce desmoplasia, decompress blood vessels, and increase cytotoxic T lymphocytes. In this study, the investigators specifically depleted CXCR4 in α-SMA+ CAFs, which led to a 2-fold increase in response to immune checkpoint blockade therapy. This provides further evidence supporting the use of combinatorial strategies, targeting CAFs to enhance the efficacy of other treatments, such as immunotherapy.
A clinical phase 1 trial in HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer patients investigated CXCR4 antibody balixafortide in combination with eribulin chemotherapy (NCT01837095), with promising results. The phase 3 trial for this treatment regimen in metastatic breast cancer patients was terminated (NCT03786094). This underscores the difficulties in translating preclinical results to humans with similar efficacy.
In pancreatic cancer, a different CXCR4 antagonist (motixafortide [BL-8040]) was tested in combination with PD-1 blocker pembrolizumab in a phase 2 trial (NCT02826486) where an increase of CD8+ effector T cells into tumors, a decrease in myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and a decrease in Tregs was observed. These findings suggest that combination therapy involving CXCR4 and PD-1 blockade may augment the benefits of chemotherapy in PDAC patients. 
Additional clinical trials are crucial.

## CXCL12 neutralization
The chemokine CXCL12 interacts with its receptor CXCR4, forming a signaling axis that orchestrates cancer progression by modulating the TME.
In breast cancer, CAFs promote an immunosuppressive TME through the secretion of CXCL12, which recruits CD4+/CD25+ T cells and induces their differentiation into Tregs. This immunomodulatory function is primarily attributed to a specific CAF subset, CAF-S1. These findings further highlight the tumor-promoting role of immune-associated CAFs. Importantly, these preclinical insights have served as the basis for subsequent clinical trials.
In clinical phase 1/2 trials for CRC, pancreatic cancer, and GBM, the CXCL12 antibodies olaptesed (NOX-A12) and olaptesed pegol (NOX-A12) have been tested in combination with pembrolizumab for CRC and PDAC, and in GBM, combined with radiotherapy and bevacizumab (VGEF inhibitor) (NCT03168139 and NCT04121455). No results have been published to date. The GBM trial is expected to run until 2028. Further clinical investigations are certainly warranted.

## JAK/STAT inhibitors
The JAK/STAT pathway in CAFs is complex and has been the subject of extensive investigation. It can be induced by IL-1 and antagonized by TGF-β to shape CAF heterogeneity in PDAC. In organoids and mouse models, IL-1 induces downstream JAK/STAT activation, generating inflammatory CAFs. However, TGF-β can downregulate Il-1R1 expression, which antagonizes this process and promotes CAF differentiation into myofibroblast. A key question that remains from this study is which CAF subsets are tumor promoting and which are tumor restraining. A major strength of the study is its demonstration of the potential to selectively target specific CAF populations.
In endometrial cancer, CD146+ CAFs can promote progression by inducing vasculogenic mimicry and angiogenesis via the IL-10/JAK1/STAT3 pathway. CD146+ CAFs have been associated with poor prognosis, particularly due to their elevated expression of IL-10. In this study, treatment with niclosamide effectively inhibited the JAK1/STAT3 pathway. Studies like this provide a valuable foundation for future clinical trials.
The most frequently tested JAK/STAT inhibitor in clinical trials is ruxolitinib ( , NCT01594216, NCT01423604, NCT02119676, NCT02119663, NCT02117479, NCT02155465, NCT02066532, and NCT02041429). A phase 2 study combining ruxolitinib or placebo with capecitabine in patients with metastatic pancreas cancer was well tolerated and improved survival (NCT01423604). However, two phase 3 trials employing the same treatment regimen in pancreatic cancer patients were terminated early as no survival benefit was observed (NCT02119663 and NCT02117479). This serves as another example of the complexity involved in translating preclinical findings into clinical success.
In EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma patients, a phase 1/2 trial of ruxolitinib in combination with erlotinib chemotherapy was well tolerated but did not achieve clinical endpoints (NCT02155465). 
In another phase 2 trial testing ruxolitinib plus regorafenib (a multi-kinase inhibitor) in patients with relapsed/refractory metastatic CRC, similar results were seen—good tolerance but overall survival was not improved compared to the placebo. 
In metastatic HER2-positive breast cancer, ruxolitinib was combined with trastuzumab in a phase 1/2 trial (NCT02066532). Despite promising preclinical results that provided rationale for this trial, no improvement in survival could be detected as compared to historical controls. Only a phase 1 study combining ruxolitinib with paclitaxel in HER-negative metastatic breast cancer patients demonstrated evidence of clinical benefit (NCT02066532). 
The JAK/STAT pathway clearly influences certain CAF subtypes, potentially with the strongest effects on iCAFs. It remains unclear whether alternative targets are required or if combination therapies will ultimately be more effective. Further research is needed to clarify these questions.

## Hedgehog pathway
An extensive body of preclinical evidence demonstrates the sonic hedgehog signaling pathway (SHSP) influences CAFs to promote tumor growth, which has given rise to many drug candidates and preclinical and clinical studies. Targeting of SHSP in preclinical models normalized the desmoplastic TME and improved response to chemo- and nanotherapies.
Vismodegib (an SHSP inhibitor) is shown to normalize the TME by reducing proliferative CAFs and abundance of collagen and hyaluronan. In this study, normalization of the TME improved blood vessel functionality, leading to an enhanced chemotherapy response in breast tumors. This provides another example underscoring the importance of combining treatments that separately target CAFs and tumor cells.
However, in PDAC, hedgehog signaling is differentially elevated in myCAFs and iCAFs after inhibitor treatments. This varying activity led to a reduction in myCAFs and an increase in the number of iCAFs, with an additional reduction of cytotoxic T cells and Tregs observed in the pancreatic cancer TME. Differential effects on CAF subtypes, which impact the inflammatory response, thus present a critical challenge to targeting SHSP for cancer control. This is particularly relevant in light of growing evidence suggesting that iCAFs may exhibit tumor-promoting functions.
Vismodegib (GDC-0449), saridegib (IPI-926), and sonidegib are SHSP inhibitors that have been tested in multiple clinical trials (NCT00739661, NCT01130142, NCT01064622, NCT00833417, NCT01431794, NCT02027376, NCT01088815, NCT05538091, and NCT04066504). In a phase 1 trial in patients with advanced basal cell carcinoma, vismodegib demonstrated a response rate of 58% (NCT00833417). In contrast, investigations of vismodegib in patients with ovarian cancer showed no increase in progression-free survival (NCT00739661). When combining vismodegib with gemcitabine or nab-paclitaxel for pancreatic cancer patients in a phase 1b/2 study, overall survival was also not improved (NCT01130142 and NCT01088815) (NCT01130142 and NCT01088815). , Sonidegib was tested in a phase 1b study in combination with docetaxel in TNBC patients, showing antitumor activity in 3/10 patients (NCT02027376). Sonidegib, topically applied for locally advanced basal cell carcinoma, is shown to be well tolerated with manageable side effects based on interim analyses; however, the trial is still ongoing (NCT04066504). 
Based on the outcomes of the clinical trials conducted to date, it may be cautiously concluded that targeting the Shh pathway is unlikely to represent an effective translational strategy for improving patient survival. Additional evidence is needed to fully clarify its therapeutic potential.

## IL-6 signaling
IL-6, a key cytokine in immunity and inflammation, has recently gained attention for its impact on CAF biology.
CAF-derived IL-6 has been shown using in vitro analyses in human primary bladder cancer tumors to activate STAT3 and hence EMT, migration, proliferation, and invasion. Inhibition of IL-6 secretion by CAFs using a neutralizing antibody was shown to reverse the EMT phenotype. Moreover, elevated IL-6 expression has been associated with more aggressive forms of bladder cancer.
In PDAC, the iCAF subtype was found to be the primary producer of IL-6. Conflicting effects were observed in a pre-clinical study utilizing dual-recombinase genetic PDAC models. IL-6 deletion in -SMA+ CAFs improved efficacy of gemcitabine treatment; however, when IL-6 was deleted in FAP+ CAFs, there was no improvement in gemcitabine treatment response. Combining gemcitabine and anti-PD-1 immunotherapy does prolong the survival of PDAC mice in the absence of IL-6. 
Using single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, ICC CD146+ vCAFs were shown to express high levels of IL-6. In further functional investigations, epigenetic alterations in ICC cells induced by IL-6 via upregulated enhancement of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) were observed. These changes led to enhanced malignancy.
Successful IL-6 targeting in preclinical models has led to numerous clinical trials. The IL-6 inhibitor tocilizumab has been tested in recurrent ovarian cancer, metastatic HER2-positive breast cancer, metastatic pancreatic cancer, melanoma, NSCLC, and others ( ) (NCT01637532, NCT03135171, NCT02767557, NCT03999749, NCT04940299, NCT04691817, and NCT05846789). A phase 1 trial of tocilizumab combined with carboplatin/doxorubicin and IFN-a2b in patients with recurrent epithelial ovarian cancer demonstrated feasibility of this combination therapy and a potential survival benefit. Decreased levels of pSTAT3 with increased myeloid cell production of IL-12 and IL-1b were observed. Furthermore, additional activated T cells increased secretion of effector cytokines such as TNF- and IFN- . 
In ongoing melanoma phase 2 trials, tocilizumab and its analog antibody sarilumab are combined with other immune therapies like ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4), nivolumab (anti-PD-1), and relatlimab (anti-LAG-3). , The results are highly anticipated.
In a different trial, fibroblast/IL-6 inhibition enabled gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel rechallenge in pancreatic cancer patients. The study cohort received a dose of 8 mg/kg tocilizumab on day 1, followed by nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine on days 2, 9, and 16. In 4 of 10 patients, tumor reduction was observed, and a more detailed analysis of biopsy samples revealed a decrease in proliferative CAFs, as well as a decrease in phosphorylated STAT3 expression, in treatment responders. 
These findings indicate patient-specific CAF activity, highlighting the need for further clinical studies and personalized therapeutic approaches.

## Fibroblast growth factor receptor inhibitors
The FGFR is known to activate CAFs in many cancers through diverse isoforms. Paracrine signaling pathways are induced by fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2), FGF4, FGF7, and FGF20, and endocrine pathways are induced by FGF19, FGF21, and FGF23.
In preclinical in vivo models of TNBC, FGFR inhibition can boost T cell infiltration through the regulation of CAFs. Furthermore, CAF secretion of vascular cell adhesion molecule 1, as well as proliferation and migration, were inhibited by down-regulating the MAPK/ERK pathway. The addition of immune checkpoint blockade therapy may improve the therapeutic response to FGFR inhibition in TNBC tumor models. This study concluded that FGFR inhibitor treatment can convert tumors with low immune infiltration into highly immune-infiltrated tumors.
In preclinical models of prostate cancer, the addition of CAFs in co-culture reduced efficacy of FGFR inhibitor treatment, underscoring again the role of CAFs in therapeutic response. However, treatment targeting the FGFR receptor resulted in decreased tumor cell growth. A limitation of the study is that these findings are based solely on in vitro experiments and require further validation in vivo.
In lung cancers, CAF-secreted FGF2 was found to enhance tumor cell growth as well. In a mouse model of lung carcinoma, CAF overexpression of Tgfb, Mmp7, FGF9, and FGF2 and the conversion of tumor-associated macrophages to the tumor-supportive M2 phenotype was observed. Treatment with FGFR inhibitors at early development stages of lung cancer using in vivo models led to fewer and markedly smaller tumor nodules. A similar significant reduction in tumor sizes was observed in established tumor models. 
Based on this and other preclinical studies, multiple clinical trials are currently underway.
A phase 2 study of FGFR inhibitor fexagratinib (AZD4547) in stage IV squamous cell lung cancer patients showed only minimal activity, while trials in different patient populations remain ongoing (NCT02965378). 
Another FGFR inhibitor, fisogatinib (BLU-554), was tested in a phase 1 study, which validated FGFR4 as a receptor target in patients with FGF19 overexpression in advanced HCC (NCT04194801). , 
In endocrine-resistant breast cancer patients, the FGFR inhibitor fexagratinib (AZD4547) was tested in a phase 2 study, which demonstrated only minimal activity and significant toxicity (NCT01791985). 
In trials using erdafitinib (JNJ-42756493) to target advanced solid tumors with FGFR alterations, patients experienced clinical benefit and the therapy was well tolerated. , Multiple trials testing erdafitinib for patients with urothelial cancers and bladder cancers are currently ongoing (NCT04917809, NCT05775874, NCT02365597, NCT04963153, and NCT06511648).
These clinical applications—some of which have already failed in certain cancer types, while others remain ongoing in different contexts—highlight the importance of approaching each cancer entity individually. The ongoing trials are highly anticipated and may offer valuable insights into cancer-specific responses.

## Enhancing immune modulation and CAF-directed cytotoxicity

## Selective transgene expressing adenoviral vector
Genetically engineered adenoviruses represent a novel therapeutic opportunity. Their appeal for targeting CAFs lies in their ability to selectively target-specific cell types or tissues under defined conditions. The goal is to mediate highly specific gene expression within the TME.
A phase 1 study tested NG-641, a tumor-selective vector expressing a FAP-TAC bispecific antibody and immune enhancer module, in patients with advanced epithelial tumors (NCT04053283). This tumor-selective adenoviral vector is designed to activate T cells as well as CXCL9, CXCL10, and IFNa. Priming of T cells against FAP-expressing CAFs is induced through the bispecific T cell activator molecule FAP-Tac. In the clinical trial of NG-641, local immunological tumor changes were observed without systemic toxicity. In another multicenter phase 1 trial, NG-641 in combination with nivolumab is currently being tested (NCT05043714). 
This out-of-the-box approach is under evaluation in ongoing trials, whose results are eagerly awaited and may extend to other cancer entities.

## Reprogramming CAFs to a quiescent state

## Vitamin D receptor agonist
The vitamin D receptor was detected in the stroma of human PDAC tumors and has been shown to participate in the reprogramming of activated CAFs. By targeting it with calcipotriol, a reduction in inflammation markers and fibrosis in the TME was achieved, and tumors were sensitized to gemcitabine treatment. These findings formed the basis for clinical trials in patients with PDAC (NCT03331562 and NCT03520790) wherein paricalcitol was combined with the PD-1 blocker pembrolizumab and gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel. Results from these phase 2 studies are yet to be reported.

## All-trans retinoic acid receptor agonists
Numerous clinical trials, starting in 2009 and currently continuing through 2028, investigate the effect of all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) on CAF reprogramming in different cancers (NCT01048645, NCT03307148, NCT04241276, NCT03200847, NCT03572387, NCT02403778, NCT04919369, and NCT05999812) ( ). Retinoic acid has been shown to regulate CAF parent cells, such as PSCs, through the nuclear receptor complex RAR/RXR. Deactivation of PSCs and CAFs leads to a reduction in ECM production through inhibition of β-catenin and Wnt, downregulation of ERK1/2 and JNK phosphorylation, and suppression of TGF-β1 release. In a phase 2 trial for NSCLC, ATRA combined with paclitaxel and cisplatin increased progression-free survival (NCT01048645). In patients with melanoma, combining ATRA with the PD-1 blocker ipilimumab led to a decrease in myeloid-derived suppressor cells and reduced immunosuppressive gene expression (NCT02403778). 
A phase 1b trial tested the combination of ATRA with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel in patients with pancreatic cancer (NCT03307148). Results reveal ATRA modulation of the stroma, with enhanced response to chemotherapy. A phase 2 trial for this therapy regimen has been completed, but results remain forthcoming at this time (NCT04241276).
In MSS-CRC, a combination trial of ATRA with bevacizumab (Anti-VGEF) and atezolizumab (PD-1 agonist) is ongoing (NCT05999812). 
These multiple trials are promising and may help to confirm and establish the clinical relevance of ATRA. Notably, the fact that ATRA is being tested across a wide range of cancer types reflects a significant effort to develop broadly applicable cancer therapies. Such approaches could ultimately reduce treatment costs compared to highly individualized therapies.

## Interleukin-1 inhibitor
IL-1 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a key role in initiating systemic responses, such as fever, during the acute phase of inflammation. In cancer, IL-1 has been shown to promote tumor growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis. It has gained attention as a therapeutic target due to its elevated expression in CAFs and its role in shaping the TME.
In rectal cancer, IL-1-dependent signaling augments oxidative DNA damage in iCAFs, inducing senescence and impacting therapeutic response in patients. In in vivo models, IL-1 inhibition was effective in preventing iCAF senescence and sensitized them to irradiation. A recent phase 1 clinical trial combined anakinra with capecitabine in patients with rectal cancer (NCT04942626). 
Results are awaited with interest, and further trials across cancer types are needed to evaluate this approach.

## CAF depletion

## LRRC15 antibody
LRRC15+ CAFs suppress activity of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells through direct cell-cell interactions as confirmed by diminished TNF- and IFN- expression. The depletion of this CAF population reduces the total tumor fibroblast content and alleviates CD8+ T cell suppression, enhancing efficacy of checkpoint blockade therapy. In a first-in-human phase 1 trial in patients with sarcoma and other advanced solid tumors, the antibody-drug conjugate ABBV-085 revealed a safe and tolerable profile with preliminary antitumor activity (NCT02565758). 
Additionally, further trials need to be conducted.

## ECM remodeling

## Angiotensin receptor blockade
Targeting CAFs using angiotensin receptor-blocking drugs to normalize the fibrotic TME is another therapeutic strategy. Functionally, particularly through the use of agents such as losartan, it has been shown to exert antifibrotic effects within the TME by modulating TGF-β signaling, a central driver of fibrosis and CAF activation. By inhibiting angiotensin II type 1 receptor, losartan reduces TGF-β-mediated myofibroblast activation, thereby decreasing the deposition of ECM components, especially collagen I and hyaluronan, which are typically elevated in desmoplastic tumors. This leads to a less rigid and more permeable stroma, resulting in reduced interstitial fluid pressure, improved vascular perfusion, and enhanced delivery of therapeutic agents. Preclinical studies and early clinical trials in pancreatic and rectal cancers have demonstrated that angiotensin receptor blockade can effectively remodel the ECM, normalize the tumor vasculature, and synergize with chemotherapy and radiotherapy, ultimately contributing to reduced tumor progression and improved treatment response.
A phase 2 trial investigated the effect of neoadjuvant therapy combining FOLFIRINOX with losartan followed by chemoradiotherapy in patients with locally advanced pancreatic cancer (NCT01821729). Treatment enabled downstaging of PDAC and achieved a 61% margin-negative resection rate. Subsequent gene expression and immunofluorescence analyses in resected tissues revealed reduced expression of immunosuppression and pro-invasion genes. Moreover, losartan impacted the levels of circulating IL-8, sTie2, and TGF-β; led to a reduction of CD4+/FOXP3+ Tregs, and FOXP3+ cancer cells; and increased the infiltration of CD8+ T cells. The findings suggest that losartan works to improve overall survival by decreasing immunosuppression in tumors.
Another phase 2 trial combining losartan with FOLFIRINOX and 9-ING-41 (glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta inhibitor) in patients with pancreatic cancer is actively enrolling (NCT05077800). In refractory or oligometastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, a trial combining losartan with pembrolizumab (PD-1) and stereotactic body radiation is also actively recruiting (NCT06211335).
These trials are highly promising, and their results are eagerly anticipated. Notably, the use of a commercially available agent with low production costs holds the potential to benefit many patients suffering from aggressive cancers.

## MMP-9 inhibitor
MMPs are extracellular enzymes that influence tumor growth and metastasis through matrix remodeling.
The monoclonal antibody andecaliximab (ADX), an inhibitor of MMP-9, was tested in a phase 3 study in combination with mFOLFOX6 for patients with advanced GC or gastroesophageal junction cancer (NCT02545504). Despite its good safety profile, no improvement in survival was observed in patients receiving ADX.
In a phase 2 study for patients with GC, ADX was combined with nivolumab (anti-PD1) (NCT02864381). Treatment did not confer any survival benefit; however, better outcomes were observed in patients with HER2-positive disease, higher tumor mutation burden, GRB7, or low TGF-β.
These trial results support the notion that different cancer types require distinct therapeutic strategies. The lack of outcome benefit observed in GC trials may be related to the specific combination therapies used. It is possible that alternative combinations would yield different effects. In any case, additional trials across various cancer types are needed before a general conclusion can be drawn regarding the use of MMP inhibitors.

## CTGF blockade
Connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) is a cysteine-rich matricellular protein involved in proliferation, adhesion, angiogenesis, tumor development, and fibrosis. A phase 1/2 and phase 3 trial of the antibody FG-3019 (pamrevlumab) in combination with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel/FOLFIRINOX treating pancreatic cancer patients has recently ended with results forthcoming (NCT02210559, NCT03941093).

## Hyaluronan degradation
In pancreatic cancers, high levels of glycosaminoglycan hyaluronan have been detected. Preclinical investigations of hyaluronan synthases 2 and 3 (HAS2, 3) demonstrate activity in the TME with effects on tumor growth. Treating BxPC-3 pancreatic cancer cells with pegylated human recombinant hyaluronidase (PEGPH20) diminished extracellular hyaluronan, decreasing tumor growth rates. In a phase 2 study of pegvorhyaluronidase alfa (PEGPH20) combined with nab-paclitaxel/gemcitabine in patients with untreated metastatic PDAC, treatment prolonged PFS in patients with hyaluronan-high tumors (NCT01839487). The follow-on phase 3 trial established the safety profile of the treatment regimen, but without an overall improvement in survival (NCT02715804). 
A phase 1 trial in patients with HER2-negative, metastatic breast cancer combined PEGPH2 with eribulin chemotherapy but was terminated (NCT02753595).

## Synopsis and future therapeutic prospects
Our knowledge of targeting fibroblast subtypes to influence malignant cells has advanced considerably. Yet fibroblasts exert diverse, context-dependent effects across cancers, and their function may vary with TME characteristics in a patient-specific manner. Targeting CAFs is further complicated by their migration into metastases, and systematic comparisons between primary tumor-derived CAFs and MAFs remain scarce.
The TME contributes as strongly to therapeutic resistance as cancer cells themselves, making CAFs a central research focus. Tumors with highly fibrotic stroma, including breast, lung, liver, and pancreas, may be particularly responsive to CAF-directed approaches, which could overcome resistance to chemo- and immunotherapies and improve survival.
Defining factors driving CAF activation remains critical to reveal novel targets. AI tools now accelerate the analysis of large datasets, supporting development of functional classification systems that go beyond marker expression. Extensive clinical trials targeting CAFs have already been performed, but none have advanced beyond phase 3, underscoring the need for continued validation of therapeutic strategies in human tumors.
Comparing healthy and diseased tissues will further identify tumor-promoting CAF populations.
Finally, CAF-targeting strategies may also aid cancer prevention, particularly in high-risk conditions (e.g., lung fibrosis, hepatitis, and pancreatitis). Reprogramming activated fibroblasts or selectively eliminating tumor-promoting while sparing restraining CAFs would represent a transformative advance in cancer therapy.
