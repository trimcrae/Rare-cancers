## Purpose
Mutations in the crystallin beta A4 (CRYBA4) gene, which encodes βA4-crystallin, are associated with congenital cataract; yet, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms remain elusive. We systematically investigated four cataract-associated βA4-crystallin mutants (βA4-Y67N, βA4-L69P, βA4-F94S, and βA4-G147V), each representing distinct structural contexts, to assess their effects on structural destabilization and interactions with key crystallins.

## Methods
We employed a comprehensive approach, including protein expression and purification, size-exclusion chromatography, crosslinking, and circular dichroism spectroscopy, to evaluate the polymerization propensity, structural integrity, and stability of the proteins. Additionally, antibiotic resistance screening and yeast two-hybrid assays were used to examine the impact of these mutations on protein folding and interactions between proteins.

## Results
Our findings reveal that core mutations (βA4-L69P/βA4-F94S) induce severe aggregation and disrupt interactions with βB1- and βB2-crystallin, the inter-motif mutation (βA4-G147V) reduces protein flexibility, and the surface mutation (βA4-Y67N) results in mild disruption of protein interactions. Notably, αA-crystallin preferentially stabilizes core mutants but exhibits limited effects on others.

## Conclusions
The present study revealed mutations in βA4-crystallin–driven cataractogenesis via distinct mechanisms according to their distinct structural contexts. αA-crystallin functions as a primary mitigator of severe misfolding, preferentially stabilizing aggregation-prone conformers. These findings suggest that mutation-specific stabilization via small-molecule chaperones or enhanced αA-crystallin activity may represent potential precision therapeutic strategies for βA4-crystallin–associated congenital cataracts.

Because congenital cataracts occur in sensitive periods of visual development, they can lead to irreversible visual defects and more severe outcomes than the other types of cataracts. Inherited cataracts account for a large proportion of congenital cataracts. Identification of the causative gene and its function in inherited cataracts can help reveal the mechanisms underlying congenital and age-related cataract formation. – 
The lens nucleus consists of highly ordered lens fiber cells with a high concentration of crystallin proteins. The crystallins must be soluble and long lived to retain lens transparency. The ubiquitous lens crystallins are divided into three classes (α-, β-, and γ-crystallins), based on their elution order from a gel filtration column. The β-crystallins are comprised of four acidic β-crystallins (βA1–βA4) and three basic β-crystallins (βB1–βB3), named depending on their isoelectric points. The lens crystallin network requires precise stoichiometry among α-, β-, and γ-crystallins to maintain lifelong transparency. βA4-crystallin (βA4) is one of the predominant β-crystallin subtypes in the infant lens, with expression levels comparable to those of βB1-crystallin (βB1) and βB2-crystallin (βB2). βA4-crystallin plays a critical role in lens transparency through heteromeric interactions with βB1-crystallin and βB2-crystallin. Although over 20 βA4-crystallin mutations are cataractogenic, the pathogenic mechanisms may vary depending on the location of the mutation within the βA4-crystallin structure. However, few studies have characterized their biochemical characteristics and pathogenic mechanisms.
Four archetypal βA4-crystallin mutants (Y67N, L69P, F94S, and G147V) were selected based on their previously reported associations with congenital cataracts in patients. – These mutations were chosen to represent distinct structural contexts within the βA4-crystallin protein (PDB ID: 3LWK) ( ), facilitating a systematic investigation of the diverse pathogenic mechanisms underlying the role of βA4-crystallin in cataractogenesis. We investigated the effects of the four mutations on the βA4-crystallin polymerization state, thermal stability, protein folding, and interactions with other molecules by biochemical methods to explore the pathogenic mechanisms of βA4-crystallin mutants (βA4-MUTS).

## Materials and Methods

## Molecular Cloning and Protein Expression Detection
The crystallin family genes (CRYBA4, CRYAA, CRYBB1, and CRYBB2) were amplified from a cDNA library for use in this study. Four cataract-associated βA4-crystallin mutants (Y67N, L69P, F94S, and G147V) were constructed by site-directed mutagenesis. The wild-type (WT) and mutant CRYBA4 sequences were cloned into pET28a and pET30a vectors containing an N-terminal polyhistidine (His)-tag, with or without maltose binding protein (MBP) tag, respectively. The Escherichia
coli Rosetta BL21(DE3) strain was selected for protein expression. A single recombinant clone was inoculated into 500 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin and cultured in 37°C shakers until optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 1.2. Isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was then added into the culture with a final concentration of 0.3 mM to induce protein expression at 16°C overnight. The expressing bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 5000g for 10 minutes. The cell pellet was resuspended with binding buffer (250-mM NaCl, 2.5-mM imidazole, and 20-mM Tris hydrochloride [HCl], pH 8.0) at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and lysed by sonication on ice. The lysate was centrifuged to separate the soluble fraction (supernatant) from the insoluble fraction (pellet). The pellet was resuspended in an equal volume of nickel binding buffer. Equal volumes of the supernatant and resuspended pellet were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) to assess the expression levels of the recombinant proteins.

## Expression and Purification of βA4-Crystallin
The expression clones of βA4-WT, βA4-WT-MBP, and βA4-MUTS-MBP were cultured in 3 L of LB medium for protein purification. Following induction with IPTG, the bacterial cells were collected, resuspended with nickel binding buffer, sonicated, and then centrifuged. The separated supernatant was loaded onto a 5-mL HisTrap HP column (Cytiva Marlborough, MA, USA), and protein containing His tag was then eluted with elution buffer (500-mM NaCl, 400-mM imidazole, and 20-mM Tris HCl, pH 8.0) by a Cytiva ӒKTA purifier. Finally, the protein was further purified by Cytiva HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 200 pg with gel filtration buffer (250-mM NaCl and 20-mM Tris HCl, pH 8.0). The purified proteins were concentrated using a centrifugal concentrator and stored at −80°C for long-term stability.

## Western Blotting
Diluted protein or bacteria was added to SDS-PAGE loading buffer and then boiled for 15 minutes to prepare samples. After high-speed centrifugation (14000g, 10 minutes), the supernatant was loaded onto SDS-PAGE gel. Proteins were transferred from the gel to a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane (150—mA, 1.5 hours), which was then placed in blocking buffer (5% skim milk [w/v]) diluted with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST; 150-mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, and 100-mM Tris HCl, pH 7.5) and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. After washing with TBS buffer, the NC membrane was incubated with mouse anti-His antibody (D410002; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) at 4°C overnight. The membrane was then washed and incubated with HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse antibody (D110087; Sangon Biotech) for 1 hour at room temperature. The membrane was coated with developing solution, and then exposed and captured by a chemiluminescence imager (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

## Size Exclusion Chromatography
To some extent, protein polymerization caused by mutations may be one of the prerequisites for protein precipitation to cause congenital cataracts. Therefore, we used size exclusion chromatography (SEC) to analyze the polymeric state of βA4-crystallin at room temperature. Gel filtration buffer was used to dilute βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-MUTS-MBP to 1 mg/mL, and one sample was taken for each to 24 mL of a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column. Elution was conducted at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min at room temperature.

## Crosslinking
Crosslinking was also used to detect the polymerization of βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-MUTS-MBP; 10-µM MBP fusion proteins in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were mixed with 1-mM disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS) acid and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. The reaction was then terminated by adding 50-mM Tris HCl (pH 7.5). After further incubation at room temperature for 15 minutes, the protein polymerization status was analyzed by SDS-PAGE. MBP and βA4-WT were used as controls.

## Circular Dichroism
Circular dichroism (CD) is the most widely used method for determining protein secondary structure and stability and was carried out both to estimate changes in the protein fold and provide a basis for further thermal denaturation studies. Measurements were performed using a Chirascan Spectrometer (Applied Photophysics, Leatherhead, Surrey, UK). βA4-crystallin or MBP samples at 0.3 mg/mL in PBS were added to 1-mm cuvettes, and the far-ultraviolet values at each integer point from 190 nm to 260 nm were measured at 20°C with a 4-second averaging time. The sample was heated from 20°C to 90°C in 5°C steps, and the thermal denaturation experiment data at 222 nm were recorded to calculate the thermal denaturation midpoint. After performing the above experiments three times, we calculated the average value for subsequent analysis. The secondary structure and thermal stability of the protein were analyzed by data fitting results.

## Fluorescence Spectroscopy
Fluorescence spectra were acquired using an F-7100 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The protein sample concentration was maintained at 0.2 mg/mL in PBS throughout the measurements. The slit width was 5 nm for both excitation and emission. The extrinsic fluorescent probe 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) was employed to monitor hydrophobic surface exposure. The extrinsic probe ANS was added to the protein solutions at a molar ratio of 75:1 (probe: protein) and incubated for 30 minutes in the dark before measurements. The samples were excited at a wavelength of 370 nm, and emission spectra were collected from 400 to 600 nm.

## Antibiotic Resistance Screening
The pMB1-tet-pARA-bla-link_long vector was selected for antibiotic resistance screening; the vector contains TEM1-β-lactamase, which allows the insertion of exogenous genes. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into NEB 10-beta Competent E. coli (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and tetracycline-positive clones were placed in LB liquid medium for culture until the OD600 reached 0.5. Subsequently, the supernatant was removed by centrifugation and resuspended with 170-mM NaCl to reach an OD600 value of 1.0. The bacteria were then diluted in an order of magnitude from 100 to 10−6 in a 96-well plate and stamped onto plates containing 0.5 mg/mL ampicillin, 50 µg/mL tetracycline, and 0.2 % arabinose. The stamped plate was incubated at 37°C for 18 hours, and the protein folding was then evaluated for the extent of bacterial growth.

## Yeast Two-Hybrid System
A yeast two-hybrid system was used to explore interactions between βA4-crystallin and three other proteins (αA-crystallin, βB1-crystallin, and βB2-crystallin) that have been reported to interact with βA4-crystallin. , The eukaryotic transcription factor GAL4 system, containing the N-terminal binding domain (BD) and C-terminal activation domain (AD), was utilized to detect protein interactions. The target genes were cloned into GAD-T7 as prey plasmids and PGBK-T7 as bait plasmids and were then co-transformed into yeast competent AH109 cells. Cells were spread on SD-Leu-Trp plates and cultured at 30°C for 3 days. After that, three positive clones were selected from each plate, and the diluted yeast solution was stamped onto SD-Leu-Trp, SD-Leu-Trp-His, and SD-Leu-Trp-His-Ade plates. Finally, the interactions among the proteins were determined based on the growth of the yeast clones on the selective plates.

## Molecular Modeling
The crystal structure of WT human βA4-crystallin was taken from 3lwk.1.A in the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). Modeling of the mutant βA4-crystallin proteins was carried out using the Expasy SWISS-MODEL workspace (Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, Geneva, Switzerland) with the fully automated option using the 3lwk.1.A structure as a template and default parameters. This model provided 96% coverage (amino acids 8–196), 99.47% sequence identity, and a global model quality estimate of 0.89. The WT and mutant protein structures were overlaid using rigid-body alignment for selected regions (TM-align algorithm of the Protein Analysis and Modeling module of DNASTAR 17.1.1.120).

## Results

## Cataract-Related βA4-Crystallin Mutants Tend to Form Inclusion Bodies
We transformed recombinant expression vectors into the E. coli Rosetta for small-scale induced expression and analyzed protein expression in the supernatant or pellet. Several pathogenic mutations ( A– D) were successfully expressed in the E. coli Rosetta expression system ( A). βA4-WT was mainly expressed in the supernatant, whereas only a small amount of βA4-Y67N was detected in the supernatant; in contrast, βA4-L69P, βA4-F94S, and βA4-G147V were only present in the pellet ( B, C). These results suggest that these mutations result in denaturation of the mutant proteins leading to aggregation and then precipitation from solution. In addition, the density and distribution of background bands other than βA4-crystallin are similar in the WT and mutant samples, suggesting that the mutant proteins did not interact with additional proteins leading to their co-precipitation.

## Mutations Promote Protein Self-Interaction
The vast majority of the βA4-MUTS existed in pellets in the insoluble fraction of the cell lysates ( B). To study the differences in biochemical properties caused by mutations, we chose MBP as a solubilizing protein to improve the solubility of mutant proteins ( A,  B). βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-MUTS-MBP were obtained by purification. The association states of five MBP fusion proteins were analyzed by SEC. The SEC analysis revealed striking polymerization differences: βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-Y67N-MBP eluted as dimers (peaked at 14 mL, <158 kDa), but βA4-L69P-MBP, βA4-F94S-MBP, and βA4-G147V-MBP mutants formed high-order aggregates (peaked at 9 mL, >670 kDa) ( C,  D), indicating that these three mutant proteins have a much higher degree of polymerization than Y67N. Compared with the graph of protein-marker SEC ( D), βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-Y67N-MBP are dimers, whereas βA4-L69P-MBP, βA4-F94S-MBP, and βA4-G147V-MBP are aggregates. Crosslinking confirmed the hierarchy of polymerization propensity among the mutants. The same treatment was performed for MBP alone and βA4-WT alone as controls to exclude any influence of MBP. βA4-L69P-MBP, βA4-F94S-MBP, and βA4-G147V-MBP produced insoluble polymers trapped in loading wells, whereas βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-Y67N-MBP generated discrete dimer bands ( E). These data establish that mutations βA4-L69P, βA4-F94S, and βA4-G147V promote protein self-interaction based on the SEC and crosslink results.
 

## Mutations Impair βA4-Crystallin Structural Stability
Mutations of critical amino acids might lead to decreased protein stability, resulting in aggregation as observed in SEC and crosslinking analyses. CD spectroscopy revealed significant structural perturbations in mutants compared to the WT protein. 
The secondary structure curves and thermal stability curves obtained by CD showed that all mutants exhibited increased α-helical content, concomitant with reduced thermal stability ( A,  B;  ). The fitted melting temperature (Tm) values for βA4-WT-MBP, βA4-Y67N-MBP, βA4-L69P-MBP, βA4-F94S-MBP, and βA4-G147V-MBP fusion proteins were 58.09° ± 0.21°C, 57.03° ± 0.20°C, 52.34° ± 0.23°C, 51.41° ± 0.24°C, and 51.07° ± 0.14°C, respectively ( B). Notably, the slope of the thermal denaturation curve of βA4-G147V-MBP was significantly smoother and more gradual than the sharp slopes of other mutants and the WT protein ( B), suggesting that the βA4-G147V mutation may have reduced the overall uniformity of the protein melting point. ANS spectroscopy revealed the hydrophobicity profiles of the βA4-WT-MBP and the βA4-Y67N-MBP are similar, whereas βA4-L69P-MBP, βA4-F94S-MBP, and βA4-G147V-MBP induced significant increases in ANS fluorescence intensity and blue shifts in the emission maxima. These changes indicated substantial alterations in surface hydrophobic exposure, which likely contributed to the observed destabilization and aggregation propensity of these specific mutants ( C).
 

## TEM1-β-Lactamase Screening System Reveals Mutation-Specific Folding Defects
To quantify folding competence, we employed a TEM1-β-lactamase antibiotic resistance assay. The βA4-WT had clones growing at 10−3, βA4-Y67N had a few clones growing at 100, βA4-L69P and βA4-F94S had no apparent clones, and βA4-G147V still had clones growing at 10−2 ( ). Therefore, in terms of protein folding as evaluated by this assay for these four mutations, βA4-F94S and βA4-L69P had the most significant influence, followed by βA4-Y67N and βA4-G147V.
 

## αA-Crystallin Partially Rescues Mutant βA4-Crystallin Solubility
As a molecular chaperone, αA-crystallin plays a critical role in maintaining crystallin stability and solubility by preventing misfolding and aggregation. Yeast two-hybrid assays revealed mutation-dependent interactions between αA-crystallin and βA4-MUTS ( A). βA4-L69P and βA4-F94S can interact strongly with αA-crystallin, whereas βA4-WT, βA4-Y67N, and βA4-G147V can only interact weakly with αA-crystallin ( A). Co-expression studies demonstrated the functional impact of αA-crystallin: βA4-MUTS predominantly formed insoluble aggregates when expressed alone ( C), but αA-crystallin co-expression with βA4-MUTS significantly increased their presence in the soluble fraction ( B). Based on the combined yeast two-hybrid assay and co-transformed expression results, we hypothesized that αA-crystallin mitigates the effects of these mutations to some extent through binding to and solubilizing the mutant proteins.
 

## βA4-L69P and βA4-G147V Mutations Block the Ability to Interact With βB1- and βB2-Crystallin
The β-crystallin network relies on precise interactions between βA4-crystallin and basic β-crystallins (βB1/βB2). Yeast two-hybrid analysis revealed mutation-specific disruption of these interactions. The interactions with βB2-crystallin were qualitatively similar to those of βB1-crystallin: βA4-WT and βA4-Y67N interacted most strongly with βB2-crystallin, followed by βA4-F94S, whereas βA4-L69P and βA4-G147V had no interaction with βB2-crystallin ( A–C). Our findings indicate that these mutations reduce the ability of the protein to dimerize with βB1- and βB2-crystallin, contributing to lens opacity through disrupted crystallin network integrity. The mechanism for the changes in the strength of dimerization is unclear, as none of the mutations was located in the interdomain interface, and molecular modeling showed that none disrupted the protein fold near the interface ( A– D). In fact, although the mutations all destabilized the βA4-crystallin structure, only the Y67N ( A) and G147V ( D) changes distorted the protein fold at all, and then only in substituted amino acids and their immediate neighbors.
 

## Discussion
A decrease in lens transparency characterizes cataracts, which result from various molecular mechanisms. – This study systematically investigated four βA4-crystallin mutations associated with congenital cataracts, revealing distinct mechanistic pathways based on their structural localization ( A). The crystal structures of many βγ-crystallins have been determined, allowing the remainder to be accurately modeled. , In addition, the highly stable, two-domain, four Greek key motif structure of βγ-crystallin is extremely well characterized, including the contributions of tyrosine corners to the Greek key motifs and surface interactions to the stability and dimerization of the proteins. – This provides a firm theoretical basis on which effects of mutations in βγ-crystallins can be characterized. Most studies of βγ-crystallin stability have been carried out using thermal or chemical stress monitored by CD and tryptophan fluorescence, , , which have allowed accurate estimates of the melting temperature of various mutant proteins and the energetics of their denaturation. Instability of βA4-crystallin has previously required co-expression with a basic β-crystallin, with βB2-crystallin being optimal for solubilizing expressed βA4-crystallin. In order to circumvent this instability and the need for heterodimer formation, which would not allow detailed studies of βA4-crystallin mutations and their effects on stability, in this work we appended MBP tags to solubilize expressed WT and mutant βA4-crystallins. In addition to the traditional CD studies, we also utilized β-lactamase and yeast two-hybrid systems to confirm the stability changes and interactions with βB1- and βB2-crystallins to evaluate the abilities of WT and mutant proteins to dimerize with basic β-crystallins, integral to their functions in the lens.
Because adding the MBP tag to βA4-crystallin and its mutants could complicate the analysis and even potentially affect the results, we verified the correct structure of the fusion βA4-crystallin proteins in a variety of ways. First, the CD spectra of the βA4-crystallin and MBP fusion proteins maintained the typical peaks of alpha helices and especially β-structures typical of βγ-crystallins as seen in  A, in which the tracings for the fusion proteins are essentially a mass-weighted average of MBP and the WT and mutant βA4-crystallins, with some additional contribution from the linker connecting them. This was confirmed by the estimated fractions of alpha helix, beta structures, and random coil shown in the  . Second, as seen in  C, βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-Y67N-MBP migrated on SEC as dimers and the remaining mutant proteins as higher order aggregates, suggesting that the fusion proteins maintained the face-to-face domain interface critical for dimerization. Similar considerations held for binding of the WT, Y67N, and F94S βA4-crystallins to βB1- and βB2-crystallins in the yeast two-hybrid assays. MBP was also appropriate to use in the thermal denaturation experiments, as it has a melting temperature of 58°, – well above the melting points for βA4-L69P-MBP, βA4-F94S-MBP, and βA4-G147V-MBP. However, it appears to denature at the same temperature as βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-Y67N-MBP. When the MBP moiety begins to denature, this would probably further destabilize the fused crystallin, which cannot stay in solution without the MBP solubilizing it. Thus, it is possible that the true melting temperature for βA4-WT-MBP and βA4-Y67N-MBP might be higher than that suggested by the melting curves. Because the number of data points, although sufficient to estimate the approximate melting temperature, is relatively low, they could not provide an accurate estimate of changes in the enthalpy and entropy, which requires a precise measurement of the slope of the transition. Additionally, the interaction studies using yeast two-hybrid assays and co-expression experiments with αA-crystallin further supported the reliability of our structural interpretations, as these methods do not rely on the MBP tag for interaction detection. These findings all suggest a minimal impact of the MBP tag on our experimental conclusions, ensuring that our structural interpretations are reliable.
The surface mutation βA4-Y67N causes mild destabilization while retaining dimerization, whereas core mutations βA4-L69P and βA4-F94S induce severe misfolding, aggregation, and disrupted interactions with βB1-/βB2-crystallin, attributed to destabilization of the hydrophobic core. The inter-motif mutation βA4-G147V impairs conformational flexibility, leading to partial misfolding and intermediate aggregation. Interestingly, although all of the mutations destabilized the protein structure, only two caused even mild alterations in the protein fold under mild conditions; the G147V changed probably because the glycine at that position assumed a torsional angle not possible for other amino acids with a side chain, and the Y67N changed probably through altered interactions of the side chain due to changing from an aromatic hydrophobic residue to a small residue, although both are polar. The molecular chaperone αA-crystallin partially rescued solubility, particularly for core mutants, by stabilizing misfolded states. These findings highlight the mutation-specific mechanisms—surface perturbations, core destabilization, and inter-motif rigidity—underlying βA4-crystallin–associated cataracts, providing a framework for targeted therapeutic strategies.
The βA4-crystallin protein consists of two structurally similar domains, each containing two Greek key motifs. , The βA4-Y67N, βA4-L69P, and βA4-F94S mutants are located in the second Greek key motif of the amino (N) domain, whereas βA4-G147V occurs in the third Greek key motif of the carboxyl (C) domain, in the sequence connecting it to the fourth Greek key motif. Y67 is situated on the surface of the molecule; its side chain is located in a relatively spacious environment, with no direct interactions between Y67 and other amino acid side chains. Tyrosine and asparagine possess similar chemical properties, and the asparagine side chain is not bulkier than tyrosine. This mutation likely causes minimal structural disruption ( B), consistent with experimental observations showing limited effects on protein folding and stability. In contrast, βA4-L69P and βA4-F94S exhibited severe aggregation and folding defects. Structural analysis of βA4-crystallin helps elucidate how the specific mutations affect its structure and thereby its function. L69 is surrounded by several hydrophobic residues including L47, L91, F94, and W80. The change to proline is less favored in this hydrophobic environment and thus likely impairs the stability of the N domain. Additionally, because proline does not allow the same ψ and ϕ dihedral angles around the peptide bond, the βA4-L69P mutation, located in the sequence connecting two β-strands, may induce a different bending direction and thereby affect the protein folding ( C). Similarly, F94 is deeply buried in a comprehensive hydrophobic network including V16, F29, V36, V44, L47, W54, L69, W80, and L91. Its mutation to a polar serine residue is unfavorable in this hydrophobic pocket, affecting the interaction between the central β-strands and the stability of the whole domain ( D). βA4-G147V, located on the protein surface in an inter-motif region between two β-strands of the C domain, replaces glycine with valine. The limber residue G147, lacking a side chain, contributes to protein flexibility. Its mutation to hydrophobic valine reduces peptide chain flexibility and surface entropy in part through the hydration shell, destabilizing the protein. Because glycine does not interact directly with surrounding residues other than possibly C166, which has similar potential interactions with both glycine and valine residues, only the side chains of Y67, L69, and F94 are highlighted in . Together, these structural insights have elucidated how specific mutations disrupt the stability and function of βA4-crystallin. βA4-Y67N caused minimal perturbation, whereas βA4-L69P, βA4-F94S, and βA4-G147V significantly impaired folding and aggregation through distinct mechanisms.
The chaperone-like protein αA-crystallin exhibited various interaction capabilities with distinct βA4-MUTS, thereby increasing their solubility. Previous studies have established that cataract formation is closely linked to the accumulation of insoluble lens proteins, , , , verified by the present experiments: βA4-WT is mainly expressed in the supernatant, βA4-Y67N retains partial solubility, and βA4-L69P, βA4-F94S, and βA4-G147V are almost exclusively insoluble, reflecting mutation-specific structural perturbations. The effectiveness of α-crystallin in mitigating precipitation in vivo is affected by the conditions in the lens, which has protein concentrations approaching 430 mg/mL. At this concentration, crystallins interact to form an ordered array or gel, which would be expected to alter their stability and interaction with α-crystallin, although not the conformations of their protein folds or basic organization. , Thus, binding of a mutant βA4-crystallin monomer by α-crystallin might well lead to binding of an entire tetramer or octamer, or perhaps an even larger protein complex potentially including not only βA4-crystallin but also other β-crystallins. In addition, although αA-crystallin is expressed at significantly higher levels than βA4-crystallin in both the lens epithelium and nucleus (around 5:1 in the mouse lens, 10:1 in mouse epithelia cells, and 2:1 in mouse fiber cells), , these levels vary widely during development and between cortical and central nuclear fibers, although comprehensive quantitative data are lacking. Lower ratios of α-crystallin to βA4-crystallin in localized spatial areas or temporal periods during lens development could exacerbate protein precipitation. αA-crystallin preferentially binds to βA4-L69P and βA4-F94S, likely through recognition of exposed hydrophobic patches in a molten globule state resulting from core destabilization. Some mutant βγ-crystallins, however, bypass this state and escape binding by α-crystallin. The likelihood of escape would be increased by poor initial binding to α-crystallin. Thus, the interaction of α-crystallin with βA4-Y67N and βA4-G147V, which retain partial native-like folding, is significantly weaker. This selective activity suggests that αA-crystallin functions as a primary mitigator of severe misfolding, preferentially stabilizing aggregation-prone conformers. Whether this different interaction ability modulates the onset, progression, or severity of cataracts in humans remains unknown. Despite its ability to solubilize aggregation-prone mutants and its high concentration in the lens, αA-crystallin can be overwhelmed by the extremely high concentrations of mutant βγ-crystallins found in the lens. With milder mutations and in age-related cataracts, increasing sizes of high-molecular-weight aggregates composed of α-crystallin–bound, denatured βγ-crystallins can be demonstrated, until they become large enough to precipitate from solution as toxic aggregates or directly scatter light. Thus, although α-crystallins and other lens chaperones help in defense against lens opacity, they might not completely protect against all βγ-crystallin mutations, even if they bind the mutant protein. The published clinical descriptions of the cataracts studied in this report are too fragmentary to determine whether this enhanced chaperone engagement correlates with milder, slower, or, conversely, more severe opacities. – Systematic genotype–phenotype studies incorporating standardized slit-lamp imaging, genetic information, and qualitative protein–protein interaction assays will be required to test whether differential αA-crystallin interaction modulates cataract severity in patients. 
This study provides valuable insights into the structural and interactional basis of βA4-crystallin mutation pathogenesis of congenital cataracts; however, our approach has limitations. Surface hydrophobicity is known to contribute to both protein aggregation and binding by α-crystallins. In this light, it is interesting that the surface hydrophobicity of all the mutants with the exception of the Y67N βA4-crystallin mutant is increased as estimated by ANS fluorescence ( C). This is consistent with weaker binding of the Y67N mutant to α-crystallin and its greater presence in the soluble fraction. The yeast two-hybrid assay, although powerful for detecting protein–protein interactions in a cellular context, does have a potential for false positives and false negatives. However, this danger is minimized by the high specificity and affinity of β-crystallin interactions, , and this approach has been used successfully to test associations of α-, β-, and γ-crystallins. , , In addition, the use of multiple complimentary approaches to estimate stabilities of the various mutant proteins provides confidence that our current results are valid. Future experiments using alternative methods such as a quantitative yeast–yeast two-hybrid system, co-immunoprecipitation, or glutathione-S-transferase pull-down assays in human lens cell lines, as well as biophysical techniques such as isothermal titration calorimetry, could provide more comprehensive validation of the interactions observed. Additionally, our current study primarily focused on biochemical and structural analyses, so further in vivo studies in appropriate model systems would be necessary to fully understand the physiological relevance and pathogenic mechanisms of these mutations.

## Conclusions
This study systematically elucidated how distinct structural βA4-MUTS cause cataracts through divergent mechanisms. We found that core mutations induced severe aggregation and disrupted interactions with βB1-/βB2-crystallins, inter-motif mutations reduced flexibility, and surface mutations caused mild disruptions. αA-crystallin preferentially stabilized core mutants but had limited effects on others. The mutation-dependent αA-crystallin interaction profile further highlights its role as a primary mitigator of severe misfolding rather than a universal solubilizing factor, providing a theoretical basis for understanding cataract pathogenesis and developing mutation-specific therapeutic strategies.
