## Abstract
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of mortality worldwide, with their prevalence rising with advancing age. Progressive remodeling of the cardiovascular system during aging underlies increased vulnerability to cardiovascular pathologies. This review summarizes current insights into the structural, functional, and molecular hallmarks of both cardiovascular aging (CVA) and CVDs, highlighting their mechanistic convergence. By further discussing diagnostic biomarkers and intervention strategies that bridge these processes, we delineated the translational continuum linking CVA and CVDs. The understanding of their interconnections underscores the potential of targeting CVA as a strategy for CVD prevention, early diagnosis, and treatment.
Key Words: biomarker, cardiovascular aging, cardiovascular diseases, intervention, mechanism

The heart and vasculature collectively constitute the human cardiovascular system, functioning primarily to deliver oxygen and nutrients throughout the body. Acting as the central propulsive organ, the heart creates cyclical blood pressure waves through its rhythmic contractions, propelling blood unidirectionally through a closed circulatory loop. Arteries function as transport vessels with elastic walls that contract and dilate, efficiently distributing oxygenated blood to organs and tissues to sustain their physiological functions. 
With advancing age, the cardiovascular system progressively deteriorates. Cardiovascular aging (CVA) is characterized by the accumulation of dysfunctional cells, initiating cascading alterations. These include impaired contractile-relaxation function in cardiomyocytes (CMs), aberrant cardiac fibroblast (CF) activation, vascular endothelial cell (VEC) disconnection with endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), phenotypic shifts in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and accumulation of tissue-infiltrating immune cells. At the molecular level, CVA manifests through altered metabolic patterns, genomic and epigenomic instability, dysregulated proteostasis, and elevated inflammatory response. These molecular and cellular changes converge to promote cardiac hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, valvular calcification, and arterial stiffening, accompanied by progressive impairment of cardiac and vascular contractility, relaxation, and conduction capabilities. This constellation of degenerative alterations is fundamentally linked to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). 
Advanced age constitutes a principal risk factor for CVDs, with prevalence rates of approximately 40% among individuals aged 40 to 59, escalating to more than 80% in populations aged 80 and older. Despite this correlation, investigations have not fully explained how aging processes contribute to CVD development. Given the worldwide demographic shift toward aging, elucidating the complex interrelationship between CVA and CVDs has become imperative for advancing preventive strategies and therapeutic interventions for older individuals.
This review summarizes current evidence regarding the structural and functional alterations characterizing CVA, with particular focus on their connections to age-associated CVDs, and discusses the emerging approaches targeting CVA as therapeutic modalities. By framing cardiovascular pathophysiology through the lens of aging biology, we highlight the underlying mechanism, emerging diagnostic markers, and targeted interventional strategies for both CVA and CVDs.

## Structural and Functional Characteristics of CVA
Cardiac aging is characterized by distinct structural alterations including concentric cardiac hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and valvular calcification secondary to degenerative alterations ( ).
 CMs turn over from approximately 1% annually in youth to about 0.5% in aged individuals, accompanied by the accumulation of dysfunctional and senescent cells. , Surviving CMs undergo compensatory hypertrophy to preserve cardiac output, leading to reduced chamber dimensions and ventricular wall thickening, hallmarks of concentric cardiac hypertrophy. , Myocardial fibrosis, another feature of cardiac aging, increases tissue stiffness through aberrant proliferation of CFs and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. , , Furthermore, CVA is compounded by degenerative valvular changes, including calcification and fibrosis. 
These structural alterations correlate with functional deterioration, primarily manifesting as diastolic dysfunction with extended isovolumic relaxation periods and reduced ventricular filling. , , While resting cardiac parameters remain relatively preserved in the older individuals, stress-induced cardiac performance is compromised, indicating diminished cardiac reserve. Cardiac conduction efficiency also declines with age, as evidenced by age-dependent P-R interval prolongation from 159 ms in young adults to 172 ms in aged individuals. 
Meantime, major arteries, including aortic, coronary, and pulmonary arteries, age in parallel with the heart, showing characteristic structural remodeling and wall thickening ( ). , , Age-related changes in VECs include morphological flattening and compromised barrier function, resulting in increased permeability. , Simultaneously, VSMCs undergo subintimal migration and phenotypic switching, facilitating intimal hyperplasia. Aged arteries also exhibit dysregulated neurohumoral control, marked by chronic activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and impaired β-adrenergic signaling, culminating in widened pulse pressure. , This cascade establishes a vicious cycle in which arterial structural and functional deterioration amplifies cardiac preload and afterload, while concomitant cardiac aging in turn aggravates hemodynamic and vascular burden. Enhanced immune cell infiltration accelerates the process of aging, , which drives pro-inflammation and pro-fibrosis responses, also represented as a hallmark of CVA. , , , 

## CVA-Related Cellular Changes
With advancing age, various cardiovascular cell types, including CMs, CFs, valve interstitial cells (VICs), VECs, and VSMCs, undergo senescence ( ). The accumulation of these senescent cells contributes to functional decline of cardiovascular tissues. , Senescent CMs exhibit enlarged cell size, contractile dysfunction, and impaired intercellular signal transduction, leading to cardiac rhythm abnormalities. They also secrete senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), thereby sustaining chronic inflammation in surrounding tissues. The senescence of VICs exhibit an activated myofibroblast-like phenotype, leading to abnormal thickening and dysfunction of valvular tissue. Similarly, senescent VECs display morphological abnormalities and enhanced adhesion to the basement membrane, resulting in misaligned endothelial layers and aberrant responses to shear stress. , Upregulation of cell adhesion molecules, such as ICAM-1, further promotes monocyte adhesion. Together, these alterations exacerbate vascular stiffness, impair vasomotor function, promote intimal thickening, and disturb hemodynamics.
Beyond senescence, cardiovascular cells also undergo age-associated phenotypic transitions that further compromise tissue integrity ( ). One example is EndMT, characterized by VECs losing endothelial markers and gaining mesenchymal traits along with migratory, invasive, and proliferative abilities, drives structural and functional changes in CVA. , Inhibiting this transition attenuates premature aging phenotypes in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS) mouse aorta. Similarly, VSMCs progressively shift from contractile to synthetic phenotype with age, migrating from the media to intima and secreting various ECM components that stiffen the arterial wall and impair vasomotor function. , In the myocardium, CFs, the primary stromal cells, remain quiescent under physiological conditions but become activated with age, differentiating into myofibroblasts that produce excessive collagen. , This excessive ECM deposition drives cardiac interstitial fibrosis, decreased ventricular compliance, and contributes to diastolic dysfunction. Immune cells also exhibit altered phenotypes in aging cardiovascular tissues. Delgobo et al found that terminally differentiated CD4+CCR7− T cells were enriched in the hearts of aged mice. These cells accelerate CVA by increasing the secretion of TNF-α and IL-13. In parallel, resident CCR2− macrophages in the heart are gradually replaced by circulating CCR2+ macrophages during aging, with the latter releasing proinflammatory factors. , These phenotypic transitions in cardiovascular cells act synergistically to sustain chronic inflammation and fibrosis, driving the progressive deterioration of cardiovascular function.
In addition, various forms of cell death contribute to CVA ( ). Kajstura et al reported a more than 2-fold increase in CM apoptosis in aged rat left ventricles compared with younger counterparts. Similarly, Csiszar et al observed an approximately 5-fold increase in apoptotic VECs in coronary arteries of aged rats. Aging vessels also exhibit increased VSMC apoptosis, and inefficient clearance of apoptotic VSMCs can lead to secondary necrosis. , Beyond apoptosis, inflammasome-mediated CM pyroptosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced ferroptosis are documented in aged myocardium. , In addition, copper accumulation within arterial intima triggers endothelial cuproptosis. 
These interrelated cellular alterations during CVA exacerbate adverse microenvironments, forming an integrated pathophysiological network that creates a vicious cycle accelerating CVA.

## Molecular Characteristics and Driving Factors of CVA
With advancing age, the cardiovascular system undergoes multifaceted changes at the molecular level ( ). The underlying factors driving CVA include metabolic dysfunction, genomic and epigenetic instability, proteostasis dysregulation, and amplified inflammatory responses ( ).
 

## Metabolic dysfunction
The aging myocardium accumulates damaged mitochondria exhibiting distinctive morphological aberrations alongside diminished oxidative phosphorylation capacity, , which is associated with impaired mitochondrial dynamics, defective quality control, and diminished metabolic rates. For example, alterations in the expression of mitochondrial fusion- and fission-related proteins (eg, Mfn1/2 and Drp1) in the aging myocardium lead to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and impairment of energy supply (see for abbreviations of key terms). , Moreover, Kubli et al demonstrated that mice deficient of Parkin, a key protein regulating mitophagy, exhibited accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and premature cardiac aging. 
 Mitochondrial dysfunction also exacerbates the aging process. Excessive ROS generated by dysfunctional mitochondria reduce nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, thereby impairing endothelium-dependent vasodilation. 
Beyond ROS, advances in metabolomics have deepened our understanding of metabolic dysfunction in CVA. Under physiological conditions, the heart derives ∼70% of its ATP from fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and ∼20% from glycolysis. , Aging shifts this balance: CMs exhibit diminished FAO capacity with compensatory glucose utilization. , However, this contributes to energetic insufficiency owing to relatively low ATP yield of glycolysis, ultimately compromising contractile performance. Concurrently, enhanced lipid uptake combined with reduced catabolic capacity fosters accumulation of toxic lipid intermediates, including ceramides and diacylglycerol, further damaging myocardial function. , In aged mice, CD36 deletion was shown to attenuate lipid influx, reduce myocardial lipid deposition, and alleviate age-associated cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction. Metabolic perturbations also impair cardiac nutrient sensing pathways, particularly insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling. , Disruption of IGF-1 receptor in mice reduces cardiac fibrosis, hypertrophy, and inflammation. 

## Genomic and epigenomic instability
Declining DNA repair capacity induces genomic instability in aged cardiovascular system. In murine model, defects in nucleotide excision repair genes induce premature CVA. In parallel, Xu et al demonstrated that accumulation of progerin, a mutant form of Lamin A, promotes DNA damage and accelerates endothelial senescence. Telomeres, which is essential for maintaining genomic stability, , also play a key role in CVA. Minamino et al showed that telomere dysfunction in human aortic endothelial cells induced senescence, characterized by ICAM-1 upregulation and decreased endothelial NO synthase activity. In CMs, oxidative stress triggers the formation of telomere-associated foci, activating p16INK4A and p21CIP1 signaling, and ultimately driving cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis. 
Epigenetic regulation, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, heterochromatin alteration, and RNA modifications, is equally essential as genomic stability for cardiovascular homeostasis. , , , DNA methylation declines with CVA, and hypomethylation in aging aortic VSMCs upregulates COL15A1 expression, promoting VSMC proliferation and arterial fibrosis. DNMT1 is downregulated with age in the heart. However, Dnmt1 genetic deletion or pharmacologic inhibition confers cardioprotective effects, indicating its function may be context-dependent. Histone modification enzymes also modulate aging, , as SIRT1 and SIRT2 levels inversely correlate with cardiac aging, , and HADC6 deficiency predisposes mice to premature cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis when challenged with angiotensin II. Histone methylation is similarly implicated: mineralocorticoid stress perturbs the balance between SUV39H1/EZH2 and lysine demethylase, leading to heterochromatin loss, inflammatory activation, and accelerated vascular aging. In addition, reduced m6A RNA methylation and noncoding RNA all contribute to CVA. , , For example, circ-Foxo3 and long noncoding RNA-H19, promote CVA, , whereas long noncoding RNA-SNHG12 supports vascular homeostasis and protects against aging-associated dysfunction. , , Together, these genomic and epigenomic alterations form an interconnected regulatory axis linking to structural and functional deterioration in both the heart and vasculature.

## Proteostasis dysregulation
With CVA, protein quality control deteriorates due to declining proteasome activity and decreased expression of heat shock proteins, which impairs the clearance of oxidized and ubiquitinated proteins and the refolding of misfolded proteins, thereby promoting abnormal protein accumulation within cardiovascular tissues. , , , Autophagic capacity also declines, partly due to increased acetylation of autophagic regulatory proteins, which results from reduced deacetylase expression and declining nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)+ levels in the aged cardiovascular system. , de Picciotto et al demonstrated that NAD+ precursor β-nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) supplementation restores autophagy through SIRT1 activation, attenuating arterial aging.

## Amplified inflammatory responses
CVA correlates with amplified inflammatory responses, including elevated circulating proinflammatory cytokines, activated inflammatory signaling and increased immunoglobulin G in cardiovascular tissues. , , Concurrently, excessive ROS induce the release of mitochondrial DNA from mitochondria, which subsequently activates proinflammatory signaling pathways, including nuclear factor (NF)-κB and cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)/stimulator of interferon genes (STING). Moreover, elevated immunoglobulin G in CVA exacerbated inflammatory responses. 
Autophagy dysfunction and proteostasis decline synergistically contributes to mitochondrial impairment and ROS accumulation, leading to the release of mitochondrial DNA that activates innate immune pathways and amplifies proinflammatory cytokine production. In turn, inflammatory mediators like TNF-α exacerbate oxidative stress in cardiovascular cells. These molecules interact in complex feedback loops, functioning both independently and cooperatively to drive CVA.

## Clinical Manifestations and Cellular Mechanisms of CVDs
Older individuals are susceptible to a spectrum of CVDs including atherosclerosis, hypertension, myocardial infarction (MI), valvular heart disease (VHD), and cardiac arrhythmias. This section outlines the clinical features, cellular alterations, and molecular drivers underlying these age-related CVDs.

## Atherosclerosis
Atherosclerosis develops through a sequential progression of pathological events. The earliest stage involves endothelial dysfunction, characterized by reduced NO production and increased endothelin-1 secretion, promoting platelet aggregation, leukocyte adhesion, and vascular tone imbalance. , Concurrently, dyslipidemia facilitates low-density lipoprotein (LDL) deposition in the arterial intima. Oxidative or enzymatic modification of low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) enhances its uptake by macrophage scavenger receptors such as SR-A and CD36, generating lipid-laden foam cells, the hallmark of early fatty streaks. , Foam cells release proinflammatory mediators that recruit additional immune cells, amplifying local inflammation. , As lesions advance, VSMCs migrate from the media to the intima, proliferate, and secrete ECM proteins, forming the fibrous cap. Advanced plaques are structurally heterogeneous, containing lipid cores, fibrous tissue, calcium deposits, and thrombi, which together determine the plaque stability and adverse clinical outcomes. 
The molecular drivers of atherosclerosis reflect the interplay of lipid retention, endothelial activation, oxidative stress, and ECM remodeling. LDL retention and modification activate scavenger receptor pathways and trigger TLR4–NF-κB signaling, amplifying inflammation. Endothelial activation, mediated by adhesion molecules and cytokines, recruits monocytes and T cells, whereas complement activation and IL-1β signaling promote lesion progression. , Oxidative stress from NADPH oxidases (eg, NOX2, NOX4) and mitochondrial ROS further activates NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasomes, exacerbating inflammation and cell death. , ECM remodeling is driven by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) released from VSMCs and immune cells, with imbalance between MMPs and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) weakening the fibrous cap. Furthermore, a recent report has indicated that the gut microbiota–bile acid–TGR5 axis is critical for promoting platelet activation and atherothrombosis. Together, these interlinked processes form a self-perpetuating cycle of lipid accumulation, inflammation, and structural instability, underpinning the initiation, progression, and complications of atherosclerotic disease. 
CVA hallmarks, such as cellular senescence and chronic inflammation, are similarly observed in atherosclerosis. , As recent studies have shown, metabolic remodeling and epigenetic alterations in VSMCs lead to their senescence and transcription of SASP genes, thereby contributing to the development of atherosclerosis. Senescent endothelial cells show reduced NO synthesis, upregulated adhesion molecules (ICAM-1, VCAM-1), and increased permeability, promoting lipid infiltration and leukocyte recruitment. Their SASP sustains chronic inflammation within the lesion. During aging, T cells accumulate excess cholesterol. Driven by this lipid burden, regulatory T cells exhibit reduced FOXP3 and CD25 expression alongside markedly elevated interferon-γ secretion, collectively accelerating atherogenesis and its progression. 

## Hypertension
Hypertension affects nearly 70% of older adults and is a major driver of cardiovascular morbidity. Its pathogenesis is initiated by hemodynamic overload and amplified by maladaptive neuroendocrine activation, particularly the RAAS and sympathetic nervous system, as well as chronic inflammatory and oxidative stress responses, ultimately leading to progressive structural and functional decline of cardiovascular tissues. , Hypertensive vascular remodeling is characterized by wall thickening, luminal narrowing, arterial stiffening, and microvascular rarefaction. Medial hypertrophy arises from VSMC proliferation, hypertrophy, and phenotypic switching from a contractile to synthetic state, accompanied by ECM deposition. Endothelial dysfunction is marked by reduced NO bioavailability and increased production of vasoconstrictors, such as endothelin-1 and angiotensin II, contribute to the development of hypertension. , Adventitial fibroblast activation increases collagen deposition, whereas collagen-elastin imbalance, advanced glycation end-product accumulation, calcification, and impaired vasodilation reduce arterial compliance and increase pulse wave velocity (PWV), thereby elevating cardiac afterload. , Degeneration or occlusion of small arteries and capillaries causes tissue hypoperfusion and hypoxia, further activating maladaptive signaling. 
The progression of hypertension is sustained by interconnected molecular pathways. Overactive RAAS signaling via angiotensin II–AT1 receptor activation induces vasoconstriction, stimulates VSMC proliferation/migration, upregulates collagen synthesis, and promotes aldosterone-driven fibrosis through mineralocorticoid receptor pathways. , Sympathetic nervous system hyperactivity increases norepinephrine release, activating α1-adrenergic receptors to cause vasoconstriction and β1-adrenergic receptors to stimulate renin release, thereby reinforcing RAAS activity. , Mitochondrial dysfunction elevates ROS production, driven partly by NADPH oxidases (eg, NOX1 and NOX4), which activate redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP1. Chronic inflammation engages NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways, sustaining the production of cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and MCP-1, thereby promoting vascular remodeling. Abnormal calcium handling, involving dysregulation of Cav1.2 and SERCA2, impairs VSMC contractility and vascular tone regulation. Together, these processes establish a self-perpetuating cycle of vascular remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and end-organ damage in hypertension.
Senescence of endothelial cells, VSMCs, fibroblasts, and immune cells both drives and amplifies hypertensive remodeling. Senescent endothelial cells exhibit increased permeability, reduced NO production, and SASP that recruits immune cells and exacerbates vascular injury. Senescent VSMCs lose contractile function, adopt a synthetic phenotype, and show impaired matrix repair, promoting fibrosis and stiffening. Fibroblast senescence alters ECM turnover, whereas senescent immune cells perpetuate low-grade inflammation. Through paracrine SASP signaling, senescent cells propagate injury to neighboring cells, reinforcing the cycle of cardiovascular remodeling. , 

## Myocardial infarction
MI occurs 7 times more frequently in individuals aged 65–74 than in those aged 35–44 and represents an ischemia–necrosis–repair cascade triggered by acute coronary artery occlusion, leading to immediate loss of contractile myocardium and long-term deterioration of cardiac function. The pathological course evolves through distinct but overlapping phases. In the acute phase (0–72 hours), rapid necrosis and apoptosis of CMs occur in the ischemic core, resulting in akinetic or dyskinetic segments, while noninfarcted myocardium develops compensatory hypercontractility and elevated wall stress. In the subacute repair phase (3–14 days), massive infiltration of neutrophils and monocyte-derived macrophages clears necrotic debris; Ly6Chi proinflammatory macrophages dominate early and transition to Ly6Clo reparative phenotypes secreting IL-10, promoting tissue repair. , Progressive ventricular dilation and loss of ejection fraction occur during this phase. In the chronic remodeling stage (weeks to years), fibroblasts differentiate into α-smooth muscle actin–positive myofibroblasts, with CD248+ subsets driving collagen deposition and scar maturation. , Dense, noncontractile scar tissue stiffens the infarct zone, while surviving CMs in remote regions undergo hypertrophy and elongation, ultimately impairing both systolic and diastolic function and predisposing to heart failure. 
At the molecular level, dysregulation of the NRF2/HO-1 antioxidant axis promotes mitochondrial ROS accumulation, which activates the NLRP3 inflammasome. Meanwhile, TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling drives the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, along with inducible NO synthase, amplifying oxidative and inflammatory injury. , Fibrotic remodeling is mediated by TGF-β from macrophages, platelets, and fibroblasts, which activates both TGF-β/Smad and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, upregulating collagen genes and ECM cross-linking enzymes such as lysyl oxidase. Additional contributions from MMPs (MMP-2, MMP-9) and their imbalance with TIMPs destabilize ECM architecture, reinforcing adverse ventricular remodeling. These cellular and molecular events link acute ischemic injury to chronic structural deterioration in the MI condition.
As senescent cell populations accumulate with age, they precipitate MI through multiple levels. Endothelial cells in peri-infarct vasculature migrate and proliferate to promote angiogenesis, but excessive EndMT aggravates fibrosis, and VSMCs acquire a senescent phenotype with increased calcification risk. Senescent VECs and VSMCs reduce NO synthesis, increase permeability, and promote microvascular rarefaction, worsening ischemia-reperfusion injury and atherosclerotic burden. Senescent CMs downregulate contractile proteins, impair mitochondrial energy metabolism, and secrete pro-hypertrophic factors. Senescent fibroblasts produce SASP factors, including IL-6, IL-8, TGF-β, and MMP-2/9, which inhibit myocardial regeneration and perpetuate collagen deposition, creating a self-sustaining “senescence-fibrosis” loop. 

## Valvular heart disease
VHD, encompassing degenerative and calcific disorders of the cardiac valves, exhibits a steep age-related increase in prevalence, affecting 5% to 10% of individuals aged 60 to 74 and up to 20% of those older than 75. Calcific aortic stenosis, the most common form, develops through a progressive pathological sequence initiated by endothelial injury to the valve leaflets. In the early phase, abnormal hemodynamic shear stress, particularly turbulent or oscillatory flow, or lipid infiltration disrupts the integrity of valvular endothelial cells, increasing permeability, upregulating adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, ICAM-1), and triggering secretion of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8. , This facilitates the infiltration of lipoproteins, particularly LDL, into the subendothelial space, where oxidative modification to LDL occurs. Ox-LDL engages the LOX-1 on valve endothelial cells (VaECs) and VICs, activating downstream inflammatory signaling and initiating VIC activation. , In the progressive stage, quiescent VICs transdifferentiate into α-smooth muscle actin–positive myofibroblasts that secrete MMP-2 and MMP-9, degrading ECM, while TGF-β released from activated macrophages and platelets drives excessive type I and type III collagen synthesis, leading to leaflet thickening and stiffness. In parallel, angiogenic sprouting occurs in normally avascular valve tissue, facilitating immune cell trafficking. In the late stage, chronic inflammation and altered mechanical stress induce osteogenic differentiation of VICs, marked by upregulation of RUNX2, osteocalcin, and alkaline phosphatase, and the production of matrix vesicles that nucleate calcium-phosphate crystals. CD68+ macrophages and CD3+ T cells accumulate in calcification-prone regions, forming an “inflammation-calcification niche” that sustains VIC osteogenesis through secretion of TNF-α, IL-6, and BMP-2. Progressive hydroxyapatite nodules immobilize the valve leaflets, causing severe outflow obstruction and eventual heart failure. 
At the molecular level, the ox-LDL/LOX-1 axis activates NF-κB–mediated transcription of IL-6 and IL-8, establishing a chronic inflammatory microenvironment that perpetuates VIC activation. Mechanical stress sensing via the mechanosensitive channel Piezo1 triggers Yes-associated protein/transcriptional coactivator (YAP/TAZ) signaling, enhancing the deposition of fibrillar collagens and promoting leaflet stiffening. , Osteogenic programming is orchestrated by the BMP-pathway and reinforced by Wnt/β-catenin signaling, both of which drive calcium-phosphate deposition. Notch pathway dysregulation further contributes to VIC osteogenesis, while MMP-TIMP imbalance destabilizes the ECM scaffold, facilitating mineral deposition. Together, these senescence-linked molecular networks couple chronic inflammation, ECM remodeling, and osteogenic differentiation to produce the progressive fibrosis and calcification characteristic of VHD.
The senescent VaECs and VICs mirror the pathological changes observed in valvular disease. Senescent VaECs exhibit impaired barrier function and reduced anti-calcification capacity, permitting increased lipid infiltration and leukocyte adhesion. Senescent VICs produce disorganized ECM and display impaired matrix repair, while their SASP, rich in IL-6, IL-8, TGF-β, and MMPs, acts in a paracrine manner to drive neighboring VICs toward osteogenic differentiation. This is prominent in advanced disease, with clinical studies showing that p16INK4a+ cell burden in calcified valves can approach 30% and positively correlates with calcification severity. 

## Atrial fibrillation
Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most common sustained arrhythmia and a major cause of stroke, demonstrates an age-dependent prevalence—rising from <0.2% in individuals younger than 49 years to 3.7% to 4.2% in those aged 60 to 70 and up to 10% to 17% in those older than 80. AF results from a progressive remodeling process involving structural, electrical, and contractile alterations in the atria, frequently secondary to comorbidities such as hypertension, MI, and VHD. Structural remodeling is dominated by atrial fibrosis, in which chronic activation of fibroblasts and their transition into myofibroblasts lead to excessive ECM deposition and increased collagen cross-linking, disrupting the continuity of myocardial bundles. This structural discontinuity increases anisotropy of conduction and promotes the formation of micro-reentry circuits. Volume and pressure overload contribute to progressive atrial dilation, which elevates wall tension, enhances fibroblast activation, alters ion channel expression, and distorts myofibrillar alignment, further compromising electrical propagation. CM loss is accompanied by compensatory hypertrophy and polyploidization, whereas mitochondrial-myofibrillar uncoupling and heterogeneous calcium transients predispose to early afterdepolarizations. , Activated myofibroblasts modulate CM electrophysiology both through direct gap junction coupling and via paracrine factors such as TGF-β, which promote pro-arrhythmic ion channel remodeling. Infiltration by macrophages and lymphocytes is common in AF atria, where they release cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and C-reactive protein, directly injuring CMs, stimulating fibroblasts, and contributing to maladaptive electrical remodeling via suppression of sodium and L-type calcium currents and altered connexin function. 
At the molecular level, AF is driven by coordinated changes in genes of ion channel and gap junction: downregulation of potassium channels (Kv1.5, Kv4.3) and lateral redistribution of connexin-43 slow conduction velocity and increase reentry risk, whereas upregulation of Cav1.2 prolongs action potential duration. Chronic calcium overload activates the calcineurin-NFAT pathway, inducing hypertrophy-associated genes such as atrial natriuretic peptide and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and further altering electrophysiological properties. Oxidative stress, generated by mitochondrial dysfunction and NADPH oxidases, modifies ion channel proteins, disrupts calcium cycling, and activates TGF-β/Smad and NF-κB signaling, thereby promoting fibrosis, inflammation, and apoptosis. Matrix remodeling is reinforced by MMP-2/9 activation and TIMP imbalance, further disrupting atrial architecture. Collectively, these molecular events link structural disorganization, electrical instability, and chronic inflammation, enabling the initiation and maintenance of AF.
Cellular senescence integrates with these pathological processes to amplify atrial remodeling. Senescent CMs exhibit reduced contractile protein expression, impaired calcium handling, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which destabilize conduction and contractility. Endothelial and immune cell senescence exacerbates the inflammatory milieu, whereas paracrine SASP signaling from senescent cells reinforces fibroblast activation and CM injury, creating a self-perpetuating “senescence-fibrosis-arrhythmia” loop. 

## Association Between CVA and CVDs
CVA exhibits substantial convergence with age-associated CVDs, sharing core pathophysiological features at multiple levels, from structural alterations and progressive functional deterioration to characteristic cellular phenotypes and molecular signaling cascades. CVA not only predisposes individuals to disease but also amplifies disease progression once pathology is established. Consequently, elucidating the mechanisms of CVA offers a conceptual framework for developing early diagnostic markers, refining risk stratification, and designing prophylactic strategies for cardiovascular pathologies. Furthermore, targeted interventions that attenuate the biological processes of CVA, such as senomorphics, metabolic optimization, or epigenetic modulation, hold promise both as preventive modalities and as adjunctive therapies for established CVDs, representing an emerging translational frontier in cardiovascular medicine.

## Shared cellular and molecular mechanisms linking CVA and CVDs
Cellular senescence represents a unifying driver linking CVA and diverse CVDs. Across vascular and myocardial compartments, senescent cells exhibit impaired reparative capacity, altered phenotype, and persistent SASP secretion, fostering chronic inflammation, matrix remodeling, and fibrosis. These changes disrupt endothelial barrier function, compromise vascular smooth muscle contractility, impair CM performance, and perpetuate immune activation. Through paracrine SASP signaling, senescence propagates degeneration across tissues, establishing a self-sustaining “senescence-inflammation-fibrosis” status that underlies both physiological aging and a broad spectrum of cardiovascular pathologies.
At the epigenetic level, both CVA and CVD are accompanied by downregulation of protective epigenetic regulators such as SIRT2, which accelerates vascular and myocardial senescence and amplifies pathological hypertrophy. , Concomitantly, heterochromatin dissolution and chromatin decompaction activate human endogenous retrovirus K expression, both as pathological hallmarks and molecular drivers detected in dilated cardiomyopathy, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and CVA. , 
Metabolic dysregulation represents another critical intersection. Both aged myocardium and failing hearts exhibit reduced activity of PPAR-α and PGC-1α, the master regulators of mitochondrial biogenesis and FAO. , Their suppression precipitates impaired energy production, ROS accumulation, and accelerated functional decline. Inflammatory mediators, such as S100A8 and S100A9, exemplify this convergence, acting as both biomarkers and effectors of myocardial injury. , In acute MI, S100A9-driven inflammatory amplification impairs repair capacity, whereas early inhibition of S100A9 mitigates inflammation and preserves cardiac function, highlighting the translational potential of targeting these shared metabolic-inflammatory pathways between CVA and CVDs.

## Overlapping assessment indicators for CVA and CVDs
CVA shares clinical manifestations with multiple CVDs ( ). In the atrium, age-related diffuse interstitial fibrosis disrupts electrical continuity, producing conduction heterogeneity and reentry circuits, and alters electrical coupling between myofibroblasts and CMs. These changes create a substrate for arrhythmogenesis, particularly AF. Similarly, ventricular remodeling in aging hearts mirrors pathological features of systolic and diastolic dysfunction, including hypertrophy, increased myocardial stiffness, and impaired compliance, hallmarks shared with heart failure and senile cardiomyopathy. 
 Arterial aging is characterized by endothelial dysfunction, maladaptive vascular remodeling, hemodynamic alterations, and chronic inflammation, pathophysiological hallmarks also observed in hypertension, atherosclerosis, and their sequelae such as coronary artery disease and stroke. In both CVA and CVDs, diminished NO bioavailability, increased oxidative stress, and elevated proinflammatory cytokine levels converge to impair vasodilation, promote fibrosis, and increase vascular stiffness. , Phenotypic switching of VSMCs toward a synthetic state, coupled with immune cell infiltration, accelerates arterial stiffening and raises cardiac afterload. , These vascular changes serve as indicators not only for age-related decline but also for MI, heart failure, and atherosclerotic progression, with similar mechanistic implications in angina pectoris and AF. , 
The alignment between CVA and CVDs indicates that many clinical assessment indicators are shared. Early identification of these changes in healthy older individuals, especially through noninvasive or minimally invasive methods, offers opportunities for prevention and early intervention. For example, PWV, particularly brachial-ankle PWV, is both a biomarker of vascular aging and an independent predictor of cardiovascular events in older individuals. , Routine PWV assessment in primary care settings could help forecast CVD risk. Circulating IGF-1, which declines with age, supports cardiac function and arterial integrity, and its plasma levels may serve as a risk indicator for CVDs. Low vitamin D levels (<20 ng/mL) inversely correlate IGF-1 with carotid intima-media thickness, underscoring its predictive value. Similarly, BNP, a standard marker for cardiac dysfunction, rises in healthy older populations and may serve as a biomarker of cardiac aging.
Despite their promise, the clinical translation of these indicators faces key challenges: lack of large, systematic cohorts focusing specifically on older populations; absence of clearly defined thresholds to differentiate physiological aging from pathological disease; and limited understanding of sex-specific differences. Addressing these gaps warrants integrative mechanistic studies and rigorous clinical validation.

## Addressing CVDs through strategies for CVA interventions
Recent research has yielded a range of promising interventions that target CVA, offering therapeutic potential for age-associated CVDs ( ). Metformin, a well-known AMPK agonist, delays aging and protects the cardiovascular system by improving nutrient sensing, enhancing autophagy, and promoting intercellular communication, , , , thereby reducing the incidence of cardiovascular events. In parallel, Gioscia-Ryan et al demonstrated that the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant (MitoQ) selectively attenuated atherosclerosis in aged mice through upregulation of elastin expression, without affecting the aorta of young animals, indicating specificity for age-related vascular stiffness. Translational human studies confirmed that MitoQ restores cardiovascular function. Other small molecule strategies, such as uridine, also have been shown to delay senescence, with in situ cardiac delivery enhancing post-infarction myocardial repair. 
 Gene therapy approaches further expand the CVA intervention toolkit, Hoshino et al reported that CM-specific Parkin overexpression reversed cardiac aging by optimizing mitochondrial turnover, thereby improving myocardial function and reducing heart failure incidence in aged mice. Similarly, mild to moderate SIRT1 overexpression (2.5- to 7.5-fold) delays cardiac aging and reduces age-related pathology in mice. In addition, Ye et al identified SIRT2 deficiency as a driver of cardiac aging in primate models, intramyocardial lentiviral SIRT2 delivery ameliorated age-associated cardiac dysfunction, highlighting the potential of SIRT2 gene therapy for reversing age-related heart dysfunction. 
Cell-based aging intervention approaches offer additional CVD therapeutic avenues. , , , , , Transplantation of 3D mini-cardiac organoids (MCOs) (consisting of human induced pluripotent stem cell–derived CMs, and endothelial and smooth muscle cells) into a rat model of MI improved cardiac function and reduced fibrosis in the infarcted area. Lei et al found that senescence-resistant human mesenchymal progenitor cell (SRC) treatment alleviated the cardiovascular degeneration and improved the pathological progression of CVDs, underscoring the therapeutic potential of stem cells. Selective senescent cell elimination strategies also demonstrate cardiovascular benefits. Dookun et al showed that the senolytic agent navitoclax improves left ventricle function and promotes myocardial angiogenesis following ischemia-reperfusion injury by depleting senescent CMs. Similarly, selective elimination of senescent VSMCs within atherosclerotic plaques reduced plaque burden and inflammatory cytokine secretion, exerting potent anti-atherosclerotic effects. In parallel, NKG2D-CAR T cells can effectively eliminate senescent cells expressing NKG2D ligands in cardiovascular tissue, thereby mitigating aging-related CVDs. Collectively, these studies suggest that targeted aging interventions not only partially reverse CVA but also may retard CVD progression, rendering the prevention and treatment of age-related cardiovascular pathologies an increasingly achievable clinical goal.
Despite this progress, challenges hinder the translation of geroprotective strategies into routine clinical practice. Clinical trials of agents with anti-CVA potential, such as metformin, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and NAD+ precursors, illustrate these limitations. Most studies target populations with preexisting conditions (eg, diabetes, established CVDs, or other high-risk groups), making it unclear whether benefits translate to healthy aging populations. Second, follow-up periods are typically short, hindering evaluation of long-term preventive effects on CVDs. Moreover, trial endpoints often focus on disease events (eg, MI or stroke) rather than direct measures of CVA, making it difficult to determine whether observed benefits result from slowing the aging process itself. Addressing these issues will be critical to advancing the clinical implementation of anti-aging strategies for cardiovascular health.

## Conclusions and Perspectives
CVA mirrors the prodromal manifestations of multiple CVDs, preceding overt clinical symptomatology while sharing core pathological mechanisms. This mechanistic concordance has spurred both preclinical and clinical investigations targeting aging-regulatory nodes to mitigate CVD risk ( ). However, translating these advances into clinical practice remains challenging. Key obstacles include incomplete understanding of causal mechanistic links, suboptimal drug delivery specificity, insufficient follow-up durations in clinical trials, and the limited translational value of existing preclinical models.
 Future research should prioritize the following aspects: first, further developing novel intervention strategies (eg, fecal microbiota transplantation) and assessment approaches, , , and elucidating the interplay mechanisms between CVA and CVDs; second, developing advanced cardiovascular-targeted delivery platforms (eg, nanocarriers with endothelial or myocardial tropism) to enhance therapeutic specificity; third, optimizing clinical trial design by extending follow-up periods and incorporating aging-related biomarkers as endpoints; and last, improving preclinical modeling through nonhuman primates.
Beyond pharmacological interventions, lifestyle interventions remain integral to CVA prevention. Structured moderate-intensity exercise regimens, , caloric restriction protocols ( ), , and intermittent fasting have been shown to delay CVA through enhancing systemic resilience, improving endothelial function and optimizing metabolic homeostasis, thereby reducing CVD incidence. However, their precise molecular underpinnings, particularly potential pharmacological mimetics, remain incompletely defined. Moving forward, integrating pharmacological strategies with evidence-based lifestyle modifications may yield synergistic benefits. Such combination approaches, underpinned by mechanistic insight, could form the basis for comprehensive, personalized prevention and management frameworks for age-related CVDs in geriatric populations.
