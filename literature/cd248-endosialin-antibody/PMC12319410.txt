## Abstract
In a phase II trial, patients with local-regionally advanced human papillomavirus–positive oropharyngeal carcinoma (n = 35) received ipilimumab (anti-CTLA4) and nivolumab (anti–PD-1) as induction immunotherapy and concurrently with radiotherapy (NCT03799445). Coprimary endpoints included 6-month complete metabolic response rate (94%) and 2-year progression-free survival (84%). Induction yielded a 46% major histopathologic response rate. Single-cell profiling revealed responders had higher baseline intratumoral CD8+ T cells with a tumor-reactive, tissue-resident memory (TRM) phenotype and a treatment-related decrease in effector regulatory T (eTreg) cells. The eTreg decrease correlated with CD8+ T-cell clonotype transitioning from TRM to effector memory and IFNG+ effector cells. In nonresponders, clonotypes transitioned to exhausted TRM and proliferating cells. Multivariable regression modeling determined that the baseline feature most associated with reduction in tumor viability was the proportion of FCGR3A-expressing NK cells, which are capable of ipilimumab-dependent depletion of CTLA4high eTregs. eTreg depletion may be critical for major response to induction dual immune checkpoint blockade (ICB).

## Significance:
The relative contributions of CD28 costimulation restoration versus Treg depletion to clinical response to CTLA4 ICB is a matter of considerable controversy. In this study, we provide compelling data that eTreg depletion is critical to tumor clearance in patients treated with dual PD-1 and CTLA4 ICB.

## Introduction
Human papillomavirus (HPV)–positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPC) has an improved prognosis relative to HPV-negative head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNC; ref. ), due in part to enhanced immune surveillance by greater numbers of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes ( – ). Most HPV16-positive tumors contain E6- and E7-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cells with a type 1 cytokine profile (e.g., IFNγ, TNFα, and IL2; ref. ), and circulating T cells that recognize E1, E2, E4, E5, and L1 epitopes have been detected in patients with HPV-positive OPC ( ). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of intratumoral HPV16-specific PD-1+CD8+ T cells revealed stem-like phenotypes with the capacity to proliferate and differentiate into effector T cells ( ). However, HPV-positive OPC tumors also have higher ratios of regulatory T cell (Treg) to CD8+ T cells than that of several other solid tumors ( ). Imaging studies have confirmed a high density of CD8+ T cells near Tregs in HPV-positive tumors ( , ). Thus, immunotherapies capable of depleting Tregs and restoring functionality of a preexisting HPV-reactive T-cell population would have tremendous therapeutic potential in newly diagnosed HPV-positive OPC.
Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting PD-1 improves survival of recurrent or metastatic HNC relative to platinum-based chemotherapy in tumors that express its ligand PD-L1 ( ) and in platinum-refractory disease ( ). However, the addition of CTLA4 to PD-(L)1 ICB did not improve survival relative to single-agent PD-(L)1 ICB ( , ). PD-(L)1 ICB concurrent with and adjuvant to chemoradiotherapy also did not improve cancer control in patients with newly diagnosed HNC ( , ). Negative effects of high-dose radiotherapy on both T-cell priming in tumor-draining lymph nodes and on systemic immunity are hypothesized to contribute to these negative results. In contrast, neoadjuvant (i.e., systemic therapy prior to resection) ICB of intact tumors may increase antigen presentation, T-cell priming, T-cell receptor (TCR) clonality, and systemic immunity versus adjuvant ICB, in part because the patient’s immune system has not been suppressed by prior therapy ( , ). In support of this, the addition of neoadjuvant PD-1 ICB improved event-free survival versus adjuvant ICB alone in advanced, resectable melanoma ( ). Given that pathologic response to neoadjuvant therapy is a surrogate for long-term disease control in several solid tumor types, including breast cancer ( ), lung cancer ( ), melanoma ( ), and HPV-negative HNC ( ), neoadjuvant trials can also provide an early signal of clinical benefit. Phase II trials of neoadjuvant PD-1 ± CTLA4 ICB in resectable HPV-negative HNC have observed major pathologic response (<10% viable tumor) rates of 7% to 35% ( , – ). Only two trials included small subsets of HPV-positive OPC ( , ). Given that data generated in HPV-negative HNC are not applicable to HPV-positive HNC because they are epidemiologically, genomically, and clinically distinct, response to neoadjuvant ICB in the HPV-positive population is largely unexplored.
Based on an abundance of immune cells, including HPV-specific T cells and Tregs, in the tumor−immune microenvironment (TIME) of newly diagnosed HPV-positive OPC, we performed a phase II clinical trial of induction dual ICB with anti-CTLA4 (ipilimumab), capable of Treg depletion in preclinical models ( – ), and anti–PD-1 (nivolumab), followed by concurrent dual ICB with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (hereafter, XRT). We profiled tumors by paired scRNA-seq and single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) at baseline and on induction (OI) to investigate mechanisms of response and resistance. This analysis associated major histopathologic response to induction ICB (iICB) with a decrease in effector Treg (eTreg) frequency and with expansion of IFNγ+ effector CD8+ T cells, which is consistent with a hypothesis of depletion via ipilimumab-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), as observed in murine models.

## Results

## Patient Baseline Characteristics
From July 2019 to November 2021, 37 patients with newly diagnosed PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) ≥ 1, AJCC eighth edition stages I to II (T1-T3N1-N2M0, T3N0M0), HPV-positive OPC (regardless of smoking history) were treated with a 6-week cycle of ipilimumab (anti-CTLA4) and nivolumab (anti–PD-1) ICB followed by a second cycle concurrent with dose and volume response-adapted XRT (INVAX trial; , see “Methods” for details about XRT adaption). To minimize effects on tumor-draining lymph nodes, elective nodal XRT was restricted to one adjacent echelon. Coprimary endpoints included estimates of 6-month complete metabolic response (CMR) rate by fluorodeoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET) and 2-year progression-free survival (PFS).
 Two patients were excluded from clinical trial endpoint analysis due to ineligibility or a protocol deviation (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Patient characteristics are shown in Supplementary Tables S1–S3. The PD-L1 CPS score was 1 to 19 in 54% and ≥20 in 46%. HPV types in tumors by digital-droplet PCR (ddPCR) included HPV16 (83%), HPV33 (9%), and other (1 each of HPV18, HPV35, and HPV45). Five cases were of intermediate risk as per RTOG0129 criteria ( ). Fifteen (43%) were never-smokers, 9 (26%) current smokers, and 11 (31%) former tobacco users. For 13 cigarette smokers, the median pack-years was 10 (interquartile range (IQR) 5–30).

## Treatment-Related Adverse Events
One dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) of grade 3 mucositis occurred among eight patients during the safety lead-in, so the study proceeded to phase II. Maximum overall treatment-related adverse events (TRAE) were grade 1 to 2 in 34% and grade ≥3 in 66% (e.g., odynophagia and mucositis; Supplementary Tables S4–S6). Any-grade TRAEs attributable to ICB included hypothyroidism (n = 16), adrenal insufficiency (n = 2), colitis (n = 2), hyperglycemia (n = 1), pancreatitis (n = 1), and myositis (n = 1). Thirteen (37%) patients experienced a grade ≥3 TRAE attributable to ICB. The only grade 4 TRAE was asymptomatic lipase increase. ICB was discontinued for five patients due to toxicity during induction (n = 3: grade 2 myositis, grade 3 pancreatitis, or grade 3 hyperglycemia), RECIST progression after cycle 1 (n = 1), or physician concern for progression (n = 1). These patients received XRT alone per INVAX plan (n = 1) or standard-of-care (SOC) XRT alone (n = 1) or concurrent with weekly platinum chemotherapy (n = 3).

## RECIST Response and Dose- and Volume-Adapted Radiotherapy
Modified RECIST 1.1 overall response after iICB was determined per independent review. Five (14%) patients had a partial response, 29 (83%) stable disease, and one, disease progression ( ). INVAX XRT plans were evaluable for 32 patients; three patients received SOC XRT plans because of RECIST progression/physician concern or toxicity during iICB. Eleven patients received ipsilateral neck XRT for a well-lateralized tonsil primary. Dose to primary clinical target volume (CTV) was 66 Gy in 18 patients, 60 Gy in 12 patients, and 50 Gy in one patient. Dose to nodal CTV was 66 Gy in 16 patients, 60 Gy in 13 patients, and 50 Gy in two patients. Compared with plans for each patient per SOC dose/fractionation and planning parameters, INVAX plans showed significantly lower doses to the thyroid, ipsilateral parotid, and oral cavity ( ) in addition to the contralateral submandibular gland and parotid and the larynx (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

## Disease Control Endpoints
All 35 evaluable patients had an FDG-PET score of 5 per Hopkins criteria at enrollment. At 3 and 6 months after XRT, 33 patients were evaluable for FDG-PET response (1 withdrawn consent and 1 not done). The CMR rate by 6 months was 94.3% [95% confidence interval (CI): 80%–99%]. Two patients with a positive FDG-PET at 6 months had biopsy-confirmed disease (one local and one regional) and were treated with stereotactic body XRT alone or after neck dissection.
After a median follow-up of 38.5 months (data cutoff on January 24, 2025), five progression events had occurred. Patterns of failure included local (n = 2), regional (n = 2), or distant (n = 1). Local failures were in the subclinical field (i.e., 36 Gy) or outside, and regional failures were in-field (60 Gy) or in the subclinical field. The patient with distant metastasis received induction then SOC cisplatin-XRT due to pancreatitis. This patient was alive with cancer at the data cutoff. One patient died of an unknown cause. Two-year PFS and overall survival (OS) rates were 84.0% (95% CI, 67%–93%; ) and 97% (95% CI, 81%–100%; Supplementary Fig. S1C), respectively. Cancer progression was more common in patients with a history of tobacco use (5/20 vs. 0/14, Fisher exact P = 0.06).

## Histopathologic Response
Paired baseline and OI tumor biopsies with available hematoxylin and eosin–stained slides were collected from 28 patients (missing data due to COVID shutdown). Of these, 96% had ≥20% reduction in tumor viability, i.e., pathologic treatment effect ( and ; ref. ). Median tumor viability declined from baseline (95% vs. 25%; P = 4.5E−13). Thirteen (46%) patients were major histopathologic responders (MHR; <10% viable tumor in biopsy) to iICB ( ). MHR status was associated with RECIST response (Fisher exact P = 0.01). No correlation was observed between time to OI biopsy and percent reduction in tumor viability, and the timing did not differ between MHRs and non-MHRs (nMHR) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

## Dynamics of Cell-Free HPV during Therapy
Because tumor biopsies may not accurately reflect overall response, we evaluated quantitative changes in plasma cell-free HPV (cfHPV) DNA as a measure of minimal residual disease (MRD; refs. – ). Of 35 patients, 91% were positive for cfHPV (≥5 copies/mL plasma) at baseline and 26 were quantifiable (≥16 copies/mL plasma; median 255, IQR 107–589), with a median time to clearance of 10 weeks, corresponding to cycle 2 day 29 ( ). In the 20 patients with quantifiable cfHPV DNA evaluable for histopathologic response, cfHPV clearance occurred on iICB in 71.4% of MHRs versus 0% of non-MHRs (5/7 vs. 0/13, Fisher exact P = 0.001; representative plots in ). MHRs without cfHPV clearance also had residual tumor (<10%) on biopsy ( ). Thus, most MHRs were MRD-negative prior to the start of XRT.

## iICB Increased Total CD8+ T Cells and the CD8:Treg Ratio in MHRs
We conducted scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq on 65 fresh tumor biopsies, including 29 baseline and OI pairs. After quality filtering, we profiled 367,451 cells, which distributed by unsupervised clustering into 11 cell types ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3A–S3D; Supplementary Table S7). Batch effects were minimal and thus not corrected (Supplementary Fig. S3E and S3F). Here, we focus on the lymphocyte population, which included 78,622 CD4+ T cells (of which 20,006 were Tregs), 76,319 CD8+ T cells, and 8,000 NK cells.
 To gain insight into mechanisms of iICB response, we focused on differences and changes in the TIME in MHRs versus nMHRs. Malignant cells were the most abundant cell type at baseline (Supplementary Fig. S3B; Supplementary Table S7). A strong correlation was observed between reduction from baseline in malignant cell proportion in scRNA-seq data and in tumor viability in histopathology ( ). Notably, malignant cell proportion reduced significantly OI in MHRs but not nMHRs ( ; Supplementary Table S8). High rates of cfHPV clearance and reductions in malignant cell proportion in scRNA-seq data support our histopathologic classification of MHR status.
MHRs had a significant increase in the proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in response to iICB, whereas nMHRs did not ( and ; Supplementary Table S8). MHRs also had significantly higher CD8:Treg ratios OI than nMHRs ( ). Given this ratio is associated with both treatment response and survival in a variety of solid tumors ( , ), we sought to identify its underlying contributing factors in the TIME.

## iICB Decreased eTreg Frequency in MHRs
We defined 14 CD4+ T-cell clusters ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4C; Supplementary Tables S9 and S10) and observed no differences in their baseline frequency in MHRs versus nMHRs (Supplementary Table S11). OI, however, MHRs had a significant reduction in frequency of overall Tregs and the eTreg cluster but not the resting Treg cluster ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4D and S4E; Supplementary Table S11). Mean eTreg frequency declined by 60.5% in MHRs versus a 4.8% increase in nMHRs. Compared with resting Tregs, eTregs expressed high levels of FOXP3, CTLA4, IL2RA(CD25), CCR8, and the activation markers ICOS, TNFRSF4(OX40), TNFRSF9(4-1BB), and TNFRSF18(GITR) ( ; Supplementary Fig. S5), phenotypically analogous to the highly immunosuppressive Tregs reported in lung cancer, liver cancer, and melanoma ( ).
 We integrated scRNA-seq data with scTCR-seq data for the CD4+ T-cell population to further investigate eTreg changes from baseline. In MHRs but not in nMHRs, significant declines in eTreg frequency were observed OI regardless of whether the cells had clonotypes that were detected at a single timepoint or at both timepoints (Supplementary Fig. S6A). To investigate the contribution of eTreg reprogramming/phenotype switching to eTreg decline, we focused our analysis on eTregs with clonotypes detected at baseline and OI. In comparison with nMHRs, clonotype sharing in MHRs was significantly less frequent between baseline eTregs and other CD4+ clusters OI ( ). Clonotype sharing was similarly less frequent in MHRs versus nMHRs between OI eTregs and other CD4+ clusters at baseline, arguing against a treatment effect ( ). We also noted that baseline clonotype sharing between eTregs and other CD4+ clusters was higher in nMHRs than in MHRs (13% vs. 6.5%, P = 2.4E−12; Supplementary Fig. S6B). Collectively, these observations did not support a greater effect of iICB on eTreg reprogramming in MHRs versus nMHRs. We observed no changes in expression of the transcription factors FOXP3, IKZF2 (HELIOS), or PRDM1 (BLIMP1) or of the chromatin-modifying methylase EZH2, which are critical for Treg phenotype and function ( – ), in MHRs or nMHRs OI ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6C). Thus, reprogramming did not seem to explain the marked decline in eTregs in MHRs but not nMHRs. However, eTreg frequency decreased on iICB with increased CTLA4 expression, and this was significantly greater in MHRs than in nMHRs ( and ; see “Methods”). A clustering analysis limited to total Tregs confirmed the decrease was limited to subclusters with high CTLA4 expression in MHRs (Supplementary Fig. S6D and S6E). Treg clonotype frequency was very low overall and seemed unrelated to the decrease (Supplementary Fig. S6F and S6G). In vitro, Treg depletion increases as surface CTLA4 expression increases ( ). Therefore, our data are consistent with a role for eTreg depletion in response to iICB in MHRs.

## Baseline CD8+ T Cells had Higher Cytotoxicity and Frequency of Tissue-Resident Memory Cells in MHRs
We compared the characteristics of CD8+ T cells in MHRs and nMHRs to evaluate potential associations with response and/or eTreg decrease. Comparison of total CD8+ T-cell gene expression profiles revealed significant differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. S7A; Supplementary Table S12). For example, basal expression of CCL5, a proinflammatory chemokine for NK and other immune cells, was higher in MHRs, whereas basal expression of ferritin heavy chain 1, associated with Treg recruitment and poor OS in HNC ( ), was higher in nMHRs. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that several hallmark immune response pathways (e.g., IFNα and IFNγ response) were enriched in MHRs relative to nMHRs at baseline (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Moreover, baseline CD8+ T-cell cytotoxic, immune checkpoint, and tumor-reactive gene expression signature (GES) scores were notably higher in MHRs ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7C). MHRs also had a higher proportion of tumor-reactive T cells at baseline ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7D).
 To gain greater insight into CD8+ T-cell phenotypes, we performed a clustering analysis that identified 15 clusters ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S8A–S8C; Supplementary Tables S13 and S14). These included a naïve or stem-like cluster; two effector memory (TEM) clusters, three effector clusters, and four tissue-resident memory (TRM) clusters. The TEM metacluster comprised a cluster marked by high CD44 expression, TEM1, and one marked by high GZMK expression, TEM2. The effector metacluster contained clusters defined by high expression of genes encoding the proinflammatory cytokines TNFα and IFNγ, Effector1, or by IFN-stimulated genes, Effector2.
Median frequencies of CD8+ clusters at baseline did not differ between samples from primary tumor versus nodal metastases, which was also true for CD4+ clusters (Supplementary Fig. S9A–S9D). Compared with nMHRs, MHRs had a higher baseline proportion of cells within the TRM metacluster (TRM1–4; ), characterized by high expression of both ITGAE and CXCL13. The TRM metacluster had higher tumor-reactive GES scores than the other metaclusters (Supplementary Fig. S10A and S10B). Within the TRM metacluster, the immune checkpoint GES score, also an indicator of exhaustion, increased from TRM1 to TRM2 to TRM3 to TRM4, which had the highest score of any CD8+ cluster ( ). TRM4 had high CTLA4 and HAVCR2(TIM3) expression, consistent with terminally exhausted T cells ( ). TRM clusters had high frequency and abundance of cytotoxic genes ( ; Supplementary Fig. S10C). We note that mean tumor-reactive and cytotoxic GES scores for all CD8+ T-cell clusters were highly correlated ( ). Notably, CTLA4 expression was much lower in TRM4 cells than eTregs (Supplementary Fig. S10D). Multiplexed immunofluorescence imaging demonstrated a trend of higher infiltration by CD45RO+PD-L1+CD8+ T cells, which encompass TRM and other activated memory cells, in subsets of MHRs versus nMHRs (Supplementary Fig. S11A–S11C).
Several prior studies have linked TRM CD8+ T cells, enriched for tumor antigen–specific TCRs, to ICB response ( – ). We conclude that MHRs had a higher baseline population of CD8+ T cells with a tumor-reactive phenotype in the TIME than nMHRs.

## Changes in Proportions of CD8+ T-cell Clusters Correlated with eTreg Decrease
We next investigated changes from baseline in the proportion of total T cells in each CD8+ T-cell cluster OI. MHRs had more dynamic changes in cluster proportions than nMHRs ( ; Supplementary Table S15). In MHRs, the most exhausted TRM cluster, TRM4, decreased significantly ( and ), which would be predicted if TRM4 clonotypes contracted after tumor clearance. Effector1 (IFNG), which has the potential to upregulate MHC class I molecules to enhance antigen presentation in the TIME ( ), had the highest increase in odds OI ( and ). Both TEM clusters also increased significantly ( , and ). In contrast, in nMHRs, TRM3, TRM4, and the proliferating cluster increased ( ). Notably, the increased frequency from baseline in Effector1 (IFNG), as well as in TEM1 (CD44) and TEM2 (GZMK), correlated significantly with decreased frequency of the eTreg cluster ( and ). In preclinical models, expansion of the effector CD8+ compartment was observed upon IL2RA+ eTreg depletion, due in part to increased IL2 availability in the TIME ( ). In our data, expansion of effector CD8+ T cells was concurrent with a decrease in eTregs in the MHR TIME.

## MHRs had Greater CD8+ T-cell Clonality at Baseline and Clonal Dynamics on iICB
T-cell activation upon binding a cognate antigen–MHC complex results in clonal expansion. To identify clonally expanded, high-frequency TCR clonotypes, which are likely to be tumor-specific, we used the integrated scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq data to evaluate the clonal dynamics of the TIME TCR repertoire. CD4+ T cells overall had high diversity and low clonality (Supplementary Fig. S12A–S12C). Therefore, we focused on the CD8+ TCR repertoire. CD8+ T cells in MHRs had a higher degree of baseline clonality than in nMHRs, as evaluated by diversity indices and clonotype abundances ( ; Supplementary Fig. S13A–S13C). TCR clonotypes with high frequency (≥1%) mapped predominantly within the TRM metacluster ( ; Supplementary Fig. S13D) and were significantly more common in MHRs versus nMHRs at baseline and OI (Supplementary Fig. S13E and S13F), in agreement with the higher baseline tumor-reactive phenotype in the TIME of MHRs. Compared with nMHRs, the proportion of TCR clonotypes that expanded or contracted from baseline was significantly higher in MHRs ( and ). Novel clonotypes were also more frequent in MHRs (Supplementary Fig. S13G). In both patient groups, persistent TCR clonotypes had significantly higher frequencies OI and higher tumor-reactive GES scores than did novel clones regardless of whether they significantly expanded or contracted or did not significantly change (Supplementary Fig. S13H and S13I). The change from baseline in the frequency of significantly expanded clones was higher than the frequency of clones that were both novel and expanded (Supplementary Fig. S13J), favoring a greater role for clonal revival ( ) than replacement ( ).
 

## Phenotypic Difference in Expanded or Contracted Clones between MHRs and nMHRs
A total of 79 CD8+ TCR clonotypes (47 in MHRs and 32 in nMHRs) expanded significantly in response to iICB and distributed predominantly among the TEM and TRM clusters ( ; Supplementary Table S16). Expanded clonotype distribution differed between response groups ( ). Clonotypes that expanded OI were significantly more likely to be in the TEM1, TEM2, Effector1 (IFNG), and least exhausted TRM1 clusters in MHRs, in contrast to the TRM2, most exhausted TRM4, and proliferating clusters in nMHRs ( ; Supplementary Fig. S14A). Expanded novel TCR clonotypes exhibited a preference for Effector1 (IFNG) and TRM1 phenotypes in MHRs and TRM3 and TRM4 in nMHRs (Supplementary Fig. S14B–S14D).
A majority of significantly contracted TCR clonotypes had TRM or Effector1 (IFNG) phenotypes in MHRs (44 clonotypes) and TRM4 or TEM2 phenotypes in nMHRs (13 clonotypes; Supplementary Fig. S14E–S14G). In nMHRs, in which tumor cells remained detectable in the TIME, fewer instances of clonotype contraction were observed ( ).

## Greater Cell-State Transitioning among CD8+ TCR Clonotypes in MHRs
Individual TCR clonotypes were shared among several CD8+ clusters across timepoints ( ; Supplementary Fig. S15A–S15C), indicating possible transitions from one phenotype to another, i.e., cell-state transitions, in response to iICB. To evaluate the effects of iICB on cell-state transitions, we developed a transition index as a quantitative measure of TCR clonotype sharing between CD8+ clusters before and after iICB (see “Methods”). MHRs had significantly higher transition indices than nMHRs ( ). Focusing specifically on treatment-expanded clonotypes, which are more likely to be tumor-reactive, we noted that in MHRs clones transitioned from TRM3 to Effector1 (IFNG) and TEM1 (CD44) clusters, in contrast to transitions from TRM3 to TRM4 to the proliferating cluster in nMHRs ( and ). In agreement with the clonotype sharing analysis, RNA velocity analysis identified two divergent differentiation trajectories in MHRs and nMHRs. In MHRs, TRM clusters differentiated toward effector and memory states, whereas in nMHRs, these clusters differentiated toward terminally exhausted states ( and ). Cell-state transitions were less frequent among contracted clonotypes than expanded clonotypes (Supplementary Fig. S15A, C, and D).
 

## Identification of HPV-Specific TCRs among Expanded CD8+ T-cell Clonotypes
To determine whether expanded TCR clonotypes were HPV-specific, TCRs of clonotypes that were detected in HPV16-positive tumors at high frequency OI and that had undergone a cell-state transition were selected for experimental validation. A total of 30 TCRs were expressed in luciferase reporter Jurkat cells and then stimulated with peptides representing the entire HPV16 proteome (see “Methods”). Three TCR clonotypes demonstrated significant luciferase expression compared with controls in response to three distinct HPV16 peptides (Supplementary Fig. S16; Supplementary Table S16 and S17). Their cell-state distributions at baseline and OI were representative of overall differences between MHRs and nMHRs ( ).

## Baseline Frequency of FCGR3A+ NK Cells Associated with Response
In preclinical models, CTLA4 ICB has been shown to selectively deplete CTLA4+ Tregs via ADCC ( , ), which can be mediated by NK cells ( ). CD16a/Fcγ receptor IIIa (encoded by FCGR3A) activates NK cells upon binding to an antibody Fc region and is critical for ADCC ( ). We profiled 8,000 NK cells in our dataset (Supplementary Table S7). Although overall NK cell proportion did not differ at baseline, MHRs had higher baseline infiltration by NK cells expressing FCGR3A than nMHRs ( ). Compared with FCGR3A− NK cells, FCGR3A+ NK cells had higher expression of several cytotoxic mediators, including PRF1, GZMH, NKG7 ( ), and CST7 (Supplementary Fig. S17A; Supplementary Table S18; ref. ). To assess FCGR3A+ NK-cell cytotoxic potential, we examined GES scores for stimulatory and inhibitory NK-cell receptors and for cell-mediated cytotoxicity (see “Methods”; ref. ). FCGR3A+ NK cells had higher cytotoxic and stimulatory GES scores versus FCGR3A− NK cells (Supplementary Fig. S17B).
 

## TIME Features Associated with Cancer Progression
We explored TIME features in patients who did (n = 4) and did not (n = 26) experience disease progression during follow-up. Patients who progressed had a significantly lower baseline frequency of FCGR3A+ NK cells ( ; Supplementary Table S19). Moreover, patients who progressed maintained a higher frequency of eTregs OI ( ).

## Multivariable Regression Model–Associated Baseline Proportion of FCGR3A+ NK Cells with Reduction in Tumor Viability
Our analysis identified several differences between MHRs and nMHRs in baseline and OI features of the TIME, including proportions of T-cell clusters and NK-cell subsets, CD8+ T-cell GES and immune-related pathway scores, and TCR clonality. To determine which baseline TIME and clinical features (Supplementary Table S20) were most influential in predicting treatment response, we built a multivariable LASSO regression model with an outcome of percent reduction in tumor viability. The prediction of the model, which included 16 features, correlated strongly with the observed reduction in tumor viability (R-squared = 0.8882; ). Consistent with our mechanistic hypothesis of eTreg depletion driving response, the most influential factor was the basal proportion of ADCC-capable FCGR3A+ NK cells among all NK cells ( ). A separate model that evaluated changes in TIME features from baseline to OI identified percent increase in the Effector1 (IFNG) CD8 cluster, which correlated inversely with eTreg frequency ( and ), as the most influential factor for reduction in tumor viability (Supplementary Fig. S18).

## Discussion
Patients with newly diagnosed HPV-positive OPC were responsive to CTLA4 and PD-1 iICB, as demonstrated by a 46% MHR rate. High plasma cfHPV clearance rates (∼71%) in patients with MHR indicated that most became MRD-negative during induction. Single-cell profiling of paired tumor biopsy samples in patients with and without MHR revealed a higher baseline frequency of CD8+ T cells with an antigen-experienced, tumor-reactive TRM phenotype in MHRs. A significant decline in eTregs was observed in patients with but not without MHR. eTreg decrease was associated with CD8+ T-cell clonotypes undergoing cell-state transitions from TRM3 toward Effector1 (IFNG) and TEM1/2 phenotypes.
Elucidation of the biological mechanisms contributing to response to iICB will inform strategies to increase MHR rates. The relative contribution of restoration of CD28 costimulation versus Treg depletion to the clinical efficacy of ipilimumab in humans is a matter of ongoing debate ( – ). Sharma and colleagues ( ) reported that intratumoral CD8+ T-cell density measured by IHC increased in response to ipilimumab in several tumor types, whereas Treg density did not decline. Potential limitations of this analysis include lack of stratification by treatment response, use of FoxP3 alone to identify Tregs, and analysis of posttreatment rather than on-treatment tumors. In our analysis, several lines of evidence suggest that eTreg depletion is a contributing factor to response to CTLA4 and PD-1 iICB. Treg decline was observed only in MHRs, was specific to the immunosuppressive eTreg cluster with high CTLA4 expression, and increased as CTLA4 expression increased. A decrease in 4-1BB+ Tregs was similarly associated with major pathologic response to neoadjuvant CTLA4 and PD-1 in HPV-negative HNC profiled by scRNA-seq ( ). A similar eTreg population, with high expression of GITR and OX40, was reported to restrict response to PD-1 ICB in lung cancer ( ). Our data are consistent with murine models that showed that high CTLA4 expression explained the preferential depletion of Tregs by CTLA4 ICB ( ) and that tumor response was dependent upon Fc competence to mediate ADCC and Treg depletion ( – , ). Fc engineering of ipilimumab to increase affinity for CD16/FcγRIIIa resulted in more effective depletion of Tregs with higher expression of CTLA4 than CD8+ T cells ( ).
Lineage reprogramming has been proposed to explain decreased eTreg frequency ( ). Treg reprogramming due to decreased TGF-β was implicated in response of HPV-negative HNC to induction of bintrafusp alfa ( ), a bifunctional anti–PD-L1 and TGF-β trap. However, the low frequency (<6%) of TCR clonotype sharing we observed between baseline eTregs and OI CD4+ clusters was insufficient to explain the average 60% decline in eTregs in MHRs. Decreased eTreg frequency was not accompanied by decreased expression of FOXP3, TGFB1, or activated Treg markers [e.g., IL2RA(CD25) and TNFRSF9(4-1BB)] as reported in melanoma after combination therapy ( , ). Our data support eTreg depletion rather than reprogramming in MHRs. Given the more frequent clonotype sharing between eTregs and other CD4+ clusters in nMHRs versus MHRs, the TIME in nMHRs may itself promote reprogramming of CD4+ T cells to an eTreg phenotype, possibly contributing to the reduced response in this group.
HPV-positive cancers have higher infiltration by FCGR3A+ NK cells and higher CTLA4 expression than HPV-negative HNC ( ). Analogous to improved response to ipilimumab in melanomas with higher tumor-infiltrating FCGR3A+ myeloid cells ( ), MHRs had a significantly higher baseline frequency of FCGR3A+ NK cells capable of mediating ADCC. Our multivariable regression model indicated that baseline FCGR3A+ NK-cell proportion was the most influential factor in tumor-cell reduction. Our data are consistent with preclinical models that implicate ADCC by NK cells as a mechanism of response to several antitumor antibodies, including trastuzumab ( ), cetuximab ( ), and obinotumzumab ( ), and have shown that antibody engagement of FcγRIIIa on NK cells enhances NK-cell degranulation, cytokine production, TNF death receptor expression, and tumor-cell killing ( ). Treg depletion may also enhance direct killing of tumor cells by NK cells ( ). A role for innate NK cell immunity in control of HPV-positive cancers is supported by the development of HPV-positive HNC in a patient with an NK cell–specific germline IL2RG mutation and decreased functional CD16+ NK cells ( ).
In murine models of melanoma ( , ), colon cancer ( , , ), and fibrosarcoma ( ), response to CTLA4 ICB has been linked to FcR-dependent depletion of Tregs. Melanoma and fibrosarcoma models implicate CD16+ macrophages as mediating Treg depletion ( , ), whereas colon cancer models have implicated NK cells ( ). In patients with melanoma, both higher basal CD16+ monocyte infiltration ( ) and CD56 expression ( ) have been reported in patients with response to ipilimumab. Given the role of NK cells in the innate immunity and cytotoxicity of virus-infected cells ( ), it is possible that the relative contributions of NK cells and monocytes in Treg depletion and response to ipilimumab is different in virus- and non–virus-related tumors. Collectively, our data are consistent with a hypothesis of eTreg depletion by highly cytotoxic FCGR3A+ NK cells via ipilimumab-induced ADCC. This novel insight underscores the potential of optimizing ADCC by antibodies targeting intratumoral eTregs, such as Fc-enhanced anti-CD25 ( ), anti-CCR8 ( ), and anti-TIGIT ( ).
In our analysis, a decline in eTreg frequency correlated with an increase in frequency of Effector1 (IFNG) CD8+ T cells, which was the treatment-related feature with the greatest influence on tumor viability reduction per multivariable regression. Expansion of Effector1 (IFNG) and TEM clusters in MHRs is consistent with preclinical studies linking Treg depletion to an increase in T cells with an effector memory phenotype and long-lasting memory ( ), which may explain the very preliminary associations we observed between low baseline FCGR3A+ NK-cell frequency, high OI eTreg frequency, and cancer progression. Preclinical models also support increased presence of intratumoral IFNγ+ effector T cells in response to combination CTLA4 and PD-1 ICB versus PD-1 alone ( ). Based on TCR clonotype sharing among clusters across timepoints and RNA velocity analysis, our data support a model in which TRM3 cells transition toward either Effector1 (IFNG) and TEM cells or terminal exhaustion, with Treg depletion (and likely increased IL2 in the TIME) being essential to the former ( ). Although our scTCR-seq data support cell-state transitions of TRM cells toward IFNγ-producing effectors, we cannot rule out the possibility of recruitment of clonotype-identical cells from the circulation. In agreement with prior reports ( , ), we observed a decrease in frequency and clonotype contraction of TRM cells with a terminally exhausted phenotype (TRM4) in MHRs, suggesting contraction in the absence of antigen stimulation due to tumor clearance. The co-occurrence of clonotype contraction in TRM phenotypes and tumor clearance in MHRs is consistent with the T cells being primarily tumor-reactive. We cannot exclude the possibility of ipilimumab-mediated clearance by ADCC of TRM4 cells, given they highly express CTLA4. Nevertheless, the increase of cells with a TEM phenotype in MHRs suggests the possible formation of long-lasting antitumor memory. Expanded clonotypes that were confirmed to be HPV-specific in vitro had memory phenotypes in scRNA-seq data. We note that no MHRs experienced cancer progression.
Although not classified as MHRs, nMHRs nevertheless experienced a 10% to 80% reduction in tumor viability in response to iICB. In most neoadjuvant PD-1 ICB trials in HPV-negative HNC, these patients would be classified as responders ( , , ). In nMHRs, we observed significant OI expansion and TCR clonotype sharing among CD8+ T-cell clusters with high frequency and abundance of CTLA4 and PD-1 expression (i.e., TRM3/4), which also had high cytotoxic and checkpoint scores, consistent with reports that CD8+ TRM cells are tumor-reactive and responsive to PD-1 ICB ( , ). Our findings concur with a previous scRNA-seq analyses that observed ITGAE+ CD8+ T-cell expansion in six HPV-negative tumors after neoadjuvant PD-1 ± CTLA4 ICB ( ) but disagree with the expansion of ZNF683+ CD8+ T cells (i.e., TRM2) in eight HPV-negative tumors after PD-1 ICB ( ). Given eTreg frequency was not affected by iICB in nMHRs, our data suggest that reductions in tumor viability in nMHRs are largely attributable to restored proliferation and effector function of exhausted CTLA4+PD-1+ TRM cells due to dual ICB ( ). These transitions are analogous to the progenitor exhausted to exhausted/proliferative exhausted cell-state transitions among CD8+ T cells observed in response to PD-1 ICB alone ( ). Our data suggest that eTreg persistence or enhanced reprogramming toward an eTreg phenotype in the nMHR TIME may promote differentiation of CD8+ TRM cell toward exhausted phenotypes.
Several additional observations from this study should be briefly noted. As observed in melanoma, radiologic response underestimated histopathologic response and should not be used to determine benefit from iICB ( ). For example, a patient with RECIST progression had a 60% decrease in tumor viability per histopathologic review and was thus a “pseudoprogressor” by RECIST. Higher response rates reported in this study versus two neoadjuvant ICB trials with small subsets of HPV-positive OPC ( , ) may be due to the use of PD-1 ICB alone in one trial and/or our restriction of eligibility to PD-L1 CPS–positive cancers. A role for ipilimumab in increasing response is indicated by the 46% MHR rate we observed in this study in comparison with 5.9% in the CheckMate 358 trial with neoadjuvant nivolumab alone in HPV-positive tumors ( ). All progression events occurred in tobacco-exposed patients, consistent with decreased CD8+ T-cell infiltration ( ) and poor disease control in smokers ( ). Reductions in circulating NK cells ( ) and in NK-cell IFNγ production ( ) have been reported in smokers. This highlights the need for further elucidation of the effects of smoking on the TIME. Due to advanced nodal stage and 10 or more pack-years of tobacco use, 20% of patients in this study would be excluded from NRG HN005 ( ). A comparable 2-year PFS of 98% would therefore be unexpected. The 2-year PFS of 84% was similar to the cisplatin-based chemoradiation arm in RTOG1016 ( ) but failed to meet the 91% target for the study, which was based upon a historic control of patients enrolled in RTOG1016 with characteristics matching INVAX eligibility criteria. A higher-than-expected proportion of current smokers in this study versus RTOG1016 (26% vs. 10%) might have contributed to this failure. Concurrent administration of ICB with radiotherapy also might have compromised disease control ( ). Although this study was designed as an alternative approach to therapy rather than as a de-escalation protocol, the XRT plan led to a marked reduction in dose to critical structures (e.g., salivary glands and larynx). The grade ≥3 AE rate was higher during iICB than reported in recurrent or metastatic HNC (37% vs. 28.2%; ref. ), as expected in patients with intact immune systems.
Our study has several major limitations, including an analysis stratified by histopathology on a biopsy because HPV-positive OPC is treated primarily with chemoradiation and not resection. However, Ferris, Topalian, and colleagues established a precedent for use of tumor biopsies in assessment of response to neoadjuvant nivolumab in patients with HPV-positive head and neck cancers in CheckMate 358 ( ). Moreover, we observed high rates of cfHPV clearance, a biomarker of overall tumor clearance, in patients with MHR ( ). In lung cancer, clearance of cfDNA during neoadjuvant therapy was associated with pathologic complete response ( , ). Additionally, in melanoma, pathologic response in an index lymph node is highly concordant with the entire resected specimen ( ), and histopathologic features in early on-treatment biopsies associate with survival ( ). We cannot entirely exclude a possible contribution of anatomic site of the biopsy (i.e., primary vs. node) on results. Analyses based upon paired tumor samples support the inference that interval changes are due to iICB. As with most translational studies in human subjects, our results are correlative and do not directly demonstrate mechanism or cause and effect. Given all patients received both CTLA4 and PD-1 ICB, we cannot evaluate their independent effects. Our TCR validation was limited to HPV16 antigens, and the overall analysis was limited to lymphocytes. Ongoing experiments will assess the reactivity of TCRs to HPV and tumor neoantigens. Future analyses will examine the contributions of FCGR3A-expressing myeloid cells and germline FCGR3A polymorphisms ( , ) to Treg decrease and investigate associations between nonlymphoid and malignant cell phenotypes with response. Despite the small sample size of our phase II study, the correlative data presented are focused exclusively on statistically significant results and to our knowledge are the largest single-cell data from a clinical trial of induction or neoadjuvant ICB in HPV-positive OPC.
In conclusion, our single-cell profiling of HPV-positive OPC before and on iICB is consistent with a hypothesis that eTreg depletion is critical for maximal restoration of effector function. Whereas patients without eTreg depletion still benefit from release of CTLA4 and PD-1 inhibition on CD8+ TRM effector function, eTreg depletion seems critical for reshaping of the TIME. Therefore, induction/neoadjuvant immunotherapy combinations designed to enhance baseline antitumor immunity (e.g., therapeutic HPV vaccine) and eTreg depletion in the TIME have considerable promise for the treatment of newly diagnosed HPV-positive OPC. The results from this trial support further investigation of induction/neoadjuvant immunotherapy for HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancers, particularly given positive results from a clinical trial in resectable HNC (https://www.merck.com/news/mercks-keytruda-pembrolizumab-met-primary-endpoint-of-event-free-survival-efs-as-perioperative-treatment-regimen-in-patients-with-resected-locally-advanced-head-and-neck-squamous-c/).

## Methods

## Clinical Trial Protocol
Eligible patients had untreated, pathologically confirmed, AJCC eighth edition stages I to II (T1-T3N1-N2, T3N0M0; ref. ) squamous cell carcinoma of the oropharynx; p16-positive by IHC ( ); formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor–positive for HPV RNA by RNAscope HPV HR8 ( ) or HPV DNA by in situ hybridization (ISH; ref. ) or fine-needle aspiration cytology–positive by Roche Cobas ( ); PD-L1 (clone 22C3, Dako PharmDx) CPS ≥1 ( ); Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0 to 1 (asymptomatic or symptomatic but ambulatory ( ); age ≥18 years; measurable disease by RECIST 1.1 ( ); and adequate bone marrow, hepatic, and renal function. Patients with postdiagnostic tonsillectomy status were eligible if RECIST measurable nodal disease was present. Lifetime tobacco exposure and pack-years of cigarette smoking exposure were determined at enrollment. Major exclusion criteria included active autoimmune disease and diagnosis of other invasive malignancy within the prior 3 years.
For eligibility testing, FFPE tumor specimens were tested for p16 expression by IHC using a mouse mAb (MTM Laboratories) visualized with the Ventana XT autostainer using the iVIEW secondary detection kit (Ventana). p16 expression was scored as positive if strong and diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic staining were present in ≥70% of the tumor cells ( ). HPV E6/E7 mRNA expression in FFPE tumor specimens was detected by use of RNAscope ISH Probe HPV HR 8 assay (types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 45, 52, and 58) and interpreted as described by Bishop and colleagues ( ). ISH was performed with the Dako GenPoint signal amplification method for biotinylated probes (Agilent) to evaluate FFPE tumors for HPV DNA ( ). Specific staining of tumor cell nuclei for HPV in either analysis defined a positive tumor.
Treatment consisted of intravenous nivolumab at a dose of 3 mg/kg on days 1, 15, and 29 and ipilimumab at 1 mg/kg on day 1 of a 6-week cycle. A second 6-week cycle was administered concurrently with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). Acute toxicity was evaluated weekly during therapy using Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events, version 4.0 (CTCAE 4.0; ctep.cancer.gov/protocolDevelopment/electronic_applications/ctc.htm). Immunotherapy was delayed or permanently discontinued due to TRAEs per management algorithms included in the protocol. Immunotherapy was resumed if TRAEs resolved to grade ≤1 within 6 (nivolumab) or 12 (ipilimumab) weeks, and the corticosteroid dose was prednisone ≤10 mg/day.
CT or MRI of the neck was performed at baseline and after induction immunotherapy (i.e., cycle 1), and response was determined per RECIST 1.1 ( ) by independent review by a study neuroradiologist blinded to clinical data.
The trial IMRT strategy consisted of both dose and volume reduction. The primary and nodal gross tumor volume (GTV) was based on postinduction volume as assessed by the treating radiation oncologist to allow for GTV volume reduction in response to iICB. Primary and nodal disease were assessed separately, based upon categorization as low (primary <3 cm, node <4 cm) or intermediate (primary ≥3 cm, node ≥4 cm) volume. All patients received an initial plan of 36 to 44 Gy in 20 fractions over 4 weeks (36 Gy to subclinical disease and 40–44 Gy to gross disease) followed by a boost of 5 to 10 fractions. Primary/nodal disease with complete response received a 10-Gy boost in five fractions (total dose 50 Gy). Low-volume primary/nodal disease received 40 Gy followed by a 20-Gy boost at 2.0 Gy per fraction (total dose 60 Gy). Intermediate-volume disease received 42 to 44 Gy followed by a boost of 21 to 22 Gy at 2.1 to 2.2 Gy per fraction (total 63–66 Gy). All patients received dose- and volume-reduced IMRT to at-risk nodal regions to minimize potential effects on T-cell priming. The dose to the elective treatment volume was reduced to 36 Gy in 20 fractions at 1.8 Gy per fraction and at-risk first-echelon nodal stations.
FDG-PET was performed at baseline and 12 weeks after end of IMRT, and metabolic response was evaluated on a five-point scale per the Hopkins criteria ( ) as complete (1–2), indeterminant (3), or positive (4–5, i.e., concerning for possible residual tumor). Patients with a score of 4 to 5 underwent further evaluation, including biopsy and surgical resection as appropriate. Patients with an indeterminant score or without histopathologic confirmation of residual tumor had a repeat FDG-PET/CT at 6 months after the end of IMRT. The 6-month CMR rate is defined as the proportion of patients who had a score of 1 to 2 at 3 or 6 months.
To assess tumor status, physical exam and imaging studies were performed every 3 months for 2 years, every 6 months through year five, and then annually.
The protocol was registered with the NCI (NCT03799445) and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC). All patients provided written informed consent.

## Clinical Trial Design and Statistical Analysis
The study design included a safety evaluation of the treatment regimen in the first eight enrolled patients. This safety lead-in had a principal outcome of DLT, defined as follows: any grade ≥ 3 CTCAE v4 TRAE that did not resolve to ≤grade 1 within 28 days; radiotherapy delay of >1 week due to TRAE; radiotherapy discontinuation due to TRAE; or grade ≥ 3 radiation mucositis that did not resolve to grade < 1 within 28 days of end of radiotherapy. The period of observation for DLT was 28 days from the end of radiotherapy. The study would proceed to phase 2 in the event of ≤2 DLTs or be discontinued in the event of >2 DLTs. With a cohort of eight patients, the probability of the combination immunotherapy being judged to be too toxic when the true toxicity rate is 45% or higher is at least 78%. If the true toxicity rate is 20% or lower, the probability that the therapy will be safe is 80%.
The primary endpoints of the phase II study were to estimate the 6-month CMR rate by PET and the 2-year PFS rate. These were compared with an expected 87% CMR ( ) and 2-year PFS of 91% per RTOG1016 data restricted to INVAX eligibility criteria. The original noninferiority design had a sample size of 170 and 82% power to establish noninferiority of the 6-month complete response rate when compared with that expected from cisplatin-based chemoradiotherapy (0.87), but funding was discontinued by the sponsor at a planned interim analysis after 35 patients were enrolled. Based on the recommendation of the University of Texas MD Anderson Investigational New Drug Office, which served as the data safety monitoring board, two enrolled patients, one with a major protocol deviation (proton therapy) and one who was found upon review to be ineligible (presence of metastasis at enrollment), were replaced. Therefore, the coprimary endpoints are reported here for 35 evaluable patients of 37 enrolled. PFS was defined as time from the start of protocol therapy to death or the first documented relapse, categorized as local-regional (primary site or regional nodes) failure or distant metastases. OS was defined as time from the start of protocol therapy to death. OS and PFS rates were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method. The 95% pointwise CI was obtained by applying the logarithmic transformation to the Kaplan–Meier estimate of the PFS function, and the variance of the Kaplan–Meier estimate was calculated using the Greenwood formula.
Secondary endpoints included acute and chronic toxicity per CTCAE. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics and toxicity events. Fisher exact test and Wilcoxon sign-rank test were used for comparisons between groups for dichotomous and continuous variables, respectively. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate relationships between two continuous variables. Analyses were performed in STATA 13.0 (RRID: SCR_012763), SAS 9.4 (RRID: SCR_008567), or R 4.1.2 (RRID: SCR_001905).

## Comparison of Radiotherapy Plans with SOC
To assess dose and volume reductions achieved with INVAX, all INVAX RT plans (n = 32) were replanned using MDACC SOC dose/fractionation and planning parameters. SOC plans adhered to the same precise target delineation guidelines used in INVAX, utilizing advanced imaging modalities such as planning CT, MRI, dual-energy CT, and PET-CT. Similar planning target volume (PTV) margins limited to 2 to 4 mm from the CTV were used for accuracy and reproducibility. These included target coverage >95%, dose heterogeneity <110%, and specific dose constraints for key structures: larynx (<28 Gy mean), spinal cord (<45 Gy max), parotid (<26 Gy mean), and contralateral submandibular gland (SMG) (<35 Gy mean). Institutional standard dosing parameters of 66 to 70 Gy for gross disease and 56 Gy for subclinical disease in 33 fractions were used for SOC replans. In comparison with INVAX dose parameters, the SOC plans prescribed 70 Gy for intermediate-volume GTV (vs. 66 Gy for INVAX), 66 Gy for low-volume GTV (vs. 60 Gy for INVAX), 60 to 63 Gy for primary or nodal disease achieving a complete response after induction therapy (vs. 50 Gy for INVAX), and 56 Gy for elective subclinical disease (vs. 36 Gy for INVAX). Elective volumes in INVAX plans were confined to involved nodes and first-echelon nodal stations, sparing contralateral neck and certain nodal levels. In contrast, SOC plans included second-echelon stations. For instance, MDACC SOC volumes for a tongue base primary with an ipsilateral level 2 node encompassed ipsilateral levels 1 to 5 and contralateral levels 2 to 5, whereas INVAX omitted contralateral levels 3 to 5 and ipsilateral levels 4 to 5.

## Biospecimen Collection
The primary tumor or a single involved lymph node underwent core needle biopsy (2 mm × 4) at baseline and OI during cycle 1 (Supplementary Table S3). Two cores were submitted for FFPE preservation, and two were immersed in ice-cold RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS, transferred to the laboratory, and immediately processed for scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq. Whole blood was collected at baseline, days 1, 15, and 29 of cycles 1 and 2, and at 8 and 12 weeks after the end of IMRT. Plasma was separated by standard operating procedure and stored at −80°C. Missing samples for tumor or blood-based biomarkers are attributable to the COVID emergency. Baseline and OI tumor samples from two patients treated on protocol but subsequently excluded from clinical trial analysis (and replaced) due to protocol violations (indeterminant baseline pulmonary nodules subsequently confirmed by biopsy as metastases or receipt of proton therapy) were included in the single-cell data analyses comparing patients with and without major histopathologic response.

## Histopathologic Response
Hematoxylin and eosin–stained slides from FFPE samples at baseline and OI were evaluated by immune-related pathologic response criteria ( , ) by a head and neck pathologist blinded to patient response data. Percent residual viable tumor was calculated as the viable tumor area/total tumor bed area (in mm2), in which the total tumor bed was the sum of the regression bed (characterized by lymphoid infiltrate, proliferative fibrosis, and neovascularization), residual viable tumor, and necrosis. Major histopathologic response was defined as <10% residual viable tumor OI. Percent reduction in tumor viability was calculated as the baseline tumor viability minus OI viability/baseline tumor viability.

## cfHPV DNA Analysis
HPV type in tumors was detected by ddPCR in DNA purified from FFPE tumors by proteinase K digestion, phenol-chloroform extraction, and ethanol precipitation. cfDNA was purified from 4 mL plasma using QIAsymphony DSP Circulating DNA Kit (Qiagen) on the QIAsymphony SP with the circDNA_2000_DSP or circDNA_4000_DSP program, eluted in 60 µL of AVE buffer, and stored at −20°C. cfHPV DNA was quantified in copies per ml plasma by use of a ddPCR assay with a lower limit of detection of five copies and lower limit of quantification of 16 copies per ml of HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, and 68 and ERV3 as positive controls ( ). cfHPV clearance was defined as time from cycle 1 day 1 to two consecutive negative tests (i.e., <5 copies/mL plasma) and was visualized by the Kaplan–Meier method.

## scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq of Fresh Tumor Tissue
Viable single-cell suspensions were prepared from fresh tumor tissue as previously described ( ). Briefly, tissue was minced into pieces <1 mm3, placed in RPMI 1640 with 1 mg/mL collagenase A and 0.4 mg/mL hyaluronidase, and incubated at 37°C for 1 to 2 hours. After trypsin digestion, the suspension was filtered through a 70-μm strainer and centrifuged at 450 × g for 5 minutes. If present, red blood cells were removed by ammonium chloride solution. After resuspension in PBS with 0.04% BSA and passage through a 40-μm cell strainer, cells were counted and resuspended at 700 to 1,200 cells/μL to ensure >70% viability.
Single-cell capture, barcoding, and preparation of gene expression and TCR libraries were performed as recommended using a Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ library preparation kit (PN-1000006/PN-1000165) and Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Reagent Kit (PN-1000005), respectively, with V1/V1.1 chemistry reagents (10× Genomics). From our freshly prepared (never frozen) cell suspensions, we loaded approximately 8,000 single cells per sample onto the 10× controller. The final libraries containing barcoded single-cell transcriptomes and TCRs were pooled together at 10:1 ratio and sequenced at 150-bp paired-end sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina) aiming for 100 to 200 million read pairs per sample. A minimum of 5,000 read pairs/cell was sequenced for TCR libraries, and a minimum of 20,000 read pairs/cell for gene expression libraries. We performed additional sequencing run(s) for gene expression libraries that did not initially reach the desired sequencing coverage of >20,000 read pairs/cell.

## Processing and Quality Control of Single-Cell Sequencing Data
Quality control metrics indicated that single-cell transcriptome libraries were sequenced at ∼272 million reads per library and 43,000 reads per cell at 70% sequencing saturation on average. Raw sequence reads were aligned to a custom reference genome, and gene expression levels were quantified using cellranger-multi (version 6.0.0) with default parameters. The custom reference genome included both human genome reference (GRCh38 primary genome) and 13 high-risk HPV genomes from the PaVE database (pave.niaid.nih.gov; RRID: SCR_016599). Gencode human gene annotation database (release v32) was used for transcript quantification. Each single-cell V(D)J library was sequenced at greater than 5 million read pairs.
In addition to the basic filtering performed by Cell Ranger, cells with low-quality data were filtered from further analysis after reading cellranger output files as cell-by-gene count matrices using Seurat package v4.3.0 (RRID: SCR_016341; ref. ). A cell was considered to have low quality if either the number of detected genes was <250 or the number of reads detected was <350 or if either number reached the upper limit of detection. Cells were further filtered if the proportion of mitochondrial gene counts was higher than 15%. Genes including TCR variable genes, immunoglobulin variable genes, and high-abundance lincRNA genes (e.g., MALAT1) were excluded from downstream analysis (hereafter, blacklist genes). Genes encoding heat shock proteins were excluded from all clustering analyses to avoid clusters dominated by stress-response genes.

## Classification of Malignant and Normal Epithelial Cells
We combined epithelial score, HPV gene expression, and inferred copy-number aberration to distinguish malignant and normal cells in each sample. Because malignant cells in HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer have an epithelial lineage, we first calculated an epithelial score using the mean expression level of epithelial genes KRT5, KRT6A, KRT6B, KRT13, KRT14, KRT15, KRT16, KRT17, KRT18, and KRT19, which were selected using the method described by Puram and colleagues ( ). HPV gene expression was readily available from the scRNA-seq profile. To infer gene copy number from gene expression profiles, we used CopyKAT v1.0.8 (RRID: SCR_024512; ref. ) with the default parameters for 10× data. CopyKAT segments and converts cumulative gene expression levels of neighboring genes into copy numbers and outputs a normal/aneuploid label for each cell along with the inferred copy-number profiles. Because sparsity of an scRNA-seq profile often leads to drop-out of epithelial markers and HPV genes and may introduce noise in the inferred copy-number aberration, we labeled cells at the cluster level rather than directly labeling individual cells. For each sample, fine-resolution clustering was first performed using “FindClusters” with resolution set at 1.0. Cells in a cluster were then classified as malignant if the cluster met two of three criteria: (i) >50% cells expressed HPV genes, (ii) >50% cells were aneuploid, or (iii) >50% cells had positive epithelial scores.

## scRNA-seq Data Clustering and Major Cell Type Annotation
The cell-by-gene count matrices were further processed using Seurat package v4.3.0 as follows. The standard protocol in Seurat was used to normalize the expression level of genes for each cell by the total mRNA molecular count using a global scaling normalization method in the function “NormalizeData.” A total of 2,000 most highly variable genes (HVG) were identified for downstream analysis by evaluating the mean–variance relationship across all the genes in the normalized expression data through the function “FindVariableFeatures.” The above-mentioned blacklist genes were not included in the 2,000 most HVGs. After scaling and centering of the gene expression matrices using “ScaleData” function, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) based on the 2,000 most variable genes and dimension reduction using “RunUMAP” (arXiv 1802.03426v3). To perform clustering, first a shared nearest neighbor graph, which connects cells with similar gene expression patterns, was constructed with “FindNeighbors.” Then clusters were determined with “FindClusters” at resolution of 0.6. “FindClusters” uses the Louvain algorithm to iteratively cluster cells until the standard modularity function is optimized. Clusters were projected on the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plot. We used canonical marker genes, e.g., PTPRC, CD3E, CD8A, CD4, FOXP3, MS4A1, GNLY, MZB1, LYZ, KIT, COL1A1, and PLVAP, to label major cell types in each cluster, including T, B, plasma, myeloid, fibroblast, and endothelial cells. To confirm the accuracy of cell-type labeling, we ran SingleR package (RRID: SCR_023120) for each sample using the CHETAH reference dataset, which contains four tumor types: colorectal, breast, melanoma, and head and neck ( ). Cell-type annotations from these methods largely agreed for most cell types, whereas rare discrepancies were reconciled by further inspection of marker genes in the clusters. A single cluster, which expressed markers for multiple cell types consistent with the presence of doublets, was excluded, resulting in a total of 367,451 cells for further analysis.

## Evaluation of Batch Effects
Except for malignant cells, all clusters shown on the UMAP plot were cell type–specific and not patient-specific or anatomic site–specific, supporting minimal batch effects at this level of clustering analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3C and S3D). To evaluate potential batch effects on data integration across different tumor samples thoroughly, we utilized R package cluster version 2.1.4 (RRID: SCR_013505) to calculate the Silhouette score ( ), which ranges from −1 (no/limited batch effects) to 1 (greater separation across batches). We extracted total CD4+ T cells (including Tregs) and CD8+ T cells from the full dataset, identified HVGs using the “FindVariableFeatures” function, and performed PCA with 2,000 HVGs. We then integrated CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations from all samples, respectively, with harmony ( ) or reciprocal PCA batch correction from Seurat or without correction (raw) using a range of 1 to 20 PCs. We calculated a Silhouette score for each cell using the “silhouette” function and averaged across all cells to obtain a final Silhouette score for each number of PCs used. We performed this procedure 20 times, each time on 20% of total cells selected randomly, and averaged the Silhouette scores across these 20 iterations. The averaged Silhouette scores for data integrated with RPCA were similar to that of data integrated without batch correction, and both sets were slightly better (i.e., closer to −1) than that of the harmony method (Supplementary Fig. S3E and S3F). Given the minimal effect of batch correction, we chose not to use it in order to avoid overcorrecting the data and masking biological variability ( ).

## T-cell Clustering and Annotation
We performed clustering analysis as described above on CD8+ T and on CD4+ T cells (including Tregs), extracted from the full data set, using the first 20 PCs and a clustering resolution of 0.6. This analysis yielded 14 CD4+ T-cell clusters and 15 CD8+ T-cell clusters. The distributions of CD8 and CD4 clusters in each sample indicated no batch effects in the two datasets, in agreement with the above Silhouette score analysis.
Marker genes for each cluster were identified from a list of differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Tables S10 and S12) with “FindAllMarkers,” which uses a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and we created bubble plots showing expression of canonical immune markers ( – ) in each cluster. In the end, we integrated all information to define the functional states for each T-cell cluster in collaboration with immunologists on the study team.

## GES Scores
AUCell (RRID: SCR_021327; ref. ) was used to generate GES scores for gene sets based on transcriptomic data. Three distinct tumor-reactive GESs, reported by Caushi and colleagues ( ), Lowery and colleagues ( ), and Meng and colleagues ( ), respectively, were used in our analysis. CD8+ T cells were considered tumor-reactive if the AUC score was above the “Global_k1” threshold in the AUC score distribution computed by AUCell. The immune checkpoint GES score included TIGIT, PDCD1, CTLA4, HAVCR2 (TIM3), LAG3, and TOX expression. The TRM GES score included ITGAE (CD103), ZNF683 (HOBIT), ITGA1 (CD49a), CD69, CXCR6, and CXCL13 expression ( ). PDCD1 was excluded to avoid overlap with the immune checkpoint gene set. The CD8+ T-cell cytotoxic GES score included PRF1, GZMA, GZMB, GZMH, and GNLY expression, which were also in the NK-cell cytotoxic GES score, along with GZMM, FASLG, and NKG7 ( ). The proliferation score included ZWINT, E2F1, FEN1, FOXM1, H2AFZ, HMGB2, MCM2, MCM3, MCM4, MCM5, MCM6, MCM7, MKI67, MYBL2, PCNA, PLK1, CCND1, AURKA, BUB1, TOP2A, TYMS, DEK, CCNB1, and CCNE1 ( ). GES scores for inhibitory receptors (KIR2DL3, KIR2DL4, KLRC1, TIGIT, and CD96) and for stimulatory receptors (FCGR3A, NCR3, KLRF1, KLRK1, and KLRC2) were also determined for NK cells.

## Integration of scTCR-seq Data with scRNA-seq Data
Filtered and annotated T-cell contigs with high confidence were mapped to scRNA-seq data using shared cell barcodes. A total of 155,187 cells with V(D)J information was detected among all 65 samples. Among them, 84.21% of cells contained sequence data for both TCR α and β chains, 14.16% contained only TCR β sequence data, and 1.63%, only TCR α chain data. In total, 131,954 cells (85.03%) with TCR sequence data were mapped to T cells identified by scRNA-seq. TCR clonotype was determined for 80.75% of CD8+ T cells and 89.45% of CD4+ T cells. We used the amino acid sequences of both the α- and β-chain CDR3 regions for clonotype identification in downstream analysis. Clonotype frequency was defined as the proportion of a specific clonotype among all T cells with V(D)J data.

## Analysis of TCR Clonotype Frequency and Clonal Dynamics
We used the Fisher exact test to identify TCR clonotypes that significantly changed in frequency from baseline to OI. After excluding clonotypes detected only once in baseline and/or OI samples, P values were corrected for multiple testing using FDRs for each patient-specific paired sample. Frequency changes with adjusted P value <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Clonotypes were stratified into expanded clonotypes (those with significant increase OI), contracted clonotypes (those with significant decrease OI), and novel clonotypes, which were only detected OI. For clarity, some analyses were limited to expanded novel clonotypes, which significantly increased OI versus baseline frequency of zero by the Fisher exact test.

## RNA Velocity Analysis
RNA velocity analysis was limited to the CD8+ T cells with clonotypes that expanded or contracted significantly upon treatment and was stratified by histopathologic response. The unspliced and spliced reads for each gene in each cell were counted based on aligned bam files using Velocyto Python package v0.17.16 (RRID: SCR_018167; ref. ). The calculation of RNA velocity values was performed by python package scVelo (RRID: SCR_018168; ref. ), which filters out genes that are detected in fewer than 30 cells in both unspliced and spliced data and selects the top 2,000 HVGs. The count matrices were normalized by library size and logarithmized. Subsequently, a k nearest neighbor graph was built based on the top 30 principal components. The moments of normalized spliced/unspliced counts were computed based on connectivity using 30 nearest neighbors for each cell. Then the velocities were computed by setting the mode to “deterministic.” The estimated velocities were then used to construct a velocity graph with transition probabilities, and the RNA velocity graph on a grid was mapped to the CD8+ T-cell UMAP.

## Multivariable Regression Model
To identify features of the TIME associated with percent reduction in tumor viability, we fitted an elastic net regression model ( ) using the R “glmnet” package (RRID: SCR_015505; ref. ). The model was tuned by setting the elastic net mixing parameter, α, at values ranging from 0 to 1 in increments of 0.1, in which the optimal value of the regularization parameter, λ, was determined via a tenfold cross-validation. After exclusion of one patient with fewer than 100 T cells at baseline, elastic net regression model fitting was limited to the 26 remaining patients with paired baseline–OI data. For the baseline predictive model, regression covariates (P = 98; Supplementary Table S20) included patient clinical characteristics [e.g., age, gender, tobacco smoking status (never, former/current), and tumor and nodal stages] and features curated from baseline scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq data, including major cell type or T-cell cluster proportion and GES scores computed by AUCell or GSDensity ( ). Tumor stage features contained N and T stages, which were converted to dummy variables via one-hot encoding. To assess treatment effects, we developed a second elastic net model (herein referred to as “delta model”) that included both baseline and OI features. For the delta model, regression covariates (P = 90) included the difference of each TIME molecular feature between OI and baseline (P = 88) and two additional features: the number of expanded CD8+ T-cell clonotypes and the number of contracted CD8+ T-cell clonotypes. All regression covariates were standardized to have a mean of 0 and a variance of 1 prior to fitting of the elastic net regression model to avoid coefficient scaling issues. Features that had non-zero coefficients were deemed to have linear associations with percent reduction in tumor viability from baseline. In both the baseline predictive and delta model, elastic net performed best with an α at 0, which turned out to be a pure LASSO regression. Elastic net regression identified 16 baseline (baseline model) and 17 changed (delta model) features that were independently associated with reduction in tumor viability.

## Multiplexed Barcode Fluorescent Imaging
Multiplexed barcoding staining was performed on 4-µm-thick FFPE sections using a PhenoCycler (Akoya Biosciences) with antibodies against pan-cytokeratin (clone AE1/AE-3; Akoya Biosciences, cat. #4450020, RRID: AB_3083456), CD45 (clone D9M81; Akoya Biosciences, cat. #4450042, RRID: AB_2915946), CD3e (clone EP449E; Akoya Biosciences, cat. #4450030, RRID: AB_2915971), CD8 (clone C8/144B; Akoya Biosciences, cat. #4250012, RRID: AB_2915960), CD45RO (clone UCHL1; Akoya Biosciences, cat. #4250023, RRID: AB_2895053), and PD-1 (clone D4W2J; Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #86163, RRID: AB_2728833) and with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole as previously described ( , ). Entire tissue sections were imaged at 20× magnification using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Keyence BZX800). All images were processed using an Akoya Processer computer and QPTIFF image storage. A pathologist selected the entire tumor area using the QuPath 4.04 image analysis software to capture various elements of tissue heterogeneity. All the data codes were process in R Studio 4.1.2 and consolidated using phenoptr 0.3.2 and phenoptrReports 0.3.3 packet (Akoya Biosciences; RRID: SCR_026104).

## Quantification of T-cell and T-cell Subtype Proportions in Fluorescent Images
Major types and subtypes were annotated based on marker coexpression, reported with pathologists’ manual supervision. For major cell types, CD45 and CD3e identified T cells, and cytokeratin identified malignant cells. The combination of CD45/CD3e/CD8/PD-1/CD45RO was used to identify antigen-experienced TEM cells. For each sample section, cells with low detection fidelity (cellular area <10 or >1,000 μm2, nuclear area <5 or >80 μm2, or detection probability less than 0.75) or with unidentifiable type (e.g., not positive for any markers) were excluded from quantification. All analyses were performed using R 4.2.1.

## Identification of HPV16-Specific TCR Clonotypes
The full-length paired TCR α/β sequences for a selection of CD8 TCR clonotypes that expanded significantly from baseline were determined from the 5′ V(D)J single sequencing data derived from tumors of patients with HPV-positive OPC. Specifically, the data output in the AIRR file format was used. The sequence encoding the TCR constant region was replaced with the mouse counterpart ( ), and then the hybrid TCR was synthesized and cloned into the pMSGV3 retroviral backbone (GenScript). Expression of the transgenic TCRs in CD8+ Jurkat cells without an endogenous TCR and modified to express luciferase upon TCR engagement was performed via electroporation. Briefly, ∼6 million Jurkat (TCRαβ-KO) CD8+ cells (Promega, cat. #GA1162) with cell viability >90% were transfected with program CL-120 and 10 μg of TCR-pMSGV3 in a 100 µL single Nucleocuvette using SE Cell Line 4D-Nucleofector Kit and LONZA 4D-Nucleofector System. TCR expression was confirmed by flow cytometry after 24 hours. Then, Jurkat cells with TCR expression were cocultured with partially or fully HLA-matched CD3-depleted peripheral blood mononuclear cells, obtained via negative selection using magnetic beads (Miltenyi), and either CEFX or HPV16 peptide pools (JPT Peptide Technologies). The CEFX peptide pool comprises MHC class I–restricted peptide epitopes from common viruses (Cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, and Influenza virus); thus, most people should have T cells that react to this pool. The HPV16 peptide pool was designed in-house. Briefly, a set of peptides covering the entire HPV16 proteome was generated using peptides 15 amino acids in length that overlapped by 11 amino acids. The peptides were assigned to pools in a 96-well plate per the following criteria: (i) two peptides may be pooled together only once; (ii) a peptide may not be pooled with another peptide if it has already been pooled with either neighboring peptide; and (iii) each peptide should be present three times. After 6 hours of coculture, cells were processed for luciferase assay using T Cell Activation Bioassay (TCRαβ-KO) Kit (Promega, cat. #GA1162) per the manufacturer’s protocol, and the plates were read on a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Biotek). For HPV16, the designed peptide pool matrix allows for direct demultiplexing and identification of the 15mer that gave rise to a response. Furthermore, in some cases, it allows for direct identification of the 11-mer containing the minimal epitope if two neighboring 15-mers both give a positive signal. For CEFX, no demultiplexing is needed.

## Statistical Analysis of Single-Cell Data
Due to COVID19-related shutdowns, histopathologic response determination was limited to 28 (13 MHRs and 15 nMHRs) of 35 enrolled patients. Response group comparisons, i.e., MHR versus nMHR, included data from all patients except patient 26, who was excluded from T/NK cell subtype analysis at baseline due to low baseline cell counts, e.g., 24 CD4+ T cells, 13 CD8+ T cells, and 1 NK cell. Baseline and OI comparisons of paired data were limited to patients with paired samples. Patient 33 was thus excluded because of the absence of a baseline sample, and patient 26 was again excluded due to low cell counts at baseline. A third tumor sample collected at cancer progression from patient 9 was excluded from all OI analyses. Patient 26 was also excluded from the analysis of correlations between change in proportion of each CD8+ T-cell cluster and change in proportion of eTregs and from the multivariable regression model.
To compare the abundance of T-cell clusters across timepoints or response groups, the Fisher exact test was used to calculate P values. For cell fraction comparison at the patient level, Wilcoxon rank-sum was used to compare across response groups; Wilcoxon signed-rank was used to compare paired samples. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate relationships between two continuous variables, and P values were derived using an asymptotic approximation based on a t-distribution with n-2 degrees of freedom, as sample size was large enough for this method to be valid. For cell level GES score comparison, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used considering the number of cells.
Unless otherwise specified, P values are two-sided with a significance level cutoff of 0.05 and are unadjusted. An FDR correction was applied for all comparisons with multiple statistical tests. All statistical analyses were performed using R.
