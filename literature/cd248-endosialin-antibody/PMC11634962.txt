This study used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to reveal gene expression changes during ADSCs to neuronal differentiation. Sequencing of the ADSCs group, the prei-1d group, and the induction 1 h, 3 h, 5 h, 6 h, and 8 h groups was performed using the BD Rhapsody platform. Sequence data were analyzed using t-SNE, Monocle2, GO, and KEGG algorithms. Results showed that a total of 38,453 cells were collected, which were divided into 0–13 clusters. Monocle2 structured analysis revealed that ADSCs were located at the beginning of the trajectory, and the cells after 5 h of induction were mainly distributed at the end of the trajectory in branches 1 and 2. Up-regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 5 h after induction enriched GO items including cellular protein metabolism, cell adhesion, endocytosis, and cell migration. KEGG analysis showed that induced 6 h and 8 h groups mainly enriched pathways were oxidative phosphorylation, glutathione metabolism, and expression of Parkinson's disease-related genes. In conclusion, two distinct cell state mechanisms stimulate ADSCs to develop into mature neurons. ADSCs induced for 5 h had developed into mature neurons. Later, the differentiated cells undergo degenerative changes associated with senescence.

## Introduction
Neurons, as highly differentiated cells, are particularly vulnerable to irreparable damage in central nervous system (CNS) disorders, such as stroke, whose incidence has been rising globally, especially among older populations (Ye et al. ; Lu et al. ; Zhao et al. ). Cell transplantation has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for regenerating and repairing damaged neurons (Cai et al. ; Wang et al. ; Yuan et al. ). While bone marrow stromal cells and embryonic stem cells are valuable sources of stem cells, their application is constrained by high costs, limited availability, and ethical concerns (Cheng et al. ). In 2001, Zuk et al. successfully isolated adipose-derived stromal cells (ADSCs) from adipose tissue and demonstrated their potential to differentiate into various cell types, including adipocytes, cardiomyocytes, osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and neurons (Zuk et al. ; Zuk et al. ). ADSCs have since garnered significant attention due to their abundant sources, ease of collection, and reduced ethical challenges, particularly in autologous transplantation (Lu et al. ). These cells share morphological similarities with embryonic stem cells and exhibit specific marker profiles, being positive for CD29, CD44, CD105, and CD166, while negative for CD34, CD106, and HLA-DR (Mohammadi et al. ; Abdanipour et al. ). Consequently, ADSCs have become a popular cell type in regenerative medicine (Sun et al. ). However, the molecular mechanisms and genetic properties underlying their differentiation into neurons remain poorly understood. Traditional transcriptomic studies, typically conducted at the population level, fail to capture cellular heterogeneity and the molecular dynamics of individual cells (Liu et al. , ; Smajic et al. ). Advances in sequencing technology, cell capture methods, and bioinformatics—particularly single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)—now enable detailed investigations of cell heterogeneity and gene expression dynamics at the single-cell level (Olsen and Baryawno ; Schwalie et al. ). In this study, scRNA-seq was utilized to elucidate the genomic dynamic evolution features of ADSC-induced neuronal differentiation, offering novel insights into the molecular processes involved.

## Material and Methods

## Extraction and Culture of ADSCs
ADSCs were isolated from abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue waste collected from healthy adult female volunteers (aged 20–35 years) undergoing needle aspiration at Jinrong Cosmetic Hospital, following the method described by Zhang et al. ( ). The tissue was digested with 0.1% collagenase type I at a 1:1 ratio for 30 min, after which an equal volume of complete culture medium (comprising 1% double antibody, 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 84% high-glucose DMEM) was added. The suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 12 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of complete culture medium and seeded into culture flasks. ADSCs were cultured at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2, with the culture medium replaced every two days. Once the cells reached confluency at the bottom of the culture flasks, they were passaged at a 1:2 ratio and further expanded. Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers for the use of their adipose tissue waste in scientific research. We confirm that all experiments were performed according to the relevant naming guidelines and regulations. All experimental procedures and protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tangshan Kailuan General Hospital, Hebei Province.

## Recovery of Frozen Cells
The extracted ADSCs were cryopreserved in a − 80 °C freezer. When required, the cells were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath, ensuring the process was performed swiftly to minimize the toxic effects of the cryopreservation solution. The thawed cells were transferred into a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 5 mL of culture medium, gently pipetted several times to resuspend, and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of fresh culture medium. The suspension was gently mixed and transferred into a new culture vessel, labeled appropriately, and placed in the incubator for subsequent culture.

## Preparation of Pre-induction Solution
Using a sterile transfer pipette, 10 mL of FBS and 3.5 μL of β-mercaptoethanol were added to a sterile 50 mL centrifuge tube. Subsequently, 40 mL of high-glucose DMEM was transferred into the tube using a transfer pump. The mixture was pipetted gently for 1 min to ensure thorough mixing, followed by sterilization and filtration. The final concentration of β-mercaptoethanol was adjusted to 1 mmol/L. The prepared medium was sealed with a protective film and stored at 4 °C in the dark to maintain stability.

## Preparation of Induction Solution
Using a sterile transfer pipette, 17.5 μL of β-mercaptoethanol was added to a sterile 50 mL centrifuge tube. A transfer pump was then used to add 50 mL of high-glucose DMEM to the same tube. The solution was pipetted gently for 1 min to ensure thorough mixing. The mixture was sterilized and filtered using a sterile filter, achieving a final β-mercaptoethanol concentration of 5 mmol/L. The prepared solution was stored at 4 °C, protected from light.

## ADSCs-Induced Differentiation Reaction Experiment and Grouping
Third-generation actively proliferating ADSCs were used for induction experiments. The old culture medium was removed, and the cells were gently rinsed three times with warmed saline. Subsequently, 5 mL of pre-induction solution, prepared and stored in the dark, was added dropwise to the culture. The cells were labeled and cultured for a 24-h pre-induction period, referred to as the pre-induction group. After 24 h, the pre-induction solution was removed, and the cells were again rinsed three times with normal saline. Next, 5 mL of induction solution was added dropwise, and the culture was labeled for the respective induction periods of 1, 3, 5, 6, and 8 h, forming the 1 h, 3 h, 5 h, 6 h, and 8 h groups. Morphological changes in the cells during different time points were observed under a microscope. Third-generation ADSCs that did not undergo induction served as the control (uninduced) group.

## Preparation of Cell Suspension
The biosafety cabinet was sanitized with UV radiation for 60 min. Trypsin, normal saline, and full DMEM culture medium storage bottles were warmed in a 37 °C water bath for 60 min. During the operation, the warmed solutions and culture bottles containing ADSCs, prei-1d, 1 h, 3 h, 5 h, 6 h, and 8 h groups were placed in the sterilized biosafety cabinet. The old culture medium was discarded, and the cells were gently washed three times with physiological saline. Subsequently, 700 μL of trypsin was pipetted into each culture bottle, which was then sealed and gently agitated. The cells' condition was monitored under an optical microscope. Once a significant number of cells were suspended, 5 mL of complete culture medium was added to neutralize the trypsin. The cell suspension was then transferred into a 15 mL centrifuge tube for further processing.

## Sequencing by BD Rhapsody System

## Sample Pretreatment
The prepared single-cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was carefully removed, and 500 μL of sample buffer (BD Rhapsody) was added to the pellet. The suspension was centrifuged again at 300 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Another 500 μL of sample buffer was added to resuspend the cells. The resuspended cells were then placed on ice for subsequent use.

## Cell Count and Viability Detection
To get rid of cell clumping, pass the resuspended cells through a cell sieve. Cell counting and activity detection are then carried out on the fluorescent cell counter Countstar by aspirating 10 μL of cell suspension from the collection tube, adding 10 μL of AO/PI (Acridine Orange/Propidium Iodide), dropping the cell suspension into the cell counting plate, and allowing it to stand for 60 s (Countstar Rigel S2).

## Sequencing
To collect single cells, the single-cell solution is dispersed at random across 200,000 microwells. To make the magnetic beads containing oligonucleotide barcodes magnetically couple with the cells in the microwells, add the beads until saturation is reached. To hybridize mRNA poly-A to magnetic beads, add cell lysis buffer. Each cDNA molecule is marked at the 5' end during cDNA synthesis with a unique molecular identifier (UMI) (Smith et al. ) and a cellular barcode to identify its cellular source. The BD Rhapsody Single-Cell Whole-Transcriptome Amplification Procedure was used to create whole-transcriptome libraries. First, second-strand cDNA is created, and then WTA adapters are joined for all-purpose amplification. Amplification of cDNA products via PCR. Sequencing libraries were measured using a Bioanalyzer 2200 and a Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Analyzer utilizing an Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Chip (Thermo Fisher Science). HiSeq XTen was used to perform 150 bp paired-end sequencing on each sample library (Illumina). Shanghai Liebing Biological Corporation, China, performed the experiment for this sequencing project. The ADSCs group were third-generation human adipose stromal cells that we cultured. Third-generation human adipose stromal cell lines were employed in the prei-1d, 1 h, 3 h, 5 h, 6 h, and 8 h groups. The sequencing was done in four sessions: the first for the prei-1d, 1 h, and 3 h groups, the second for the 6 h and 8 h groups, the third for the ADSCs group, and the fourth for the 5 h group.

## Sequencing Data Analysis

## Data Preprocessing
Fastp (Chen et al. ) default settings were used to filter adapter sequences, and low-quality reads were deleted. The UMI program (v1.0.0) was used to detect cellular barcodes using data supplied by the BD platform. To acquire UMI counts for each sample, raw data were plotted using STAR (v2.5.2b), which includes default values in the UMI Tools standard guide. The human genome (ensemble hg38, v91) was used as a reference in all analyses. We calculated two quality control measures for each cell separately: the proportion of mitochondrial genes expressed and the number of total genes expressed. Cells with less than 20% mitochondrial gene expression and more than 200 total expressed genes were maintained. Mitochondrial genes were eliminated from the list of genes expressed.

## Data Normalisation
For further analysis, the gene expression matrix findings were imported into the R (v3.5.1) statistical environment. Scran (v1.10.2) in the R package was used to normalize the data. The fastMNN function is used to correct batch effects between samples using the closest neighbor approach (Gao ).

## Data Dimension Reduction Clustering and Cell Type Identification
The standardized and batch-corrected data were imported into Seurat (v2.3.4) for downstream analysis and visualization, and the integrated data matrix was subjected to principal component analysis (PCA). Choose TOP10 PCs for downstream analysis using Seurat's Elbowplot function, and apply unsupervised clustering based on the Graphcluster method, with the resolution default value set to 0.8 (res = 0.8), cluster the cells, and use t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) for visualization (Kobak and Berens ). The cell types were subsequently identified using marker genes.

## Pseudo-chronological Analysis
We used Monocle2's DDR-Tree and default settings for single-cell trajectory analysis to examine the pseudo-chronological evolution trajectory of ADSCs induced to differentiate into neurons. Sort cells according to their differentiation stage and recreate their "trajectories" when cells develop or go through other biological changes (Wu et al. ).

## Analysis of Stemness-Related Genes During ADSCs Induction and Differentiation
Using Cellular Trajectory Reconstruction Analysis using gene Counts and Expression (CytoTRACE) to identify the developmental potential and stemness of cells by analyzing the number of expressed genes detected in each cell in conjunction with the RNA copy number of each gene, and using CytoTRACE to analyze the strongest correlation with cell stemness and the weakest 10 genes each (Gulati et al. ).

## Analysis of Differential Gene Expression
To determine the expression of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between different cell subpopulations during ADSCs induction and differentiation, the default parameters of the Seurat software package's functions FindAllMarkers (comparison of multiple conditions) and FindMarkers (comparison of two conditions) were used to screen out the significantly differentially expressed genes with an adjusted P value less than 0.05 and an average folding rate greater than 1.5. A Seurat heatmap was used to display the top 20 genes in each cluster (Sun et al. ).

## Analysis of Transcriptional Regulation
Regulatory network analysis of intracellular transcription factors using SCENIC (Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering) (Aibar et al. ; Li et al. ). SCENIC is a tool for inferring the Transcription Factor Regulatory Network (Gene Regulatory Network) and its associated Cell State in cellular data based on the co-expression of motifs (DNA signature sequences that bind transcription factors), transcription factors (TFs), and their target genes in the target cell population. GRNBoost was used to estimate co-expression modules between transcription factors and potential target genes (Gradient Boosting). We used RcisTarget to analyze the genes in each co-expression module and found the modules and targets that were enriched by transcription factor motifs. We calculated the regulatory genes of each transcription factor in the cell and its regulatory strength (AU Cell Score), and we assessed the activity of each Regulon (transcription factor and target gene combination) in cells. This approach allows us to determine the regulation of transcription factors in distinct cell populations as well as the unique transcription factors of each cell cluster.

## Gene Enrichment Analysis
On the genes with up-regulated expression in each time group during the process of ADSC-induced differentiation, gene functional annotation (Gene ontology, GO) of biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) was performed, and all the GO involved in the gene were obtained. The Fisher test was used to assess the significance level (P Value) of each GO, and the significant GO of gene enrichment was screened (Ashburner et al. ; Jager et al. ; Toma et al. ; Wolbert et al. ). Similarly, the genes listed above were annotated by Pathway using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, and all Pathway Terms implicated in the gene were retrieved. The Fisher test was used to assess the significance level (P Value) of Pathway, and therefore the significant Pathway Term of gene enrichment was examined (Kanehisa et al. , ; Chen et al. ).

## Analysis of Pseudo-sequential Evolution Trajectory of ADSC-Induced Differentiation into Neuronal Cells
The Monocle2 method was employed to analyze temporal variations in cell differentiation during ADSC-induced differentiation and to construct a tree-like trajectory of differentiation evolution (Fig.  a). Cell subpopulations were grouped based on gene expression similarities, forming bifurcated clusters along the trajectory. The starting trunk branch (left side, time point 1) gave rise to three distinct branches, designated as cell state 1, cell state 2, and cell state 3 branches (Fig.  b). Additionally, a quasi-sequential map of cell subpopulations during ADSCs differentiation was generated (Fig.  c). 
Figure  d–j demonstrate that ADSCs occupy the starting points of all three branches. Subsets 2 and 9, representing cells at 1 h and 3 h, are distributed along the entire differentiation trajectory. Subsets 7, 12, and 13 are primarily localized in the middle sections of the trajectory, spanning branches 1 and 2. In contrast, prei-1d, 6 h, and 8 h cells—subsets 0, 3, 4, 6, and 10—are concentrated in the central region of the trajectory, particularly along branches 1 and 2. At 5 h, cells from subset 1 predominantly localize at the termini of branches 1 and 2.
Similarly, ADSC subset 5 is largely positioned at the start of branch 3, with minimal presence at the ends of branches 1 and 2. Subset 8 also clusters at the base of branch 3. Subset 11 cells are distributed along the 3rd and 2nd differentiation trajectories of the first branch, as well as the 3rd and 1st trajectories of the original trunk.

## Discussion
Our study identified that ADSCs predominantly clustered within clusters 5, 8, and 11. Cells stimulated to differentiate for 1 h were primarily located in cluster 2, while clusters 7 and 9 were dominant at 3 h. At 5 h, cluster 1 contained the majority of differentiated cells, and clusters 0, 3, and 10 were predominant at 6 h and 8 h. Pseudo-chronological trajectory analysis revealed that clusters 5, 8, and 11 of ADSCs were positioned at the initiation points of the three differentiation branches. These clusters predominantly differentiated along two trajectories, with a few cells from clusters 5 and 8 located at the termini of the first and second differentiation tracks, while cluster 11 remained along the first trajectory. Among the top 20 genes with significantly elevated expression at various time points during ADSCs induction, COL3A1, TGFBI, and COL5A2 emerged as potential markers of immature neurons. This insight lays the groundwork for identifying novel cell marker genes and advancing cell type characterization. At the 6 h, 8 h, and prei-1d time points, clusters 0, 3, 4, 6, and 10 were predominantly distributed along the bifurcation and differentiation trajectories of the first and second branches. DEGs analysis highlighted specific upregulation of MT1G, SERPINB4, IL1RN, MT1X, IL33, PTGS2, IL6, SERPINB7, IL24, CXCL1, CXCL3, IL36B, MYO10, and other genes in ADSCs, designating these as significant ADSCs signature genes. Similarly, genes such as COL3A1, FN1, TGFBI, COL1A1, COL1A2, IGFBP4, THY1, and COL5A2 were consistently up-regulated across time points, suggesting their association with neural stem cells. Furthermore, MYL9, TIMP3, CCDC80, and ZNF90 exhibited specific upregulation in the 1 h and 3 h groups, implying their roles as potential markers of immature neurons. Conversely, genes exclusively up-regulated in the 5 h, 6 h, and 8 h groups, such as COL6A3, FBN1, and others, may serve as markers of mature neurons. These genes were primarily involved in biological processes including protein metabolism, cell adhesion, endocytosis, cell migration, lipid metabolism, and cholesterol metabolism. Previous studies have confirmed the involvement of genes like TGFBI, COL1A1, COL1A2, IGFBP4, TIMP3, CCDC80, and COL6A3 in neuronal development and maturation (Wang et al. ; Das et al. ; Deng et al. ; Fang et al. ; Brusegan et al. ; Lam et al. ). However, the specific roles of COL3A1, FN1, THY1, COL5A2, MYL9, ZNF90, and FBN1 in neuronal differentiation remain unexplored, warranting further investigation. Single-cell transcriptomics analysis demonstrated that ADSCs stimulated for 5 h reached a terminal state, forming mature differentiated cells. These cells exhibited essential physiological functions such as adhesion, endocytosis, and migration, alongside active participation in lysosomal function, glutathione metabolism, and endoplasmic reticulum protein processing pathways. However, prolonged induction led to degenerative changes in these differentiated cells, resulting in diminished physiological activity.
According to single-cell transcriptome sequencing data, cells undergoing ADSC-induced neuronal differentiation were visually classified into 14 distinct clusters, with considerable specificity in the DEGs among these clusters. ADSCs predominantly belonged to clusters 5, 8, and 11, while cells at 1 h post-induction were mainly in cluster 2, cells at 5 h were primarily in cluster 1, and cells at 6 h and 8 h were concentrated in clusters 0, 3, and 6. Notably, recent studies have shown that by the fifth hour of ADSCs induction, cells exhibit the morphological, ultrastructural, and electrophysiological characteristics of mature neurons. However, extending the induction period beyond this point leads to degenerative changes in the differentiated neurons, accompanied by reduced electrophysiological function (Ye et al. ; Lu et al. , ; Cai et al. ; Wang et al. ; Sun et al. ; Ou et al. ). Regarding DEGs, the top 20 genes positively expressed in ADSCs but negatively expressed in other time groups primarily included MT1G, SERPINB4, IL1RN, MT1X, IL33, PTGS2, IL6, SERPINB7, IL24, CXCL1, CXCL3 in cluster 5; IL36B, IL33, IL1RN, MT1X, MT1G, IL6, MYO10 in cluster 8; and MT1G, SERPINB7, IL24, and others in cluster 11. These genes, particularly IL33, MT1G, MT1X, and IL1RN, which belong to the top 10 genes with the lowest cell stemness and strong gene stability, can be considered hallmark genes of ADSCs. Additionally, the top 20 DEGs positively expressed in each time group during ADSCs induction and differentiation included genes such as COL1A2, COL1A1, SPARC, SERPINH1, P4HA1 in cluster 0; FN1, IL6ST, ITGAV, C1R, COL6A3 in cluster 1; SERPINE1, NQO1 in cluster 2; CKS2, GDF15, TUBA1B in cluster 3; ZYX, TPM1, FERMT2, FHL2 in cluster 4; MT1X, MT2A in cluster 5; HMOX1, C16orf72, GPX4, MSX1, MMP14, CTSK, CYGB, OAF, HSPA5, DNAJB9, OLFML3, TXNRD1 in cluster 6; HSPB1, SLC3A2, HSPA8, CAMTA2, XBP1, ATF4, PPP1R15A, CREBRF in cluster 7; MT2A, PID1 in cluster 8; FHL2, RPL18, RPS27L, GPX1, SLC3A2, FDXR, RPS13, GDF15, MDM2, S100A6, RPS16, RPL13 in cluster 9; AKR1C1, SERPINE2, LAPTM4A, CLU, CALM1, MRPS6, IFI27, S100A10 in cluster 10; CANX, HSPA5, DNAJB9, SEC11C, SLC3A2, UFM1 in cluster 11; CAVIN1, PKM, HMGA1, ACTB, CFL1, PFN1, ANXA2, S100A11, CCDC80, FAM129B, VIM, PSMD2, PPIA, RAB3B, CAPNS1, FKBP1A, CCND1, EIF5A, TLN1 in cluster 12; and MALAT1, LGALS1, S100A11, ATP5E, TMSB4X, CFL1, RPS20, RPS29, S100A6, RPL31, RPL27, NEAT1, RPL37A, RPL37, RPL30, PFN1, RPL38, RPS18, RPS8, RPS14 in cluster 13. Notably, LGALS1, RPS8, ATP5E, RPS14, RPS29, and S100A6 from this group belong to the top 10 genes with the strongest cell stemness, indicating their relevance as marker genes for ADSCs as pluripotent stem cells. Similarly, the top 20 DEGs that were exclusively negative in ADSCs but positive in differentiated cells at other time points included genes like COL3A1, COL5A1, TGFBI, LOXL2, PSD3, COL5A2, SLC6A15, BGN in cluster 0; CD36, BGN, LAMA4, REV3L, COL12A1 in cluster 1; TRNP1, TIMP3 in cluster 2; HMGB2, SMC4 in cluster 3; PTX3, MYL9 in cluster 4; WISP2 in cluster 6; HMOX1, MKNK2, CREBRF in cluster 7; FDXR, APOBEC3C, MKNK2 in cluster 9; PRG4, AKR1C2, FBN1, WISP2, ADD3, IGFBP6 in cluster 10. These genes, including COL3A1, TGFBI, and COL5A2, can serve as marker genes for immature neurons.
Numerous studies have highlighted the effectiveness of ScRNA-Seq combined with pseudo-chronological analysis in modeling cellular developmental lineages. This approach captures dynamic gene expression changes across different cell subpopulations over time, allowing for the inference of cellular transformations and sequential transitions along developmental trajectories (Treutlein et al. ; Hook et al. ; Hammond et al. ; Li et al. , ; Mukherjee et al. ; Zhang et al. ; Gerrits et al. ; Sadick et al. ; Tang et al. ; Wei et al. ; Xing et al. ; Yang et al. ; Zhou et al. ).
Based on the findings of this study, clusters 5, 8, and 11, composed of ADSCs, are predominantly located near the initiation points of the three branches in the early differentiation trajectory. These clusters undergo division and maturation until reaching the bifurcation point. Subsequently, differentiated cells follow distinct branching pathways. A small subset of cells from cluster 5 persists at the terminal regions of the first and second branches, while cluster 8 is located at the end of the second branch, and cluster 11 is confined to the end of the first branch. These observations suggest that different ADSC clusters can differentiate into target cells through specific evolutionary trajectories. For instance, cluster 11 primarily follows the first branch, cluster 8 predominantly progresses along the second branch, and cluster 5 exhibits the flexibility to develop into target cells via both pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that cells differentiated 1 h and 3 h post-induction exhibit characteristics of immature neurons, as evidenced by their morphology, ultrastructure, and immunohistochemical profiles (Ye et al. ; Lu et al. ; Cai et al. ; Ou et al. ). The analysis revealed that cells from the 2nd and 9th clusters, observed at 1 h and 3 h post-ADSCs induction, were distributed across the differentiation growth trajectory, while cells from the 7th cluster were predominantly concentrated at the bifurcation point and along the initial two differentiation trajectories. This gene-level information provides valuable insights into the early differentiation processes during the induction of ADSCs into neurons. At 5 h, when ADSCs had differentiated into mature neurons, cells from the 1st cluster were primarily located near the endpoints of the first and second differentiation trajectories. This finding suggests that, although ADSCs differentiation progresses through distinct branching pathways, the final target cells exhibit a consistent gene expression state. This indicates that multiple cell state pathways can converge to produce the same terminal cell phenotype. These results are consistent with previous research demonstrating that ADSC-derived neurons at this stage exhibit morphological, structural, and electrophysiological characteristics typical of mature neurons (Ou et al. ). However, the 0th, 3rd, and 10th clusters, representing the 6th and 8th hours of ADSCs differentiation, were predominantly distributed along the bifurcation and development tracks of the first two branches. This pattern suggests a decline in gene expression levels and the onset of degenerative changes. KEGG pathway annotation further revealed that differentiated cells at these time points exhibit heightened activity in metabolic and signaling pathways associated with neurodegenerative disorders, including Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.
We conducted an in-depth analysis of the top 20 genes with significantly increased expression during each time group of ADSCs induction and differentiation. Notably, in the 1 h and 3 h time groups, the up-regulated genes were primarily MYL9, TIMP3, CCDC80, and ZNF90. Further examination of the ADSC-induced differentiation pathway revealed that genes such as COL3A1, FN1, TGFBI, COL1A1, COL1A2, IGFBP4, THY1, and COL5A2 exhibited upregulation across all time groups. In contrast, MYL9, TIMP3, CCDC80, and ZNF90 were specifically up-regulated in the 1 h and 3 h groups. These genes were associated with key biological processes such as mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled to proton transport, ATP biosynthesis, and NADH to ubiquinone activities. Regarding molecular functions, they demonstrated proton transporter activity, cytochrome c oxidase activity, transforming growth factor binding, and protein labeling. Cellular structure analysis highlighted significant mitochondrial activity during these stages. These findings underscore the pivotal role of mitochondrial activity in the early phases of ADSC-induced differentiation, aligning with observations from previous research (Wang et al. ). Similarly, the key up-regulated genes COL6A3 and FBN1 were specifically observed in the 5 h, 6 h, and 8 h groups. Notably, COL6A3 was identified as a significant DEG during the 5 h differentiation phase, indicating its potential as an essential marker gene for mature neurons. Cells differentiated at the 5 h time point exhibited diverse biological activities, including protein metabolism, cell adhesion, endocytosis, cell migration, lipid metabolism, and cholesterol metabolism. Additionally, these cells were involved in molecular functions such as collagen binding and glycosaminoglycan binding. Structurally, their cellular components included the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and melanosome, reflecting their advanced functional specialization at this stage (Cai et al. ). Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were associated with pathways including ribosome, ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, lysosome, glutathione metabolism, endoplasmic reticulum protein processing, protein digestion and absorption, fatty acid metabolism, histidine metabolism, β-alanine metabolism, and other related metabolic or signaling pathways. Further investigation into the gene regulatory network activity in terminally differentiated cells indicated that cells at 6 h and 8 h post-ADSC differentiation exhibited diverse biological functions, such as protein metabolism, cell division, cell cycle regulation, fibroblast growth factor binding, electron transfer activity, transforming growth factor binding, protein kinase binding, protease binding, calcium binding, and collagen binding. Structurally, these cells demonstrated active roles for the endoplasmic reticulum and cytoskeleton. These findings suggest that endoplasmic reticulum functionality is critical during the late stages of ADSC-induced differentiation. Moreover, cells at various differentiation stages exhibit distinct activation of specific genes, with their biological roles and associated signaling pathways varying accordingly.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, ADSCs undergo neuronal differentiation via two distinct cellular mechanisms. By the 5 h of induction, fully differentiated mature neurons are formed, exhibiting key physiological functions such as adhesion, endocytosis, and migration. These neurons actively participate in metabolic and signaling pathways, including lysosomal activity, glutathione metabolism, and endoplasmic reticulum protein processing. However, extended induction periods may lead to degenerative aging changes in the differentiated cells, potentially impairing their physiological functions.
