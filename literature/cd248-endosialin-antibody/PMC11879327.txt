In the past decade, considerable advances in our understanding of the clinical and pathological heterogeneity of IIM and the discovery of novel autoantibody profiles including the myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs) and myositis-associated autoantibodies (MAAs), facilitated the subdivision of IIM into distinct syndromes [ ]. These advancements have refined our classification of IIM and contributed to the understanding of the pathomechanisms of some myositis subtypes. In this review, we will explore the recent advancements in our understanding of the pathomechanisms underlying IBM.

## How is IBM distinct from the other IIMs?
IBM is distinct from the other forms of IIMs due to its characteristic clinical presentation and refractoriness to current pragmatic therapeutic strategies. In addition, the lack of efficacy shown in clinical trials of novel agents underscores the need to explore innovative pathomechanisms in IBM.
IBM is more common in men than in women with an age of onset typically 45 years or older and does not affect children. IBM presents a unique clinical phenotype characterized by slow-onset asymmetric weakness and atrophy, affecting both proximal and distal limb muscles [ , ]. Notably, the deep finger flexors are preferentially involved at an early stage, along with knee extensors and ankle dorsiflexors, as well as the facial and pharyngeal muscles [ , , ]. However, it has become widely recognized that IBM may affect younger individuals and may present with weakness beyond finger flexors and knee extensors [ , , ]. Clinical heterogeneity and a lack of disease awareness are associated with an average diagnostic delay of 5–6 years, with the condition often being initially misdiagnosed [ , , ]. Various conditions can resemble IBM due to overlapping clinical features. Inflammation on muscle biopsy is a feature shared with other IIM, and IBM is often initially misdiagnosed as being another IIM [ ]. Of note, inflammation does not exclusively occur in IBM (and other IIMs) and can also be seen in muscular dystrophies, such as facioscapulohumeral dystrophy (FSHD), dystrophinopathies, dysferlinopathies, calpainopathies and anoctaminopathies [ ]. Subtypes of limb-girdle muscular dystrophies, such as anoctaminopathy, may exhibit asymmetrical muscle involvement. Myotonic dystrophy type 2 exhibits similarities to IBM, particularly affecting distal muscles. Likewise, neurogenic disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Kennedy's disease (spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy) and certain forms of motor neuron disease (MND) can present initially with muscle weakness and atrophy that mirrors IBM's clinical presentation. A retrospective review by Dabby et al. [ ] found that 13% of their patients initially diagnosed with ALS or MND were later reclassified as having IBM. In addition, myofibrillar myopathies, characterized by abnormal protein aggregation in muscle fibres, share clinical features of weakness and atrophy with IBM.

## Degenerative processes in IBM: impaired autophagy, proteasomal dysregulation and mitochondrial dysfunction
A triad of histological features involving degenerative, mitochondrial and inflammatory processes is characteristic of IBM [ , ].
The predominant degenerative hallmark of IBM is protein aggregation. A combination of processes has been described to contribute to this. Alterations to protein translation, specific post-translational modification, impaired protein degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagy pathway, coupled with oxidative stress and the aged milieu, foster damage and the misfolding/unfolding of proteins. Among the degenerative features observed in histology are sarcoplasmic rimmed vacuoles, congophilic coarse inclusions, sequestosome-1/p62 and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) aggregates, TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) translocation from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, and cytoplasmic and/or intranuclear tubulofilaments discernible via electron microscopy [ , ].
Rimmed vacuoles are believed to arise from an overabundance of autophagosome formation, hindrance in autophagosome fusion with lysosomes, or a failure of autophagolysosomes to degrade their contents effectively, leading to the accumulation of detrimental cellular debris and the release of toxic lysosomal enzymes [ ].
Fratta et al. [ ] noted proteasomal dysfunction within the muscle fibres of individuals with IBM associated with the accumulation of several ubiquitinated, misfolded multiprotein, potentially cytotoxic aggregates, including amyloid-beta, phosphorylated Tau and heat shock proteins (HSP), among others [ ]. These aggregates could potentially contribute to the observed muscle degeneration in IBM. However, despite promising studies in in vitro and in vivo models [ ], Arimoclomol, investigated for its ability to upregulate the heat shock response, was explored as a potential therapeutic approach in IBM but did not show improved efficacy outcomes ( ) [ ]. Therefore, it remains to be determined whether the accumulation of misfolded proteins such as HSP are a cause or consequence of pathology in IBM. TDP-43 mislocalisation could be relevant, either via cytoplasmic toxic gain of function, nuclear loss of function, or both. However, the precise molecular mechanisms downstream of TDP-43 mislocalisation remain incompletely understood. A key consequence of nuclear TDP-43 loss of function, which has emerged over recent years, is the occurrence of splicing defects leading to the erroneous inclusion of intronic sequences into mature mRNA, forming so-called ‘cryptic exons’ [ ]. The incorporation of cryptic exons can result in deleterious downstream consequences, namely loss of functional proteins, nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) of the cryptic-containing transcript, or translation of a novel cryptic peptide [ ]. A study by Britson et al. revealed that cryptic exon inclusion was observed in 84% of IBM muscle biopsies and only 1.3% of control biopsies, suggesting that the loss of TDP-43 function is specific to IBM compared with other inflammatory myopathies. Moreover, this mechanism appeared to be independent of inflammation, since in a humanized xenograft rodent model of IBM, anti-CD3 antibody treatment successfully depleted T cells from muscle and suppressed MHC-1 upregulation but not degenerative pathology [ ]. This was corroborated by a more recent work by Wilson et al. that aimed to characterize regenerating myoblasts from IBM patient muscle biopsies compared with controls. The authors showed that IBM myoblasts proliferate at a slower rate and generate fewer differentiated myotubes compared with non-IBM muscle. IBM myoblasts were also seen to exhibit increased beta-galactosidase staining, suggesting senescence, along with evidence of cryptic exon incorporation and loss of nuclear TDP-43 [ ].
 Mitochondrial abnormalities are another key pathological hallmark of IBM and maybe indicative of accelerated ageing [ ]. The aetiology driving mitochondrial dysfunction in IBM is likely to be complex and currently unclear. Mitochondrial DNA deletions have been noted in muscle tissue from IBM patients containing ragged red and COX negative fibres [ ]. One hypothesis that has been postulated is that chronic inflammation and endoplasmic reticulum stress propagate mitochondrial damage [ ].

## The role of autoimmunity in IBM

## T cells in IBM
In the 1980s, the crucial discovery of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells invading myofibres of patients with IBM provided the rationale for autoimmunity in the pathogenesis of IBM [ , ]. A significant increase in chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) and chemokine ligand 6 (CCL6) levels within the muscle of IBM patients strongly indicated activation of T cells upstream and the production of IFN gamma (IFN-γ) [ ].
In IBM there appeared to be a predominance of CD8+ T cells compared with CD4+ T cells circulating the blood and in skeletal muscle tissue of IBM Patients [ , ]. Some evidence has suggested there a decrease in CD4+ T regulatory cells (Tregs) observed in IBM patients [ ] which may further contribute to the dysregulated inflammatory milieu and promote autoimmunity.
CD8+T-bet+ cells have been identified as a predominant biomarker for IBM, with substantial changes observed within CD8+ cells compared with myositis controls [ ]. Quinn et al. have recently utilized a novel PET ligand, 89Zr-Df-crefmirlimab to investigate the CD8 T cell infiltration in four IBM patients [ ] IBM patients compared with the control group which comprised of age-matched patients with cancer, who previously underwent Zr-Df-crefmirlimab PET-CT scans before initiating immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. The authors demonstrated significant recruitment of CDB T cells to upper and limb skeletal musculature, in particular quadriceps, forearm finger flexors and triceps. In comparison no significant signal was detected in other organs. Detailed immunophenotyping has demonstrated that higher proportions of CD8+ T cells displaying a terminally effector memory (TemRA) phenotype in IBM patients, which is more highly differentiated stage of effector CD8+ T cells [ , ].TemRA T cells loose CD28 expression and re-express CD45RA, this signifies a switch to a chronically activated state and prolonged survival [ , ]. Furthermore these CD8+ TemRA cells in IBM patients appear to express increased levels of markers associated with greater degrees of cytotoxicity for example CD56 [ ].
McLeish et al. have recently investigated immune signatures of T cell populations in 81 IBM patients using machine learning technique called K means clustering [ ]. IBM patients in this cohort was categorized into 3 main sub-populations based on their immune signatures. One cluster of patients demonstrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells with a pro-inflammatory state. A second cluster of patients who demonstrate limited inflammation. A final subset of patients demonstrates highly differentiated T cell population. No significant differences in the clinical phenotypes between these groups. However, the authors describe trend for the third cluster of patients demonstrate lower two-min walking and timed up and go distances.
As previously alluded to, there is mounting evidence implicating the type II interferon pathway in IBM [ , ]. Highly differentiated T cells secrete IFN-γ, which in turn stimulates the expression of muscle antigens on MHC class I molecules via the immunoproteasome within muscle fibres. When these muscle antigens are presented on MHC class I molecules, they form a complex that can activate cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. These activated CD8+ T cells further produce IFN-γ, contributing to a cycle of immune activation. In addition, IFN-γ may play a role in inducing protein aggregates within muscle cells. Furthermore, IFN-γ activates macrophages and promotes the expression of MHC class II molecules, which can present muscle autoantigens to CD4+ Th1 T cells. This activation of macrophages is associated with myophagocytosis, where they engulf necrotic muscle fibres.
The activation of the type II interferon pathway in IBM involves a complex interplay of immune cells, cytokines and muscle fibres, contributing to the chronic inflammation and degeneration characteristic of the disease. This, coupled with, the insights into the spatial distribution and intensity of CD8+ T cell infiltration provide valuable information for understanding the pathophysiology of IBM and potentially guiding future diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, including T cell-targeted therapies [ , , , ].

## B-lineage cells in IBM
Although the identification of autoantibodies and the progressive subcategorization of PM and DM commenced in 1976 [ , ], it was not until 2013 that the discovery of the molecular target cytosolic 5’-nucleotidase 1A (cN1A; encoded by NT5C1A) of the IBM circulating autoantibody was made [ , ]. This discovery marked a shift from the previous understanding of IBM as exclusively mediated by T cell-specific autoimmunity towards the potential involvement of humoral immunity in IBM. The delay in this discovery has been attributed to early observations noting a paucity of CD20+ endomysial B cells in IBM muscle samples [ ]. Subsequent studies revealed significantly elevated immunoglobulin transcripts in IBM muscle samples indicating a rapid transition of CD20+ B cells to CD19+ plasmablasts and clonal CD138+ plasma cells [ ]. This observation aided in the identification of circulating blood autoantibodies and the target antigen of these autoantibodies, cN1A.
Autoantibodies targeting cN1A exhibit high specificity and moderate sensitivity in IBM [ ]. The sensitivity of anti-cN1A antibody assays ranges from 33–89% [ ], and specificity ranges from 80–100% [ ]. The presence of anti-cN1A antibodies in other inflammatory myopathies is lower compared with IBM. In DM, PM, ASS and IMNM, the occurrence of these antibodies ranges from 0–20.8%, 0–30%, 8.7–25.9% and 0–25%, respectively [ ]. Patients with other systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases including SS, and SLE, on the other hand, often demonstrate a relatively high frequency of positive results for anti-cN1A antibodies. The prevalence can be as high as 36.4% in patients with SS and 20.5% in patients with SLE, respectively [ ], restricting the specificity of IBM diagnosis in these individuals. Differences in the functional capacity and effector mechanisms may explain the pathogenic potential of anti-cN1A antibodies in those with IBM or not. Yamashita et al. have recently developed a mouse model for IBM in which they have actively immunized mice with five different anti-cN1A peptides [ ]. This model was able to recapitulate some histopathological hallmarks of IBM including infiltration of non-necrotic fibres, p62 aggregates and CD8+ T cell invasion of endomysium. Depending on which peptides were used for immunization, some mice displayed reduced motor activity. Further studies are warranted to understand the relevance of these findings for IBM patients.
Contrary to the other IIMs, where MSAs play multifaceted roles as important diagnostic markers and prognostic indicators, guiding treatment strategies effectively [ ], anti-cN1A antibodies serve primarily as an adjunctive tool in supporting the diagnosis of IBM. Efforts to ascertain the predictive value of anti-cN1A antibodies in delineating specific clinical phenotypes, defining disease characteristics, correlating with histopathological findings, or predicting survival outcomes have yielded limited informative results [ ].
Uncertainty, however, persists regarding whether the plasma cells detected in IBM originate externally and migrate to the muscle or if they are locally generated within the muscle tissue. There are studies that provide evidence for a muscle antigen-driven humoral response in IBM. Elevated levels of B cell activating factor (BAFF), a cytokine crucial for the survival, maturation and differentiation of B cells, were observed in the serum of individuals with IBM, highlighting the local maturation of B cells into antibody-producing plasma cells in response to muscle antigens [ ]. Consistent with these findings, a recent study revealed distinctive features in the B cell receptor (BCR) repertoire within IBM muscle such as increased IgG isotype usage and elevated frequency of IgM somatic hypermutation in IBM, setting it apart from other IIMs [ ].

## Preceding infection in IBM
Similar to the immunopathogenesis of a variety of autoimmune conditions, IBM has been associated with preceding viral infections that may trigger dysregulated immune responses. HIV has been associated with developing IBM in younger individuals [ ]. Potential hypotheses for the association between IBM and HIV include the predilection for autoimmunity due to depleted regulatory T cells and mitochondrial dysfunction induced by anti-retroviral drugs. Similarly, a link between human T cell lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV-1) infection and IBM has been described in a variety of reports [ , , ]. However, given that IBM is rare, it is plausible that specific virus infections may trigger IBM in those with an inherent risk.

## Immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory treatments in IBM: a plethora of failed trials
Although certain treatments have shown promising results, with efficacy being suggested [ , , ], these findings have not been consistently replicated in other studies. Moreover, these studies were often small, lacked appropriate sample size calculations, did not demonstrate consistency across the outcome measures assessed, and involved confounding factors that must be considered, such as open-label designs, concomitant pharmacological or non-pharmacological treatments, and comparisons with historical groups of patients not matched for the interventions [ ].
While autoimmunity has long been recognized as a potential pathogenic factor, the paucity of successful treatment ( ) has prompted the consideration of an alternative perspective: that other mechanisms might be accountable for IBM. The limited effectiveness of treatment methods employing broad non-selective lymphocyte depletion with alemtuzumab [ ] and anti-thymocyte globulin [ ], or the inhibition of proliferating cells with steroids [ ] and methotrexate [ ] indicates that the autoimmunity observed in IBM muscle differs notably from that seen in other forms of IIM.

## Why explore the role of immunosenescence in IBM?

## Role of expanded CD8± large granular lymphocytes
In their 2016 study, Greenberg et al. [ ] found that 58% of individuals diagnosed with IBM exhibited expanded populations of large granular lymphocytes (LGLs). These resembled the phenotype of T cell large granular lymphocytic (T-LGL) cells which are associated with T cell large granular lymphocytic leukaemia (T-LGLL). Notably, these LGL populations remained stable in IBM patients upon longitudinal analysis. This suggests that the underlying pathogenesis of IBM may extend beyond a mere autoimmune process to resemble more of a neoplastic-like process. T-LGLL is a rare and indolent form of leukaemia, that is more common in elderly patients [ ]. T-LGLL has also been reported in patients with other autoimmune disorders such as RA and SS. Greenberg et al. also observed a correlation between the extent of CD8+ and CD57+ cells in IBM muscle tissue and the size of LGL populations in the blood, suggesting a link between immune cell infiltration in muscle tissue and systemic lymphocyte abnormalities ( ). Patients with T-LGL expansions demonstrated a more pronounced decline in muscle strength compared with those without such expansions, indicating a potentially more aggressive disease course associated with T-LGL expansions.
 Limaye described a report of a patient with IBM and SS, that was subsequently treated with rituximab after being diagnosed with coexisting T-LGLL [ ]. Rituximab helped improve muscle weakness and the ability to climb stairs in the short term, however, despite treatment this patient continued to progress. The authors were unclear on how B cell depletion provided benefit but postulated the potential indirect effects on T cell function could be mediated by modulating humoral immunity.
A recent study conducted by McLeish et al. in 2023 [ ] examined the occurrence of CD8+ T-LGL lymphoproliferative disorder in 85 individuals with IBM, as well as functional changes in those with expanded populations of LGLs. The study revealed that 40% of IBM patients exhibited an expansion of T-LGLs. Importantly, individuals with expanded T-LGLs were more likely to depend on mobility aids, suggesting a higher degree of disease severity in this subgroup. No significant relationship was noted between the proportion of LGLs and anti-cN1A seropositivity.
It is interesting to note that the variability in diagnostic criteria and disease definition for T-LGLL can significantly impact the reported prevalence of T-LGLL in patients with IBM. Changes in the classification of LGL leukaemia over time reflect evolving understanding in the field. Different studies have used varying criteria to diagnose T-LGLL, such as thresholds for LGL counts, presence of clonality markers, and association with autoimmune diseases. In the aforementioned study by Greenberg et al. [ ], the diagnostic criteria included a peripheral expansion of LGLs (<0.5 × 10^9/l; normal value <0.3 × 10^9/l), lasting >6 months, excluding transient or reactive LGL proliferations accompanied by an autoimmune disease (e.g. IBM) [ ]. Upon re-evaluation using criteria from Lamy and Loughran [ ], which defines diagnosis based on circulating LGL counts >0.5 × 10^9/l with clonal rearrangement of TCR-γ gene using PCR, only 34% met the criteria.

## Role of highly differentiated effector memory and cytotoxic T cells
The clonal expansion and replacement of naïve or early effector memory T cells (TEM1 and TEM2 cells) in both the blood and muscle tissue of IBM patients not only provide evidence of chronic antigen-driven T cell activation [ ] but also represents immunosenescence. While the specific antigen responsible for triggering autoreactivity of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells remains elusive, the enduring presence of the highly differentiated effector memory and cytotoxic CD4+ (CD4+CD28-) and CD8+ T cells (CD8+CD28-, CD8+CD244+, CD8+CD57+, CD8+ Killer cell lectin-like receptor G1 [KLRG1]+) infiltrating the muscles suggests evasion from the regulatory immune mechanisms that typically control autoimmunity [ , ].
A significant contrast between PM and IBM lies in the notable presence of highly differentiated CD8+CD57+ T cells in muscle tissue obtained from IBM patients. The expression of the natural killer cell marker CD57 and CD244 on CD8+ T cells is widely believed to indicate persistent antigenic stimulation [ , ] and confers a higher cytotoxic potential [ ]. Following on from their observations on circulating LGLs in IBM, Greenberg et al. demonstrated the correlation of KLRG1 gene expression with cytotoxicity in IBM [ ] The co-expression of CD57 and KLRG1, along with the loss of CD28, serve as surrogate markers of T cell senescence [ , ]. These highly specialized senescent cells, unlike exhausted cells, maintain the ability to produce elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-gamma and cytotoxic granules. This capacity plays a crucial role in causing injury to IBM myofibers [ , ]. Moreover, the highly differentiated T cell population is known to be relatively resistant to apoptosis and other mechanisms of immunosuppression, and corticosteroids are largely ineffective [ ]. In addition to CD8+ T cells, KLRG1 expression has also been noted to increase on circulating CD4+ T cells in IBM patients whereas KLRG1 expression on circulating Tregs is comparatively reduced in IBM [ ].
These observations suggest a role for T cell immunosenescence in the pathogenesis of IBM ( ), offer a distinctive immunophenotypic approach for categorizing IBM patients, and could potentially aid in developing a prognostic tool and identifying appropriate candidates for future targeted therapies. Currently, a phase 2/3 (NCT05721573) study in IBM is evaluating ABC008/Ulviprubart, a humanized monoclonal antibody against KLRG1, which selectively depletes highly differentiated T cells. The mechanisms by which ABC008/Ulviprubart mediates the killing of KLRG1+ lymphocytes include the induction of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC). Early data from a pilot study in three IBM patients demonstrated that ABC008 can selectively lower the proportion of circulating KLRG1+ CD8+ T cells and T-LGLs [ ]. Circulating Tregs were not depleted with the drug. Some improvement in functional measures was noted in these patients.
Rapamycin (Sirolimus), used to prevent rejection of organ transplantation, was repurposed in a recent phase 2 clinical trial to determine its effect in IBM [ ]. Sirolimus inhibits the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway which has various effects on cell metabolism, including promoting autophagy [ ]. Furthermore, it has a role in immunomodulation by upregulating Tregs and the suppression of effector CD8/CD4+ T cells. Therefore, the drug may have a multifaceted effect on IBM. In this phase 2b trial, treated patients demonstrated depleted levels of CD8+ T effector memory cells. Other significantly improved outcome measures included Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index, forced vital capacity, and thigh fat fraction as assessed by quantitative MRI [ ]. A phase 3 clinical trial of sirolimus in IBM is ongoing (NCT04789070).
Despite the observations described above, a counter argument exists as to whether the presence of these highly differentiated cytotoxic T cell populations is just an epiphenomenon in the context of chronic inflammation rather than actually driving pathogenesis in IBM [ ]. This argument needs further evaluation over time and the development of therapies targeting these cell populations may provide further insight into this.

## Role of senescent fibro-adipogenic progenitors
Fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) are muscle-resident mononucleated cells involved in skeletal muscle homeostasis and regeneration [ ] by orchestrating regulatory interplay between muscle stem cells and immune cells. Excess accumulation and/or aberrant differentiation drives muscle fibrosis, adipogenesis and/or calcification. These dysregulated responses contribute to FAP-mediated replacement of myofibres with connective tissue and fibrosis. Recent work by Nelke et al. [ ] identified a cluster of FAPs that exhibited key hallmarks of senescence, including a pro-inflammatory secretome, expression of p21, increased beta-galactosidase activity and engagement of senescence pathways in patients with IBM. The study authors additionally illustrated that senescent FAPs lose collagen type XV expression, which is essential for maintaining the structural integrity of myofibres and facilitating the formation of neuromuscular junctions in vitro.
Notably, the supportive role of FAPs in myogenesis and tissue repair may become compromised with ageing. Ex vivo experiments by Lukjanenko et al. have confirmed that ageing results in diminished expansion, impaired adipogenesis and heightened fibrogenic differentiation during the regeneration process [ ]. In addition, the interaction and signalling between FAPs and muscle stem cells within the muscle stem cell niche are slowed down with age. Consequently, aged FAPs exhibit reduced capacity for muscle stem cell expansion and differentiation due to decreased secretion of pro-myogenic factors, thereby indirectly influencing the myogenic potential of muscle stem cells [ ].
There is still a need to understand the precise pathways that dictate the development of pathogenic FAP fates. Further studies are warranted to unveil strategies aimed at restoring normal FAP physiology, supplementing ongoing drug development endeavours focused on inhibiting FAP dysfunction in diseased muscles in IBM.

## Conclusions and future perspectives
The pathogenesis of IBM is likely due to the complex interplay between dysregulated immunity and degenerative processes, in an ageing environment of genetically predisposed individuals. Due to a lack of response to conventional immunosuppression, other therapeutic avenues have been explored such as the modulation of protein homeostasis. However, more recent investigations into the association of IBM with T-LGLL and the presence of highly differentiated cytotoxic T cells, have reignited an interest in the role of immunosenescence in IBM. Evidence has demonstrated a predilection for the expression of markers, such a CD57 and KLRG1 on highly differentiated cytotoxic T cells. It remains to be established as to whether the presence of such cell populations in blood and muscle of IBM patients is an epiphenomenon or a driver for pathogenesis. Further research is required into these T cell populations to help determine whether they have a causative role. The monoclonal antibody ABC008/Ulviprubart has been developed to target KLRG1 expressing T cells and is currently being evaluated in a large pivotal trial. Further advancement in the understanding of the role that immunosenescence plays in IBM may improve prospects for developing targeted therapies against this complex disease.
