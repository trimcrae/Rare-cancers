## Abstract
Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain malignancy in adults, is characterized by extensive vascularization and resistance to conventional anti-angiogenic therapies. In this study, through comprehensive integrative analyses of bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq data, we identify COL4A2 as a critical orchestrator of vascularization in GBM. Elevated COL4A2 not only promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in glioma cells, but also increases vascularization in GBM. Multi-omics profiling and mechanistic investigations reveal that aberrant expression of the anti-pause enhancer JMJD6 mediates the upregulation of COL4A2 in GBM. Furthermore, we demonstrate that COL4A2 promotes GBM vascularization by activating PI3K-AKT and MAPK-ERK signaling through interaction with ITGA1/ITGB1 receptors on tumor-associated endothelial cells (TECs). Pharmacological inhibition of the COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 axis with obtustatin attenuates pro-angiogenic signaling, suppresses vascularization, and prolongs survival in orthotopic GBM models. Collectively, our findings establish JMJD6-driven COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 axis as a novel anti-angiogenic therapeutic vulnerability, offering a promising strategy to disrupt TEC-tumor symbiosis and impede GBM progression.

## Introduction
Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain malignancy in adults, remains a therapeutic challenge with a median survival of less than 16 months despite multimodal interventions including surgery, radiotherapy, and temozolomide chemotherapy [ , ]. A hallmark of GBM pathogenesis lies in its exuberant vascularization, driven by hypoxia-induced angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [ – ], which are prime targets for anti-angiogenic therapies [ ]. Although anti-angiogenic agents like bevacizumab (BEV), a VEGF-A monoclonal antibody [ , ], transiently improve progression-free survival in recurrent GBM, they consistently fail to extend overall survival due to rapid therapeutic resistance, even in combination with radiotherapy-temozolomide therapy [ , ]. Moreover, combinations of BEV with sorafenib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor blocking VEGFRs, have not improved patient outcomes [ ].
Recent studies highlight the pivotal role of tumor-associated endothelial cells (TECs) in fostering a pro-angiogenic microenvironment through aberrant crosstalk with glioma cells [ ]. Unlike normal endothelial cells (NECs), TECs exhibit distinct phenotypic and transcriptomic profiles, including enhanced angiogenic potential and resistance to anti-VEGF therapies [ – ]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying TEC-tumor interactions and their contribution to therapy resistance remain poorly characterized. Exploring these interactions could reveal novel therapeutic targets to disrupt the tumor-supportive relationship between TECs and GBM cells, potentially inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis.
In this study, our comparative analysis revealed significant enrichment of TECs in glioblastoma relative to low-grade gliomas, with extensive TECs infiltration predicting poor survival outcomes. By analyzing both bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, we identified the COL4A2 as a linchpin of GBM vascularization. Integrative transcriptomic and epigenomic analyses further revealed that JMJD6-dependent epigenetic mechanisms underlie the aberrant overexpression of COL4A2. Functional validation revealed the dual oncogenic role of COL4A2: it orchestrates mesenchymal transition in glioma cells while simultaneously activating ITGA1/ITGB1-mediated PI3K-AKT and MAPK-ERK signaling cascades in TECs to fuel pathological angiogenesis. Therapeutic intervention with obtustatin, a selective integrin antagonist, effectively disrupted this pro-angiogenic axis, resulting in vascular normalization, tumor growth suppression, and significantly extended survival in preclinical models. Notably, our findings also revealed a correlation between COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 activation and anti-VEGF therapy resistance, suggesting a promising new strategy to overcome therapeutic resistance in GBM.

## Materials and methods

## Datasets and samples
Data from the merged GBMLGG cohort (TCGA, 2016) and the CGGA mRNAseq-325 dataset were downloaded from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) [ ] and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) data portal (http://www.cgga.org.cn/) [ ], respectively. Data for the BEV treatment cohorts (GSE72951, GSE79671, and GSE81465) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-sequencing data (GSE74529) for Pol II, Jumonji C-domain-containing protein 6 (JMJD6), and the dynamic enhancer mark lysine-27 acetylation of histone 3 (H3K27Ac) in the mesenchymal glioblastoma model were downloaded from GEO and leveraged to reveal key transcriptional regulators [ ]. This study included six glioma tissue samples from patients undergoing surgical treatment at Zhengzhou Central Hospital Affiliated to Zhengzhou University.

## Single-cell RNA-seq analysis
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from high-grade glioma tissue cells obtained from eight human patients were accessed via GSE103224 [ ]. The scRNA-seq analyses were performed using the Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub 2 (TISCH2, http://tisch.comp-genomics.org) platform [ ], which implements the MAESTRO workflow for standardized data processing. During quality control, cells with fewer than 200 detected genes or more than 10% mitochondrial reads were removed. Then, UMI counts were normalized to counts per 10,000 and log1₀(x + 1) transformed. Batch effects were corrected via Harmony integration when merging multiple samples. For dimensionality reduction, highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis, and the top components were used to compute UMAP embeddings. Cell-type annotation leveraged a manually curated panel of ∼ 30 canonical marker genes (e.g., CD3D for T cells, PECAM1 for endothelial cells) combined with automated labeling by SingleR against the Human Primary Cell Atlas, with all labels cross-checked against original publication metadata. Cell–cell communication inference was carried out using the CellChat package: average expression per cell type was used to score ligand–receptor interactions from the CellChat database, with a built-in permutation test (n = 1,000) to derive P-values. Interactions with P < 0.05 were deemed significant and are presented in our figures. Additional scRNA-seq data (GSE84465 and GSE57872) were analyzed through CancerSEA [ ].

## Analysis of the abundance of tumor-associated endothelial cells
The R package “IOBR”, a computational tool for effective Immuno-Oncology Biological Research, was used to analyze the tumor microenvironment in this study [ ]. The xCell and EPIC algorithms in “IOBR” were selected to calculate the abundance of tumor-associated endothelial cells for each sample [ , ].

## DEGs screening and enrichment analysis
Differentially expressed genes (DEGs, p < 0.05 and |FoldChange| ≥ 1.5) between low- and high-infiltration groups were screened using the R package “limma” [ ]. GO enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape (https://metascape.org) [ ]. KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using the KOBAS-i (http://bioinfo.org/kobas) [ ].

## Weighted correlation network analysis
Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using the R package “WGCNA.” Outlier genes and samples were excluded with the “goodSamplesGenes” function from the same package. Subsequently, a weighted adjacency matrix was created using a power function. By selecting a power of 8, the adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). Genes were then grouped into different modules based on TOM-based dissimilarity measures. Modules with a height less than 0.25 were merged. Module Membership (MM) and Gene Significance (GS) were calculated, and based on the cut-off criteria (|MM| >0.8 and |GS| >0.5), three genes with high connectivity in the clinically significant module were identified as hub genes.

## Cell culture
HUVECs, GL261, and 293T cells were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. HUVECs were maintained in endothelial cell medium (ECM, ScienCell) supplemented with 1% ECGS, 5% foetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. GL261 and 293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin.

## Gene Silencing and overexpressing
For transient knockdown, negative control siRNA and target siRNAs (Supplementary Table ) synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd were transfected according to the manufacturer’s instructions for Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015). After 48 h of transfection, cells were harvested for subsequent mRNA or protein expression analysis. COL4A2 and JMJD6 were stably knocked down in GL261 cells using Lenti-shCOL4A2 and Lenti-shJMJD6, constructed by OBiO Technology Co., Ltd and Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd, respectively (Supplementary Table ) Overexpression of COL4A2 was achieved using Lenti-dCAS9-VP64 and Lenti-sgRNA-COL4A2, constructed by Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd (Supplementary Table ).

## Quantitative real-time PCR
RNA was extracted from harvested cells using Trizol (Thermo Fisher), and then 1 µg of total RNA was utilized to prepare cDNA using NovoScript® Plus All-in-one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (gDNA Purge) (Novoprotein E047-01B) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Thereafter, relative gene expression was evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein E096-01 A). The GAPDH was utilized as an internal control, and all primer sequences have been listed in Supplementary Table .

## Western blotting
Cells were harvested and lysed in lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Epizyme), incubated on ice for 15 min, and cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay kits (Epizyme). Equal amounts of protein were separated by electrophoresis, electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk or BSA in TBST solution for 2 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against COL4A2 (PA5-79058, Invitrogen), JMJD6 (16476-1-AP, Proteintech), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, Proteintech), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP, Proteintech), Akt (4691, CST), Phospho-Akt (4060, CST), p38 (8690, CST), Phospho-p38 (4511, CST), JNK (9252, CST), Phospho-JNK (4668, CST), ERK (4695, CST), Phospho-ERK (4370, CST), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech). After washing the blots three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (SA00001-2, Proteintech) or HRP-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (SA00001-1, Proteintech) for 2 h at room temperature. Labeled proteins were then detected using ECL reagent. Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ and normalized to β-actin.

## Cell proliferation and colony formation assay
Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies) and EdU kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the colony formation assay, 1 × 103 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plate culture. The culture medium was changed every 3 days, and the cell status was monitored. Colony formation was observed after 14 days of culture.

## Tube formation
Tube formation assays were conducted using Ibidi µ-Slide Angiogenesis (Ibidi) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HUVECs were seeded onto polymerized Matrigel at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. Tube formation was observed using an inverted microscope after 24 h of incubation and analyzed using ImageJ software.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
The CDS region of JMJD6 was cloned into the eukaryotic expression vector pcDNA3.1, and the promoter sequence of the COL4A2 gene was constructed into the GPL4-basic vector. pcDNA3.1-basic or pcDNA3.1-JMJD6 and GPL4-basic or GPL4-promoter-COL4A2 were co-transfected into 293T cells with GPL4-Renilla vector. 48 h after transfection, cells were harvested for analysis using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (E1960, Promega).

## Animal study
C57BL/6J mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from BEIJING HFK BIOSCIENCE CO., LTD. The mice were housed in sterile individually ventilated cages (IVCs) in an isolation facility, maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and provided with sterilized food and water. For the establishment of xenografts in C57BL/6J mice, 1 × 107 cells suspended in 100 µl PBS solution were injected subcutaneously into the left hypogastrium of each mouse. Tumors were harvested after 4 weeks for subsequent analysis.
To establish in situ intracerebral models in C57BL/6J mice, 2 × 105 cells suspended in 2 µl PBS solution were injected into the right caudate nucleus of the brain. Mice were used for survival studies and monitored for neurological symptoms, or sacrificed on day 21 for subsequent analysis.
For treatment effect studies, xenograft mice were treated with blank or Obtustatin (MCE, HY-P1408) every 2 days starting on day six. Mice were sacrificed on day 28 for subsequent analysis. All in vivo procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou Central Hospital Affiliated to Zhengzhou University.

## Hematoxylin-eosin staining
Frozen sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. They were then stained with hematoxylin for 5–10 min, followed by eosin staining for 30 s to 2 min. After washing with 70% ethanol, the sections were dehydrated, made transparent, and sealed. They were subsequently observed and photographed under a microscope.

## Immunofluorescence assay
Sections were fixed, subjected to antigen retrieved, permeabilized and blocked with donkey serum for 1 h. They were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies against COL4A2 (PA5-79058, Invitrogen), vWF (66682-1-lg, Proteintech), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, Proteintech), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP, Proteintech), and Ki-67 (28074-1-AP, Proteintech). After washing with PBST (5 min, 3 times), the sections were incubated with either Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 555, ab150078) or Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647, ab150115). Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI-containing anti-fluorescence quenching blocker and visualized using a laser confocal microscope.

## Statistical analysis
The significance of differences in infiltrating cells and gene expression was analyzed using one-way ANOVA, t-tests, and the Wilcoxon test. The log-rank test was employed to assess the significance of Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 and R software, with P values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Tumor-associated endothelial cell infiltration predicts poor prognosis in GBM
Anti-angiogenic therapy for glioma presents significant challenges, as initial benefits are often short-lived and soon countered by the development of drug resistance, rendering gliomas increasingly refractory to treatment and more aggressive over time. To investigate the role of TECs in this process, we assessed TEC infiltration levels in the TCGA GBMLGG and CGGA mRNAseq-325 cohorts using the XCELL and EPIC algorithms. Our results revealed a significant increase in TECs in GBMs compared to low-grade gliomas (Fig.  A and C). Additionally, TEC infiltration varied across glioma subtypes (Supplementary Fig.  ), with a notable accumulation in IDH wild-type gliomas compared to IDH mutant gliomas (Fig.  D and E). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct separation between high and low TEC infiltration groups (Fig.  F). Critically, elevated TEC infiltration strongly correlated with reduced overall survival in both the TCGA GBMLGG and CGGA mRNAseq-325 cohorts (Fig.  G and H), underscoring its prognostic relevance.
 

## COL4A2 emerges as a key hub gene in TEC-driven vascularization
To elucidate the mechanisms by which malignant tumor cells drive TEC infiltration, we initially identified the DEGs (p < 0.05, |FoldChange| >1.5) between high- and low-TEC infiltration groups in the TCGA GBMLGG and CGGA mRNAseq-325 cohorts. A total of 1,397 DEGs were identified, including 958 up-regulated genes and 439 down-regulated genes (Fig.  A). Enrichment analysis indicated that up-regulated DEGs were significantly involved in vascular-related pathways, including vasculature development, extracellular matrix organization, response to wounding, regulation of angiogenesis, collagen formation, burn wound healing (Fig.  B). To unveil candidate regulatory genes, we performed weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) by integrating the expression profile of DEGs and clinical traits (Fig.  C and E). Four modules (gene sets) strongly correlated with TEC infiltration were identified (Fig.  D), and the following hub genes were screened out: CD248, CLIC1, COL1A2, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A1, COL6A2, GDF15, GJC1, GPX8, IGFBP2, KIAA0040, PCOLCE, PDLIM1, PLAT, PLEK2, TIMP1 (Fig.  E).
 We then applied the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) to determine the optimal number of prognostic factors for a multivariate regression model (Fig.  F and G). Multivariate Cox analysis subsequently identified COL4A2, GJC1, and IGFBP2 as positive prognostic factors for glioma survival (Fig.  H, Supplementary Fig.  ). Based on the expression of these three genes, we constructed a TEC-related signature in the TCGA cohort (Fig.  H), which was successfully validated in the CGGA #325 cohort (Supplementary Fig.  A). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed that high-risk scores were associated with poor overall survival (Fig.  I, Supplementary Fig.  B). Moreover, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that the TEC score exhibited high sensitivity and specificity in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival in glioma patients (Fig.  J, Supplementary Fig.  C). Additionally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, which included the TEC score as well as clinical factors such as age, gender, WHO grade, IDH status, and MGMT promoter status, indicated that TEC infiltration is an independent predictor of glioma (Supplementary Figs.  D- E).
We also explored the relationship between risk scores and clinical phenotypes (Supplementary Figs.  A- D) and observed increased TEC infiltration in the high-risk group (Supplementary Figs.  E- F). Moreover, TEC infiltration was positively correlated with the risk score and hub genes IGFBP2, COL4A2, and GJC1 (Fig.  K and L, Supplementary Fig.  G). Finally, our analysis indicated that the TEC-related signature also correlated with poor prognosis across multiple cancer types, including ACC, BLCA, KIRP, MESO, STAD, and UVM (Supplementary Fig.  H).

## Single-cell transcriptomics reveals COL4A2-mediated TEC-tumor crosstalk
To validate our bulk transcriptome findings, we analyzed a massively parallel single-cell RNA-Seq data (GSE103224) from the GEO database, which comprised 17,185 cells derived from eight high-grade glioma patients (Fig.  A). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis identified 28 unsupervised clusters (Fig.  A), which were categorized into major cell lineages based on specific markers: AC-like malignant cells (59.3%), NB-like malignant cells (16%), OC-like malignant cells (10.3%), OPC-like malignant cells (4.2%), monocytes/macrophages (7.7%), and endothelial cells (2.2%) (Fig.  B). We then analyzed the expression of the TEC signature (collapse genes by mean), as well as IGFBP2, COL4A2, and GJC1 across these cell lineages. High expression of these genes and TEC signature was observed in AC-like malignant cells, particularly in cluster 22 (Fig.  C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that cluster 22 was enriched for pathways involved in ECM receptor interaction and leukocyte transendothelial migration (Supplementary Fig.  A). Additionally, hallmark gene sets associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and angiogenesis were upregulated in cluster 22 (Fig.  D and F), which were further corroborated by other scRNA-seq datasets (GSE84465, GSE57872) (Fig.  G). Cell-cell interaction analysis revealed robust intercellular communication between the AC-like cluster 22 and the endothelial cluster 19 (Fig.  H, Supplementary Figs.  B- C). Strikingly, the COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 ligand-receptor pair emerged as a dominant interaction, with ITGA1/ITGB1 highly co-expressed in both endothelial cluster 19 and AC-like cluster 22 (Fig.  I, Supplementary Figs.  D- E). These results indicate that COL4A2 not only mediates vascular crosstalk between tumor cells and endothelial cells but may also influence tumor cell proliferation and phenotypic characteristics.
 

## COL4A2 drives EMT and angiogenesis via PI3K-AKT/MAPK pathways
To investigate the functional role of COL4A2 in glioma, we silenced its expression in GL261 cells. This intervention significantly suppressed EMT (Figs.   A- C), while concurrently reducing cellular proliferation and impairing clonogenic survival capacity (Fig.  D and F). Next, we assessed the role of COL4A2 in angiogenesis. Knockdown of COL4A2 reduced its protein concentration in culture supernatants (Fig.  G), and co-culturing these cells with HUVECs markedly diminished the angiogenic potential of HUVECs compared to control cells (Fig.  H). In contrast, overexpression of COL4A2 in GL261 cells enhanced EMT (Fig.  I and K) and increased both proliferative and clonogenic capacities (Fig.  L and N). Moreover, overexpression led to elevated COL4A2 protein levels in the supernatant (Fig.  O), which in turn enhanced the angiogenic capacity of HUVECs (Fig.  P).
 To gain insights into the mechanism by which COL4A2 regulates angiogenesis, we analyzed activated signaling pathways in endothelial cells using data from GSE103224. Our analysis revealed that the PI3K-AKT and MAPK signaling pathways were active in endothelial cells (Fig.  Q). We then examined whether COL4A2 modulates these pathways. Our results showed that knockdown of COL4A2 in GL261 cells led to a reduction in the phosphorylation of AKT and ERK in co-cultured endothelial cells, whereas overexpression of COL4A2 increased phosphorylation of AKT and ERK (Fig.  R and T). These findings suggest that COL4A2 promotes angiogenesis through the activation of the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways.

## COL4A2 promotes GBM progression and vascularization
To further investigate the clinical relevance of COL4A2, we analyzed its expression in the TCGA and CGGA datasets, which revealed a significant upregulation in GBMs (Fig.  A). Analysis of the GSE59612 dataset unveiled a progressive upregulation of COL4A2 from normal tissue to the tumor margin and core (Fig.  A). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that high COL4A2 expression correlated with poorer overall survival in glioma patients (Fig.  B). To validate these findings, we assessed COL4A2 expression in glioma tissues and confirmed its upregulation in GBM tissues compared to lower-grade gliomas (Fig.  C and D). We then established a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model and observed significant inhibition of tumor growth upon COL4A2 knockdown (Fig.  E and F). We also developed an in situ GBM model and demonstrated a marked prolongation of overall survival in the COL4A2 knockdown group (Fig.  G and H). Immunofluorescence assays showed a reduction in ki67+ cells and vWF+ cells (% of area), decreased fluorescence intensity of N-cadherin, and increased fluorescence intensity of E-cadherin in the COL4A2 silencing group compared to the control group (Fig.  I and M). Collectively, these results suggest that silencing COL4A2 expression suppresses GBM cell proliferation and EMT, while significantly reducing vascularization in the GBM model.
 

## JMJD6 epigenetically regulates COL4A2 expression in GBM
To elucidate the mechanism underlying COL4A2 upregulation in GBM, we performed an enrichment analysis of transcriptional regulators within the AC-like malignant cluster 22. Our results indicated that JMJD6 may serve as a pivotal regulator within this cluster (Fig.  A). Previous studies have demonstrated that JMJD6 and Brd4 co-bind as anti-pause enhancers, thereby regulating the release of promoter-proximal pausing for a substantial subset of transcription units through long-range interactions. Integrative analysis of ChIP-seq data (GSE74529) revealed co-occupancy of RNA polymerase II (Pol II), JMJD6, and the active enhancer mark H3K27ac at the COL4A2 genomic locus, suggesting JMJD6-mediated enhancer activation (Fig.  B). Furthermore, we observed promoter hypomethylation of COL4A2 among high-grade glioma samples (Fig.  C), reinforcing the transcriptional activation mechanism. Notably, low methylation levels were associated with a poorer survival prognosis, and a significant negative correlation was observed between methylation levels and COL4A2 expression (Fig.  D and E).
 Consistent with its regulatory role, JMJD6 expression progressively escalated with glioma malignancy across TCGA, CGGA, and GSE59612 cohorts (Fig.  F), paralleling COL4A2 upregulation, and immunohistochemistry confirmed elevated JMJD6 protein levels in high-grade tumors (Fig.  G). Subsequent correlation analysis indicated a robust positive correlation between JMJD6 and COL4A2 expression cross datasets further supported their regulatory relationship (Fig.  H). Functional validation demonstrated that JMJD6 knockdown in GL261 cells significantly suppressed COL4A2 transcription and protein levels (Fig.  I and L), while dual-luciferase assays confirmed JMJD6’s direct enhancement of COL4A2 promoter activity (Fig.  M). Collectively, these findings establish JMJD6 as a critical epigenetic driver of COL4A2 overexpression through enhancer-mediated transcriptional activation in GBM.

## JMJD6 promotes GBM progression and vascularization
We next investigated the potential role of JMJD6 in glioma. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that high JMJD6 expression correlated with poorer overall survival in glioma patients compared to those with low expression (Fig.  A). Furthermore, JMJD6 expression was associated with endothelial cell infiltration (Fig.  B). Knockdown of JMJD6 in GL261 cells inhibited EMT (Fig.  K and L) and reduced their proliferation and colony formation capacity (Fig.  C and E). JMJD6 knockdown also decreased COL4A2 protein concentration in cell culture supernatants (Fig.  F). When co-cultured with these cells, HUVECs exhibited diminished tube formation ability compared to controls (Fig.  G), alongside reduced phosphorylation of AKT and ERK (Fig.  H and I).
 To confirm that the anti-angiogenic and anti-proliferative phenotypes observed following JMJD6 knockdown were mediated specifically by COL4A2 deficiency, we performed rescue experiments. Restoring COL4A2 expression in JMJD6-knockdown GL261 cells reversed the EMT suppression (Fig.  J and K) and proliferation inhibition caused by JMJD6 knockdown (Fig.  L and M). Co-culture experiments further demonstrated that rescuing COL4A2 expression restored HUVEC tube formation capacity (Fig.  N) and significantly increased AKT and ERK phosphorylation levels in endothelial cells (Fig.  O and P).
In vivo, JMJD6 knockdown significantly suppressed subcutaneous xenograft growth (Fig.  A) and prolonged survival in an orthotopic GBM model (Fig.  B and C). Immunofluorescence assays showed decreased ki67+ cells and vWF+ cells (% of area), along with reduced fluorescence intensity of JMJD6 and COL4A2 in JMJD6 knockdown tumors compared to controls (Fig.  D and G). These results indicate that JMJD6 promotes GBM progression and vascularization by regulating COL4A2 expression. These data demonstrate that JMJD6 drives GBM progression and vascularization by upregulating COL4A2.
 

## Blocking COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 interaction inhibits vascularization in GBM
In the present study, we identified that malignant glioma cells primarily interact with endothelial cells through the binding of COL4A2 (collagen type IV alpha 2) to the α1β1 integrin receptor, composed of ITGA1 and ITGB1. Notably, upregulation of β1 integrin promotes resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy in GBM patients following BEV failure and in BEV-resistant xenograft models [ , ]. To further investigate the role of COL4A2 and ITGA1 in resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy, we conducted Kaplan-Meier analysis, which revealed that high expression levels of COL4A2 and ITGA1 were significantly associated with poor prognosis in the BEV treatment cohort (Fig.  A). Additionally, COL4A2 expression was notably higher in non-responders compared to responders within the BEV treatment cohort (Fig.  B). Prolonged BEV treatment resulted in an increase in ITGA expression in xenograft models, relative to initial treatment (Fig.  C). Responders exhibited significantly reduced COL4A2 and ITGA1 levels post-BEV treatment, whereas non-responders showed no significant change (Supplementary Fig.  ). Collectively, these results highlight the critical role of the COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 axis in GBM vascularization and bevacizumab resistance.
 Obtustatin, a 41-residue non-RGD type disintegrin isolated from the venom of Vipera lebetina obtusa (Fig.  D), is a potent and selective inhibitor of the adhesion of type IV collagen to integrin α1β1. Therefore, we explored whether blocking COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 using obtustatin could inhibit vascularization in GBM (Fig.  E). In a subcutaneous xenograft model, obtustatin treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig.  F and G). Immunofluorescence assays further revealed a reduction in Ki67 + and vWF + cells (percentage of area) in the obtustatin-treated tumors compared to the controls (Fig.  H and I). Moreover, obtustatin treatment effectively reversed the increased angiogenesis (Supplementary Fig.  ) and elevated phosphorylation of AKT and ERK induced by COL4A2 overexpression, suggesting that obtustatin inhibits angiogenesis by blocking the PI3K-AKT and MAPK signaling pathways (Fig.  J and L). Furthermore, obtustatin also reversed the upregulation of key EMT-related transcription factors Twist1 and Snail1 caused by COL4A2 overexpression (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Discussion
High vascularization within GBM tumors, primarily driven by hypoxia, supports their rapid growth and diffuse infiltration into surrounding brain tissue [ ]. Despite the approval of anti-VEGF therapies for recurrent glioblastomas [ , ], these treatments have failed to demonstrate significant overall survival benefits [ – ]. While angiogenesis inhibitors combined with anticancer drugs have been effective in the early stages of other cancers, resistance typically develops over time, limiting their long-term therapeutic efficacy. Recent studies indicate that TECs exhibit abnormalities compared to NECs, such as increased angiogenic potential [ ], chromosomal abnormalities [ ], and a distinct transcriptomic profile characterized by elevated expression of COX-2, VEGF, CXCR7, LOX, and PTGIR [ – ], as well as upregulation of stem cell markers [ , ]. These changes are driven by the tumor microenvironment, which is shaped in part by cytokines secreted by both tumor and stromal cells, ultimately contributing to the development of drug resistance in cancer [ ]. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying the interaction between tumor cells and TECs may reveal novel therapeutic targets to inhibit angiogenesis and impede GBM progression.
Through bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq, we identified a specific interaction-COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1-that is significantly correlated with TEC accumulation in GBM and may serve as a key mediator of high vascularization and GBM progression. Collagen IV has been implicated in the molecular pathogenesis of multiple tumors, including esophageal, lung, and prostate and live cancers, by regulating tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and immune responses [ – ]. In this study, we demonstrated that high COL4A2 expression exacerbates tumor cell proliferation and induces EMT in GBM. As a member of the type IV collagen family, COL4A2 is normally secreted by tumor cells and endothelial cells. Newly synthesized COL4A2 assembles into heterotrimeric pro-collagen complexes with COL4A1 and COL4A3 and is secreted via the classical secretory pathway. Then COL4A2 protein can be incorporated into the insoluble basement membrane extracellular matrix, where they structurally supports endothelial cell adhesion through integrin α1β1 binding, a prerequisite for angiogenic endothelial migration [ ]. We observed that, beyond its architectural role, COL4A2 in the microenvironment functionally activates PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling cascades in TECs, mimicking VEGF-driven pro-angiogenic mechanisms that sustain endothelial survival, proliferation, and motility [ , ]. Therefore, COL4A2 can coordinate vascular remodeling, thereby fueling GBM’s aggressive growth and infiltrative spread.
These findings further propose that the COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 axis may contribute to resistance against anti-angiogenic therapies in GBM. Integrins are well-established regulators of both physiological and pathological angiogenesis [ ]. Previous studies have shown that knocking out α1 integrin in animal models reduces tumor vascular density [ ]. Moreover, β1 integrin has been found to be functionally upregulated in GBM patients who failed bevacizumab (BEV) treatment, as well as in BEV-resistant xenograft models [ , ], where it promotes therapy resistance by enhancing various malignant processes through interactions with the tumor microenvironment [ , ]. Our analysis of BEV-treated cohorts extends these insights, showing that COL4A2 and ITGA1 are upregulated in resistant tumors, suggesting their role in sustaining vascularization under therapeutic pressure. This aligns with evidence that integrin-mediated signaling may compensate for VEGF pathway inhibition by reactivating PI3K/MAPK cascades. To therapeutically exploit this axis, we targeted the COL4A2-integrin interface using obtustatin-a 41-residue non-RGD disintegrin. This intervention blocked PI3K-AKT and MAPK signaling, attenuated aggressive tumor phenotypes and suppressed vascularization in GBM models. These results position COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 as a multifaceted therapeutic node with dual potential: suppressing primary tumor vascularization and potentially overcoming anti-angiogenic resistance.
While our study establishes the functional importance of the JMJD6–COL4A2–α1β1 axis in GBM angiogenesis and demonstrates obtustatin’s antitumor activity, several limitations and translational questions remain. First, although JMJD6 is reported to interact with BRD4 and to demethylate histone arginine residues [ , ], we did not dissect which regulatory mechanism predominates at the COL4A2 enhancer. Future studies employing co-immunoprecipitation of JMJD6-BRD4 complexes, ChIP-qPCR for histone arginine methylation marks, and catalytically inactive JMJD6 mutants will clarify the precise mode of action. Second, our proof-of-concept study utilized a fixed dose (5 mg/kg) of obtustatin based on prior anti-angiogenic studies [ ], without performing dedicated dose-optimization, toxicity evaluations, or comprehensive PK/PD analyses. Comprehensive dose optimization, systemic toxicity assessment (e.g., hepatorenal/hematological safety), and pharmacokinetic studies (e.g., plasma half-life, brain penetration) in glioma models remain essential to define its therapeutic index. Finally, while we demonstrate that obtustatin effectively inhibits COL4A2–α1β1 signaling in isolation, we did not evaluate its efficacy in combination with standard-of-care anti-angiogenic therapies (such as bevacizumab or VEGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors). Future studies should therefore investigate potential additive or synergistic effects of such combination regimens to optimize therapeutic outcomes.

## Conclusions
Our study identifies the JMJD6-driven COL4A2-ITGA1/ITGB1 interaction as a key driver of GBM vascularization (Fig.  M). Pharmacological disruption of this axis using obtustatin significantly attenuated tumor vascularization and extended survival in GBM models, demonstrating its potential as a novel therapeutic strategy for GBM.
