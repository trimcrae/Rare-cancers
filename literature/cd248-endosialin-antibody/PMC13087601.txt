## Abstract
Immune infiltration is a key factor of inflammatory responses and tissue remodeling during tissue injury and degeneration. In the immune microenvironment of degenerated intervertebral discs, the dynamic balance between M1 and M2 macrophage polarization is disrupted, impairing both frontline anti-inflammatory defense and rear tissue repair. Conventional single-target strategies are limited in their ability to simultaneously suppress inflammatory amplification and stabilize reparative capacity--often resulting in a “frontline unsettled, rear compromised” scenario. Here, we developed an “offense-defense synergy” macrophage reprogramming hydrogel platform that integrates dual physical-chemical regulatory mechanisms to achieve synergistic intervention at both the offensive and defensive ends. The platform incorporates reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive dynamic covalent bond-based phenylboronic ester-functionalized nanoparticles, enabling precise scavenging of excessive ROS during the inflammatory phase, thereby blocking the M1-driven inflammatory amplification loop and rapidly attenuating frontline inflammatory signaling. Concurrently, an embedded poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) scaffold delivers localized electrical stimulation during the reparative phase to prevent rear M2 repolarization and promote extracellular matrix (ECM) regeneration, achieving full-cycle precision control over macrophage fate. Experimental results in a rat puncture-induced intervertebral disc degeneration model showed that this strategy enhanced disc repair outcomes, indicating its potential for multifaceted therapeutic intervention. This “offense-defense synergy” immunoregulatory approach provides a potential material-based strategy for intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) and may inform the design of precision immunomodulation platforms.
Keywords: Injectable hydrogel, Macrophage reprogramming, Disc degeneration, Immunomodulation

## Introduction
Immune infiltration represents a key feature of the host's response to pathological insults, playing an important role in the onset, progression, and resolution of a broad spectrum of human diseases [ , ]. Aberrant infiltration and functional dysregulation of immune cells frequently disrupt the local microenvironment, precipitating chronic inflammation, tissue injury, and disease exacerbation [ ]. In chronic pathological states, immune cells and resident tissues engage in intricate intercellular communication networks, mediated by cytokines, metabolites, and extracellular vesicles, to orchestrate tissue repair and maintain functional homeostasis [ , ]. Consequently, precise modulation of immune infiltration and immune cell function has emerged as a key strategy in contemporary medicine.
Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a leading cause of chronic low back pain, with a pathological cascade characterized by nucleus pulposus cell senescence, extracellular matrix breakdown, and activation of inflammatory responses [ , , , ]. In the healthy state, the avascular nature of the disc confers an immune-privileged status, restricting immune cell infiltration [ ]. During degeneration, however, disruption of structural barriers permits aberrant accumulation of immune cells, fostering a persistent inflammatory microenvironment [ ]. Among these, macrophages--often the earliest infiltrating immune cells--play a critical role [ , ]. When polarized toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, they secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, accelerating functional decline of nucleus pulposus cells [ ]. Through metabolic crosstalk and signal transduction, these macrophages establish a self-perpetuating inflammatory feedback loop, positioning themselves as key regulators in disease progression [ , ]. Unlike the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, M2 macrophages play a critical role in anti-inflammatory regulation and tissue repair in IVDD. Driven predominantly by cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, and IL-10, these cells polarize via canonical signaling cascades, exhibit high expression of markers including CD206 and Arg-1, and secrete anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10 and TGF-β [ , ]. Notably, the relative abundance of M1 and M2 macrophages undergoes dynamic shifts over the course of IVDD: M1 cells predominate during the early inflammatory phase, whereas M2 cells become more prevalent in the later reparative phase [ ]. However, under conditions of persistent inflammatory stimulation, M2 polarization is often insufficient or functionally impaired, resulting in inadequate repair responses and the perpetuation of chronic inflammation.
A growing body of biomaterials‐based strategies has been developed to modulate macrophage function in the context of tissue pathology. On the chemical regulation front, researchers have engineered diverse drug‐delivery systems--such as IL-10, siRNA, and antioxidant carriers or harnessed natural biopolymers, including chitosan and alginate, to drive macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby attenuating inflammatory responses. For example, Shardool Jain [ ] and colleagues designed tuftsin‐modified alginate nanoparticles capable of efficiently delivering IL-10 plasmid DNA, which in a rheumatoid arthritis model robustly induced M2 polarization, reprogrammed the immune microenvironment, suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and mitigated joint damage. In terms of physical regulation, modalities such as low‐intensity pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMF), micro‐electrical stimulation, and piezoelectric materials have been employed to modulate membrane potential and ion channel activity, thereby influencing macrophage phenotype and secretory behavior. Kong [ ] and co‐workers reported that β-PVDF piezoelectric materials, when driven by ultrasound, release surface charges that activate the Ca2+-CAMK2A-NF-κB signaling axis, enhancing M1 polarization and suppressing tumor cell proliferation--offering a novel electro‐genetic tool for immunotherapy.
In the pathological microenvironment, the balance between M1 and M2 macrophages is dynamic, shifting in response to disease stage, local signaling cues, and metabolic context. Persistent inflammation and oxidative stress drive the accumulation of M1-type macrophages, which secrete abundant pro-inflammatory mediators, accelerating extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and cellular injury [ , , , ]. Effective suppression of M1 polarization is therefore needed. Concurrently, M2-type macrophages--normally anti-inflammatory and pro-repair--can undergo repolarization to a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype under pathological conditions, further amplifying the inflammatory cascade. Thus, it is necessary not only to block the generation and expansion of M1 cells, but also to prevent the pathological conversion of M2 to M1 within an adverse microenvironment [ , , ]. Only by addressing both fronts can the inflammatory response be brought under control and a regenerative milieu established. Conventional single-target strategies typically act on only one side of this imbalance, failing to both restrain M1 expansion and stabilize M2 populations. This often results in a “frontline unsettled, rear compromised” scenario--where inflammation remains unchecked and reparative capacity cannot be sustained. To overcome these constraints, we propose an integrated and precision-driven “offense-defense” macrophage reprogramming strategy. In the “offense” phase, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are selectively scavenged via engineered nanoparticles, disrupting the M1-driven inflammatory amplification loop and rapidly silencing pro-inflammatory signaling. In the “defense” phase, targeted electrical stimulation prevents the adverse microenvironment-driven repolarization of M2 macrophages toward the M1 phenotype, thereby sustaining their reparative function and promoting ECM regeneration. Acting in concert, these two arms establish a dual-layered regulatory system-front-line clearance coupled with rear-guard stabilization, enabling integrated control over macrophage fate.
Despite these advances, existing chemical and physical strategies often face important limitations. Natural biopolymers such as chitosan and alginate offer favorable biocompatibility and have been extensively explored in biomedical applications. Nevertheless, some limitations remain. For chitosan, issues such as variability in purity, solubility, and mechanical strength can hinder large-scale reproducibility [ ]. Alginate, while widely used for its gel-forming properties, has been reported to exhibit batch-dependent immune responses, which may affect its reliability in controlled biomedical applications [ ]. Similarly, piezoelectric polymers such as PVDF can generate electrical signals, yet their non-degradable nature and potential cytotoxicity [ ] limit their suitability for disc regeneration. These shortcomings highlight the need for a platform that can simultaneously suppress pro-inflammatory signaling and sustain reparative macrophage function.
Phenylboronic acid derivatives form dynamic covalent bonds with ROS, enabling selective scavenging and reversible crosslinking under inflammatory conditions, thus providing a potential approach to manage uncontrolled oxidative stress [ ]. Gallic acid, a natural polyphenol, provides intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity while maintaining excellent biocompatibility and degradability [ ]. The complementary chemical properties of phenylboronic acid derivative and gallic acid therefore offer a basis for constructing a material capable of both mitigating oxidative stress and stabilizing anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes. In this study, to achieve an “offense-defense” synergistic reprogramming of macrophage function, we engineered a hydrogel platform integrating both physicochemical regulatory modalities ( ). The platform is based on a PLGA-PEG-PLGA thermosensitive hydrogel matrix, within which phenylboronic ester-functionalized nanoparticles serve as the bioactive core, embedded in a poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) scaffold. At its functional core are phenylboronic acid nanoparticles embedded within a PLLA matrix, which provide ROS scavenging and electrical stimulation. This design aims to balance pro-inflammatory suppression and reparative modulation within the immune microenvironment. The nanoparticles incorporate dynamic, ROS-responsive covalent bonds, imparting the hydrogel with reversible crosslinking capacity and microenvironmental adaptability. Under inflammatory conditions, this design allows on-demand release of functional payloads, executing the “offense” mission--eliminating excess ROS, inhibiting M1 polarization, and rapidly dampening pro-inflammatory signaling. Concurrently, PLLA and the hydrogel act as complementary scaffold components, providing robust mechanical support and gel stability while offering tunable degradation kinetics to fulfill the “defense” role--delivering electrical cues during the tissue repair phase to prevent the adverse microenvironment-driven repolarization of M2 macrophages toward the M1 phenotype, thereby sustaining their reparative function and promoting ECM regeneration. Through this dual physical-chemical regulatory mechanism, the hydrogel modulates macrophage polarization, establishing an integrated “offense-defense” immunoregulatory platform. This hydrogel platform integrates ROS-scavenging and electrical stimulation capabilities, offering a potential material-based strategy for immunomodulation in intervertebral disc degeneration.

## Results
2.1.

## Characterization and ROS-responsiveness of dextran-based nanoparticles
To construct ROS-responsive nanocarriers, phenylboronic acid-functionalized dextran (PHB-dextran) was synthesized via a one-step esterification between dextran hydroxyl groups and carbonyldiimidazole-activated phenylboronic acid derivative (PBA-COOH) ( A). The resulting amphiphilic polymer spontaneously self-assembled into spherical nanoparticles in aqueous solution [ ]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed spherical nanoparticles with smooth surfaces across multiple magnifications ( B). At lower magnifications, the particles appeared dispersed with a narrow size distribution. High-magnification SEM images further confirmed the spherical morphology and surface smoothness. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed successful esterification, as evidenced by the appearance of a strong ester carbonyl (C=O) stretching band (∼1735 cm−1), characteristic B-O (∼1330-1440 cm−1) and B-C (∼700-900 cm−1) vibrations from the phenylboronic acid moieties, and a broad O-H stretching band (∼3200-3500 cm−1) from residual hydroxyl groups ( C). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed a narrow particle size distribution, with a mean hydrodynamic diameter of ∼155 nm and a low polydispersity index (PDI <0.2), indicating good dispersion uniformity ( D). Zeta potential measurements showed a consistently positive surface charge (∼28 mV) ( E), suggesting favorable electrostatic repulsion for colloidal stability. To evaluate ROS-responsiveness, nanoparticle dispersions were incubated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and in the presence of 100 μM H2O2. After incubation, the H2O2-treated suspension appeared less turbid compared to the PBS control, indicating particle response to ROS ( F). In contrast, particles remained stable in PBS, indicating selective responsiveness to ROS stimuli.
 
2.2.

## PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogels and PLLA scaffolds characterization
To evaluate the thermal responsiveness of PLGA hydrogels, visual observations were performed at 25 °C and 37 °C over a 15-min period. At room temperature (25 °C), the hydrogel exhibited a low-viscosity sol state, whereas at physiological temperature (37 °C), a gradual sol-to-gel transition was observed, accompanied by a marked increase in apparent viscosity. This temperature-induced phase transition is consistent with the requirements for injectable in situ-forming hydrogel platforms ( A). Thermal and mechanical properties of PLGA-PEG-PLGA thermosensitive hydrogels were further characterized using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and rheological analysis across a range of polymer concentrations (2%-10% (w/v)) ( B). At 2% (w/v), the thermogram featured multiple weak low-temperature events (∼40-50 °C) and a broad, intense endotherm above ∼70 °C, consistent with high free-water content and loosely packed micellar assemblies. Increasing the concentration to 4% (w/v) yielded a sharp, well-defined transition near 50 °C, indicative of cooperative PEG-segment melting and micellar core-shell reorganization within a more ordered network. The 6% (w/v) formulation exhibited a largely featureless profile with only a modest shift near 70 °C, reflecting a stable, tightly packed micellar arrangement with suppressed cooperative transitions. At 8% (w/v), a minor low-temperature feature (∼25 °C) preceded a broad high-temperature transition (>70 °C), suggesting enhanced polymer-polymer interactions, denser micellar packing, and reduced water mobility. The 10% (w/v) hydrogel displayed a broad, complex endotherm near 50 °C, likely arising from overlapping PEG melting, micellar rearrangement, and release of tightly bound water, consistent with a highly interconnected supramolecular architecture. Collectively, these data indicate that increasing polymer concentration progressively enhances micellar packing and reduces free-water mobility, with corresponding changes in thermal response profiles. At low concentration (2% (w/v)), both storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were low and temperature-sensitive, suggesting weak mechanical integrity. As concentration increased, G′ became dominant and more stable across temperatures, indicating improved elasticity and gel formation. The 10% (w/v) PLGA hydrogel exhibited the highest G′ values and minimal temperature dependence, consistent with a structurally stable and mechanically robust network. Rheological analysis further confirmed the viscoelastic nature of the hydrogels ( C). At 2% (w/v), low and temperature-sensitive moduli with G″ ≥ G′ indicated a weak, sol-like network. From 4% (w/v) to 8% (w/v), G′ dominated over G″, with a consistent ∼50 °C transition attributed to PEG melting and micellar rearrangement, alongside progressively higher G′ values, reduced temperature sensitivity, and denser micellar packing. At 10% (w/v), the hydrogel showed the highest G′, minimal temperature dependence, and a broad 30-90 °C transition, reflecting overlapping thermal processes within a tightly packed, mechanically robust, and thermally stable network. Overall, increasing concentration shifted the platform from a weak sol to a stable elastic gel with enhanced mechanical integrity.
 Fibrous scaffolds were prepared from PLLA using an electrospinning technique ( D). The resulting membrane displayed a homogeneous white appearance and retained good flexibility ( ). FTIR spectroscopy confirmed the chemical integrity of the PLLA fibers, with characteristic absorption bands corresponding to ester carbonyl (C=O), alkyl (CH2), and ether (C-O) functional groups ( ). SEM imaging revealed that the electrospun PLLA scaffolds comprised a continuous, randomly oriented fibrous network at low magnification ( E). Higher-magnification views confirmed that the individual fibers exhibited smooth, defect-free surfaces and uniform diameters, indicative of stable electrospinning conditions and consistent fiber morphology. PLLA membranes generated reproducible voltage signals under ultrasound stimulation in both dry and hydrated conditions ( ). In dry samples, millivolt-level outputs were observed but the response to increasing power was relatively modest, likely reflecting limited mechanical–electrical coupling in the absence of a fluid medium. In contrast, after 24 h immersion in culture medium, PLLA exhibited a clear dose-dependent response, with peak-to-peak voltage rising from ∼4.4 mV at 0.25 W to ∼5.2 mV at 1 W. These results show that PLLA retains piezoelectric activity in vitro and that its electrical output can be modulated by stimulation intensity.
To further characterize the composite platform, we examined the physicochemical properties of the phenylboronic acid nanoparticles, the hydrogel matrix, and their mixtures with PLLA scaffolds. FTIR spectra exhibited characteristic absorption bands near ∼500 cm−1 (backbone vibrations), ∼1500-1600 cm−1 (aromatic C=C/amide II), and ∼3000 cm−1 (aliphatic C-H stretching), confirming the coexistence of hydrocarbon and aromatic functional groups within the composite ( ). These features indicate the incorporation of phenylboronic acid moieties into the hydrogel-scaffold system and suggest that the chemical structure of the composite remains intact after assembly. DLS analysis further revealed a bimodal particle size distribution, with a predominant peak around 100 nm and a secondary peak near 1000 nm ( ). Together, these findings indicate that the composite possesses chemical integrity and hierarchical structural organization.
To evaluate the biocompatibility of PLLA and the nanoparticles, we examined the viability of macrophages and nucleus pulposus (NP) cells after treatment. Live/Dead staining showed that both cell types maintained high viability comparable to the control groups, with only minimal cell death observed ( ). Quantitative analysis further confirmed the absence of cytotoxicity. Consistently ( ), CCK-8 assays revealed no reduction in the metabolic activity of either macrophages or NP cells following exposure to the hydrogel system ( ). Taken together, these findings show that the material did not induce detectable cytotoxicity under the tested conditions.

2.3.

## Electrical stimulation modulates macrophage polarization and mitochondrial metabolism via synergistic immunometabolic reprogramming
Having confirmed the physicochemical properties, structural stability, and suitability of the hydrogel-scaffold platform, we next examined the immunometabolic effects of hydrogel-mediated electrical stimulation in combination with gallic acid on macrophage polarization and mitochondrial activity ( A). Murine macrophages were induced toward either the M1 or M2 phenotype. Cells were allocated into six experimental groups ( ). We first examined whether PLLA-mediated electrical stimulation contributes to the observed therapeutic effects. We conducted experiments on macrophages under three culture conditions: disease control group (D), PLLA treatment group (DP), and PLLA combined with electrical stimulation group (DPES). PLLA alone induced changes in inflammatory markers. In contrast, the addition of electrical stimulation suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and increased the expression of the M2 macrophage marker CD163 ( ). Quantitative analysis showed that, compared with PLLA alone or the disease control group, the PLLA combined with electrical stimulation group exhibited lower pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and higher CD163 expression. These results show that scaffold-mediated electrical stimulation plays a critical role in regulating macrophage polarization, synergizing with PLLA to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis demonstrated that LPS stimulation, either in M1 model (L-D) or following IL-4 priming (IL-D), induced M1-associated transcripts IL-6 and CD80, with the repolarization model additionally exhibiting elevated TNFα and diminished M2 marker CD163 relative to their respective controls. Gallic acid intervention in the M1 model reduced IL-6 and CD80 induction compared to the disease control. Daily electrical stimulation combined with gallic acid in the repolarization model reduced IL-6, CD80, and TNFα levels and increased CD163 expression relative to the repolarization disease control. These interventions converged on a common outcome----selective repression of pro-inflammatory M1 polarization while safeguarding the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype ( B). Western blot (WB) analysis corroborated the transcriptional findings. In the M1 model, L-D group increased protein levels of IL-6 and CD80 compared with untreated controls (L-C), whereas gallic acid treatment (L-T) reduced both proteins. In the repolarization model, LPS challenge following IL-4 priming (IL-D) similarly elevated IL-6 and CD80 relative to IL-4 controls (IL-C), while daily electrical stimulation combined with gallic acid (IL-T) reduced their expression.
 In contrast, the M2-associated proteins CD163 and Arginase-1 remained stable across groups in the M1 model, but in the repolarization model, IL-D reduced CD163 expression, which was restored by IL-T, while Arginase-1 levels were unaffected. These results show that the combined intervention selectively downregulates pro-inflammatory M1 markers while preserving, and in some cases restoring, the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype at the protein level ( C).
The results demonstrate that both single treatments (offense only, DN; defense only, DE) exerted modest effects, with partial restoration of ECM markers (Col1a2) and suppression of inflammatory signals (Tnf-α) compared with the disease group. However, the combined offense-defense strategy (DC) produced the most pronounced changes: Col1a2 and Postn were significantly upregulated, while Tnf-α was strongly downregulated. These findings indicate that the dual-function hydrogel achieves more than the additive effects of its individual components, consistent with a synergistic mechanism of ECM regeneration and inflammation suppression ( ). To validate the ROS-responsive delivery system, macrophage-conditioned medium was applied to nucleus pulposus cells under three conditions: disease (D), disease with blank nanoparticles (N), and disease with drug-loaded nanoparticles (Dr). Western blot analyses showed that blank nanoparticles did not affect disease-induced pro-inflammatory markers, whereas drug-loaded nanoparticles reduced Cd80 and Il-6 levels and maintained Arginase-1 levels ( ).
To further evaluate mitochondrial function, we performed oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis using a standard mitochondrial stress test ( D). Disease conditions impaired mitochondrial respiration, as evidenced by reductions in basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity ( E). The combined treatment of electrical stimulation and gallic acid restored all four parameters, indicating enhanced mitochondrial bioenergetics ( F). Collectively, these results show that electrical stimulation and gallic acid, when combined, are associated with reduced M1 marker expression and increased mitochondrial respiratory parameters under the tested conditions.

2.4.

## RNA-seq reveals immunoregulatory and regenerative pathways
To systematically investigate molecular alterations associated with disease onset and therapeutic intervention, we conducted integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of cells and corresponding culture supernatants ( A). The Disease group represented macrophages subjected to degenerative stimuli, while the Treatment group referred to diseased macrophages subsequently exposed to electrical stimulation. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation among control, disease, and treatment groups in both transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets ( B). Consistently, hierarchical clustering heatmaps further demonstrated distinct expression and metabolite profiles across groups, with samples clustering tightly within the same condition and showing clear divergence between different conditions ( ). Differential expression analysis revealed transcriptional changes between the control and disease groups, as well as between the disease and treatment groups (|log2FC| > 1, FDR <0.05; ). To capture dynamic expression patterns, we applied Mfuzz-based soft clustering, which partitioned genes into ten distinct temporal clusters ( ). We focused on two clusters exhibiting opposing expression trajectories between disease onset and post-treatment states. Genes upregulated in disease but downregulated after treatment may represent drivers of pathological processes suppressed by therapy, whereas genes downregulated in disease but restored after treatment may correspond to protective or reparative pathways re-activated during recovery. Venn diagram analysis showed significant overlap between these gene sets and Mfuzz-defined clusters, supporting their biological relevance to disease progression and therapeutic response ( C).
 Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of genes upregulated in disease and attenuated by treatment showed enrichment in immune-related and proliferative pathways ( D). Enriched terms included “Immune system process” (184 genes; -log10Q = 25.62), “Regulation of immune system process” (119 genes; -log10Q = 23.24), “Immune response,” “Defense response,” and “Inflammatory response.” Conversely, genes suppressed in disease and restored upon treatment were enriched in developmental and signaling-related processes ( E). “Multicellular organism development” and “System development” (52 and 51 genes; -log10Q = 2.41) suggested inhibition of normal developmental programs in disease and their reactivation during recovery. Other enriched terms--such as “Regulation of cell communication,” “Regulation of signaling,” and “Regulation of developmental process”-further supported this interpretation. Interestingly, “Response to growth factor” was significantly enriched despite involving fewer genes (n = 14; -log10Q = 2.12), highlighting its potential contribution to therapeutic repair.
To identify central regulatory modules, we constructed protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks for each gene set. In the group of genes activated under degenerative stimulation but broadly suppressed following therapeutic intervention, the dominant module was enriched for mitotic and cell-cycle checkpoint programs, comprising densely interconnected immune and proliferative regulators. Key hubs included CDK1, AURKA/AURKB, PLK1, TOP2A, PRC1, and members of the KIF motor family (KIF11, KIF15, KIF20A, KIF2C), together with spindle assembly checkpoint components (BUB1, MAD2L1, SGO1), replication/segregation factors (CDC45, ESPL1, CENPA, CENPE), and proliferation markers (MKI67, UBE2C, CCNB1, CCNA2). In the group of genes activated under degenerative stimulation and attenuated following treatment, the dominant module was enriched for mitotic and cell-cycle checkpoint programs. Key hubs included CDK1, AURKA/AURKB, PLK1, TOP2A, PRC1, and members of the KIF motor family (KIF11, KIF15, KIF20A, KIF2C), together with spindle assembly checkpoint components (BUB1, MAD2L1, SGO1), replication/segregation factors (CDC45, ESPL1, CENPA, CENPE), and proliferation markers (MKI67, UBE2C, CCNB1, CCNA2) ( F(i)). The CytoHubba-derived subnetwork highlighted mitotic hubs such as CDK1, AURKB, BUB1, CDC20, TOP2A, KIF11/KIF2C, BIRC5, NUSAP1, and DLGAP5 ( F(ii)). Conversely, the network comprising genes attenuated during disease progression and re-engaged upon treatment was centered on ECM organization and tissue remodeling ( F(iii)). Prominent nodes included collagens (COL1A2, COL18A1), small leucine-rich proteoglycans (DCN, BGN), adhesion/remodeling mediators (THBS2, SPP1), matrix turnover enzymes (MMP2, MMP3), and chemotactic regulators (CXCL12). The representative restored module further included VEGFC/VEGFD, PDGFRB, CD248, FBN1, TNXB, LAMA4, ADAMTS2, COL5A2, COL5A3, COL6A2, PCOLCE, MMP13, MMP14, and DPT ( F(iv)).
Integrated analysis of metabolomic and transcriptomic datasets revealed substantial pathway-level overlap, with metabolic pathways, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, and neurodegeneration-related processes consistently enriched across both layers ( ). O2PLS modeling identified transcripts such as Itpk1, Hdgfl3, and C1ql1 tightly coupled to metabolite variation ( ), implicating them as mediators of NP cell responses to macrophage-derived cues. Joint loading analysis further highlighted discriminatory metabolites (alpha-Kainic acid, Methyl 4-aminobutyrate) and their coordinated associations with immune and ECM-related genes ( ). Correlation heatmaps confirmed compound-gene pairs spanning oxidative stress, neuroendocrine signaling, and matrix remodeling ( ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that therapeutic intervention restores NP cell homeostasis through coordinated metabolic and transcriptional remodeling.
To delineate the individual contributions of each strategy, we conducted targeted molecular validation in disease (D), gallic acid-treated (DG, offense only), and electrical stimulation (DE, defense only) groups. Western blot analyses demonstrated that gallic acid primarily restored ECM-associated and DNA repair markers (Brca1), whereas electrical stimulation mainly enhanced immunoregulatory signaling (Cxcl12) ( ). These results highlight distinct yet complementary mechanisms, consistent with the synergistic integration observed in the combined omics profiles.
Metabolomic GO enrichment analysis revealed that metabolites downregulated in disease and restored upon treatment were primarily involved in nucleotide, fatty acid, and amino acid metabolism ( G). “Purine metabolism” and “Nucleotide metabolism” were markedly suppressed in disease and recovered upon treatment, indicating impaired nucleic acid turnover. “Fatty acid metabolism” and “Fatty acid elongation” followed similar trends, suggesting disrupted lipid biosynthesis. Additionally, “Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism” and “ABC transporters” were significantly enriched in the treatment group, implying improved amino acid handling and molecular transport. For metabolites elevated in disease and attenuated by treatment, heatmap visualization ( H) revealed distinct clustering patterns across experimental groups. Metabolomic profiling of NP cells further identified a set of metabolites markedly reduced in degenerative disc disease and restored upon intervention. Despite their chemical diversity, these metabolites converge on core nutrient metabolic pathways in NP cells--amino acid, nucleotide, and fatty acid/carbohydrate metabolism--which are known to be suppressed under the high-ROS, nutrient-limited microenvironment of disc degeneration. Such suppression impairs protein synthesis and antioxidant capacity (e.g., 3-oxopentanoic acid, 3-coumaric acid), DNA/RNA synthesis and repair (e.g., 2-pyridin-4-yl-quinoline-4-carboxylic acid), and membrane biosynthesis and energy supply (e.g., volemitol, aromatic sulfonic derivatives) [ , , ]. The treatment group re-engaged these pathways by improving nutrient availability, alleviating oxidative stress, and modulating key signaling cascades, thereby restoring metabolite levels toward homeostasis.

2.5.

## Hydrogel treatment alleviates disc degeneration in vivo
To functionally validate the omics-resolved programs, we established a macrophage-conditioned medium model with three groups: control (basal medium), disease (LPS 100 ng/mL + IL-4 20 ng/mL), and treatment (disease + gallic acid 50 μM with once-daily hydrogel-mediated electrical stimulation for 48 h). Conditioned media were transferred to NP cells for 48 h prior to qPCR, Western blot, and apoptosis assays ( A).
 qPCR demonstrated that disease conditioning downregulated ECM-associated genes (Col1a2, CXCL12, Postn) and upregulated inflammatory mediators (TNFα, SYK, TREM2) and apoptosis-related regulators (CDK1, BRCA1, TOP2a) relative to control, whereas treatment conditioning significantly restored ECM transcripts and suppressed inflammatory and apoptotic signatures toward baseline ( B). Consistently, WB corroborated transcriptional trends at the protein level, with treatment reversing disease-induced ECM loss and attenuating pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic protein expression ( C). Flow cytometry revealed increased apoptotic fractions in NP cells exposed to disease conditioned media, which were significantly reduced by treatment ( D). Together, these data functionally validate that therapeutic modulation of the macrophage milieu dampens inflammatory amplification, preserves matrix homeostasis, and limits NP cell apoptosis, in line with the integrated omics signatures.

2.6.

## Immunological and structural restoration by hydrogel-mediated macrophage reprogramming
To further substantiate the immunological effects of hydrogel-mediated stimulation, we performed flow cytometry to quantify macrophage phenotypes. In the disease group, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages predominated. Electrical stimulation reduced M1 prevalence and modestly increased M2 cells, while synergistic regulation produced the strongest effect, decreasing M1 and enhancing M2 polarization ( ). To assess the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the injectable hydrogel--mediated electrical stimulation strategy, we employed a rat model of IVDD alongside healthy controls. Animals were assigned to three groups: control (healthy), disease (IVDD), and treatment (IVDD + hydrogel-mediated electrical stimulation). Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were performed to evaluate disc tissue integrity and ECM composition. Masson's trichrome staining revealed pronounced disruption and loss of collagen architecture in the disease group relative to controls, whereas the treatment group exhibited partial restoration of disc structure and collagen content ( A). Immunohistochemical staining for Aggrecan, a principal proteoglycan of the intervertebral disc ECM, demonstrated markedly reduced expression in the disease group, while levels in the treatment group were comparable to controls, indicating preservation or recovery of matrix composition ( B). Similarly, collagen type II (COLII), a hallmark of healthy disc ECM, was diminished in the disease group but restored following treatment ( C). Semi-quantitative histological grade evaluation of Masson's trichrome-stained sections corroborated these findings: degeneration scores were significantly elevated in the disease group and markedly reduced following treatment ( D), while Aggrecan and COL II levels followed the same trend, with significant recovery in the treatment group ( E and F). Safranin O staining further revealed marked proteoglycan loss in the disease group, whereas treatment restored staining intensity and positive area, consistent with ECM preservation ( ).
 Collectively, these results demonstrate that hydrogel-mediated electrical stimulation mitigates intervertebral disc matrix degradation and promotes structural regeneration in vivo, underscoring its potential to preserve disc integrity and counteract degenerative progression.

## Discussion
IVDD is a major contributor to spinal instability, radiculopathy, and disability, with incidence increasing with age [ ]. Accumulating evidence suggests that excessive ROS and oxidative stress contribute to ECM degradation and NP cell apoptosis [ ]. However, the avascular nature of the disc limits drug penetration, and conventional anti-inflammatory therapies can only transiently relieve pain without eliminating ROS or repairing oxidative damage [ ]. Even advanced biomaterial-based delivery platforms that reduce ROS often fail to remove oxidatively damaged organelles, leaving metabolic dysfunction unresolved and impairing tissue repair. This underscores the need for strategies that both neutralize ROS and restore organelle quality control to halt IVDD progression at its root.
In this work, we present a multi-modal therapeutic platform that embodies an “offense-defense synergy” to counteract intervertebral disc degeneration. On the offensive front, injectable ROS-responsive hydrogel microspheres deliver gallic acid directly into oxidative inflammatory niches, scavenging excess ROS and preventing the initiation of pro-inflammatory M1 polarization. On the defensive front, localized electrical stimulation is associated with the phenotypic transition of macrophages from M1 toward a reparative M2 state, enhancing mitochondrial bioenergetics and regenerative signaling. By integrating these complementary biochemical and biophysical cues, our strategy modulates macrophage immunometabolism, supports extracellular matrix homeostasis, and links immune modulation with regenerative engineering--offering a potential framework for treating inflammation-driven degenerative diseases.
Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that the in vitro intervertebral disc degeneration model exhibited coordinated activation of immune and inflammatory pathways, accompanied by broad suppression of developmental, signaling, and biosynthetic networks, including nucleotide, fatty acid, and amino acid metabolism. Therapeutic intervention reduced these immune-proliferative signals and was associated with increased expression of reparative transcriptional programs and key metabolic pathways involved in ECM synthesis and tissue homeostasis. Network analysis identified central immune and cell-cycle regulators as potential contributors to pathology, whereas developmental/signaling hubs were associated with recovery. Collectively, these multi-omics findings suggest that the therapeutic strategy involves both resolution of pathological immune activation and restoration of metabolic and developmental pathways associated with structural regeneration.
Previous studies have established that the accumulation of reactive ROS and the resulting oxidative stress are key drivers of IVDD, and that targeted ROS scavenging can effectively delay ECM degradation and cell apoptosis. For example, Yu et al. [ ] developed a ROS-responsive diselenide-based nanosystem capable of releasing isoginkgetin (IGK) in high-ROS microenvironments, thereby eliminating ROS and enhancing autophagy to protect NP cells and slow IVDD progression. Similarly, Wang et al. [ ] employed a phenylboronic acid (PBA)-modified ROS-responsive nanosystem in a rheumatoid arthritis model to regulate macrophage polarization and improve the inflammatory microenvironment. While these studies highlight the therapeutic potential of ROS-targeted delivery, most focus on a single mechanism (e.g., ROS scavenging or autophagy enhancement). In contrast, our study showed at the transcriptomic level that treatment reduced immune-activation pathways and increased developmental and signaling programs, while metabolomic profiling revealed changes in multiple anabolic pathways, including purine, fatty acid, and amino acid metabolism. This pattern of immune-metabolic modulation suggests that the strategy may contribute to both inflammation resolution and ECM synthesis.
In sum, the strategy may offer insights for the treatment of IVDD and potentially inform the design of regenerative therapies targeting the immune-metabolic-matrix axis. Despite the promising outcomes, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current findings are derived from rodent models and in vitro systems, which only partially recapitulate the complexity of the human intervertebral disc microenvironment, including differences in immune cell composition, cytokine gradients, and mechanical loading. Second, while our integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses provide valuable insight into the global regulatory landscape, the precise molecular intermediates and cell-type-specific responses remain to be elucidated; high-resolution single-cell multi-omics will be essential to dissect the heterogeneity of immune and stromal cell populations under treatment.
Collectively, this work presents a multi-modal platform that combines immunometabolic modulation with structural regeneration, offering a potential strategy for treating intervertebral disc degeneration and other inflammation-driven degenerative diseases.

4.

## Conclusion
In summary, we developed an “offense-defense synergy” macrophage reprogramming hydrogel that integrates ROS-responsive biochemical modulation with localized electrical stimulation to achieve full-cycle precision control of macrophage fate in the degenerated intervertebral disc microenvironment. By simultaneously suppressing M1-driven inflammatory amplification and preventing pathological M2 repolarization, this dual-mode strategy effectively restored immune balance, promoted ECM regeneration, and significantly improved disc repair outcomes. This work not only offers a novel material-based therapeutic avenue for IVDD, but also provides a broadly applicable paradigm for precision immunomodulation in other inflammation-associated degenerative diseases.

## Materials and methods
5.1.

## Bioinformatics analysis
The RNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Raw RNA-seq reads were subjected to quality control using fastp (v0.18.0) with parameters -q 20 -u 50 -N 15 -l 50 to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences. Reads mapping to ribosomal RNA were filtered out by Bowtie2 (v2.2.8, --local). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0, --rna-strandness RF). Transcript assembly and gene-level quantification were performed with StringTie (v1.3.4) and RSEM (v1.2.31) using default parameters. Differential expression analysis was conducted in R using DESeq2 (v1.20.0), with thresholds set at |log2FC| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. GO enrichment was performed with the clusterProfiler package, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was based on KEGG release 101.0. Enrichment significance was assessed using the phyper function in R (lower.tail = FALSE) for hypergeometric testing, and multiple testing correction was applied using p.adjust (method = “FDR”). All downstream visualizations, including PCA plots, volcano plots, and heatmaps, were generated in R using standard plotting packages.

5.2.

## Materials
Phenylboronic ester-functionalized nanoparticles were obtained from Yuju Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PLGA-PEG-PLGA was provided by Shanghai Institute of traumatology and orthopedics (Shanghai, China). All other reagents and materials used in this study are detailed in the corresponding experimental sections.

5.3.

## Preparation of injectable hydrogels
Briefly, 1 g of PLLA was dissolved in 5 mL of dichloromethane under magnetic stirring, then transferred to a medical-grade syringe for electrospinning [ ]. The electrospinning process was performed using electrospinning apparatus (SS-X1, Yongkang Leye Technology Development Co., LTD., China) under the following conditions: positive voltage 15 kV; flow rate 1.5 mL/h; 23G needle; and tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm. Electrospun PLLA films were collected and pulverized using a high-speed grinder to yield nanoscale fibrillar components for incorporation into the composite matrix [ ].
Hydrogel was prepared by dissolving 4% (w/v) PLGA-PEG-PLGA in ultrapure water and stirring for 2 h at 25 °C until fully hydrated. Separately, phenylboronic ester nanoparticles (2.5% (w/v)) were dispersed in distilled water via sonication for 10 min at 25 °C. The nanoparticle suspension was then added dropwise into the PLGA-PEG-PLGA solution under gentle stirring to ensure homogeneous distribution [ ]. Next, PLLA nanofibrils were gradually blended into the nanoparticle--PLGA-PEG-PLGA mixture and stirred at low speed for 4 h at room temperature to form the hydrogel precursor. The resulting nanocomposite hydrogel was used for subsequent characterization and biological assays.

5.4.

## Characterization of injectable hydrogels

I)The microstructure and pore morphology of the injectable hydrogels were observed by SEM (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan) after lyophilization and gold sputtering.
II)The viscoelastic properties, including storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″), were evaluated using a rotational rheometer (Mars40, Haake, Germany) under a frequency sweep from 0.1 to 100 Hz at 25 °C.
III)The thermal behavior of the injectable hydrogels was analyzed by DSC (DSC 250, TA Instruments, USA) under a nitrogen atmosphere, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 25 °C to 300 °C.
IV)The chemical composition and functional group interactions of hydrogel components were examined by FTIR (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode over a range of 500-4000 cm−1.
V)The internal architecture and nanostructural characteristics of hydrated hydrogel networks were visualized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

5.5.

## Sequencing of mRNA
For transcriptomic analysis, RNA-seq was performed on NP cells following stimulation with macrophage-conditioned media generated under different experimental conditions. Macrophages were first subjected to one of three treatments to obtain the corresponding conditioned media: Control macrophage-conditioned medium: collected from untreated macrophages cultured under baseline conditions. Disease macrophage-conditioned medium: collected from macrophages exposed to degenerative stimuli. Treatment macrophage-conditioned medium: collected from macrophages exposed to degenerative stimuli and subsequently subjected to electrical stimulation.
Each type of conditioned medium was transferred to NP cell cultures and incubated for 48 h. After stimulation, NP cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted for library preparation and sequencing. All samples were processed under identical culture and handling conditions to ensure comparability across groups.
I) Total RNA was extracted from hydrogel-treated samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. II) Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted using the Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina, USA), and the remaining mRNA was enriched for downstream processing. III) The enriched mRNA was reverse-transcribed into double-stranded complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). IV) The cDNA ends were repaired, and the sequencing library was constructed by PCR amplification with adapter ligation using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, USA). V) Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China), generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Bioinformatic analysis was conducted on the Omicsmart online platform (http://www.omicsmart.com), including quality control, transcript quantification, and differential gene expression analysis.

5.6.

## Western blot
Protein lysates were prepared from experimental samples, followed by the addition of protease inhibitor cocktail (G2006-250UL, Servicebio) to prevent degradation. Samples were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels and separated by electrophoresis under standard conditions. Proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes (WGPVDF45, Servicebio) using a wet transfer system. Blocking was carried out using 5% BSA solution to reduce non-specific binding. Membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies targeting ACTIN (GB15003, Servicebio, 1:5000), Arginase 1 (GB115724, Servicebio, 1:1000), CD163 (GB113751, Servicebio, 1:1000), IL-16 (32611-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), CD80 (GB114055, Servicebio, 1:1000), Col1a2 (ab308221, abcam, 1:1000), CXCL12 (PA5-30603, Thermo Fisher Scientific,1:500), Postn (ab152099, abcam, 1:1000), CDK1 (GB11398, Servicebio, 1:500), BRCA1 (ab238983, abcam, 1:500), TOP2a (20233-1-A, Proteintech, 1:1000), TNF-α (GB11188, Servicebio, 1:500), SYK (GB11789, Servicebio, 1:500), and Trem2 (68723-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:500). After rinsing with TBST buffer, membranes were incubated for 3-4 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (GB23303, Servicebio, 1:3000) at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using ECL substrate (G2014-50 ML, Servicebio), and protein bands were imaged with a digital chemiluminescence system (SCG-W3000, Servicebio).

5.7.

## Real-Time quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan) to synthesize cDNA. Quantitative PCR amplification was conducted with SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The PCR primer sequences are provided in .

5.8.

## Cell culture
Nucleus pulposus cells were isolated from the caudal intervertebral discs of 8-10 week-old C57BL/6 mice under aseptic conditions. Following euthanasia, the entire tail was excised and placed in ice-cold PBS. Individual motion segments were dissected under a stereomicroscope, and the annulus fibrosus was carefully incised to expose the gelatinous NP tissue. NP tissue from each disc was gently extruded using fine forceps and pooled in sterile PBS. The collected tissue was minced into ∼1 mm3 fragments and digested in 0.2% type II collagenase containing 0.01% DNase I in serum-free Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium/F-12 (DMEM/F-12) at 37 °C for 45-60 min with gentle agitation. The digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and the cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove debris. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 300g for 5 min, resuspended in complete culture medium (DMEM/F-12 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin), and seeded into culture flasks at a density of 1-2 × 104 cells/cm2. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, with medium changes every 2-3 days, and cells at passages 1-2 were used for subsequent experiments.
Murine RAW264.7 macrophages (derived from C57BL/6 background, MIC-iCell-i017) were seeded into standard 6-well plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 1.0 × 106 cells per well and cultured in complete Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were divided into three groups: Control, Disease, and Treatment. The Control group received no stimulation; the Disease group was treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h to induce a pro-inflammatory phenotype; the Treatment group was co-treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and gallic acid (50 μM; Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h. After stimulation, cells were harvested and stored at −80 °C for downstream analyses. Macrophages in all groups were stimulated with interleukin-4 (IL-4, 20 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h to promote M2 polarization. The Treatment group additionally received daily electrical stimulation using previously optimized parameters (2 mA, 0.5h) during this period. After IL-4 stimulation, the culture medium was refreshed, and LPS (100 ng/mL) was added to Disease and Treatment groups for another 48 h. All cells were then collected and cryopreserved for further analysis.

5.9.

## Flow cytometry
Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-AbFluor™ 647/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Cat # KTA0004, Abbkine). The kit contains Annexin V-AbFluor™ 647, Propidium Iodide (PI), and 5 × Annexin V binding buffer. The 5 × binding buffer was diluted with deionized water to 1 × working solution prior to use. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1 × binding buffer at a density of approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL. Annexin V-AbFluor™ 647 was added according to the manufacturer's instructions and gently mixed. The mixture was incubated at room temperature (25 °C) in the dark for 15-20 min. Subsequently, PI was added to a final concentration of 5 μg/mL and incubated for an additional 5-10 min. Samples were analyzed immediately using a flow cytometer (CytExpert, Beckman), and data were processed with FlowJo (v10.8.1) software.
Cells were stained with FITC-conjugated antibodies against iNOS (M1 marker) and Arg-1 (M2 marker), and analyzed using a flow cytometer. Data were processed with FlowJo software to calculate M1 and M2 cell counts.

5.10.

## Tissue sections and H&E staining
Tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were obtained using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235) and mounted onto glass slides. For histological evaluation, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min followed by eosin for 2 min. After dehydration and clearing, the stained sections were sealed with neutral resin and imaged using a bright-field microscope.

5.11.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. For comparisons among three or more independent groups, ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed under the assumptions of Gaussian distribution and equal standard deviations, followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test, comparing each experimental group to the designated control group. For omics datasets involving multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction and, where appropriate, false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment were applied to control for type I error. For comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests were used. All experiments included at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Exact p values are reported where applicable, and significance levels are denoted as: ns (not significant), ∗ (p < 0.05), ∗∗ (p < 0.01), ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001), and ∗∗∗∗ (p < 0.0001).

5.12.

## Animal experiment
The animal experiment in this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval number: 2025SQXB000088). A rat model of intervertebral disc degeneration was established by caudal neddle puncture: under anesthesia, the rat tail segments (Co7-Co8) were exposed, and a 20G needle was inserted transversely along the intervertebral disc for about 5 mm, rotated 360° and pulled out to destroy the structure of the annulus fibrosus and induce degeneration. In the control group, only the caudal vertebra was exposed without puncture.
In vivo electrical stimulation was applied externally using a non-invasive ultrasound protocol to activate the piezoelectric effect of the PLLA scaffold. Ultrasound was delivered at a frequency of 40 kHz and a power density of 0.33 W/cm2, with each treatment lasting approximately 30 min, administered once daily for two weeks. This controlled stimulation ensured stable and reproducible electrical output, independent of physiological motion.
After operation and during the stimulation period, the rats were fed routinely, and histological evaluation was performed at the set time points to confirm the success of the model and to assess the effects of electrical stimulation.

5.13.

## Metabolomic profiling and data analysis
Metabolomic profiling was performed using UHPLC-MS/MS on two platforms (Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC coupled to AB Triple TOF 6600, and Vanquish UHPLC coupled to Orbitrap Exploris™ 480). Samples were separated on a HILIC column at 25 °C with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and 2 μL injection volume. Mobile phases consisted of water with 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide (A) and acetonitrile (B), with a gradient from 95% to 40% B and re-equilibration to 95% B. QC samples were interspersed to monitor stability.
Mass spectrometry was conducted in both positive and negative ion modes. For the AB Triple TOF 6600, MS1 spectra were acquired over 60-1000 Da and MS/MS over 25-1000 Da using data-dependent acquisition. For the Orbitrap Exploris™ 480, MS1 and MS/MS spectra were collected at 60,000 resolutions over 70-1200 Da.
Raw data were converted to “.mzML” format with ProteoWizard and processed using XCMS for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. Features with >50% missing values or RSD >50% were removed, and missing values were imputed by KNN. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching accurate m/z (<10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra against internal databases and authentic standards.

5.14.

## Piezoelectric characterization of PLLA scaffold
PLLA membranes were fabricated by solvent casting and thermally annealed to enhance crystallinity. Samples were cut into strips and mounted between copper electrodes. Ultrasound stimulation (0.1-1 W, 1 MHz) was applied using a calibrated transducer in both dry and hydrated (culture medium immersion for 24 h) conditions. Voltage signals were recorded with a high-impedance digitizer at a sampling interval of 0.1 ms.

5.15.

## Biocompatibility assay
The biocompatibility of the hydrogel and its components was evaluated in macrophages and NP cells using Live/Dead staining (Cat#: KTA1001, Abbkine) and CCK-8 viability assays. Cells were exposed to the materials for 24 h, 72 h, and 120 h. For Live/Dead staining, cells were incubated with Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) following the manufacturer's instructions, and fluorescence images were acquired to assess viable and dead cells. Quantitative analysis was performed using Leica software. For the CCK-8 assay, CCK-8 reagent was added after the designated treatment period, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured to determine metabolic activity. Each condition was assessed using four technical replicates.

5.16.

## Safranin O staining and quantification
Intervertebral disc tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to Safranin O/Fast Green staining to evaluate proteoglycan content and extracellular matrix integrity. Stained sections were imaged under a light microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software, and the positive Safranin O staining area (%) was calculated by normalizing the Safranin O-positive region to the total tissue area. All analyses were conducted in a blinded manner to minimize bias.
