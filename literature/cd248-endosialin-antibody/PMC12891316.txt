## Introduction
CAR-T therapy is a cutting-edge immunotherapy that utilizes the patient’s own immune system. This innovative treatment involves extracting T cells from the patient’s body, genetically modifying them to have specific antigen receptors (CARs) on their surface, enabling them to precisely identify and bind to tumor cells that express the corresponding antigens. After these modified cells are multiplied extensively in laboratory conditions, they are reintroduced into the patient’s bloodstream, where they go on the offensive against cancerous cells, essentially turning the body’s own immune defenses into a targeted strike force against tumors [ ]. CAR T cells have five generations of structure. The initial iteration of CAR T-cell therapy incorporates a single-chain variable fragment (ScFv) designed to identify extracellular antigens. This construct comprises both light and heavy chains along with an intracellular T-cell activation segment derived from CD3ζ. Building upon this initial framework, the next-generation CAR incorporates an extra intracellular costimulatory domain, which may include either CD28 or 4-1BB signaling elements. The third generation takes it a step further by integrating both the CD28 and 4-1BB signaling domains, enhancing the overall functionality [ ]. Recent advancements in CAR-T cell engineering have given rise to so-called “fourth and fifth generation” constructs, marking significant progress in immunotherapy. The fourth-generation CAR refers to CAR-T cells that can secrete specific proteins such as cytokines in addition, while the fifth-generation CAR integrates cytokine receptor domains (such as IL-2Rβ) intracellularly to achieve self-activation of CAR-T cells (Fig. ) [ ]. This groundbreaking approach has demonstrated remarkable efficacy against hematological malignancies, with particularly impressive outcomes in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, multiple myeloma, and large B-cell lymphoma [ ]. For instance, a landmark trial evaluating CAR-T therapy (axi-cel) for refractory large B-cell lymphoma reported complete responses in 54% of the 111 participants [ ]. However, solid tumors present a tougher nut to crack. Unlike their liquid counterparts, these malignancies create physical and biological barriers that severely limit CAR-T cell infiltration, compounded by the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment [ ]. In addition to these obstacles, there are also issues such as antigen loss, metabolic limitations, and treatment-related toxicity, all of which are key obstacles that researchers must overcome to expand the scope of CAR-T therapy (Fig. ) [ ].

 

 

Despite the ongoing hurdles in CAR-T therapy, the research suggests it holds immense promise. This overview begins by examining how CAR-T therapy is targeted within solid tumors and potential tactics to combat antigen evasion. It delves into the impact of the tumor’s immune microenvironment on CAR-T therapy, along with strategies to enhance it. The paper also outlines innovative approaches to CAR-T cell design and synergistic therapy combinations. Lastly, it touches on the limitations of these strategies. The authors hope this comprehensive look will offer fresh insights and guidance for future studies on CAR-T therapy in treating solid tumors.

## Target selection of CAR-T therapy in solid tumors

## Explored targets
CAR-T therapy is like a precision-guided missile for cancer, targeting and destroying tumors by locking onto unique markers on the cancer cells’ surfaces. Picking the right targets is crucial, as it’s the key to maximizing the therapy’s effectiveness [ ]. A significant roadblock in CAR-T cell therapy stems from on-target, off-tumor toxicity, a situation where the modified T cells inadvertently wage war on healthy tissues that display similar surface proteins to those found on cancerous cells. To maximize the treatment’s effectiveness while avoiding collateral damage to normal tissues, our goal is to pinpoint a tumor-specific antigen that’s uniquely present on malignant cells and completely absent in healthy ones [ ]. Unfortunately, these specific markers are seldom found on the exterior of cancerous cells. In contemporary immunotherapy approaches for solid tumors, the majority of chimeric antigen receptor T-cells (CAR-T) are designed to recognize tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), which unfortunately aren’t exclusive to malignant tissue - they’re also present in healthy cells throughout the body [ ]. A groundbreaking study has recently pinpointed Zona Pellucida Glycoprotein-4 (ZP4) as a promising new target for treating triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Unlike previously investigated targets, ZP4 shows remarkably high expression levels in TNBC patient samples and PDX models, while being virtually absent in normal tissues except for oocytes. This unique expression pattern indicates that ZP4 could provide exceptional specificity and a favorable safety profile for targeted therapies. Capitalizing on this discovery, scientists engineered specialized CAR-T cells designed to attack ZP4. Laboratory tests yielded impressive results, with these engineered immune cells dramatically curbing the progression of ZP4-positive TNBC tumors and effectively suppressing metastatic spread [ ]. Furthermore, Martin-Otal et al. constructed EDA CAR-T and BCMA CAR-T cells based on the adnexin proteins EDAnnexin and BCMAnnexin in their research. The research results show that these CAR-T cells can effectively kill phosphatidylserine (PS) -positive tumor cells both in vivo and in vitro. Based on this, they developed a type of CAR-T cell that binds annexin V (Anxa CAR-T). This CAR-T cell therapy specifically targets phosphatidylserine-expressing tumor cells and demonstrates no detectable toxicity in murine models in vivo, despite the potential risk of CAR-T cell fratricide. This breakthrough hints at PS as a potentially fruitful target for the advancement of CAR-T cell therapy [ ]. The research on targets such as HER2 [ ], MUC1 [ ], TROP2 [ ], ROR1 [ ]and EGFR [ ] has shown promising research prospects in recent years. We will focus on introducing them in this section. Table presents other related targets of CAR-T cells in solid tumors.

 

## HER2
HER2, also referred to as ERBB2, is a member of the receptor tyrosine kinase group [ ]. The research has found that HER2 is expressed in various cancers such as breast cancer and gastric cancer, as well as in normal epithelial tissues. This indicates that targeted HER2 therapy is feasible, but it also implies that non-targeted and off-target toxicities may occur [ , ]. Recent studies have shown that by reducing the affinity of single-chain variable fragments (scFv), depleting lymphocytes, and controlling the infusion dose of CAR-T cells, it is possible to effectively avoid the previously reported off-target toxicity risks of HER2 CAR-T cell therapy while achieving a good anti-tumor effect. Shabaneh et al. discovered that the affinity of the scFvdirectly influences the anti-tumor activity and safety of HER2 CAR-T cells; reducing its affinity significantly enhances the tumor-suppressing effects of CAR-T cells and improves treatment safety [ ]. A Phase I clinical trial examining patients with advanced sarcoma revealed that among the 14 participants who underwent HER2 CAR-T infusion following lymphocyte depletion, 50% of the patients’ conditions have been controlled. The only significant adverse reaction associated with CAR-T therapy is the cytokine release syndrome (CRS), with the incidence of 1–2 grade CRS being 79%. Only two patients who received the maximum dose of HER2 CAR-T cells developed grade 3–4 CRS. (NCT00902044) [ ].

## MUC1
MUC1 is a glycosylated heterodimeric transmembrane protein with cytoplasmic, transmembrane, and extracellular domains [ ]. The study found that 67% of TNBC patients showed overexpression of MUC1. Moreover, the upregulation of MUC1 expression was also observed in pancreatic cancer, non-small cell lung cancer and other types of cancer. This protein appears to fuel the development, spread, and invasion of malignant cells through multiple pathways, including modulating tumor metabolic processes, boosting PD-L1 expression, enhancing T cell exhaustion, and triggering the JAK1-STAT1-IRF1 signaling cascade, all of which enable cancer cells to dodge immune detection [ – ]. Recent research has bolstered the case for MUC1 as an attractive candidate for CAR-T cell therapy. A particular investigation revealed that when CAR-T cells targeting MUC1 were engineered to co-express the TR2.4-1BB receptor, they showed remarkable efficacy in suppressing breast tumors infiltrated with myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [ ]. Although the CAR-T cells targeting MUC1 seem to have promising results, they are only applicable to preclinical models. We still need sufficient data to support the efficacy and safety of these CAR-T cells in vivo [ ].

## TROP2
TROP2, or trophoblast cell surface antigen 2, is a glycoprotein embedded in the cell membrane. It is highly expressed in various solid tumors. For instance, 80% highly expressed in breast cancer, 50% highly expressed in pancreatic cancer, and 71% highly expressed in prostate cancer. However, no expression was found in many normal tissues such as the heart, colon, and ovary, making it an extremely attractive focus for therapeutic intervention [ – ]. Mounting research indicates that TROP2 fuels the growth and multiplication of cancer cells through its involvement in various biological processes, including the MAPK signaling cascade, while also enabling the spread of solid tumors by kickstarting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM) [ ]. At present, several clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy and safety of antibody-drug conjugates targeting TROP2. In patients with HR + and HER2 metastatic breast cancer, the targeted antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) sacituzumab govitecan prolonged the overall survival by 3.2 months compared to chemotherapy. Additionally, among all patients treated with sacituzumab govitecan, neutropenia and diarrhea were the most common adverse events, with the incidence rates of grade 3 and above being 51% and 27%, respectively. During the entire treatment period, only one treatment-related death event was reported [ ]. In another trial evaluating datopotamab deruxtecan in non-small cell lung cancer patients, in 50 patients receiving the recommended dose, the objective response rate (ORR) reached 26%. Notably, the most common treatment-related adverse events (TEAE) were stomatitis, nausea, and alopecia, although common, only 8 out of 180 patients with dose adjustments reached grade 3 or above severity. Although there are no exact clinical data on targeted TROP2 CAR-T cells yet, the research reports on TROP2-related ADCs can provide us some insights [ ].

## ROR1
ROR1, a transmembrane glycoprotein known as receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1, runs rampant in numerous cancer types, including breast cancer and prostate cancer, according to research [ ]. Evidence suggests that ROR1 calls the shots when it comes to tumor cell survival, proliferation, and drug resistance by getting its hands dirty with major signaling pathways like AKT, ERK, and STAT3 [ , ]. Given its scarce presence in healthy adult tissues, ROR1 stands out as a golden opportunity for developing cancer-specific therapies [ ]. The advancement of ROR1-targeted therapies has shown notable progress, reinforcing its validity as a therapeutic target. For example, Wei et al. developed two monoclonal antibodies, h1B8 and h6D4, which exhibited strong binding to ROR1 and notable inhibitory activity. Following humanization, these antibodies effectively curbed the growth and spread of ROR1-positive tumor cells, though their exact anti-tumor mechanisms are not yet fully understood [ ]. A report on the in vivo administration of CAR-T cells targeting ROR1 showed that 14 out of 18 patients with solid tumors developed CRS, including 4 cases of grade 3 CRS. Only one patient who received the highest dose of CAR-T cells observed dose-limiting toxicity (DLT). Although the CAR-T cells had limited tumor-killing effect due to limited infiltration in solid tumors, this fully confirmed the sufficient safety of ROR1 as a therapeutic target (NCT02706392) [ ].

## EGFR
The EGFR and HER2 are both part of the receptor tyrosine kinase family. When EGFR links up with its binding partners, it forms a pair, and this triggers structural alterations in the kinase part, which in turn switches on a series of signaling routes. Interestingly, these routes have a lot in common with those controlled by HER2 [ ]. EGFR is abnormally expressed in many types of cancer, such as gastric cancer, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). In TNBC, 36% to 89% of cases show upregulated expression, which is closely related to cancer development [ , ]. It is notable that EGFR is also expressed at certain levels in normal tissues, including the brain, lungs, skin, and liver. The safety of therapies targeting EGFR is somewhat worrying [ ]. However, the main drug for targeting EGFR, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), has already advanced to the third generation. In NSCLC patients, a clinical trial reported that the main adverse reaction rates of the third-generation TKI Aumolertinib were significantly lower compared to the first-generation gefitinib, such as elevated AST (29.0% vs. 54.0%), elevated ALT (29.4% vs. 55.8%), rash (23.4% vs. 41.4), and patients showed good tolerance, making it very promising as the preferred drug for EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients [ ].

## Antigen escape
CAR-T treatment exhibits favorable outcomes in blood cancers, yet faces numerous challenges in treating solid tumors, including the issue of antigen variability [ ]. Tumor cells have developed a clever survival strategy by dampening or eliminating the presence of antigens on their outer membrane or tweaking their antigen configuration, which essentially renders CAR-T cells blind to their targets and gives the malignant cells a fighting chance to evade destruction. This is called antigen heterogeneity and is also known as antigen escape [ ]. The underlying mechanisms involved mainly include downregulation of gene expression, epigenetic changes, antigenic epitope mutations, and splicing variations [ ]. Antigen escape leads to the inability of CAR-T cells with a single target to completely clear tumor cells, which is also one of the key factors for the limited therapeutic effect or disease recurrence of cancer patients after receiving CAR-T therapy [ ]. In patients with multiple myeloma, treatment with belantamab or CAR-T cells targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) will lead to a reduction in BCMA expression on tumor cells, which subsequently results in treatment resistance [ ]. Similarly, in the mouse in vivo model, the main mechanism by which CAR-T cells developed resistance against STEAP1 was that the target antigen STEAP1 underwent antigen loss or antigen escape after treatment [ ].

## Multi-target strategy and logic-gated design
Scientists have developed innovative multi-target approaches and logic-controlled CAR systems (including AND, OR, and NOT gates) to enhance CAR-T cell therapy. These advanced techniques work by either engaging multiple tumor-specific markers at once or through sophisticated on/off switching mechanisms for CAR-T cell activity. Such strategies significantly boost both the precision and safety profile of this cutting-edge immunotherapy [ , ]. Immunotherapy using chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) has shown remarkable efficacy in treating multiple myeloma. Despite this breakthrough, the treatment faces challenges with frequent disease relapse. To address this limitation, scientists have designed a novel dual-target BCMA-CD24-CAR-T cell that can simultaneously recognize BCMA and CD24 antigens. This innovative strategy has demonstrated superior tumor suppression compared to single-target therapies [ ]. A recent investigation into natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (NKTCL) employed an innovative tandem CAR-T (Tan CAR-T) cell approach, where scientists engineered CD38-LMP1 CAR-T cells by linking two distinct single-chain variable fragments (scFv) to simultaneously target dual antigens. The findings revealed that this Tan CAR-T approach outperformed conventional single-target CAR-T therapy, exhibiting markedly superior effectiveness along with a pronounced synergistic boost in therapeutic impact [ ]. Logic-gated CAR-T cell therapy represents a significant advancement in safety and efficacy, minimizing harmful effects on healthy tissues while maintaining robust anti-tumor activity. Moghimi and colleagues engineered a sophisticated GD2-B7H3 CAR-T cell system incorporating SynNotch technology, employing an AND-gate mechanism to ensure activation occurs exclusively when both GD2 and B7H3 tumor antigens are present. Research demonstrates that this dual-targeting approach outperforms conventional B7H3 CAR-T cells in tumor suppression while significantly reducing off-target activation in normal tissues. Additionally, these modified T cells exhibit enhanced persistence with slower depletion rates, leading to more sustained therapeutic effects against tumors. This innovative design effectively balances potency with precision, offering a promising solution to longstanding challenges in CAR-T cell therapy [ ].
It must be pointed out here that although multi-target and logic gate control strategies can significantly enhance the efficacy and safety of CAR-T cells, due to manufacturing costs and challenges such as immunosuppression, their accessibility in the future remains to be seen. For instance, in the design of dual-targets, the design and validation of the two svFv fragments, as well as the screening, expansion and amplification of double-positive CAR-T cells, all greatly increase the production costs [ ]. When tumor tissues and normal tissues coexist, the SynNotch receptor may exert targeted toxicity on the normal tissues. Therefore, the selection of the targeting antigen and the optimization of the CAR structure are issues that cannot be ignored [ ]. Furthermore, there are several clinical trials (NCT06051695) (NCT06682793) regarding logical gate-controlled CAR-T that are currently in recruitment. When it is used in humans, it may also need to confront challenges such as immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment.

## Improve the tumor microenvironment
CAR-T cells are subject to numerous limitations within the tumor microenvironment, including various immunosuppressive cells and soluble immunosuppressive molecules, as well as the extracellular matrix barrier and metabolic competition. These factors severely impede the functional performance of CAR-T cells at the tumor site [ ]. Among them, immunosuppressive cells such as (myeloid-derived suppressor cells) and immunosuppressive molecules (PD-1/PD-L1, TGF-β) are generally regarded as the most direct and primary inhibitory factors. Multiple preclinical studies and clinical trials have also confirmed this [ – ]. Secondly, the dense physical barrier formed by extracellular matrix greatly hinders the intratumoral infiltration of CAR-T cells, preventing them from reaching the effective sites to kill tumors [ ]. Therefore, the latest research tends to favor combined multi-strategy treatment.

## Barrier limiting infiltration of CAR-T cells
Within the tumor microenvironment, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) stand out as the most prevalent and diverse group of fibroblasts. Extensive research has demonstrated that these cells are key players in driving tumor growth, specialization, and spread via intricate biological pathways. To counteract CAFs’ ability to hinder CAR-T cell effectiveness, scientists must first unravel their unique properties and multifaceted roles in cancer progression [ – ]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) mainly come from regular fibroblasts that are already present in tissues. When tissue gets injured, these fibroblasts spring into action and change their stripes. This transformation is kicked off by a variety of players, including TGF-β, CXCL12, and reactive oxygen species, which all pull the strings behind the scenes [ ].
When it comes to the role of stromal regulation, cancer-associated fibroblasts that have been activated go into overdrive, pumping out substantial quantities of materials like collagen and fibronectin. This creates a thick extracellular matrix (ECM) that essentially gives tumors a solid foundation to grow on while simultaneously throwing up roadblocks that prevent immune cells from infiltrating the area [ ]. CAFs also release small extracellular vesicles enriched with lysyl oxidase (LOX). When these vesicles attach to type I collagen, they facilitate the formation of collagen cross-links, which in turn stiffen the extracellular matrix (ECM). This heightened rigidity then triggers epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by activating YAP-dependent mechanotransduction pathways [ ]. Myeloid cells, including macrophages, churn out matrix metalloproteinases like MMP2 that break down the existing ECM basement membrane and stromal elements, setting the stage for ECM remodeling and giving tumor cells a free pass to invade and spread to other parts of the body [ ]. Conversely, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) release a range of signaling molecules, including IL-6, IL-1β, and CXCL1, which play a dual role in tumor progression. Beyond directly stimulating cancer cell growth, these cytokines attract and activate immune cells that suppress the body’s defenses, such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs). This process further amplifies the immune-evasive nature of the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Collectively, CAFs drive tumor progression, cellular development, and spread via multiple pathways while fostering immunosuppressive environments.

## CAR-T cell response in immunosuppressive milieus
In the tumor’s backyard, so to speak, the immune soldiers have a two-pronged approach to the cancer’s game plan. On the one side, these fighters can put the brakes on cancer growth with their immunoregulating moves; on the flip side, they might end up helping the cancer cells pull a fast one and slip out of the immune system’s grasp, all while setting up a cozy, immunosuppressive haven for the cancer to thrive in [ ]. Suppressive immune cells in the TME, such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), significantly hinder the infiltration and function of CAR-T cells, leading to reduced efficacy of CAR-T therapy [ ].
TAMs are divided into two distinct categories: the M1 phenotype, which turns up the heat on inflammation and puts the brakes on tumor growth, and the M2 phenotype, which dials down inflammatory responses while rolling out the red carpet for tumor progression [ ]. Research indicates TAM phenotypes display adaptable plasticity, and shifting their polarization to lower the M2/M1 ratio may improve tumor immunotherapy outcomes [ , ]. TAMs generally create an immunosuppressive microenvironment through four key approaches. First, they express immune checkpoint proteins like PD-L1 or release immunosuppressive substances such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which put the brakes on T cell activation and function. Second, they starve T cells of crucial amino acids needed for their activation, including arginine and tryptophan, effectively forcing these cells to undergo metabolic reprogramming. Third, by secreting factors like VEGF, PDGF, and MMP, TAMs help tumors build new blood vessels and remodel their structural support. Finally, they bring in other immunosuppressive cells, which team up to indirectly dampen T cell activity [ – ]. For example, Research conducted by Tharp and colleagues demonstrates that TAMs stimulate extracellular matrix (ECM) production via an autocrine TGF-β1 signaling loop, which in turn blocks CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. Additionally, the synergistic effect of ECM stiffness and TGF-β signaling can enhance arginine utilization, further suppressing T cell activation. In parallel, ornithine, a byproduct of arginine metabolism, stifles the growth and tumor-fighting capabilities of CD8⁺ T cells [ ].
MDSCs are a specific subset of myeloid cells that thrive within TME. They have a unique talent for dampening the immune response [ ]. Research indicates that activation of the NF-κB pathway is a key mechanism for the recruitment and activation of MDSCs in the TME. Activation of this pathway can upregulate the expression of various cytokines and inhibitory molecules, or further drive the CXCL1/2–CXCR2 axis signaling, promoting the migration of MDSCs to the TME and suppressing T-cell function [ – ]. Emerging research indicates that triggering Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and the advanced glycation end products (RAGE) receptor can phosphorylate key signaling molecules within the NF-κB, JAK-STAT, and MAPK pathways. This cascade plays a pivotal role in driving monocyte differentiation into myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) while simultaneously impairing T-cell activity. In particular, these signaling pathways’ activation can result in the enhancement of molecules such as PD-L1, ROS, arginase 2 (ARG2), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), and CD274, which directly or indirectly cause T-cell dysfunction [ ]. Additionally, interactions between MDSCs and other cells in the TME are also important mechanisms of immunosuppression. For example, MDSCs can secrete inhibitory factors such as TGF-β and interleukin-10 (IL-10), inducing the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs), while tumor cells can directly promote the expansion of MDSCs by secreting granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) [ , ].

## Effects of metabolic competition on CAR-T cells
A signature feature of tumor cells is that they utilize glucose through aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) to meet their high energy requirements for survival and metabolism, as glycolysis produces ATP at a faster rate [ ]. CAR-T cells in TME use glucose to obtain ATP in two ways: Under normal circumstances, CAR-T cells consume glucose at a lower rate through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS); When CAR-T cells are activated, they undergo metabolic reprogramming and rely on the glycolytic pathway to obtain a large amount of ATP to meet the needs of cell proliferation and differentiation. However, the oxygen concentration in the TME is relatively low, which limits the occurrence of oxidative phosphorylation. Moreover, tumor cells’ voracious appetite for glucose starves CAR-T cells of the fuel they need for glycolysis. This metabolic roadblock drains CAR-T cells’ energy reserves and cripples their effectiveness [ , ]. On the other hand, due to the limitations of tumor cells themselves, they consume a large amount of arginine in TME, resulting in insufficient arginine, which inhibits the activation of CAR-T cells, and tumor cells also metabolize tryptophan to produce toxic metabolites such as kynurenic acid to induce T cell exhaustion [ ]. In addition, metabolic substances produced by tumor cells, such as lactic acid and adenosine, also inhibit the immune function of CAR-T cells [ ].

## Strategies to improve CAR T cell function in TME

## Armored CAR-T cell
Scientists have put forward various approaches to help armored CAR-T cells combat immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments. Liang and his team engineered HER2-specific CAR-T cells that also express SMAD7. Research indicates that these cutting-edge CAR-T cells can effectively shut down the TGF-β/SMAD signaling cascade, enabling them to eliminate cancer cells while preventing TGF-β from causing T cell burnout. Additionally, by suppressing TGF-β signal transmission and subsequent phosphorylation along the NF-κB pathway, the simultaneous expression of SMAD7 curbed cytokine release, which in turn substantially decreased the likelihood of triggering CRS [ ]. Zhao et al. engineered CAR-T cells capable of secreting IL-10, demonstrating superior functionality compared to conventional CAR-T therapies. These modified cells exhibited enhanced oxidative phosphorylation via mitochondrial pyruvate carriers, improving their survival in the harsh, oxygen-deprived conditions of tumor microenvironments. Additionally, they effectively reduced the expression of inhibitory receptors like PD-1, boosting their tumor-killing efficacy while promoting stem cell-like memory responses for sustained immunity. Interestingly, though IL-10 typically acts as an immunosuppressive cytokine in tumors, these engineered CAR-T cells appeared to counteract this effect by reshaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby maintaining their therapeutic potency [ ]. In the clinical trial, the anti-tumor efficacy and safety of GPC3 CAR-T cells and GPC3 CAR-T cells co-expressing IL-15 were compared in patients with solid tumors positive for GPC3. The results showed that co-expression of IL-15 achieved a 66% disease control rate and a 33% anti-tumor response rate. In terms of adverse events, the main manifestation was an increase in the incidence of CRS, but the symptoms could be rapidly alleviated with available medications [ ].
Although the research on armored CAR-T cells has yielded surprising results, the issue we must consider is how to strike a balance between its safety and effectiveness. Additionally, the complexity of the structural design and the manufacturing cost are also inherent difficulties of armored CAR-T cells [ ]. It should be noted that the armored CAR-T cells were developed to overcome the immunosuppressive microenvironment, but they did not address adverse reactions such as CRS and neurotoxicity. For instance, CD-19-targeted CAR-T cells that secrete IL-18 showed similar safety profiles when treating relapsed or refractory lymphomas compared to CAR-T cells that only target CD-19, resulting in a 62% incidence of CRS and immune effect-related neurotoxicity. Moreover, it is still unknown whether the armor itself may pose potential risks [ ].

## Strategies for improving CAR-T cell infiltration
The dense network of stromal cells and extracellular matrix within TME creates a formidable barrier that limits CAR-T cell penetration. Disrupting this stromal architecture can dramatically improve CAR-T cell efficacy against tumors. In a groundbreaking approach, Wehrli’s team engineered mesothelin-targeting CAR-T cells equipped with fibroblast activation protein (FAP) capability, enabling them to dismantle cancer-associated fibroblasts. This strategic modification clears a path through the tumor stroma, allowing CAR-T cells to effectively reach and attack malignant tissue [ ]. Similarly, Ash and his colleagues designed an E3K CAR-T cell targeting CD248, which can break down stromal cells near the tumor and promote infiltration. Nevertheless, monitoring these administered E3K CAR-T cells and evaluating their long-term effectiveness continues to pose significant hurdles for the research team [ ]. Recent studies on non-small cell lung cancer reveal that microwave ablation (MWA) possesses the unique ability to remodel the tumor microenvironment. By employing MWA, researchers have observed enhanced infiltration and activation of CAR-T cells, effectively bypassing the need for intricate CAR-T modifications typically required to target stromal cells. Notably, this approach has demonstrated a strong safety profile, with no adverse effects reported thus far [ ]. In addition to targeting stromal cells, improving tumor vascular abnormalities can also promote the delivery and migration of CAR-T cells at the tumor site. Studies have shown that blocking vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signal transduction can induce the normalization of tumor blood vessels and significantly improve the infiltration and distribution of CAR-T cells in glioblastoma (GBM) [ ].

## Local delivery of CAR-T cells
While many engineering approaches aim to enhance CAR-T cell infiltration and distribution within solid tumors, localized delivery offers a promising alternative to boost both effectiveness and safety. This method proves particularly advantageous for targeting hard-to-treat cancers like central nervous system tumors, where direct administration frequently yields superior antitumor responses [ ]. A Phase I clinical trial for patients with recurrent glioblastoma reported encouraging results using a locally delivered regimen of IL-13Rα2-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells. Half of the patients achieved stable disease or better, with a median overall survival of 7.7 months. Furthermore, no dose-limiting toxicities were observed, and 35% of patients experienced Grade 3 or higher adverse events related to CAR-T therapy [ ]. Tang et al. developed a polymer ring-shaped helical particle (TSP), which has the advantages of biocompatibility and degradability in vivo. By implanting the TSP embedded with CAR-T cells near the tumor through minimally invasive surgery or biopsy needles, persistent CAR-T cell release can be achieved. Compared with systemic administration and intratumoral delivery, CAR-T cell delivery using TSP significantly inhibited the growth of solid tumors and had lower toxicity [ ]. Emerging research indicates that hydrogels can stimulate localized inflammation in tumor environments while simultaneously enhancing the proliferation and anti-cancer activity of CAR-T cells. Building on these findings, Li et al. developed a hydrogel-based delivery system for CAR-T cells combined with mitochondrial autophagy boosters. Their experiments demonstrated that this approach significantly boosts CAR-T cell performance against triple-negative breast cancer tumors [ ].

## Metabolic regulation
Research indicates that modulating metabolic pathways can enhance the infiltration and efficacy of CAR-T cells within tumor environments. One study demonstrated that overexpressing adenosine deaminase (ADA-OE) accelerates adenosine breakdown, generating inosine as a key metabolite. This inosine suppresses glycolysis in CAR-T cells while boosting their mitochondrial function, glycolytic flexibility, glutamine catabolism, and polyamine production. Furthermore, the findings revealed an increase in CAR-T cell stemness. Together, these metabolic shifts significantly amplify the cells’ therapeutic potential [ ]. Huang et al.‘s study revealed that blocking CD38, a key marker of CAR-T cell exhaustion, disrupts both the CD38-CADPR-Ca2+ and CD38-NAD⁺-SIRT1 signaling pathways. This interference subsequently curtails T cell glycolysis, effectively slowing the depletion of CAR-T cells [ ]. Studies have shown that the amino acid metabolism process also affects the therapeutic effect of CAR-T cells. For example, Yang et al. demonstrated that upregulating branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase kinase (BCKDK) in CAR-T cells effectively suppresses the breakdown of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs). This manipulation boosts intracellular BCAA concentrations, leading to a marked improvement in the cells’ antitumor activity while also extending their proliferation and longevity in peripheral blood. On the flip side, when BCKDK expression was silenced, CAR-T cells showed substantially diminished viability and antitumor efficacy [ ]. In summary, by regulating metabolism, the tumor-killing potency of CAR-T cells can be effectively enhanced. However, the current data mainly focus on preclinical studies. In the field of solid tumors, we urgently need sufficient clinical efficacy evaluations to confirm this conclusion.

## Mitochondrial targeting strategy
Emerging research suggests that manipulating mitochondrial function presents a promising approach to enhance CAR-T cell therapy. Akhtar and colleagues engineered a novel CAR-T cell variant (MCAR T-2) capable of secreting GLP-1. This modified cell line capitalizes on the combined action of GLP-1 receptor activation and urolithin A (UrA) to boost autophagic processes, particularly mitochondrial autophagy. By doing so, MCAR T-2 not only amplifies the anti-tumor capabilities but also helps mitigate the likelihood of CRS development [ ]. The depletion and malfunction of mitochondria are key factors contributing to the fatigue of CAR-T cells. In a groundbreaking study, Jeremy G. Baldwin and his team uncovered the existence of nanotube bridges linking bone marrow stromal cells with T cells. Capitalizing on this revelation, they devised a daring new approach involving mitochondrial transfer engineering to restore mitochondria to T cells from bone marrow stromal cells. Their findings demonstrated that T cells with rejuvenated mitochondria not only enhanced their growth and ability to infiltrate tumors but also significantly reduced the incidence of T cell exhaustion [ ]. Overall, the innovative strategies for regulating mitochondria have not only enhanced the anti-tumor activity of CAR-T cells but also have not revealed any serious adverse events in both in vitro and in vivo models. Nevertheless, a great deal of research is still needed in terms of long-term safety, clinical translation, and manufacturing costs.

## Adverse reactions of CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors
Safety remains a paramount consideration when it comes to the real-world use of CAR-T cell therapy. While existing studies highlight its impressive effectiveness, some related clinical trials have been terminated due to adverse events. The most common adverse events associated with CAR-T cells are primarily cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [ ].

## Cytokine release syndrome
CRS is a dangerous inflammatory response triggered by an overwhelming surge of cytokines, often leading to systemic complications like high fever, dangerously low blood pressure, rapid breathing, and elevated heart rate. In extreme cases, this condition can become fatal. Both CAR-T cell therapies and the patient’s own immune cells play a role in triggering CRS. At the molecular level, the cytokines IL-6 and IL-1 are recognized as key drivers of this potentially lethal immune overreaction [ ]. Studies in SCID-beige mouse models have shown that inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) is upregulated at tumor sites, and the nitric oxide (NO) produced causes vasodilation and hypotension, resembling the clinical manifestations of CRS. Further research revealed that the IL-1 blocker anakinra can reduce iNOS expression in macrophages and alleviate CRS symptoms in mice. Additionally, IL-6 blockers also decreased iNOS expression; however, simultaneous administration of IL-6 and IL-1 blockers did not further reduce iNOS levels [ ]. Currently, the drugs widely used in clinical practice to mitigate CRS include the IL-6 blocker tocilizumab and glucocorticoids [ ]. New findings propose that the medication metoprolol, a type of β1-blocker, could be a promising therapeutic option for CRS. Studies show that metoprolol hampers the synthesis of the IL-6 protein by focusing on the eEF2K-eEF2 pathway, consequently lessening IL-6 output and subsequently easing CRS symptoms. Subsequently, a phase I/II clinical trial (NCT04082910) was embarked upon to test metoprolol’s effectiveness in mitigating CRS. The trial’s findings indicated that metoprolol not only maintained the CAR-T cells’ anti-cancer power but also drastically diminished the intensity of CRS, with only three of the sixteen metoprolol-treated patients experiencing grade 3 CRS [ ]. This provides a novel therapeutic strategy for CRS management.

## Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome
ICANS represents a potentially serious complication stemming from CAR-T cell therapy, which typically manifests through a range of neurological disturbances, including disorientation, difficulty concentrating, and excessive lethargy. The development of ICANS is tied to the release of numerous inflammatory cytokines and chemokines by CAR-T cells, macrophages, and other immune components, while also compromising the integrity of the blood-brain barrier [ ]. However, a recent study suggested that ICANS is not solely caused by cytokines; rather, the CXCL16/CXCR6 axis drives central inflammatory processes contributing to the progression of ICANS. Although this study proposed a novel mechanism involving the CXCL16/CXCR6 axis, it did not include a comparative analysis of patients without ICANS, limiting the generalizability of the findings [ ]. Corticosteroids and tocilizumab are currently the main treatments for ICANS; however, these drugs often diminish the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T cells, posing a challenge for clinical management [ ]. Predicting the occurrence of ICANS may offer significant advantages in mitigating such adverse effects. For instance, Amidi et al. developed a predictive model for ICANS that can determine whether a patient receiving CAR-T cell therapy will develop ICANS and when it might occur, thereby facilitating preemptive clinical measures. However, the study utilized a relatively homogeneous cohort and did not account for other confounding factors, so the predictive accuracy of this model remains to be further validated [ ].

## Application of the new CAR-T design

## Universal CAR-T
CAR-T cells were previously extracted and prepared relying on the same patient sources. However, the T cells in the patient’s body are severely depleted and unable to meet the large demand for T cells. In recent years, universal CAR-T cells (UCAR-T) have been proposed. They are prepared using allogeneic CAR-T cells and can achieve mass production of products, solving the problems of manufacturing challenges and long vein-to-vein time for cancer patients [ ]. Gene editing, specifically in the realm of CAR-T cell manipulation, is a hot topic in current scientific research (Fig. ). Take Chen and colleagues’ study, for instance, where they employed CRISPR/Cas9 to eliminate both the T-cell receptor (TCR) and human leukocyte antigen I (HLA-I), thereby creating versatile CAR-T cells. Essentially, these T cells can be tailored to recognize various antigens by simply fusing the single-chain variable regions of antibodies specific to those antigens with the CAR-T cells in a permanent bond [ ]. A Phase I clinical trial (NCT04438083) administered allogeneic CD70-targeting CAR-T cell therapy to 16 individuals grappling with recurrent or treatment-resistant advanced clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC). The treatment hit the mark, with disease stabilization or improvement in 81.3% of participants. Only eight patients experienced Grade 1 or 2 CRS, and no serious Grade 3 or higher cases emerged. Furthermore, the treatment remained undetected for neurotoxic effects, graft-versus-host disease (GvHD), or other dose-limiting adverse events, indicating a favorable safety profile [ ]. Research indicates that cytotoxic T lymphocytes, particularly CD8 + T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells play a crucial role in rejecting allogeneic CAR-T cells. In a recent study, Zhao at al. developed a novel CAR-T cell therapy. They engineered these cells by removing TCRαβ and HLA-I through a process of TALEN-based gene editing and then introducing HLA-E via an AAV6 vector-mediated system. These CAR-T cells can resist attacks from CD8 + cells and NK cells, thereby significantly prolonging their in vivo persistence and efficacy. Furthermore, the advantage of this universal CAR-T cell lies in its ability to retain and allow endogenous effectors (such as tumor-associated lymphocytes) to work synergistically with CAR-T cells. However, Zhao et al. discovered the random or non-targeted integration of HLA-Em and CARm in their research, which is also one of the limitations of TALEN gene editing [ ].
Although gene editing technology has to some extent solved the problem of large-scale production of CAR-T, we still need to remain vigilant. For instance, gene editing may lead to off-target risks, resulting in modifications at incorrect locations, which may cause abnormal states or functions of the cells, and even increase the risk of gene mutations [ ]. Additionally, transporting gene editing tools to the designated location within T cells is also one of the key bottlenecks. Traditional viral vectors may cause risks such as immune rejection and random integration, while electroporation may cause certain damage to the cell membrane [ ]. However, some studies have pointed out that electroporation delivery does not affect the expansion of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells that integrate PD-1 in the body, although it causes damage to the cell membrane [ ].
Universal CAR-T cells employ an alternative design strategy, the core of which involves a “bridging” mechanism to connect intracellular signaling domains with extracellular antigen-recognition switch molecules. This approach distinguishes them from the conventional scFv-based CAR-T structure that targets a single antigen (Fig. ) [ ]. This modular CAR-T cell system significantly enhances controllability by allowing modulation of the structure and dosage of the switch molecule. In the event of severe adverse reactions, clearing the switch molecule can promptly terminate CAR-T function. Moreover, simply replacing the switch molecule enables targeting of different antigens, eliminating the need for large-scale reproduction of CAR-T cells [ ]. Studies have shown that modifying CAR-T cells using the biotin-binding monomer streptavidin-2 (mSA2) domain and using biotin-conjugated antibodies (such as biotin-conjugated trastuzumab) as soluble molecular linkers can demonstrate a powerful tumor-killing effect in HER2-positive tumor models. Moreover, this engineered CAR-T cell has excellent properties of penetrating the extracellular matrix (ECM). Most importantly, targeting different antigens can be achieved by altering the soluble molecular linkers, which fully demonstrates the great potential of this CAR-T cell [ ].

 

The emergence of allogeneic T cells has brought new hope to CAR-T cell therapy. However, how to overcome GvHD and host-versus-graft rejection (HvGR) is an aspect that cannot be ignored before the application of allogeneic T cells [ ]. OX40 ligand (OX40L) is an antigen related to GvHD and HvGR. Rui et al. developed a regulatory T cell expressing OX40L ligand (OX40L-CAR-Treg), which can inhibit the proliferation of allogeneic reactive T cells and the transformation of monocytes into dendritic cells. Compared with common CAR-T cells, it can inhibit the occurrence of GvHD and HVGR, thereby improving the safety of CAR-T cells [ ]. Recently, Perica et al. proposed to utilize the mechanism of lymphotropic viruses (such as the Human immunodeficiency virus, HIV) evading immunity to reduce immune rejection suffered by allogeneic T cells. The viral evading hormone produced by the virus not only reduces the expression of HLA-I but also reduces activation-induced T cell exhaustion through serine/threonine kinase Pka2. This approach considerably enhances CAR-T cell therapeutic efficacy [ ].

## Kill switch and security optimization
Research has utilized the tyrosine kinase inhibitor dasatinib in tandem with an early round of CAR-T cell infusions. Findings indicate that dasatinib can effectively shut down CAR-T cell signaling, which in turn dials back their tumor-fighting prowess and their ability to multiply. This inhibitory impact can be turned off once dasatinib is stopped. Serving as a molecular “switch”, dasatinib has the potential to tweak CAR-T cell behavior, lessening the risk of negative side effects linked to CAR-T cell therapy while allowing for the treatment to be closely observed [ ]. However, recent research has demonstrated that triggering apoptosis in CAR-T cells through activation of the inducible caspase 9 (iCasp9) suicide gene provides a faster resolution to the side effects associated with CAR-T therapy. Unlike tyrosine kinase inhibitors, this genetic switch offers pinpoint control at the DNA level, delivering superior precision and safety. When the designated activator drug is introduced, it wipes out the bulk of CAR-T cells within a short period, dramatically lowering the chances of treatment-related complications [ ]. Although the iCasp9 suicide gene seems to enhance the safety of patients during CAR-T treatment, the activation of this system does not eliminate all CAR-T cells, and this process is irreversible. This means that pinpointing the perfect moment to activate the iCasp9 safety mechanism is still a tough nut to crack, as finding that sweet spot between therapeutic effectiveness and patient safety continues to be a major hurdle in the field.

## Combination treatment strategy of CAR-T therapy

## Combined immune checkpoint inhibitors
Research indicates that PD-1/PD-L1 pathway activation causes T cell impairment, while its inhibition potentiates antitumor cytotoxicity [ ]. Long-established therapies, including immune checkpoint modulators like PD-1 and CTLA4 inhibitors, are cleared for combating various cancers [ ]. In addition, recently, a number of new immune checkpoints, such as LAG-3 [ ] and B7-H3 [ ], have also entered clinical trials one after another. However, these checkpoint inhibitors did not achieve satisfactory therapeutic effects when used alone. Therefore, the combination of ICI and CAR-T cells can precisely utilize the advantages of both, thereby enhancing the killing effect on tumors. For instance, research has found that the use of PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab after CD19 CAR-T treatment can improve the clinical outcomes of some B-cell lymphoma patients who did not respond or relapsed after CAR-T treatment. Among the 12 patients, 33% benefited. Importantly, this therapy led to a significant reduction in T cell depletion. Moreover, the research indicated that the incidence of adverse effects was notably low [ ]. However, recent researchers have changed this strategy. For instance, Shi et al. constructed a CAR-T cell (Trim21 KO anti-CD19 CAR-T) that completely knocked out immune checkpoint-related genes. Their findings revealed that the absence of TRIM21 substantially boosted the tumor-fighting capabilities of these modified T cells. In comparison to traditional ICI treatments, this gene knockout method proves far more effective at amplifying the therapeutic impact of CAR-T cells while also mitigating T cell exhaustion [ ].

## Combination chemotherapy or radiotherapy
Although chemotherapy has serious adverse reactions and treatment resistance, it is still an indispensable means to treat various cancers, such as breast cancer [ ]. In breast cancer cases that test positive for HER2, the protein DLL4 plays a pivotal role in helping the cancer cells withstand neoadjuvant chemotherapy. What’s more, it operates on two fronts: it not only reorganizes the tumor’s microenvironment, allowing it to sidestep the immune system, but it also endows the cancer with a direct immunity against the chemotherapy drug paclitaxel (THP). Therefore, they designed a CAR T cell targeting DLL4 that can effectively kill tumor cells, a finding that provides a dual intervention target for reversing drug resistance [ ].
Research indicates that radiotherapy can significantly boost the effectiveness of CAR-T cell therapy against tumors. In a groundbreaking study, Wang and colleagues employed radiation pretreatment to increase B7-H3 expression across multiple cancer types before administering B7-H3-specific CAR-T cells. Their findings revealed a dramatic improvement in the CAR-T cells’ ability to infiltrate tumors and destroy cancer cells. These results held true in both laboratory settings and live animal models, confirming the synergistic potential of combining radiotherapy with CAR-T cell treatment [ ]. Ni et al. found that FLASH radiation was able to reprogram tumor-associated macrophages towards M1 polarization and ameliorate T cell depletion, in addition to reducing peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) and Arg-1 expression. The results show that FLASH radiation can improve the treatment of medulloblastoma with CAR T cells, effectively paving the way for fresh opportunities in the battle against solid tumors [ ].

## Combine with oncolytic virus
The emergence of oncolytic viruses has brought a new choice for the treatment of cancer patients. This is a drug based on the anti-tumor effect of the virus. CAR-T cells zero in on cancer by detecting telltale antigens on tumor surfaces, teaming up with oncolytic viruses to turn the tables on malignancies. This one-two punch leverages the viruses’ knack for popping cancer cells open, which in turn unveils more targets, giving the CAR-T cells a clear shot at taking down the tumor more efficiently than going it alone [ ]. A recent study engineered an oncolytic virus to produce a shortened version of the CD19 protein (CD19t). When this modified virus infected tumor cells, it triggered the expression of CD19t on their surface. This clever approach significantly boosted the ability of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells to recognize and destroy the cancerous cells [ ]. A separate investigation explored the synergistic effects of oncolytic viruses engineered to produce the chemokine CXCL11 alongside CAR T-cell therapy for glioblastoma. The study revealed that oAd-CXCL11 not only exerted its own anticancer activity but also remodeled TME. This modification led to a shift in the balance between immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive cell populations within the tumor. Notably, this approach markedly boosted the efficacy of B7-H3-targeted CAR T cells in combating tumor growth [ ].

## Application of nanomaterials in CAR-T cells
Recent research has consistently demonstrated that combining materials science with immunotherapy produces remarkably promising results. That said, concerns about safety and affordability remain largely overlooked. By engineering nanomaterials, scientists can now direct CAR-T cells with pinpoint accuracy to cancerous tissues while even regulating drug release rates. Despite the challenges, this innovative strategy retains substantial potential for advancing cancer treatment.
Owing to their diminutive dimensions, distinctive characteristics, and capacity for biodegradation, nanomaterials have become a cornerstone in modern disease treatment. These tiny powerhouses have opened new avenues in healthcare, primarily because they can enhance the precision of drug delivery, optimize therapeutic release mechanisms, and bolster pharmaceutical stability. As a result, the scientific community has been abuzz with research exploring the synergistic potential of pairing nanomaterials with CAR-T cell therapies, marking a promising frontier in the fight against complex diseases [ ]. In their research, Pfister and colleagues employed superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) to manipulate CAR-T cells for targeted magnetic therapy. These particles can suppress the production of inflammatory cytokines by CAR-T cells, and they can also alter the method of tumor cell death, favoring apoptosis over pyroptosis. This technique significantly cuts down on the occurrence of CRS. Essentially, it boosts the effectiveness and safety profile of CAR-T cell therapy. Nevertheless, more thorough studies on the nanoparticles’ activity and safety in living organisms are necessary [ ]. Chen et al. developed a multi-drug assembled TME-responsive nano-immunomodulator (FMANAC). FMANAC decomposes and releases the drugs CCL5 and NLG919 in the TME at a low PH value. These drugs can increase the intratumoral infiltration of CAR-T cells and reverse immunosuppression in the TME. In addition, this kind of nano-modulator can be combined with CAR-T cells to be programmed for administration under the guidance of multiplex near-infrared fluorescence imaging, which is conducive to the real-time observation of anti-tumor responses [ ]. Li et al. have come up with a groundbreaking approach called triboelectric immunotherapy, which pairs with CAR-T cell therapy. They’ve developed a triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) that can produce a steady stream of pulsing direct current. This current can spur immunogenic cell death in cancer cells, kickstart the maturation of dendritic cells, and improve their ability to present antigens. It can also reprogram the immunosuppressive environment, boosting the cancer-fighting power of CAR-T cells. What’s really neat about this triboelectric immunotherapy is that it doesn’t need any extra medication, which slashes the risk of side effects and slashes the price tag for patients [ ].

## Current clinical trials of CAR-T products for solid tumors
While CAR-T cell therapies for solid tumors remain largely in the predevelopment phase, the landscape has shifted significantly as of late, and its preliminary results have demonstrated its potential and challenges. Efficacy and safety are the core metrics for evaluating CAR-T cell products; however, large-scale clinical evidence supporting their long-term efficacy and safety in solid tumor treatment is still lacking. Table  summarizes some of the recent CAR-T products for solid tumor treatment that have entered clinical trials.
In terms of efficacy, CAR-T cells targeting specific targets have shown promising responses in certain solid tumors. For instance, it was reported that CAR-T cells targeting Claudin18.2 achieved an objective response rate of 38.8% and a disease control rate of 91.8% in digestive system tumors [ ]. Similarly, in clear cell renal cell carcinoma, the disease control rate of anti-CD70 CAR-T cells reached 81%, and the median progression-free survival for patients was 2.9 months [ ]. These data all reveal a common point: the selection of highly specific target points is crucial for the efficacy of CAR-T therapy. It is worth noting that CAR-T cells targeting GPC3 had limited efficacy when used alone in liver cancer patients, and the treatment effect was significantly improved by co-expressing IL-15. This further validates the importance of the design of the CAR-T structure [ ].
In terms of safety, we can observe that the CRS that occurred in most trials was a mild event and was controllable. We also noticed that the safety varies significantly among different targets and trials. For example, in a phase II trial targeting gastric cancer or gastroesophageal cancer, the incidence of CRS in patients receiving CLDN18.2-targeted CAR-T cells was as high as 95%, and the incidence of grade 3 and more severe adverse events was as astonishing as 99% [ ]. Additionally, in an EGFR/IL-13Rα2 dual-targeted CAR-T trial for glioblastoma (GBM), 10 patients reported grade 3 immune effector cell-related neurotoxic syndrome, suggesting that CAR-T therapy for central nervous system tumors requires additional prevention of neurotoxicity [ ].
It is notable that CAR-T therapy has achieved breakthrough progress in some pediatric and central nervous system solid tumors. Besides the previously mentioned EGFR/IL-13Rα2 CAR-T cells, it has been reported that CAR-T cells targeting GD2 enabled 3 out of 8 glioblastoma (GBM) patients to achieve partial remission, 1 patient to have stable disease, and only a few mild central nervous system symptoms and hematological toxicity [ ]. More significantly, the exploration in fatal brain tumor diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG) has shown promising results. A clinical trial targeting CAR-T cells with B7H3 as the target achieved good results in patients treated, with a median overall survival of 10.7 months after infusion. Although the patients experienced symptoms such as headache and nausea, no cases of CRS or ICANS were found [ ]. The initial success of CAR-T products in GBM and DIPG not only brings new hope to patients but also provides a good example for overcoming solid tumors.

 

## Conclusion and prospect
CAR-T cell therapy has achieved remarkable success in the treatment of hematological malignancies. However, its application in solid tumors still faces multiple challenges, including tumor heterogeneity, antigen escape, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and treatment-related toxicities. To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed various novel engineering strategies, such as logic-gated CAR-T cells, armored CAR-T cells, microenvironment reprogramming, and combination therapies, aiming to enhance the infiltration, persistence, and anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T cells. Nevertheless, these strategies still have significant drawbacks: the design of structurally optimized CAR-T cells is complex and costly; armored CAR-T cells may cause unknown adverse reactions due to overactivation; the potential risks of gene editing and the additive toxicities from combination therapies also warrant caution. As of now, there’s been no worldwide approval for a CAR-T therapy for treating solid tumors. We’re still lacking comprehensive clinical evidence to back up the safety and effectiveness of these treatments. It’s crucial to get more CAR-T therapies through the clinical trial phase and set up the necessary biomarker tracking, efficacy benchmarks, and side-effect surveillance. Moreover, the intricate relationship between genetic regulatory networks and the tumor’s local environment during cancer’s advance is still shrouded in mystery. To uncover these fundamental processes, cutting-edge technologies like systems biology and high-precision spatial and temporal omics research are essential. Notably, artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated great potential in antigen screening, CAR structure optimization, and treatment response prediction. Meanwhile, targeted delivery and real-time regulation systems based on TME-responsive nanosensors offer new avenues for achieving precise spatiotemporal control of CAR-T cells.
Although the research on CAR-T cells has presented us with unprecedented opportunities, during the process of transitioning from preclinical to clinical stages, we cannot merely focus on technological innovation and the enhancement of the activity of CAR-T cells. The safety risks of CAR-T cells in the body, the stability of the production process for large-scale production, and the affordability of manufacturing and application costs all require precise management. For instance, when expanding the production of genetically edited CAR-T cells to meet clinical dosage requirements, how to avoid the occurrence of genomic mutations or off-target editing products. Moreover, regulatory agencies (such as the FDA) currently hold a cautious attitude towards these engineered immune cells. To gain their support, more comprehensive pharmacological, toxicological, and long-term follow-up data must be provided.
In summary, this review provides a systematic analysis of the key challenges facing CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors and reviews existing strategies for enhancing its efficacy. We propose that the development of next-generation CAR-T technology should move beyond singular engineering approaches towards the design of a multi-modular, synergistic, dynamically regulatable, and tumor-ecosystem-adaptive “intelligent immune system.” On the horizon, with a seamless blend of diverse disciplines like immunology, synthetic biology, nanotech, and AI, it’s anticipated that CAR-T cells will really make waves in tackling solid tumors.
