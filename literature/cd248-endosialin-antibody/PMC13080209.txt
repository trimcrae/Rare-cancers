Many of these systems are limited by self-affinity which limits the dynamics or causes a false-positive high background signal. To overcome these challenges, it is necessary to either modify the interacting residues of established split fragments or by exploring new split-sites. Using the computational modeling tool SPELL to identify stable candidates, we developed five unique split sites for TEM-1 β-lactamase to compare structure and activity versus the original split site. We monitored structural changes through MMS IR spectroscopy as well as functional activity of each split enzyme. Expected structural changes of each fragment and changes in self-affinity driven complementation were observed. By utilizing specific binding proteins attached to the split enzyme, we monitored enzyme activity with forced complementation when bound to the target compared to auto-complementation when the enzymes were auto-complexed in solution. Of the five new split sites, one maintained high binding-mediated complementation activity with significant reduction in background auto-complementation. Changing the split site is a relatively simple approach to reduce background activity while maintaining on-target activity. The ability to tune split-systems has the potential to enable more sensitive and complex studies that previously were limited by the high background from self-affinity.

## Introduction
Split-proteins have been utilized for various application, with the first split systems being used for detection of protein-protein interactions (PPI) in protein complementation assays [ – ]. Recent applications have expanded to biosensors [ , ], molecular switches [ , ], conditional gene editing [ ], and therapeutics [ ]. A major challenge in developing new split-systems is the propensity for background activity from the split protein fragments to due to high self-affinity. Importantly, the high self-affinity or “auto-complementation” of the constructs can lead to negligible off rates creating an always “on” system which prevents any dynamic and may induce a false interaction driven by the split protein instead of the target. [ , – ] Strategies to optimize these platforms [ ] include modifying the interaction fragments using directed evolution [ ], site directed mutagenesis [ ], or other rational design. For example, the Tobacco-Etch Virus (TEV) system is known to have high background signal from auto-complementation and directed mutagenesis was used to decrease the interactions at the interface to force complexation only when the two target proteins fused to split-TEV interact [ ]. While modifications of the split-fragments can overcome limitations caused by auto-complementation, these campaigns are often time and labor intensive.
Another strategy to reduce high background is to split the protein in to additional fragments, as reported by our group and other investigators with versions of the tri-part NanoLuc® system [ – ] and the tri-part GFP system [ , ]. Each of these studies indicate that suppressed background signals were seen upon further splitting of the proteins [ , , , , ]; however, the complexity of a three-part split protein platform is not always amenable to a given application, especially in developing split proteins for therapeutics.
Alternatively, new split-sites can be generated and explored for improved properties. Recent advances in computational tools enable an evaluation of current split proteins and identification of new, including the Split Protein rEassembly by Ligand or Light (SPELL) approach [ ]. SPELL has been used for various different studies both to find new split sites to tune existing systems but also to find potential split sites for proteins that previously have never been split [ – ]. Using the SPELL tool, we set out to reduce auto-complementation of split TEM-1 β-lactamase by investigating new stable split sites.
Insights into structural changes can facilitate a more rational design of split systems; yet, the highly flexible nature and size of many split proteins makes structural determination of the split parts or the complemented system difficult using crystallization or Cryo-electron microscopy due to the increased flexibility of the protein [ ]. RedShift Bio® developed a microfluidics modulation spectroscopy (MMS) IR-spectroscopy instrument for structural determination with increased sensitivity measurements and decreased sample volumes. The MMS instrument has been validated on a range of different protein systems to monitor subtle changes in secondary structure and protein stability [ – ]. We apply this technique to the TEM-1 split protein sites to determine changes in structure and potential stability issues of the individual proteins fragments and the complexed system, allowing us rapidly to explore several candidate split-sites.
The TEM-1 enzyme was one of the first split-enzymes and was developed by Galarnaeu et al. to investigate PPI by monitoring interactions via substrate turnover by TEM-1. It has since been used for multiple PPI type assays in various fields of study [ – ]. Our group adapted this enzyme to improve upon a previously reported therapeutic approach called “Antibody Directed Enzyme Prodrug Therapy” (ADEPT) [ ], which had previously failed to reach clinical approval due to the high background activity of the enzyme causing off target toxicities [ ]. Instead of using an intact enzyme like the original ADEPT, we fused target binding proteins to the split halves of the TEM-1 enzyme creating a complementation dependent enzyme prodrug therapy (CoDEPT) [ ]. The core innovation is the split-system, which requires simultaneous binding of the split-enzyme fragments to the target, forcing the split fragments into proximity and facilitating the complementation of an active enzyme to convert a prodrug into an active drug. By limiting enzymatic activity to a target-bound construct, the off-target toxicity seen with the always “on” nature of ADEPT is significantly reduced. Although our study displayed reduced activity compared to the intact enzyme, we still observed background activity of the split enzyme in the absence of the target that resulted from auto-complementation.
Building on our previous study, we engineered new split proteins fused to the same designed ankyrin repeat proteins (DARPins) [ , ] that bind specifically to human epithermal growth factor 2 (HER2) which is overexpressed in many forms of breast, ovarian and gastrointestinal cancers [ ]. HER2 is also a primary target for multiple targeted therapies allowing for ample comparison to current targeted therapies for these cancer types [ – ]. Most targeted therapies for treating HER2 positive cancers are currently antibody drug conjugates (ADC) which rely on internalization of the antibody to release the drug [ ]. This mechanism already shows resistance with one form being an increase in HER2 mutations and expression levels that render the ADC ineffective [ , ]. The CoDEPT approach can both exploit these mutations by binding on the surface and still releasing ample drug into the tumor microenvironment, but also has the potential to deliver more drug to the tumor site as it is not limited by a conjugated payload.
Here, we utilized the SPELL algorithm to find new split-sites and monitored structural changes through MMS instrument to provide lead candidates that were further evaluated for effects of structure on function. Combined, the process is a facile approach to identify stable split protein fragments that can be evaluated for reduced background activity while maintaining high on-target activity.

## Methods

## SPELL for New Split Determination:
The program Split Protein Reassembly by Ligand or Light (SPELL) was used to determine new split sites that could potentially decrease the self-affinity of the protein switch. The crystal structures for β-lactamase TEM-1 (PDB: 1ZG4, 1XPB, 5HW1) were given to the algorithm and ~ 10 split sites were returned for each crystal structure with 90 % being the same sites [ ]. Of these 10 new sites, five were chosen that disrupt the binding pocket and two of the five also break up the catalytic triad. These were selected as the most promising candidates to decrease overall self-affinity.

## Split Constructs Cloning:
Each fusion protein was made using the original scaffold of the C-terminal and N-terminal constructs on a PF1K E. coli expression vector with a Kanamycin resistance gene. The DARPiNs remained on the designated sides, DARPiN G3 on the C-terminus and DARPiN 9.29 on the N-terminus of the split β-lactamase. All C-terminal new split fusion proteins were cloned using standard restriction site cloning into the original PF1K-G3-bC vector. All N-terminal new split fusion proteins were cloned using standard restriction site cloning into the original PF1K-bN-9.29 vector. A total of 10 new plasmids were made and verified through whole plasmid sequencing using Plasmidsaurus©.

## Protein Production:
All proteins used in this study were produced in NEB T7-Shuffle Expression E. coli (Cat# C3029J) based on previous lab convention using the following method. Overnight cultures were grown in 10 mL of Luria broth (LB) media (RPI Cat# L24080–500.0) with 50 μg/mL of kanamycin at 37 °C. The overnight culture was diluted 25 mL into 1 L of LB Kan media and grown at 37 °C until an OD600 of ~0.5 was reached. The cultures were then induced with 500 μM IPTG (GoldBio Cat# I2481C), and temperature was lowered to 30 °C to express protein overnight. Cells were then pelleted, resuspended in His-Tag buffer (100 mM Phosphate, 150 mM NaCl pH 7.0), freeze thawed 3× and sonicated prior to purification. Sonication was performed on the BioLogics Ultra-sonic Homogenizer for 15 min with pulses every 15 s with an amplitude of 60 %.

## Protein Purification:
Samples were loaded onto a Co-NTA (ThermoFisher Scientific© HisPurr® Superflow Cobalt Resin Cat# 25228) column for his-tag purification on AKTA FPLC. A three-step gradient was used to elute the protein in different percentages of 200 mM Imidazole (5 %, 20 % and 100 %). Fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE gel and pure fractions were combined and concentrated on 3 K spin concentrators (ThermoFisher Scientific© Pierce® Protein Concentrators PES Cat# 88515). All proteins were then dialyzed overnight into histidine buffer (5 mM histidine, 150 mM NaCl at pH 7.4) using 3.5 kDa dialysis tubing (ThermoFisher Scientific© SnakeSkin Tubing Cat# 88242). Proteins were quantified using a nanodrop.

## MMS Sample Preparation and Analysis:
After dialysis and quantitation, proteins were adjusted to reach a concentration of 1 mg/mL. In the designated wells, 1 mL of the protein was added, ensuring no bubbles were seen at the bottom of the wells. In the designated wells, 1 mL of the dialysis buffer was added to the reference wells. Each protein was run on the AQS3pro instrument using the same standard method and analyzed using the RedShift Bio® analysis software. HOS graphs were imported into GraphPad Prism (10.5.0), and 2-way ANOVA was performed to determine statistical significance.

## Auto-complementation Kinetic Assays:
In a clear non-binding 96-well plate (CellTreat® 96-well tissue culture plate Cat# 229195), protein complexes were added in triplicate starting at 1 μM final concentration and serially diluted down to 1 nM final concentration. Centa was diluted to 100 nM final concentration and added to each well. Absorbance at 405 nm was monitored every 10 min for 18 h on BioTek Gen5 Plate Reader at room temperature. Initial rates were determined for each concentration and subtracted from the blank signal. The same concentration of protein was added to black, clear bottom, 96-well plates (ThermoFisher Scientific© Fluorescent Microplate) and serial diluted down from 1 μM to 1 nM final concentration. In each well, 1 M of ampicillin sodium salt was added to each well. The fluorescence was monitored every 10 min for 5 days (excitation 346 nm, emission 422 nm) on a Tecan® InfinitePro Plate Reader at room temperature. Initial rates were determined for each concentration of each complex.

## Complementation Kinetic Assays:
In a high binding clear (ThermoFisher Scientific© MaxiSorb® Cat# 44–2404) and high binding black (ThermoFisher Scientific© PolySorb® Cat# 437112), clear bottom 96-well plates, 35 nM of HER2 was added to each well, covered and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rocking. The wells were washed 5× with PBS. Each plate was blocked with 1 % BSA in PBS, covered and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rocking. The plates were washed 5× with PBS and bound with the complexed protein serial dilutions from 1 μM down to 1 nM. The plates were incubated overnight at 4C while rocking. The plates were washed 5× with PBS and 100 nM of Centa and 1 M of ampicillin were added to the clear and black plates respectively. The Centa plate monitored absorbance at 405 nm every 2 min for 2 h on BioTek Gen5 Plate Reader at room temperature. The ampicillin plate monitored fluorescence every 10 min for 3 days (excitation 346 nm, emission 422 nm) on a Tecan® InfinitePro Plate Reader at room temperature. Initial rates were determined for each concentration of each complex.

## Cell Assays:
Cell lines were obtained from ATCC, and reagents were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific® (Waltham, MA, USA). MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 were cultured in DMEM (high glucose, pyruvate, Gibco™) supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1× Antibiotic-Antimycotic solution. NCI-N87 were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (ATCC modification, Gibco™ Cat# 11875093) supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1× Antibiotic-Antimycotic solution. SK-OV-3 were cultured in McCoy’s (ATCC modification, Gibco™ Cat# 16600082) supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1× Antibiotic-Antimycotic solution. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 and used at passage 12 or below. Cells were seeded at 2,000 cells/well per lab convention and literature standard and then adhered to the plate overnight. Growth media was replaced with media containing 500 nM of either the b63 or b196 split systems and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. The enzyme solution was removed and 200 μL of C5-FU prodrug at a serial dilution starting at was added to the wells for both enzyme split system and incubated for 6 days at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. Max concentration of 100 μM was used as the prodrug will hydrolyze slowly in solution and higher concentrations can lead to more cellular killing not catalyzed by the enzyme. The media was replaced with 1:6 CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution (Promega Cat# G3582) reagent and plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 and absorbance read at 490 nm. IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism -inhibition-concentration curve fit.

## Computational Analysis and Graphics:
AlphaFold2, ColabFold (v1.5.5) [ ] was used to for structure prediction. The individual protein sequences were input to the program and run for structure prediction using the preset values. For the complexes predictions, the two sequences were input into AlphaFold Server in the protein docking analysis and run using default settings. Five top predictions were output with heat map coloring to indicate confidence (blue = high confidence, red = low confidence). Molecular graphics and analyses were performed with UCSF ChimeraX (1.10), developed by the University of California, San Francisco, with support from National Institutes of Health R01-GM129325 [ ].

## Results
We first used the split SPELL algorithm to determine possible new sites for engineering β-lactamase. The SPELL algorithm’s main use is to determine stable protein fragments when splitting the system; although, the algorithm also accounts for internal hydrogen bonds that are broken and become solvent exposed and may correlate to reduced self-affinity. Inputting the crystal structures for TEM-1 (PDB: 1ZG4, 1XPB, 5HW1) into the SPELL algorithm provided us with 10 different split sites that overlap between models and are ranked based on predicted stability of the constituent fragments - ideal energetics of the individual pieces should sit in a minimum of a well indicating stability of both alone. Of the 10 predicted sites, one was the original split site at amino acid 196 that is extensively reported and used in split- β -lactamase constructs. Of the remaining 9 sites, we intentionally chose 5 that break up the binding pocket and the catalytic triad ( & ). Specifically, the split sites at between amino acids 63/64, 86/87, 93/94, 174/175, and 215/216 were chosen to be made into fusion constructs along with the original 196/198 split-site, and are henceforth referred to as bN63, bC64, bN86, bN87, etc., s where bN refers to the N-terminus side of the split and bC is the C-terminus side (Table in ).
Using these predicted split-sites, we engineered the constructs based on our original platform, retaining the fusions to the DARPiNs on either the N-terminal (DARPiN 9.29) or C-terminal (DARPiN G3) of the enzyme. After producing and purifying the new split proteins, we analyzed the secondary structure of the proteins using microfluidics modulation spectroscopy (MMS) [ , ], a microfluidic IR system ( ). Since all the proteins are fusion constructs, we analyzed the binders DARPiNs G3 and 9.29 independently to determine the contribution of the signal from the DARPiNs. For each sample, the contribution of the binding DARPins was subtracted from the split enzyme portions to give an accurate representation of the enzyme fragments alone.
By analyzing the contribution of individual secondary structures, we detected differences between fragments that correspond to changes predicted in the structure ( ). The most variable changes occurring in the alpha region of the individual N-terminal and C-terminal enzyme fragments as well as the complexes. Likely, these differences arise from changes to the solvent accessibility of alpha helices that previously were buried in the core of the enzyme. The most significant structural changes are seen with the extreme splits b63 and b215 that produce small and large fragments, respectively, with smaller changes observed from fragments derived from more internal splits ( , ).
We also analyzed the intact TEM-1 enzyme to determine if changes can be seen between the complexed split enzyme forms and intact, non-split enzyme. All samples were run at high concentrations to induce complementation of the two fragments. We expected each complex to closely match the structure of the intact enzyme, TEM-1; however, we found several detectable changes between complexes, with b196 showing the most significant structural differences compared to intact TEM-1 ( ). The b63 and b215 complexes have no statistical significance from the intact enzyme, which is expected considering the split structure is only slighter perturbed by the removal of the small protein fragments. More variability is seen between the more internal splits, b86, b93, b174 and b196, likely due to the larger fragments having more differences in solvent exposed residues. Intriguingly, there are no statistically significant differences detected between the split complexes, although we note that this is likely a result of higher variability within replicates that results from the complexity of the samples.
In addition to MMS analysis, we also looked at computational modeling to determine possible structural differences between the different proteins and complexes form. Using AlphaFold, each protein was analyzed for folding and secondary structure and given confidence intervals for the probability of each structure. From , we can see greater variability in the N-terminal proteins with high variability in the bN63 and bN86 proteins. The MMS displayed greatest variability in the alpha region for the N-terminal proteins seeing the greatest alpha characteristic in the bN215 which displays great predictive folding into a defined alpha structure. The C-terminal proteins are more predictive with only small areas being low confidence. The C-terminal proteins display subtle variability mainly in the turn and unordered regions with bC198 having an increase in unordered and decrease in turn which correlates with the uncertainty in the predicted structure ( ).
By inspecting the docking events for each complex, we can confirm the trends seen with MMS. The b63, b86, b93 and b215 all have high predictive structures that are similar to the intact enzyme and these three are likewise the most similar to the intact enzyme by MMS analysis ( ). Conversely, the b174 and b196 complexes display very low predictive confidence and look less similar to the intact enzyme. These two complexes differ the most from Tem1 by MMS analysis. These results are expected as the smaller fragments removed should look more similar to the intact enzyme, while splits that are more equal, should have more variability as more of the structure is perturbed.
We further compared the original split site, b196, to b63 which was predicted to have the most structural difference based on the bN63 protein being the smallest of the split halves produced. The greatest differences in the individual pieces of the b63 are evident, and as expected, since this split site produces large and small fragments versus the b196 which results in fragments closer in size ( Table inset). The complexes of these two split-enzymes do not show statistically significant differences compared to the intact enzyme, but we performed a calculated average curve with the RedShift Bio analytics software to determine if the complex is forming between two fragments or if we see signal detected based only on the mixture of two proteins ( ). Both split enzymes display structural differences between the calculated average that would result from simple mixing, compared to the complex signal that would result from complementation of the fragments. Closer inspection of the individual parts for the b63 complex shows that the bN63 protein has the lowest alpha characteristic compared to the other samples. As the signal is expected to be higher given the solvent accessibility of the single predicted alpha helix, the decrease could indicate some unfolding of the helix which could explain the reduced auto-complementation detected.
Following biophysical characterization of the different split sites, we performed functional assays to determine changes in activity. Enzyme concentration was varied to determine levels of substrate turnover on target (complementation) and off-target (auto-complementation) first using Centa, a standard chromogenic β-lactamase substrate used in kinetic assays. As expected, all split sites retain activity when bound to HER2 even with low concentrations of enzyme ( ). Auto-complementation levels were significantly lower for the b63 split-construct compared to not only the original b196 split, but most of the other split sites tested as well. The b86 split-construct also displays reduced auto-complementation; however, the bC87 fragment was surprisingly unstable and prone to precipitation at higher concentrations. Based on selection of candidate split sites with lower hydrogen-bonding interactions, we anticipated each to have decreased auto-complementation, yet we did observe an increase in auto-complementation with the b93 split-site, suggesting that these fragments have higher self-affinity compared to the other split site fragments.
Since all 6 split sites perturb the binding pocket to a different degree, we hypothesized there would be changes in reaction rates among the different complexes and further tested the specificity and activity of each complex by evaluating turnover of a secondary substrate, ampicillin. Although auto-complementation is seen at the highest concentration of enzyme (1000 nM) for all the split-sites, except b86 ( ), there is significant variability in reaction rates at this concentration, with b93 having the highest rate. Only the b174 and b196 displayed a significant signal at 100 nM and none of the split-enzymes displayed detectable signal at lower concentrations. For both ampicillin and Centa substrates, the b63 complex only displays auto-complementation at the highest concentration of 1 μM.
We extended our functional analysis to determine if changing the split site can still provide a functional enzyme following target-mediated complementation on HER2 positive cells to produce the active chemotherapeutic drug, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) from a prodrug (C5-FU) [ ]. The prodrug C5-FU is an inactive version of the chemotherapeutic 5-FU made with a β-lactam ring that once opened by a β-lactamase converts into active 5-FU. Based on the in vitro functional data above, we chose to test the b63 complex for prodrug turnover on cells compared to the original b196 split site. Specifically, b63 showed consistently low auto-complementation activity with either Centa or ampicillin even at high 1000 nM of enzyme in the absence of target but retains high complementation-based activity. The two split enzymes were kept at a constant concentration of 500 nM and used to treat cells expressing high, medium and low levels of HER2 on the cell surface [ ] to further assess the impact of varying levels of target. After incubation of the enzyme complexes on cells for four hours, the media was removed leaving only enzyme bound to cells and new media with the prodrug was added to the cells. Monitoring the decrease in viability in cells treated with each of the split constructs, we determined that b63 maintains the essential ability to convert prodrug to drug and shows statistically equivalent efficacy over the original b196 split ( ). Interestingly, we saw similar killing on all three cell lines indicating the level of HER2 on the cell surface is likely not a limiting factor in activity for this type of therapeutic platform.

## Conclusion
In this study, we examined the structural differences in splitting the β-lactamase enzyme at different amino acid residues and determined that, while the complexation of the two constituent fragments is not significantly different for most complexes, the individual parts have variation that correlates to the expected changes in structure. The functionality of each split site in vitro was monitored to determine changes in activity and revealed that all split sites displayed active substrate turnover, but the rates differed, therefore indicating that changing the split site can be used to tune enzyme activity. It is also worthwhile noting that the differences in activity were different between the substrates, further suggesting that fragments might be engineered to increase specificity for known or new substrates. After functional testing, we determined the b63 split has the best auto-complementation to complementation ratios and maintains activity on cells. Furthermore, we found that the new split site of b63 maintains functional activity, including cell killing, and outperforms the b196 and can be dosed at lower concentrations.
