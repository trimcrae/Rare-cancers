## Abstract
The genetic instability of cancer cells leads to cellular resistance against most targeted cancer drugs. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) infiltrate all carcinomas and are genetically stable. Using antibody–drug conjugates (ADC), we exploit the unique properties of a rapidly recycling endocytic receptor, uPARAP, to achieve highly efficient CAF-mediated drug delivery and killing of carcinomas. This receptor is generally not present on carcinoma cells and is only expressed in a restricted group of mesenchymal cancer cell types, which are sensitive to uPARAP-directed ADCs. However, we show that uPARAP is highly expressed in CAFs in all carcinoma types examined. This property is recapitulated in mouse xenograft carcinoma models. In these models, despite the absence of uPARAP on the carcinoma cells, uPARAP-targeting ADCs with clinically validated payloads, monomethyl auristatin E and deruxtecan, eradicated tumors with remarkable efficiency. Systemic treatment with anti-uPARAP ADC led to the permanent eradication of tumors in mice carrying subcutaneous xenografts with human EBC-1 lung carcinoma cells. A pronounced repression of tumor growth and a strongly increased mouse survival rate were also obtained with human HT29 colon adenocarcinoma cells, both when these tumors were growing subcutaneously and after the homing of tumor cells to bone from the circulation. CAFs were largely refractory to ADC treatment and retained a high expression of uPARAP. uPARAP-expressing fibroblasts could also process an anti-uPARAP ADC in vitro and deliver the cytotoxic component to carcinoma cells. The current bystander mechanism may be exploited in the majority of the most prevalent solid cancers, thus making uPARAP an extraordinarily versatile target for ADC-based cancer treatment.

## Introduction
Antibody–drug conjugates (ADC) are a rapidly advancing treatment modality for several cancer types. These drugs are at the center of targeted treatment strategies and stand out in their ability to specifically deliver highly efficient drugs to cancer cells. Thus, 13 ADCs are currently approved by the FDA, whereas many more are in clinical and preclinical development ( ). The basic principle in this treatment concept relies on the binding of a cytotoxin-conjugated mAb to a target protein that is strongly upregulated on cancer cells. After binding and internalization of the ADC, the cytotoxin is released, resulting in the death of the target cell.
In addition to target-dependent cell kill, many cytotoxic payload molecules show a “bystander” effect, in which after release from dying cells, they can permeate into neighboring cancer cells irrespective of their expression of the ADC target ( – ). The bystander effect is an important contributor to the antitumor activity of many ADCs because the target proteins are often not uniformly expressed on all tumor cells. Nevertheless, even for bystander-competent ADCs, the substantial heterogeneity of target expression represents a major challenge.
Intriguingly, however, model studies have also shown examples in which none of the malignant cells express the ADC target, but tumor growth is still counteracted by ADC treatment ( ). This effect is caused by the presence of various stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME). It is well recognized that both immune cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) take part in an interplay with the malignant cells, supporting cancer progression. This includes extracellular matrix production and modulation, as well as the secretion of signaling factors contributing to cancer cell growth, stemness, angiogenesis, invasion, and resistance to therapy ( , ). To target the supportive role of CAFs in tumor growth, a number of potential treatment strategies have been developed, but despite considerable efforts, this approach has so far proven difficult. Thus, only limited outcomes have been obtained in early clinical trials with strategies such as blocking fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a central CAF component, or interfering with hedgehog-dependent signaling between tumor cells and CAFs ( , ). A likely complicating factor in these strategies is the existence of different subpopulations of CAFs with different functional properties ( , ).
As an alternative strategy, targeting CAFs may be desired for the eradication of these cells, CAF-mediated drug delivery, or both ( , , ). CAFs are known to express various cell surface proteins, which may be exploited as targets for ADCs or radionuclide therapy.
The collagen receptor, uPARAP (also designated Endo180 or CD280; product of the MRC2 gene), is expressed on certain tumor cells of mesenchymal origin ( , ), and ADCs against uPARAP have been shown to kill sarcoma and mesothelioma cells ( , , ).
In contrast to mesenchymal cancers, the cancer cells in mammary carcinoma and carcinomas of the head and neck have generally been found not to express uPARAP. However, in these cancers, a pronounced expression of the receptor has been observed on CAFs ( – ).
In this study, we show that uPARAP is strongly upregulated in CAFs in most carcinoma types with a desmoplastic stromal reaction. Furthermore, we demonstrate a highly effective uPARAP-dependent stromal targeting of carcinomas in mouse xenograft models. To enable this study, we employ uPARAP-directed ADCs with reactivity against both the human and the murine target protein, coupled with clinically approved cytotoxic payloads.

## Cell lines and mice
The following cell lines were purchased from the indicated cell banks or commercial suppliers with designations as specified: human osteosarcoma SaOS-2 cell line (RRID:CVCL_0548, European Collection of Animal Cell Cultures; ECACC 89050205), human squamous cell lung carcinoma EBC-1 cell line (RRID:CVCL_2891, JCRB Cell Bank; JCRB0820), human osteosarcoma 143B cell line (RRID:CVCL_2270, ATCC; CRL-8303), human colorectal adenocarcinoma HT29 cell line (RRID:CVCL_A8EZ, ATCC; HTB-38), and luciferase-transfected HT29 (Luc-HT29) cells (RRID:CVCL_5J00, Revvity; IVISbrite HT29 Red F-luc, BW124353). uPARAP-deficient 143B cells were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing as described ( , ). Primary fibroblasts were isolated from the dermis of newborn uPARAP-deficient mice and wild-type littermates as in ref. ( ). To prevent genetic drift, cell lines used for in vivo studies were cultured for less than eight sequential passages after receipt from the above-indicated provider. EBC-1 and HT29 cell lines were authenticated by genotyping (cell line authentication service from Eurofins Genomics) on April 17, 2025. Authentication of wild-type and uPARAP-deficient primary murine fibroblasts was performed by in-house genotyping ( ). SaOS-2 and 143B cell lines were used exclusively in vitro for demonstration of antibody specificity and/or as positive controls for ADC sensitivity. These cell lines were not authenticated by genotyping. For these cells, only low-passage cells were used, and the morphology and growth pattern of the cell lines were monitored and confirmed to be in accordance with the information supplied by the cell bank. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma (latest test was on April 10, 2025, qPCR-based test by Eurofins Genomics). Cells were tested for pathogens by IMPACT (human and murine), including mycoplasma (IDEXX BioAnalytics) shortly before inoculation of the mice.
Female CB17-SCID (CB-17/lcr-PrkdcSCID/SCID/Rj, RRID:IMSR_RJ:CB17-SCID) mice for studies with subcutaneous tumor models and female NMRI nude (Rj:NMRI-Fox1nu/nu, RRID:IMSR_RJ:NMRI-NUDE) mice for studies with intra-caudal artery (CA) injection of tumor cells were obtained from Janvier Labs.

## Histologic analyses
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections of paired carcinoma and normal tissue were purchased from TissueArray.com. These sections included pulmonary small cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma tissue (code nos. NCT076 and NCT086, respectively) and colon adenocarcinoma tissue (code no. NCT061). Six additional archival FFPE specimens containing lung squamous cell carcinoma tissue were obtained from surgical pulmonary lobectomies performed at the Department of Thoracic Surgery and analyzed at the Department of Pathology, Copenhagen University Hospital, Denmark, from 2022 to 2024. Human tumor tissue microarrays (TMA) were purchased from TissueArray.com and included two multiple-organ cancer TMAs, one of which contained specimens from adenocarcinomas of the breast, colon, pancreas, and prostate as well as lung squamous cell carcinomas (38 specimens for each cancer type, code no. BC000119b), whereas the other contained specimens from oro-pharyngo-laryngeal squamous cell carcinomas and adenocarcinomas of the stomach, ovary, and uterus as well as hepatocellular carcinomas (38 specimens for each cancer type, code no. BC000120b), a pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma TMA comprising 18 cases of patient-matched tumor tissue, nontumorous tissue and cancer-adjacent tissue (code no. BC04022a), a colon cancer TMA with 18 cases of patient-matched adenocarcinoma tissue, nontumorous tissue and cancer-adjacent tissue samples (code no. BC05023a), a colon primary cancer and metastatic cancer TMA comprising 30 cases of primary adenocarcinoma, 30 metastatic adenocarcinomas (four cases matched with primary adenocarcinoma), and 3 normal tissue samples each from the colon, liver, and lymph nodes (code no. CO702d).
Fixation, processing, and sectioning of harvested organs from mice were done as described in ( ), except that bones were incubated for 48 hours in 4% paraformaldehyde prior to decalcification and that xylene was replaced by Tissue-Clear.
Histologic analysis was performed on 3.5 µm FFPE tissue sections, which were stained with hematoxylin and eosin on the Gemini AS Automated Slide Stainer (Epredia). When immunostainings for uPARAP and human-specific lamin B1 were performed in the same specimens, parallel sections were used. For all stainings, soft tissue samples were heated for 60 minutes, and bone samples were heated for 120 minutes at 60°C prior to deparaffinization and rehydration on Gemini AS or BOND RX for manual or automatic staining, respectively.
Murine tissue samples were stained manually, as described in ( ) with a few adjustments: The anti-uPARAP antibody, R-2h9 (described in reagents), was applied at a concentration of 10 µg/mL and detected with EnVision+ HRP anti-rabbit secondary antibody. For the human-specific lamin B1 staining, antigen retrieval was performed by heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) with citrate buffer (10 mmol/L sodium citrate, pH 6.0) at 98°C for 15 minutes. The human-specific lamin B1 antibody was applied at a concentration of 24 µg/mL and detected with Rat IgG VisUCyte HRP polymer antibody. For both stainings, the sections were finally incubated for 5 minutes with DAB+ and were dehydrated on Gemini AS.
Human tissue samples were stained manually for FAP using the same procedure as above, except that antigen retrieval by HIER with citrate buffer was done at 98°C for 10 minutes. The rabbit anti-FAP mAb was applied at a concentration of 1.9 µg/mL and detected with the EnVision + HRP anti-rabbit secondary antibody. For uPARAP, the human tissue samples were stained on the BOND RX autostainer (Leica Biosystems) utilizing the BOND Polymer Refine Detection Kit and the following protocol: Bake and Dewax: Bake for 30 minutes at 60°C, incubate with 150 µL dewax solution for 30 seconds, wash twice with 150 µL Dewax solution, wash three times with 150 µL 99% ethanol, and wash three times with 150 µL wash solution. Antigen retrieval by HIER: Wash with 150 µL BOND Epitope Retrieval Solution 2 buffer and incubate with 150 µL BOND Epitope Retrieval Solution 2 buffer for 20 minutes at 100°C, wash with 150 µL BOND Epitope Retrieval Solution 2 buffer, wash four times with 150 µL wash solution, and incubate for 3 minutes with 150 µL wash solution. Staining: Incubate in 150 µL peroxide block for 5 minutes, wash twice in wash solution, incubate with MRC2 mouse mAb (25 ng/mL, OTI9G4) for 15 minutes, wash three times with wash solution, incubate with 150 µL post primary (rabbit anti-mouse IgG) for 8 minutes, wash three times for 2 minutes in wash solution, incubate for 8 minutes with 150 µL polymer (anti-rabbit Poly-HRP-IgG), wash twice for 2 minutes with 150 µL wash solution, rinse with 150 µL deionized water, incubate with 150 µL mixed DAB Refine for 10 minutes, rinse three times in 150 µL deionized water, incubate with 150 µL hematoxylin for 5 minutes, and rinse with 150 µL deionized water, then 150 µL wash solution, and finally 150 µL deionized water.
All sections were scanned and examined as described in ref. ( ).

## Western blotting of whole cell lysates
Preparation of whole cell lysates and Western blotting procedures for the detection of uPARAP expression were conducted as described ( ), except that the primary antibody was detected with an IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse secondary antibody diluted 1:6,000 in PBS with 0.1% Tween20, and the membrane was scanned on the Odyssey Imager (LI-COR Biosciences).

## Flow cytometry
For evaluation of uPARAP expression in human cancer cell lines, the cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in a 24-well plate format in normal cell culture medium. After overnight attachment, the cells were incubated in normal cell culture medium with 2 µg/mL of AF647-conjugated anti-uPARAP mAb 2h9 or negative control mouse IgG for 4 hours at 37°C. The cells were detached using trypsin-EDTA and washed once in PBS, after which cell suspensions were analyzed on a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were processed using FlowJo software (RRID:SCR_008520).
Analysis of the cellular composition and uptake of uPARAP antibody in subcutaneous mouse tumors, adjacent normal skin, liver, lung, colon, kidney, and heart was performed by flow cytometry of single-cell suspensions. For analysis of uPARAP expression and uptake of uPARAP-directed antibody in these tissues, mice received AF647-conjugated anti-uPARAP mAb 2h9 or mouse negative control IgG in a dose of 2 mg/kg by tail i.v. injection. Twenty-four hours after injection, the mice were anesthetized with Zoletil, and intracardiac perfusion was performed with 20 mL PBS prior to the excision of the tissues. After excision, the tissues were disaggregated into single-cell suspensions by mechanical and enzymatic digestion as in ref. ( ). Single-cell suspensions were subsequently subjected to the following steps: (i) lysis of red blood cells using red blood cell lysis buffer at room temperature for 10 minutes; (ii) staining of cells with Zombie Violet live/dead marker (1:200 in PBS) for 10 minutes at room temperature; (iii) Fc receptor blocking using a mixture of mouse Fc-block solution (1:50, v/v), human Fc block solution (1:50, v/v), and normal murine serum (1:10, v/v) diluted in PBS with 1% BSA (w/w) for 10 minutes on ice; (iv) incubation for 1 hour on ice with anti-CD45 (Brilliant Violet 605, 1:100), anti-FAP (biotin, 1:50), anti-human CD29 (Percp-Cy5.5, 1:100, tumors), anti-human HLA (PE, 1:100, tumors), anti-mouse CD31 (Percp-Cy5.5, 1:100, normal tissues), and anti-mouse EpCAM (APC-Cy7, 1:100, normal tissues) in the Fc-blocking buffer; and (v) incubation for 20 minutes on ice with streptavidin-APC-Cy7 (1:100, tumors) or streptavidin-PE (1:100, normal tissues). Following each of these steps, cells were washed once using PBS (before live/dead stain) or PBS containing 1% BSA (in steps after live/dead stain). The cell suspensions were analyzed on a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were processed using FlowJo software (RRID:SCR_008520).

## Construction of ADCs
ADCs with mAbs 9b7 and aTNP were produced by coupling maleimide-derivatized linker-payload MC-VC-PAB-MMAE to partially reduced IgG, according to ref. ( ) with minor modifications. Briefly, purified IgG (3 mg/mL in 50 mmol/L NaCl, 50 mmol/L NaBO4, pH 8.0) was subjected to mild reduction by the addition of 2.5 mmol/L dithiothreitol and incubated for 30 minutes at 25°C. This was followed by two rounds of buffer exchange to PBS with 1 mmol/L EDTA using Zeba spin desalting columns. A 5-fold molar excess of MC-VC-PAB-MMAE was added from a freshly prepared 2.7 mmol/L stock solution [dissolved in 80% PG and 20% DMF (v/v)]. The contents of PG and DMF were adjusted to final concentrations of 20% and 5% (v/v), respectively. After 2 hours at 25°C, uncoupled toxin was removed, and the resulting ADCs were buffer-exchanged to PBS, pH 7.4, using Zeba spin desalting columns. The products were characterized by ultraviolet-visible absorption spectroscopy. The DAR of each ADC was calculated based on measured absorption values and known extinction coefficients at λ = 248 nm (Amax of MMAE) and λ = 280 nm, respectively [see ref. ( )]. The average DAR values obtained were 4.1 and 4.3 for 9b7-MMAE and aTNP-MMAE, respectively. Electrophoretic characterization, ultraviolet-visible spectra, and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) analysis of these ADCs are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

## Electrophoretic analysis of ADCs
Samples of unconjugated mAbs and their corresponding ADCs were treated with 40 mmol/L dithiothreitol in 1× NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (cat. no. NP008, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 100°C for 5 minutes for the reduction of disulfides. Samples of 1 µg (MMAE-based ADCs) or 2.5 µg (Dxd-based ADCs) of protein were then loaded and separated by SDS-PAGE on NuPAGE BOLT 4 to 12 Bis-Tris Plus gels (cat. no. NW04125BOX, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.

## SEC analysis of ADCs
Analytic SEC was performed using an ÄKTA pure 25 L1 system (GE HealthCare, cat. no. 29-0182-25, RRID:SCR_023461) equipped with a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE HealthCare, cat. no. 28990944). Prior to analysis, ADCs were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 minutes. Twenty micrograms of sample was injected per run, using manual sample injection. PBS was used as the mobile phase, and the analysis was carried out at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/minute. Protein detection was monitored by UV absorbance at 280 nm. Data were processed using UNICORN 7 software.

## Cell survival assays in vitro
Assays for cell survival in the presence of free MMAE toxin, ADCs, and control reagents in vitro were performed as described ( , ) with minor modifications. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 4,000 cells per well in tissue culture–treated 96-well plates (Corning) and allowed to attach for 24 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. The medium was replaced with 100 µL of complete growth medium with toxin or ADC, added as a dilution series spanning eight wells with triplicates of each condition. Wells with DMSO/untreated control cells were also included. After incubation for 72 hours, the cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay reagent (MTS), adding 15 µL of MTS reagent to each well and incubating for 1 hour at 37°C with 5% CO2. Absorbance was then measured at 490 and 650 nm (for background subtraction) on a SpectraMax Plus plate reader (Molecular Devices). The relative cell viability at different free toxin/ADC concentrations was calculated as the ratio of the absorbance of the treated cells to that of the DMSO/untreated control cells.

## Transwell coculture assay
For analysis of a fibroblast-mediated bystander effect in vitro, cultures with either uPARAP-positive or uPARAP-deficient murine fibroblasts and EBC-1 cells were established in clear six-well plates with 0.4 µm pore polyester membrane Transwell inserts (cat. no. 3450, Corning). EBC-1 cells were seeded in the bottom chamber at a density of 1.2 × 105 cells per mL in 2.5 mL of complete growth medium. Fibroblasts were seeded in the Transwell inserts at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL in 1.5 mL of complete growth medium. The cells were allowed to attach for 24 hours, after which the medium was replaced with complete growth medium containing ADCs or buffer alone. Following incubation for 96 hours, the cell viability in each compartment was assessed by MTS assay and absorbance readings as above.

## Animal experiments
Treatment experiments with subcutaneous tumor models were conducted in 6- to 8-week-old female CB-17 SCID mice. Studies without ADC treatment or with treatment using MMAE-containing ADCs were performed at the animal facility of the host institution (University of Copenhagen), whereas studies with Dxd-based ADCs were performed by the contractor company Minerva Imaging ApS (Ølstykke, Denmark). Cells [1 ×106 (EBC-1) or 1.5 × 106 (HT29)] in 100 µL of injection medium [a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of sterile PBS and Cultrex UltiMatrix Reduced Growth Factor Basement Membrane Extract] were injected subcutaneously into the upper right flanks. Mice were included and randomized into treatment groups once the tumor volume had reached 80 to 150 mm3 (for treatment studies with MMAE-based ADCs) or 90 to 260 mm3 (for Dxd-based ADCs). Treatment reagents were administered by tail i.v. injections, with four doses evenly distributed over 2 weeks. Concentrations of ADCs or mAbs were adjusted using sterile PBS and a dosing volume of 5 mL/kg. Tumor length and width were recorded three times a week using an electronic caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: V = (L*W2)/2. Body weight was recorded three times a week (Supplementary Fig. S2), and the mice were monitored for signs of distress or abnormal behavior. Predetermined humane endpoints for euthanasia of the mice were tumor size exceeding 1,000 mm3 or 12 mm in length or >10% weight loss (for treatment studies with MMAE-based ADCs) or tumor size exceeding 1,500 mm3 or >20% weight loss (for treatment studies with Dxd-based ADCs).
Studies with intra-CA injection of Luc-HT29 cells were conducted in 6- to 8-week-old female NMRI nude mice. Cancer cells were injected as described in ref. ( ). Briefly, 1 × 106 cells in 100 µL sterile PBS supplemented with 3% FBS were injected into the CA. Tumor growth was followed by In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS)-based imaging as described in ref. ( ). Mice with bone signals above 1 × 106 p/second (total flux) were included and randomized into treatment groups. Treatment reagents were administered as described above, every 3 days for a total of three doses. Predetermined humane endpoints for euthanasia were a bone signal (single leg) above 1 × 109 p/second (total flux) or >10% weight loss.
In all studies, mice were euthanized following Zoletil anesthesia. At termination, intracardiac perfusion with 10 to 20 mL PBS was performed, and tumors and specified organs were harvested and prepared for histologic analysis (see the “IHC” section) or flow cytometry (see the “Flow cytometry” section).

## Statistics
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 for Windows PC (RRID:SCR_002798). For single comparisons, an unpaired t test was performed. Multiple comparisons were performed with one-way ANOVA (Tukey correction for multiple comparisons). The Mantel–Cox log-rank test was used for comparison of Kaplan–Meier survival curves with post hoc Bonferroni corrections.

## Results

## Ubiquitous expression of uPARAP on CAFs in carcinomas
To study the distribution of uPARAP-expressing cells in various carcinoma types, we examined several sections from resected tumors as well as TMAs by IHC. These specimens covered examples from the colon, lung, breast, prostate, pancreas, stomach, liver, ovary, uterus, and larynx, thus representing the majority of the more prevalent types of carcinoma ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Table S2). The carcinoma cells were mostly negative for uPARAP-specific staining or stained very weakly. In contrast, positive staining for uPARAP was observed in tumor-infiltrating stromal cells with fibroblastic morphology. These cells also showed positive staining for the CAF marker FAP ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4). A positive staining for uPARAP on CAFs was observed in more than 90% of cases, except for hepatocellular carcinoma, in which approximately two thirds of cases stained positive (Supplementary Table S2). This was very similar to the staining pattern observed for FAP (Supplementary Table S3).
 

## Mouse xenograft models with uPARAP-negative carcinoma cells
The pronounced expression of uPARAP on CAFs opened the possibility that this receptor could be utilized for stromal targeting, using an ADC directed against it. To enable a study of stromal targeting exclusively, we established mouse xenograft models with uPARAP-negative cancer cells, thus excluding a direct effect of an anti-uPARAP ADC against these cells. We identified a human squamous cell lung carcinoma cell line, EBC-1, and a human colon carcinoma cell line, HT29, with undetectable levels of uPARAP ( and ). Both cell types were capable of growth as subcutaneous tumors in immunocompromised mice, as also shown by others (Supplementary Fig. S5; refs. , ). Examination of the xenograft tumors by IHC revealed that (i) the cancer cells were negative for uPARAP expression, even when grown in vivo, and (ii) a pronounced infiltration of fibroblast-like cells occurred, with strong expression of uPARAP ( ).
 To learn more about these cell populations and their capacity for uptake of a uPARAP-directed antibody, we injected tumor-bearing mice intravenously with a fluorescent anti-uPARAP mAb and subsequently analyzed the cellular composition of the tumors by flow cytometry ( ). Cells of human origin (cancer cells) and the murine stromal cells could readily be distinguished using species-specific markers (cancer cells being positive for hCD29 and hHLA). We focused on the nonleukocyte cell population and found that in both carcinoma models, the cancer cell and stromal cell fractions of the tumors were of roughly equal size (40–60% of the nonleukocyte cells). When gating on the stromal cells only, more than 80% were found to be positive for both FAP and the uptake of the anti-uPARAP mAb. Notably, when comparing the uPARAP fluorescence signal in the tumor stromal cells with that of various normal tissues, the signal was considerably lower in the latter, in which a pronounced antibody uptake was observed only in the liver [ (left)]. This signal in the liver was not due to a uPARAP-mediated effect, as the same uptake level was observed for a control antibody [ (right)], reflecting the general mechanism of liver-mediated clearance of antibodies.
Altogether, these models recapitulated essential properties of human carcinomas with negative expression of uPARAP on cancer cells and infiltration of strongly uPARAP-positive CAFs.

## Stromal targeting of carcinomas with a uPARAP-directed ADC
For targeting experiments, we utilized a uPARAP-specific antibody, mAb 9b7 ( ), which is cross-reactive against both human and murine uPARAP (Supplementary Fig. S6). This antibody was coupled to the cytotoxin MMAE via a cathepsin-cleavable linker ( ). As a control ADC, we prepared an equivalent cytotoxin conjugate with the nontargeted antibody, aTNP ( ). Analyses of the resulting products, 9b7-MMAE (uPARAP-targeted ADC) and aTNP-MMAE (nontargeted control ADC), revealed that they were electrophoretically indistinguishable (Supplementary Fig. S1A), and both had an average DAR between 4.0 and 4.5.
As expected, EBC-1 and HT29 cells were largely insensitive to the uPARAP-directed ADC when cultured in vitro, showing very little concentration dependence in cell survival assays and with sensitivity curves similar to those found with the control ADC [ (left and center)]. We ascertained that this was not due to a lack of specific efficiency of the targeted ADC as the uPARAP-positive osteosarcoma cell line, SaOS-2, was sensitive to very low concentrations of 9b7-MMAE [ (right)]. The lack of sensitivity of the two carcinoma cell lines toward 9b7-MMAE was also not due to drug resistance mechanisms as they were both killed at very low concentrations of the free cytotoxin ( ).
 The ADCs were then used for therapy studies in vivo, performed by systemic treatment of tumor-bearing mice. In these studies, treatment with the uPARAP-targeted ADC proved strikingly effective. In mice with EBC-1 tumors, treatment with four doses of 6 mg/kg not only led to complete eradication of all tumors but also to a permanent cure of all mice, with no tumor recurrence detected in a follow-up period of 100 days ( ) and without affecting the weight of the mice (Supplementary Fig. S2). Doses of only 3 mg/kg also led to the disappearance of palpable tumors in all mice and a marked increase in median overall survival compared with the nontargeted control ADC (101 vs. 22 days after the first treatment). With this dose, three out of eight mice were cured, whereas the remainder experienced tumor regrowth after a highly variable latency period (10–90 days; ).
No effect was obtained after treatment with uncoupled mAb 9b7 ( ), thus excluding that a mere blocking of receptor function was responsible for the effect obtained with the targeted ADC. The control ADC did show some antitumor activity ( and ) although this unspecific effect was only partial and temporary. This observation is in accordance with general findings in mouse treatment experiments with ADCs, reported by others ( , ); see “Discussion”. As would be expected, the effect of the control ADC was most pronounced at the higher dosage ( ).
A similar pattern emerged for mice carrying HT29 tumors, with complete disappearance of palpable tumors in all mice upon treatment with the uPARAP-targeted ADC ( ). However, although this treatment was tested only with the higher ADC dose (6 mg/kg) and did result in markedly prolonged survival, the eradication of tumors was, in this case, not complete as regrowth was ultimately observed in all mice.

## Tumor eradication is due to a CAF-mediated bystander effect
The observed counteraction of tumor growth could either reflect an eradication of stromal cells with essential roles, or the stromal cells could serve to liberate the free cytotoxin in the TME, resulting in the killing of cancer cells (bystander effect; ref. ). To learn about these possibilities, we focused on the EBC-1 model as this model showed the strongest response to treatment.
Mice with EBC-1 tumors were treated with two doses of 3 mg/kg of 9b7-MMAE or aTNP-MMAE, after which tumors were collected during the regression phase. A histologic examination confirmed an extensive eradication of tumor cells in mice treated with the uPARAP-targeted ADC. At treatment initiation, the uPARAP-negative tumor cells were abundant and were found in between uPARAP-positive stromal cells in a dense network [ (left)]. Treatment with 9b7-MMAE led to a strong reduction of tumor cells, whereas fibroblast-like cells were still abundant and still stained positive for uPARAP [ (right)]. In accordance with the results above, a partial antitumor effect was also found after treatment with aTNP-MMAE [ (middle)].
 Flow cytometry analysis of the cellular composition of the regressing tumors confirmed these observations ( and ). In mice treated with 9b7-MMAE, the cancer cell population was greatly reduced (2.5% of all cells vs. 10.5% in aTNP-MMAE–treated mice and 17% in untreated mice), whereas FAP-positive fibroblasts remained abundant. Strikingly, uPARAP expression by these cells was not affected by treatment with 9b7-MMAE.
Together, these observations point to a bystander effect in which uPARAP-positive fibroblasts remain unaffected by the treatment and mediate the release of active cytotoxin, resulting in the eradication of the cancer cells. To further investigate this proposed mechanism, we employed an in vitro setup to evaluate the efficiency of uPARAP-expressing fibroblasts in drug delivery to cancer cells after exposure to a uPARAP-targeted ADC.
Fibroblasts isolated from newborn wild-type mice express uPARAP and efficiently internalize uPARAP-directed antibodies, whereas this ability is absent in fibroblasts from littermate uPARAP-deficient mice ( – ). In a comparative setup, strictly parallel cultures of uPARAP-expressing and uPARAP-deficient fibroblasts were grown in the upper chambers of Transwell units, in which EBC-1 cells were grown in the lower chambers [ (sketch insert)]. In addition, monocultures with EBC-1 cells were included to evaluate the direct effect of the ADCs on cell viability. The mono- and cocultures were incubated with uPARAP-targeting or nontargeted control ADCs or with buffer alone, after which the viability of the cancer cells and fibroblasts was determined.
In the absence of fibroblasts, an approximately 25% loss of EBC-1 cells was noted upon ADC treatment for 96 hours [ (middle)]. This effect was identical when comparing the uPARAP-targeted and the control ADC and was in good accordance with the unspecific effect observed at the same ADC concentration in the other in vitro setting used above (40 nmol/L; ), considering the prolonged duration of cell culture. When wild-type fibroblasts were present, treatment with the uPARAP-targeted ADC led to a strong increase in the killing of EBC-1 cells [∼60% loss; (left)]. Importantly, this was a uPARAP-specific event. In cocultures with uPARAP-deficient fibroblasts or in units with wild-type fibroblasts treated with the control ADC, the survival of EBC-1 cells was identical or very close to that observed in the absence of fibroblasts.
Thus, in this bystander-mimicking assay, the uPARAP-expressing fibroblasts uniquely facilitated the eradication of the uPARAP-negative cancer cells after the addition of the uPARAP-targeted ADC. Importantly, the fibroblasts themselves were not significantly affected by treatment with either of these ADCs [ (right)].

## Colon carcinoma cells are eradicated by a uPARAP-directed ADC after dissemination to bone
Colon adenocarcinomas can metastasize to the bone compartment, and bone metastases are associated with a poor prognosis ( ). Unlike most other cell types in the normal body, certain bone-residing cells of the osteoblastic lineage, including bone lining cells and reversal cells, have a pronounced expression of uPARAP ( ). Furthermore, the latter cell type is typically found on eroded bone surfaces ( ), and uPARAP-positive cells of unidentified lineage have been observed in the stroma of bone metastases originating from uPARAP-negative mammary carcinomas ( ). Therefore, we considered that carcinoma metastases in bone might be sensitive to uPARAP-dependent drug delivery from neighboring bone cells.
In this context, an experimental technique has been developed recently to allow tumor cells to home to bone from the circulation. This method depends on the injection of tumor cells into the CA in mice and leads to bone-resident tumors in the hind limbs, providing a model with experimental bone metastases ( ). To investigate whether this system could be employed for treatment studies with a uPARAP-directed ADC, we set up a study with intra-CA injection of Luc-HT29 cells in mice, followed by the recording of tumor growth using IVIS-based monitoring of luciferase activity (see “Materials and Methods”).
After injection of 1 × 106 Luc-HT29 cells, bone-resident tumors could be detected by IVIS measurement as early as 2 days after injection, after which consistent tumor growth could be observed by the continuous increase in the IVIS signal (Supplementary Fig. S7). Examination of the affected bones (femurs and tibiae) after 14 to 16 days showed clearly recognizable bone tumors after hematoxylin and eosin staining [ (first panel)]. These tumor cells were indeed the human carcinoma cells as they stained positive with a species-specific antibody against human lamin-B1 [ (second panel)]. When the same specimens were immunostained for uPARAP expression, the tumor cells were negative as expected, whereas an almost contiguous layer of uPARAP-positive host cells was found contacting the tumor islands [ (third and fourth panel)].
 We then set up a treatment study with bone tumor–bearing mice. After the emergence of established bone tumors (day 6 after tumor cell injection), mice received a total of three treatment doses, given at 3-day intervals. Treatment was performed with PBS (vehicle) or 6 mg/kg of aTNP-MMAE, uncoupled mAb 9b7, or 9b7-MMAE ( and ).
Although the tumor signals in mice treated with PBS or unconjugated mAb 9b7 were indistinguishable and grew rapidly, the tumors treated with 9b7-MMAE showed pronounced regression. This regression continued until day 22 when all mice in the two aforementioned groups had been terminated due to tumor growth (see “Materials and Methods” for termination criteria). After this point, some tumor regrowth could be detected in about half of the 9b7-MMAE–treated mice. The other half retained a very low, stable signal level until about day 30 when regrowth was noted in most or all mice ( ). This effect of 9b7-MMAE was highly target-specific. Thus, although the tumors in mice treated with the control ADC also showed some delay in tumor growth, all mice in this group had rapidly growing tumors after day 15. From day 12 until the termination of the control ADC-treated group at day 30, tumors in this group displayed an IVIS signal 100- to 150-fold higher than that in the group treated with 9b7-MMAE. Correspondingly, the 9b7-MMAE–treated group had a significant, 2-fold extended median survival compared with the group treated with the control ADC.
Although this system was set up for studies on cancer cells homing to bone ( ), 24 out of 34 mice also had tumor foci at a different position, with the IVIS signal suggesting tumors in the thoracic region [ (left)]. Monitoring of the IVIS signal revealed that these tumors only grew slowly, even in PBS-treated mice. However, although this situation was similar in mice treated with unconjugated mAb 9b7 or control ADC, treatment with the anti-uPARAP ADC led to consistent tumor regression [ (middle and right)]. After regression, these tumors remained with an IVIS signal close to the detection limit until day 35, after which regrowth was noted in all mice.
In these experiments, the time of termination was dictated by the progress of the bone tumors in the same animals, which precluded a prolonged study of tumor growth in the thoracic region. A histologic examination of the lungs of PBS-treated mice at termination did not allow for the identification of well-defined lung metastases, suggesting that either the tumor foci responsible for the IVIS signals were very small or that the tumors were not localized in the lung. A single small tumor was observed, associated with muscular tissue in the thorax in one mouse (Supplementary Fig. S8). Nevertheless, the results above clearly showed an effect of the anti-uPARAP ADC against tumor foci with carcinoma cells seeded from the circulation, also at locations distinct from the bone compartment.

## The bystander effect of uPARAP-directed ADC is recapitulated with different ADC formats
The experiments so far were performed with a single uPARAP-targeting antibody, mAb 9b7, and using a single payload, MMAE, which is the cytotoxin employed in a major part of the clinically approved ADCs ( ). Therefore, in a final experiment, we investigated whether the successful bystander effect obtained above was a specific property of this particular ADC or rather a more general property of ADCs directed against uPARAP. Recently, very promising outcomes have been achieved with various ADCs utilizing a different class of payloads, topoisomerase inhibitors such as Dxd. Due to their relatively high tolerability in human patients, ADCs belonging to this class are typically constructed with high DAR values (often DAR 8) and can be used at doses higher than those tolerated for MMAE-based ADCs (typically at least 3-fold higher; ref. ).
Consequently, we investigated a uPARAP-targeted ADC with Dxd as the cytotoxin component. This ADC was based on another anti-uPARAP mAb, 2h9, which, just like 9b7, cross-reacts with murine and human uPARAP (Supplementary Fig. S6) and has been studied previously in an ADC format with MMAE, in which it proved efficient against uPARAP-positive tumor cells ( ). This new ADC, 2h9-Dxd, was created along with a Dxd-coupled nontargeted control antibody (anti-HyHEL-10–Dxd), both with a DAR of 8 (see “Materials and Methods”; Supplementary Fig. S1). These ADCs were tested in the EBC-1 xenograft model.
As in the equivalent experiment with 9b7-MMAE ( ), treatment with four injections of 2h9-Dxd led to tumor regression with the disappearance of all palpable tumors and no sign of regrowth until day 28, at which point the tumors in most of the mice in the vehicle-treated group had reached the termination endpoint ( ). After this, tumor regrowth was observed in the 2h9-Dxd–treated group at variable rates, reaching the termination endpoint between day 55 and 80. Treatment with the control ADC also had some effect, leading to apparent tumor stasis for 10 to 15 days, after which tumor regrowth occurred. All mice in this group reached the endpoint before day 48.
 Thus, a uPARAP-targeting ADC carrying a Dxd payload was capable of eradicating tumors derived from uPARAP-negative cancer cells in vivo, very similar to the MMAE-based ADC.

## Discussion
The concept of targeting nonmalignant stromal cells in malignant tumors is becoming increasingly recognized in the development of precision medicine. In addition to the fact that these cells play crucial roles in tumor growth and metastasis ( , , , ), they also present physical barriers against other means of treatment and have specific cellular properties that make them attractive treatment targets. Most importantly, as opposed to cancer cells, stromal cells such as CAFs do not possess an inherent mutation tendency, which makes them much less susceptible to the development of resistance to targeted treatments. Furthermore, although the low proliferation rate of CAFs makes them quite resistant to most anticancer drugs, this property is actually beneficial in a strategy in which live CAFs are utilized for a bystander effect with the delivery of drugs to neighboring cancer cells.
In this work, we exploit a unique set of properties of the collagen receptor uPARAP, which makes this protein an ideal target for stromal drug delivery to carcinoma cells in the TME. First, we show that uPARAP is strongly expressed on the CAFs of most or all major types of carcinomas. Second, the cellular function of this receptor is to internalize its extracellular ligands and direct them to lysosomal degradation ( , , ). This routing enables the efficient lysosomal release of the cytotoxic component of an ADC ( ). Third, uPARAP is a rapidly recycling endocytic receptor, trafficking from the cell surface to the endosomal compartment and back with a cycling time of only a few minutes ( – ), thus enabling a highly efficient import of an ADC and potential reuse of the receptor molecule.
Our results strongly suggest that these properties of uPARAP are a major factor in the tumor targeting obtained in this work and that the primary underlying mechanism is indeed a CAF-mediated release of cytotoxin to neighboring carcinoma cells. In our xenograft studies, we found that ADC-treated tumors in the regression phase had a strong decrease in the content of the target-negative cancer cells, whereas the content of the target-positive stromal cells seemed to be unchanged ( and ). In the same manner, the in vitro system with fibroblast-mediated ADC processing showed that this processing ability was confined to target-positive fibroblasts treated with the targeted ADC ( ).
Treatment with the uPARAP-targeted ADC was remarkably efficient in vivo. In our studies with uPARAP-negative EBC-1 tumors, this treatment led to a permanent cure of three out of eight tumor-bearing mice when using doses of 3 mg/kg. Using escalated doses (6 mg/kg), all mice carrying these tumors were cured. Although a regular toxicology study should be done in different species, we noted that treatment had no effect on the weight of the mice, even when using the escalated dose. Notably, the stromal targeting activity was not limited to subcutaneous tumors as a strong antitumor effect was also obtained in tumors established in bone from the circulation, as well as in smaller tumor foci in the thoracic region ( ).
Our observations are in line with an increasing number of observations supporting the importance of bystander effects in ADC-based treatment of solid tumors ( ), including studies with CAF-mediated targeting ( ). A common observation in these and our studies is the relatively strong resistance of CAFs toward the ADCs used.
A few studies have been published in which MMAE-based ADCs against other CAF-expressed membrane proteins have been used in connection with stromal targeting in mouse xenograft tumor models. Notably, these include ADCs against leucine-rich repeat containing 15 (LRRC15; ref. ) and tumor endothelial marker 8 ( ). However, neither of these targets is known to be constitutively recycling endocytic receptors. Although encouraging results have been obtained in these studies, a permanent eradication of tumors has either required very high doses of the ADCs employed ( ) or has only been possible in combination with other means of treatment ( ). Similarly, very high doses were needed to obtain efficient stromal targeting with an ADC against FAP, using a different cytotoxic component [the tubulysin TAM470 ( )]. The results of a phase I study with the LRRC15-directed ADC have been published, including the notion of preliminary antitumor activity in patients with osteosarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma ( ). However, the stromal targeting element in this treatment cannot be interpreted in isolation because, in these cancers, LRRC15 is expressed both on the cancer cells and on stromal cells.
In addition to the targeting of cell surface proteins mediating ADC internalization, the concept of a stromal bystander mechanism has also been supported by an alternative strategy, in which the ADC is directed toward noninternalizing antigens and depends on extracellular cleavage of the ADC linker [reviewed in ref. ( )]. As an example, an ADC against the extra domain B splice variant of fibronectin was constructed to enable extracellular ADC binding and toxin release in the TME. This ADC mediated an efficient bystander kill in mouse models ( ) although a direct comparison with our study is not possible due to the use of different cytotoxic payloads.
The effect achieved with the uPARAP-directed ADCs in our study was highly target-specific. In all in vivo experiments, the antitumor effect was much stronger than that obtained with the nontargeted ADCs. However, treatment with the nontargeted ADCs did lead to measurable effects. This observation is in accordance with the results obtained in several other mouse tumor model studies with ADCs, including ADCs with MMAE or Dxd payloads ( , ). The background for this phenomenon probably includes several factors. One of these is likely to be the enhanced permeability and retention effect in tumors, whereas another factor may be the incomplete serum stability of the ADC linkers employed. In this connection, it is well established that unspecific linker cleavage takes place to a higher degree in murine than in human plasma ( ). In the same manner, the weak target-independent cytotoxicity observed in vitro when using high concentrations of the ADCs may also reflect low amounts of cytotoxin released through extracellular linker cleavage during prolonged cell culture ( ).
The antitumor activity of the uPARAP-targeted ADCs was strictly dependent on the presence of a coupled cytotoxin. Although uPARAP does play a role in cancer invasion ( , , ), a mere blocking of receptor function was not an important element in the antitumor effect of our ADCs at the doses used. Thus, both in subcutaneous tumors and in tumors established in the bone, treatment with uncoupled mAb against uPARAP had no effect on tumor growth ( and ). In this connection, it may be noted that inhibition of tumor-mediated bone destruction has been achieved previously in a mouse bone tumor model by antibody-mediated downregulation of uPARAP ( ). However, this effect was limited to the bone degradation process, whereas tumor growth was not affected.
Although a cytotoxic payload is essential for this type of stromal targeting, we demonstrate that this can be achieved with at least two of the currently most important payload classes, that is, tubulin-disrupting agents and topoisomerase inhibitors. MMAE, a tubulin inhibitor, is one of the most widely used payloads in the clinically approved ADCs to date, whereas the number of ADCs based on the topoisomerase inhibitor Dxd is rapidly expanding ( ).
In previous studies with ADCs, direct targeting of uPARAP has been accomplished in model systems with less prevalent cancer types such as sarcomas and mesothelioma, in which the receptor is expressed on the cancer cells themselves ( , , ). In contrast, the presence of infiltrating CAFs with prominent uPARAP expression seems to be a property shared by most or all types of carcinoma ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Table S2). In this article, we have demonstrated uPARAP-mediated drug delivery in two mouse tumor models in which the cancer cells are uPARAP-negative to document the stromal targeting effect in isolation. Although it would be interesting to study this effect in a broader range of tumor models, including syngeneic tumors, it should be noted that the identification of suitable tumor models is not trivial. Thus, our pilot investigations suggested that relatively few cancer cell lines combine the necessary properties of sensitivity to the free cytotoxic payload, lack of uPARAP expression, and capability for growth in vivo.
Nevertheless, it seems likely that the CAFs in most or all carcinomas will possess a capacity for specific internalization and processing of a uPARAP-directed ADC. Our studies with EBC-1 and HT29 cells suggest that differences in targeting efficiency exist between different models ( ). Although the same is likely to be the case in human cancer types, the current targeting principle may open a new opportunity for the treatment of several very frequently diagnosed cancer types. If so, stromal drug delivery with uPARAP-targeting ADCs will offer a very important extension of the potential indications for treatment.
Altogether, this may point to uPARAP as an extraordinarily versatile target for ADC-based cancer therapy.
