## Abstract
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignant cancers and its incidence is steadily rising particularly in young patients. While screening measures and the widespread availability of surgical treatment have led to an impressive improvement of prognosis within the overall CRC population, patients with metastatic CRC still face 5-year survival rates of around 10–25%. Despite continuous development of new systemic treatment strategies that include cytotoxic chemotherapy and targeted therapy, most patients with metastatic CRC eventually progress. However, a small proportion of patients with mismatch repair-deficient or microsatellite unstable CRC responds exceptionally well to treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors, thereby proving that CRC is in principle amenable to immunotherapy and showing that long-term disease stabilisation can be achieved even in metastasised stages. However, the reasons for the lack of response to immunotherapy in the vast majority of CRC cases remain to be elucidated. Yet, recent evidence suggests that the tumour stroma, which includes non-immune cells in the colorectal tumour microenvironment, mediates immunosuppressive mechanisms that prevent effective immunotherapy. These findings open new avenues for the development of advanced immunotherapies for CRC. In this review, we summarise major developments in the systemic therapy of CRC within the last couple of decades, provide an overview of emerging and soon-to-be implemented therapeutic strategies and present concepts from clinical and preclinical research to manipulate tumour cells and the tumour stroma to sensitise microsatellite stable colorectal tumours to immunotherapy.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
The tumour microenvironment (TME), which includes tumour-infiltrating immune cells and stromal cells such as cancer-associated fibroblasts, can serve as a pool of predictive and prognostic biomarkers in CRC, with the immunoscore being an important example.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
Together with approaches that aim to augment the immunogenicity of pMMR/MSS tumours, TME-targeted and stroma-targeted therapies represent a promising strategy to overcome ICB resistance.

## Introduction

## Epidemiology of colorectal cancer
 Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most frequent cancer worldwide, and its incidence, prevalence and mortality remain high in Western populations and are even increasing in patients under 50 years. Whereas the introduction of large-scale screening programmes has greatly improved the early detection of CRC, still around 20–25% of CRC cases show metastatic dissemination already at the time of diagnosis and another 10–25% of patients with initially no distant metastasis (stage I–III) eventually progress to metastatic stage IV CRC , with the highest risk in stage III patients. More recent cohort studies found a decrease of relapse risk over the years, most likely resulting from advances in imaging techniques and standardisation of diagnostics, surgical procedures and adjuvant therapy. Molecularly, CRCs can be subdivided into mismatch repair-proficient (pMMR)/microsatellite stable (MSS) and mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR)/microsatellite instability high (MSI-H) tumours. For a brief overview of other common genetic alterations and molecular targets used in clinical practice, please refer to . Compared with pMMR/MSS tumours, dMMR/MSI-H CRCs are highly immunogenic and respond well to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) that has become the standard of care for this type of CRC and greatly improved prognosis. However, depending on disease stage, only around 3–15% of CRCs classify as dMMR/MSI-H, whereas the remaining ones are pMMR/MSS. pMMR/MSS tumours, on the other hand, do not respond to ICB and systemic treatment options for stage IV pMMR/MSS CRC are limited. Hence, and despite the progress that has been made in the treatment of this disease within the last years, half of patients with mCRC still die within approximately 30 months of diagnosis. This high lethality of mCRC and the rising mCRC prevalence due to demographic changes contribute to an estimated increase in global CRC-related deaths from around 915 000 in 2020 to over 1.5 million in 2040, underscoring the urgent need for the improvement of current and development of new treatment strategies, especially for pMMR/MSS mCRC. In this context, the manipulation of the tumour stroma—which comprises all non-tumour and non-immune cell types within a tumour such as fibroblasts and endothelial cells—represents a promising approach.
In this review, we provide an overview of the clinical and molecular foundations of CRC, we describe the state-of-the-art of systemic CRC treatment and aim to provide a comprehensive overview of the evolution of CRC therapy for both clinicians and preclinical scientists. We discuss currently used drugs and treatment schemes that mainly (but not exclusively, as exemplified by anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy and ICB) target the different types of tumour cells within CRCs and expound promising new biomarker-driven therapeutic concepts. Further, given the importance of the tumour microenvironment (TME) including the tumour stroma for the prognosis and treatment of CRC and the enormous potential of immunotherapy, we summarise biomarkers that predict success of immunotherapies in dMMR/MSI-H and pMMR/MSS patients. Finally, we introduce preclinical and early-phase clinical strategies that aim at modulating both tumour cells and the TME of mCRC to enable immunotherapy and long-term survival of pMMR/MSS patients.

## Evolution and status quo of systemic therapy for colorectal cancer
We primarily focus on systemic therapy for stage IV metastatic CRC (mCRC), with only brief references to other clinical situations such as neoadjuvant treatment of locally advanced rectal cancer. While surgical resection of metastases offers the only chance for long-term survival, it is feasible in just 10–30% of patients, resulting in only modest improvements in survival, mostly among select patients in clinical trials ( ). Due to the small number of individuals eligible for curative treatment, survival parameters improved modestly and mostly in patients treated in clinical trials. These patients were usually young, fit, had favourable biomarkers or had access to immunotherapeutic approaches . This improvement of overall survival has not been seen in the majority of real-world cohorts, except for patients with long-term survival who most likely have a particularly benign and treatment-responsive tumour biology. , Therefore, the indicated survival times in should be carefully considered for patients without therapy restrictions who can be treated according to clinical trial protocols and sequences and cannot be used for patient prediction for a general population. Further, trial data are mostly including patients with CRC liver metastasis, excluding or at least under-representing especially peritoneal metastasis as a major unmet research need. In conclusion, for the vast majority of patients who are elderly or unfit, biomarker negative and with MSS/pMMR mCRC, new treatment options and especially access to immunotherapy are urgently needed to improve outcomes and enable long-term survivorship.
 

## Current standard of care for treatment of metastasised colorectal cancer
Because treatment standards are rather region and country specific, we usually relate to standard of care in the western world (European Union and USA) and local approval status should always be considered. Despite their significant age, chemotherapies that target dividing tumour cells are still the mainstay of most cancer treatments including CRC. So far, chemotherapy has improved overall survival from approximately 6 months in untreated historical cohorts to 16–20 months for the doublet chemotherapy regimen with intravenous fluorouracil/leucovorin and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) and doublet chemotherapy regimen with intravenous fluorouracil/leucovorin and irinotecan (FOLFIRI) combination therapy within clinical trials. Further improvement of overall survival was mainly achieved by addition of targeted agents. In contrast to the rather unspecific tumour cell killing of systemic chemotherapy, targeted therapies offer a more individualised treatment approach. The first biological agents targeting the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and VEGF (receptor) (VEGF(R)) further improved systemic treatment; ongoing trials can be found in . Nevertheless, in stark contrast to the later discussed molecular transcriptomic subclassification systems, the current international guidelines recommend stratifying patients with mCRC before first-line systemic treatments based on just a few genetic (KRAS and NRAS mutations, BRAF p.V600E mutation, MSI-H/MSS and/or dMMR/pMMR status) and clinical markers (location of the primary tumour and biological age/clinical performance status) for the selection of an optimal systemic therapy. As the current standard of care, including the biomarkers, has been reviewed already elsewhere in sufficient detail, we kindly refer to the supplementary material for a tabular summary of current standard treatment recommendation and ongoing clinical trials with established drugs and biomarkers. An increasing number of new biomarker-driven chemotherapy-free targeted therapy regimens for defined molecular mCRC subtypes have been shown to be effective and are approved or are close to being approved and can be included in different lines of treatment. 
 

## Emerging biomarker-driven therapeutic concepts for mCRC

## KRAS p.G12C
Recently, selective and irreversible inhibitors of KRAS (Kirsten rat sarcoma) p.G12C have been developed and investigated in mCRC. KRAS p.G12C is an exon 2 mutation which is found in approximately 3% of patients with CRC. Its prognostic relevance in comparison to other KRAS mutations in mCRC is discussed controversially. , 
The phase I CodeBreak100 trial investigated the safety and efficacy of the KRAS p.G12C inhibitor sotorasib in patients with advanced solid tumours harbouring a KRAS p.G12C mutation, including 42 participants with mCRC. An overall response rate (ORR) of only 7.1% and median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 4.0 months were observed in the group of patients with mCRC. Preclinical and clinical data suggest that resistance to KRAS p.G12C inhibition is dependent on receptor tyrosine kinase activity, especially epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-signalling. Indeed, the phase III, multicentre, open-label, randomised CodeBreaK300 trial that assigned patients with chemorefractory KRAS p.G12C-mutated mCRC to either sotorasib plus the anti-EGFR antibody panitumumab or investigators’ choice of trifluridine/tipiracil or regorafenib demonstrated a superior median PFS of 5.6 months in the sotorasib (at a dosage of 960 mg) plus panitumumab group compared with 2.2 months in the trifluridine/tipiracil or regorafenib group. Overall survival (OS) data of this study are not yet mature. Adagrasib, another selective, irreversible KRAS p.G12C inhibitor, was investigated in the phase I and II KRYSTAL-1 trial as a monotherapy or in combination with EGFR inhibitor cetuximab. Again, the combination of adagrasib plus cetuximab was more effective than adagrasib monotherapy. In a pooled analysis of 94 patients from the phase I and phase II cohort treated with the combination adagrasib plus cetuximab, an ORR of 34% and a disease control rate of 85.1% was reported. mPFS and median OS (mOS) were 6.9 months and 15.9 months, respectively. The phase III KRYSTAL-10 trial is currently recruiting, comparing adagrasib plus cetuximab to FOLFOX or FOLFIRI in the second-line treatment of KRAS p.G12C -mutated mCRC (NCT04793958). In addition, several other drugs targeting KRAS mutations, including the most frequent p.G12D mutation and pan-RAS (rat sarcoma) inhibitors, are being investigated in clinical studies ( ).
 

## ERBB2/HER2
In approximately 3% of mCRC, ERBB2 is amplified and/or overexpressed, although the prevalence is higher in left-sided, RAS and BRAF wild-type tumours (5–7%). , Additionally, the prevalence of erb-b2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (ERBB2)-low disease in mCRC is between 6% and 30%. Despite being implicated in primary and acquired resistance to anti-EGFR antibody therapy, the prognostic relevance of ERBB2 amplification itself in mCRC remains controversial. Several clinical trials investigated anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) therapy for ERBB2-positive tumours in patients with chemorefractory mCRC. Strong preclinical evidence suggests that dual inhibition of ERBB2 is superior to monotherapy due to enhanced efficacy and reduced therapy resistance , and therefore the majority of trials have investigated combinatorial approaches. For example, the phase II HERACLES trial reported an ORR of 28%, median PFS of 4.7 months and median OS of 10 months in KRAS wild-type patients treated with the anti-ERBB2 antibody trastuzumab (binding the extracellular receptor domain and preventing receptor dimerisation) plus lapatinib (dual small molecule inhibitor for EGFR and ERBB2, blocking the intracellular kinase activity). Several phase II trials investigated the combination of trastuzumab and pertuzumab (binding to a different epitome of ERBB2) such as the MyPathway study (ORR 32%), the TAPUR basket study (ORR 14%) and the ctDNA-guided TRIUMPH study (ORR 30%). Recently, the phase II MOUNTAINEER study provided evidence in the largest cohort of patients in comparing ERBB2-specific tyrosine kinase inhibitor tucatinib with or without trastuzumab in RAS wild-type and ERBB2-positive mCRC (ORR 38.1%, mPFS 8.2 months, mOS 24.1 months). Additionally, the MOUNTAINEER study provided the first time clinical evidence of the superiority of dual ERBB2 targeted therapy compared with monotherapy. 
Currently, international guidelines therefore recommend testing for ERBB2 amplification, especially in left-sided RAS wild type metastatic or unresectable CRC. The approval status of combinatorial ERBB2 targeted approaches varies by regions and should be carefully evaluated before initiating treatment. Additionally, ongoing clinical trials such as MOUNTAINEER-03 (NCT0525365), which is evaluating tucatinib combined with trastuzumab and mFOLFOX6 versus mFOLFOX6 with or without cetuximab or bevacizumab as a first-line treatment for ERBB2-positive mCRC, and the multicentre, randomised phase II trial SWOG S1613 (NCT03365882), which is enrolling patients with RAS and RAF wild-type, ERBB2-positive mCRC after one prior treatment line to compare trastuzumab plus pertuzumab with cetuximab plus irinotecan, will provide further insights into the efficacy of dual ERBB2-targeted treatment strategies. A comprehensive overview of further ongoing phase II/III clinical trials is provided in . Another important question to be answered is whether the higher proportion of patients with ERBB2-low disease will also benefit from ERBB2-targeted therapeutic approaches, particularly with potent antibody-drug conjugates, as it has been observed and approved in ERBB2-low breast cancer. , 
 

## Antibody-drug conjugates
Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) represent one of the most promising developments in precision oncology in recent years. To date, 11 ADCs have been Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for 20 specific indications and the global market is growing rapidly. , ADCs consist of a monoclonal antibody targeting a surface molecule on tumour cells and a cytotoxic or immunomodulatory payload connected via a chemical linker. This design ensures high target specificity while providing the long circulation half-life of an antibody. On ADC binding to its target receptor, the receptor-ADC complex is internalised via the endosome/lysosome pathway and the payload is released by linker cleavage. Furthermore, due to paracrine release of the cytotoxic payload, surrounding (cancer) cells are likewise attacked, which is called the cytotoxic bystander effect. 
Recently, the first promising clinical results for ADCs in mCRC have been presented. The ERBB2-directed antibody and topoisomerase I inhibitor conjugate trastuzumab-deruxtecan (T-DXd) was investigated in the phase II DESTINY-CRC01 trial in ERBB2-overexpressing patients with RAS and BRAF p.V600E wild-type mCRC who had progressed on more than two previous regimens. In the more recent phase II DESTINY-CRC02 trial, the ORR for T-DXd treated patients with advanced and pretreated mCRC was 37.8% in the 5.4 mg/kg arm and 27.5% in the 6.4 mg/kg arm. Based on these results, the FDA recently approved T-DXd for the treatment of patients with unresectable or metastatic ERBB2-positive (IHC3+) solid tumours who have received prior systemic treatment. While this effective and promising approach is limited to the approximate 3% of patients with mCRC that show ERBB2 expression, it can be considered as a proof of principle that ADCs show clinical efficacy in mCRC. Although ADCs are considered a targeted treatment approach, their substantial toxicity (primarily affecting the bone marrow) must be considered. Notably, ERBB2-targeted ADCs have also been frequently associated with life-threatening cases of pneumonitis and interstitial lung disease.
Despite this high clinical potential and the recent progress in the development of ERBB2-targeted ADCs, the success of ADCs in patients with treatment-refractory mCRC is still limited and the majority of clinical trials that tested ADCs targeting carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 5 (CEACAM5; CEA), EGFR and trophoblast cell surface antigen-2 (TROP2) with various cytotoxic payloads showed the insufficiency of ADCs to overcome therapy resistance or have been highly toxic. , 
Theoretically, the ideal target surface antigen for an ADC would be expressed exclusively on tumour cells at a sufficient density and would be rapidly internalised on ADC binding. Unfortunately, especially in solid malignancies, only a few tumour antigens meet these criteria. Most antigens are more tumour-associated than specific and therefore, the ablation of healthy cells expressing the target antigen limits the therapeutic index of ADCs. Further, patient stratification and biomarker identification are crucial to select patients who would benefit from ADC treatment as antigens are expressed differently in human tumours compared with preclinical animal models with respect to orientation, polarity and cell surface expression. Cancer stem cells that express markers of a fetal gene programme associated with aggressiveness represent interesting targets for ADCs. For example, the glycoprotein TROP2, originally described as a surface marker of the embryonal trophoblast, is frequently upregulated in CRC and serves as a negative prognostic marker. The TROP2-targeted ADCs sacituzumab govitecan (SG) and datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) showed promising clinical efficacy in several cancer entities, including locally advanced or metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, hormone receptor-positive and HER2-negative breast cancer and locally advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma. SG is a humanised TROP2 antibody conjugated with a cytotoxic SN38 payload, which is the active metabolite of irinotecan. In June 2024, the phase 2/3 multicentre randomised clinical trial TROPHI1 (NCT0624339310) started recruitment investigating SG in the third-line therapy of metastatic CRC. Further, the phase 2 trial TROPION-PanTumor03 (NCT0548921111) is enrolling patients with solid cancers for treatment with Dato-DXd. An important possible limitation of approaches targeting cancer stem cells is their tremendous phenotypic plasticity, which recently has been reviewed in more detail elsewhere. In order to exploit the enormous potential of ADCs, future clinical trials should focus on previously studied antigens (such as HER2, EGFR, TROP2 and CEA) with novel linker technologies such as linkers that are exclusively cleaved by tumour-specific proteases to increase the therapeutic index (reviewed in ), new, diverse and perhaps even dual payloads selection of patients without an intrinsic resistance towards the cytotoxic payload and higher drug-to-antibody ratios. For an overview of ongoing clinical trials of ERBB2-targeted ADCs, the reader may refer to , and for an overview of ongoing trials of ADCs targeting various additional targets and carrying other payloads as well as novel ADC technologies being investigated, to .
 

## Additional concepts for molecular therapy of mCRC
Further molecular targets including phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) family, rearranged during transfection (RET) receptor tyrosine kinase and proteins involved in DNA damage control are currently being investigated but shall not be discussed in detail here, as clinical evidence is currently very limited (reviewed in ). For reference, ongoing clinical trials investigating these approaches are summarised in . For the rare treatment-refractory neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor gene rearrangements/fusion-positive mCRCs, larotrectinib and entrectinib are FDA-approved and EMA (European Medicines Agency)-approved treatment options. Ongoing clinical trials in the evolving field of bispecific antibodies are summarised in . These antibodies include bispecific T cell engagers (reviewed in , ) that provide a potential additional tool to direct immune cells into immune-cold tumours and could be combined with other forms of immunotherapies. 
 

## Immune checkpoint blockade in MSI-H/dMMR metastatic colorectal cancer
In contrast to all other currently established therapies that improve patient with mCRC survival by a few months, ICB which comprises treatment with monoclonal antibodies against programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein (CTLA) enable long-term tumour control. A seminal phase II trial from 2015 showed that ICB with the anti-PD-1 antibody pembrolizumab resulted in durable responses in more than 50% of patients with dMMR/MSI-H but not in pMMR/MSS mCRC. In 2020, the phase III Keynote-177 trial confirmed the superiority of pembrolizumab compared with chemotherapy with or without anti-VEGF/anti-EGFR in the first-line treatment of metastatic dMMR/MSI-H CRC. Consequently, anti-PD1 blockade with pembrolizumab became the new standard of care for patients with dMMR/MSI-H mCRC. Recent results from the Checkmate 8HW trial confirmed the outstanding efficacy of dual ICB with the anti-CTLA-4 antibody ipilimumab and the anti-PD-1 antibody nivolumab in the first-line treatment of metastatic dMMR/MSI-H mCRC, also in comparison to nivolumab monotherapy, in addition to the already established effectiveness of this combination in primary dMMR/MSI-H and second or later line treatment of dMMR/MSI-H mCRC. Unfortunately, until now, these impressive results are limited to the rather small cohort of dMMR/MSI-H patients and careful analysis of clinical trials shows that a relevant proportion of patients with metastatic dMMR/MSI-H CRC still do not respond to immune checkpoint blockade with tumour progression within the first 6 months (up to 50% and up to 25% in single and double agent ICB therapy, respectively), highlighting the need for a better understanding of immunotherapy resistance in non-responding dMMR/MSI-H mCRC. A novel interesting approach was recently published, identifying local enrichment of cytotoxic lymphocytes with an interferon-high immunophenotype that induces antigen-presentation in adjacent macrophages and tumour cells as a positive response predictor in subsets of dMMR/MSI-H but also pMMR/MSS CRC. Currently, multiple clinical trials investigate novel immune checkpoint inhibitors ( ), novel ICB combinations ( ) and dual antibodies targeting immune checkpoints ( ) in both MSI-H/dMMR and MSS/pMMR mCRC. For an overview of POLE/POLD1, an emerging biomarker for treatment with checkpoint blockade, please refer to .
 

## Immune checkpoint blockade in MSS/pMMR metastatic colorectal cancer
dMMR/MSI-H tumours accumulate mutations and display a high neoantigen load which results in an immune-hot microenvironment and facilitates T cell-mediated killing, thus explaining their responsiveness to ICB. In contrast, approximately 90% of patients with pMMR/MSS CRC have a low tumour mutational burden, are immune-cold and do not respond to ICB ( ). However, subgroup analyses of several clinical studies , suggest that some patients with pMMR/MSS CRC can successfully be treated with ICB, especially when combined with additional cytotoxic and targeted agents, proving that at least a subset of these tumours in principle is ICB-responsive. One example of a targeted therapy that has been approved and acts synergistically with ICB is the VEGF/VEGFR pathway, which has been shown to enhance immunosuppressive signalling. Therefore, anti-VEGF(R) treatment exhibits a promising immunogenic effect, which has been reviewed extensively elsewhere. , Consequently, one of the major current challenges is to identify biomarkers such as the immunoscore (IS) that could help to select those patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC who will respond.
 

## The predictive value of the immunoscore
The IS represents a standardised quantitation of overall CD3 T cell and cytotoxic CD8+ T cell densities in the tumour core and at the invasive margins of primary tumours and metastases, stratifies survival of patients with stage I–IV CRC with a high IS being associated with a superior prognosis in both dMMR/MSI-H and pMMR/MSS cohorts. It is important to note that the IS was originally developed for localised stage I–III CRC but since then has been further adapted for mCRC. A modification of the IS, termed “Immunoscore-Immune Checkpoint” (IS-IC), a systematic analysis of the density and vicinity of PD-L1 positive tumour cells and CD8+ T cells, prognostically stratifies patients with mCRC treated with the combination of FOLFOXIRI, bevacizumab and with or without anti-PD-L1 antibody atezolizumab. In the AtezoTRIBE study, a high IS-IC before initiation of treatment was associated with superior survival in the atezolizumab-treated pMMR/MSS mCRC subgroup. This upcoming clinical evidence on IS-IC needs further validation in different cohorts. In line with both scores, the infiltration of PD1-positive CD8+ T cells predicted the response to combinatorial ICB with nivolumab and ipilimumab in patients with pMMR/MSS in the NICHE (Neoadjuvant Immune Checkpoint Inhibition and Novel IO Combinations in Early-Stage Colon Cancer) study. Patients with dMMR/MSI-H cancer responded better, but an efficacy signal was also noticed in the pMMR/MSS group, although this was in a non-metastatic, locally advanced setting. The phase II FFCD 1703-POCHI trial recruited patients with MSS/pMMR mCRC and high immune infiltrates based on the IS and/or TuLIS score (another scoring system based on the extent of leucocyte infiltration into the tumour) for treatment with CAPOX (doublet chemotherapy regimen with intravenous oxaliplatin and oral capecitabine), bevacizumab and pembrolizumab (NCT04262687). Preliminary results from this trial show a 10-month PFS rate of almost 70% in the group of patients with a high extent of tumorous leucocyte infiltration. While CD8+ T cell-based biomarkers such as the IS and its derivatives hold great potential as predictive and prognostic biomarkers in CRC therapy, it is important to state that the sole presence of activated CD8+ T cells does not necessarily go along with productive T cell-mediated killing of tumour cells. In fact, in pMMR/MSS CRC, interferon gamma-positive activated T cells exist but display a less diverse T cell receptor repertoire and reduced neo-antigen reactivity, resulting in a tumour-unspecific, bystander immune activity when compared with dMMR/MSI CRC. Accordingly, an algorithm that classifies bulk CRC transcriptomes with respect to the ratio of tumour-specific to tumour-unspecific CD8+ T cell activity has prognostic implications in patients with pMMR/MSS and stratifies response to ICB. 
An unspecific T cell response due to low neo-antigenicity, low tumorous T cell infiltration and suppression of tumour-infiltrating immune cells are three major obstacles that preclude successful application of ICB. Importantly, all three mechanisms are influenced by the tumour stroma. Stroma-related mechanisms of immunosuppression provide attractive targets to overcome resistance of pMMR/MSS CRC to ICB and other types of immunotherapy. Combinatorial strategies that augment tumour immunogenicity and alleviate the detrimental effects of some TME components on the antitumour immune response are of particular interest and described in the last section.

## Strategies to overcome resistance of pMMR/MSS colorectal cancer to immunotherapy
The CRC TME comprises several innate and adaptive immune cell subtypes, endothelial cells and fibroblasts that can act in a protumourigenic and antitumourigenic fashion and determine therapy response and patient prognosis. The profound impact of the TME on human CRC was illustrated by a landmark study from 2015 that developed a bulk transcriptomic classification of primary CRC, the so-called ‘Consensus Molecular Subtypes’ (CMS). Two of these subtypes, CMS1 and CMS4, are characterised by a pronounced activation of the TME. CMS1 tumours show a strong association with dMMR/MSI-H and, hence, display features of a cytotoxic adaptive immune reaction and respond to ICB, whereas CMS4 tumours are marked by stromal signatures including cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and endothelial cells. Importantly, the stroma-rich CMS4 tumours are associated with immunosuppression and a significantly worse survival compared with the other three subtypes. In colorectal liver metastases, transcriptomic classification using a modified CMS classifier resulted in an enrichment of the CMS2 and CMS4 subtypes compared with primary tumours, suggesting that the tumour stroma represents an attractive therapeutic target in mCRC to revert immunosuppression and immune exclusion not only in primary but also in metastatic CRC. Interestingly, the newer intrinsic CMS (iCMS), which is based on single cell RNA sequencing data from the epithelial fraction of primary human colorectal cancers, revealed that the majority CMS4 tumours could be grouped into one of the two major iCMS subtypes, defined by a distinct gene expression, DNA copy number and gene regulatory network. Therefore, a subset of fibrotic tumours in the iCMS2 and iCMS3 group is expressing high levels of fibroblast, endothelial and immune-specific genes, indicating that iCMS2 and iCMS3 are genetically distinct, but independent of those genetic profiles some tumours develop fibrotic stromal signatures resulting in CMS4-like tumours. Additionally, new data highlight the role of transcriptional phenotypic plasticity over the mutational profile during the course of cancer progression and metastasis. Therefore, in advanced CRC stages, the tumour niche (primary vs different sites of metastasis) and host specific factors are important factors in establishing an immunosuppressive CMS4-like CRC. Of note, although we are here mostly focusing on stage IV CRC, this niche-dependent transcriptomic heterogeneity may also help to explain differences in response to (immuno)therapy between primary CRC and CRC metastasis, like, for example, in the superior response rate data for the (dual) checkpoint inhibition in primary pMMR/MSS and dMMR/MSI-H CRC compared with stage IV CRC. 

## Augmenting tumour immunogenicity and inducing an antitumour immune response
A low tumour mutational burden and weak neo-antigenicity are typical characteristics of pMMR/MSS tumours. Several strategies exist to augment the production of neo-antigens or strengthen the response towards existing antigens, which include oncolytic viruses, tumour antigen vaccinations and genotoxic therapies. As an additional layer of complexity, stromal cells can influence the outcome of such interventions in a variety of ways. Oncolytic viruses selectively infect and kill cancer cells, thereby having a direct therapeutic effect as cytotoxic agents. But, even more important, they can lead to the induction of an adaptive immune response by releasing tumour and viral antigens from dying cancer cells. Moreover, oncolytic viruses can be armed with vectors that encode for pro-inflammatory or anti-immunosuppressive transgenes, thereby in theory promoting both the immunogenicity of the tumour cells and intratumoral inflammation. The oncolytic vaccinia virus pexastimogene devacirepvec (PexaVec), which carries the pro-inflammatory cytokine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor as a transgene is capable of selectively targeting colorectal tumour cells and inducing immunogenic cell death. In pMMR/MSS mCRC, PexaVec showed modest clinical activity as a monotherapy as well as in combination with ICB. Intriguingly, CAF-derived fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) dampened the intrinsic antiviral response in pancreatic cancer cells and sensitised pancreatic tumours to oncolytic viral therapy in vivo, suggesting that FGF2-rich and CAF-rich tumours might be particularly susceptible to oncolytic viral therapy. Conversely, CAFs can undermine oncolytic viral therapy by upregulating type I interferons and increasing an antiviral response in tumour cells. In line with that, oncolytic viruses armed with a bispecific T cell engager against the CAF surface marker fibroblast activation protein (FAP) led to depletion of FAP-positive cells from the TME, increased tumorous T cell accumulation and showed superior activity compared with the unarmed parental virus, insinuating that depletion of specific stromal subsets might synergise with oncolytic viral therapy. Tumour-specific (neo-)antigen vaccines are another powerful tool to strengthen the adaptive immune response against tumours. In pancreatic cancer, a personalised RNA vaccine against computationally predicted tumour neo-antigens, autogene cevumeran, resulted in impressive recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates in vaccine responders. Autogene cevumeran is currently being tested in a global phase II trial for the adjuvant treatment of high-risk stage 2 and stage 3 colorectal cancer (NCT04486378). Furthermore, an amphiphile vaccine targeting p.G12D and p.G12R mutant KRAS has recently been tested in a phase I study for the adjuvant treatment of pancreatic cancer and CRC and showed good tolerability and promising efficacy. Preclinical data suggests that the ROS-producing enzyme NOX4 in CAFs impedes successful tumour vaccination by exclusion of T cells in several CRC mouse models. This effect could be reversed by pharmacological NOX4 inhibition. Although the clinical data are currently mostly covering adjuvant treatment of high-risk localised primary CRC, the induction of a personalised antitumour immune response through vaccination could also be a promising strategy for patients with mCRC. The combination of genotoxic chemotherapy and radiotherapy with immunotherapy is a third approach to foster immune activation in pMMR/MSS CRC and is based on the rationale that cytotoxic treatment would result in genomic instability and cell death, thereby stimulating both the production of neo-antigens and eliciting an adaptive immune response that can be further unleashed by ICB. Indeed, several studies suggest that successful cytotoxic and targeted therapies correlate with the susceptibility to ICB. Based on these insights, most clinical trials that evaluate the efficacy of ICB in pMMR/MSS CRC combine cytotoxic and/or other targeted therapies together with ICB, , and it seems plausible that strategies that increase the susceptibility of tumour towards conventional therapies will be beneficial for subsequent immunotherapy. The ability of CAFs to promote tumorous resistance against cytotoxic therapy is well documented. For example, CAFs form reservoirs for oxaliplatin in the CRC TME which increases tumour aggressiveness and therapy resistance and inflammatory CAFs that undergo senescence on radiotherapy promote therapy resistance of rectal cancers, partly through exclusion of cytotoxic T cells from irradiated tumours. Intriguingly, this effect could be reverted by administration of the recombinant interleukin 1 receptor antagonist anakinra, which led to the initiation of a phase I study of anakinra together with neo-adjuvant radiotherapy in the treatment of locally advanced rectal cancer. Additionally, CAFs subvert anti-VEGF and anti-EGFR therapy in CRC liver metastases by promoting tumour angiogenesis and resistance to cetuximab-induced cell death, respectively. One common mechanism by which CAFs confer resistance to chemotherapy and other antimitotic drugs is the induction of a revival colonic stem cell (revCSC) phenotype in tumour cells. RevCSCs are induced by stromal cues such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) in a Yes-associated protein (YAP)-dependent manner and are characterised by a slow-cycling, drug persisting phenotype. Consequently, YAP inhibition reverses the revCSC phenotype and synergises with chemotherapy. Moreover, YAP inhibition downregulates immune checkpoint molecules, increases major histocompatibility complex (MHC) expression on tumour cells and impedes regulatory T cell activity, all in all pointing towards a potential synergism of YAP inhibition and immunotherapy. , Finally, enrichment of dMMR clones within MMR heterogeneous tumours represents an interesting strategy to foster immunogenicity of CRC. Enrichment of dMMR clones can be achieved by treatment with chemotherapeutics such as temozolomide (TMZ) and 6-Thioguanine, to which dMMR tumour cells show higher intrinsic resistance than pMMR cells. Indeed, treatment of patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC with TMZ gave rise to mutations in MMR genes, increased tumour mutational burden and subsequent treatment of TMZ-responding patients with low-dose dual ICB led to durable clinical responses. Of note, fibroblast-like endothelial cells within the tumour stroma of glioblastoma attenuate the efficacy of TMZ treatment and genetic loss of the hepatocyte growth factor receptor c-Met in endothelial cells synergised with TMZ treatment in a mouse model, suggesting that pharmacologic c-Met inhibition might synergise with TMZ treatment and subsequent ICB in CRC.

## Overcoming T cell exclusion
Even when a tumour-specific adaptive immune response is mounted, effector T cells often do not infiltrate CRCs in sufficient amounts. This phenomenon called immune exclusion is mediated by the tumour cells themselves but also by stromal components. For example, adequate levels of T cell-attracting chemokines such as chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 9 (CXCL9), CXCL10 and CXCL11 are required for migration of T cells into tumours and TGF-β, a cytokine produced by CAFs and other TME cells and signature molecule of CMS4 tumours, inhibits the production of these chemoattractants. Apart from this anti-inflammatory effect, TGF-β is the prime cytokine that activates fibroblasts to acquire a myofibroblastic, that is, an ECM producing and contractile, phenotype which fosters T cell exclusion through the generation of physical barriers and associates with a worse prognosis in a multitude of cancer entities. , A landmark study from 2018 demonstrated that TGF-β contributes to immune exclusion in colorectal liver metastasis and that inhibition of the TGF-β receptor 1 using the drug galunisertib enhanced tumorous infiltration with T cells, inhibited outgrowth of liver metastases and synergised with anti-PD-L1 therapy to eradicate established lesions. Immune exclusion by TGF-β and other mediators is also an important mechanism of resistance towards chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T and other adoptive cell transfer therapies and could in part explain why these types of immunotherapies have so far been unsuccessful in the treatment of solid malignancies like CRC. Other explanations for this lack of efficacy of CAR therapies are provided by references. , 

## Overcoming immunosuppression
Lastly, stromal populations are critical in establishing and maintaining an immunosuppressive niche that impairs the function of infiltrating endogenous or adoptively transferred T cells. For example, loss of functional MHC class I (MHC-I) molecules on tumour cells due to genetic or epigenetic alterations or transcriptional downregulation occurs in 30–40% of MSS tumours and precludes antigen-dependent tumour cell killing by CD8+ T cells. TGF-β is capable of downregulating MHC-I, suggesting that CAFs can promote MHC-I loss in CRC. Given that MHC-I-defective tumour cells can still be killed by natural killer cells and gamma delta (γδ) T cells, it is interesting to note that γδ T cells can be skewed towards an IL-17-positive, immunosuppressive regulatory subtype by microenvironmental TGF-β and CAF-derived IL-6. In contrast to the strong preclinical evidence for the synergism of TGF-β and immunotherapies, several clinical studies that subsequently investigated this combinatorial approach in mCRC and other tumour entities failed, highlighting the complexity of translating results from mice to humans. One reason for the lacking efficacy might be suboptimal selection of patients according to tumour stage. For example, a recent phase II study demonstrated that neoadjuvant treatment of patients with locally advanced rectal cancer might represent a potential setting for TGF-β inhibition: here, the combination of galunisertib and radiochemotherapy showed a promising rate of complete responses. Furthermore, ICB particularly facilitates the destruction of small tumours and micrometastases, in which PD-L1 positivity and T cell infiltration are higher compared with larger metastases. This argues for the use of ICB in the neo-adjuvant setting and, indeed, perioperative ICB enabled eradication of micrometastases after resection of the primary tumour in a mouse model of pMMR/MSS CRC. In contrast, in human stage IV metastatic CRC, mechanisms of immunosuppression and exclusion are highly evolved and targeting of one molecule such as TGF-β might be insufficient to revert immunosuppression in that setting. Accordingly, a combination of, for example, cytotoxic and/or pro-inflammatory agents and TGF-β blockade might be needed to induce an effective antitumour immune response. For example, the pro-inflammatory Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) agonist polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly I:C) augmented the antitumour immune response in a mouse model of stroma-rich metastatic CRC and a TLR3-related gene expression signature associated with a better prognosis of patients with stroma-rich CRC. It is important to stress that pMMR/MSS CRCs, including CMS4 tumours, do harbour neoantigen-reactive T cells, suggesting that combinatorial strategies to alleviate immunosuppression in these tumours are of great potential. It is worth noting that an interesting approach to overcoming immunosuppression is to target myeloid-derived suppressor cells, a topic that has been excellently reviewed elsewhere. 

## Conclusions and outlook
In view of the long history of and tremendous efforts in preclinical and clinical CRC research, progress in the treatment of especially advanced pMMR/MSS CRC has been limited and overall survival rates of patients with mCRC have risen only modestly and especially in patients treated within clinical trials. This is in part attributable to the circumstance that only a few molecular markers, which reflect genomic or epigenomic alterations of cancer cells (eg, MMR status, RAS, BRAF) in the tumour, are currently being used to guide therapy decisions, whereas qualitative or compositional changes in tumour and TME cells are only starting to be appreciated. The introduction of ICB into the clinic was a breakthrough for the treatment of patients with dMMR/MSI-H mCRC, but only a small minority of the pMMR/MSS cohort profited in clinical studies. Hence, the discovery and clinical validation of new predictive biomarkers to define those patients with pMMR/MSS who will have the greatest benefit from ICB is mandatory. Immune and stromal cells within the CRC TME are important determinants of immunotherapy success and TME-based biomarkers such as the IS and its derivatives or stromal signatures will likely be a major component of future therapeutic decision making, identifying the subset of patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC that benefits from ICB. Modern single-cell and spatial sequencing methods that have enabled investigations into the composition of primary and metastatic CRCs at unprecedented resolution will help to define clinically relevant cellular activation states and molecules. Also, the development of methods to test for TME biomarkers non-invasively will greatly facilitate their introduction into clinical routine and first concepts for non-invasive imaging of the TME in CRC are underway but still far away from clinical translation. Apart from that, the discovery of new targets to manipulate tumour and TME cells for effective immunotherapy will be crucial to enable long-term remissions and cure of patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC. To that end, the development of new and refinement of current in vitro and in vivo models that more closely mimic the intricacies of pMMR/MSS CRC such as organoid-based tumour models and mice with a humanised immune system is needed. Given the enormous potential of immunotherapeutic strategies including tumour vaccination, cell-based therapies and ICB, high-quality preclinical platforms to study potential synergisms with other therapeutic measures will greatly facilitate CRC research. We believe that the use of these platforms to explore combinations and sequential approaches of various immunotherapeutic modalities in combination with TME-targeted therapies holds great promise and will help to tailor therapies that eventually allow long-term tumour control in patients with MSS/pMMR mCRC.
