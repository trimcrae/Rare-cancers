## Key Points

 

## Introduction
Cancer therapy is highly individual for every patient case, especially in advanced stages, specialists of various fields discuss each case together to find the most suitable therapeutic option. In recent years, immunotherapies, including adoptive T cell therapy, have added another pillar to the multimodal therapy concept.
Adoptive T cell therapies are utilizing patient-derived T cells to target tumor cells directly. They can be divided into three major approaches, using either tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), T cell receptor (TCR) or chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-modified T cells. Among these groups, CAR T cells have been proven clinically effective and six different CAR T cell therapies received approval of the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency for their application in B cell malignancies [ , ]. A CAR is a synthetic construct, classically consisting of an extracellular single chain variable fragment (SCVF) to recognize antigens on target cell surfaces. Linked through an extracellular spacer, transmembrane domain, and varying costimulatory domain(s), the single-chain variable fragment connects to an intracellular signaling domain of the T cell receptor (CD3ζ) to induce T cell activation upon antigen binding [ , ]. The activated CAR T cell mediates their cytotoxic effects through three mechanisms: exocytosis of cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes, Fas-Fas L interaction, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines including interferon γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) [ ]. For a detailed presentation of the CAR T cell mechanism of action, please refer to Benmebarek et al. [ ]. Importantly, the Food and Drug Administration has recently submitted a statement in response to the risk of T cell malignancies, calling for life-long monitoring of patients after receiving CAR T cell treatment for new, potentially CAR transgene-related malignancies [ ].
Apart from their success in hematological malignancies, such as B cell lymphoma, B cell leukemia, and multiple myeloma, CAR T cell therapy is struggling to translate the same clinical benefit to solid tumors. Several clinical trials with CAR T cells targeting different solid tumor antigens (i.e., epidermal growth factor receptor variant III [EGFRvIII], EpCAM, carcinoembryonic antigen [CEA], GD2, mesothelin [MSLN], ErbB2, interleukin 13Rα2 [IL-13Rα2] have been conducted, demonstrating safety and some response, yet the overall clinical benefit remained limited [ – ]. Tumor localization, heterogeneity, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) pose major challenges for CAR T cell therapy in solid tumors.
Local interventions, such as surgery, radiation, or ablation, have a high standing in cancer therapy, even in advanced stages [ – ]. They can be applied alone with curative or palliative intent, or used in a multi-modal settings to augment treatment effects [ ]. Surgery can reduce tumor burden, which can decrease tumor heterogeneity and adverse events related to massive tumor cell death or immune cell over-activation, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [ – ]. Local drug delivery using injections or catheter systems can increase the therapeutic dosage on site while reducing systemic toxicities, they can additionally decrease the frequency of drug application and duration of hospital stays [ ]. Ablation and radiotherapy (RT) can not only kill cancer cells directly but induce forms of immunogenic cell death (ICD), alter the composition of the TME, and contribute to therapeutic effects systemically through abscopal effects [ – ].
These effects have triggered promising combinatorial approaches of local interventions and immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [ ]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors are monoclonal antibodies that bind to immune checkpoint proteins, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4) on T cell surfaces, as well as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) on tumor cells to prevent downregulation of the T cell-mediated immune response [ , ]. Studies have shown increased anti-tumor efficacy [ , ], reduced toxicities [ , ], and even reversal of acquired resistances to ICIs [ , ], when local therapies and ICIs were combined. In this review, we show the synergistic potential of local therapies and CAR T cell treatment to overcome challenges posed by solid tumors and provide an overview into the current state of research combining these two strategies.

## Current Limitations for CAR T Cell Therapy in Solid Tumors
Reasons for the poor performance of CAR T cell therapy in solid tumors include insufficient trafficking of CAR T cells into the tumor, difficulty in the choice of target antigen, and the immunosuppressive TME (as summarized in Fig. ). The first challenge is posed by migration to their target antigen after CAR T cells are administered. While the target antigen in hematological malignancies is abundant and easily accessible, in solid tumors, CAR T cells have to localize the tumor and infiltrate through physical barriers of the stroma. This is further complicated by the often elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), disorganized extracellular matrix (ECM), and dysfunctional vascularization [ , ]. Altered tumor vasculature does not only hinder T cell delivery into the tumor and extravasation owing to changed endothelial properties but further gives rise to a hypoxic environment that impairs CAR T cell survival and proliferation [ ]. 
The heterogeneity of solid tumors and the presence of tumor-associated antigens on healthy cells, which can lead to on-target off-tumor toxicities, are further hurdles when targeting solid tumors [ , ]. The systemic administration of CAR T cells via an intravenous infusion is associated with systemic toxicities, such as CRS or immune effector-cell associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [ , ]. These toxicities are often dose limiting and therefore further decrease the chances of a clinical benefit. Interestingly, a meta-analysis of clinical studies has revealed that CRS and ICANS occur less frequently in solid tumors compared with hematologic malignancies [ ], which might be tied to their reduced treatment response.
Finally, solid tumors are often considered “cold” tumors, meaning they respond poorly to immunotherapy because of a TME that excludes immune cells and exhibits low expression of immune checkpoints such as PDL-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, and LAG-3 [ , ]. The TME comprises a heterogeneous composition of tumor cells and stroma, which is made up by endothelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, ECM, and immune cells. The immunosuppressive factors include immune suppressor cells and soluble mediators. Cells of the TME often secrete chemokines that recruit immunosuppressive cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) and interfere with the localization of T effector cells [ ]. Tregs play a key role in tumor-associated immune suppression, they deprive T effector cells of IL-2 and secrete inhibitory cytokines (IL-10, transforming growth factor β [TGF-β], IL-35), and they can further mediate resistance to ICIs and impair antigen presentation [ , ]. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells dampen the cytotoxic activity of T effector cells by producing suppressive cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β, IL-1RA) and metabolites (nitric oxide, methylglyoxal) [ , ]. In the TME, tumor-associated macrophages are polarized by Th 2 cytokines (IL-10, IL-4, IL-13) into M2 type macrophages that further produce anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10, IL-13) and contribute to tumor progression [ ].
The challenges CAR T cell therapy faces in solid tumors are as multifaceted as the approaches to tackle them. These range from the engineering focus on CAR T cells to enhance migration [ , ], penetrate the ECM [ ], or shield them against immunosuppression [ , ] to the exploration of combinatorial therapies [ ]. Here, we focus on how established local therapies might hold the key to addressing several issues simultaneously and unlock the full potential for CAR T cell therapy in treating solid tumors.
The treatment of cancer has evolved in recent years with advances in both local and systemic therapies. In the following, we discuss how local therapies, including targeted drug delivery, surgery, ablative techniques, and RT, can complement CAR T cell therapy to improve patient outcomes.

## Locoregional Delivery of CAR T Cells
Locoregional drug delivery refers to the targeted administration of therapeutics directly or in close vicinity to the area of interest. It provides several advantages over systemic delivery by overcoming the issues of limited trafficking, first-pass metabolism, systemic toxicities, and non-targeted delivery. Targeted dosing reduces overall doses and decreases toxicities, as it avoids first-pass metabolism, referring to drug metabolism before reaching its target, which leads to a reduced active concentration of site [ ] and off-target delivery. As a result, the use of intra-arterial application of chemotherapy (e.g., via the hepatic artery) or local internal irradiation (brachytherapy) has increased and helped to improve survival rates in patient care over the past few decades [ – ].
Because of direct anti-tumoral effects of locoregionally delivered agents, dying cancer cells can undergo forms of ICD, thus contributing to the tumor-targeted immune reaction like an in situ tumor vaccine (Fig. ). In this context, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), calreticulin, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), or nucleotides, as well as tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) are released from dying tumor cells, leading to increased adjuvanticity as well as antigenicity [ ] (Fig. ). Damage-associated molecular patterns attract dendritic cells (DCs), enforce the uptake of dying cancer cells and subsequent antigen presentation, followed by upregulation of the co-stimulatory domains CD80/86 [ , ]. Dendritic cells then migrate into tumor-draining lymph nodes where they present tumor antigens and co-stimulate T cells via engagement of CD80/86 to secrete IL-2 and to facilitate T cell activation (Fig. ). Consequently, locoregional delivery of anti-tumoral agents may synergize with various forms of cancer immunotherapy, including T cell therapy (Fig. ). 
Moreover, delivering (immuno)therapy directly into the tumor may help overcome problems of localization and infiltration faced by CAR T cell therapy in tackling solid tumors. Additionally, the locoregional application site could reduce systemic toxicities, such as CRS, and minimize damage to healthy tissues (on-target off-tumor effects).
Techniques of locoregional delivery vary based on the cancer type and location. Application routes include intratumoral, intra-arterial, intraperitoneal, or intrapleural, with most studies carried out on tumors of the central nervous system (CNS) where intraventricular, intracavitary, or intratumoral injections are used to circumvent the blood–brain barrier. In primary and secondary hepatic tumors, intra-arterial infusion via the hepatic artery (HAI) is used to reduce peripheral toxicity significantly as the agent will be metabolized by the liver. Further local administration can be facilitated by the anatomical specialties of cavities, such as an intrapleural or intraperitoneal application for ovarian cancers or malignant mesothelioma [ ]. In the following, we summarize the current application of these techniques to CAR T cell therapy.

## Intra-arterial
The method of intra-arterial infusion of chemotherapy has been established in the treatment of retinoblastoma [ ], primary liver cancer [ – ], and secondary liver cancer [ , ] as well as head and neck cancer [ ], even in advanced and pre-treated disease, and has further gained recognition in the treatment of pancreatic cancer [ ]. In these instances, delivery of chemotherapy through the ophthalmic artery, HAI, and selective intra-arterial cerebral infusion improved response rates [ ] and overall survival [ ] while reducing systemic complications [ ] and off-target effects, such as neurotoxicity [ ].
These clinical success stories prompted trials with regional infusion of cellular immunotherapies in hepatic and pancreatic malignancies, showing improved persistence of lymphocytes in the liver after HAI and little systemic escape, confirmed by flow cytometry and polymerase chain reaction of peripheral blood [ ]. In a phase I trial assessing the safety of intrahepatic delivery of anti-CEA CAR T cells via percutaneous HAI (NCT01373047), no patient experienced grade 3 or 4 adverse events. Treatment efficacy was indicated by decreased CEA levels, elevated serum IFN-γ, and an increase in liver metastasis necrosis or fibrosis. Meanwhile, trials with a systemic adoptive transfer of CEA-reactive T cells caused severe dose-limiting inflammatory colitis [ ] and pneumonia in patients, most likely due to on-target off-tumor effects because the CEA protein was subsequently downregulated in the regenerating mucosal tissue [ ].
Based on these results, the HITM-SIR trial (NCT02416466, Table ) aimed to follow HAI of anti-CEA CAR T cells with selective internal radiation therapy to benefit from the synergistic effects between radiation and immunotherapy [ ]. Similarly to the previous study, no adverse event above grade 3 was observed, with survival times comparing favorably to other second-line and third-line regimens for colorectal and pancreatic cancer, as patients were already in advanced stages of the disease [ ]. 
Apart from advantages in migration, reduced toxicities, and on-target off-tumor effects, the hydrodynamic infusion force may aid with infiltrating the tumor by overcoming elevated IFP that can inhibit CAR T cells from penetrating solid tumors [ , ]. The previously mentioned HITM-SIR trial was followed by an open-label phase I study HITM-SURE (NCT02850536, Table ) where anti-CEA CAR T cells were delivered by HAI using pressure-enabled drug delivery technology [ ]. This could enhance intrahepatic CAR T cell delivery 5.2-fold [ ], and consistent with previous studies there were no adverse effects above grade 3 observed. In one study participant with stage IV pancreatic adenocarcinoma, liver metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy, a complete metabolic response of metastatic disease was achieved for 13 months, with an overall survival of 23.2 months compared with the median survival of 5 months for the majority of stage IV patients [ ].
Similar trials of locoregional CAR T cell delivery have used MSLN-targeting CAR T cells [ ]. This was supported by murine orthotopic studies with intrapleural injections where CAR T cell numbers could be reduced 30-fold compared with a systemic injection, achieving long-term complete remissions, and outperforming systemic application in CD4+ T cell activation, tumor eradication, and persistence. Mesothelin overexpression is present in most pancreatic tumors, and a clinical trial has tested HAI in the delivery of MSLN-targeting CAR T cells for advanced pancreatic cancer with results still pending (NCT02706782, Table ).
Two phase I trials using HAI delivery for NKG2D CAR T cells are currently ongoing for liver metastatic colorectal cancer (NCT05248048) and relapsed/refractory (R/R) NKGD2-positive solid tumors (NCT05131763, Table ). Preliminary trials have explored HAI of anti-glyptican-3 CAR T cells in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (NCT02715362, NCT02959151), and a currently recruiting trial (NCT05410717) for advanced solid tumors expressing either Claudin6, MSLN, or AXL offers the administration of CAR natural killer (NK) cells systemically, intra-arterially, or intratumorally (Table ). It remains to be seen if the promise of enhanced activity with improved safety will be achieved here as well.

## Intratumoral
Image-guided intratumoral injection is another viable locoregional delivery method, particularly beneficial for patients with multiple metastasis. In this scenario, stimulation of an immune response through injection on one site could confer some treatment effect to non-injected lesions, leveraging abscopal effects [ , ]. Intratumoral immunotherapy allows for the rapid delivery of agents into tumor-draining lymph nodes, which is critical in shaping and maintaining an anti-tumoral immune response [ ]. A direct intratumoral injection further opens access to tertiary lymphoid structures within the tumor [ , ]. With the additional benefit of sequential biopsies with every injection, it provides a unique chance to monitor intratumoral dynamics under immunotherapy [ ]. A high local concentration upon injection with gradual absorption into the peripheral blood has been shown to render various immunotherapies more tolerable, such as the IL-15 superagonist ALT-803 or the anti-CD40 monoclonal antibody selicrelumab (NCT02304393) [ , ].
In a transgenic murine progressive ErbB2-expressing breast cancer model, intratumoral application of ErbB2-targeted CAR T cells resulted in growth arrest and eventual elimination of tumors [ ]. No difference in the distribution of CAR T cells in lymphoid organs could be observed between intratumoral and systemic injections. Intratumorally injected CAR T cells, however, were found at remote tumor sites and were able to control secondary tumors when administered repeatedly [ , ].
Preliminary clinical results with intratumoral CAR T cell injection stem from a phase I trial with multifocal injections of panErbB-specific CAR T cells in head and neck squamous cancer without prior lymphodepletion. No adverse events above grade 2 were observed and intratumoral persistence was demonstrated by an injection of radiolabeled cells (NCT01818323). Disease stabilization could be achieved in 60% of patients 6 weeks after CAR T cell treatment. In one subject, complete clinical response for over 3 years was achieved with subsequent treatment with pembrolizumab and T-VEC oncolytic virus, opening the possibilities of combining immunotherapies to a greater clinical benefit [ , ].
A phase I dose-escalation trial is planned for advanced sarcoma, with patients receiving NKG2D CAR T cells either systemically or intratumorally, depending on the accessibility of their disease manifestations (NCT06087341). Another study is investigating vascular interventional therapy or intratumoral injections of GPC3/CEA/MSLN-targeted CAR T cells in the liver metastasis of different primary tumors (NCT02959151, Table ). 
Even when the obstacles of migration and penetration are addressed by an intratumoral application, armoring T cells to persist in the immunosuppressive TME, supplementary immunotherapy such as ICIs or priming of tumors with low-dose RT is likely necessary to optimize this approach. Although clinical expertise on locoregional CAR T cell delivery remains limited, it is a field of growing interest.

## Intraperitoneal and Intrapleural
Because of the unique anatomy of serosa-covered body cavities, peritoneal cancers have already benefitted from regional delivery of chemotherapies. Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemoperfusion (HIPEC) is one of the more recent advances, where chemotherapy can be offered intraoperatively or through a peritoneal catheter [ ]. The benefit of intraperitoneal chemotherapy compared with systemic application alone in patients with ovarian cancer and peritoneal carcinomatosis has been established since 2006 [ , ]. Challenges with this approach arise from toxicities associated with the administered agents and catheter-based complications, such as intra-abdominal adhesions and infections. When administering CAR T cells, direct toxicities associated with chemotherapeutic agents might be alleviated, while technical difficulties remain.
In a murine orthotopic model of lung cancer, intrapleurally infused MSLN CAR T cells demonstrated superior performance compared with systemic infusions. Here, the required T cell dose for durable remission could be reduced 30-fold when CAR T cells were administered intrapleurally. Even when equivalent numbers of CAR T cells were present in the tumor, intrapleurally injected CAR T cells outperformed systemic delivery in terms of activation, tumor eradication, and persistence [ , ].
This effect was dependent on early CD4+ T cell activation, owing to an earlier encounter of the target antigen, and associated with a higher intratumoral CD4/CD8 cell ratio and long-term memory. Authors attribute the rapid kinetic of differentiation to be one of the reasons why intrapleurally administered CAR T cells outperformed systemic injections even when the same amount of CAR T cells were present in the tumor. Additionally, the lack of pulmonary sequestration of CAR T cells following an intrapleural injection and a rapid antigen encounter lead to early migration of the activated CAR T cells out of the pleural cavity and into metastatic sites, resulting in long-lasting systemic efficacy [ ].
Intracavitary applications such as intrapleural or intraperitoneal infusions have been reported in a phase I trial evaluating intrapleural injections of MSLN-targeting CAR T cells for the treatment of mesothelioma (NCT02414269, Table ) [ ]. Intrapleural administration was shown to be safe and feasible and led to longer lasting systemic circulation [ , ]. In a follow-up, no CAR T cell-related toxicities above grade 2 were reported in 20 patients treated for pleural metastases (18× mesothelioma, 1× lung cancer, 1× breast cancer) with MSLN CAR T cells. In combination with anti-PD-1 therapy, two complete and five partial PET anti-tumor responses were observed [ ]. The phase I/II clinical study (NCT02414269, Table ) is currently still active and intrapleural administration of 0.3–0.6 million MSLN CAR T cells per kilogram resulted in a median overall survival of 23.9 months from the CAR T cell infusion alone (1-year overall survival 83 %) compared with 18.8 months following first-line treatment with cisplatin and pemetrexed and the addition of bevacizumab [ , ]. 
Efficient systemic expansion of CAR T cells may contribute to promote endogenous immune responses. This concept was supported by clinical investigations involving intrapleural infusions of CAR T cells targeting the fibroblast activation protein for the treatment of metastatic malignant mesothelioma (NCT01722149), demonstrating the safety of the approach. Notably, despite administration of subtherapeutic doses of CAR T cells (1 Mio CAR T cells absolute dose per patient), good systemic expansion and elevation of proinflammatory cytokines were observed [ ]. Remarkably, two out of three treated patients arrived at the 18-month mark post-treatment [ ], underscoring the beneficial effects of local CAR T cell therapy in engaging the endogenous immune response.
A wide range of primary or metastatic tumors manifest in the intraperitoneal cavity, including gastric, ovarian, pancreatic, and colon cancers. In addition to established intraperitoneal chemotherapy, intraperitoneal injections of EpCAM CAR T cells have proven safety and efficacy in delaying disease progression in xenograft models of peritoneal, ovarian, and colorectal cancers [ ]. In a recent study, intraperitoneal injections of CAR macrophages in a mouse model of ErbB2-positive gastric cancer peritoneal carcinomatosis demonstrated both anti-tumoral efficacy and safety [ ]. Meanwhile, clinical data on systemic autologous macrophage injections did not substantiate notable anti-tumor effects [ , ].
In a murine model of epithelial ovarian cancer, intraperitoneal injections of ErbB2 CAR T cells performed better in terms of survival compared with the systemic route, while exhibiting fewer on-target-off-tumor toxicities [ ]. Interestingly, characterization of ErbB2 CAR T cells administered intraperitoneally versus the intravenous route revealed an increase in PD-1 and TIM-3 exhaustion markers of intraperitoneal injected CAR T cells in the spleen [ ]. Nonetheless, intraperitoneal administration demonstrated prolonged survival and limited distribution of CAR T cells in peripheral blood and other organs, suggesting a better safety profile compared with intravenous administration [ ].
Superior anti-tumor activity was recently confirmed for intraperitoneal administration of anti-CEA CAR T cells in a mouse model, demonstrating effective persistence and complete response after mice were rechallenged with the tumor. Interestingly, systemic effects on induced liver and spleen tumors were stronger for the intraperitoneal injected CAR T cell group, resulting in complete tumor regression, presumably because of better systemic expansion [ ]. This effect persisted even in subcutaneously injected flank tumors. Notably, better systemic expansion of intraperitoneal injected CAR T cells was observed even without peritoneal cancer manifestation. This challenges the theory that a faster encounter of their target antigen might lead to advantageous expansion.
Currently, several ongoing clinical trials are employing intraperitoneal infusions of CAR T cells targeting MSLN, CEA, CD70, and EpCAM, as well as one study using CAR NK cells against NKG2D (Table ). Preliminary findings of a study using an intraperitoneal infusion of MLSN-targeting CAR T cells in escalating doses (from 1.0 × 107 up to 5.0 × 108 cells/dose) indicated no adverse events above grade 3 (NCT03608618). Notably, out of 11 enrolled patients, three showed stable disease [ ], proving the feasibility and safety of the approach.
In conclusion, findings from preclinical and clinical studies have outlined the benefits of intraperitoneal and intrapleural CAR T cell administration on survival, reduced toxicities, and increased systemic anti-tumor responses. It is therefore not surprising that several clinical trials taking advantage of this approach are currently underway (Table ).

## Central Nervous System (CNS)
Tumors of the CNS present unique challenges in terms of treatment because of the intricate anatomy of their location, and the presence of the blood–brain barrier. Traditional systemic therapies therefore often have limited effects on the tumor target, and neurotoxic side effects are frequent. This and the often detrimental prognosis have encouraged numerous trials on locoregional applications of CAR T cells, either directly intrathecally via a ventriculoperitoneal shunt [ ] or by repurposing the surgically implanted Ommaya/Rickkam reservoir to deliver cells directly into the tumor cavity and/or ventricles. These approaches circumvent the blood–brain barrier, thereby addressing the poor chemotaxis of T cells into brain tumors and the spine [ , , ]. A particular concern of CAR therapy in CNS tumors is the inherent risk of ICANS, which is already a concern for CAR T cells when administered systemically [ ].
In a xenograft mouse model of cerebral ErbB2-positive breast cancer metastasis, an intraventricular infusion of ErbB2 CAR T cells resulted in robust anti-tumor efficacy in the treatment of multifocal brain metastases and leptomeningeal disease, with a 100% survival rate even after a rechallenge [ ]. In a model of atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor, typically emerging in the CNS of children below the age of 3 years, intraventricular administration of B7-H3-targeted CAR T cells resulted in enhanced tumor clearance and further traffic into the periphery, leading to antigen-specific protection of a tumor rechallenge in the brain and periphery [ ].
Intrathecal delivery of EPHA2, ErbB2, and IL-13Rα2-targeting CAR T cells demonstrated high efficiency in the treatment of metastatic mouse models of recurrent medulloblastoma and ependymoma, for which no targeted therapies are currently approved [ ]. Further preclinical studies emphasized the success of locoregional delivery into the CNS [ – ], while describing trafficking issues when applying CAR T cells in the intravenous route, where some CAR T cell models did not migrate into the CNS at all [ ]. A specific problem occurs, when symptoms of neurotoxicity and rising brain pressure must be countered with the administration of corticosteroids, as they impede CAR T cell function. However, Brown et al. could show that at least in a murine model, a low-dose application of dexamethasone was sufficient to attenuate edema and improve symptoms without impeding CAR T cell anti-tumor efficacy [ ].
The locoregional application of ErbB2-specific CAR T cells into the CNS via a catheter was proven feasible and tolerable in repetitive local dosing to children with recurrent/refractory CNS tumors (NCT03500991, Table ) [ ] while showing signs of local immune activation. In adults with recurrent glioblastoma, the efficacy and safety of intratumoral and intracavitary injections of IL-13Ra2 CAR T cells was reported (NCT00730613) [ ], showing manageable temporary brain inflammation. 
In one patient with recurrent multifocal glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), multiple infusions of intracranially delivered anti-IL-13Rα2 CAR T cells led to the regression of all intracranial and spinal lesions, with corresponding increases in cytokines and immune cell numbers as detected in the spinal fluid. This clinical response was stable for 7.5 months after T cell injection (NCT02208362) [ ]. Remarkably, an initial intracavitary injection reduced disease progression in the injected lesion, while new lesions appeared in the brain and spine and previous existing lesions at non-injected sites continued to progress. Therefore, additional intraventricular infusions were administered to improve CAR T cell trafficking to multiple foci, resulting in the complete clearance of the lesions after ten cycles. Neither mode of infusion led to toxic effects of grade 3 or higher.
Ongoing studies are investigating local administration of CAR T cells against GD2 [ , ], B7-H3 [ ], EGFR [ , ], ErbB2, IL-13Rα2, CD147 [ , ] as well as a bivalent construct targeting EGFR and IL-13Rα2 [ ] (Table ) in the treatment of CNS tumors. Promising preliminary results regarding safety, local immune activation, and CAR T cell persistence in the cerebrospinal fluid have been observed in early studies [ , , ]. Analysis of cytokine levels in the cerebrospinal fluid post-infusion is particularly interesting, as elevated levels of CCL2 and CXCL10 have been reported across different studies in pediatric CNS tumors, whereas serum levels of chemokines remained stable [ , ]. In most studies, no CAR T cell DNA could be observed in the peripheral blood, when cells were applied intraventricularly [ ]. Even though intracranial delivery seems to be superior to systemic administration of CAR T cells, the ideal route of application for CNS tumors remains a matter of debate and may vary depending on the context and location of the disease.

## Surgery
Surgical intervention for tumor elimination or bulk reduction is a central pillar of oncological care in the curative and palliative setting. In many patients with cancer, microscopic residual cells after excision can lead to tumor recurrence, treatment resistance, and metastasis [ , ]. Surgery can induce upregulation of genes associated with adhesion, invasion, and angiogenesis [ ], among them the immunosuppressive factor cyclooxygenase-2 [ ].
Additionally, the perioperative stress reaction and surgical wound can lead to a temporarily suppressed local immune response that promotes motility, invasion, and proliferation of residual cancer cells as well as the suppression of anti-tumoral leukocytes, NK cells, and DCs [ , ]. Adjuvant and neoadjuvant treatments, such as chemotherapy, RT, targeted agents, and more recently, immunotherapy, have been employed to increase the success of a surgical tumor resection. In the context of CAR T cell therapy, it is important to determine the best sequential combination of these treatments.
Park at al. prepared a hydrogel scaffold to apply various immunomodulatory compounds, including anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4, celecoxib, sting-agonist STING-RR, and the Toll-like receptor agonist R848. They could demonstrate that local continuous administration of innate immunity agonists (STING-RR and R848) prevented tumor recurrence and eradicated previously established metastases [ ]. Application of biodegradable polymers, such as microparticles based on poly(lactic-co-hydroxymethyl-glycolic acid) loaded with immune-modulatory antibodies or ICIs like CTLA-4 [ ], may reshape the TME in a favorable way, suggesting that this approach may become a platform for combination treatment.
Surgery could also be explored as a means of local drug application. For example, CAR T cell application in fibrin gel to GBM resection cavities [ ] allows the gradual release of CAR T cells into the inflamed post-surgical environment and enhanced anti-tumor effects.
In another preclinical study, adjuvant local application of MSLN-targeted CAR T cells in a fibrin-glue-based carrier into the resected tumor cavity cleared residual tumor cells after incomplete surgery. Proof of concept was provided in mouse models of triple-negative breast cancer and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma without negative implications for wound healing [ ].
Moreover, in an immunocompetent mouse model, infusion of EpCAM CAR T cells successfully reduced distant metastases and inhibited local tumor recurrence driven by surgery-induced release and intravasation of large numbers of circulating tumor cells [ ]. In clinical settings, enhanced anti-tumor effects were reported post-surgically in patients after an intracavitary infusion of CAR T cells upon subtotal glioblastoma resection (NCT02208362) [ ].
A preclinical study indicated that intracavitary application of CAR T cells in a fibrin gel leads to better persistence of CAR T cells compared with intracavitary infusion in free suspension [ ]. Hu et al. adopted a similar approach combining CAR T cells with anti-PD-L1-conjugated platelets in a hydrogel against partially resected WM-115 tumors. Hydrogel application of CAR T cells increased persistence and proliferation threefold in comparison to free CAR T cells. This combination gel treatment significantly reduced the growth of the primary tumor, while conveying systemic anti-tumor immunity on a secondary tumor [ ]. Overall, the sequential combination of CAR T cells with surgical intervention presents a compelling rationale, yet clinical validation is warranted.

## Local Ablation
Tumor ablation is an example of a therapeutic interventional procedure to destroy tumor cells in situ with the advantage of being minimally invasive, meaning less trauma and shorter healing periods for patients. There are several techniques of non-ionizing energy-based ablation, including radiofrequency ablation (RFA), cryothermal and photothermal ablation, microwave ablation (MWA), irreversible electroporation (IRE), ultrasound ablation, and magnetic ablation. These physical therapies are employed to directly kill cancer cells and have found their way into standard-of-care treatment, such as RFA in early-stage HCC [ ].
In several preclinical and clinical studies, local ablation has additionally demonstrated immune-regulatory effects regardless of the technique used. Release of chemokines, activation of immune cells, and localized inflammation have been reported [ , – ] with considerable effects on distant lesions through abscopal effects. Because of this priming of the TME, several synergistic effects with immune checkpoint blockades have been reported, for example between cryoablation and anti-CTLA-4 therapy [ ] with reports that IRE and cryoablation could reverse acquired resistance to ICIs [ , ]. These success stories fuel hope for combination treatments with CAR T cell therapy. Here, we showcase immunological effects of non-ionizing energy-based ablation techniques on the TME and potential synergies with CAR T cell therapy (summarized in Fig. ). 
Cryoablation causes necrosis in the central zone where temperatures below -40°C induce ice crystal formation and vascular constriction, leading to ischemia. Necrotic cells release DAMPs and large amounts of well-preserved TAAs, enhancing antigen presentation on dendritic cells (DCs) with costimulation via CD80/86. In contrast, reversible damage in the peripheral zones triggers tumor cell apoptosis, potentially exerting anti-inflammatory effects.
Irreversible electroporation induces programmed tumor cell death through the formation of nanopores in cell membranes. This
leads to the release of DAMPs and the largest amount of preserved TAAs compared with other ablative techniques. This process enhances antigen presentation on DCs and the activation and recruitment of more immune cells. Additionally, IRE increases
microvessel density in the TME, thus promoting oxygenation and immune cell recruitment into the tumor, while increasing the
production of reactive oxygen species.

## Hyperthermal Ablation
Hyperthermic injury can be induced by RFA, MWA, laser (photothermal) ablation, and HIFU. In the central zone, where temperatures exceed 50 °C, tumor cell necrosis occurs because of protein denaturation, collapse of the cell membrane, and mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Additionally, delayed cellular damage develops after cessation of heat application. Preclinical and clinical studies have reported this phenomenon to be likely mediated by programmed forms of cell death, cytokine release, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and the release of lysosomal contents in the necrosis zone. Increased proliferation and infiltration of immune effector cells into the tumor further contribute to delayed cellular damage [ , – ]. These effects promote tumor control, when hyperthermia is combined with radiation and/or chemotherapy [ – ] (Fig. ).
The peripheral zone of the heat-treated tumor usually only heats to temperatures around 40 °C, ‘mild hyperthermia’ due to the heat-sink effect from blood circulation [ , ]. In this zone, the blood flow is increased because of vasodilation, resulting in increased oxygenation. Thus, facilitating the formation of reactive oxygen species, while rendering the tumor more susceptible to radiation and improving chemotherapy delivery. The surrounding damaged tissue and endothelial injury stimulate the expression of vascular adhesion markers and chemokines [ , , ]. This setting may also improve infiltration of endogenous immune cells or CAR T cells into the tumor and promote their proliferation (Fig. ).
Mild hyperthermia has been explored in a preclinical B16 murine model [ ]. Heating the primary tumor to 43 °C for 30 min resulted in the activation of DCs and subsequently CD8+ T cells in the draining lymph node, conferring resistance against rechallenge with B16. Interestingly, this effect was proportional to the heated tumor volume, as mice with larger heated tumors showed greater resistance against secondary tumors [ ].
Preclinical studies where near-infrared light-mediated photothermal therapy was applied to a murine melanoma model 2 h before CAR T cell treatment confirmed increased intratumoral infiltration of CAR T cells, better tumor control, and increased cytokine production (IL-6, IL-2, IFN-γ) [ ]. Similar improvement in tumor control could be confirmed in xenograft CD19+ K562 tumors, as well as in a syngeneic B16-F10 melanoma model in immunocompetent C57BL/6 J mice [ ].
Interestingly, exposure of CAR T cells to hyperthermia (40 °C) reduced their cytotoxicity against NALM6 both in vitro and in vivo, as measured in reduced secretion of IL-2, IFN-γ, and IL-8 by CAR T cells. This effect could be reversed when supplementing arginine [ ].
While no clinical studies currently explore the synergism between CAR T cell therapy and hyperthermia, interesting approaches are investigating photothermal therapy to induce synthetic gene switches in CAR T cells through temperature modulation between 40 and 42 °C for better treatment control and safety [ , ].

## Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA)
Radiofrequency ablation is another modality of thermal ablation, where high-frequency alternating currents are generating frictional heat with temperatures of 60–100 °C within the tumor, leading to coagulative necrosis [ ]. Radiofrequency ablation has been applied in several solid cancers, such as the kidneys [ ], breast [ , ], and most commonly in primary cancer and metastases of the liver [ , , , ] where it is a first-line treatment option for early stages of HCC.
While coagulative necrosis dominates in the center of RFA application, the damage in the peripheral zone is sublethal and induces programmed forms of cell death. Here, inflammatory infiltrates, including DCs, NK cells, as well as B and T cells specific to the ablated tissue have been reported [ – ]. Radiofrequency ablation triggers the release of intracellular antigens and inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, which in turn stimulate innate and adaptive immune responses [ , – ]. It further confers specific anti-tumor responses owing to an increase in tumor antigens, including neoantigens [ , ].
However, ablation therapy is rarely sufficient to achieve permanent tumor clearance, RFA in HCC has high recurrence rates between 50-70% [ ] and its application is mostly restricted to small tumors below 3 cm in diameter [ , ]. Experimental evidence indicates that although RFA treatment of liver metastases of colorectal cancer increased T cell infiltration in primary and secondary tumors, this effect is quickly antagonized by the upregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 expression [ ].
Therefore, preclinical and clinical studies have recently focused on the combination of RFA with immunotherapeutic agents in order to increase anti-tumor efficacy. Preclinical studies combining RFA with ICIs, such as anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1, showed an increase in antigen-specific CD8+ T cell induction and boosted overall anti-tumor immunity, including protection against tumor rechallenges [ , , ].
A few preclinical trials have investigated the combination of RFA with adoptive cell therapy. Here, RFA with autologous retronectin-activated killer cells demonstrated the safety of the approach [ ]. Radiofrequency ablation and adoptive DCs in a colon cancer model in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice led to stronger inhibition of tumor growth as well as increased infiltration of CD8+ lymphocytes [ ]. In syngeneic murine models of colon cancer and melanoma [ ], just 1 min of RFA before adoptive transfer of in vitro activated tumor-specific CD8+ cells enhanced their trafficking into tumor-draining lymph nodes, elevated ICAM-1 expression on endothelial cells, and increased therapeutic efficacy.
A clinical study involving 62 patients with HCC compared RFA alone to RFA with cellular immunotherapy (induced NK cells, γδT cells, and cytokine-induced killer cells). The study indicated higher progression-free survival when cellular immunotherapy was administered, while no severe adverse events were reported [ ]. Even though no specific combinatorial studies of RFA and CAR T cells have been undertaken so far, the promising results of RFA in combination with various other immunotherapies should prompt further investigation in this field.

## Microwave Ablation (MWA)
Microwave ablation employs oscillating electromagnetic waves at frequencies of 900–2500 MHz to induce hyperthermic cell damage through an intratumorally positioned antenna [ ]. In contrast to RFA, it does not rely on conduction properties of the tissue and is therefore suitable for tissues with higher impedance, such as the lungs and bone, and can heat tissue up to 2 cm away from its antenna because of reduced susceptibility to the heat-sink effect [ , ]. The immunogenic profile of thermal ablation has been described in the sections above, yet stimulation of proinflammatory cytokines is significantly lower than in other ablative techniques [ – ].
While fewer studies have focused on MWA in comparison to RFA, a recent preclinical study explored the combined approach of MWA with AXL-specific CAR T cells in metastatic NSCLC xenograft models. This study showed how MWA reshapes the TME and thereby facilitates activation, infiltration, and eventually the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR T cells [ ]. In this study, AXL CAR T cells were infused either alone or after the application of MWA. Even though the CAR T cell-only group received twice as many cells compared with the combination group, more CAR T cells were found intratumorally and in the peripheral blood when MWA was added. This effect was attributed to MWA breaking down the physical tumor barrier by lowering levels of hyaluronic acid, increasing blood flow an oxygenation, and reducing IFP. This preclinical success warrants further trialing of the combinatorial approach.

## High-Intensity Focused Ultrasound (HIFU)
High-intensity focused ultrasound is a non-invasive ablation modality that has been applied to diverse solid tumors, including breast, prostate, liver, and pancreatic cancer [ , , , ]. High-intensity focused ultrasound uses focused acoustic waves at intensities greater than 5 W/cm2 to trigger both mechanical and thermal effects in the TME.
While the hyperthermic effects can raise temperatures up to 60 °C in the focal area causing coagulative necrosis [ ], the mechanical aspects can induce cell death into subcellular fragments, disrupt the ECM to release DAMPs, and thereby stimulate the innate immune system and increase the immunogenicity of the tumor [ , ]. In contrast to heat-based denaturation of proteins, mechanical HIFU preserves subcellular component integrity by disrupting cell membranes and producing peptide tumor antigens with retained immunogenicity [ ]. Similarly to the periphery of hyperthermia-treated tumors, signs of marked inflammation and abundant leukocyte infiltration have been observed at the margins of HIFU-induced coagulative necrosis [ , , , ].
In a randomized clinical trial of 48 patients with breast cancer undergoing HIFU ablation before surgery, increased numbers of tumor-infiltrating CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, B lymphocytes, and NK cells were observed [ ]. A histological examination revealed that HIFU induced coagulative necrosis of the target tumor and its surrounding normal tissue, indicating treatment efficacy [ ].
While trials involving CAR T cells and HIFU are lacking, its mode of action may indicate potential for future synergies. In any case, HIFU is being explored to gain acoustogenetic control of CAR T cells, using a heat shock protein promotor to induce CAR expression upon focused ultrasound heat activation to facilitate safer cell therapy [ ].

## Cryoablation
Cryoablation damages tumor cells directly via repeated freeze-thaw cycles below – 40 °C using liquefied gases. This leads to crystallization, vascular stasis, ischemia, and mechanical cell destruction, resulting in apoptosis and necrosis [ , ] (Fig. ). Because of its minimal invasiveness, few complications, and a shorter duration of hospitalization, it has been applied to various solid tumors of the skin, liver, lung, breast and bone [ ]. For complete ablation, − 20 to – 40 °C are necessary and must persist for 1 cm beyond the tumor periphery, thus limiting its applicability to tumors that can be properly targeted [ ].
Apart from the central necrosis, surrounding cells receive potentially reversible damage that induces cell death and leads to increased immunogenic reactions. Necrotic cells spread DAMPs, such as HSP70, HMGB1 and uric acid, resulting in co-stimulation of T cell activation and proliferation. When phagocytosed by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in the absence of DAMPS, dying cell-derived antigens are presented via major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules without co-stimulation of T cells. Additionally, bona fide apoptotic tumor cells may even secrete immunosuppressive cytokines. Thus, the balance between immunogenic and non-immunogenic modes of tumor cell death may impact the proinflammatory/anti-inflammatory environment [ , , ] (Fig. ).
Induction of immunogenic forms of cell death has made cryoablation an interesting combination partner for immunotherapy. Notably, cryotherapy has been reported to induce higher post-ablative immunogenicity compared with other modalities of ablation [ ]. In particular, higher levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α and increased amounts of antigen accumulation in DCs could be demonstrated compared to RFA and MWA [ , , , , , ]. One explanation for this might be that in contrast to hyperthermia-induced protein denaturation, cryoablation results in the release of intact antigens [ ]. While the exact mechanisms for enhanced anti-tumor immunity with cryotherapy remain unclear, a multi-omics approach of the distal TME after cryoablation confirmed large amounts of tumor antigens, sterile inflammation in distal tumors, activation of lysosome-related pathways, as well as the suppression of immunosuppressive factors [ , ].
A preclinical combination of the HPV-DNA vaccine with cryotherapy confirmed that the combination led to better tumor control, with increased CD8+ T cells in the spleen and reduced numbers of immunosuppressive MDSCs in the tumor [ ]. Several preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the synergistic effect of cryoablation with immunotherapies, such as ICIs, adoptive DCs, and NK cells (NCT02843815) on tumor elimination as well as metastatic control [ , – ].
While preclinical data seem promising, it remains to be clinically trialed whether cryoablation could serve as a novel bridging strategy for CAR T cell treatment. Along these lines, a recent case report explored cryoablation as bridging strategy in two cases of R/R B cell malignancies. Patients were treated with either BCMA or CD19 CAR T cells after receiving percutaneous cryoablation 1 week prior, leading to partial responses in both patients with tolerable adverse effects [ ]. These early examples underpin the need to further study such combinations.

## Irreversible Electroporation (IRE)
Irreversible electroporation uses high-voltage electric pulses for milliseconds to form nanopores in cellular membranes, thus inducing programmed cell death without thermal damage [ ]. Accordingly, intracellular components and extracellular vessels are well preserved, facilitating the infiltration and presentation of tumor antigens through APCs [ , , ] (Fig. ). As the effects of IRE are more restricted to target cells, it can be applied to larger amounts of tissue with higher safety [ , ] and even in fragile organs such as the pancreas, as scaffolding tissue is barely affected [ ]. The technique has been approved for clinical use in solid tumors of diverse tissues, including the liver, pancreas, lung, and kidney [ , ].
Preclinical trials suggest that IRE may outperform heat and cryoablation techniques in the amount of tumor antigens released, leading to increased T cell activation [ ], as well as a higher infiltration of macrophages and T cells [ , , ]. Cancer-specific immunity can be retained after IRE via an in situ vaccination, as shown in the successful rejection of tumor rechallenges in a murine model [ ].
A more striking feature of IRE, setting it apart from other ablation techniques, is not only the depletion of Tregs from the tumor and spleen [ – ] but actual reversal of tumor resistance to the anti-PD-1 blockade, presumably via modulation of the TME [ , ]. Apart from combination with ICIs [ – ], Lin et al. reported the combination of IRE with allogenic γδT cells in a clinical trial involving 62 patients with locally advanced pancreatic cancer (NCT03180437). Prolonged overall and progression-free survival was observed in the combination group, with a reduction in the tumor markers, circulating tumor cells, and increased CD4+, CD8+, NK cells, TNF-β, and Th1 cytokines (IL-2, IFN-γ) versus IRE monotherapy [ ]. Similar results were obtained in two clinical trials showing enhanced anti-tumor efficacy when IRE was combined with adoptively transferred NK cells [ , ]. The combination with CAR T cells and IRE remains to be explored.

## Radiotherapy (RT)
Radiotherapy is the standard-of-care in various malignancies, with around 60% of solid cancers receiving some form of irradiation treatment in the course of disease. Even though there are limited clinical trials combining RT and CAR T cell therapy at this point, an increasing number of studies indicates a strong synergy between these treatments. Radiotherapy can break down physical barriers, enhance (CAR) T cell migration and infiltration, and reshape the TME to promote adaptive immunity (Fig. ). 

## RT Improves Immune Cell Homing into the Tumor
Radiotherapy can debulk tumors, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and the extracellular matrix through DNA damage and reactive oxygen species signaling [ ]. Additionally, it can improve immune-cell infiltration by normalizing the dysfunctional tumor vasculature. Radiotherapy induces the expression of intracellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and the vascular-cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) on the endothelium, which facilitates the adherence and extravasation of lymphocytes into the TME [ , ] (Fig. ). The normalization of tumor vascularization also alleviates the hypoxic tumor environment, thus supporting immune cell survival and proliferation (Fig. ). Active migration of T cells into the tumor is enforced through RT-dependent activation of a type I interferon response, which induces the secretion of several T cell-attracting chemokines [ , ] such as CXCL9-11, CCL5, and CCL8 [ – ] in tumor cells and tumor-resident myeloid cells (Fig. ). Enhanced extravasation and increased homing of GD2 CAR T cells could be demonstrated in an immunocompetent orthotopic GBM model, when CAR T cell administration was followed by total body irradiation [ ].

## RT Increases Tumor Immunogenicity
Higher doses of RT lead to forms of immunogenic cell death, as dying cells release TAAs and DAMPs. Damage-associated molecular patterns include nucleic acids that have been altered because of the ionizing radiation. When these nucleotides bind to pattern recognition receptors of nearby cells, a type I interferon (including IFN-α and IFN-β) cascade is triggered. This is further augmented by activation of the cGAS-STING pathway [ , ]. The type I interferon response stimulates genes involved in immunomodulation, resulting in chemokine secretion, activation of APCs, and enhanced antigen presentation and cross-presentation. The cross-presentation of antigens to recruit and activate effector T cells links the innate to the adaptive immune response and (re-)activates the endogenous anti-tumor immunity, in the sense of an in situ vaccination scenario [ , , – ]. This effect is augmented by RT-induced upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class I molecules on APCs [ ] (Fig. ). Synergistic effects leading to abscopal-like responses have been reported in a patient with multiple myeloma, treated with BCMA-targeted CAR T cells and local RT (NCT03070327) [ ].
Another indicator of synergism with CAR T cell therapy is the upregulation of CAR-targeted antigens on tumor cell lines upon irradiation. This was demonstrated for CD20 expression in Burkitt lymphoma cell lines [ , ], but also for CEA and MUC-1 in human colon carcinoma cell lines that were upregulated 1–4 days after radiation in a dose-dependent manner [ , ] (Fig. ). In a study with murine and human CD70-bearing GBM cell lines, irradiation of the tumor cells with 6 Gy enhanced their CD70 antigen expression, resulting in increased recognition by anti-CD70 CAR T cells, measured in IFN-γ release [ ]. Similarly, Weiss et al. could show that cranial irradiation with 4–7 Gy of two NKG2D-expressing orthotopic GBM models resulted in prolonged survival when combined with NKG2D-targeting CAR T cells, as a consequence of increased IFN-γ production and cytolysis, potentially attributable to the induction of the NKG2D ligand as well as increased migration of CAR T cells into the tumor [ ]. The mechanism of antigen upregulation upon irradiation is both dose and time dependent, with a peak around 24 h after irradiation. The different studies report a mechanism at a transcriptional level [ , ], potentially linked to redox regulation, as antioxidants could decrease protein expression [ ].

## RT Reshapes the TME
In addition to increasing the number of immune cells present in the TME, RT-induced proinflammatory cytokines contribute to switching immune-excluded “cold” tumors into inflamed “hot” tumors that are more susceptible to ICIs or immune-targeted therapies such as CAR T cells [ – ]. Radiotherapy can modify the immunosuppressive composition of many solid tumors by reducing the frequency of immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [ – ] while increasing infiltration of NK cells [ ] (Fig. ). However, it is notable that these effects are highly dose dependent, as high doses of 20 Gy per fraction can recruit Tregs and MDSCs into the TME and thus create a more hostile environment for CAR T cell proliferation [ – ]. Along these lines, RT at higher doses can also enhance the expression of immune suppressive checkpoints such as PD-L1 [ ], CTLA-4 [ , ], and LAG-3 [ , ], which can interfere with T cell function but at the same time increase the efficacy of ICIs [ – ].

## RT as a Lymphodepleting Regimen Before CAR T Cell Therapy
Increased homing of immune cells into solid tumors along with reprogramming of the TME into a proinflammatory milieu already bear great potential to augment CAR T cell therapy. When applied as a conditioning regimen, RT may promote the local expansion of CAR T cells within the tumor by depleting local naive lymphocytes [ ] to increase the pool of interleukins available for tumor-specific CAR T cells [ , ] (Fig. ). In a phase II clinical trial including patients with R/R diffuse large B cell lymphoma, preconditioning with RT before CD19 CAR T cell therapy was related to efficient CAR T cell expansion, additionally showing a higher response rate compared with chemotherapeutic conditioning and a reduced frequency of severe CRS and neurotoxicity, probably due to optimal debulking (NCT03196830) [ ]. Even after relapse upon CAR T cell treatment, an expansion of CAR T cells was achieved upon administering RT, opening the potential of RT as a salvage option for patients relapsing after CAR T cell therapy [ ].

## Combining RT and CAR T Cell Therapy: A Challenge of Optimizing Dosage and Sequence
With promising evidence of synergistic effects between RT and CAR T cell therapy brought forward, more clinical trials in the area are required to figure out the optimal sequence and therapeutic dosage. For example, while RT can deplete local lymphocytes and therefore promote CAR T cell expansion when administered as a depleting conditioning treatment, if administered after CAR T cell infusion, the lymphodepleting effects will in turn antagonize CAR T cell function.
A study compared low-dose radiation (1, 2, or 6 Gy) delivered by Lutetium-177 or Actnimum-225 radionucleotides combined with CAR T cell application. Here, increasing radiation doses resulted in lower CAR T cell viability while simultaneously enhancing their cytotoxic activity against a GD2-expressing neuroblastoma cell line [ ].
Among currently ongoing trials combining RT and CAR T cell therapy, there are two phase I trials in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. One phase Ib trial used systemic administration of prostate stem cell antigen-targeting CAR T cells and simultaneously administered RT of local metastasis (NCT05805371, Table ). The second study uses KLK2-directed CAR T cells and implements RT as a bridging therapy (NCT05022849, Table ). 
Another study explored the application of epidermal growth factor variant III-targeted CAR T cells in patients with newly diagnosed grade IV malignant gliomas after they received standard-of-care treatment with RT and temozolomide. The study has recently been terminated with results still pending (NCT02664363, Table ).
The MD Anderson Cancer Center is currently recruiting patients who received CAR T cell therapy and RT in hematologic malignancies for an observational study (NCT04888338, Table ). A similar multicentric retrospective analysis of 37 patients who received CAR T cell therapy for DCBCL with either bridging or salvage RT showed a lower incidence of neurotoxicity associated with bridging RT. Additionally, salvage RT improved overall survival, especially in patients with localized relapse [ ]. More clinical trials, especially with solid tumors, are required to optimize the dose, timing, and sequence of these treatments.

## Conclusions
The combination of CAR T cell therapy with local interventional treatment modalities, including RT, surgery, local ablation, and locoregional delivery presents a promising avenue for enhancing efficacy in solid cancer treatment. These synergistic approaches harness the strengths of both systemic immunotherapy and local tumor treatment, aiming to overcome the challenges posed by local barriers, tumor heterogeneity, and an immunosuppressive TME. Through mechanisms involving enhanced antigen presentation, reprogramming of the TME, increased intratumoral recruitment of CAR T cells, and (re-)activation of systemic anti-tumor immune responses, combination strategies hold the potential to improve response rates, prolong survival, and broaden the applicability of CAR T cell therapies across various cancer types and stages. However, continued research efforts, including preclinical studies and well-designed clinical trials, are warranted to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, refine treatment regimens and sequences, and ultimately maximize the therapeutic benefits of combining CAR T cell therapies with local treatments in the fight against solid cancers.
