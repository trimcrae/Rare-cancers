## Abstract
Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are one of the main components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) of solid tumors. They have several functions, which all contribute to tumor growth and immune evasion. CAF presence is associated with poor clinical response to standard therapy and immunotherapy. However, its heterogeneity blunts their understanding and functionality. New analytical techniques are being applied to better understand their role in cancer. Meanwhile, CAF stratification and their immunomodulatory capabilities are the scope of many studies, also in the context of ionizing irradiation. In that regard, new immunotherapy strategies have set their sights on targeting CAF to modulate the TME into a more immune-stimulating environment. Among the different approaches, CAF depletion, reprogramming, and CAF-directed antibodies and cytokines can be distinguished, supported by substantial preclinical evidence and encouraging, but still preliminary clinical findings, mainly from early-phase (phase I) trials. Additionally, CAF-directed radionuclides have gained interest and are in clinical trials for therapeutic as well as diagnostic purposes. Here we summarize the studies and clinical developments of CAF-targeted therapies alone and in combination with radiotherapy.

## Introduction
 Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key stromal components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) that profoundly influence cancer progression and therapeutic outcomes. Unlike normal fibroblasts, CAFs exhibit a persistently activated phenotype, characterized by extensive extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and secretion of a broad repertoire of growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines. Through these functions, CAFs promote tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion, and metabolic adaptation, ultimately fostering a protumorigenic and immunosuppressive milieu. High CAF abundance has been consistently associated with poor prognosis across multiple cancer types, underscoring their central role in tumor maintenance and resistance to therapy.
Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have revealed remarkable CAF heterogeneity across and within tumor types, reflecting their diverse origins, phenotypes, and functions. Distinct subsets—such as myofibroblastic (myCAFs), inflammatory (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting—exert specialized roles in ECM organization, cytokine signaling, and immune modulation. Importantly, CAF phenotypes are dynamic and can shift in response to environmental and therapeutic pressures, including exposure to cytotoxic treatments. This functional versatility suggests that CAFs may not only survive conventional anticancer therapies but also actively contribute to treatment resistance, particularly to radiotherapy and immunotherapy.
CAFs are pivotal regulators of the TME, orchestrating ECM remodeling, immunosuppression, and therapy resistance. Their marked heterogeneity and plasticity—including myofibroblastic, inflammatory, and antigen-presenting subtypes—represent both a therapeutic opportunity and a major translational challenge. CAF-directed strategies encompassing depletion, reprogramming, functional inhibition, and radioimmunoconjugates have demonstrated robust activity in preclinical models, enhancing immune infiltration, reducing stromal barriers, and sensitizing tumors to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. In contrast, clinical efficacy to date has been more limited and largely confined to early-phase trials.
A principal obstacle to successful translation lies in the functional diversity and context dependency of CAF states. Targeting approaches based on broadly expressed markers such as fibroblast activation protein (FAP), alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), or platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) may not adequately distinguish tumor-promoting from tumor-restraining fibroblast subsets, potentially limiting efficacy or inducing unintended effects. Furthermore, CAF plasticity and compensatory stromal remodeling under therapeutic pressure may attenuate responses or promote adaptive resistance. These biological complexities, often incompletely recapitulated in current preclinical systems, likely contribute to the discrepancy between promising experimental findings and modest clinical outcomes. Advanced technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics can identify distinct CAF subpopulations and their contributions to tumor progression. This knowledge is essential to develop therapies that selectively target protumorigenic CAFs while sparing tumor-suppressive or homeostatic fibroblasts. Targeting CAF-derived factors or signaling pathways, or reprogramming CAFs into less protumorigenic states, represents promising strategies to modulate the TME without broad stromal depletion.
Emerging evidence nonetheless supports the rationale for combinatorial strategies integrating CAF modulation with immune checkpoint blockade, radiotherapy, or cellular therapies. However, their clinical success will depend on biomarker-driven patient stratification, optimized treatment sequencing, rational dose exploration to balance efficacy and stromal homeostatic functions, and the integration of pharmacodynamic and toxicity biomarkers for safety monitoring. Improved translational platforms—including patient-derived organoids, xenografts, and humanized mouse models—together with high-resolution single-cell and spatial profiling approaches, will be essential to define actionable CAF programs and predictive pharmacodynamic biomarkers. Additionally, CRISPR-based gene editing, nanotechnology, and advanced drug delivery systems offer opportunities to improve specificity, optimize dosing, and minimize off-target toxicity.
In summary, the clinical translation of CAF-targeting strategies remains constrained by CAF heterogeneity, the dynamic nature of the TME, and limitations of existing experimental models. Progress will require precision modulation of defined CAF states rather than broad stromal depletion, alongside adaptive clinical trial frameworks incorporating longitudinal biomarker assessment, dose optimization, and safety monitoring. Such an approach may ultimately bridge the gap between strong preclinical rationale and durable immunotherapeutic benefit.

## Role of CAF on therapy resistance
The influence of CAFs on therapy resistance has become a subject of growing attention, as accumulating evidence indicates that their biological properties can modulate tumor sensitivity to both radiation and immune-based interventions. In solid tumors, CAFs are located intermingling and in close proximity to cancer cells and are thus subjected to the full radiation dose during radiotherapy. The effects of radiation on CAFs and the subsequent implications for therapy outcomes remain debated. Research has shown that fibroblasts possess an intrinsic resistance to radiation, with numerous in vitro studies indicating that CAFs can evade cell death after radiation exposure while developing a senescent phenotype characterized by reduced proliferation and migration capabilities. Investigations into the role of CAFs in tumor radioresistance have revealed potential radioprotective effects of CAFs on cancer cells. Some studies have demonstrated that CAF-conditioned medium enhances the survival and colony-forming abilities of various cancer cells, including pancreatic cancer, lung adenocarcinoma and other tumor types, through the release of soluble factors such as IGF-1, CXCL12, CXCL1, TGF-β, and IL-6. Additionally, other research suggests that radiation may amplify the radioprotective and pro-malignant properties of CAFs, leading to increased cell proliferation and tumor growth when cancer cells are co-cultured or co-injected with pre-irradiated fibroblasts. Conversely, some studies report a decline in protumorigenic functions of CAFs post-irradiation or no significant change in their radioresistant properties, along with decreased expression of modulators such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Some researchers propose that the tumor-promoting effects of radiation-induced senescent stromal cells may be limited to specific organs or tissue types, highlighting the need for further validation across diverse tumor contexts. 
In clinical settings, elevated levels of CAF-specific markers or CAF-related gene signatures in tumor samples have been linked to differential responses to radio(chemo)therapy and consistently correlate with poor prognoses across several cancers, including cervical, breast, prostate, oral squamous cell carcinoma, colon, and rectal tumors. This consistency suggests that CAFs may significantly contribute to radiation resistance in multiple solid tumors. However, worsened tumor development after curative, adjuvant, or neoadjuvant (chemo)radiotherapy is rarely observed, even though local progression under or post-treatment may occur in small subgroups of patients. In contrast, improved outcomes are often reported when radiation is administered preoperatively or postoperatively compared with surgery alone. Such long-term benefits have been observed across multiple malignancies, ranging from early-stage breast cancer to locally advanced colorectal cancer (CRC). Given that nearly all solid tumors contain stroma and CAFs at all developmental stages, clinical evidence indicates that any potential activation of CAFs following radiotherapy is unlikely to be a primary determinant of therapy outcome.
Beyond their interaction with radiation, CAFs also play a pivotal role in shaping the tumor–immune interface, thereby influencing responses to immunotherapy. Stromal and immune cells within tumors engage in a bidirectional crosstalk mediated by both paracrine signaling and contact-dependent interactions. Pro-inflammatory cytokines released by tumor-infiltrating immune cells, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interleukin (IL)-1β, can promote the recruitment and activation of mesenchymal progenitor cells into the tumor bed. In turn, tumor-activated fibroblasts, that is, CAFs, amplify inflammatory reactions by secreting a wide array of chemokines and cytokines that sustain immune cell chemotaxis and polarization within the TME. As a consequence, resident and infiltrating immune cells are frequently skewed towards immunosuppressive phenotypes that favor tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.
Given their central role as regulators of tumor immunity, CAFs are increasingly recognized as key contributors to resistance against immunotherapy. In a landmark study, Kraman and colleagues demonstrated that depletion of FAP+ CAFs enhanced the efficacy of anticancer vaccination in a preclinical lung cancer model. Similarly, targeting CXCL12 derived from FAP-expressing CAFs sensitized otherwise unresponsive pancreatic tumors to combined α-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and α-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) checkpoint blockade. Multiple mechanisms underpin CAF-mediated resistance to immunotherapy. CAFs display constitutive upregulation of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2. Notably, PD-L1 blockade alone does not inhibit PD-1/PD-L2 interaction, suggesting that PD-L2 expression in CAFs may represent an alternative pathway of immune evasion. Furthermore, antigen cross-presentation via major histocompatibility complex-I complexes can induce antigen-specific upregulation of Fas/FasL and PD-1/PD-L2 interactions between T cells and CAFs, leading to selective elimination of tumor-specific T cells. CAF-derived TGF-β signaling represents another hallmark mechanism of immunotherapy resistance: TGF-β directly drives T-cell dysfunction, promotes the expansion of FOXP3+ regulatory T cells expressing PD-1 and CTLA-4, and enhances immune tolerance. Autocrine TGF-β signaling in CAFs also reinforces desmoplasia and ECM remodeling, resulting in the physical exclusion of CD8+ T cells from the tumor parenchyma. Notably, in preclinical models, combined inhibition of TGF-β and PD-L1 reversed these effects and facilitated T-cell infiltration. 
CAFs further contribute to immunosuppression through the recruitment and functional modulation of immune cells via paracrine signaling. They promote T-cell dysfunction by upregulating immune checkpoints such as T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3), PD-1, and CTLA-4, leading to decreased interferon (IFN)-γ, TNF-α, and CD107a expression and an overall reduction in antitumor activity. Additionally, CAFs recruit and polarize myeloid populations, inducing reactive oxygen species–producing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and favoring the accumulation of tumor-promoting M2 macrophages. 
Finally, CAFs actively shape the metabolic landscape of the TME to sustain immune tolerance. Their high glycolytic activity depletes glucose availability, while the release of lactate modulates CD4+ T-cell polarization by suppressing Th1 responses and enhancing regulatory T cell (Treg) accumulation. IFN-γ-activated fibroblasts further contribute to immunosuppression through increased expression of amino acid-degrading enzymes such as indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO) and arginase II, which deplete arginine and impair T-cell function. Moreover, CAFs express elevated surface levels of the ectonucleotidases CD39 and CD73, catalyzing the conversion of extracellular ATP, ADP, and AMP into adenosine—an immunosuppressive metabolite that dampens cytotoxic immune responses. Collectively, these mechanisms highlight CAFs as crucial architects of an immune-suppressive microenvironment that limits the efficacy of current immunotherapeutic interventions.

## CAF-targeting strategies
Given their multifaceted contributions to therapy resistance, encompassing both radiotherapy and immunotherapy, CAFs represent central modulators of the TME that can directly and indirectly protect cancer cells from therapeutic interventions. Their capacity to remodel the ECM, secrete a broad repertoire of cytokines and growth factors, modulate immune cell recruitment and function, and shape the metabolic landscape underscores their pivotal role in sustaining tumor survival and limiting therapy efficacy. These protumorigenic functions, together with their complex interactions with immune cells and their relative genetic stability, position CAFs as compelling targets for anticancer interventions. Targeting CAFs offers the opportunity to remodel the TME into a more permissive, immune-infiltrated, and therapeutically responsive state. The following section outlines five promising CAF-targeted strategies ( ), supported by preclinical ( ) and clinical studies ( ), that are being explored as monotherapies or in combination with conventional and immune-based treatments.
 

## CAF depletion
Among the diverse strategies explored to target CAFs, direct depletion has emerged as a compelling approach to revert the immunosuppressive TME characteristic of CAF-rich malignancies. Selective elimination of CAF may enhance immune infiltration and improve responsiveness to immunotherapy and radiotherapy.

## Immunotherapy-based CAF depletion strategies
Vaccination-based strategies have demonstrated promise for targeting CAFs through immune-mediated elimination of FAP-expressing stromal cells. Early studies using whole-cell tumor vaccines engineered to express murine or human FAP as a xenoantigen induced potent CD8+ T cell-mediated responses against both tumor and stromal compartments, resulting in reduced tumor growth and enhanced intratumoral immune infiltration. More recent work has further refined this concept using epitope-based and minigene vaccine platforms targeting FAP, which elicited antigen-specific T-cell responses and conferred antitumor activity in syngeneic models such as Lewis lung carcinoma and 4T1 breast cancer. Additional refinements to these vaccines—such as encoding DNA-based constructs targeting FAP alone or in combination with tumor antigens and negative regulators of dendritic cell function—have further enhanced immunogenicity and antigen spreading within the TME. , 
Adoptive cell therapies have expanded the toolkit for CAF depletion. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells specific for FAP have shown the ability to deplete fibroblasts and suppress tumor progression in murine models of desmoplastic tumors. Notably, in lung cancer models, preclinical studies have confirmed the efficacy of FAP-targeted CAR-T cells in reducing stromal content and restraining lung tumor growth. More recently, targeted lipid nanoparticle delivery of FAP-CAR messenger RNA has enabled in vivo engineering of CAR-expressing T cells and achieved significant tumor growth inhibition in pancreatic cancer models, highlighting a scalable alternative to conventional adoptive CAR-T approaches. In clinical settings, early-phase trials evaluating FAP-targeted CAR-T cells are ongoing. A phase I study in patients with malignant pleural mesothelioma (NCT01722149) assessed the safety and feasibility of locally administered FAP-specific CAR-T cells, reporting low toxicity and preliminary signs of activity. Another first-in-human trial (NCT03932565) is testing a fourth-generation CAR-T construct targeting both FAP and Nectin-4 in patients with advanced solid tumors.
Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and their derivatives have also been used to deplete CAFs. Sibrotuzumab, a humanized version of the murine anti-FAP antibody mAbF19, has been evaluated in patients with metastatic colorectal and non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs). In a phase I trial (NCT02198274), repeated administration was well tolerated, although no objective responses were observed. More recently, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) targeting FAP have entered early clinical development. Among them, OMTX705, a first-in-class anti-FAP ADC conjugated to a novel tubulysin payload, has shown promising preliminary activity. In a first-in-human phase I dose escalation trial (NCT05547321), OMTX705 was administered either as monotherapy or in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with advanced solid tumors, predominantly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC. According to interim results presented at American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) 2025, the agent demonstrated a favorable safety profile with no dose-limiting toxicities reported across tested cohorts. In combination with pembrolizumab, partial responses were observed in a subset of patients, including heavily pretreated PDAC and MSS CRC cases, with durable responses extending beyond 8–11 months. Moreover, target lesion reductions and prolonged progression-free survival were noted in select patients with PDAC, CRC, and NSCLC. Correlative biomarker analyses revealed high FAP expression in the majority of PDAC and CRC samples, along with increased CD8+ and CD56+ immune infiltration and reduced immunosuppressive cytokine levels in best responders. Histologic analysis confirmed the delivery of the tubulysin payload to both CAFs and tumor epithelial regions, suggesting dual targeting of the stromal and malignant compartments.
CAF depletion represents a promising strategy to overcome stromal-mediated resistance and enhance antitumor immunity. While preclinical and early clinical data are encouraging, further studies are needed to optimize selectivity and minimize potential off-tumor effects, particularly in tissues with physiological FAP expression.

## CAF-targeted radioligand therapy
In line with depletion strategies that seek to eliminate the tumor-supportive functions of CAFs, radiation-based approaches have gained traction through the development of CAF-targeted radionuclide conjugates. These radioimmunoconjugates consist of FAP-binding moieties linked to diagnostic or therapeutic radionuclides, allowing for selective delivery of ionizing radiation to the tumor stroma.
Preclinical studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of this approach. Zboralski et al developed an FAP-targeting peptide (FAP-2287) conjugated to the β-emitting radionuclide lutetium-177 (177Lu), forming 177Lu-FAP-2287. This compound showed selective accumulation in FAP-expressing tumors and resulted in targeted stromal irradiation and tumor control in murine models. A clinical analog, 177Lu-FAP-2286, is currently under evaluation in a first-in-human phase I/II trial (LuMIERE, NCT04939610) for patients with advanced solid tumors.
Beyond therapy, FAP-targeted radionuclides have been applied in diagnostic imaging. Using the same peptide backbone, 68Ga-FAP-2286 enabled high-contrast positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with persistent tumor uptake and favorable biodistribution in preclinical models. These findings were translated to clinical settings, where 68Ga-FAP-2286 PET demonstrated strong tumor retention and good tolerability in patients with adenocarcinomas (NCT05392205). Additional studies are ongoing to further evaluate its diagnostic utility (NCT05180162, NCT04621435).
Earlier efforts also explored radiolabeling of anti-FAP monoclonal antibodies. Sibrotuzumab, the humanized version of mAbF19, was labeled with iodine-131 (131I) in a phase I trial for advanced NSCLC, though toxicity concerns limited its development (NCT02209727). Subsequent refinements using 177Lu-labeled antibodies improved tumor specificity and delayed tumor progression in preclinical models. Additionally, a closely related compound, (177Lu) Lu-NNS309, is under investigation in a phase I study (NCT06562192) for advanced or metastatic pancreatic, lung, breast, and CRCs, further expanding the potential application of FAP-targeted radioligand therapies. Altogether, radionuclide-based CAF targeting represents a promising avenue to selectively disrupt the tumor stroma and may synergize with existing therapies, especially in desmoplastic tumors where stromal shielding impairs therapeutic efficacy. Future studies are warranted to assess their potential in combination with established modalities such as immunotherapy and chemotherapy.

## CAF-directed immunomodulators
Beyond depletion, an emerging strategy leverages CAFs not as targets to eliminate, but as anchoring points to deliver immunomodulatory signals into the TME. These approaches aim to functionally disarm CAFs by converting them into vehicles for localized immune activation, thereby limiting systemic toxicity while enhancing antitumor responses.
Several bispecific constructs and fusion proteins have been developed in this context. More recently, this concept has been advanced through tumor-selective delivery platforms, such as oncolytic viruses engineered to locally express an FAP×CD3 T-cell engager, enabling targeted recruitment and activation of T cells within FAP-rich stromal compartments while limiting systemic exposure. In a related strategy, Claus et al designed an FAP-targeted 4–1BB agonist to promote intratumoral CD8+ T-cell co-stimulation. This construct was specifically optimized to minimize hepatotoxicity, a known limitation of systemic 4–1BB agonists, and showed selective tumor accumulation in non-human primates. Preclinical validation in human primary cells demonstrated enhanced T-cell activation and cytotoxicity. 
Further innovations include an FAP-CD40 fusion protein designed to activate antigen-presenting cells within the TME. This strategy translated into improved T-cell infiltration and tumor control in murine models. A more selective version using designed ankyrin repeat proteins led to MP0317 (α-FAP×CD40), a molecule that restricts CD40 signaling to FAP-positive regions. A phase I clinical trial is currently underway to assess its safety and tolerability in patients with advanced solid tumors (NCT05098405).
CAF-targeted immunocytokines are also under development. One example is FAP-IL2v, an IL-2 variant fused to an FAP-binding domain and engineered to preferentially activate IL-2Rβγ without stimulating regulatory T cells. This construct has shown enhanced CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell activation with a more favorable toxicity profile compared with native IL-2. Similarly, an FAP-targeted IL-12 fusion protein (mIL12-7NP2) demonstrated potent antitumor efficacy and immune activation in preclinical models, while potentially mitigating systemic toxicity. 

## CAF reprogramming
The plasticity and dynamic behavior of CAFs during tumor progression, along with their functional heterogeneity, have prompted the exploration of CAF reprogramming as an alternative therapeutic strategy within broader CAF targeting approaches. Instead of depleting or eliminating CAFs, this strategy aims to revert them to a quiescent or tumor-restraining phenotype, thus remodeling the TME into a less immunosuppressive and more therapy-permissive landscape.
One of the most studied agents in this context is all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), a compound clinically approved for the treatment of acute promyelocytic leukemia. ATRA has shown the ability to induce quiescence in activated fibroblasts, particularly pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), which are a major source of CAFs in PDAC. Mechanistically, ATRA binds to retinoic acid receptor beta on PSCs, leading to reduced ECM deposition and inhibition of tumor cell invasion. Clinical studies have demonstrated that the combination of ATRA with gemcitabine enhanced antitumor efficacy in KPC murine models (NCT04241276). Clinically, the addition of ATRA to standard chemotherapy regimens has shown promising results. A phase I trial combining ATRA with gemcitabine-nab-paclitaxel in patients with PDAC reported a good safety profile and a median overall survival (OS) longer than historically observed with chemotherapy alone (NCT03307148). Beyond pancreatic cancer, ATRA in combination with pembrolizumab demonstrated notable activity in patients with metastatic melanoma, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 71% and a 50% complete response rate, suggesting potential immunomodulatory effects beyond CAF reprogramming (NCT03200847).
In parallel, vitamin D analogs (also known as calcipotriol) have been investigated as stromal reprogramming agents. Calcitriol, a synthetic vitamin D receptor (VDR) signaling within CAFs, resulting in a more normalized stroma and enhanced chemotherapy penetration in PDAC. Beyond preclinical evidence, vitamin D treatment has also been associated with improved relapse-free and OS in patients with early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and low vitamin D levels, suggesting a potential prognostic or adjunctive therapeutic role. However, clinical results in advanced disease remain inconclusive, as shown by the lack of objective responses with seocalcitol in pancreatic cancer and no observed benefit when added to chemotherapy in CRC (NCT01516216). These findings underscore the therapeutic value of combining stromal reprogramming agents with conventional cytotoxic or immunotherapeutic regimens.
More recently, CAF reprogramming has been expanded to include metabolic interventions that functionally rewire stromal cells without depleting them. In pancreatic cancer, CAFs were shown to reutilize tumor-derived lactate to sustain a fibrotic and immunosuppressive microenvironment, and disruption of this metabolic coupling altered CAF function and stromal immune suppression, supporting CAF metabolism as an actionable axis for stromal reprogramming. 

## Inhibition of CAF functions and activation
An alternative therapeutic avenue involves disrupting the signaling pathways driven by CAF-derived factors that support tumor progression and immune evasion.
In this context, TGF-β plays a central role in cancer progression and CAF-mediated immunosuppression, acting as a key regulator of fibroblast activation and differentiation within the TME. Inhibition of TGF-β signaling has been shown to suppress the myCAF phenotype in vitro and to increase CD8+ T-cell infiltration and tumor control in vivo. Furthermore, targeting downstream effectors such as NADPH oxidase 4, a reactive oxygen species(ROS)-generating enzyme activated by TGF-β, has demonstrated the potential to overcome CAF-mediated immune exclusion and sensitize desmoplastic tumors to vaccination and immune checkpoint blockade. Nevertheless, the efficacy of TGF-β inhibition appears to be context-dependent. Biffi et al reported no significant effect on tumor growth on pharmacologic TGF-β receptor blockade, and instead highlighted the dynamic interplay between CAF subtypes and their cytokine environment. Moreover, non-canonical TGF-β signaling pathways, such as TAK1-mediated activation, may sustain fibroblast activation despite upstream inhibition. Dual targeting of TGFBR1 and TAK1 showed improved responses and metastatic control in preclinical models, suggesting that combinatorial blockade may be required for effective reprogramming. In a complementary study, Grauel et al demonstrated that TGF-β inhibition in CAFs led to the emergence of a distinct interferon-responsive CAF population (ilCAF), marked by upregulated IFN-stimulated genes and enhanced immunoregulatory properties. This highlights the potential of pathway interference not only to block CAF activation but to reshape their functional phenotype. At the clinical level, multiple pharmacological approaches have been developed to inhibit TGF-β signaling. Galunisertib (LY2157299), a selective TGF-βR1 kinase inhibitor, and LY3200882 have undergone clinical evaluation in solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, showing acceptable safety profiles (NCT02304419, NCT02008318). Other small-molecule inhibitors such as vactosertib have shown efficacy in preclinical models of multiple myeloma and CRC and are currently under investigation in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (NCT03143985). Alternative strategies include the use of TGF-β ligand traps. AVID200, a computationally optimized receptor ectodomain that sequesters TGF-β1 and TGF-β3 isoforms, has shown to enhance T cell-mediated cytotoxicity and potentiate anti-PD-1 responses in syngeneic tumor models. Its clinical safety is being assessed in early-phase trials (NCT03834662). Monoclonal antibodies targeting TGF-β ligands have also been developed. Fresolimumab, a pan-TGF-β neutralizing antibody, showed initial antitumor activity in a phase I trial (NCT00356460), but subsequent studies failed to confirm significant immunomodulatory benefit or clinical efficacy, particularly in high-grade glioma (NCT01472731). Similarly, SAR439459 demonstrated synergy with anti-PD-1 therapy preclinically, but its clinical development was discontinued due to limited efficacy and adverse events, including bleeding complications (NCT03192345). Among bifunctional approaches, the fusion protein M7824 (bintrafusp alfa), combining a TGF-β trap with an anti-PD-L1 antibody, exhibited promising preclinical activity, a novel bifunctional fusion protein targeting the PD-L1 and TGF-β pathways) but failed to show superiority over standard-of-care pembrolizumab in a phase III trial for NSCLC (NCT02517398). Finally, SRK-181, a latent TGF-β1 activation inhibitor, has shown potential to enhance anti-PD-1 responses without inducing systemic toxicity in preclinical models. A phase I trial reported favorable safety as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab, although clinical efficacy is still under investigation (NCT04291079). Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity of targeting the TGF-β axis in the context of CAF-driven immunosuppression. While some approaches show promise in reshaping the TME and improving immune responses, challenges related to specificity, safety, and tumor context remain significant.
CAF-mediated ECM remodeling leads to increased tissue rigidity and interstitial pressure, creating a dense stromal barrier that limits immune infiltration and drug delivery. Strategies to counteract this include targeting ECM components or associated pathways. LOXL2, involved in collagen crosslinking, promotes stiffness and angiogenesis; its inhibition reduces tumor burden. Hyaluronan, a major ECM component secreted by CAFs, contributes to elevated interstitial pressure and therapeutic resistance. Its enzymatic degradation with PEGPH20 decompresses tumor vessels and enhances drug perfusion. However, clinical trials with PEGPH20 showed limited benefit in unselected PDAC populations and the trial was discontinued due to toxicity and modest efficacy (NCT00834704). Novel delivery platforms, such as the oncolytic virus VCN-01 encoding hyaluronidase, have shown promising results in early-phase trial (NCT02045602). Additionally, angiotensin inhibition (eg, losartan) has been shown to modulate ECM composition and improve vascular perfusion. Other stromal targets include focal adhesion kinase (FAK), a regulator of fibrosis and immune exclusion. While MMPs degrade ECM, they paradoxically promote remodeling and tumor progression. MMP inhibitors (eg, S-3304) have entered clinical trials in solid tumors (NCT00033215, NCT00033566, NCT00078390), though with limited success to date.

## CAF pathway specific inhibitors
CAFs shape the TME through secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that drive immunosuppression and resistance. Targeting the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis limits MDSC and macrophage recruitment, improving response to ICIs. Inhibitors like AMD3100, BL-8040, and ulocuplumab are under clinical evaluation have shown clinical benefit when combined with chemotherapy or ICIs.
IL-6, a key CAF-derived cytokine, promotes Treg induction and suppresses cytotoxic immunity. Clazakizumab, another anti-IL-6 antibody, showed benefit in cancer cachexia. Downstream, the JAK/STAT3 pathway is a critical axis of IL-6 signaling and is being targeted using ruxolitinib and STAT3-directed shRNAs (NCT02117479). MSC-1, an anti-LIF antibody targeting another iCAF-secreted cytokine with JAK/STAT-activating potential, is also in clinical trials (NCT03490669). Other ILs like IL-10, IL-11, and IL-22 also contribute to immune evasion, with various antibodies and receptor modulators in early trials.
For instance, IL-10 neutralization has shown enhanced antitumor immune activity in preclinical CRC models, while paradoxically, IL-10 receptor agonists like pegilodecakin have demonstrated ORRs of up to 43% in NSCLC when combined with PD-1 blockade, though results in PDAC have been less encouraging.
CAF-secreted CXC chemokines like CXCL1 and CXCL8 drive tumor progression and immune resistance. Inhibiting CXCL1 reversed radioresistance in esophageal carcinoma models, and CXCL8 blockade has shown synergy with ICIs in early trials. CXCR2 inhibitors like AZD5069 are being assessed in combination with enzalutamide or ICIs in prostate cancer, while dual CXCR1/2 inhibition (eg, SX-682) is under clinical testing in combination with cancer vaccines or ICIs.
Beyond immunomodulatory cytokines, CAFs produce growth factors such as hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which activates c-MET signaling on tumor cells, promoting proliferation and metastasis. Anti-HGF antibodies (eg, ficlatuzumab, emibetuzumab) have shown limited monotherapy efficacy (NCT01900652), (NCT00725634). Additionally, the FGF/FGFR pathway, often altered in cancer, is being successfully targeted with selective TKIs (eg, erdafitinib, pemigatinib), showing ORRs of ~40% in urothelial carcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma. Other FGFR inhibitors (AZD4547, dovitinib, ponatinib) and FGFR-targeted antibodies (bemarituzumab, LY3076226) (NCT02529553) are under clinical evaluation with variable success, with some studies reporting high response rates in biomarker-enriched subgroups. Combining FGFR-targeted agents with ICIs or chemotherapy may enhance efficacy in selected patients.
Collectively, targeting CAF-derived cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors—especially IL-6, CXCL12/CXCR4, HGF/c-MET, and FGF/FGFR signaling—represents a promising therapeutic strategy to reprogram the TME and overcome immunosuppression, with several agents currently in preclinical and clinical development.

## CAF-targeting in combination with other therapies
In view of the promising results by some of the abovementioned strategies, they were combined with other therapies preclinically and mainly clinically ( ). Among CAF depletion strategies, FAP-targeting approaches have been explored in combination with radiotherapy and immunotherapy. A FAP-based vaccine combined with hypofractionated irradiation (3×8 Gy) modulated the immunosuppressive TME, reducing lung metastasis and increasing CD8+ T-cell-dependent antitumor activity while decreasing Tregs and MDSCs in 4T1 tumors. Similarly, co-targeting CAFs and tumor cells using FAP-directed and EphA2-directed CAR-T cells enhanced antitumor efficacy and survival compared with single-target therapies. In pancreatic cancer, the FAP inhibitor UAMC-1110 combined with hypofractionated radiotherapy (3×10 Gy) delayed tumor growth and promoted immune infiltration, although tumor control remained limited, and the addition of anti-PD-1 failed to improve efficacy. 
Radioligand-based FAP targeting has also shown promise in combination settings. In murine fibrosarcoma, ¹⁷⁷Lu-FAP-2287 synergized with PD-1 blockade, enhancing CD8+ T-cell infiltration, upregulating STING-related IFN-I genes, and promoting a proinflammatory myeloid phenotype. Likewise, the dimeric construct ¹⁷⁷Lu-DOTA-2P(FAPI)₂, designed for improved FAP binding and tumor retention, achieved complete tumor control and 100% survival in FAP-overexpressing CT26 tumors when combined with PD-L1 blockade, with neutrophil-mediated CXCL2 expression contributing to antitumor efficacy. 
Within CAF-directed immune modulation, several bispecific and immunocytokine-based strategies have been explored to enhance T-cell activation and antitumor immunity. FAP-targeted 4–1 BBL constructs have shown strong potential when combined with other immunotherapies. In preclinical models, co-stimulation through FAP-4-1BBL together with T-cell activation by carcinoembryonic antigen T-cell bispecific antibody (CEA-TCB) or radiotherapy enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration, tumor clearance, and survival, while irradiation was shown to improve intratumoral FAP expression and ligand delivery. The human surrogate FAP-4-1BBL (RO7122290) demonstrated T-cell activation and proliferation in peripheral blood and tumors of patients treated with atezolizumab, with ongoing clinical evaluation in combination with bispecific T-cell engagers (NCT04826003). Other fibroblast-directed immunomodulators have also shown promise. The bispecific FAP-CD40 agonist promoted CD8+ T-cell-dependent and cDC1-dependent tumor control with a favorable safety profile compared with systemic CD40 agonists, both in preclinical models and early clinical studies (NCT04857138). Likewise, the immunocytokine FAP-IL2v enhanced NK and CD8+ T-cell responses and prolonged survival in combination with HER2, PD-L1, CD40, or CEA-targeted agents, with encouraging immunological activity observed in patients with cervical squamous cell carcinoma treated alongside atezolizumab (NCT03386721, NCT03875079). , Finally, ADCs incorporating an FAP-targeting moiety have shown preclinical synergy with PD-1 blockade, leading to tumor regression and increased CD8+/CD4+ T-cell ratios. A phase I trial is currently assessing their safety and efficacy in combination with pembrolizumab in advanced solid tumors (NCT05547321). 
CAF reprogramming strategies aim to revert activated fibroblasts to a quiescent state, thereby reducing desmoplasia and improving therapeutic penetration. Clinical trials have evaluated this concept in pancreatic cancer, where stromal fibrosis limits drug delivery. The vitamin A derivative ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid) combined with gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel demonstrated safety and tolerability in a phase I trial (NCT03307148), with an ongoing phase II study assessing efficacy and resectability (NCT04241276). Similarly, the VDR agonist paricalcitol is being investigated in combination with gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel in metastatic pancreatic cancer (NCT03520790), aiming to remodel the stromal barrier and enhance chemotherapy response.
CAF pathway–specific inhibitors target signaling axes that mediate fibroblast-driven immunosuppression and therapy resistance. The CXCL12–CXCR4 pathway, a key regulator of immune exclusion, has been extensively explored in combination settings. In the clinic, a phase II trial of CXCR4 antagonism combined with PD-1 inhibition in metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma demonstrated increased intratumoral T-cell recruitment, but also revealed a concomitant accumulation of immunosuppressive macrophages. Other CXCR4 antagonists, such as balixafortide combined with eribulin in metastatic breast cancer demonstrated favorable safety but limited efficacy (FORTRESS trial, NCT03786094), while the CXCL12 inhibitor olaptesed plus pembrolizumab induced Th1-skewed immune signatures and expansion of promonocytic subsets in colorectal and pancreatic cancer (NCT03168139). Other stromal signaling pathways are under investigation. IL-6/IL-6R blockade using siltuximab with PD-1 inhibition (spartalizumab) showed acceptable tolerability but no objective responses in advanced pancreatic cancer (NCT04191421), with ongoing correlative analyses on stromal and immune biomarkers. Similarly, inhibition of LIF signaling with MSC-1 combined with durvalumab and chemotherapy is being evaluated for safety and preliminary activity in advanced solid tumors (NCT04999969). Finally, anti-HGF/MET inhibitors such as ficlatuzumab and emibetuzumab have shown modest antitumor effects when paired with EGFR or VEGFR blockade in head and neck (NCT03422536) and hepatocellular carcinomas (NCT02082210), respectively.
CAF function inhibition therapies aim to neutralize fibroblast-driven signaling and ECM remodeling that promote therapy resistance. Among these, TGF-β pathway blockade has been most actively explored. The TGF-βR inhibitor galunisertib combined with PD-1 blockade showed transcriptional signatures of IFN-γ activation and reduced cell adhesion in early trials (NCT02423343), while durvalumab plus TGF-β inhibition demonstrated acceptable safety but limited activity in metastatic pancreatic cancer (NCT02734160). Radiotherapy combinations have also been tested: TGF-βR inhibition with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) in hepatocellular carcinoma enhanced circulating PD-1+TIGIT+ CD8+ T cells and reduced activated Tregs (NCT02906397). 
Beyond TGF-β, multiple ECM-modulating approaches have been evaluated to improve drug and immune cell penetration. Hedgehog and LOXL2 inhibitors (vismodegib, simtuzumab) failed to enhance chemotherapy efficacy in CRCs (NCT01064622, NCT01479465, NCT01472198). Likewise, hyaluronan degradation using PEGPH20 did not improve survival, despite acceptable safety, halting its clinical development (NCT02715804, NCT03634332).
Other stromal signaling modulators have shown more promise preclinically. Angiotensin receptor blockers such as losartan reprogrammed the tumor matrix, improving immunotherapy responses and radiosensitivity in murine breast and pancreatic models. Clinical studies are ongoing testing losartan with chemotherapy (NCT05077800), with SBRT and pembrolizumab in head and neck cancer (NCT06211335), and with radiotherapy to mitigate fibrosis (NCT05637216, NCT01821729). FAK inhibition also enhances chemosensitivity and immunotherapy efficacy in fibrotic tumors ; clinical evaluation of defactinib plus gemcitabine and pembrolizumab confirmed safety and immune activation (NCT02546531), with ongoing SBRT combinations (NCT04331041). Finally, MMP9 inhibition (andecaliximab) and integrin α5 targeting have been investigated to limit ECM deposition and restore PD-L1 blockade efficacy. 

## Summary
CAFs are pivotal regulators of the TME, orchestrating ECM remodeling, immunosuppression, and therapy resistance. Their marked heterogeneity and plasticity—including myofibroblastic, inflammatory, and antigen-presenting subtypes—represent both a therapeutic opportunity and a major translational challenge. CAF-directed strategies encompassing depletion, reprogramming, functional inhibition, and radioimmunoconjugates have demonstrated robust activity in preclinical models, enhancing immune infiltration, reducing stromal barriers, and sensitizing tumors to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. In contrast, clinical efficacy to date has been more limited and largely confined to early-phase trials.
A principal obstacle to successful translation lies in the functional diversity and context dependency of CAF states. Targeting approaches based on broadly expressed markers such as FAP, α-SMA, or PDGFR may not adequately distinguish tumor-promoting from tumor-restraining fibroblast subsets, potentially limiting efficacy or inducing unintended effects. Furthermore, CAF plasticity and compensatory stromal remodeling under therapeutic pressure may attenuate responses or promote adaptive resistance. These biological complexities, often incompletely recapitulated in current preclinical systems, likely contribute to the discrepancy between promising experimental findings and modest clinical outcomes. Advanced technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics can identify distinct CAF subpopulations and their contributions to tumor progression. This knowledge is essential to develop therapies that selectively target protumorigenic CAFs while sparing tumor-suppressive or homeostatic fibroblasts. Targeting CAF-derived factors or signaling pathways, or reprogramming CAFs into less protumorigenic states, represents promising strategies to modulate the TME without broad stromal depletion.
Emerging evidence nonetheless supports the rationale for combinatorial strategies integrating CAF modulation with immune checkpoint blockade, radiotherapy, or cellular therapies. However, their clinical success will depend on biomarker-driven patient stratification, optimized treatment sequencing, rational dose exploration to balance efficacy and stromal homeostatic functions, and the integration of pharmacodynamic and toxicity biomarkers for safety monitoring. Improved translational platforms—including patient-derived organoids, xenografts, and humanized mouse models—together with high-resolution single-cell and spatial profiling approaches, will be essential to define actionable CAF programs and predictive pharmacodynamic biomarkers. Additionally, CRISPR-based gene editing, nanotechnology, and advanced drug delivery systems offer opportunities to improve specificity, optimize dosing, and minimize off-target toxicity.
In summary, the clinical translation of CAF-targeting strategies remains constrained by CAF heterogeneity, the dynamic nature of the TME, and limitations of existing experimental models. Progress will require precision modulation of defined CAF states rather than broad stromal depletion, alongside adaptive clinical trial frameworks incorporating longitudinal biomarker assessment, dose optimization, and safety monitoring. Such an approach may ultimately bridge the gap between strong preclinical rationale and durable immunotherapeutic benefit.
