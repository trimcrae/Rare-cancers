## O1 Whole-Body MRI Quantitative Biomarkers as Early Prognostic Tools in patients with metastatic bone disease from breast cancer

## Caterina Pizzi1, Caterina Sattin1, Fabio Zugni2

## 1University of Milan, Milan, Italy; 2European Institute of Oncology, Milan, Italy

## Correspondence: Caterina Pizzi, caterina.pizzi@ieo.it
Cancer Imaging, 25(1): O1

Purpose

To evaluate the prognostic potential of quantitative whole-body MRI (WB-MRI) biomarkers (size, apparent diffusion coefficient [ADC], and relative fat fraction [rF%]), and of the response assessment category (RAC) from MET-RADS-P guidelines in patients with bone-metastatic breast cancer (bMBC).

Material and methods

We enrolled bMBC patients who underwent WB-MRI at baseline and every 12 weeks until progression after systemic anti-cancer therapy (SACT). We correlated WB-MRI biomarkers and maximum RAC at time point 1 (TP1) with overall survival (OS). Patients were grouped into maximum RAC 1 (decrease in size, ADC ≥ 1400 µm²/s or ADC increase ≥ 40% from baseline or rF% ≥ 20%) and RAC 2-3-4-5 (size stable or smaller with ADC from ≤1000 µm²/s to <1400 µm²/s or >25% but <40% ADC increase from baseline or any rF% for RAC 2; size stable with ADC < 1400 µm²/s and rF% < 20% for RAC 3; size bigger with any ADC value and rF% < 20% for RAC 4-5). OS was analysed with Kaplan-Meier curves, log-rank tests, and Cox regression hazard ratios (HR), comparing three-year survival and median survival.

Results

Of 38 patients, RAC 1 (n=13) showed significantly better OS than RAC 2-3-4-5 (n=25) at TP1 (p=0.025). Median OS was not reached for RAC 1, while was 36 months for RAC 2-3-4-5. HR for RAC 2-3-4-5 was 7.4 (95% CI 0.9–58.6). Three-year OS was 85.7% for RAC 1 versus 42.3% for RAC 2-3-4-5 (p=0.0025).

Conclusion

Quantitative WB-MRI biomarkers and RAC at TP1 demonstrate significant prognostic value in bMBC patients receiving SACT.

## O2 Lesion Detection: intraindividual comparison of high-relaxivity gadopiclenol at 0.05 mmol/kg versus gadobutrol at 0.1 mmol/kg

## Wolfgang Schima1, Jeong-Min Lee2, Dow-Mu Koh3

## 1Goettlicher Heiland Krankenhaus Department of Diagnostic and Interventional Radiology, Vienna, Austria; 2Seoul National University Hospital, Seoul, Republic of Korea; 3Royal Marsden Hospital, London, United Kingdom

## Correspondence: Dow-Mu Koh, dow-mu.koh@icr.ac.uk
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O2

Objective

To intra-individually compare 0.05 mmol/kg of high-relaxivity gadopiclenol with an approved 0.1 mmol/kg gadobutrol dose for lesion detection in the pelvis, abdomen, and musculoskeletal system. 

Methods

Re-evaluation of anonymised body MR images from a phase III cross-over trial (PROMISE) was performed in randomised order by three blinded radiologists. Number and location of lesions identified by each reader on each image set were recorded and mapped. Detected lesions were tracked and matched by an additional “concordance” reader. Inter- and intra-reader agreement was determined. Finally, potential clinical impact of discordant findings was assessed by the blinded readers in consensus.

Results

Overall, 170 patients were evaluated (58, 95 and 17 referred for imaging of the pelvis, abdomen and musculoskeletal system, respectively). Readers 1, 2, and 3 detected 545, 543, and 475 lesions with gadopiclenol and 548, 532, and 464 lesions with gadobutrol, respectively. Concordance in detection was achieved for 94.3%, 94.4% and 96.6% of all lesions detected with gadobutrol by readers 1, 2 and 3, respectively. Inter-reader agreement of 75.9% and 77.7% was noted for gadobutrol and gadopiclenol, respectively. The reason for most discordant findings was reader related. “Contrast agent” was identified as cause for discordance for 7 lesions seen only with gadopiclenol. A potential impact on patient management was determined for three patients for lesions seen only with gadopiclenol.

Conclusion

Lesion detectability is similar for 0.05 mmol/kg gadopiclenol and 0.1 mmol/kg gadobutrol across different body regions despite only half the amount of administered gadolinium with gadopiclenol.

## O3 Multiparametric MRI for prediction of HCC response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy with and without radiotherapy

## F. Restrepo1, E. H. Bhuiyan1, O. Bane1, E. Ozkaya1, M. Liu1, P. Kennedy1, M. Shareef1, A. Geahchan1, P. Hamon1, M. Buckup1, S. Gnjatic1, L. Brennan2, S. Hectors2, H. K. Cheung2, E. Miller2, M. I. Fiel1, S. Ward1, M. Schwartz1, T. Marron1, M. Merad1, B. Taouli1

## 1Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, New York, USA; 2Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Tarrytown, USA

## Correspondence: F. Restrepo, sturlita@gmail.com
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O3

Aim

To assess the value of multiparametric (mp)MRI - T1 mapping, MR elastography (MRE), dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI), and intravoxel-incoherent motion model of diffusion-weighted imaging (IVIM-DWI) - for predicting resectable HCC response to immunotherapy with/without radiotherapy. The added value of circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) for HCC response prediction was also assessed.

Methods

This IRB-approved prospective study included 35 patients (M/F:28/7, age:63.8±10.9y, range:38-82y) scheduled for HCC resection. 17 patients received two cemiplimab (anti-PD-1) doses before resection; 18 received 3x8Gy doses of radiation before cemiplimab. Tumour native and hepatobiliary phase (HBP) T1 relaxation time, stiffness, and flow/perfusion parameters were measured on baseline mpMRI before and after injection of hepatobiliary contrast agent. Pre-treatment ctDNA was quantified for 16 patients using a tumour-specific, 16-clonal-variant sequencing assay. The performance of mpMRI parameters with/without ctDNA in predicting significant tumour necrosis (STN≥50%) on resection and radiographic response (RECIST/mRECIST) was evaluated. Area under the curve (AUC) and Mann-Whitney U-tests were used for responders/non-responders classification.

Results

Baseline native (AUC=0.76, p=0.022) and HBP (AUC=0.83, p=0.004) T1 were significantly higher in tumours showing STN, while uptake peak concentration (AUC=0.75, p=0.025) was lower. RECIST responders had higher tumour stiffness on 3D-MRE (AUC=0.79, p=0.044). Tumour native T1 (AUC=0.79, p=0.022) and diffusion coefficient (AUC=0.75, p=0.043) identified mRECIST responders. In combination with ctDNA, tumour HBP T1, time-to-peak, upslope, peak concentration, mean transit time, arterial flow and diffusion coefficient were significantly associated with STN (AUC>0.84, p<0.040). 

Conclusion

mpMRI can identify HCC responders to neoadjuvant immunotherapy with/without radiation therapy, especially in combination with ctDNA. 

## O4 FAPI PET and MRI Correlates of Tumour Microenvironment Dynamics in Hepatocellular Carcinoma: A Preliminary Study

## Bhanu Prakash KN1, Robert John Walsh2, Juncheng Hwang3, Lim Mei Chin3, Chee Cheng Ean4, Pek Lan Khong5,6

## 1Lead, Quantitative Imaging & Data Driven Modelling, Clinical Imaging Research Centre, Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore, Singapore, Singapore; 2Department of Haematology-Oncology, NCIS, National University Hospital, Singapore, Singapore; 3Department of Diagnostic Imaging, Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore, Singapore, Singapore; 4Adjunct Associate Professor, Department of Haematology-Oncology, NCIS, National University Hospital, Singapore, Singapore; 5Professor, Department of Diagnostic Imaging, Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore, Singapore, Singapore; 6Director, Clinical Imaging Research Center, Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore, Singapore, Singapore

## Correspondence: Bhanu Prakash KN, bhanu@nus.edu.sg
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O4

Objective

This preliminary study aims to correlate fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI) PET uptake with multiparametric MRI biomarkers to elucidate the relationship between tumour microenvironment (TME) characteristics - specifically stromal fibroblast activity - and imaging phenotypes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). 

Methods

Ten HCC subjects (stages/grades varied) underwent FAPI PET/MRI with Primovist-enhanced DCE-MRI, diffusion-weighted imaging (ADC/TRACE), structural sequences (T1 VIBE, T2 HASTE), and multiphase post-contrast imaging. Automated liver segmentation via nnUNet (validated against manual ground truth) enabled regional classification of FAPI uptake (SUV-based: normal, low, medium, high). Lesion uptake patterns and correlations with DCE-MRI (wash-in/wash-out rates, enhancement maps) and diffusion parameters were analysed. 

Results

Venous-phase nnUNet segmentation showed optimal accuracy (Dice score: 0.96). Medium/high FAPI uptake regions (predominantly lesions) demonstrated arterial hyperenhancement, elevated TRACE values (p<0.001), marginally higher ADC (vs. low/no uptake) (p<0.05), rapid wash-in/wash-out rates (p<0.001), and delayed-phase hypoenhancement (p<0.001). These findings align with stromal-rich TME profiles, where fibroblast-driven vascularization may accelerate contrast kinetics. High stromal activity, inferred from FAPI avidity, correlated with aggressive hemodynamic and diffusion phenotypes. 
Clinical Significance: FAPI PET/MRI non-invasively maps stromal activity, offering a biomarker for HCC aggressiveness and TME-driven vascular remodelling. 

Conclusion

This pilot study establishes FAPI PET/MRI as a promising tool for stratifying HCC based on stromal biology. Recruitment is ongoing to validate these findings in a larger cohort. If confirmed, this approach could refine personalised therapy by integrating fibroblast activity and hemodynamic MRI biomarkers.

Grant acknowledgement

NUS Start-up grant (NUHSRO/2021/073/Startup/08)

 

## O5 Right to remain silent? Evaluating LLMs on complete and incomplete radiology reports for ccRCC likelihood scoring

## Amit Gupta, Pallavi Jha, Ajeith S, Krithika Rangarajan

## All India Institute of Medical Sciences New Delhi, New Delhi, India

## Correspondence: Amit Gupta, amit.aiims2014@gmail.com
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O5

Purpose

To evaluate large-language-model (LLM) performance to assign Clear-Cell-Likelihood-Scores (ccLS) from renal mass MRI reports - specifically assessing performance on complete versus deliberately incomplete inputs.

Methods

Fifty complete and fifty information-deficient fictitious MRI reports were generated across 25 ccLS scoring scenarios. Two radiologists (5-y and 2-y experience) provided reference interpretations. GPT-4o, Gemini-Flash-2.0, and their reasoning versions (GPT-o4-mini, Gemini-Pro) were evaluated under zero-shot (ZS), few-shot (FS), and FS + chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting at various temperatures. Accuracy, hallucination rates, and inference latency were measured.

Results

Incomplete Reports: ZS prompting failed catastrophically (GPT/ZS accuracy = 0.14; hallucination rate = 90%). For non-reasoning models, FS/CoT improved performance modestly (Gemini/FS accuracy = 0.42). Only reasoning-enabled models matched human rejection behaviour; achieving 98% accuracy with ≤2% hallucinations (vs. humans 98–100%).
Complete Reports: ZS prompting again lagged (accuracy ≤0.32), but all models improved markedly with FS/CoT strategies. Both reasoning and non-reasoning LLMs achieved near-perfect accuracy (GPT-o4-mini/FS and Gemini-Pro/FS = 1.00), closely matching radiologists (accuracy = 0.96–1.00).
Inference Time: LLMs using FS/CoT prompting responded in <1 s (GPT-4o/FS = 0.66 s), substantially faster than human readers (11–19 s).

Conclusion

LLMs can accurately assign ccLS when complete radiology report information is available, especially with few-shot or chain-of-thought prompting. However, they struggle to recognise when input is insufficient, often hallucinating confident but incorrect predictions. Newer reasoning models, by contrast, demonstrate near-human accuracy and the crucial ability to remain selectively silent when faced with underspecified.

## O7 Advancing clinically relevant research using The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA)

## Linda Larson-Prior, Tracy Nolan, Kirk Smith, Sonya Utecht, Fred Prior

## University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences, Little Rock, USA

## Correspondence: Linda Larson-Prior, LJLarsonprior@uams.edu
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O7

Aim

We assess the clinical and scientific impact of Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) and how this open access data sharing resource has added to our knowledge of cancer biology and clinical decision support. 

Materials & methods

TCIA publishes imaging together with data from collection authors about each clinical trial or research project it hosts. Submission details (from internal records) and usage statistics (via Google Analytics as well as citations in scientific publications, patents, and FDA-approved medical devices), were mined to accumulate metrics of scientific and clinical utility.

Results

TCIA collections represent worldwide imaging data from 102 unique sites. Data are derived from selected research projects (173), clinical trials (25), and challenge competitions (22). Data cover >100 cancer types imaged across 60 body regions; 68 of 220 collections contain Radiology/Pathology correlations. As of May 2025, 3121 peer reviewed publications cite reuse of TCIA data, 1216 exploring AI and machine learning and 267 with clinical applications. TCIA data has formed the basis of 111 graduate theses and dissertations. For Translation of medical device research to the clinic it frequently begins with the patent process followed by governmental approval. TCIA data has been used in 36 US patents and 3 FDA approved medical devices.

Conclusions

The promise of open science: sharing of research and clinical data, lies in transparent reproducible dissemination of large amounts of data across representative human populations, opening new avenues for both clinical and basic science research. Open repositories like TCIA provide valuable scientific discovery unique educational opportunities.

## O8 Artificial intelligence and radiologists in pancreatic cancer detection using standard of care CT (PANORAMA)

## Natalia Alves1, Megan Schuurmans1, Dawid Rutkowski2, Anindo Saha1, Pierpaolo Vendittelli1, Tijmen de Haas1, Nancy Obuchowski3, Marjolein Liedenbaum4, Ingfrid Haldorsen4, Anders Molven5, Derya Yakar6, Jeroen Geerdink7, Sebastiaan van Koeverden1, Deniece Riviere1, Wulphert Verderink1, Robbert de Haas6, Namkug Kim8, Matthias Löhr2, Garima Suman9, Klaus Maier-Hein10, Horst Hahn11, Weichung Wang12, Alan Yuille13, Avinash Kambadakone14, Elliot Fishman13, Caroline Verbeke15, Geert Litjens1, John Hermans1, Henkjan Huisman1

## 1RadboudUMC, Nijmegen, Netherlands; 2Karolinska, Stockholm, Sweden; 3Cleveland Clinic, Cleveland, USA; 4Haukeland University Hospital, Bergen, Norway; 5University of Bergen, Bergen, Norway; 6UMC Groningen, Groningen, Netherlands; 7Ziekenhuis Groep Twente, Almelo, Netherlands; 8i University of Ulsan College of Medicine, Seoul, Korea, Republic of. 9Mayo Clinic, Rochester, USA; 10German Cancer Research Center Heidelberg, Heidelberg, Germany; 11University of Bremen and Fraunhofer Institute for Medical Imaging MEVIS, Bremen, Germany; 12National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan; 13Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, USA; 14Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, USA; 15University of Oslo, Oslo, Norway

## Correspondence: Tijmen de Haas, tijmen.dehaas@radboudumc.nl
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O8
Background

Pancreatic cancer has the worst prognosis of major cancers, largely due to late diagnosis on contrast-enhanced CT. Artificial intelligence (AI) may improve detection, but large-scale comparisons to radiologists are limited. We created an open-source benchmark to evaluate AI for pancreatic cancer detection on CECT and compared its performance to radiologists.
Methods

In this international, paired, non-inferiority study, the AI system was trained and externally validated using 2310 patients from four centres (Netherlands, USA), and tested on a cohort of 1130 patients from five centres (Netherlands, Sweden, Norway). A reader study involving 68 radiologists (40 centres, 12 countries; median 9 [IQR 6–14.5] years of experience) was conducted on a subset of 391 patients from the test set. The reference standard was based on histopathology and at least three years of clinical follow-up. Primary endpoints were sensitivity, specificity, and AUROC. The protocol prespecified a non-inferiority margin of 0.05, followed by a superiority test.
Findings

Among 3440 patients (Jan 2004–Dec 2023), 1103 had pancreatic cancer. In the 391-patient reader subset (144 positive), AI achieved non-inferior performance (p<0·0001) with an AUROC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.89–0.94), compared to the radiologist average AUROC of 0.88 (95% CI 0.85–0.91) with superiority pending. In the full test cohort (n=1130, 406 positive), AI achieved an AUROC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.90–0.93).
Interpretation

AI demonstrated non-inferior performance to radiologists for detecting pancreatic cancer on routine CECT scans. Given its consistently high AUROC, AI holds promise for aiding earlier diagnosis and improving outcomes.

## O9 Automated Radiomics Prediction Model for Therapy Response and Minimal Residual Disease from Baseline MRI in Multiple Myeloma

## Fabian Bauer1,2, Marina Hajiyianni3, Niels Weinhold3, Hartmut Goldschmidt3,4, Tim F. Weber5, Heinz-Peter Schlemmer1, Klaus H. Maier-Hein6,4, Peter Neher6,4, Markus Wennmann1,5

## 1Division of Radiology, German Cancer Research Center (DKFZ), Heidelberg, Germany; 2Institute for Diagnostic and Interventional Radiology, Faculty of Medicine and University Hospital Cologne, University of Cologne, Cologne, Germany; 3Internal Medicine V, Hematology, Oncology and Rheumatology, Heidelberg University Hospital, Heidelberg, Germany; 4National Center for Tumor Diseases (NCT), University Hospital Heidelberg, Heidelberg, Germany; 5Diagnostic and Interventional Radiology, University Hospital Heidelberg, Heidelberg, Germany; 6Division of Medical Image Computing, German Cancer Research Center (DKFZ), Heidelberg, Germany

## Correspondence: Fabian Bauer, fabian.bauer@uk-koeln.de
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O9

Aim

To establish and validate automated radiomics models for predicting therapy response (TR) and minimal residual disease (MRD) from baseline MRI in newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (MM) patients 

Materials & Methods

This retrospective multicenter study analyzed 118 MM patients enrolled in the GMMG-HD7 trial (EudraCT: 2017-004768-37) who underwent baseline MRI and had TR and/or MRD data following induction therapy. Patients were divided by center into a training cohort (centers 1–2; n = 79) and a test cohort (centers 3–10; n = 39). TR was defined as very good partial response or better. An in-house developed nnU-Net model was used to segment pelvic bone marrow, from which 245 radiomics features were extracted and normalized using the piriformis muscle. Random forest classifiers were built to predict TR and MRD using: (I) radiomics features alone, (II) radiomics plus general confounders, (III) radiomics combined with myeloma-specific clinical data, and (IV) clinical data alone. Predictive performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and F1 score.

Results

The radiomics-only model (I) achieved the best performance for predicting TR in the independent test cohort, with an AUROC of 0.70 (F1 score: 0.84). For MRD prediction, AUROC values across radiomics-driven models (I–III) ranged between 0.52 and 0.54 (F1 scores: 0.44 to 0.48).

Conclusions

Machine learning models leveraging radiomics features from baseline MRI can moderately predict TR in MM. This predictive value was demonstrated in an external, multicenter cohort, underscoring the potential role of automated MRI-based analysis in clinical decision-making.

## O10 PSMA-PET PRIMARY Score Improves the Prediction of Extracapsular Extension and Seminal Vesicle Invasion in Prostate Cancer

## Cristiano Pini1,2, Paolo Zaurito3, Simone Scuderi3, Armando Stabile3, Francesco Barletta3, Daniele Robesti3, Riccardo Leni3, Pietro Scilipoti3, Leonardo Quarta3, Maria Picchio1,2, Ana Maria Samanes Gajate1, Gaia Ninatti1, Alexandra Lazar1,2, Enrico Checcucci4, Francesco Porpiglia4, Edoardo Cisero4, Daniele Amparore4, Cristian Fiori4, Francesco Montorsi3,2, Giorgio Gandaglia3,2, Arturo Chiti1,2, Alberto Briganti3,2

## 1Department of Nuclear Medicine, IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele, Milan, Italy; 2Università Vita-Salute San Raffaele, Milan, Italy; 3Department of Urology, IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele, Milan, Italy; 4Department of Urology, San Luigi Hospital, Orbassano, Italy

## Correspondence: Cristiano Pini, pini.cristiano@hsr.it
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): O10

Aim

Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) has limited sensitivity for extracapsular extension (ECE) and seminal vesicle invasion (SVI) in prostate cancer (PCa). PRIMARY score, derived from PSMA-PET, improves detection of clinically significant PCa, but its value for local staging remains underexplored. We assessed whether adding PRIMARY to current parameters refines prediction of ECE and SVI in men scheduled for radical prostatectomy (RP).

Methods

We retrospectively analysed 219 patients from two referral centres who underwent mpMRI, [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET and RP. PRIMARY and PI-RADS scores, mpMRI T-stage and ISUP-grade group were recorded. We performed multivariable logistic regressions for each pairwise combination of PRIMARY and PI-RADS score, adjusting for mpMRI-stage and ISUP-grade group. We calculated ROC-derived AUC, calibration, and net benefit, and compared these metrics to models including only mpMRI and clinical features.

Results

Fifteen (7%), 110 (50%) and 94 (43%) patients had PI-RADS 3, 4, and 5 lesions; 26 (12%), 67 (31%) and 126 (58%) were PRIMARY 3, 4, and 5. Median PSA was 7 ng/mL. Pathology revealed ECE in 168 (77%) and SVI in 73 (33%) patients. Combined PI-RADS 5 + PRIMARY 5 conveyed the highest risk of ECE (OR 5.6, 95% CI 1.8-20) and SVI (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.1-6.4). Models including PRIMARY increased AUC from 0.60 to 0.66 for ECE and from 0.60 to 0.68 for SVI, with superior calibration and higher net benefit.

Conclusion

PSMA-PET PRIMARY score can improve the prediction of ECE and SVI, refining local disease staging in patients scheduled for RP.

## P1 MRI in staging of Endometrial Cancer with updated 2023 FIGO classification - a pictorial assay

## Aditi Chandra, Divya Midha, Priya Ghosh, Akshat Shah, Anisha Gehani

## Tata Medical Center, Kolkata, India

## Correspondence: Aditi Chandra, draditichandra@gmail.com
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): P1

Aim

The last decade has seen a paradigm shift in the diagnosis of endometrial carcinoma (EC) and the classification has evolved from a dualistic clinico-pathological one to a histotype-based classification and more recently to a prognostically significant non-overlapping molecular subtyping. With the emergence of the molecular classification of EC, the new International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system of EC published in 2023 metamorphosed from an anatomical staging system (FIGO 2009) to a staging system encompassing histological typing, lympho-vascular space invasion, size of nodal metastasis, and integrates molecular classification into its domain while distinguishing between synchronous and metastatic endometrioid endometrial and ovarian cancers.

Methods

MRI with corroborative sequences were studied. Final stage (FIGO 2009 and FIGO 2023) of histopathology specimen of each case was derived from electronic medical records and compared to the MRI findings.

Results

The main highlights of FIGO 2023 staging system of EC is as follows:
• Incorporates tumour type and tumour grade as staging parameters by distinguishing EC into two principal categories based on their degree of aggressiveness: aggressive and non-aggressive histologic types
• Incorporates LVSI as a staging parameter
• Incorporates size of lymph node metastasis as a staging parameter
• Introduces stage IA3 for synchronous low grade Endometrial and Ovarian Endometrioid Carcinomas
• Integrates Molecular Classification of EC into staging of EC

Conclusion

MRI helps in accurate staging based on the updated FIGO classification by providing anatomical details of involvement. The pictorial assay will detail each stage of the updated classification with radiological, pathological/molecular correlation.

## P2 Pilot Study: Can a Multiparametric-MRI-derived Model Outperform RECIST Predicting Pathology Response in Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS)?

## Sam Lo, Raul Valenzuela, Behrang Amini, Elvis Duran-Sierra, Mathew Antony, William A. Murphy, John E. Madewell, Colleen Costelloe

## The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, Houston, USA

## Correspondence: Raul Valenzuela, rfvalenzuela@mdanderson.org
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): P2
Background and purpose

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common soft-tissue sarcoma in the paediatric population and an aggressive cancer subtyped as embryonal, alveolar, pleomorphic, and NOS. It demonstrates post-therapeutic haemosiderin deposition, granulation-tissue formation, fibrosis, and calcification. This pilot study aims to provide initial supporting evidence for a multiparametric-MRI (mp-MRI) predictive-response model to outperform RECIST.

Methods

An IRB-approved retrospective study of 11 extremity and pelvic RMS patients with presurgical mp-MRI, including diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), contrast-enhanced susceptibility-weighted imaging (CE-SWI), and perfusion-weighted imaging with dynamic-contrast-enhancement (PWI/DCE), with surgical resection 02/2021-06/2024.
Lesions were categorised into six CE-SWI and six PWI/DCE morphologic patterns. Time-intensity curves (TICs) were classified as types I-V. Pathology-assessed treatment effect (PATE) in the surgical specimen categorised patients as responders (R=>90%, n=3) and partial/non-responders (P/NR=<90%, n=8).

Results

The six extremity RMS ADC-mean increased minimally from 1,425±476x10-6mm2/s at baseline (BL) to 1,494±386x10-6mm2/s at presurgical-timepoint (PS). The ADC-mean for the five pelvic RMS increased from 1,093±342x10-6mm2/s at BL to 1,677±313x10-6mm2/s at PS. 
All responders and partial/non-responders presented presurgical RECIST, WHO, and volume stability.
At PS, 50% of responders displayed CE-SWI-Complete-Ring (P=0.5578), PWI/DCE-Capsular-pattern (P=0.6065), and TIC-Type-2 (P=0.6065).
ADC+CE-SWI radiomics and semi-quantitative-PWI/DCE, did not show significance separating R vs P/NR, yet on ROC-analysis, the predictive response-model based on PS-CE-SWI-GLSZM-Large-Area-High-Gray-Level-Emphasis yielded perfect performance (AUC=1.0), outperforming ADC-Maximum-2D-Diameter-Slice (0.83), CE-SWI-Complete-Ring (AUC=0.67), PWI/DCE-Capsular (0.67), RECIST (0.67), and TIC-type-2 (0.6).

Conclusion

A response-model based on single-CE-SWI-GLSZM-texture-feature achieved perfect classification-performance (AUC=1.0), indicating its potential in predicting RMS-response, outperforming RECIST (AUC=0.67).

## P3 Decoding renal tumours with multiparametric MRI

## Aditi Chandra, Saugata Sen, Sumit Mukhopadhyay, Anisha Gehani, Priya Ghosh, Jeevitesh Khoda, Arghya Chatterjee

## Tata Medical Center, Kolkata, India

## Correspondence: Aditi Chandra, draditichandra@gmail.com
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): P3
Aim

Imaging sequences in multiparametric MRI of renal tumours.
MRI features of different types of renal tumours.

Pre-operative evaluation in relation to minimal access surgery and nephron-sparing surgery.

MRI for active surveillance

Methods

MRI with corroborative sequences was studied. Multiparametric MRI with contrast enhancement in the cortico-medullary phase and the nephrogenic phase are necessary for optimum imaging of renal lesions. Diffusion-weighted imaging, fat suppressed images and Dixon images with in- and out-of-phase imaging are mandatory. The histopathology specimen of each case was derived from the electronic medical records (EMR) and findings with MRI correlated. 

Results

Multiparametric MRI of renal tumours help us assess size, location, enhancement pattern as well as cellularity and micro as well as macroscopic fat. Advances in MRI have helped the radiologist determine the subset of patients that may undergo nephron-sparing surgery.
MRI also allows the radiologist to determine the pathological type of renal tumours. Macroscopic fat is found in angiomyolipoma. Microscopic fat is often found in clear cell renal cell carcinoma and sometimes in the papillary variety. 
Response to targeted agents like TKI inhibitors can be assessed accurately by MRI. Imaging features of MRI are being relied upon to offer active surveillance to surgically unfit and elderly patients with small renal tumours. 

Conclusion

MRI provides additional information on the staging and pre-operative work-up of renal neoplasms. MRI can also predict the histopathology of renal tumours.

## P4 Pictorial review and management of uncomplicated retroperitoneal lymphangiomas

## Mohammad Bilal Fazal, Maria Asad, Syahminan Suut

## Salford Royal Hospital, Salford, United Kingdom

## Correspondence: Mohammad Bilal Fazal, bilalmuhammadno.1@gmail.com
Cancer Imaging,
25(1): P4
Retroperitoneal lymphangiomas are extremely rare, benign cystic tumours making up only 1% of all lymphatic malformations. Less than 200 cases have been reported in the literature. They represent a diagnostic challenge, as there are shared features with other retroperitoneal cystic tumours. 
Large or symptomatic retroperitoneal lymphangiomas usually undergo complete surgical removal. However, there is no consensus or established guidelines regarding the surveillance of small or asymptomatic retroperitoneal lymphangiomas. 
We present four cases of small, asymptomatic retroperitoneal lymphangiomas which were found incidentally on imaging. In all cases, no complicating features were demonstrated such as intracystic bleeding, rupture or compression of adjacent structures. Given their benign nature, a conservative approach with regular surveillance scans was agreed upon after discussion in our local multidisciplinary meeting, as opposed to surgical resection. 
We suggest that if cases of retroperitoneal lymphangiomas are small, asymptomatic and demonstrate no complications, then an annual follow-up with CT or MRI is an appropriate management option. If the growth rate on serial scans is insignificant over a period of at least five years, it may be appropriate to consider less frequent imaging, with a view to eventually discharge patients if continued stability in size and morphology is demonstrated.

Background

Although each version of Liver Imaging Reporting and Data Systems (LI-RADS®) has been informed by the best available evidence, there are insufficient high-quality data and lack of consensus among experts on the newer component of LI-RADS which is a treatment response algorithm.

This study aims to identify, evaluate, and summarise the findings of all relevant individual studies that assess LI-RADS® treatment response assessment following locoregional therapy among patients with HCC.

Methods

This systematic review employed data-based convergent synthesis design. Records were identified through PubMed, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov, ICTRP (WHO), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, MEDLINE and Embase. A total of 46 studies were screened and 12 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis. LI-RADS treatment response algorithm can be applied for prognostication and early detection of pathologic tumour viability. However, there is a need for multicentre prospective studies to further validate its performance.

Objective

Approximately 25% of high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) will be resistant to standard platinum-based chemotherapy. We aimed to use CT-based deep-learning (CT-DL) to predict response to platinum-based chemotherapy in HGSOC.

Methods

Consecutive patients with primary HGSOC were retrospectively recruited from five centres. Four hundred and fifty-five patients (age 54.9 ± 9.4) were recruited. The proposed CT-DL model was able to predict response to platinum-based chemotherapy with area under the curve (0.759), sensitivity (60.0%), specificity (77.1%), and accuracy (71.4%) in validation set. 

Conclusion

CT-DL model could effectively predict platinum responsiveness in HGSOC, offering tailored individual treatment strategies.

Radiologists should be familiarised with imaging features of these emergencies for early diagnosis and improved outcomes.

This prospective, multicentre study will enrol 334 high-risk individuals (PLCOm2012 score>4%, age>50 yrs) into two cohorts: Cohort 1 will undergo LDCT with [18F]FDG PET/CT, and Cohort 2 will undergo LDCT alone. LDCT will be assessed using Lung-RADS 1.1 criteria. PET/CT is considered positive with significant FDG uptake in pulmonary or non-pulmonary lesions. AI models will integrate clinical and imaging data to refine risk stratification, guide follow-up, and personalise screening intervals. 

Results

As of June 2025, 21 participants have been enrolled in Cohort 1 (age 66 yrs; female: male ratio 12:9), all with a history of heavy smoking. No suspicious lung nodules were identified. One participant showed a Lung-RADS 4 A nodule with no FDG uptake, and two had multiple non-avid nodules attributed to prior tuberculosis. A 6-month follow-up CT was recommended instead of 3-month follow-up. One participant had a FDG-avid left adrenal mass, and an abdominal MRI was recommended for further evaluation.

Conclusions

Early findings suggest that combining PET/CT with LDCT may improve screening results by reducing false positives, guiding follow-up and potentially detecting non-pulmonary, smoking-related cancers. The AI models under development aim to support personalised screening strategies by optimising patient selection, minimising unnecessary imaging and procedures, and reducing healthcare costs.

## P23 Abdominal and pelvic lymph nodes: Time for a formal anatomical classification?

## Stephan Edey, Anum Pervez, Salil Patel, Cynthia Funso Fyomi, Sultana Hasso, Olwen Westerland, Audrey Jacques, Sarah Natas

## Guy's and St. Thomas'Hospital, London, United Kingdom

## Correspondence: Sarah Natas, sarahnts.6@gmail.com
Cancer Imaging,

Purpose

Accurate delineation of anatomical lymph node stations within the abdomen/pelvis is crucial to provide clarity between radiologists and clinical colleagues, particularly where radiotherapy or surgery is required. Classification systems for lymph node levels in the neck and thorax are in standard practice, but no formal nomenclature exists for the abdomen/pelvis. We devised a schema to fill this gap. 

Method

This retrospective study included 69 patients with primary cervical cancer and presumed nodal metastatic disease. Two radiologists of varying experience (14 and 4 years respectively) independently reviewed baseline imaging (CT, MRI and PET/CT), classifying lymph nodes levels (1-9), encompassing the upper abdomen to the inguinal regions, with further delineation in relation to the main abdomino/pelvic vessels: anterior (A), posterior (P), lateral (L), medial (M). CT images were evaluated as first preference, with MRI or PET/CT reviewed where not available. Lymph nodes > 10 mm or greater in the retroperitoneum and > 8 mm or more in the pelvis were classified abnormal. Recorded nodal stations were compared using Cohen's Kappa to assess inter-observer variability.

Results

Overall agreement across all lymph node levels was 94.6%. Pelvic agreement was near perfect (0.82); and moderate in the abdomen (0.48), with no significant effect when differing imaging modalities were analysed.

Conclusion

The proposed standardised abdomino/pelvic lymph node classification system demonstrates reasonable agreement within the abdomen and very good agreement in the pelvis and may form the basis for additional studies, particularly involving clinical colleagues to further refine and clarify the schema.

## P24 Texture Analysis of Melanoma Lesions as Imaging Biomarker of Response to Pembrolizumab

## National Cancer Institute, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil

## Correspondence: Helcio Pereira, helcioradio@hotmail.com
Cancer Imaging,

Aim

To evaluate whether the reduction in heterogeneity of target melanoma lesions, assessed through histogram-based texture analysis, correlates with clinical response to pembrolizumab.

Methods

This retrospective study included 120 patients with metastatic melanoma treated with pembrolizumab. After applying exclusion criteria - including stable disease, treatment discontinuation, and death before response assessment - 56 patients were selected for final analysis: 30 with partial response and 26 with progressive disease, as defined by iRECIST criteria. Target lesions were segmented from contrast-enhanced CT scans obtained before and after treatment.
