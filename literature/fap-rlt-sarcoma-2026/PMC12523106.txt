## Abstract

## Background
Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm that typically occurs in the pleura. Prostatic SFT is uncommon, and periprostatic origin is exceedingly rare. To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of a patient who underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic prostate-sparing resection of a periprostatic SFT.

## Case presentation
 A 66-year-old male with no urinary or systemic symptoms was incidentally diagnosed with a periprostatic mass during routine imaging. Core needle biopsy revealed a neoplastic spindle cell proliferation exhibiting nuclear expression of signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 and cluster differentiation 34, consistent with a periprostatic SFT. Intraoperative findings revealed a well-circumscribed tumor originating from the prostatic fascia, enveloping the dorsal venous complex and neurovascular bundle. A prostate-sparing tumor resection was performed, achieving complete excision while maintaining both sexual and urinary functions. Postoperative surveillance over 12 months revealed no evidence of recurrence or metastasis on imaging and clinical evaluation.

## Conclusion
 Periprostatic SFT represents an extremely rare clinical entity requiring individualized management strategies to balance oncological control with functional preservation. This report provide a management paradigm for preserving genitourinary function. Enhance the imaging, diagnosis, treatment and prognosis of periprostatic SFT among clinical physicians.

## Introduction
Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) was first reported by Klemperer and Rabin in 1931 as a spindle cell neoplasm predominantly originating in the visceral pleurae [ ]. It typically presents as an isolated mass connected to the pleura with well-defined peripheries and often a pedicle [ ]. Clinically, pleural SFTs commonly manifest with nonspecific symptoms such as cough, chest pain, or dyspnea, though it is frequently detected incidentally during imaging. In contrast, extrapleural lesions typically present as slow-growing, painless masses, with site-specific manifestations such as constipation (rectal involvement), urinary retention (prostatic tumors), and hematuria (bladder tumors) [ ]. A review of the literature identified 42 articles that reported prostatic SFT, among which only one case focused on periprostatic SFT (Table  ). In our case, we reported it for the first time of a patient who underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic prostate-sparing resection of a SFT limited to the prostatic fascia and neurovascular tissues of the prostate. We simultaneously achieved tumor resection and functional preservation. The short-term follow-up of 12 months indicated a favorable outcomes, which can provide certain insights for clinicians when dealing with similar cases. In addition, this case report highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of periprostatic SFTs and aims to fill a gap in the existing literature by providing a clearer framework for differentiating these tumors from other spindle cell lesions based on imaging, histology, and immunohistochemistry (IHC).
 

## Case presentation

## Preoperative clinical assessment
A 66-year-old male with no urinary symptoms or significant family medical history underwent routine ultrasound, which revealed a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass (4.0 × 3.6 cm) adjacent to the left prostatic lobe (Fig.  ). The International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) is 4 to 6, and the International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) is 21 to 23. Blood tests, including prostate-specific antigen (PSA, 0.62 ng/mL), were within normal limits. Digital rectal examination showed a smooth, non-tender prostate with well-defined borders and no palpable nodules.
 

## Imaging findings and core needle biopsy
Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig.  ) showed a well-circumscribed mass (5.0 × 2.1 × 1.5 cm, without capsular invasion or lymphadenopathy) arising from the left prostate, displacing the prostate gland to the right. The mass exhibited hyperintense signal on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (Fig.  A). The boundary between the central gland area and the peripheral zone of the prostate was indistinct, and the signal on T2-weighted image (T2WI) (Fig.  B) was hypointense. While the signal on DWI was slightly increased, bilateral seminal vesicles gland T2WI imaging signal decreased as Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System was 3 lesions. Ultrasound-guided systematic core needle biopsy of the prostate gland (needle biopsy No.1 to 10) showed histology consistent with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), however, the periprostatic mass (needle biopsy No.11 to 18) showed a spindle cell neoplasm of mesenchymal origin (Fig.  ). IHC findings showed signal transducers and activators of transcription 6 (STAT6)(+) and cluster differentiation 34 (CD34)(+), B cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2)(+), progesterone receptor (PR)(+), β-Catenin(+), Ki-67(2%+), Desmin(+), CD117(scattered +), P16(-), murine double minute 2 (MDM2)(-), soomth muscle actin (SMA)(-), S100(-). Resulting in a diagnosis of a periprostatic SFT due to positive for STAT6 and CD34. To assess whether there are metastatic lesions, the patient underwent fluoro deoxy glucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT). Imaging (Fig.  ) demonstrated intense FDG uptake in the left periprostatic primary lesion, with indistinct boundaries involving left seminal vesicle, left prostatic lobe, and left pelvic floor musculature [ ].
 

## Treatment
The patient demonstrates a strong willingness to proceed with surgical intervention. A multidisciplinary team consisting in urology, oncology, pathology, radiology, and nuclear medicine specialists formulated a thorough treatment plan and prognostic assessment, concluding that the tumor likely originated near the prostate. The team preoperatively devised two surgical strategies, with the final approach determined intraoperatively based on the anatomy location between tumor and prostate: (1) If the tumor originating from the prostatic gland, radical prostatectomy (RP) with tumor resection would be performed. (2) If not, radical tumor resection alone would be undertaken. Consequently, Da Vinci robotic-assisted laparoscopic resection was planned to achieve complete tumor excision while preserving functional anatomy. Fig.  presents the schematic of an abdomen with port placement. Surgical procedure: (1) Expose the Retzius space, incise the endopelvic fascia. (2) The tumor (Fig.  ) was well circumscribed and smooth, located lateral to the left prostate. It is originating from the prostatic fascia, adherent to the neurovascular bundle (NVB), and encased by the dorsal venous complex (DVC). With no evidence of penetration of the prostatic capsule or lymph node infiltration. (3) Expose the upper pole of the tumor. Use bipolar electrocoagulation and scissors for sharp dissection of the plane between the tumor and the prostatic capsule (Intrafascial dissection method), with particular attention to protecting the DVC and the left NVB. (4) Several nutrient vessels at the base of the tumor were visible. These vessels are carefully ligated and secured with meticulous hemostasis, while taking great care to avoid rectal injury. (5) The tumor was separated from the adhesion to the left pelvic wall and completely dissected. In this surgical procedure, we based on the preoperative imaging, puncture pathology, and intraoperative findings, it was confirmed that the tumor did not invade the prostatic gland. The anatomical location of the tumor allowed for complete resection, while preserving the prostate gland, thereby ensuring the maintenance of both sexual and urinary functions.
 

## Histopathological confirmation
Histopathological analysis showed a spindle cell neoplasm with small and fusiform cells. IHC showed strong nuclear positivity for STAT6 (Fig.  A-B) and diffuse cytoplasmic positivity for CD34 (Fig.  C-D), CD99(+), SMA(+), P53(10%+), Ki-67(10%+), Integrase interactor-1‌(INI-1)(+), P16(+), CD10(+), Desmin(+), β-catenin(+), CD68(-), CK(-), S100(-), MDM2(-), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)(-), One or two mitotic figures were observed per 10 high-power-fields (HPF). No lymphovascular invasion or tumor necrosis was observed. These cells exhibited no remarkable atypia or pleomorphism. Consequently, the patient was definitively diagnosed with periprostatic SFT based on comprehensive histopathological evaluation and IHC analysis confirming characteristic nuclear STAT6 expression and CD34 positivity. Given the benign histopathological features and low-risk profile of the tumor, adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy was deemed unnecessary.
 

## Follow-up and prognosis
At the 12 months follow-up, pelvic MRI (Fig.  ) demonstrated no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis. The patient maintained intact urinary continence and erectile function (the IPSS is 3 to 5, and the IIEF-5 is 21 to 23), the improvement of the patient’s IPSS after the operation may be due to the alleviation of local compression symptoms after tumor resection. Up to now, remained asymptomatic during postoperative surveillance, and expressed satisfaction with the therapeutic outcome.
 

## Discussion
For prostatic SFT, Stolzenbach et al. [ ] first to demonstrate the feasibility of bilateral nerve-sparing robot-assisted RP supported by frozen section. In our case, nerve-sparing robot-assisted surgery enabled the successful resection of a SFT intimately adhered to the prostate, with complete preservation of the gland. Postoperative urinary and erectile functions were well maintained, a phenomenon that remains scarcely documented in the current literature. Due to the rarity of cases, it is necessary to collect and record the existing literature. Therefore, we comprehensively summarized and compared the clinical data of previous cases and our case of prostatic SFT in Table  , including detailed clinical data such as age, symptoms, PSA, tumor size, location, treatment, surgical margins, IHC, and follow-up.

## Epidemiology
SFTs exhibit a broad age distribution (21–87 years), peaking in the fourth to sixth decades with a slight female predominance [ ]. While classically pleural in origin, extrapleural SFTs have been documented across nearly all anatomical regions. Including the head and neck, extremities, pelvic cavity, soft tissues, central nervous system, meninges, and visceral organs (e.g., thyroid, liver, gastrointestinal tract, adrenal glands, bladder, prostate, spermatic cord and testis) [ ]. Macroscopically, prostatic SFTs show considerable size heterogeneity, ranging from 2 to 17 cm in diameter and weighing between 5 and 351 g [ ].

## Clinical presentation
While SFT of the prostate is often initially asymptomatic, these slow-growing masses progressively enlarge, resulting in compressive or obstructive symptoms at the involved anatomical site. The most frequent clinical manifestations include urinary frequency, dysuria, acute urinary retention, constipation, and localized abdominal pain [ ]. Notably, a subset of patients may present with only mild lower abdominal discomfort or remain entirely asymptomatic throughout the disease course [ ]. In our case, the patient was completely asymptomatic, underscoring the insidious nature of the tumor.

## Laboratory examination
Serum PSA levels typically remain within normal limits. Paraneoplastic hypoglycemia, attributed to tumor secretion of insulin-like growth factor, is observed in a minority of cases [ ].

## Imaging examination
Current imaging modalities, including ultrasonography, CT, FDG PET/CT, and MRI, demonstrate limited specificity in distinguishing prostatic SFT from other prostatic neoplasms and often fail to definitively determine the mass origin. Retrospective analyses from PubMed-indexed studies indicate these techniques primarily serve to delineate tumor topography and evaluate mass effects on adjacent structures. Emerging evidence suggests Fibroblast Activation Protein Inhibitor PET/CT may serve as an ancillary diagnostic tool for prostatic SFT identification through selective molecular targeting [ ].
SFTs can arise in virtually any body compartment, exhibiting distinctive anatomical diversity and intricate relationships with adjacent organs. Consequently, comprehensive preoperative imaging optimization is paramount for precise surgical planning. Advanced cross-sectional imaging enables meticulous evaluation of tumor topography, vascular architecture, and potential compromise of adjacent organs. In our illustrative case, preoperative imaging suggested that the tumor may originated from the prostate due to its close adjacency to the prostate gland. The MRI did not clearly delineate a distinct prostate gland, and intraoperative findings confirmed the tumor’s close association with the prostate. Therefore, preoperative imaging provides crucial information for predicting the tumor’s anatomical structure [ , ]. Our case expands the limited radiologic literature on periprostatic SFT by characterizing distinctive imaging features: (1) Heterogeneous enhancement patterns reflecting tumor vascularity; (2) Mass-effect displacement without frank invasion; and (3) Interface ambiguity with pelvic organs. While PET/CT provides excellent spatial resolution for surgical navigation, MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast for fascial plane delineation. Given the rarity of SFT, it is imperative that radiologists and clinicians consider this condition as part of the differential diagnosis for tumor etiology [ ].

## Histopathological examination
These lesions are characterized by well-demarcated, grayish-white nodular architecture with cross-sectional areas exhibiting dense fibrous stroma [ ]. Histopathological biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, enabling both tumor characterization and grading prior to therapeutic planning. IHC reveals highly specific nuclear STAT6 expression, particularly NGFI-A binding protein 2 (NAB2-STAT6 fusion) with variable CD34, CD99, and BCL2 co-positivity [ – ]. Consequently, ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy coupled with comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis is strongly recommended for diagnostic confirmation [ , ].
The prognostic significance of histopathological features in extrapleural SFTs, particularly urogenital tract tumors, remains inconclusive due to their extreme rarity and heterogeneous molecular pathogenesis [ ]. This biological variability manifests as substantial intertumoral morphological heterogeneity, frequently resulting in discordance between histology and clinical behavior.
Consequently, conventional morphological evaluation of limited prostate biopsy specimens demonstrates poor diagnostic reliability, necessitating multimodal immunohistochemical verification. However, caution must be exercised in interpreting immunohistochemical results, as certain markers (CD34, CD99, BCL2 and PR) are not entirely specific to SFT [ , , ].
In the diagnostic evaluation of mesenchymal neoplasms involving the prostate, immunohistochemical staining for STAT6 should be routinely performed and is considered the gold standard for confirming SFT [ ]. When STAT6 analysis is unavailable or inconclusive, pathologists should be aware that SFT morphology and immunoprofile vary, and diagnosis requires combined analysis of multiple immunohistochemical markers [ ]. While excluding differential diagnoses using appropriate negative markers.
A persistent diagnostic challenge in prostatic SFT is the lack of universally accepted histopathological criteria to reliably predict malignant potential. We applyed the stratification scheme previously reported by Demicco et al. [ , ] for SFT incorporating patient age, tumor size, mitotic activity, tumor necrosis to predict risk of malignant potential. In our case, patient age ≥ 55 years, tumor size 5.0 cm, mitotic count 1–2/10 HPF, and absence of necrosis. Therefore, risk stratification indicated low risk in our case. Notably, even histologically “benign”-appearing tumors may exhibit aggressive behavior, emphasizing the need for risk stratification based on mitotic activity, necrosis, and tumor size [ ]. Furthermore, the higher local recurrence rates observed in extrapleural SFTs (including prostatic cases) compared to their pleural counterparts suggest site-specific biological differences warranting further investigation [ ].

## Differential diagnosis
A subset of patients with prostatic SFT were initially misdiagnosed with and treated for BPH, frequently due to incomplete diagnostic evaluation rather than symptom relief [ , ]. Such therapeutic interventions may inadvertently delay appropriate management of the underlying condition. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the characteristics of its differential diagnosis is of vital importance for timely and accurate diagnosis of SFT.
Given the variability in clinical outcomes and treatment strategies, it is crucial to differentiate prostatic SFT from other spindle cell lesions and assess its malignant potential. Macroscopically, benign SFT typically presents as a well-circumscribed, homogeneous, grayish-white mass with partial encapsulation. In contrast, malignant counterparts frequently exhibit regions of swelling and necrosis, with infiltrative margins that lack a well-defined capsule. Microscopically, benign SFT is characterized by a haphazard arrangement of spindle-shaped fibrocytes within a collagenous matrix, which may surround and infiltrate blood vessels in the subcutaneous tissue. The malignant potential of prostatic SFTs is challenging to predict, however, several histopathological features have been associated with increased malignancy risk, including tumor diameter bigger than 10 cm, mitotic activity exceeding 4 per 10 high-power fields, nuclear pleomorphism, cellular atypia, necrosis, lack of encapsulation, infiltrative margins, and elevated Ki-67 labeling index [ , ]. Interestingly, our review of existing literature [ , – ] reveals that cases exhibiting a Ki-67 index ranging from 3% to 15% have not demonstrated tumor recurrence or metastasis during follow-up periods spanning from 3 months to 3 years. In our case, the Ki-67 index is 10%, a favorable prognosis was observed within the 1-year follow-up period. Nevertheless, extending the duration of clinical follow-up remains critically important for better understanding the biological behavior of this rare neoplasm.
The clinical and morphologic spectrum of prostatic spindle cell lesions shows broad overlap. SFT must be differentiated from numerous benign and malignant spindle cell lesions involving the prostate. ## Treatment and management
Upon histopathological confirmation of prostatic SFT, the following management protocol is recommended: (1) Diagnostic refinement: Perform extended template biopsies or transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) to establish tumor architecture heterogeneity and exclude high-grade transformation; (2) Function-preserving surgery: For patients aged < 65 years with symptomatic masses (IPSS > 15) or radiographically aggressive features, nerve-sparing RP should be prioritized to maintain genitourinary function and optimize long-term quality of life; (3) Risk-stratified surveillance: Implement intensive monitoring (every 3–6 months imaging and PSA) for elderly patients with locally advanced or unresectable lesions, particularly those with comorbidities contraindicating radical surgery [ , ].
Therapeutic modalities for prostatic SFT encompass: TURP, simple prostatectomy, RP, enucleation and complete tumor resection, cystoprostatectomy and pelvic tumor resection [ ]. The surgical approach is determined by the anatomical location of the primary tumor and involved structures, similar to managing most soft tissue sarcomas. Critically, surgical planning must prioritize achieving negative margins while preserving adjacent organ function, as margin status strongly correlates with recurrence risk [ , ]. Chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy are reserved for unresectable or metastatic tumors [ , ]. Our case demonstrates that complete periprostatic SFT resection (without RP) can achieve durable local control, highlighting the imperative for personalized therapeutic strategies. This outcome aligns with evolving paradigms favoring organ-preservation approaches when oncological control is maintained.
In both prostatic and non-prostatic SFT, removal of the prostate and, in some cases, adjacent organs may be required for effective disease management. However, in our case, prostate-sparing resection of periprostatic SFT is viable, leveraging advanced surgical techniques to balance oncologic control with functional preservation. Robotic-assisted laparoscopy platforms provide superior visualization and enable meticulous interfascial dissection for periprostatic tumors while minimizing trauma to adjacent structures.
For cases where complete resection is not feasible or carries a high risk of recurrence, adjuvant radiotherapy (typically 50–60 Gy in conventional fractionation) may be employed to control local disease and mitigate recurrence risk. Adjuvant radiotherapy is particularly indicated for cases with microscopic residual disease or high-risk features, such as tumor necrosis or elevated proliferative indices. For metastatic disease, systemic therapy regimens incorporating antiangiogenic agents such as pazopanib and sorafenib demonstrate improved progression-free survival, though they require rigorous monitoring for hepatotoxicity [ ]. Importantly, surveillance protocols employing contrast-enhanced MRI or CT at 6-month intervals remain appropriate for small, asymptomatic lesions exhibiting indolent radiographic features. Unlike our case, in the case reported by Yılmaz et al. [ ], a patient diagnosed with low-risk SFT of the prostate, who was concerned about potential urinary incontinence and erectile dysfunction associated with surgery, opted for active surveillance. Three years later, the histopathology of the patient’s tumor remained stable. Therefore, if histopathological examination reveals no evidence of malignancy, careful monitoring of the patient can be carried out. In other circumstances, radical surgery is highly recommended instead of palliative measures. This therapeutic hierarchy, recently codified in the 2025 National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Soft Tissue Sarcoma Guidelines, underscores the critical transition from empirical treatment selection to biomarker-informed decision-making in SFT management.

## Prognosis
While the majority of SFTs exhibit benign behavior, their potential for malignant progression remains uncertain and requires vigilant monitoring [ ]. Notably, approximately 10% behave aggressively, manifesting as local recurrence or distant metastasis, despite lacking conventional histological features of malignancy [ , ]. Previous studies indicate a marginally higher actuarial risk of local recurrence for extrapleural SFTs compared to pleural SFTs, regardless of histologic grade, tumor size, or margin status [ , ]. Consequently, patients should be subjected to rigorous long-term follow-up. As exemplified by our case, an aggressive postoperative monitoring protocol integrating a range of imaging examinations is of utmost importance for the early detection of disease recurrence.
We searched PubMed using the terms “ ((prostatic solitary fibrous tumor) OR (solitary fibrous tumor of prostate)) AND (periprostatic) ” for literature published through July 2025. Only one case report of periprostatic SFT was found [ ]. In contrast to our case, this patient presented with complaints of constipation and lower abdominal pain, and underwent an exploratory lower midline laparotomy. To date, documented cases, especially of periprostatic SFTs, remain limited, resulting in scarce relevant data and insufficient follow-up duration. Further basic and clinical research at molecular, cellular [ ], and clinical levels is imperative to elucidate the etiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment strategies for this condition.
Current limitations, including a scarcity of large-scale studies and an incomplete understanding of molecular drivers, call for multicenter collaborations to establish evidence-based guidelines. Future research should prioritize elucidating the role of the NAB2-STAT6 fusion in tumor progression and exploring advanced imaging modalities (e.g., functional MRI) to refine preoperative risk assessment [ ]. Ultimately, multidisciplinary synergy integrating pathologists (refining grading criteria), radiologists (optimizing multiparametric imaging), and urologists (tailoring surgical extent) is paramount to mitigating the diagnostic and therapeutic uncertainties surrounding this rare disease.
Several limitations need to be taken into consideration in this case report: (1) This is only one case and the restricted generalizability of the results. Consequently, extrapolating these findings to a wider population is challenging. (2) We were unable to count non-English case reports about prostatic or periprostatic SFT, this may hinder a more comprehensive understanding of this rare disease. (3) The case reported by Tanaka Y et al. [ ] was followed up for 54 months, local recurrence in the pelvis and multiple lung metastases were observed. In contrast, our case were followed up for merely 12 months, and long-term surveillance is critical given potential late recurrence. (4) Due to the tumor’s special location and the rarity of cases, there is still a lack of standardized treatment protocols. Therefore, more similar cases need to be collected to establish standardized surgical approaches and functional evaluation systems. Multi-center collaboration or the establishment of case database for systematic reviews of periprostatic SFTs. This involves gathering larger numbers of cases along with comprehensive long-term follow-up data. Such efforts are essential for deepening our understanding of this rare tumor, optimizing treatment strategies, and ultimately selecting the most advantageous treatment regimens for patients.
In summary, for periprostatic SFTs, the non-specific radiological characteristics may pose challenges to diagnosis. Tumor puncture biopsy, histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis remains the gold standard for diagnosis. Including STAT6, CD34, BCL2, and particularly NAB2-STAT6, are of significance. In terms of treatment, organ-preserving radical tumor resection is the initial option. Considering the risks of recurrence and metastasis, active follow-up is crucial for achieving a favorable prognosis.

## Conclusion
SFT, a rare mesenchymal neoplasm, should be included in the differential diagnosis of prostatic spindle cell lesions. Management of prostatic SFT necessitates multidisciplinary evaluation to optimize tumor resection while preserving functional integrity through tailored therapeutic strategies. We have reported a rare case of periprostatic SFT. This case demonstrates that radical resection with prostate preservation is feasible for periprostatic SFT, providing a function-sparing alternative to RP.
