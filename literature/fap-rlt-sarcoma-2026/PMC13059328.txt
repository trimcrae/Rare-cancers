## Abstract
Hyaluronidase is a carbohydrate-active enzyme that cleaves glycosaminoglycans, particularly hyaluronic acid. By transiently degrading the extracellular matrix, it facilitates reliable subcutaneous delivery of high-dose protein therapeutics. Despite its proven utility, challenges remain in preserving enzyme stability and ensuring consistent catalytic performance across formulations. Recent efforts have therefore focused on excipient and pH optimization to maintain protein integrity while sustaining enzymatic activity, device engineering to enhance injectability and patient usability, and pharmacokinetic modeling to correlate local tissue dispersion with systemic exposure. In oncology, hyaluronidase is increasingly incorporated into anticancer regimens to remodel the tumor microenvironment, alleviate stromal resistance, and promote immune-cell infiltration, thereby enabling synergistic antitumor effects across multiple therapeutic modalities. This review first classifies hyaluronidases by their biological sources and catalytic mechanisms, then summarizes advances in drug delivery with particular emphasis on recombinant human hyaluronidase in clinical use. Finally, it highlights mechanistic and translational insights that guide the rational design of safer, more effective, and clinically adaptable hyaluronidase-enabled delivery systems for both subcutaneous and tumor-targeted applications.
Keywords: Hyaluronidase, Extracellular matrix remodeling, Subcutaneous delivery, Tumor targeting, Enzyme-enabled formulation, Translational pharmacology

## Introduction
The extracellular matrix (ECM) constitutes a dynamic microenvironment that not only provides structural scaffolding for cells but also regulates a broad spectrum of physiological processes, including cell adhesion, migration, differentiation, and signal transduction [ ]. The ECM is primarily composed of collagen, elastin, proteoglycans, and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), whose composition and organization determine the mechanical strength and biological responsiveness of tissues [ ]. Among these components, hyaluronic acid (HA) is a linear, high-molecular-weight polysaccharide composed of repeating disaccharide units of d-glucuronic acid (GlcA) and N-acetyl-d-glucosamine (GlcNAc) linked through β-1,4 and β-1,3 glycosidic bonds. It is abundantly distributed in connective tissues and the ECM, where it imparts viscoelasticity to the interstitial fluid, contributes to soft-tissue stabilization and shock absorption, and maintains tissue hydration [ ].
Enzymes that depolymerize GAGs are generally categorized into two major families—glycoside hydrolases and polysaccharide lyases—based on their catalytic mechanisms and cleavage patterns [ ]. Hyaluronidases belong to these GAG-degrading enzyme families and act on various substrates, preferentially cleaving HA, whereas chondroitin, chondroitin sulfate, and dermatan sulfate are degraded more slowly and with limited efficiency [ ]. In 1928, Duran-Reynals first observed that extracts from mammalian testes and other tissues contained a “spreading factor” that enhanced the diffusion of subcutaneously injected dyes and antiviral vaccines [ , ]. Later, in 1940, Chain and Duthie identified this substance as an enzyme that hydrolyzes HA and coined the term “hyaluronidase” [ ].
Physiologically, hyaluronidase participates in diverse biological processes, including ECM turnover, tissue remodeling, and modulation of interstitial transport. These intrinsic functions have inspired its therapeutic exploitation across multiple biomedical domains, ranging from drug permeation enhancement and aesthetic medicine to oncology and targeted drug delivery [ ]. Building upon these foundations, this review comprehensively examines the physiological and clinical roles of hyaluronidase, with a particular focus on two therapeutic delivery paradigms: (1) co-formulation strategies that improve subcutaneous (SC) delivery of biopharmaceuticals and (2) tumor-targeted applications that enhance local penetration and anticancer efficacy. Finally, emerging challenges and future directions for the clinical translation of hyaluronidase-based therapies are discussed.

## Hyaluronidase
This section provides a structured overview of hyaluronidases, progressing from general classification principles to enzyme characteristics and clinically relevant translation. It outlines the major classification frameworks first, and then discusses representative hyaluronidases according to their biological sources and biochemical properties. Lastly, we focus on recombinant human PH20 (rHuPH20) as a paradigmatic example of successful clinical development and therapeutic integration.
2.1.

## Classification of hyaluronidases
Multiple approaches have been proposed for the classification of hyaluronidases, of which the four most widely recognized are based on: (1) source of origin, (2) optimal reaction pH, (3) amino acid sequence homology, and (4) substrate specificity and catalytic mechanism.
First, classification by origin reflects differences in catalytic mechanism and products [ ] and typically divides hyaluronidases into three domains: eukaryotic, prokaryotic, and viral enzymes [ ]. This origin-based grouping captures evolutionary divergence as well as variations in biochemical behavior among the enzymes.
Second, classification according to pH-dependent activity distinguishes acid-active hyaluronidases, which function at pH 3.0–4.0, from neutral-active hyaluronidases, which exhibit activity at pH 5.0–8.0 [ ]. Acid-active enzymes include human liver, serum, and some venom-derived hyaluronidases, while neutral-active enzymes comprise PH20 as well as snake and bee venom hyaluronidases. This pH-based classification reflects adaptation to distinct physiological environments and biological functions.
Third, classification based on amino acid sequence homology groups functionally equivalent hyaluronidases according to their tertiary fold, catalytic residues, and mechanistic features, as defined in the CAZy database [ ]. In this framework, mammalian and insect hyaluronidases are most often classified in the GH16, GH56, and GH84 families; leech and arthropod enzymes within GH79; and bacterial HA lyases within the PL8, PL16, and PL31 families. Sequence-based classification enables structural comparison across species and helps elucidate enzyme evolution and substrate preference.
Finally, classification according to substrate specificity and catalytic mechanism represents the most fundamental approach for understanding functional differences among hyaluronidases. The classical system was first established by Meyer in 1971 [ ], and identifies three major subclasses ( ). HA 4-glycanohydrolases (EC 3.2.1.35) possess endo-β-N-acetylhexosaminidase activity, hydrolyzing β-1,4-glycosidic linkages in HA, chondroitin, chondroitin sulfate, and dermatan sulfate [ , ]. The major products are even-numbered oligosaccharides, such as tetra- and hexasaccharides, with N-acetylglucosamine at the reducing end [ ]. In addition, vertebrate hyaluronidases exhibit transglycosidase activity [ , ], whereby at least one HA disaccharide fragment is transferred between nonreducing termini of oligosaccharides [ ]. Such activities are typically found in mammalian, reptilian, insect, or venom-derived enzymes [ ]. HA 3-glycanohydrolases (EC 3.2.1.36) display endo-β-d-glucuronidase activity, cleaving β-1,3-glycosidic linkages in HA to yield primarily tetrasaccharides bearing GlcA at the reducing end [ , , ]. More recent studies, however, demonstrated that HA 3-glycanohydrolases can further degrade hyaluronan oligomers to disaccharides as final products [ ]. These enzymes are present in leeches, helminths, and certain arthropods [ ]. Finally, HA lyases (EC 4.2.2.1) act via a β-elimination mechanism, cleaving β-1,4-glycosidic bonds to generate unsaturated end groups and characteristic disaccharide products, such as 2-acetamido-2-deoxy-3-O-(β-d-gluco-4-enepyranosyluronic acid)-d-glucose [ ].

## Characteristics of hyaluronidases according to their sources
Hyaluronidases are broadly distributed in nature [ ]. Owing to their critical utility in biotechnology and pharmaceutical applications, the discovery of novel hyaluronidases with unique properties remains an important avenue for overcoming current limitations and driving future advances. As summarized by Zhang et al., in 2022 [ ], hyaluronidases from diverse origins have been identified and characterized to date. In this section, representative hyaluronidases are discussed within the framework of substrate specificity and catalytic mechanism, as introduced in Section , given that these features most directly shape their biochemical behavior and translational relevance. In this regard, the discussion is organized around the three major enzymatic subclasses defined by Meyer—HA 4-glycanohydrolases (EC 3.2.1.35), HA 3-glycanohydrolases (EC 3.2.1.36), and HA lyases (EC 4.2.2.1)—while summarizes recently studied hyaluronidases according to their biological sources.

## HA 4-glycanohydrolases (EC 3.2.1.35)
2.2.1.1.

## Vertebrate hyaluronidases – human hyaluronidases
Humans express six highly homologous hyaluronidases: hyaluronidase-1 (HYAL-1), hyaluronidase-2 (HYAL-2), hyaluronidase-3 (HYAL-3), hyaluronidase-4 (HYAL-4), sperm adhesion molecule 1 (SPAM1, also known as PH20), and hyaluronidase pseudogene 1 (HYAL-P1 or HYAL-6), which share 33–42% sequence homology [ ]. HYAL-1, -2, -3, and -4 are classified as acid-active enzymes [ ], while PH20 exhibits optimal activity at pH 5.0–8.0 [ ].
To date, only HYAL-1, HYAL-2, and PH20 are confirmed to possess catalytic activity toward HA [ ]. HYAL-1 is a 57 kDa glycoprotein with maximal activity at pH 3–4.5 [ , ], consistent with its primary localization and function within acidic intracellular compartments, particularly lysosomes. It is detected in plasma, urine, liver, kidney, spleen, and heart. Its plasma concentration is relatively low (approximately 60 ng/mL) [ ], whereas an inactive 45 kDa form accumulates in urine at roughly 100-fold higher levels [ ]. HYAL-2 is predominantly expressed in liver, kidney, and spleen (but not in the adult brain) and is localized at the plasma membrane via glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchoring, with subsequent trafficking through endosomal compartments [ ]. Although initially described as an acid-active enzyme with maximal activity below pH 4 [ ], more recent studies of recombinant HYAL-2/CD44 complexes have demonstrated optimal activity at pH 6.0–7.0 [ ], highlighting the influence of molecular context and cellular environment on enzyme function.
Across multiple somatic tissues—including cartilage and skin—HYAL-1 and HYAL-2 are key regulators of ECM HA turnover, acting in a coordinated, mutually dependent manner [ , ]. The coordinated degradation pathway mediated by HYAL-2 followed by HYAL-1, together with associated endocytic receptors, is schematically illustrated in . Initially, HYAL-2 cleaves high-molecular-weight HA chains (HMW-HAs) at the cell surface or within the ECM into intermediate-sized HA fragments. These fragments are then internalized and transported to lysosomes, where HYAL-1, functioning as the principal lysosomal hyaluronidase, further degrades them into tetrasaccharides. The resulting products are then processed into monosaccharides by β-d-glucuronidase and β-N-acetyl-d-hexosaminidase [ , , ].
 CD44 is a broadly expressed transmembrane glycoprotein that functions as a principal cell-surface receptor for HA, mediating HA binding, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and downstream signaling events linked to cytoskeletal organization and cell migration. Beyond its role in ECM turnover, CD44 is critically involved in physiological processes such as leukocyte trafficking and tissue repair, and has been widely implicated in tumor progression and metastasis [ , ]. However, in liver sinusoidal and lymphatic endothelial cells, the canonical HA receptor CD44 plays a relatively minor role in HA endocytosis and clearance. Instead, HA uptake and clearance are predominantly mediated by specialized endocytic receptors, hyaluronan receptor for endocytosis (HARE) in the liver and lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 (LYVE-1) in lymphatic vessels [ , ]. Dysregulated HYAL-1/2 activity, in concert with altered HA–CD44 signaling, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of cancer progression and recurrence, dermatological disorders, pulmonary hypertension, and renal injury [ , ].
PH20 is primarily expressed in the testes but is also detected in the epididymis, prostate, placenta [ ], as well as in certain tumors [ ]. Similar to HYAL-1, PH20 hydrolyzes both HA and chondroitin sulfate [ ]. It exists as both a GPI-anchored isoform localized to the sperm plasma membrane and inner acrosomal membrane, and a soluble isoform lacking a GPI linkage [ , ]. Functionally, PH20 facilitates HA degradation within the cumulus ECM during fertilization and has been implicated in Ca2+ signaling through HA binding [ ]. Notably, the membrane-bound isoform is active only at neutral pH, whereas the acrosomal isoform remains active at both neutral and acidic pH due to structural modifications following proteolysis [ ].
By contrast, HYAL-3, HYAL-4, and HYAL-P1 have uncertain enzymatic activities and are of lesser biological significance. HYAL-3 is mainly expressed in the testes and bone marrow and may act as a non-enzymatic regulator of HYAL-1, though no intrinsic catalytic activity has been confirmed [ , ]. HYAL-4 exhibits a substrate preference for chondroitin sulfate rather than HA, likely due to divergence at key catalytic residues, and its physiological role remains unclear [ ]. HYAL-P1 represents a pseudogene in humans and is not translated into a functional protein [ ].
Beyond the canonical HYAL family, emerging evidence suggests that non-classical hyaluronan-binding proteins, specifically HYBID and TMEM2, also play pivotal roles in HA catabolism [ ]. The cell migration-inducing protein (CEMIP), also known as the HA-binding protein involved in HA depolymerization (HYBID), encoded by KIAA1199, is a non-classical HA-degrading protein implicated in HA turnover in mesenchymal tissues such as dermal fibroblasts and synovium [ ]. Although its mechanism differs from that of enzymatic hyaluronidases, HYBID has been linked to ECM remodeling in inflammatory and malignant contexts [ , , ].
Transmembrane protein 2 (TMEM2) is a hyaluronidase-like cell-surface protein whose role in HA degradation remains controversial, with conflicting reports on whether it possesses intrinsic catalytic activity or functions indirectly as a regulator or requires cofactors [ , , ]. TMEM2 has been implicated in neural crest and tissue development, ER stress responses, and tumor invasion [ , , ].

2.2.1.2.

## Vertebrate hyaluronidases – bovine testicular hyaluronidases
Bovine and ovine testicular hyaluronidases, present as components of the membrane-associated PH20 complex, have been applied therapeutically across multiple medical fields for decades owing to their high catalytic activity [ ]. Among them, bovine testicular hyaluronidase (BTH) has the broadest clinical utility [ ]. BTH shares 22.9–25.2% sequence identity with human hyaluronidases and preferentially hydrolyzes HA, with weaker activity toward chondroitin sulfate [ , ]. Commercial BTH is formulated in soluble form and exhibits maximal activity around pH 4.
BTH recognizes (GlcUA–GlcNAc)4 as a substrate, cleaving the first β-1,4-N-acetylhexosaminide bond to produce the hexasaccharide (GlcUA–GlcNAc)3; the remaining (GlcUA–GlcNAc) disaccharide can then be transferred to the nonreducing terminus of another oligosaccharide by transglycosylation to yield the tetrasaccharide (GlcUA–GlcNAc)2 [ ]. When the substrate is a hyaluronan octasaccharide bearing an unsaturated glucuronic acid at the nonreducing end, cleavage of the second bond generates only the disaccharide as the final product. Hydrolytic activity is optimal at pH 4.0 in the presence of NaCl, whereas transglycosylation is favored at pH 7.0 without NaCl, yet still proceeds under the hydrolytic conditions [ ]. Notably, crude sperm extracts showed higher activity at pH 7 than at pH 4 [ ].
Despite its long-standing clinical use, BTH preparations have historically been derived from crude tissue extracts, often containing low proportions of active enzyme and exhibiting considerable batch-to-batch variability [ , ]. As summarized in , although reported turnover numbers for BTH are comparable to or slightly higher than those of rHuPH20, its substantially lower specific activity necessitates higher dosing to achieve comparable permeation-enhancing effects [ , ].
 
2.2.1.3.

## Venom hyaluronidases
Venom hyaluronidases have been identified and characterized in the venoms of snakes, bees, spiders, and scorpions, among others [ , , ]. These enzymes share about 30% homology with mammalian hyaluronidases and exhibit optimal activity between pH 4.0 and 6.0 [ ]. HA is the principal substrate, with chondroitin sulfate being weakly susceptible. Venom hyaluronidases display robust HA-depolymerizing activity within the ECM, thereby acting as spreading factors that facilitate the diffusion of toxins through tissues and into the circulation [ ]. They can also provoke severe allergic reactions in humans, mediated by IgE responses to protein epitopes and glycans on the enzyme surface as well as by the generation of low-molecular-weight HA fragments (LMW-HA) during HA degradation [ , , , ]. Consequently, venom hyaluronidases are frequently used in the development and evaluation of hyaluronidase inhibitors as adjuncts to antivenom therapy [ ]. As outlined in , venom-derived hyaluronidases, despite their robust HA-depolymerizing activity, remain limited in clinical translation due to immunogenicity and variability.

## Invertebrate hyaluronidases – leech hyaluronidases
Leech hyaluronidases (LHyal) were first described in 1937 (Claude, 1937), although the responsible genes and their enzymatic properties have been elucidated only recently [ , ]. LHyal cleaves β-1,3-glycosidic linkages to generate HA oligosaccharides bearing d-glucuronic acid at the reducing end [ ]. The enzyme is sensitive to metal ions (Mn2+, Cu2+, and Fe3+), shows optimal activity at pH 6.5 and 45 °C, and is notably thermostable. LHyal acts specifically on HA without transglycosylation activity and exhibits stronger catalytic activity than BTH [ ], with no risk of interspecies pathogen transmission [ ]. Its favorable properties, including the facility for producing LMW-HA with defined size distributions, underscore its potential in medical and biotechnological applications [ , ]. Recent efforts have focused on engineered recombinant LHyal variants with improved thermal stability and catalytic efficiency [ , , , ].

2.2.2.2.

## Invertebrate hyaluronidases – hookworm hyaluronidases
Hookworm larvae produce hyaluronidases with high specificity for HA and optimal activity at near-neutral pH (approximately 5.0–6.0) [ , ]. Although similar to LHyals, their activity is unaffected by copper sulfate, zinc chloride, calcium chloride, manganese chloride, or ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Functionally, these enzymes act as spreading factors that disrupt host tissue barriers to facilitate larval penetration, migration, and development, and they can attenuate host immune defenses. The hyaluronidase from Ancylostoma caninum shares the highest sequence similarity (about 52.6%) with human HYAL-2 [ , ], suggesting potential utility in drug delivery applications that merits further investigation.

## Microbial hyaluronidases
Microbial hyaluronidases (mHyals) are found in bacteria, fungi, and bacteriophages [ ]. Well-characterized bacterial and phage mHyals belong predominantly to the PL8 and PL16 families, which generate unsaturated disaccharides by cleaving β-1,4-glycosidic bonds, whereas several fungal mHyals have been assigned to the GH16 family. Most exhibit optimal activity at 37–45 °C and pH 5.5–7.0 and are sensitive to metal ions such as Mn2+ and Ni2+. As ECM-targeted spreading factors, mHyals promote invasion and infection by pathogenic bacteria through specific degradation of HA. This process yields unsaturated oligosaccharides that can be catabolized as energy sources [ ] or leveraged to evade host immune responses [ , ]. In the gut, mHyals produced by commensal microbes can reshape the microbiota and influence intestinal metabolism [ ]. Therapeutically, mHyals are being explored to enhance anticancer drug penetration and restrain tumor growth and progression [ , , ], and to disrupt biofilms for the treatment of multidrug-resistant pathogens [ ]. Bacterial hyaluronidases, in particular, are attractive for bioproduction owing to streamlined manufacturing, high purity, and reduced environmental burden relative to animal-derived HA-processing enzymes [ , ].
mHyals, including those derived from Streptomyces hyalurolyticus and Clostridium perfringens, are primarily supplied as research-grade enzymatic reagents rather than clinically approved biologics. Accordingly, systematic human immunogenicity assessments have not been conducted [ , ]. Nonetheless, elevated anti-hyaluronidase titers have been documented in post-streptococcal infectious settings, indicating that bacterial hyaluronidases can elicit host antibody responses under natural exposure. Although certain mHyals demonstrate high catalytic efficiency under optimized conditions, uncertainties in manufacturing standardization, regulatory pathway, and clinical validation continue to limit their pharmaceutical translation ( ).

2.2.4.

## Comparative immunogenicity across hyaluronidase sources
As summarized in , the immunogenicity profiles of hyaluronidases vary substantially depending on their biological source, which critically influences their clinical translatability. Human hyaluronidases, being endogenous proteins, are inherently less likely to trigger immune responses under physiological conditions.
In contrast, animal-derived preparations such as BTH possess recognized antigenicity and may elicit antibody formation upon repeated administration. Venom-derived hyaluronidases are well-established allergens capable of inducing IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reactions. Diagnosis of Hymenoptera venom allergy is often confounded by IgE cross-reactivity between honey bee venom (HBV, Apis mellifera) and yellow jacket venom (YJV, Vespula vulgaris), particularly involving shared allergens like hyaluronidase. Recent studies highlight an asymmetric pattern where sensitization to vespid hyaluronidases (e.g., Ves v 2 or Pol d 2) frequently leads to cross-reactivity with the honey bee counterpart (Api m 2), whereas the reverse is less common [ ].
By comparison, invertebrate and mHyals have been investigated primarily in infectious or experimental contexts, and their immunogenic behavior in therapeutic settings remains incompletely defined. Notably, rHuPH20, discussed in Section , has demonstrated low immunogenicity in clinical studies, with predominantly non-neutralizing antibodies and no consistent impact on pharmacokinetics or safety outcomes.

2.3.

## Recombinant human PH20
Since the 1940s, animal-derived hyaluronidases have been used as spreading factors; however, their clinical utility is constrained by low active enzyme content, substantial batch-to-batch variability in crude extracts, and modest specific activity (about 70 U/mg), which limit repeated clinical use [ ]. Contaminating impurities also raise concerns regarding allergic reactions and anaphylaxis [ ].
rHuPH20, engineered from the human PH20 gene, has emerged as a promising alternative. rHuPH20 is a soluble protein lacking the C-terminal GPI-anchor region; a cDNA encoding amino acids 1–447 is expressed in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells for controlled large-scale production [ ]. Protein engineering of PH20 has focused on enhancing structural stability, catalytic efficiency, and manufacturability through glycoengineering and targeted amino-acid substitutions. Precise preservation and regulation of the six major N-linked glycosylation sites are critical for maintaining catalytic-domain integrity and protecting the enzyme from proteolytic degradation in vivo [ ]. Experimental deglycosylation markedly reduces enzymatic activity and tissue-dispersion capacity, underscoring the essential role of glycan-mediated structural stabilization [ , ]. In parallel, rational amino-acid substitutions within α-helical and linker regions have been introduced to improve recombinant expression yield, catalytic performance, and thermal stability relative to the wild-type enzyme [ ]. Engineered variants retain more than 50% of enzymatic activity at temperatures exceeding 50 °C and demonstrate increased resistance to serum-derived inhibitory factors, thereby sustaining consistent permeation-enhancing effects under physiologically relevant conditions [ ].
Collectively, these engineering advances have enabled production of rHuPH20 preparations with ≥90% purity, <0.5 EU/mg endotoxin, and an in vitro specific activity of ≥100,000 USP U/mg—approximately 140–200-fold higher than animal-derived hyaluronidase preparations [ , ]. In commercial formulations, rHuPH20 typically exhibits enzymatic activity in the range of 50,000–100,000 U/mg [ ]. This performance advantage over biologically derived hyaluronidases is summarized in .
Mechanistically, rHuPH20 cleaves the β-1,4-glycosidic bond between d-N-acetylglucosamine and d-glucuronate, identical to native human PH20, while retaining physiological glycosylation sites that support in vivo stability [ ]. Upon SC administration, rHuPH20 rapidly depolymerizes HA within minutes, transiently reducing the viscoelasticity of the SC matrix [ ]. This effect is local and short-lived, with the HA barrier re-forming and normal tissue viscoelasticity returning within 24–48 h [ ]; in another animal study, reconstitution of SC HA was observed within about 15–20 h post-injection [ ]. Consistent with these properties, no tissue inflammation or histopathological damage has been observed at injection sites.
These features confer several clinical advantages over animal-derived PH20. rHuPH20 transiently expands SC capacity, enabling rapid dispersion of large fluid volumes and co-administered drugs [ , ]. In single-site infusions, it reduces tissue deformation and pain and increases achievable flow rates by up to about 20-fold. Relative to administration of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) alone, rHuPH20 can enhance bioavailability, increase maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), shorten time to reach Cmax (Tmax), and reduce pharmacokinetic variability [ ]. Consequently, rHuPH20 has facilitated SC delivery and rapid onset of action for diverse therapeutics, including hydration fluids [ ], insulin [ , ], monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) [ , ], immunoglobulin [ , ], and small molecules [ , ].
Given its recombinant human origin, rHuPH20 was developed to minimize the antigenicity observed with animal-derived preparations. Across clinical studies, anti-rHuPH20 antibodies have been detected at relatively low frequencies, with incidence rates ranging from 1.7% to 18.1%, depending on assay sensitivity and study population. IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reactions remain rare (<0.1%) [ , ]. Additional study-level details are summarized in . Importantly, most detected antibodies have been non-neutralizing. Neutralizing antibodies were either not observed or identified at very low titers. Integrated analyses have shown no consistent association between antibody positivity and alterations in pharmacokinetics, clinical efficacy, or safety outcomes [ ]. Furthermore, repeated dosing in approved indications has not been associated with cumulative immune sensitization [ ]. These findings collectively support the favorable immunogenicity profile of rHuPH20 in therapeutic applications, particularly when contrasted with the recognized antigenicity of non-human hyaluronidase preparations [ ].

3.

## Clinical applications of hyaluronidase
All currently marketed hyaluronidase products act by partially depolymerizing HA within the ECM ( ). In normal body fluids and tissues, HA typically has a molecular weight of 1000–8000 kDa [ ]. Experimental data indicate that rHuPH20 degrades this polymer into LMW-HA of approximately 100–1000 kDa [ ]. By cleaving HA, rHuPH20 also reduces the viscosity of the SC interstitium and shifts transport from diffusion-dominated to convective bulk flow, thereby lowering the hydraulic resistance of the ECM and increasing tissue permeability [ , ]. Furthermore, the permeability-enhancing effect of rHuPH20 is localized and reversible. HA within the ECM is typically reconstituted within 24–48 h, after which tissue integrity and viscoelastic properties return to baseline [ , ]. This rapid turnover limits the prolonged accumulation of LMW-HAs, thereby reducing the likelihood of sustained systemic inflammatory activation [ ].
 This restoration reflects the rapid turnover of HA in SC tissue and de novo synthesis mediated by hyaluronan synthases (HAS), among which HAS2 serves as the principal isoform responsible for HA production and ECM homeostasis [ ]. Whereas HAS1 and HAS3 typically synthesize shorter HA chains (approximately 200–2000 kDa), HAS2 predominantly generates higher-molecular-weight polymers (about 2000 kDa), which are generally associated with tissue hydration and structural homeostasis [ ]. Moreover, rHuPH20 itself is rapidly cleared from the circulation, with an elimination half-life of approximately 5–6 min, further supporting its short-lived and localized biological activity [ ].
3.1.

## Clinical applications of hyaluronidase as a permeation enhancer
The first animal-derived hyaluronidase approved for clinical use was Vitrase® (ovine testicular hyaluronidase), authorized by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) in 2004 to facilitate the SC dispersion and absorption of co-administered drugs [ ]. Subsequently, additional animal-derived products (Amphadase® and Hydase®) and a recombinant human formulation (Hylenex®) were developed, bringing the total number of US FDA-approved preparations to four ( ). In addition, other hyaluronidase products—such as Hylase Dessau, Hyalase, Liporase, and Hirax—have been approved and are used clinically in several countries [ ]. Through their common mechanism of ECM softening, these agents are employed for the indications outlined below.
 3.1.1.

## Enhancement of drug dispersion and systemic absorption
Hyaluronidase functions as a permeation enhancer and spreading factor by hydrolyzing HA, thereby increasing tissue permeability, broadening the diffusion field of injectates, and enhancing the onset and extent of local anesthesia [ , ].
The clinical benefits of hyaluronidase as a local anesthetic spreading agent have been demonstrated in multiple studies. In a series of 72 dermatologic procedures, the addition of hyaluronidase to a local anesthetic minimized surface contour distortion and facilitated tissue undermining and dissection within SC tissue planes [ ]. Randomized controlled trials have shown that hyaluronidase enhances the diffusion field of lidocaine and prolongs the duration of cutaneous analgesia. In a study evaluating 1% lidocaine with 15 IU/mL hyaluronidase, the treated group exhibited a significantly broader anesthetic field and reduced tissue distortion, although injection-related pain was modestly increased [ ].
Beyond cutaneous applications, ophthalmic and neurosurgical studies provide additional support. In cataract surgery, incorporation of hyaluronidase into retrobulbar or sub-Tenon blocks has been associated with faster onset of akinesia, improved ocular immobility, and, in some reports, a reduction in anesthetic volume without increased complications [ ]. Similarly, in elective craniotomy, the addition of hyaluronidase to a lidocaine–bupivacaine mixture for a scalp nerve block resulted in more rapid sensory blockade and improved intraoperative conditions compared with the anesthetic alone [ ]. Enhanced tissue permeability has also been shown to reduce local infusion pressure during intradermal injection, potentially improving procedural tolerability in human subjects [ ].
In hypodermoclysis—the infusion of large fluid volumes into the subcutaneous tissue—hyaluronidase promotes rapid absorption and reduces tissue distension [ ]. It has also been combined with insulin to accelerate absorption, increase peak concentrations, and reduce glycemic variability [ ].

3.1.2.

## Management of edema, fluid accumulation, and procedural complications
By degrading HA within the ECM, hyaluronidase acts as a spreading agent that facilitates redistribution and resorption of interstitial fluid and hematomas, thereby reducing local pressure in settings such as extravasation, edema, and hematoma [ , , ]. Clinically, improvements have been reported for lower-eyelid edema following aesthetic procedures [ ], and for orbital swelling after intracranial aneurysm surgery [ ], often permitting lower corticosteroid exposure relative to dexamethasone-based regimens [ ]. It is also effective for ganglion cysts [ ] and can accelerate liquefaction and resorption of post-traumatic facial hematomas, shortening treatment duration [ ].
Hyaluronidase has also become an indispensable tool in aesthetic medicine for managing HA filler complications. With the rising use of HA fillers, complications such as overfilling, vascular occlusion, necrosis, and nodules have become more frequent. Hyaluronidase selectively degrades HA fillers and serves as an emergency rescue treatment for filler-related adverse events; at appropriate doses, it can rapidly dissolve even cross-linked products [ ]. High-dose pulsed hyaluronidase is widely adopted for acute management and prevention of sequelae, although standardized dosing protocols remain variable across reports [ , ].
The same ECM-softening mechanism also assists surgeons. In ophthalmic surgery, hyaluronidase has long been used to facilitate vitreous-body liquefaction and to enhance the spread and efficacy of local anesthesia [ ]. In reconstructive surgery, its injection at the recipient site can reduce ECM viscoelasticity and support adipocyte survival, improving fat-graft retention [ ]. Historically, hyaluronidase has also been employed in SC urography to accelerate systemic absorption of subcutaneously administered contrast media when venous access was limited [ ].

3.1.3.

## Remodeling pathological ECM
In keloids and hypertrophic scars, excessive HA accumulation and fibroblast proliferation thicken the ECM and limit drug diffusion. Hyaluronidase reduces HA content and alters fibroblast secretory activity, thereby decreasing collagen synthesis and softening the matrix. Clinical reports describe improved penetration of concomitant therapies, such as triamcinolone (TAC), 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), and 5-aminolevulinic acid, along with attenuation of fibrosis [ ]. In a study involving twenty patients with keloids or hypertrophic scars, monthly injections of a cocktail containing TAC, 5-FU, and 1500 IU of hyaluronidase flattened lesions without systemic side effects. Subsequent randomized trials comparing TAC monotherapy with hyaluronidase-containing combinations reported that the latter achieved similar efficacy but with fewer adverse events.
Beyond scarring, hyaluronidase-mediated ECM remodeling has been leveraged to soften sclerotic tissues. In patients with systemic sclerosis, perioral injections of hyaluronidase for microstomia improved mouth opening and functional impairment scores [ ]. In cutis verticis gyrata, injection of hyaluronidase into scalp furrows enabled nonsurgical management with clinical improvement [ ].
In oncology, an HA-rich tumor microenvironment (TME) increases interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) and hinders drug penetration [ ], and preclinical studies have shown that hyaluronidase can improve intratumoral distribution and therapeutic response [ , ]. In Kaposi's sarcoma and dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, peritumoral administration of hyaluronidase has been explored to reduce tumor size preoperatively or to enhance penetration of cytotoxic agents [ ].

3.2.

## Clinical applications of hyaluronidase in drug delivery
Beyond its long-standing role as a tissue permeation enhancer, hyaluronidase has increasingly been incorporated into drug delivery strategies that deliberately exploit ECM remodeling to improve drug transport. In this context, enzymatic degradation of hyaluronan is not merely used to facilitate local dispersion, but is strategically integrated into formulation design and therapeutic regimens to enhance drug absorption and intratumoral distribution. This shift has been particularly impactful in two settings: enabling high-dose SC administration of protein therapeutics and enhancing penetration of anticancer agents within hyaluronan-rich TMEs. Representative clinical and translational advances in these areas are summarized below.
3.2.1.

## SC formulations of protein therapeutics
rHuPH20 transiently degrades HMW-HA in the SC space, creating aqueous microchannels approximately 200 nm in diameter that facilitate drug dispersion and absorption [ ]. Leveraging this mechanism, high-dose SC formulations of multiple protein therapeutics are now actively being developed.
In 2013, HYQVIA® received European Medicines Agency (EMA) approval for the treatment of primary immunodeficiency [ ]. HYQVIA® is a co-packaged product containing hyaluronidase (160 U/mL) and human immunoglobulin (IgG, 100 mg/mL), enabling monthly SC IgG infusions in place of weekly or daily SC regimens and allowing self-administration at home, unlike intravenous (IV) IgG. A Phase 3 program is ongoing for TAK-881 (IgG 20% + rHuPH20), which doubles the IgG concentration relative to HYQVIA® [ ]. In the same year, HERCEPTIN® SC (trastuzumab combined with hyaluronidase) was approved by the EMA for patients with HER2-positive breast cancer [ ]. In the phase III HannaH trial, fixed-dose SC trastuzumab (600 mg) demonstrated pharmacokinetic non-inferiority to the standard weight-based intravenous regimen. The SC formulation achieved higher steady-state trough concentrations and comparable antitumor efficacy, with pathologic complete response (pCR) rates of 45.4% (SC) versus 40.7% (IV). No clear exposure–response relationship for efficacy or Grade ≥3 adverse events was observed, supporting the use of a fixed-dose SC regimen across a broad body weight range without increased toxicity [ ].
To date, one sequentially administered combination product and eight rHuPH20 co-formulated products have reached the global market ( ), and an additional 28 candidate programs are being pursued to expand into new combinations and indications ( ). According to ClinicalTrials.gov, three Phase 3 studies are evaluating co-formulations: amivantamab with lazertinib, nivolumab with relatlimab, and pembrolizumab, as a broadly used immuno-oncologic agent. Such formulations extend dosing intervals and increase maximum deliverable volumes per injection, easing burdens on patients and providers and enabling self-administration that can reduce healthcare system costs [ ].
 
3.2.2.

## Tumor targeted delivery and therapy
In solid tumors, excessive accumulation of HMW-HA within the TME compresses intratumoral vasculature and elevates IFP, thereby impeding penetration and distribution of anticancer agents [ ]. Hyaluronidase-mediated ECM remodeling alleviates these biophysical constraints by reducing hydraulic resistance and facilitating convective drug transport [ ]. In human prostate cancer (PC3) xenografts, intravenous pegvorhyaluronidase alfa (PEGPH20) significantly decreased tumor IFP within 2 h, leading to vascular decompression and reperfusion [ ]. Similarly, hyaluronidase treatment in osteosarcoma xenografts lowered IFP by 63–84% and enhanced vascular patency within 1 h [ ].
Clinically, two hyaluronidase-based approaches have been investigated ( ). PEGPH20 was combined with cytotoxic regimens to enzymatically deplete tumor HA. Although early Phase 1/2 studies suggested increased drug uptake and antitumor activity in HA-high tumors [ , ], the Phase 3 HALO 301 trial failed to meet its primary endpoint, and development was discontinued [ ]. Investigators posited that mere substrate depletion may be insufficient, and that intrinsic tumor features—such as low immunogenicity, low mutational burden, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition—may have contributed to failure [ ]. The HALO 301 results underscore the limitations of stromal depletion as a standalone strategy.
The second approach employs VCN-01, an oncolytic adenovirus engineered to selectively replicate in cancer cells and express hyaluronidase. By degrading tumor stroma, VCN-01 improves penetration of chemotherapeutics and antibodies. Preclinical and proof-of-concept clinical studies have shown stromal depletion, enhanced drug delivery, and favorable tolerability when VCN-01 was combined with chemotherapy [ ]. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating VCN-01 with diverse anticancer regimens ( ), suggesting that this strategy may integrate stromal remodeling with immune modulation.

4.

## Recent advances in hyaluronidase co-formulation strategies for SC delivery
The incorporation of hyaluronidase has enabled the SC administration of high-concentration protein therapeutics at volumes that were previously impractical by lowering ECM viscoelasticity and transiently expanding SC capacity [ , ]. By modulating hyaluronan-rich ECMs at the injection site, rHuPH20 co-formulation has expanded the feasibility of large-volume SC delivery while preserving systemic pharmacokinetic exposure and patient convenience. provides an overview of current research directions in rHuPH20-enabled SC drug delivery.
 Despite this progress, several unmet needs persist. These include (1) the injectability burden associated with highly viscous co-formulations, which translates into elevated plunger forces and musculoskeletal strain for both patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs); (2) the dual-stability challenge of maintaining both the biologic API and the hyaluronidase enzyme; (3) limitations in device platforms that still constrain self-administration at larger volumes; and (4) the need for quantitative PK and immunogenicity modeling frameworks capable of guiding rational dose selection and lifecycle optimization. The following sections synthesize current formulation and device strategies and highlight directions for clinical and translational research that are actively evolving in this sector.
4.1.

## Formulation optimization
Hyaluronidase-enabled co-formulations are typically viscous, particularly at the protein concentrations required for dose consolidation and reduced injection frequency. Elevated viscosity increases plunger force, compromises patient comfort and HCP ergonomics, and can exacerbate shear-induced instabilities [ ]. Concomitantly, co-formulations must safeguard two actives—the enzyme and the API—whose optimal microenvironments may differ. Formulation parameters therefore require holistic optimization, not only to prevent antagonistic degradation pathways but also to preserve catalytic activity. Thus, optimization of formulation parameters for rHuPH20 co-formulations has converged on three interrelated aspects: pH and buffer selection, excipient-driven stabilization, and surfactant-mediated control of interfacial stress. Representative compositions of marketed co-formulations are summarized in .
 4.1.1.

## pH and buffer selection
In protein drug formulations, appropriate buffers are employed to maintain solution pH, thereby stabilizing protein structure and preserving biological activity [ ]. In hyaluronidase co-formulations, histidine and acetic acid/sodium acetate buffers are most common ( ), providing a mildly acidic milieu (pH 5–6) that balances API and enzyme stability [ ]. Histidine, whose pKa is approximately 6.0, provides effective buffering capacity across pH 5.5–6.5 and has been reported to confer multiple formulation benefits [ ]. However, histidine (especially as the HCl salt) can interact with polysorbates, accelerating ester hydrolysis under certain conditions—a risk that should be addressed in formulation assessments [ ]. Acetate buffers can reduce injection-site pain relative to histidine in some contexts [ ]. By contrast, phosphate buffers—historically used in standalone hyaluronidase products—exhibit temperature-dependent pH drift and have been associated with increased injection discomfort, making them less favored for modern SC biotherapeutics [ , ].

4.1.2.

## Excipient-driven stabilization
In protein therapeutics, stabilizing excipients are employed to suppress denaturation and aggregation during manufacturing and storage, as well as to adjust tonicity or osmolality [ ]. In hyaluronidase co-formulations, commonly used stabilizers include sugars (e.g., trehalose, sucrose), sugar alcohols (e.g., sorbitol), amino acids (e.g., methionine), salts (e.g., sodium chloride), and chelators (e.g., EDTA, diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid [DTPA]) ( ).
Nonreducing disaccharides decrease local mobility and water activity, stabilizing proteins against unfolding and aggregation while contributing to isotonicity [ ]; trehalose often shows superior hydrolytic stability for long-term storage. Sugar alcohols can lower aggregation propensity without posing a Maillard reaction risk and may modestly reduce viscosity in high-concentration IgG solutions [ ].
Amino acid excipients are useful for suppressing protein oxidation and aggregation. Among these, l-methionine is frequently added as an antioxidant in protein formulations; under oxidative stress, methionine is preferentially oxidized, thereby protecting methionine residues in the API from damage [ ]. Other amino acids—such as glycine, arginine, and proline—can likewise function as protein-stabilizing cosolutes. However, at excessive concentrations amino acids may themselves compromise stability; for arginine, cases of diminished physical stability at high levels have been reported [ , ]. Further work is warranted to evaluate other amino acids, and careful concentration selection is likely required.
NaCl is the prototypical tonicity agent, used to adjust osmolality toward physiological levels to minimize tissue irritation upon injection. Appropriate ionic strength from NaCl can also attenuate excessive electrostatic repulsion and nonspecific aggregation, thereby improving protein stability [ ]. Among marketed products, only Vyvgart Hytrulo (180 mg/mL) contains sodium chloride both as a viscosity-reducing agent and a tonicifier; other products with protein concentrations of 40–125 mg/mL do not include viscosity-reducing agents [ ].
For hyaluronidase itself, Ca2+ ions are required for structural stabilization and catalytic activity; accordingly, CaCl2 is included at defined levels to maximize enzymatic activity [ ]. Because trace transition metals can catalyze protein oxidation, chelators are commonly incorporated to mitigate this risk [ ]. EDTA and DTPA tightly sequester trace metals, suppressing metal-catalyzed protein oxidation and polysorbate peroxidation, among other degradation pathways [ , ]. Notably, EDTA is non-selective and will also chelate divalent cations such as calcium; thus, when EDTA is used in formulations that require Ca2+ (e.g., hyaluronidase), co-formulation with CaCl2 to rebalance free calcium becomes an important optimization consideration [ ].
Preservatives are often added to multi-dose presentations. Earlier animal-derived hyaluronidase products contained trace levels of the organomercurial preservative thimerosal, which—although rare—has been associated with local or systemic hypersensitivity reactions consistent with contact allergy [ ].

4.1.3.

## Surfactant effects on interfacial stress
Small amounts of surfactant are routinely added to protein formulations to prevent irreversible aggregation and interfacial adsorption. Nonionic polysorbates (Tween® family) are the most widely used: even at low levels, they occupy container and air–liquid interfaces, limiting protein crowding and nonspecific hydrophobic association, thereby improving physical stability. Indeed, more than 90% of commercial antibody products contain polysorbate-20 (PS20) or polysorbate-80 (PS80), underscoring their status as standard excipients for biologics [ ].
The principal difference between PS20 and PS80 lies in fatty-acid ester composition; PS80 contains an oleate (C18:1) chain, rendering it longer and unsaturated relative to PS20. Consequently, PS80 can be somewhat more prone to oxidative degradation, potentially generating peroxides and oxidized fatty acids; however, for certain proteins, PS80 can better suppress aggregation than PS20 [ ]. Choice of polysorbate is therefore protein- and formulation-dependent, and the two are not typically mixed.
Recent experience has revealed polysorbate hydrolysis and oxidation during long-term storage and/or in the presence of trace metals or container–closure interactions [ ]. As mitigations, some formulations employ the nonionic polymeric surfactant poloxamer 188 (Pluronic® F68) or avoid classical surfactants altogether by using polyethylene glycol (PEG) 3350 for interfacial protection.
To date, most excipient studies have focused on effects on individual components (the API or hyaluronidase) rather than on potential incompatibilities within co-formulations; systematic documentation of inter-component stability conflicts remains limited. Three cases have been studied in more detail. First, phenolic preservatives commonly used to preserve protein APIs (phenol, m-cresol, benzyl alcohol) can reduce the melting temperature (Tm) of rHuPH20 and thereby compromise its conformational stability [ ]. For such presentations, combinations of stabilizers (e.g., sugars, amino acids, surfactants) are recommended; representative matrices include trehalose with PS20 or PS80 in a histidine buffer [ ].
Additionally, a near-neutral (approximately pH 7.2) liquid combination of rHuPH20 with immunoglobulin maintained >90% enzymatic activity for six months at room temperature when optimized with excipients such as glycine and polysorbate [ ]. Consistent with these findings, approved co-formulations—e.g., AbbVie's HyQvia® and Genentech's Herceptin® SC—use buffers near pH 7 with stabilizers such as human albumin or amino acids to maintain both components [ ].

4.2.

## High-concentration/high-volume delivery devices
Leveraging hyaluronidase enables a substantial expansion of SC injection volumes—from the conventional approximately 2.25 mL ceiling to as much as about 15 mL in practice. Nevertheless, most marketed products still require administration by HCPs, which can limit patient access and erode the economic value proposition of SC formulations ( ). In addition, hyaluronidase co-formulations often employ relatively larger-gauge needles to accommodate higher viscosity, a factor associated with increased injection pain [ ]. Continued device-level optimization is therefore needed to improve usability and patient satisfaction.
 4.2.1.

## Needle design and injection-rate optimization
Needle gauge directly influences delivery performance. Larger gauges (21G–25G) reduce flow resistance, better accommodate viscous formulations, and allow faster injections with lower required force; however, they are frequently linked to greater tissue trauma and pain at the injection site [ ]. Conversely, finer gauges (27G–30G) are generally better tolerated but impose higher hydraulic resistance, demanding greater actuation force and potentially compromising ease of use [ ].
Design studies aimed at jointly optimizing patient experience and delivery efficiency have highlighted the value of geometry and wall profile. For solutions in the 10–50 cP range, tapered needles reduced required injection force by 30–50%; notably, a 27G ultra-thin-wall tapered needle outperformed a standard 27G needle at higher viscosities [ ]. A 29G thin-needle-nozzle design lowered force relative to a conventional thin-wall needle of the same gauge and, for shear-thinning fluids, even required less force than a 27G thin-wall needle [ ].
Device optimization tailored to hyaluronidase-enabled formulations remains at an early stage. To fully realize the high-concentration, high-volume SC potential unlocked by rHuPH20, future work should co-optimize needle geometry and rate/force profiles against the rheology and tissue-modulating characteristics of the rHuPH20 formulation.

4.2.2.

## Self-injection-friendly platforms
Pre-filled syringes and auto-injectors are now among the most prevalent and promising delivery platforms in biopharmaceutics [ ]. On-body delivery systems function as hybrids between wearable pumps and auto-injectors, enabling delivery of 5–20 mL (or more) over extended periods with patient-convenient operation [ ]. Even so, improvements are still needed regarding self-administration suitability, injection tolerability, and pharmacokinetic (PK) equivalence. For example, prolonged use of large-volume auto-injectors (LVAI) can lead to early device removal if pain becomes excessive [ ], and tissue back-pressure can challenge SC auto-injector performance [ ].
Hyaluronidase appears to be a practical enabler of rapid, large-volume SC delivery—combining generally well-tolerated local reactions with favorable PK profiles—and its potent tissue-spreading effect can help accelerate development of portable, high-volume self-injection devices [ ]. In preclinical studies using self-injection paradigms, adding rHuPH20 improved SC performance versus placebo at high injection rates up to 2 mL [ ]. In a phase 1 LVAI study, approximately 10 mL of IgG (100 mg/mL) co-formulated with rHuPH20 (4000 U/mL) was delivered in 30 s without serious adverse events and with acceptable usability [ ]. Using a miniature-swine model, Connor et al. evaluated inline pressure and required force for Ig solutions (20–200 mg/mL) with or without rHuPH20 (2000 or 5000 U/mL). Required force emerged as a practical endpoint for monitoring high-speed, high-volume SC injections; increasing needle diameter markedly reduced applied force, enabling faster and larger-volume injections [ ].
These considerations are especially salient for regimens requiring repeated dosing [ ]. To date, however, much of the clinical experience remains concentrated at lower volumes; expanding evidence across a broader range of doses and viscosities will be important to generalize device–formulation guidance for hyaluronidase-enabled SC delivery.

4.3.

## PK modeling in rHuPH20-enabled SC delivery
Compared with IV administration, SC formulations are attractive from the standpoints of patient convenience and cost; however, prediction of SC absorption and bioavailability remains uncertain because the immediate post-injection physicochemical processes in tissue are difficult to quantify [ ]. In addition, animal studies indicate that the HA-degrading activity of rHuPH20 is confined to the injection site and declines rapidly—within about 15 min post-dose—with no detectable activity in plasma or lymph [ ]. Accordingly, the enzyme's effect cannot be represented as a simple systemic term. These considerations motivate the need, early in development and prior to clinical data, for modeling platforms that can quantitatively predict SC absorption and long-term safety and—specifically for rHuPH20 co-formulations—capture how the enzyme modifies local tissue properties. Recent reviews note that, although many models for SC absorption have been proposed since 2010, specialized models tailored to rHuPH20 co-formulations remain limited, underscoring the need for bespoke model development [ ].
4.3.1.

## PK models for rHuPH20 co-formulations
For rHuPH20-enabled SC PK prediction, four complementary platforms are being applied: multiphysics models, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK)/mini-PBPK models, population pharmacokinetic (Pop-PK) models, and multiscale hybrid models. These frameworks incorporate the HA-degrading action of rHuPH20 by partitioning its impact on the ECM into (1) immediate post-injection changes in tissue mechanics and interstitial fluid dynamics, (2) time-dependent absorption pathways and rates, and (3) systemic distribution/elimination parameters.
In multiphysics models, the SC tissue is represented as a three-dimensional, homogeneous, poroelastic medium, and interstitial flow around the bolus is described by Darcy's law. The model reported by Zheng et al. simulates the concomitant rise in tissue pressure, the change in porosity, and interstitial velocity upon injection, and traces drug transport via diffusion/convection and lymphatic uptake. The predicted rapid rise-and-gradual recovery of pressure and porosity, as well as prolonged lymphatic absorption, agree with experimental observations [ ]. Building on this, Corpstein et al. linked critical local transport attributes at the injection site to whole-body PK within a single framework and identified membrane permeability, microvascular density, and diffusion coefficient as dominant determinants of SC absorption rate [ ]. Such models support sensitivity analyses over injection conditions (e.g., volume, rate, needle gauge) and tissue properties, providing quantitative rationale for the physical effects of rHuPH20 co-formulation.
PBPK/mini-PBPK models represent the physiologic compartments governing vascular and lymphatic uptake and systemic disposition after SC dosing. This approach has already been demonstrated by a concise mini-PBPK description of SC absorption with lymphatic entry [ ], and by whole-body PBPK that jointly predicts systemic and SC behavior [ ]. Subsequent studies have refined the injection-stage initial conditions and extended to more detailed formulations. In 2023, Pepin et al. formalized the injection phase—dominated by device–formulation–tissue interactions—using a physiology-based biopharmaceutics model (PBBM; SubQ-Sim) that computes time profiles of backpressure, depot geometry, spatial concentration fields, and dose loss due to early needle withdrawal (back-flow), and uses these as initial conditions for subsequent SC absorption modeling [ ]. More recently, Stader et al. captured site-dependent dynamics of free neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) in draining lymph nodes and showed that antigen-presenting cell uptake, FcRn affinity, and dose substantially influence SC bioavailability [ ]. This family of models is well suited to extrapolate across anatomical variations such as SC fat thickness and lymph flow. Moreover, by construction, the framework can be extended to test hypotheses regarding the impact of tissue-environment changes induced by rHuPH20 by adjusting absorption rates and lymphatic-pathway weights. Multiple reviews have emphasized that PBPK is particularly useful for predicting systemic exposure of proteins and peptides with complex physiology and elimination pathways [ , ]. That said, systematic external validation and standardization of PBBM/PBPK predictions and extrapolation remain ongoing.
In clinical development, the most widely used platform is Pop-PK, which enables rapid fitting of clinical data with parsimonious structures such as a two-compartment model with first-order absorption. A 2024 platform Pop-PK analysis of rHuPH20 co-formulations fitted SC profiles of 10 monoclonal antibodies to a common structure and found that absorption rate constant (ka) and bioavailability (F) converge into a relatively narrow, molecule-agnostic range; on average, rHuPH20 co-administration increased ka by approximately 30%, while F was equivalent to or slightly improved relative to IV [ ]. Such platform priors are useful for setting initial dose/interval hypotheses when switching new mAbs to SC and for providing model-informed justification in regulatory submissions.
Multiscale hybrid models integrate micro-scale injection-site physics (multiphysics) with systemic-scale PK (PBPK/Pop-PK) in a single framework. Zheng et al. presented a hybrid model linking early changes in tissue pressure/porosity to whole-body distribution, mapping multiphysics-derived tissue property parameters into PBPK absorption-rate and pathway-weight terms [ ]. This enabled simulation of how rHuPH20 dose, injection site, and volume affect systemic exposure and PK parameters. The platform is now being extended to multi-component formulations and disease-specific ECM alterations.

4.3.2.

## Mechanistic insights and empirical trends
Recent studies increasingly quantify how rHuPH20 modulates SC PK across various drugs. Gao et al. compared SC datasets with and without rHuPH20 and showed faster absorption and improved bioavailability with the enzyme, consistent with the mechanistic expectation of reduced ECM resistance due to HA cleavage; despite limited datasets, a trend toward reduced interindividual variability was also noted, suggesting improved predictability with rHuPH20 [ ]. A 2023 Janssen abstract reported a modified mechanistic PK model incorporating rHuPH20 effects as increased absorption parameters, reproducing the observed Cmax rise and Tmax shortening under rHuPH20, thereby illustrating the feasibility of rHuPH20-tailored mechanistic modeling [ ]. While still early, these studies provide initial evidence for directly embedding the enzyme-specific mechanism and for exploring common parameter ranges applicable across different biologic molecules.

4.4.

## Immunogenicity modeling and safety considerations
Because SC regimens are frequently administered chronically and rHuPH20 co-formulations contain an additional protein (the enzyme), anticipating immunogenicity and long-term safety is critical. To date, clinical experience indicates no major safety signals beyond local reactions, with low incidence of anti-PH20 and minimal clinical impact. Nevertheless, systematic long-term data remain limited, and rare hypersensitivity reactions have been reported, reinforcing the need for model-based, prospective risk assessment under repeated dosing [ ]. Notably, in co-formulations, one must consider two immunogenicity risks—anti-PH20 and anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) against the parent mAb [ ], necessitating a model-based, prospective risk assessment for repeated dosing.
Accordingly, three modeling avenues are being actively explored. First, Pop-PK–ADA linked models treat ADA onset/titer as time-dependent covariates to quantify exposure loss and support dose/interval scenario testing [ , ]. Second, PBPK/quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) hybrids integrate FcRn recycling, antigen-presenting cell trafficking, and immune activation along the SC-lymph-systemic axis; site-dependent free-FcRn dynamics have been shown to shape SC bioavailability, and the same framework is extensible to rHuPH20 by adjusting absorption rates and pathway weights [ , , ]. Third, multiphysics models provide injection-site initial conditions (e.g., backpressure, depot geometry, interstitial flow) for PBPK coupling, enabling multiscale assessment of how injection parameters and tissue properties propagate to systemic exposure [ , ].
Nonetheless, current models have been developed predominantly around mAbs, and external validation and standardization across modalities and indications are still limited [ ]. From an immunogenicity and safety perspective, systematic validation of state-of-the-art PBBM/PBPK, including cross-species and cross-formulation extrapolation, remains an open task, and continued integration with large clinical and nonclinical datasets will be essential to strengthen generalizability [ ].

5.

## Current strategies in tumor-targeted hyaluronidase delivery
In parallel with advances in SC co-formulation strategies, hyaluronidase-integrated tumor-targeted delivery approaches are being actively investigated. As discussed above in the mechanistic framework, solid tumors frequently develop a dense, HA-rich stroma that elevates IFP and impedes the penetration and homogeneous distribution of therapeutics [ ]. Within this pathophysiological context, an increasing number of studies have explored the use of hyaluronidase to remodel the ECM and thereby facilitate intratumoral transport.
This section first outlines the quantitative evidence supporting hyaluronidase co-therapy based on comparative preclinical and clinical data and then proposes a conceptual framework for rationally implementing hyaluronidase-integrated drug-delivery strategies.
5.1.

## Quantified benefits of hyaluronidase co-therapy
Building on the recognition that HA accumulation in solid tumors impedes drug transport, numerous studies have evaluated hyaluronidase as a co-therapy to enhance intratumoral delivery across anticancer modalities ( ).
 Within the cytotoxic chemotherapy class, multiple preclinical investigations show that hyaluronidase given as a pretreatment or co-administration can markedly potentiate therapeutic efficacy. In the SKOV3/HAS3 tumor model, PEGPH20 pretreatment improved intratumoral paclitaxel distribution and substantially increased tumor growth inhibition [ ]. Recent preclinical work further suggests that embedding hyaluronidase in nanoplatforms (via co-formulation or surface display) to co-deliver it with chemotherapeutics yields superior outcomes compared with free enzyme or drug-only nanoparticles. For example, a doxorubicin (DOX) nanocarrier incorporating rHuPH20 within a PEG shell achieved enhanced tumor penetration and distribution at lower doses and suppressed tumor growth more strongly than free DOX or unmodified nanoparticles [ ]. In another study, layered coating of epirubicin (EPI)-loaded nanoparticles with hyaluronidase consistently increased tumor penetration, drug accumulation, and antitumor activity relative to the same nanoparticles without hyaluronidase, while avoiding the body-weight loss (systemic toxicity) observed with free EPI [ ].
A rapidly advancing line of research involves inserting hyaluronidase-encoding genes into oncolytic viruses to achieve selective ECM degradation within tumors, often in combination with chemotherapy agents such as nab-paclitaxel and gemcitabine [ ]. In a pancreatic cancer model, oncolytic vaccinia virus expressing Hyal1 combined with gemcitabine achieved two complete responses and three partial responses among eight mice, outperforming monotherapies [ ]. Clinically, VCN-01 has progressed to a phase II trial (VIRAGE), where combination arms showed a tendency toward longer progression-free and overall survival than standard chemotherapy (hazard ratio ≈ 0.55–0.57), providing initial clinical evidence that hyaluronidase may augment the efficacy of cytotoxic regimens. However, detailed statistics remain pending and require follow-up reporting.
A broad array of oncolytic viruses is currently under active investigation. The platform trajectory began with ICOVIR17, an adenoviral backbone (ICOVIR15, Δ24/RGD) engineered with soluble human PH20 complementary DNA (cDNA), which demonstrated improved intratumoral spread and antitumor activity over ICOVIR15 [ ]. Subsequently, loading ICOVIR17 onto mesenchymal stem cells in a post-resection glioblastoma model delayed recurrence and prolonged survival versus direct injection, indicating dual advantages of in situ PH20 expression and vector shielding [ ]. As the platform matured to VCN-01, it retained RGD-mediated targeting and PH20 expression, achieving favorable tumor distribution and propagation after intravenous dosing and demonstrating safety and biological activity in early clinical settings [ , ]. PH20-hypersecreting variants (ICO15K-40SAPH20 and ICO15K-E1aPH20) further increased intratumoral T-cell infiltration and antitumor effects, supporting synergy with immunotherapy [ ]. Additional engineering—such as incorporation of an albumin-binding domain in VCN-11 to achieve self-albumin coating for neutralizing-antibody evasion and enhanced spread [ ], or iRGD conjugation in ICOVIR17K-iRGD to increase vascular permeability and tissue penetration [ ]—has conferred efficacy gains over parental viruses. Most recently, multi-payload systems [e.g., rPRV-IL-18-γ-PH20 inserting PH20, IL-18, and IFN-γ; and KLS-3020 inserting PH20, IL-12, and soluble PD-1 (sPD-1)-Fc into an oncolytic vaccinia virus] have been explored to simultaneously improve viral dissemination, immune-cell infiltration, and immune activation [ ].
In immunotherapy, hyaluronidase co-administration has delivered pronounced benefits across cell therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Decorating chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells with hyaluronidase increased tumor growth inhibition to 76% in the A20 B-lymphoma model, and co-loading hyaluronidase with anti-PD-L1 on the same platform raised inhibition to 95% [ ]. Conjugating a hyaluronidase nanogel to CAR-T cells likewise outperformed free-enzyme combinations, achieving 83% inhibition in the Raji model [ ]. With ICIs, efficacy was most evident in HA-rich tumors. In 4T1/HAS3 breast cancer, intravenous PEGPH20 given 24 h prior to anti-PD-L1 improved tumor-growth-inhibition metrics by 40% at low ICI dose and 56% at high dose versus anti-PD-L1 alone, and extended survival by 15–22 days; in EMT-6, the combination likewise yielded significantly better control and survival than monotherapy [ ]. In CT26/HAS3 and MH194/HAS3 models, PEGPH20 + ICI produced 79% inhibition, exceeding that of ICI alone (33–60%) or PEGPH20 alone (43%) [ ]. These sensitization effects coincide with immunologic remodeling—greater CD8+ T-cell and NK-cell infiltration, reduced myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and lower PD-L1 expression—providing mechanistic plausibility.
Triple combinations that integrate targeted therapy, ICI, and ECM degradation are emerging to elevate immune responsiveness ceilings. In strongly immunosuppressive contexts such as KPC-derived pancreatic liver metastasis models, adding hyaluronidase to focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibition plus anti-PD-1 increased CD8+ T-cell activation and infiltration, reduced MDSCs, and tended to lower metastatic burden while improving survival. Notably, concurrent HA depletion and FAK blockade correlated with heightened responsiveness to anti-PD-1, underscoring the value of jointly relieving stromal and mechanical barriers and signaling-axis suppression rather than targeting a single immunosuppressive pathway [ ].
Hyaluronidase has also been combined with tumor vaccines to improve intratumoral antigen access and immune priming, yielding robust cytotoxic T-cell responses and superior antitumor efficacy. For instance, delivering the hyaluronidase gene (Spam1) to tumors using a pH-responsive carrier based on PEG and polyethylenimine (PEI) increased T-cell infiltration and perfusion, alleviated hypoxia, and—when combined with an ovalbumin (OVA) + CpG vaccine—amplified antitumor immunity and therapeutic benefit [ ]. Moreover, local intratumoral hyaluronidase alone increased CD103+ dendritic cells and antigen presentation in draining lymph nodes and elicited OVA-specific CD8+ T-cell responses, indicating that focal ECM remodeling can activate systemic (nodal) immune priming [ ].
In physics- and energy-based therapies, hyaluronidase helps condition tumors for radiotherapy (RT) and photothermal or photodynamic therapies (PTT/PDT). In HA-rich BxPC3/HAS3 tumors, PEGPH20 plus RT increased tumor oxygen partial pressure, induced re-oxygenation, and significantly prolonged survival versus either RT or hyaluronidase alone; conversely, no benefit was observed in HA-low BxPC3 tumors [ ]. These findings support hyaluronidase as a means to relieve oxygen-diffusion limits and enhance radiosensitivity. When physical modalities are combined with ICIs, durable immune-memory formation that suppresses recurrence and metastasis has been associated with decreased HIF-1α, increased M1 macrophages, reduced regulatory T cells (Tregs), MDSCs, and M2 macrophages, and augmented CD8+ T-cell infiltration [ ].
Finally, emerging strategies couple ECM degradation with nucleic acid delivery. Co-administration of hyaluronidase with nanoparticles carrying soluble human PD-L1 (shPD-L1) siRNA enhances nanoparticle penetration through HA-dense stroma and facilitates PD-L1 silencing. In the B16/F10 model, the hyaluronidase and nanoparticle combination produced stronger tumor control than either monotherapy, highlighting the utility of ECM targeting to unlock gene-therapy efficacy [ ].

5.2.

## Defining therapeutic window through dose and activity control
Hyaluronidase-based combination strategies can enhance intratumoral drug distribution and antitumor efficacy ( ); however, the optimal extent and duration of HA depletion—namely, the practical therapeutic window—have not yet been fully defined. This unresolved variability underscores the biological and clinical complexity of stromal modulation. Accordingly, HA depletion is increasingly conceptualized not as a binary intervention but as a tunable process requiring context-sensitive control.
Emerging evidence suggests that variations in administered dose and enzymatic activity can shift the balance between therapeutic benefit and adverse stromal remodeling, particularly in tumors with a high HA burden. In prostate cancer models, simultaneous modulation of HAS and Hyal-1 demonstrated that increased HA synthesis combined with Hyal-1 overexpression promoted tumor growth and metastasis, whereas Hyal-1 suppression attenuated invasiveness and tumor progression [ ]. These findings underscore that excessive or dysregulated HA degradation may paradoxically facilitate tumor progression.
Recent translational platforms further emphasize the importance of defining a clinically relevant therapeutic window. Preclinical studies of hyaluronidase-assisted nanoplatforms have highlighted the need for repeated-dose evaluation and longitudinal safety monitoring prior to clinical translation [ ]. Similarly, early-phase trials of PH20-expressing oncolytic viruses have adopted cautious dose-escalation strategies with close immune surveillance during repeated administration [ ]. In randomized studies of PEGPH20 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, dose selection required careful balancing of stromal modulation against safety risks, particularly thromboembolic events [ ]. These data support a dose-dependent paradigm in which controlled, partial matrix remodeling is preferable to complete HA ablation.
In response to these dose-dependent and safety constraints, engineering strategies have increasingly focused on tumor-targeted approaches to spatially confine HA degradation. Among these, a trispecific antibody–enzyme (AbEn) platform was engineered to target cancer-associated fibroblasts via fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and leucine-rich repeat-containing 15 (LRRC15). By conjugating rHuPH20 to this bispecific antibody framework, the platform enables selective HA degradation within the tumor stroma, thereby reducing IFP and enhancing both drug delivery and immune cell infiltration. Beyond AbEn constructs, additional tumor-targeted platforms—including nanoparticles, hydrogels, cell-based systems, and gene-delivery approaches—are actively being investigated to achieve more precise spatial and temporal control of enzymatic activity [ ].
Despite these advances, most available evidence remains preclinical, and the studies summarized in are likewise largely derived from non-clinical models. Therefore, further investigation is required to refine both dosing and activity control strategies. In clinical settings, individualized approaches incorporating tumor-specific HA burden and microenvironmental profiling may help optimize treatment intensity. Given the potential risks of excessive HA degradation, including disruption of normal tissue architecture and facilitation of metastasis [ ], cautious dose escalation, biomarker-guided monitoring, and accumulation of long-term safety data will be essential to define a safe and effective therapeutic window for hyaluronidase-based combination therapies.

5.3.

## Engineering hyaluronidase for controlled delivery
Building on the need to define and maintain an optimal therapeutic window, engineering strategies have been developed to achieve more precise spatial and temporal control of hyaluronidase delivery and activity. Rather than simply increasing systemic exposure, these approaches aim to confine HA modulation to tumor sites, prolong local activity, and minimize off-target effects. While early efforts such as PEGylation extended circulatory half-life [ , ], later platforms have prioritized tumor localization, activation specificity, and depot longevity to translate stromal modulation into reproducible therapeutic benefit. summarizes representative engineering approaches currently under investigation.
 5.3.1.

## Local depots
Local sustained-release systems keep the enzyme where it is needed and for as long as it is needed, thereby preserving ECM remodeling while limiting systemic exposure.
One approach encapsulates hyaluronidase within injectable hydrogels to enable gradual peritumoral release. In a study co-encapsulating anti-CTLA-4 with hyaluronidase and injecting the gel peritumorally, antibody delivery to tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) was faster and more durable, antitumor activity improved, and comparable or superior efficacy was achieved with >80% lower dose than systemic administration—suggesting reduced toxicity with maintained (or enhanced) benefit [ ].
Alternatively, hyaluronidase can be incorporated into gels or solid matrices to achieve depot behavior. For example, blending BTH into a poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) solution and injecting it intratumorally produced an in situ–forming implant (ISFI) that enabled sustained enzyme release for up to 21 days. This prolonged release achieved near-complete depletion of tumor-associated HA (approximately 98%), durably remodeling the tumor extracellular matrix and improving regional transport toward TDLNs. As the PLGA matrix degraded, acidic byproducts further lowered peri-implant pH, potentially augmenting HA cleavage alongside enzymatic activity [ ].

5.3.2.

## Particulate carriers
Micro- and nanoparticles can stabilize, protect, and control the release of the enzyme. Recent studies illustrate three representative engineering strategies. In the most established approach, hyaluronidase is covalently conjugated to the nanoparticle surface via chemical linkers, enabling immediate extracellular HA degradation upon tumor exposure. For example, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) functionalized with amine groups were chemically crosslinked to hyaluronidase using carbodiimide coupling chemistry, while carboplatin was loaded into the nanopores. This configuration retained >80% enzymatic activity relative to free enzyme and achieved tumor growth inhibition exceeding 70%, significantly outperforming free hyaluronidase plus drug (approximately 45%) [ ].
Polymer-based molecular envelope technology (MET) nanoparticles loaded with the poorly soluble multikinase inhibitor CUDC-101 and surface-coated with hyaluronidase achieved high drug loading and colloidal stability [ ]. Moreover, conjugating rHuPH20 to nanoparticle surfaces and capping them with a thin PEG layer preserved enzymatic activity while minimizing rapid clearance, increasing tumor accumulation up to fourfold [ ]. Similarly, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) bearing surface-bound hyaluronidase and internally loaded small molecules such as quercetin degraded HA barriers while releasing payloads, effectively suppressing tumor growth in pancreatic models [ ].
Beyond simple surface conjugation, more sophisticated designs incorporate tumor-targeting peptides to achieve spatially refined ECM remodeling. In a representative example, mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with DOX were surface-functionalized with a conjugate comprising a collagen IV-binding peptide (C4BP) linked to PEGylated bromelain, an ECM-degrading protease [ ]. The targeting peptide enabled selective localization to collagen IV–rich triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) matrices, and bromelain-mediated ECM remodeling enhanced penetration, resulting in improved tumor accumulation and DOX delivery without promoting metastasis.
An alternative strategy employs electrostatic immobilization of hyaluronidase onto drug-loaded nanocarriers. In a representative multistage design, hyaluronidase was assembled onto drug-loaded nanocarriers via layer-by-layer (LbL) electrostatic interactions, enabling extracellular HA degradation while maintaining controlled drug-release behavior. This configuration significantly enhanced tumor penetration and therapeutic efficacy compared with non-enzyme-coated formulations [ ]. Similarly, hyaluronidase adsorbed onto layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanodisks and co-loaded with DOX retained over 80% enzymatic activity and effectively degraded intratumoral HA, thereby enhancing cellular uptake, deep tumor spheroid penetration, and in vivo tumor growth inhibition compared with enzyme-free counterparts. The LDH carrier also exhibited accelerated drug release under mildly acidic tumor conditions [ ].

5.3.3.

## Stimuli-responsive activation
Two complementary axes are used: exploiting endogenous TME cues (e.g., pH, matrix metalloproteinases [MMPs], reactive oxygen species [ROS], hypoxia) to confine hyaluronidase activation and release to tumors, and employing exogenous triggers (e.g., light, heat, ultrasound, magnetic fields) to switch the enzyme on with spatiotemporal control.
In endogenous-triggered designs, aberrant TME cues are harnessed to restore or release hyaluronidase selectively within tumors, thereby increasing precision and minimizing systemic exposure. The TME exhibits a distinct biochemical and biophysical milieu that diverges markedly from normal tissues. The tumor extracellular pH is acidic, typically ranging from pH 6.5 to 6.9, owing to high glycolytic flux and poor perfusion, which in turn shapes invasion and therapeutic response [ , ]. The TME also exhibits elevated MMP activity and overexpression, driving ECM remodeling, invasion, and disease progression [ ]. In parallel, ROS are increased in tumors, modulating stromal and immune components and contributing to immune evasion and therapy resistance [ , ].
Leveraging these differences, Wang et al. chemically conjugated hyaluronidase to a pH-sensitive dextran via a traceless cleavable linker, thereby restricting enzymatic activity at neutral pH while enabling selective HA cleavage under tumor acidity. As an immunotherapy adjuvant, this platform relieved hypoxia and significantly enhanced outcomes with PDT plus anti-PD-L1, including abscopal and antimetastatic effects [ ]. In contrast to linker-cleavage strategies, other systems employ conformationally pH-switchable hyaluronidase conjugated to polymeric micelles, thereby enabling TME-specific enzymatic activation. Such pH-dependent regulation permits selective HA degradation under tumor-acidic conditions while minimizing enzymatic activity at physiological pH, leading to enhanced intratumoral penetration and tumor growth inhibition compared with free drug treatment [ ]. In addition, hybrid smart systems have been developed in which DOX and hyaluronidase are co-loaded within mesoporous silica nanopores and cloaked with a pH-responsive cancer cell membrane shell. This biomimetic coating preserves tumor-specific membrane proteins, enabling homologous targeting and reduced macrophage uptake, while remaining stable at physiological pH and destabilizing under acidic tumor conditions to facilitate co-release of both components. The combined ECM degradation and chemotherapy promote enhanced tumor accumulation, deeper intratumoral penetration, and improved antitumor efficacy, particularly in stroma-rich pancreatic tumor models [ ].
MMP-responsive approaches activate or liberate hyaluronidase upon MMP-2 cleavage. Xu et al. engineered an MMP-2-responsive hyaluronidase on the surface of nanovesicles carrying the sonodynamic sensitizer purpurin 18, achieving selective activation in the TME, enhanced intratumoral delivery, and hypoxia relief that potentiated sonodynamic therapy (SDT) [ ]. An MMP-2-cleavable peptide plus hyaluronidase on PDT liposomes enabled laser-triggered co-release of photosensitizer and enzyme, improving penetration and therapeutic effect in a breast cancer model; residual hyaluronidase was subsequently inactivated by photothermal heating to mitigate off-target effects [ ]. Glycogen-based nanoparticles were also constructed with an MMP-2-cleavable peptide linking PEG to the particle surface, where hyaluronidase was electrostatically retained within the glycogen matrix. Upon MMP-2-mediated cleavage of the peptide, PEG shielding was removed, reducing steric hindrance and facilitating the release of hyaluronidase, which resulted in selective enzyme activation and enhanced DOX delivery in the TME, ultimately yielding superior in vivo tumor control [ ].
ROS-responsive strategies have been developed to anchor hyaluronidase-loaded nanogels onto CAR-T cell surfaces in a microenvironment-adaptive manner. The nanogels remain stable during circulation but undergo ROS-mediated cleavage within tumors, enabling localized enzyme release. This redox-triggered activation promotes selective degradation of hyaluronic acid-rich ECM, facilitating deeper CAR-T infiltration while limiting off-target matrix remodeling [ ].
Additionally, within the endogenous TME framework, several platforms exploit tumor-associated or intracellular hyaluronidase as a biochemical trigger to initiate drug release or functional switching. In these “reverse-trigger” systems, HA serves as an enzyme-cleavable gatekeeper, enabling hyaluronidase-mediated activation selectively within tumors. Hyaluronidase-responsive nanoparticles with HA shells have demonstrated tumor-selective chemotherapy through localized enzymatic degradation and enhanced intratumoral accumulation [ ]. Similarly, HA-masked nanocarriers have been engineered to conceal nuclear localization or cell-penetrating domains that are exposed only upon hyaluronidase-mediated cleavage, thereby promoting intracellular trafficking and amplifying cytotoxic efficacy in tumor cells [ ].
In exogenous-triggered or externally programmable systems, controllable inputs—such as photothermal irradiation or small-molecule chelators—are used to initiate enzymatic release or amplify therapeutic responses, promoting deeper penetration and therapeutic synergy. Liu et al. used mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (mPDA), which intrinsically function as NIR-absorbing photothermal agents, to generate localized heating that induces microenvironmental changes and thereby enhances hyaluronidase-mediated HA degradation. Under photothermal stimulation, the boosted enzymatic activity selectively degraded HA, reduced HIF-1α expression, alleviated immunosuppression, and synergistically suppressed tumor growth [ ].
In another study, hyaluronidase was integrated into a metal–polyphenol network formed by coordination between Fe3+ and pentagalloylglucose, stabilized with poly(vinylpyrrolidone), creating a structurally simple and stable nanoplatform in which Fe3+ exerted a reversible inhibitory effect on the enzyme. Upon administration of the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO), Fe3+ was sequestered, triggering network disassembly and intratumoral re-activation and release of hyaluronidase, which led to effective HA degradation, improved tumor perfusion, and enhanced immune cell infiltration. The resulting ECM remodeling facilitated deeper penetration of near-infrared photosensitizers, thereby amplifying PTT/PDT-induced immunogenic tumor cell death and overall antitumor efficacy [ ].

5.3.4.

## Targeting systems
Targeting spans from classical molecular strategies—ligands and antibodies that enrich the enzyme at diseased sites—to biologic carriers that actively home to tumors (e.g., immune cells, bacteria, viruses) and release hyaluronidase in situ. As an example of ligand-guided delivery, folate was displayed on PH20-functionalized exosomes via DSPE–PEG anchors to exploit folate-receptor overexpression in tumors. Following intravenous administration, the platform accumulated in tumor tissues, where surface-presented PH20 degraded hyaluronan, reduced matrix density, and enhanced intratumoral drug penetration. Notably, HA fragmentation also promoted M1 macrophage polarization, thereby coupling ECM remodeling with immune activation to achieve synergistic antitumor effects [ ].
For living carriers, hyaluronidase can be expressed or conjugated on CAR-T cells or macrophages to couple active homing with local ECM degradation. A GPC3-targeted CAR-T co-expressing IL-7 and PH20 improved intratumoral infiltration and expansion, accelerated tumor control, and increased resistance to relapse in hepatocellular carcinoma models [ ]. Mesothelin-directed CAR-T cells co-expressing secreted PH20 to remodel peritumoral HA enhanced tumor infiltration and antitumor efficacy in gastric cancer models [ ]. Orthogonal, ROS-responsive conjugation of hyaluronidase to CAR-T cells, combined with a PD-L1-targeting antibody, guided the construct to PD-L1+ tumor cells and enabled tumor-triggered HA degradation, thereby increasing both drug and immune-cell penetration [ ].
Engineered microbes and oncolytic viruses provide additional vectors. Attenuated, tumor-tropic Salmonella typhimurium expressing bacterial hyaluronidase selectively depleted HA in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, with arabinose-inducible control restricting activity to the tumor site [ ]. Genetically programmed, tumor-colonizing E. coli co-expressing collagenase and hyaluronidase markedly reduced stromal collagen and HA in pancreatic tumors and significantly potentiated gemcitabine efficacy [ ].
In contrast to most hyaluronidase-enabled approaches, silencing endogenous hyaluronidase can prolong the intratumoral residence of therapeutic depots. Yang et al. developed a nanosponge co-delivering DOX and siRNA against Hyal-1. The lipoprotein-mimetic construct, decorated with an apoA-I-mimicking peptide, undergoes tumor-specific swelling driven by intra- and intercellular pH shifts, and achieved efficient tumor suppression by curbing local hyaluronidase activity while sustaining depot retention [ ].

6.

## Conclusion
Hyaluronidase has evolved from a passive spreading factor into a programmable modulator of the ECM, reshaping strategies for therapeutic delivery. In SC administration, transient ECM remodeling enables the reliable delivery of high-concentration and large-volume biologics while maintaining pharmacokinetic exposure and substantially enhancing patient convenience. Accumulating clinical experience with rHuPH20-based co-formulations has demonstrated that immunogenicity can be effectively managed through rational protein–enzyme pairing, formulation stabilization, and device–human-factor optimization.
Despite these advances, key translational challenges remain. Ensuring dual stability of both biologic and enzyme, maintaining injectability at scale, validating real-world self-administration performance, and linking local tissue transport to systemic pharmacokinetics through model-informed approaches require further investigation. Establishing evidence-based dosing frameworks and predicting long-term immunogenicity remain critical priorities. Emerging machine learning tools offer new opportunities, including human leukocyte antigen class I-based epitope prediction [ ] and sequence-driven modeling of SC absorption kinetics [ ]. Integration of such predictive frameworks may enable proactive de-immunization and concurrent optimization of quality attributes during development. In parallel, coordinated chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC)–device co-development strategies will be essential to preserve product comparability across lifecycle modifications.
In oncology, hyaluronidase transforms the tumor ECM from a static barrier into a controllable therapeutic interface. Selective loosening of hyaluronic acid-rich stroma enhances penetration of cytotoxic drugs, antibodies, immune therapeutics, oncolytic vectors, and nanocarriers while avoiding indiscriminate matrix depletion. Future directions emphasize precision: logic-gated formulations and tumor-specific pro-enzyme designs may enable conditional activation within defined tumor microenvironments [ , ]. At the systems level, implantable microdevices and enzyme-guided chemobots suggest the possibility of dynamic, feedback-controlled ECM modulation [ ], although quantitative definitions of activity thresholds and therapeutic windows are still lacking.
Defining the therapeutic window—dose intensity, frequency, and route—will be central to balancing stromal remodeling with safety. Imaging biomarkers, including magnetic resonance imaging-derived predictors of PEGPH20 responsiveness and enzyme-sensitive probes for real-time activity monitoring, provide tools for stratification and longitudinal assessment [ , ]. Ultimately, integration of quantitative ECM characterization, biomarker-guided imaging, and artificial intelligence-driven analytics may enable algorithm-based selection of enzyme type, dose, and delivery route tailored to TME heterogeneity.
The future of hyaluronidase will depend on aligning molecular engineering, device innovation, predictive modeling, and regulatory strategy within a coherent translational framework. Through quantitative control and biomarker-informed personalization, hyaluronidase may evolve into a versatile platform for controlled delivery across SC biologics and precision tumor-directed therapies.
