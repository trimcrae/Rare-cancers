## Abstract
Recent advances in musculoskeletal (MSK) radiology have markedly improved diagnostic accuracy through innovations in MRI, CT, and artificial intelligence (AI). This review summarizes 7 key domains shaping current MSK imaging: (1) CT-like contrast MRI techniques for bone visualization, (2) quantitative MRI approaches, (3) AI applications in image reconstruction and diagnostic support, (4) MR spectroscopy (MRS) for metabolic assessment, (5) whole-body MRI for systemic disease evaluation, (6) positron emission tomography (PET) for metabolic and inflammatory imaging, and (7) advanced CT techniques such as weight-bearing CT. Zero echo time and ultrashort echo time MRI sequences enable the visualization and quantitative assessment of short-T2 tissues such as cortical bone, tendons, and fibrocartilage. Deep learning–based image reconstruction improves SNR and shortens scan time, enhancing image quality and diagnostic confidence. In parallel, AI-driven diagnostic support systems, including convolutional neural networks for lesion detection and natural language processing for report generation, are transforming workflow efficiency and consistency. MRS offers metabolic insights into muscle disorders such as sarcopenia, and whole-body-MRI provides comprehensive, radiation-free evaluation of tumor burden and inflammatory joint or enthesis involvement, making it valuable in oncology and rheumatic diseases. PET complements MRI by identifying metabolically active or inflammatory lesions. CT-based innovations further contribute to evaluating joint biomechanics with high spatial resolution. Together, these technological developments are enabling earlier disease detection, more precise diagnosis, and improved treatment monitoring, representing a paradigm shift in MSK imaging and clinical practice.
Keywords: computed tomography–like contrast magnetic resonance imaging, deep learning reconstruction, musculoskeletal imaging, quantitative magnetic resonance imaging, whole-body magnetic resonance imaging

## Introduction
Musculoskeletal (MSK) radiology plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up of disorders affecting bones, joints, and soft tissues. With growing clinical demand for detailed structural and functional evaluation, imaging technologies in this field have undergone remarkable advancements in recent years. Conventional radiography and CT remain useful for assessing osseous structures but are limited in soft-tissue and cartilage evaluation, whereas MRI provides superior contrast and multiplanar capability, making it indispensable in contemporary MSK imaging.
Recent innovations have further expanded the scope of MSK radiology. Novel MRI sequences such as zero echo time (ZTE) and ultrashort echo time (UTE) imaging allow visualization of short-T2 tissues—such as tendons, ligaments, and cortical bone—that were previously difficult to depict. The introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) has also transformed MSK imaging, with deep learning reconstruction (DLR) improving image quality, reducing artifacts, and shortening acquisition times across modalities; a recent study has shown that DLR improves MR myelography in patients with spinal canal stenosis. Additionally, AI-based diagnostic support has shown promise in improving lesion detection and diagnostic consistency. – 
Beyond conventional MRI, several complementary techniques have emerged. MR spectroscopy (MRS) enables noninvasive metabolic evaluation in MSK tissues. Proton MRS is useful for assessing tumor malignancy through choline quantification, while phosphorus-31 MRS provides insights into muscle bioenergetics and is valuable in the evaluation of sarcopenia. Whole-body MRI (WB-MRI), particularly with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), provides comprehensive, radiation-free evaluation of systemic diseases such as bone metastases, multiple myeloma, and inflammatory disorders. Positron emission tomography (PET) adds molecular insight into bone and muscle metabolism, inflammation, and tumor activity. Moreover, CT-based innovations further contribute to evaluating joint biomechanics, and among these, advances in weight-bearing CT (WBCT) have refined this assessment by enabling 3D imaging under physiological loading. 
This review provides a comprehensive overview of recent advances in MSK radiology, focusing on 7 major domains: (1) CT-like contrast MRI techniques for bone visualization; (2) quantitative MRI approaches for short-T2 tissues; (3) AI in image reconstruction and diagnostic support; (4) MRS for metabolic assessment; (5) WB-MRI for systemic disease evaluation; (6) PET for metabolic and inflammatory imaging; and (7) emerging CT technologies. By adopting a multidisciplinary perspective, this review highlights how advanced imaging techniques are reshaping diagnostic precision, treatment planning, and personalized care in MSK medicine.

## The Development of CT-like Contrast MRI Techniques
Recent advancements in MRI technology have enabled the generation of images with a CT-like contrast, particularly for bone visualization, using specialized sequences. Among these, ZTE and FRACTURE (fast field echo resembling a CT using restricted echo-spacing) are representative techniques that provide imaging of bone and calcifications without radiation exposure, offering a valuable alternative to conventional CT in MSK assessments.

## ZTE MRI
ZTE MRI is a state-of-the-art imaging technique developed to visualize tissues with extremely short T2 relaxation times, such as cortical bone and calcifications—structures typically invisible on conventional MRI. , By initiating data acquisition immediately after RF excitation with an echo time approaching zero, ZTE allows CT-like contrast bone imaging. Grayscale inversion of ZTE images enhances the depiction of osseous structures, enabling radiograph- or CT-equivalent visualization within an MRI examination. Clinically, ZTE has demonstrated utility in diverse MSK applications, including the evaluation of bone erosions in rheumatoid arthritis ( ), heterotopic ossifications ( ), and abnormally mineralized bone in Paget’s disease ( ).

 

## FRACTURE
Another MRI-based technique, FRACTURE, employs a 3D multi-echo fast field echo sequence using multiple in-phase echoes (first TE = 2.3 ms, echo spacing = 2.3 ms). CT-like contrast bone images are generated through subtraction of the final echo from a composite of earlier echoes, followed by grayscale inversion. FRACTURE’s advantage lies in its ability to produce CT-like contrast images using conventional hardware, without the need for specialized gradient coils or RF systems. , 

## ZTE MRI vs. FRACTURE imaging
While a direct comparison between ZTE and FRACTURE has not been conducted, several distinct advantages of ZTE emerge, supporting its potential as a more robust and clinically versatile modality for bone imaging. One notable benefit of ZTE is its significant reduction in acoustic noise. Conventional MRI sequences typically generate absolute noise levels in the range of 90–110 dB, whereas ZTE has been reported to produce noise levels only slightly above ambient background noise (approximately 69 dB), with a difference of about 1 dB compared to the surrounding environment. This near-silent acquisition is particularly advantageous in pediatric and fetal imaging, where minimizing patient stress is crucial.
Another strength of ZTE is its lower susceptibility to high-signal artifacts that may mimic bone. FRACTURE imaging can sometimes be confounded by high signals from non-osseous structures such as cerebrospinal fluid, air, dura, hemorrhage, cyst walls, or vascular malformations, potentially leading to diagnostic errors. While ZTE also requires careful interpretation—particularly in distinguishing gas from calcifications—it is generally less affected by such artifacts. Moreover, ZTE has been shown to reduce metal-induced artifacts, facilitating clearer joint imaging in postoperative patients. In terms of technical performance, ZTE offers a higher SNR and improved acquisition efficiency compared to FRACTURE imaging. Clinically, ZTE has demonstrated broad utility across a wide spectrum of MSK imaging applications, including the evaluation of arthritis, degenerative changes, trauma, bone tumors, and pediatric conditions.

## Clinical positioning of CT-like contrast MRI: a technological leap forward
Recent advances in CT-like contrast MR imaging have brought its quality close to that of conventional CT, enabling near-CT visualization of cortical bone and calcifications without radiation exposure. Importantly, CT-like contrast MRI can integrate quantitative mapping and microstructural techniques such as DWI, providing tissue-level insights beyond what CT can offer. These capabilities make it a powerful tool for comprehensive MSK assessment, especially for pediatric patients, longitudinal monitoring, and radiation-sensitive populations. Rather than serving merely as an alternative to CT, CT-like contrast MRI expands the diagnostic spectrum by combining detailed bone visualization with superior soft-tissue contrast and microstructural evaluation, supporting more integrated and patient-centered care. Limitations remain, including potential gas-related high-signal artifacts and longer acquisition times, but the technique represents a major step forward in MSK imaging. provides a structured overview of CT-like contrast MRI techniques discussed in this section.
 

## Emerging Quantitative MRI Techniques in MSK Imaging
Recent advancements in MRI technology have enabled the implementation of quantitative imaging techniques that provide objective biomarkers for tissue composition and structural integrity. These methods allow for a more sensitive and reproducible evaluation of MSK tissues, complementing conventional morphological assessments. In particular, techniques such as T2 mapping, 3D T1rho mapping, and UTE–T2* mapping have shown promise in visualizing tissues with short T2 or T2* relaxation times—such as fibrocartilage, tendons, and cortical bone—that were previously challenging to assess. This section introduces key quantitative MRI approaches and their emerging applications in MSK imaging.

## T2 mapping
T2 mapping is a versatile non-invasive MRI technique that has gained widespread clinical adoption across multiple anatomical regions. In MSK imaging, it has proven particularly valuable for evaluating the biochemical composition of articular cartilage and menisci, especially in the knee joint. , This technique plays a vital role in understanding the pathophysiology of osteoarthritis (OA), assessing the efficacy of emerging therapeutic agents, and monitoring postoperative outcomes following cartilage repair procedures. When incorporated into conventional knee MRI protocols, T2 mapping significantly enhances the sensitivity for detecting early cartilage degeneration ( ). This technique quantitatively measures transverse relaxation time (T2), which reflects various tissue characteristics such as water content, the state of bound water, and the organization of collagen fibers. The imaging is typically performed using a multi-section, multi-echo spin echo sequence, although this method is associated with relatively long acquisition times, usually ranging from 5 to 10 mins. To overcome this limitation, accelerated sequences such as GRAPPATINI (a combination of generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisition [GRAPPA] and model-based accelerated relaxometry by iterative non-linear inversion [MARTINI]) have been developed, reducing scan time to about 6 mins while enabling the generation of synthetic morphological images. A study has reported the application of this technique to T2 mapping of peripheral nerves and muscles. Because different joint tissues exhibit unique T2 values, this method offers the potential to optimize echo times for imaging specific anatomical structures, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and tissue characterization.
 

## 3D T1rho mapping
3D T1rho mapping is an advanced MRI technique developed to evaluate the biochemical properties of soft tissues in the knee, particularly articular cartilage. – It is sensitive to biochemical changes and has been shown to correlate with proteoglycan content, making it a promising biomarker for early OA. Beyond cartilage, 3D T1rho is also useful for assessing MSK tissues with short T2 properties, such as tendons and ligaments, providing insights into their biochemical and biomechanical integrity. The technique measures spin-lattice relaxation in the rotating frame by applying a spin-lock RF pulse in the transverse plane. 3D T1rho imaging typically begins with a 3D T1rho preparation module followed by various readout sequences, including 3D magnetization-prepared angle-modulated partitioned k-space spoiled gradient echo snapshots (MAPSS), 3D fast advanced spin echo (FASE), and 3D UTE, each offering unique advantages and trade-offs. Notably, by combining T1rho with UTE acquisition, 3D UTE T1rho imaging enables signal detection from tissues that are difficult to visualize with conventional methods, such as cortical bone, osteochondral junctions, tendons, and ligaments. In healthy knees, menisci tend to exhibit lower T1rho values than cartilage. These sequences are optimized for clinical feasibility, generally requiring less than 5 mins of scan time. Overall, 3D T1rho mapping complements T2-based techniques by offering unique sensitivity to proteoglycan depletion and matrix degeneration, particularly in short-T2 tissues.

## UTE–T2* mapping
UTE–T2* mapping is a specialized MRI technique designed to assess the integrity and degeneration of collagen-rich tissues that have extremely short T2 relaxation times—such as fibrocartilage, menisci, tendons, and ligaments—which are not well visualized with conventional MRI sequences. – This method captures signals from tissues with rapid signal decay by employing ultrashort echo times, typically ranging from 0.032 to 0.096 ms, enabling quantification of transverse relaxation time (T2*) in these structures. , Data are typically acquired using advanced pulse sequences like 3D cone UTE, which allows for multiple acquisitions at very short echo times to generate accurate T2* maps. UTE–T2* mapping is particularly effective in detecting subtle changes in deep-layer fibrocartilage, such as the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFC) of the wrist, as well as in evaluating the biomechanical environment of joints. For instance, a study has demonstrated that wrist pronation, which induces acute loading and tensile strain, leads to decreased T2* values in the TFC, reflecting the functional response of fibrocartilage to mechanical stress ( ). Unlike conventional T2 mapping, which is more influenced by water content, UTE–T2* values are believed to primarily reflect the microstructural integrity of collagen networks. This makes UTE–T2* mapping a valuable tool for assessing load-bearing tissues and their biomechanical properties in MSK imaging.
 These quantitative MRI techniques provide complementary strengths for assessing diverse MSK tissues and disease processes. While T2 mapping is well established for detecting early biochemical changes in articular cartilage, newer methods such as 3D T1rho and UTE–T2* mapping offer enhanced sensitivity and tissue specificity. 3D T1rho is particularly effective for evaluating proteoglycan loss and matrix degeneration, while UTE–T2* enables visualization of short-T2 tissues such as fibrocartilage, ligaments, and tendons—structures that conventional techniques often fail to capture. Together, these modalities expand the scope of non-invasive, quantitative assessment of tissue integrity in MSK imaging. Key features of each technique are summarized in .
 

## AI in MSK Imaging
AI, particularly deep learning techniques, has rapidly expanded its presence in MSK imaging. These advancements span 2 key domains: image quality enhancement and diagnostic support. In this section, we highlight representative applications of AI in MRI, including DLR and diagnostic support using classification models and natural language processing (NLP). 

## DLR for image quality enhancement
DLR techniques have revolutionized MRI by enabling shorter scan times and improved image quality. – These AI-driven advancements significantly reduce image noise and enhance anatomical clarity, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, by minimizing the need for repeat scans, DLR contributes to more efficient workflows and offers tangible benefits for patient care.
Their clinical utility has been reported not only in MSK imaging but also across a wide range of diagnostic applications throughout the body. , – In MSK imaging, DLR has shown promise in enhancing image quality ( . A recent study comparing 1.5T MRI with DLR to conventional 3T MRI found that 1.5T/DLR images had lower noise, better visualization of structures such as the meniscus and ligaments, and greater overall diagnostic acceptability. These results suggest that DLR can significantly improve 1.5T MRI performance, potentially offsetting some limitations of 3T systems, including higher cost and patient-related constraints, such as contraindications in patients with pacemakers approved only for 1.5T.
 These advancements highlight the potential of DLR to improve diagnostic confidence in challenging postoperative MSK imaging scenarios. DLR has also demonstrated substantial value in improving the visualization of soft tissue structures. In finger MRI, image quality is often degraded by susceptibility artifacts caused by surrounding air. To address this, radial sampling techniques such as PROPELLER (periodically rotated overlapping parallel lines with enhanced reconstruction; GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) have been utilized to reduce susceptibility-induced distortions. Equivalent techniques include BLADE (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) and MultiVane XD (Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, the Netherlands), which employ similar rotating k-space acquisition strategies to minimize motion and susceptibility artifacts. Given the need for high-resolution imaging of the fingers within a practical scan time, DLR plays a key role by enabling accelerated acquisition without compromising image quality. When combined with radial sampling methods like PROPELLER, DLR further enhances image quality and diagnostic confidence by reducing noise and improving anatomical delineation, thereby increasing the clinical utility of small joint MRI. Furthermore, even small-volume abscesses within the intervertebral disc can be clearly visualized using PROPELLER-DWI with DLR ( ). The impact of DLR is particularly notable in sequences with inherently low SNR, such as short tau inversion recovery and T1-weighted fast spin echo, where AI-based denoising improves visualization of soft tissue contrast and implant-related pathology. These advances contribute to more efficient postoperative surveillance and reduce the need for repeat scans.
 

## AI-based diagnostic support
AI-based diagnostic support tools, particularly those using convolutional neural networks and large language models (LLMs), have been shown to enhance radiologists’ diagnostic performance. – In MSK imaging, recent AI studies have reported how AI and its subsets, machine learning and deep learning, are being applied in medical imaging, particularly in fracture detection. These technologies have shown the potential to shorten diagnostic time and improve the performance of less experienced physicians. Recent studies have explored the effectiveness of transfer learning approaches for the diagnosis of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries. – Researchers compared the diagnostic performance of AI with that of radiologists using a newly modified MRI sequence (dual precision positioning of thin-slice oblique sagittal fat suppression proton density-weighted imaging, DPP-TSO-Sag-FS-PDWI). The results demonstrated that AI outperformed radiologists, particularly in detecting grade 1 and grade 2 ACL injuries. These findings highlight the potential of AI to assist radiologists in the stratified diagnosis of ACL tears. Despite the promise of AI in diagnostic imaging, several limitations hinder its clinical application. Deep learning models often require vast amounts of annotated medical imaging data for training from scratch, posing significant challenges in data acquisition and labeling. Moreover, diagnostic inaccuracies may arise due to model misinterpretation of specific pathological features. For instance, full-image input models may misclassify thickened synovium or increased joint effusion as tissue disruption. A reported case involved a patient with an intact ACL who was misdiagnosed with a grade III tear due to periligamentous fluid mimicking torn ligament ends. These issues underscore the need for careful model validation, high-quality training data, and clinical oversight when integrating AI into diagnostic workflows.
In parallel, LLMs have rapidly advanced in the field of diagnostic imaging and are beginning to play a significant role. , In the MSK domain, one study developed a deep learning-based NLP model capable of automatically identifying patients with bone metastases from Japanese free-text radiology reports following report completion by radiologists. Using a dataset of 1749 Japanese-language reports, the model achieved excellent performance, with a mean precision of 0.912 and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.968. This system enables automated alerts to attending physicians and other healthcare staff, facilitating closer follow-up and timely intervention for affected patients, and ultimately contributing to the prevention of skeletal-related events. The integration of LLMs in radiology holds promise for streamlining report interpretation, identifying high-risk patients, and prompting timely intervention. These emerging applications of AI not only improve workflow efficiency but also enhance diagnostic consistency, particularly in high-volume or resource-limited settings. summarizes the main techniques discussed in this section.
 

## Advances in MRS for MSK Imaging
MRS is a non-invasive imaging technique that allows for in vivo quantification of metabolites, complementing conventional MRI by providing biochemical information about tissues. – Unlike standard anatomical imaging, MRS can reveal metabolic alterations that precede visible structural changes.
In MSK imaging, proton MRS (1H-MRS) complements conventional MRI by providing metabolic information for lesion characterization. The detection of a choline peak at 3.2 ppm is a key marker of malignancy and has been reported in osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and high-grade soft-tissue sarcomas, whereas many benign lesions lack a significant choline signal – ( ). Changes in choline concentration after chemotherapy may reflect treatment response, and postoperative MRS can help distinguish recurrence from scar tissue when choline levels are negligible. Additional applications include quantifying intramuscular fat, evaluating muscle metabolism in myopathy or radiation injury, and assessing bone marrow composition. , Overall, 1H-MRS expands MSK imaging from purely structural assessment to metabolic evaluation, aiding diagnosis and treatment monitoring.
 Recently, phosphorus-31 MRS (31P-MRS) has emerged as a valuable tool for assessing muscle bioenergetics by quantifying metabolites such as phosphocreatine (PCr), inorganic phosphate, and adenosine triphosphate. – 
31P-MRS requires dedicated hardware and pulse sequences, including a 31P-specific or dual-tuned (1H/31P) coil and spectroscopy sequences optimized for phosphorus signal acquisition. Higher magnetic field strengths are preferred to achieve sufficient SNR and reliable metabolite quantification. In MSK imaging, it provides insights into mitochondrial function and energy metabolism, extending diagnostics beyond morphology. Sarcopenia in patients with advanced cancer has been reported to be associated with poor prognosis, and its clinical significance is increasingly being recognized. A study in sarcopenia has shown increased energy cost during contraction, impaired mitochondrial function, and correlations between 31P-MRS–derived indices and biopsy-based enzyme activity. Elderly patients with sarcopenia also demonstrate reduced PCr and elevated phosphodiester 2 levels, both linked to loss of muscle mass and function.

## WB-MRI in MSK Imaging
The term whole-body MRI (WB-MRI) can refer either to general whole-body imaging protocols or to diffusion-weighted imaging with background body signal suppression (DWIBS), depending on the clinical and technical context. In this review, WB-MRI is used in a broad sense to encompass both approaches, focusing on their applications in oncologic and systemic MSK disorders. WB-MRI has emerged as a valuable tool in MSK imaging, providing comprehensive assessment of both skeletal and soft tissue diseases without exposure to ionizing radiation. Originally developed and applied primarily in oncology, WB-MRI is increasingly recognized for its role in the diagnosis, staging, and monitoring of a wide range of MSK disorders. 
In oncology, WB-MRI, particularly when combined with DWI, enables sensitive and specific detection of bone metastases, multiple myeloma, and lymphoma. – Meta-analyses have demonstrated the high diagnostic performance of WB-MRI across various malignancies. For bone metastases, WB-MRI achieved sensitivities and specificities of 95% and 92% per patient, and 92% and 95% per lesion. In multiple myeloma, pooled estimates showed a sensitivity of 93% (95% CI, 75%–98%), specificity of 57% (95% CI, 21%–87%), and area under the curve of 0.92. For Hodgkin lymphoma, WB-MRI demonstrated excellent concordance with fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT, with pooled sensitivities/specificities of 94%/99% for nodal and 90%/100% for extra-nodal staging. Its ability to provide both anatomical detail and whole-body disease assessment in a single examination has made WB-MRI an attractive alternative to traditional imaging modalities such as bone scintigraphy and PET/CT. WB-MRI has been endorsed by international guidelines as the imaging reference standard for assessing bone marrow involvement in multiple myeloma, including smoldering and asymptomatic cases. Importantly, WB-MRI has demonstrated superiority over limited spine and pelvis MRI in detecting skeletal lesions involving the extremities, which may significantly impact therapeutic decisions ( . A study evaluated the usefulness of DWIBS for monitoring treatment response and predicting prognosis in patients with bone metastases from castration-resistant prostate cancer receiving Ra-223 therapy. Changes in total diffusion volume on DWIBS were found to correlate with survival, suggesting that DWIBS could provide an objective marker of treatment response. WB-MRI may also be valuable for follow-up of tumors with low FDG avidity and minimal CT enhancement, such as myxoid soft-tissue tumors, where MRI can provide incremental diagnostic and monitoring benefits. Recent technical advances, including faster acquisition times, higher spatial resolution, and improved patient comfort, have further enhanced the clinical utility of WB-MRI. The integration of tissue characterization imaging with DWI, quantitative biomarkers such as apparent diffusion coefficient values, and the potential for combining WB-MRI with PET in hybrid systems are expected to broaden its applications, particularly in oncology. In this context, a study comparing PET/MRI and PET/CT for whole-body cancer staging showed that PET/MRI not only improves lesion detection in certain cancers but also significantly reduces radiation exposure, making it especially beneficial for younger patients. These findings suggest that PET/MRI may become a valuable complement to PET/CT in the future.
 In the context of rheumatic and related diseases, WB-MRI generally refers to comprehensive WB imaging protocols designed to evaluate inflammatory and structural changes across multiple joints, tendons, and entheses in a single examination. In this field, WB-MRI offers a global evaluation of inflammatory joint diseases such as spondyloarthritis and psoriatic arthritis. , It can detect both axial and peripheral joint involvement, allowing for the early identification of active inflammation (e.g., bone marrow edema) and chronic structural changes (e.g., erosions and ankylosis). This comprehensive approach facilitates early diagnosis, informs treatment initiation, and enables objective monitoring of treatment response. WB-MRI is also useful in assessing enthesitis, synovitis, and soft tissue inflammation, providing valuable insights into disease extent that may be missed on conventional localized MRI. Beyond these well-established applications, WB-MRI is gaining ground in the evaluation of multifocal skeletal and neuromuscular disorders, such as chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis, SAPHO syndrome, and muscular dystrophies. – In these conditions, WB-MRI assists in disease extent assessment, biopsy site selection, and therapeutic response monitoring. Standardized scoring systems for WB-MRI in inflammatory diseases are still being developed (e.g., JAMRIS-TMJ), , and further validation is needed for widespread clinical implementation. While dynamic contrast enhanced–MRI has been used to assess synovial inflammation quantitatively, recent advances in deep learning have enabled fully automatic quantification of hand synovitis, offering a faster and more standardized approach that may complement whole-body inflammatory assessment.
Overall, WB-MRI provides a non-invasive, radiation-free, and comprehensive assessment of the MSK system, with proven benefits in cancer staging, inflammatory arthritis, and multifocal diseases. As technology continues to evolve, WB-MRI is likely to play an increasingly central role in personalized patient management in MSK imaging. summarizes the main techniques discussed in this section.
 

## Novel PET Tracers in MSK Diseases
Recent reports highlight the potential of novel PET tracers in MSK and related disease imaging. [68Ga] fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI) PET/CT has shown superior detection of joint involvement in rheumatoid arthritis compared with [18F]FDG PET/CT, with uptake correlating closely with clinical and laboratory markers of disease activity. [18F]FAPI-74 PET has also been reported to identify activated fibroblasts in muscle sarcoidosis, detecting chronic fibrotic changes even in asymptomatic cases overlooked by conventional [18F]FDG PET ( . 
18F-prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) PET/CT is increasingly used in the initial staging of prostate cancer due to its superior accuracy in detecting lymph node metastases, – although robust evidence regarding its role in bone metastasis assessment has been limited. In contrast, [18F]sodium fluoride (NaF) PET/CT has long been recognized as highly sensitive for detecting osseous involvement, as it reflects osteoblastic remodeling secondary to metastatic disease, whereas [18F]PSMA PET/CT directly targets prostate cancer cells. A prospective oncology trial demonstrated that [18F]PSMA PET/CT and [18F]NaF PET/CT provided comparable diagnostic accuracy for bone metastases in high-risk prostate cancer; importantly, [18F]NaF PET/CT offered no additional value when [18F]PSMA PET/CT was negative.

## Recent Advances in CT for MSK Imaging
Recent developments in CT have enhanced MSK imaging through innovations such as WBCT and dual-energy CT (DECT). WBCT enables 3D assessment of joint alignment under physiological loading, allowing realistic evaluation of deformities in conditions such as knee OA and complex foot and ankle disorders. A recent study using upright WBCT demonstrated that the 3D mechanical axis progressively shifts posteromedially with increasing Kellgren–Lawrence grade, and in advanced OA (KL 3–4), the axis can be displaced outside the joint space. DECT further expands CT capability by differentiating urate, calcium pyrophosphate, and hydroxyapatite crystals, thereby improving the diagnosis of crystal deposition diseases, visualizing fine tendon structures, and detecting bone marrow edema or occult fractures. In addition to these advances, other recent CT applications have been reported. CT-based metrics such as the tibial tubercle–medial epicondyle distance aid in the quantitative assessment of patellofemoral instability and serve as objective parameters for surgical planning. Furthermore, the combination of DLR and single-energy metal artifact reduction (SEMAR) on pelvic CT has been shown to reduce metal artifacts and improve overall image quality in patients with hip prostheses. Together, these developments extend CT beyond traditional structural imaging, providing complementary diagnostic and quantitative information that supports MRI-based assessment in MSK radiology. summarizes the main techniques discussed in this section.
 

## Future Directions and Research Gaps
As MSK imaging continues to evolve, several key areas warrant further research and development to fully realize the potential of recent technological advances. First, the integration of AI into clinical workflows remains a major priority. While AI applications in image reconstruction and diagnostic support have shown promising results, their routine adoption in clinical practice requires careful validation, regulatory approval, and optimization of interoperability with existing systems. Second, there is a need for validation of novel imaging sequences, such as advanced MRI techniques (e.g., ZTE, UTE), in multicenter, prospective studies. Such validation will help establish standardized protocols, assess reproducibility, and confirm clinical utility across diverse populations and healthcare settings. Third, standardization of acquisition parameters and analysis protocols is essential to ensure consistency in imaging quality and quantitative biomarkers. Harmonization efforts across institutions will facilitate collaborative research, enable large-scale data pooling, and accelerate the translation of imaging biomarkers into clinical decision-making. Although these emerging MRI and AI-based techniques show great potential, their availability remains limited in many institutions due to differences in hardware, software, and infrastructure requirements, as well as associated costs. Broader access and standardization will be essential for widespread clinical adoption. Addressing these research gaps will be critical for enhancing diagnostic accuracy, improving patient outcomes, and promoting widespread adoption of emerging technologies in MSK radiology.

## Conclusion
Recent advances in MSK imaging have expanded diagnostic and prognostic capabilities. CT-like contrast MRI techniques such as ZTE and FRACTURE enable radiation-free visualization of cortical bone and calcifications, while quantitative MRI approaches including T2, T1rho, and UTE–T2* mapping provide reproducible biomarkers of cartilage, fibrocartilage, and tendon integrity. AI-driven reconstruction improves MRI efficiency and image quality, and diagnostic AI models show increasing potential in supporting interpretation. MR spectroscopy offers metabolic and bioenergetic information useful in tumors and muscle disorders. WB-MRI provides comprehensive, radiation-free assessment of oncologic and inflammatory diseases. Novel PET tracers expand molecular imaging applications, and advances in CT, including weight-bearing CT and dual-energy CT, enhance evaluation of joint biomechanics and crystal deposition. Challenges remain in clinical validation, protocol standardization, and integration of AI into workflows. Collaborative multicenter studies and continued refinement will be essential for routine adoption. These innovations hold promise for more precise and patient-centered MSK imaging.
