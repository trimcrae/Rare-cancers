Despite MTC representing 5–10% of thyroid malignancies, it contributes 15% of thyroid cancer-related mortality ( , ). Approximately 80% of MTC cases occur sporadically, with the remaining 20% inherited as familial MTC or multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) spectrum. Familial non-RET MTC with autosomal dominant mode of inheritance with linkage analysis showing the location of chromosome 4 has rarely been described ( ).
Predominant clinical presentations involve neck swelling, typically characterized by an indolent course. Additional manifestations encompass diarrhea (30%) when the disease burden is very high with a high level of calcitonin and calcitonin gene-related peptide and symptoms associated with metastatic dissemination ( ).
This case series highlights ten atypical presentations of MTC in nine cases: 3 unusual tumors along with MTC, namely chondrosarcoma, carcinoma prostrate, and ectopic Cushing’s syndrome; 4 unusual associations or presenting manifestations: pneumoconiosis masquerading as lung metastasis, Marfanoid habitus in MEN2A and VHL spectrum disease, 1 with skull metastasis 2 cases had tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI)-related complications in the form of glomerulonephritis and gut perforation emphasizing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges and rare tumor associations.

## Methodology
This retrospective analysis presents multiple cases of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) with atypical clinical manifestations, encompassing both tumor-related and coexisting medical conditions not directly attributable to MTC itself, thereby highlighting significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges encountered during the management of these patients.
This is an analysis of hospital records of 15 years from 2010 to 2025 and included patients who presented to our hospital with histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of MTC and remained under our care. The assessment included clinical symptoms, blood biochemistry, imaging characteristics (ultrasonography, contrast-enhanced computer tomography (CECT) scan, and 68gallium DOTA scan as deemed necessary). As per institutional policy and availability of modalities, investigations were performed at respective time frames. Although 18F DOPA PET-CT is available in our institute and has higher specificity as compared to 68gallium DOTA scan, because of higher cost and lack of theranostic utility, it was not preferred. All cases were surgically verified by detailed histopathology and immunohistochemistry for calcitonin and CEA. The management of individual cases was determined through multidisciplinary team discussions, with therapeutic decisions tailored according to the availability of treatment modalities and adherence to the prevailing standards of care, as informed by the most current evidence and institutional protocols at the time of intervention. Specific details of challenging cases are described separately.
The prevailing therapeutic paradigm involves surgical resection followed by administration of TKIs (sorafenib or lenvatinib in the initial phase and cabozantinib or vandetanib in the later phase when available in our country) and/or Lutathera and fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI)-based peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) to patients with disseminated disease. Adjunctive external beam radiotherapy was given to some of our patients with loco-regional relapse, microscopic involvement of the removed lymph nodes shown on histopathology, extranodal extension, or residual disease (microscopic/macroscopic).

## Results
Over a 15-year period, we identified and longitudinally managed a cohort of 80 patients with histopathologically confirmed MTC, all of whom remained under active follow-up at our institution until the time of this review or until their demise, as applicable. In the cohort of 80 patients, 4 patients posed significant diagnostic challenge at the time of their presentation. Three patients posed therapeutic doubts related to the choice of therapy and subsequent development of treatment-related complications. MTC associated with other diseases was seen in two patients.
A summary of the cases is presented below.

## Case vignettes

## Diagnostic dilemmas

## Case 1
A 48-year-old male presented with a one-year history of limping gait and progressive swelling of the left hip and upper thigh area, which had not previously been evaluated. Radiographic assessment, including X-ray and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), revealed a space-occupying lesion at the left femoral neck extending into the greater trochanter. Concurrently, the patient reported a five-year history of anterior neck swelling. Clinical examination identified a World Health Organization (WHO) grade 2 goiter with mated cervical lymphadenopathy.
Biochemical evaluation demonstrated normal thyroid function tests, negative anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) antibodies, and unremarkable blood biochemistry and hormonal profiles. Thyroid ultrasonography revealed a TIRADS 3 lesion. Notably, the serum calcitonin level was markedly elevated at 2,017 pg/mL (reference range: 0–6 pg/mL), raising suspicion for MTC with possible osseous metastasis, in line with Occam’s razor for diagnostic reasoning.
Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) of the thyroid confirmed MTC. A 99mTc MDP bone scan demonstrated increased radiotracer uptake in the left femoral neck and greater trochanteric region, suggestive of active bone pathology. However, subsequent biopsy of the femoral lesion established a diagnosis of chondrosarcoma, rather than metastatic MTC. The patient underwent surgical resection of the thyroid followed by adjuvant radiotherapy to the neck targeting residual cervical lymph nodes. The serum calcitonin level decreased to 15 pg/mL after surgery and RT, and the patient remained clinically stable over a five-year follow-up period. The most recent 68gallium DOTA scan, performed three months before, showed no evidence of somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) expressing lesions. He also underwent wide resection for chondrosarcoma followed by total hip replacement and radiotherapy to the surrounding area.
This case exemplifies the diagnostic complexity encountered when evaluating bone lesions in patients with MTC, as osseous metastases are common and associated with poor prognosis, particularly when osteolytic in nature. The differential diagnosis of bone lesions in the context of MTC should include primary bone malignancies, such as chondrosarcoma, and metastatic diseases. Multimodal imaging and histopathological confirmation are essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management ( ).
 

## Case 2
A 68-year-old male presented with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). Initial evaluation revealed a markedly elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level of 135 ng/mL (reference range: 3.5–5.5 ng/mL) and ultrasonographic evidence of prostatomegaly. Given the patient’s age and PSA elevation, a 68gallium prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) scan was performed, which unexpectedly demonstrated increased tracer uptake in the thyroid bed ( ). Subsequent thyroid ultrasonography identified a TIRADS 5 lesion. Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) of the thyroid confirmed MTC, and the preoperative serum calcitonin was significantly elevated at 278 pg/mL (reference range: 0–6 pg/mL).
 The patient underwent total thyroidectomy, followed by surgical management of the prostate lesion, which was histopathologically confirmed as adenocarcinoma of the prostate. Postoperatively, he received adjuvant radiotherapy to the neck and was initiated on sorafenib (400 mg in divided doses) for residual cervical lymphadenopathy after 1 year due to elevated calcitonin with rapid doubling time (597 pg/mL).
After one year of sorafenib therapy, the patient developed nephrotic syndrome. Renal biopsy revealed membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, which was resolved upon discontinuation of sorafenib. The patient was not re-challenged with TKI therapy. At the most recent follow-up at 6 years of RT, he remains clinically stable, with 68gallium DOTA PET demonstrating only faint uptake in the thyroid bed, a serum calcitonin of 34 pg/mL, and normal carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels. He continues under close surveillance.
This case highlights several important clinical considerations. First, the incidental detection of MTC during evaluation for prostate malignancy underscores the importance of comprehensive imaging and vigilance for synchronous primary tumors, particularly in elderly patients. While PSMA PET-CT is primarily utilized for prostate cancer staging, non-prostatic uptake, especially in the thyroid bed, should prompt further investigation, as both benign and malignant thyroid lesions can demonstrate PSMA avidity. Second, the development of biopsy-proven membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis secondary to sorafenib is a rare but recognized complication of TKI therapy. In retrospect, a wait-and-watch approach would have been much better considering the severe adverse event that happened in the subject and considering the toxicity profile of TKI.

## Case 3
A 30-year-old male with no significant prior medical history presented with a 15 kg weight gain over one year, generalized weakness, profound difficulty rising from a seated position, increased bruisability, and bilateral peripheral edema. Additional symptoms included facial puffiness, a palpable goiter, abdominal distension, hypertension, and recent-onset diabetes mellitus. Physical examination revealed multiple violaceous abdominal striae, axillary hyperpigmentation, and a cervical mass causing compressive symptoms.
Biochemical evaluation demonstrated adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-dependent Cushing’s syndrome, with an ACTH level of 107 pg/mL (reference: 7.2–63.3 pg/mL) and a serum cortisol of 996 nmol/L. Further assessment of the thyroid mass revealed markedly elevated carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) at 355 ng/mL (reference: <3 ng/mL) and calcitonin at 5,383 pg/mL (reference: 0–6 pg/mL). Contrast-enhanced MRI of the pituitary was unremarkable, and a high-dose dexamethasone suppression test failed to suppress cortisol secretion.
A 68Ga DOTA PET-CT scan identified a thyroid lesion with loco-regional metastasis and increased uptake in both adrenal glands. The clinical and biochemical findings were consistent with ectopic Cushing’s syndrome secondary to a neuroendocrine tumor. Given the severity of hypercortisolemia and the anticipated incomplete resection of the thyroid lesion, a multidisciplinary tumor board recommended bilateral adrenalectomy as the initial intervention. One month later, the patient underwent total thyroidectomy with modified radical neck dissection; however, residual disease persisted postoperatively. Calcitonin levels decreased to 350 pg/mL post-surgery and further to 231 pg/mL following adjuvant radiotherapy to the tumor bed.
Histopathological analysis confirmed medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) with immunohistochemical positivity for both calcitonin and ACTH on double immunofluorescence, confirming the diagnosis of MTC with ectopic ACTH secretion ( ). Genetic analysis revealed a heterozygous SDHB mutation (c.287-9C>A, intron 3), but there was no germline RET mutation. There was no biochemical or imaging evidence of paraganglioma at the last follow-up.
 Currently, the patient remains clinically stable, with resolution of hypertension and diabetes, and no evidence of paraganglioma or progressive metastatic disease. This case has been published as a case report elsewhere ( ).

## Case 4
A 56-year-old female presented with a gradually enlarging, painless swelling in the occipital region over two years. There was no history of trauma, constitutional symptoms, or prior malignancy. Clinical examination revealed a firm, non-tender mass in the occipital scalp, without neurological deficits. FNAC of the occipital lesion suggested a neuroendocrine tumor. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of the cell block was positive for calcitonin and CEA, raising suspicion for MTC. Biochemical evaluation revealed markedly elevated serum calcitonin (786 pg/mL; reference: 0–6 pg/mL) and CEA (78.5 ng/mL; reference: <5 ng/mL).
A 68Ga DOTA PET-CT scan demonstrated a metabolically active occipital lesion with additional uptake in the thyroid bed and cervical lymph nodes, consistent with metastatic disease. Thyroid ultrasonography revealed a TIRADS 5 lesion in the right lobe, further supporting the diagnosis of primary thyroid malignancy. The patient underwent total thyroidectomy with central and lateral neck lymph node dissection. Histopathological examination of the thyroid confirmed MTC, with IHC positive for calcitonin and CEA. Postoperatively, the patient received adjuvant local radiotherapy to the occipital region to address residual disease and reduce the risk of local recurrence, in line with current recommendations for unresectable or incompletely resected metastatic foci.
At the last follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, with no evidence of disease progression on imaging and stable serum tumor markers ( ).
 

## Management dilemmas

## Case 5
A 19-year-old female presented with a progressive neck swelling over nine years, associated with compressive symptoms. Examination revealed a large goiter and multiple cervical lymph nodes. Notably, the patient exhibited Marfanoid habitus, characterized by tall stature, long limbs, and other connective tissue features. An 18F FDG PET CT scan demonstrated metastatic disease involving the lungs and skeletal system.
She underwent total thyroidectomy with regional lymph node dissection. Postoperatively, she received adjuvant radiotherapy and started on TKI (lenvatinib, 10 mg/day). Whole exome sequencing (WES) identified a germline RET proto-oncogene mutation at codon 634, with no additional pathogenic variants, including FBN1. At her last follow-up, her serum calcitonin was 223 ng/mL.
The RET codon 634 mutation is the most prevalent in MEN2A and is associated with a high risk of MTC, pheochromocytoma, and primary hyperparathyroidism. The clinical phenotype can be variable, even among individuals with the same mutation. While Marfanoid habitus is a hallmark of MEN2B (typically associated with RET M918T mutation), it is rarely reported in MEN2A or in patients with codon 634 mutations. The presence of Marfanoid features in this patient, in the absence of mucosal neuromas or other MEN2B stigmata, and with a negative FBN1 mutation screen, is highly unusual and, to the best of our knowledge, has not been described in the literature yet ( ). His both parents and one sibling were negative for germline mutation.
 

## Case 6
A 60-year-old male presented with episodes of paroxysmal hypertension and tachycardia. Biochemical evaluation confirmed bilateral pheochromocytoma, with elevated metanephrine and normetanephrine. The patient underwent successful bilateral adrenalectomy. During routine follow-up, a markedly elevated serum calcitonin (345 pg/mL) was detected. A 68Ga DOTA PET-CT scan revealed abnormal uptake in the thyroid bed. Ultrasound-guided FNAC of the thyroid confirmed MTC. The patient subsequently underwent total thyroidectomy. Three months postoperatively, his serum calcitonin decreased to 56 pg/mL, and he was kept under surveillance.
During follow-up, 68Ga DOTA PET-CT demonstrated non-avid lesions in both lung fields, and serum calcitonin increased to 123 pg/mL. Given the doubling of calcitonin within six months, the possibility of metastatic lung disease was considered, and the patient was started on sorafenib 400 mg per day in divided doses. However, a subsequent transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) revealed pneumoconiosis rather than metastatic disease ( ). Sorafenib was discontinued as it was not warranted and associated with severe palmar desquamation ( ). During 7 years of follow-up, both 68Ga DOTA PET-CT and calcitonin values remained stable, with no evidence of progressive disease. His genetic study was suggestive of germline RET mutation at codon 918 (RET M918T). Detailed history could not reveal any family history of MTC or pheochromocytoma and mucosal neuroma. The index case had no mucosal neuroma anywhere in the body. The lung lesions posed a considerable diagnostic and therapeutic challenge throughout the patient’s course, especially in view of high-grade mutation.
 This case illustrates the complex interplay between biochemical surveillance, imaging, and histopathology in the management of MEN2-associated MTC and pheochromocytoma. It highlights the potential for non-malignant pulmonary pathology to confound the diagnosis of metastatic MTC, the necessity of tissue confirmation before systemic therapy even in cases of high-grade RET mutation, and the value of long-term, multidisciplinary follow-up in optimizing patient outcomes.

## Case 7
A 42-year-old male presented with a progressively increasing neck swelling; examination revealed a goiter with lymphadenopathy. He underwent total thyroidectomy and neck node dissection. Postoperatively, he was started on lenvatinib (4 mg/day), which was later escalated to 10 mg/day due to progressive metastatic disease.
After one year of lenvatinib therapy, the patient developed acute abdominal pain and features of peritonitis. Imaging confirmed bowel perforation near the ileocecal region, without evidence of diverticular disease. Emergency bowel resection and peritoneal lavage were performed, but the patient succumbed to sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction syndrome. Histopathology revealed bowel perforation without metastasis at the site of perforation and no evidence of diverticular disease anywhere in the inspected gut. The perforation was attributed to lenvatinib. Risk factors for lenvatinib-induced perforation include pre-existing bowel disease, tumor infiltration of the GI tract, prior abdominal surgery, and high-dose therapy. However, none of these risk factors are present in our case. The clinical presentation is typically acute, with abdominal pain and signs of peritonitis. Imaging and prompt surgical intervention are essential, but the prognosis is poor, especially in the context of immunosuppression and advanced malignancy. Despite the best of efforts, mortality remains very high ( ).
 

## Syndromic association and rare co-occurrence

## Case 8
A 40-year-old male presented with loose stools for four months (5–6 episodes/day), a significant weight loss (nearly 10 kg in three months), and a swelling on the left side of his neck. FNAC of the thyroid lesion suggested MTC. He underwent total thyroidectomy and radical neck dissection. Residual neck nodes adherent to vascular structures were confirmed intraoperatively and on imaging; these were kept under surveillance. His postoperative calcitonin and CEA were 210 pg/mL and 34 ng/mL, respectively. Due to progressively increasing calcitonin and CEA (507 pg/mL and 79 ng/mL, respectively), he was offered local exploration to which he declined. Subsequently, he received 54 Gy of external radiotherapy in 28 fractions.
On follow-up, the patient was found to have persistent polycythemia with normal leukocyte and platelet counts. Targeted mutational analysis for JAK2 V617F was negative. WES revealed a pathogenic nonsense mutation in the ataxia telangiectasia gene (ATM p. Arg23Ter). The patient had a significant family history of malignancy (father: hepatocellular carcinoma; mother: lung adenocarcinoma). He was managed with 75 mg aspirin and regular phlebotomy to maintain safe hemoglobin levels. At the last follow-up, his disease remained stable and non-progressive. This case highlights a rare intersection of MTC, persistent JAK2-negative polycythemia, and a pathogenic ATM mutation in a patient with a strong family history of malignancy. The findings underscore the importance of a comprehensive genetic evaluation in atypical cancer presentations and suggest that ATM mutations may contribute to a broader spectrum of hereditary cancer syndromes, including thyroid malignancy.

## Case 9
A 45-year-old female presented with a long-standing thyroid swelling, which had recently increased in size and was associated with respiratory difficulty. MRI revealed thyroid enlargement with multiple cervical lymph nodes. She underwent total thyroidectomy and regional lymph node dissection; histopathology confirmed MTC. Preoperative serum calcitonin and CEA levels were markedly elevated (2,000 pg/mL and 149.5 ng/mL, respectively), and both decreased postoperatively.
Despite initial management, follow-up MRI indicated residual thyroid lesion and persistent lymphadenopathy. She underwent redo surgery with modified neck dissection and central node removal, followed by external beam radiotherapy (44 Gy). Subsequent 18F FDG PET-CT revealed FDG-avid soft tissue in the interpolar region of the left kidney, a cystic lesion in the pancreas, and perinephric fat stranding. Biochemical evaluation showed elevated urinary metanephrine and normetanephrine.
The patient underwent bilateral adrenalectomy and left-sided nephron-sparing surgery. Pathology confirmed pheochromocytoma in the adrenal tissue and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC) in the nephrectomy specimen (positive for CK7, CD117, and AMACR). Genetic analysis identified a germline VHL mutation but was negative for RET proto-oncogene mutation.

## Discussion
In this case series, nine cases of MTC with 10 uncommon manifestations were described. They were either related to diagnosis, management, or association of MTC with other organ system involvement, creating doubt in the diagnosis and posing difficulty in the management of the patients. In the literature, the most common presenting complaint was a goiter with lymphadenopathy. Some studies demonstrated that weight loss and diarrhea are common in these patients ( , ). Rarely, patients may present with metastasis and related complications. In one of the cited studies, nearly 25% of patients present with metastasis and related complications ( ). Pheochromocytoma and parathyroid adenomas are usually associated with MTC. Association of MTC with chondrosarcoma and prostate carcinoma is infrequent.
In the present study, the first patient exhibited a limping gait, a bony lump, and a swelling in the neck. The neck pathology suggested medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), and the bony lump was initially thought to be a metastatic deposit due to an elevated serum calcitonin level though modestly. However, further investigation revealed that the patient actually had chondrosarcoma. This case illustrates Hikam’s dictum, which states that there can be as many explanations for various manifestations of disease in an individual patient. It is worth noting that, retrospectively, the calcitonin level was markedly elevated, indicating the possibility of extra-thyroidal metastasis likely. The association of MTC with chondrosarcoma has not been documented in the literature and may simply be a coincidence.
In the second case, a 68Ga PMSA PET-CT scan of a patient with prostatic carcinoma revealed uptake in the thyroid bed. Histopathological examination confirmed that the lesion in the neck was MTC. The patient later developed glomerulonephritis on sorafenib and stopped thereafter. While PSMA PET-CT is primarily utilized for prostate cancer staging, non-prostatic uptake, especially in the thyroid bed, should prompt further investigation, as both benign and malignant thyroid lesions can demonstrate PSMA avidity. Second, the development of biopsy-proven membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis secondary to sorafenib is a rare but recognized complication of TKI therapy, likely mediated by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway inhibition, while proteinuria is a more common renal adverse event, occurring in approximately 26.3% of patients in the REFLECT trial (hepatocellular carcinoma) and 33.7% in the SELECT trial (differentiated thyroid cancer), with grade ≥3 proteinuria reported in 5.9 and 10.7% respectively ( , ). A real-world Japanese study reported proteinuria in 60.8% of patients receiving lenvatinib for radioactive iodine-refractory thyroid cancer, suggesting a higher incidence outside of clinical trials ( ). Biopsy-confirmed glomerulonephritis – including thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), minimal change disease, or membranous nephropathy – has only been reported in isolated case studies, and severe renal impairment (potentially encompassing these) was observed in 1.9% of patients in the SELECT trial ( ). Pathogenesis is primarily attributed to VEGF pathway inhibition, which disrupts glomerular endothelial and podocyte integrity, leading to proteinuria and, in rare cases, structural glomerular damage ( ). Ongoing renal function test and urine analysis is essential during therapy, with dose modifications or discontinuation required for significant proteinuria or renal dysfunction. In our patient, the nephrotic syndrome completely disappeared after the stoppage of sorafenib, and he had a stable disease in the absence of TKI. The coexistence of prostatic malignancy and MTC is rare in the literature. While 68Ga PMSA PET-CT can show uptake in the thyroid gland, this is primarily linked to benign conditions ( ). Such findings may occur coincidentally, as prostatic cancer is relatively common in the general population.
The third case was a young male in his third decade of life and presented with features of Cushing’s syndrome. Hormonal profile indicated ACTH-dependent Cushing’s syndrome, and later, MTC was identified as the underlying cause of the ectopic Cushing’s syndrome. Double immunofluorescence performed on tumor tissue was positive for both ACTH and calcitonin and CEA, and they were co-localized to a single cell, suggesting that the tumor cells were secreting both the hormones and the tumor marker. In cases of Cushing’s syndrome due to ectopic ACTH, bilateral adrenalectomy is usually reserved to control symptoms of overwhelming hypercortisolism with neuro-psychiatric manifestation or severe sepsis, in cases of occult carcinoma or in patients who are otherwise inoperable or in whom incomplete removal is anticipated due to advanced disease. Since in this case, the source of ACTH overproduction was known, and the patient was a 30-year-old otherwise healthy individual, ideally, he should have been subjected to curative thyroid surgery; however, in a multidisciplinary meeting, the operating ENT surgeon anticipated incomplete removal of all the lymph nodes, which actually happened in this case. Therefore, bilateral adrenalectomy was justified. On follow-up, he had multiple cervical and mediastinal lymph nodes for which local radiotherapy was given.
Moreover, the germline SDH-B mutation is associated with the development of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma. In this case, till date, he has not developed any paraganglioma and a review of the histopathology specimen of adrenals did not reveal any features of pheochromocytoma.
A recent systematic review and meta-analysis revealed 96 cases of MTC presenting with ectopic Cushing’s syndrome; most of these patients had metastatic disease, and their outcomes were relatively worse compared to other causes of ectopic Cushing’s syndrome ( ). The occurrence of ectopic Cushing’s syndrome secondary to MTC is considered very rare, seen in less than 1% of cases ( , ).
Like our case 4, scalp metastasis has been reported in 2–3% of follicular thyroid carcinoma; it is very rare in MTC ( ). In a recent systematic review, only 38 cases of thyroid malignancy have been reported with scalp metastasis, out of which only 6 cases were MTC ( ).
In our case 5, de novo germline RET mutation involving codon 634 of exon 11 occurred. This is also most common in European and non-European countries. Codon 634 is associated with MEN2A syndrome involving the parathyroid gland, pheochromocytoma, and medullary thyroid carcinoma. Marfanoid habitus is classically described with MEN2B syndrome almost 75–100%, and the underlying mutations are in codons 804 and 918. Marfanoid habitus in the codon 634 mutation is rarely reported in the literature ( , , ). Even with extensive metastatic MTC, primary thyroid surgery is indicated to reduce the disease burden ( , ). So we subjected our patient to total thyroidectomy with radical neck dissection even if there was vascular invasion with favorable outcome. Marfanoid appearance refers to physical characteristics similar to those seen in Marfan syndrome, such as long limbs, arachnodactyly (long, slender fingers), and a tall, thin body habitus. These features can also be present in other connective tissue disorders. In our patient, WES was negative for FBN1 mutation as well ( , ).
Case 6 developed hand-foot syndrome after treatment with TKI, which was initiated for suspected lung metastasis, since he had a high-risk RET proto-oncogene mutation (RET p.M918T) ( ). In him, the TKI treatment was stopped when it was discovered that the lung lesion was due to pneumoconiosis. This case underscores the importance of correlation of serum calcitonin levels in determining the presence of extra-thyroid and lung metastases, as most instances show levels >400 pg/mL, while our index case exhibited a level below 200 pg/mL ( ).
Case 7 experienced a bowel perforation six months after starting treatment with lenvatinib, a multi-TKI. While lenvatinib has been associated with bowel perforation in the literature ( ), such occurrences typically happen in areas of metastasis or if there is pre-existing pathology like diverticular disease, which was not the case here. This complication may be attributed to the anti-VEGF properties of the TKI, as similar issues have been reported in patients receiving systemic bevacizumab ( ). He was managed by bowel resection but succumbed to the illness re-emphasizing close monitoring and early diagnosis of hollow viscose ruptures in patients receiving a multi-kinase inhibitor.
Case 8, who had MTC and polycythemia vera, had a strong family history of malignancy and had a tier 2 mutation in the ATM gene (ataxia telangiectasia gene). The ATM gene has been implicated in various malignancies, including thyroid, but less often. ATM acts as a DNA repair gene. Loss of function leads to a spectrum of diseases with maximum severity, including ataxia telangiectasia and associated malignancy ( , ). The relationship between ATM mutation, MTC, and PV is yet to be elucidated.
Case 9 presented with von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease in conjunction with medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC). The significance of VHL’s role in MTC necessitates further investigation. Research has identified mutations in the VHL gene within MTC tissues, which correlated with an increased expression of carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX) and HIF1α in both primary tumors and lymph node metastases, but this could not be confirmed in the somatic tumor tissue in our case ( ). Five out of nine cases (cases 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8) in this small series received external beam radiotherapy for microscopic involvement of lymph nodes on histopathology and/or following loco-regional relapse or residual extension to surrounding structures. However, in a recent systematic review, which examined 27 non-randomized studies within 4 cohort studies, radiotherapy was found to have no significant effect on overall survival. Our patients were probably over-treated due to unavailability of clear consensus during those days ( ).
The limitations of our study are retrospective nature, non-performance of genetic analysis on the tumor tissues for RET and other genes, and treatment with non-specific TKIs as per availability, which are inferior to selpercatinib, and external beam radiotherapy in some of the patients, which has no survival advantage according to a recent systematic review.

## Conclusion
The presentation of MTC is usually straightforward; however, it may occasionally pose significant diagnostic dilemmas and therapeutic challenges. The association of it with other diseases can be unrelated or may be genetically driven, especially malignancy. Genetic evaluation for germline mutations and somatic hits in tumor tissue is warranted to further elucidate the pathogenesis associated with MTC.
