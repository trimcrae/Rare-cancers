## Background
Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) has emerged as a critical biomarker in the tumor microenvironment of various cancers. Radioligands targeting FAP have shown promise for cancer theranostics. To advance cancer imaging, we synthesized a dimeric radioligand based on a 4-quinolinoyl-glycyl-2-cyanopyrrolidine scaffold and conducted studies in cells and mouse tumor models to evaluate its target-binding affinity and PET imaging performance.

## Results
OncoFAP, a commercially available FAP ligand, was conjugated via amide coupling with 1,4-butanediamine to generate a monomeric intermediate, hmFAP1. A homodimeric molecule, hmFAP2, was synthesized by tethering two hmFAP1 moieties into a single construct. In enzymatic inhibition assays, both hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 demonstrated specific antagonist activity against FAP, with hmFAP2 exhibiting a 14-fold increase in inhibitory potency compared to hmFAP1. Cy5.5 fluorescent derivatives of hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 were generated for cell-binding assays in HeLa cells and xenografted tumors with positive FAP expression, revealing enhanced targeting efficacy of Cy5.5-hmFAP2. The IC₅₀ values derived from cell-binding curves were 130 nM for hmFAP1 and 8 nM for hmFAP2 (P < 0.001). Using DOTA as the chelator, both ligands were radiolabeled with ⁶⁸Ga, yielding stable products [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 for PET imaging. Consistently, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 demonstrated superior tumor uptake with high specificity in mice bearing HeLa, MDA-MB-231, and HEK293T xenografts with variable levels of FAP expression. The liver and intestinal radioactivity showed no difference between the groups of mice imaged with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1.

## Conclusions
hmFAP2 markedly enhances FAP-targeting efficiency, providing higher binding affinity, improved tumor uptake, and reduced nonspecific distribution compared with its monomeric counterpart. The favorable imaging properties of hmFAP2 position it as a promising candidate for translation into clinical PET imaging and as a potential scaffold for developing FAP-targeted theranostic agents.

## Introduction
Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) is a type II transmembrane serine protease expressed primarily on the surface of activated fibroblasts (Brennen et al. ; O’Brien and O’Connor ). FAP is minimally expressed in healthy adult tissues, but it is highly upregulated in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) of epithelial tumors (Franco et al. ; Mueller and Fusenig ; Kalluri and Zeisberg ) and fibrotic diseases, such as pulmonary fibrosis, liver cirrhosis, and cardiac fibrosis. A new class of FAP inhibitor (FAPI), with 4-quinolinoyl-glycyl-2-cyanopyrrolidine scaffold binding specifically to FAP, has emerged as promising molecular ligands/probes for cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) targeting and cancer detection (Gascard and Tlsty ; Fitzgerald and Weiner ; Loktev et al. ; Dendl et al. ; Lindner et al. ; Giesel et al. ). PET imaging with FAPI radioligands has been utilized for cancer diagnosis, with at least 15 different agents tested in clinical settings, demonstrating high uptake in tumors and relatively low uptake in healthy tissues, indicating exceptional potential in PET imaging across a wide range of cancers, including breast, colorectal, pancreatic, and lung cancers (Giesel et al. , ; Loktev et al. ; Kratochwil et al. ; Lindner et al. ). FAPI-based PET imaging offers several advantages over conventional [¹⁸F]-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-based PET imaging, the latter has limitations such as low specificity, high background signal, and non-specific tracer uptake in the heart, brain, and other organs (Giesel et al. , ). In contrast, FAPI-targeted PET imaging demonstrates superior tumor specificity, minimal uptake in normal tissues, and high image quality. FAPI radioligands exhibit rapid tumor uptake and are unaffected by the patient’s fasting status or blood glucose levels, making them particularly beneficial for patients with diabetes or those unable to comply with FDG-PET preparation requirements.
While FAP-targeted PET imaging shows promise for cancer diagnosis, not every FAPI radioligand offers a clear advantage for all cancer types or clinical applications. To optimize various aspects of cancer diagnosis and therapy using FAP-targeted strategy, multiple FAPI variants, from FAPI-02 to the latest FAPI-74, have been synthesized and radiolabeled with the goal of improving cancer detection, enhancing imaging quality, optimizing pharmacokinetics, and exploring therapeutic applications. FAPI-04 has shown promising activity in patients for the diagnosis of breast cancer (Giesel et al. , ). FAPI-34 has been used for diagnosing ovarian and pancreatic cancer in patients (Loktev et al. ). FAPI-46 has been applied for both diagnosis and therapy in patients with lung metastases of sarcoma (Kratochwil et al. ). FAPI-74 has exhibited promising results in patients for the diagnosis of lung cancer (Lindner et al. ).
OncoFAP, a small molecule with a quinolinoyl- cyanopyrrolidine scaffold, is a high-affinity FAP ligand that has recently gained attention as a powerful FAP-targeting agent due to its superior potential in both PET imaging and theranostics compared to other FAPI compounds. Its enhanced pharmacokinetics and strong tumor selectivity make it particularly promising for clinical applications (Millul et al. ; Backhaus et al. ). Although OncoFAP has been shown as a feasible PET radiotracer, its relatively short tumor retention (less than one day) limits its potential as a therapeutic β-emitting radioligand when labeled with ¹⁷⁷Lu or ²²⁵Ac for targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT) (Galbiati et al. , , ). To overcome this limitation, researchers have developed dimeric and trimeric derivatives, BiOncoFAP and TriOncoFAP, designed to enhance tumor targeting, improve imaging contrast, and expand therapeutic potential (Galbiati et al. , , ). The development of these multivalent OncoFAP variants is based on the principle that increasing the number of binding sites enhances affinity, retention, and tumor uptake. The dimeric and trimeric forms contain multiple FAP-binding motifs, enabling stronger and more sustained interactions with FAP-expressing tumors, thereby improving in vivo retention and tumor uptake compared to the monomer (Galbiati et al. , ; Pang et al. ; Zhong et al. ). By increasing tumor-specific binding while preserving rapid clearance from normal tissues, OncoFAP multimers enhance tumor-to-background contrast, resulting in sharper and more detailed PET images. This is particularly valuable for imaging cancers in high-background regions such as the liver, intestines, or sites of inflammation, making them promising candidates for cancer TRT. To further optimize FAP-targeting capabilities, we chemically modified OncoFAP by incorporating a unique linker 1,4-butane diamine (BDA) into the molecule, generating a monomeric intermediate named hmFAP1. A homodimeric molecule, hmFAP2, was then synthesized by tethering two hmFAP1 moieties into a single structure. To create a dimeric radioligand for PET imaging, hmFAP2 was conjugated with the macrocyclic chelator 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (DOTA), enabling radiolabeling with ⁶⁸Ga, 64Cu, and 177Lu. ⁶⁸Ga, produced from a ⁶⁸Ge/⁶⁸Ga generator, is ideal for rapid PET imaging due to its short half-life of 67.7 min.
In this study, we investigated the dimerization of hmFAP1 to form hmFAP2 and evaluated the biological efficacy of hmFAP2 and binding properties of fluorescent Cyanine5.5 (Cy5.5)- and ⁶⁸Ga-labeled dimeric hmFAP2 to assess its FAP-targeting capabilities. By comprehensively characterizing the molecular targeting properties of hmFAP2 in comparison with hmFAP1, our goal was to develop next-generation FAP-targeted theranostic agents with improved tumor selectivity, enhanced delivery to tumor tissues, and prolonged retention at the target site. Our in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrate that hmFAP2 exhibits superior binding kinetics, greater inhibition of FAP enzymatic activity, and increased tumor uptake compared to the monomeric hmFAP1.

## Materials and methods

## Chemicals and reagents

 

All chemical reagents, including 1,4 diamino butane, Boc-L-glutamic acid, HBTU, TEA, and solvents were purchased from Millipore Sigma (St. Louis, MO). Fluorogenic FAP assay kit was purchased from BPS Bioscience (catalog # 80210). DOTA NHS ester was purchased from Macrocyclics (Dallas, TX). OncoFAP and Sulfo-Cy5.5 NHS ester triethyl amine were purchased from ChemSuttle (Burlingame, CA). 68GaCl3 was eluted from an Eckert & Ziegler 68Ge/68Ga generator in 6 mL of 0.1 M HCl.

## Synthesis and 68Ga radiolabeling of hmFAP1 and hmFAP2
As illustrated in Fig.  , hmFAP1 was synthesized via amide coupling of BDA and OncoFAP using HBTU and TEA in DMF. The hmFAP2 dimer was obtained by amide coupling of two hmFAP1 moieties with Boc-Glu-OH using HOBt, HBTU, and TEA in DMSO, followed by Boc deprotection with TFA in DCM to yield hmFAP2. 1H spectra of hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 samples were recorded on a Bruker 400 MHz AvanceCore NMR spectrometer (Billerica, Massachusetts) in deuterated methanol (CD3OD) and analyzed using Bruker software to confirm expected chemical shifts and characteristic resonances. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of the hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 were recorded on Thermo Finnigan LCQ Fleet using electrospray as the ionization method. Figure  depicts the conjugation and radiolabeling reactions of hmFAP2 with ⁶⁸Ga. hmFAP2 was conjugated with DOTA-NHS ester in the presence of TEA in DMSO to produce DOTA-hmFAP2. The reaction mixture was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using a C18 column (250 × 10 mm, 100 Å) with gradient elution in a water/acetonitrile (0.1% TFA) solvent system at a flow rate of 4 mL/min. MS data of DOTA-hmFAP1 and DOTA-hmFAP2 conjugates were recorded as described above. The purified conjugate was lyophilized to provide a precursor for long-term storage. For radiolabeling, a solution of ⁶⁸GaCl₃ (370–555 MBq) was added to a 50 nmol aliquot of DOTA-hmFAP2 and incubated at 95 °C for 15 min to yield [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2. Radiochemical yield and purity were determined by RP-HPLC using a C18 column with gradient elution (solvent A: 0.1% TFA in water; solvent B: 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Similarly, hmFAP1 was conjugated with DOTA-NHS ester to produce DOTA-hmFAP1, which was subsequently radiolabeled with ⁶⁸Ga to generate the monomeric FAP radioligand, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1. Details about hmFAP1 conjugation, 68Ga-radiolabeling, and chemical structures are presented in Supplementary Information (SI).

 

The stability of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 was assessed by incubating the radiolabeled compound in 50% mouse serum in PBS at 37 °C for up to 4 h, and its structural integrity was analyzed using HPLC.

 

## Fluorescent labeling of hmFAP1/hmFAP2 with Cy5.5
hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 were labeled with Cy5.5 fluorescent dye (Excitation/Emission Max 678/695 nm), by reacting with Sulfo-Cy5.5 NHS ester in the presence of TEA in DMSO to yield Cy5.5-hmFAP1 and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 (Fig.  ). The coupled product was purified by semi-preparative HPLC using a C18 column (250 × 10 mm, 100 Å) with gradient elution in a water/acetonitrile (0.1% TFA) solvent system. Pure fractions were collected, lyophilized, and characterized by mass spectrometry.

## Enzymatic inhibitory binding assays with hmFAP1 and hmFAP2
The binding affinity of the synthetic FAP inhibitors, hmFAP1 and hmFAP2, was determined using a commercial fluorogenic FAP assay kit (Brennen et al. ; O’Brien and O’Connor ). In this assay, the DPP fluorogenic substrate reacts with a sample containing the FAP enzyme to produce a fluorophore, which is then measured using a fluorescence reader. The IC₅₀ values of hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 were determined by measuring FAP activity upon the addition of various concentrations (nM) of the FAP inhibitors from 0.05 to 10,000 for hmFAP1 and from 0.0005 to 500 for hmFAP2 by triplicated assays.

## Engineering HeLa cells and HEK293T cells for hFAP expression
HeLa (ATCC CCL-2) and HEK293T (ATCC CRL-3216) cells were engineered to express human FAP (hFAP), generating HeLaFAP and HEK293TFAP lines, with their parental counterparts serving as FAP-negative controls. Lentiviral particles encoding hFAP were produced by co-transfecting cells with an hFAP expression plasmid, packaging plasmids (pMDLg/pRRE, pRSV-Rev), and the envelope plasmid (pMD2.G) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). After 48 h, viral supernatant was harvested, filtered (0.45 μm), and used to transduce HeLa and HEK293T cells in the presence of polybrene (8 µg/mL). Stable hFAP-expressing lines were established through puromycin selection (1–2 µg/mL). For high-titer virus production, clonally selected Lenti-X cells yielded up to 10⁸ IFU/mL. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ and used for binding assays at ~ 75% confluency.

## Verification of FAP expression in HeLaFAP cells by Western blot
Following transduction, FAP expression in HeLaFAP cells was evaluated by Western blot. FAP-positive and FAP-negative HeLa cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed for 30 min on ice in buffer. Lysates were centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Thermo Scientific). Equal protein amounts (50 µg) were denatured (100 °C, 10 min), separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h, incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-FAP-α antibody (ab53066, Abcam), then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (GE Healthcare). β-actin served as a loading control. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Scientific).

## Measurement of FAP expression levels of tumor cells by flow cytometry
FAP surface expression in engineered HeLaFAP and HEK293TFAP cells, as well as the human breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231 (Jia et al. ), was evaluated by flow cytometry. Cells (1 × 10⁶) were incubated with mouse anti-human FAP-α antibody (ab53066, Abcam) for 30 min on ice, washed, and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen) for 30 min in the dark. After a final wash, samples were filtered through a 40 μm mesh and analyzed on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) with 488 nm excitation, using 137 mM NaCl/2.8 mM KCl as sheath fluid. A total of 10,000 events were collected per sample. For trypan blue quenching, samples were split into control and quenched aliquots to assess non-specific fluorescence. Data were analyzed with WinMDI 2.8 (Scripps Research Institute), and FAP expression was quantified relative to isotype and secondary-only controls.

## Cell binding assay and confocal Immunofluorescence imaging of Cy5.5-hmFAP1/Cy5.5-hmFAP2
The targeting capacity of Cy5.5-hmFAP1 and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 toward HeLaFAP cells was measured using cell saturation binding assays conducted in triplicate. HeLaFAP cells were seeded in 96-well plates one day prior to the experiment. Before ligand incubation, cells were blocked with 1% BSA for 20 min at room temperature to minimize nonspecific binding. Cells were then incubated with varying concentrations of Cy5.5-hmFAP1 or Cy5.5-hmFAP2 at 37 °C for 1 h. Fluorescence intensity of bound ligands was measured using a BioTek microplate reader, and saturation binding curves were generated by plotting fluorescence intensity against ligand concentration.
Immunofluorescence staining of HeLaFAP and parental HeLa cells was performed using an anti-FAP antibody (Invitrogen, PA551057), Cy5.5-hmFAP1, and Cy5.5-hmFAP2, respectively. Cells were seeded onto imaging plates at ~ 50% confluence, blocked with 1% BSA for 20 min at room temperature, and sequentially incubated with the anti-FAP primary antibody, TRITC-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Excitation/Emission Max 552/578 nm), and Cy5.5-labeled hmFAP1 or hmFAP2 (30 min each at 37 °C). Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and mounted with anti-fade medium containing DAPI (Excitation/Emission Max 358/461 nm). Confocal images were acquired using a FluoView 3000 microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified in ImageJ using thresholding and binary masking to measure integrated intensity.

## Mouse tumor models with various levels of FAP expression
Three subcutaneous tumor models were established in female athymic nude mice (6–7 weeks old; The Jackson Laboratory) using MDA-MB-231, HeLaFAP, and HEK293TFAP cells. FAP expression in these cell lines was determined by flow cytometry prior to implantation. Cell suspensions (1 × 10⁶ cells/100 µL in Matrigel) were injected subcutaneously into the left or right shoulder using 27-gauge needles. In vivo imaging studies were initiated once tumors reached ≥ 8 mm in diameter.

## Optical imaging of mice with xenografted tumors
Fluorescence imaging was performed in mice bearing HeLaFAP xenografts over 24 h following intravenous injection of 100 µL Cy5.5-hmFAP1 or Cy5.5-hmFAP2 (10 nmol in PBS). Images were acquired using the IVIS® Spectrum system (Revvity Inc., Waltham, MA). At the end of the imaging session, animals were euthanized, and tumors along with major organs were collected for ex vivo fluorescence analysis.

## PET/CT and PET/MR imaging of mice with xenografted tumors
PET/CT imaging with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 (n = 4) and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 (n = 4) was performed in mice bearing MDA-MB-231 xenografts using a Siemens Inveon PET/CT system. Under 1.5% isoflurane anesthesia, approximately 3.7 MBq of radiotracer was administered intravenously via the tail vein. PET images were acquired at 1 h and 2–3 h post-injection and co-registered with CT. Image reconstruction was performed using the manufacturer’s two-dimensional ordered-subsets expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm.
Mice bearing HEK-293TFAP tumors were positioned prone and imaged using an integrated 7T PET/MR system (MR Solutions, Guildford, UK) at 1 h and 2–3 h following intravenous injection of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 (n = 5) or [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 (n = 6) (100–150 µL, 3.7–7.4 MBq) via the tail vein. PET data were acquired simultaneously with T1-weighted anatomical MR images (3D FLASH sequence). After PET/MR imaging, sequential CT scans were obtained using the Click-on BenchTop CT scanner (MR Solutions, Guildford, UK). PET images were reconstructed using a 3D OSEM algorithm with standard corrections (decay, dead time, normalization, and randoms). PET, MR, and CT datasets were co-registered using VivoQuant™ 5.4 software (Invicro, Needham, MA, USA). Three-dimensional regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated on tumors and selected organs using MR images as anatomical references. Quantitative PET data were expressed as counts per second (CPS) per cubic millimeter of tissue (CPS/mm³).
An experimental blocking study was conducted in four mice bearing HEK-293TFAP xenografts by co-injecting 3.7–7.4 MBq of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 with non-radioactive hmFAP2 (50 nmol, ~ 60 µg/mouse). PET, MR, and CT imaging were performed following the same procedure as described above. Radioactive accumulation of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 (CPS/mm³) in tumors and major organs, determined by ROI analysis, was compared between the non-blocked group and the cold ligand–blocked group.

## Biodistribution studies
At the end of the PET/MR/CT imaging session, all mice were euthanized. Blood, tumors, and major organs were collected, weighed, and analyzed for radioactivity using a Packard gamma counter (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT). Tissue radioactivity was expressed as the percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g).

## Histological analysis and immunohistochemical assays
Tumor xenografts were fixed in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess tumor architecture and cancer cell growth. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), 5 μm sections were processed using the Bond Polymer Refine Kit (Leica, cat# DS9800) with endogenous peroxidase blocked at 20 °C for 5 min. Sections were incubated with a sheep polyclonal anti-FAP antibody (1:200) for 15 min, followed by a 15 min incubation with peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-Armenian hamster IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat# 127-585-160). Staining was visualized with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin.

## Statistical analysis
Quantitative data was expressed as mean ± SD. Means were compared using the student test. 95% confidence level was chosen to determine the significance between groups, and P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Results of hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 synthesis, Conjugation, and radiolabeling
The results detailing the synthesis, conjugation, and radiolabeling of hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 are available in SI. Both [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 were obtained with an 80%-90% decay-corrected yield and a radiochemical purity (RCP) greater than 98% after purification. The specific activity was estimated to be 1.7–2.3 MBq/nmol.
Subsequent radio-HPLC analysis revealed no detectable spectral changes in [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 or [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 incubated in 50% mouse serum, suggesting that the radiotracer maintains reasonable thermodynamic stability for in vivo studies for up to 4 h at 37 °C (see SI).

## Expression levels of hFAP in tumor cell lines
Following FAP transduction, HeLaFAP cells exhibited significantly higher hFAP protein expression, as determined by Western blot, compared with naïve HeLa cells, which showed undetectable levels of hFAP (Fig.  A and B). Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that HEK293TFAP cells had the highest hFAP expression, with 81.1% of the population staining positive. In contrast, HeLaFAP cells showed markedly lower expression, with only 14.1% of cells positive for hFAP. MDA-MB-231 cells exhibited negligible expression, with only 0.28% hFAP-positive cells, indicating an almost complete absence of FAP in this triple-negative breast cancer cell line (Fig.  C).

## hmFAP2 binding affinity
Based on FAP enzyme inhibition assays, both hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 demonstrated specific antagonist activity against FAP. Figure  A presents the inhibitory binding curves of FAP activity as a function of increasing concentrations of synthetic hmFAP1 and hmFAP2. The IC₅₀ values, derived from the binding curves, were 130 nM for the hmFAP1 monomer and 8 nM for the hmFAP2 dimer (P < 0.001). This comparison indicates that hmFAP2 exhibits 16-fold greater FAP inhibition compared to hmFAP1.

 

 

At the cellular level, the binding affinity of Cy5.5-hmFAP1 and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 was assessed by determining dissociation constants (Kₐd), derived from the saturation binding curves of Cy5.5-conjugated FAP ligands in HeLaFAP cells with increasing inhibitor concentrations (Fig.  B). The saturation-derived Kₐd values for hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 were 200 nM and 14 nM, respectively, indicating that hmFAP2 exhibits a 14-fold greater binding affinity than hmFAP1.

## Confocal immunofluorescence, Cy5.5-hmFAP1 and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 imaging
Figure  A shows confocal images comparing anti-FAP immunofluorescence, Cy5.5-hmFAP1, and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 in HeLaFAP and naïve HeLa cells. Green fluorescence from anti-FAP antibody staining was detected only in HeLaFAP cells, confirming successful FAP transduction, while naïve HeLa cells showed no signal. In the red channel, Cy5.5-hmFAP2 produced stronger fluorescence than Cy5.5-hmFAP1 in HeLaFAP cells, indicating enhanced binding of the dimeric inhibitor. Orthogonal views showed both antibody and inhibitor signals localized mainly on the cell membrane. Minimal uptake of Cy5.5-hmFAP1 and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 was observed in naïve HeLa cells, consistent with the lack of FAP expression.

 

As shown in Fig.  B, signal intensities from Cy5.5-hmFAP1 and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 were normalized to the corresponding TRITC-conjugated anti-FAP signals to account for variability in FAP expression. This normalization revealed a markedly higher relative binding of hmFAP2 compared to hmFAP1 in the engineered HeLaFAP cells. Comparisons with negative controls further confirmed that the uptake of both fluorescence FAP ligands was specific to FAP-expressing cells.

## Optical images of xenografted tumors
Figure  shows comparative imaging of Cy5.5-hmFAP1 and Cy5.5-hmFAP2 in dual xenograft models (HeLaFAP, left shoulder; MDA-MB-231, right) at 21 h post-injection. HeLaFAP tumors with enforced FAP expression exhibited higher fluorescence than MDA-MB-231 xenografts. Both ligands accumulated more in HeLaFAP tumors, with detectable uptake in MDA-MB-231. Cy5.5-hmFAP2 showed stronger whole-body fluorescence, consistent with prolonged circulation and slower clearance. Ex vivo imaging demonstrated greater retention of Cy5.5-hmFAP2, particularly in kidneys and liver, and superior tumor targeting compared with Cy5.5-hmFAP1 (Fig.  C, D).

 

## [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 PET/CT imaging of mice with MDA-MB-231 tumor xenografts
Figure  shows representative PET/CT images of mice bearing MDA-MB-231 xenografts imaged with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 at 1-hour and 2-hour post-injection. At 1 h, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAP1 showed low-to-moderate tumor uptake, whereas [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 exhibited markedly higher tumor uptake. Relatively greater liver and soft-tissue activity of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 was observed at 1 h. Both tracers showed prominent renal and bladder activity. By 2 h, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAP1 activity at tumors had largely cleared, while [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 retained higher tumor uptake, though reduced compared to 1 h. Liver and soft-tissue uptake of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 decreased to levels similar to [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAP1, improving tumor-to-background contrast, with minimal intestinal signal. The mouse imaged with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAP1 in Fig.  , a co-implanted HeLaFAP xenograft, showed moderately higher uptake than the MDA-MB-231 tumor, though lower than that of MDA-MB-231 tumors imaged with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2. Biodistribution measurements (Fig.  ) confirmed significantly higher tumor uptake (%ID/g) for [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 than [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAP1 (1.45 ± 0.21 vs. 0.85 ± 0.08, P < 0.01).

 

 

## [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 PET/MR/CT imaging of mice with HEK293TFAP tumors
Figure  presents representative PET/CT images of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 in mice bearing HEK293TFAP tumors. Compared with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 demonstrated superior tumor detectability in tissues with equivalent FAP expression from 1 to 2 h post-injection. In non-target tissues, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 exhibited moderately higher kidney activity relative to [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1. Apart from bladder radioactivity attributable to urinary excretion, radioactive uptake in other organs, including bone and soft tissue, remained markedly lower than tumor uptake. Despite its enhanced tumor accumulation, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 showed consistently low hepatic activity, comparable to [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1, at both 1 and 2 h post-injection. Representative blockade images are also shown in Fig.  , where administration of the cold ligand substantially reduced tumor uptake of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2. Under blockade conditions, PET/CT images at 1 and 2 h showed that the tumors were nearly indistinguishable from the soft tissue background, confirming the specific FAP binding of this dimeric radioligand.

 

Figure  presents representative 3D renderings and serial coronal slices of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 PET/MR images in a mouse bearing the xenografted HEK293TFAP tumor on the right shoulder at 1 and 2 h post-injection. Co-registered PET/MR images clearly delineate the distribution of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 in the tumor and other organs. Robust tumor uptake was observed at both time points, demonstrating consistent intratumoral radioactivity retention. Tomographic slices revealed markedly increased focal uptake within the tumor, with an inhomogeneous intratumoral distribution. Low to moderate radioactive accumulation was noted in the upper abdomen, likely reflecting hepatobiliary excretion into the gallbladder and proximal small intestine, while kidney uptake remained minimal.
Based on ROI analysis on PET images collected at 2–3 h post-injection, the average tumor radioactivity (CPS/mm³) of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2, corrected for radioactive decay and injected dose, are shown in Fig.  . The average tumor-to-muscle radioactivity ratio of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 was 14.6 ± 5.0. In contrast [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 demonstrated significantly higher tumor radioactivity, which was effectively blocked by co-administration of the cold ligand. At 2 h post-injection, the average tumor-to-muscle ratio of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 was 28.4 ± 3.0, decreasing to 11.8 ± 3.1 under blockade conditions. Compared with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 exhibited moderate liver uptake without a significant difference. ROI analysis further revealed that renal radioactivity of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 was significantly higher in mice receiving cold ligand blockade.

 

 

## [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 biodistribution in mice with HEK293TFAP tumors
The ex vivo biodistribution measurements of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 in mice bearing HEK293TFAP tumors at the end of PET/CT or PET/MR imaging sessions are summarized in Fig.  . Tumor uptake of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 was significantly higher than that of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-hmFAP1 (P < 0.01). Competitive inhibition of tumor uptake using 50 nmol of unlabeled hmFAP2 reduced the uptake of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 from 8.18 ± 0.62 to 2.81 ± 0.37%ID/g, approximately a 4-fold decrease compared to mice imaged without cold ligand blockade (P < 0.001). In contrast, liver [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 radioactivity exhibited no difference between the groups with and without blockade. Kidney uptake of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 was significantly higher in mice receiving the blocking dose than in those without hmFAP2 blockade.

## Histological and immunohistochemistry analysis
Figure  presents microphotographs of H&E-stained and anti-FAP IHC-stained sections of xenografted tumors. Extensive tumor cell growth was evident within the tumor sections of HeLaFAP, MDA-MB-231, and HEK293TFAP xenografts. Positive FAP expression was confirmed by anti-FAP IHC staining. HeLaFAP tumors are composed of poorly differentiated polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic to amphophilic cytoplasm and large pleomorphic nuclei. The stroma is sparse and loosely organized, separating clusters of tumor cells. Moderate membranous and cytoplasmic FAP staining is detected in the tumor cells. MDA-MB-231 tumors contain polygonal cells with moderate cytoplasm and pleomorphic nuclei, separated by a prominent desmoplastic stroma. Weak-to-moderate FAP positivity is observed in stromal fibroblasts, showing scattered distributions among tumor nests. HEK293TFAP tumors are composed of densely packed, round-to-oval cells with moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm and dense nuclei with minimal pleomorphism. Strong, diffuse membranous and cytoplasmic FAP positivity is observed in multiple cell types.

 

## Discussion
FAP, the type II serine protease of the dipeptidyl peptidase subfamily, is highly expressed in ~ 90% of epithelial tumors but largely absent in normal adult tissues (Brennen et al. ; O’Brien and O’Connor ; Franco et al. ; Mueller and Fusenig ; Kalluri and Zeisberg ). With overexpressed by activated CAFs, FAP promotes cancer cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion (Kalluri and Zeisberg ; Orimo et al. ). FAP targeting radioligands, particularly quinoline-based FAPIs, for cancer diagnostic and therapeutic applications have been extensively studied with respect to cellular binding, biodistribution, and pharmacokinetics in animals and humans (Gascard and Tlsty ; Fitzgerald and Weiner ; Loktev et al. , ; Dendl et al. ; Lindner et al. ; Moon et al. ; Feng et al. ; Ruger et al. ; Chakravarty et al. ; Mori et al. ; Prive et al. ; Giesel et al. , ). Among those, FAPI-04 and FAPI-46 have emerged as the most promising candidates, showing superior tumor targeting compared with [¹⁸F]FDG across lung, liver, colon, and pancreatic cancers (Bentestuen et al. ). The transition from FAPI-04 to FAPI-46 represents significant advancement, as FAPI-46 exhibits improved pharmacokinetics and enhanced tumor retention. However, important limitations remain in the clinical utility of FAP-targeted radioligands. Suboptimal tumor retention, rapid systemic clearance, and off-target uptake reduce target-to-background contrast. The short biological half-life of monomeric FAPIs limits penetration into the tumor core, posing challenges in cancers with heterogeneous or deeply embedded stroma, such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. To overcome these issues, numerous studies have pursued structural modifications of FAPI molecules, primarily through substitutions at the quinoline ring positions 6 and 7 while retaining a monomeric scaffold (Giesel et al. , ; Loktev et al. ; Kratochwil et al. ; Lindner et al. ). More recent approaches extend beyond these positions, exploring linker, chelator, and dimeric designs to improve targeting performance and broaden clinical applications.
FAPI-based homodimers can enhance tumor uptake and retention but also present limitations that may hinder optimal radio-theranostic use (Galbiati et al. ; Zhong et al. ; Moon et al. ; Zhao et al. ; Martin et al. ; Lappchen et al. ). Their larger size and bivalency prolong circulation, increasing tumor accumulation but also elevating background activity in bone marrow and other non-target tissues. High affinity may saturate peripheral stroma, limiting penetration into dense or fibrotic tumors and producing heterogeneous intratumoral distribution. Slower clearance from inflamed or fibrotic non-tumor sites can further reduce tumor-to-background ratios. Off-target uptake, particularly in the liver, pancreas (Galbiati et al. ; Zhao et al. ), may compromise abdominal imaging contrast. Prolonged radiation exposure in the kidneys, liver, and bone marrow increases dosimetry burden and limits the maximum tolerated dose for TRT with alpha beta emitters such as ²²⁵Ac and ¹⁷⁷Lu.
The dimeric hmFAP2 described in this study was constructed from the OncoFAP scaffold using a BDA linker. OncoFAP and its multimeric derivatives (BiOncoFAP and TriOncoFAP) have demonstrated superior tumor uptake and retention compared with FAPI-46, yielding higher tumor-to-organ ratios and improved diagnostic performance (Galbiati et al. , , ; Bocci et al. ). Collectively, current findings validate the OncoFAP scaffold as promising probes for both PET imaging and targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT). Unlike BiOncoFAP and TriOncoFAP, which incorporate rigid or sterically bulky linkers, the introduction of BDA in hmFAP2 reduces steric hindrance and conformational strain, thereby improving molecular flexibility and potentially reducing intramolecular folding that can impair target binding. This optimized structural design may contribute to enhanced chemical stability, improved radiolabeling efficiency, and stronger tumor-targeting performance observed in our study. In addition, hmFAP2 exhibits favorable biodistribution relative to published data on BiOncoFAP and TriOncoFAP. While multimeric OncoFAP variants can show increased hepatic or gastrointestinal accumulation due to their larger size or higher lipophilicity, hmFAP2 maintains low liver and bowel uptake, resulting in consistently high tumor-to-background ratios. This is particularly advantageous for imaging malignancies in the digestive tract, where background signal is often a major limitation. Furthermore, the modular synthesis strategy used to generate hmFAP2 based on assembling two hmFAP1 units around the BDA linker allows facile incorporation of different chelators, linkers, and optical labels. This versatility supports efficient radiolabeling with isotopes including ⁶⁸Ga, ⁶⁴Cu, and ¹⁷⁷Lu, enabling application across PET, SPECT, and TRT modalities. Overall, hmFAP2 offers several advantages over existing OncoFAP multimers, including improved molecular flexibility, reduced off-target uptake, high tumor-to-background contrast, and a streamlined, scalable synthetic route. These features collectively position hmFAP2 as a strong candidate for next-generation FAP-targeted theranostics.
In our comparative studies, hmFAP1 demonstrated feasible FAP targeting in tumors with low to moderate FAP expression; however, its tumor uptake was relatively low, likely due to the inherent limitation of a single binding site in low-density FAP settings and potential non-specific binding (Galbiati et al. , ). In contrast, hmFAP2 exhibited enhanced target binding and retention, resulting in superior imaging characteristics, particularly in tumors with heterogeneous or low FAP expression, thereby improving the accuracy of PET-based tumor detection. Compared with its parent molecule, hmFAP2 demonstrated an approximately 14-fold increase in FAP binding in both cell-based and enzyme inhibition assays, accompanied by markedly improved PET imaging performance in solid tumors. Quantitative PET analysis further revealed that tumor uptake of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 was roughly 20-fold higher than that of hmFAP1. The extended tumor retention of hmFAP2 makes it a strong candidate for TRT when labeled with ¹⁷⁷Lu or ²²⁵Ac, enabling delivery of higher radiation doses to tumors and improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing radiation exposure to normal tissues.
The findings of this study were derived from cell lines and their corresponding tumor xenografts with distinct FAP expression profiles. HEK-293TFAP cells, generated from HEK-293T cells that normally lack FAP, served as a stable positive model with constitutive FAP overexpression (Zhang et al. ). HeLa and MDA-MB-231 cells typically lack FAP expression, as FAP is largely restricted to CAFs and mesenchymal-like cells. Genetically engineered HeLaFAP cells provided an intermediate-expression model, while MDA-MB-231 cells, which do not inherently express FAP, served as a negative control. As a TNBC cell line with mesenchymal features, MDA-MB-231 has a strong capacity to recruit and activate stromal fibroblasts (Lu et al. ; Sharma et al. ). Upon implantation in mice, cancer cells recruit and activate host-derived fibroblasts, initiating paracrine signaling that drives tumor-stroma crosstalk. Tumor-secreted cytokines and growth factors promote fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblast-like CAFs, thereby remodeling the microenvironment into a fibroblast-rich, FAP-positive stroma (Sharma et al. ; Abe et al. ; Suh et al. ). Furthermore, hypoxia-driven HIF-1α activates pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) that reinforce CAF differentiation and FAP expression. The transition from FAP-negative tumor cells to FAP-positive stroma underscores the TME remodeling that underlies the rationale for FAP-targeted imaging and therapeutic strategies.
We observed differences in the biodistribution of ⁶⁸Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and ⁶⁸Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 in non-tumor tissues on PET imaging. Notably, renal uptake of the dimeric radioligand was higher than that of the monomeric analogue, particularly after blocking with the cold ligand. The enhanced kidney accumulation of hmFAP2 may be attributable to its dimeric structure, in which increased molecular size and hydrophilicity can slow glomerular filtration and promote tubular reabsorption. The presence of two FAP-binding motifs and the linker may also increase plasma protein binding or modify charge distribution, contributing to greater retention in renal tissue. In addition, following receptor blockade with the non-radiolabeled ligand, tumor-specific uptake is competitively reduced, increasing the fraction of circulating tracer that undergoes renal excretion and transient kidney accumulation. Importantly, the dimeric design did not increase radioligand uptake in the liver or bowel. This favorable biodistribution profile is particularly advantageous for ⁶⁸Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 PET imaging in cancers of the digestive system, as it leads to improved tumor-to-background contrast.
This study has several limitations. Although FAP enzyme inhibition and cell-binding assays of the unlabeled inhibitors and their Cy5.5-labeled derivatives demonstrated high affinity and specificity for FAP, direct in vitro comparison between [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 at the cellular level was not performed due to an issue of ⁶⁸Ga availability. Nonetheless, in vivo blocking studies with co-injection of nonradioactive hmFAP2 confirmed the target-specific binding of the dimeric radioligand. In initial imaging experiments with [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1, co-implantation of HeLaFAP and MDA-MB-231 cells in the same mouse resulted in disparate tumor growth rates. HeLaFAP tumors became oversized and necrotic, whereas MDA-MB-231 tumors remained small. The inconsistent tumor growths potentially lead to substantial differences in tumor vascularization, perfusion, and metabolism, thereby confounding interpretation of radiotracer uptake between the two distinct tumor types. To minimize these effects, subsequent [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 studies were conducted in mice bearing only MDA-MB-231 xenografts, although possible uptake differences between single and co-implanted models cannot be excluded. Finally, the in vivo evaluations of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP1 and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 were performed in three tumor models with small sample sizes, and PET images were acquired using two different scanners with distinct detector characteristics, resulting in variability in biodistribution visualization. In particular, PET images acquired with the older Siemens Inveon system exhibited spillover artifacts and image distortion due to intense bladder radioactivity.
To enhance clinical translatability, we are developing an ¹⁸F-labeled hmFAP2 radioligand for PET imaging of FAP expression. Future studies will focus on the synthesis and evaluation of both ¹⁸F-labeled hmFAP1 and hmFAP2 analogs to enable comprehensive in vitro and in vivo comparative characterization. These investigations will include full-scale assessments of kinetics, binding affinity, and target specificity across multiple tumor models, including xenograft and spontaneous models that preserve the native tumor microenvironment and FAP expression. Collectively, these efforts aim to advance clinical translation of this novel dimeric FAP-targeted ligand for cancer theranostics.

## Conclusions
We present a newly designed FAP-targeted dimeric ligand, hmFAP2, which demonstrates markedly superior FAP targeting compared to monomeric FAP ligands. The chemical modification strategy applied to FAP-targeted ligand design allows for improved scalability in synthesis and supports potential clinical translation. The capabilities of hmFAP2 were demonstrated through studies in human cell lines, functional inhibition assays of FAP enzymatic activity, and imaging applications for tumor detection in various tumor models with differing levels of FAP expression. [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-DOTA-hmFAP2 exhibits significantly improved radiopharmaceutical kinetics and biodistribution in PET imaging, effectively localizing a range of tumors. This makes it a promising tool for investigating CAF activation and cancer-associated fibrosis, ultimately contributing to the development of more effective cancer radio-theranostics.
