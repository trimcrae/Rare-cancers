## Background
Sarcomas represent a heterogeneous group of mesenchymal tumors that, despite accounting for only 1% of cancers worldwide, rank among the top five causes of cancer-related deaths in patients under 20 years old. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for these malignancies, as effective systemic therapies are limited, particularly for high-grade disease. However, surgical outcomes are often compromised by incomplete resection, leading to high local and distal recurrence rates. Intraoperative molecular imaging has emerged as a promising approach to improve surgical outcomes but has been hindered by the lack of tumor-specific targeting agents. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP), selectively expressed by mesenchymal tumors and absent in healthy tissues, presents a promising target for fluorescence-guided cancer resections.

## Results
We demonstrate that 41% of human sarcomas express FAP, with expression correlating with higher histologic grade. The FAP-S0456 optical tracer specifically bound to FAP-expressing sarcomas with minimal binding to normal tissues, exhibiting excellent tumor-to-background ratios (3.8 ± 0.43). In vitro studies confirmed FAP-S0456’s specificity for human FAP with a dissociation constant of approximately 10 nM. In murine xenograft models, the tracer accurately identified both primary tumors and pulmonary metastases of FAP-expressing sarcomas. Importantly, using needle confocal laser endomicroscopy, we demonstrated that FAP-S0456 enables real-time, single-cell visualization of metastatic tumor cells during surgery, including micrometastases not detected by conventional imaging.

## Conclusions
FAP-S0456 represents a promising molecular imaging agent for the detection and surgical removal of primary and metastatic sarcomas. Its high specificity for FAP-expressing tumor cells, favorable biodistribution profile, and ability to detect microscopic disease offer potential to improve complete surgical resection. These findings support the development of FAP-targeted fluorescence-guided surgery for sarcoma patients, which may lead to improved oncologic outcomes, particularly for those with pulmonary metastases where complete surgical clearance is critical for survival.

## Introduction
Sarcomas are a heterogeneous group of mesenchymal tumors that occur at various anatomic sites, including bone, cartilage, and soft tissues [ ]. Although sarcomas account for only 1% of cancers worldwide, they are a top five cause of cancer-related deaths for those under 20 years old, leading to high productivity loss globally [ ]. Due to their divergence, there is a lack of effective systemic therapies available for the treatment of aggressive and high-grade disease [ , ]. Surgical resection of the primary tumor has been shown to improve overall survival, but 20–50% of patients still develop pulmonary recurrences within 5 years due to incomplete disease clearance during the index surgery, which is a major cause of sarcoma-related morbidity [ , , – ]. Pulmonary metastasectomy has been shown to improve oncologic and survival outcomes in those with optimal primary disease control. However, this is predicated upon complete R0 resection and occult disease clearance, which is often challenging, as these lesions are often missed on high-fidelity cross-sectional screening imaging.
As mesenchymal tumors such as sarcomas undergo cellular growth, matrix adhesion, migration, and invasion, they upregulate fibroblast activation protein (FAP) on their cell surface [ – ]. FAP, also known as seprase, is a cell surface serine integral membrane proteinase that acts on various hormones and extracellular matrix components (Fig.  ). It has been shown to be a marker of tumor aggressiveness, increased metastatic potential, and poor prognosis in mesenchymal tumors [ , , ].

 

This feature of selective FAP expression by mesenchymal tumor cells makes it an attractive molecular target. In fact, Kessler et al., in their prospective observational trial investigating the use of FAP for positron emission tomography, demonstrated high PPV and sensitivity for sarcoma detection [ ]. Additionally, FAP is selectively expressed by primary tumor cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the tumor microenvironment, and chronic fibrotic tissues but not normal lung parenchyma. This allows FAP to be explored for highly specific tumor labeling in various investigative molecular imaging trials. However, most of these clinical investigations have focused on preoperative screening or staging and not during real-time surgical explorations, such as intraoperative molecular imaging (IMI)-guided (also known as fluorescence guided surgery (FGS)) resections.
IMI has become increasingly studied in the surgical management of soft-tissue malignancies [ , ]. The technology allows fluorescent tracers to be conjugated to a ligand (small molecule, antibody, etc.) that is targeted to cancer cells, which then allows microscopic cancer detection. Tracers that emit fluorescence in the near infrared (NIR) spectrum allow for deeper organ penetration and lesion detection than non-NIR fluorophores [ ]. Some of the currently investigated fluorochromes include indocyanine green (ICG), S0456 (OTL38), IR800, and BM-104 (SGM-101) [ ]. Current fluorescent tracers lack specificity due to their non-targeted mechanisms (such as ICG) or their development toward single ligands on tumor surfaces, which limits their utility in diverse clinical contexts. ICG has gained widespread adoption based on its favorable cost and safety profile, but it relies on passive accumulation in tumor tissues through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect mediated by leaky tumor capillaries. This mechanism significantly limits its effectiveness in fibrotic tumors such as sarcomas, where poor vascularization impedes uptake, and rapid washout from tumor surfaces leads to diminished signal intensity and contrast. The development of sarcoma-specific molecular imaging probes is therefore critically needed to enhance surgical precision and improve patient outcomes. The selective overexpression of fibroblast activation protein (FAP) in sarcomatous tissues positions it as an optimal molecular target for the FAP-S0456 conjugate, offering the potential for highly specific tumor visualization during surgical resection [ ].
IMI has been demonstrated to be a favorable adjunct during pulmonary metastasectomies for sarcoma. Compared to standard metastasectomy, patients in the IMI groups tend toward higher disease clearance, better progression-free survival, and an increased rate of systemic adjuvant therapies [ ]. However, the vast majority of trials concluded to date have utilized nontargeted ICG tracers, which can be associated with a high false positivity rate. To overcome these limitations, several translational clinical trials have investigated potential molecular targets for intraoperative molecular imaging-guided sarcoma resections, with particular emphasis on pulmonary metastases. These studies have evaluated probes targeting the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), sarcoma-specific integrins, cathepsin-activated near-infrared probes, and high-dose ICG protocols [ – ]. While these approaches have shown promising results and demonstrated improved surgical outcomes in small patient cohorts, significant gaps remain in our targeting strategies. Despite the selective expression of fibroblast activation protein in sarcomatous tissues, FAP-targeted near-infrared fluorescence imaging remains largely unexplored in this clinical context, representing a compelling opportunity to address current limitations in sarcoma-specific intraoperative guidance.
In this study, we describe the use of a novel fluorescently (S0456)-labeled FAP-targeting ligand for the detection of human FAP-expressing sarcomas for both primary and metastatic disease. First, we validated FAP as a viable target by evaluating its expression in soft tissue sarcomas from a surgical population at our institution. We then evaluated the dosing, timing, and efficacy of FAP-S0456 in vitro and in murine models of FAP-expressing fibrosarcomas. Finally, we developed a xenograft model of pulmonary sarcoma metastasis and evaluated the ability of FAP-S0456 to identify metastatic tumors using novel confocal laser endomicroscopic single-cell evaluation during thoracotomy.

## Results

## Fibroblast activation protein-alpha expression in human primary sarcomas
To validate FAP as a suitable target for image-guided surgery, we explored the pattern and rate of FAP expression in surgically resectable sarcomas from 75 consecutive patients at our institution and compared our results to large national databanks. IHC staining for FAP demonstrated that 31/75 (41%) of patients in our cohort had at least 1 + FAP expression. There was a statistically significant trend of FAP expression in soft-tissue sarcomas compared to osteogenic sarcomas (Fig.  A) (20/31 vs. 11/31, p < 0.05). Details of demographics and histologic parameters are shown in Table  . On multivariate analysis, the FAP expression level was noted to be more likely to be detected in a younger cohort of patients (< 56) and was associated with increasing tumor histopathologic grade (G2-G3 vs. G1) (p < 0.05, Chi-square effect size 0.42, post-hoc power: 85% (alpha = 0.05))) (Table  ).

 

 

We then compared FAP mRNA expression in TCGA, CCLE and CBioPortal databanks to confirm our institutional results in large national datasets. Analysis of TCGA cohorts showed sarcomas with a median FAP mRNA Log2 (value + 1) of 878 (0-4671) levels. There was no statistically significant difference in FAP mRNA expression between sarcomas and other well-documented high FAP-expressing tumors, such as pancreatic adenocarcinoma, breast carcinoma, and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (p = 0.12). Analysis of the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) human cancer cell line database showed sarcoma expression lower than known carcinomas, but this did not reach statistical significance. Subset analysis of the TCGA database showed that FAP expression is associated with inferior progression-free survival with a median survival period of 12.34 months for the sarcoma cohort and is associated with a higher likelihood of recurrence after primary disease control. While TCGA data do not discriminate the location of disease recurrence, historical literature reports have postulated up to a 50% likelihood of distal pulmonary recurrence, particularly in higher grade disease. Metastasectomy with R0 resection in this cohort has been associated with improved survival, and FAP-targeted precision surgery should be explored in this cohort.
In summary, FAP is expressed in aggressive soft-tissue sarcomas with higher grade and has similar expression to other well-established high FAP-expressing solid organ malignancies.

## Spectrophysical parameters of FAP-S0456
Our goal was to assess the spectroscopic properties of FAP-S0456 and compare it to known NIR tracers in the literature. FAP-S0456 is a FAPL linked to the known NIR tracer S0456, which emits and absorbs light at NIR wavelengths. FAP-S0456 in different solvents, including saline, FBS, PBS, and methanol, showed an excitation wavelength of 770 nm and emission of 790 nm (Fig.  A, B) with the highest emission intensity in methanol, which is a known characteristic of S0456 conjugated tracers.
FAP-S0456 fluorescence intensity measured as signal to background ratio (SBR) or mean-fluorescence intensity absorbance units (MFI A. U) were compared to established and clinically tested NIR conjugates (OTL38, ICG, FITC) (Fig.  C). Penetration of NIR light through structures deeper than 1 cm is a known limitation; therefore, capsules of 10 nM tracers were implanted at different depths in 25% agarose gels (S- ). At the surface level, FAP-S0456 had an SBR of 8.4 (6.7–10.1), OTL38 SBR was 11.7 (9.7–13.7), ICG SBR was the highest 15.5 (13-17.5), and FITC had the lowest SBR of 5.1 (3.8–6.4). There was a linear and statistically significant decrease in SBR for all tracers with increasing depth, but FAP-S0456 SBR remained relatively consistent and matched other tracers until 4.5 mm depth (except FITC) (p < 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences in SBR between FAP-S0456, ICG, and OTL38 past 5 mm depth (p = 0.11). FITC fluorescence was not detected past 5 mm depth.
We then explored the concentration-dependent fluorescence of the NIR tracers (Fig.  D, E) and noted similar characteristics between the tracers, with a loglinear increase in MFI with increasing concentration. In the subset analysis (Fig.  E), we noted that the MFI of FAP-S0456 was significantly lower in the 1 nM range (third well) than that of free S0456 and OTL38, but these concentration-dependent fluorescence differences were not apparent at higher concentrations.

 

## In vitro analysis of FAP-S0456 specificity for human FAP-expressing cell lines
We considered the tracer conjugate’s specificity for tumors expressing human fibroblast activation protein by flow cytometry. Multiple cell lines including I45 (mesothelioma), 3T3 (normal fibroblasts) with both mouse and human transfected FAP gene, A549 (NSCLC), HT-1080 wild type and HT-1080 human fap transfected (hFAP), and B6PRG (syngeneic fibrosarcoma) unknown FAP expression). Cells expressing hFAP (HT1080hFAP and 3T3-hFAP) showed significant FAP-S0456 uptake by flow cytometry. HT-1080hFAP cells had the highest MFI (7614) and highest dye binding expression. Cells not expressing hFAP did not have dye binding expression (Fig.  A, S- ). Additionally, we performed FACS analysis directly looking for FAP expression using a FAP antibody, which showed that HT-1080hFAP cells expressed FAP, whereas wild-type cells did not. B6PRG cells, which were unknown for FAP expression, showed low to moderate dye binding expression (MFI 1748) and low to moderate FAP expression on FACS analysis (Fig.  C). HT-1080hFAP cells were selected as a positive control and transfected with green fluorescence protein (GFP) for in vitro confirmatory analysis (Fig.  B). Flow cytometry analysis of GFP and hFAP purity showed that cells expressing GFP also expressed hFAP (S- , S- ). Therefore, HT-1080 wild-type cells were selected as a negative control. B6PRG cells were used as cells of interest.
We then confirmed our flow analysis data for FAP expression with SDS Page western blot analysis for FAP protein expression, which showed concordant strong FAP expression in HT-1080 hFAP cells (MFI 312), moderate FAP expression in B6PRG cells (MFI 187), and no expression in HT-1080 WT cells (MFI 11). (Fig.  D).

 

In summary, flow cytometry analysis of various cell lines shows that FAP-S0456 has strong dye binding expression in hFAP-expressing cells, which is concordant with FAP protein expression in these cell lines.

## FAP-S0456 conjugate tracer is specifically, targeted and internalized by FAP-expressing cells
While flow cytometry analysis demonstrated FAP-specific dye binding expression of FAP-S0456, we explored whether the FAP-S0456 conjugate is specifically targeted to FAP-expressing cells by immunofluorescence microscopy. HT-1080hFAP, B6PRG, and HT-1080 WT cells were incubated with FAP-S0456, which showed that only cells expressing FAP had tracer uptake (Fig.  A). HT-1080hFAP had the highest SBR (6.1 (SD 1.1) vs. B6PRG (4.9 (SD 0.67) vs. HT-1080 WT (1.0 (SD 0.05)). FAP expression correlated with the degree of FAP-S0456 uptake and fluorescence (p < 0.05), observed difference 3.8 ± 0.43 vs. 1.0 (effect size d = 6.7), Post-hoc power analysis (n = 5 per group, α = 0.05): >99%)). To further evaluate tracer specificity, cells with FAP expression that internalized FAP-S0456 underwent competitive inhibition of free FAP ligand at 100 times the tracer dosing, which prevented FAP-S0456 uptake and produced no fluorescence for both cell lines (SBR 1.1 and 1.05, respectively, effect size d = 6.7, power > 99%) (Fig.  B Top Panel).

 

Since FAP and DPP-IV share approximately 70% similarity in sequence and catalytic region with DPP-IV (Fig.  ), we evaluated whether FAP-S0456 specifically targets FAP and does not interact with DPP-IV (Fig.  B bottom panel). HT-1080 hFAP cells were incubated with DPP-IV inhibitor only (sitagliptin) at 100x concentration of FAP-S0456 and did not prevent FAP-S0456 uptake (SBR 6.45 (SD 0.78))(S- ). Similar observations were noted for B6PRG (S- ).
IF analysis of FAP-S0456 tracer uptake showed that the NIR conjugate is highly specific for cells expressing FAP with ~ Kd = 10 nM (Fig.  D). SDS Page IF of HT-1080hFAP and HT-1080 WT cells with FAP-S0456 showed HT-1080hFAP expression of FAP at approximately 100 kDa. Additionally, serial dilution of HT-1080hFAP/GFP cells showed that at decreasing concentrations, only HT-1080 hFAP produced FAP-S0456 fluorescence, as they were identical to cells that had GFP expression on IF (Supplementary Figs.  , ).

## Toxicity, dosing, timing, biodistribution, specificity of FAP-S0456 NIR conjugate in murine xenografts
We sought to determine the ideal dosing and timing of FAP-S0456 in murine xenograft models of sarcoma. To first establish the safety of the tracer, nontumor-bearing mice were injected with escalating doses of FAP-S0456 (Fig.  A) to determine safety and biodistribution. There were no adverse events noted in any of the doses injected, and mouse weights remained stable at all concentrations (20.23 ± 3.22 g (0 nmol) vs. 19.67 ± 2.67 g (20 nmol)(p = 0.122)). Organs from the mice were harvested, and FAP-S0456 fluorescence was measured. In nontumor-bearing mice, the kidneys were the only organs with dye uptake, consistent with known S0456 excretion (SBR 3.87 ± 0.33) (p < 0.05) (Fig.  B). Importantly, the lungs did not show any tracer uptake, making it a suitable target for the detection of FAP-expressing metastatic sarcomas.

 

Next, mice bearing human sarcoma (HT-1080hFAP) flank xenografts (tumor size 250 ± 50 mm3, n = 5 per group) were injected with escalating doses of FAP-S0456, and TBRs were measured. The highest TBRs were noted for doses of 2 and 4 nM at 24–48 h (TBR 3.75 ± 0.45) compared to 20 nM dosing (2.11 ± 0.23). The high-dose group had significantly higher background fluorescence, making it difficult to distinguish the tumor margins from the surrounding musculature (Fig.  C). Histologic IF assessment demonstrated that FAP-S0456 indeed accumulated in the tumor cells (Fig.  E), and there was no fluorescence noted in the surrounding stroma and stromal fibroblasts, as aSMA staining in this setting was negative (S- ).
After determining the optimal dosing and timing of FAP-S0456, we explored whether FAP-S0456 can accurately label FAP-expressing flank tumors. Twenty-four hours after 2 nM injection of FAP-S0456, FAP-expressing tumors (HT-1080hFAP, B6PRG) demonstrated NIR fluorescence with an average TBR of 3.8 ± 0.43, whereas tumors with no FAP expression (HT-1080 WT) had no detectable fluorescence (TBR 1.0). HT-1080hFAP-bearing mice were also injected with a similar concentration of free S0456, which did not produce fluorescence (TBR 1.0) in the tumors but was noted to be excreted in the kidneys (S- ). To evaluate FAP-S0456 specificity toward FAP, competitive inhibition with 100x FAPL was performed, which did not demonstrate any fluorescence (TBR 1.0 (FAPL) vs. TBR 3.8, p < 0.05), except that the tracer was noted to be excreted in the kidneys, consistent with known S0456 pharmacodynamics (S- ).
We then explored whether FAP expression density was associated with FAP-S0456 fluorescence by examining excised xenografts via macroscopic tumor fluorescence imaging, hematoxylin and eosin staining, FAP immunohistochemistry, and fluorescence microscopy (Fig.  ). We then performed a FAP density analysis of the sectioned tumors by analyzing FAP staining at the center and at four locations 90 degrees apart at the periphery per high-powered field (HPF). Not surprisingly, HT-1080hFAP flank tumors displayed the highest FAP density, with a mean density of 94.1% (± 1.1%) vs. B6PRG (61.33%±0.4.22), and minimal to no FAP expression was noted in HT-1080 WT cells (5%±2.5). Interestingly, any presence of FAP expression (> 25%) noted on IHC was associated with the presence of NIR fluorescence (Fig.  , S- ), and the degree of FAP expression was not associated with TBR variation, i.e., 1+ (TBR 3.45 ± 0.31), 2+ (TBR 3.23 ± 0.45), and 3+ (TBR 3.67 ± 0.41) did not have a statistically significant TBR difference among them (p = 0.29).
On histopathologic and IF analysis, FAP-S0456 appeared to be localized to areas of FAP expression, with IF imaging showing tracer uptake in fibrosarcoma cells and not stromal myofibroblasts (Fig.  ), with an average SBR of 3.98 ± 0.49. This was significantly higher than that in HT-1080 WT cells, which did not demonstrate any fluorescence uptake with an SBR of 1 (p < 0.05).

 

## Fluorescent labeling of fap expressing pulmonary sarcomatous metastases by FAP-S0456
Given the high risk of developing pulmonary metastasis in patients with sarcomas and major (> 50%) site distal recurrence, we explored whether FAP-S0456 could detect FAP-expressing pulmonary metastatic lesions in murine models. Mice were injected with 200,000 cells (n = 5 each group) intravenously and followed longitudinally. Once the mouse body weights dropped 1 STD below their normal, they were imaged with respiratory-gated microCT scanning (Figs.  and ). On average, the time to development of symptoms was shortest for HT-1080 hFAP-injected mice (47 days (STD 9)) compared to HT-1080 WT mice (61 days (STD 13)).
Once tumors were identified on imaging, mice were injected with 2 nM FAP-S0456, sacrificed 24 h later, and underwent NIR-guided thoracic exploration. HT-1080hFAP mice with metastasis produced pulmonary nodule fluorescence (In vivo TBR 5.8, ex vivo TBR 8) (Fig.  ). Histopathologic evaluation of fluorescent lesions demonstrated high FAP expression on IHC (3/5 (3 + Staining, 2/5 with 2 + Staining). There was no statistical correlation between FAP staining scores and TBR measurements. Similar observations were noted for B6PRG mice, where all the mice with pulmonary lesions demonstrated FAP-S0456 fluorescence (TBR 5.4 in vivo), with 3/5 mice having 2 + FAP staining (2/5 3+). The intensity of FAP staining did not correlate with TBR variation and fluorescence emission intensity as long as there was FAP expression (i.e., no significant difference between 1+,2+,3 + FAP IHC scoring) (p = 0.9). Further IF analysis demonstrated localization of FAP-S0456 to neoplastic cells in hFAP + tumors but no detection in hFAP- HT-1080 WT cells (Fig.  , S- ). HT-1080 WT mice with pulmonary metastases and FAP-S0456 did not demonstrate any NIR fluorescence (TBR 1.1), with histologic analysis showing no FAP staining in any of the samples.

 

To further investigate whether FAP-S0456 can label hFAP + tumor cells on a single-cell basis, we performed nCLE NIR-guided optical biopsy of pulmonary lesions during thoracotomy (Fig.  ) (Video ). A biopsy needle equipped with the nCLE probe described in the methods was inserted into the area of the tumor. hFAP + cells demonstrated FAP-S0456 labeling in the tumor core but not the surrounding microenvironment. HT-1080 WT cells did not demonstrate any FAP-specific labeling. This novel technology demonstrates proof of principle real-time intraoperative assessment of FAP + tumors during pulmonary resection in vivo.
In summary, FAP-S0456 accurately fluorescently labels FAP-expressing metastatic sarcomas and can aid in the resection of hFAP + metastases in vivo.

 

## Discussion
Targeted intraoperative molecular imaging (IMI) has the potential to address the challenges posed in the surgical management of sarcomas [ ]. IMI success is closely tied to the targeting of tumor-specific proteins, and in this study, we explored fibroblast activation protein, which is expressed in a number of mesenchymal malignancies, as a potential target. Our goal in this study was to evaluate the specificity of FAP-S0456 in identifying sarcomas in a preclinical setting. We observed that FAP-S0456 specifically binds to and produces fluorescence in FAP-expressing fibrosarcomas in in vitro and in vivo models. Furthermore, FAP-S0456 identified pulmonary sarcomatous FAP-expressing metastatic lesions. This study represents one of the few results in the literature of FAP-S0456 use in IMI-guided sarcoma resection.
Wide expression of FAP in sarcoma tumor cells exclusive to cancer-associated fibroblasts has been documented in the literature [ – , , ]. Although there is controversy, limited results suggest worse outcomes in tumors with FAP expression secondary to upregulation of integrin-mediated signaling pathways [ , ]. Therefore, it is not surprising that FAP expression has been found to be elevated in high-grade sarcomas in previous studies [ , , , ]. Furthermore, another unique characteristic of FAP is that it is not expressed in normal cells and tissues [ ]. This makes it an attractive target for cancer-specific imaging studies compared to other NIR conjugates. For example, ICG targets leaky capillaries of tumors that are nonspecific, and OTL38 targets folate receptors on adenocarcinomas but can label tumor-associated macrophages that exhibit a similar receptor profile [ ]. For this reason, there are currently multiple trials both in-vivo and early clinical trials, attempting to find NIR probes that are specific to sarcomas that are designed to help in primary tumor resection as well as margin assessment. The probes in question focus on commonly expressed proteins such as VEGF, EGFR, and integrins which are found on majority of aggressive sarcomas but not on all [ , , – , ]. FAP has a distinctive advantage in being present in the tumor microenvironment in tumor associated fibroblasts as well as being expressed in aggressive sarcomas. Ultimately, due to heterogeneity of the tumors, combination approach might be utilized to improve sensitivity and specificity.
In our histologic analysis of 75 consecutive sarcoma patients, 31/75 (41%) had some type of FAP expression, and there was a trend toward increased expression with higher histologic grade, particularly in soft-tissue sarcomas (Fig.  A). Similarly, when analyzing large national databases for FAP expression, we identified a considerable number of malignancies that express FAP, and sarcoma has been consistently one of the top malignancies in this realm (Fig.  ). These results are consistent with recent literature results exploring FAP as a viable radiopharmaceutical tracer for cross-sectional imaging [ , , ]. However, when comparing our findings to other clinical studies performed by Crane and Beer et al., FAP expression appears lower (Crane 75%, Beer 85%). There are several potential explanations for these findings which include detection methodology and scoring criteria which varies compared to other institutions. Additionally, we found differential FAP expression based on tumor subtype heterogeneity (higher in G2 and G2 disease), higher expression in younger patients but results were comparable to TCGA and CCLE national databases. Ultimately, our results will need further validation in clinical trials, including multi-center clinical trials to further corroborate FAP guided IMI resections.
IMI as a technology has garnered interest across the surgical oncology community over the last two decades [ , ]. This has led to the development of various optical tracers, each with unique receptor targeting and fluorescence parameters. The results from our research collaborative in this area have demonstrated that tracers that emit light at higher wavelengths also penetrate deeper into parenchyma and produce higher tumor fluorescence intensity along with decreased normal background autofluorescence. Therefore, the current focus on tracer development has been on those that function in the NIR range (> 700 nm), i.e., ICG, S0456, BM-104, and IR800, and have been shown to be more efficacious than those in the visible and far-red wavelengths (400–699 nm) (i.e., FITC) [ ]. Our spectroscopic analysis demonstrated that FAP-S0456 produces emission at 790 nm across various solvent types and performs similarly to other established NIR tracers (Fig.  ). The tracer conjugate additionally had a higher SBR than FITC (8.4 vs. 5.1, p < 0.05) (Fig.  ), consistent with literature reports and clinical experience [ ]. Therefore, FAP-S0456 appears to have similar spectroscopic characteristics to those of other NIR tracers that have been FDA approved (ICG) and are currently undergoing advanced phase III clinical trials (Pafolacianine).
FAP-specific targeting and fluorescent labeling by FAP-S0456 is critical. Low et al. have previously demonstrated that our FAPL specifically binds human fibroblasts in an idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis model, but similar detailed results in sarcomas have not been performed to date [ ]. In vitro analysis additionally demonstrated that FAP-S0456 was highly specific to its target receptors with an ~ Kd of 10 nM. Competitive inhibition in FAP-expressing cell lines did not produce any fluorescence (Fig.  , S- ). Similar observations were noted on SDS–PAGE analysis of probe-labeled species (Fig.  E).
Since FAP shares over 50–70% similarity in the sequence and catalytic region with DPP-IV, most FAP targeting ligands also bind DPP-IV [ , ]. This is important, as there is ubiquitous expression of DPP-IV in normal human tissues, and potential nonspecific binding of FAP-S0456 in this setting will render the tracer ineffective in IMI-guided sarcoma resections, leading to potentially false positive results. Therefore, it was important to establish the specificity of FAP-S0456 for FAP over DPP-IV prior to developing preclinical models. In vitro blocking of DPP-IV in FAP-positive cell lines with sitagliptin (10x-1000x) did not change the binding and fluorescence parameters of FAP-S0456 (SBR 6.45 with DPP-IV vs. SBR 6.1 without DPP-IV, p = 0.39) (Fig.  B, S- , S- ). These results demonstrate FAP-specific targeting of FAP-S0456 in vitro, making it a suitable target for further preclinical studies.
While multiple recent studies have explored the potential role of FAP as a radiotracer for its potential use in imaging-based surveillance, its role in IMI-guided cancer resections has not been explored. Our data indicate that FAP-S0456 has an excellent biodistribution profile, with only kidneys producing fluorescence secondary to known renal clearance of the probe (Fig.  ). This is consistent with the fact that FAP is only expressed in malignant processes and not normal tissues [ , , ]. This makes FAP an attractive target compared to small molecule vitamin-based targeting, as these molecule receptors/transporters are widely expressed in the gastrointestinal tract, leading to false fluorescence detection and background autofluorescence. Additionally, 2 nM (2 mg/kg) dosing produced high tumor fluorescence (TBR 3.75 ± 0.45) at 24 h, which is a significantly lower dose compared to antibody-based tracer conjugates studied in the literature (Fig.  ).
In murine models of primary and metastatic sarcoma, FAP-S0456 identified FAP-expressing tumors with high specificity. The average TBR for FAP-expressing tumors was 3.8 ± 0.43 compared to no fluorescence detection in HT-1080 WT tumors (p < 0.05) (Figs.  and ). Histopathologic analysis demonstrated that FAP-S0456 fluorescence was observed only in tumors with FAP expression (Figs.  and ). Additionally, given that FAP is also expressed by CAFs in the tumor stroma, our histologic analysis showed FAP staining in tumor cells only (fibroblasts vs. stromal fibroblasts) and the absence of aSMA (marker of CAFs) staining in these tissues (S- ).
FAP-S0456 accurately identified macro- and micrometastatic disease with histologic analysis, confirming localization of the tracer to the malignant cells (Figs.  and ). No observable fluorescence was noted in HT-1080 WT cells in either xenograft model. These findings were shared at both the macrofluorescence and single-cell nCLE detection levels. FAP-S0456 specifically labeled hFAP + metastatic tumors, which allowed for real-time lesion localization during thoracotomy. FAP-S0456 detected all the preoperative CT lesions in addition to occult lesions not detected using cross-sectional imaging. Single-cell FAP-labeled fluorescence confirmed FAP-specific fluorescence during resections (Fig.  , Video 1). Given the high incidence of distal pulmonary recurrence, particularly in high-grade disease, FAP-S0456 identification of pulmonary metastases in preclinical models is encouraging [ , ]. As surgical disease clearance in patients with sarcomatous pulmonary metastases has shown improved outcomes, FAP-S0456 can be an important adjunct in IMI-guided exploration for these patients [ – ].
There are several limitations to this study. Despite the promise of FAP-S0456 for sarcoma labeling, murine models and cell culture analysis only crudely simulate sarcoma evolution in humans. Additionally, although not expressed in normal human tissues, FAP expression is well documented in organs with fibrotic disease secondary to collagen deposition by myofibroblasts and tissues undergoing remodeling after injury [ , , ]. This can lead to false fluorescence observation and tumor detection. Therefore, the efficacy of FAP-S0456 will need to be further investigated and validated in small and large animal models prior to advancing it to clinical settings. We anticipate future work to further clarify these issues, which will help develop FAP-targeted approaches for IMI-guided sarcoma resection of pulmonary metastasis and potentially FAP-targeted immunochemotherapy delivery.

## Conclusion
In summary, our study demonstrated that FAP-S0456 binds FAP-expressing sarcomas both in vitro and in xenograft models. Additionally, FAP-S0456 can identify macroscopic and microscopic pulmonary metastatic disease. Our analysis demonstrates the high affinity and specificity of FAP-S0456 for FAP-expressing tumors, tumor-specific biodistribution in normal tissues, and excellent safety profile, making FAP-S0456 a promising imaging agent for IMI-guided cancer resections. Further preclinical studies are needed to confirm its safety and efficacy, but the results indicate clear clinical promise of FAP-S0456 in the management of patients with primary and metastatic sarcomas.

## Materials and methods
All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. No samples or animals were excluded from data analysis.

## Assessing FAP expression in human sarcoma tissues

## Immunohistochemistry
Under a University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board-approved protocol, histologic specimens from 75 consecutive sarcoma patients were obtained from the Hospital University of Pennsylvania’s Biobank. Samples were prepared and immunostained for FAP using anti-FAP antibody (Abcam, ab53066) [ , ]. Once stained, a certified pathologist manually scored the specimens using an established scoring system ranging from 0 to 3+. The tumor was considered positive when more than 10% of malignant cells were positively stained. Overexpression of FAP was defined as a score of 2 + or 3+, and moderate expression of FAP was defined as a score of 1+.

## TCGA and cBioPortal FAP expression primary tumor-derived data set analysis
Messenger RNA (mRNA) expression data of FAP in human cell lines generated by RNA-seq analysis were downloaded from the GDC data portal (TCGA), CCLE, and cBioPortal on 9/1/2021 and sorted by primary tumor “type” [ ]. Samples with no data or no FAP expression were excluded. FAP expression was then compared to normal human tissues using the cBioPortal Integrative Analysis Platform.

## Study drug, qualitative and quantitative spectroscopic analysis

## Fluorescent conjugate
FAP-S0456 (Chemical Formula: C75H85F2N10Na3O22S5, MW: 174.81911) is a high-affinity FAP targeting ligand linked to the NIR fluorophore S0456 (Fig.  A) (excitation 770 nm, emission 790 nm). FAP-S0456 (≥ 96% purity) was obtained via collaboration with Philp Low, PhD (Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN) and On Target Laboratories (West Lafayette, IN). FAP-S0456 was synthesized and manufactured in compliance with Good Manufacturing Practices and was stored at -20 °C in vials containing 6 mg FAP-S0456 in 3 mL water. Before utilization, the frozen vials were thawed, vortexed, and then diluted with 0.9% NaCl, dPBS or culture media. FAP-S0456 optical properties were analyzed after dilution in various solvents using a UV visible/NIR spectrophotometer with a single monochromator and dual detector for the wavelength range from 190 to 2700 nm (Jasco, Oklahoma City, OK). Serial dilutions of FAP-S0456 were performed, and the fluorescence intensity was then compared to ICG, FITC, OTL38, and free S0456 at similar dilutions.

## In vitro studies

## Cell lines
The B6PRG syngeneic fibrosarcoma cell line was a generous gift from Dr. Malay Haldar at University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine and was used for experimentation as previously described. The fibrosarcoma cell line HT-1080 and FAP cDNA-transfected HT-1080 cell lines were a generous gift from Dr. Steven Albelda at the University of Pennsylvania. 3T3 fibroblasts were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). Cell lines were cultured in DMEM with 10% heat inactivated FBS (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg.ml streptomycin (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Cells were passaged and harvested using 0.05% trypsin/EDTA solution (GIBCO). All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate on separate days to ensure reproducibility. All cells were confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma contamination as assessed by the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza).

## Live cell imaging, FAP-S0456 cell uptake
Cell lines were cultured on poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips in 6-well plates with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, L-glutamine, and penicillin/streptomycin for 24 h. For internalization time course studies, cells were treated with 10 µM FAP-S0456 for 2 h. Coverslips were removed from the culture after varying intervals (5 min, 10 min, 30 min, 60 min, 120 min, 180 min). To examine dose dependence, cells were incubated with different concentrations of FAP-S0456 (1 µM, 5 µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM) for 2 h. To determine FAP-S0456 specific binding to FAP-expressing cells, HT0180hFAP cells were transfected with green fluorescence protein. Cells were then analyzed for GFP and FAP fluorescence at differing concentrations. The cells were mounted on glass slides with ProLong Gold antifade reagent with DAPI (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and imaged on a Leica DM6 B fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

## Cellular uptake studies
To assess the specific uptake of FAP-S0456, cells were preblocked with 100x the concentration of FAP-S0456 and the DPP-IV-specific inhibitor sitagliptin (Fischer Scientific, 654671-77-9). The cells were mounted on glass slides with ProLong Gold antifade reagent with DAPI (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and imaged on a Leica DM6 B fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). All cellular uptake and binding studies were performed in triplicate and repeated three times.

## Western blot analysis for cell-line FAP expression
Cells were harvested and lysed for Western blot analysis. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and blocking were performed. Membranes were incubated with antibodies to detect FAP expression (Abcam, ab2077178) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Signals were visualized with an Amersham Imager 680 (GE Life Sciences).

## Flow cytometry analysis of cell lines for FAP expression and FAP-S0456 binding
Tumor cell lines were plated in a 24-well plate and incubated with 60 nM for 4 h. Cells were washed 4 times with DPBS before being lifted with trypsin. Cells were then run immediately on the BD LSRII. The NRI-FAP dye was excited with a red laser 633, and emission was detected in the APC/CY7 range of 750–810 nm. Samples were analyzed using FlowJo.

## Murine studies
Animal studies were performed according to protocols approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines. Female 6- to 8-week-old ATHYMIC NUDE mice were purchased from Charles River and maintained on normal rodent chow. Mice were housed in a sterile environment on a standard 12-hour light and dark cycle for the duration of the study.

## Pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies
Athymic nude mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the flank with 1 million FAP-positive HT-1080hFAP, B6PRG, and FAP-negative HT-1080 WT cells. Mice were monitored for tumor size and used for imaging and biodistribution when the size of the tumors reached at least 250 ± 50 mm3. Tumor volume was periodically calculated using the formula (3.14 x long-axis x short-axis2).
Once the tumors reached the appropriate minimum volume threshold, HT-1080hFAP xenograft-bearing mice were injected with FAP-S0456 at different concentrations (0 nM-20 nM) (n = 5 per dosing level). NIRF images were obtained on the LI-COR Pearl Impulse Imager using an excitation wavelength of 785 nm and a detection wavelength of 800 nm. Mice were sacrificed at various time points, and whole-body images were obtained. Following this, organs of interest were harvested and imaged ex vivo on a Petri dish. Data were displayed and analyzed using LI-COR Pearl Impulse Software, and the fluorescence intensity was corrected for the background signal and normalized to the surrounding tissue.

## Evaluation of in vivo sarcoma labeling by FAP-S0456 in murine models
After determining the optimized dosing and timing parameters, female athymic nude mice bearing flank xenografts (n = 5 per cell line) were intravenously administered FAP-S0456 at a dose of 2 mg/kg. Twenty-four hours after FAP-S0456 injection, mice were euthanized and imaged with the Iridium Imaging System (Vision Sense, New York, NY). Tumors were removed and imaged with the Odyssey Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE). Tissue sections were further analyzed by hematoxylin and eosin staining, immunohistochemistry staining for FAP and fluorescence microscopy (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

## Pulmonary metastasis model
Athymic nude mice were injected with 200,000 tumor cells via tail vein injection. Mouse weights were measured twice weekly. Once the mouse weights decreased one standard deviation below normal, the mice were imaged with cross-sectional imaging at a small animal imaging facility and subsequently euthanized. Organs were then harvested and imaged with an Iridium Imaging System (Vision Sense, New York, NY). Tumors were removed and imaged with the Odyssey Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE). Tissue sections were further analyzed by hematoxylin and eosin staining, immunohistochemistry staining for FAP, and fluorescence microscopy (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

## NIR needle confocal laser endomicroscopic intratumoral cytologic evaluation
The needle probe used in the experiments contains 10,000 optical fiber bundles with an imaging depth of 55 μm, a lateral resolution of 3.5 μm and a field of view of 325 μm.
The probe was used in conjunction with a commercially available CLE system (CellVizio, Mauna Kea Technologies, Paris, France) that generates an excitation light with a 785 nm laser that is expanded by a beam expander and reflected by a dichroic mirror. Subsequently, a two-dimensional scanning system scans the beam in the two-dimensional plane. After scanning, the beam is relayed into the back aperture of the objective lens, which couples the excitation light into the proximal end of the optical fiber bundle in the NIR-nCLE probe. Finally, the beam is focused on the sample by a near-infrared miniature objective (at the imaging depth), which also collects the emitted fluorescence from the sample. The fluorescence at longer wavelengths is transmitted along the same path and passed through the dichroic mirror and a bandpass filter (800 to 890 nm). After passing the bandpass filter, the fluorescence is focused by a lens to pass through a pinhole that is placed at the focus of the condenser. The pinhole is used to reject the out-of-focus light. Finally, a photodiode is used to acquire the optical signals and convert them into electrical signals. With the X-Y scanners, the imaging speed can reach 8 to 12 frames per second, which makes imaging in real time possible. Each frame consists of 320 × 322 pixels. The probe is inserted into the xenograft, which detects fluorescence emission targeted to the S0456 fluorochrome wavelength.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± SEM or ± SD unless noted otherwise. Statistical differences were assessed using t tests with GraphPad Prism software and SPSS version 27 (IBM Technologies). One-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey’s test was used to analyze differences between groups. Fluorescence parameter analysis was performed by ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) and MATLAB image segmentation analysis (MathWorks). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
