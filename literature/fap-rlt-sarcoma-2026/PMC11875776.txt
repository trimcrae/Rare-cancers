## ABSTRACT
Cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key components of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Given their various roles in tumor progression and treatment resistance, CAFs are promising therapeutic targets in cancer. The elimination of tumor‐promoting CAFs has been investigated in various animal models to determine whether it effectively suppresses tumor growth. Based on recent evidence, several simple strategies have been proposed to eliminate tumor‐promoting CAFs and attenuate these features. In addition, attention has focused on the critical role that CAFs play in the immunosuppressive TME. Therefore, the functional reprogramming of CAFs in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors has also been investigated as a possible therapeutic approach. However, although potential targets in CAFs have been widely characterized, the plasticity and heterogeneity of CAFs complicate the understanding of their properties and present difficulties for clinical application. Moreover, the identification of tumor‐suppressive CAFs highlights the necessity for the development of therapeutic approaches that can distinguish and switch between tumor‐promoting and tumor‐suppressive CAFs in an appropriate manner. In this review, we introduce the origins and diversity of CAFs, their role in cancer, and current therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting CAFs, including ongoing clinical evaluations.
Keywords: cancer‐associated fibroblasts, FAP, immunotherapy, reprogramming, tumor microenvironment

## Introduction
Tumors are sustained by the tumor microenvironment (TME), which consists of a complex community of both cancerous and noncancerous cells. The formation of the TME involves a diverse array of cells, including cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), vascular endothelial cells, immune cells, and other normal epithelial/nonepithelial cells [ ]. Over the past decade, research on CAFs has made significant advancements, elucidating their critical role in cancer progression through the production and remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM), the secretion of various inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, and the promotion of immune evasion within tumors. On the basis of these findings, CAFs have been proposed as key tumor‐promoting players and promising therapeutic targets for cancer [ ]. Moreover, the advent of single‐cell sequencing technology in cancer research has enabled further classification of CAFs, including the existence of tumor‐suppressive CAFs [ , ]. Therefore, cancer treatment strategies targeting CAFs should be conducted on the basis of a deeper understanding of the functional heterogeneity of CAFs. This review aims to summarize the origins and heterogeneity of CAFs and their biological functions and discuss recent advancements that have led to new therapeutic strategies.

2.

## Origin and Cellular Diversity of CAFs

CAFs with diverse characteristics are intermingled in the TME, suggesting that CAFs originate from a variety of precursors. The most common source of CAFs has been suggested to be resident fibroblasts, which are already present in the tissues where tumors develop. For example, stellate cells in the pancreas and liver also differentiate into CAFs, promoting tumor progression. Other possible origins include mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), epithelial cells that undergo epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), endothelial cells that undergo endothelial–mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), pericytes, and adipocytes [ ]. In fact, the exact origin of CAFs is still poorly understood, largely because of the predominance of in vitro experiments or prediction from their gene signature. Recently, via lineage tracing in mice, putative colorectal mesenchymal stem progenitor cells, which are present in the pericrypt space and marked by the leptin receptor (LepR), were reported to be major sources of protumorigenic CAFs with melanoma cell adhesion molecules (MCAMs) and α‐SMAs [ ]. Another study using an in vivo lineage tracing technique demonstrated that pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) give rise to a small subset of CAFs in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [ ]. This finding suggests that other predominant origins of CAFs exist in addition to PSCs in PDAC. Further investigations utilizing in vivo lineage tracing models will be needed to obtain more definitive insights into the origins of CAFs in various organs.
With extensive research in various cancers over the years and advances in single‐cell analysis technology, CAFs have been broadly classified into three subtypes [ ]: myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), which exhibit high expression of α‐smooth muscle actin (αSMA) and are enriched in ECM genes; inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), which are characterized by low αSMA expression and high cytokine and chemokine expression; and antigen‐presenting CAFs (apCAFs), which were recently demonstrated to be derived from mesothelial cells via in vivo lineage tracing in mice [ ]. Although attempts to distinguish between normal fibroblasts, CAFs, and their subtypes have often relied on protein markers that have a characteristic expression pattern, the overlapping marker expression between CAFs and other types of normal cells often causes obstacles in the process of CAF isolation. For example, while αSMA and podoplanin (PDPN) are widely accepted CAF markers, αSMA is also expressed in the smooth muscle of organs such as blood vessels, the gastrointestinal tract, and bronchi; PDPN is also found in lymphatic endothelial cells. Moreover, atypical subpopulations of CAFs, such as ECM‐producing CAFs with low αSMA expression [ , ] and CXCL12‐secreting CAFs with high αSMA expression [ ], have been identified. Taken together, careful consideration is required when selecting markers for CAF isolation or in vivo depletion experiments.

## The Role of CAFs in Cancer
CAFs are known to be induced and maintained through crosstalk with cancer cells, including through transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) signaling in the TME [ , ]. According to transcriptome analysis results for CAFs and noncancer fibroblasts (NFs), TGF‐β1 functions as a key regulator of the upregulation of CAF‐related gene expression, leading to increased motility in the ECM [ ]. Platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF) ligands are another important factor that activates tumor‐promoting CAFs. PDGFR is widely expressed in CAFs, and its downstream signals are activated; thus, pharmacological blockade of this pathway could inhibit tumor progression [ ]. In addition, we demonstrated that PDGF receptor beta (PDGFRβ) is predominantly expressed in diffuse‐type gastric cancer stroma and that these CAFs play a role in forming an immunosuppressive environment [ ]. Among interleukins, IL‐1 induces iCAFs through the activation of JAK/STAT signaling, whereas TGF‐β promotes differentiation into myCAFs, suggesting that a distinct fibroblast niche is associated with CAF heterogeneity [ ]. Thus, CAFs arise from normal cells by functional changes via various ligands from malignant cells and by epigenetic changes, while genetic mutations in CAFs are rare [ ].
Once activated, CAFs contribute to tumor progression through a variety of mechanisms [ ] (Figure  ). (1) CAFs promote cancer cell proliferation. They secrete growth factors such as epidermal growth factor (EGF), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and insulin‐like growth factor (IGF), accelerating cancer cell proliferation. These growth factors activate signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, in tumor cells. (2) CAFs modulate the ECM. They are involved in remodeling the ECM, which provides a favorable environment for cancer cells, including tumor growth via CAF‐derived ECM, such as collagens. The physical barrier created by the ECM derived from CAFs has been shown to adversely affect the motility of immune cells, leading to attenuated immune surveillance of tumors [ ]. (3) CAFs promote cancer progression through angiogenesis. They secrete VEGF‐A via activation of tumor‐driven Hedgehog (Hh) signaling, which promote angiogenesis [ ]. (4) CAFs enhance distant metastasis. They product some molecules that activate WNT signaling, such as periostin and tenascin C, which maintain cancer stemness and promote metastasis formation [ , , ]. (5) CAFs participate in immune regulation. In particular, iCAFs can suppress the antitumor immune response, contributing to immune evasion. Accumulating evidence has shown that CAFs release cytokines and chemokines, such as TGF‐β, CXCL12, and IL‐6, which recruit immunosuppressive cells such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) to tumor sites. These cells inhibit the function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells, which are critical components of the antitumor immune response [ , ]. Through these actions, such as the physical blockade of immune cell invasion into the ECM and specific chemokine secretion, CAFs play critical roles in forming an immunosuppressive microenvironment. These findings suggest that CAFs are attractive targets for enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. However, the complexity of cancer immunity is expanding, and other perspectives in addition to CAFs are discussed more comprehensively from various points of view [ , ].
 While CAFs are often associated with tumor‐promoting activities, recent studies have revealed the presence of tumor‐suppressive CAFs, which highlights the heterogeneity of the CAF population (Figure  ). For example, the existence of CAFs marked by Meflin, a glycosylphosphatidylinositol‐anchored protein, is correlated with favorable outcomes in PDAC patients. Consistent with these findings, Meflin‐deficient mice developed remarkable tumor progression with poorly differentiated histology in a PDAC mouse model [ ]. Several studies have shown that the activation of the Hh signaling pathway in CAFs reduces tumor progression via the restriction of the stem cell signature through the downregulation of bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling [ ] and the inhibition of tumor angiogenesis [ ]. Other compelling studies demonstrated that the depletion of αSMA+ CAFs exacerbated PDAC in Ptf1a‐Cre;LSL‐Kras

Although CAFs play dual roles that can either promote or suppress cancer progression, the underlying mechanism by which these different CAF subsets are switched remains largely unclear. Understanding the heterogeneity of CAFs with these dual faces will be essential for developing strategies to modulate their activity and improve cancer treatment outcomes.

## New Subset of Targetable CAFs

New subpopulations of CAFs have been identified via single‐cell analyses, and their therapeutic targetability is attracting attention. LRRC15+ CAFs programmed by TGF‐β signaling have been identified in human PDAC. The existence of novel LRRC15+ CAFs was significantly correlated with poor outcomes in anti‐PD‐L1 therapy in clinical trials using immunotherapy [ ]. The depletion of LRRC15+ CAFs in LRRC15‐diphtheria toxin receptor (DTR) mice relieved the suppression of tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells, enhancing the effectiveness of anti‐PD‐L1 therapy [ ]. Moreover, combined MEK and STAT3 inhibition attenuated the LRRC15+ CAF phenotype, leading to the reprogramming of the immune microenvironment and thus the potential for anti‐PD‐1 therapy in mouse and human PDAC [ ]. The presence of these immunosuppressive LRRC15+ CAFs has been reported in not only PDAC but also breast cancer [ ], non‐small cell lung cancer [ ], head and neck cancer, and other cancers [ ], indicating potential therapeutic targets across organs. Notably, studies characterizing the heterogeneity of fibroblasts in healthy and various tumors have shown that LRRC15+ CAFs are present in tumor regions and appear to represent a perturbation‐specific, activated fibroblast state, and a terminal differentiated state of myofibroblast differentiation [ , ]. Since LRRC15+ CAFs are linked to a functionally immune‐excluded microenvironment, strategies to eliminate LRRC15+ CAFs or reprogram them to normal fibroblasts could represent a new approach for enhancing cancer immunotherapy.
The CD10+/GPR77+ CAF subset promotes cancer stemness and chemoresistance by providing a survival niche via the secretion of IL‐6 and IL‐8 in human breast and lung cancer. Since GPR77 plays a central role in maintaining these CAFs through the activation of persistent NF‐κB, targeting CD10+/GPR77+ CAFs with a neutralizing anti‐GPR77 antibody abrogates tumor growth and restores chemosensitivity [ ]. In bladder cancer, SLC14A1+ CAFs confer stemness to cancer cells via the paracrine activity of WNT5A. Moreover, the presence of these SLC14A1+ CAFs is significantly associated with poor prognosis and a worse response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy immunotherapy in patients. Because IFNs are crucial for the induction of SLC14A1+ CAFs, the inhibition of STING, which induces IFN secretion, reduces the population of SLC14A1+ CAFs, leading to the suppression of tumor growth [ ].
The identification of novel subsets of targetable CAFs contributes to the development of more precise CAF‐targeted therapies. It is essential to carefully consider whether these newly identified subsets are completely independent of or overlapping with the already known CAF populations and whether these CAFs can be distinguished from fibroblasts present in normal tissue. As the targeted properties of these CAFs are being demonstrated in cancer mouse models, further investigations to verify their functional significance and transition in status over the course of treatment are needed for their translation to clinical success in patients with cancer.

5.

## Therapeutic Strategies Targeting CAFs

As stated above, CAFs secrete numerous protumorigenic factors, such as tumor growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines, creating a favorable TME for cancer cells. Owing to their nature, various approaches have been explored for the development of anticancer therapies that target CAFs. Treatments targeting CAFs can be classified into several categories according to strategy (Figure  ).
 5.1.

## Targeting ECM Components
The ECM is a three‐dimensional structural network that fills the space between cells; it consists of proteins/glycoproteins such as collagen, elastin, and laminin, as well as proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans, hyaluronan, and integrins [ ]. The ECM plays a critical role in both cell survival signaling and physical immune evasion during cancer progression. The latter process is mediated by CAF‐driven ECM remodeling, particularly through collagen secretion and polymerization [ ]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting the ECM have emerged as promising approaches for enhancing immune responses within tumors. Currently, clinical trials are underway using compounds such as losartan and minnelide, which serve to inhibit the production and polymerization of collagen and hyaluronic acid [ , ] (for further details, please refer to Section  ).

5.2.

## Depletion of Tumor‐Promoting FAP+ CAFs

One of the leading attempts to target CAFs has focused on fibroblast activation protein (FAP) because of its selective expression in CAFs across various cancers [ ]. Several studies have demonstrated that FAP+ CAFs are deeply involved in attenuating antitumor immunity. The depletion of FAP+ CAFs in FAP‐DTR mice rapidly induces TNF‐α‐ and IFN‐γ‐mediated hypoxic necrosis in murine lung and pancreatic cancers, highlighting the immunosuppressive role of FAP+ CAFs [ ]. Similarly, in mouse PDAC, the depletion of FAP+ CAFs resulted in prolonged survival through immune‐mediated tumor regression [ ].
A noteworthy approach for the depletion of FAP+ CAFs is chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)‐T cell‐based therapy. The depletion of FAP+ CAFs by FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells suppresses MDSC recruitment and increases the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells, thereby reversing immune exclusion in mouse PDAC. Additionally, the combination of FAP‐CAR‐T cells and anti‐PD‐L1 therapy facilitates the further recruitment of CD8+ T cells, leading to reduced tumor growth and prolonged survival. Moreover, the removal of CAFs promotes the physical infiltration of CAR‐T cells, improving the efficacy of CAR‐T cell therapy, which targets cancer cells marked by claudin18.2 and mesothelin [ , ]. In a murine model of multiple myeloma, dual‐targeted CAR‐T cells against FAP+ CAFs and B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA)+ multiple myeloma cells significantly extended survival by enhancing tumor immunity through CD3+ T cell infiltration into tumors [ ]. Another study revealed that coexpressing chemokines such as IL‐7, CCL29, and IL‐12 in Nectin4‐targeted CAR‐T cells, which are directed against cancer cells, increases the persistence of CAR‐T cells. Additionally, the combination of FAP‐targeted CAR‐T cells and the aforementioned Nectin4‐targeted CAR‐T cells results in the eradication of lung metastasis and prolongs survival [ ].
Other strategies utilizing bispecific antibodies that recognize FAP and immune cell activators, such as CD40 or IL‐2, have also been shown to inhibit tumor growth by increasing the number of tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells and DCs in colorectal cancer and PDAC mouse models [ , ]. Notably, the development of near‐infrared photoimmunotherapy and radioligand therapy targeting FAP has also been the subject of considerable interest. Near‐infrared photoimmunotherapy using IR700‐conjugated anti‐FAP antibody‐depleted CAFs in combination with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy has been shown to reduce tumor growth by increasing the number of tumor‐infiltrated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in a mouse colorectal cancer model [ ]. 177Lu‐radiolabeled anti‐FAP antibodies induce DNA strand breaks in FAP+ CAFs and its neighboring tumor cells, leading to the suppression of tumor growth [ , ].
The depletion of FAP+ CAFs has been shown to reverse the immunosuppressive characteristics of the TME, thereby amplifying antitumor immune responses. These findings indicate that combining FAP+ CAF depletion strategies with immune checkpoint inhibitors could offer synergistic therapeutic benefits. Given the encouraging results from preclinical studies, which have shown promising outcomes, the results of clinical trials evaluating the effectiveness of these combined approaches are eagerly awaited.

5.3.

## Functional Reprogramming of CAFs

The following strategies have been reported to convert tumor‐promoting CAFs to quiescent cells and change the TME to a cancer‐suppressive state: all‐trans retinoic acid (ATRA) [ , , ] and calcipotriol (a vitamin D3 analog) [ , ]. As mentioned above, one of the key roles of tumor‐promoting CAFs is to induce immunosuppression in the TME. Therefore, the functional reprogramming of CAFs in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors has recently been investigated as a potential therapeutic approach. Several studies have reported that the blockade of TGF‐β signaling in CAFs facilitates intratumoral T cell infiltration, which enhances tumor regression in combination with the use of anti‐PD‐L1 antibody immunotherapies [ , ]. We recently reported that the blockade of PDGF signaling in CAFs strongly inhibits CAF proliferation and cytokine production, which stimulates polymorphonuclear myeloid‐derived suppressor cell (PMN‐MDSC) migration. The administration of regorafenib, a multikinase inhibitor that inhibits PDGF signaling, reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment, enhancing anti‐PD‐1 immunotherapy [ ]. In addition, we demonstrated that lactate released by glycolysis‐enhanced PDAC cells plays a key role in the formation of an immunosuppressive TME, a process driven by CAFs. Lactate‐stimulated CAFs promote the secretion of IL‐6, which suppresses the activity of cytotoxic immune cells. Consequently, inhibiting lactate production in cancer cells with the LDHA inhibitor FX11 enhances antitumor immunity mediated by CD8+ T cells [ ]. These findings suggest that combining lactate metabolism modulation with anti‐PD‐L1 therapy enhances antitumor immune responses and improves therapeutic outcomes.
The phenotypic conversion of CAFs from cancer‐promoting to cancer‐suppressing forms is also an intriguing strategy. In a cohort of non‐small cell lung cancer patients, the presence of Meflin+ CAFs was significantly associated with prolonged survival and a favorable response to immune checkpoint therapy [ ]. In addition, Am80, a synthetic nonnatural retinoid, induced the expression of Meflin in CAFs and enhanced the effect of immune checkpoint therapy in an Am80‐treated mouse model of PDAC and urothelial carcinoma [ , ]. These findings suggest that the induction of cancer‐suppressing CAFs can open a new window for the development of treatment strategies that target CAFs.
Within this decade, efforts to improve the TME through the modulation of CAFs have been ongoing. Early approaches focused on methods to induce a quiescent state in tumor‐promoting CAFs. Recently, research has shifted toward the development of techniques for the depletion of CAFs and methods to reprogram CAFs into tumor‐suppressing phenotypes. Nevertheless, the difficulty of selectively targeting specific CAFs persists, as CAF markers are frequently shared with various normal cells. Furthermore, as Am80 possesses multiple biological activities, including the modulation of immunity and the induction of cell differentiation, the precise mechanism underlying the induction of definitive cancer‐suppressing CAFs remains unclear. Therefore, further elucidation of the properties of CAFs and the precise mechanisms underlying the induction of cancer‐suppressive CAFs is crucial in future investigations.

## Attempts at Clinical Applications Targeting CAFs

With the increasing understanding of CAFs, several clinical trials have been conducted (Table  ). For example, altering the TME by targeting the ECM has been successful in several clinical trials. A phase II clinical trial (NCT01821729) revealed that total neoadjuvant therapy with losartan, which reduces stromal collagen and hyaluronic acid production [ ], FORFIRINOX, and chemoradiotherapy improved the R0 resection rate and prolonged the survival rate in patients with locally advanced PDAC [ ]. Additionally, a phase I clinical trial (NCT01927965) of minnelide, which prevents hyaluronan synthesis and collagen stabilization [ ], confirmed the safety and tolerability of minnelide in patients with advanced gastrointestinal cancer [ ]. A phase II clinical trial involving PDAC patients is currently underway (NCT03117920). Given that the ECM affects immune cell infiltration into tumors, there is a growing expectation that ECM‐targeted drugs in combination with immunotherapy will be developed as therapeutic strategy to increase treatment efficacy [ ].
 Clinical trials to deplete specific CAFs have also been initiated. The FAPME study, a phase I clinical trial (NCT01722149) targeting FAP in patients with malignant pleural mesothelioma, confirmed the safety of a single infusion of FAP CAR‐T cells [ ]. Another phase I clinical trial (NCT03932565) employing fourth‐generation CAR‐T cells that target both Nectin4 and FAP [ ] is currently underway. Recently, notable advancements have been made in the development of antitumor therapies using antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), and the application of this technology to target the TME, including CAFs, has also been addressed [ , ]. OMTX705, an anti‐FAP ADC, was shown to inhibit tumor growth by increasing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells in a mouse model with a humanized immune system [ ]. On the basis of the results of that preclinical study, a phase I clinical trial (NCT05547321) is currently in progress. The LuMIERE study, a phase I/II clinical trial (NCT04939610) utilizing 177Lu‐FAP‐2286 (177Lu‐radiolabeled anti‐FAP antibody) in patients with advanced solid tumors, reported initial results, with no severe adverse effects observed [ ]. The first‐in‐human phase I clinical trial of ABBV‐085 (NCT2565758), which targets LRRC15 [ ], revealed its safety and tolerability in patients with osteosarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma [ ].
As a notable strategy for CAF reprogramming, IL‐1 inhibition via anakinra prevents iCAF senescence with irradiation, thereby increasing therapeutic efficacy [ ]. IL1R1+ iCAFs have been shown to play a role in protumorigenic phenomena, and both CAF‐specific IL1R1 knockout and anakinra treatment reduce tumor growth by decreasing intratumoral Th17 cell infiltration [ ]; these findings support the therapeutic value of targeting IL1R1+ CAFs. The ACO/ARO/AIO‐21 investigator‐driven, open‐labeled, phase I clinical trial (NCT04942626), which combines fluoropyrimidine‐based preoperative chemoradiotherapy with anakinra, demonstrated the safety and tolerability of this approach [ ]. In an attempt to convert tumor‐promoting CAFs into tumor‐suppressive CAFs, the investigator‐initiated phase I/II clinical trial MIKE‐1 (NCT05064618), which evaluated the safety and effect of Am80 in combination with conventional chemotherapies, including gemcitabine/nab‐paclitaxel, in pancreatic cancer, is ongoing [ ].
In contrast to success in the translational phase based on the results of animal studies, unexpected negative results may be obtained in clinical trials. Therefore, we need to be aware of these gaps and take appropriate measures to address them. PEGPH20 is an agent that promotes the degradation of hyaluronic acid, a key component of the ECM. In a mouse model of pancreatic cancer, the combination of PEGPH20 and gemcitabine improved the TME, leading to prolonged survival [ ]. However, the combination of PEGPH20 and fluorouracil/leucovorin/irinotecan/oxaliplatin (FOLFIRINOX) or nab‐paclitaxel/gemcitabine did not improve OS or PFS in several clinical trials for patients with metastatic PDAC [ , ]. There are several possible explanations for this discrepancy. First, genetically engineered PDAC mouse models do not completely mimic human PDACs, especially the TME. Second, differences in PEGPH20 pharmacokinetics between mice and humans may be involved. Taken together, these explanations highlight that the potential capacity of modulating the TME in clinical trials remains inconclusive. Therefore, further improvements in modalities targeting CAFs and animal models are required, and subsequent clinical trials will open the next era of CAF‐targeted therapy.

7.

## Conclusion
Advancements in single‐cell analysis have revealed the diversity of CAFs. However, insights gained from such analyses represent only a snapshot of the TME, with limitations in determining the lineage of these cells. To elucidate the cell‐of‐origin of CAFs in tumor tissues, further research using lineage tracing models is needed.
The success of developing effective therapies targeting CAFs will depend on the validation of multiple potential targets and the selection of an appropriate approach, that is, whether to eliminate or reprogram CAFs. Additionally, the evaluation of CAF‐targeting therapies in combination with other agents, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, will lead to the development of novel strategies for cancer eradication. However, for example, CAF elimination via CAR‐T cells may also affect normal fibroblasts, which could lead to unexpected outcomes, such as physical impairment of tissue homeostasis and repair during damage. To overcome this issue, a detailed analysis to clarify that the target molecules are more specifically expressed in CAFs than in normal fibroblasts at the protein level is needed. For the direct reprogramming of tumor‐promoting CAFs to tumor‐suppressing CAFs, the key step is to identify true tumor‐suppressing CAFs with multiple markers. In addition, establishing appropriate biomarkers is crucial for evaluating whether reprogramming has sufficiently completed a state in which tumor‐promoting CAFs are converted to normal fibroblasts or tumor‐suppressive CAFs. Besides, understanding the potential background that defines the identity of CAFs, such as transcription factor networks that regulate the expression of functional marker genes, could overcome reprogramming difficulties. Most importantly, we need to examine the patient as a whole, not just the tumor. Off‐target investigations in normal tissues are essential for the development of cancer treatment strategies targeting CAFs. Currently, precision medicine is the mainstream form of cancer treatment. In the future, a deeper understanding of the complexity of cancer, including the diversity of CAFs, will lead to the development of innovative cancer therapies and benefits for patients.
