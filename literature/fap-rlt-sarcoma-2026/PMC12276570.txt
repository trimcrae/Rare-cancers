## Graphical abstract
CAFs are divided into three main subtypes: myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs). CAFs influence treatment outcomes via seven primary mechanisms: physical barrier formation, metabolic reprogramming, exosome secretion, DNA repair enhancement, bypass signaling activation, activation of multidrug resistance proteins and immune checkpoint inhibition. These mechanisms result in resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune escape, underscoring the critical role of CAFs in the TME and their impact on treatment efficacy. 

The resistance to cancer treatment is a major clinical obstacle, being strongly influenced by the tumor microenvironment (TME). Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are critical elements of the TME. CAFs are heterogeneous and are activated through diverse pathways. These CAFs engage in reciprocal interactions with tumor cells, driving tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. In this review, we discuss the role of CAFs in the development of tumor resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Besides, we summarize recent clinical trials in CAF-targeted therapies. The development of resistance involves physical barrier formation, metabolic reprogramming, exosome release, DNA repair, bypass pathway activation, multidrug resistance protein upregulation, and immune checkpoint inhibition. Challenges remain in addressing drug resistance despite the therapeutic potential of targeting CAFs: the cellular origins of CAFs need to be clarified, and their limited clinical applications need to be increased. Future studies should focus on elucidating the reasons for CAF heterogeneity, developing precise targeting strategies, and validating the clinical safety and efficacy of CAF-based therapies to overcome treatment resistance and improve patient outcomes.

## Introduction
Cancer remains one of the world's main public health threats given its high morbidity and mortality rates. , Various factors affect the development of cancer, including intrinsic factors, such as genetic factors, and extrinsic factors within the tumor microenvironment (TME), including the surrounding cells. , Many patients encounter recurrences and metastases during or after the treatment process despite the substantial advancements achieved in cancer treatments such as in surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Recurrence and metastasis result in treatment failure, emphasizing the challenge posed by treatment resistance, and indicating the need to increase the efficacy of cancer treatments.
The TME is the local milieu in which tumor cells multiply. The TME consists of the surrounding blood vessels, the extracellular matrix (ECM), various signaling molecules, and noncancerous cells such as immune cells, fibroblasts, and adipocytes. The TME physically supports tumor cells and regulates their growth, invasion, and metastasis, as well as their resistance to treatment. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are TME constituents that have received considerable attention because of their involvement in cancer development. , CAFs have become a focus in oncology research owing to their contribution to tumorigenesis and treatment resistance. Historically, CAFs were identified through negative selection, excluding epithelial, endothelial, and leukocyte markers, as well as alongside morphological evaluation. However, CAF characterization has been transformed by developments in single-cell omics. For instance, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been used to distinguished several CAF subtypes according to particular gene expression patterns and geographic distribution. These CAFs subtypes include matrix (mCAFs), immune (iCAFs), vascular (vCAFs), dividing (dCAFs), tumor-like CAFs (tCAFs), interferon response (ifnCAFs), antigen-presenting (apCAFs), and reticular-like (rCAFs) CAFs, as well as heat shock protein high tCAFs (hsp_tCAFs). Therefore, modern definitions emphasize the positive identification of CAFs based on fibroblast-specific gene signatures, including markers such as fibroblast activation protein alpha (FAP), actin alpha cardiac muscle 2 (ACTA2), platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha/beta (PDGFRα/β), and collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1). , , 
CAFs are a highly diverse population of stromal cells with varying origins, phenotypes, and functions. CAFs encourage the development, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells through releasing different cytokines, growth factors, chemokines, and matrix-remodeling enzymes. CAFs have different morphological characteristics, gene expression profiles, and functional characteristics than normal fibroblasts (NFs). CAFs respond to signals from tumor cells, which activate and recruit more CAFs via autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, establishing a positive feedback loop that drives tumor progression. CAFs play a major role in promoting tumor therapy resistance. CAFs lead to therapy resistance via modifying the ECM, producing immunosuppressive molecules, and influencing tumor cell signaling pathways. , CAFs may initially function to prevent malignant transformation; however, CAFs are later become misdirected, supporting tumor growth and metastasis. A better understanding of CAF functioning in treatment resistance is essential to advanced cancer therapies and overcome therapeutic barriers.
This review focuses on the complex involvement of CAFs in cancer therapy resistance, and explores the various potential therapeutic strategies specifically targeting CAFs. We discuss the biological characteristics of CAFs, their origin, and their activation mechanisms. Moreover, we detail the wide heterogeneity of CAFs, their complex reciprocal interactions with tumor cells, and the underlying biological mechanisms through which CAFs augment therapy resistance. We also review the current state of clinical research in this area and suggest future research directions to provide new perspectives and strategies for improving cancer therapy.

## CAF origins and regulation
2.1

## Cellular CAF sources
The precise cellular origins of CAFs, which need to be identified to understand tumor-related processes, remain elusive due to the lack of definitive CAF biomarkers. However, lineage-tracing studies have identified a diverse array of precursor cells contributing to CAF populations ( ). , NFs are the most notable source of CAFs. The results of in vitro and in vivo tracing experiments conducted with high-precision methods and advanced imaging tools have shown that bone-marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (BM-MSCs) can produce CAFs. CAFs can also originate through transdifferentiation processes, such as endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Other possible sources of CAFs include pericytes, lipocytes, macrophages, , , and mesothelial cells. , , 
The TME has considerable cellular plasticity. Notably, immune cells, especially macrophages, actively experience a macrophage-myofibroblast transition (MMT), which results in CAF production across multiple types of malignancies. SMAD family member 3 (SMAD3)-mediated MMT facilitates CAF formation and promotes tumor progression through enhanced fibrotic remodeling in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Oxidative stress, which is mediated by the p38 signaling pathway, promotes the transdifferentiation of monocytes into myofibroblasts in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). These process describe an aspect of cellular transformation and indicate a direct connection between the inflammatory signaling mechanism and the stromal reprogramming process occurring in the TME. Malignant effusion macrophages demonstrate a strong capacity for converting to fibroblasts, further emphasizing the role of the TME in shaping cellular identity. MSCs contribute to CAF pools through the MMT in ovarian and pancreatic cancers, whereas organ-specific precursors such as hepatic stellate cells and pancreatic stellate cells produce different CAF subtypes in cholangiocarcinoma and PDAC, respectively. , , The evidence is increasing showing that CAF differentiation patterns are organ-specific in breast cancer and ovarian cancer. Breast cancer CAFs derive from BM-MSCs via myeloid zinc finger 1/tumor growth factor-beta1 (TGF-β1) signaling, whereas ovarian cancer cells promote BM-MSC differentiation through homeobox A9-mediated TGF-β2 activation. , These findings demonstrate that the CAF population is diverse and has a range of origins, being influenced by intrinsic factors and TME signals.

2.2

## Mechanisms of fibroblast-to-CAF transformation
The transformation of quiescent stromal cells into activated CAFs involves dynamic molecular reprogramming, which is driven by tumor-derived signals ( ). Cancer cells secrete multiple bioactive factors including TGF-β family ligands, PDGF, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and interleukins (IL-1 and IL-6), which activate downstream signaling cascades through cognate receptors. , Hypoxic conditions and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in the TME, often due to rapid cancer cell growth, further potentiate CAF activation by stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) and upregulating stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1/CXCL12), enhancing stromal-tumor communication. 
Epigenetic regulation is a critical mechanism sustaining CAF phenotypes. , Leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), when secreted in the TME, initiates an epigenetic switch involving precisely coordinated changes in histone acetylation and DNA methylation, which are crucial for the cell state transitions, leading to the constitutive activation of Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), which maintains CAF functionality. Tumor-derived exosomes containing noncoding RNAs, such as miR-1247 from metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma cells, further reinforce tCAF activation in distant metastatic niches. Once established, CAFs sustain autocrine activation through metabolic and secretome alterations, whereas direct physical interactions with tumor cells via Notch signaling reinforce their pro-tumor functions. Direct cell-cell contact through Notch signaling and the persistent autocrine loops caused by metabolic rewiring complete this complex activation network. 
CAFs play two roles in tumor progression. NFs exert tumor-suppressive effects in early-stage tumors through restricting growth and facilitating immune surveillance. However, cancer cells hinder these functions through progressive epigenetic reprogramming, converting fibroblasts into tumor-promoting CAFs. This functional transition highlights the therapeutic challenges faced in targeting CAFs, as their tumor-promoting activities become entrenched through self-sustaining signaling networks. The interplay between the cellular origin, activation mechanism, and tissue-specific microenvironment ultimately determines the functional diversity of CAFs, the understanding of which has substantial implications for therapeutic targeting and biomarker development.

## CAF heterogeneity
3.1

## Molecular classification of CAF subtypes
The heterogeneity of CAFs is indicated by the differences in their molecular profiles, which form the basis for classifying the different CAF subtypes. The core subtypes include myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), iCAFs, and apCAFs. PDGFRα, collagen genes (e.g., COL1A1), and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) are all highly expressed in myCAFs. myCAFs are involved in ECM remodeling and metastasis, particularly in pancreatic and colorectal cancer. Conversely, iCAFs are characterized by the increased release of cytokines such IL-6 and CXCL12, as well as the activation of inflammatory pathways such as the JAK/STAT pathway. , These CAFs promote cancer stemness and immunosuppression, particularly in breast cancer and NSCLC. Activated apCAFs express major histocompatibility complex II (MHC-II) molecules (e.g., HLA-DRA) and costimulatory markers such as CD74, which modulate T cell responses and play key roles in immune modulation in pancreatic and lung cancers through complex signaling pathways. , , , 
Other subtypes of CAFs have also been identified, including tCAFs, FerroCAFs, and lineage-specific CAFs. tCAFs, marked by CD10+/CD73+ expression, are often located near tumor cells and engage in metabolic crosstalk with tumor cells via the Warburg effect. This crosstalk supports tumor growth, particularly in breast cancer. FerroCAFs are iron-enriched CAFs that secrete cytokines such as CXCL1, CCL2, and CSF1, which recruit myeloid cells that suppress the immune system, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). FerroCAFs strongly contribute to immune evasion and foster therapy resistance specifically in prostate cancer. The cellular origins are reflected in lineage-specific CAFs, such as vCAFs and dCAFs, reflect their cellular origins and play notable roles in ECM organization and tumor-stroma crosstalk, particularly in breast cancer. 
CAFs possess multiple biomarkers. However, unique CAF markers remain scarce. CAFs from different sources or the TME possess different marker expression profiles. Fibroblast-specific protein 1 (FSP1), FAP, PDGFRα/β, and α-SMA are common indicators used for CAF identification.

3.2

## Functional CAF heterogeneity and therapy resistance
The functional plasticity of CAFs is intrinsically linked to their molecular profiles, with subtypes exerting opposing effects on tumor progression, immune responses, and therapy resistance. Several CAF subtypes aid in tumor suppression and treatment sensitization; others encourage tumor growth and immune evasion. For instance, GPR77+ /CD10+ CAFs sustain breast cancer stem cells (CSCs) through nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling, contributing to chemoresistance, particularly to paclitaxel. CD146+ CAFs in luminal breast cancer activate the ERK signaling pathway, promoting resistance to tamoxifen treatment. , Additionally, CAFs use CXC-chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) and CXC-chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) interactions to increase CSC proliferation. Certain CAF subtypes exhibit tumor-suppressive functions. For example, myCAF-derived type I collagen physically limits the progression of PDAC, ovarian cancer, and colorectal cancer (CRC) via constraining tumor expansion. , CD200+ CAFs are essential for increasing the effectiveness of EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in NSCLC. CD200+ CAFs markedly increase the effectiveness of the treatment through sensitizing cancer cells to gefitinib via the secretion of insulin-like growth factor (IGF) and its binding protein (IGFBP). , Furthermore, the depletion of PDAC-specific myofibroblasts with the genetic marker α-SMA impaired immune surveillance and increased the proportion of Treg, leading to aggressive tumor progression and substantially lowered animal survival rates. These findings highlight the opposing characteristics of CAFs: where they can function as protumorigenic and tumor-suppressive agents based on the molecular context of the TME.

3.3

## Interplay between molecular and functional heterogeneity and clinical implications
The interplay between molecular heterogeneity and functional plasticity further complicates the understanding of the participation of CAFs in cancer. CAFs dynamically adjust their functions in response to cues from the TME, shifting between tumor-promoting and tumor-restraining roles. For example, myCAFs, which can promote metastasis through ECM remodeling, also constrain PDAC progression through collagen-mediated mechanical suppression. Similarly, iCAFs can shift between proinflammatory and immunosuppressive states depending on the signals received from the TME. In addition, leucine-rich repeat containing 15 (LRRC15) positive CAFs exhibit both tumor-promoting and immunosuppressive properties. This flexibility allows CAFs to adapt to the ever-changing tumor environment, contributing to the challenges associated with developing effective cancer therapies.
CAF heterogeneity is clinically relevant. High stromal CAF density, which is commonly observed in a wide range of cancers, is closely associated with a poor prognosis. High stromal CAF density frequently correlates with immune infiltration and the emergence of therapeutic resistance, creating challenges in cancer treatment. Targeting specific CAF subtypes, such as FerroCAFs in prostate cancer, offers a promising approach to overcoming resistance and enhancing patient outcomes. Developing subtype-specific therapies could increase the efficacy of the TME, modulation, thereby increasing the efficacy of current cancer treatments.
In summary, the heterogeneity of CAFs reflects their broad ranges of molecular diversity and functional adaptability, which are crucial in driving cancer progression and therapeutic resistance. The ability of CAFs to shift between pro-tumorigenic and tumor-suppressive functions highlights their complex and context-dependent nature. Future studies, particularly those employing single-cell multiomics approaches, will be crucial in further understanding the complexity of CAF plasticity and in developing more targeted and effective therapies to address CAF-mediated therapy resistance.

4

## CAFs in cancer treatment resistance
Resistance to cancer treatment remains a substantial barrier in clinical therapy, with the TME serving as a key contributor to treatment resistance. CAFs, as prominent components of the TME play a crucial role in promoting tumor progression and therapy resistance through diverse mechanisms. CAFs directly or indirectly alter their response to cancer therapy via influencing the biological behavior of tumor cells. We explore the role of CAFs play in immunotherapy, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy resistance.
4.1

## Physical barrier formation
The physical barrier effect refers to the role of CAFs in altering the structure of the ECM within the TME, which results in a denser and more rigid tumor stroma. This stroma impedes the effective penetration of therapeutic drugs, radiation, and immune cells, thereby enabling tumor evasion of chemotherapy and contributing to resistance to radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapies. The ECM, which is composed of macromolecules such as collagen, hyaluronic acid, elastin, and fibronectin, serves as the tumor "scaffold'' and is crucial for tumor growth and treatment resistance. , , 
4.1.1

## CAFs and MMPs
CAFs contribute to the creation of this physical barrier through secreting dense fibrogenic stroma. This process involves producing and activating enzymes such matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade the ECM components, thereby increasing tumor rigidity and limiting drug penetration. CAFs can promote the production of ECM components such as collagen and hyaluronic acid, which further contribute to the tumor stromal stiffness, creating a protective barrier around the tumor cells. CAFs promote matrix remodeling through secreting MMPs such MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-3, and MMP-9, which enable tumor cells to infiltration into adjacent tissues, movement, and spreading. This degradation of the adjacent matrix tissues, also provides space for tumor cell growth. ECM components generate new signaling pathways that promote tumor cell survival. In addition to their role in matrix degradation, CAFs activate MMPs through the secretion of cytokines such as TGF-β, IL-6, and HGF, which further strengthen the invasiveness of tumor cells. The ability of CAFs to promote matrix degradation contributes to the immune evasion of tumors, as ECM breakdown disrupts immune cell infiltration and hinders the effectiveness of immune surveillance. The breakdown decreases the effectiveness of the immune system responses, including T-cell and natural killer (NK) cell infiltration. , Moreover, CAF-secreted MMPs aid in tumor angiogenesis via promoting the development of new blood vessels that provide oxygen and nutrients to the expanding tumor by releasing proangiogenic factors like VEGF. Additionally, MMPs possess the capacity to effectively activate pivotal signaling pathways such as FGF-1/−3/FGFR4. Simultaneously, MMPs are involved in the activation of the SDF-1/SATB-1 pathway. MMPs initiate a series of reactions through their catalytic activities that remodel the ECM. This remodeling process is essential, creating a more accommodating and optimized microenvironment that is highly conducive to tumor cell growth and invasion. Consequently, this microenvironment increases the speed of malignant tumor progression. These intertwined processes have two roles: substantially supporting tumor growth and metastasis as well as increasingly contributing to the emergence of treatment resistance, thus complicating the context of cancer treatment strategies.
CAFs reduce the effectiveness of treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy by loosening the tumor matrix and enhancing repair mechanisms, hindering therapeutic agents from reaching their targets and preventing immune cells from effectively eliminate tumor cells. Furthermore, factors such as TGF-β regulate MMP expression and perpetuate a feedback loop, in which CAFs continuously promote fibrosis and MMP production, further enhancing tumor invasiveness and resistance to treatment. For instance, TGF-β exposure activates CAFs in ovarian cancer, thus upregulating Versican (VCAN) gene expression and inducing EMT, both of which promote MMP production. Similarly, CAF-derived TGF-β1 powerfully stimulates EMT markers such as zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 2 (ZEB2) in the complex bladder cancer microenvironment. CAF-derived TGF-β1 initiates a cascade through ZEB2NAT transcripts that further promotes MMP expression, advancing tumor progression. Elevated MMP levels contribute to the breakdown of tissue barriers, enabling cancer cell invasion and metastasis. As such, CAFs and MMPs work together to create a hostile environment that fosters tumor progression and therapy evasion.

4.1.2

## Impact of physical barriers on cancer treatment
The dense, rigid ECM produced by the CAFs within the TME strongly hinders the penetration of therapeutic agents, immune cells, and radiation, contributing to tumor resistance to therapy. Chemotherapy drugs depend on their ability to penetrate tumor cells and exert their effects intracellularly. However, the dense ECM formed by CAF-secreted components (e.g., collagen and hyaluronic acid) creates a barrier, particularly for large-molecule drugs, limiting drug delivery to the tumor site. This barrier results in suboptimal drug concentrations reaching the tumor and the development of drug resistance. 
A dense tumor stroma reduces the interaction between therapeutic agents and their targets, diminishing the efficacy of targeted therapies. Immunotherapy, which heavily relies on immune cells such as T cells and NK cells to target tumors, is similarly affected by this physical barrier. Because the contribution of CAFs to ECM stiffness severely inhibits immune cell penetration, the effectiveness of the immune responses to the tumor is considerably diminished. Radiotherapy, which targets tumor cells by damaging their DNA, is also impacted by this physical barrier, as the ECM limits radiation penetration, particularly in the deeper tumor regions. MMPs, TGF-β, and other factors associated with radiation-induced enhancement promote carcinogenesis in irradiated fibroblasts in animal models, reducing treatment success. 

4.1.3

## Targeting CAFs: clinical implications and strategies
Targeting CAFs and their secreted ECM components may offer new therapeutic avenues for avoiding treatment resistance, but several challenges must be considered before these strategies can be broadly implemented in clinical settings. For instance, TGF-β-neutralizing antibodies could inhibit EMT and CSC processes, reducing treatment resistance. However, a main challenge with targeting TGF-β is pleiotropic effects, which could lead to off-target consequences. TGF-β has two roles in the development of cancer: suppressing and promoting tumors in the early and later stages, respectively, which complicates the use of TGF-β inhibitors. Additionally, CAF plasticity, where CAFs revert to a less differentiated state or acquire an alternative phenotype, may limit the long-term effectiveness of such therapies by enabling tumor cells to adapt to the treatment and re-establish a supportive stromal environment. MMP inhibitors may also reduce CAF activity and increased the permeability of therapeutic drugs. , These inhibitors, given their mechanisms, could enhance drug delivery and immune cell function within the tumor microenvironment; however, these inhibitors are not without limitations. For instance, MMP inhibition may disrupt normal tissue homeostasis, leading to side effects such as impaired wound healing or fibrosis in healthy tissues. Moreover, MMP inhibitors may not be effective against all types of CAFs, especially CAFs that are less reliant on MMP-mediated ECM remodeling. In addition, antihyaluronic acid antibodies can reduce matrix density, thereby increasing the accessibility of therapeutic agents to the ECM and boosting the anti-tumor immune response. However, targeting the hyaluronic acid in the ECM could affect the normal function of connective tissues, as hyaluronic acid is crucial for maintaining tissue hydration and cellular signaling. Additionally, the targeting of hyaluronic acid could lead to compensatory ECM remodeling by other fibroblastic populations, limiting the therapeutic benefit of these interventions. 
In summary, targeting CAFs and their secreted components shows promise for overcoming cancer resistance; however, the complexity of CAF biology, the pleiotropic effects of certain molecules such as TGF-β, and the potential off-target consequences or compensatory mechanisms pose considerable challenges. These limitations underscore the need for a more nuanced approach that not only targets CAFs but also considers the dynamic and plastic nature of the TME.

4.2

## Metabolic reprogramming
Cancer cells thrive in nutrient-deprived or hypoxic environments, enhancing their metabolic processes to ensure survival under these challenging conditions. This metabolic adaptation, which is referred to as metabolic reprogramming, a distinct hallmark of cancer cells. Metabolic reprogramming endows cancer cells with the ability to survive and vigorously proliferate within the complexities and challenges of the TME. , This reprogramming involves shifts in metabolic pathways, such as the Krebs cycle and the Warburg effect, which lead to increased glycolysis even in the presence of oxygen. These metabolic shifts enable cancer cells to meet the high energy demands required for growth and invasion, thus driving tumor progression. However, not only cancer cells undergo metabolic reprogramming within the TME. CAFs also contribute to these metabolic shifts through complex signaling pathways. These cells, through their unique biochemical properties, strongly influence the metabolic state of cancer cells. CAFs initiate a cascade of metabolic changes that effectively promote tumor growth, immune evasion, and metastasis. The nutrient and oxygen availability in different TME regions further contributes to the metabolic heterogeneity observed within tumors, where cells adapt to local environmental conditions. , The key CAF-driven metabolic reprogramming mechanisms has been summarized ( ). 
4.2.1

## CAFs and the Warburg effect: the reverse Warburg effect
CAFs and cancer cells engage in metabolic interplay that enhances tumor progression. CAFs secrete metabolic byproducts such as lactate and pyruvate, which are then use by tumor cells to meet their energy and biosynthesis needs. A characteristic of this metabolic interaction is increased fermentation of glucose into lactate, which is a hallmark of the Warburg effect. This process occurs even in the presence of oxygen, where mitochondrial function remains intact, supporting rapid cell proliferation in the TME. CAFs contribute to this process through a mechanism known as the reverse Warburg effect (RWE), where CAFs undergo aerobic glycolysis and release lactate, which is then absorbed by adjacent cancer cells to fuel their growth. 
The glycolytic activity of CAFs is partly regulated via epigenetic modifications, particularly in HIF-1α and key glycolytic enzymes. Fibroblasts undergo epigenetic reprogramming under hypoxic conditions, shifting their prototype toward a glycolytic phenotype similar to that of CAFs. The glucose metabolism in CAFs shifts during tumor progression, with oxygen-dependent epigenetic mechanisms promoting the processes that support breast cancer progression. , Activated ATM oxidase enhances the glycolytic function of CAFs in these hypoxic regions, leading to lactate production. This lactate, produced as a by-product of cellular metabolism, powerfully increases the metabolic crosstalk between CAFs and cancer cells. Lactate activates the signaling pathways crucial to this process, such as TGF-β1/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK)/MMP2/9. These pathways then drive cancer cell invasiveness and metastasis specifically in breast cancer, leading to more aggressive disease progression. Similarly, CAFs facilitate the invasion of oral squamous cell carcinoma cells by releasing lactate through ITGB2. The model is different in ovarian cancer, where the CAFs in the hypoxic regions of the TME exhibit anaerobic glycolysis, producing lactate for their use through an interaction mediated by the long noncoding RNA LINC000924. CAFs promote glycolysis through the monocarboxylate transporter MCT1 in prostate cancer, leading to the decreased expression of glucose transporter GLUT1 and increasing lactate uptake. This metabolic shift highlights the flexibility of CAFs in supporting cancer cell metabolism across different cancer types. Therefore, gaining an in-depth knowledge of metabolic CAF heterogeneity is essential for developing targeted therapies and increasing treatment efficacy.

4.2.2

## Lipid metabolism reprogramming in CAFs
In addition to glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism is another critical aspect of metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells, where CAFs play an essential role. Cancer cells require substantial amounts of lipids for membrane biosynthesis and energy production. Moreover, lipid intermediates act as signaling molecules that activate oncogenic pathways, driving tumor progression. 
The exosomal protein heat shock protein containing 111 (HSPC111), secreted by CRC cells, altered lipid metabolism in CAFs, thereby promoting liver metastasis. HSPC111 phosphorylates ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) under specific cellular conditions, rapidly increasing the acetyl-CoA levels in CAFs, which impact their metabolic function. This metabolic reprogramming leads to increased H3K27 acetylation, promoting the expression and secretion of CXCL5. The CXCL5–CXCR2 axis, through a signaling mechanism, then strongly enhances HSPC111 release. This enhanced HSPC111 release effectively promotes premetastatic niche formation and accelerates CRC liver metastasis, worsening prognosis. This highlights the potential of targeting lipid metabolism reprogramming in CAFs in CRC as a strategy to prevent metastasis. Lipid metabolism reprogramming in CAFs promotes not only metastasis but also tumor progression via modulating exosomal signaling. These findings suggest that HSPC111 may be a therapeutic target for the prevention of CRC liver metastasis. Thoroughly understanding the molecular mechanisms governing these processes could open new avenues for therapeutic intervention in metastatic cancer.

4.2.3

## Glutamine metabolism reprogramming in CAFs
Glutamine metabolism is another critical process regulated by CAFs that supports cancer growth. , Glutamine provides the nitrogen sources necessary for biosynthesis and energy production, supporting the tricarboxylic acid cycle and ATP generation. , CAFs promote glutamine metabolism in lung adenocarcinoma through a long noncoding RNA (lncRNA), LINC01614, which upregulates the expression of glutamine transporters via the NF-κB signaling pathway. This process increases the uptake of glutamine by cancer cells, driving the tricarboxylic acid cycle and tumor growth. The interaction between CAFs and tumor cells forms a positive feedback loop, further driving the metabolic changes that support tumor progression. The elevated expression of LINC01614 in CAFs is linked to poor prognosis in patients with lung adenocarcinoma, highlighting its potential as a prognostic marker. A similar feedback loop is observed in renal cancer, where CAFs enhance glutamine metabolism to support tumor growth and alter immune cell behavior. These findings emphasize the need to explore the metabolic crosstalk between CAFs and cancer cells, which could inform personalized treatment strategies.

4.2.4

## CAFs in immune modulation and chronic inflammation
CAFs interact with different immune cells in the TME, markedly contributing to the metabolic reprogramming that causes chronic inflammation and immunological suppression ( ). , , , , This interaction is critical for the creation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which facilitates tumor growth and promotes metastasis. , , , , CAFs facilitate the robust expansion and full-fledged functional activation of Tregs in the highly dynamic and complex TME via the coordinated and active secretion of soluble factors such as TGF-β, CXCL12, IDO, and VEGF. This secretion occurs while these factors simultaneously enhance the upregulation of immunosuppressive cytokines, including TGF-β, IL-35, and IL-10, which ultimately overcomes the immune response to favor of tumor progression. , , , , This cytokine milieu dampens T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity, contributing to immune evasion. Additionally, CAFs hinder dendritic cell (DC) maturation and function through the activation of the Wnt2 signaling pathway and the secretion of IL-6, VEGF, and TGF-β. , , , , IL-6, VEGF, and TGF-β impair antigen presentation and T-cell priming, further suppressing antitumor immune responses. NK-cell activity is similarly compromised by CAFs, inducing NK-cell apoptosis via the secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 as well as inhibiting their cytotoxic function through TGF-β and PGE2 production. , , , , CAFs contribute to modulating macrophage polarization within the TME, promoting the protumorigenic M2 phenotype through factors such as VEGF, IL-10, IL-6, and IDO, while suppressing the antitumor M1 phenotype via TSLP, IDO, IL-10, and CXCL12. , , , , In addition to their immune-modulatory functions, CAFs profoundly influence on tumor progression. They actively contribute to tumor progression by remodeling the ECM, thus accelerating tumor development. CAFs create a dense and rigid stromal architecture that facilitates tumor cell survival, invasion, and metastasis through the secretion of MMP-1, TGF-β, type I collagen, and fibronectin. , , , , This stromal remodeling, driven by the CAF secretion of various matrix-modifying factors, further enhances the ability of tumors to resist therapeutic interventions. Overall, these findings indicate that CAFs are critical players in the progression of and therapy resistance in cancer. Moreover, CAFs actively secrete chemokines and cytokines contribute to the formation of premetastatic niches and support tumor cell metastasis by modulating the immune environment. This continuous and intricate collaboration between CAFs and immune cells creates a hostile TME that facilitates malignant tumor progression. Given these complex interactions, an in-depth understanding of how CAFs contribute to immune regulation in the TME is essential for the development of therapies aimed at effectively reversing immune suppression and enhancing antitumor immunity. 

4.3

## Exosome-mediated drug resistance signaling
Exosomes, a subset of extracellular vesicles (EVs) with diameters of 30–150 nm, are derived from the endosomal pathway through multivesicular body formation. Exosomes carry unique molecular cargos, including nucleic acids (miRNAs, lncRNAs), proteins, and lipids, unlike other EVs, such as microvesicles or apoptotic bodies. These cargos endow exosomes with the ability to mediate intercellular communication within the TME. Exosomes play pivotal roles in the TME in supporting tumor progression, facilitating metastasis, and strengthening therapy resistance. Notably, CAFs use exosomes to transfer oncogenic signals between tumor, immune, and endothelial cells. For instance, CAF-derived exosomes, which are highly enriched with miR-181d-5p, are readily internalized by breast cancer cells. CAF-derived exosomes effectively suppress PTEN expression once inside the cells and activate the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) pathway, contributing to chemotherapy resistance. , , As such, the mechanisms underlying CAF-derived exosomes must be understood to develop innovative treatment approaches.
Exosomal noncoding RNAs, similar to circular RNAs (circRNAs) and lncRNAs, are key factors in the process of mediating drug resistance within cancer cells. CAFs secrete exosomes containing circHIF1A under hypoxic conditions, which is transferred to breast cancer cells. circHIF1A sponges miR-580–5p in the recipient cells, downregulating CD44 expression and enhancing CSC plasticity. This relationship increases resistance to targeted treatments and supports tumor aggressiveness. In addition, CAF-derived exosomes deliver LINC00659 to tumor cells. This lncRNA directly binds to miR-342–3p, halting its suppression of Annexin A2 (ANXA2), a prometastatic factor. Elevated ANXA2 expression drives CRC cell proliferation, migration, and EMT. , Similarly, exosomal MELTF-AS1 facilitates Y-Box binding protein 1 (YBX1) phase separation in NSCLC, activating ANXA8 and promoting tumorigenesis. , 
Exosomes are critical mediators of resistance in endocrine and chemotherapy treatments. For instance, in breast cancer, CD63+ CAFs secrete miR-22-enriched exosomes that target estrogen receptors alpha (ERα) as well as phosphatase and PTEN, suppressing their expression and driving tamoxifen resistance. Strategies that target SFRS1 (a key regulator of miR-22 packaging) or use cRGD-miR-22 sponge nanoparticles restored tamoxifen sensitivity in preclinical models. Additionally, CAF-derived exosomes contain miR-423–5p, which enhances cell proliferation and suppresses apoptosis by activating the STAT3 pathway in prostate cancer. This mechanism contributes to paclitaxel resistance, underscoring the importance of exosomes in chemotherapy resistance. 
Radiotherapy, despite being effective in treating many cancers, can lead to resistance, particularly when the TME is unfavorable, such as a hypoxic environment, immunosuppression, or inflammation. , CAFs play a crucial role in radiotherapy resistance via secreting exosomes that shield CRC cells from DNA damage. These exosomes increase proinflammatory mediators such granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), IL-6, IL-8, and COX-2. These exosomes also activate the NF-κB pathway, which fosters a tumor-promoting environment and reduces sensitivity to radiation. , , , Furthermore, the exosomal TGF-β from radiation-treated CAFs enhances the properties of CSCs by activating the RAS/MAPK and laminin pathways, which are critical for metastasis and therapy resistance. These findings suggest that targeting CAF-derived exosomes increases radiotherapy efficacy and improves patient outcomes.
Exosomes promote resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy via modulating these molecular pathways. Targeting exosomes and their molecular contents offers a promising strategy for overcoming resistance and improving therapeutic outcomes in cancer treatment.

4.4

## DNA repair
Tumor cell resistance to radiotherapy and especially chemotherapy, which mainly causes double-strand DNA breaks, depends on DNA repair. Chemotherapy and radiation therapy exert their cytotoxic effects through inducing DNA damage, which must be repaired for tumor cells to survive ( ). However, tumor cells activate DNA mechanisms to repair this damage, thereby evading treatment and developing drug resistance. 
4.4.1

## CAF-mediated DNA repair and chemoresistance
Chemotherapeutic agents, such as cisplatin, induce double-stranded breaks in tumor cells, triggering DNA repair mechanisms that enable continued tumor cell survival. The common DNA repair pathways include homologous recombination repair (HRR), nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ), and nucleotide excision repair (NER). , CAFs are essential for repairing the DNA of tumor cells, which leads to chemotherapy resistance. CAFs secrete various cytokines, such as TGF-β, and metabolic enzymes that facilitate DNA repair in neighboring tumor cells. For example, the TGF-β secreted by CAFs in the TME activates the ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM)/ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related protein (ATR) signaling pathway. This activation effectively promotes double-strand break repair, allowing tumor cells to survive the cytotoxic impacts of chemotherapy. This crosstalk between CAFs and tumor cells creates a feedforward loop in which CAFs sustain tumor cell viability via enhancing DNA repair capacity, thereby supporting chemotherapy resistance.

4.4.2

## CAF-mediated DNA repair and radiotherapy
Radiotherapy is a common cancer treatment, but the efficacy of radiotherapy is frequently hindered by the capacity of tumor cells to repair radiation-induced DNA damage. The radiation dose is a critical factor influencing the cellular responses to DNA damage, determining whether cells arrest at specific cell-cycle phases, enter senescence, or proceed to apoptosis. Radiation doses generally range between 0.1 and 3 Gy in clinical practice. However, CAFs display resistance to radiation and fail to undergo apoptosis even when exposed to high-dose radiation, such as 30 Gy. Instead, CAFs enter a senescent phase after exposure to radiation doses exceeding 10–12 Gy. At 18 Gy, CAFs sustain their DNA damage response but rapidly senesce within a few days after radiation and remain viable in tissue culture. Exposure to 18 Gy radiation had strong immunosuppressive effects on CAFs in human lung cancer models, particularly on T-cell proliferation and migration. CAFs also secrete proinflammatory cytokines and angiogenesis-regulating factors, such as basic FGF (bFGF) and SDF-1, further enhancing tumor resistance to radiation therapy. However, radiation treatment reduced the secretion of angiogenic factors such angiopoietin and thrombospondin-2 (TSP-2) but increased bFGF secretion. 
CAFs contribute to immune evasion following radiation. Radiation-treated CAFs suppress M1 macrophage polarization via inhibiting nitric oxide production and proinflammatory cytokine secretion. Moreover, CAF-derived kynurenine blocks DC maturation in liver and lung cancer, and CAF-secreted TSLP skews the immune response toward a Th2 phenotype, further impairing antitumor immunity. These results highlight the intricate functioning of CAFs in regulating radiotherapy-resistant tumors. CAFs increase the levels of antioxidant enzymes including catalase and superoxide dismutase (SOD) in response to the oxidative stress caused by radiation-induced ROS, which damages the DNA in tumor cells. This adaptive response reduces oxidative stress, facilitates DNA repair, and enhances tumor cell survival. For example, CAF-secreted COL1 further boosts radiotherapy resistance in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma through stabilizing the tumor matrix and activating CXCL1-CXCR2/STAT3 signaling, which promotes DNA repair and tumor cell survival. 

4.5

## Bypass signaling activation
Bypass signaling activation refers to the activation of alternative pathways by tumor cells to evade the effects of targeted therapies. shows that, CAFs are crucial in this process because CAFs secrete substances such as hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which activates the MET transition factor (Met) receptors of tumor cells. This interaction between secreted substances and cell receptors activates signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK. These pathways promote cancer cell survival, rapid proliferation, and metastasis, thus contributing to the development of resistance to targeted therapies. For instance, CAF-derived HGF stimulates the GRP78 and c-Met/PI3K/AKT pathways in ovarian cancer, increasing the resistance to therapy and negating the inhibitory effects of paclitaxel on tumor cell proliferation. CAFs support resistance development in NSCLC through promoting collagen deposition, reducing T-cell infiltration, and enhancing the resistance to osimertinib, as demonstrated in the H1975/OSI cell line. CAFs activate Wnt and Notch signaling pathways in the TME, influencing CSC behavior, EMT, angiogenesis, and cancer invasion, all of which increase resistance to therapy. The dysregulation of these pathways amplifies cancer cell invasiveness and therapeutic resistance, which ultimately compromise treatment effectiveness. 
Targeting bypass signaling pathways offers a promising approach to avoiding resistance. The use of inhibitors targeting the HGF/Met, Wnt, and Notch signaling pathways, if combined with existing therapies, could provide a strategy to counteract tumor resistance. Met inhibitors and HGF-neutralizing antibodies showed antitumor potential in preclinical studies. Additionally, nanoparticles designed to deliver Notch signaling regulators into tumor-associated macrophages may remodel the TME and enhance ICI efficiency, producing antitumor effects. 

4.6

## Activation of multidrug resistance proteins
P-glycoprotein (P-gp), also referred to as multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR) or ABCB1, is a highly conserved and widely expressed transmembrane protein. P-gp is present across multiple crucial biological barriers such as the blood–brain barrier, protecting the body from various xenobiotics. P-gp primarily functions to expel foreign substances, such as chemotherapy drugs, from cells, protecting the body from harmful agents. However, P-gp overexpression in cancer cells is a key chemotherapy resistance mechanism. P-gp prevents intracellular drug accumulation and causes drug resistance through actively pumping anticancer medications out of tumor cells. Consequently, overcoming P-gp-mediated drug efflux is a current focus in cancer therapy. Inhibitors of P-gp functions may help increase the accumulation of anticancer agents within cancer cells, increasing treatment effectiveness through vying for P-gp receptor binding sites, thus reducing drug efflux from tumor cells. For example, breast cancer resistance proteins, a critical MDR protein family, are primarily responsible for conferring resistance to certain treatments used against various forms of breast cancer and leukemia. These proteins are essential for efflux of medications from tumor cells, leading to resistance in some cancer forms, such as leukemia and breast cancer. 
Glypican-1 (GPC1) expression sharply increases in PDAC, strongly negatively correlating with patient survival. Immunohistochemical analyses, which are rigorously conducted on samples from people with PDAC, revealed that GPC1 is highly expressed in tumor cells and CAFs, with expression levels highest in CAFs. A GPC1-targeted antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) with monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), GPC1-ADC (MMAE), was developed assess the antitumor activity of GPC1 in preclinical models. The experimental results indicated that GPC1-ADC (MMAE) suppresses GPC1-positive PDAC cell lines and exhibits antitumor effects in PDAC-derived xenograft (PDX) models, showing potential for future therapies. Although CAFs do not undergo apoptosis, the MMAE released from CAFs via MDR-1 induces cancer cell apoptosis, limiting PDAC tumor growth. CAFs activate MDR-1 expression through secreted substances, leading to drug efflux from tumor cells and diminishing therapeutic efficacy. These findings suggest that targeting GPC1-ADC (MMAE) could prevent MDR-associated resistance, offering a promising therapeutic strategy.
CAFs impact the responses to immunotherapy and shape the TME via secreting substances that activate MDRs and enable immunological escape. Traditional static biomarkers are less effective as predictive tools for solid tumor immunotherapy owing to the dynamic nature of CAFs. As a result, dynamic biomarkers are receiving attention as predictors of treatment outcomes. Various methods such as longitudinal biopsies, blood-based biomarkers, and radiomics may enable the monitoring of changes in the immune escape mechanisms of tumors and MDR expression over time, enabling the development of personalized immunotherapy strategies.

4.7

## Immune checkpoint inhibition
CAFs contribute to resistance to ICIs, which are increasingly being used in cancer immunotherapy. CAFs substantially bolster immune evasion in the TME by upregulating the expression of PD-L1 and other immune checkpoint proteins. Li et al. demonstrated that CAF-derived LOX enhances PD-L1 expression through histone lactylation and modulates EMT in gastric cancer via TGFβ/IGF1 signaling pathways. In addition, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) upregulates CAF exosomal PD-L1 levels in melanoma, which suppress T-cell activity and promote immune evasion. The role of CAFs in suppressing CD8+ T-cell infiltration enhances ICI resistance to cancer therapy. Additionally, CAFs can expel ICIs from tumor cells via MDR proteins, reducing the efficacy of these therapies. Consequently, CAFs contribute to immune escape and hinder the effectiveness of ICIs, which are increasingly being used in cancer immunotherapy. These mechanisms highlight how CAFs contribute to immune escape in the context of immunotherapy, emphasizing the need to address CAF-driven immunosuppression in combination with ICI therapies.
CAFs are targets for refining current oncologic strategies owing to the central function of CAFs in mediating therapeutic resistance and immunosuppression in cancer treatment. Evidence suggests that the heterogeneity in the CAF subpopulation composition and tertiary lymphoid structure maturation can be used as predictive biomarkers for ICI responsiveness, enabling more personalized treatment approaches.

## Clinical trials in CAF-targeted therapies: mechanisms and progress
Strategies aimed at overcoming treatment resistance by targeting CAFs, such as the development of CAF-targeted drugs, combined treatment regimens, and gene or cell therapy approaches, offer avenues for improving cancer treatment outcomes. summaries the current clinical trials targeting CAFs to overcome drug resistance. 
Furthermore, several targets and signaling pathways have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential in clinical trials. For example, anlotinib, a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, in combination with immune checkpoint blockade, can induce CAF apoptosis, remodel TME and enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration. In a lung adenocarcinoma murine model, the combination of anlotinib and a PD-1 inhibitor achieved a 71.5% tumor suppression rate (P < 0.0001) and prolonged survival. Additionally, SFRP2-high CAFs create perivascular immunosuppressive niches, hindering the infiltration of antitumor immune cells. In clinical trials, the SFRP2-neutralizing antibody OMP-54F28 demonstrated good safety and partial responses in Phase I, and an ongoing Phase Ib study is evaluating its synergy with immunotherapy. THBS2+ CAFs drive chemoresistance via COL8A1 secretion and binding to tumor cell surface ITGB1 receptors, activating the PI3K/AKT-FAK pathway and inducing EMT, thereby conferring oxaliplatin resistance in colorectal cancer. Preclinical studies showed that THBS2-targeted siRNA or monoclonal antibodies improved oxaliplatin efficacy by 50%. A phase I trial evaluates THBS2 inhibitors, with THBS2-high expression correlating with poor prognosis (HR = 2.1), underscoring its dual role as a predictive biomarker and therapeutic target. In immunometabolic regulation, FAP-high CAFs promote tumor progression by ECM remodeling and immunosuppression. Radiolabeled FAP inhibitors like 90Y-FAPI-46 achieved a 65% disease control rate in metastatic breast cancer. However, on-target toxicity in normal tissues, such as wound-healing sites, requires further optimization of therapeutic windows. Notably, CAFs recruit immunosuppressive cells via the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis and promote immune evasion through a lactate-acidified TME and glutamine metabolic reprogramming. , In this context, the glutaminase inhibitor Telaglenastat (CB-839) significantly prolonged progression-free survival (HR = 0.72) in a Phase II trial of triple-negative breast cancer when combined with chemotherapy.

## Summary and prospects
CAFs are vital components of the TME, for tumor development and spread, as well as in the resistance to cancer treatment. CAFs affect the biology of tumor cells through various processes, such as encouraging the growth, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells as well as increasing tumor cell stemness, leading to resistance to cancer therapies. CAFs directly or indirectly alter the effectiveness of tumor therapies through remodeling the ECM, secreting immunosuppressive factors, and influencing metabolic pathways. CAFs play a central role in cancer therapy resistance through complex mechanisms from forming physical barriers that impede drug penetration to secreting factors that modulate tumor cell signaling and suppressing the immune response. The interactions between CAFs and tumor cells result in resistance to established and novel treatments in various malignancies, such as colorectal, lung, and breast cancers.
However, the origin of CAFs remains unclear. Furthermore, in vivo tracing studies are lacking, which underscores the need for further research on CAF population heterogeneity and the roles of CAFs in tumor biology and clinical outcomes. Future studies should focus on understanding the heterogeneity and functional diversity of CAFs, as well as their interactions with other cancer cells within the TME. Additionally, new techniques and methods should be developed to specifically target CAF subpopulations. The roles of CAFs in different cancer types and treatment stages should be investigated for determining the optimal timing and strategies for therapeutic interventions. Clinical research is required to assess treatment strategies, both monotherapies and combination therapies, targeting CAFs. The clinical efficacy of CAF-targeted therapies must be evaluated for validating their potential to improve treatment outcomes and the standard of living for people with cancer.
In conclusion, CAFs play essential roles in tumor therapy resistance. As such, therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs are promising. However, the heterogeneity and plasticity of CAFs present unique treatment challenges. Future research should aim to further our understanding of the biological characteristics of CAFs and develop more specific methods for targeting CAFs; clinical studies are necessary to assess how CAF-targeted treatments improve the results of cancer treatment.
