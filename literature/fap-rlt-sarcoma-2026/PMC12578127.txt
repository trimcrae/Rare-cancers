## Introduction
The chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model has emerged as an ethically viable and cost-effective preclinical research tool, originating from the fusion of mesodermal layers of the allantois and chorion in chick embryos. Serving as an intermediary between in vitro cell cultures and in vivo xenotransplantation models, the CAM assay provides a highly accessible platform for diverse biomedical applications[ ]. Its extensive vascular network and ease of access make it a widely utilized system for investigating tumor metastasis, angiogenesis[ - ] stem cell biology, tissue grafting, regenerative medicine, and retinal surgery simulations[ ]. Additionally, it is employed in studying biomaterial interactions, human surgical techniques, implant evaluations[ ], photodynamic therapies, and drug delivery strategies. Advanced imaging methodologies such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), computed tomography (CT), ultrasound (US) imaging, and artificial intelligence-driven detection algorithms further expand the utility of the CAM model in biomedical investigations[ - ]. Notably, the CAM assay has been explored as a model for xenograft implantation, particularly in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) research. Studies indicate that IPF xenografts successfully integrate with the chick embryo’s circulatory system, preserving their structural integrity and exhibiting growth on the CAM. Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis using Ki67 and pro-collagen type I markers has confirmed active cell proliferation, emphasizing the model’s relevance in IPF drug testing and therapeutic development[ ]. From an ethical standpoint, the CAM model aligns with the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), as European regulations (Directive 2010/63/EU) do not classify chick embryos as protected organisms before embryonic day 14 (E14)[ , ]. This makes the CAM assay a viable alternative for early-stage research, reducing dependence on mammalian models. Historically, the CAM system was first utilized in vascular studies involving tumor grafts, later advancing to the use of methylcellulose discs for controlled release of pro- and anti-angiogenic factors. Over time, researchers have transitioned toward 3D vascular invasion models and alternative techniques to enhance accuracy in angiogenesis studies[ – ]. Given its immunotolerant properties and capability for real-time imaging, the CAM model is increasingly being recognized as a valuable tool in molecular imaging and regenerative medicine. This review aims to consolidate its well-established applications while exploring how its integration with advanced molecular imaging technologies can further enhance biomedical research[ ].

## Decoding the chicken genome
The chicken genome, the first avian genome to be sequenced, holds significant importance in agriculture, developmental biology, and evolutionary studies. It serves as a genetic link between mammals and birds, offering valuable insights into vertebrate evolution. Consisting of approximately 1.2 billion base pairs distributed across 39 chromosome pairs, including both macro- and micro-chromosomes, it’s genetic analysis dates back to 1936[ , ]. The landmark sequencing project in 2004, facilitated by bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) libraries and a comprehensive physical map, provided a foundation for detailed genomic research[ ]. Furthermore, the discovery of 2.8 million single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) has significantly contributed to understanding genetic diversity and trait associations[ ]. Despite its relatively smaller size compared to mammalian genomes, the chicken genome exhibits substantial gene conservation, making it a valuable model for transgenic studies[ ].The chick embryo (CE) model benefits from this genetic similarity, offering a versatile system for investigating cell transplantation, tissue development, and biomedical research applications[ , ].

## Chorioallantoic membrane: structural composition and developmental dynamics
The chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) in birds develops similarly to the allantois in mammals, extending from the ventral wall of the endodermal hindgut as shown in Fig. . However, in birds, it merges with the chorion to form the CAM. Initially lacking blood vessels, it rapidly establishes a complex vascular network with distinct arteries and veins. Its development begins on embryonic day 3 (Hamburger and Hamilton stage 18), completes by day 10, and reaches full maturation by day 13. This growth demands high cell proliferation, as shown by bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation studies. The CAM primarily functions as the respiratory organ of embryo and contains both blood and lymphatic vessels, with the latter closely resembling mammalian lymphatics. Structurally, the CAM consists of an upper ectodermal epithelium, a mesodermal stroma, and a lower endodermal epithelium. The vascular and lymphatic networks are embedded within the stroma, requiring any applied compounds to pass through the upper layer before reaching the vessels. Maintaining proper humidity is essential, as low humidity in ex ovo cultures leads to excessive epithelial thickening and keratinization, hindering molecule absorption. The CAM, which differs from the yolk membrane, attaches to the eggshell to facilitate calcium absorption and gas exchange. By day 4–5, it adheres to the inner shell membrane, making it necessary to open fertilized eggs before day 3 to prevent CAM rupture. For later-stage experiments, careful dissection is required to expose the CAM without damaging its vasculature[ ]. 

## Chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay
The chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) of the chicken embryo (CE) is essential for embryonic development, facilitating gas exchange, nutrient uptake, and metabolic waste elimination[ ]. It arises from the fusion of the chorion, derived from the trophoblast, and the allantois, an outgrowth of the embryonic intestine. The fully developed CAM is composed of three distinct layers: an outer epithelial layer, a mesodermal layer enriched with connective tissue and blood vessels, and an innermost vascular network that plays a pivotal role in angiogenesis research[ , ]. The extensive vasculature of the CAM supports oxygen-carbon dioxide exchange, calcium absorption from the eggshell, and metabolic waste removal via the allantoic compartment[ ]. CAM formation begins in the early stages of incubation, with the allantois developing around Hamburger and Hamilton (HH) Stage 8 (72–84 hours) and subsequently fusing with the chorion by HH Stage 15 (156–168 hours), leading to a fully functional membrane. The CAM assay involves creating a small opening in the eggshell to expose the membrane, providing direct access for experimental studies such as tumor grafting and drug testing. Various microscopy techniques, including bright-field, fluorescence, and confocal microscopy, are employed to examine blood vessel morphology, quantify vascular density, and analyze branching patterns[ ]. Due to its physiological relevance, the CAM model is widely utilized for studying angiogenesis, drug screening, and cancer progression by assessing tumor growth and vascularization (Fig. )[ ]. In developmental biology, it serves as a valuable tool for investigating vascular development and the impact of genetic modifications on embryogenesis[ ]. Since the angiogenic processes in the CAM closely resemble those in human tissues[ ], this model is instrumental in researching diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular disorders. From an ethical standpoint, the CAM model offers distinct advantages over traditional mammalian models by enabling rapid experimental procedures within a shorter developmental timeframe, reducing ethical concerns associated with vertebrate animal research[ ]. 

## CAM-Based assays: innovations in preclinical drug testing and biomedical research
For more than a century, the chicken chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model has played a fundamental role in medical research, as outlined in Table . Its significance was first demonstrated by Rous and Murphy, who successfully grafted chicken sarcoma cells onto the CAM, leading to observations of tumor formation. Over the years, researchers identified its effectiveness in studying vascular responses, solidifying its status as a reliable in vivo model for angiogenesis research[ , ]. The CAM model has since become instrumental in assessing both pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic compounds, significantly advancing drug screening methodologies and vascular therapy research (Fig. )[ ]. In recent years, its applications have expanded into bioengineering[ , ] cancer investigations, tissue transplantation, and pharmaceutical development. The adoption of ex ovo (shell-less) CAM assays further improved its research potential by enhancing the visualization of vascular structures and biomaterial interactions. These modified models not only support high survival rates but also offer a larger experimental workspace, increasing their practical utility. The CAM’s affordability, ease of use, and physiological resemblance to human tissues make it a valuable preclinical research tool. It serves as a crucial link between laboratory-based studies and in vivo mammalian experiments, particularly in therapeutic kinetics, biodistribution, and pharmacological research[ , ]. 
 

## Angiogenesis insights using the CAM model
Angiogenesis, the process of forming new blood vessels from pre-existing vasculature, is essential for tissue development, wound healing, and regeneration. However, it also plays a pivotal role in tumor progression by facilitating oxygen and nutrient supply. In the CAM model, after xenograft transplantation, tumor cells initially rely on diffusion for nutrient intake, with visible growth emerging within 2–3 days. The vascular response in this model typically presents a “spoke-wheel” pattern, where blood vessels converge around the implanted tumor[ ]. Unlike normal vasculature, tumor-associated blood vessels display irregular branching, uneven distribution, and dilation[ ]. Various scaffolds, including collagen, Matrigel, and fibrin, enhance vascularization, thereby supporting tumor development[ – ]. While vascular sprouting remains the most extensively studied form of angiogenesis, certain conditions promote an alternative mechanism known as intussusception, wherein an existing blood vessel undergoes division to form new branches. A study utilizing 33-hour intravital microscopy on the CAM model revealed a novel non-sprouting angiogenic mechanism termed “coalescent angiogenesis.” In this process, capillary networks merge, eliminating internal tissue pillars and restructuring the vascular network into a more efficient tree-like structure, thereby optimizing blood flow and ensuring rapid vascular expansion while maintaining functionality[ ]. The CAM assay is frequently employed to evaluate angiogenesis-regulating compounds, including methylated resveratrol derivatives, curcumin-capped copper nanoparticles[ ] and FO-ARS-123, a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR-2) inhibitor[ ]. Additionally, dihydroartemisinin has demonstrated anti-angiogenic properties in breast cancer models. Histological techniques such as hematoxylin-eosin staining[ ] and immunohistochemical analyses using CD31 antibodies[ ] are widely applied to examine vascular development and interactions within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, the growing interest in tumor spheroids and 3D cell cultures is attributed to their ease of production and biomedical relevance. Research indicates that co-culturing endothelial and hepatic cells enhances angiogenesis-related protein expression and stimulates endothelial cell sprouting, even in the absence of VEGF, at an optimal Hep-G2/ECV304 ratio of 1:2. The inclusion of VEGF, urea, and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein further amplifies angiogenic gene expression and CD34 marker levels. Moreover, aqueous two-phase Pickering emulsion templates have been demonstrated to effectively vascularize 3D cell co-cultures without requiring additional stimulants. The identification of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as the functional receptor for the virus has significantly propelled the development of antiviral therapies and vaccines[ ]. This innovative approach can be applied to both cell lines and biopsy-derived tissues, expanding its potential in biomedical research[ ].

## Lymphangiogenesis
Lymphangiogenesis plays a critical role in various pathological conditions, including cancer metastasis, lymphedema, and transplant rejection, with lymphatic involvement often worsening patient prognosis[ , ]. Compared to blood vessels, lymphatic vessels facilitate tumor spread at a rate 3–5 times higher due to their increased dilation, which enhances cancer cell infiltration[ ]. Several in vitro and in vivo models are utilized to study lymphangiogenesis, including endothelial cell cultures, thoracic duct explants, and embryoid body-based systems[ , ]. Among preclinical methods, the aortic ring assay serves as a tool for studying angiogenesis and evaluating potential therapeutic compounds; however, it lacks the full complexity of physiological interactions[ ]. To investigate lymphatic dysfunction, models such as VEGF-C overexpression in tumor cells, Freund’s adjuvant injections, and microsurgical lymphatic resection have been developed[ – ]. The mouse tail model is commonly employed for assessing lymphedema therapies, while fluorescent reporter mice allow for real-time imaging of lymphatic dynamics[ ]. Furthermore, rat and rabbit models contribute to refining surgical techniques for lymphedema, though species-specific immune responses often pose challenges. The CAM model serves as a valuable platform for investigating lymphangiogenesis, immune cell migration, and metastatic mechanisms. Studies have demonstrated that VEGF and VEGF-C play crucial roles in promoting lymphatic vessel formation and cancer dissemination, particularly through the VEGF-C/flt4 signaling pathway[ ]. Additionally, tumor-driven lymphangiogenesis has been observed in pancreatic carcinoma and glioma models, with cytokines like VEGF-A contributing to the development of both blood and lymphatic vessels. Everolimus has been identified as a regulator with dual effects on lymphatic growth, while Burkitt lymphoma xenografts provide opportunities for real-time metastasis tracking. However, one limitation of the CAM model is the lack of antibodies specifically targeting chick embryo antigens, which can hinder certain immunohistochemical analyses[ ].

## Retinal surgery
The chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model offers a practical, cost-effective, and ethical platform for studying retinal surgical techniques, vascular responses, and drug delivery strategies. Due to its dense vascularization, the CAM closely mimics the retinal vasculature, making it an excellent substitute for testing surgical tools, coagulation methods, and novel therapeutics without the need for live animal models. In retinal surgery research, the CAM has been employed to compare the efficiency of microsurgical scissors with the Pulsed Electron Avalanche Knife (PEAK), assessing parameters such as vessel manipulation, bleeding control, and coagulation effectiveness[ ]. Its thin and transparent membrane enables precise visualization of microsurgical procedures using fluorescence microscopy and an X-Y-Z micromanipulator, ensuring accurate evaluation of tissue response. However, limitations exist, as gel matrices do not always replicate the biomechanical properties of retinal tissues, potentially leading to inconsistent results. Additionally, commonly used viability markers, including trypan blue and propidium iodide, face diffusion challenges, and Calcein acetoxymethyl ester (AM) preloading is complex and difficult to image effectively. Although live animal eye models provide an alternative for testing, they are expensive, require more time, and raise ethical concerns, particularly for early-stage experimental evaluations[ , ]. The CAM model addresses these issues by allowing partial tissue excision while maintaining blood circulation, thereby providing an in vivo-like setting under controlled conditions for vascular intervention studies. Its vessel diameters are comparable to those of retinal blood vessels, facilitating investigations into cannulation, artery-vein differentiation, and real-time blood flow analysis. Furthermore, the CAM model has been instrumental in testing fibrinolytic drug applications, such as the Pulsed Liquid Microjet for targeted clot dissolution, as well as in studying aneurysm formation through fine-gauge needle techniques[ , ]. The ability to replicate conditions like retinal hemorrhage, vascular occlusion, and microsurgical interventions further reinforces the CAM model’s value in refining retinal surgical approaches and advancing therapeutic innovations[ ].

## Neurological surgery
The quail CAM model, alongside the widely used chicken model, has been instrumental in neuroblastoma (NB) research. Azar et al established an ex ovo CAM assay to investigate IGFBP-2-mediated angiogenesis, which involved incubating quail eggs, inoculating cells into collagen sponges, and analyzing vascularization patterns[ , ]. Hecht and colleagues employed the in ovo CAM model to explore the role of Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) and c-Met in NB invasiveness. Their experiment introduced 3 × 106 cells in 50 μL of medium into a Thermanox ring placed on the CAM of an 8-day incubated embryo. After 6 days of incubation, the developed tumors were examined for further analysis. Ribatti et al pioneered the use of the chicken CAM model for neuroblastoma studies[ , ]. This model has also been utilized to induce tumors from circulating cancer stem cells in breast cancer patients, highlighting its significance in cancer research. With technological advancements, repetitive ultrasonography has emerged as a valuable tool for enhancing tumor monitoring and follow-up studies within CAM models[ ].

## Gynecological and urological surgery
A novel in vivo application of the CAM model for investigating gynecological and urological cancers offers a cost-effective, time-efficient, and labor-saving alternative, particularly for initial screening experiments. The implementation of this model could significantly benefit the 177 000 individuals diagnosed annually with these cancers[ ]. To expand its utility, researchers have refined the CAM model for tumor engraftment, leveraging the immunocompromised state of early chicken embryos. This adaptation facilitates the implantation of both mouse and human cancer cells, making it a valuable tool for studying various malignancies. The model has demonstrated success in engrafting ovarian, kidney, prostate, and bladder cancers, efficiently supporting the growth of murine and human tumor cell lines. Additionally, freshly obtained primary human tumor tissues, whether processed into single-cell suspensions or implanted as solid tissue fragments, exhibit high engraftment rates, further validating the model’s effectiveness[ , ].

## Therapeutic kinetics and biodistribution

## Targeted drug carrier
The CAM model serves as a valuable platform for assessing drug delivery systems (DDSs) designed to target specific disease sites or wounds[ ]. A primary role of DDSs is to regulate drug release rates, ensuring a controlled and sustained delivery mechanism. Since pharmacokinetics and biodistribution are crucial in evaluating drug effectiveness, various administration techniques have been investigated[ ]. While topical application on the CAM enables drug absorption into circulation, recent advancements emphasize direct vascular injection, offering greater precision and a closer resemblance to clinical applications. This method enhances therapeutic accuracy, mimics real-world treatment conditions, and significantly improves drug bioavailability for targeted therapies[ , , ].

## Biocompatibility
Biocompatibility refers to the ability of implanted tissues, biomaterials, or synthetic scaffolds to integrate seamlessly into the host system without inducing immune rejection, inflammation, or adverse effects. The chicken chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model has been widely employed to explore allogenic and xenogeneic tissue and organ transplantation. One of the earliest studies in this field was conducted by Murphy, who transplanted various chicken tissues, including the spleen, liver, kidney, and bone marrow, observing high survival rates following homologous grafting. Over time, researchers expanded their work by successfully transplanting allogenic tissues such as the liver[ ] mesonephros, adrenal gland, and cerebellum. Additionally, xenogeneic grafts involving human bone[ , ] kidney, skin[ ], and ovaries[ ] have been investigated. The immature immune system of the chick embryo before embryonic day 15 plays a crucial role in enabling successful graft acceptance. Transplanted tissues survive and develop by forming vascular anastomoses and benefiting from CAM-driven neoangiogenesis, which supplies nutrients and essential growth factors. In recent years, the CAM model has gained prominence in stem cell research and biomaterial testing, supporting angiogenesis and tissue regeneration[ ]. Biodegradable synthetic polymers such as polylactic acid (PLA)/ poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) scaffolds have been employed to deliver growth factors like VEGF, fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), enhancing vascular development[ ]. Alternative angiogenic agents such as 2-deoxy-D-ribose and 17β-Estradiol have also demonstrated significant potential[ ]. The primary goal of tissue engineering remains to improve biocompatibility, reduce inflammatory responses, and achieve controlled growth factor release to optimize tissue integration and regeneration[ , ].

## Drug effectiveness
The CAM model is extensively utilized to evaluate the activity, toxicity, and efficacy of various drugs, particularly in relation to grafted tumors. It plays a crucial role in investigating both pro- and anti-angiogenic agents, including natural compounds, growth factors, antibodies, synthetic drugs, anticancer treatments, and antibiotics. Through this model, researchers gain critical insights into drug mechanisms and therapeutic applications, making it a valuable tool in preclinical drug assessment[ , ].

## Pro-angiogenic factors
Pro-angiogenic agents facilitate the formation of new blood vessels, improving blood circulation and supporting tissue regeneration. The CAM assay has been instrumental in evaluating over 140 stimulators[ ] often incorporated into nanoparticles, drug conjugates, or scaffolds for targeted therapeutic applications[ ]. Various growth factors, including VEGF121, VEGF165, and FGF, have been studied in fibrin matrices to assess their angiogenic potential,[ ]. These agents play a pivotal role in regenerative medicine and wound healing, primarily by promoting vascularization[ , ].

## Anti-angiogenic factors
The CAM assay is a well-established in vivo model for assessing the efficacy of anti-angiogenic agents[ ]. Approximately 360 such agents have been evaluated, categorized into direct inhibitors, which act on endothelial cells, and indirect inhibitors, which block pro-angiogenic factors[ ]. Avastin (bevacizumab), an anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody, has exhibited significant angiogenesis inhibition in CAM models, leading to a 32% increase in the avascular surface[ ]. The yolk sac membrane (YSM) has also been employed to verify Avastin’s effectiveness. Additionally, Sunitinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, functions as a negative control in angiogenesis studies[ ]. The anti-cancer drug Paclitaxel has demonstrated anti-angiogenic effects, with various polymeric carriers influencing its drug release rates[ ]. Moreover, photodynamic therapy (PDT) utilizing photosensitizers, along with radiotherapy combined with radiosensitizers, further enhances anti-angiogenic efficacy by suppressing vascular growth[ ].

## Skin grafting
The chimeric skin-CAM system presents a viable alternative to animal-based sensitization models, particularly when utilizing human grafts. Initially introduced by Goodpasture, this model supports skin maintenance in a physiological state by facilitating oxygenation through vascular connections, in contrast to in vitro organotypic explants. The continuous blood supply sustains graft viability for the lifespan of the chick embryo, allowing the preservation of epidermal Langerhans cells (LCs) for several days[ ]. Unlike organotypic models, this system has demonstrated LC migration in reaction to allergens, making it a valuable tool for assessing allergic responses and sensitization potential[ , ].

## Lotusine in diabetes management: from HepG2 Cells to CAM model
Lotusine (LT), a natural compound from Nelumbo nucifera, is being explored as a safer antioxidant therapy for diabetes. This study investigates LT’s protective effects against glucose-induced oxidative stress in HepG2 cells by assessing antioxidant enzymes and insulin signaling. Additionally, the chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model is proposed to evaluate its in vivo vascular and therapeutic responses[ ].

## Implantations

## Tumor implantation
When assessing the CAM model in tumor biology, a notable angiogenic response occurs when tumor tissues are implanted between embryonic days (ED) 8 and 10, whereas this response is diminished when implantation takes place on ED 11 or 12[ ]. This variation is linked to the high mitotic activity of CAM endothelial cells up to ED 10, which subsequently declines. Research indicates that osteosarcoma cells implanted at ED 9[ ] and urological cancer cells introduced at ED 10[ ] successfully form tumor growths. Furthermore, while HuH7 liver cancer cells have been introduced as early as ED 7, the optimal implantation period is generally considered to be ED 8–10, as it maximizes vascularization and cell proliferation[ ].

## Bone implantation
The study investigates the potential of CAM implantation for human bone cylinders in facilitating active bone remodeling and ectopic bone formation. To analyze bone formation and resorption, high-resolution micro-CT and histological techniques were employed[ ]. The ex ovo CAM model was explored as a bioreactor for assessing the osteogenic potential of biomaterials, with a focus on Runt-Related Transcription Factor 2 (RUNX-2), a key regulator of bone development and osteoblast differentiation[ , ]. Findings revealed early bone formation in Functionalized Apatite Calcium Phosphate Scaffold 1 (FACaP1) and Functionalized Apatite Calcium Phosphate Scaffold 2 (FACaP2), showing similarities to the Demineralized Bone Matrix (DBM) positive control, whereas FA (negative control) exhibited only background RUNX-2 expression. Notably, FACaP2 demonstrated higher RUNX-2 expression compared to FACaP1[ ] and DBM, which correlated with increased osteopontin expression and enhanced mineral deposition. This was attributed to its high CaP content and the presence of OCP/HA phases, which promoted osteoprogenitor cell differentiation. In contrast, DBM, lacking a mineral phase, depended on bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) for osteogenic differentiation. These findings indicate FACaP2 as a promising scaffold for early osteogenic marker expression[ ]. Recognizing the dynamic nature of bone, the study utilized an innovative 3D registration approach for sequential scans, allowing the differentiation between pre-existing bone and newly formed mineralized tissue. This methodology addresses a key challenge in bone research. The results support CAM as a reliable short-term in vivo model for studying bone regeneration, serving as a crucial link between in vitro and in vivo biomaterial testing[ , ].

## Lung tissue implantation
The optimal embryonic development stage for xenograft implantation was evaluated using Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) lung tissue fragments, revealing a higher success rate (96%) on Days 9–10 compared to 48% on Days 7–8. Similarly, IPF fibroblast-derived xenografts implanted on Day 9 exhibited an engraftment success rate exceeding 95%. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining confirmed the preservation of nuclear morphology after 7 days of cultivation on the CAM model. Histological analysis identified airway structures consisting of a single epithelial cell layer (CK18) surrounded by fibroblasts (TE-7) and alpha-smooth muscle actin (ɑ-SMA)-positive blood vessels, maintaining features observed in original and implanted tissues. To verify whether IPF xenografts retained characteristics of the native tissue, airway structures and immune microenvironments were examined, demonstrating structural similarity. Fibroblast-derived xenografts developed fibroblastic foci-like formations, composed of dense ɑ-SMA-positive myofibroblasts and TE-7-positive fibroblasts, resembling IPF lung tissue. Furthermore, human leukocytes (CD45+) and macrophages (CD68+ and CD163+) persisted in IPF lung-derived xenografts cultivated on the CAM. To extend this investigation, green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing IPF fibroblasts were introduced onto the CAM, where they migrated from the Matrigel into the membrane, exhibiting an activated state marked by fibroblast activation protein (FAP), α-SMA, and pro-collagen type I expression. Additionally, mesenchymal and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling gene expression was significantly upregulated in CAM-cultured fibroblasts compared to in vitro controls, confirming an enhanced activation phenotype in the CAM model[ ].

## Dental implantation
The CAM model has emerged as a valuable preclinical platform for dental research, particularly in exploring tooth regeneration. A groundbreaking tooth organoid, derived from mouse dental stem cells, has been successfully developed, laying the foundation for human tooth organoid generation. These tissue-derived organoids are engineered by embedding stem cells within a 3D extracellular matrix – such as Matrigel – and exposing them to growth factors that simulate developmental signaling pathways. Compared to traditional 2D cultures, organoids more accurately replicate the structural and functional properties of native dental tissue, establishing them as a promising tool for dental tissue engineering and regenerative medicine[ ].

## Innovative approaches in anti-vascular therapy and diagnosis
The chicken chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model serves as an advanced platform for assessing anti-vascular therapies and diagnostic techniques applicable to human surgical procedures. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) employs light-sensitive agents to target and disrupt tumor vasculature, while radiotherapy examines the effects of radiation-induced angiogenic changes and tumor response. PET/CT imaging enhances tumor visualization through precise tracer mapping, whereas ultrasound imaging allows for real-time monitoring of vascular structures and tumor progression. Additionally, AI-driven detection methods, utilizing deep learning algorithms, improve embryo viability screening, supporting advancements in cancer therapy, surgical techniques, and drug development.

## Photodynamic therapy
The highly vascularized nature of the chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model makes it an effective platform for studying the vascular impact of photodynamic therapy (PDT). This treatment involves the activation of a photosensitizing agent (PS), which, when exposed to light of a specific wavelength, interacts with molecular oxygen, leading to therapeutic effects[ – ]. Due to their hydrophobic nature, photosensitizers are typically incorporated into liposomes, biocompatible solvents like N-methyl-pyrrolidone, or diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride to improve delivery and therapeutic effectiveness. The CAM model offers critical insights into PDT-induced vascular changes, making it a valuable tool in preclinical research[ , ].

## Radiotherapy
Chicken embryos demonstrate notable resistance to high doses of X-rays, making them a valuable model for irradiation studies. Exposure to X-rays has been observed to reduce blood vessel formation, likely due to vascular targeting within the initial hours. Furthermore, X-ray exposure affects the chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) by triggering early apoptosis, facilitating the screening of radioprotective compounds, modifying extracellular matrix proteins involved in angiogenesis, and influencing tumor-induced vascularization. Recent applications of the CAM model include evaluating radiosensitizers, such as etanidazole, a hypoxic cell radiosensitizer, in tumor-bearing chick embryos, reinforcing its significance in radiation research[ , ]. Non-invasive radiographic methods have become crucial for identifying, diagnosing, and managing malignant tumors in both humans and animals. They offer targeted and accurate treatment with fewer side effects compared to traditional surgical or conventional approaches. However, high costs and complex procedures limit their accessibility. Integrating advanced technologies such as nanotechnology, artificial intelligence, and machine learning could enhance their precision and affordability, making them more widely usable[ ].

## Positron emission tomography (PET)/Computed tomography (CT) imaging
The CAM model has been effectively employed for in vivo Positron Emission Tomography (PET)/Computed Tomography (CT) imaging in a human glioblastoma model. By integrating anatomic CT with PET imaging, this approach allows for accurate tumor tracer localization while minimizing interference from embryonic uptake. The use of the Minerve small-animal imaging cell facilitated smooth transitions between PET and CT modalities, ensuring stability throughout the imaging process. Additionally, its anesthesia and warming features contributed to consistent imaging results. The implementation of 3D image reconstruction in combination with Fluorine-18 Sodium Fluoride (18F-NaF) imaging further validated both imaging and anesthesia protocols, underscoring the CAM model’s significance in advanced preclinical imaging research[ ].

## Ultrasound imaging
The chicken embryo and CAM model serve as a versatile platform for ultrasound imaging studies, including Intravascular Ultrasound (IVUS) and photoacoustic imaging, both with and without contrast agents, in 2D and 3D imaging[ ]. This model also facilitates the development of novel ultrasound pulsing techniques and transducers. Furthermore, it supports the evaluation of innovative contrast agents and their vascular behavior under flow conditions, making it a valuable tool for tumor imaging and blood flow analysis through Contrast-Enhanced Ultrasound (CEUS). Typically, tumors are grafted onto CAM vessels of 8–9-day-old embryos, which are cultivated ex ovo after extraction at day 3 of incubation, ensuring optimal conditions for imaging research[ – ].

## AI-driven detection and algorithms
Chick embryos serve as a vital system for virus culture in vaccine production, where the quality of the embryo directly influences vaccine efficacy. It is essential to eliminate unsuitable embryos (such as those that are dead, damaged, contaminated, or weak), with CAM blood vessel morphology during specific embryonic development (ED) stages playing a key role in this selection[ ]. To improve detection, machine inspection techniques utilizing image processing methods such as segmentation and classification focus on identifying major blood vessels. However, challenges persist due to image quality and the complexity of embryo structures[ ]. The introduction of advanced AI algorithms, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), has significantly improved image reconstruction and classification accuracy[ ]. A breakthrough in this field came in 2017 when Geng et al developed a CNN-based viability detection method, achieving an accuracy rate of 99.5%[ ]. However, detecting weak embryos remained a challenge until Chen et al introduced WEDNet, which incorporated Residual Multiscale Fire Blocks (RMFB) to enhance accuracy to 99.35%. These AI-driven, non-invasive techniques now play a crucial role in improving embryo selection for vaccine production and CAM-based research[ ]. Recent advancements in anatomical imaging have transformed veterinary diagnostics by enabling quicker and more precise disease detection. These technologies support less invasive techniques, ensuring better patient comfort. They also aid in detailed surgical preparation, improving treatment outcomes[ – ].

## Advantages
The chicken chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model presents multiple advantages, making it an essential tool in biomedical research. Its short experimental timeframe and ease of use allow for efficient studies with minimal resource investment. Additionally, the model is cost-effective, supports high-throughput screening, and enables multiple tests within a single CAM, improving both reproducibility and data reliability. With its highly vascularized environment, the CAM model provides ideal conditions for tumor growth and angiogenesis studies, allowing for continuous visualization and in vivo imaging without requiring animal restraint or anesthesia. Moreover, the naturally immunodeficient nature of the CAM facilitates the engraftment of primary human cell lines, making it a promising platform for personalized medicine research. The well-characterized biology and physiology of the CAM, along with its varied drug administration routes, enhance its versatility in pharmacological and toxicological studies. As a closed system, it effectively minimizes external variables, ensuring controlled experimental conditions. A key advantage is its ability to provide real-time monitoring of cellular responses, offering valuable insights into tumor progression and drug efficacy, further reinforcing its significance in preclinical research[ ].

## Disadvantages
The CAM model, while valuable in biomedical research, has several limitations that impact its applicability. One primary drawback is its short observation window, which restricts its use in long-term studies. Additionally, technical challenges, such as the presence of eggshell fragments, may inadvertently influence angiogenesis. Another limitation is the inability to evaluate tumor-immune interactions, as the chick immune system remains immature during early development and later generates only non-specific inflammatory responses. Furthermore, distinguishing newly formed vessels from pre-existing ones presents a challenge. The model is also highly sensitive to variations in pH, osmolarity, and oxygen levels, potentially affecting experimental consistency. The limited availability of species-specific antibodies and cytokines complicates tissue characterization, while species-specific physiological responses – including differences in drug metabolism and immune function – reduce the model’s translational relevance to human studies. This makes it difficult to directly apply findings to human research. Moreover, the oral administration of drugs cannot be tested, and the lack of systemic circulation comparable to mammals may limit its utility in accurate drug distribution studies[ ].

## Future perspective and challenges
The chicken chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model has emerged as a valuable platform in biomedical research, offering a highly vascularized system for investigating angiogenesis, tumor biology, drug delivery, and biomaterial integration[ ]. Its cost-effectiveness, ethical feasibility, and rapid experimentation make it a compelling alternative to traditional mammalian models[ ]. However, certain limitations affect its direct application in human surgeries and implant research. The physiological and immunological disparities between avian and human tissues raise concerns regarding its clinical relevance. Additionally, the short experimental timeframe – restricted to the embryonic period before hatching – poses challenges for long-term studies on implant stability and tissue regeneration. Another drawback is the limited availability of avian-specific antibodies and molecular markers, which complicates biochemical analyses. Despite these challenges, the future of the CAM model in human surgery and implant research appears promising. Advances in 3D bioprinting, tissue engineering, and gene editing may enable the development of more sophisticated and humanized CAM models[ ]. Furthermore, high-resolution imaging and AI-driven analysis can enhance data accuracy, improving predictions of human physiological responses (Table )[ ]. By standardizing experimental protocols and incorporating microfluidic systems, researchers can refine implant testing methodologies. As regulatory agencies recognize its potential, the CAM model could become a key component in preclinical validation pipelines, expediting the development of safer and more effective surgical implants and tissue grafts. 

## Discussion and conclusion
The chicken chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model has become an adaptable and cost-efficient platform for preclinical research, particularly in human surgeries and implant evaluations. Its unique structural composition and rapid vascularization make it an essential tool in various biomedical applications. In the study of angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis related to gynecological and urological malignancies, the CAM model enables the observation of tumor-driven vascular growth, offering crucial insights into tumor progression and metastatic behavior. Research has shown that tumor cells transplanted onto the CAM can trigger angiogenic responses within 72 hours, closely resembling early-stage tumor vascularization in humans[ ]. The integration of AI-driven detection algorithms has transformed CAM-based assay analyses by improving image processing for vascular pattern identification, tumor progression tracking, and drug response assessment. Machine learning models facilitate the precise quantification of angiogenesis, automate blood vessel segmentation, and predict drug efficacy, thereby reducing human error and enhancing reproducibility[ ]. Beyond oncology, the CAM model has been effectively utilized in retinal surgery simulations. Researchers have leveraged this model to evaluate novel surgical tools and techniques, such as microinjections and endoscopic procedures, prior to transitioning to mammalian models. Its immunodeficient environment also supports the engraftment of human tumor tissues, allowing for the controlled study of tumor behavior and therapeutic responses. Additionally, the CAM model plays a critical role in evaluating drug pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and biocompatibility. AI-assisted computational approaches complement these investigations by predicting drug absorption and clearance rates, optimizing experimental protocols, and reducing the reliance on extensive animal testing[ ]. Despite its numerous advantages, the CAM model has inherent limitations. Its short developmental timeframe restricts its use for long-term studies, and the immature immune system does not fully replicate immune-tumor interactions observed in humans. Moreover, variations in tumor growth dynamics on the CAM require careful standardization for each specific application. In conclusion, the CAM model serves as a crucial intermediary between in vitro studies and mammalian in vivo research. The incorporation of AI-driven imaging and analytical tools is expected to expand its capabilities further, establishing it as a key resource in preclinical investigations. Future advancements will focus on integrating deep learning frameworks and AI-powered diagnostics to enhance experimental precision, ultimately bridging the gap between laboratory-based research and clinical applications.
