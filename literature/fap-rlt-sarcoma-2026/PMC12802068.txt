## INTRODUCTION
Gastric cancer remains a major global health challenge, ranking fifth in terms of incidence and cancer-related mortality worldwide [ ]. In Korea, it is the fourth most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with a persistently high disease burden despite advances in screening and treatment [ ]. Accurate staging at diagnosis is essential for selecting appropriate therapy, predicting prognosis, and avoiding unnecessary surgery [ ].
Current imaging strategies for gastric cancer rely primarily on endoscopy and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) for anatomical staging [ ]. Positron emission tomography (PET), which visualizes tumor metabolism by detecting positron-emitting radiotracers, is an important adjunct to oncological imaging. The most widely used tracer, 2-[18F]-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose ([18F]FDG), allows the early detection of metabolically active diseases and quantitative assessment of parameters such as standardized uptake value (SUV), metabolic tumor volume (MTV), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) [ ]. Integration with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging further enhances lesion localization, forming the basis for hybrid PET imaging [ ].
However, the diagnostic performance of [18F]FDG PET/CT in gastric cancer varies widely depending on the tumor histology and molecular phenotype. Poorly cohesive, mucinous, and signet ring cell (SRC) carcinomas often exhibit low or absent [18F]FDG uptake owing to reduced glucose transporter (GLUT) expression and abundant mucin content, leading to false-negative findings and underestimation of the disease burden. Consequently, the role of [18F]FDG PET/CT in gastric cancer remains complementary rather than definitive in current clinical practice, primarily aiding the detection of distant metastases or equivocal lesions [ ].
To overcome these limitations, novel radiotracers that target the tumor microenvironment have gained attention. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP)-targeted tracers visualize cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), a dominant component of the tumor stroma that contributes to invasion, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Early studies have shown that FAP-targeted PET imaging demonstrates high tumor-to-background contrast and robust uptake across various malignancies, including gastric cancer [ ].
This review summarizes the evolving role of PET/CT in gastric cancer, with a focus on [18F]FDG- and FAP-targeted imaging. We discuss the respective diagnostic performance, clinical applications, and potential implications for patient management, highlighting how FAP-targeted imaging may complement or surpass conventional metabolic PET for selected gastric cancer subtypes.

## [18F]FDG PET
[18F]FDG, the most widely used radiopharmaceutical in oncological PET imaging, is a radioactive glucose analog in which the hydroxyl group on the 2-carbon of glucose is replaced with fluorine-18 [ ]. After entering cells via GLUTs, it is phosphorylated by hexokinase to form [18F]FDG-6-phosphate, which cannot proceed through glycolysis and therefore becomes metabolically trapped within the cell. Malignant cells exhibit markedly increased glycolytic activity due to the overexpression of GLUT1 and GLUT3 and the upregulation of hexokinase, resulting in enhanced [18F]FDG uptake compared with that in normal tissues [ ]. As glucose competes with [18F]FDG for uptake, hyperglycemia or recent insulin administration can reduce tumor visibility on PET/CT. Therefore, the patients are required to fast before scanning to ensure optimal serum glucose levels.
Physiological [18F]FDG uptake is observed in the brain, myocardium, liver, spleen, and gastrointestinal tract, with urinary excretion through the kidneys and bladder [ ]. Uptake in the stomach wall is typically homogeneous and low; however, it may appear as a round focal area of moderate activity in the contracted stomach [ ]. Koga et al. [ ] examined normal gastric [18F]FDG distribution in 22 patients and found significantly higher uptake in the upper stomach than in the middle and lower portions.
Understanding the physiological and benign causes of [18F]FDG uptake, including inflammation, infection, and benign tumors, is essential, as these factors, along with variable avidity among different malignancies, can complicate image interpretation.

## Initial staging
[18F]FDG uptake in primary gastric cancer varies according to the lesion size, histology, and molecular subtype. Small or early-stage (T1–T2) lesions may be underestimated owing to partial volume effects [ ], and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guideline states that initial workup with [18F]FDG PET/CT may not be appropriate for stage T1 [ ]. Furthermore, tumor histology is a major determinant of [18F]FDG uptake in gastric cancer. Using the World Health Organization classification and grading system, tubular adenocarcinoma exhibits higher uptake than poorly cohesive carcinoma or SRC carcinoma, and well- or moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma shows greater uptake than poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma [ ]. Low [18F]FDG uptake in SRC is mainly attributed to decreased GLUT1 expression and hexokinase activity, together with low cellular density and abundant mucin, which reduce apparent tracer accumulation. Chon et al. [ ] evaluated 727 patients with advanced gastric cancer. They found that the average SUVmax of SRC was 51% lower than that of well-to-moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma (4.5±1.9 vs. 9.2±6.1, P<0.001), and most of the patients with SRC histology had SUVmax <5 [ ]. Similarly, according to the Lauren classification, the intestinal subtype exhibits higher [18F]FDG activity and detection rates than non-intestinal subtypes [ ]. Beyond morphological and histological variations, molecular features also influence [18F]FDG avidity in gastric cancer, with Epstein-Barr virus-positive and microsatellite instability-high subtypes tending to show higher uptake, which may be associated with lymphoid-rich stroma and increased glucose metabolism of infiltrating immune cells within the tumor microenvironment [ ].
 The [18F]FDG uptake pattern in lymph node (LN) and distant metastases corresponds to that of the primary tumor, reflecting similar histological characteristics across disease sites. In a prospective study, Kim et al. [ ] reported that the sensitivity for detecting LN metastasis was lower in SRC than in other histological subtypes, and the primary tumor SUV was the only independent factor significantly associated with detection sensitivity in multivariate analysis. Similarly, Yoshioka et al. [ ] found that LN and liver metastases showed higher SUV in well- and moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas than in poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas or SRC. Additionally, Dębiec et al. [ ] reported that non-intestinal or mucinous histology, including SRC, was associated with an increased likelihood of false-negative findings for distant metastases on [18F]FDG PET/CT.
Meta-analyses have revealed that [18F]FDG PET/CT has high specificity but moderate sensitivity for staging gastric cancer. Zhang et al. [ ] (58 studies, 9,997 patients) and Kim et al. [ ] (19 studies, 2,195 patients) reported pooled sensitivities and specificities of approximately 45%–60% and 87%–97%, respectively, for detecting nodal and distant metastases, indicating better specificity but lower sensitivity than CECT. Despite its limited accuracy in nodal diseases, [18F]FDG PET/CT remains useful for confirming distant metastases and identifying occult metastatic lesions. In a systematic review of 1,422 patients, Foley et al. [ ] found management changes in 3%–29% of cases, primarily by preventing unnecessary surgery. Accordingly, the 2022 and 2024 Korean Practice Guidelines for Gastric Cancer recommend [18F]FDG PET as a supplementary diagnostic tool in staging workup [ ].
In summary, [18F]FDG PET/CT is useful for the initial staging of gastric cancer, particularly in patients with locally advanced disease or equivocal metastatic findings on conventional imaging. Recognition of the histological and molecular variability in [18F]FDG uptake is essential for accurate interpretation and appropriate clinical application.

## Recurrent disease evaluation
[18F]FDG avidity of the primary tumor is associated with diagnostic sensitivity for recurrent gastric cancer, with higher detection rates observed in patients with [18F]FDG-avid primary tumors [ ]. In a retrospective study, [18F]FDG PET/CT showed higher sensitivities for detecting overall, non-anastomotic, and peritoneal recurrence in patients with [18F]FDG-avid primaries (SUVmax ≥4) than in those with non-avid tumors (SUVmax <4) [ ]. Therefore, baseline [18F]FDG PET/CT facilitates the evaluation of recurrence by serving as a reference for metabolic comparison.
Meta-analyses have shown that [18F]FDG PET/CT and CECT demonstrate comparable diagnostic performance for recurrence, with pooled sensitivities of 70%–80% and specificities of 80%–90% [ ]. While [18F]FDG PET/CT provides higher specificity and better detection of bone metastases, CECT remains more sensitive in identifying peritoneal diseases [ ]. In patients with suspected recurrence based on CT findings, elevated tumor markers, or clinical symptoms, [18F]FDG PET/CT offers additional value for treatment planning [ ]. Accordingly, the 2022 and 2024 Korean Practice Guidelines for Gastric Cancer recommend [18F]FDG PET/CT as a supplementary tool for evaluating suspected recurrence after curative surgery [ ].

## Treatment response evaluation
Several studies have investigated the role of [18F]FDG PET/CT in evaluating treatment response after neoadjuvant therapy for gastric cancer. While some studies have demonstrated a significant association between metabolic changes (e.g., SUV reduction) and pathological response [ ], others have reported no such correlation [ ]. Although the results across studies remain inconsistent, [18F]FDG PET/CT still appears clinically valuable, as a cohort study demonstrated that a ≥35% reduction in tumor SUV yielded a 90% negative predictive value for an early pathological response, supporting its potential for identifying non-responders to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [ ]. A recent retrospective study assessed the diagnostic performance of metabolic parameters from baseline and post-treatment [18F]FDG PET/CT in predicting pathological responses in patients with locally advanced gastric cancer [ ]. Post-treatment MTV and TLG cutoff values of 1.68 cm3 (area under the curve [AUC], 0.683) and 4.71 cm3 (AUC, 0.683) were suggested for predicting poor responders. Meanwhile, an SUV reduction cutoff of 4.82 yielded the highest accuracy for tumor regression grade 0 (AUC, 0.923). Similarly, Jin et al. [ ] demonstrated through multiparametric analysis that the Lauren intestinal subtype and a reduction in TLG of >45.2% were independent predictors of pathological remission after neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
Despite these promising results, substantial heterogeneity exists among studies in terms of treatment regimens, timing of posttreatment [18F]FDG PET/CT, metabolic parameters analyzed, and reference standards used. While [18F]FDG PET/CT shows potential for assessing the therapeutic response in gastric cancer, its clinical utility remains to be validated through well-designed prospective trials.
While [18F]FDG PET/CT remains the most established molecular imaging tool for gastric cancer, its diagnostic performance varies considerably depending on tumor histology and molecular subtype. Poorly cohesive, mucinous, and SRC carcinoma often show low or absent [18F]FDG uptake, which limits lesion detectability. Moreover, the physiological uptake in the stomach and surrounding structures can further complicate image interpretation. These limitations have prompted the exploration of alternative tracers, such as fibroblast activation protein inhibitors (FAPIs), which target the tumor stroma rather than cancer cell metabolism and may offer improved lesion contrast in [18F]FDG-negative gastric cancer subtypes.

## FAPI PET
FAP is a type II transmembrane serine protease that is highly expressed in CAFs, a major stromal component of the tumor microenvironment. CAFs, which constitute up to 80% of the stromal fraction in solid tumors, play crucial roles in tumor progression, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and metastasis via complex crosstalk with cancer cells [ ]. Although FAP expression is minimal in most normal adult tissues, it is markedly upregulated in over 90% of epithelial malignancies, particularly at the invasive margins of tumors, making it an attractive molecular target for imaging and therapy [ ].
Radiolabeled FAPIs have been developed to visualize and potentially treat FAP-expressing tumors by targeting the stromal compartment rather than the tumor cells. This mechanism enables high lesion contrast even in cancers with low metabolic activity on [18F]FDG PET, such as mucinous or SRC carcinomas. Moreover, FAPI tracers exhibit favorable biodistribution, characterized by low physiological uptake in the brain, liver, and gastrointestinal tract, as well as rapid renal clearance, which collectively contribute to a high tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) [ ]. Unlike [18F]FDG, FAPI PET does not require fasting or glycemic control, allowing for simpler patient preparation and more consistent image quality [ ].
Early clinical studies have demonstrated intense [68Ga]Ga-FAPI uptake across various malignancies, including sarcoma, breast, lung, cholangiocarcinoma, and gastrointestinal cancers, with notably lower physiological background signals [ ]. Among these, gastric cancer has emerged as one of the most promising indications, as FAPI PET achieves superior lesion conspicuity compared with [18F]FDG, particularly in histological subtypes such as poorly cohesive carcinoma or mucinous adenocarcinomas that are often [18F]FDG-negative [ ]. This is particularly relevant for the diffuse-type, which is characterized by extensive stromal components that may contribute to increased FAP expression within the tumor microenvironment [ ].

## Initial staging
Several studies have investigated clinicopathological factors that influence FAPI uptake in gastric cancer. In Jiang et al.’s study [ ], [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 demonstrated higher mean SUVmax in advanced (T2–T4) compared with early (T1) tumors (9.7±4.4 vs. 3.1±1.5, P<0.001) and in larger (>4 cm) compared with smaller (≤4 cm) lesions (11.0±4.5 vs. 4.5±3.2, P=0.002). However, no significant difference in uptake was observed among different histological subtypes (adenocarcinoma vs. SRC, P=0.599) or degrees of differentiation (well/moderate vs. poor, P=0.158) [ ]. Similarly, a multicenter study by Chen et al. [ ] demonstrated that [68Ga]Ga-FAPI uptake was consistently high across SRC carcinomas and other subtypes, with tumor size (rather than histology) being the major determinant of detectability. These results suggest that FAPI accumulation reflects stromal activation associated with tumor progression, rather than epithelial histology.
Clinically, FAPI PET/CT has shown better performance than [18F]FDG PET/CT, particularly in poorly cohesive, mucinous, and SRC carcinomas. In a recent comparative study, [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI PET showed significantly higher SUVmax and TBR than [18F]FDG PET in the primary tumors (SUVmax: 5.2 vs. 2.2, P=0.001; TBR: 7.6 vs. 1.3, P<0.001), LNs (SUVmax: 6.8 vs. 2.5, P<0.001; TBR: 5.8 vs. 1.3, P<0.001), and bone and visceral metastases (SUVmax: 6.5 vs. 2.4, P<0.001; TBR: 6.3 vs. 1.3, P<0.001), leading to greater sensitivity than [18F]FDG PET for detecting primary tumor (73% vs. 18%, P<0.001), LNs (73% vs. 18%, P<0.001), and distant metastases (93% vs. 39%, P<0.001) [ ]. Similar findings have been reported in other studies where [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 PET/CT demonstrated higher detection rates for primary tumors of poorly cohesive, SRC, and mucinous subtypes, as well as for LN and peritoneal metastases from gastric mucinous adenocarcinoma and SRC carcinoma [ ].  Two meta-analyses confirmed the high diagnostic performance of the FAPI for gastric cancer. The pooled detection rates of FAPI PET/CT for primary and distant lesions were 95% and 97%, respectively, with sensitivities and specificities of 75% and 89% for nodal metastases [ ]. Another meta-analysis of 358 patients demonstrated that [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI achieved a significantly higher overall sensitivity (84% vs. 46%), specificity (91% vs. 88%), and AUC (0.92 vs. 0.52) than [18F]FDG [ ]. These superior diagnostic characteristics suggest that FAPI PET/CT substantially influences the clinical staging and treatment planning for gastric cancer. In a prospective study evaluating the incremental value of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI PET/CT in addition to CECT for the initial staging of gastric cancer, stage modification occurred in 14 of 84 patients, including 10 who were upstaged and four who were downstaged. These findings resulted in management changes in 13 patients [ ].
In summary, FAPI PET/CT demonstrated consistently high uptake irrespective of the histological subtype, reflecting stromal activation rather than tumor cell metabolism. This histology-independent detectability translates into superior diagnostic performance compared with [18F]FDG PET/CT, particularly in subtypes with low [18F]FDG avidity, such as poorly cohesive, mucinous, and SRC carcinomas. As a result, FAPI PET/CT enables a more reliable identification of primary, nodal, and peritoneal diseases, with the potential to refine clinical staging and inform therapeutic decision-making.

## Recurrent disease evaluation
Recent meta-analyses comparing FAPI-based tracers with [18F]FDG have demonstrated the superior diagnostic performance of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI for detecting the local recurrence of gastric cancer, with pooled sensitivities of 100% (95% confidence interval [CI], 89%–100%) versus 48% (95% CI, 29%–68%) for [18F]FDG [ ]. In the postoperative setting, a retrospective study reported that [18F]AlF-NOTA-FAPI-04 showed significantly higher uptake than [18F]FDG in local recurrent lesions (SUVmax: 11.650 vs. 3.480, P<0.001; TBR: 12.930 vs. 2.940, P<0.001), LN metastases (SUVmax: 13.450 vs. 3.050, P=0.004; TBR: 12.430 vs. 2.210, P=0.002), and distant metastases (SUVmax: 11.890 vs. 2.960, P<0.001; TBR, 13.320 vs. 2.320, P<0.001) [ ]. The diagnostic accuracies of [18F]AlF-NOTA-FAPI-04 PET/CT were 95.7% for local recurrence, 100% for nodal metastases, and 97.9% for distant metastases.
In a separate retrospective study, 28 postoperative patients underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI PET/CT after CECT for suspected recurrence. Additional distant metastases were newly identified in seven patients, resulting in major treatment changes in 6 patients [ ].
Collectively, these findings highlight the potential clinical utility of FAPI PET/CT for evaluating gastric cancer recurrence, offering superior lesion detectability and a meaningful impact on patient management compared with [18F]FDG PET/CT.

## Treatment response evaluation
Only a few studies have explored the role of FAPI-based PET/CT in assessing treatment response in gastric cancer. In a prospective study of 28 patients with locally advanced disease, Miao et al. compared [18F]FDG PET/CT and [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 PET/CT scans performed at baseline and after one cycle of neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The study demonstrated that the percentage changes in [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04-derived parameters, including SUVmax, SUVpeak, and TBR, were significantly lower in major pathological responders than in minor responders, whereas the [18F]FDG-based indices showed no significant differences between the 2 groups. These findings suggest that metabolic changes on FAPI PET serve as early predictors of response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [ ].
Rong et al. [ ] evaluated [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 PET/CT in 21 patients with metastatic gastric cancer treated with a programmed cell death protein 1 blockade. The pretreatment uptake of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 was significantly higher in patients with progressive disease than in those with disease control, indicating a potential link between FAPI uptake and resistance to immunotherapy. Among the 2 patients who underwent post-treatment imaging, the immune checkpoint blockade-responsive patient showed decreased tumor volume, peritoneal uptake, and ascites, whereas the non-responsive patient demonstrated new peritoneal metastases with increased tumor markers [ ]. These findings are consistent with the preclinical evidence that FAP-expressing fibroblasts promote immune evasion by enhancing programmed death-ligand 1-mediated T-cell dysfunction [ ].
Overall, these preliminary studies suggest that FAPI PET/CT provides complementary information for monitoring therapeutic responses and identifying treatment resistance in gastric cancer. If validated in larger prospective cohorts, FAPI PET could serve not only as a noninvasive biomarker for chemotherapy response but also as a potential imaging marker of immunotherapy resistance.

## Therapeutic application
Radionuclide therapy uses a radioactive isotope linked to a tumor-targeting ligand to deliver therapeutic radiation selectively to cancer tissue, thereby inducing DNA damage and cell death. Depending on the radionuclide used, the emitted radiation can act locally within the tumor or exert a broader “crossfire” effect, allowing effective treatment of small and heterogeneous lesions [ ]. FAP-targeted ligands labeled with therapeutic radionuclides have expanded the role of FAPI tracers from diagnosis to therapy, forming the basis for a theranostic approach that enables personalized treatment planning [ ]. Several FAP-directed β-emitting radioligands, such as [177Lu]Lu-DOTAGA.(SA.FAPi)2, [177Lu]Lu-FAP-2286, [177Lu]Lu-LNC1013, and [177Lu]Lu-PNT6555, have demonstrated favorable safety and preliminary efficacy in various solid tumors, including gastric cancer treated with [177Lu]Lu-LNC1013 [ ].
In the phase I/II LuMIERE study, [177Lu]Lu-FAP-2286 was well tolerated by heavily pretreated patients with advanced or metastatic solid tumors, with few dose-limiting toxicities and grade 3 adverse events [ ]. Similarly, the FRONTIER phase I trial showed that [177Lu]Lu-PNT6555 was well tolerated with dose escalation, although the absorbed tumor doses were relatively low [ ]. In parallel, ongoing efforts are focused on improving the tumor affinity, in vivo stability, and retention time of FAP ligands to enhance therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Continued refinement of FAP ligands and accumulation of clinical data will clarify whether FAP-targeted radionuclide therapy can offer a new treatment avenue for patients with advanced or refractory gastric cancer.

## Limitations and future perspectives
Although published studies have consistently demonstrated the promising role of FAPI PET/CT in gastric cancer, several limitations must be acknowledged before it can be integrated into routine clinical practice. FAPI uptake is not entirely specific to malignancy. Benign, inflammatory, infectious, or degenerative conditions may also result in increased uptake and complicate image interpretation [ ]. Moreover, FAP expression varies substantially across tumor types, treatment states, and microenvironmental conditions, resulting in variability in FAPI uptake and diagnostic interpretation [ ]. Existing evidence is largely based on small or retrospective cohorts, underscoring the need for larger prospective studies to validate clinical utility, determine clinically relevant uptake thresholds, and establish standardized interpretation criteria in comparison with [18F]FDG.
Beyond these clinical interpretive issues, several technical and procedural factors limit the broader implementation and commercialization of FAPI PET/CT. The wide range of structurally distinct FAPI ligands, differences in radiolabeling strategies (68Ga vs. 18F), and non-uniform acquisition and reconstruction protocols contribute to substantial interinstitutional variability, restricting reproducibility and regulatory readiness [ ]. The practical constraints of 68Ga-based tracers, including limited generator output, short half-life, and small batch capacity, also challenge high-throughput clinical operations. Addressing these barriers will require the standardization of radiopharmaceutical production, harmonization of imaging protocols, and development of scalable 18F-labeled formulations suitable for centralized manufacturing. In parallel, regulatory approval and reimbursement will depend on prospective multicenter evidence demonstrating a meaningful impact on management, as well as the accumulation of dosimetry, safety, and therapeutic data to support the expansion of FAP-targeted theranostic applications.

## CONCLUSION
[18F]FDG PET/CT remains the most widely used molecular imaging modality for gastric cancer and provides valuable information for staging, recurrence detection, and treatment response assessment. However, its diagnostic performance is highly dependent on tumor histology and molecular subtype, with limited sensitivity for poorly cohesive, mucinous, and SRC carcinomas. These limitations highlight the need for alternative tracers to overcome the metabolic and morphological heterogeneity of gastric cancer. FAPI PET/CT has emerged as a promising tool that targets the tumor stroma rather than tumor cell metabolism, offering high histology-independent lesion detectability. Early evidence demonstrates superior diagnostic performance compared with [18F]FDG PET/CT, particularly in [18F]FDG-negative gastric cancer subtypes, and suggests potential applications for treatment response monitoring and therapeutic guidance. Furthermore, the theranostic extension of FAP ligands opens new avenues for personalized radionuclide therapy in advanced diseases. Nevertheless, most available studies have been limited to small retrospective cohorts, primarily focusing on comparative diagnostic performance against [18F]FDG PET/CT. Larger, prospective, and mechanistically oriented studies are warranted to define the clinical impact of FAPI PET on patient outcomes and establish standardized imaging protocols and quantitative thresholds for clinical decision-making.
