## Abstract

## Objective
Metabolic reprogramming emerges as a central driver of therapy resistance and survival disadvantage in ovarian cancer. We recently demonstrated that inhibiting the enzyme Deiodinase type 3 (DIO3) reduces ovarian cancer growth, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

## Methods
We studied DIO3 role in metabolism in genetically manipulated ovarian cancer cells using protein expression analysis, integrative proteomics, endogenous and extracellular metabolomics, metabolic assays including lactate and glutamate secretion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and the Seahorse Cell Mito Stress test.

## Results
We reveled that inhibiting DIO3 suppresses glycolysis while enhancing ATP production through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). We corroborated these findings using two models of ovarian cancer xenografts, demonstrating a marked reduction in glycolytic proteins upon silencing or inhibiting DIO3 using our first in class small molecule. Moreover, altered glutamine metabolism was also documented, favoring urea cycle and TCA cycle engagement over antioxidant production, accompanied by elevated ROS. Intriguingly, DIO3 depletion in fallopian tube cells, the precursor of HGSOC, displayed distinct metabolic adaptations, including enhanced glycolysis and lipid metabolism, suggesting tissue-specific roles for DIO3.

## Conclusions
These collective findings position DIO3 as a potential regulator of ovarian cancer metabolism, with implications for targeting this enzyme to disrupt tumor energetics as a novel therapeutic approach.

Keywords: Deiodinase type 3, Thyroid hormones, Gynecological malignancy, Ovarian cancer, Metabolism

## Highlights
•DIO3 is a key regulator of metabolic plasticity in ovarian cancer and fallopian tube cells.
•DIO3 silencing suppresses glycolysis and enhances ATP production, disrupting the Warburg effect in ovarian cancer.
•DIO3 silencing alters glutamine-related metabolic pathways.
•DIO3 depletion in fallopian tube cells reveals distinct metabolic adaptations, underscoring tissue-specific functions.

## Introduction
High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), one of the most common causes of cancer-related death in women, relies on dysregulated metabolic pathways to supply sufficient energy for enhanced cell proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance [ , ]. This metabolic reprogramming includes alterations in lipid and amino acid metabolism, dependence on unique oncometabolites, and utilization of mitochondria for bioenergetics and anabolic needs. The most well-known altered metabolic pathway is aerobic glycolysis, also known as the “Warburg Effect.” In this phenomenon, glucose uptake in tumor cells, unlike in differentiated tissues, is enhanced to generate energy primarily through aerobic glycolysis instead of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [ , ]. In addition to upregulating glucose consumption, many tumors also increase the absorption of amino acids such as glutamine [ ]. This nonessential amino acid serves as a carbon source for the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and plays a vital role as a nitrogen source for amino acid and nucleotide biosynthesis. Moreover, glutamine contributes to redox homeostasis by increasing glutathione, a critical antioxidant that protects cancer cells from reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage [ ]. Collectively, these bioenergetic flexibilities facilitate tumor progression and render cancer cells resistant to therapeutic approaches [ , ]. Despite extensive research, the precise molecular events contributing to distorted metabolism in ovarian cancer remain incompletely deciphered. Identifying new players in this process could improve our understanding of the disease pathogenesis and develop alternative treatment strategies.
We have recently established that Type 3 deiodinase (DIO3), a thyroid hormone-catabolizing enzyme, plays a vital role in HGSOC [ ]. Expression of this enzyme has also been identified in the secretory cells of the fallopian tube, considered the cell of origin for this disease [ , ]. We further demonstrated that inhibition of this enzyme, using novel small compounds or silencing techniques, attenuates tumor growth in vitro and in vivo [ , ]. However, the exact mechanism of action of this effect is not fully elucidated. Here, we present a unique role for DIO3 in the metabolic rewiring of ovarian cancer and fallopian tube cells, positioning this enzyme as a metabolic target for inhibition in this malignancy.

## Methods
2.1.

## Reagents and antibodies
All reagents were from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Primary antibodies are listed in . The DIO3 inhibitor ITYR-DBRMD was synthesized as described [ ].

2.2.

## Bioinformatics tools
Survival analysis from ovarian cancer patients was extracted from TCGA using Kaplan–Meier Plotter [ ], and therapy response was assessed via ROC Plotter [ ].

2.3.

## Cell cultures
HGSOC ES2 cells were purchased from the ATCC (Cat# CRL-1978). Immortalized fallopian tube cells (FT282) were provided by Dr. Ruth Perets (Rambam Medical Center, Haifa, Israel). OVCAR8 cells were provided by Prof. Dan Peer (Tel Aviv University, Tel Aviv, Israel). ID8-WT cells were provided by Prof. Neta Erez (Tel Aviv University, Tel Aviv, Israel) and ID8 Trp53−/− cell line (ID8 p53-KO) was provided by Professor Iain McNeish, Imperial College London (London, UK). ES2 cells were grown in RPMI1640 medium, FT282 cells were grown in DMEM-F12 medium and ID8 cells were grown in DMEM medium (Biological Industries, Beit Haemek, Israel). All cells were supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotics. STR/mutation profiling was used for authentication and mycoplasma was screened periodically.

2.4.

## Generation of DIO3 knockdown (DIO3-KD) cells
ES2 and FT282 cells were seeded (0.5 × 106/6 well plates) and stably transfected with a pool of 3 human DIO3 shRNA plasmids (sc-77150-SH) or a nonspecific scrambled control shRNA plasmids (sc-108060), from Santa Cruz Technologies (Dallas, TX, USA). Transfections were performed following manufacturer’s instructions with shRNA transfection reagent (Santa Cruz).

2.5.

## Generation of MCT8 over-expression (MCT8-OE) cells
OVCAR8 cells were seeded (2 × 105/6 well plates) and stably transfected with an MCT8 CRISPR activation plasmid (sc-403824-ACT) or control CRISPR activation plasmid (sc-437275), from Santa Cruz Technologies (Dallas, TX, USA). Transfections were performed following manufacturer’s instructions.

2.6.

## Absolute cell counts
Performed using MACSQuant flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

2.7.

## Microscopy
Images were obtained by an Olympus microscope equipped with a camera (model IX71 Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Image analysis was performed by cellSens Entry Olympus imaging software.

2.8.

## Western blots (WB)
Total proteins were extracted and separated on 10–12.5% polyacrylamide gels, fast-transferred to PVDF membranes and analyzed by Western blot using specific primary antibodies. Bound antibodies were visualized using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies goat anti-Rabbit IgG or goat anti-Mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA), followed by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (PIERCE, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Integrated optical densities (IOD) of the bands was measured by Las3000 imaging system, analyzed by Multi-gauge v3.0 software (Fujifilm Life Science, Tokyo, Japan) and normalized to protein loading using Ponceau S stain and/or β-tubulin/β-actin.

2.9.

## T3 detection
ELISA was performed on ES2 cells treated with ITYR-DBRMD using T3 Enzyme Immunoassay Kit (ARBOR ASSAYS, MI, USA).

2.10.

## Lactate and glutamate secretion
ES2 control and DIO3-KD cells (1000 cells/96-well plates) and FT control and DIO3-KD cells (3000 cells/96-well plates) were seeded in triplicates. The culture media was collected after 96 h and the Lactate-Glo™ Assay or Glutamate-Glo™ Assay were performed, following the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation of 60 min with detection reagent, luminescence was recorded using Tecan Infinite F200 Microplate Reader (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland).

2.11.

## Seahorse Cell Mito Stress test
Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test (Agilent Technologies, Cat #103015) was used to assess mitochondrial function parameters (basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal and reserve capacities, and non-mitochondrial respiration) by directly measuring oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of the cells. 4 × 104 ES2 control and DIO3-KD cells were seeded for a day. 1 h prior to running the test, complete media was exchanged with basal Seahorse media supplemented with glucose, glutamine, and pyruvate, and the cells were allowed to equilibrate in a non-CO2 37 °C incubator. Following basal OCR measurements, Oligomycin (1.5 μM, ATP synthase inhibitor) was injected. The second injection included FCCP (1.5 μM, an uncoupling agent) followed by the third injection of rotenone (0.5 μM, a complex I inhibitor).

2.12.

## ROS production
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in ES2 control and DIO3-KD cells was measured using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA), a key ROS indicator (cat#D399, Invitrogen, Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 15 × 104 cells were incubated with 2 μM of the ROS indicator for 30 min and next were analyzed by flow cytometry.

2.13.

## Proteomics analysis
Total proteins were extracted from ES2, DIO3-KD ES2, FT282 and FT282 DIO3-KD cells (duplicates), digested by trypsin and analyzed by LC-MS/MS on Q Exactive™ HF-X Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were identified by Discoverer software version 1.4 against the Human sequence using the search algorithms Sequest (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Mascot search engines. Identified peptides were filtered with high confidence, top rank, mass accuracy, and a minimum of 2 peptides. High confidence peptides have passed the 1% false discovery rate (FDR) threshold (estimated false positives in a list of peptides). Semi quantification was done by calculating the peak area of each peptide. Keratins were filtered out since they might be a contamination that origins from dust, hair and skin.

2.14.

## Proteins and metabolites enrichment analysis and classification
MetaboAnalyst web tool (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/home.xhtml) was used for multivariate cluster analysis using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and heatmap [ ]. Proteomaps were visualized using the Bionic visualization program (https://bionic-vis.biologie.uni-greifswald.de/) [ , , ]. Metabolomics enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Venny tool was used for comparing proteomics analysis (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/).

2.15.

## Metabolite extraction of cancer cells
Extraction and analysis polar metabolites was performed as previously described in Zheng et al. (2015) with some modifications: The medium from plates was aspirated, then plates were washed with 2 mL of ice cold saline x2 and 500 μL ice cold LCMS-grade methanol:DDW (1:1) was added. The samples were scraped from the plates into the safe-lock Eppendorf tubes and 10 μL of internal standards: C13 and N15 labeled amino acids standard mix (1:10) (Sigma, 767964) was added. The tubes were vortexed for 15 s and then sonicated for 30 min in ice-cold sonication bath (taken for a brief vortex every 10 min), and centrifuged at max speed at +4 °C. The supernatant was moved to a new Eppendorf tube and lyophilized. Finally, samples were re-suspended in 150 μL of the LCMS-grade methanol:DDW (1:1), centrifuged twice the remaining soup was transferred into HPLC vials.

2.16.

## LC-MS polar metabolite analysis
Metabolic profiling of polar phase was done as described at Zheng et al. (2015) [ ] with minor modifications described below. Briefly, analysis was performed using Acquity I class UPLC System combined with mass spectrometer Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) which was operated in a negative ionization mode. The LC separation was done using the SeQuant Zic-pHilic (150 mm × 2.1 mm) with the SeQuant guard column (20 mm × 2.1 mm) (Merck). The Mobile phase B: acetonitrile and Mobile phase A: 20 mM ammonium carbonate with 0.1% ammonia hydroxide in DDW:acetonitrile (Hypergrade, Merck) (80:20, v/v). The flow rate was kept at 200 μL∗ min−1 and gradient as follows: 0–2min 75% of B, 14 min 25% of B, 18 min 25% of B, 19 min 75% of B, for 4 min, 23 min 75% of B.

2.17.

## Data analysis of polar metabolites in cancer cells
Data processing was done using Progenesis QI (Waters). Detected compounds were identified by accurate mass, retention time, isotope pattern, fragments and verified using in-house-generated mass spectra library.

2.18.

## LC-MS and data analysis for T4 metabolite
Polar extracts of cancer cells were checked for presence of T3, T4 and rT3. The samples were measured by UPLC-ESI-MS/MS equipped with Acquity UPLC I class system (Waters). MS detector (Waters Xevo TQ-XS) was equipped with ESI source. The measurement was performed in positive and negative ionization modes using MRM. Chromatographic separation was performed based on Waters Application Note on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.0 × 100 mm, i. d., 1.8 μm) (Waters Corp., MA, USA). The mobile phase A consisted of 0.3 mM Ammonium Fluoride in DDW and mobile phase B was methanol. The column was maintained at 45 °C and flow rate of mobile phase was 0.11 mL∗min-1. Mobile phase B was run for 0.5 min at 40%, then it was gradually increased to 100% at 20 min, run at 100% until 22 min, and then set to 40% at 22.1 min and run at 40% until 24 min. The resulting data was analyzed using TargetLynx software (Waters). T3 and rT3 in the samples were below detection limits; for T4, a relative abundance in positive ionization mode was reported.

2.19.

## Total RNA extraction
Total RNA was extracted using the RNA isolation kit (Macherey–Nagel, Duren, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

2.20.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data processing and quality control
RNA-Seq (TruSeq stranded mRNA - NovaSeq X was performed) at Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam, Netherlands). Raw RNA-Seq count data were obtained and imported into R (v4. x). Gene annotations were retrieved from GENCODE v38 GTF files and processed using rtracklayer to extract Ensembl gene IDs and gene symbols, removing version suffixes for consistency. The raw count matrix rows (Ensembl gene IDs) were matched to gene symbols, replacing missing symbols with Ensembl IDs, and aggregated where necessary to merge duplicate gene entries. Lowly expressed genes were filtered by requiring ≥10 counts in at least 3 samples. Quality control was performed using summary statistics, log-transformed count distributions (boxplots and density plots), and principal component analysis (PCA) to assess sample clustering and detect outliers. Samples failing QC were excluded from downstream analyses.

2.21.

## Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) via ssGSEA
To evaluate pathway-level activity, single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the GSVA R package with MSigDB Hallmark gene sets retrieved using the msigdbr package (species = Homo sapiens, Hallmark collection). The log2-transformed, filtered count matrix was used as input for ssGSEA to compute enrichment scores for each sample and pathway. For pathway-specific comparisons, samples were annotated with treatment conditions (e.g., DMSO, ITYR) and timepoints (72 h). Enrichment scores were compared across treatments relative to DMSO controls within each timepoint using two-tailed Student’s t-tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing, implemented using the ggpubr package. Pathways demonstrating significant differences (adjusted p < 0.05) were visualized using ggplot2, displaying boxplots with individual sample jitter and significance annotations.

## HGSOC xenograft model
We utilized representative tumors collected from selected groups of our recently published in vivo study [ ]. Mice were injected with ES2 control and DIO3-KD cells and treated with ITYR-DBRMD or vehicle. Study was performed in compliance with the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in accordance with institution guidelines. This study was approved by the Israeli animal ethics committee (IACUC# 59-08-2018).

## Statistical analysis
Experiments were performed in duplicates/triplicates and analyzed using t-tests, ANOVA, and GraphPad Prism 10.2.

## Results
3.1.

## DIO3 rewires energy production from glycolysis towards oxidative phosphorylation in ovarian cancer cells and mouse xenografts
To investigate DIO3 role in ovarian cancer metabolism, we utilized ES2 cells, which were recently shown to be of HGSOC type [ , , ]. These cells were stably transfected with shRNA specific for DIO3 to generate DIO3 knock-down cells (DIO3-KD), while scrambled transfected cells served as controls. The DIO3-KD cells exhibited a ∼50% reduction in DIO3 protein ( A), leading to reduced cell growth, as we previously reported [ ]. These cells displayed increased intracellular thyroid hormone concentration, as expected when DIO3, the thyroid hormones catabolizing enzyme, is inhibited ( B). The control and DIO3-KD cells were next analyzed by proteomics and the differentially expressed proteins were visualized by generating proteomaps via the BionicVis tool. Results revealed that from 98 differentially expressed proteins ( C, left panel) the majority of which were linked to glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation ( C, right panel and ). Western blot analysis identified a significant reduction in the glycolytic enzymes hexokinase 1 (HK1), HK2, 6-phosphofructokinase platelet type (PFKP), Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), while ATP5A1, a subunit of ATP synthase, which catalyzes ATP formation in the mitochondrial electron transport chain [ ], was elevated ( D). DIO3-depleted cells also showed decreased fructose 6-phosphate ( E) and reduced lactate secretion ( F), indicating impaired glycolysis [ , ]. Seahorse analysis revealed a significant increase in basal respiration and ATP production ( G), with an elevated ATP/ADP ratio ( H), suggesting a metabolic shift toward oxidative phosphorylation [ ]. To corroborate these findings in additional human ovarian cancer model, we utilized OVCAR8 cells engineered to overexpress the thyroid hormone transporter MCT8 which display decreased expression of DIO3 ( I). The pattern of the metabolic proteins in this cell model was comparable with that of the DIO3 silenced ES2 cells, confirming a shift in energy production when DIO3 expression is limited. In addition, since p53 was recently shown to directly regulate DIO3 transcription [ ], we used p53 knock-out (p53-KO) mouse ID8 ovarian cancer cells that display downregulated DIO3. These cells exhibited a similar reduction in the glycolytic proteins HK1 and HK2 ( J). Lastly, we set to inhibit DIO3 enzymatic activity utilizing ITYR-DBRMD [ ], our first in class DIO3 inhibitor. ES2 cells treated with this compound showed significantly increased intracellular thyroid hormones ( K), confirming that DIO3 activity was inhibited. These cells were treated with ITYR-DBRMD (250nM-10μM) for 96 h, resulting in a significant reduction of HK1, PFKP and GAPDH, but not PKM2, representing glycolysis reduction ( L). RNA-Seq on an additional HGSOC cell model (OVCAR8) treated with ITYR-DBRMD, similarly indicated a reduction in glycolysis at the transcriptional level ( M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DIO3 depletion or inhibition shifts energy metabolism from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation in ovarian cancer cells.
 After confirming that DIO3 silencing reduces glycolysis in vitro, we sought to replicate this effect in vivo. To accomplish this, we used tumors collected from our recently published study [ ], in which tumors were generated by subcutaneously inoculating control ES2 cells into the left flank and DIO3 knockdown cells (DIO3-KD) into the right flank ( A). As previously reported, the DIO3-KD group exhibited significantly reduced tumor growth and tumor weight at study end. WB analysis of two representative DIO3-KD tumors revealed a significant reduction in several glycolytic proteins including HK1, HK2, PFKP, PKM2, and GAPDH, compared to control tumors ( B). This finding is consistent with the in vitro results, suggesting that the glycolytic phenotype observed in culture was preserved in the xenograft model. Moreover, we examined the expression of these glycolytic proteins in tumors from mice treated daily with intraperitoneal (IP) injections of either vehicle (control) or ITYR-DBRMD, a DIO3 inhibitor ( C). Following enzymatic inhibition of DIO3, the levels of the glycolytic proteins were markedly diminished ( D), and was more pronounced than the effect observed in the DIO3-KD tumors. Notably, in TCGA data from tumors of HGSOC patients, high expression of DIO3 and the glycolysis proteins correlated with worse survival and poor treatment response ( ), while the opposite trend was observed for the oxidative phosphorylation gene ( ), suggesting clinical relevance. This, together with the collective in vitro and in vivo results proposes that modulating DIO3, whether by gene silencing or pharmacological inhibition, may hold potential to effectively attenuates glycolysis in ovarian cancer.
 
3.2.

## Metabolomics analysis in DIO3-depleted HGSOC cells reveals metabolic alterations
We next performed polar metabolite profiling to explore changes in endogenous metabolites following DIO3 silencing in comparison to control cells. This analysis identified 72 significantly altered metabolites, 45 of which were induced and 27 of which were reduced ( ). To examine whether DIO3 reduction also affects the cells' microenvironment, we conducted polar metabolite profiling of the culture media from control and DIO3-KD cells. In total, 99 metabolites were identified in the media, 88 of which were induced and 11 of which were reduced upon DIO3 silencing ( ). Cluster segregation of the altered intracellular metabolites between control and DIO3-KD cells was confirmed by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and a heatmap ( A). To understand the functional roles of these intracellular metabolites, we performed Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis (MSEA) via MetaboAnalyst, using the Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB). The significantly enriched pathways are presented in B. We carried out similar analyses for the extracellular metabolites, which included PCA and a heatmap ( C), as well as an enrichment analysis ( D). Both intracellular and extracellular analyses revealed alterations in metabolites not only related to glucose metabolism (highlighted in blue) but also to pathways involving glutamine (highlighted in red). These pathways include glutamate and glutathione metabolism, ammonia recycling, the urea cycle, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, also known as the citric acid/Krebs cycle.
 Glutamine is a nonessential amino acid widely used by proliferating cancer cells [ ]. Inside the cell, glutamine is converted to glutamate, which can then be released into the serum through specific transporters, supporting tumor growth—a common feature of several malignancies [ ]. To better understand how glutamine metabolism pathways change when DIO3 is silenced, we combined metabolomics data with proteomics results and metabolic assays. In DIO3-KD cells, glutamine levels compared to controls were higher in the cell media and were reduced inside the cancer cells ( A). In addition, glutamate secretion was attenuated, indicating an intracellular increase in glutamate. Metabolomics data also revealed significant shifts in metabolites that may correlate with upregulation of the Krebs/tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle ( B). Similar to glucose, glutamine serves as an energy source for the TCA cycle by converting glutamate and pyruvate into alanine and α-ketoglutarate. The elevated levels of the nonessential amino acid alanine in DIO3-KD cells suggest increased conversion and entry of α-ketoglutarate into the TCA cycle. Notably, the levels of the cycle intermediate succinate and secreted citric acid were both increased. We also detected lower intracellular levels of NAD+ and FAD—two metabolites that enter the TCA cycle—potentially indicating increased production of NADH and FADH2, which are electron carriers in oxidative phosphorylation. Additionally, we observed elevated intracellular concentrations of N-acetylaspartylglutamate (NAAG), as well as intracellular and extracellular levels of N-acetylaspartate (NAA) and β-alanine ( C). These molecules are derived from aspartate, which requires glutamate for its synthesis. Accordingly, these findings are consistent with increased intracellular glutamate following DIO3 silencing.
 Conversion of glutamine into glutamate generates ammonia, which is detoxified in the urea cycle, a process requiring the essential activator N-acetyl-l-glutamate (NAG) [ ]. In line with increased ammonia flux through the urea cycle, we observed significantly higher levels of both intracellular and extracellular NAG in DIO3-KD cells ( D). Likewise, the elevated secretion of the urea cycle intermediates citrulline, arginine, and ornithine reinforces the upregulation of this pathway [ ].
A common biological hallmark of cancer cells—where redox reactions are markedly elevated—is the elimination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through enhanced production of the tripeptide antioxidant glutathione [ ]. This process relies on cysteine, which is either generated from homocysteine via cystathionine or imported extracellularly. The latter mechanism involves a glutamate/cystine antiporter that couples cystine uptake with glutamate release [ ]. Metabolomics data from DIO3-KD cells indicated lower levels of cystathionine and reduced secretion of glutamate, suggesting diminished intracellular cysteine ( E). Concurrently, our proteomics analysis revealed significant downregulation of Glutathione Synthetase (GSS), a key enzyme in glutathione biosynthesis [ ]. These observations align with the elevated ROS levels in DIO3-depleted cancer cells. Taken together, we propose that, following DIO3 silencing, ovarian cancer cells channel glutamate toward ammonia recycling and the TCA cycle rather than antioxidant production. ## The metabolic roles of DIO3 in fallopian tube cells differ from those in HGSOC
To fully elucidate the biological function of DIO3 in both HGSOC and non-tumor tissues, we silenced DIO3 in immortalized secretory fallopian tube cells (FT282), which are considered a relevant cell-of-origin model for HGSOC. These cells harbor a clinically significant gain-of-function mutation in TP53 [ ], making them a robust model for studying HGSOC evolution. Similar to HGSOC cells, DIO3-knockdown (DIO3-KD) FT cells exhibited reduced DIO3 expression and lower cell density compared to scrambled control cells ( A), as well as a decreased proliferation rate ( B). Upon depletion of DIO3, the thyroid hormone catabolizing enzyme, intracellular levels of T4 were elevated ( C). To investigate the metabolic effects of DIO3 depletion in these cells, we performed integrative proteomics analysis on control and DIO3-KD FT cells. This analysis identified approximately 104 significantly altered proteins ( ). Using the BionicVis tool, we determined that lipid and steroid metabolism were the most significantly affected pathways ( D). For example, fatty acid synthase (FASN) was downregulated, while CPT1A, the rate-limiting enzyme in fatty acid oxidation [ ], was upregulated. Since DIO3 has been linked to glucose metabolism in ovarian cancer, we further analyzed alterations in this pathway within the fallopian tube cell model. We observed increased expression of HK1 and PFKP ( E), accompanied by elevated lactate secretion ( F). These results contrast with observations in HGSOC cells, suggesting distinct metabolic roles for DIO3 in fallopian tube cells versus tumor cells. A Venn diagram analysis of altered proteins in DIO3-KD HGSOC and FT cells ( G) revealed that most proteins were distinct, with only four shared proteins: Protein-glutamine gamma-glutamyltransferase 2 (TGM2), Carboxypeptidase A4 (CPA4), Sodium bicarbonate cotransporter 3 (SLC4A7), and Neurofilament light polypeptide (NEFL). Interestingly, these shared proteins were downregulated in HGSOC cells but upregulated in FT cells. Polar metabolite profiling identified 119 significantly altered metabolites, with 41 upregulated and 78 downregulated in DIO3-KD FT cells ( ). A comparison of altered endogenous metabolites between HGSOC and FT cells revealed 50 shared metabolites ( H), most of which displayed opposite trends between the two models. For instance, fallopian tube cells showed reduced levels of NAA, NAG, and ATP, alongside increased FAD, whereas the opposite pattern was observed in HGSOC cells. Additional analysis of extracellular metabolites revealed 123 significantly altered metabolites in the DIO3-KD FT cell culture media (data not shown). Interestingly, most of the metabolites in the media exhibited similar trends between HGSOC and FT cells ( ). These findings suggest that while the extracellular metabolic profiles of ovarian cancer and fallopian tube cells are similar, DIO3 plays distinct intracellular metabolic roles in these models, driving different pathways in each.

## Discussion
Deregulated cellular metabolism is a hallmark of cancer, characterized by altered pathways such as aerobic glycolysis and glutamine metabolism, which support enhanced growth, survival, and chemoresistance [ ]. We were interested to explore whether DIO3 facilitates tumor growth via metabolic adaptations in ovarian cancer. As HGSOC tumors preferentially rely on glycolysis for energy production rather than mitochondrial OXPHOS [ , ], we examined the glucose metabolism profile in the DIO3-depleted cells. These cells exhibited an accumulation of the biologically active thyroid hormone, T3, which is a transcription factor via binding to its nuclear thyroid receptors [ ]. T3 has been shown to influence mitochondrial processes, including mitogenesis, proton leak, and OXPHOS [ ], and was previously reported to inhibit tumor growth by reprogramming tumor metabolism [ ]. Consistent with these findings, DIO3 silencing led to elevated levels of TCA cycle intermediates, such as citric acid and succinate, as well as β-alanine, which has been associated with increased cellular oxygen consumption [ ], indicating enhanced mitochondrial energy production. Supporting this, we observed increased mitochondrial ATP production and upregulation of ATP synthase complex subunits, consistent with the role of T3 in upregulating ATPase gene expression [ , ]. ATP also acts as a negative feedback regulator of glycolysis by inhibiting key glycolytic enzymes, including HK and PFK, and by allosterically inhibiting PKM2, similarly to T3 [ , ]. Accordingly, we noted a downregulation of glycolytic enzymes following DIO3 inhibition both in vitro and in vivo and propose that T3 mediates these alterations at the transcriptional level. Further evidence of glycolysis attenuation was the reduced expression of GAPDH, and LDH, which catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactate.
Additionally, NAD+, a cofactor for GAPDH and LDH enzymatic activity [ ], was decreased. Collectively, these findings may indicate that DIO3 silencing in HGSOC cells shifts energy production from glycolysis to OXPHOS.
Maintenance of cellular redox homeostasis is another critical feature of cancer cells [ ]. Under DIO3 depletion, ROS levels were significantly increased. Elevated thyroid hormones, as seen in our DIO3-depleted cells, are known to promote oxidative stress by accelerating basal metabolism and enhancing ROS generation in the mitochondrial respiratory chain [ , , ]. Redox balance is also maintained through the biosynthesis of the antioxidant glutathione [ ]. We have indications for a reduction in cysteine, the rate-limiting substrate for glutathione synthesis, under DIO3 depletion, suggesting impaired glutathione production. This is further supported by the downregulation of cystathionine, an important intermediate in cysteine synthesis [ ], and a reduction in secreted glutamate, which is counter-transported with cysteine [ ]. Additionally, reduced levels of glutathione synthetase, the second enzyme in glutathione biosynthesis, provided further indication for decreased glutathione production [ ].
A central characteristic of many cancers, including that of the ovary, is glutamine addiction, as this amino acid is essential for sustaining cancer cell growth [ ]. In our study, DIO3 silencing resulted in reduced glutamine levels, impacting several glutamine-related pathways. For instance, we observed an increase in the urea cycle, indicating higher ammonia production via the conversion of glutamine to glutamate. The urea cycle integrates with the TCA cycle, which relies on glutamate to produce various metabolites [ ], including the aspartate derivative NAA. This metabolite has been reported to be secreted by ovarian cancer cells under glutamine deficiency [ ], consistent with its elevation in DIO3-silenced cells.
Finally, we compared the effects of DIO3 silencing in HGSOC cells and fallopian tube cells, which are considered the disease cell-of-origin [ , ]. Unlike HGSOC cells, glycolysis was over-activated in fallopian tube cells, as indicated by changes in glycolytic proteins and metabolites. Furthermore, DIO3 depletion resulted in mutually exclusive patterns of altered proteins between the two models. Only four shared proteins were identified, including TGM2, a multifunctional enzyme associated with glycolytic metabolism [ ], which showed differential expression. These findings suggest that DIO3 plays distinct metabolic roles in non-transformed cells compared to ovarian cancer cells. However as HGSOC is a heterogeneous disease, corroborating these results in additional cell models is merited.
In conclusion, this study demonstrates, through global metabolic assays and multi-omics, that DIO3 is involved in multiple metabolic pathways that support tumor growth, providing the mechanistic rational for inhibiting this enzyme as a new treatment modality in DIO3-addicted malignancies.
