## Summary
Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, characterized by complex interactions between tumor metabolism and immune evasion mechanisms. This review explores the emerging field of immunometabolism, highlighting how metabolic reprogramming within lung tumors not only fuels cancer progression but also shapes the tumor immune microenvironment (TME). Key metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and lipid metabolism, are extensively altered in lung cancer cells, facilitating immune suppression through mechanisms such as nutrient competition, lactate accumulation, and modulation of immune checkpoints. Immune cells, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells, undergo functional impairment due to these metabolic constraints. The review further discusses therapeutic strategies targeting immunometabolic pathways, including inhibitors of glucose and amino acid transporters, lipid biosynthesis enzymes, and immune-metabolic checkpoints such as IDO and CD73. Despite promising preclinical outcomes, challenges such as metabolic plasticity, systemic toxicity, and limited biomarker availability hinder clinical translation. Future directions emphasize the integration of multi-omics, metabolic profiling, and combinatory immunotherapy to personalize treatment and overcome resistance. A deeper understanding of immunometabolic crosstalk is pivotal for advancing precision medicine in lung cancer.

## Introduction

## Overview of lung cancer and its interaction with the immune system
Lung cancer (LC) is a life-threatening malignancy characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of pulmonary epithelial cells, and it remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide due to its generally poor prognosis. While historically more prevalent in males, recent epidemiological trends indicate a rising incidence among females, particularly in industrialized nations, alongside a decline in male cases. LC is broadly categorized into two major types: small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). NSCLC accounts for approximately 80–85% of all cases and encompasses subtypes such as adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma. In contrast, SCLC comprises 10–15% of lung cancer cases and is characterized by rapid growth and early metastatic potential. Tobacco smoking is the most significant risk factor, increasing the likelihood of developing lung cancer by 15–30 times compared to non-smokers. Other notable risk factors include exposure to secondhand smoke, environmental carcinogens such as asbestos, and genetic susceptibility. 
Lung cancer treatment strategies are influenced by multiple factors, including tumor histology, the extent of disease dissemination, and the patient’s performance status. While surgery and radiotherapy remain the primary local treatment modalities, chemotherapy continues to serve as the standard systemic approach. In recent years, targeted therapies aimed at specific molecular alterations within tumors have gained prominence, offering more personalized treatment options. Given the complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and the variability in immune cell infiltration, both of which critically influence therapeutic outcomes, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a first-line treatment for patients with advanced NSCLC. Nonetheless, the proportion of patients demonstrating a favorable response to immunotherapy remains limited, with response rates around 63%. Lung cancer often evades immune surveillance through complex mechanisms involving both intrinsic genetic alterations and dynamic interactions with immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME). These multifaceted processes contribute to tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis, ultimately complicating the development of effective immune-based treatments. 
The TME plays a pivotal role in supporting tumor progression both at primary and metastatic sites. This complex milieu comprises a diverse array of stromal and immune cells, including T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, cancer-associated fibroblasts, adipocytes, vascular endothelial cells, NK cells, and pericytes. These components not only interact with tumor cells through paracrine and juxtacrine signaling but also influence cancer cell behavior via a phenomenon termed oncomodulation. Such interactions are integral to tumor cell survival, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Immune cell subsets within the TME have distinct roles in shaping clinical outcomes: Th1 cells, activated CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and γδ T cells are typically associated with robust type I immune responses and favorable prognoses in lung cancer, whereas Th2, Th17, and Treg cells are often linked to immunosuppressive environments and poorer outcomes. , Advances in immunotherapy -particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, as well as chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapies-have significantly enhanced interest in manipulating the TME to improve anti-tumor responses. 
B cells play complex and context-dependent roles in the tumor immune response during lung cancer progression. Tumor-infiltrating B lymphocytes (TIBs) are present across all stages of lung cancer and are found in varying densities depending on the disease stage and histological subtype, highlighting their potential involvement in tumor development and immune modulation. Because of their diverse immunosuppressive subtypes, B lymphocytes may have pro-tumor effects, according to some research, while other studies demonstrate their ability to trigger and sustain advantageous anticancer activity. 
Myeloid cells are major modulators of lung cancer development, progression, and therapeutic response. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) create an immunosuppressive niche through the secretion of IL-10, TGF-β, arginase-1 activity, and reactive oxygen/nitrogen species, which suppress CD8+ T cell function, promote regulatory T cell expansion, and facilitate angiogenesis and matrix remodeling. TAMs display marked metabolic and phenotypic plasticity in the lung tumor microenvironment, where lipid uptake (e.g., via CD36), oxidative metabolism, and itaconate production favor M2-like, pro-tumor programs that support invasion and therapy resistance. MDSCs accumulate in both primary and metastatic sites and inhibit anti-tumor immunity by depleting arginine and cysteine, producing immunosuppressive metabolites, and expressing checkpoint ligands, mechanisms that correlate with poor outcomes and reduced responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Neutrophils and tumor-associated neutrophil subsets (including pro-tumor N2 phenotypes and NET-forming populations) further promote metastasis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance via NETosis, protease release, and metabolic competition for glucose. Together, these myeloid populations interact dynamically with cancer and stromal cells to establish metabolic and immunologic barriers to therapy, making them attractive targets for combinatorial strategies that reprogram myeloid metabolism and alleviate immunosuppression to improve responses to chemo-therapies and immune-therapies.

## Definition and significance of immunometabolism
Immunometabolism is an emerging interdisciplinary field that explores the intricate relationship between metabolic processes and immune system activity, bridging the disciplines of immunology and metabolism. Over the past decade, growing evidence has highlighted that cellular metabolism is fundamental to immune cell function under both physiological and pathological conditions. The recognition that metabolic pathways govern immune cell activation, differentiation, and fate, while also playing key roles in the pathogenesis of infectious diseases, inflammatory disorders, and cancer, underscores the pivotal role of metabolism in immune regulation. Moreover, the dynamic crosstalk between immune and metabolic systems is essential for maintaining overall homeostasis in the organism. 
Immune cells residing in metabolic tissues such as adipose tissue and the liver play pivotal roles in regulating key metabolic processes, including lipolysis and insulin signaling, while also maintaining tissue homeostasis. Dietary intake of macronutrients -carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins-along with their metabolic processing, significantly influences immune responses. Chronic low-grade inflammation, termed metaflammation, is a defining feature of obesity-associated metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). This inflammation arises from disruptions in immune-metabolic equilibrium and primarily affects organs including adipose tissue, the liver, and the pancreas. Macrophage accumulation and activation within these tissues are central to the pathogenesis of metabolic dysfunction. Recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses have identified specialized subsets of macrophages called lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs) that are characterized by adaptations to lipid-rich environments and exhibit unique metabolic signatures linked to lipid handling. The fact that cellular metabolism intricately governs immune cell activation and function adds complexity to the immunometabolic interface. Innate immune cells, particularly macrophages, undergo metabolic reprogramming in response to environmental cues, with different activation states corresponding to distinct metabolic profiles. This metabolic flexibility underlies macrophage plasticity and functional diversity. 
Moreover, metabolic reprogramming is a central feature of trained immunity, a form of innate immune memory whereby prior exposure to specific stimuli enhances the inflammatory response to subsequent unrelated challenges. This phenomenon suggests that innate immune cells can develop non-specific memory-like characteristics that are metabolically imprinted. These insights have paved the way for innovative therapeutic strategies, including the application of PPARγ agonists to enhance insulin sensitivity, immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 blockade) for cancer immunotherapy, and the targeting of glycolytic pathways in macrophages to mitigate autoimmune inflammation. 

## The role of metabolic reprogramming in tumor progression
Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, allowing tumor cells to adjust to the dynamic conditions of the TME and meet their elevated demands for energy production, biosynthesis, and redox homeostasis necessary for rapid proliferation. This reprogramming alters the intracellular and extracellular concentrations of key metabolites, influencing gene expression, cellular phenotype, and the composition of the TME to promote tumor progression. Among the major metabolic pathways implicated in cancer are glucose, glutamine, and lipid metabolism. A central aspect of this metabolic shift is the Warburg effect, wherein cancer cells preferentially engage in aerobic glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), even in oxygen-rich conditions. This metabolic strategy supports the generation of ATP and anabolic precursors (such as nucleotides and lipids), while the secretion of lactate acidifies the TME, further enhancing tumor progression. 
Importantly, the TME is highly heterogeneous, comprising hypoxic cores, invasive fronts, and immune niches, each with distinct metabolic profiles. Hypoxic regions drive HIF-1α-mediated glycolysis and angiogenesis, while normoxic invasive fronts rely more on OXPHOS and fatty acid oxidation, conferring spatial metabolic plasticity. This heterogeneity enables tumors to dynamically redistribute metabolic fluxes under therapy-induced stress, promoting survival and immune evasion. Moreover, immune cells adapt differently: Tregs thrive in lactate-rich niches by sustaining OXPHOS, whereas CD8+ T cells experience metabolic exhaustion, impairing cytotoxic activity. Targeting these spatially resolved metabolic vulnerabilities may therefore enhance therapeutic efficacy.
Within this nutrient-restricted environment, immune cells and tumor cells compete for shared metabolic substrates. Although both cell types possess adaptive metabolic flexibility, tumor cells often reshape the TME in a way that compromises immune cell function. This hostile metabolic landscape leads to immunosuppression, particularly by impairing effector T cell activity, thereby facilitating immune evasion and supporting tumor growth. 
Tumor cells extensively exploit glutamine metabolism by upregulating glutaminase, which converts glutamine into glutamate to fuel the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and support the synthesis of non-essential amino acids necessary for proliferation. Within the TME, metabolic crosstalk contributes to the formation of immunosuppressive niches. Competition for key nutrients such as glutamine and methionine deprives effector immune cells, particularly CD8+ T cells, of essential substrates, thereby diminishing their cytotoxic function while promoting Treg activity. Additionally, the accumulation of lactate and oncometabolites such as 2-hydroxyglutarate further suppresses anti-tumor immune responses by inhibiting immune cell activation and stabilizing oncogenic signaling pathways, including hypoxia-inducible factor-alpha (HIF-α) and c-Myc. Beyond glycolysis and glutaminolysis, additional metabolic pathways play key roles in lung cancer progression. Purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis is upregulated to sustain DNA and RNA synthesis, driven by the activation of enzymes such as IMPDH2 and CAD, whose inhibition has shown tumor-suppressive effects in NSCLC models. , 
Serine/glycine one-carbon metabolism, mediated by PHGDH, SHMT1/2, and MTHFD2, provides one-carbon units for nucleotide synthesis and supports epigenetic modifications (DNA/histone methylation) that promote immune evasion. Dietary or pharmacological restriction of serine/glycine has been shown to impair tumor growth and enhance sensitivity to ICIs in preclinical studies. Polyamine metabolism is also frequently dysregulated; upregulation of ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) and increased spermidine/spermine levels promote tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and M2 macrophage polarization, while inhibiting CTL activity. Targeting ODC with difluoromethylornithine (DFMO) is being evaluated in early-phase clinical trials for lung cancer. 
Beyond immunosuppression, glycolysis directly promotes metastasis. Nuclear translocation of PKM2 activates STAT3 and β-catenin signaling, driving EMT and invasion. Lactate-mediated histone lactylation enhances the transcription of pro-metastatic genes (MMP9 and VEGFA), while PFKFB3 upregulation facilitates cytoskeletal remodeling, migration, and intravasation. These findings indicate that glycolytic intermediates function as signaling molecules that couple metabolic rewiring with metastatic potential. Tumors also reprogram polyamine biosynthesis and tryptophan catabolism, particularly through the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) pathway, to suppress NK cell activity and facilitate the recruitment and expansion of immunosuppressive MDSCs. 

## Metabolic pathways in lung cancer
Lung cancer cells undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming that not only sustains rapid proliferation but also reshapes the TME, profoundly affecting immune responses. A hallmark of this reprogramming is the adoption of aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect), wherein glucose is preferentially converted to lactate even in the presence of oxygen. This lactate accumulation acidifies the tumor milieu, impairing cytotoxic T lymphocyte function and promoting the M2 polarization of TAMs, thereby facilitating immune evasion. Lactate additionally suppresses dendritic cell maturation, diminishes NK cell cytotoxicity, and stabilizes FOXP3 expression in regulatory T cells, reinforcing immunosuppression. Beyond immune suppression, lactate has recently been shown to induce histone lactylation in TAMs, epigenetically reprogramming their transcriptional landscape toward pro-tumorigenic phenotypes. 
In addition to glycolysis, lung cancer cells activate the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) to support proliferation and counter oxidative stress. The PPP generates ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis and NADPH for lipid biosynthesis and ROS detoxification, further influencing immune cell function under stress conditions. Crosstalk between glycolysis and PPP provides metabolic plasticity; under nutrient stress, accumulation of glycolytic intermediates diverts flux into the PPP, generating NADPH that supports fatty acid synthesis and limits ferroptosis in tumor and immune cells. 
Alterations in amino acid metabolism, particularly glutaminolysis, represent another adaptive mechanism. Glutamine fuels the TCA cycle and supports macromolecular synthesis, but its depletion by tumor cells can suppress the function of infiltrating immune cells. Targeting glutamine metabolism thus emerges as a dual therapeutic strategy to inhibit tumor growth and restore anti-tumor immunity. Recent evidence indicates that glutamine deprivation impairs effector T cell proliferation and IFN-γ production while enhancing Treg stability, thereby tilting the immune balance toward tolerance. 
Lipid metabolism is also reprogrammed in lung cancer, with increased lipogenesis and modified fatty acid oxidation supporting membrane biosynthesis and energy production. Moreover, lipid-derived mediators contribute to immunosuppression within the TME. For instance, arachidonic acid metabolites such as PGE2 inhibit NK cell function and dendritic cell priming, while cholesterol accumulation within TAMs and DCs disrupts antigen presentation and promotes an immunosuppressive phenotype. 
These metabolic alterations are tightly integrated with immune regulatory mechanisms. Metabolites such as lactate and nutrient competition, particularly for glutamine, can attenuate local immune activity. Importantly, crosstalk between glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, and lipid pathways establishes metabolic checkpoints that shape immune cell fate. mTORC1 integrates glucose and amino acid signals to sustain effector T cell activity, while AMPK senses energetic stress and promotes fatty acid oxidation in T cells and macrophages, maintaining metabolic flexibility. Understanding these interconnections is vital for developing combination therapies that address both metabolic vulnerabilities and immune suppression in lung cancer.

## Glycolysis and the Warburg effect
NSCLC cells undergo marked metabolic reprogramming, distinguishing them from normal cells. Glucose uptake is enhanced via overexpressed glucose transporters (GLUTs) and phosphorylated by hexokinase (HK) to glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), committing glucose to metabolism and fueling glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). Despite glycolysis yielding only 2 ATP per glucose compared to OXPHOS, which can produce up to 38 ATP, NSCLC cells preferentially utilize glycolysis because it supplies biosynthetic precursors essential for rapid proliferation, including ribose, amino acids, and lipids. 
G6P is isomerized to fructose-6-phosphate (F6P) by glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI), directing substrates toward glycolysis or the PPP. The disruption of GPI increases intracellular G6P and reduces lactate production, forcing reliance on OXPHOS and confirming GPI’s role in maintaining glycolytic metabolism. Phosphofructokinase (PFK), catalyzing F6P to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP), is a key regulatory enzyme whose activity is enhanced by fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP) produced by PFKFB isoforms, especially PFKFB3, associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes. The final glycolytic step involves the conversion of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate to pyruvate, which in NSCLC is predominantly reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), regenerating NAD+ to sustain glycolytic flux. Lactate export occurs via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), notably MCT4, even under acidic conditions created by lactate buildup. LDH inhibitors (e.g., GSK2837808A, GNE-140, FX11, and oxamate) have demonstrated efficacy in reducing lactate production, shifting metabolism toward mitochondrial oxidation, and inducing oxidative stress, thereby impairing tumor growth. 
Several glycolytic enzymes also modulate oncogenic signaling. Pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), preferentially expressed in cancer cells, exists mainly in a dimeric form with low catalytic activity, favoring the accumulation of intermediates for biosynthesis. PKM2 translocates to the nucleus following post-translational modifications and regulates gene expression, promoting proliferation and survival (e.g., c-Myc and cyclin D1). Fructose-bisphosphatases (FBP1 and FBP2) antagonize glycolysis by converting F1,6BP back to F6P; FBP2 can promote PKM2 ubiquitination, thus inhibiting glycolysis and tumor progression. Other enzymes, such as triosephosphate isomerase (TPI) and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), are upregulated in NSCLC and serve as prognostic markers and therapeutic targets, with inhibitors under investigation. 
Additional enzymes such as phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1), phosphoglycerate mutase (PGAM1), and enolase (ENO1), further underscore metabolic complexity. PGK1 generates ATP and acts as a protein kinase, inhibiting mitochondrial oxidation and promoting autophagy. PGAM1 supports nucleotide synthesis via PPP, and its inhibition reduces tumor growth and metastasis. ENO1 facilitates the conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), influencing migration and metastasis; its inhibition disrupts the Warburg effect, elevating oxidative stress and impairing tumor progression ( ).
 The Warburg effect profoundly impacts the TME by lactate accumulation and acidification (pH 6.0–6.5), which enhances tumor invasion through the activation of signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB and PI3K-AKT-CREB) and modulates immune cell behavior. High lactate polarizes TAMs toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, impairs dendritic cell maturation, inhibits CD8+ T and NK cells, while Tregs resist suppression via FOXP3 upregulation. Moreover, metabolic competition for glucose between tumor cells and effector CD8+ T cells reduces T cell glycolytic flux and IFN-γ production, driving functional exhaustion. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) also participate in the reverse Warburg effect by secreting lactate, which tumor cells recycle to fuel mitochondrial metabolism, further reinforcing tumor proliferation. 

## Lipid metabolism and its impact on tumor growth
Recent research highlights that metabolic reprogramming in cancer extends beyond the Warburg effect to involve significant alterations in lipid metabolism. In lung cancer, this includes enhanced de novo fatty acid synthesis, dysregulated cholesterol biosynthesis, and disrupted sphingolipid and phospholipid homeostasis, all contributing to tumor growth and survival. Moreover, microenvironmental stressors such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation force both cancer and stromal cells to adapt their lipid metabolism, thereby shaping the anti-tumor immune response. Notably, tumor cells often transition from reliance on exogenous fatty acids to robust de novo lipogenesis to meet demands for membrane synthesis, energy storage, and lipid-based signaling. 
Key lipogenic enzymes, including fatty acid synthase (FAS), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), are commonly upregulated in lung tumors, channeling glucose-derived carbon toward lipid biosynthesis. Immunohistochemical analyses have revealed FAS overexpression in a substantial subset of NSCLC cases, correlating with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis. Moreover, Noto et al. have shown that the enzyme stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) is a key factor for lung cancer-initiating cells. SCD1 catalyzes the conversion of saturated fatty acids into monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), a process that modulates membrane fluidity and facilitates oncogenic signaling. Alterations in phospholipid metabolism -driven by increased activity of enzymes such as phospholipase A1/A2 (PLA1/2)- lead to the production of lysophospholipids and free fatty acids, which further stimulate tumor cell proliferation and migration. Collectively, these changes in fatty acid and phospholipid metabolic pathways support not only the structural demands of rapidly dividing tumor cells but also generate signaling lipids that promote lung cancer progression.
Dysregulated cholesterol metabolism is another hallmark of lung cancer, contributing to both membrane biogenesis and the activation of oncogenic pathways. The activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in lung tumor cells elevates intracellular cholesterol levels, supporting membrane synthesis and generating lipid intermediates that drive aberrant signaling. Cholesterol itself can function as a signaling molecule; for instance, it directly binds to Smoothened receptors, thereby activating Hedgehog signaling and promoting tumor growth and progression. Mutations in oncogenes such as KRAS stimulate the activation of sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs), which in turn upregulate cholesterol biosynthesis and accumulation in cancer cells. Furthermore, clinical studies suggest that cholesterol sustains primary tumor growth and promotes epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) and invasion mechanisms implicated in metastasis. Similar to breast cancer models, cholesterol has been shown to suppress immune cell function and enhance resistance to ferroptosis, with comparable mechanisms likely present in lung cancer. Recent studies also emphasize that cholesterol-rich lipid rafts enhance clustering of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1/PD-L1, thereby dampening T cell receptor (TCR) signaling and cytotoxic responses. Moreover, cholesterol accumulation within dendritic cells impairs antigen presentation, while ACAT inhibition restores immunogenicity and T cell priming. , 
Sphingolipids, including ceramide and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), play critical roles in regulating cell fate by balancing apoptosis and survival in lung cancer cells. Ceramide, produced via de novo synthesis or sphingomyelin hydrolysis, typically mediates cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis, serving as an important anti-tumor factor. Conversely, S1P activates receptors that trigger Ras/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and STAT3 signaling cascades, thereby supporting proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. , Gangliosides such as GD2 and GD3, along with their O-acetylated derivatives, are frequently overexpressed in lung cancers and modulate growth factor receptors that facilitate tumor invasion and metastasis. 
Additionally, lipid rafts such as cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich microdomains in the plasma membrane serve as platforms for the clustering of oncogenic receptors such as IGF and CD44, enhancing signal transduction processes critical for tumor progression. These interconnected alterations in sphingolipid metabolism and membrane architecture not only support lung cancer cell survival but also promote cellular motility and invasiveness. 
Lipid droplet accumulation in tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells has recently been recognized as a major cause of impaired antigen cross-presentation, leading to weakened CD8+ T cell activation. Similarly, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), derived from arachidonic acid metabolism, fosters an immunosuppressive milieu by promoting MDSC expansion and reducing NK cell cytotoxicity. 
The TME in lung cancer is marked by nutrient deprivation and hypoxia, compelling both immune and tumor cells to rely on reprogrammed lipid metabolism. Effector cells such as CTLs and NK cells utilize fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to sustain their energy demands and anti-tumor responses under lipid-stressed conditions. The activation of nuclear receptors such as PPARα and PPAR-γ in CD8+ T cells enhances FAO and helps preserve T cell effector functions within the hostile TME. Experimental studies have shown that inhibiting cholesterol esterification through ACAT inhibitors promotes T cell receptor clustering, thereby boosting CTL-mediated anti-tumor activity in lung cancer. However, excessive lipid accumulation in the TME may impair immune cell function and promote the expansion of immunosuppressive populations such as MDSCs, which further facilitate tumor growth. Thus, the balance of lipid availability and metabolism within the TME is a critical determinant of immune efficacy or evasion in lung cancer. Cross-talk between lipid metabolism and glycolysis has also been reported, where lactate-driven HIF-1α activation stimulates Sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1)-dependent lipogenesis, linking the Warburg effect to lipid synthesis. Similarly, glutamine metabolism fuels citrate production, which serves as a substrate for fatty acid biosynthesis, establishing a metabolic interdependence that supports tumor progression and immune evasion. 
Clinical investigations indicate that expression levels of key lipogenic enzymes are strongly correlated with tumor aggressiveness and patient prognosis in lung cancer. For instance, FAS overexpression is observed in a significant subset of NSCLC cases, and elevated expression in stage I tumors is associated with poorer prognosis, suggesting its potential as an early marker of tumor aggressiveness. Similarly, SCD1 -which governs the ratio of saturated to monounsaturated fatty acids-has been linked to malignant transformation, enhanced proliferation, and survival of lung cancer-initiating cells. , ATP Citrate Lyase (ACLY), an enzyme connecting glucose metabolism to lipid biosynthesis, has also been explored as a prognostic marker; its overexpression predicts poorer overall survival in older patients, despite being associated with improved outcomes in younger cohorts. 
Comprehensive lipidomic analyses have revealed substantial alterations in phospholipid species, including sphingomyelins, phosphatidylinositols, phosphatidylserines, phosphatidylethanolamines, and phosphatidylcholines, that may serve as promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for lung cancer. Additionally, specific lipid metabolites such as glycerophospho-N-arachidonoyl ethanolamine (GpAEA) and sphingosine have demonstrated diagnostic potential comparable to traditional biomarkers, offering novel avenues for early detection and disease monitoring. In summary, lipid metabolic reprogramming in lung cancer encompasses enhanced de novo fatty acid synthesis, dysregulated cholesterol biosynthesis, and altered sphingolipid turnover, metabolic shifts that collectively support tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis. , These adaptations not only fulfill the biosynthetic and energetic demands of cancer cells but also modulate key signaling networks and the immune microenvironment, thereby promoting aggressive tumor behavior. Clinical data suggest that lipogenic enzymes and lipid species hold promise as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Targeting these metabolic nodes may offer new strategies to curb tumor progression and overcome resistance to conventional therapies, paving the way for more personalized treatments in lung cancer. , 

## Amino acid metabolism (e.g., glutamine and arginine) in lung cancer

## Arginine metabolism in lung cancer
Lung cancer cells rewire arginine metabolism to support rapid growth, survival, and adaptation to the tumor microenvironment. Arginine, a conditionally essential amino acid, is sourced from diet, protein catabolism, and endogenous synthesis via the urea cycle. Beyond its role in protein synthesis, arginine serves as a precursor for critical bioactive molecules, including nitric oxide (NO), polyamines, proline, and agmatine, which collectively influence cell signaling, proliferation, and immune modulation. Under physiological conditions, the urea cycle produces arginine through mitochondrial and cytosolic steps involving ornithine transcarbamylase, argininosuccinate synthetase (ASS1), and argininosuccinate lyase. , 
In lung cancer, ASS1 is frequently downregulated, leading to arginine auxotrophy. To meet increased demand, cancer cells upregulate cationic amino acid transporters (CAT1–3) for enhanced uptake. Intracellularly, arginine is metabolized by arginase into ornithine, which can enter the polyamine synthesis pathway via ornithine decarboxylase (ODC), promoting tumor cell proliferation. Arginine is also processed by nitric oxide synthases (NOS) to generate NO, which exerts context-dependent effects: promoting angiogenesis at physiological levels but inducing apoptosis at higher concentrations. Importantly, arginine depletion by MDSCs through arginase-1 activity suppresses CD8+ T cell proliferation and downregulates the TCR ζ-chain, thereby impairing cytotoxic responses. Therapeutic arginine supplementation or pegylated arginase inhibitors are under investigation to restore anti-tumor immunity (NCT02903914).
Furthermore, arginine decarboxylase (ADC) converts arginine into agmatine, a modulator that inhibits NOS activity and influences the balance between NO and polyamine pathways. 

## Glutamine metabolism as a central driver in lung cancer
Glutamine, the most abundant circulating amino acid, is essential for lung cancer progression by supplying both nitrogen and carbon to support biosynthesis, energy production, and redox homeostasis. Lung cancer cells exhibit elevated glutamine uptake, primarily through the overexpression of ASCT2 (SLC1A5), a hallmark of squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. Once internalized, glutamine undergoes mitochondrial catabolism by glutaminase, yielding glutamate, which is converted to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) to fuel the TCA cycle, thereby supporting ATP generation and anabolic processes. 
Additionally, glutamine is involved in a reciprocal transport mechanism via LAT1 (SLC7A5), exporting intracellular glutamine in exchange for essential amino acids such as leucine. This exchange activates the mTOR signaling pathway, promoting protein synthesis and tumor cell proliferation. , Glutamine also donates nitrogen for nucleotide biosynthesis, contributing to inosine monophosphate (IMP) production. Furthermore, in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP), glutamine combines with glucose through the enzyme GFAT to generate UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), a critical substrate for protein glycosylation involved in signal transduction, adhesion, and immune modulation. 
In the TME, glutamine competition between tumor cells and effector T cells suppresses anti-tumor immunity, as glutamine deprivation impairs T cell proliferation and cytokine production. Interestingly, glutamine metabolism also supports PD-L1 expression in lung tumor cells, linking nutrient availability with immune checkpoint regulation. Altogether, glutamine metabolism in lung cancer integrates nutrient uptake, TCA cycle anaplerosis, nucleotide and protein biosynthesis, and mTOR pathway activation, while also enhancing resistance to oxidative stress and therapy , ( ).
 Effector immune cells, particularly activated CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and classical dendritic cells, are metabolically dependent on glutamine for multiple cell-intrinsic functions. Glutamine supports T cell anaplerosis and mitochondrial respiration (via conversion to α-KG), sustains biosynthetic programs required for clonal expansion, and maintains signaling through mTORC1 and c-Myc that drives glycolysis and effector differentiation. When tumor cells outcompete immune cells for glutamine, the resulting glutamine scarcity leads to acute metabolic stress in effector T cells characterized by decreased mTORC1 activity, reduced c-Myc expression, impaired glycolytic and mitochondrial fitness, and failure to sustain nucleotide biosynthesis, collectively causing cell-cycle arrest, reduced cytokine production (e.g., IFN-γ), and an exhaustion-like phenotype. These mechanisms have been demonstrated in multiple models showing that glutamine deprivation limits T cell proliferation and effector function. , 
Glutamine shortage also skews CD4+ T cell differentiation toward regulatory phenotypes: low glutamine availability favors Foxp3 induction and Treg stability via altered AMPK/mTOR signaling and epigenetic changes, thereby enhancing immune tolerance within the TME. At the same time, glutamine-deprived dendritic cells show defective antigen processing and cross-presentation, reduced type I interferon responses, and impaired capability to prime naive CD8+ T cells, further compromising adaptive immunity. These DC defects under glutamine limitation have been directly observed in both in vitro and in vivo studies. 
Finally, glutamine scarcity perturbs redox homeostasis by limiting glutamate supply for glutathione synthesis, which can increase cellular oxidative stress and impair immune cell survival and function ; conversely, some tumor cells adapt by rewiring metabolism to maintain redox balance, thereby preserving their proliferative capacity while immune cells fail to adapt. Collectively, these cell-type-specific vulnerabilities explain how tumor-driven glutamine consumption generates an immunosuppressive, therapy-resistant microenvironment.

## Serine and cystine metabolism in lung cancer
Serine metabolism is markedly reprogrammed in lung cancer to sustain the biosynthetic needs of proliferating tumor cells. Serine provides essential carbon units for nucleotide and lipid biosynthesis, supporting membrane formation and DNA replication. Lung cancer cells acquire serine both from extracellular sources and through the activation of the de novo serine synthesis pathway, particularly via the upregulation of phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), which enhances purine and pyrimidine synthesis required for rapid proliferation. Recent findings indicate that serine-derived one-carbon metabolism is critical for epigenetic regulation, supporting DNA and histone methylation in NSCLC. Dietary restriction of serine and glycine has been shown to synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors, by limiting nucleotide pools and enhancing T cell activity in preclinical lung cancer models. 
Preclinical data indicate that the dietary restriction of serine and glycine can impair lung tumor growth by limiting nucleotide and lipid availability, highlighting a metabolic vulnerability. Cystine, the oxidized dimeric form of cysteine, is essential for maintaining redox balance in lung cancer through its role in glutathione (GSH) synthesis, a major cellular antioxidant. Lung tumor cells enhance cystine uptake via the cystine/glutamate antiporter system xCT (SLC7A11) to ensure adequate cysteine supply for GSH production, thereby neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and preventing lipid peroxidation. , Elevated xCT activity confers resistance to ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent cell death, and promotes therapy resistance. Targeting xCT with inhibitors such as sulfasalazine has demonstrated potential in restoring ferroptosis sensitivity in lung cancer cells. Moreover, cystine metabolism in TAMs has been linked to immunosuppressive polarization, further highlighting its role in immune evasion. This adaptation is particularly critical in cancers with elevated ROS due to oncogenic mutations and serves as a key mechanism for resisting ferroptosis and supporting tumor survival. Collectively, the altered metabolism of serine and cystine in lung cancer underpins both anabolic growth and redox defense, offering promising targets for metabolic therapies.

## Immune system modulation by metabolism

## Metabolic reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages
TAMs represent key immunosuppressive components within the lung cancer microenvironment, where they contribute to tumor progression, angiogenesis, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Their abundance is positively correlated with tumor size and invasion depth. TAMs are characterized by surface markers such as CD68, CD163, CD204, and CD206 and demonstrate considerable phenotypic plasticity, adopting either a proinflammatory M1-like or an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype. The M2-polarized TAMs, in particular, secrete cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which facilitate tumor growth and immune evasion. Additionally, M2-like TAMs promote the expression of PD-L1, thereby diminishing the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade therapies. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting TAMs -especially in combination with chemotherapy and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors-have demonstrated promising efficacy in preclinical studies. 
Lipid metabolism is altered in TAMs, which affects their polarization, function, and phenotype, and influences tumor progression. Lipid metabolism in TAMs involves increased uptake and oxidation of fatty acids. , In lung cancer, TAMs undergo metabolic changes, switching from a glycolysis-dependent M1 anti-tumor phenotype to an OXPHOS and fatty acid oxidation (FAO)-dependent M2 state. This change occurs under the influence of the lipid-rich TME. Increased lipid uptake by CD36 and cholesterol efflux by ABCG1 facilitate this transition, leading to enhanced immunosuppressive functions and tumor progression. Key lipid metabolites such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) also promote M2 polarization and create a tumor-supportive environment. Targeting these pathways, such as inhibiting CD36 or FAO, could reverse TAM-induced immunosuppression and offer a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in lung cancer. In addition, metabolic intermediates such as citrate, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), and itaconate are produced through alterations in the TCA cycle. Itaconate plays an important role in suppressing inflammation by inhibiting the enzyme succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and regulating inflammasome activation through NLRP3-dependent pathways. These metabolic changes contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME and offer potential therapeutic targets to counteract tumor immune tolerance. Strategies to prevent TAM recruitment, reduce their numbers, or switch from M2 to M1 are promising. Also important signaling pathways, such as CSF-1/CSF-1R and IL-4/IL-13–STAT6, are involved in TAM polarization. Nanomaterials can alter the polarization of macrophages in the TME, which increases the accumulation of therapeutic agents at the tumor site and acts as TAM regulators ( ).
 Recent single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have revealed that TAMs in lung cancer are highly heterogeneous and cannot be fully described by the M1/M2 dichotomy. Distinct TAM subsets have been identified (for example, lipid-associated/TREM2+ and SPP1+ populations, among others) that exhibit unique transcriptional programs and metabolic signatures, some enriched for FAO and cholesterol handling, others for glycolysis and hypoxia-responsive pathways, correlating with spatial niches (hypoxic core vs. invasive margin) and clinical outcomes. , These single-cell resolved TAM states show differential associations with T cell exclusion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metastatic propensity, highlighting the need for subset-aware therapeutic approaches.
Mechanistically, TAM intrinsic metabolism directly modulates canonical macrophage functions.

## Phagocytosis/efferocytosis
Rapid actin remodeling and phagosome formation require ATP and NADPH. Acute phagocytic bursts are supported by glycolysis (providing ATP at the cortical cytoskeleton), whereas sustained clearance and efferocytosis depend on mitochondrial OXPHOS and FAO to supply ATP over longer periods and to regenerate NAD+/NADPH pools for ROS production via NADPH oxidases. The disruption of these metabolic pathways impairs phagocytic capacity and apoptotic cell clearance, with downstream effects on antigen availability and tolerance.

## MHC-II antigen presentation
Lipid composition of endosomal and phagosomal membranes (cholesterol and sphingolipid content) influences MHC-II trafficking and peptide loading; one-carbon metabolism and glycosylation pathways (e.g., UDP-GlcNAc availability) affect MHC and co-stimulatory molecule maturation and surface stability. Thus, TAMs with altered cholesterol handling or impaired serine/one-carbon flux show defective antigen presentation and reduced T cell priming. 

## Cytokine secretion and inflammasome activity
TCA cycle metabolites act as signaling mediators; succinate stabilizes HIF-1α, promoting IL-1β secretion, whereas itaconate exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting SDH and modulating NRF2. These metabolic regulators determine the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine programs within TAMs and thereby shape local immune polarization. 

## Clinical implications and therapeutic opportunities
TAM metabolic states provide both biomarkers and druggable vulnerabilities. Metabolic signatures derived from single-cell and spatial assays, such as lipid-associated gene programs, SPP1/TREM2 expression, and FAO signatures, can stratify patients with immunosuppressive microenvironments and predict poor ICI responses. Interventions aimed at TAM metabolism, such as CD36 blockade, FAO inhibitors, ACAT inhibitors, or small molecules that modify itaconate/succinate signaling, have shown proof of concept efficacy in preclinical models. Combining TAM-directed metabolic reprogramming with ICIs or chemotherapy, including nanoparticle-mediated, tumor-targeted delivery to limit systemic toxicity, represents a rational strategy to restore antigen presentation, enhance phagocytosis, and promote T cell infiltration. However, clinical translation must account for intra-tumoral spatial heterogeneity, compensatory metabolic circuits, and potential off-target effects on host myeloid cells; accordingly, biomarker-guided patient selection and local or targeted delivery approaches are likely to improve therapeutic index.

## T cell exhaustion and metabolic constraints
T cell exhaustion is a hallmark of chronic infections and cancers, including lung cancer, characterized by the progressive loss of T cell effector functions and sustained expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, and LAG-3. In the lung cancer TME, continuous exposure to antigen and immunosuppressive signals causes CD8+ T cells to enter a state of exhaustion and lose their ability to effectively eliminate tumor cells. This exhaustion process leads to reduced cytokine production, reduced proliferation, and reduced cytotoxicity, all of which contribute to tumor immune evasion and disease progression. Metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic changes, such as silencing of effector genes in T cells, work together to cause T cell exhaustion in lung cancer. , , Lung cancer metabolism has a profound impact on T cell function, creating a competitive and suppressive microenvironment that reduces the immune response of these cells. Tumor cells consume large amounts of glucose and glutamine, which are essential nutrients for T cell activation and proliferation, and this nutrient deficiency leads to a weakened T cell immune response. Glutamine restriction severely impairs the proliferation of CD8+ cells and their secretion of cytokines, which are essential for effective anti-tumor activity. In addition, lactate accumulation due to tumor glycolysis acidifies the environment and further inhibits T cell activation and cytotoxic function. Also, high levels of adenosine, produced by enzymatic activities involving CD39 and CD73, bind to adenosine receptors, suppressing T cell proliferation and IFN-γ secretion, allowing the tumor to evade immune recognition. Furthermore, hypoxia in the TME induces metabolic stress, which exacerbates T cell exhaustion by impairing mitochondrial function and inducing the expression of inhibitory receptors. Enzymes such as indoleamine 2, 3-dioxygenase (IDO) contribute to immunosuppression by degrading tryptophan, leading to the cessation of T cell proliferation and exhaustion. This metabolic intervention disrupts T cell signaling pathways, including calcium signaling and nuclear translocation of transcription factors such as NFAT, and reduces the expression of effector genes. Therapeutic regulation of these metabolic pathways, such as inhibiting IDO or restoring the function of metabolic enzymes in T cells, has the potential to counteract immunosuppression and improve T cell efficacy against lung cancer. Exhausted CD8+ T cells gradually accumulate large amounts of lipids. This lipid accumulation is associated with impaired cell function and is characterized by the increased expression of fatigue markers such as PD-1 and TIM-3 146. The accumulation of lipids, such as cholesterol and lipid droplets, in T cells contributes to cellular aging and dysfunction, impairing their ability to respond effectively to pathogens or tumor cells. Targeting metabolic pathways could help rehabilitate exhausted T cells and improve anti-tumor immunity in lung cancer. Also, combining therapies that correct T cell metabolic deficiencies with other therapies could improve lung cancer treatment outcomes ( ).
 Specific metabolic alterations are described in greater detail, explaining how they mechanistically undermine responses to contemporary immunotherapies. Substrate competition in the TME for glucose and glutamine suppresses mTORC1–c-Myc signaling in effector T cells, reducing glycolytic flux, nucleotide synthesis, and biosynthesis required for clonal expansion and IFN-γ production, thereby limiting the pool of reinvigoratable T cells available for PD-1/PD-L1 blockade to act upon. Clinical studies demonstrate that glutamine deprivation lowers c-Myc and mTOR activity in T cells and restricts proliferation and effector function. 
Mitochondrial fitness is a critical determinant of durable ICI responses. Chronic antigen exposure, hypoxia, and persistent inhibitory receptor signaling such as PD-1 drive mitochondrial depolarization, loss of spare respiratory capacity, and disrupted mitochondrial dynamics in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells; these bioenergetic defects correlate with poor response to checkpoint blockade. Strategies that restore mitochondrial biogenesis, such as PGC-1α overexpression or metabolic cofactors, improve T cell persistence and sensitize tumors to PD-1 inhibition, and metabolic engineering of CAR-T cells to enhance mitochondrial function has shown improved efficacy in solid-tumor models. 
Metabolite-mediated immunosuppression, such as lactate, adenosine, kynurenine, and oxidized lipids, directly impairs TCR signaling, cytokine production, and proliferation. Lactate accumulation and acidic pH inhibit T cell glycolysis and NFAT activation; adenosine signaling via the A2A receptor elevates cAMP and suppresses effector programs; kynurenine engages AhR-dependent pathways that favor Treg differentiation. Clinical and preclinical efforts targeting the adenosinergic axis (A2A antagonists) and lactate/LDH pathways are ongoing to potentiate checkpoint blockade. 
Lipid/cholesterol dysregulation in TILs perturbs membrane organization and TCR clustering, contributing to functional impairment. Pharmacologic inhibition of ACAT1 to increase plasma-membrane cholesterol enhances TCR signaling and potentiates anti-PD-1 responses in preclinical studies; genetic or pharmacologic approaches that boost membrane cholesterol or prevent deleterious lipid accumulation have emerged as promising combination strategies. , 
These mechanistic insights inform rational combination strategies, including pairing ICIs with metabolic adjuvants (e.g., AMPK/PGC-1α activators, ACAT inhibitors, and A2A antagonists), timing metabolic modulation to avoid transiently impairing immune responses, and ex vivo metabolic engineering of adoptive T cells are promising routes to improve response rates. Importantly, implementation requires predictive metabolic biomarkers and spatially resolved assays to guide patient selection and therapy sequencing. 

## The role of NK cells and dendritic cells in lung cancer metabolism
NK cells and dendritic cells exhibit different metabolic changes and adaptations in the lung cancer TME, which significantly affect their immune function and their interaction with tumor cells. NK cells rely heavily on metabolic pathways such as glycolysis and OXPHOS to fuel their cytotoxic functions and cytokine production. Once activated, NK cells increase both glycolysis and OXPHOS to meet the increased energy requirements for their anti-tumor activities. TME is unfavorable with limited nutrients and factors that suppress the immune system, such as TGF-β; this disrupts the metabolism of NK cells, weakening them to kill tumor cells. , TGF-β not only suppresses NK cell metabolism but can also induce a shift in NK cell phenotypes toward less cytotoxic and proangiogenic states, further supporting tumor progression.
While tumor and T cell glycolytic activity remain the dominant contributors to glucose depletion, eosinophil infiltration can further exacerbate nutrient competition in spatially restricted niches. Under conditions where eosinophil density is high, their glycolytic activity competes for glucose, potentially limiting NK cell bioenergetics and cytotoxic responses. This effect appears to be context-dependent rather than universal, and thus eosinophils should be considered a complementary, not primary driver of NK cell metabolic suppression. 
Cellular iron metabolism also plays an important role in NK cell function; increased iron uptake via the transferrin receptor CD71 is essential for NK cell proliferation and effector maturation, suggesting that iron metabolism acts as a metabolic checkpoint in NK activity. The dysregulation of iron metabolism in lung cancer creates a favorable environment for tumors by fueling cancer cell proliferation and suppressing the anti-tumor functions of NK cells. Tumor cells use elevated iron and ferritin to protect themselves from nitric oxide toxicity from NK cells. Meanwhile, NK cells rely on regulated iron uptake for optimal proliferation and activation, and disruption of iron homeostasis can impair their cytotoxic capacity. Therapeutic targeting of iron metabolism pathways, such as ferritin inhibitors and ferroptosis inducers has shown preclinical potential to restore NK cell function and synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors. 
DCs in lung cancer undergo metabolic abnormalities such as lactate accumulation, hypoxia, aberrant lipid metabolism, and immunosuppressive cytokines that cause defective maturation and tolerogenic polarization. Lung cancer cells primarily rely on anaerobic glycolysis, which results in high levels of lactate in the TME, which creates an acidic and suppressive environment for DCs. This lactate-rich environment inhibits the activation and maturation of DCs and maintains them in a tolerogenic state, limiting their ability to effectively present antigens to T cells. , Hypoxia also drives metabolic reprogramming in lung tumors through increased glucose transporters and lactate dehydrogenase, thereby further enhancing glycolysis and creating an immunosuppressive environment that impairs dendritic cell differentiation and function. The tumor environment disrupts dendritic cell metabolism by increasing the expression of immunosuppressive enzymes such as IDO, particularly the tryptophan-metabolizing IDO2 variant, and drives them into immune tolerance. Lipid metabolism is particularly influential in the function of dendritic cells in the tumor context; abnormal lipid accumulation and lipid droplet formation in dendritic cells impair their ability to cross-present tumor antigens and, consequently, impair the initiation of effective cytotoxic T cell responses. Furthermore, TGF-β in lung cancer TME upregulates molecules such as B7H1 and GITRL on dendritic cells, enhances the expansion of Treg, and enhances immunosuppression. Recent studies demonstrate that targeting DC lipid metabolism and lactate signaling (e.g., with MCT inhibitors) can restore antigen-presenting capacity and improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in lung cancer models. , These metabolic changes contribute to a tolerogenic and less inflammatory phenotype of dendritic cells in lung cancer ( ). Targeting these metabolic pathways offers promising avenues to restore DC function and improve immunotherapy outcomes in lung cancer.
 

## Therapeutic implications
Metabolic modulators have long played a role in oncology, with early chemotherapeutics targeting folate and nucleotide pathways. In lung cancer, therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting the uptake of key metabolites such as glucose, lactate, and amino acids have shown promise. Preclinical studies support the efficacy of inhibitors targeting transporters for lactate (MCTs), glucose (GLUTs), glutamine (ASCT2), and cystine (xCT/SLC7A11). Several compounds modulating glucose metabolism are in development. GLUT inhibitors such as fasentin, WZB117, DRB18 (a pan-GLUT inhibitor), and STF31 (a GLUT1-selective inhibitor) are currently under preclinical evaluation, with WZB117 and DRB18 showing anti-tumor activity in NSCLC models both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, shikonin, a pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) inhibitor,has been used in advanced lung cancer cases where conventional therapies are not feasible. Cancer-associated suppression of PKM2 activity contributes to enhanced glycolysis relative to glucose oxidation, highlighting PKM2 as a therapeutic target. 
Despite the growing interest in targeting tumor metabolism, glycolysis inhibitors are not currently under active clinical investigation. However, a Phase II clinical trial evaluated the lipoate analog CP-613 (also known as devimistat) in a small cohort of 12 patients with recurrent SCLC. CP-613 targets two key mitochondrial enzymes, pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (KGDH), which are essential for oxidative metabolism. Among the treated patients, those who received CP-613 in combination with topotecan exhibited notable responses, despite the typically limited efficacy of topotecan alone, which yielded no partial or complete responses in this cohort. Furthermore, in vitro studies demonstrated a synergistic effect between CP-613 and topotecan, suggesting the potential for combining metabolic inhibitors with conventional chemotherapy in future therapeutic strategies. 
Targeting intracellular enzymes involved in critical metabolic pathways -such as purine nucleotide synthesis, glutathione production, oxidative phosphorylation, lactate dehydrogenase activity, glutamine metabolism, and components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain-has also been explored as a therapeutic strategy in lung cancer. One notable metabolic vulnerability involves the loss of methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP), an enzyme frequently deleted in various tumor types, including NSCLC. MTAP deletions are observed in approximately 13% of NSCLC cases, most commonly in adenocarcinomas but also in squamous cell carcinomas and other histological subtypes. MTAP loss leads to the accumulation of its substrate, methylthioadenosine (MTA), which binds to and inhibits protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5), thereby altering epigenetic and metabolic regulation. , Preclinical data support the use of PRMT5 inhibitors, as well as combination therapies with PARP inhibitors, in MTAP-deficient NSCLC. Additionally, mutations in KEAP1 and NRF2, which frequently co-occur with KRAS-driven NSCLC, enhance glutaminolysis and confer susceptibility to glutaminase (GLS) inhibition. , However, a Phase II clinical trial evaluating the GLS inhibitor telaglenastat in combination with standard chemoimmunotherapy in KEAP1/NRF2-mutant NSCLC was terminated due to limited efficacy. Nevertheless, ongoing trials are assessing telaglenastat in EGFR-mutant NSCLC and in combination with other glutamine antagonists. Moreover, fatty acid synthesis inhibitors are currently under investigation for use in KRAS-mutant NSCLC, reflecting a broader effort to exploit metabolic dependencies in genetically defined lung cancer subsets. 
Significantly, elevated cellular cholesterol specifically enhanced the expression of the transcription factor XBP-1 in CD8+ T cells. Additionally, the transplantation of XBP-1-knockdown CD8+ T cells into mice with B16 lung tumors resulted in reduced levels of the immune checkpoints PD1 and 2B4 compared to control CD8+ T cells. The activity of XBP-1 triggered an unfolded protein response and endoplasmic reticulum stress, which led to diminished mitochondrial function and heightened markers of immune exhaustion. The efficient removal of impaired mitochondria via lysosomal degradation is crucial for facilitating metabolic adaptations in lung cancer stem-like cells, enhancing innate immune detection, contributing to chemotherapeutic resistance in SCLC, and fostering drug-tolerant persister cancer cells in lung adenocarcinoma. , Although autophagy modulation in lung cancer has been examined in early-phase clinical trials with limited success, further research is required to assess more selective autophagy inhibitors (e.g., next-generation agents targeting specific autophagy mechanisms instead of altering lysosomal pH) and to identify pertinent biomarkers in lung cancer that could enhance the efficacy of autophagy manipulation in specific patient groups.
Gemcitabine, a nucleoside analogue, is a well-established pyrimidine antimetabolite widely used as a first-line chemotherapeutic agent for pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and it has demonstrated clinical utility in the treatment of solid tumors, including breast, ovarian, and NSCLC. In its active triphosphate form, gemcitabine incorporates into replicating DNA, thereby inhibiting DNA synthesis and inducing cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis. Similarly, pemetrexed, a synthetic folate analogue, has been employed for nearly two decades as an effective antimetabolite chemotherapy. Like the pioneering folate antagonists developed by Farber, pemetrexed exerts its cytotoxic effects by disrupting purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis, primarily through the inhibition of thymidylate synthase, dihydrofolate reductase, and glycinamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase. This inhibition leads to folate depletion and impairs nucleotide synthesis, thereby suppressing tumor cell proliferation. In a clinical study, Karayama et al. compared pemetrexed and docetaxel in chemotherapy-naïve patients with nonsquamous NSCLC, reporting that those treated with pemetrexed experienced significantly prolonged toxicity-free survival. 
Emerging clinical studies underscore the translational potential of targeting tumor immunometabolism in lung cancer. Glutaminase inhibitors such as telaglenastat (CB-839) are being evaluated in combination with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy in NSCLC (NCT04250545), showing promising early safety and efficacy data. Similarly, inhibitors of fatty acid synthase (FASN) and ACAT are being tested to remodel the lipid-rich tumor microenvironment, thereby restoring dendritic cell function and enhancing CTL activity. 
Importantly, many of these metabolic interventions not only target tumor cells but also have profound effects on immune cell function. For instance, the inhibition of glycolysis or lactate transport can normalize the tumor pH, thereby restoring CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and dendritic cell maturation. Glutaminase inhibition reduces tumor nutrient competition, reprograms T cell metabolism toward memory-like phenotypes, and enhances responsiveness to PD-1 blockade. Inhibiting xCT-mediated cystine uptake sensitizes tumors to ferroptosis and simultaneously promotes NK cell activation. Moreover, FASN inhibition decreases lipid-driven M2-like TAM polarization, creating a more immunostimulatory TME and improving the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. These immunomodulatory effects highlight the rationale for rational combination regimens that pair metabolic inhibitors with ICIs, CAR-T cells, or cancer vaccines to achieve durable tumor control.
Preclinical studies have demonstrated that combining metabolic interventions with ICIs synergistically improves anti-tumor immunity by simultaneously relieving metabolic suppression and blocking inhibitory signaling pathways. For example, dual targeting of glycolysis (via PFKFB3 inhibitors) and PD-1 blockade significantly improved CD8+ T cell effector function and tumor regression in murine lung cancer models. Moreover, the pharmacological inhibition of xCT-mediated cystine uptake sensitizes tumors to ferroptosis and enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. 
However, subsequent investigations evaluating pemetrexed in combination with platinum-based agents as a first-line regimen revealed no substantial improvement in overall efficacy compared to conventional chemotherapy protocols. These findings underscore the importance of patient selection and combination strategies when incorporating antimetabolites such as pemetrexed into lung cancer treatment paradigms. 

## Challenges and future directions

## Limitations of targeting metabolic pathways in cancer treatment
Despite the promising preclinical and clinical evidence supporting the targeting of metabolic pathways in lung cancer, several critical limitations hinder their therapeutic translation. One major challenge is the metabolic plasticity of tumor cells, which enables them to adapt dynamically to therapeutic pressure by switching between energy sources such as glucose, glutamine, and fatty acids. This flexibility contributes to drug resistance and tumor persistence. Additionally, many metabolic pathways targeted in cancer cells are also essential for the function of normal proliferating cells, including immune cells, raising concerns about potential off-target effects and systemic toxicity.
Recent clinical observations show that targeting glycolysis or glutamine metabolism can transiently suppress T cell effector function, necessitating careful dosing and scheduling to avoid immune suppression. , The development of tumor-selective drug delivery systems, such as nanoparticle-based carriers and antibody-drug conjugates, offers a promising solution to minimize systemic toxicity while maximizing intratumoral drug concentration. 
Furthermore, the heterogeneity of metabolic reprogramming across different tumor types, stages, and even intratumoral regions complicates the identification of universally effective metabolic targets. The lack of reliable biomarkers to predict metabolic dependencies or treatment response further limits the clinical implementation of metabolic therapies. Finally, most current metabolic inhibitors exhibit limited bioavailability, suboptimal pharmacokinetics, or poor tumor-specific delivery, necessitating the development of more selective and effective agents. 

## Potential biomarkers for immunometabolic interventions
The identification of robust biomarkers is essential for the stratification of patients who may benefit from immunometabolic therapies. Metabolic enzymes such as fatty acid synthase (FASN), stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), and ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), which are frequently upregulated in lung cancer, have shown prognostic value and could serve as potential biomarkers for metabolic vulnerability. Additionally, transporter proteins including GLUT1, ASCT2, and xCT may reflect specific metabolic dependencies.
Recent studies emphasize that the expression of these biomarkers varies across lung cancer subtypes, with FASN and SCD1 more frequently upregulated in adenocarcinoma, while GLUT1 expression and lactate accumulation are predominant in squamous cell carcinoma. In small-cell lung cancer (SCLC), metabolic signatures are characterized by higher glycolytic and glutaminolytic flux, suggesting distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities. 
Multi-omics studies integrating transcriptomics and metabolomics have identified lactate to glucose ratios, kynurenine/tryptophan ratios (reflecting IDO1 activity), and lipidomic signatures as predictors of ICI response. Liquid biopsy-based detection of these metabolic biomarkers, combined with immune profiling (e.g., PD-L1 expression and TMB), may enable dynamic monitoring of treatment response and guide therapy adaptation. 
Metabolomic profiling has revealed alterations in key metabolites such as lactate, glutamate, and cholesterol that could be harnessed as diagnostic or prognostic indicators. Furthermore, the expression of immunometabolic checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1, IDO, CD39, and CD73 can provide insights into the immunosuppressive metabolic landscape of the tumor microenvironment. Integrating multi-omics data, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, may enable a more comprehensive biomarker discovery pipeline to guide personalized immunometabolic interventions. 
Despite these promising findings, several challenges remain before clinical translation. Tumor heterogeneity, both spatial and temporal, complicates biomarker reliability, while inter-patient metabolic variability may require repeated sampling or longitudinal monitoring to capture dynamic changes. Moreover, many assays for metabolite detection and lipidomic profiling are not yet standardized for clinical use, which limits their applicability outside research settings. Addressing these gaps through prospective biomarker-driven trials and harmonized diagnostic platforms will be critical for clinical implementation. 

## Future prospects in precision medicine and lung cancer immunometabolism
Advancing precision medicine in the context of lung cancer immunometabolism requires the integration of high-resolution technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolic flux analysis. These tools can unravel the complex cellular and metabolic heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment and help identify actionable targets.
A proposed roadmap includes (i) single-cell multi-omics profiling to identify immune-metabolic phenotypes, (ii) computational modeling using AI/ML algorithms to predict therapeutic vulnerabilities, (iii) ex vivo functional assays (e.g., organoids and co-culture models) to validate drug responses, and (iv) early-phase adaptive clinical trials to test rational combinations of metabolic modulators and immunotherapies. The integration of spatial metabolomics with imaging mass cytometry can further map nutrient gradients and immune infiltration patterns, guiding locoregional drug delivery strategies. , 
Importantly, subtype-specific precision approaches are emerging, with metabolic targeting strategies tailored to KRAS-mutant adenocarcinoma (e.g., GLS inhibitors), LKB1-deficient tumors (AMPK activators), and SCLC (OXPHOS inhibitors). The integration of multi-omics data with radiogenomic features could further refine patient selection for these therapies and predict therapeutic responses with higher accuracy. , 
Future therapeutic strategies may involve combinatorial approaches that couple metabolic inhibitors with immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint blockade, CAR-T cells, or cancer vaccines to overcome resistance and potentiate anti-tumor immunity. 
Recent preclinical studies show that combining glutaminase inhibitors (e.g., telaglenastat) with PD-1 blockade significantly enhances T cell infiltration and tumor regression in NSCLC models. , Similarly, xCT inhibitors sensitize tumors to ferroptosis and synergize with ICIs, opening a new therapeutic avenue. 
Challenges remain in clinical translation, including metabolic pathway redundancy, adaptive resistance, and on-target/off-tumor toxicity affecting normal immune and stromal cells. These concerns highlight the need for adaptive trial designs, real-time metabolic imaging, and biomarker-guided dose optimization to maximize efficacy while minimizing toxicity. 
Moreover, personalized metabolic therapy guided by patient-specific metabolic and immune profiling is expected to improve treatment efficacy and reduce toxicity. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of immunometabolic interactions will pave the way for innovative and individualized strategies to treat lung cancer more effectively.
