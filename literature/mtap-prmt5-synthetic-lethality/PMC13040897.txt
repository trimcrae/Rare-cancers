## Introduction
Clinical trials are the key mechanism for evaluating the safety and efficacy of novel cancer treatment candidates. Nevertheless, <10% of adults with cancer participate in treatment trials, and many trials struggle to enroll enough patients to meet their accrual goals and answer their scientific questions. , , This has driven interest in artificial intelligence (AI) tools that promise to optimize the clinical trial pipeline. Oncology applications represent the most common disease focus for AI-driven clinical trial acceleration efforts. 
Given the breadth of data modalities to which modern AI can be applied and the complexity of cancer drug development, stakeholders may have diverse impressions of how AI could, or should, accelerate drug development. In this review, we summarize recent developments in AI applications for oncology clinical trials, divided into four key areas: (1) AI-driven drug design; (2) trial risk assessment, or predicting whether a novel therapy candidate will be safe and efficacious; (3) trial matching—that is, retrieval of reasonable trial options for specific patients, or patients for specific trials; and (4) trial eligibility screening or pre-screening, given a candidate patient–trial match ( ). 

## AI in drug design and identification
Drug design has traditionally been time-consuming and expensive, often relying on high-throughput screening and iterative laboratory experiments over long time horizons with low chances that any individual drug candidate will succeed. This has driven interest in AI tools that can accelerate identification of novel treatment candidates. For example, AlphaFold laid foundational groundwork that can support drug design by predicting 3D protein structures crucial for structure-based drug discovery. Tools such as AlphaMissense and AlphaGenome can generate predictions of the impact of a genetic sequence on gene regulation and protein function.
Companies have also begun to deploy specialized AI pipelines for target identification and drug design. This year, for example, several cancer drugs with AI-informed designs have entered clinical trials, including a MAT2A inhibitor for MTAP-deleted tumors , a MALT1 inhibitor for B cell lymphomas, and a selective RAS–PI3KA interaction inhibitor. , AI-designed drugs have also begun to enter the clinic outside of oncology, including the TNIK-targeted drug rentosertinib. The models used by pharmaceutical companies for drug design are generally proprietary, though a few open models have been released. , 

## AI for predicting clinical trial success
AI tools are in development to predict the ‘risk’ of a clinical trial, which from a drug development perspective may be defined as prediction of toxicity and efficacy in clinical trials, as well as successful execution of the trial and advancement of a drug candidate. , When a drug is already approved in another context and/or other members of its class are approved, ‘real-world’ data may also inform these assessments. If observational data on treatment patterns and outcomes for a drug or similar drugs are available, ‘causal machine learning’ can be deployed to estimate treatment effects while attempting to control for confounding variables as much as possible. 
Nevertheless, caution is warranted with respect to this use case. In the general AI domain, the extent to which large language models can discover new knowledge, as opposed to simply reformulating and regurgitating training data, remains a point of controversy. A similar concern could extend to tools developed to predict the success of drug candidates, especially for agents that have a truly novel target or mechanism of action and/or no historical use data. Excessive dependence on AI tools to predict the probability of a drug’s efficacy could also drive drug development towards mechanistically similar agents rather than novel molecules. Well-designed tools that enable investigators to revise eligibility criteria to understand the impact on the probability of success—for example, by loosening traditional strict restrictions on comorbidity and previous treatments , —could expand access to clinical trials while accelerating drug development, however .

## AI for clinical trial ranking and matching
Searching for suitable clinical trials for patients with cancer—or finding appropriate patients for a trial—is a complex, information-intensive task. Oncologists and patients must sift through lists of trials available at various sites, each of which has intricate inclusion and exclusion criteria, while trial investigators would need to review hundreds or thousands of patient records to find matches manually.
AI has demonstrated the potential to accelerate and improve this process. Early efforts in oncology focused on using rule-based engines and Natural Language Processing to parse trial eligibility text and patient data. IBM Watson for Clinical Trial Matching employed Natural Language Processing to extract key clinical features from unstructured medical records and trial protocols. In a lung cancer pilot, Watson Clinical Trial Matching used AI-driven matching to assist trial coordinators and awareness of trial options. Watson, however, encountered well-documented challenges with the generalizability of its pipeline. 
More recently, large language models have driven broader interest in AI for trial matching. Several tools, with distinct areas of focus, have evolved in the commercial space. Molecular testing labs have deployed clinical trial matching to promote utilization and utility of their results and expand their revenue streams. This may be as simple as structured matching between test results and biomarker eligibility criteria, or more complex, involving AI-based matching. Some commercially available tools offer patient-facing functionalities for clinical trial matching, which may include analysis of linked molecular and clinical datasets, as well as virtual trial navigation and chatbot interfaces. Several companies focused on creating and marketing large-scale ‘real-world’ clinical datasets have also incorporated clinical trial matching tools into their products. , , Academic institutions and hospitals have also developed AI-driven clinical trial matching software. For example, the open-source MatchMiner program can match patients to clinical trials based on cancer type and structured genomic data, while MatchMiner-AI and TrialMatchAI do so based on unstructured electronic health records. , 
Efforts have also been undertaken to deploy AI to identify patients with progressive disease in near-real time at scale in an academic health system and deliver genomically matched clinical trial information at those timepoints. Despite good performance at identifying patients likely to change treatment, it has been challenging for these interventions to significantly increase trial accrual. , , This may demonstrate the importance of deploying AI trial matching in settings where patients and clinicians may be less familiar with trials and enabling AI pipelines to find appropriate matches for specific patients based on a multidimensional analysis of the electronic health record.

## AI for clinical trial eligibility pre-screening
The trial ranking and matching task—retrieving relevant trials for patients or vice versa—is related to, but distinct from, granular pre-screening or formal screening of a patient to determine whether he or she is eligible for a specific trial. The latter task requires evaluation of each inclusion and exclusion criterion against the patient’s history. Tools such as TrialGPT contain modules for trial matching as well as criterion-level pre-screening. 
Formal screening for trial eligibility has generally required a manual or ‘human-in-the-loop’ process to date. One challenge inherent to automated screening is that clinical trials often require a battery of tests to confirm eligibility once consent is signed, such that eligibility criteria may include elements that are not assessed until screening is formally under way. These may include confirmation of measurable disease per RECIST criteria; testing for tumor biomarkers not routinely tested in clinical practice; and imaging studies or consultations that are not otherwise standard of care. Application of AI to assist with pre-screening or screening would therefore benefit from annotations indicating which eligibility criteria can or should be assessed using data already present in the electronic health record.

## Conclusion and future directions
AI tools have the potential to speed up the development of new cancer treatments by accelerating work that has historically been slow and resource-intensive, including drug design, risk prediction, patient–trial matching, and pre-screening or formal screening. For maximum impact, it will be critical to deploy and formally evaluate these tools in contexts where the need for clinical trial participation opportunities for patients is greatest. Future directions may include incorporating AI into clinical trial design and site selection to facilitate optimal access and efficient accrual; and leveraging AI to enhance not just initial patient recruitment but longitudinal retention efforts to maximize trial completion rates, statistical power, and successful drug development.
