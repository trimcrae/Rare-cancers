## Summary
Multiple cancers exhibit aberrant protein arginine methylation by both type I arginine methyltransferases, predominately protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1) and to a lesser extent PRMT4, and by type II PRMTs, predominately PRMT5. Here, we perform targeted proteomics following inhibition of PRMT1, PRMT4, and PRMT5 across 12 cancer cell lines. We find that inhibition of type I and II PRMTs suppresses phosphorylated and total ATR in cancer cells. Loss of ATR from PRMT inhibition results in defective DNA replication stress response activation, including from PARP inhibitors. Inhibition of type I and II PRMTs is synergistic with PARP inhibition regardless of homologous recombination function, but type I PRMT inhibition is more toxic to non-malignant cells. Finally, we demonstrate that the combination of PARP and PRMT5 inhibition improves survival in both BRCA-mutant and wild-type patient-derived xenografts without toxicity. Taken together, these results demonstrate that PRMT5 inhibition may be a well-tolerated approach to sensitize tumors to PARP inhibition.

## Introduction
Activity and stability of proteins is controlled by post-translational modifications including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, and methylation. Recent advances have highlighted the importance of protein methylation in a variety of cancer-associated processes, leading to development of compounds targeting protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) as potential anti-cancer therapies. In proteins, arginine residues in the glycine- and arginine-rich protein domains (GAR motifs), such as RGG/RG/RXR repeats, are recognized by PRMTs, which then methylate the nitrogen atoms of arginine. Methylated arginine can be classified as three types: monomethylarginine (MMA), symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA), and asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), with ADMA being the most prevalent modification. The PRMTs that generate methylated arginine are divided into three types: type I enzymes, such as PRMT1 and CARM1/PRMT4, which generate ADMA; type II enzymes, such as PRMT5, which generate SDMA; and type III/IV enzymes, which generate MMA. PRMT1 generates ∼90% ADMA, whereas PRMT5 is responsible for the vast majority of SDMA. 
The classical role of PRMTs is to regulate gene transcription and RNA splicing. For example, PRMT1 can interact with and methylate CBP/p300, supporting the transcription of target genes. In addition, arginine residues methylated by PRMTs can affect protein phosphorylation or degradation, modulating protein function and cell signal transduction. Members in the PRMT family can share the same substrates, such as conserved targeting of histone proteins. PRMT1 and PRMT5 can both cause the increase of H4R3 methylation. On the other hand, PRMTs also have many unique, non-overlapping substrates. , 
Here, we sought to understand how pharmacological modulation of PRMT activity altered cancer cell signaling. To do so, we treated a panel of 5 cancer cell lines with inhibitors of type I PRMT PRMT1/6/8 (MS023), type I PRMT CARM1/PRMT4 (TP064), and type II PRMT PRMT5 (EPZ015666). Targeted proteomics identified the key DNA replication stress response protein ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related kinase (ATR) as being suppressed at the total protein level and at the phospho-protein level. This PRMT inhibitor (PRMTi)-mediated suppression of ATR led to defective DNA replication stress response pathway activation. We pharmacologically leveraged this induced replication stress response defect by combining PRMTis with PARP inhibitors (PARPis) known to induce DNA replication stress, and found inhibition of either PRMT1 or PRMT5 greatly sensitized cancer cells to PARPi-induced DNA damage. While both compounds induced synergistic inhibition of tumor cell growth, inhibition of PRMT1 exhibited significantly more toxicity to non-malignant mammary epithelial cells. Further pre-clinical studies with PRMT5i revealed potent synergy in both primary patient-derived organoids and patient-derived xenografts in vivo. Consistent with in vitro results, PRMT5i alone or in combination with PARPi in vivo was well tolerated, with no deleterious effects on mouse bodyweight or hematological parameters observed. Taken together, these results indicate that combined inhibition of PARP and PRMT5 may serve as a potent, well-tolerated approach for the treatment of cancer.

## Results

## Targeted proteomics reveals conserved and unique modulation of cancer-associated signaling pathways following inhibition of PRMT1, PRMT4, and PRMT5
To better understand the role of arginine methylation in cancer, we inhibited the core members of the PRMT family using MS023 (inhibits type I PRMTs producing ADMA, namely PRMT1, which produces ∼90% ADMA ), TP064 (PRMT4/CARM1, type I PRMT), and EPZ015666 (PRMT5, type II PRMT) in a panel of 12 cancer cell lines including 3 ovarian cancer cell lines (OVCAR8, COV362, UWB1.289), 4 breast cancer cell lines (BT549, MDA-MB-436, HCC1395, HCC1937), 3 pancreatic cancer cell lines (PANC1, MiaPaca2, CaPan1), and 2 liver cancer cell lines (Hep3B, HepG2) ( ), followed by targeted proteomics using reverse-phase protein array (RPPA), a highly sensitive antibody-based method for simultaneous quantification of 100s of proteins ( A; A). Analyzing proteomic changes following PRMT inhibition with a generalized linear mixed-effects model taking each cell line as a random effect, we found that there was a significant positive correlation in changes induced by each compound. As expected, the changes induced by type I PRMTis targeting MS023 and TP064 showed a much stronger correlation (R = 0.56) than the type II PRMT PRMT5i EPZ015666 and MS023 (R = 0.43) or TP064 (Spearman R = 0.36) ( B). Likewise, EPZ015666 exhibited the fewest significantly altered proteins overlapping with the other inhibitors, with 18.7% shared, compared with either 71.0% shared for MS023 or 45.9% shared for TP064 ( C). Further analysis of all proteins significantly altered by at least one PRMTi ( D; B) revealed that the unique protein changes following treatment with EPZ015666 were predominately down-regulation of proteins associated with mTOR/AKT/metabolic signaling that either trended toward or were significantly up-regulated following PRMT1/4 inhibition, including mTOR, Rictor, RSK1, GSK-3B, S6, Tuberin, acetyl-CoA synthetase (AceCS1), and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC1). Upon analyzing conserved changes, we found that there were 4 proteins significantly altered by all 3 PRMTis: ATR, FAK, MSH6, and LC3A-B, all of which were down-regulated, with ATR being the most strongly suppressed ( C and 1D). Phosphorylated ATR was also suppressed by both MS023 and EPZ015666. 

## Transcriptional suppression of ATR by PRMT1i and PRMT5i inhibits activation of DNA replication stress response
ATR is an important kinase involved in DNA replication stress response signaling. During DNA replication, single-stranded DNA generated at stalled replication forks recruits replication protein A and subsequently induces phosphorylation/activation of ATR and its downstream signaling partner CHK1 to slow DNA replication and allow for repair. In addition to total ATR, RPPA analysis also indicated significant suppression of ATR pS428 following both MS023 and EPZ015666 ( D). ATR phosphorylation at S428 is up-regulated following induction of single-strand breaks, such as those generated by UV damage. As multiple lines of evidence indicate that the replication stress response may serve as an attractive therapeutic target in cancer, we sought to further validate this observation. Validation of the RPPA findings by western blot demonstrated robust down-regulation of total ATR and ATR pS428 with both MS023 and EPZ015666, whereas TP064 failed to consistently decrease ATR and ATR pS428 across the cell lines analyzed ( A). 
To determine if this ATR suppression was sufficient to interfere with the DNA replication stress response, we induced exogenous replication stress by depleting nucleotides using hydroxyurea (HU) in a panel of three cell lines. HU treatment consistently induced robust activation of CHK1 in DMSO-treated cells, as indicated by phosphorylation of CHK1 at both the S296 and S345 site. However, this activation was largely mitigated in the presence of either the PRMT1i MS023 or the PRMT5i EPZ015666, indicating that PRMTi-mediated ATR suppression was sufficient to induced a replication stress response defect ( B). These results indicate that decreases in phosphorylated ATR are likely associated with overall loss of ATR, not a decrease in endogenous levels of replication stress.
To understand if the observed suppression of ATR was regulated at the transcriptional or post-translational level, we next analyzed whole-transcriptome profiling of a panel of 4 cell lines (OVCAR8, COV362, BT549, and MDA-MB-436) treated with the PRMT5i EPZ015666 ( A and S3B). Comparing the 65 differentially expressed total proteins from RPPA with gene expression changes showed a significant positive correlation between changes at the gene and protein levels following PRMT inhibition (R = 0.51, p = 2.2 × 10−5; C). Of these 65 proteins, 42 were differentially expressed at the gene level, with 38 showing concordant alterations between protein and gene expression, including ATR. Gene expression was similar when analyzing cells at an earlier time point ( D). We confirmed this transcriptional regulation of ATR with qPCR following treatment with the PRMT5i EPZ015666 or the PRMT1i MS023 and observed that inhibition of either PRMT reduced levels of ATR gene expression ( D). As PRMT5 is known to play a role in RNA splicing, we next evaluated if ATR suppression was associated with alternative splicing of the ATR transcript. Analysis of alternative splicing following EPZ015666 treatment identified 2,071 significant differential splicing events, of which ∼50% were retained introns ( E). No significant alterations in ATR splicing were detected ( E), suggesting that this down-regulation was due to transcriptional suppression.

## Blockade of arginine methylation synergizes with PARP inhibition by increasing PARP-induced DNA damage
After observing the failure of cells treated with PRMTi to respond to DNA replication stress, we hypothesized that treatment with PRMTis may exacerbate DNA damage caused by PARPis, which induce replication stress properly. Consistent with HU, treatment of cells with the clinically approved PARPi olaparib resulted in phosphorylation of CHK1 in control cells but not those treated with either MS023 or EPZ015666 ( A). We next evaluated if this failed activation of CHK1 with PRMT inhibition increased DNA damage following PARP inhibition. While olaparib alone was able to induce a minimal amount of both single-strand DNA breaks, as detected by native CldU staining of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), and double-strand DNA breaks/general DNA damage, as detected by γH2AX, DNA damage was greatly exacerbated by combination with either MS023 or EPZ015666, indicated by a significant interaction effect between PARPis and PRMTis ( A–3C). Since PARPi primarily induces DNA damage in actively cycling cells, analysis was limited to cells currently synthesizing DNA as detected by positivity for EdU incorporation. Further interrogation of cell cycle during this treatment revealed that both MS023 and EPZ015666 resulted in a decrease in the fraction of G0/G1 cells, predominately due to an increase in S-phase cells ( B and S2C). Suppression of PRMT5 with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs)-phenocopied EPZ015666, including down-regulation of ATR and exacerbation of HU-induced DNA damage ( D and S2E). 
To evaluate if this exacerbated DNA damage coincided with improved therapeutic efficacy, we next examined the viability of cells treated with PRMTi, PARPi, or a combination thereof. We found that both the PRMT1i MS023 and the PRMT5i EPZ015666 exhibited potent synergy with the PARPi olaparib in OVCAR8 ovarian cancer cells ( A, 4B, and A). Analysis of models from pancreatic and breast cancer where PARPis are also being explored showed that this synergy was conserved in both PANC1 pancreatic cancer cells and BT549 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells ( A and 4B). We further validated the combination efficacy of PARPi and PRMTi in a panel of 8 cell lines including one liver cancer cell line (Hep3B), a pancreatic cancer cell line (PANC1), three ovarian cancer cell lines (OVCAR8, UWB1.289, COV362), two TNBC cell lines (HCC1806, BT549), and a non-malignant mammary epithelial cell line (MCF-10A). All cancer cell lines exhibited improved therapeutic efficacy with the combination of the PARPi and either MS023 or EPZ015666 ( C and 4D). Notably, this combination efficacy was still observed when treating homologous recombination (HR)-deficient models of ovarian cancer (UWB1.289, COV362). Equivalent results were obtained when combining PRMTis with the PARPi BMN673 instead of olaparib ( B and S3C). 
Upon analyzing the viability of non-malignant MCF-10A mammary epithelial cells, we observed that PRMT5 inhibition with EPZ015666 resulted in minimal alterations in cell viability but that PRMT1 inhibition with MS023 caused an appreciable reduction in cell viability ( C, 4D, and ), indicating that inhibiting MS023 may have more toxicities in non-transformed healthy cells than inhibiting PRMT5. Further analysis of Prmt5 knockout in healthy versus pre-malignant hematopoietic cells indicated that of the 11 genes we previously identified as down-regulated at both the protein and gene expression levels with PRMT5 inhibition ( D), 9 genes, including Atr, were down-regulated in pre-malignant cells, as opposed to only 2 (Acss2, Irf3) in healthy cells ( A). Moreover, upon analyzing effects of PRMT5 knockdown on cell growth in breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancer cell lines, we found that the degree of replication stress response activation was strongly correlated with dependence on PRMT5 (R = −0.66, p = 1.5 × 10−3; B). In contrast, dependence on PRMT1 was not associated with degree of replication stress response activation (R = −0.07, p = 0.76; C). This correlation between replication stress response activation and PRMT5 dependence further indicates that PRMT5 inhibition may preferentially target cancer cells due to their higher levels of replication stress compared to normal cells. 
Based on reduced toxicity and transcriptional rewiring in healthy cells, we selected to move forward with the PRMT5i EPZ015666. To further validate the therapeutic efficacy of the combined PARPi and PRMT5i in a more physiologically relevant environment, we assessed the efficacy of the combination or of monotherapies in primary patient-derived ovarian cancer organoids and again found potent synergistic activity ( E).

## Combination PRMT5 and PARP inhibition is well tolerated and inhibits patient-derived xenograft growth in vivo
To assess the therapeutic efficacy of combined PRMT5 and PARP inhibition in vivo, we used the TNBC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model BCM-7482, which harbors a germline BRCA1 mutation. Following implantation into SCID/beige mice, tumors were allowed to reach ∼175 mm3 prior to treatment initiation with either vehicle control, EPZ015666 PRMT5i, olaparib PARPi, or combination thereof for 6 weeks or until maximum tumor volume was reached. No monotherapy efficacy was observed for EPZ015666, although olaparib extended overall survival to a maximum of 7 weeks post-therapy cessation (13 weeks post-initiation), at which time point 42.8% mice treated with combination therapy were still alive ( A). Combination therapy offered improved overall survival relative to monotherapy with either EPZ015666 (p = 2.8 × 10−8) or olaparib (p = 2.0 × 10−3). Repeating this study in BRCA1/2 wild-type ovarian cancer PDX OV2428 found no benefit from olaparib monotherapy and a modest benefit from EPZ015666, with the combination therapy again producing significantly longer overall survival compared to either treatment alone ( B). Inhibition of PRMT5, as indicated by SDMA levels, was similar between the two different PDX models ( ). 
Consistent with minimal cytotoxicity in non-malignant cells in vitro, combination olaparib and EPZ015666 was well tolerated in vivo. No bodyweight differences were observed between treatment arms up until the time point vehicle control mice required euthanasia for tumor burden, and endpoint mice treated with combination therapy exhibited a moderately higher bodyweight, likely due to increased age at endpoint ( C). To further evaluate for potential toxicities, we performed hematological analysis on mice undergoing treatment. Neither hematocrit/red blood cell counts ( D) nor white blood cell counts ( E) were altered between groups. Upon further analysis of white blood cells, we also observed no differences in the amount of neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, or eosinophils across treatments ( F). Taken together, these results indicated that the combination of PRMT5 inhibition with EPZ015666 and PARP inhibition with olaparib may serve as an effective and well-tolerated anti-cancer therapeutic strategy.

## Discussion
PARPis have shown great promise for extending progression-free survival in patients with BRCA-mutant cancers. However, despite initially encouraging objective response rates and improvements in progression-free survival, these promising therapies have not consistently led to improvements in overall survival. A recent review led to the withdrawal of approvals for single-agent olaparib, rucaparib, and niraparib in HR-deficient/BRCA-mutant ovarian cancers, as well as maintenance therapy with niraparib and rucaparib in non-BRCA-mutant tumors. Likewise, in BRCA-mutant breast cancer, talazoparib also failed to improve overall survival. Thus, there is an urgent need for combination treatment strategies to further improve patient outcomes. To that end, many promising combination approaches have been pursued; however, these are often plagued by toxicity issues from combining PARPis with secondary agents. Here, we identified that PRMT5 inhibition with EPZ015666 not only significantly improved response to PARP inhibition both in vitro and in vivo but was well tolerated in vivo with negligible hematological toxicities often observed with PARP combination approaches. As we found the combination of EPZ015666 with PARPis to be effective for BRCA-mutant tumor models both in vitro and in vivo, the addition of EPZ015666 to standard-of-care PARPis would offer an ideal setting for a clinical trial. Moreover, as EPZ015666 is orally bio-available, it could be easily implemented with currently approved maintenance treatment regimens with PARPis.
DNA replication stress occurs when cells attempt to replicate DNA with limiting nucleotides, pre-existing DNA lesions, or other obstacles to DNA synthesis. To counteract this DNA replication stress, cells rely on activation of the S-phase checkpoint known as the DNA replication stress response, primarily coordinated by ATR/CHK1 and ATM/CHK2 for ssDNA and double-stranded DNA breaks, respectively. Once ssDNA is generated from replication stress, it is first coated with replication-protein A (RPA) to protect it from further enzymatic degradation. This RPA-bound ssDNA then acts as a platform to load ATR and its binding partner ATRIP, ultimately facilitating downstream phosphorylation/activation of CHK1 and subsequent cell-cycle arrest. Hyperproliferation from oncogene activation in cancer cells can drive a higher amount of basal replication stress, and while this elevated replication stress may promote genomic instability, it also offers a therapeutic vulnerability for specific targeting of cancer cells. 
We found that PRMT inhibition led to suppressed expression of the key replication stress response kinase ATR in cancer cells, ultimately inactivating the ability of cells to respond to replication stress. These results in solid tumors mirror recent findings in liquid, where the PRMT5i EPZ015938 reduced expression of the ATR gene. In contrast to efforts to induce defects in HR repair to sensitize to PARPis as previously noted in liquid tumors, interfering with replication stress response independent of HR stands to sensitize both HR-proficient and HR-deficient tumors to PARP inhibition. While combination of PARPis or DNA-damaging agents with direct ATR inhibitors has resulted in tolerability problems from overlapping toxicities, indirectly down-regulating ATR via PRMT5 inhibition appeared to circumvent these issues while maintaining combination efficacy. The observed improvement in therapeutic index may be attributable to the apparent cancer specificity of ATR suppression with loss of PRMT5 activity, as ATR suppression was largely restricted to malignant/pre-malignant cells. The benefit of preferential targeting of ATR in cancer cells may be further compounded due to higher levels of basal replication stress in cancer cells compared to healthy cells. , Indeed, PRMT5 dependence was strongly associated with activation of the DNA replication stress response. In contrast, PRMT1 dependence was independent of DNA replication stress response activation, potentially explaining the reduced therapeutic index observed with MS023, even though we and others have observed synergy of MS023 with PARPis in vitro. 
While we have primarily focused our evaluation of PARPi/PRMT5i combination efficacy in cancers with clinical approvals for use of PARPis, emerging evidence also indicates that PRMT5 may offer enhanced therapeutic efficacy in tumors with loss of methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) , and represents a recurrent deletion in multiple tumor types at varying frequencies including up to 40% glioblastomas, ∼20%–25% bladder, pancreatic, and lung cancers, and 5%–10% breast, ovarian, and liver cancers. An alternative approach to enhancing efficacy of PARPi in cancers that generally show therapeutic activity for PARPis, as performed here, could be to evaluate this combination in MTAP-deleted cancers that are preferentially sensitive to PRMT5i. Therapeutic index could be further enhanced in this context using so-called “second-generation” PRMT5s that are only active in the presence of high methylthioadenosine (MTA) from MTAP loss. In glioblastoma, MTAP deficiency has been shown to correspond with suppressed expression of multiple Fanconi anemia members, which are known to be involved in DNA HR repair. This combination of increased PRMT5i sensitivity, loss of ATR following PRMT5i, and decreased HR repair in MTAP-deficient tumors could combine for an attractive therapeutic approach in a secondary subset of patients with cancer.
In summary, we identify that PRMT5 inhibition preferentially suppresses ATR in cancer cells, leading to a defect in DNA replication stress response activation and sensitizing to treatment with PARPis in cell lines, patient-derived organoids, and in vivo PDXs. This combination was well tolerated, with no detectable hematological toxicities that commonly occur with PARP combination therapies. The existing approvals for clinical use of PARPis and ongoing trials with PRMT5is (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03573310, NCT04676516, NCT02783300, NCT05094336, NCT03854227, NCT03886831, and NCT04089449) should facilitate clinical translation of the potent and well-tolerated combination of PARP and PRMT5 inhibition.

## Limitations of the study
Although our pre-clinical models suggest combination of PARPis and PRMT5is may be broadly applicable, this study only demonstrates in vivo efficacy in breast and ovarian cancers, so future studies will be required to determine if the treatment may be expanded to other types of cancers. Optimization of dosing and scheduling may also be required for clinical translation. Mechanistically, this study does not ascertain how PRMT5 inhibition suppresses ATR gene expression, nor the relative contribution of ATR suppression versus other potential pathways modulated by PRMT5 inhibition in promoting sensitivity to PARPis.

## Mouse models for in vivo studies
Experiments were performed on BRCA1-mutant triple negative breast cancer PDX model BCM-7482 or BRCA-wild type ovarian cancer PDX OV2428. Fresh PDX tumor tissue fragments were transplanted into the cleared #4 fat-pad (right abdominal) of four-week-old female SCID/beige (Envigo, C.B-17/IcrHsd-PrkdcscidLystbg-J) mice. When tumors reached an average size of ∼175mm3 (length x width2 x 0.5), animals were randomized (N = 7–8) onto one of four treatment arms: 1) vehicle control, 2) Olaparib, 3) EPZ015666, or 4) Olaparib + EPZ015666. Olaparib was solubilized in 10% DMSO and 10% captisol and administered by oral gavage 3x weekly at 50 mg/kg. EPZ015666 was solubilized in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (SelleckChem) and administered by oral gavage 5x weekly at 200 mg/kg. Treatments lasted for six weeks or unless maximum tumor burden led to early euthanasia. At six weeks, treatment was halted and the remaining tumors were monitored until maximum tumor burden was reached. At harvest, whole blood was collected in EDTA tubes and analyzed by the Department of Veterinary Services core facilities at MD Anderson Cancer Center. Animals were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions under standard laboratory conditions (at 20°C–22°C with defined light/dark cycle and access to food and water ad libitum), with all animal experiments approved by the BCM Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

## Method details

## Reverse-phase protein array (RPPA)
Cells were harvested after 5 days of treatment with PRMT inhibitors targeting PRMT1 (MS023, 5 μM), PRMT4 (TP064, 10 μM), PRMT5 (EPZ015666, 5 μM), or DMSO vehicle control. Cell lysate was collected by RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche) and then mixed with 4xSDS buffer. All samples were boiled for 5 min and stored at −80°C for submission to RPPA Core Facility at MD Anderson Cancer Center as previously described. Samples were analyzed in duplicate. For statistical analysis, z-normalized duplicates were averaged and a generalized linear mixed effects model was used taking cell line as a batch effect. Resulting values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to determine false discovery rate (FDR), with and FDR<0.10 taken as significant. Raw data are available in A and analyzed data are available in B.

## Western blotting
Whole cell lysate was prepared by scraping cells directly from 6-well plates in urea lysis buffer (8M urea, 1% β-mercaptoethanol, 50 mM Tris pH 7.5). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel prior to transferring onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% BSA before probing with desired primary antibodies and corresponding horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibodies. Proteins were detected by radiography using ECL with film in dark room. Antibodies for western blotting were developed as previously described for anti-CARM1 substrates (NRIBme2a), acquired either from Invitrogen (anti-ATR #710746), Millipore (anti-PRMT5, #07–405), or Cell Signaling (anti-ADMA, #13522, anti-SDMA, #13222, anti-ATR pS428 #2853, anti-CHK1 pS296 #2349, anti-CHK1 pS345 #2348, anti-CHK1 # 2360, anti-Actin #3700, anti-Mouse HRP #7076, anti-Rabbit HRP #7074).

## RNAseq analysis
Cells were harvested after 5 days of treatment with PRMT5 inhibition (EPZ015666, 5 μM) or DMSO vehicle control and RNA isolated using a Qiagen RNeasy Kit. Stranded RNA sequencing was performed by NovoGene in duplicate. Data were aligned and quantified using Rsubread and differential expression analysis performed using edgeR. , BAM files generated from Rsubread were used for splicing analysis with SpliceWiz. For all analyses, statistics were performed pooling all samples, considering individual cell lines as a batch effect.

## Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)
Cells were treated for 5 days with PRMT inhibitors targeting PRMT1 (MS023, 5 μM), PRMT5 (EPZ015666, 10 μM), and DMSO vehicle control. The cells were collected, and RNA was isolated as the manufacturer’s protocol supplied with (RNeasy Plus Mini Kit, Qiagen). Complimentary DNA was synthesized using 1 μg of total RNA following the manufacturer’s protocol supplied with (iScript cDNA synthesis kit, BioRad). qPCR was performed with iTaq universal SYBR Green on Bio-Rad Real-Time PCR system. Each reaction mix contained 5μL of 2X universal SYBR Master Mix, 1μL of this cDNA, 500 nM forward and reverse primers, and water to make the total volume to 10μL. The sample was then incubated at 95°C for 30 s followed by qPCR reaction for a total 39 cycles (95°C for 15s, 55°C for 30s) and finally, the melt curve was plotted at 95°C. The raw data was normalized and quantified using the ΔΔCt method. For normalization, the Ct values of ATR were subtracted from Ct values of the 18s rRNA. Then the ΔΔCt was calculated by subtracting ΔCt value of DMSO samples from the ΔCt value of the treated sample. Primer sequences were: ATR forward 5′-GAAAGAGGCTCCTACCAACG-3′, ATR reverse 5′-CATTTCTAGTCGAGCTACCCAG-3′, 18s forward 5′-CTTAGAGGGACAAGTGGCG-3′, 18s reverse 5′-ACGCTGAGCCAGTCAGTGTA-3’.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Immunocytochemistry was performed and analyzed as previously described. , Cells were pre-incubated in CldU (25 μm) for 48 h before staining for native CldU staining to detect ssDNA, and then transferred to EdU (20 μm) for half an hour before staining (vehicle control) or drug treatment to identify cells actively synthesizing DNA. Insoluble proteins were extracted by cytoskeletal buffer and stripping buffer. Then cells were fixed with formaldehyde, permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100, and blocked in 5% horse serum. Cells were first stained with rat anti-BrdU/CldU antibody clone BU1/75(ICR1) (Abcam) and mouse anti-γH2AX clone JBW301 (Millipore). EdU was then biotinylated via CLICK reaction by 15-min incubation in CLICK reaction buffer (10 mM HEPES, 10 mM sodium ascorbate, 10 μM azide-PEG3-biotin, and 2 mM CuSO4 in PBS). Cells were then incubated with fluorescently-conjugated antibodies from Abcam, specifically anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 568 (ab175473), anti-rat AlexaFlour488 (ab150165), and streptavidin AlexFluor647 (ab272190). Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Cells were imaged by fluorescence microscopy. For DNA damage quantification, nuclei were segmented and analyzed for staining intensity of ssDNA, γH2AX, and EdU. Levels of ssDNA and γH2AX were then analyzed specifically in EdU positive cells. For cell cycle analysis, cells were gated based on integrated DAPI intensity and EdU positivity to determine G0/G1 (1N DNA, EdU−), S (EdU+) and G2/M (2N DNA, EdU−) cells.

## RNA interference
Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (ThermoFisher) per manufacturer’s protocols with siRNAs acquired from Sigma: siPRMT5-1 (SASI_Hs01_00027637), siPRMT5-2 (SASI_Hs02_00308610), or siControl (SIC001). After 3 days, cells were either harvested for Western blot or treated with olaparib and stained for analysis of DNA damage foci.

## Viability assays and synergy analysis
For 2D viability assays, cells were directly plated into 96 well plates. For organoid viability assays, organoids were dissociated in TrypLE, plated in Cultrex (Trevigen), and cultured in DMEM/F12 Advanced media supplemented with 50 ng/mL EGF, 1x B27, 1x GlutaMax, 10 mM HEPES, 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine, and 100 μg/mL Primocin. The day after plating, cells were moved to either DMSO vehicle control, PRMTi, PARPi, or combination thereof. Cell viability was assessed by CellTiter-Glo kit (Promega) per manufacturer’s instructions, and quantified as percent viability relative to DMSO vehicle-treated control. For clonogenic assays, cells were seeded in 12-well plates with the treatment of PRMTi, PARPi, or the combination. Concentrations are given in . After 2 weeks, Colonies were fixed with 10% buffered formaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and photographed for visualization. Synergy was determined by combination index, using CompuSyn by the method of Chou and Talalay. CI < 1 indicates synergistic effects, CI > 1 indicates antagonistic effects, and CI = 1 indicates additive effects.

## Tissue staining
Formalin-fixed, paraffin embedded tissues were sectioned, baked at 60°C for 30 min, dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through an ethanol gradient per standard procedures, and then subject to antigen retrieval with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker. Tissues were washed in PBS-T (PBS +0.05% Triton X-100), blocked with 2% BSA in PBS-T, and then incubated overnight with 1:1000 anti-SDMA produced as previously described. Tissues were washed, stained with anti-rabbit HRP (Cell Signaling), and Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) was performed with CF550R Dye (Biotium, #96077) in amplification buffer (Akoya Biosciences). Slides were subject to additional antigen retrieval in citrate buffer, and then the procedure repeated with anti-pan cytokeratin (clone AE1/3, Bethyl), anti-mouse HRP (Cell Signaling), and CF680R (Biotium, #92196). Finally, slides were mounted using Vectashield with DAPI (Vector Labs) and imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2-e Fluorescence microscope. At least 5 images per tissue were captured and quantified using a custom MATLAB algorithm (R2020a, Mathworks). SDMA is reported as average SDMA intensity in the tissue after subtracting background intensity determined from a control with no primary antibody. All images per tissue were averaged to determine the SDMA intensity in that tissue.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Unless otherwise noted, comparisons between groups were assessed using a Student’s t test and correlations assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients. Overall survival of mice bearing PDXs following treatment was assessed by log rank test.
