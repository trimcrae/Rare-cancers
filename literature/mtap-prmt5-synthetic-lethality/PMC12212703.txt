## Abstract
The role of protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) in benign and malignant liver diseases has garnered considerable attention. PRMTs play a key function in regulating protein methylation modification in diseases such as alcoholic fatty liver disease, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, viral hepatitis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. This review explores the mechanisms of action of PRMTs in these diseases, with a focus on their effects on cell signaling, transcriptional regulation, cell proliferation, and metabolism. We also discuss potential therapeutic strategies targeting PRMTs and propose future research directions. This review helps deepen the understanding of the important role of arginine methylation modification in the malignant progression of liver diseases and provides guidance for future clinical treatment and drug development.
Keywords: Metabolism, Therapeutic strategies, Arginine methyltransferases, Liver diseases, Alcoholic liver disease, Non-alcoholic liver disease, Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, Viral hepatitis, Hepatocellular carcinoma

## Introduction
Liver diseases encompass a spectrum of conditions, including alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), viral hepatitis, liver tumors, and other benign and malignant liver diseases, all of which pose significant global health challenges. AFLD arises from prolonged alcohol abuse and manifests in stages, including fatty liver, alcoholic hepatitis, and cirrhosis [ ]. NAFLD, now also called metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), stems from disruptions in liver lipid metabolism linked to metabolic syndrome and obesity [ , , ]. Viral hepatitis, caused by liver-infecting viruses such as hepatitis B virus and hepatitis C virus (HBV and HCV), can lead to liver inflammation and severe functional impairment. Approximately 1 million people worldwide succumb annually to hepatitis B or C infection [ , ]. Which can progress to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), resulting in patient mortality. Comprehensive research into the mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of benign liver diseases and hepatocellular carcinoma is crucial for the development of prevention strategies and novel therapeutic targets.
Post-translational modification (PTM) refers to the covalent attachment of functional groups to protein substrates through the action of enzyme activity and include the addition of chemical groups (methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation) or polypeptide chains (ubiquitination, SUMOylation). PTMs change the structure of the substrate and can subsequently alter the activity, stability, protein interaction, and intracellular localization of the target protein [ ]. PTMs also play an important role in liver disease by regulating metabolism [ ]. For example, phosphorylation of dual specificity tyrosine phosphorylation regulated-kinase 1 A (Dyrk1A) inhibits the expression of lipogenic proteins and is implicated in the pathological process of obesity [ ]; Deacetylation of the Lys685 residue in Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) regulates HFD-induced leptin resistance and obesity [ ].
Arginine methylation, one of the main PTMs, plays a key role in cellular metabolic function and the occurrence of diseases [ ]. Arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) are the enzymes that regulate arginine methylation and are classified into type I (PRMT1, PRMT2, PRMT3, PRMT4, PRMT6, PRMT8), type II (PRMT5, PRMT9), and type III enzymes (PRMT7) [ , ]. PRMTs influence protein function and interactions by methylating specific arginine residues, thereby modulating crucial biological processes such as cell signal transduction, transcriptional regulation, gene expression, and metabolic pathways [ ]. Increasing evidence has highlighted the significant role of PRMTs in both benign and malignant liver diseases, positioning them as potential therapeutic targets for liver diseases.
This review discusses the current research on the mechanism of action of PRMTs in AFLD, MASLD, viral hepatitis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, with a focus on the significance of PRMTs in the onset and progression of liver diseases. A comprehensive investigation into the impact of PRMT-mediated regulation of protein methylation in liver diseases is anticipated to offer new insights into disease pathogenesis and establish a theoretical foundation for the treatment and prevention of these conditions ( ).
 
2.

## Role of arginine methylation in MASLD
PTM plays an important role in the pathogenesis of MASLD. PTMs, such as phosphorylation, acetylation, and glycosylation, regulate the metabolic function, inflammatory response, and cell death of hepatocytes. Specifically, abnormal PTM patterns can lead to pathological changes such as liver fat accumulation, insulin resistance, and liver fibrosis, which in turn promote the progression of MASLD. Therefore, in-depth research on the role of PTM in this context may provide new targets for early diagnosis and treatment. In this manuscript, we mainly discuss the mechanism by which PRMTs play a role.
2.1.

## Regulation of lipid metabolism by PRMTs affects the progression of MASLD
PPARγ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) and liver X receptors (LXRs) are expressed in various tissues, including the liver. These proteins interact with various transcription factors and coactivators to regulate the expression of numerous genes involved in metabolism, thereby facilitating lipid metabolism. PRMTs modulate lipid metabolism by influencing PGC-1α and LXRs and the related protein pathways, thereby impacting MASLD.
The role of PRMT1 in the progression of MASLD has been a subject of debate. Some studies showed that PRMT1 indirectly enhances c-Jun N-terminal kinase phosphorylation and directly suppresses lipid degradation via the AMPK pathway by downregulating CASP8 and FADD like apoptosis regulator, thereby promoting fat accumulation and contributing to the onset of Non-Alcoholic Steatohepatitis, a subtype of NAFLD [ , ]. Furthermore, increased PRMT1 expression boosts the activity of PGC-1α, leading to heightened lipogenesis and inflammatory responses [ ]. However, some studies suggest that PRMT1 may mitigate MASLD development and proposed that inhibiting PRMT1 activity decreased liver lipogenesis and inflammation, thereby alleviating MASLD severity [ ]. Chandra et al. discovered that PRMT1 regulates PGC-1α through hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF-4α). HNF-4α is a crucial transcription factor that governs gluconeogenesis and lipid metabolism in the liver by transcriptionally activating PGC-1α. The activity of HNF-4α is influenced by PTMs, particularly arginine methylation catalyzed by PRMT1, which alters its DNA binding capability through a tightly linked quaternary folding surface. Arginine methylation plays a pivotal role in maintaining the structural integrity and function of HNF-4α. Therefore, modulating this methylation may alter lipid and glucose metabolism, potentially preventing the onset of MASLD [ ].
PRMT1 methylates HNF-4α, which promotes its recruitment to the PGC-1α promoter, thereby enhancing PGC-1α-mediated fatty acid oxidation and consequently reducing liver fat accumulation [ ]. In addition to its interaction with HNF-4α, PRMT1 (especially its variant PRMT1V2) directly enhances PGC1α activity through arginine methylation [ ]. PRMT1 also influences fat metabolism by methylating histone H4R3me2a, which then regulates the transcription factors PPARγ and C/EBPβ [ , ].
PRMT2 regulates STAT3 signaling through arginine methylation, influencing energy balance and metabolism. One study showed that PRMT2 deficiency resulted in reduced methylation of hypothalamic STAT3 and heightened leptin signaling, leading to decreased appetite and increased resistance to obesity. This mechanism contributes to mitigating the progression of MASLD by enhancing energy homeostasis and reducing fat accumulation [ ]. PRMT3, a member of the type I arginine methyltransferases, exerts its influence on MASLD through mechanisms distinct from PRMT1. PRMT3 regulates lipid metabolism by acting as a coactivator of liver X receptor 2, thereby impacting hepatic triglyceride synthesis. Inhibition of PRMT3 not only markedly reduces hepatic triglyceride accumulation and plasma triglyceride levels, but also induces adipocyte shrinkage and promotes brown adipose tissue formation, which helps alleviate MASLD [ , ]. PRMT3 also impacts lipid metabolism through the regulation of LXRα transcriptional activity. Treatment with palmitic acid (PA) enhances PRMT3 expression and its translocation to the nucleus, where it binds to LXRα, leading to upregulated expression of lipogenic proteins. Moreover, increased PRMT3 binding to LXRα was observed in individuals on a high-fat diet and in patients with MASLD, which promotes the development of fatty liver [ , ]. Similar to the results for PRMT1, inhibition of PRMT4 by the TP-064 inhibitor reduced weight gain and plasma triglyceride levels by suppressing the expression of PPARγ target genes in adipose tissue and the glycogen metabolism–related protein G6pc in the liver [ ].
PRMT5 regulates liver lipid metabolism primarily through two mechanisms. First, PRMT5 suppresses the expression of PPARα and PGC-1α by enhancing AKT phosphorylation and activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [ ]. Second, studies showed that knocking down PRMT5 increases MDM2 activity, leading to Drosha degradation, thereby ameliorating abnormal lipid metabolism and alleviating symptoms of MASLD, This suggests that PRMT5 may lead to the occurrence of MASLD by regulating the degradation of Drosha [ ]. PRMT6 exhibits a function similar to PRMT3, as it activates LXRα through asymmetric dimethylation of arginine residues, thereby enhancing the transcriptional activity of SREBP-1c and promoting the expression of lipogenic genes [ ].

2.2.

## PRMTs promote the development of MASLD through glucose metabolism
Insulin resistance and glucose metabolism disorders are pivotal factors in the onset and progression of MASLD, and they exhibit an interconnected relationship. Enhancing insulin sensitivity and managing blood glucose levels are effective strategies for preventing and treating MASLD [ ]. PRMT1 modifies FoxO1 via arginine methylation, disrupting its phosphorylation by Akt, thereby augmenting FoxO1 activity and promoting hepatic gluconeogenesis; elevated gluconeogenesis contributes to higher blood sugar levels [ ]. PRMT6 interacts with the transcriptional regulator CRTC2, facilitating CRTC2 asymmetric dimethylation to enhance its binding to the transcription factor CREB, thereby promoting the transcriptional activation of gluconeogenic genes. Ectopic expression of PRMT6 leads to increased blood sugar, while, conversely, its knockdown reduces blood sugar and improves pyruvate tolerance [ ]. Glucose metabolism disorders may lead to the excessive accumulation of fat in the liver, potentially contributing to the occurrence of MASLD.
Research in liver fibrosis showed that PRMT1 plays a role in the progression of MASLD by activating hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). Overexpression of PRMT1 enhances the expression of fibrogenic genes, while inhibition or knockdown of PRMT1 attenuates HSC activation and fibrosis. PRMT1 affects lipid and glucose metabolism by regulating the pro-inflammatory NF-κB and pro-fibrotic TGF-β signaling pathways, thus alleviating liver fibrosis. Therefore, targeting PRMT1 is a potential strategy for the treatment of MASLD and liver fibrosis [ , ]. The reduction of symmetric dimethylation of histone H3 arginine 2 (H3R2me2s), catalyzed by PRMT5, leads to decreased Gh gene expression and lower GH levels. This alteration results in weakened GH signaling, ultimately contributing to liver lipid and glucose metabolism disorders, which can lead to MASLD [ ].

3.

## Correlation between arginine methylation and AFLD
The effects of arginine methylation on alcoholic liver disease are primarily manifested in two aspects: exacerbating the progression of alcoholic liver disease and augmenting the ability of alcohol to promote the occurrence of liver cancer. Studies have found that the expression of DDAH in the liver tissue of patients with alcoholic hepatitis (AH) is reduced and the expression of PRMT1 is increased. DDAH is an enzyme that metabolizes ADMA, while PRMT1 is an enzyme that synthesizes ADMA. This imbalance in the expression of DDAH and PRMT1 may lead to the accumulation of ADMA in the liver of AH patients, thereby inhibiting eNOS activity, ultimately affecting vascular function and aggravating portal hypertension [ ]. Another study showed that the expression of PRMT1 is increased in the liver tissue of rats with AFLD; inhibition of PRMT1 improved liver function and reduced inflammation, oxidative stress, pathological changes, and hepatocyte apoptosis in the liver tissue of rats with AFLD [ ].
PRMT1 inhibition (e.g., alcohol-induced inhibition) can lead to epigenetic changes, resulting in the loss of Hnf4α; this effect potentially contributes to the promotion of liver tumors by alcohol [ ]. Furthermore, PRMT1 expression is correlated with STAT3 activation in human HCC specimens, indicating that PRMT1 plays a role in alcohol-related tumor progression via the PRMT1-IL-6-STAT3 axis [ ].

## Arginine methylation is a target for viral hepatitis to regulate viral hepatitis progression
Arginine methyltransferases typically do not promote the progression of viral hepatitis and in some cases may even directly inhibit it. However, both HBV and HCV can accelerate their own replication and progression by inhibiting the negative regulation of arginine methyltransferases and promoting their positive regulation.
Hepatitis B x protein (HBx) is essential for initiating and maintaining HBV replication [ ]. Studies have shown that PRMT1 is recruited to HBV DNA and interacts with HBx, and HBx directly inhibits PRMT1-mediated protein methylation. Moreover, PRMT1 activity in cells is downregulated during HBV replication. These results suggest that HBx binding to PRMT1 may facilitate viral replication by alleviating the inhibitory activity of PRMT1 on HBV transcription [ ]. Similar to PRMT1, PRMT5 restricts HBV replication through two mechanisms, which include epigenetic inhibition of cccDNA transcription and interference with pre-genomic RNA encapsidation [ ]. However, HBx can induce the intracellular E3 ubiquitin ligase containing DDB1 to degrade WDR77, a protein that regulates the enzymatic activity of PRMT5, resulting in the loss of PRMT5 methyltransferase activity. This indicates that HBx can establish a positive feedback loop of HBx-DDB1/WDR77/PRMT5/H4R3me2s/cccDNA/HBV/HBx in the liver to impair the function of PRMT5, thereby promoting HBV replication [ ]. Notably, the arginine methylation of HSPA8 mediated by PRMT9 inhibits ferroptosis, while HBx promotes the PRMT9/HSPA8/CD44 axis, thereby accelerating the progression of HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma [ ].
In HCV infection, the induction of protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) overexpression represents an important mechanism for aggravating hepatitis and inducing liver cancer, which is primarily achieved by inhibiting the activity of arginine methyltransferases. Yuan et al. showed that HCV infection induces overexpression of the catalytic subunit of PP2A (PP2Ac), which inhibits the activity of PRMT1. PRMT1 is crucial in epigenetic histone modification and DNA damage repair. PP2Ac overexpression leads to inhibition of histone H4 methylation/acetylation and histone H2AX phosphorylation, thereby altering the expression of genes associated with liver cancer. Additionally, PP2Ac overexpression itself impairs DNA damage repair [ ].
Moreover, the upregulation of PP2A enhances the enzymatic activity of an important viral protein, NS3 helicase, which also inhibits the enzymatic activity of PRMT1. PP2Ac also inhibits interferon-induced signaling and disrupts the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), which is crucial for the cell’s defense against viruses, thereby promoting the development of hepatitis C [ ]. Additionally, PP2A regulates IFN-α signal transduction by strongly inhibiting PRMT1. Inhibition of PRMT1 can reduce the methylation level of STAT1 and increase the binding of its inhibitor PIAS1 to STAT1. This phenomenon is observed in hepatitis caused by HBV and HCV, leading directly to resistance to pegIFN-alpha treatment [ ].

## Arginine methylation promotes HCC progression through multiple mechanisms
The pathological types of liver cancer include hepatocellular carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and mixed cell carcinoma. The progression of several benign diseases discussed in this review often culminates in hepatocellular carcinoma. In this section, we primarily focus on the mechanism of PRMTs in the occurrence and development of hepatocellular carcinoma (see ).
 Unlike the debatable role of PRMT1 in viral hepatitis, the malignant effect of PRMT1 in liver cancer is indisputable. Many studies have demonstrated that different strategies that downregulate PRMT1 can inhibit the occurrence and development of HCC [ , , ]. Moreover, clinically, the overexpression of PRMT1 significantly correlates with the poor prognosis of many HCC patient groups. Additionally, studies have shown that after PRMT1 knockdown, HCC cell lines exhibit an inhibition of cell growth and spheroid formation, an increase in sub-G1 cells, and enrichment of cell cycle pathways, suggesting that PRMT1 promotes the progression of HCC and is intricately linked to the cell cycle [ ].
Many groups have conducted extensive research on the cancer-promoting mechanism of PRMT1 in HCC. Most studies have shown that the cancer-promoting effect of PRMT1 is mediated by PRMT1 regulating the metabolism of HCC. Some researchers have also found that PRMT1 can promote the progression of HCC through various classical signaling pathways.
Mitochondrial fission induced by mitochondrial fission factor (MFF) triggers mitochondrial autophagy and asymmetric stem cell division. PRMT1 directly binds to the promoter of MFF, mediates the transcriptional activation of MFF through histone modification, and shifts the metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, thereby enhancing the stemness and tumor initiation ability of non-liver cancer-initiating cells, resulting in the malignant progression of HCC [ ].
Wang et al. found that the increase in serine levels in HCC results from the methylation of PHGDH at arginine 236 mediated by PRMT1, leading to the enhancement of PHGDH catalytic activity. PRMT1-mediated PHGDH methylation and activation enhanced serine synthesis, improved oxidative stress, and promoted HCC growth in vitro and in vivo. PRMT1-mediated PHGDH methylation is associated with PHGDH overactivation and serine accumulation in human HCC tissues and predicts poor prognosis in HCC patients [ ]. One study indicated that PRMT1 may also exert its carcinogenic effects by modulating the immune microenvironment and fatty acid metabolism, although the specific mechanism requires further study [ ]. Moreover, PRMT1 upregulates the phosphorylation of Smad2/3 to activate the TGF-β1/Smad pathway and also activates the STAT3 signaling pathway to promote liver cancer growth and metastasis, and make poor prognosis in vitro and in vivo [ , ]. However, the specific mechanism remains unclear.
As members of the type I arginine methyltransferases, PRMT2, PRMT3, and PRMT4 exhibit mostly similar cancer-promoting effects in HCC, similar to PRMT1, but each has its unique features. For example, PRMT2 enhances STAT3 to promote Bcl gene expression, but PRMT2 does not directly act on STAT3; instead, it increases the accessibility of STAT3 through H3R8 asymmetric methylation (H3R8me2a) [ ]. The expression of PRMT3 is also significantly up-regulated in HCC, and its high expression is associated with poor prognosis, also by regulating metabolism. Studies have found that PRMT3 interacts with lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and mediates asymmetric dimethylarginine modification at arginine 112 to promote HCC cell proliferation, glycolysis, and tumor growth; in contrast, inhibiting PRMT3 produces the opposite result [ ]. PRMT3 mediates oxaliplatin resistance through its methylation of IGF2BP1 at R452. Methylated IGF2BP1 stabilizes the mRNA levels of HEG1 (an effector of the PRMT3-IGF2BP1 axis), which is crucial for its functionality [ ]. Inhibiting PRMT4 or PRMT1 delays the progression of HCC cells by inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway [ , ].
PRMT5 has been demonstrated by many studies to exert carcinogenic effects and is a recognized target for tumor therapy. Accordingly, PRMT5 inhibitors have entered clinical trials. A role for PRMT5 as a therapeutic target has also been shown in liver cancer. PRMT5 is significantly overexpressed in HCC compared with liver tissue, and increased PRMT5 expression is associated with lower overall survival and poor prognosis in patients after radical hepatectomy [ , ]. PRMT5 promotes HCC growth mainly through metabolism and the cell cycle and promotes HCC invasion and metastasis by upregulating EMT marker proteins. Dysregulation of the sterol regulatory element–binding transcription factors sterol regulatory element binding protein (SREBP) and SREBF activates de novo lipogenesis in cancer cells to a high level, a key event that drives malignant growth [ ]. Symmetrical dimethylation of SREBP1a by PRMT5 prevents SREBP1a from being ubiquitinated and degraded by FBW7, thereby increasing the biosynthesis of cholesterol, fatty acids, and triglycerides in cells, and ultimately promoting the growth of liver cancer cells [ , ]. Moreover, PRMT5 promotes the cell cycle of HCC through two mechanisms. First, by competitively inhibiting the interaction with CDK4 and CDKN2A, PRMT5 promotes glucose-induced HCC cell cycle progression and tumor growth [ ]. Second, PRMT5 induces ERK phosphorylation to regulate BTG2 expression in HCC cells, which upregulates the expression of cyclin D1 and cyclin E1 proteins and promotes the G1 phase to S phase cell cycle transition [ ]. B. Zhang et al. found that targeting PRMT5 downregulates β-catenin, indicating that the expression of PRMT5 promotes EMT progression [ ]. In addition, K. Zhu et al. found that the PRMT5 and the metadherin complex promote the nuclear entry of β-catenin, leading to the transcriptional activation of mesenchymal markers [ ]. A recent study reported that the symmetrical dimethylation of H3R2 and H4R3 on the promoter of PRMT5 target genes can activate DVL3 expression while inhibiting E-cadherin [ ]. These studies have shown that PRMT5 upregulates the expression of β-catenin and enhances its transcriptional activity, while inhibiting the levels of epithelial markers, thereby leading to the invasion and metastasis of HCC.
The role of PRMT6 in HCC cells is controversial. Studies have reported that PRMT6 mainly produces biological effects in HCC through the ERK signaling pathway. PRMT6 methylates CRAF at arginine 100, reducing the RAS binding potential and thereby inhibiting the growth of HCC [ ]. PRMT6 methylation of CRAF at arginine 100 further changes the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)-mediated nuclear relocation of pyruvate kinase M2 isoform (PKM2) (a key regulator of the Warburg effect) into the nucleus. This regulation affects aerobic glycolysis, potentially promoting the development of HCC [ ]. Additionally, PRMT6 deficiency in HCC reduces BAG5 post-translational methylation, leading to increased HSC70 stability and induction of autophagy. This process ultimately promotes tumorigenicity and cell survival in the harsh microenvironment of hepatocellular carcinoma tumors, suggesting a dual role where PRMT6 expression both inhibits and promotes the progression of HCC [ ].
PRMT7 and PRMT9 have been identified as oncoproteins in HCC and promote the progression of the disease through activation of multiple signaling pathways. Studies have shown that upregulation of PRMT7 may lead to the development of HCC-related phenotypes in vitro, in vivo, and clinically, through a mechanisms potentially involving the p53 signaling pathway [ ]. However, the specific mechanism underlying this effect remains unclear. PRMT9 expression also activates the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β/Snail and Notch signaling pathways, promoting EMT progression in HCC. This role in signaling accelerates the malignant phenotype of HCC, contributing significantly to invasion and metastasis [ , ].

## Non-canonical functions of PRMTs in liver diseases and PRMT inhibitors
PRMTs mostly affect the progression of liver disease through methylase activity. Some non-canonical functions of PRMTs have also been reported [ ]. In the liver, PRMTs participate in the formation of certain complexes that regulate DNA expression and the production of miRNA, thereby influencing the progression of liver disease. For example, ILF3 forms a complex with LRH-1 and two other nuclear receptor co-activators, PRMT1 and PGC-1α, synergistically promoting LRH-1-dependent gene expression with target genes that encode factors involved in regulating bile and cholesterol homeostasis [ ]. PRMT3 also binds to LXRα as a transcriptional cofactor, thereby inducing lipogenesis [ ]. PRMT5 has many functions that are not solely dependent on enzyme activity. In viral hepatitis, beyond its inhibitory effect on cccDNA transcription, PRMT5 also inhibits the production of HBV core particle DNA independent of its methyltransferase activity [ ]. The complex formed by PRMT5 and MTDH plays a key role in liver cancer metastasis by regulating the WNT-β-catenin signaling pathway [ ]. PRMTs also regulate RNA metabolism, primarily by regulating conventional and alternative splicing. PRMT5 is particularly important for the composition of the spliceosome and the accuracy of the splicing process [ ]. While this aspect of its function in liver diseases has been rarely studied, a previous study reported that PRMT5 in the liver can regulate the biosynthesis of miRNA through the PRMT5-MDM2-Drosha signaling pathway, which is crucial for PA-induced non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [ ].
Given the role of PRMTs in various diseases in the liver and in promoting the occurrence and development of different tumors, various PRMT inhibitors have been developed, with most showing excellent inhibitory effects in research studies ( ). A number of inhibitors have entered clinical trials, which are primarily focusing on their anti-cancer effects. Clinical trials of PRMT5 inhibitors for solid tumors, which include liver cancer, are currently underway, particularly for tumors with MTAP deficiency. The effects of inhibitors that exploit PRMT5-MTAP synthetic lethality are expected to be more favorable [ , ]. Several clinical trials have shown that PRMT5 inhibitors, namely GSK3326595, JNJ-64619178, and PF-06939999, have demonstrated significant anti-cancer effects and good prognostic outcomes in several cancers. However, some clinical trials have been terminated, mainly because these inhibitors cause serious adverse reactions and have limited clinical efficacy, such as the type I PRMT inhibitor GSK3368715 [ ]. PRMTs also play an important role in many benign liver diseases. As more research emerges, an increasing number of clinical trials on PRMT inhibitors for benign liver diseases may also be initiated.

## Outlook and conclusion
Research into the role of arginine methylation modifications in the malignant progression from benign and malignant liver diseases is gaining increasing attention. PRMTs, the primary enzymes that regulate arginine methylation, play a crucial function in the pathogenesis of liver diseases. Previous studies have highlighted the impact of PRMTs on alcoholic liver disease, non-alcoholic liver disease, viral hepatitis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. However, the specific underlying mechanisms have not been extensively explored. Notably, several substrates and tumor-promoting mechanisms regulated by PRMTs have been identified in HCC, some of which are also observed in other cancers. Moreover, there are unique mechanisms involving homologous substrates in HCC that warrant further investigation [ , ].
Inhibitors targeting various PRMTs have shown significant therapeutic benefits in some studies; however, they also exhibit non-specific side effects and excessive toxicity [ , ]. Future research efforts should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms of PRMTs in liver disease progression, exploring novel interactions with other regulatory factors, and deciphering the regulatory networks of signaling pathways. A comprehensive understanding of the roles of PRMTs in liver diseases is expected to unveil new therapeutic targets and strategies. This knowledge will pave the way for the development of more targeted drugs for clinical applications, thereby advancing the field of personalized treatment for liver diseases and contributing to the realization of precision medicine.
In summary, the link between arginine methylation modification and both benign and malignant liver diseases represents a rapidly evolving research area. This review highlights recent advances in the field, deepening our understanding of the pivotal role of arginine methylation in the progression of liver diseases. By discussing the current research on these mechanisms, the review also offers insights into the broader implications of the PRMT family across various biological systems. Ultimately, these findings provide valuable guidance for future clinical treatments and drug development strategies.
