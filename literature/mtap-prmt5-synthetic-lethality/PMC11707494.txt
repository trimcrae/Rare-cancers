## Abstract

## Aims
Loss of heterozygosity in chromosome 9p21, common in urothelial carcinoma (UC), typically involves deletion of CDKN2A and MTAP genes. MTAP loss is emerging as a promising therapeutic target and predictive biomarker in UC. This single‐centrre retrospective study examined the incidence of CDKN2A deletions and MTAP loss in muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC), investigating their correlations with clinical, pathological, and genomic features, as well as patient outcomes.

## Methods
Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were performed on 302 MIBC specimens and 63 biopsy‐proven metachronous urothelial metastases to assess CDKN2A deletions and MTAP protein expression.

## Results

CDKN2A homozygous deletion (HD), identified in 30.3% of MIBCs, and MTAP loss, found in 28.8% of MIBCs, were both significantly associated with the luminal‐URO subtype, FGFR3 mutations, and normal/wildtype p53 IHC (P < 0.05). Loss of MTAP expression was significantly correlated with CDKN2A HD, with 84.0% sensitivity, 92.3% negative predictive value (NPV), 96.3% specificity, and 91.9% positive predictive value (PPV). MTAP expression was 100% concordant between primary tumours and nodal metastases. Patients with MTAP loss had a higher incidence of visceral metastases (50%) compared to bone/soft tissue (35.7%) and nodes (14.3%). Mean progression‐free survival and overall survival were shorter for patients with MTAP loss, although not statistically significant.

## Conclusion
Our findings highlight CDKN2A HD and MTAP loss as prevalent genetic alterations in MIBC and mUC, particularly within the luminal‐URO subtype and FGFR3‐mutated, p53‐normal/wildtype tumours. MTAP IHC can serve as a surrogate marker for 9p21.3 HD, highlighting its clinical relevance and potential as a therapeutic target and predictive biomarker in MIBC.

## Introduction
Despite notable advancements in immunologic and precision therapies, muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) remains a major cause of cancer‐related deaths, underscoring the urgent need for better characterization and targeting of its distinctive molecular subtypes. , At the molecular level, MIBC is a heterogeneous disease characterized by genomic instability, a high mutation rate, and notable transcriptional alterations. Transcriptome profiling has enabled the categorization of MIBC into distinct molecular subtypes, facilitating more precise patient risk stratification and therapy decision making. Various molecular classification systems have been proposed delineating specific molecular features and pathological insights. Despite variances in classification approaches, MIBC can be broadly categorized into basal and luminal subtypes that have distinct prognostic implications and treatment responses. , , 

Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in chromosome 9p21.3 is a common genetic aberration in bladder cancer, resulting in the loss of the tumour suppressor gene CDKN2A (p16) and codeletion of CDKN2B and MTAP. Although LOH of CDKN2A and reduced p16 expression are typically regarded as early genetic events primarily associated with nonmuscle‐invasive bladder cancer, they have also been linked to muscle invasion in FGFR3 mutated tumours. , Aberrant CDKN2A (p16) expression, attributed to genetic or epigenetic alterations, has been correlated with poor treatment response and worse outcomes in MIBC. Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) has demonstrated utility in detecting CDKN2A deletions in bladder cancer, offering potential prognostic value, but it is a costly and time‐consuming method requiring expertise for interpretation. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for p16 is a suboptimal surrogate for CDKN2A FISH due to its variable concordance with CDKN2A status. , Thus, a new IHC surrogate marker is needed.
The MTAP tumour suppressor gene, located on chromosome 9p21.3 near CDKN2A, has been identified as an alternative reliable IHC surrogate for CDKN2A FISH in mesothelioma and other tumours. , , Supporting this, a recent study by Vlajnic et al. demonstrated an 89% concordance between MTAP loss detected by IHC and CDKN2A homozygous deletions identified via FISH in a cohort of nonmuscle‐invasive, muscle‐invasive, and metastatic urothelial carcinomas. Notably, MTAP encodes a key rate‐limiting metabolic enzyme essential for polyamine and purine metabolism, playing a significant role in the purine/methionine salvage pathway; thus, its loss renders tumours susceptible to synthetic lethality by antifolates targeting de novo purine synthesis. , Several clinical trials are currently underway to evaluate inhibitors for MTAP‐deficient advanced solid tumours, including urothelial carcinoma (NCT03744793).
This study presents a single‐centrre retrospective analysis, investigating the incidence of CDKN2A deletions and MTAP loss in MIBC and metastatic urothelial carcinoma. Additionally, it examines novel correlation of these genetic alterations with clinical, pathological, and genomic features, as well as patient outcomes. These findings have the potential to guide future trial design for novel therapeutics targeting this subgroup of patients, as well as establishing a new predictive and prognostic biomarker in MIBC.

## Methods

## Case selection and morphologic review
This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre (Toronto, Ontario, CA) (REB 187‐2016), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The MIBC cohort consisted of 302 cases of high‐grade muscle‐invasive urothelial carcinomas treated by cystectomy at Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre between 1999 and 2019. Additionally, a cohort of 63 metastatic urothelial carcinomas was included, comprising: (1) biopsy‐proven, metachronous metastatic urothelial carcinoma, and (2) cystectomy with urothelial carcinoma and pelvic node dissections. Both cohorts were identified through a retrospective search of the laboratory information system, Sunquest CoPath, as previously detailed. 

Only muscle‐invasive urothelial carcinomas were included in the study cohort. This included urothelial histology and recognized subtypes, as well as cases with less than 50% divergent differentiation. Exclusion criteria encompassed cystectomy cases with lower stage than pT2 and/or with nonurothelial histology (e.g. pure squamous carcinoma, pure glandular carcinoma), cases with divergent differentiation (squamous, glandular, or sarcomatoid) greater than 50%, and any cases with the presence of a neuroendocrine carcinoma component. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides for each case were retrieved and reviewed by a pathologist with subspecialty training in genitourinary pathology, who confirmed and documented the following information: tumour histology, grade (2016/2022 World Health Organization/International Society of Urologic Pathology), pathologic stage (TNM 8th edition); the presence of carcinoma in situ (CIS), margin status, lymphovascular invasion, and the presence of nodal metastases. For each case, additional data collected included sex, age, date of surgery, neoadjuvant therapy, date of last known follow up, date of recurrence, and date of death, if applicable. Notably, only deaths recorded in the electronic patient record were available.

## Tissue microarray construction, immunohistochemistry, and interpretation
Tissue microarray (TMA) blocks were created using a Beecher Instrument (Beecher Instruments, Silver Springs, MD, USA), as previously described. Potential heterogeneity was addressed by carefully selecting cases with predominant urothelial carcinoma for the construction of the TMA. For each case, a single tissue block was utilized. To account for intratumoral heterogeneity, three different representative tissue regions (superficial, mid, and deep tumour) were selected and triplicate cores of 1 mm in diameter were extracted to construct the TMA. Tissue sections of 4 μm were then cut from the TMA for IHC staining. To address any variability in histochemical scoring between the three cores, the average scores across triplicate cores were calculated. GATA3 and CK5/6 expression was evaluated on TMA sections by IHC, as previously described. , Each tumour was classified as basal (CK 5/6+, GATA3−), luminal (CK 5/6−, GATA3+), or double‐negative (CK 5/6−, GATA3−). , , These classifications align with the Kamoun et al. consensus classification for MIBC, corresponding to the Basal/Squamous, luminal and luminal nonspecified (LumNS) classes, respectively, although the correlation is not exact. The luminal group was further divided into two subgroups: genomically unstable (GU, p16+) or urothelial‐like (Uro, p16−). , , , The distinction between the two luminal subtypes, Uro and GU, aligns with the Kamoun et al. consensus classification MIBC subtypes luminal papillary (LumP) and luminal unstable (LumU), respectively.
p16 expression was evaluated as either negative (complete absence of staining) or positive, defined by the presence of any staining. For p53 expression, IHC staining was defined as either abnormal (0% or >50% nuclear staining) or normal (1–49% nuclear staining), as previously described. MTAP by IHC (Abnova, Taipei City, Taiwan, clone 2G4) was performed on a single block for each specimen (metastasis, cystectomy, and positive lymph node) and interpreted as the presence or absence of labelling, defined as the presence of any staining or complete loss of staining, respectively. All samples were evaluated by a pathologist (M.R.D.) experienced in genitourinary pathology and blinded to 9p21.3 FISH status.

## RNA extraction and mRNA expression analysis
RNA extraction was performed as previously described using the High‐Pure FFPET RNA Isolation Kit (from Roche, Basel, Switzerland), following the manufacturer's instructions. The expression of CDKN2A was determined using a custom NanoString probeset (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA, USA), as described previously. Data normalization was achieved through the use of internal negative controls, synthetic positive controls, and five housekeeping genes.

## 
CDKN2A fluorescent in situ hybridization

CDKN2A deletion status was assessed by FISH, using a dual‐colour FISH assay on 4‐μm paraffin‐embedded TMA sections. FISH was performed on the Dako Omnis (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) instrument, using SureFISH probes for chromosome 9 centromere and CDKN2A gene region (SureFISH CHR9CEP 637KB P100 GR/Product#: G110144G‐8 and SureFISH 9P21.3 CDKN2A 351 KB P20/Product#: G110402R‐8). The SureFISH 9p21.3 CDKN2A probe covers a 350 Kb region spanning chr9:21,825,950‐22,177,175 (hg19), which includes the CDKN2A, CDKN2B genes and the majority of the MTAP gene (from intron 4 to 3′).
The procedure included pretreatment using Dako ISH Pre‐Treatment Solution for 30 min at 97°C, proteolytic digestion using Dako ISH Pepsin for 20 min at 32°C, denaturation for 10 min at 66°C, hybridization for 120 min at 45°C and posthybridization wash for 5 min at 58°C. CDKN2A and CEP9 signal counts were assessed in 100 nuclei per case, counted manually at the microscope. FISH slides were examined by two observers (K.J.C. and A.O.), including a molecular pathologist with extensive FFPE FISH experience, who confirmed the sufficient quality of the FISH signals, and that signal counts were representative of tumour cells. CDKN2A mean signal count of <0.5 per cell was used as a cutoff to define homozygous deletion (the vast majority of HD cases had a mean CDKN2A signal count of <0.2 per cell), between 0.5 to 1.3 per cell used to define heterozygous deletion, and a ratio of CDKN2A:CEP9 < 0.7 used to define an otherwise unspecified deletion (imbalance).

## 

FGFR3
 mutation analysis

FGFR3 mutation analysis was conducted on 50 cases from the TMA cohort in a research lab as previously described, utilizing a NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with BWA enrichment and BaseSpace enrichment application. Metastatic urothelial cases and cystectomy specimens (n = 25) were sequenced in a clinical lab using the OCAv3 somatic mutation panel on the S5 Prime Thermo‐Fisher (Waltham, MA, USA) next‐generation sequencer at a mean depth of over 1500×. The bioinformatic pipeline analysis employed Ion Reporter v. 5.18 (Thermo‐Fisher), with variant interpretation based on AMP/ASCO/CAP somatic variant classification.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Mean (SD) was calculated for normally distributed and median (IQR) for nonnormally distributed continuous variables. Counts and percentages were calculated for categorical data. The correlation between data was assessed using one‐way analysis of variance (anova) for continuous variables and χ
2 test for categorical variables. The utility of MTAP IHC as a surrogate marker of CDKN2A genomic alterations detected by FISH analysis was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) calculations. ROC analysis was performed using the SPSS Statistics software and area under the curve (AUC) analysis was used to determine the predictive power of CDKN2A mRNA expression for homozygous and heterozygous CDKN2A deletions. Recurrence‐free survival (RFS) was defined as the period from the date of cystectomy to radiologic recurrence, as determined by the treating clinician. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the period from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause. RFS and OS were visualized using Kaplan–Meier plots and significance calculated using the log‐rank test. A two‐sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## 
CDKN2A FISH in muscle‐invasive bladder cancer
To investigate the incidence of CDKN2A deletions in MIBC, we performed FISH analysis on a cohort of 302 MIBC cystectomy specimens. The cohort consisted of 225 males (74.5%) and 77 females (25.5%), with an average age of 71 years (range: 33–93 years) at the time of surgery. Of the 302 patients, 59 received neoadjuvant therapy. The pathological stage was pT2 in 50 cases (16.6%), pT3 in 162 cases (53.6%), and pT4 in 90 cases (29.8%). Molecular subtyping via IHC identified 84 cases (27.8%) as basal, 119 cases (39.4%) as luminal‐Uro, 89 cases (29.5%) as luminal‐GU, and 10 cases (3.3%) as double negative (CK 5/6−, GATA3−). Successful CDKN2A FISH analysis was achieved in 267 of 302 MIBCs, with CDKN2A deletions in 45.3% (121/267) tumours, including 81 homozygous deletions (30.3%), 17 heterozygous deletions (6.4%), and 23 additional nonhomozygous deletions showing an imbalanced polysomic pattern (8.6%). A representative image of CDKN2A FISH signals is shown in Figure  . Chromosome 9 copy number variations were observed in 99 cases, including eight cases (3.0%) showing a monosomy pattern and 91 cases (33.8%) with polysomy. No significant correlation was found between CDKN2A status and clinicopathological features such as age and stage (P > 0.05; Table  ). Thirty‐five cases failed FISH testing because they were missing from the TMA, or had poor‐quality signals.
 The luminal‐URO subtype had the highest prevalence of CDKN2A deletions at 68.9%, while the luminal‐GU subtype had the lowest at 13.6%, with a frequency of 49.3% observed in the basal subtype (P < 0.0016; Table  , Figure  ). In a subset of tumours assessed for FGFR3 mutations (n = 46), CDKN2A deletions were present in seven out of eight (87.5%) FGFR3 mutated and 13 out of 38 (34.2%) wildtype cases (P = 0.006; Table  ). Additionally, CDKN2A deletions were significantly associated with normal p53 IHC (P < 0.001).
 In a subset of cases evaluated (n = 62), a decrease in CDKN2A mRNA expression levels, correlated with the presence of CDKN2A deletions (7.86 vs. 10.89, AUC = 0.843, P < 0.001; Figure  ). Further, a significant association was observed between CDKN2A deletion and loss of p16 expression by IHC (P < 0.001; Table  , Figure  ). Among 173 p16− MIBCs, 65.3% (113/173) had a CDKN2A deletion, compared to 8.5% (8/94) of p16+ MIBCs with a deletion. Thus, the absence of p16 expression predicted a deletion of CDKN2A with a high sensitivity of 93.4% and NPV of 91.5%, but a low specificity of 58.9% and PPV of 65.3%. For CDKN2A homozygous deletions, sensitivity and NPV were 100%, but specificity was only 54.6% and PPV 52.3%.

## MTAP immunohistochemistry in primary muscle‐invasive bladder cancer
MTAP expression loss, detected by IHC, was observed in 28.8% (87/302) MIBCs, comprising 58 luminal‐URO subtype (48.7%), 28 basal (33.3%), and one double negative (1.3%), while no loss was noted in the luminal‐GU subtype (χ

P < 0.001; Table  ). Representative images of MTAP loss and retained MTAP expression are shown in Figure  . There was no significant correlation between MTAP loss and clinicopathological features such as age, stage, and nodal status (P > 0.05; Table  ). In a subset of cases assessed for FGFR3 mutations (n = 50 cases), MTAP loss was found in six out of eight (75.0%) FGFR3 mutated and 8 out of 42 (19.0%) wildtype cases (P = 0.006; Table  ).
 Next, we analysed MTAP loss by IHC as a potential surrogate biomarker for CDKN2A deletions in MIBC. Among 121 tumours with CDKN2A deletions, 70 (57.9%) had MTAP loss, while 140/146 (95.9%) cases without CDKN2A deletions retained MTAP expression. There was an overall significant concordance between MTAP and CDKN2A status in 210 of 267 cases (78.7%, P < 0.001), with a sensitivity of 57.8%, NPV of 73.3%, specificity of 95.9%, and PPV of 92.1%. Further, among 81 tumours with CDKN2A homozygous deletions, 69 (84.0%) exhibited MTAP loss, while 157/163 (96.3%) of cases lacking CDKN2A homozygous deletions retained MTAP expression (Table  ). Thus, MTAP IHC had improved sensitivity for homozygous deletions at 84.0%, NPV 92.3%, specificity 96.3%, and PPV 91.9%.
 

## Characterization of MTAP expression in metastatic urothelial cancer
In 255 MIBC cases with documented nodal status (57.6% N0, 42.4% N1), MTAP loss was identified in 29.4% (n = 85) of primary carcinomas, showing the same prevalence across N0 (n = 48/163, 29.4%) and N1 cases (n = 37/126, 29.4%). Among 82 matched primary tumours and N1 cases, MTAP expression demonstrated complete 100% concordance between primary and synchronous nodal metastases (Figure  ).
Among 63 cases of biopsy‐proven metachronous urothelial metastases, 22% (14/63) exhibited MTAP loss: visceral (7/21, 33.3%), bone/soft tissue (n = 5/28, 17.9%), nodes (2/14, 14.3%). Patients with MTAP loss in urothelial metastases exhibited visceral metastases in 7/14 cases (50%), bone/soft tissue in 5/14 cases (35.7%), and nodes in 2/14 cases (14.3%; P = 0.314). Further, among nine cases where FGFR3 status was determined via next‐generation sequencing, one case was identified as FGFR3 mutated while being MTAP‐proficient.

## Clinical outcomes analysis
We evaluated the prognostic significance of CDKN2A deletions and MTAP loss in MIBC. In our cohort, the mean follow‐up was 29 months (range: 0–227 months), with 107 (42.8%) recurrences and 69 (25.7%) deaths reported.
The recurrence rates for patients with homozygous, heterozygous and no CDKN2A deletions were 41.9% (26/62), 37.5% (6/16), and 43.8% (53/121), respectively, while all‐cause mortality rates were 20.8% (15/72), 37.5% (6/16), and 25.2% (32/127), respectively. There was no significant difference in RFS and OS between patients with CDKN2A deletions and those without (Figure  ).
 Patients with MTAP loss experienced recurrence and all‐cause mortality rates of 45.6% (31/68) and 25.3% (19/75), respectively, compared to 41.8% (76/182) and 25.8% (50/194) for those with retained MTAP. Mean RFS and OS were shorter for patients with MTAP loss (RFS: 23 months, OS: 24 months) compared to those with retained MTAP (RFS: 29 months, OS: 31 months), although these differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.336 for RFS and P = 0.703 for OS; Figure  ). Similarly, in the luminal‐URO MIBC subgroup, RFS was shorter for patients with MTAP loss (mean: 27 months) compared to those with retained MTAP (mean: 37 months, P = 0.169), but statistical significance was not reached (Figure  ).
 

## Discussion
This study was a comprehensive analysis of CDKN2A and MTAP alterations in MIBC and metastatic urothelial carcinoma, along with their clinical correlations. We found CDKN2A deletions in 45.3% of MIBCs, with homozygous deletions in 30.3% of cases, and MTAP loss in 28.8% of cases, akin to prior reports. , , , , , , Additionally, FGFR3 mutations were significantly associated with CDKN2A deletions and MTAP loss, supporting prior evidence linking 9p21 loss with muscle invasion in FGFR3‐mutated tumours. 

In our study, MTAP loss demonstrated a high concordance rate of 92.6% with CDKN2A homozygous deletions, compared to 73.6% for p16, indicating its potential as a clinically accessible surrogate biomarker for 9p21 loss in MIBC. MTAP IHC is a more convenient, rapid, and economical test to perform than FISH. Our findings align with a recent study by Vlajnic et al., which reported an 89% concordance rate between MTAP loss detected via IHC and CDKN2A homozygous deletions identified by FISH in a cohort of nonmuscle‐invasive, muscle‐invasive, and metastatic urothelial carcinomas. Of note, in our cohort, 16.0% of cases and in Vlajnic et al.'s cohort, 18.9% of cases with CDKN2A homozygous deletion did not demonstrate MTAP loss. Previous studies have suggested that CDKN2A and MTAP homozygous deletions co‐occur in 80–90% of deleted tumours, depending on the extent of the deletion. Recent genomic analysis by Basin et al. supports this, demonstrating that 18% of urothelial carcinomas harbouring a CDKN2A deletion lack a concurrent MTAP deletion. Further, in our cohort and in Vlajnic et al.'s cohort, MTAP loss without a CDKN2A homozygous deletion was observed in 4.1% and 7.0% of cases, respectively. This discrepancy may arise from MTAP homozygous deletions occurring independently of CDKN2A deletions, as documented in rare instances across other malignancies. , Other possible explanations may involve MTAP mutations or alternative epigenetic or posttranslational mechanisms leading to MTAP loss of expression or protein inactivation/degradation. Additionally, factors such as tissue fixation, intratumoral heterogeneity, and the efficacy of FISH probes or IHC antibodies may influence interpretation, potentially contributing to the discordance between CDKN2A and MTAP status.
MTAP loss is emerging as a novel promising therapeutic target as well as a potential predictive and prognostic biomarker that defines a clinically relevant subgroup of bladder cancers. Our study provides new insights into its prevalence in the luminal‐URO MIBC subtype and FGFR3‐mutated, p53 normal/wildtype tumours, supporting prior research linking FGFR3 alterations to luminal MIBC subtypes. Within the luminal‐Uro subtype, distinguishing between MTAP deficient and MTAP proficient subgroups may improve risk assessment and guide tailored therapeutic strategies, addressing the heterogeneous clinical behaviour of luminal MIBC. Notably, intratumoral heterogeneity of IHC‐based molecular subtypes has been described in MIBC. This heterogeneity could impact the effectiveness of molecular subtype‐guided personalized treatment strategies, warranting further investigation. Previous studies have demonstrated that MTAP loss in urothelial carcinomas is significantly associated with visceral metastases. In our study, no significant differences were observed between the small subgroups analysed.
Previous studies have associated CDKN2A deletions with the progression of nonmuscle‐invasive bladder carcinomas, while MTAP mutations and loss of protein expression have been linked to worse PFS and OS in metastatic urothelial carcinomas. , , Notably, high CDKN2A RNA expression has been correlated with worse prognosis in MIBC, which contrasts with findings in nonmuscle‐invasive bladder cancer. Possible explanations may involve DNA methylation and/or other alternative posttranslational mechanisms. Data on the correlation between CDKN2A and MTAP alterations and patient outcomes in localized MIBC is limited. Our study found no association between CDKN2A deletions and patient outcomes. Further, MTAP loss showed a trend towards shorter RFS and OS, but did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the limited sample size and variable follow‐up times. Larger studies are needed to investigate the prognostic value of MTAP loss in localized MIBC.
This is among the first studies to demonstrate absolute concordance in MTAP expression between paired primary tumours and synchronous nodal metastases in MIBC, indicating stability in MTAP status. This has important practical implications, suggesting that MTAP status can be determined from either the primary tumour or the metastatic site, provided that the specimen size is adequate. MTAP status holds significant potential in guiding the management of metastatic urothelial carcinoma, with emerging evidence supporting its role as a therapeutic target and predictive biomarker across various treatment modalities, including chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Several ongoing early‐stage clinical trials are investigating targeted therapeutic strategies for MTAP‐deficient solid tumours, including metastatic urothelial cancer (NCT03744793). MTAP loss leads to an accumulation of its substrate, methylthioadenosine, rendering tumour cells potentially sensitive to methionine depletion and inhibitors of purine synthesis, such as methylthioadenosine cooperative protein arginine methyltransferase 5 inhibitors (NCT05094336, NCT06360354, NCT06333951, NCT05275478, NCT05732831, NCT05245500, NCT05975073, NCT06130553, NCT06137144) and methionine adenosyltransferase 2A inhibitors (NCT06188702, NCT04794699). Recent studies have also demonstrated that MTAP‐deficient tumours exhibit sensitivity to antifolates targeting de novo purine synthesis, such as pemetrexed, through synthetic lethality, currently being investigated in clinical trials (NCT04895735, NCT05335941). Further, the accumulation of methylthioadenosine inhibits the methylation of STAT1, consequently suppressing interferon signalling pathways and PD‐L1 expression. MTAP loss has been associated with a “cold” immunophenotype and lower response rates to immune checkpoint inhibitors. This is currently under investigation in clinical trials for metastatic MTAP‐deficient urothelial carcinoma (NCT05335941).
Our study has the limitation of being a retrospective study from a single institution, with variability in follow‐up times. Additionally, the sample size may lack sufficient statistical power to ascertain significant associations with clinical outcomes and further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate the findings. Despite the above limitations, our study provides significant insights into the prevalence of CDKN2A and MTAP alterations in MIBC and metastatic urothelial carcinoma, their prognostic clinical correlations, and emerging therapeutic implications.
In conclusion, CDKN2A deletion and MTAP loss define a prevalent subtype of MIBC, associated with luminal‐URO MIBC and distinct genetic alterations, such as FGFR3 mutations and normal p53 IHC. We show that MTAP loss is a clinically accessible surrogate biomarker for homozygous 9p21.3 (CDKN2A) deletions in addition to being an emerging novel therapeutic target with potential predictive and prognostic significance. MTAP status is concordant across primary tumours and synchronous nodal metastases, highlighting its clinical relevance and the potential for guiding patient stratification for therapy and the development of alternative therapeutic interventions.
