## ABSTRACT
Patients with triple‐negative breast cancer (ER−, PR−, and HER2−) are routinely treated with chemotherapies that induce DNA damage. However, around 30% of patients display resistance, owing largely to increased DNA repair mechanisms, upregulated to allow cancer cells to escape such therapies. PRMT1 and PRMT5, the two main protein arginine methyltransferases, are involved in several biological pathways, including DNA repair signaling, where they contribute to ensuring DNA integrity. We then speculated that targeting their enzymatic activity may sensitize TNBC cells to chemotherapeutic agents inducing DNA double‐strand breaks. Here, we showed that PRMT1 and PRMT5 are recruited to DNA double‐strand breaks upon doxorubicin or carboplatin treatment, two chemotherapies currently used to treat TNBC patients, and are preferentially involved in the homologous recombination pathway. By combining PRMT inhibitors with doxorubicin or carboplatin, we increased DNA double‐strand breaks and impaired TNBC cell proliferation and clonogenicity in vitro and sensitized patient‐derived models of TNBC to carboplatin treatment. These preclinical data provide a rationale for the clinical evaluation of PRMT inhibitors as combinatorial agents to improve chemotherapy efficacy for TNBC patients.

Our work makes the proof of concept that there is a clinical benefit to target both PRMT1 and PRMT5 to maximize the anti‐tumoral effect of chemotherapy in triple negative breast cancer.

## Treating Doxorubicin‐Resistant TNBC Cells With PRMTi Sensitizes Them to Doxorubicin
We then generated MDA‐MB‐231 cells with acquired resistance to doxo (DoxoR) by exposing them to increasing doses of the drug for 6 months. The control cell line (DoxoS) was grown under the same conditions by exposing them to DMSO. Of note, 25 nM of doxo was sufficient to significantly reduce DoxoS cell proliferation (Figure  , left), whereas 75 nM of doxo showed a small reduction in DoxoR cell proliferation (Figure  , left). We then confirmed the inhibitory effect of PRMTi on the proliferation of DoxoS cells, either alone or in combination, with or without 15 nM doxo (Figure  , center and right). Interestingly, while PRMTi alone or in combination had a moderate effect on the growth of DoxoR cells (Figure  , center), combining doxo with both PRMTi strongly inhibited their proliferation (Figure  , right). We finally confirmed these findings in MDA‐MB‐231 DoxoR spheroids cultured in Matrigel (Figure  ), indicating that PRMTi re‐sensitizes doxo‐resistant TNBC cells to chemotherapy in vitro.

## Combining PRMTi With Carboplatin Decreases Growth of TNBC Patient‐Derived Organoids (PDO) and Patient‐Derived Xenografts (PDX)
Given that patient‐derived organoids (PDOs) retain the molecular features of the original tumor and recapitulate tumor heterogeneity better than traditional two‐dimensional cell culture models, they are frequently used as ex vivo models for drug response prediction [ , ]. Here, we derived organoids from a TNBC PDX, HBCx‐39, a model cross‐resistant to anthracyclines and platinum drugs [ , ]. Firstly, we established the IC50 values for G715, G595, and carbo at 0.05 μM, 0.08 μM, and 8 μM, respectively (Figure  ). We then evaluated the synergism between carbo and type1 PRMTi (G715) and PRMT5i (G595), separately, using a 6X6 matrix design and the Loewe synergy model (Figure  ). Synergy between carbo and PRMT1/5i was found at the lowest concentrations of PRMT1i and PRMT5i. Based on the results, we decided to test the triple combination using 100 nM of carbo and 5 nM of G715 and G595 on the viability of HBCx‐39 organoids. As expected from our in vitro results, the most significant decrease in cell viability was observed when the three drugs were combined (Figure  ).
 The same combination of treatments was then tested on the HBCx‐39 PDX, previously classified as poorly responsive to platinum drugs and homologous recombination proficient [ , ]. The PDX was totally resistant to both PRMTi and the triple combination increased the response to carboplatin, although modestly (Figure  ). This result was confirmed by an increase in mouse survival from 25 days to 42 days when carbo was combined with both PRMTi compared to PRMTi alone (Figure  ). We validated the adequate functioning of PRMTi by assessing aDMA and sDMA using pan antibodies through immunohistochemistry (Figure  ). aDMA, reflecting PRMT1 activity, is detected in the nucleus of tumor cells, while sDMA, showing PRMT5 activity, is mostly localized in the cytoplasm. Upon PRMTi treatment, both nuclear aDMA and cytoplasmic sDMA staining are reduced, consistent with effective inhibition of PRMT1 and PRMT5 in our model. We then confirmed our findings in another TNBC PDX model (HBCx‐158), classified as carboplatin‐resistant but with an HRD phenotype due to BRCA1 promoter methylation [ , ]. In this PDX model, treatment with carbo for 38 days resulted in tumor growth inhibition identical to that induced by both PRMTi, but tumors were still progressing (progressive disease). The combination of all three significantly reduced tumor growth compared to carbo or PRMTi alone (p = 0.0004; Figure  ). Of note, treatment with GSK715 + GSK595 resulted in a more pronounced tumor growth inhibition in the HR‐deficient HBCx‐158 compared to HBCx‐39 model, indicating increased sensitivity to PRMTi in the context of homologous recombination deficiency. The efficacy of PRMTi inhibitors decreased aDMA and sDMA in this model was also verified (Figure  ). Collectively, these results validate our hypothesis ex vivo and in vitro, that the combination of PRMTi enhances the activity of the chemotherapeutic agent.

## 
PRMT1 and PRMT5 Are Present at DNA Repair Sites, and Their Inhibition Increases the Impact of Chemotherapies
Given the involvement of PRMT1 and PRMT5 in the activity of DNA‐damaging chemotherapies, we wondered whether they played a role in DNA repair of DSBs. We first set up proximity ligation assays (PLA) in MDA‐MB‐231 cells to ascertain that PRMT1 and PRMT5 are present at DNA repair sites, using an anti‐γH2AX and anti‐PRMT1 or PRMT5 antibodies (Figures  and ). The interaction between γH2AX (marker of double‐strand breaks) and PRMT1 and PRMT5 increased upon treatment with doxo (Figure  ) and carbo (Figure  ), suggesting that PRMT1 and PRMT5 are present at DNA repair sites and act in the DNA repair process.
 To gain further insight into the role of PRMT1 and PRMT5 in this process, we treated MDA‐MB‐231 cells with doxo alone or in combination with G715 or G595 and quantified DSBs using γH2AX foci analysis. After 24 h of doxo treatment alone, about 10 foci per cell were counted, whereas cells treated with doxo and G715 retained significantly more DSBs (28 foci per cell), indicating a defect in DSB repair (Figure  ). In contrast, G595 alone had no effect, and the PRMTi combination with doxo had the same effect as G715 with doxo. Upon carbo treatment, cells treated with G715 retained significantly more DSBs compared to carbo alone (Figure  ). To ensure that inhibition of PRMT1 and PRMT5 was responsible for sustaining DNA damage, we performed the same experiments by knocking down PRMT1 or PRMT5 expression via siRNA. PRMT1 and PRMT5 knockdown significantly increased the number of γH2AX foci in MDA‐MB‐231 cells treated with doxo (Figure  ) and carbo (Figure  ). As γH2AX is not an exclusive marker of DSBs, we performed the same experiment to assess 53BP1 foci formation (Figure  ). Following treatment with doxo and carbo, cells treated with G715 and G595 exhibited a higher number of 53BP1 foci compared to chemotherapy alone. By contrast, the combination of PRMTi with carbo or doxo drastically increased the number of 53BP1 foci. Hence, our results show that PRMT1 and PRMT5 are involved in DSB repair.

## 
PRMT1 and PRMT5 Act Synergistically to Enhance DNA Repair by Homologous Recombination
We then sought further mechanistic insight into the role of PRMT1 and PRMT5 in DSB repair, focusing on the repair pathway (HR or NHEJ) favored by these methyltransferases. We tested a novel CRISPR/Cas9‐based method capable of quantitatively measuring both HR and NHEJ activities [ ]. We treated MDA‐MB‐231 cells with DMSO, G715, G595, or both, or with a specific inhibitor of HR (AZD7762, 1 mM) or NHEJ (NU7441, 1 mM) and quantitatively measured the impact on pathways. Of note, AZD7762, an inhibitor of the Checkpoint kinase 1 that hampers assembly of Rad51 foci, specifically inhibited HR activity and not NHEJ, while NU7441, a DNA‐PK inhibitor, significantly blocked the NHEJ pathway but strongly increased HR activity (Figure  ). G715 significantly decreased HR activity, whereas G595 had no impact, and their combination had a stronger impact on HR compared to each individual inhibitor (Figure  left). In contrast, both PRMTi had no effect on NHEJ activity (Figure  right). Furthermore, immunostaining for Rad51 shows that G715 and the G715/G595 combination significantly reduce Rad51 foci formation in doxo‐ or carbo‐treated MDA‐MB‐231 cells (Figure  ). Taken together, PRMT1 and PRMT5 are recruited to DSBs and synergistically participate in HR pathways, partly through their enzymatic activities.

## Discussion
Drug resistance represents a major challenge in cancer treatment, justifying the development of novel therapies to prevent recurrence. This is frequently the case with treatments such as chemotherapy, where efficacy is often limited by intrinsic or acquired drug resistance [ ]. Intrinsic resistance is largely caused by factors that existed prior to treatment, whereas acquired resistance is an adaptive multifactorial response to therapy that enables cancer cells to survive in adverse environments. It is essential to identify the key players involved in drug resistance processes, including increased drug efflux, enhanced anti‐apoptotic or DNA repair pathways [ ]. In this context, most patients with TNBC are treated with DNA‐damaging chemotherapies, although 30% of patients develop resistance to such treatments. Here, we reveal that PRMT1 and PRMT5 are promising targets for improving the efficacy of chemotherapy in TNBC. Our in vitro and in vivo experiments clearly demonstrated that combining inhibitors targeting PRMT1 and PRMT5 enhanced the efficacy of doxorubicin and carboplatin to inhibit TNBC proliferation, likely by disrupting DNA repair pathways. Indeed, previous studies have shown that PRMT1‐deficient cells are hypersensitive to DNA‐damaging agents such as etoposide and cisplatin [ , ]. Similarly, loss of PRMT5 triggers a DNA damage response and elevates transposable elements in primordial germ cells [ ]. Moreover, PRMT1 and PRMT5 inhibition sensitizes cancer cells to DNA‐damaging agents including etoposide, camptothecin and ionizing radiation [ , , ]. G715 and G595 have different effects in our in vivo models. Combination of both PRMTi showed enhanced efficacy in HR‐deficient PDX model suggesting that homologous recombination status modulates sensitivity to PRMT inhibition. Mechanistically, PRMT1 and PRMT5 regulate methylation of HR factors such as MRE11, BRCA1, and other DNA repair proteins, and inhibition of these enzymes has been shown to impair HR and promote DNA damage accumulation in cancer cells [ ]. PRMT5 inhibition downregulates the expression of HR‐associated genes BRCA1/2 and RAD51 and synergizes with DNA damage–targeted therapies, further supporting the concept that HR deficiency creates a context in which PRMT inhibitors display increased antitumor activity [ ]. Differences observed between PDOs and PDXs likely reflect the influence of the tumor microenvironment and other in vivo factors that are absent in our organoid system. On the other hand, organoids provide a controlled environment that isolates tumor‐intrinsic drug sensitivity but may not fully recapitulate the complexity of murine xenograft settings. PDOs could be used as a first‐line approach to rapidly screen multiple single or combinatory treatment options. Promising candidates will be validated in matched PDX to assess tumor‐microenvironment interaction, kinetics and toxicity. Concordant results between models could increase confidence in clinical relevance while divergent results could identify context‐dependent effects of treatment. Thus, the complementary use of both models is necessary.
Mechanistically, our work provides a general overview of the role of both PRMTs in DSB repair mechanisms. In prostate cancer, knock down of PRMT5 was reported to reduce the expression of proteins involved in HR and NHEJ, including BRCA1 and BRCA2, as well as DNA‐PK. PRMT5 has also been shown to regulate epigenetic marks by methylating RUVBL1, activating TIP60 activity towards H4K16 acetylation, decreasing 53BP1 binding to DNA ends, and facilitating HR [ ]. PRMT1 methylates the tumor suppressor BRCA1, a crucial factor of HR repair, acting by displacing the NHEJ protein 53BP1 from DSBs. The silencing of PRMT1 in breast cancer cells was reported to block its nuclear translocation, resulting in the loss of HR‐mediated DSB repair after ionizing radiation [ ]. PRMT5, as a key regulator of genome stability, also methylates DDR proteins such as 53BP1 and RUVBL1. The methylation of five arginine residues located in the GAR domain of 53BP1 increases its stability, contributing to NHEJ and HR‐mediated repair processes [ , ]. Our data suggest that DNA damage response and the effects on cell viability and tumor growth could be mediated through different mechanisms. The lack of additive effect on DNA damage markers indicates that PRMT1 inhibition is the primary driver of this phenotype. On the other hand, cell viability and tumor growth assay may be led by additional downstream effects, including transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, and effects on cell survival pathways. Interestingly, while PRMT5 inhibition alone did not significantly affect HR as measured by the HR reporter assay, RAD51 foci formation was reduced following chemotherapy, suggesting a context‐dependent role of PRMT5 in DNA repair where PRMT5 might contribute to HR response primarily under conditions of DNA damage. The increased efficacy of combined PRMTi with carbo, compared with PRMT1 inhibition alone, likely reflects complementary functions of PRMT1 and PRMT5. PRMT1 may regulate methylation of HR factors and early repair signaling, whereas PRMT5 may influence HR‐mediated repair under conditions of genotoxic stress, possibly by modulating the recruitment or stability of repair factors at DNA damage sites.
Together, these effects could synergistically impair DNA repair and increase tumor cell sensitivity to carboplatin. Thus, while DNA damage appears to be predominantly PRMT1‐dependent, the broader cellular and tumor growth effects of combined inhibition may reflect contributions from other key mechanisms distinct from DNA damage response pathways.
Owing to this role and others in multiple cancers, PRMT1 and PRMT5 have emerged as promising therapeutic targets [ ], prompting the development of specific PRMTi. Here, one of the limitations of our study was the use of G715, the only Type 1 inhibitor, to target PRMT1. Indeed, G715 is not specific to PRMT1, as it has nM range affinity for PRMT6 and PRMT8, the latter being expressed specifically in the neurons and the central nervous system [ ]. Hence, we cannot exclude the involvement of PRMT6 in the results observed; nevertheless, we showed that PRMT1 is bound to DNA breaks and that knocking down PRMT1 increased DNA DSBs upon doxorubicin and carboplatin treatment. Conversely, the PRMT5 inhibitor, G595, is highly specific to PRMT5 and has been tested in phase I and II clinical trials, either alone or in combination with pembrolizumab [ ].
Though we focused on DNA repair pathways, PRMT1 and PRMT5 may also reduce sensitivity of TNBC cells to chemotherapy by promoting the formation and maintenance of drug‐tolerant cells via other mechanisms such as altered drug efflux transporters, autophagy, or epithelial‐mesenchymal transition. This may particularly be the case for the HBCx‐158 PDX model, which is HR deficient with a hypermethylated BRCA1 promoter.
Our results also substantiated previous studies on the crosstalk between PRMT5 and PRMT1 Synergistic effects of combined inhibition of type I PRMTs and PRMT5 on cancer cell proliferation, have been shown in pancreatic cancer cell lines and Diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma [ ]. Furthermore, PRMT5 and PRMT1 inhibition have shown synergistic effect in small cell lung cancer, where MTAP‐deleted cells, displaying attenuated PRMT5–MEP50 signaling, are generally more sensitive to PRMT1 inhibition [ ]. This functional interplay could be due to shared substrates, though the number of arginine residues methylated by both PRMT is limited. It seems more likely that the two proteins may interfere with each other. Altogether, our results clearly demonstrate that targeting the enzymatic activities of PRMT1 and PRMT5 enhances the sensitivity of TNBC to chemotherapies, such as doxorubicin and carboplatin. Enhanced knowledge of the complex functions of arginine methylation and PRMTs in drug resistance will guide the future development of PRMT inhibitors and may help identify new clinical indications.
