## Abstract
Precision medicine is a likely future for all cancer treatment but may have its greatest impact on less common, high-mortality, and molecularly heterogeneous cancers. TFCP2-rearranged rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a rare, aggressive cancer with poor survival due to the lack of effective therapies and relevant models to facilitate research. In this study, we establish the first matched patient-derived xenograft and cell line model for TFCP2-rearranged intraosseous RMS, coupled with comprehensive multiomic and functional analyses, to discover and preclinically validate novel actionable molecular targets for this malignancy. Sequencing analyses of matched patient tumor and xenograft material revealed alterations in gene networks associated with the oncogenic, potentially targetable PI3K/AKT pathway. Preclinical assessments revealed that targeting the pathway with a small-molecule PI3K/mTOR inhibitor dactolisib presents a promising treatment approach for this rare cancer, decreasing cancer cell viability in vitro and significantly reducing tumor growth in vivo. Parallel identification of the codeletion of adjacent genes cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A and methylthioadenosine phosphorylase in these tumors led us to further explore protein arginine methyltransferase 5 inhibition as a potential therapeutic approach. Strikingly, combined inhibition of protein arginine methyltransferase 5 and PI3K/mTOR signaling synergistically enhanced antitumor response and significantly improved survival in vivo. This study highlights the importance of new patient-derived models for the elucidation of the biology of rare cancers and identification of new therapeutic entry points, with clear implications for the future treatment of TFCP2-rearranged intraosseous RMS.

## Introduction
Molecular-guided therapy is transforming the clinical management of a variety of solid cancers ( ), including some of the hardest-to-treat malignancies ( ). For many rare cancers, highlighting sarcoma as an example, new effective therapies are lacking and integrative computational analyses, robust preclinical models, and functional methodologies are required to improve disease control and survival.
Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) with TFCP2 rearrangement has recently been described as a new subtype of sarcoma ( ). RMS is a rare cancer in adults with only 0.4 to 0.8 cases per million adults per year ( ). The prevalence of TFCP2 fusions in RMS is poorly understood because of its recent characterization and low frequency ( – ). Primarily occurring in the bones of the head and neck region in young adults, TFCP2-rearranged RMS poses a significant clinical challenge both diagnostically and in terms of treatment. TFCP2 is a transcription factor with a pleiotropic role in cancer, with tumor-promoting effects observed in hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic and breast cancers but with tumor-suppressive roles in melanoma ( ).
Molecular studies of patient cases with TFCP2-rearranged RMS have proposed interesting targets, such as overexpression of anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), with ALK inhibitors thus far yielding mixed responses ( , , ). Most recent assessment of the histologic and molecular characteristics of TFCP2-rearranged RMS revealed frequent methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) deletion ( ), with pharmacologic and genetic targeting of protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) yielding promising preliminary efficacy in other cancer types that are MTAP deficient ( , ). Research into understanding the molecular factors that underpin poor disease control of intraosseous TFCP2-rearranged RMS is still in its infancy, in part due to a lack of preclinical models, with median patient survival of only 30 months ( ).
Using cutting-edge model development technologies ( , ), here we provide new insights into the complex molecular landscape of intraosseous RMS (IORMS) with TFCP2 rearrangement and enable discovery and functional assessment of novel therapeutic vulnerabilities. We establish and characterize a world-first patient-derived xenograft (PDX) and matched patient-derived cell line (PDCL) model of TFCP2 fusion–defined RMS. Integrative whole-genome and transcriptome analyses reveal significant shared features across the matched patient tumor and models and a distinct molecular landscape from other RMS subtypes, highlighting the unique nature of TFCP2-rearranged RMS. Pairing these multiomic insights with in vitro and in vivo functional studies, we uncover a novel tailored treatment regimen involving dual targeting of tumor-promoting PI3K/AKT and PRMT5 signaling. Collectively, these preclinical findings may offer promising new opportunities in subtype-specific targeting and treatment of TFCP2 fusion–defined RMS.

## Materials and Methods

## Patient material
The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient tissue was acquired through the Department of Tissue Pathology and Diagnostic Oncology, Royal Prince Alfred Hospital, on June 11, 2018. The patient provided his written informed consent to participate in this study. The studies were reviewed and approved by the Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee, NSW Health (X19-0282/ETH1216) and St Vincent’s Hospital Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (2020/ETH01283).

## Patient-derived model generation
The TKCC-IORMS PDCL and PDX were established at the Garvan Institute of Medical Research in accordance with methods previously published by our group ( , ). Briefly, patient tumor tissue was mechanically and enzymatically dissociated using Collagenase/Hyaluronidase (STEMCELL Technologies). Cells were then labeled using APC anti–mouse MHC H-2Kd antibody (BioLegend, Cat. # 116619, RRID: AB_10640118), PerCP-eFluor 710 anti–human EpCAM antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. # 46-9326-41, RRID: AB_1834414), and PE/Cyanine7 anti–human CD298 antibody (LNH-94, BioLegend, Cat. # 341707, RRID: AB_2819970) before cell sorting on the FACSAria Fusion flow cytometer (Becton, Dickson and Company) to remove MHC-positive mouse cells and purify CD298- and EpCAM-expressing cells. The sorted cells were plated onto a flask coated with 0.2 mg/mL rat tail I collagen (BD Biosciences) and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 in DMEM/F-12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone), 15 mmol/L HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 400 ng/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 ng/mL human recombinant EGF (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.1 IU/mL insulin (Novo Nordisk), and 1× MEM vitamins (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with in vitro growth rates shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A. Mycoplasma testing was performed using MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza Bioscience) and found to be negative on August 11, 2020. The established PDCL was confirmed by short tandem repeat profiling to be unique (cellbankaustralia.com) on August 10, 2022. The PDCL was used between passages 5 and 14 for all experiments. The tumor donor was biologically male.

## Animal studies
For PDX generation and tissue banking, patient tumor tissue (∼4 mm3) was implanted into the left and right flanks of NOD/SCID/IL-2Rγnull (NSG, RRID: IMSR_JAX:005557) mice and Balb/c nude mice. Tumors were grown to the size of ∼1,500 mm3; at which point, a portion was divided to ∼4-mm3 pieces and re-transplanted into additional mice. The remaining tumor tissue was banked for future experiments. The engraftment rate of the PDXs improved across passages P0 to P3 and stabilizing at P4 (Supplementary Fig. S1B).
For subcutaneous treatment studies, fourth-passage PDX pieces were implanted into the right flank of NSG mice. Tumor dimensions were assessed two times per week using calipers, and tumor volumes were determined using the formula 0.5 × length × width2. Once tumors reached a volume of 150 mm3 (100%), mice were randomized into one of the following treatment groups: (i) crizotinib at 25 mg/kg administered orally five times weekly via gavage, (ii) LLY-283 at 50 mg/kg administered orally three times weekly via gavage, (iii) dactolisib at 50 mg/kg administered orally five times weekly via gavage, and (iv) a combination of LLY-283 (50 mg/kg) three times weekly and dactolisib (50 mg/kg) five times weekly. Measurement of long-term treatment response included a 14-day recovery period before subsequent treatment cycles. Animals were euthanized at a defined ethical endpoint (tumor volume >400%), and tissues were collected for further analyses. All NSG and Balb/c nude mice used for the establishment of TKCC-IORMS-01 PDX model and in vivo drug treatment studies were of 5- to 8-weeks of age and were bred at the Australian BioResources under research protocols approved by the Garvan Institute of Medical Research Animal Ethics Committee (21/07, 24/05).

## DNA and RNA sequencing
DNA and RNA were extracted from fresh frozen tumor tissues of the patient with RMS and TKCC-IORMS-01 PDCL using the AllPrep Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantification of DNA and RNA was carried out using Qubit v2.0 HS assays (Invitrogen). Evaluation of DNA integrity was performed using the CytoSNP-850K array (Illumina). Sample ploidy and purity were assessed using the “allele-specific copy number analysis of tumors” (ASCAT; RRID: SCR_014695) to determine the presence of sufficient tumor cellularity. RNA quality was assessed using the bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). RNA was prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo-Zero kit.
Both DNA sequencing and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) were conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (NovaSeq Xp kit) at the Australian Genome Research Facility using 150-bp paired-end reads on the S4 flow cell. Tumoral DNA was processed with a sequencing depth of 60×, whereas nontumoral DNA was sequenced at 30×.

## Whole-genome sequencing analysis
Sequencing reads from fresh frozen IORMS tumor tissue of patients, healthy oral mucosa, and TKCC-IORMS-01 PDCL were aligned to the reference human genome (GRCh38, index v0.6.0) using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (v0.7.17; RRID: SCR_010910; ref. ) in pair mode. SNP and insertion/deletion variants were identified by combining Strelka2 (v2.9.7; RRID: SCR_005109; ref. ) and Mutect2 (via GATK v4.1.3; RRID: SCR_001876; ref. ) workflows in default configuration for somatic variants. Reliable calls were further annotated using the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (v97), the NCBI database of genetic variation (Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database, v138; ref. ) and ClinVar ( ) databases. For structural variants, chromosomal rearrangements were identified using DELLY (v0.8.7; RRID: SCR_004603; ref. ), whereas copy-number (CN) variations were estimated using the purity ploidy estimator PURPLE (v2.54; RRID: SCR_022999), as described by the developer at Hartwig Medical Foundation. SNPs, insertion/deletion variants, and structural variants were visualized using Circos (v0.69-9; RRID: SCR_011798; ref. ). Mutational signature analysis was performed using SigProfilerExtractor (v1.1.23; RRID: SCR_023122; ref. ), and Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer v2 as reference database ( ). Activities of CN signatures were evaluated under the method developed by Macintyre and colleagues (via CINSignatureQuantification v1.2; refs. and ).

## RNA-seq analysis
Whole transcriptomes of patients with RMS from a previous study were reanalyzed along with the transcriptomic landscape of the IORMS-01 tumor sample. STAR (v2.7.10a; RRID: SCR_004463; ref. ) was used to align the reads to the human reference genome in pair-end mode. Subsequently, paired read counts were calculated using featureCounts (v2.06; RRID: SCR_012919; ref. ) based on the refFlat annotation database. Downstream analysis, including principal component analysis and differential expression, was conducted by using DESeq2 (v1.42; RRID: SCR_015687; ref. ) in which the genes that were differentially expressed (FC ≥ |3|; P adj < 0.01) were further analyzed using clusterProfiler (v4.8.3; RRID: SCR_016884; ref. ) for the gene set enrichment analysis. TIMER2.0 (RRID: SCR_018737; ref. ) was used to determine percentages of immune cells in patient tumor sample IORMS-01. EPIC was used as the immune deconvolution method ( ).

## 
In vitro drug sensitivity and synergy studies
For measurement of drug sensitivity, cells were seeded into wells of a 96-well plate 24 hours prior to the addition of drugs (detailed in Supplementary Table S1). Cell viability was assessed 72 hours after drugging using alamarBlue (Ex 530–560 nm, Em 590 nm; Life Technologies) and a FLUOstar Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech). IC50 values were determined using Prism (v10.1.1, GraphPad). Drug synergy was measured in a similar manner, with seeded cells instead treated with a combination of drugs at a predetermined IC15 concentration.

## Wound healing assay
Cells were seeded in 12-well plates and allowed to reach confluency in 48 hours. Wounds were then generated on the monolayer using a P200 pipette tip, and drugs were added at IC30 concentrations. Images were taken with a Leica DM IL LED Inverted microscope every 24 hours, and the wound area was marked and measured using ImageJ software.

## Western blotting
Cell lysates were prepared for Western blotting by resuspending log-phase cells in lysis buffer (10% glycerol, 0.03% MgCl2, 1.2% HEPES, 1% SAPP, 1% Triton X-100, 0.8% NaCl, 0.4% NaF, 0.04% EGTA, 21 µmol/L MG-132, 1.5 µmol/L aprotinin, 1 µmol/L DTT, 23 µmol/L leupeptin, 1 mmol/L sodium vanadate, and 1 µmol/L PMSF) at 4°C. Protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). About 20 μg of lysate was separated by SDS-PAGE on a 4% to 12% Bis-Tris gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (0.45 µmol/L) membrane (Life Technologies). The membranes were blocked using 5% nonfat milk in TBST (0.87% NaCl, 0.12% Tris, and 0.1% Tween 20) followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C followed by a 2-hour incubation in horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody, which has been specified in Supplementary Table S2. Detection was carried out using the horseradish peroxidase–conjugated enhanced chemiluminescence–based system (PerkinElmer), and relative protein expression was quantified using ImageJ2 software (v2.1.0, NIH).

## Histology, IHC, and immunofluorescence assessments of primary tumor and PDX tissues
Patient and PDX tumor tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, dehydrated using a graded series of ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm and mounted on to Superfrost Plus slides (Menzel-Glaser). For hematoxylin and eosin staining, slides were stained on the Autostainer XL (Leica Biosystems) and coverslipped using a coverslipper (Leica Biosystems). Slides were scanned using a NanoZoomer slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics). For IHC analyses, sections were deparaffinized, and antigen retrieval was achieved by incubating sections in antigen retrieval solution (pH 6 or 9, Dako Corporation) for 30 minutes at 93°C. Staining was performed on either the BOND RX or Microsystems Bond-III system (Leica Biosystems) using antibodies specified in Supplementary Table S3, using published protocols ( , – ). The Envision+ Detection System–mouse/rabbit (Dako Corporation) was used to visualize primary antibodies. Hematoxylin counterstaining was performed on the Autostainer XL. Slides were then coverslipped and scanned and the analysis of staining coverage conducted using QuPath software ( ).
For immunofluorescence staining, sections were deparaffinized and antigen retrieval was achieved by incubating sections in antigen retrieval solution (pH 6 or 9, Dako Corporation) for 30 minutes at 93°C. Slides were then blocked using 3% BSA (Merck) in PBST (0.3% Triton X-100/PBS with 0.04% glycine) and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C. Conjugated secondary antibodies were then added for 1 hour at room temperature followed by 1 µg/mL 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution for 15 minutes. Slides were then coverslipped using a 10% Mowiol solution (Merck) and scanned on a SLIDEVIEW VS200 (Olympus).
For Picrosirius red staining, sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated in graded ethanol washes, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Sections were then stained using 0.02% phosphomolybdic acid and 0.1% Picrosirius red (Polysciences) for the presence of collagen types 1 and 3. Slides were scanned using the Aperio Slide Scanner, and the analysis of Picrosirius red coverage was carried out using ImageJ software. For birefringent signal analysis, polarized light images of Picrosirius red–stained slides were taken using the Olympus U-POT Polarizer in combination with the Olympus U-ANT transmitted light analyzer fitted to the microscope. Quantitative intensity measurements of fibrillar collagen and birefringent signal were performed on polarized light images using ImageJ software. For each polarized light image, hue–saturation–balance thresholding was applied: for red-orange, high birefringent fibers (1 ≥ H ≤ 27 | 0 ≥ S ≤ 255 | 7 ≥ B ≤ 255); for yellow, medium birefringent fibers (28 ≥ H ≤ 54 | 0 ≥ S ≤ 255 | 7 ≥ B ≤ 255); and for green, low birefringent fibers (1 ≥ H ≤ 100 | 0 ≥ S ≤ 255 | 7 ≥ B ≤ 255). The relative area [% total fibers (1 ≥ H ≤ 100 | 0 ≥ S ≤ 255 | 7 ≥ B ≤ 255)] was then calculated. Picrosirius red coverage and birefringence were analyzed using ImageJ software.

## FISH
Next, 4-μm patient tumor sections were deparaffinized and treated using a neutral buffered solution (SPoT-Light Tissue Pretreatment solution) for heat-induced epitope retrieval pretreatment. Proteolytic digestion of the sections was performed using protease 1. Probe application and denaturing of both the target chromosome and gene probe for 5 minutes at 95°C was completed and followed with overnight probe hybridization at 37°C. Dehydration and counterstaining using DAPI (SlowFade Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI, Invitrogen) were carried out. Enumeration of the interphase signals in 100 tumor cell nuclei was performed using an epifluorescence microscope (Zeiss).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (v10.1.1, GraphPad). Comparison of Western blot quantification and IHC staining was performed using the ANOVA and Student t test, wherein appropriate. Survival analyses were performed using the log-rank test. Significance levels are indicated by using asterisks wherein *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; and ****, P < 0.0001. Data are reported as mean and error bars showing SE unless stated otherwise.

## Results

## Clinical course and model generation
A 28-year-old male presented with a submucosal swelling in the maxillary gingiva with an associated loose left lateral incisor. Previous medical history included intraabdominal non–Hodgkin lymphoma treated with bowel resection and adjuvant chemotherapy when 7 years old (without radiotherapy), achieving complete remission. CT confirmed a lytic lesion (35 × 30 × 10 mm) involving the left maxillary alveolar bone ( ).
 The patient underwent left maxillectomy and elective neck dissection ( and ). Patient treatment journey ( ) involved adjuvant radiotherapy of primary tumor (60 Gy in 30 fractions over 6 weeks) and concurrent chemotherapy (vincristine/actinomycin/cyclophosphamide). Seven months after treatment, extensive local recurrence was detected in the soft tissue adjacent to the site of resection. Palliative treatment with radiotherapy (35 Gy in 5 fractions over 11 days) decreased tumor volume, as observed using MRI. Chemotherapy (cisplatin/doxorubicin) was initiated but rapidly ceased because of toxicity, primarily intractable nausea, and vomiting. Subsequent 3-month follow-up revealed progressive disease with a new lesion detected in the mandible. Based on low-level ALK positivity of the primary tumor sample and the lack of specific additional treatment options, the patient received palliative ALK inhibitor crizotinib ( ) therapy but progressed rapidly and died 3 months later.
Fresh tumor tissue, harvested from the surgical resection specimen was cut into small pieces and directly implanted into immunocompromised NSG mice to establish the first PDX of IORMS ( ). In parallel, fresh tumor was enzymatically dissociated, cell sorted, and cultured to generate a primary, early passage IORMS PDCL (TKCC-IORMS-01; ). Cultured cells exhibited a mixed spindle and epithelioid morphology, characteristic of RMS ( ), with a doubling time of about 54 hours (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Fibroblasts were removed by serial, differential trypsinization and cell sorting. Histopathology of the primary tumor revealed a high-grade spindle cell neoplasm with strong staining of malignant cells for desmin and MyoD1 and weak positivity for ALK (<5%; ). FISH analysis using break-apart probes identified 20.5% of tumor nuclei with FUS rearrangement ( ). These key histologic and molecular features were observed in the matched PDX model ( and ). The rate of establishment of the PDX improved from P0 to P3 and remained stable after passage P3 (Supplementary Fig. S1B), with subcutaneous injection of the early-passage PDCL yielding robust tumors (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Both the PDX- and PDCL-derived tumors displayed the mixed spindle and epithelioid histology of the primary tumor (Supplementary Fig. S1C and S1D).

## The immunosuppressive nature of the IORMS microenvironment
The prevalence of specific tumor immune infiltrate in various cancers is strongly associated with patient prognosis and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors ( ). To characterize the immune microenvironment of human IORMS, we performed IHC analysis of CD8, FoxP3, and F4/80 as markers of CD8-positive (CD8+) cytotoxic T cells, regulatory T cells (Treg), and tumor-associated macrophages, respectively, on the excised primary tumor specimen ( ). With an increasing wealth of data available on the correlation of immune cell infiltration and immunotherapy response in selected solid cancers, such as non–small cell lung cancer ( ) and triple-negative breast cancer ( ), we first examined our results in the context of findings from these cancer types. Quantified immune infiltration comparison against a well-annotated non–small cell lung cancer cohort revealed elevated intratumoral levels of CD8+ T cells and reduced ratio of FoxP3+ Treg:CD8+ T cells, which have been associated with improved immunotherapy response. The quantified FoxP3+ Treg:CD8+ T-cell ratio in the IORMS tumor closely aligned with the relative levels previously reported for nonresponders to anti–PD-1 therapy ( ). We also observed low levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) in the IORMS biospecimen ( ), performed by two pathologists as published ( ), when compared with a cohort of triple-negative breast cancer, wherein the average TIL levels were 9% in nonresponders and 39% in responders to anti–PD-1 therapy ( , ). Due to the differences in the analytic methods used, direct comparison with published sarcoma datasets ( – ) proved more difficult. Nevertheless, the application of the TIMER2.0 ( ) algorithm and the EPIC immune deconvolution tool ( ) to the IORMS mRNA data predicted relatively low levels of TIL infiltration in the IORMS biospecimen ( ) in comparison with the study by Goff and colleagues ( ), which included a broad range of soft-tissue sarcomas. In agreement, the analysis predicted comparable levels of CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Supplementary Fig. S1E) when assessed in the context of diverse sarcomas from The Cancer Genome Atlas cohort ( ). Collectively, our data suggest low levels of immunostimulatory TILs and a potentially immunotherapy-resistant environment of IORMS with TFCP2 rearrangement.

## Genomic analyses identify novel molecular targets in IORMS
Next, we used WGS analyses of the coding regions in the patient tumor and matched PDCL (TKCC-IORMS-01) DNA to map the molecular mechanisms driving IORMS biology and treatment response. We confirmed the presence of FUS–TFCP2 gene fusion in both samples ( ), consisting of the first 254 aa of FUS and 460 aa of the TFCP2 protein. This corresponded to the first six and last 14 exons of the fused proteins, respectively. The fusion consists of the transcriptional activation and RGG1 domain of FUS with all functional domains of TFCP2 (Supplementary Fig. S2A). In line with previous reports ( ), we observed a deletion on chromosome 9 encompassing the tumor suppressors MTAP and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A; ).
 Of further note, the deletion of CDKN2 and IFN gene cluster were identified in both IORMS-01 and PDCL in a contiguous region extending over 2.6 Mb in chromosome 9p21.3 ( ; Supplementary Table S4). This deletion cluster has been reported to promote disrupted tumoral type-I IFN signaling and increased immune evasion, metastasis, and immunotherapy resistance in a mouse model of pancreatic cancer ( ). Comparison of the genomic landscapes of bulk tissue from the patient tumor and matched, tumor-enriched PDCL revealed high concordance in terms of overall gene alterations (overlap coefficient = 0.91; ; Supplementary Tables S4 and S5), including CN variation and additional point mutations identified in the PDCL ( ), suggesting that the TKCC-IORMS-01 model recapitulates the human IORMS subtype and presents a useful tool for addressing key preclinical and clinical research questions. Comparison of the total gene modifications detected herein with existing RMS WGS datasets (n = 44; ref. ) revealed a shared set of only 27 altered genes, highlighting the unique nature of TFCP2 fusion–defined RMS ( ; Supplementary Table S6), and the already established high genomic heterogeneity observed in broad RMS settings ( ). Application of reference pan-cancer mutational ( ) and CN ( ) signatures to our data revealed the presence of broad cancer-associated and aging-related mutational and CN signatures (SBS1 and 5; ; CN1; Supplementary Fig. S2B and S2C) and a high APOBEC activity signature (SBS13; ) associated with poor cancer prognosis ( ).
A comparison of the mutational landscape of the patient with IORMS and PDCL specimens in this study against known actionable cancer targets (TSO500 and MSK-IMPACT clinical gene panels; ref. ) highlighted 10 to 15 shared altered genes ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2D; Supplementary Table S7) with established roles as oncogenic drivers (CRKL, GATA4, RIT1, and ZBTB7A) or tumor suppressors [neurofibromin 1 (NF1), PTEN, CDKN2A/2B, and MTAP]. In addition to the potentially druggable MTAP loss, several identified gene alterations converged on another potential therapeutic target, the PI3K/mTOR pathway. Activation of the CT10 regulator of kinase-like (CRKL) gene coupled with the loss of negative regulator PTEN or the loss of NF1 in the IORMS case have been shown to activate oncogenic PI3K/mTOR signaling in cancer cells ( , ). RAS “orphan” member RIT1 and MAPK1 both showed gene amplification and are potent activators of the RAS/MAPK cascade, which exhibits extensive cross-talk with the PI3K/mTOR axis ( ).
Integration of generated CN and mutational data with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes confirmed dysregulation of the PI3K/mTOR signaling in IORMS ( ), associated with measurable AKT and p-AKT (Ser473) proteins in both patient and PDX biospecimens ( ). Our analysis also illustrated interconnected immune signaling gene networks triggered by CN loss alterations and mutations within regions encoding type-I interferon response genes (IFNA1-2, IFNA4-8, IFNA10, IFNA13, IFNA16-17, IFNB1, and IFNE), together with immunophenotyping assessments supporting an immunosuppressive IORMS tumor microenvironment and general mechanisms that promote tumor evasion ( ).

## Transcriptomic landscape of IORMS
Using bulk-tissue RNA-seq, we next sought to understand the transcriptional mechanisms that underpin the ecosystem of IORMS. Due to the rarity of these cases, we compared the IORMS-01 transcriptome with a publicly available small round cell sarcoma dataset (Supplementary Table S8; ref. ). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis of the most variably expressed genes identified two major cluster groups ( ), with our FUS–TFCP2–defined IORMS aligning more closely with three previously characterized FET–TFCP2 fusion–positive RMS cases ( ). Principal component analysis further highlighted this distinct biology, with significant heterogeneity present within the individual TFCP2 fusion–positive RMS subtype ( ), with most differentially expressed genes aligning to biological processes such as keratinization (KRT19, KRT34, KRT17, KRT9, KRT80, and multiple keratin-associated genes; Supplementary Fig. S2E; Supplementary Tables S9 and S10) and neuronal and muscle function (genes involved in ion channel function including CACNA1A, GRIN1, KCNQ2, and GABRB3; Supplementary Fig. S2E; Supplementary Tables S9 and S10). Similar processes were detected by Schöpf and colleagues ( ) when identifying differentially expressed genes between TFCP2-rearranged RMS and other RMS cases. In comparison with other TFCP2-rearranged RMS cases, our patient sample exhibited upregulation of several pro-invasive genes and lower expression of tumor suppressors including genes associated with the PI3K/AKT pathway ( ). In contrast to the previously reported TFCP2-rearranged cases in which ALK overexpression was a key molecular tumor feature (n = 30/39; refs, , , , ), we observed low ALK mRNA expression in the FUS–TFCP2 IORMS ( ), aligned with low ALK protein levels ( ). Altogether, these observations highlight the heterogeneity of IORMS and define a new ALK-low subtype of TFCP2-rearranged aggressive RMS.

## 
In vitro functional assessment of molecular targets for IORMS therapy
To systematically assess potentially druggable targets in IORMS, we used two-dimensional (2D) 72-hour proliferation assays in the newly established TKCC-IORMS-01 model to evaluate the in vitro efficacy of a panel of contemporary chemotherapies and targeted agents with diverse mechanisms of action (n = 22 drugs; ).
 To determine the relative in vitro drug susceptibility, calculated IC50 values were compared with existing drug sensitivity data from the “Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer” (Wellcome Trust) database ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B; ref. ). Interestingly, the TKCC-IORMS-01 PDCL exhibited broad resistance to most chemotherapies, including DNA damage–inducing agents (DNA alkylators, antimetabolites, and topoisomerase inhibitors). These findings are not surprising, given the absence of signatures of DNA damage response deficiency in the IORMS genome. The PDCL further displayed in vitro resistance to Ras/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling and to JAK1/2 and cell-cycle inhibitors (antimicrotubule agent docetaxel ( ) and CDK4/6 inhibitor; ), with vinorelbine ( ), a vinca alkaloid microtubule inhibitor being an exception [IC50 = 0.005 µmol/L; (lower)]. Associated with the identified PI3K/AKT alterations in the IORMS genome as a new opportunity for molecular subtype–directed treatment selection, we found potent in vitro cellular sensitivity to the dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor dactolisib (IC50 = 0.023 µmol/L; ref. ) and selective mTOR inhibitor (mTORi) vistusertib (IC50 = 0.642 µmol/L; and ; ref. ), which may represent new opportunities for molecular subtype–directed treatment. Other potential molecular targets included MTAP loss, confirmed at the protein level in the PDCL model using Western blot analysis ( ). The loss of MTAP has been previously shown to suppress the activity of PRMT5, rendering cells more susceptible to further PRMT5 depletion. However, in this setting, MTAP loss did not lead to a measurable response to LLY-283 ( ), a small-molecule inhibitor of PRMT5 (IC50 > 10 µmol/L; ; ref. ). Treatment with the ALK/ROS1/cMET inhibitor crizotinib elicited a significant response in vitro (IC50 = 1 µmol/L; and ). Western blotting after crizotinib treatment showed decreased p-cMET levels in the IORMS-01 PDCL ( and ), although conversely, ALK phosphorylation remained unchanged ( and ), suggesting alternative mechanisms may interfere with the observed growth inhibitory effects, in line with published evidence ( ). Finally, although the TKCC-IORMS-01 PDCL was highly sensitive to dasatinib (IC50 = 0.02 µmol/L; ref. ), due to its non–selective binding to numerous kinases (BCR–ABL, SRC family, c-KIT, EPHA2, and PDGFRβ), its application as part of tailored combination regimens may be limited, although assessment of more selective SRC inhibitors may provide an interesting avenue. Taken together, these data suggest that in IORMS PI3K/mTOR dysregulation may support sensitivity to PI3K/mTOR inhibition.

## Drug combination screen enables identification of new promising therapeutic combinations for IORMS
To systematically identify active drug combinations, we measured the effects of 210 pairwise drug combinations ( ; Supplementary Table S11) on cell viability in the IORMS culture. We used the same panel of clinically approved chemotherapeutics, targeted agents in clinical development, and selected investigational compounds. To screen efficiently, we applied monotherapies and pairwise combinations in a matrix format, at the predetermined IC15 concentration from single-agent dose–response curves, or at a single 10-µmol/L concentration [in which PDCL was resistant to specific treatments, for instance with erlotinib ( ), methotrexate ( ), pemetrexed ( ), or LLY-283].
Overall, 16.2% of the 210 combination pairs indicated potential synergy in IORMS (Bliss excess score less than −0.1; ; ref. ). Of the 21 drugs assessed in our combination screen, 71% of all combinations contained 1 (52%) or 2 (19%) chemotherapies. Although chemotherapeutic–chemotherapeutic and chemotherapeutic–targeted drug combinations may mechanistically be of interest, given the rapid onset of toxicity observed with the first-line palliative therapy in the patient, particularly with platinum-based treatment, we focused on novel targeted–targeted drug combinations in this study.
Interestingly, despite showing no effect on cell viability as a monotherapy, PRMT5 inhibitor LLY-283 demonstrated pronounced synergy in combination with several agents from distinct drug classes. We observed the strongest synergy when LLY-283 was paired with drugs targeting PI3K/mTOR signaling (dactolisib: −0.26 Bliss excess and vistusertib: −0.37 Bliss excess; ). We also detected other synergistic combinations, including EGFR targeting (erlotinib: −0.16 Bliss excess) and those that have thus far shown limited anticancer activity in clinical trials for other cancer types including dual targeting of the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathway with trametinib ( ) and sorafenib (−0.28 Bliss excess; ; refs. , ). Aligned with the strong in vitro response to PI3K/mTOR inhibitors and their significant synergy with PRMT5 inhibitor LLY-283 in TKCC-IORMS-01, subsequent secondary assessment of the drug combination across expanded concentrations of the two PI3K/mTOR inhibitors confirmed a robust synergistic drug–drug interaction in this IORMS setting ( and ). Moreover, Western blot analysis of the PDCL treated with dactolisib (IC15), LLY-283 (10 µmol/L), or a combination of the two agents confirmed target modulation via a marked reduction of p-p70S6K ( and ) and p-4EBP1 ( and ), and symmetric dimethylarginine expression ( and ), in the monotherapy and combination therapy groups. As the PRMT5/AKT1 axis may promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in certain solid tumors ( ), we assessed for the modulation of key epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition markers. We found a reduction of snail family transcriptional repressor-1 after treatment with dactolisib as a monotherapy and in combination with LLY-283 and the downregulation of twist family bHLH transcription factor-1 after combination therapy ( ). Cells exhibited a decrease in cell migration when treated with dactolisib monotherapy, which was more pronounced in combination with LLY-283 ( ).
 

## PI3K/mTOR inhibitor monotherapy and combination therapy with PRMT5 blockade significantly improve survival of mice engrafted with IORMS tumors
Therapeutic response in the first PDX of IORMS was determined by assessing tumor growth until the predefined ethical endpoint was reached ( ), as previously described ( ). Strikingly, molecular-guided therapy using mTORi, dactolisib significantly delayed tumor growth ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2C) and improved overall survival when compared with vehicle or targeted therapy using ALK/cMET inhibitor crizotinib ( ; P = 0.0027 and P = 0.0014, respectively). Despite demonstrating significant cancer cell sensitization in vitro, IORMS PDX was resistant to crizotinib in vivo ( and ), in line with the observed lack of patient response ( ). These studies underscore the limitations of 2D culture in evaluating therapeutic efficacy, likely due to the established role of the complex tumor microenvironment in treatment resistance.
 As our earlier experiments revealed a significant antiproliferative and antimigratory activity of mTOR, PRMT5 inhibition, and dual targeting in vitro, we sought to assess whether dactolisib and combinations elicited effects on TFCP2-rearranged IORMS in vivo. Treatment with PRMT5 inhibitor, LLY-283, significantly inhibited tumor growth and increased survival in vivo, an effect that was further enhanced when LLY-283 was administered in combination with mTORi, dactolisib ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S4), building on our in vitro findings.
“On-target” in vivo modulation was confirmed in tumors collected at ethical endpoints, detected by decreased p-AKT (Ser473) and symmetric dimethylarginine levels in dactolisib-treated and LLY-283–treated IORMS PDX tumors, respectively ( and ), with further decreases observed in the combination therapy arms. As PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling plays an important role in promoting chemotherapy resistance in tumor cells via cell-cycle alterations and induction of DNA repair ( , ), we next assessed key protumorigenic features in IORMS tumors after drug treatment. Analysis of LLY-283, dactolisib, and LLY-283/dactolisib–treated tumor tissue revealed significant inhibition of proliferation (Ki-67; ), but no significant changes in markers of apoptosis (cleaved caspase 3; Supplementary Fig. S5A), hypoxia (HIF1α; Supplementary Fig. S5B), or DNA damage (p-H2Ax, Supplementary Fig. S5C), likely due to the timing of the collection of the tissue, as the tumors develop resistance to targeted therapies. Of the assessed stromal elements, we observed a significant decrease in intratumoral CD31 staining after treatment with PRMT5i LLY-283 and our PRMT5/mTOR dual-targeting approach ( ). This effect was pronounced at the tumor invasive edge ( ; ref. ), an area in which neovascularization plays an important role in promoting cancer cell intravasation and ultimately cancer spread. IHC analysis revealed no change in α-smooth muscle actin expression (Supplementary Fig. S5D), a prototypical marker of activated cancer-associated fibroblasts, which drive the extensive fibrosis and associated therapeutic resistance in various solid tumor types ( ). In line with this, we observed no alterations in collagen I/III content (Picrosirius red staining) or remodeling (birefringence; Supplementary Fig. S5E–S5G), a major component of the fibrotic extracellular matrix ( , ). Comparative IHC staining revealed reduced CD31 levels as the only significant alteration in the PDX induced by crizotinib treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6A–S6I). Collectively, the significant delay in disease progression observed in IORMS PDX after treatment ( and ) and detailed analysis of molecular markers of response further support the idea that, in addition to its cytostatic activity, dual pharmacologic inhibition of PI3K/mTOR and PRMT5 networks may reduce tumor angiogenesis, presenting a novel and promising tailored treatment approach for TFCP2 fusion–defined IORMS.

## Discussion
Rare cancers, constituting nearly 25% of all cancer cases ( ), remain understudied, with few translational or clinical studies aimed at enhancing diagnosis and therapy ( ). Relevant preclinical models that enable valuable understanding of the pathobiology of rare cancers are unfortunately lacking. In this study, we developed and functionally assessed the first matched in vivo and in vitro patient-derived models of a rare IORMS subtype with TFCP2 rearrangement. Comprehensive analyses of patient cancer and matched PDCL genomes revealed two potentially promising and as yet unexplored molecular targets in RMS, namely, activated PI3K/mTOR signaling and MTAP loss. Drug screens using clinically relevant agents revealed potent efficacy for single-agent mTOR inhibition therapy and combinations with PRMT5 inhibition, coupled with significant improvement in long-term survival in vivo using this combination strategy.
Our findings have important therapeutic implications in clinical oncology. First, response of rare TFCP2 fusion–defined IORMS to PI3K/mTOR and PRMT5 blockade could be potentially predicted by integrative genomic analyses. Notably, several detected genetic alterations (PTEN, NF1, RIT1, and MAPK1) have been previously associated with heightened activity of the PI3K/mTOR pathway ( , ) and increased sensitivity to targeted agents in a range of cancer types ( ). mTORis have shown measurable efficacy for the treatment of soft-tissue and bone sarcomas in phase II (NCT00093080; ref. ) and phase III trials (NCT00538239; ref. ), leading to significant tumor regression in patients with metastatic sarcomas. Guiding this type of targeted therapy using molecular profiling in rare cancers such as TFCP-rearranged RMS may optimize personalized treatments for patients, comparable with targeting EGFR gene mutations in lung cancer with EGFR inhibitors ( ), and most recently KRAS-directed breakthrough therapies for diverse solid malignancies ( , ), thus broadening the precision medicine paradigm for cancer treatment ( ).
Using a multilayered approach of tumor molecular profiling and in vitro and in vivo functional assessments of matched patient-derived models, we developed a novel, rationally designed, and tailored combination therapy approach targeting the PI3K/mTOR and PRMT5 axis. Recent preclinical findings in models of breast cancer highlight the highly interconnected nature of PI3K/mTOR and PRMT5 networks in driving tumor progression ( ), suggesting the potentially broader relevance of our findings beyond RMS and, as we demonstrate, as part of tailored therapy. Importantly, using conventional clinical regimens, this potentially promising combination is unlikely to be administered in current first-line or palliative settings for IORMS with TFCP2 rearrangement, as highlighted by the clinical journey of the presented patient case, who experienced rapid progression on non–biomarker-driven polychemotherapy and targeted therapy. Our immunophenotyping analyses of patient tumor further indicate an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, suggesting limited potential of current immuno-oncology approaches. In the literature, there are limited clinical data on the use of immunotherapy in sarcoma, with some benefits reported in specific subtypes but not in RMS ( , ).
Second, the preclinical proof-of-concept data provided herein support the use and further investigation of PRMT5 inhibitors to treat RMS cancers harboring MTAP deletions. MTAP encodes a key rate-limiting metabolic enzyme that plays a major role in the regulation of the purine/methionine salvage pathway ( ). The loss of MTAP, frequently detected in cancers, including RMS ( , ), leads to elevated intracellular levels of substrate MTA, which competes with S-adenosyl methionine for binding to PRMT5 methyltransferase and confers a selective cellular dependence on PRMT5 ( ). The effects of LLY-283 monotherapy, a first-generation S-adenosyl methionine–competitive PRMT5 inhibitor ( ), were limited to the in vivo MTAP-deficient IORMS setting, leading to a significant survival advantage. The mechanisms that drive the response to PRMT5 inhibition may be nuanced and multifaceted and involve contribution of stromal elements ( ), which are absent in a 2D culture. Assessment of next-generation PRMT5 inhibitors such as MRTX1719, which display more selective antitumor activity in MTAP-deleted cancer models and patients ( , ), represents an attractive and exciting therapeutic avenue for MTAP-deleted RMS as part of combination therapy, given the aggressive nature of these cancers.
Third, we demonstrate that even within rare sarcoma subsets, such as TFCP2 fusion–defined RMS, significant genomic and molecular heterogeneity exists. Recent analysis by Schöpf and colleagues ( ) has revealed frequent genomic alterations in the ALK gene in a series of RMS cases (9/12), with associated ALK overexpression (9/13) and variable clinical response to ALK inhibition (1/3 with radiographic response data; refs. , , , ). In agreement, earlier case studies of FET–TFCP2–rearranged RMS highlighted frequent ALK overexpression by using IHC (30 of 39; refs. , , , ). In comparison, the FUS–TFCP2 IORMS case presented herein harbored no ALK gene alterations and low tumor ALK mRNA and protein levels. When administered as last-line therapy, ALK inhibitor crizotinib did not elicit a clinical response in the patient. In concordance, the matched PDX (TKCC-IORMS-01) displayed no response to crizotinib in vivo, with the PDCL displaying short-term sensitivity in vitro. The mechanisms that drive the response or intrinsic and/or acquired resistance to ALK inhibition in RMS may be dependent on the type of ALK alteration identified ( ) and remain to be systematically investigated. In the context of acquired resistance in ALK-rearranged tumor types, most documented mechanisms for bypassing ALK signaling blockade involve activation of EGFR ( , ) and the RAS/MAPK pathway ( ). The detected amplification of RIT1 and MAPK1 genes, key mediators of RAS/MAPK activation, may potentially contribute to the observed patient and PDX intrinsic resistance to crizotinib, with further research being necessary to explore these dynamic mechanisms.
This study highlights the considerable power of multiomic analyses, combined in vitro and in vivo preclinical tools, and functional validation for the discovery of novel, tailored treatment combinations and companion biomarkers, especially for cancers that demonstrate near-uniform lethality. Collectively, we provide preclinical proof-of-concept for the efficacy of a personalized treatment strategy using combined mTOR and PRMT5 inhibition to treat PI3K-activated, MTAP-deleted IORMS and provide a scientific rationale for the design of future tailored combinations in this aggressive and treatment-refractory cancer. More broadly, through establishment of new disease models, our approach builds research capacity and improves our understanding of the biology that drives progression and treatment resistance in rare, high-mortality cancers.
