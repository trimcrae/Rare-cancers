## Abstract
Metal ions are essential in regulating protein functions through interactions with residues such as cysteine, but comprehensive mapping of metal-specific metalloproteomes in mammals remains limited. Here, we introduce CysMP, a cysteine-centered metalloprotein profiling strategy to profile the metalloproteomes of 11 key metal ions. CysMP identified 8895 metal-binding sites across 4150 proteins, enabling quantitative comparisons between different metals and revealing both their binding promiscuity and preferences. Notably, zinc and copper ions exhibit the broadest protein interaction profiles. CysMP uncovers numerous potential metalloproteins. We demonstrate that copper and zinc bind to and inhibit 5′-methylthioadenosine phosphorylase, resulting in the accumulation of 5′-methylthioadenosine. Furthermore, copper binding suppresses phosphoglycerate kinase 1 activity, leading to a down-regulation of glycolysis. Our work not only establishes a valuable resource for a dual-specific metalloproteome database but also paves the way for understanding the molecular insights of metalloprotein functions.

CysMP enables metal ion–specific, cysteine-centered metalloproteome analysis to uncover metal-protein interactions and regulation.

## INTRODUCTION
Metal ions play critical roles in various biological processes and are essential for the proper functioning of many physiological systems. In human health, metal ions are indispensable. Iron, for example, is a key component of hemoglobin, responsible for oxygen transport in the blood. Iron deficiency can lead to anemia, a condition characterized by fatigue and weakened immune function ( , ). Similarly, zinc and copper are also vital metals, playing various roles in immune response ( – ), DNA synthesis ( ), cell division ( , ), and energy production ( ). Their deficiency or excess can impair many physiological functions ( – ).
In the cellular environment, metal ions can coordinate with amino acid residues, forming metalloproteins, which represent over one third of the entire proteome ( ), to execute their functions. Although metal ion binding may occur with a broader range of amino acids, the binding specificity and affinity are prominent for the amino acid residues cysteine, histidine, aspartate, and glutamate ( , ). These metal ions are involved in catalyzing biochemical reactions, maintaining structural stability, and regulating cellular processes ( ). For instance, zinc ions coordinate with cysteine and histidine residues in zinc finger proteins, stabilizing their structure and allowing them to interact with DNA ( , ).
The characterization of metalloproteins involves various techniques including the use of radioactive metal ions, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), structural elucidation, and fluorescent probes ( , ). For example, the Metal Isotope Native Radioautography in Gel Electrophoresis technology has been used to identify metal-binding proteins in microorganisms such as Pyrococcus furiosus ( ) and Escherichia coli ( ). More recently, the study of the metalloproteome has advanced with the help of chemical and computational proteomics tools, enabling large-scale profiling of certain classes of metalloproteins ( – ). In particular, cysteine-specific chemical probes have been used to capture zinc-binding proteins ( , – ); thermal stability shift analysis has revealed 17 proteins as potential metalloproteins ( ). However, several limitations and challenges remain unresolved. For example, the cysteine labeling methods typically focus on a single metal ion, and metal ion binding that does not affect or only marginally affects thermal stability would not be revealed by stability-based experiments. Moreover, these datasets are usually incapable of accurately identifying and characterizing metal ions and their binding sites due to methodological limitations and the diverse and dynamic nature of the coordination chemistries of the metal-protein interactions.
To address these challenges, in this study, we developed the cysteine-centered metalloproteome profiling, termed CysMP, to specifically profile all cysteine-coordinating metalloproteins using a cysteine-reactive chemical probe. Our CysMP strategy is highlighted by the dual specificity of metal ions and their binding sites. With CysMP, we drafted metalloproteome maps for 11 metal ions, finding many potential metalloproteins. This provides valuable insights into metal ion selective interactions with cysteine residues and their cellular homeostasis. We show that 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA) phosphorylase (MTAP) and phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) can be regulated by zinc or copper ions, leading to inhibitory effects on their enzymatic activities and the related pathways. Together, our CysMP data not only provide deeper characterization of metal ion–specific metalloproteins but also advance our understanding of metal ion function and regulation in biology and human health.

## RESULTS

## Establishing CysMP for metal ion–specific metalloproteome analyses
To analyze cysteine-centered metalloproteomes, we established the CysMP strategy, which uses a cysteine-reactive probe, EZ-Link iodoacetyl–PEG2 [(polyethylene glycol)2]–biotin (iodo-APB), with potent and nonselective reactivity toward all free cysteine residues. In the standard CysMP process, cell lysates are first treated with the chelating agent EDTA to remove all metal ions from the proteins. After removing EDTA using a membrane filter, different types of metal ions are then added back. Cysteines with the potential to bind metal ions become coordinated with these metal ions and are therefore not alkylated by iodoacetamide (IAA). Next, in the presence of EDTA and the addition of the iodo-APB probe, cysteine sites bound to metal ions are labeled by the probe. The labeled proteins are subsequently digested with trypsin, and the probe-modified peptides are enriched using the avidin beads and analyzed by MS ( ).
 To achieve greater analysis depth and reproducibility with fewer missing values, we used both DDA (data-dependent acquisition) and DIA (data-independent acquisition) MS data collection modes. The DDA files were used to construct a spectral library, which was then used for library-based DIA analysis. In summary, using the established CysMP strategy, we can obtain metal ion–specific metalloproteome data and identify potential metal-binding cysteine sites.

## Optimizing the experimental conditions for CysMP
To determine the optimal concentrations and reaction times of the reagents used in the CysMP strategy, we applied a slightly modified protocol to human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cell lysates without stripping endogenous metal ions, allowing free cysteine and metal ion–bound cysteine to be labeled with IAA and iodo-APB, respectively (fig. S1A). The labeled proteins were visualized using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–streptavidin. The data showed that 1 mM EDTA was sufficient to remove endogenous metal ions (fig. S1B) and 10 mM IAA was adequate to alkylate free cysteine (fig. S1C). The optimal concentration of iodo-APB was 50 μM, with a reaction time of 60 min (fig. S1, D and E). These conditions were further confirmed to be suitable using the recombinant human copper-binding antioxidant protein 1 (ATOX1) and the zinc-binding protein alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) (fig. S1, F to U).

## Validation of CysMP strategy using recombinant proteins
We next tested the CysMP protocol on several selected proteins. To determine whether the “add-back” metal ions can rebind to metalloproteins at their correct conformational positions after EDTA chelation, we designed a reversed CysMP protocol for five proteins: commercially available ADH, human recombinant E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RING-box protein 1 (RBX1), human recombinant copper-binding protein ATOX1, human recombinant zinc- and cobalt-binding protein glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1 (GLI1), and human recombinant manganese-binding protein isopentenyl-diphosphate delta isomerase 1 (IDI1) (fig. S2A). In this reversed approach, the iodo-APB probe reacts with free cysteine, resulting in a reduced probe signal for metalloproteins. The results showed that the readded metal ions could rebind to the metalloproteins with a metal ion selectivity, regardless of whether the interaction was strong—such as between cysteine and copper or zinc—or relatively weak, as observed with cobalt or manganese-cysteine binding (fig. S2, B to K).
Moreover, we performed regular CysMP analysis on six purified proteins (fig. S3A). Among these proteins, ADH (zinc), RBX1 (zinc), ATOX1 (copper), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (zinc) are metalloproteins. GAPDH is a metalloprotein in which the zinc metal ion is coordinated by Glu ( ), while the other three proteins coordinate metal ions with Cys. Albumin and α-casein were used as negative controls since they are not metalloproteins.
Our data show that the CysMP strategy specifically labels physiological cysteine-coordinated metalloproteins ( ), while noncysteine metal ion–binding residues or nonmetalloproteins could not be labeled by the probe iodo-APB ( ). Furthermore, MS analysis revealed notable labeling of cysteine residues in ADH at positions Cys44, Cys98, Cys101, Cys104, and Cys112 ( and fig. S3B), consistent with previously reported cysteine residues in ADH involved in zinc ion coordination ( ). As shown by the example MS2 for Cys44, the identification confidence for iodo-APB–modified peptides is high ( ). In addition, MS analysis also identified two cysteine residues in ATOX1 involved in copper ion binding (fig. S3, C to E) and six cysteine residues in RBX1 involved in zinc ion binding (fig. S3, F to H), consistent with its known metal ion–binding sites according to the protein structure. Together, these findings further support the idea that CysMP is capable of labeling and analyzing cysteine-centered metalloproteins and providing detailed, site-specific reactivity information in their physiological states.

## Eleven metal ion–specific metalloproteomes
To obtain comprehensive cysteine-specific metalloproteomes, we applied the CysMP strategy to 11 different metal ions, each at a concentration of 20 μM, in HEK293T cell lysates. The metal ions included zinc (Zn2+), copper (Cu2+), calcium (Ca2+), cobalt (Co2+), nickel (Ni2+), manganese (Mn2+), iron (Fe3+ and Fe2+), antimony (Sb3+), chromium (Cr3+), and tin (Sn2+), which are relatively abundant in human blood, urine, or other biological samples ( – ). The MS data were acquired using both DDA and DIA modes. Metal ion–free water served as the control for all metal ions. A DDA-based spectral library was constructed with Spectronaut (version 15.5), containing 20,427 iodo-APB–modified peptides corresponding to 5740 proteins ( ). In the library-based DIA analysis, we identified 17,783 cysteine-containing peptides modified with iodo-APB, corresponding to 4976 proteins. Of all metal ions, zinc, copper, antimony, and chromium had the highest numbers of protein groups and peptides ( and data S1). Comparing DDA and DIA results, the DIA identified more protein groups, modified sequences, peptides, and precursors (fig. S4, A to D). The reproducibility of DIA quantification, assessed by the coefficient of variation, was well controlled across all datasets (fig. S4E). The mean values of the quantitative fold change for all metal ions were calculated (fig. S4F).
 Next, we defined potential metal ion–coordinated cysteine sites as those with a fold change of ≥2 and P < 0.05, resulting in 8895 cysteine sites corresponding to 4150 protein groups. Overall, different metal ions exhibited distinct cysteine reactivity maps ( ). Some sites were selectively enriched for only one metal ion, while others could bind multiple metal ions. Volcano plots for each metal ion demonstrated substantially elevated labeling with the addition of metal ions, particularly for zinc and copper ions ( , and fig. S5, A to I). The number of metal ion–coordinated cysteine sites for each metal ion varied ( ). This distribution is consistent with the metalloprotein distribution in MetalPDB (https://metalpdb.cerm.unifi.it/), where zinc-binding and copper-binding proteins represent the largest populations. Pearson correlation analysis of notably labeled proteins revealed varied similarities among different metal ions ( ).

## Functional analyses of metalloproteomes
Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of these 4150 potential metalloproteins indicated substantial enrichment in pathways such as metal ion binding, zinc ion binding, protein binding, and metabolic pathways (fig. S6, A to C). Protein domain analysis showed remarkable enrichment in various types of zinc fingers (fig. S6B). Notably, the significance of the same pathway varied for different metal ions ( and fig. S6D), such as zinc showing higher enrichment in the metal ion–binding pathway compared to others.
Next, we generated HEK293T cell–specific metalloprotein database by comparing proteins annotated as metal proteins in UniProt with the HEK293T proteome (fig. S7A). Our CysMP strategy could identify 60% of all metalloproteins and 59% of all zinc-binding proteins annotated in this HEK293T metalloprotein database ( and fig. S7B). Furthermore, CysMP improved a lot in zinc-binding cysteine sites identification, compared with recent published work using cysteine-reactive probe labeling and supplementation of zinc ( ) (fig. S7C).
 

## Metal promiscuity and selectivity
Metal promiscuity, an inherent property of many metalloproteins, refers to their ability to bind and use multiple metals ( ). Metal substitution frequently occurs in response to environmental changes ( ). To leverage this feature, we investigated whether a given cysteine site is preferentially coordinated by a single type of metal ion or by multiple types of metal ions, which can serve as an indicator of metal promiscuity. Our data showed that approximately one-fourth of all metal-binding sites are specifically enriched for only one metal ion, and the number of specific binding sites for different metal ion varied, with the largest numbers for zinc (Zn2+)–, copper (Cu2+)–, antimony (Sb3+)–, and chromium (Cr3+)–binding sites ( ). Functional analysis of all specific sites showed substantial enrichment in various pathways and domains with highlights in zinc finger and zinc-binding protein–related pathways (fig. S8, A to C). A total of 1392 cysteine sites, corresponding to 1032 proteins, were identified as metal ion–binding sites for more than five types of metals, suggesting that these sites exhibit high metal promiscuity and are particularly susceptible to metal substitution. Molecular functional analysis of these 1032 proteins reveals enrichment in multiple binding pathways, with a notable emphasis on the metal ion–binding pathway ( ). Among these proteins, 493 have been annotated as metal-binding proteins in UniProt, with 173 identified as zinc-binding proteins and 161 as magnesium-binding proteins ( ). This observation suggests that proteins binding to zinc and magnesium may be more susceptible to metal substitution.
The further analysis at individual protein level revealed that although most proteins had only one identified metal ion–binding site, nearly 50% of zinc and copper proteins had at least two binding sites identified ( ). Quantitative fold changes increased slightly with the number of identified sites, suggesting stronger coordination bonds when multiple binding cysteine sites present ( , and fig. S8, D to L). Moreover, the quantitative fold changes of different types of zinc finger structures vary, and proteins belonging to different functional categories also showed quantitative differences ( ). Moreover, the fold change values of zinc finger CCCH domain–containing protein 7A quantitatively correlate well with its binding affinities and dissociation constants ( ), exhibiting the strongest binding to copper ions, followed by zinc, cobalt, and the weakest binding to iron ions (fig. S8M). In addition, we show that zinc finger protein 664 (ZNF664), cysteine- and histidine-rich domain–containing protein 1 (CHORDC1), and CCHC-type zinc finger nucleic acid–binding protein (CNBP) were identified with multiple cysteine sites binding to zinc ions ( ). CNBP, with multiple CCHC-type zinc fingers, was identified with five zinc-binding cysteine sites ( ).

## Validation of potential metalloproteins using iodo-APB probe
To verify the potential metalloproteins, we used the reversed CysMP labeling strategy for several selected proteins with multiple metal ion–binding sites (fig. S9, A to F). In particular, in line with the CysMP results, the cysteine reactivity of MTAP was affected by Zn and Cu ( ), and PGK1 was affected by Cu ( ). In addition, general transcription factor 3C polypeptide 4 (GTF3C4) ( ), tubulin-tyrosine ligase-like protein 12 (TTLL12) ( ), glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1-like protein (GPD1L) ( ), and rhotekin (RTKN) ( ) were all affected notably by copper, while Sn, Zn, and Sb ions could mildly affect their cysteine reactivity. These results suggest that these proteins are putative metal-binding proteins, which could be identified by our CysMP strategy.
 

## Zinc and copper ions bind to MTAP inhibiting its enzymatic activity
Because of the involvement of metal ions in metabolic pathways (fig. S6C), we selected two metabolic enzymes for further functional validation. First, we chose to purify recombinant human MTAP (fig. S10A), given its essential roles in the purine/methionine salvage pathway ( , ). We applied the reversed CysMP labeling on the purified protein, and the results indicated that zinc and copper ions inhibit cysteine’s reactivity, suggesting that these two ions can bind to the MTAP protein ( ). We then dialyzed the purified MTAP after being incubated with copper or zinc ions in a tris-HCl buffer without any other metal ions and measured the protein-binding metal ions using the ICP-MS. The analysis revealed substantially higher amount of zinc and copper in MTAP compared to the control ( ). To verify whether MTAP can selectively bind to zinc and copper, we incubated MTAP with zinc, copper, calcium, and magnesium simultaneously, precipitated the proteins with acetone, and performed ICP-MS. The results showed zinc, copper, and magnesium can selectively bind to MTAP (fig. S10B), consistent with previous findings that describe MTAP as a magnesium-binding protein (Protein Data Bank, 5TC8). In addition, using immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC)–selective affinity gel with different metal ions, we show that IMAC-Zn and IMAC-Cu affinity beads could pull down MTAP, providing further evidence that the zinc and copper ions can bind to MTAP ( ). Collectively, our compelling evidence suggests that zinc and copper ions can bind to MTAP.
 MTAP is the only enzyme in mammalian cells capable of catalyzing the breakdown of MTA into adenine; hence, the inhibition of MTAP would lead to MTA accumulation and inhibit the downstream protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) activity ( ) ( – ). PRMT5 activity can be assessed by Western blot analysis of symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) marks ( ). We established an in vitro MTAP enzymatic assay using recombinant human MTAP and measured the MTA quantity using MS after a 30-min coculture of 2 mM MTA and 0.05 μg of MTAP together. The results showed that zinc and copper could inhibit the MTAP activity, thereby leading to a decreased consumption of MTA ( and fig. S10C).
In HEK293T cells treated with zinc and copper ions, the cellular MTA accumulated notably over time, especially after a 48-hour treatment ( ). Western blot analysis showed that the accumulated MTA was sufficient to inhibit PRMT5 activity ( ). Although zinc and copper ions’ inhibitory effects on MTAP were weaker than MTAP inhibitor (MTAPi), MT-DADMe-ImmA ( ) (fig. S10D), they still followed a dose-dependent trend in living cells (fig. S10, E to J). These findings indicate that zinc and copper inhibit MTAP activity, leading to MTA accumulation and subsequent inhibition of PRMT5 in cells.

## Copper ions bind to PGK1 inhibiting its enzymatic activity and induce cell death
Next, we further investigated the roles of copper ions in regulating PGK1 activity and its implication in the glycolysis. We purified recombinant human PGK1 (fig. S11A) and conducted the same reversed CysMP labeling assay. The results indicated that copper ions inhibit PGK1’s cysteine reactivity with the probe ( ). The ICP-MS analysis of recombinant human PGK1 showed a much higher copper content compared to the control ( ). Incubation of PGK1 with zinc, copper, calcium, and magnesium together, followed by protein precipitation and ICP-MS, revealed that copper ions could selectively bind to PGK1 (fig. S11B). IMAC-selective affinity assays showed that only IMAC-Cu affinity beads could pull down PGK1 from HEK2923T and JHH7 cell lysates ( and fig. S11C). Metal ions were reported to enhance the thermal stability of metalloproteins ( , ). We conducted thermal shift assays with human recombinant PGK1 and cell lysates in the presence of copper ions. The results showed that copper ions increased PGK1 thermal stability ( , and fig. S11, D to G).
 PGK1 is an enzyme in glycolysis that catalyzes adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) production. The glycolytic enzyme activity of PGK1 assay was performed as described previously ( , ). Purified PGK1 activity was measured as the rate of reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) (oxidized form) (NADH) consumption according to the NADH absorbance value at 340 nm (fig. S11H). Copper ions inhibited PGK1 activity in a concentration-dependent manner, with a median inhibitory concentration (IC50) of ~0.59 μM ( , and fig. S11I). To explore how copper ions inhibit PGK1 activity, we added ATP and 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG), the substrate of PGK1, in the enzymatic activity assays. ATP supplementation reversed copper ion inhibition, whereas 3-PG had little effect ( ). This suggests that copper ions inhibit PGK1 activity by interfering with ATP binding. Furthermore, we used ATP beads to pull down PGK1 incubated with different copper ion concentrations. Higher copper ion concentrations resulted in less PGK1 being pulled down by ATP beads ( ), which further supports that copper affects the binding between ATP and PGK1.
To further investigate the binding sites between PGK1 and copper ions, we applied the reversed CysMP workflow to human recombinant PGK1, revealing changes in reactivity at five cysteine sites upon copper ion treatment (fig. S11J). Human recombinant PGK1 variants with single cysteine mutations and a variant with mutations at all five cysteine sites (PGK1-5Cys-mut) were generated (fig. S11K), and their enzymatic activity was assessed. The results showed that mutations at Cys108 and Cys367 notably reduced the inhibitory effects of copper ions. Mutation at all five cysteine sites resulted in the most pronounced attenuation from copper ion inhibition ( ). In addition, PGK1 Cys367 was predicted to bind copper ion by AlphaFold 3, further validating the significance of copper-binding cysteine residues uncovered through the CysMP strategy ( ). These findings together suggest that the cysteine sites identified by CysMP are targeted by copper ions and play critical roles in regulating the enzymatic activity of PGK1.
Previous studies have indicated that copper ions inhibited cell viability ( , ), and PGK1 knockdown also inhibited cell viability ( , ). We hypothesized that the inhibitory effect of copper ions on cells is, at least partially, due to the copper ion–induced PGK1 inhibition in living cells. We first examined PGK1 expression levels in HEK293, JHH7, and HepG2 cell lines (fig. S12A) and found that PGK1 was highly expressed in JHH7 cells. Next, we tested the effect of copper ions on cell viability in JHH7 cells and HEK293T cells using the copper ionophore elesclomol to shuttle copper into the cells ( ), observing substantial inhibitory effects ( and fig. S12B). Consequently, we constructed stable PGK1 knockdown JHH7 cell lines and PGK1 overexpression HEK293T cell lines to investigate copper ions’ impact on cell viability according to the PGK1 expression levels in cells. Transient transfection showed that shPGK1-1 had the best knockdown effect (fig. S12, C and D), and the stable knockdown cell lines were generated on the basis of this short hairpin RNA (shRNA). In JHH7 PGK1 knockdown cell lines, copper ions further reduced cell viability ( ). Overexpression of PGK1 in stable cell lines rescued cell viability in HEK293T cells treated with copper ions ( ). Thus, copper ions inhibit cell viability partly by targeting PGK1.
 We performed experiments to assess cellular metabolite changes in response to copper ion treatment ( ). Analysis of PGK1-related glycolytic metabolites showed an obvious decrease in abundance, such as glucose, glucose-6-phophate, 3-PG, pyruvate, and lactate, supporting the inhibition of PGK1 and glycolysis activity. In addition, MTA accumulated in cells treated with copper ions ( ; fig. S12, E to R; and data S2). This is highly consistent with our findings on copper-inhibited MTAP and literature reports of MTA accumulation and decreased glycolytic metabolites in copper-treated ABC1 cells ( ). Our results indicate that copper ions inhibit PGK1 activity, leading to reduced glycolysis, thereby providing mechanistic insights into copper-induced cell death ( ).

## DISCUSSION
Metalloproteins are a notable class of proteins within the entire proteome and play a wide range of roles in regulating biological processes. As a major residue in coordinating metal ions, the cysteine was used to develop the CysMP method. The labeling of the chemical probe has high reactivity and specificity toward cysteine residues and can be used for visualization or enrichment of metalloproteins. Compared to cysteine-labeling probes that use low-efficiency alkyne-azide click chemistry, our one-step iodo-APB approach offers substantially higher labeling efficiency. Notably, the CysMP is capable of providing information about both metal ion and cysteine sites, bridging the gaps between the two components in metal-residue interactions.
The CysMP used an integrated pipeline, including specific cysteine labeling, various metals, and parallel DDA and DIA proteomic analyses, achieving in-depth coverage compared to previous large-scale characterization attempts ( , , , ). Previous cysteine labeling–based proteomic methods aimed at characterizing metal ion–binding cysteine sites typically focused on single ions, such as zinc ( , , ) or iron ( ). These methods involved culturing cells in either metal ion–deficient or supplemented media, followed by cysteine labeling with IAA probes and subsequent click chemistry reactions. However, these approaches have several limitations that should be considered. For example, supplementing a specific metal ion does not necessarily affect endogenous metal ion–binding cysteine residues. Instead, it primarily influences unoccupied cysteine sites, leading to quantitative changes that may be small and nonphysiological. Moreover, avoiding the impact of metal ion substitution is challenging when a particular ion is depleted, often resulting in a limited number of identified metalloproteins. In contrast, our CysMP strategy reconstructs metalloproteomes by reintroducing specific metal ions, enabling quantitative comparisons between different metals and offering insights into both primary and alternative binding ions, thereby providing clear evidence of the metal promiscuity and substitution tendencies of many metalloproteins. Furthermore, the advanced DIA methods used by CysMP facilitate reproducible, in-depth analysis of metalloproteomes. A recent metal extraction-triggered agitation logged by thermal proteome profiling (METAL-TPP) method, based on detecting thermal stability changes, identified 125 metalloproteins from HeLa cells ( ). The relatively low number of metalloproteins reported could be due to the minimal effect of metal ions on thermal stability or the heat insensitivity nature of some proteins. This is supported by the identified metalloprotein MTAP, whose protein abundance remains stable between 45° and 65°C (fig. S13, A and B). Therefore, MTAP can only be identified by CysMP and further verified by ICP-MS but not by thermal stability shift methods.
As a resource, CysMP offers additional binding site information for metalloprotein studies and serves as a comprehensive dataset to metal-protein interactions that are largely governed by the Irving-Williams series. A recent study using a fluorescence-based probe (Cr3+-NTA-AC, nitrilotriacetic acid–aminocoumarin) identified eight chromium-binding proteins ( ). CysMP further characterized potential cysteine-binding sites in four of these proteins (fig. S13, C to F), providing deeper mechanistic insights into chromium coordination. In biological systems, metal ions at the top of the Irving-Williams series ( ), such as zinc and copper, tend to preferentially bind to metalloproteins, forming more stable complexes and often outcompeting metals with lower binding affinities. As a result, single-metal depletion strategies remain limitation to comprehensively characterize protein interactions involving metals lower in the Irving-Williams series. By introducing different metal ions at equal concentrations, our strategy enables the unbiased profiling of cysteine-associated binding sites, thereby highlighting potential interactions with lower-affinity metal ions that are otherwise masked under physiological conditions. The addition of specific metal ions may not completely restore endogenous conditions, as metal ion substitution occurs frequently. However, CysMP is a quantitative method and can be used to assess the binding affinity and metal promiscuity among different metal ions at the same site. Therefore, our data can also be used to predict metal ion–binding changes caused by variations in the environment, such as alterations in metal ion availability, pH, or the presence of competing ions, in living organisms.
Notably, CysMP reveals many potential metalloproteins. In this study, we functionally validate two of them, MTAP and PGK1, demonstrating the regulation of these proteins’ functions by metal ions. Specifically, we provide CysMP MS evidence showing that PGK1 is a copper-binding protein and further offer multiple biological pieces of evidence indicating that copper ions can bind to and inhibit PGK1. This interaction leads to the down-regulation of related glycolytic pathway metabolites such as 3-PG and ATP. These findings enrich our understanding of the involvement of metal ions in regulating glycolysis and copper-induced cell death. In light of these results, we are confident that many yet-to-be-identified metalloproteins can be mined from our extensive datasets.
The CysMP essentially measures the difference in cysteine reactivity with the probe to capture metal ion–coordinating cysteine sites. However, metal ions may also induce slight protein conformation changes, which can affect reactivities at some distal allosteric cysteine sites. Consequently, we should not exclude the possibility that some cysteine changes captured by CysMP may not directly result from metal ion binding. Furthermore, iron ions show relatively fewer reactive sites in CysMP data. This may be due to the fact that cysteine residues often coordinate iron via iron-sulfur clusters, which 1 mM EDTA incubated for 30 min may not effectively remove (fig. S13, G to J), thereby affecting reactivation with the probe ( , ).
In this study, we focused exclusively on cysteine for several reasons. First, cysteine is a prevalent amino acid that coordinates with metal ions. Second, chemical probes for cysteine reactivity are highly efficient and specific. In light of CysMP, other metal ion–binding residues, such as histidine ( – ), can also be used to develop similar strategies in the future. A comprehensive metalloproteome matrix consisting of different residues and various metal ions will further advance our understanding of the essential roles these small inorganic metal ions play in biology.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Cell culture and lysis
HEK293T and JHH7 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. HEK293T cells and JHH7 cells were lysed in an ice-cold lysis buffer containing 1% NP-40, 50 mM tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and EDTA-free protease inhibitor, followed by sonication for 30 s. The lysate was then centrifuged at 14,000g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay.

## Antibodies
The following antibodies were used: HRP-labeled streptavidin (Beyotime, A0305), monoclonal HRP-labeled anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, A8592), anti–immunoglobulin G (IgG)–LCS (light chain subunit) mouse antibody (Abbkine, A25012), monoclonal anti-MTAP rabbit antibody (ABclonal, A22045), monoclonal anti-PRMT5 rabbit antibody (Abcam, ab109451), monoclonal antisymmetric dimethyl arginine rabbit antibody (Beyotime, AG0408), polyclonal anti-histone H4 rabbit antibody (Abcam, ab10158), monoclonal HRP-conjugated anti–β-tubulin mouse antibody (ABclonal, AC030), polyclonal anti-PGK1 rabbit antibody (ABclonal, A14039), monoclonal anti–cofilin 1 rabbit antibody (Beyotime, AG1593), polyclonal HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG(H+L) antibody (Beyotime, A0208), and polyclonal HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG(H+L) antibody (Beyotime, A0216).

## Labeling of metal ion–coordinating cysteine by iodo-APB
Two hundred microliters of the lysate (1 mg/ml) was transferred into a membrane filter with 3-kDa cutoff (Sigma-Aldrich, UFC5003). EDTA (1 mM) was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Lysis buffer (200 μl) was added and centrifuged six times to remove EDTA and endogenous metal ions. Next, the lysate was incubated with 20 μM various metal ions [ZnCl2, CuSO4, CaCl2, Co(NO3)2, Ni(NO3)2, Mn(NO3)2, FeCl3, FeSO4, SbCl3, CrCl3, and SnCl2 in water] or water as control for 30 min, followed by the addition of 10 mM IAA to react with the free cysteine residues. FeSO4 solution was prepared freshly before use. Lysis buffer (200 μl) were again added six times to remove excess metal ions and IAA. The samples were then treated with 1 mM EDTA and 50 μM iodo-APB at room temperature for 60 min, and the resulting labeled proteins were ready for digestion.

## In-solution digestion and enrichment of probe-labeled peptides
Following chemical labeling, the probe-labeled proteins were precipitated on ice by adding 4 vol of cold acetone. The protein pellets were obtained by centrifugation at 14,000g for 15 min and then redissolved in a buffer containing 8 M urea and 500 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.5). Disulfide bridges were reduced by adding tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine at a final concentration of 5 mM for 20 min. Reduced cysteine residues were then alkylated by adding 10 mM IAA and incubating for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. The urea concentration was reduced to 2 M by adding 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.5) and 1 mM CaCl2. The protein mixture was digested overnight at 37°C with trypsin (Promega, V5111) at an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:100 (w/w).
The resulting probe-labeled peptides were then enriched using streptavidin agarose beads in tips, following a previously described method ( ). After incubating with the beads for 4 hours at 4°C, washing five times with 1% SDS in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer and liquid chromatography (LC)–MS grade water, the labeled peptides were eluted with a solution of 50% acetonitrile (ACN) and 2% formic acid (FA) in water. The eluted peptides were dried by centrifugation under vacuum at 4°C and subsequently resuspended in 0.1% FA for the subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis.

## LC-MS/MS analysis

## 
Data-dependent acquisition

The probe-labeled peptides were analyzed using a nanoElute LC system coupled online to a hybrid trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS) quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF) mass spectrometer (Bruker timsTOF Pro) via a CaptiveSpray nanoelectrospray ion source. Peptides were separated on an in-house packed analytical column (250 mm by 75 μm, 1.5-μm C18 resin; Dr. Maisch GmbH) with a 120-min gradient at a flow rate of 300 nl/min [solvent A (0.1% FA in water); solvent B (0.1% FA in ACN): 5% at 0 min, 29% at 90 min, 45% at 100 min, 80% at 110 min, and 80% at 120 min]. The column oven temperature was maintained at 50°C. The dual TIMS analyzer was operated with a fixed duty cycle and a ramp time of 100 ms. The total cycle time was ~1.17 s. DDA was performed in PASEF (parallel accumulation serial fragmentation) mode, with 10 PASEF scans per topN acquisition cycle across a mass range from 100 to 1700 mass/charge ratio (m/z), encompassing charge states from 0 (unassigned) to 5+. Ion mobility was scanned from 0.75 to 1.40 V·s/cm2. Precursors reaching a target intensity of 10,000 were selected for fragmentation and dynamically excluded for 0.4 min (mass width, 0.015 m/z; 1/K0 width, 0.015 V·s/cm2). The quadrupole isolation width was set to 2 m/z for m/z < 700 and 3 m/z for m/z > 700. Collision energy was linearly interpolated based on 1/K0 values, ranging from 20 eV at 1.6 V·s/cm2 to 59 eV at 1.6 V·s/cm2, with values kept constant above or below this range. The TIMS elution voltage was calibrated linearly to derive reduced ion mobility coefficients (1/K0), using three selected ions from the Agilent ESI-L Tuning Mix (m/z, 622, 922, and 1222).
The MS data for purified ADH, ATOX1, and RBX1 were acquired using an online EASY-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a standard shotgun method.

## 
Data-independent acquisition

The same probe-labeled peptides were separated using identical chromatographic conditions as used in DDA on the timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer but under the diaPASEF (parallel accumulation–serial fragmentation combined with data-independent acquisition) mode. The capillary voltage was set to 1400 V. MS with ion mobility scanned from 0.75 to 1.3 V·s/cm2. Precursors reaching a target intensity of 10,000 were selected for fragmentation and dynamically excluded for 0.5 min. The ramp time was set to 100 ms. Collision energy was ramped linearly as a function of mobility, ranging from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.6 V·s/cm2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.6 V·s/cm2. Isolation windows of 50 m/z width were set to cover the mass range from 165 to 1655 m/z in the diaPASEF method.

## Identification and quantification of probe-labeled cysteine sites
Spectronaut (version 15.5.211111.50606, Biognosys AG, Schlieren, Switzerland) was used for library generation and diaPASEF data analysis. The database search engine Pulsar was used to generate a library-based DIA library from the DDA PASEF data using the SwissProt human protein sequence database containing 20,379 entries. The settings for Pulsar and library generation included trypsin/P as the specific enzyme, with variable modifications of carbamidomethyl on C (Δ mass, 57.02 Da), oxidation on M (Δ mass, 15.99 Da), acetyl at the protein N terminus (Δ mass, 42.01 Da), and iodo-APB on C (Δ mass, 414.19 Da). The false discovery rate was set to 0.01 at the peptide-spectrum match (PSM), peptide, and protein levels, with a minimum fragment relative intensity of 1%, retaining three to six fragments for each precursor and applying a posttranslational modification (PTM) localization filter with a minimum localization threshold of 0.75. The generated library was used to analyze the diaPASEF data by Spectronaut with the default parameters.

## Purification of recombinant human PGK1 and MTAP from E. coli
The full-length cDNA sequence of human PGK1 was obtained from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) reference sequence NM_000291.4. The cDNA of PGK1 was then cloned from the chromosomal DNA of HEK293T cells and subcloned into a pET-28a(+) vector containing a 6×His tag. The resulting plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells (Yeasen, 11804ES80). The transformed cells were cultured in LB medium at 37°C until reaching an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.6. Protein overexpression was induced by adding isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside to a final concentration of 0.5 mM. Following induction, the cultures were maintained at 16°C for 20 hours. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 30 min and then stored at −80°C until further processing.
The full-length cDNA sequence of human MTAP was obtained from NCBI reference sequence NM_001396043.1. The cDNA of MTAP was then cloned from the chromosomal DNA of HEK293T cells and subcloned into a pET-28a(+) vector containing a 6×His tag, following the same protocol as for PGK1.
Frozen bacterial pellets were resuspended and sonicated in buffer consisting of 50 mM tris-HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and EDTA-free protease inhibitor (pH 8.0). The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000g for 30 min and loaded onto a nickel-chelating resin column (His-Trap HP, Cytiva). The target proteins were eluted using an imidazole gradient ranging from 10 to 500 mM. Bovine thrombin (Yeasen, 20402ES03) was added to cleave the 6×His fusion tags, and the cleavage reaction was carried out overnight at 4°C. The recombinant proteins underwent a final purification step by size exclusion chromatography using Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL (Cytiva) in 100 mM tris and 50 mM NaCl (pH 8.0). The purified target protein was then stored at −80°C.

## PGK1 enzyme activity assay
The activity of PGK1 enzyme was assessed using a commercial phosphoglycerate kinase activity assay kit (Abcam, ab252890), following the manufacturer’s instructions. In the first step of this enzymatic assay, PGK converts 3-PG and ATP to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and adenosine 5′-diphosphate (ADP). The nascent intermediate is detected via a series of enzymatic reactions that lead the oxidation of NADH to NAD, which can be easily detected (OD = 340 nm).

## Overexpression of recombinant proteins in mammalian cells
Human recombinant MTAP, PGK1, GTF3C4, TTLL12, GPD1L, and RTKN fused with a Flag-tag were obtained by polymerase chain reaction amplification and subcloned into the pCMV-N-FLAG vector (Beyotime, D2722). For transient transfection, HEK293T cells were transfected with the respective plasmids using PolyJet reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (GenJet Plus DNA In Vivo Transfection Reagent, SignaGen) and incubated for 48 hours.

## Establishing stable cell lines

## 
PGK1 knockdown

For the all-in-one vector used to knock down PGK1 in HEK293T and JHH7 cells, the pLKO.1-puro-shPGK1-1 to shPGK1-4 vector was used as a backbone for inserting the shRNAs targeting PGK1: shPGK1-1, GCCAAGATTGTCAAAGACCTA; shPGK1-2, CCTATGAAGAACAACCAGATA; shPGK1-3, CTGACAAGTTTGATGAGAATG; shPGK1-4, GCAAGGATGTTCTGTTCTTGA.
The targeting vector pLKO.1-puro-shPGK1#1~4, along with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 and envelope plasmid pMD2.G, were cotransfected into HEK293T cells using PolyJet reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The virus-containing media were collected 48 hours later. HEK293T and JHH7 cells were infected with lentivirus for 72 hours, after which cells were pelleted to remove the virus and reseeded into fresh culture medium containing puromycin dihydrochloride (Beyotime, ST551). Control cells were infected with pLKO.1-puro nontargeting shRNA (Sigma-Aldrich, SHC002V). Knockdown efficiency was assessed by Western blot analysis, with shPGK1-1 demonstrating the highest efficiency.

## 
PGK1 overexpression

The PGK1 gene was cloned into the LentiJ plasmid fused with 3×FLAG. Virus production and cell transduction were conducted as described previously, followed by selection with Blasticidin S HCl (Beyotime, ST018).

## Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry
Purified recombinant PGK1 was incubated with Cu2+, followed by dialysis overnight at 4°C in a buffer containing 100 mM tris and 50 mM NaCl (pH 8.0). Purified recombinant PGK1 was incubated with Cu2+, Zn2+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ and then precipitated PGK1 with acetone. PGK1 (100 μg) was analyzed by ICP-MS using an Elan DRC-e spectrometer. Purified recombinant MTAP was incubated with Cu2+ or Zn2+, followed by dialysis overnight at 4°C in a buffer containing 100 mM tris and 50 mM NaCl (pH 8.0). Purified recombinant MTAP was incubated with Cu2+, Zn2+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ and then precipitated PGK1 with acetone. Nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide were added to samples for microwave nitration. Then, samples were diluted and analyzed by iCAP Qnova Series ICP-MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## IMAC-selective affinity gel assay
IMAC-selective affinity gel (Sigma-Aldrich, I1408) was prepared by washing and loading with metals (0.2 M ZnCl2, NiCl2, or CuCl2 or 1 mM EDTA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. HEK293T or JHH7 cells, in which MTAP or PGK1 was overexpressed, were lysed using the lysis buffer and loaded onto the column. After washing the column three times with lysis buffer, proteins were eluted using 500 mM imidazole elution buffer. The resulting proteins were subsequently analyzed by Western blotting.

## Thermal shift assay
Copper ions (20 μM) were added to HEK293T cell lysates (2 mg/ml) and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The samples were then heated to temperatures ranging from 45° to 65°C for 3 min. After heating, the samples were centrifuged at 14,000g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for Western blotting analysis.

## ATP agarose pull-down assay
Purified recombinant PGK1 protein was incubated with the different concentrations of Cu2+ at room temperature for 30 min. ATP agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, A2767) was washed three times with Dulbecco’s PBS (D-PBS). Subsequently, 100 μl of ATP agarose was added to the protein solution and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. The beads were washed three times with D-PBS. PGK1 bound to the ATP agarose was then eluted with elution buffer containing 5 mM ATP. The eluate was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected and analyzed by Western blotting.

## Cell viability assay
JHH7 cells with stably overexpressing PGK1 or with knockdown were plated at 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates. Different concentrations of Cu2+ were added 24 hours after plating, with at least six replicates for each condition. Cell viability was assessed 72 hours (unless otherwise stated) after Cu2+ addition using CellTiter-Glo (Promega), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Metabolite extraction and analysis by LC-MS

## 
MTAP enzyme activity assay sample preparation

MTAP enzyme activity was determined by measuring MTA using LC-MS. Recombinant MTAP (0.5 μg), 20 μM of the indicated metal ions, and varying concentrations of 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA) were added to 50 mM Na2HPO4 (pH 8.0) at room temperature for 30 min, for a total volume of 200 μl. A final concentration of 5% FA was used to stop the reaction. Samples were extracted using a methanol (MeOH):ACN (1:1, v/v) solvent mixture. A volume of 800 μl of cold solvent was added to each sample, vortexed for 30 s, and sonicated 10 min in 4°C water bath, followed by centrifugation at 14,000g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was taken and evaporated to dryness in a vacuum concentrator. The dry extracts were then reconstituted in 100 μl of ACN:H2O (1:1, v/v), sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 14,000g for 15 min at 4°C to remove insoluble debris. We kept the supernatant in −80°C before LC-MS analysis.

## 
HEK293T and JHH7 metabolite extraction

Cell samples were extracted using a solvent mixture of MeOH:ACN:H2O (2:2:1, v/v). A volume of 1 ml of cold solvent was added to each cell pellet, vortexed for 30 s, and incubated in liquid nitrogen for 1 min. The samples were then allowed to thaw at room temperature and sonicated for 10 min. This freeze-thaw cycle was repeated three times in total. To precipitate proteins, samples were incubated for 1 hour at −20°C, followed by centrifugation at 14,000g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was taken and evaporated to dryness in a vacuum concentrator. The dry extracts were then reconstituted in 100 μl of ACN:H2O (1:1, v/v), sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 14,000g for 15 min at 4°C to remove insoluble debris. We kept the supernatant in −80°C before LC-MS analysis.

## 
LC-MS analysis and data processing

The LC-MS analysis protocol followed our previous publication ( ). The LC-MS analyses of MTA samples were conducted using an ultrahigh-performance LC (UHPLC) system (1290 series, Agilent Technologies) coupled to a QTOF mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 6600, SCIEX). A Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH amide column {particle size, 1.7 μm; 2.1 mm [inside diameter (ID)] by 100 mm (length)} was used for the LC separation, and the column temperature was maintained at 25°C. The flow rate was set as 0.5 ml/min, and the sample injection volume was 2 μl. Mobile phase A was water with 25 mM ammonium acetate (NH4OAc) and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH), and mobile phase B is ACN for positive [electrospray ionization (ESI)+] modes. The column was eluted with a linear gradient system: 0 min, 95% B; 0.5 min, 95% B; 7 min, 60% B; 8 min, 40% B; 9 min, 40% B; 9.1 min, 95% B; 12 min, 95% B. The data acquisition was operated using the information-dependent acquisition mode. The source parameters were set as follows: ion source gas 1 (GAS1), 60 psi; ion source gas 2 (GAS2), 60 psi; curtain gas (CUR), 30 psi; temperature (TEM), 600°C; declustering potential (DP), 60 or −60 V in positive or negative modes, respectively; ion spray voltage floating (ISVF), 5500 or −4000 V in positive or negative modes, respectively. The TOF MS scan parameters were set as follows: mass range, 60 to 1200 Da; accumulation time, 200 ms; dynamic background subtract, on. The product ion scan parameters were set as follows: mass range, 25 to 1200 Da; accumulation time, 50 ms; collision energy, 30 or −30 V in positive or negative modes, respectively; collision energy spread, 0; resolution, UNIT; charge state, 1 to 1; intensity, 100 counts per second (cps); exclude isotopes within 4 Da; mass tolerance, 10 parts per million (ppm); maximum number of candidate ions to monitor per cycle, 6; exclude former target ions, for 4 s after two occurrences. Skyline software (version 20.1.0.155) was used for data extraction and peak area integration.
The LC-MS analyses of HEK293T and JHH7 samples were conducted using a Vanquish UHPLC coupled to a Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column [particle size, 2.6 μm; 2.1 mm (ID) by 100 mm (length)] was used for the LC separation, and the column temperature was maintained at 25°C. The flow rate was set as 0.3 ml/min, and the sample injection volume was 2 μl. Mobile phase A was water with 0.01% acetic acid, and mobile phase B was isopropyl alcohol (IPA):ACN (1:1, v/v) for both positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI−) modes. The column was eluted with a linear gradient system: 0 min, 0% B; 1 min, 0% B; 7 min, 60% B; 8 min, 100% B; 9 min, 100% B; 9.1 min, 0% B; 12 min, 0% B. In addition, a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH amide column was used for LC-MS analysis as described above. The MS data acquisition was conducted in full MS scan mode and dd-MS2 scan mode. The source parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, 3500 or −2800 V for positive or negative modes, respectively; aux gas heater temperature, 350°C; sheath gas, 50 arbitrary units (arb); aux gas, 15 arb; capillary temperature, 400°C. The resolution for full MS scan mode was set as 60,000, and AGC target was set as 1 × 106 for both positive and negative modes. Maximum injection time (IT) was set as 100 ms. Mass range was set as 70 to 1200 Da. For the dd-MS2 scan mode, MS resolution was set as 30,000, and AGC target was set at 1 × 105. Maximum IT was set as 60 ms. The topN setting was set as 6. Isolation width was set as 1.0. The collision energy was set as stepped normalized collision energy (SNCE) 20/30/40%. The dynamic exclusion was set as 3.0 s, and isotope exclusion was on.
Raw MS data (.raw) files were transformed to the mzXML format by ProteoWizard (version 3.0.20360). Then, we applied XCMS package (version 3.2; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/xcms.html) to process MS data for peak detection, retention time correction, and peak alignment. Key parameters were set as follows: method, “centWave”; ppm, 10; snthr, 3; peakwidth, c (5,30); minfrac, 0.5. Metabolite annotation was performed using MetDNA (version 1.2.2; http://metdna.zhulab.cn/). The metabolite annotation parameters were set as “HILIC” or “RP” according to LC mode and “30” or “SNCE_20_30_40%” for collision energy.

## Statistical analysis
Quantifications of immunoblots and Coomassie blue staining were performed using ImageJ (version 1.8.0). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5, and data were presented as means ± SD, unless otherwise noted. For comparisons across two normally distributed groups, Student’s t test was performed. The level of significance was determined as P < 0.05. Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were carried out using DAVID package with adjusted P < 0.05 considered significant. For all figures, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.
