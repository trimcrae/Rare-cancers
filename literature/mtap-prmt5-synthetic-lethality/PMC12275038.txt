## Summary
Protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) is a promising cancer target, yet it is unclear which PRMT5 roles underlie this vulnerability. Here, we establish that PRMT5 inhibition induces a special class of unspliced introns, called detained introns (DIs). We used the depletion of CLNS1A, a PRMT5 cofactor that specifically enables Sm protein methylation, to interrogate the impact of DIs. We found that the disruption of Sm protein methylation is sufficient to induce DI upregulation, cell cycle defects, and loss of viability. Finally, we discovered that PRMT5-regulated DIs, and the impacted genes, are highly conserved across human, and also mouse, cell lines but display little interspecies conservation. Despite this, human and mouse DIs have convergent impacts on proliferation by affecting essential components of proliferation-regulating complexes. Together, these data argue that the PRMT5-splicing axis, and particularly appropriate DI splicing, underlie cancer’s vulnerability to PRMT5 inhibitors.

## Introduction
PRMT5 is the major type II arginine methyltransferase and the primary enzyme responsible for catalyzing the symmetric dimethyl arginine mark (SDMA). PRMT5 is highly expressed in stem cells, and its expression decreases as cells enter post-mitotic, terminally differentiated fates. Importantly, PRMT5 is re-expressed in nearly all cancers, and chemical or genetic inhibition shows drastic anti-proliferative effects. , Many PRMT5 inhibitors (PRMT5i) show promising preclinical results in a wide variety of tumor types, , , , and several PRMT5i are in, or moving toward, clinical trials. 
PRMT5 writes the SDMA mark on proteins involved in cellular processes, including transcription, translation, and splicing. PRMT5 functions in a hetero-octameric complex comprised of four PRMT5 molecules and four cofactor molecules. , One of these cofactors, MEP50, is required for efficient PRMT5 activity. Additional co-factors, CLNS1A, RIOK1, and COPR5, augment PRMT5 activity by recruiting the complex to specific targets associated with splicing, translation, or transcription, respectively. , The PRMT5-MEP50-CLNS1A complex, referred to as the methylosome, , is the most studied of these complexes. It binds to and promotes the methylation of three Sm proteins: SmB/B′, SmD1, and SmD3. , , , These methylated Sm proteins are then incorporated into a larger, seven-member Sm protein ring, which is loaded around an snRNA to form an snRNP – a core component of the spliceosome. , PRMT5 is known to methylate other splicing factors, but does so without CLNS1A.
Which aspect(s) of PRMT5 biology underlie the vulnerability of cancer cells to PRMT5i remains a key question in the field. Because the methylosome is one of PRMT5’s most studied roles, much attention has been focused on assessing splicing problems upon PRMT5 inhibition. In a seminal study, Bezzi et al. examined PRMT5 deletion in neural stem and progenitor cells and showed that this was sufficient to cause neonatal inviability. They showed that the PRMT5-deficient neural cells display an array of splicing defects, highlighting the inclusion of a cassette exon in MDM4 and consequent p53 activation, but also noting elevated levels of unspliced introns. In a different study, we identified PRMT5 as the top hit in an in vivo screen for essential genes in glioblastoma, and our molecular analysis revealed that the predominant impact on splicing was increased levels of introns. Notably, based on their relative stability and presence in poly-adenylated mRNAs, we concluded that these were likely a special class of unspliced introns, called detained introns (DIs). DIs occur in fully processed transcripts that remain in the nucleus, making them unavailable for translation. This distinguishes them from retained introns (RIs), which exist in transcripts that reach the cytoplasm and typically undergo nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). It is now common to use splicing defects as evidence of PRMT5i action, , , , , , , , , , reinforcing the prevailing view that PRMT5’s splicing role largely underlie the vulnerability of cells to PRMT5 inhibition. However, it is important to note that previous studies use experimental approaches that disrupt all PRMT5 functions. Therefore, while splicing defects are evident, it is an open question whether these are responsible for PRMT5i sensitivity versus other roles of PRMT5. Intriguingly, a recent study showed that the bulk depletion of CLNS1A, the methylosome co-factor, reduced the viability of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma KP4 cells. This observation is consistent with a key role for the PRMT5-splicing axis, but, unfortunately, this study did not look at the impact of CLNS1A loss on splicing.
In this current study, we conduct in-depth analyses of PRMT5-regulated splicing events and directly test their essentiality. We demonstrate that elevated intron levels are the predominant splicing defect and confirm that these are bona fide DIs. We specifically probe the functional importance of the methylosome through CLNS1A depletion, which leaves other PRMT5 functions intact. We find that CLNS1A is essential for viability in some, but not all, cell lines. Importantly, loss of viability directly reflects the loss of Sm protein methylation and upregulation of DIs. Finally, we find that the identities of PRMT5-regulated DIs are highly conserved across human cell lines, as well as mouse cell lines, irrespective of tissue/tumor type. In contrast, there is little conservation at the DI or gene level between human and mouse DIs, even within the same tissue type. Despite this, human and mouse DI-containing genes strongly converge on biological processes associated with cell proliferation. This reflects the fact that many DI-regulated genes encode for proteins that are essential, but largely non-overlapping, components of multi-protein complexes that play central roles in proliferation. Together, these data suggest that maintaining appropriate DI splicing in core proliferation-associated genes plays a critical role by which PRMT5 enables cell viability.

## Results

## PRMT5-regulated intronic splicing events are bona fide detained introns
We began our study by analyzing the splicing changes resulting from PRMT5 inhibition. We conducted a 3-day PRMT5i treatment using 10 nM JNJ-64619178 in U87 glioblastoma cells, which we have previously shown to be PRMT5i-sensitive. We enriched for poly-adenylated mRNAs in whole cell extracts, performed deep sequencing (> 40 million reads/sample), and conducted analyses to identify and quantify PRMT5-regulated splicing changes. The majority of significant changes (padj < 0.05) were elevated levels of introns, with the remainder being low levels of exon-based splicing events [cassette exons (CAS), mutually exclusive exons (MXE) and alternative 5’ (A5PSS) or 3’ (A3PSS) splice sites] ( A–1C). For each of these splicing events, the impact of JNJ-64619178 showed high concordance with findings from our prior study, in which we treated U87 cells with a first-generation PRMT5i, EPZ015666 for 3 days ( A). Notably, JNJ-64619178 and EPZ015666 have distinct mechanisms of action (targeting SAM versus substrate binding, respectively), arguing that the preferential impact on elevated intron levels is a general consequence of PRMT5 inhibition. Moving forward, we used JNJ-64619178 as our PRMT5i, unless otherwise stated. 
Our next goal was to establish that the persisting introns were bona fide DIs, not RIs. A defining hallmark of DI-containing transcripts is their nuclear localization. Thus, we repeated the 3-day 10 nM PRMT5i treatment of U87 cells and performed cellular fractionation, confirming efficacy by western blotting ( B and S1C). We conducted deep sequencing and quantified the levels of intron-containing, versus coding (fully spliced), isoforms within the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments ( B), as well as the impact of PRMT5i treatment ( D, 1E, and D). We detected 5,885 intron-containing isoforms and found that the vast majority (94%) showed profound nuclear enrichment ( D), which is entirely consistent with these being DIs. Most were lowly present in the absence of PRMT5 inhibition, befitting the known occurrence of DIs in normal cells. In response to PRMT5i, 44% of intron-containing isoforms (2,618/5,885) showed significant changes ( D). Importantly, the vast majority (94%) of these PRMT5i-regulated isoforms were DIs, as judged by their predominant nuclear localization ( D and 1E). For these PRMT5i-regulated DIs, treatment rarely impacted the cytoplasmic levels, but instead evoked profound changes in nuclear levels ( D and 1E). Moreover, in most cases (77%), the nuclear levels were dramatically increased by PRMT5i treatment ( D and 1E).
Our working model is that DI formation, and consequent nuclear trapping of mRNAs, leads to low levels of coding transcripts. Thus, we examined the levels of coding transcripts, initially considering all detected species ( E and S1F). Most were observed in both compartments and were unaffected by PRMT5i treatment ( E and S1F). We then considered the coding isoforms of genes that contained PRMT5-regulated DIs ( E). Their behavior matched the reciprocal effect predicted by our hypothesis; all nuclear-enriched DIs showed concomitant decreases in the cytoplasmic representation of their coding isoforms ( E). Since labs in the PRMT5 field often use whole cell lysates to assess splicing defects, we asked whether these compartment-associated effects can still be discerned from whole cell extracts. Nuclear fractionation increases the detection sensitivity of sequencing reads in DI, compared to whole cell lysates, but the latter method is sufficient to detect basal levels and drug-induced DI upregulation with appropriate depth of sequencing ( F). Additionally, data from whole cell lysates correlated reasonably well with that from the nuclear fraction for DI isoforms (r = 0.70) or the cytoplasmic fraction for coding isoforms (r = 0.83; G). Most importantly, we could directly validate the drug-induced changes on transcript and protein levels in whole cell lysates. Specifically, qRT-PCR confirmed significantly higher levels of DI-containing transcripts (2.5- to 24-fold over vehicle, p < 0.01) as well as significantly reduced levels of the coding transcripts (42–78% of vehicle, p < 0.01) in response to 3-day PRMT5i treatment ( H). In each case, this resulted in lower protein levels (21–57% of vehicle) detected by western blotting after 5 days of treatment, as the drug-induced splicing changes take effect on the proteome.
As a final proof that the PRMT5i-upregulated introns are DIs, we examined the impact of NMD, which would only affect RI levels. For this, we treated U87 cells with 10 nM PRMT5i for 3 days, and introduced siRNAs against UPF1, an NMD-initiating factor, or a non-targeting control, for the last 2 days of treatment. We confirmed significant siRNA-mediated UPF1 knockdown through qRT-PCR (p < 0.01) and western blotting ( G and S1H), and then established by qRT-PCR that PRMT5i treatment increased the intron levels within DNA2 (> 6-fold, p < 0.05) and EIF4E (> 20-fold, p < 0.01), but that the depletion of UPF1 did not further increase DI levels ( I). Taken together, our data show that PRMT5 inhibition specifically promotes the formation of bona fide DIs, thereby reducing the levels of cytoplasmic coding transcripts and protein product.

## Detained introns are highly conserved across human cell contexts
Having demonstrated that DI formation is the major splicing change in response to PRMT5 inhibition in U87 cells, we wanted to determine if DIs are conserved across different human cell lines. For this analysis, we examined six additional lines, including four different cancers, a virally transformed cell line, and an immortalized normal cell line ( A). We treated these with 10 nM PRMT5i for 3 days, generated whole cell extracts, validated the inhibition of SDMA, and performed deep sequencing and AS analyses ( A and S2B). DIs accounted for the majority (62–75%) of AS events significantly altered (padj < 0.05) in response to PRMT5 inhibition in every context ( B and C). Collectively, we identified 2,794 unique DIs that were significantly altered (padj < 0.05) in response to PRMT5 inhibition in at least one cell line ( C). Like U87 cells, the vast majority (87–98%) of DIs were upregulated in response to drug treatment ( C and C). We then investigated the degree to which significant PRMT5i-regulated AS events (padj < 0.05) were conserved across human cells. Initially, we performed pairwise comparisons between all seven cell lines, plotting both the degree of pairwise conservation and absolute number of conserved events for each AS class ( D). This revealed remarkably little conservation for any of the exon-based AS events, but high conservation of DI identity, for all pairwise comparisons, affecting more than 1,000 individual DIs ( D). Given the high pairwise DI conservation, we then considered the overlap across all lines ( E and D). This revealed remarkable conservation of PRMT5i-regulated DIs; more than 500 were significantly upregulated (padj < 0.05, log2FC > 0) in 7/7 cell lines, and more than half of the DIs in any one cell line were conserved in at least 5/7 lines ( E and D). These data indicate that PRMT5 inhibition upregulates a common set of DIs, irrespective of tissue type, driving mutation, or oncogenic status. 
Given this high conservation, we next determined the biological processes that PRMT5-regulated DIs are associated with. Gene ontology (GO) analysis for all upregulated DIs (padj < 0.05, log2FC > 0), showed strong enrichment of core proliferation-associated processes, including multiple cell cycle-associated gene sets, in all seven lines ( F), consistent with our prior analysis of U87 cells. Together, these data support a model where PRMT5 enables efficient intron splicing of a conserved set of DIs in proliferation-associated genes.

## Detained intron upregulation from methylosome impairment mirrors viability impairment
Our observations fit the prevailing view that the vulnerability of cancer cells to PRMT5i results from splicing defects that impact proliferation control. However, neither our, nor existing, data directly address the importance of the PRMT5-splicing axis because they disrupt all PRMT5 functions ( A). To address this limitation, we interrogated the necessity of the methylosome, which mediates PRMT5’s core splicing role, using the dTag degron system to deplete CLNS1A, the PRMT5-cofactor that is essential for this function ( A). We selected three of our seven PRMT5i-responsive cell lines – CAL51, HEK293T, and HCT116 ( A) – and introduced the dTag degron domain, which also contains an HA-tag, into the endogenous CLNS1A locus. For each line, we identified four clones that have homozygous degron cassette insertion, as determined by a 12 kDa molecular weight increase for CLNS1A and HA positivity ( B and A). We challenged parental cell lines with the degron inducer, dTag-13, for 6 days and showed that doses up to 1 μM avoid off-target toxicity ( C). We then treated all of our clones for 3 days with 1 μM dTag-13 and showed that this achieved high levels of CLNS1A depletion for all cell lines ( D). 
Having validated our dTag-CLNS1A clones, we directly compared their sensitivity to CLNS1A depletion versus PRMT5 inhibition. Specifically, we treated each with vehicle, 1 μM dTag-13, or 10 nM PRMT5i for various timepoints. First, we conducted molecular analyses, using a 3-day timepoint to match the preceding studies. PRMT5i behaved as expected; it had no detectable effect on the levels of HA-CLNS1A ( A and 4B) but caused a dramatic and significant (p < 0.01) downregulation in the methylation of SmB ( A and 4B), as well as all other SDMA-modified proteins ( A). Consistent with our clone validation ( D), dTag-13 treatment induced robust and significant (p < 0.01) depletion of HA-CLNS1A in all cases ( A and 4B). Unexpectedly, we observed two distinct downstream molecular phenotypes, which depended on the cell line and were consistent across all clones. CAL51 and HEK293T displayed one set of phenotypes: dTag-13 treatment caused significant (p < 0.05) loss specifically of SDMA on SmB ( A and 4B), but not other PRMT5 targets ( A), and yielded robust DI induction ( C and C). Importantly, there was high conservation in the identity of DIs affected by CLNS1A-depletion in the clones versus PRMT5i treatment of their relevant parental CAL51 or HEK293T lines ( D), and less overlap for other AS events ( D–S4G). The HCT116 clones displayed starkly different downstream molecular phenotypes. Specifically, dTag-13 treatment yielded no significant loss of methylation on Sm proteins ( A and 4B), and almost no significant induction of DIs, or other AS changes ( C, 4D, and C–S4G). 
PRMT5 inhibition is known to yield proliferation defects, which can trigger cell death or senescence. Thus, we screened the CAL51, HEK293T, and HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A #1 clones for these defects, directly comparing the effects of treatment with dTag-13 with those of PRMT5i. Exactly as expected, PRMT5i induces cell cycle defects, and then apoptosis, determined by Annexin-V positivity ( E, A, S5B, S6A, and S6B). In contrast, dTag-13 treatment of dTag-CLNS1A #1 clones yielded distinct outcomes in the CAL51 and HEK293T, versus HCT116, in agreement with their differential molecular response. The first observed difference was in cell cycle phasing, determined by EdU incorporation and DAPI staining via flow cytometry after 2, 4, or 6 days of treatment ( E, A, and S5B). For the 2-day timepoint, all three lines largely resembled the vehicle control ( A). By day 4, and continuing on day 6, the CAL51 and HEK293T clones both showed significant differences in the distribution of G1, S, and G2/M populations, indicating cell cycle progression defects ( E, A, and S5B). Moreover, by day 6, both lines showed a significantly higher level of cells with sub-G1 DNA content, indicating cell death ( A and S5B). To reinforce this finding, we conducted Annexin-V and propidium iodide (PI) staining on 6-day treatment samples and confirmed that dTag-13 significantly increased the level of apoptotic and dead cells in both CAL51 and HEK293T ( E, A, and S6B). Importantly, for both CAL51 and HEK293T clones, the nature, magnitude, and time of appearance of the cell cycle and apoptotic defects were strikingly similar for dTag-13 versus PRMT5i treatment. As noted above, we observed a different outcome with HCT116. Specifically, dTag-13 treatment of the HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A clone had minimal impact on the cell cycle distribution, the level of sub-G1 cells, or the degree of Annexin-V/PI staining at any time point, while PRMT5i induced cell cycle defects and apoptosis ( E, A, S5B, A, and S6B). These differential phenotypic effects between dTag-13 and PRMT5i treatment correlated perfectly with their differing abilities to impact SmB methylation and DI formation.
To further probe the differential cell cycle and apoptotic responses of CAL51, HEK293T, and HCT116 dTag-13, we compared the relative viability of all four dTag-CLNS1A clones to their parental controls across a range of dTag-13 concentrations ( F). The dTag-13 response of the four HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A clones was indistinguishable from that of their parental control. In contrast, the CAL51 and HEK293T dTag-CLNS1A clones all showed exceptionally heightened sensitivity (p < 0.0001). To confirm that these dTag-13 effects were on target, we generated dTag-CLNS1A #1 variants that ectopically expressed FLAG-tagged CLNS1A ( A). We treated these cells with dTag-13 and found that ectopic CLNS1A-FLAG rescued the drug induced loss of SmB protein methylation, induction of DIs, and reduced viability of CAL51 and HEK293T clones, essentially restoring them to the parental state ( A and S7C). Taken together, these data show that selective loss of SDMA on Sm proteins is sufficient to phenocopy central effects of PRMT5 inhibition, including the upregulation of DIs, and subsequent activation of cell cycle defects, and cell death.

## CLNS1A is fully dispensable in some contexts
The finding that all four HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A clones were insensitive to CLNS1A depletion was highly unexpected, given that it is believed to be cell essential. We realized that dTag-13 treatment does not achieve complete CLNS1A loss, and the residual levels in HCT116 are typically higher than those observed in CAL51 and HEK293T clones ( A and 4B). To determine whether a more prolonged treatment would reveal an impairment in HCT116 cells, we treated two HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A clones with 1 μM dTag-13 for 50 days, harvesting and replating cells every 5 days. These cells showed no perceptible growth impairment and no noticeable loss of SmB methylation, even though CLNS1A was efficiently depleted throughout the time-course ( A). Thus, HCT116 can tolerate a dramatic, long-term CLNS1A depletion. 
Given this finding, we wondered whether CLNS1A is fully dispensable in HCT116 cells. We deployed CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene targeting with the goal of generating CLNS1A knock-out (KO) lines, and identified two independent KO lines (sgCLNS1A-1 and sgCLNS1A-2), carrying different homozygous deletions that each caused frameshifts and premature termination codons (PTCs), as well as two independent hypomorphic clones (sgCLNS1A-3 and sgCLNS1A-4), in which one allele bore a small in-frame deletion, Δ117-118, and the other was disrupted by different frameshift mutations and consequent PTCs ( A). Western blotting confirmed that the KO clones lacked detectable CLNS1A and revealed that the hypomorphic clones had effectively undetectable protein levels ( B and B), indicating that these in-frame deletions impair protein stability. We then analyzed the properties of our sgCLNS1A clones, and found that Sm protein methylation was retained at relatively normal levels in two clones and with only a modest reduction in two others ( B and B), and all four sgCLNS1A clones proliferated at the same rate as the sgCtrl cells ( C). These data argue that HCT116 cells thrive in the absence of CLNS1A.
Given how unexpected this finding was, we wondered whether the sgCLNS1A clones had altered PRMT5-associated processes to compensate. We realized that there is an annotated alternatively spliced CLNS1A isoform, which skips the exon targeted by our sgRNA, and screened for full length (FL)- and truncated-CLNS1A transcripts in the KO clones. Both had significantly reduced levels of FL-CLNS1A mRNA and upregulated levels of truncated-CLNS1A, albeit from near absence in sgCtrl cells to low levels in the KOs ( C and S8D). Alphafold2 , yielded a protein structure for truncated-CLNS1A, but the portion encoded by the skipped exons makes critical contacts to Sm protein substrates and also within the CLNS1A protein, likely supporting stability ( E). Accordingly, we introduced FL- or truncated-CLNS1A into sgCLNS1A-1 cells using retroviruses at a similar multiplicity of infection, and observed lower protein levels of the truncated versus FL-CLNS1A ( F). We also considered the possibility of altered expression for other PRMT5 pathway components. Specifically, we interrogated PRMT5, MEP50 (PRMT5’s essential co-factor), PRMT9 (an alternate SDMA-writing enzyme), ROIK1 (a co-factor that competes with CLNS1A), and SNRPB/SmB, using qRT-PCR and western blotting, but found no differences between the four sgCLNS1A clones and the sgCtrl controls ( G).
Since CLNS1A functions in a complex with PRMT5, we anticipated that CLNS1A deficiency, and subsequent ectopic expression, would modulate the sensitivity of the HCT116 cells to PRMT5i. Thus, we treated sgCLNS1A-1 and sgCLNS1A-4 with a range of PRMT5i concentrations for 6 days, and found that both showed significantly (p < 0.01) increased susceptibility to PRMT5i, compared to the parental control ( D). We then examined the effect of the ectopic expression of core PRMT5 pathway components on this phenotype. As expected, FL-CLNS1A made all sgCLNS1A clones significantly (p < 0.01) more resistant to PRMT5i ( A and S9B). In contrast, truncated-CLNS1A had no effect in the sgCLNS1A-1 cells ( C). Finally, we found that the ectopic expression of PRMT5, MEP50, or PRMT9 did not modulate the PRMT5i-sensitivity of sgCLNS1A-1 or sgCLNS1A-3 ( D and S9E). These data yield two major conclusions. First, they show that truncated-CLNS1A is non-functional, demonstrating that the sgCLNS1A clones are truly CLNS1A-deficient, and establishing that CLNS1A is dispensable in HCT116 cells. Second, they show that CLSN1A loss increases the vulnerability of HCT116 cells to PRMT5 inhibition, which cannot be rescued by the overexpression of single PRMT5 pathway players.
We hypothesized that we could exploit the collaborative effects of CLNS1A deficiency and PRMT5 inhibition to further explore the relationship between the PRMT5-splicing axis and cellular viability. For this, we returned to the HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A clones, and cultured two alongside the parental line, with either vehicle control or 1 μM dTag-13 across a range of JNJ-64619178 ( E) or EPZ015666 ( F) concentrations. As expected, dTag-13 treatment did not alter the responsiveness of the parental HCT116 to either PRMT5i, but it made the dTag-CLNS1A lines significantly more sensitive to both inhibitors (p < 0.001). The largest viability differences with or without dTag-13 treatment were observed at 0.5 and 1 nM of the PRMT5i JNJ-64619178 ( E Center, red triangle and star). Thus, we focused on these concentrations to determine whether differences in Sm protein methylation and DI levels correlated with the observed viability differences. At both 0.5 and 1 nM PRMT5i, HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A #1 exhibited a greater loss of Sm protein methylation when dTag-13 treatment was added ( F). Moreover, decreased Sm protein methylation was accompanied by a significant (p < 0.01) increase in the levels of a canonical DI in EIF4E ( G). Importantly, there was no difference in the levels of other (non-methylosome) PRMT5 targets between minus and plus dTag-13 treatment at 0, 0.5, and 1 nM PRMT5i. Thus, CLNS1A depletion significantly sensitizes cells to PRMT5 inhibition, and this, again, tracks with the magnitude of impairment of Sm protein methylation and increased DI formation.

## MTAP deficiency increases dependence on methylosome activity
We also considered how the status of methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) modulates the influence of the methylosome and DI regulation on cell viability. MTAP is frequently deleted in many human tumors due to its adjacency to the CDKN2A tumor suppressor locus. , , , , , MTAP loss causes the accumulation of its substrate, MTA, which acts as an endogenous inhibitor of PRMT5. , If DIs represent the core vulnerability of cancer cells, MTAP loss should collaborate with CLNS1A deficiency in HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A clones. To address this, we used CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene targeting to generate two independent MTAP-deficient variants of HCT116 dTag-CLNS1A #1 ( A). MTAP deficiency had no effect on the normal rate of proliferation ( B). However, it altered the response to treatment with 1 μM dTag-13, exactly as expected; compared to MTAP-proficient cells, MTAP-deficient cells showed lower levels of SmB methylation and significantly upregulated canonical DIs in EIF4E and SNRNP200 after 3 days of treatment, and significantly reduced viability after 6 days ( C–S10E). Finally, we asked whether the collaboration between CLNS1A depletion and PRMT5 inhibition in HCT116 cells was modulated by MTAP deficiency. We treated our sgCtrl and sgMTAP lines in the presence or absence of 1 μM dTag-13 for 6 days, together with JNJ-64619178 or two next-generation PRMT5 inhibitors that are known to be MTA-cooperative (AMG-193 and TNG908) over a range of concentrations ( A). First, we considered the no dTag-13 condition (i.e., CLNS1A proficient) and found that the PRMT5i behaved exactly as expected; JNJ-64619178 had similar IC50s for sgCtrl and sgMTAP cells, while the MTA-cooperative inhibitors had lower IC50s in sgMTAP cells. We then considered how dTag-13 addition (i.e., CLNS1A deficient) altered this response. For both sgCtrl and sgMTAP cells, dTag-13 increased sensitivity to all three PRMT5i, particularly at the lower drug doses. The viability difference between dTag-13 plus and minus cells was most pronounced in sgMTAP cells, beginning with the no PRMT5i point, suggesting that it largely reflects the collaborative effects of CLNS1A deficiency and MTAP loss. This was apparent when we replotted the data, setting the no drug concentration viability to 1 for both dTag-13 plus and minus cells ( B). Based on these experiments, PRMT5 inhibition appears to be largely additive with CLNS1A deficiency and MTAP loss, at least in the setting of this single cell line.

## Detained intron identity is conserved across mouse cell lines to regulate proliferation-associated processes
Our data show that DIs are highly conserved across human cell types and are a key vulnerability to PRMT5 inhibition. Given this, we expanded our analysis to murine cells. Specifically, we treated murine lung and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma lines for 3 days with PRMT5i [JNJ-64619178 (100 nM) or EPZ015666 (10 μM)] ( A, A, and S12B). Similar to human cells, DIs accounted for the majority of significant (padj < 0.05) AS events irrespective of cell line or inhibitor ( B and C). Moreover, DI identity was highly conserved across murine cell lines ( C–6E), and much more so than exon-based AS events ( D). Indeed, nearly 1,000 individual DIs were common to all murine cell lines examined ( E). Thus, similar to our finding in human cells ( C–2E), PRMT5 regulates a conserved set of murine DIs. We performed GO analysis on all upregulated DIs for each murine line (padj < 0.05, log2FC > 0) and observed strong enrichment for core proliferation-associated processes ( F). Collectively, our data show that PRMT5 inhibition drives the formation of a conserved set of DIs in murine cells that impact proliferation-associated processes. 

## Detained introns are not conserved across species, but the biological complexes and processes they affect are highly conserved
Our analyses indicate that DIs impinge on similar biological processes in human and mouse cells ( F and F). To explore this overlap, we conducted pairwise comparisons of the processes affected by PRMT5i-induced DIs in the human H23 lung cancer line with those detected in each of the murine lung cancer lines, KP393T5 and KP1233, and observed 71% ( A and 7B) and 79% ( A) overlap, respectively. Again, there was a high representation of core proliferation-associated processes. Similarly high conservation (56%) was observed when comparing human PANC1 and murine MDM1402 pancreatic cancer lines ( B). This high inter-species conservation of DI-affected biological processes was also agnostic of tissue type, as comparison of the biological processes conserved across all human lines with those conserved across all murine lines showed 67% conservation ( C). 
We anticipated that the inter-species overlap would reflect the high conservation of the DI-containing genes. However, we observed a striking lack of inter-species conservation in DI-containing genes, even when we considered a specific tissue type (21%–25%; A, A, and S13B) or the genes that were commonly regulated in all human versus all mouse lines (25%; C). Moreover, when regulated genes were conserved between human and mouse, the DIs typically affected distinct introns. Thus, our data show that PRMT5i-induced DIs are poorly conserved across species at the intron or gene level, yet impact the same biological processes. As a possible explanation, we realized that many DI-containing genes encode for components of multi-protein complexes. Consequently, we performed CORUM analysis to identify complexes affected by DI-containing genes in mouse versus human cells and then quantified the degree of overlap. For both comparisons within specific tissue types ( A, A, and S13B) and comparison of DIs commonly regulated in all human versus all mouse lines ( C), we found a strong overlap in complex identity (53–59%), which was much higher than the DI-containing gene identity, and approached the levels of biological process conservation. Examples of affected complexes are shown in C, D, and S13E. Moreover, the examination of representative complexes revealed many cases where the PRMT5-regulated DIs impacted more than one complex component within a single species ( C, D, and S13E), which would further exacerbate defects in the assembly and levels of functional complexes. Finally, analyses at the global level ( D and F) clearly illustrate how DI upregulation in species-specific genes converges on multi-protein complexes to impact common, fundamental biological processes.

## Discussion
The primary goal of this study was to address the prevailing view that PRMT5’s role in splicing is largely responsible for its oncogenic activity and therefore the cellular vulnerability to PRMT5 inhibitors. We began by investigating the spectrum of splicing problems that result from PRMT5 inactivation. Consistent with our prior study, we find that the predominant significant changes are alterations in intron inclusion. We further establish that the vast majority of these are bona fide DIs, as judged by nuclear localization and independence from NMD, and most are upregulated as a consequence of PRMT5 inhibition. This DI upregulation occurs irrespective of the mechanism of action of the PRMT5i and is remarkably well conserved. Importantly, DI upregulation has regulatory consequences. We see, via both genome-wide analyses and validation of identified DI-regulated genes, a corresponding decrease in the levels of coding transcript available in the cytoplasm for translation. Additionally, western blot analyses confirm consistent reduction in protein levels. Notably, GO analyses show that the PRMT5i-responsive DIs selectively impact genes that control cell cycle regulation and proliferation, the biological processes that are well known consequences of effective PRMT5 inhibition. Accordingly, we find that DI upregulation is followed by cell cycle defects, induction of apoptosis, and loss of cell viability.
In a “separation of function” approach, we used the depletion or deletion of CLNS1A to specifically inactive the methylosome, and thus the PRMT5-splicing axis, while maintaining other PRMT5 functions. We observed a strong depletion of CLSN1A in all three cell lines examined. In two cases (CAL51 and HEK293T), this resulted in effective and specific methylosome inhibition (as judged by loss of SDMA marks on Sm proteins, but not other PRMT5 targets) while the third cell line (HCT116) showed no methylosome inhibition. Importantly, when methylosome inhibition was observed, we saw robust DI formation and subsequent cell cycle defects, apoptosis, and loss of viability. Moreover, in CAL51 and HEK293T cells, the kinetics and magnitude of the cellular defects were strikingly similar between CLNS1A depletion and PRMT5 inhibition. Collectively, our data show that PRMT5’s splicing role is sufficient to account for PRMT5’s cell essentiality. We note that these findings do not rule out roles for other PRMT5 functions. It is possible that the selective inactivation of other functions could be lethal too, or could serve to augment the key role of splicing. We also note that our separation of function experiments do not dissect the various aspects of splicing. Obviously, we favor the notion that DI regulation is the critical driver, based on their prevalence, high conservation, and enrichment in core proliferation regulators. However, we are not ruling out a role for exon-based AS events. Indeed, there is compelling evidence that PRMT5 inhibition can drive alternative splicing of MDM4, which results in p53 activation. However, given the relatively low levels and conservation of exon-based AS events, we hypothesize that they have contributing roles in a context-dependent manner.
The finding that HCT116 cells were unaffected, phenotypically, by CLNS1A depletion or complete knockout was completely unexpected, as it is widely believed to be pan-cell essential. The existing view is based primarily on large-scale depletion screens, which effectively test fitness rather than essentiality. It remains an open question how HCT116 cells can survive without CLNS1A. Interestingly, we did not observe any obvious compensation in our CLNS1A KO cells via the upregulation of PRMT5, MEP50 or PRMT9, or downregulation of the competing cofactor, RIOK1. Since PRMT5-MEP50 complexes are sufficient to bind and methylate Sm proteins, at least in vitro, , we speculate that there is enough PRMT5-MEP50 activity in HCT116 cells to survive without CLNS1A. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, our analyses of CLNS1A-deficient and KO HCT116 cells support the essentially of methylosome activity. Specifically, these cells show no detectable perturbation of Sm protein methylation, DI levels, cell cycle phasing, or cellular viability. However, they do display increased sensitivity to PRMT5i or genetic ablation of MTAP. Importantly, this increased sensitivity reflects diminished Sm protein methylation and upregulated DI formation. The observed collaboration of CLNS1A deficiency with low level PRMT5i or MTAP loss in HCT116, at both the molecular and cellular levels, argues that these perturbations are working via a shared mechanism that converges on robust DI induction as a trigger for cell cycle defects and loss of viability.
Our study also reveals fascinating similarities, as well as differences, in DI biology between humans and mice. With respect to similarities, we find that on the order of 1000–2000 genes are affected by PRMT5i-responsive DIs in either human or mouse cells. These DIs are remarkably well-conserved in an intra-species manner; for example, approximately half of the PRMT5i-responsive DIs were conserved in all 7 human cell lines analyzed. This conservation is agnostic to tissue type, whether the cells are transformed or normal, or the nature of the driving mutations within the cancer cells. As noted above, many of the affected genes are cell essential and significantly enriched for roles in cellular proliferation. We also found that the same core biological processes are affected in mouse and human cells. However, remarkably, there is minimal conservation between these two species with regard to the identity of the DIs, or even the affected genes. Instead, our analyses argue that these targets frequently represent distinct, rather than overlapping, components of multi-protein complexes involved in proliferative control. Interestingly, in many cases, we find that more than one component of a given complex are encoded by DI-containing genes. It is easy to envisage that the upregulation of DIs, occurring simultaneously in hundreds of core proliferation regulators and affecting numerous essential proliferation-associated complexes, creates profound challenges for cell cycle progression. It is fascinating to consider how two species have developed a way to maintain regulation at the complex level without conservation at the DI or gene level. Presumably, with so many genes in play, a species could afford to lose or gain individual DIs, without significant perturbation of overall cell cycle control, as long as impact on core proliferation-associated complexes is conserved. This would allow drift at the DI and gene level, while maintaining conservation at the complex and process level. Notably, we believe that control of DI levels by the methylosome is a general mechanism of proliferation control, used by normal cells to regulate proliferative capacity throughout development, which is co-opted by cancer cells through the near universally observed upregulation of PRMT5 in malignancies.
Numerous PRMT5i are currently in, or approaching, clinical trials without a clear understanding of the root cause of cancer vulnerability. Our study strongly validates the prevailing view that the effectiveness of PRMT5i reflects the induction of splicing defects in proliferating cells. To improve cancer cell selectivity, the field has already generated PRMT5i that specifically target MTAP-deficient cells. Our data argue that the perturbation of MTAP also impinges on splicing, and more specifically, DI biology. In furtherance of increased efficacy, we hypothesize that PRMT5i that more specifically target the PRMT5-splicing axis, alone or in combination with inhibitors that target DI-regulated complexes, might further increase potency, assuming a viable therapeutic window exists.

## Limitations of the study
Our study establishes that DI regulation is a key driver of cellular vulnerability to PRMT5 inhibition, but some limitations should be noted. First, although we clearly show that the inactivation of the PRMT5-splicing axis is sufficient to cause lethality, we note that our study does not address potential contributions by other PRMT5 functions. Second, our analysis notes that DIs are by far the most prevalent and most conserved AS events, and their formation correlates with inviability, but we cannot rule out that rarer and less well-conserved exon-based AS events contribute in some settings. Third, the identification of a single cell line, HCT116, that was impervious to CLNS1A loss, showing no splicing defects, was completely unexpected. We did not observe any obvious compensatory mechanisms, and thus, the underlying molecular basis for this remains to be determined. Additionally, it remains unclear if this is the sole exception or whether other cell lines can also survive without CLNS1A. Finally, we hypothesize that widespread DI induction and the consequent impairment of numerous core proliferation-associated complexes provide redundancy to the system, thereby enabling the drift of DI identity across species. However, we have not specifically tested this model. We acknowledge these limitations and underscore the need for deeper mechanistic understanding to fully exploit the PRMT5-splicing axis as a therapeutic vulnerability.

## Cell line generation

## dTag-CLNS1A knock-in vector generation
dTag (pCRIS-PITChv2-Puro-dTAG and pCRIS-PITChv2-BSD-dTAG) and PITCh (pX330A-1x2 and pX330S-2-PITCh) vectors were obtained from Addgene (91793, 91792, 58766, 63670). The PITCh vector (pX330A-1x2) was modified to contain an sgRNA targeting the N-terminus of CLNS1A as previously described. Finally, pX330A-CLNS1A/PITCh was generated using Golden Gate Assembly.

## dTag-CLNS1A knock-in Cell line generation
Knock-in cell lines were generated as previously described. Briefly, 106 cells were plated in a 10-cm dish prior to transfection. The following day, cells were transfected with 2 μg pX330A-CLNS1A/PITCh and 1 μg pCRIS-PITChv2-Puro-dTAG-CLNS1A using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher, 11668027). After 8 h, media was replaced and supplemented with 500 nM NU7441 (Selleck Chemicals, S2638) to inhibit NHEJ. CAL51 cells did not tolerate NU7441 treatment, so this addition was omitted for that cell line. After 48 h, cells were selected with puromycin. Following selection and outgrowth, cells underwent a second transfection with 2 μg pX330A-CLNS1A/PITCh and 1 μg pCRIS-PITChv2-BSD-dTAG-CLNS1A, using the same transfection workflow to enrich for homozygous knock-ins. Following blasticidin selection, cells were single cell cloned by dilution. CLNS1A knock-in was confirmed by gDNA PCR and western blot analysis.

## Knockout cell line generation
LentiCRISPRv2 Puro and lentiCRISPRv2 Hygro were obtained from Addgene (98290 and 98291). Vectors were modified to contain an sgRNA targeting MTAP, CLNS1A or a non-targeting control. For cell line generation, cells were infected with lentivirus containing lentiCRISPRv2 or lentiCRISPRv2 Hygro with a sgRNA targeting the gene of interest, or a control sgRNA (sgCtrl). Virus was incubated with cells for 2 days, after which puromycin or hygromycin was added for selection. Following selection cells were single cell cloned by dilution. For MTAP cell lines, knockout was confirmed by western blot analysis. For CLNS1A knockout cell lines, the CLNS1A locus was amplified and subcloned by Gibson Assembly into pcDNA3.1+ (ThermoFisher, V79020). Following transformation into DH5α cells, colonies were sequenced by Sanger Sequencing. CLNS1A knockout was additionally confirmed by western blot analysis.

## Overexpression cell line generation
Overexpression studies constructs utilized the pUltra system, which we modified to incorporate a blasticidin or hygromycin resistance cassette. Briefly, pUltra Hot was digested with XbaI and AgeI-HF, and DNA containing the antibiotic resistance cassette introduced via Gibson assembly, generating the pUltra Blast or pUltra Hygro vector. Full length human CLNS1A was ordered as a gBlock (IDT). CLNS1A Truncation, PRMT5, PRMT9, and MEP50 were cloned from cDNA. Genes were cloned into the pUltra backbone via Gibson Assembly, which also incorporated a 3xFLAG-tag or 3xHA-tag at the C-terminus of the gene. Cells were infected with lentivirus to overexpress the gene of interest, or an empty vector control. Virus was incubated with cells for 2 days, after which antibiotic was added to generate stable cell lines.

## RNA extraction, sequencing and alternative splicing analysis

## Whole cell lysate RNA extraction
Following 3-day treatment with either DMSO (vehicle), PRMT5i, or dTag-13, cells were harvested by scraping. RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106) according to manufacturer’s instructions, including the optional DNaseI addition to remove contaminating gDNA (Qiagen, 79254).

## Cellular fractionation and RNA Isolation from cellular fractions
U87 cells were seeded in a 15 cm plate at a 1.5 × 106 cell/plate. One day after plating, cells were treated with vehicle (DMSO) or 10 nM PRMT5i (JNJ-64619178). After 3 days in drug, cells were harvested via trypsinization, pelleted, and separated into nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions as previously described. Briefly, cell pellets were resuspended in cytoplasmic lysis buffer [0.15% (v/v) NP40, 10 mM Tris (pH7), 150 mM NaCl] supplemented with 10 U SUPERase.IN (Thermo Fisher, AM2694) and EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (MilliporeSigma, 11836170001). Cells were then incubated on ice for 5 min. Cell lysate was layered on top of sucrose buffer [10 mM Tris (pH 7), 150 mM NaCl, 25% (w/v) sucrose] supplemented with 10 U SUPERase.IN (Thermo Fisher, AM2694) and EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (MilliporeSigma, 11836170001). Cells were then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant contains the cytoplasmic fraction and was harvested for RNA extraction. The remaining pellet contains the nuclear fraction and was washed twice with 800 μL cold PBS, centrifuging at 16,000 × g for 1 min at 4°C after each wash. The pellet was resuspended in cold RIPA buffer [50 mM Tris (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 1% (v/v) NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% (w/v) SDS] supplemented with 10 U SUPERase.IN (ThermoFisher, AM2694) and EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (MilliporeSigma, 11836170001). Lysate was incubated for 30 min on ice, with vortexing every 10 min, and then pelleted at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant contains the nuclear fraction and was harvested for RNA extraction.
The RNA from the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596026) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, to each aqueous fraction, 0.5 mL of isopropanol and 1 mL of TRIzol Reagent was added, followed by an incubation on ice for 10 min. Samples were then centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 × g at 4°C, pellets were washed in 1 mL 75% ethanol, and centrifuged again for 5 min at 7,500xg at 4°C. Pellets were air dried for 10 min and resuspended in RNase-free water.

## Alternative splicing analysis
Raw RNA-seq reads were mapped using STAR aligner version 2.5.3a with the following parameters:

 STAR

 --runMode alignReads

 --runThreadN 16

 --sjdbOverhang 149

 --outReadsUnmapped Fastx

 --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate

 --outFilterMultimapNmax 20

 --outFilterMismatchNmax 999

 --outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.04

 --alignIntronMin 70

 --alignIntronMax 500000

 --alignMatesGapMax 500000

 --alignSJoverhangMin 8

 --alignSJDBoverhangMin 1

 --outSAMstrandField intronMotif

 --outFilterType BySJout

 --twopassMode Basic

Additionally, raw RNA-seq reads were mapped using Bowtie 1.2.3 using the following parameters:

 bowtie -n 1 -l 30 -e 70 -t -p 4 -m 1 -X 500 -S

Detained intron and alternative splicing event identification was performed as previously described, , with the following modifications: Mapped reads were filtered using Bedtools v2.26.0 to remove reads overlapping expressed repeats from the UCSC genome browser RNA repeats and Gencode hg38 or mm10 and coding exons annotated in the Gencode hg38 or mm10 annotations. Alternative and constitutive classifications were performed using custom Python scripts and are agnostic with regard to existing annotations other than known gene boundaries. Importantly, all cell lines were run in parallel to ensure that the annotations would be consistent between the cell lines.

## Detained intron and alternative splicing event quantification (DEXSeq)
Detained intron and alternative splicing events were quantified using DEXSeq. A genome annotation including all alternative splicing events and detained introns was generated using custom python scripts and python scripts that accompany DEXSeq. Reads were then quantified to this genome using the DEXSeq python script with the following parameters:

 -p yes -s reverse -f bam -r pos

Finally, differential splicing was quantified using the DEXSeq package within R. Alternative splicing events and detained introns were called as significant if padj < 0.05 and lowly expressed exons and introns were filtered out if their normalized counts were below 80.

## Detained intron and coding isoform quantification (RSEM-DESeq2)
Transcript annotation was performed as previously described. Briefly, using custom Python scripts, we derived a complete transcriptome annotation based on Gencode hg38 gene start and end boundaries and the location of polyA sites for data derived from the human cell lines, the genomic locations of detained introns (as determined above), and the genomic locations of all splice junctions (from the STAR alignment). For comparisons of isoforms containing detained introns to the consensus coding sequence, the annotation for each gene was set to identify the consensus isoform, i.e., the junctions defining each exon are the most frequently used junction detected within all of the combined samples. Each detained intron within a gene was then assigned to an additional transcript.

## Gene and isoform expression
Isoform-level quantification was performed using RSEM v1.3.1 using the custom genomic annotation described above, as well as Gencode hg38 annotations with the parameters:

 rsem-calculate-expression --forward-prob 0 --no-bam-output --paired-end

Differential expression analysis of gene and isoform count tables was performed using DESeq2 v1.42.0. Normalized count tables were obtained from DESeq2. Gene and isoform expression differences were considered significant if padj < 0.01.

## Dice Similarity Score
Dice Similarity scores were calculated for each AS event type across all pairwise cell line combinations using the following equation:%Similarity=100%×2×IntersectionofASEvents#ASEventsCellLine1+#ASEventsCellLine2

## Gene ontology analysis
Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted using DAVID. , Genes who contained a significant DI upon PRMT5i (padj < 0.05, log2FC > 0) were run against the KW Biological Process category. A hypergeometric test was used to determine the probability of observing a certain overlap between the inputted gene list and a specific GO term, compared to the background gene list. For these comparisons, the background gene list was all genes in the human or mouse genome. To account for multiple hypothesis testing, we report the Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected FDR. Gene sets were considered significant if the corrected FDR < 0.01. Gene sets are displayed if they met the significance threshold in at least one line.

## Inter-species gene name conversion and complex analysis
Lists of genes containing a significant DI upon PRMT5i (padj < 0.05, log2FC > 0) for each cell line of interest was created. Mouse gene annotations were converted to human gene annotations using NicheNet v2.1.7. The Comprehensive Resource of Mammalian Protein Complexes (CORUM) v4.1 was used to match DI-containing genes to annotated human protein complex members. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted using DAVID as described above. The DI-containing genes for each protein complex were used to connect complexes to related biological processes. We directly compared PRMT5 DI-regulated genes, protein complexes, and biological processes between human and mouse data based on tissue of origin (Lung: H23 & T5, Pancreas: PANC1 & PDAC), as well as data derived from PRMT5 DI-containing genes shared between all human cell lines (n = 7) or all mouse cell lines (n = 3). Similarities were determined via a Dice Similarity Score as described above.

## Dose-response curves
For each dose-response curve, cells were plated in a volume of 150 μL media into each well of a 96-well plate in the following densities: CAL51 – 250 cells/well, HEK293T – 100 cells/well, and HCT116 – 150 cells/well. Perimeter wells were filled with 200 μL of media to avoid edge effects. The following day, 50 μL of 4× the final drug concentration in media was added to each well in triplicate. For the double-drug dose-response curves, cells were plated in 100 μL media, and then were drugged. The following day, for each drug, 50 μL of 4× the final drug concentration in media was added to each well. After 6 days of treatment, CellTiter-Glo (Promega, G7570) was used per manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence was measured with a Tecan M200 Pro plate reader. Background signal was determined by averaging the luminescence of cell-free wells, and was subtracted from all other viability measurements. Average signal for each dose triplicate was computed, and drug-treated cells were normalized to their DMSO control by division. To generate dose-response curves, normalized viability values for each sample were fit to the following nonlinear model:Viability=Ainf+A0--Ainf1+([drug]EC50)nwhere Ainf and A0 are the lowest and highest drug doses, respectively. Area under the curve was computed and used for statistical testing.

## Proliferation assay
For each cell line, 150 cells were plated in each well. Two replicate plates were plated. The following day, a plate was washed and frozen to serve as counting control. After 6 days, the other plate was washed and frozen. Cyquant was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoFisher, C7026). Fluorescence was measured with a Tecan M200 Pro plate reader. Background signal was determined by averaging the fluorescence of cell-free wells and was subtracted from all other measurements. Population doublings were calculated via log-transformation of normalized fluorescence.

## Alphafold2 prediction
Full length or Truncated CLNS1A protein sequences were inputted into the Alphafold2 ColabFold v1.5.5 software, using the default settings. Structure predictions were visualized using PyMOL v2.5.2.

## Statistical analysis and plotting software
R v4.3.2, GraphPad Prism v10.1.0, and Adobe Illustrator v28.2 were used for generating and plotting figures.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
R v4.3.2 and GraphPad Prism v10.1.0 were used to perform statistical analyses. Data are shown as mean with SD (error bars), unless otherwise stated. All statistical tests used are indicated in the figure legend and/or section. All statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t test unless the variance was significantly different between the samples (calculated with the F-test) in which case it is then specified as Welch’s t test. For statistical significance, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and p = n.s. refers to not statistically significant (p > 0.05).
