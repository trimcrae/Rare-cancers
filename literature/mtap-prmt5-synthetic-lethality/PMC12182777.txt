## Abstract

## Background
Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of unknown primary (SCCUP) refers to any SCC for which the primary tumor origin cannot be identified despite guideline-directed evaluation. Although strategies employing comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) to identify targets for non-SCC carcinomas of unknown primary (CUPs) have shown benefit, the genomic landscape in SCCUP remains poorly defined. Here we describe results of CGP in patients with SCCUP to identify potential therapy targets and characteristic biomarkers.

## Patients and methods
Cases of advanced SCCUP were identified in the FoundationCORE® database by review of clinical history and pathology records. Samples underwent DNA extraction and sequencing to identify genomic alterations (GAs), microsatellite instability (MSI) status, tumor mutational burden (TMB), genomic mutational signatures, and viral reads. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression was determined by immunohistochemistry.

## Results
443 SCCUP cases were identified. Common presentation sites included lymph nodes (41.1%), liver (15.6%), and soft tissue (15.1%). A mean of 6.5 GAs were observed per case. The most frequent non-targetable GAs involved TP53 (62.5%), CDKN2A (37.0%), CDKN2B (19.6%), KMT2D (18.3%), and TERT (16.0%); the most frequent GAs potentially targetable in biomarker-driven trials included PIK3CA (27.3%), PTEN (15.1%), MTAP (13.1%), KRAS (10.4%), and NOTCH1 (8.4%). Among all SCCUP cases, 2.0% had MSI-high (MSI-H) status and 33.9% had TMB ≥10 mutations/megabase. Among 204 cases with available PD-L1 testing, 39.2% were low-positive [tumor proportion score (TPS) 1%-49%] and 29.9% were high-positive (TPS ≥50%). SCCUP cases presenting with liver involvement had fewer GAs per tumor and lower TMB compared with other SCCUP cases. In contrast, cases presenting with inguinal, pelvic, or retroperitoneal involvement were more likely to demonstrate evidence of human papilloma virus (HPV) infection and apolipoprotein B mRNA editing catalytic polypeptide-like (APOBEC) genomic signatures.

## Conclusions
CGP of this SCCUP cohort identified GAs that may guide consideration of molecularly targeted therapy via tumor-agnostic indications and/or treatment in biomarker-driven trials. SCCUPs frequently exhibit biomarkers associated with response to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Key words: carcinoma of unknown primary (CUP), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), squamous cell carcinoma of unknown primary (SCCUP), comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP), biomarker, molecularly targeted therapy

## Introduction
Approximately 2%-5% of malignancies are classified as carcinomas of unknown primary (CUPs), defined as metastatic cancers for which a primary tumor origin cannot be identified despite a full diagnostic evaluation. , CUPs comprise a heterogenous group of cancers. While ∼10%-15% of CUPs can be classified into favorable, more localized disease subsets, the majority present with disseminated disease and are classified as unfavorable. Treatment of unfavorable CUPs has traditionally relied upon platinum-based chemotherapy doublets. However, prognosis remains extremely poor with this approach, with a median overall survival of ∼3 months, and 1-year survival of ∼20%. , 
Investigational efforts in the last decade have focused on approaches to tailor therapy to molecularly based tumor characteristics. Molecular testing to determine the presumptive tissue of origin and subsequently administer site-specific therapy demonstrated promise in retrospective and single-arm prospective studies. , However, larger randomized trials comparing presumptive tissue of origin–based site-specific therapy versus standard platinum-based chemotherapy failed to demonstrate an advantage with this approach. , 
More recently, strategies utilizing comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) to identify targetable genomic alterations (GAs) have shown promise. Retrospective CGP studies of CUP cohorts revealed pathogenic alterations in >95% of patients, with potentially targetable alterations in ∼one-third of patients. , , The phase II CUPISCO trial randomized patients with unfavorable CUP with non-squamous histology who had stable disease after initial platinum-based chemotherapy to receive treatment with either continued chemotherapy or molecularly guided therapy (MGT). Progression free survival (PFS) was significantly improved in those treated with MGT versus continued chemotherapy, with median PFS of 6.1 versus 4.4 months, respectively [hazard ratio (HR) 0.72, P = 0.008]. Several small single-arm trials evaluating treatment of unfavorable CUP with immune checkpoint inhibitors have revealed objective response rates (ORR) ranging between 16%-22%, with significantly superior responses seen in patients with high tumor mutational burden (TMB) and/or elevated programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression. , , These results highlight the importance of CGP and consideration of biomarker-directed therapy in patients with CUP.
While most CUP cases are comprised of adenocarcinoma or undifferentiated carcinoma histology, CUPs with squamous cell histology (SCCUPs) represent ∼5%-15% of cases. , Retrospective CGP studies suggest that SCCUPs harbor distinct molecular profiles from adenocarcinoma CUPs, with lower frequency of potentially targetable receptor tyrosine kinase/RAS alterations. However, published CUP CGP studies contain limited numbers of patients with SCCUP, , , , , , and the molecular landscape of SCCUPs remains poorly defined. Given the observed efficacy of MGT in non-squamous CUPs in the CUPISCO trial, a better understanding of the molecular characteristics of SCCUPs is paramount.
The aim of our study was to characterize the molecular landscape of SCCUPs, with attention to types and frequencies of potentially actionable alterations. Here we report a retrospective analysis of CGP results from a cohort of 443 SCCUP cases identified in the FoundationCORE® database.

## Methods

## Identification of SCCUP cases
Approval of this study, including a waiver of informed consent and a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act waiver of authorization, was obtained from WCG Institutional Review Board (protocol number 20152817).
More than 750 000 cancer cases accrued during routine clinical care in the FoundationCORE® database were reviewed. From these, potential SCCUP cases were identified based on a diagnosis of CUP as determined by the clinical provider ordering CGP testing. Cases were then centrally evaluated to confirm SCC differentiation and to exclude cases for which a tumor origin was evident or likely evident based on histology, accompanying pathology reports, site of sample procurement, and clinical information provided. Histology and immunohistochemistry findings were directly reviewed when available. When combined, these parameters confirmed that (i) the submitted sample was obtained from a metastatic carcinoma site, (ii) the sample had SCC histology, and (iii) at the time of sequencing, a primary tumor site was not known by the ordering provider or evident based on review of available documentation. Patient age, biological sex, and site of specimen collection were extracted from accompanying pathology reports.

## Comprehensive genomic profiling
CGP was carried out at a College of American Pathologists-accredited and Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–certified laboratory (Foundation Medicine, Inc. Cambridge, MA). In brief, a minimum of 50 ng of DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin embedded blocks, and next generation sequencing was carried out using hybridization-capture of adapter-ligated DNA libraries via the Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA), as previously described. , , , The assay utilized in this study [FoundationOne® CDx (F1CDx)] interrogates the entire coding region of 324 cancer-related genes and additional select introns from 34 genes commonly rearranged in cancer. Sequencing data were processed using a customized computational analysis pipeline that detects all classes of GAs, including base substitutions, short insertions and deletions (indels), copy number alterations (amplifications and homozygous deletions), and genomic rearrangements (including gene fusions). TMB was determined by counting somatic alterations on 0.8 megabase (Mb) pairs of targeted sequence and was reported as mutations/Mb. Microsatellite instability (MSI) status was determined using >2000 mononucleotide repeat loci. As self-reported race was not available, predominant patient ancestry was determined for each specimen using a custom single nucleotide polymorphism –based classifier, as previously described. Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) was measured by HRDsig using a broad set of genome-wide copy number features, as previously described. Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) trinucleotide single base pair substitution signatures were determined using a non-negative matrix factorization method, as previously described. Viral signatures were determined by competitive alignment of de novo assembled off-target sequence reads by BLASTn to the National Center for Biotechnology Information database of known viral nucleotide sequences, as previously described. Tumor cell PD-L1 expression was determined by immunohistochemistry (Dako 22C3, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) carried out and reported by Foundation Medicine, with tumor proportion score (TPS) of 0% classified as negative, 1%-49% as low expression, and ≥50% as high expression.

## Statistical analyses
Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact testing and continuous variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U testing. Two-sided P values were calculated for each comparison and then adjusted for multiple hypothesis correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Statistical significance was set at a false discovery rate–corrected alpha of <0.05. Statistics, computation, and plotting were carried out using Python 2.7.18 (Python Software Foundation) and R 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

## Results

## SCCUP cases
From a series of >750 000 cases submitted for routine CGP evaluation, 443 cases of SCCUP were identified. Clinical characteristics of SCCUP cases are shown in . The most common presentation sites included lymph nodes (41.1%), liver (15.6%), soft tissue (15.1%), bone (7.2%), and brain (4.7%). The most frequently involved lymph node sites were inguinal (14.4%), axillary (9.0%), non-head and neck SCC (non-HNSCC) supraclavicular (4.5%), pelvic (3.8%), and retroperitoneal (3.2%). Slightly more than half of cases were male (53%), with a median age at diagnosis of 66 years. Most cases had genomic ancestry consistent with European descent (79.9%), whereas 10.4%, 6.8%, 2.0%, and 0.9% had African, Admixed American, East Asian, and South Asian ancestry, respectively.
 

## Comprehensive genomic profiling results
A summary of genomic findings is shown in . A mean of 6.5 GAs were observed per case, with 13 cases in the cohort harboring zero pathogenic GAs. displays detailed results of CGP across the entire cohort. The most frequently altered genes were TP53 (62.5%), CDKN2A (37.0%), PIK3CA (27.3%), CDKN2B (19.6%), and KMT2D (18.3%). The most frequently altered genes potentially targetable in biomarker-driven trials included PTEN (15.1%), MTAP (13.1%), KRAS (10.4%), and NOTCH1 (8.4%), in addition to PIK3CA. EGFR alterations were identified in 5.0% of cases, with 4.1% harboring EGFR amplifications and 0.9% harboring short-variant mutations. Less than 0.1% of cases had EGFR mutations known to be targetable based on approvals in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Among all SCCUP cases, 9 (2.0%) had MSI-H status; 150 (33.9%) had a TMB ≥10, whereas 87 (19.6%) had a TMB ≥20. Among 204 cases with available PD-L1 testing, 80 (39.2%) had a TPS score of 1%-49% (low-positive), while 61 (29.9%) had a score of ≥50% (high-positive).
 An HRD signature was found in 4.8% of tested cases. Identified HRD-associated alterations included ATM (2.9%), BRCA1 (2.7%), BRCA2 (3.4%), and RAD21 (4.0%). The most frequently observed COSMIC trinucleotide genomic signature was apolipoprotein B mRNA editing catalytic polypeptide-like (APOBEC), found in 10.6% of cases. Meanwhile, 8.4% of SCCUP cases had a UV damage signature, suggesting a likely cutaneous primary, and 2.5% had a tobacco-associated signature. Evidence of HPV infection via detection of high-risk HPV reads was seen in 23.3% of cases. Four (0.9%) cases harbored TMPRSS2 fusions, suggesting potential occult prostate primaries. 

## SCCUPs with liver presentation
As liver metastases (LMs) are known to be associated with poor prognosis and are also a common site of metastasis in CUP, , we evaluated molecular characteristics among cases presenting with liver involvement ( ). Sixty-nine of 443 SCCUP cases (15.6%) in the cohort presented with LMs. Compared with cases presenting with involvement at sites outside of the liver, those with LMs had significantly fewer GAs per tumor (5.10 versus 6.71, P = 0.005). They also had significantly lower TMB (mean 4.1 versus 14.1, P < 0.0001), with a TMB ≥10 found in 8.7% of cases with LMs versus 38.0% of cases without LMs (odds ratio [OR] 0.16, P < 0.0001). TMB ≥20 was found in 1.4% of cases with LMs versus 23.0% of cases without LMs (OR 0.05, P < 0.0001). No significant differences were seen in PD-L1 expression or frequency of MSI-H status. A non-statistically significant trend toward higher frequency of KRAS alterations in those with versus without LMs was also observed (21.7% versus 8.3%, P = 0.07). Among the 15 SCCUP cases with LMs harboring KRAS alterations, most were G12D (n = 12); none were G12C.
 Despite the lower frequency of GAs per tumor and lower TMB observed in SCCUPs presenting in the liver compared with those presenting outside of the liver, ∼36.2% still harbored alterations that could potentially guide targeted therapy decisions. For instance, a 62-year-old female presenting with SCCUP in the liver had CGP results notable for homozygous deletions of CDKN2A/B and MTAP genes on chromosome 9. Amplifications of EPHB4 and MDM2 were also present along with mutations in BRIP1 and PIK3CA. This patient would be potentially eligible for a PRMT5 inhibitor clinical trial targeting MTAP-deleted, advanced solid tumors ( A and B).
 

## SCCUPs with inguinal, pelvic, and retroperitoneal lymph node involvement
Ninety-seven of 443 SCCUP cases (21.9%) presented with inguinal, pelvic, or retroperitoneal (I/P/RP) involvement ( ). These cases are often suspected to have genitourinary or gynecologic primaries and may therefore have unique genomic features. Compared with SCCUP cases presenting at other sites, those presenting with I/P/RP involvement were more likely to be female (63.9% versus 41.9%, P = 0.001). Furthermore, those with I/P/RP involvement were less likely to have alterations in CDKN2A (19.6% versus 41.9%, P = 0.002), KRAS (2.1% versus 12.7%, P = 0.02), and TP53 (37.1% versus 69.7%, P < 0.0001). I/P/RP cases were significantly more likely to have APOBEC genomic signatures (21.6% versus 7.5%, P < 0.0001) and evidence of HPV infection (54.6% versus 14.4%, P < 0.0001). Two cases presenting with I/P/RP involvement harbored alterations in TMPRSS2, suggesting potential occult prostate primary. 
More than half (54.6%) of I/P/RP SCCUP cases had at least one finding on CGP that could potentially guide targeted therapy decisions, including consideration for treatment in biomarker-driven trials. For instance, a 78-year-old female included in this cohort presented with a retroperitoneal mass confirmed to be SCC on biopsy ( C and D). CGP from the mass showed homozygous deletion of CKDN2A/B and MTAP, and a missense mutation in FGFR3. The FGFR3 mutation suggests that this tumor may have originated in the urothelium. Erdafitinib is an FGFR inhibitor approved by the Food and Drug Administration for previously treated, metastatic urothelial carcinoma with susceptible FGFR3 alterations. , Although there are limited data supporting use of erdafitinib in tumors with SCC histology including SCCUP, results from an ongoing phase II study demonstrated promising activity in a tumor-agnostic setting, including in patients with CUP, HNSCC, and lung SCC. This patient would also potentially be eligible for a clinical trial evaluating a PRMT5 inhibitor targeting MTAP-deleted, advanced solid tumors.

## Discussion
Unfavorable CUPs have a dismal prognosis, with median survival of well under 1 year. The recently reported CUPISCO trial demonstrated an improvement in PFS in patients with unfavorable, non-squamous CUP treated with MGT compared with continued platinum-based chemotherapy after an initial chemotherapy induction. This represents the first randomized trial to demonstrate benefit of MGT for treatment of CUPs and highlights the importance of early CGP in this population.
The benefit of MGT for treatment of SCCUPs remains unclear, as this subset was excluded from the CUPISCO trial. Furthermore, the molecular landscape and frequency of potentially targetable molecular alterations by MGT in SCCUP remains poorly defined, as prior studies describing molecular characteristics of CUPs either included limited numbers of SCCUP cases or excluded SCCUPs altogether. , , , , , For instance, a prior report evaluating CGP results among CUP cases identified from the FoundationCORE® database included only 8 SCCUP cases among 200 total CUP cases, making it difficult to draw meaningful conclusions from this population. To our knowledge, our study provides the first description of CGP results from a cohort composed entirely of patients with SCCUP, with a total of 443 cases included. Our results suggest significant opportunities to identify potential biomarker-based therapies and inform trial designs for patients with SCCUP using CGP.
As with prior studies reporting CGP results in CUPs, , , alterations in TP53, CDKN2A, and CDKN2B were frequently observed in our SCCUP cohort. However, several findings appear unique to our SCCUP cohort and deserve mention. First, alterations in PIK3CA and PTEN were observed relatively frequently in our cohort, in 27.3.% and 15.1% of patients, respectively. In contrast, these alterations have been reported in <10% of non-squamous CUPs. , This finding is of interest given the potential use of targeted therapies in this setting. For instance, the CUPISCO trial incorporated an arm using the PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitor ipatasertib plus paclitaxel for patients with alterations in PIK3CA, PTEN, or AKT. Of note, 11 of 121 patients with PIK3CA alterations in our SCCUP cohort harbored more than one pathogenic PIK3CA short-variant alteration, a finding that has been associated with increased sensitivity to targeted pathway inhibition. 
In contrast, alterations meeting criteria for treatment within several other targeted therapy arms in CUPISCO (BRAF V600E, NTRK fusion or ROS1 rearrangement, ALK fusion or RET rearrangement, ERBB2 alterations) were rare or nonexistent in our SCCUP cohort. For example, we observed no BRAF alterations, while studies of non-squamous CUPs have reported BRAF V600E alterations in ∼3%. , 
Evidence of homologous repair deficiency was observed in ∼5% of SCCUP cases in our cohort, with BRCA1 and BRCA2 alterations found in 2.7% and 3.4%, respectively, suggesting a potential vulnerability to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor therapy. This is similar to reported frequency of BRCA1/2 alterations in SCC cancers with known primaries, with one study reporting these alterations in ∼10%, 5%, and 4% of cases of cutaneous SCC, esophageal SCC, and HNSCC, respectively. Although PARP inhibitors are approved for use in specific tumor types including breast, prostate, ovarian, and pancreatic cancers, tumor-agnostic and/or basket trials suggest activity in other tumor types harboring evidence of HRD, especially in those with BRCA alterations. For instance, early data from the tumor-agnostic KEYLYNK-007 study (NCT04123366) evaluating olaparib plus pembrolizumab in cancers with evidence of HRD demonstrated promising activity, with responses seen in a variety of tumor types including those with SCC histology. Meanwhile, a tumor-agnostic basket study demonstrated activity of olaparib monotherapy in patients with BRCA alterations. Preclinical and early clinical studies show promise of PARP inhibitors in tumors with SCC histology specifically. , 
Biomarkers predictive of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors were enriched in our SCCUP cohort. For instance, 22.8% had TMB of ≥16 mutations/Mb, which was the cut-off used for treatment with atezolizumab monotherapy in the CUPISCO trial. In contrast, TMB of ≥16 mutations/Mb has been reported in only ∼10% of non-squamous CUPs. , , Meanwhile, 33.9% of SCCUP cases in our cohort had a TMB of ≥10 mutations/Mb, and would therefore meet the Food and Drug Administration tumor-agnostic indication for therapy with pembrolizumab. SCCUP cases in our cohort also had high levels of PD-L1 expression compared with reports from non-squamous CUP cohorts, , with 69% having a PD-L1 TPS ≥1%, and 30% having a PD-L1 TPS ≥50%. These findings corroborate results from a prior study showing enriched PD-L1 expression in patients with SCCUP presenting with cancer in the head and neck area in comparison with HNSCC cases with known primary tumor origin. Although the ability of PD-L1 to predict response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy is complex and varies by cancer type, it is an established predictive biomarker in SCC tumors including HNSCC and cervical cancer. , 
A phase II, single-arm trial recently evaluated the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab for treatment of unfavorable CUP relapsed or refractory to platinum-based chemotherapy (NCT04131621). In this very difficult-to-treat population, ORR was 16.2% and median PFS was 2.5 months. Importantly, high TMB (≥10 mutations/Mb) was associated with superior response to therapy, with a reported ORR of 60% versus 7.7% and a median PFS of 18.3 versus 2.4 months in those with high versus low TMB, respectively. While this study included only five patients with SCCUP, it suggests that TMB may be an important biomarker associated with immune checkpoint inhibitor response in the CUP population. Two additional single-arm trials evaluating anti-PD-1 monotherapy in relapsed or refractory CUP also noted enhanced response in those with high TMB and/or positive PD-L1 expression. , 
Of note, patients with SCCUP presenting with liver involvement were less likely to have high TMB compared with SCCUP involving other sites. This fits with reports from other cancer types demonstrating that LMs portend inferior response to immune checkpoint inhibitors. , Nevertheless, ∼9% of SCCUP cases with liver involvement still had a TMB ≥10. Further investigation of response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy in this population is warranted.
Novel biomarker-directed agents continue to emerge, and opportunities to employ targeted therapies for biomarkers beyond those explicitly included in CUPISCO arms are anticipated to expand. For instance, 10.3% of patients with SCCUP in our cohort harbored KRAS alterations. While not approved for tumor-agnostic indications or for tumors with SCC histology, KRAS G12C inhibitors have received accelerated approval for use in specific disease indications, and inhibitors targeting other KRAS alterations are under development. , In addition, 13.1% of patients with SCCUP in our cohort harbored MTAP alterations. Preliminary results from early-phase trials suggest activity of PRMT5 inhibitors in cancers with MTAP deletion, , and trials evaluating this target are ongoing (NCT05245500, NCT05275478, NCT05732831).
In addition to identifying biomarkers with potential treatment indications, CGP in SCCUP may provide insight into underlying tumor origins. For example, 8.4% of SCCUP cases exhibited UV damage signatures. This finding is strongly suggestive of an underlying cutaneous primary. , Furthermore, 2.5% had a tobacco mutational signature pointing to a tobacco-related primary, whereas 23.3% had evidence of HPV infection. While rare, four cases in our cohort harbored alterations in TMPRSS2, suggesting potential occult prostate primary tumors. 
Our study has several limitations. First, we had access to limited clinical data. A diagnosis of CUP was based upon the diagnosis entered by the provider submitting tissue for CGP, and the lack of evident features pointing to a specific tumor of origin. Thus, it is possible that cases submitted as CUP had incomplete diagnostic evaluations, and that diagnostic testing carried out after submission for CGP may have clarified a tumor of origin. Next, CGP was assessed using a single biopsy site at a single timepoint for each patient, thereby preventing assessment of tumor heterogeneity and evolution. Additionally, we did not have access to treatment or outcome data for patients in our cohort, preventing exploration of utilization of or response to biomarker-directed therapies. Thus, while this study provides insight into the frequency of biomarkers potentially targetable based on tumor-agnostic indications or in tumor-agnostic clinical trials, it does not contribute to understanding of clinical activity of targeted agents in this setting. Use of molecular targeted therapies outside of tumor-agnostic indications in SCCUP requires further investigation. The majority of patients in our cohort had European ancestry based on CGP assessment, and we did not have information regarding self-reported patient race or ethnicity to better understand how well our cohort reflects broader populations.
Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insight into the genomic characteristics of SCCUPs. Given the frequency of potentially targetable alterations and biomarkers observed, especially for immune checkpoint inhibitors, this study also supports the value of CGP in SCCUPs. Further evaluation of the utility of MGT in SCCUPs is warranted.
