## Abstract
TKIs, or tyrosine kinase inhibitors, are pharmaceutical agents used in cancer treatment because they can block or inhibit enzymes called tyrosine kinases. These enzymes are essential in regulating cell processes such as growth, differentiation, metabolism, and survival. Multi-TKIs target several tyrosine kinases at once, improving therapeutic outcomes while affecting multiple signaling pathways simultaneously. However, a major challenge with multi-TKIs is that they often impact normal cells that rely on the same pathways, creating a need to balance treatment effectiveness with manageable side effects. TKIs play a crucial role in immune system regulation by modifying cell activation and development, either enhancing or suppressing immune responses. They can influence various cells in both innate and adaptive immunity and act on immunological checkpoints that control T cell activity. Additionally, TKIs can be part of resistance mechanisms, requiring careful understanding of their effects. Gaining insight into how TKIs interact with the immune system enhances patient care and improves medication efficacy.

## Introduction

## Overview of tyrosine kinase inhibitors and their function in cancer treatment
Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, or TKIs, are drugs that are designed to treat cancer. More specifically, these drugs block or inhibit certain enzymes called tyrosine kinases. These enzymes are required for managing cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, metabolism, and survival [ ]. Tyrosine kinases influence protein activity and are essential for cell signaling because they transfer phosphate groups from ATP to tyrosine residues on proteins [ ]. They can be categorized into two main types. First, receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), located on the cell membrane, bind ligands such as growth factors, thereby activating their intracellular kinase domains. Some famous ones are EGFR, HER2, VEGFR, and PDGFR [ ]. The second category includes Non-receptor tyrosine kinases (NRTKs), which are intracellular signaling enzymes that are not receptors. BCR-ABL, which is associated with chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), is a typical example [ ]. Based on the chemical structure and mechanism of action, TKIs could be broadly divided into two categories: small-molecular TKIs, which are often administered orally, and those that target particular tyrosine kinases, which are within the first category. This group of medications includes imatinib, sunitinib, erlotinib, gefitinib, and crizotinib [ ]. The second group also includes monoclonal antibodies targeting tyrosine kinases, such as trastuzumab for HER2-positive tumors. They can block the activity of receptor tyrosine kinases, even if they are not strictly TKIs [ ]. Multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors, often referred to as multi-TKIs, are a class of drugs that interfere with the action of multiple tyrosine kinases through inhibition. Compared with drugs that act through a single kinase, these drugs can enhance therapeutic outcomes by concurrently targeting various signaling pathways, especially in malignancies with a complex molecular profile [ , ]. On the other hand, multi-TKIs will also have unwanted effects on normal, healthy cells that depend on such pathways. One of the main challenges is striking a balance between treatment effectiveness and tolerable side effects [ ].

## Importance of understanding their immunomodulatory effects
It is essential to consider the immunomodulatory action of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) for several reasons, especially in cases where cancer, autoimmune diseases, or the immune system regulation as a whole is needed [ , ]. By modulating the tumor microenvironment, several TKIs may enhance the immune response. They may help immune effector cells, such as dendritic cells and T lymphocytes, to target the tumor more effectively. But in certain situations, they may also suppress immune responses, maintaining a delicate balance between activating an anti-tumor immune response and preventing an overactive immune response [ , ].
The onset of autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, or colitis may be triggered or worsened by the use of TKIs. This occurs through changes in immune cell signaling pathways (such as JAK-STAT or PI3K-AKT), which lead to the T cell co-receptor with a specific target [ ]. In general terms, depending on the medicine and its target, TKIs may enhance inflammation, suppress it, or have no effect at all. For example, some processes may increase the inflammatory response, while others use medications that block VEGF signaling. This duality must be carefully monitored throughout the treatment cycle to prevent adverse outcomes [ , ]. Individual immune responses to TKIs can depend on genetic polymorphisms, tumor type, or other medical conditions. Personalized cancer treatments are made possible by a thorough understanding of these effects, allowing TKIs to be tailored to each patient’s unique immune profile [ , ].
TKIs have wide-ranging immunomodulatory effects, affecting several physiological functions. Their role, especially in cancer immunotherapy and inflammatory regulation, needs careful consideration to avoid adverse effects. Particularly in the age of combination therapy and personalized medicine, a thorough understanding of how TKIs interact with the immune system improves patient care and the effectiveness of these medications [ ]. In this study, we examine how TKIs, specifically multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors, interact with the immune system in cancer treatment.

## Effects on immune cell populations

## Selumetinib
Selumetinib, also known as Koselugo, is a selective inhibitor of MEK1/2 enzymes (mitogen-activated protein kinase kinases 1 and 2). MEK1 and MEK2 kinases activate ERK1/2 by phosphorylating them. Activated ERK, in turn, translocates into the nucleus and regulates the transcription of genes responsible for differentiation, apoptosis, and cell cycle progression [ ]. Selumetinib inhibits downstream pathways that are frequently hyperactivated in several malignancies, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and neurofibromatosis type 1, by blocking MEK1/2 activity in the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK pathway [ ]. Consequential activation of the MAPK/ERK pathway occurs in a variety of malignancies due to upstream component mutations, such as Ras, or to overexpression of hyperactive growth factor receptors [ ]. By inhibiting this cascade, selumetinib promotes apoptosis, cell-cycle arrest, and inhibition of tumor growth [ ]. MEK inhibition may be combined with PI3K/Akt signaling and may enhance anti-tumor activity, since the MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways are interconnected, and often both pathways contribute to survival and proliferation in cancer [ ].
While the target of selumetinib is primarily cancer cells, the drug also affects immune cells and the immunological microenvironment. Studies have shown that MEK inhibition modifies the composition of immune cells infiltrating tumors. MEK inhibition promotes the functionality of CD8 + cytotoxic T cells by upregulating the production of T cell activation molecules such as granzyme B and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [ ]. These alterations may fortify the immune system’s capability to fight the tumor. In other contexts, MEK inhibition has immunosuppressive activity by promoting regulatory T cells (Tregs), which could interfere with the immune response as a whole [ ].
MEK inhibition has been shown to affect dendritic cells in the context of innate immunity. DCs play an essential role in inducing immune responses, particularly in presenting antigens to T cells. Various studies have been performed that showed selumetinib caused some substantial changes in maturation and function of dendritic cells by inhibition of surface marker expression and cytokine secretion, which may reduce T-cell activation. The exact impact may again depend on tumor type and microenvironment; MEK inhibitors, such as selumetinib, could alter DC maturation and antigen presentation [ , ]. MDSCs are a heterogeneous population of immune cells that facilitate tumor growth by suppressing T cell responses. The available data indicate that MEK inhibition may reduce the immunosuppressive effects of MDSCs on anti-tumor immunity by lowering their density in specific cancers [ ]. MEK inhibitors may affect macrophage polarization. Selumetinib may shift macrophage phenotype in certain malignancies from M2 to M1, which is associated with increased inflammation and immune activation against the tumor. This effect may enhance anti-tumor immunity [ , ].
Recent data have suggested that MEK inhibitors can affect the expression of immunological checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1. Some studies indicate that selumetinib reduces PD-L1 expression in tumour cells, potentially enhancing the efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors, including anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [ – ].
The administration of selumetinib in combination with other approaches has shown promise and has been tested in clinical trials in a variety of malignancies, including melanoma, thyroid cancer, and NSCLC. While the overall immunomodulatory effects remain under clinical investigation, the use of selumetinib to inhibit the MAPK pathway has been shown to potentiate the effects of immunotherapies in specific malignancies by altering immune cell dynamics [ – ].

## Erdafitinib
The drug, also known as Balversa, is used to treat metastatic urothelial cancer and is described as a pan-FGFR kinase inhibitor. By selectively inhibiting FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, and FGFR4, erdafitinib results in reduced activation of the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways, which are critical for the survival, proliferation, and angiogenesis of tumor cells. Erefitinib is active against other kinases, including FLT4, KIT, VEGFR2, RET, CSF1R, and PDGFRA. This breadth of activity could impart additional effects both on tumor biology and on the immune system [ , ].
Erdafitinib, by inhibiting FGFR signaling, exerts multiple effects on immune cells in the tumor microenvironment; however, most of these effects have only been partially investigated in ongoing clinical research. In certain malignancies, dysregulated FGFR signaling may promote immune suppression by either attracting immune cells that are ineffective at killing tumor cells or by creating an immune-suppressive microenvironment. Erdafitinib, upon FGFR inhibition, may help reduce immunosuppressive effects in the TME and further increase immune cell numbers [ ].
However, most of the time, the tumor microenvironment does not allow activation of cytotoxic T cells, which are crucial for immune-mediated tumor killing. Erdafitinib treatment, by altering the TME, may help bypass some of these suppressive mechanisms and thus improve, to some extent, T cell infiltration and function. Some data indicate that FGFR targeting can reduce the number of immune-suppressive cells (e.g., Tregs or MDSCs) within the TME. This reduction may enhance the overall antitumor immune response [ – ].
There is little data on innate immunity and its relationship to the effects of Erdafitinib on dendritic cells. Still, it has been shown that combining it with immunotherapy increases dendritic cell activation. In this regard, Tumor-Associated macrophages represent a significant portion of the tumor microenvironment and can accelerate tumor growth through pro-inflammatory signals. Some studies have suggested that FGFR inhibitors might alter TAM polarization, reducing their protumor M2 phenotype and increasing their anti-tumor M1 phenotype. This shift might strengthen the immune barrier against the malignancy [ , ].
Immune checkpoint inhibitors are also being studied in combination, such as with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, with FGFR inhibitors like Erdafitinib. Erdafitinib could facilitate immune checkpoint blockade by altering the tumor microenvironment, thereby boosting the immune response [ , , ].

## Pexidartinib
The oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor pexidartinib is commercially known as Turalio. It primarily inhibits the colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor(CSF1R), which is essential for macrophage survival, proliferation, and differentiation. Symptomatic tenosynovial giant cell tumors(TGCT) are indicative of their use. Pexidartinib decreases the number and activity of macrophages in the tumor microenvironment by disrupting CSF1R signaling. Pexidartinib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that, besides CSF1R, also inhibits KIT and FLT3 tyrosine kinase receptors, which play a role in tumor growth, cell proliferation, and survival [ ].
The most crucial action of Pexidartinib is the decrease in the number of macrophages and other myeloid cells within the tumor microenvironment. This is mediated through CSF-1R-expressing cells, with TAMs as a significant target, as well as monocytes and MDSCs. Many of these immune cells frequently perform tumor-promoting activities in addition to suppressing anti-tumor immune responses, including accelerating tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [ ]. Both protumoral and anti-tumoral activities have been ascribed to macrophages, but in lesions such as the TGCT, they are generally pro-tumorigenic and promote tumor growth and tissue remodeling. A decrease in their numbers thus potentially limits tumor growth [ ]. Pexidartinib can shift macrophage polarization beyond mere reduction. The M2 state of macrophages, which includes those that help in tissue healing, angiogenesis, and immune suppression, is relatively common in the tumor microenvironment. Though the exact effect on polarization in vivo is still under investigation, CSF1R inhibition may change the phenotype of macrophages, and their movement toward an M1 (anti-tumor) state is possible [ , ].
In addition to this, pexidartinib affects DC development and function. Inhibition of CSF1R and FLT3 signaling was shown to decrease the frequency of circulating DC subsets and impair cytokine generation, possibly limiting their ability to activate T cells [ ].
It can modulate the TME by reducing TAMs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, decreasing immunosuppressive effects, and enhancing the activity of cytotoxic T cells and other immune effector cells, leading to a more effective anti-tumor immune response. Treatment with pexidartinib in studies caused changes in the T cell population within the TME. In particular, this corresponds to a post-treatment decrease in the absolute count of T cells, including CD8 + cytotoxic T cells and regulatory T cells. The key issue here is that the ratio of CD8 + T cells to Tregs increases, thereby providing a more favorable immune environment for anti-tumor activity. Treatment with pexidartinib reduces CCL22 levels, a chemokine that chemoattracts Tregs to the tumor site, thereby creating a less immunosuppressive environment that favors increased activity of CD8 + T cells [ , , ].
Synergistic effects with immune checkpoint inhibitors: Pexidartinib is promising in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as PDL-1 antibodies. Combination therapy, by further reducing immune suppressive mechanisms in the TME, enhances anti-tumour efficacy and consequently improves T cell responses against tumors [ – ].

## Lorlatinib
Lorbrena is another oral multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor. It was developed for the treatment of ALK-positive NSCLC by targeting anaplastic lymphoma kinase and C-ros oncogene one kinase. In cancers driven by ALK or ROS1, lorlatinib potently blocks tumor growth and induces apoptosis by inhibiting the abovementioned mechanisms. Moreover, it has been shown that this drug can inhibit several other kinases, including TYK1, FER, and TRK receptors, which could further enhance its anti-tumor activity. Further studies also reveal that Lorlatinib suppresses the VEGF and TGF-β pathways, which decreases fibrosis and angiogenesis in the tumor microenvironment [ , ].
Lorlatinib affects the immune cell population present within the TME. Lorlatinib can modestly affect macrophage counts. Even though the total amount of these cells might not change, lorlatinib could influence the functional state or polarization of macrophages. Through altering other immune cells, such as neutrophils, lorlatinib might indirectly affect macrophage behavior. TANs, often associated with an immunosuppressive environment in tumor development, are inhibited by lorlatinib. Lorlatinib, by reducing neutrophil infiltration, may promote the immunological microenvironment and enhance macrophage anti-tumor function [ ]. According to research, ALK/ROS1 inhibition may enhance dendritic cell activity in the TME, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and effector T cell activation [ ].
Although studies on the direct effects of lorlatinib on T-cell populations and function are limited, lorlatinib may indirectly affect T-cells by modulating the immune system at the tumor site [ ]. However, further research is required to completely understand how it affects T cells in different cancer scenarios. However, immune evasion tactics that inhibit T cell function are frequently seen in ALK-based malignancies. Lorlatinib’s impact on ALK may improve T cell immunity by lessening immunological suppression linked to tumors [ ].
As mentioned above, neutrophils often facilitate the recruitment and function of Tregs and can create an immune-suppressive niche for tumor development. Lorlatinib might indirectly change the dynamics of Tregs in the TME and possibly reduce their suppressive impact on effector T cells by reducing neutrophil counts and the immune-suppressive functions they have been associated with [ ].
Preclinical evidence has shown that lorlatinib enhances the sensitivity of immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly those targeting PD-1. Lorlatinib’s ability to enhance T cell activation suggests that it can be combined with other immunotherapeutic approaches. For example, lorlatinib could help bypass some forms of resistance observed in tumors with high densities of myeloid-derived suppressor cells when given in combination with PD-1 inhibitors [ , ].

## Dacomitinib
This oral medication, dacomitinib, inhibits all EGFR family tyrosine kinases. It acts upon EGFR/HER1, HER2, and HER4. It is primarily used to treat NSCLC with specific EGFR mutations. It suppresses several key signal transduction pathways, which include EGFR signaling, the PLK1-FOXM1 pathway, Aurora kinase B, and Survivin2. This prevents tumor growth and proliferation of cancerous cells and may increase chemosensitivity in certain types of cancer [ , ].
Dacomitinib, a pan-HER TKI, also blocks Syk and Src kinases in macrophages and T cells. Syk and Src kinases must be present during the regulation of innate and adaptive immune responses; medicines targeting these enzymes have been studied as therapies for inflammation and autoimmune diseases [ , ]. Dacomitinib blocks Src kinases, especially lymphocyte-specific tyrosine kinase (Lck), but increases MHC complexes in tumor cells. Thus, dacomitinib could prevent activation of immune system cells [ , ]. Research determined the immunoregulatory effects of fulvestrant and dacomitinib, a therapy that recently demonstrated anticancer synergy in immunodeficient lung tumor models involving KRAS-mutant adenocarcinoma [ ]. In addition, investigators assessed the effects of fulvestrant and dacomitinib on murine bone marrow-derived macrophages and CD8 + T cells, as well as the combination’s effectiveness in vivo, using the KRAS-mutant syngeneic lung cancer model FVBW-17. While this combination effectively suppressed FVBW-17 cell growth, it had unintended consequences for immune cells, reducing CD8 + T cell function and macrophage phagocytosis while increasing PD-1 expression. The results were mainly ascribed to dacomitinib, which reduced the expression of Src family kinases and Syk in immune cells. In a subcutaneous flank model, the combination induced an inflammatory tumor microenvironment characterized by increased numbers of myeloid cells, cancer-related macrophages, and CD8 + T cells, as well as elevated PD-1 expression in the spleen [ ]. In another study, dacomitinib blocked MAPK signaling while enhancing the immune response in A549 xenografts. Dacomitinib-treated xenografts showed substantial inhibition of the phagocytosis checkpoint stanniocalcin-1 (STC1) [ ].
Incubating tumor cells with dacomitinib plus IFN-γ resulted in higher surface levels of HLA-DR complexes than with IFN-γ stimulation alone. Initial treatment of cancer cells with dacomitinib increased the activation of HER-3-reactive CD4 T cells. This data shows that a pan-HER-family blocker might be used as an adjuvant for anticancer immunotherapy, as this medication enhances cancer detection via HER-3-reactive CD4 T cells [ ].

## Entrectinib
Entrectinib is an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting several cancer cell proliferation and survival pathways. The compound mainly inhibits TRK, ROS1, and ALK signaling by preventing their activation, suppressing tumor cell proliferation and growth, and inducing apoptosis. Because of its mechanism of action, it is effective in solid tumors containing NTRK gene fusions and non-small cell lung cancer with ROS1 mutations, as well as in tumors of the central nervous system [ , ].
Entrectinib selectively inhibits NLRP3 without affecting other inflammasomes through its interaction with NEK7 arginine 121. In vivo, entrectinib improves models of NLRP3 inflammasome-associated disorders in mice, therefore presenting as a promising drug for the treatment of inflammasome-associated disorders [ ].
Also, entrectinib therapy significantly enhanced the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in mice with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) models. RNA sequencing demonstrated that entrectinib inhibited neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor 1 (NTRK1) signaling in cancer cells, thereby enhancing complement C3 production, which in turn boosted T cell and myeloid cell infiltration and promoted M1-like macrophage polarization in the tumor. NTRK1 signaling has a function in regulating interactions among the cancer cells and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, providing a possible treatment option for addressing immunotherapy resistance in patients with NSCLC with NTRK1 wild-type [ ]. An examination found that entrectinib reduces plasma cells (BLIMP1/IRF4 Double-Positive B Cells) within B cell populations while having little impact on live CD19-positive, BLIMP1-, and IRF4-negative cells. Furthermore, entrectinib could decrease the total growth of CD19-positive B cells, as determined by thymidine incorporation [ ]. In a clinical study, 23% of patients who received entrectinib showed a reduction in neutrophil count due to suppression of NTRK, ROS1, and ALK [ ]. Furthermore, in another experiment, entrectinib-treated tumor cells exhibited reduced expression of inflammation-associated genes, including NFκB, ERK, AKT, VEGFR2, VEGF, Snail, and β-catenin [ ].

## Alectinib
Alectinib is a selective inhibitor of the anaplastic lymphoma kinase used in ALK-positive NSCLC treatments. It inhibits several critical signaling pathways, including but not limited to ALK, mTOR, STAT3, PI3K/Akt, and TOPK. Alectinib suppresses ALK signaling directly and, as such, reduces its downstream effectors critical for cell proliferation and survival. In addition, it controls STAT3 activation, promoting survival and cell proliferation. The complex mode of action targeting multiple pathways advances the therapeutic applicability of alectinib in resistant cancers [ , ].
A research group used three EML4-ALK lung cancer mouse models to examine immune system function following alectinib treatment. The cancerous cell groups (E1, E2, and E3) were grown orthotopically in the lungs of immunocompetent and immunocompromised mouse models and then treated with alectinib. The alectinib treatment affected all cell types; in immunocompetent mice, the orthotopic carcinoma showed a long-lasting reduction, whereas in immunocompromised mice, all cancer types proliferated after the TKI treatment. E2 tumors had a complete response to the alectinib treatment, whereas the E1 and E3 tumors developed residual malignancy within a short period of the treatment. Alectinib therapy also reduced the number of alveolar macrophages while increasing neutrophil content in E1 tumors. Alectinib treatment augmented IFNα and IFNγ levels, as well as transplant rejection and inflammation. Major cell expansion pathways were markedly increased in alectinib-treated cells, indicating a similar degree of growth suppression. Alectinib markedly increased CXCL10 and CCL5 levels in E2 and E3 cells, but had minimal effect on E1 cells. E2 tumors with complete response after alectinib treatment expressed higher levels of the T cell-attracting chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10, with more infiltration of CD8 + T cells. Together, the findings imply the importance of adaptive immunity in the persistence of TKI effects and show that the immune cell populations of the tumor microenvironment predict the response to alectinib treatment [ ].
Researchers evaluated alectinib’s impact on human monocyte-derived DCs (moDC), the strongest antigen-presenting cells, as well as its ability to reduce DC stimulation, migration, and cytokine secretion, all of which are critical regulators of T cell responses. Alectinib therapy did not affect CD14 or CD1a, and no significant changes were observed in the production of activation markers CD40, CD54, CD80, CD83, and CD86 in moDCs treated with alectinib after (TLR)-ligation. Antigen presentation remained intact, and CCR7 and CCL19 levels did not change significantly. However, alectinib increased IL-12 generation, suggesting it may enhance DC-driven T cell reactions [ ].
Scientists discovered that alectinib lowered the rate of expansion of B-cell populations [ ]. Studies have also shown that this drug reduces neutrophil counts. A Japanese surveillance trial found grade 1 neutropenia in 4.1% of patients, with dose-limiting grade 3 neutropenia in 1 patient. A systematic review found a modest rate of febrile neutropenia in alectinib-treated patients, with a reduction in neutrophil numbers being the most prevalent grade three side effect in 20% of patients [ – ].

## Cobimetinib
Cobimetinib is an oral MEK inhibitor of the mitogen-activated extracellular kinase pathway. It is mainly prescribed for the management of unresectable or metastatic melanoma harboring BRAF V600 mutations. It inhibits MEK1 and MEK2, disrupting the MAPK/ERK signaling cascade, which is essential in cell proliferation, survival, and tumor growth. Further downstream targets of this agent include EK activation, cell cycle progression, and the regulation of apoptosis. It enhances anti-tumor effects when combined with vemurafenib [ , ].
The study found that cobimetinib treatment increased CD8 + T cell populations in 79% of individuals with solid tumors, but a poor reaction rate of 19% limits its application. MHC I levels improved in 43% of individuals. Combining atezolizumab with cobimetinib led to a 50.0% increase in CD8 + T cell proportion and a high MHC I H-score [ ]. The study compared the effects of vemurafenib and cobimetinib on the tumor microenvironment and immune function in melanoma patients. Both treatments increased CD8 + and CD4 + TILs, but over time, CD4 + cells showed a decrease in CD45RO + cells, suggesting a majority of naïve T-cell population. T cell levels did not increase, but tumor migration was produced, suggesting tumor-infiltrating T cells represent neo-immunity against cancer [ ].
In another research on triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) individuals, biopsies from cobimetinib-treated patients were analyzed and shown a rise in CD8 + T cell invasion and PD-L1 production with therapy in the basal subtype [ ]. MEK/ERK blocker Cobimetinib resulted in dose-dependent cell shrinkage and death of 4T1 cells, enhanced the production of HMGB1 and ATP, and improved membrane exposure of calreticulin. ICD was achieved by inhibiting p-ERK expression and increasing procaspase-3 and − 8 activity. Cobimetinib enhanced survival in immunocompetent mice and increased the number of CD8 + T cells. This points out that cobimetinib is an ICD activator and an immunoregulator that can be employed for the treatment of TNBC through immunotherapy, thereby acting as a strong immunoregulator [ ].
Researchers found that DCs exposed to vemurafenib or vemurafenib/cobimetinib change cytokine production and external marker expression patterns. When CD8 + and CD4 + T cells were antigen-specifically stimulated with these DCs or T2.A1 cells, they expressed fewer activation markers and secreted fewer cytokines. However, incubation with these inhibitors did not alter the avidity of CD8 + T cells. Vemurafenib and vemurafenib/cobimetinib fully inhibited helper T-cell-mediated induction of CD70, CD80, and CD86, but not CD25 on DC cells [ ]. Cobimetinib increases the production of costimulators (CD80, CD86) by BMDC (on bone marrow-derived (BM) mouse DC) if added with vemurafenib, and enhances MHCII expression if added alone. Cobimetinib entirely abolished the increased expression of IL-1β induced by vemurafenib. High concentrations of vemurafenib increased IL-12 levels, which were significantly reduced when combined with cobimetinib. Vemurafenib and cobimetinib both reduced IL-10 levels. Combined therapy with cobimetinib inhibits IL-1β generation in LPS-stimulated BMDC cells [ ].
Cobimetinib, a new adipocyte fatty acid-binding protein (A-FABP) blocker, effectively reduced the induction of the JNK/c-Jun signaling pathway in murine macrophages without generating evident cytotoxicity [ ]. In addition, A-FABP may increase the production of many inflammation-related genes in macrophages, particularly MCP-1, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [ ]. Cobimetinib decreases the number of B regulatory cells (Bregs) in vitro and in tumor-draining lymph nodes in a colorectal cancer model in vivo. Cobimetinib reduces the number of T2-MZP Bregs, B10 Bregs, and TIM1 + Bregs in mouse tumor-draining lymph nodes by disrupting chronic BCR signaling, while increasing functional B cell anti-cancer immune responses. MEK suppression does not impair anti-cancer humoral immunity, and B cells play an essential role in anti-tumor immunity during MEK blocker treatment. Treatment with cobimetinib is associated with increased T cell recruitment (CD8 + and CD4 + T cells) and improved response to anti-PD1 therapy. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MEK suppression may diminish Bregs while increasing anti-tumor B cell activity, thereby enhancing immune responses against tumors [ ]. In a case report, biopsy of a myeloma patient showed neutrophilic lobular panniculitis with vasculitis, termed erythema induratum [ ]. In a phase II clinical trial, individuals with stage III melanoma were given 12 weeks of vemurafenib and cobimetinib. Despite a few exceptions, pre- and post-treatment PD-L1 production were identical. Pathologic response was associated with increased CD8 + T cells and CD8 + central memory T cells. T cells showed a reduction in cell surface PD-L1 following cobimetinib and atezolizumab therapy. Numerous CD4 + T cell subgroups, such as naïve, effector, and central memory T cells, showed substantial therapeutic enhancement [ ].
In a phase I-b clinical trial (NCT01656642) with the combination of atezolizumab, cobimetinib, and vemurafenib in melanoma patients, the tumor CD8 + T cell population increased; this outcome could lead to improved immunotherapy response [ ]. Combining cobimetinib and atezolizumab could be an appropriate strategy for treating solid tumors, including melanoma, because MEK suppression could enhance immunotherapy effectiveness. Preliminary investigations have shown that MEK suppression could attract immune cells, thereby improving the efficacy of PD-L1 inhibitors [ , ].

## Afatinib
Afatinib is a critical agent for certain patients with EGFR mutation-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [ ]. It is a potent, irreversible inhibitor of the ErbB family of receptor tyrosine kinases, with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), HER2, and HER4 as preferred targets [ ]. Its mode of action involves covalent binding to the specific cysteine residues in the kinase domains of the receptors, thereby inhibiting tyrosine kinase activity [ ]. This inhibition results in reduced receptor numbers and auto- and transphosphorylation, thereby abolishing the signaling pathways that promote tumor cell growth and survival [ ].

## Immune enhancement vs. suppression mechanisms
The effects of afatinib on the tumor microenvironment (TME) underscore its role as a targeted EGFR inhibitor and demonstrate its capacity to modulate the TME in complex ways [ ]. Specifically, afatinib enhances immune cell infiltration into the TME, including T cells (CD3 + and CD8+), natural killer (NK) cells, and B cells, thereby bolstering the immune response against tumors [ – ]. Under conditions of low baseline PD-L1 expression or in EGFR-driven tumors with intact antigen presentation machinery, afatinib promotes immunologic anti-tumor immunity by increasing T-cell infiltration and enhancing T-cell activation***; this occurs through mechanisms such as the upregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I on tumor cells, which improves recognition of tumor antigens by CD8 + T cells and subsequently drives tumor cell lysis [ , ]. Preclinical models, including syngeneic mouse tumors, have shown that this MHC-I enhancement is mediated by reduced EGFR signaling, which alleviates transcriptional repression of antigen-processing genes and thereby fosters a more immunogenic TME [ , ].
However, under certain conditions, afatinib can also suppress immune responses, leading to contradictory effects that highlight the dual nature of TKI immunomodulation. For instance, in tumors with high proliferative demands or under prolonged exposure, afatinib induces the expression of immunomodulatory markers such as PD-L1, which can paradoxically increase T-cell numbers while rendering the cells functionally exhausted, thereby impairing overall anti-tumor efficacy [ ]. The underlying mechanism here involves EGFR inhibition, which activates the STAT3 pathway and transcriptionally upregulates PD-L1 and other checkpoint molecules, creating an immunosuppressive niche that counteracts the initial infiltration benefits [ ]. Another suppressive mechanism is afatinib’s off-target inhibition of metabolic enzymes, such as carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase 2, aspartate transcarbamylase, and dihydroorotase (CAD), which disrupts de novo pyrimidine synthesis and directly blocks CD8 + T-cell proliferation [ ]; this effect is particularly pronounced in rapidly dividing immune cells during the early phases of combination therapy. These contradictory outcomes—enhancement via antigen presentation versus suppression via exhaustion or metabolic interference—arise from the context-dependent balance between direct EGFR blockade in tumor cells and indirect effects on immune metabolism, often influenced by tumor type, mutation status, and dosing regimen [ , ]. Critical analysis of studies reveals inconsistencies, such as robust T-cell activation in some models versus proliferation blocks in others, potentially due to differences in tumor models (e.g., subcutaneous vs. orthotopic), afatinib dosing (low vs. high, affecting off-target effects), or immune checkpoint expression baselines; future research could involve temporal pharmacodynamics or single-cell RNA sequencing to map T-cell states and resolve these issues.
Regarding inflammation, afatinib’s effects vary with its targets and the inflammatory context, aligning with broader patterns observed with multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (MTKIs). In normal circumstances, afatinib can enhance inflammation by indirectly disrupting VEGF signaling through ErbB crosstalk with VEGFR pathways, thereby promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine release (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) and endothelial activation, similar to the effects seen with VEGFR or PDGFR inhibition [ – ]. This enhancement is evident in preclinical models of EGFR-mutant NSCLC, where afatinib increases vascular permeability and leukocyte recruitment, potentially exacerbating acute inflammatory responses but aiding immune surveillance [ ]. Conversely, afatinib suppresses inflammation through inhibition of EGFR-mediated downstream signaling, which reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-8 and CCL2 [ ]; this mirrors the anti-inflammatory actions of TKIs targeting Src or Flt3, as EGFR shares overlapping pathways with these kinases in myeloid cell activation [ ], including blockade of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages [ ]. In scenarios where afatinib exerts no significant impact on inflammation, this neutrality often stems from low expression of ErbB receptors in immune cells or compensatory activation of parallel pathways (e.g., HER3 heterodimerization), as observed in non-inflamed, EGFR-low tumor models where cytokine levels remain unchanged post-treatment [ , ]. These distinct effects—enhancement via vascular modulation, suppression via cytokine blockade, or neutrality due to pathway redundancy—require careful consideration in patient selection, with references emphasizing the need for biomarker-driven stratification.
Although some studies have investigated combination approaches that include afatinib, the immunomodulatory effects of such approaches have not been sufficiently established. The study by Yu et al. demonstrated that afatinib suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell proliferation and augments the efficacy of immunotherapy by upregulating PD-L1 expression via the STAT3 pathway, creating synergistic effects with anti-PD-1 treatments; however, this also underscores the need to time combinations to mitigate exhaustion risks [ ]. In contrast, a study using MC38 and LLC mouse tumor models found that simultaneous afatinib and anti-PD-1 combination therapy yielded only marginal improvements over sequential administration, potentially due to the aforementioned CAD-mediated T-cell suppression, which may narrow the therapeutic window [ ]. Critically, these findings reveal inconsistencies across studies: while some report robust T-cell activation, others highlight proliferation blocks, possibly attributable to differences in tumor models, afatinib dosing, or baseline immune checkpoint expression. Addressing these discrepancies could involve future research on temporal pharmacodynamics or single-cell RNA sequencing to map T-cell states, clarifying why similar EGFR inhibition yields variable outcomes.
Recent advances illuminate afatinib’s immunomodulatory potential and resistance-overcoming strategies. For instance, a 2024 preclinical study screened 50 TKIs and found afatinib uniquely enhances CAR-T cell efficacy by inhibiting the TCR-PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway, reducing glycolysis while boosting oxidative phosphorylation, and shifting T cells toward memory subsets (Tn, Tcm, Tscm) with lower exhaustion markers [ ]; in Nalm-6 leukemia mouse models, afatinib-pretreated CAR-T cells reduced tumor burden and prolonged survival (p < 0.05), suggesting a role in cellular immunotherapies to counter resistance via metabolic reprogramming [ ]. Another 2024 study on immune dynamics in EGFR-mutated NSCLC noted afatinib’s role in altering PD-L1 regulation and TME, with increased NK/NKT cells and decreased exhausted CD8 + T cells in combination settings [ ]. Early-phase clinical data from a phase I/Ib trial (2015–2018, updated analyses 2024) combining afatinib with pembrolizumab in advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC post-TKI progression reported an ORR of 20% (2 PRs), with PFS ranging 7–510 days; biomarkers like high baseline effector memory CD8 + T cells (p = 0.0023) and increased CD4/CD8 ratio (p = 0.042) predicted benefit, though no DLTs but 36% irAEs [ ]. A phase II trial (NCT03695510, 2019–2020, data 2022–2024) of afatinib + pembrolizumab in platinum-refractory R/M HNSCC achieved ORR 41.4% (95% CI 23.5%−61.1%), mPFS 4.1 months (95% CI 1.9–6.3), mOS 8.9 months (95% CI 4.1–10.8), with EGFR amplification predicting 100% ORR (p = 0.07) and MTAP loss associating with poorer outcomes (HR 4.2 for PFS/OS, p = 0.014/0.015); paired biopsies showed upregulated antigen presentation and NK cytotoxicity pathways (FDR q < 0.1) [ ]. A 2024 randomized trial of afatinib + HAD-B1 (herbal) vs. afatinib alone in EGFR + NSCLC showed no significant DCR/PFS/OS differences but improved physical functioning (p = 0.0475) and fewer AESIs (p = 0.01), with preclinical hints at immune modulation via apoptosis/Hedgehog pathways [ ]. Breakthroughs in antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and bispecific antibodies include afatinib-payloaded derivatives bypassing resistance in 70% of xenografts via targeted delivery while preserving immunomodulation [ ]. Emerging biomarkers, such as MTAP alterations (suppressive, p = 0.046 for ORR) [ , , ], EGFR amplification (predictive) [ , ], PD-L1 dynamics (high TPS/CPS numerically better) [ , ], and circulating EGFR ctDNA (correlating with ORR, r = 0.68) [ ], show preliminary promise, though larger validations are needed.

## Axitinib

## Immune enhancement vs. suppression mechanisms
As an immuno-oncology agent, axitinib exerts selective influences on the immune ecosystem, facilitating the recruitment of CD8 + T cells and the depletion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). It diminishes the immunomodulatory capacity of MDSCs, steering them toward phenotypes that favor antigen presentation [ , ]. Additionally, axitinib curtails the population of M2 macrophages and drives polarization toward the M1 subtype, thereby amplifying anti-tumor immunity [ , ]. The agent further bolsters NK cell reactivity against tumor cells by upregulating NKG2D ligands [ ]. These actions are partly attributable to the suppression of STAT3 signaling and the mitigation of reactive oxygen species production [ ]. Nevertheless, the overall efficacy of axitinib may be tempered by the tumor microenvironment (TME) and emergent immune evasion strategies, underscoring the imperative for combinatorial therapeutic regimens [ – ].
In terms of immune response modulation, axitinib augments responses under conditions characterized by elevated MDSC presence or immunosuppressive mast cell activity, where it fosters T-cell infiltration and diminishes exhaustion indicators such as PD-1 and TIM-3 on CD8 + T cells [ ]. This augmentation is mediated by pathways, including STAT3 downregulation, which curtails MDSC accumulation, and vascular normalization, which promotes immune cell migration [ ]. On the other hand, suppression of immune responses manifests in scenarios involving elevated dosages or prolonged administration, wherein axitinib hampers T-cell proliferation and potentially elevates regulatory T cells (Tregs), culminating in paradoxical immunosuppressive outcomes. Such opposing effects stem from dosage-dependent equilibria: lower doses optimize vascular normalization and T-cell stimulation, whereas higher doses lead to excessive vessel pruning, thereby exacerbating hypoxia and augmenting Tregs [ ]. Moreover, axitinib functions as an immunosuppressive entity when released by pro-tumor mast cells or macrophages, though its targeted blockade mitigates this effect [ ].
With respect to inflammatory responses, axitinib’s effects vary with contextual factors and molecular targets, in line with patterns observed across multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (MTKIs). It may amplify inflammation by promoting M1 macrophage polarization and Th1-oriented responses through TLR3 upregulation, thereby supporting anti-tumor immune responses in RCC [ ]. This amplification parallels the inhibition of anti-inflammatory kinases, such as PDGFR, by MTKIs, leading to heightened cytokine secretion and leukocyte mobilization [ ]. In contrast, axitinib dampens inflammation via VEGFR blockade, diminishing vascular permeability and pro-inflammatory agents in models of interstitial cystitis and atherosclerotic plaques [ ]. This dampening effect is consistent with MTKI’s targeting of inflammation-associated kinases, such as Src, which attenuates NF-κB activation and the expression of cytokines such as IL-6 [ , ]. In instances of neutrality, axitinib exerts minimal influence when compensatory pathways involving ErbB or analogous signaling prevail, particularly in environments with low VEGFR expression in immune components [ ]. These varied outcomes—amplification through vascular modulation, dampening via cytokine inhibition, or neutrality owing to pathway redundancy—demand biomarker-guided patient stratification, as emphasized in supporting literature.
Combinatorial strategies incorporating axitinib have yielded notable successes, particularly when paired with pembrolizumab in advanced RCC [ ]. The integration of oncolytic viruses alongside axitinib and anti-PD-1 therapy has demonstrated encouraging results in patients with mucosal melanoma [ ]. Preclinical investigations reveal that axitinib, when combined with peptide-vectored vaccines, enhances antitumor efficacy by suppressing immunosuppressive mechanisms and invigorating T-cell responses [ ]. The synergistic interplay arising from concurrent intratumoral dendritic cell antigen presentation, axitinib-induced suppression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, and CTLA-4 inhibition further augments dendritic cell antigen-presenting capacity, nullifies the suppressive influence of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and instigates a systemic antitumor immune response, alongside enhanced survival in melanoma models [ ]. When combined with anti-PD-1 antibodies, axitinib exhibits synergistic effects on T-cell activation, culminating in improved therapeutic efficacy [ , ].
A 2025 preclinical inquiry into the co-delivery of axitinib and PD-L1 siRNA utilizing NGR-modified liposomes in RCC models illustrated synergistic outcomes, normalizing tumor vasculature (evidenced by diminished CD31 expression and elevated α-SMA coverage) and fortifying immune responses (manifested as a 5-fold surge in CD8 + T cells alongside reductions in Tregs and MDSCs), resulting in pronounced tumor suppression and metastasis abatement (p < 0.001) [ ]. The 2025 five-year follow-up of the KEYNOTE-426 trial (phase III) affirmed enduring advantages: an ORR of 60.6% (contrasted with 39.6% for sunitinib), mPFS of 15.7 months (HR 0.69, p < 0.001), and mOS of 47.2 months (HR 0.84) [ ]. A 2024 phase II neoadjuvant trial involving toripalimab and axitinib achieved an ORR of 45% and a pCR of 20%, with DFS not yet reached; biomarkers such as PD-1 + CD8 + cell density predicted responsiveness [ ]. A 2024 real-world analysis of avelumab combined with axitinib (post-approval phase) disclosed an ORR of 76% and mPFS of 24 months. Preliminary biomarkers include T cell infGEP for enhanced ORR, PFS, and OS (p < 0.0001), as well as PD-1 + CD8 + infiltration density and miRNAs such as miR-133a, although larger-scale validations remain essential [ ].
A critical examination of prior studies uncovers inconsistencies; for instance, axitinib bolsters T-cell infiltration in specific models yet impedes proliferation in others, attributable to variations in dosage, tumor models (e.g., MC38 vs. LLC1), or TME factors such as immunogenicity [ ]. Preclinical investigations exhibit robustness in syngeneic models but are constrained by translation to human contexts, where small sample sizes and heterogeneous patient profiles may amplify discrepancies [ , ]. To address these, future endeavors could leverage single-cell RNA sequencing to delineate Treg trajectories and temporal pharmacodynamics, thereby clarifying the variability in EGFR inhibition outcomes.

## Cabozantinib
Cabozantinib is a small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) approved for treating advanced malignancies, including renal cell carcinoma (RCC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and medullary thyroid cancer [ , ]. Its mechanism of action involves inhibiting multiple receptor tyrosine kinases critical for tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis, such as MET, VEGFR, AXL, RET, and FLT3. This multi-target approach not only directly suppresses tumor proliferation but also modulates the tumor microenvironment (TME) to influence immune dynamics [ ].
Immune Enhancement vs. Suppression Mechanisms:
Cabozantinib, a multi-target TKI, exhibits remarkable immunomodulatory effects in cancer treatment. In hepatocellular carcinoma models, it improves anti-tumor immunity by decreasing regulatory T cells (Tregs) and enhancing neutrophil infiltration, fostering a more effector-cell-dominant TME [ ]. Research by Scirocchi et al. indicates that cabozantinib enhances immunotherapeutic therapies for prostate cancer by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) through calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 release, while directly activating dendritic cells to improve antigen presentation [ ]. Cabozantinib alters tumor cell morphology, making them more susceptible to immune-mediated killing when combined with cancer vaccines, potentially via increased surface antigen expression [ ]. In advanced urothelial carcinoma, cabozantinib remodels disease-infiltrated myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, reducing their inhibitory functions [ , ]. These findings demonstrate that cabozantinib holds potential to enhance immunotherapy efficacy across a spectrum of cancers, particularly in combination settings.
Immune enhancement typically occurs under conditions of high baseline immunosuppression (e.g., elevated Tregs/MDSCs in MET/VEGFR-driven tumors) with short-term, low-dose exposure, where cabozantinib disrupts STAT3 signaling, reduces ROS, and promotes T-cell infiltration and NK cell activation via NKG2D upregulation [ , ]. Suppression may arise in hypoxic or prolonged high-dose contexts, where off-target effects elevate PD-L1 on tumor cells or inhibit T-cell proliferation through metabolic interference, creating contradictory effects due to dose-dependent pathway balances [ , ]. Critical analysis reveals inconsistencies across studies (e.g., Treg reduction in HCC models vs. potential PD-L1 induction in RCC), attributable to tumor heterogeneity, dosing variations, or preclinical vs. clinical translation; future directions include single-cell RNA sequencing to resolve dynamic immune states.
Cabozantinib’s inflammatory profile varies with targets and context, aligning with MTKI patterns. It enhances inflammation in immunogenic settings by inducing ICD and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., via VEGFR inhibition, promoting endothelial activation and IL-6/TNF-α release), as seen in prostate cancer models [ ]. Suppression occurs through macrophage modulation, attenuating lipopolysaccharide-induced responses and NF-κB activation, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α in OA-like models [ , ]. Neutral effects stem from compensatory pathways in low-MET/VEGFR immune compartments [ ]. These scenarios—enhancement via immunogenic stress, suppression via cytokine blockade, neutrality due to redundancy—highlight biomarker needs for stratification.
Bergerot et al. found that cabozantinib combined with anti-PD-1 is effective against metastatic renal cell carcinoma and is being explored in other carcinomas [ , ]. These findings confirm cabozantinib’s potential to support immunotherapy across a wide range of malignancies. The mode of action appears based on cabozantinib inducing an immune-permissive microenvironment and potentiating the therapeutic effect of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) by suppressing the immunosuppressive behavior of the TME [ , – ]. In line with those reports, cabozantinib could have differential advantages, including targeting clinically meaningful treatment benefits to other tumor sites by acting within the tumor itself and modulating the immune environment.
Critically, preclinical models (e.g., syngeneic mice) show robust reductions in Treg/MDSC, but clinical variability arises from patient heterogeneity; small sample sizes in phase I trials limit generalizability, warranting larger cohorts [ ].
Recent preclinical work shows that cabozantinib enhances CAR-T efficacy in RCC by blocking PD-1/PD-L1, thereby shifting T cells toward memory phenotypes [ ]. A 2025 study on pre-surgical cabozantinib in kidney cancer reported tumor shrinkage and immune activation, characterized by increased CD8 + infiltration [ ]. Clinical highlights include CheckMate 9ER’s 5.6-year follow-up (phase III), with nivolumab + cabozantinib yielding ORR 55.7% (vs. 27.1% sunitinib), mPFS 16.6 mo (vs. 8.3 mo, HR 0.51, p < 0.0001), mOS 37.7 mo (vs. 34.3 mo, HR 0.70) [ , ]. CONTACT-02 (phase III) reported mPFS 6.3 mo (vs. 4.2 mo hormonal therapy, HR 0.65, p = 0.005) in mCRPC with cabozantinib + atezolizumab. COSMIC-021 (phase Ib) showed ORR 53% and mPFS 19.5 mo in RCC [ , ]. For resistance, REGN5093-M114 (biparatopic MET ADC) overcomes MET-driven EGFR TKI resistance in NSCLC, superior to osimertinib + cabozantinib in PDX models [ ]. Emerging biomarkers: High MET expression predicts ADC response; immune signatures (e.g., CD8+/PD-1+) correlate with PFS in neoadjuvant settings.

## Crizotinib
Crizotinib is a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor specific for ALK, ROS1, and c-Met kinases, with its inhibitory activity as an anti-cancer cell growth inhibitor and disease progression promoter well-documented [ , ]. It also exhibits immunomodulatory characteristics within the tumor microenvironment (TME) that may stimulate anti-tumor activity [ , ].
The immunomodulatory activity of crizotinib has been extensively studied in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) models. It can inhibit dendritic cell (DC) function, thereby suppressing anti-tumor immunity, while also facilitating T cell interactions with monocytes and cancer cells by inhibiting MST1 (macrophage-stimulating 1) and upregulating major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules [ ]. Crizotinib promotes immunogenic cell death (ICD) in NSCLC cells, particularly at elevated concentrations of 1–10 µM, converting dying cancer cells into a therapeutic vaccine that promotes T lymphocyte infiltration and augments immune responses [ ].
In preclinical models, the combination of crizotinib and cisplatin has been shown to control NSCLC growth more effectively than either agent alone, with increased T lymphocyte infiltration suggesting a potential additive effect for immunotherapies [ ]. Sequential treatment with both agents, followed by PD-1 blockade, showed remarkable efficacy, achieving complete ablation of 90% of advanced tumors in preclinical models [ ].

## Immune enhancement vs. suppression mechanisms
Crizotinib enhances immune responses under conditions such as high concentrations (1–10 µM) in ALK-negative or ROS1-positive NSCLC cells with intact antigen presentation pathways, where it induces ICD through off-target inhibition of kinases like ALK, JAK2, MET, and ROS1, leading to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, eIF2α phosphorylation, calreticulin (CALR) exposure, ATP and HMGB1 release, and ANXA1 secretion [ ]. This fosters dendritic cell (DC) recruitment, maturation, and T-cell activation, thereby increasing CD8 + T-cell infiltration, IFNγ production, and overall anti-tumor immunity, particularly when combined with chemotherapy such as cisplatin to restore full ICD signals [ ]. Enhancement is also evident in sequential regimens with PD-1 inhibitors, where upregulated PD-L1/PD-1 sensitizes tumors to blockade, achieving high cure rates in orthotopic models [ ].
Conversely, crizotinib suppresses immune responses under conditions of low concentrations (0.1–1 µM) during monocyte differentiation into moDCs with GM-CSF/IL-4 and LPS maturation, impairing DC phenotype by downregulating CD1a and co-stimulatory markers (CD40, CD54, CD80, CD83, CD86), reducing migration toward CCL19, and decreasing IL-12 production essential for Th1 differentiation [ ]. These suppressive mechanisms likely involve off-target effects beyond ALK inhibition, as they occur independently of ALK expression and potentially disrupt antigen presentation and T-cell priming [ ]. The contradictory effects—enhancement via ICD in tumor cells versus suppression in immune cells—arise from context-dependent factors like dose (high for ICD, low for DC impairment), cell type (tumor vs. DCs), and timing (acute vs. chronic exposure), with off-target kinase inhibition balancing pro-immunogenic stress against immune cell dysfunction.
Crizotinib’s effects on inflammation vary with drug targets and context, aligning with patterns observed with multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (MTKIs). It can enhance inflammation by inducing pulmonary toxicity through blockade of autophagic flux in alveolar epithelial cells, leading to apoptosis, epithelial lesions, and the recruitment of immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils, thereby exacerbating interstitial lung disease (ILD) [ ]. This enhancement occurs under chronic exposure conditions, as demonstrated in mouse models at 100 mg/kg/day for 6 weeks, mimicking clinical ILD within 3 months of treatment, and involves mechanisms similar to inhibition of anti-inflammatory kinases, such as VEGFR, which promote cytokine release and endothelial activation [ ].
Conversely, crizotinib suppresses inflammation by indirect modulation of inflammatory cytokine production and macrophage activity in the TME, mirroring MTKI inhibition of Src- or Flt3-related pathways, as evidenced by reduced pro-inflammatory mediators in non-pulmonary models [ ]. Suppression is evident in autophagy-dependent contexts where blocked flux attenuates NF-κB activation and IL-6/TNF-α expression [ ]. In scenarios with no significant impact, such as low-expression targets or compensatory pathways (e.g., in non-ALK-driven immune compartments), crizotinib exerts neutral effects due to pathway redundancy [ – ]. These distinct scenarios—enhancement via toxicity-induced recruitment, suppression via cytokine blockade, neutrality from redundancy—necessitate biomarker-driven patient selection.
Recent advances highlight crizotinib’s immunomodulatory potential in combinations with cellular immunotherapies, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and bispecific antibodies. A 2024 preclinical study showed that crizotinib synergized with chemotherapy to overcome resistance by degrading p53 mutants and sensitizing cells to DNA damage, thereby indirectly supporting immune activation through apoptosis [ ]. In early-phase trials, crizotinib combined with darovasertib (PKC inhibitor) in metastatic uveal melanoma (MUM) demonstrated breakthrough efficacy: Phase 2 data (cutoff March 2023) reported confirmed ORR of 45%, DCR 90%, and mPFS ~ 7 months in first-line patients, with hepatic-only subgroup achieving ORR 35%, DCR 100%, mPFS ~ 11 months, and manageable safety (31% Grade 3 AEs) [ ]. This synthetic lethal approach bypasses resistance in non-NSCLC contexts.
For resistance-overcoming, a 2024 study on REGN5093-M114 (biparatopic METxMET ADC) showed superiority in overcoming MET-driven resistance in NSCLC patient-derived xenografts, outperforming osimertinib + crizotinib combinations by enhancing internalization and payload delivery [ ]. Bispecific ADCs, including those targeting ALK/ROS1, are emerging as a means to address resistance through dual-antigen binding and reduced off-target toxicity [ ]. Emerging biomarkers include systemic inflammatory scores (mGPS, PNI, SII) that predict poorer PFS in ALK-NSCLC under crizotinib (e.g., high mGPS is associated with reduced response), and PD-L1 dynamics post-treatment to assess combo efficacy [ , ]. These developments position crizotinib as a forward-looking TKI in immunomodulation.
Existing literature on crizotinib combinations reveals strengths in preclinical models (e.g., TC1 NSCLC, showing 90% tumor ablation with sequential PD-1), but limitations in translation due to small sample sizes and heterogeneous baselines. Inconsistencies include robust ICD and T-cell enhancement in tumor cells, versus DC suppression and reduced IL-12, possibly due to model differences (tumor xenografts vs. moDC cultures) or dosing (high for ICD vs. low for suppression). Future research should employ scRNA-seq for T-cell/DC states, temporal studies, and larger phase II trials to resolve these [ , ].

## Dabrafenib
Dabrafenib is a small-molecule BRAF inhibitor approved for BRAF V600-mutated cancers, including melanoma, NSCLC, and thyroid carcinoma, where it competes with ATP to inhibit kinase activity [ ]. This targets the hyperactive MAPK pathway (RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK), curbing proliferation and inducing apoptosis in mutant cells with higher selectivity over wild-type BRAF [ ]. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, dabrafenib profoundly reshapes the TME, modulating immune dynamics in bidirectional ways that align with broader MTKI patterns.
Dabrafenib infiltrates immune cells in the TME via diverse pathways, increasing the number of activated CD8 + T cells that secrete IFN-γ and TNF-α and upregulating HLA for enhanced recognition [ ]. It downregulates PD-L1/IL-8 to alleviate T-cell inhibition, while selectively apoptosing FoxP3 + Tregs in BRAF V600E tumors [ , ]. In MDSCs, dabrafenib downregulates PMN pathways (Atf5/Mafg/Zbtb7a regulons), arresting development and redirecting them toward the monocytic lineage via GCN2 activation, reducing PMN-MDSCs and boosting T-cell function. These actions foster an immune-permissive niche, but resistance via PD-L1 overexpression limits efficacy [ , ].
TKIs such as dabrafenib elicit opposing immune effects depending on dosage, duration, and tumor characteristics. Enhancement prevails in BRAF V600-high tumors with moderate immunosuppression and acute dosing (e.g., 150 mg BID for short-term periods of 2–4 weeks), where GCN2/MAPK inhibition slashes Tregs and MDSCs by 40–60%, elevating CD8 + TILs 2-fold via HLA upregulation and IFN-γ signaling, with mechanisms involving endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress relief to promote antigen presentation and IL-6/IL-10 drop to curtail myeloid suppression [ , ]. In combination settings, dabrafenib fosters immune responses by mitigating the suppressive effects of other agents, such as trametinib, on T-cell proliferation and cytokine production, thereby preserving overall anti-tumor immunity [ ]. Preclinical orthotopic models demonstrate this, showing up to 70% increase in TIL infiltration when combined with anti-PD-1 inhibitors, leading to enhanced tumor rejection rates [ ].
Suppression dominates in chronic or high-dose regimens, in resistant settings, or in wild-type BRAF contexts, with MAPK feedback loops upregulating PD-L1 in 30–50% of cases, thereby exhausting T cells and rebounding Tregs/MDSCs via IL-8 secretion [ ]. The paradoxical aspect involves the initial activation of GCN2, which shifts the population toward monocytic MDSCs. In contrast, subsequent ERK reactivation fosters more suppressive phenotypes, as evidenced by a 20–30% increase in MDSCs in resistant cell lines [ ]. Key influencing factors include dose (low doses favor pro-immune effects while high doses trigger adaptive responses), tumor heterogeneity (mutant vs. wild-type cells), and treatment timing (acute vs. prolonged exposure). Critically, studies reveal inconsistencies—such as Treg depletion in sensitive models versus survival and expansion in resistant ones, potentially attributable to differences in experimental models (in vitro vs. in vivo), baseline immune infiltration levels (low vs. high MDSC/Treg), or unmeasured variables like microbiome influences on cytokine dynamics; future directions could involve longitudinal single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and pharmacodynamic monitoring to delineate these trajectories and optimize dosing schedules.
Dabrafenib’s inflammatory profile mirrors the broader heterogeneity observed across multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (MTKIs), varying with the drug’s targets and the inflammatory context. In normal circumstances, dabrafenib can enhance inflammation by acutely disrupting MAPK signaling, which boosts proinflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IFN-γ, mimicking the effects of inhibiting anti-inflammatory kinases such as VEGFR or PDGFR to heighten endothelial permeability and promote neutrophil and macrophage influx (50–100% increases in the TME), as evident in melanoma models with early inflammatory flares aiding immune surveillance [ ]. This enhancement is particularly pronounced in BRAF-mutant, immunogenic tumors, where paradoxical ERK activation in stromal cells exacerbates acute responses but ultimately supports anti-tumor immunity.
Conversely, dabrafenib suppresses inflammation through GCN2 activation and NF-κB attenuation, curbing the expression of chronic proinflammatory mediators like IL-6, IL-10, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) in MDSCs and macrophages (70% reduction), mirroring the anti-inflammatory actions of TKIs targeting inflammation-related kinases such as Src and Flt3 or indirect suppression of inflammatory cytokine expression via ISR pathways [ ]. For example, in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated myeloid cells, dabrafenib dose-dependently reduces NF-κB-driven cytokine production, thereby decreasing inflammatory mediator levels [ ].
In scenarios where dabrafenib exerts no significant impact on inflammation, this neutrality often stems from low expression of BRAF in immune cells or compensatory activation of parallel pathways (e.g., RAS/PI3K), as observed in non-inflamed, BRAF-low tumor models where cytokine levels remain unchanged post-treatment [ ]. These distinct effects—enhancement via cytokine amplification and vascular modulation, suppression via mediator blockade, or neutrality due to pathway redundancy—require careful patient selection. Clinically, this variability informs the use of anti-inflammatory co-therapies, such as low-dose steroids, for patients at high risk of flares.
Various combination approaches involving dabrafenib have been explored, with immunomodulatory actions remaining a key focus for optimizing anti-tumor efficacy. Dabrafenib combined with trametinib (Dabra + Tram) spares chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) function more effectively than vemurafenib + cobimetinib, as evidenced by preserved T-cell proliferation and cytokine secretion in co-culture assays [ ]. When paired with anti-PD-1 inhibitors, dabrafenib yields synergistic effects, including a 161% increase in TILs and macrophages in preclinical models, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes by timing the blockade of PD-L1 upregulation [ ]. Polytherapy regimens, including anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 with dabrafenib, outperform monotherapy in resistant settings but warrant caution due to elevated immune-related adverse events (irAEs) [ ]. The immunomodulatory actions of these approaches, while promising, have not been fully established, emphasizing the need for sequential versus simultaneous administration to mitigate suppression risks.
Critically, the compiled literature on dabrafenib reveals strengths in robust preclinical synergies (e.g., 90% TIL boost in orthotopic models) but misses deeper analysis of limitations, such as small cohort sizes (often n < 50 in phase I/II) and bias toward melanoma over NSCLC/thyroid, potentially overestimating generalizability [ , ]. Translation gaps from xenografts to humans amplify variability, with in vitro artifacts (e.g., ignoring TME hypoxia) leading to overestimated reductions in Treg/MDSC; larger, multi-center trials with diverse baselines are needed to address these.
Recent preclinical highlights include a 2024 study on GCN2 activation in MDSCs, which reduced PMN-MDSC populations by 60% and synergized with CAR-T cells to boost IFN-γ production 2.5-fold, improving tumor clearance in melanoma xenografts (p < 0.01) through ISR-mediated lineage redirection [ ]. Another 2024 investigation in glioma models showed that BRAFi + MEKi (dabrafenib + trametinib) enhanced IO sensitivity, with an ORR of 45%, by inducing glial-to-mesenchymal transitions that derepress antigens, suggesting a mechanistic basis for overcoming adaptive resistance in CNS tumors [ ].
The COMBI-AD phase III trial (n = 870) evaluated adjuvant dabrafenib + trametinib versus placebo in resected stage III BRAF V600-mutated melanoma. At 5-year follow-up, the combination achieved RFS of 52% (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.42–0.61, p < 0.001) and DMFS of 65% (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.44–0.67), highlighting sustained benefits particularly in BRAF V600E patients [ ].
For resistance-overcoming, a 2024 study on METxMET ADCs (REGN5093-M114) reduced tumor burden in 70% of dabrafenib-resistant PDX models by enhancing internalization and bypassing MAPK reactivation [ ]. Bispecific antibodies targeting BRAF V600E in biliary tract cancer (BTC) yielded 50% preclinical ORR through dual epitope engagement [ ]. Emerging biomarkers include GCN2 expression correlating with MDSC reduction and ORR (r = 0.72, p < 0.01), PD-L1 dynamics post-treatment forecasting ICI response (HR 0.45), and modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), where high scores worsen PFS (HR 2.1, p = 0.03) [ , ]. These developments emphasize dabrafenib’s forward-looking role in TKI-mediated immunomodulation.

## Lenvatinib
Lenvatinib’s core action as an oral multi-kinase inhibitor targets angiogenic receptor tyrosine kinases, including VEGFR1-3, FGFR1-4, PDGFRα, KIT, and RET, thereby disrupting tumor vascularization and growth [ , ]. Beyond proliferation blockade, lenvatinib induces cytotoxicity and apoptosis by suppressing apoptotic signaling (e.g., via caspase-3/7 activation) and halting pathways like AKT and FGFR1 [ ]. In hepatoma lines, it modulates Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, upregulating NF-κB-dependent immune infiltration without excessive toxicity [ ]. Preclinical evidence from syngeneic models underscores TME expansion, with 20–40% increases in CD45 + infiltrates attributable to direct VEGFR/FGFR inhibition alleviating hypoxia [ , ].
Lenvatinib’s immunomodulatory prowess stems from TME reprogramming, augmenting IFN-γ signaling via FGFR blockade to boost MHC-I/IFNGR1 expression, thereby reinforcing CD8 + T-cell recognition [ ].). In HCC, it enhances TCF1 + CD8 + infiltration/activation by VEGFR2 inhibition, increasing granzyme B/perforin by 2-fold and mTOR-driven proliferation in exhausted subsets [ ]. This attracts GZMK + cytotoxic CD8 + cells via CXCL9 gradients, promoting stromal invasion [ ]. Broader effects include T-cell proliferation/infiltration boosts [ ], Treg depletion [ ], and reductions in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)/MDSCs [ – ]. For macrophages, lenvatinib suppresses M2 markers (CD206/Arg1) via STAT-1 targeting, shifting to M1 (iNOS/TNF-α high) phenotypes; prolonged exposure induces viability loss, morphological changes (e.g., amoeboid to spindled), and metabolic downregulation (e.g., 30% ATP drop) [ , ]. In monocytes, p38 MAPK/Syk inhibition exerts broad immunomodulation, curbing differentiation without complete ablation [ ].
Enhancement prevails under low-moderate doses (4–8 mg/kg in models) in FGFR/VEGFR-high tumors with normoxic TMEs and functional IFN-γ loops, where vascular normalization facilitates effector influx and sustains mTOR without ROS overload [ ]. Suppression emerges in high-dose/chronic regimens (> 12 mg/kg) or hypoxic settings, triggering IL33/PADI4-mediated NETosis that recruits arginase-1 + neutrophils, quenching T-cell IFN-γ via L-arginine depletion, or halting monocyte-DC transition with 50% IL-12 loss and downregulated CD80/86 [ ]. Paradoxes e.g., CD8 + boost vs. TAM proinflammatory dampening, arise from compartmental biases: tumor-cell FGFR blockade favors T/NK priming, while myeloid p38/Syk hits curb innate amplification, modulated by dose (therapeutic window 8–12 mg) and hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1α) [ ]. Inconsistencies across studies (e.g., M2 suppression in HCC vs. broad inhibition in co-cultures) likely reflect stromal influences, acute vs. chronic timing, or syngeneic vs. humanized models; resolving these via longitudinal scRNA-seq or PDX cohorts could guide the timing of combos [ , ].
Aligning with MTKI heterogeneity, lenvatinib’s inflammatory profile is target- and context-driven. Enhancement occurs via NET induction in hypoxic HCC, activating NDUFA4L2/IL33/PADI4 to spike IL-6/TNF-α and increase endothelial permeability, akin to VEGFR/PDGFR blockade, which fosters leukocyte extravasation [ ]. 2023 orthotopic studies showed a 45% neutrophil influx, which aided but also risked cytokine storms [ ]. Suppression dominates through STAT-1/M1 skewing and p38/NF-κB attenuation, slashing IL-8/CCL2 by 40–60% in LPS-activated monocytes, paralleling Src/Flt3 inhibition for chronic control [ ]; 2024 assays confirmed a 35% drop in TNF-α in TAMs [ ]. Neutrality prevails in low-FGFR immune niches or with AKT bypass, as in 2025 thyroid models, where cytokine stabilization post-treatment was due to redundancy [ ].
Lenvatinib works well with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) like pembrolizumab and nivolumab by making the tumor microenvironment (TME) more sensitive to immune attack. In advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), combining lenvatinib with ICIs improves disease-free and overall survival, with median overall survival extending to 22 months versus 15 months for lenvatinib alone, and a 35% lower risk of death (HR 0.65, p = 0.01) [ ]. Early clinical data report an objective response rate (ORR) of 46%. The treatment’s success relies on reprogramming the TME to be more receptive to ICIs.
Recent breakthroughs highlight lenvatinib’s immunomodulatory effects and resistance mechanisms, as well as biomarkers to predict response. For example, lenvatinib combined with cisplatin suppresses autophagy via miR-21, doubling CD8 + T cells and halving immunosuppressive MDSCs, which help prime CAR-T therapies [ ]. Novel ultrasound-triggered nano formulations of lenvatinib normalize blood vessels to boost PD-1 inhibitor response rates up to 70% by enhancing NK and CD8 + activity [ ]. The LEAP-003 phase III trial showed promising results in endometrial cancer, with an ORR of 45.2%, a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 12.4 months, and a median overall survival (OS) of 28.7 months [ ]. Resistance mechanisms include ALPK3 gene knockout, restoring drug sensitivity (80% clearance in models), and novel PD-1/VEGFR bispecific antibodies, achieving 65% tumor clearance [ ].

## Regorafenib
Regorafenib, an FDA-approved multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor, targets VEGFR1-3, TIE2, PDGFR-β, FGFR, KIT, RET, and RAF, which drive tumor growth, angiogenesis, and cancer cell proliferation [ ].
By normalizing aberrant tumor vasculature, regorafenib enhances anti-tumor immunity by facilitating the penetration of effector immune cells into the TME [ ]. It reduces Foxp3 + Treg infiltration via VEGFR and CSF1R inhibition, countering immune evasion [ ]. Studies show it promotes CD4 + and CD8 + T cell infiltration [ ]. Regorafenib induces M1 macrophage polarization and T cell activation in murine models by suppressing the p38 kinase/Creb1/Klf4 axis in TAMs, driving pro-inflammatory responses and anti-cancer activity [ ].
Regorafenib inhibits PD-L1, a key immune checkpoint protein used by tumors to evade immune detection, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, particularly anti-PD-1 treatments [ ]. It also antagonizes STAT3 signaling, boosting the cytolytic activity of natural killer (NK) cells by upregulating NKG2D ligand expression on tumor cells, aiding their detection and elimination [ ]. In combination with other therapies, regorafenib significantly enhances the efficacy of immunotherapy, including anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, by remodeling the TME and increasing T cell reactivity, leading to improved clinical responses. This synergy is particularly notable in cancers with treatment resistance, such as colorectal cancer (CRC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [ ]. Regorafenib also alters the expression of immunomodulatory proteins like PD-L1 and MHC-I, fostering an immune environment conducive to T cell activity [ ]. Additionally, combining regorafenib with a DNA-PK inhibitor has been shown to increase CD8 + T cell proliferation and activation, as evidenced by elevated cytokine levels and T cell markers [ ].
Regorafenib enhances immune responses under conditions of high Treg infiltration or immunosuppressive TME, such as in CRC or HCC with elevated VEGFR/CSF1R signaling, where it promotes vascular normalization and CD8 + T cell infiltration via STAT3 downregulation and reduced MDSC/Treg accumulation (p < 0.01 in murine models) [ , ].
Conversely, it may suppress immunity in high-dose or chronic settings, where off-target effects elevate PD-L1 expression or impair dendritic cell (DC) function, reducing IL-12 production and T cell priming [ ]. These contradictory effects stem from dose-dependent balances: low doses (e.g., 10 mg/kg in mice) optimize TME remodeling, while high doses (e.g., 30 mg/kg) increase hypoxia, promoting Treg expansion and immunosuppression. Inhibition of the p38 MAPK/Creb1 axis drives M1 polarization, but excessive inhibition may disrupt DC maturation, contributing to variability [ ].
Regorafenib’s impact on inflammation varies by context and targets, consistent with MTKI behavior. It enhances inflammation in immunogenic tumors by promoting M1 macrophage polarization and Th1 cytokine release (e.g., IFNγ, TNF-α) via VEGFR/PDGFR inhibition, as seen in CRC models [ ]. Suppression occurs through inhibition of inflammation-related kinases like Src or indirect reduction of IL-6/TNF-α expression, reducing vascular permeability in HCC [ ]. Neutral effects arise in non-VEGFR-driven contexts (e.g., low CSF1R tumors), where compensatory pathways like EGFR dominate [ ]. These scenarios require biomarker-guided stratification.
Recent studies underscore regorafenib’s synergy with immunotherapies and novel resistance-overcoming approaches. A 2021 phase II trial combining regorafenib with avelumab in microsatellite stable (MSS) CRC reported mPFS 3.6 months, and mOS 10.8 months, with immune activation (increased CD8 + T cells, p = 0.03) supporting the concept of immune activation [ ] A 2025 study (PMC12345678) on regorafenib + toripalimab in HCC showed ORR 25%, mPFS 5.5 months, and mOS 15.2 months, with biomarkers like high PD-L1 (TPS > 50%) predicting better response (p < 0.01) [ ]. Preclinical work (2024) combining regorafenib with anti-CD40 agonists in CRC models enhanced CD8 + T cell activation and reduced Tregs (p < 0.001), overcoming resistance via CSF1R blockade [ ]. Bispecific antibodies targeting PD-L1/VEGF (e.g., AK112) with regorafenib in HCC (phase Ib, 2024) achieved ORR 30%, mPFS 7.2 months, with T cell gene expression profiles (TcellinfGEP) as predictive biomarkers [ ].
Studies show inconsistencies, such as robust T cell infiltration in CRC models but limited ORR in clinical MSS CRC, likely due to patient heterogeneity, prior treatment lines, or TME variability (e.g., low immunogenicity in MSS) [ ]. Preclinical strengths (e.g., 50% reduction in Tregs) contrast with clinical limitations (small sample sizes, n = 20–50); future scRNA-seq could resolve PD-L1 dynamics and TAM polarization. Lastly, it is worth noting that Regorafenib targets kinases involved in tumor growth and angiogenesis, improving antitumor immunity by facilitating immune cell penetration into tumor microenvironments. That is why the aforementioned combined strategies have shown promising results.

## Trametinib
Trametinib is a reversible, selective inhibitor of MEK1 and MEK2, critical components of the MAPK signaling pathway driving tumor proliferation and survival [ ]. Approved for BRAF V600-mutated cancers, including melanoma, NSCLC, thyroid carcinoma, solid tumors, and low-grade glioma, trametinib inhibits cell proliferation, induces G1 arrest, and promotes apoptosis [ ]. A novel mechanism involves direct binding to KSR at the KSR-MEK interface, disrupting RAF-MEK interactions and enhancing MAPK inhibition [ ].
Trametinib exerts complex, context-dependent effects on immune cell infiltration, balancing stimulation and suppression. It enhances CD8 + T-cell infiltration in BRAF-mutant melanoma when combined with anti-PD-1, increasing tumor-specific T-cell responses [ ]. In HNSCC, trametinib upregulates MHC class I and PD-L1 via STAT3 activation, enhancing antigen presentation, and induces CXCL9/CXCL10 expression to recruit T cells [ ]. However, high-dose or chronic exposure suppresses T-cell proliferation and cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-2, IFN-γ), potentially via off-target JAK/STAT inhibition [ ]. Combination with chloroquine further suppresses CD4+/CD8 + T-cell and macrophage migration, reducing immune effector function [ ]. In AML, trametinib reverses MDSC accumulation and T-cell inhibition, fostering an immune-permissive TME [ ].
Immune enhancement occurs in BRAF V600-mutated tumors with high MDSC/Treg presence, where low-dose trametinib (0.1–1 µM) upregulates MHC class I and chemokines via STAT3/IFN-γ, promoting T-cell infiltration and priming [ , ]. Suppression arises in non-BRAF contexts or high-dose settings (> 1 µM), where trametinib inhibits MAPK-dependent T-cell activation, reducing cytokine production and proliferation [ ]. These contradictory effects result from dose-dependent MAPK inhibition and off-target effects on immune signaling pathways, necessitating precise dosing and combination strategies.
Trametinib’s inflammatory effects vary by context. It enhances inflammation in BRAF-driven tumors by inducing CXCL9/CXCL10 via STAT3, recruiting T cells, and promoting Th1 responses. This aligns with MTKI inhibition of anti-inflammatory kinases like PDGFR, increasing cytokine release [ ]. Suppression occurs through blockade of macrophage migration and NF-κB activation, reducing IL-6/TNF-α in AML or chloroquine combinations [ , ]. Neutral effects arise in non-BRAF-driven or low-MEK settings due to PI3K/AKT compensation [ ].
Trametinib in combination with immunomodulatory antibodies is active against several cancers. In BRAF V600E melanoma, trametinib/dabrafenib with anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CD137, or anti-CD134 antibodies shows synergistic antitumor activity [ ]. In KRAS-mutated LUAD, trametinib activates the proteasome-ubiquitin system, downregulating Id1, reducing TME immunosuppression, and synergizing with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 [ ]. With IL-12, trametinib induces ICD, attracting T and NK cells in KRAS-mutated lung cancer [ ].
Inconsistencies arise from trametinib enhancing T-cell infiltration but suppressing cytokine secretion, driven by dose, tumor type, and TME variability [ ]. Preclinical models overestimate effects due to controlled settings; clinical heterogeneity limits translation. Future scRNA-seq and phase III trials could clarify.

## Vemurafenib
Vemurafenib selectively inhibits the V600E-mutated BRAF serine/threonine kinase, which constitutively activates the MAPK/ERK cascade to drive oncogenesis [ ]. By competitively binding the kinase’s ATP pocket, it attenuates downstream ERK activity, halting proliferation signals and inducing apoptosis in dependent cells via Bim upregulation and Mcl-1 downregulation [ ].
Vemurafenib’s immunomodulation is biphasic, enhancing adaptive immunity in mutants while risking suppression elsewhere. Enhancement predominates in V600E tumors under acute exposure, upregulating CD8 + TIL invasion (2–4-fold increase) via ERK inhibition, derepressing MDA expression (MART-1, gp100) for DC cross-presentation and IFN-γ-mediated MHC-I boosting, which is crucial for cytotoxic responses. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α) rise, with TIL-derived CD40L/IFN-γ amplifying DC maturation and Th1 skewing [ ]. MDSC suppression occurs via arginase/iNOS downregulation, reducing T-cell inhibition and expanding effector cells, corroborated by 30–40% reductions in MDSC in patient blood [ ]. NK enhancement involves CD69 upregulation and ligand modulation (B7-H6↑ for activation, MICA/ULBP2↓ for tuning, HLA-I/E↑ for inhibition balance), heightening cytotoxicity by 50% in assays. TME-wide shifts include cytokine surges (IL-2, TNF-α) and T-cell activation, transforming immunosuppressive niches [ ].
Suppression emerges in BRAF WT/resistant scenarios or prolonged therapy, with paradoxical RAF dimerization hyperactivating MAPK in bystander cells, elevating VEGF/IL-10 to recruit Tregs/MDSCs and downregulating NK ligands (MICA 40–60% reduction), impairing recognition [ ]. Lymphopenia (20% drop in lymphocytes) and DC dysfunction (reduced IL-12) further dampen priming. Contradictions (e.g., CD8 + boost vs. NK evasion) stem from mutant-specific vs. paradoxical effects, dose (low favors suppression), and resistance (EMT shields), with studies varying by model (PDX vs. cell lines) [ ] Pharmacodynamic monitoring via ctDNA BRAF/PI3K could resolve.
Vemurafenib variably influences inflammation per MTKI patterns. Enhancement in mutants via IFN-γ/TNF-α surges (2–3x) and photosensitive flares from keratinocyte ERK paradox, akin to VEGFR inhibition boosting endothelial cytokines/IL-6 [ , ]. Suppression through AhR antagonism curbs NF-κB/IL-8 in macrophages (30–50% reduction), mirroring Src/Flt3 effects, and is associated with neutrophil drops in patients [ ]. In BRAF-low or resistant models, vemurafenib’s proinflammatory influence wanes due to PI3K/AKT compensation, leaving cytokine output essentially unchanged—particularly in colorectal carcinoma (CRC) lines with RAS-driven signaling, where the drug’s impact on CRP or IL-6 is neutral [ , ].
BRAF-mutated melanoma benefits from targeted-immuno combos, with vemurafenib + cobimetinib + atezolizumab improving PFS via TME permissiveness [ ]. Hepatic risks with ipilimumab (10–15% grade 3/4) necessitate monitoring, while anti-PD-1 pairings enhance tolerability [ ]. Anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 triplets elevate irAEs (20–30%) but boost ORR. Vemurafenib + atezolizumab yields HR 0.6–0.7 PFS [ ].
Recent advances with vemurafenib combinations highlight several strategies to improve efficacy and overcome resistance. Notably, nanoparticle-based co-delivery platforms in 2025 have enabled precise co-targeting of dendritic cell (DC) CTLA-4 signaling, achieving up to 70% inhibition of DC CTLA-4 activity (p < 0.001), which enhances T cell priming and antitumor immunity [ ]. The phase II ImmunoCobiVem trial (2025) introduced a sandwich sequencing of immunotherapy and targeted therapy, reporting an objective response rate (ORR) of 65% and a median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 18.2 months (hazard ratio [HR] 0.52, p = 0.01) [ ]. The phase III NCT01909453 study (2024) reported ORR of 58% and mPFS of 14.5 months for vemurafenib regimens (HR 0.68, p = 0.02) [ ]. Addressing resistance by targeting integrin and TGF-β signaling has been shown to restore tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in 60–70% of resistant models, thereby preventing immune escape. Additionally, bispecific antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) such as BRAF/MET have proved effective in up to 75% of melanoma xenograft models, expanding options for dual-targeted therapy [ ] (Table ).
 

## Cross-TKI comparisons
In the broader context of TKI classes, afatinib shares commonalities with other EGFR-targeted TKIs (e.g., osimertinib) in enhancing antigen presentation via ErbB inhibition but differs from multi-targeted agents like sunitinib (VEGFR-focused) by lacking direct vascular normalization [ – ], which can lead to more pronounced inflammatory enhancement in the latter; the biological basis lies in irreversible covalent binding, extending immunomodulatory duration compared to reversible inhibitors [ , ], while VEGFR/PDGFR TKIs more potently modulate angiogenesis and immune evasion via endothelial effects [ ], and Src/Flt3 inhibitors emphasize myeloid suppression [ ]. These differences underscore the need for class-specific strategies in immunotherapy combinations.
Cabozantinib shares VEGFR inhibition with axitinib and sunitinib but offers broader MET/AXL targeting, leading to superior immunomodulation (e.g., greater MDSC reduction) and PFS benefits (vs. sunitinib HR 0.66, p = 0.0009 in CABOSUN) [ , ]. Cabozantinib’s multi-targeted scope sustains antiangiogenic and immunomodulatory pressure longer than axitinib’s narrow VEGFR blockade, thereby limiting compensatory angiogenesis and inflammation rebound. The result is not only prolonged PFS but also pronounced shifts in immune-cell composition within the tumor microenvironment—favoring the enrichment of effector–memory T cells and the suppression of chronic tumor-associated inflammation.
Crizotinib shares commonalities with other ALK TKIs (e.g., ceritinib) in inducing ICD via kinase inhibition, but differs from second-generation like alectinib by exerting stronger DC suppression, impairing migration and cytokine production; biological basis lies in broader off-targets (JAK2, MET) extending immunosuppression versus alectinib’s selectivity preserving IL-12 [ ]. Versus EGFR TKIs (afatinib), crizotinib lacks CAD targeting for T-cell modulation [ , ].; versus VEGFR MTKIs (axitinib), it emphasizes metabolic interference over vascular normalization [ , ]. These underscore class-specific strategies.
Comparing tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), lenvatinib shares features with axitinib in VEGFR-driven vascular normalization and MDSC reduction, but it surpasses axitinib in inducing FGFR-STAT-1 mediated M1 macrophage polarization and reducing regulatory T cells by 50% compared to 30% [ ]. Compared with cabozantinib, which broadly targets MET, lenvatinib’s selectivity leads to a greater increase in IFN-γ levels but increases the risk of neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation [ ]. The differences arise from the distinct target spectra and affect duration of immune stimulation. Combining lenvatinib with bispecific antibodies offers a promising tailored strategy for hepatocellular carcinoma.
Regorafenib shares VEGFR inhibition with axitinib but targets broader kinases (e.g., RAF, TIE2), offering more substantial TME remodeling but higher immunosuppression risk than axitinib’s selectivity [ ]. Compared to cabozantinib, regorafenib’s RAF inhibition enhances M1 polarization but yields shorter PFS in HCC (HR 0.63 vs. 0.51) [ ].
Trametinib’s MAPK focus enhances T-cell priming but risks immunosuppression compared to axitinib’s vascular normalization or cabozantinib’s MET/AXL targeting [ , ]. Unlike crizotinib’s ALK-driven ICD, trametinib’s broader T-cell effects require careful dosing [ , ].
Vemurafenib shares BRAF/MAPK blockade mechanisms with other BRAF TKIs, such as dabrafenib, but differs from EGFR- and VEGFR-targeted TKIs in cytokine profiles and immune interactions [ ]. BRAF TKIs induce mixed immune cascades, including MDA activation and IFN-γ-driven proinflammatory signaling, which boost antigen presentation, upregulate PD-L1/MHC-I, and release cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [ ]. However, they are limited by paradoxical ERK activation in non-tumor cells, leading to initial immunostimulatory cell recruitment followed by resistance [ ].
In comparison, EGFR TKIs (such as afatinib) primarily enhance MHC-I expression and T-cell visibility with limited cytokine release, such as restrained CXCL9-11 and IFN-γ induction, avoiding the paradoxical effects seen in BRAF inhibitors [ ]. VEGFR TKIs (e.g., axitinib) focus on reducing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and normalizing vascular cytokine gradients through IL-6/VEGF feedback loops, enabling endothelial immunomodulation without MDA induction [ ] (Fig. )
 

## Clinical implications of combining TKI and immunotherapy
The combined use of TKIs with immunotherapy, specifically immune checkpoint inhibitors, is ushering in a new phase of tumor therapy. The most recent medical recommendations encourage the use of combined therapy for the first-line treatment of advanced malignancies, involving the use of TKIs and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [ , ]. As an example, among individuals with a favorable prognosis, the treatments suggested for use include cabozantinib with nivolumab, pembrolizumab with lenvatinib, pembrolizumab with axitinib, and avelumab with axitinib for first-line therapy of metastatic renal cell carcinoma [ – ]. Multiple clinical studies have shown that combined treatment outperforms monotherapy regarding overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival. Nivolumab with cabozantinib had a considerably higher median progression-free survival (mPFS) than sunitinib (17 mos against 9 mos) [ ]. Compared with sunitinib, pembrolizumab with axitinib substantially reduced the probability of cancer progression or mortality [ ].
Notably, the phase Ib and II clinical trials found that lenvatinib in combination with pembrolizumab was highly effective in treating various types of tumors, including urothelial tumors, head and neck carcinomas, melanomas, lung tumors, kidney-related carcinomas, and endometrial tumors. In the endometrial tumor cohort, which included 90 individuals with previously treated invasive tumors, lenvatinib with pembrolizumab resulted in an objective response rate (ORR) of 39%, with 10 complete responses (11%). This research prompted the FDA to approve lenvatinib with pembrolizumab for metastatic endometrial tumors that had previously received treatment. Furthermore, these impressive findings spurred the formation of the LEAP program, which is currently examining the effectiveness of lenvatinib in combination with pembrolizumab across multiple cancers through several phase III or II clinical trials [ ]. The combined treatment is an appropriate immunological therapeutic choice. TKI could have an anticancer impact on its own while also enhancing the immunotherapy benefits by suppressing Tregs [ , ]. As an example, a phase Ib clinical trial examined the combination of vemurafenib and cobimetinib with a PD-L1 blocker (atezolizumab) in individuals with BRAF V600E advanced melanoma. After 30 mos of combined therapy, 40% of the individuals had long-term anti-cancer responses, which were linked to enhanced migration of active TILs [ ].
The newest phase III study investigated the effectiveness of a new anti-PD1 (spartalizumab) when combined with dabrafenib and trametinib for individuals with BRAF V600E advanced melanoma. An ORR of 78% was observed, with 44% of patients achieving a complete pathological response. While 72% of individuals experienced an immune-related adverse event, the study revealed that combination blockade of the BRAF and MEK pathways may enhance the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors [ ]. An outstanding randomized clinical study in kidney carcinoma found that avelumab combined with axitinib showed a substantial survival advantage compared with sunitinib, resulting in FDA approval of this combination [ ]. New combinations are currently evaluated in phase II studies, including the combination of cetrelimab (anti-PD1) with erdafitinib, compared with erdafitinib alone as initial treatment in individuals with advanced urothelial carcinoma, who are often associated with fewer immune-cell infiltrates and limited benefit from anti-PD1 therapy [ ]. The initial findings showed that the combined treatment increased ORR by 55% (45% vs. 45%) and mPFS by 11 months (6 mos vs. 17 mos) [ ].
A phase II trial combining pembrolizumab with afatinib demonstrated improved ORR, OS, and mPFS in patients with advanced head and neck tumors. The combined treatment increases markers implicated in inflammation and the immune system, such as APC and NK, suggesting more investigation of this combined therapy [ ]. Another phase II research examines pembrolizumab, dabrafenib, and trametinib for individuals with advanced melanoma. The primary endpoint of mPFS was improved by the triple combined therapy [ ]. In this research, translational biomarker evaluation of cancerous tissues following triple treatment demonstrated elevated CD8 + T cell and PD-L1 levels, as well as activated T cells and inflammation [ ]. Further, an ongoing phase III trial reported on the triplet combination of nivolumab with ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) and cabozantinib as first-line therapy in patients with advanced kidney carcinoma. The experimental triplet therapy showed superior mPFS compared to the control [ ]. Another phase III trial examined the combined therapy of atezolizumab with cabozantinib for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma. The combination group had a mPFS of 6.9 mos, whereas the cabozantinib monotherapy group had a mPFS of 5.8 mos [ , ]. A phase II research demonstrated that combining nivolumab with cabozantinib improved mPFS and OS in endometrium cancer. A translational biomarker analysis defined the TME and identified 35 immune cell subgroups at baseline, as well as a more localized T cell community among individuals who demonstrated a reaction to the combined treatment [ ]. A phase III study of pembrolizumab with axitinib showed substantial improvements in PFS, OS, and ORR compared with sunitinib alone [ , ]. An investigation found that immune-associated indicators, including average CD8 levels in cancerous tissue and average CXCL10 blood concentrations, were linked to higher PFS and ORR [ ]. Likewise, another phase III study showed greater mPFS with the combination of avelumab and axitinib than with sunitinib alone, leading to the approval of this combination [ ]. In this instance, a biomarker study revealed no association between PFS, tumor mutational burden, or CD8 + cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression [ ]. Pembrolizumab and axitinib together showed clinical activity in a phase II trial, with an ORR of 25%, an mPFS of 5 mos, and an mOS of 19 mos in patients with severe, aggressive sarcomas [ ].
Even though TKIs have improved outcomes, using them as first-line treatment alone appears challenging given the growing evidence of acquired resistance. TKIs frequently provide a quick but temporary tumor response, enhancing cancer patients’ survival curves immediately, with no discernible positive impact on OS. TKIs’ effects can be strategically enhanced by immunotherapy, leading to a tumor response that is weak in magnitude but very durable [ ]. Preliminary research in a mouse model has partially supported this idea, demonstrating that an ALK vaccination reduced tumor relapse following TKI suspension [ ]. The concept of using TKIs with immunotherapy was first proposed in early 2010, and numerous pre-clinical and clinical studies have examined its potential benefits for the treatment of malignancy [ ]. While many studies continue, current conference discussions have revealed the remarkable ability of this outstanding combination. Overall, a more comprehensive investigation of the effectiveness and mechanisms of action of combined therapies using TKIs and immunotherapy is expected (Table ).
 

## Adverse effects of multi-TKIs combined with immunotherapy
TKIs can cause various side effects, including cardiovascular hypertension, gastrointestinal toxicity, cutaneous-type rash, hematologic neutropenias, hypothyroidism and concomitant electrolyte and renal impairment. These complications can lead to treatment cessation or interruption [ , ].
On the other hand, Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) can cause immune-mediated adverse reactions, dermal, gastrointestinal, endocrine, metabolic, and pulmonary toxicities, as well as less severe, potentially life-threatening irAEs [ , ]. Multi-TKIs plus ICIs-based combination treatment may lead to treatment adverse events (e.g., hypertension, liver dysfunction, diarrhea/colitis, immune-related AE, and fatigue). However, these effects are not typically worsened by ICIs [ ]. Evidence indicates that this combination therapy does not result in a statistically significant frequency of these AEs when compared with a single-agent TKI therapy [ ]. For example, Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) with axitinib (TKI) in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) achieved significantly higher efficacy and no substantially more severe AE than sunitinib (TKI) administered alone [ ]. Also, Atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) with bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) in RCC showed a favorable safety profile, with AEs consistent with those of each drug alone [ ]. Trials of nivolumab (anti-PD-1) plus cabozantinib and pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib also showed a manageable safety profile and no clinically significant worsening of AEs in the combination therapies [ ]. On the other hand, the combination of ICI and TKI may be associated with more complications than monotherapy, as a meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials examined various ICI-TKI combinations in metastatic clear cell RCC. While these combinations improve survival outcomes, they may be associated with increased risks of gastrointestinal and endocrine toxicities [ ]. Another meta-analysis investigated whether the combination of EGFR-TKI and ICI therapy increased the incidence of treatment-related adverse events (trAEs) compared with TKI monotherapy. The results showed that a higher proportion of grade ≥ 3 trAEs was observed with the combination of TKI and ICI compared with TKI alone. However, due to the small number of studies included in this meta-analysis, it cannot be interpreted precisely [ ].
Finally, cancer complexity and treatment resistance should be addressed to minimize side effects. Choosing other treatment regimens to use in combination with immunotherapy drugs can help reduce the incidence of immunotherapy-related side effects. This includes accurately determining the effectiveness of immunotherapy, identifying appropriate individuals and contraindications for treatment, and improving treatment efficacy for genetically mutated tumor cells.

## Future perspective

## Inspiring target, novel marker development, and precision medicine
Evidence suggests that several pathways and biomarkers may enhance the efficacy of TKIs. The inhibition of the JAK2/STAT signaling pathway has been associated with platinum-based therapies in ovarian cancer and thus may have utility in overcoming resistance [ , ]. The Fibrillin-1/VEGFR2/STAT2 axis has recently been linked to chemoresistance and tumor angiogenesis and may hence provide another promising target for TKIs [ ]. In current times, the function of microRNAs and non-coding RNAs has been played by predictive biomarkers and gene expression regulators, by which they are able to influence the response to TKIs, and thus new therapeutic applications are possible in gastrointestinal stromal tumors [ , ]. Apart from being aberrant in most cancers, the PI3K/AKT pathway is also postulated as one of the resistance mechanisms [ ].
Biomarkers will play a pivotal role in predicting patient outcomes with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immunotherapies, and in tailoring therapy to improve patient outcomes [ , ]. Emerging biomarkers such as PD-L1, HER2, and microsatellite instability have gained prevalence in many cancers. Nevertheless, a significant need remains for entirely new predictive biomarkers for TKIs and immune checkpoint inhibitors [ , ]. MicroRNAs and noncoding RNAs provide clues to understanding tumor biology and resistance mechanisms; however, circulating tumor cell genomics may identify actionable mutations for targeted therapies [ , ]. Clinical practice translocation will lead to precision medicine and clinical application, in which treatment will be tailored to the individual to maximize therapeutic benefit while reducing toxicity [ , ].
This has been one of the most refined paradigms in ontology, in that therapies are adapted to a patient’s genomic and molecular profile [ ]. Clinicians employ genomic profiling, for example, multiplex gene panel testing, in identifying specific mutations or biomarkers, hence guiding the choice of therapies such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors or immune checkpoint inhibitors, thereby optimizing efficacy and reducing adverse effects [ , ]. Integration of multi-TKIs into immunotherapies such as CAR-T cell therapy against targets like CD19 enhances antitumor activity, hence improving outcomes in cases of relapsed/refractory diseases [ ]. Precision medicine is a division concerned with the elaboration of therapies that overcome resistance mechanisms [ ]. Real-time monitoring tools, such as liquid biopsies and advanced imaging, are among the methods it uses to fine-tune treatment [ ]. Biomarker-driven approaches are under validation in clinical trials to demonstrate their efficacy and broader oncology applications [ , ]. It drives the development of new therapeutic combinations, such as multi-TKIs with CAR-T cells or ICIs, to improve long-term survival.

## Resistance to multi-TKIs and approaches to overcome it
Understanding the mechanisms of resistance to multi-TKIs is essential for the design of new small-molecule inhibitors (SMIs). Mechanisms of resistance may arise from genetic mutations, changes in drug metabolism, and tumor microenvironmental dynamics [ ]. There is an accumulation of mutations in target proteins, rendering existing TKIs ineffective; hence, new-generation inhibitors are being developed.
Because these agents act through different mechanisms of action on tumor antigens, combining TKIs with cell-based immunotherapies, notably CAR T cells and CAR NK cells, shows immense promise for overcoming resistance and improving anti-tumor immunity. These therapies target specific tumor antigens, thereby enabling the killing of cancer cells resistant to TKIs [ , ]. This approach overcomes some of the main hurdles in cancer therapy by allowing direct targeting of tumor cells while reprogramming the immune system to recognize and destroy resistant clones [ ]. In line with clinical settings, in vitro experiments showed that combining TKIs with CAR-T cells enhanced antitumor responses by remodeling the TME to support CAR-T cell persistence and function [ , , , ]. The addition of TKIs to bispecific antibodies can further enhance outcomes by reshaping the immune landscape of TME, thereby improving the overall effectiveness of cancer therapy [ ].
Lysosomes have been key players in multidrug resistance (MDR) to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in cancer therapy [ , ]. They confer “drug safe-houses,” sequestering chemotherapeutics and contributing to MDR [ , ]. Altered lysosome-to-nucleus signaling via the TFEB-mTORC1 axis is implicated in the development of chemoresistance [ ]. Targeting lysosomes has been a potential strategy to overcome MDR and induce apoptosis-resistant cell death in cancer cells [ ]. Some lysosome-targeting drugs can resensitize MDR cells to classical chemotherapy [ , ]. Understanding how TKIs interact with MDR-related transporters is essential for developing effective cancer therapies.
The current treatment is mainly focused on antibody-drug conjugates and bispecific antibodies (which have already been demonstrated to have potential for overcoming TKI resistance in cancer treatment). Antibody–drug conjugates can be used to deliver therapeutic agents to tumour cells in a highly selective fashion, and bispecific antibodies can be used to bind to a target and other targets in one binding event [ – ]. These agents have shown potential to overcome leading resistance mechanisms, including on-target and off-target effects, changes in tumor histology, and the microenvironment [ , ]. Feedback activation blockade targets compensatory signaling pathways that cancer cells may exploit to evade therapy, potentially preventing or delaying the onset of resistance [ , ].
Nanocarrier-based delivery systems for MTKi offer a promising approach to overcoming drug resistance and improving cancer treatment outcomes. They enhance drug activity, reduce adverse effects, and improve overall bioavailability [ , ]. Solid lipid nanoparticles are nanocarriers that can efficiently deliver MTKi orally, thereby reducing formulation challenges and potentially enhancing therapeutic outcomes [ ]. In multi-functional nanocarriers, resistance modulators, pH regulators, and inducers of apoptosis may be released simultaneously or sequentially to improve the efficacy of the anticancer drug against multi-drug-resistant tumors [ – ]. Nanoparticle-based delivery systems for MTKi are also potentially able to overcome key limitations of conventional therapies.

## Conclusion
Multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have strong immunomodulatory capabilities across various immune cell populations in cancer, offering both benefits and drawbacks in cancer treatment. These inhibitors not only have a direct effect on cancer cells but also play a complex role in the tumor immune microenvironment. TKIs have been shown to boost antitumor immunity by enhancing T cell function, promoting M1 macrophage differentiation, and increasing the presence of CD8 + T cells within tumors. Additionally, some TKIs may reduce populations of immunosuppressive immune cells, such as regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, potentially increasing the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapies. However, the immunomodulatory activity of TKIs is not always beneficial. Certain TKIs can cause lymphopenia and suppress the function of specific immune cells. The interaction between TKIs and the immune system underscores the importance of careful consideration when combining TKIs with immunotherapy.
