## Background & Aims
Previous data suggest that patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) have a better prognosis compared with those with other biliary tract cancers (BTC). However, granularity on outcomes for advanced extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) (including distal- [dCCA] and perihilar cholangiocarcinoma [pCCA]) and gallbladder cancer (GBC) is lacking. With targeted therapies being developed for these subgroups, benchmark data are of value to understand the natural history and inform future trial design.

## Methods
The aim of this post hoc analysis was to provide reference survival data for patients with advanced eCCA treated with first-line cisplatin–gemcitabine (CisGem) chemotherapy within the prospective, randomised Advanced Biliary tract Cancer (ABC)-01, -02, and -03 studies. Individual-level data from patients with eCCA and GBC recruited to these studies were retrieved. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier and univariate and multivariable Cox regression. Logistic regression was utilised whenever required.

## Results
Of 534 patients recruited into the ABC-01, -02, and -03 studies, eligible patients for this analysis included a total of 117 eCCA treated with CisGem (including 68 with pCCA and 49 with dCCA) and 112 with GBC. Patients with pCCA had less previous surgery (4.08%) and the highest rate of biliary stenting (26.53%). Patients with dCCA were predominantly metastatic at study entry (77.94%), while those with pCCA had a rate of 40.82% and 59.18% for locally advanced and metastatic disease at study entry, respectively. Most patients in the GBC cohort were women (62.50%) and predominantly metastatic (81.25%). Estimated median overall survival (OS) for dCCA was 14.25 months (95% CI: 8.80–17.44) and 12.18 months (95% CI: 8.31–16.12) for pCCA. Estimated median progression-free survival (PFS) was 8.28 months (95% CI: 6.31-9.39) for dCCA and 8.37 months (95% CI: 6.53–10.61) for pCCA. PFS and OS for GBC were 6.86 months (95% CI: 5.75–8.51) and 10.84 months (95% CI: 8.97–12.41), respectively. Objective radiological response was 23.93% for eCCA: (dCCA [27.94%] and pCCA [18.37%]) and 26.79% for the GBC cohort. Prognostic factors of interest for OS included performance status (PS) and CA125 for eCCA, and grade of differentiation for GBC. For OS in iCCA, dCCA, pCCA, and GBC, multivariable analysis confirmed that patients with GBC had the shortest OS (hazard ratio 2.01 [95% CI: 1.18–3.43]; p value 0.011 [vs. iCCA]) when adjusted for other prognostic factors (PS, stage, and tumour markers).

## Conclusions
Within the BTC subgroups, GBC had the worse survival. PS and baseline tumour markers (CA125 and CEA) were strongly associated with worse survival. These subgroup data provide a reference for future studies incorporating immunotherapy and other new treatment strategies for BTCs.

## Impact and implications
The data presented here provide benchmark outcome data for subgroups of patients with eCCA and GBC treated with CisGem for future clinical trial design. Despite the absence of immunotherapy, the lack of alternative comprehensive series highlights the value of the current series. The data presented here could be utilised for interpretation of current studies and design of future trials, by providing outcome data for main BTC subgroups of interest.

## Introduction
Biliary tract cancers (BTCs), which include intrahepatic (iCCA), perihilar (pCCA), and distal (dCCA) cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), gallbladder (GBC), and ampullary carcinoma (AC), are typically diagnosed in individuals aged 50–70 years and their prognosis is poor. Incidence is increasing, predominantly in iCCA, with improvements in recognition among liver cancer and cancer of unknown primary.
Treatment for advanced BTC has changed in many aspects over the past few years. First, immunotherapy, added to cisplatin and gemcitabine (CisGem) is now considered standard of care for advanced BTC. This recommendation is based on two randomised first-line clinical trials, which tested the role of durvalumab and pembrolizumab. Both studies confirmed improved outcomes with the addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors to CisGem chemotherapy, with a similar magnitude of benefit.
Second, targeted therapies are now a treatment option for selected patient subgroups. In this regard, approved therapies are of particular relevance for iCCA, with both FGFR2 fusions and IDH1 mutations being most prevalent in this BTC subgroup. Ivosidenib is approved for IDH-1 mutated CCA, based on the ClarIDHy clinical trial, which showed improved progression-free survival (PFS) over placebo in the second-line setting, as well as improved survival after adjustment for patient crossover from the placebo arm to ivosidenib. For FGFR2 fusion-positive CCA, both pemigatinib and futibatinib have been approved based on high response rates seen in their respective phase II clinical trials after progression on chemotherapy. These two targets are mainly present in iCCA, with almost no events in extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) or GBC. Thus, precision medicine has been mainly developed in this setting. 
However, an increasing number of other targeted therapies of interest are being developed. Some of these are more frequent in other non-iCCA BTCs, such as eCCA and GBC. Many studies have shown the presence of potentially actionable targets, such as ERBB2, PIK3CA, BRAF, MDM2, KRAS, TMB, MSI, or germline mutations in BRCA and ATM, , , , , some of which are of particular interest in GBC and eCCA.
HER-2 amplifications have been described in ∼21% of non-iCCA BTC. Two clinical trials are exploring the role of HER-2-targeting agents in the first-line setting: one with zanidatamab and one with trastuzumab-deruxtecan (NCT06467357). This is building on promising findings (overall response rate [ORR] of 41–56%) identified in two phase II monotherapy trials in the second-line setting, , resulting in FDA/EMA and FDA-tumour agnostic approvals, respectively. MTAP deletions have also been described in up to 15% of patients with BTC. Recently developed PRMT5 inhibitors are now providing targeted therapy options for these patients following a synthetic lethality approach, including BTC. MDM2 amplifications have also been identified in non-iCCA BTC. Brigimadlin, an MDM2 inhibitor, showed promising activity in BTC with TP53 wild-type and MDM2 amplified tumours, which followed up on the design of a BTC-dedicated phase II study. Unfortunately, this compound is no longer being developed, but MDM2 amplification remains a potential target in these patients.
Other targets of interest include KRAS mutations, which are of particular interest in eCCA and GBC. More infrequent alterations can appear in any BTC subgroup, such as BRAF mutations, with phase II trial data supporting targeted strategies with dabrafenib and trametinib in this setting. 
In summary, targeted therapies for eCCA and GBC are rapidly evolving and understanding the natural behaviour of these tumours when treated with standard of care therapy is of importance, not only to put some of these results into context, but also to allow the design of future clinical trials for these BTC subgroups. The latest TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-9664 studies, with CisGem combined with durvalumab and pembrolizumab, respectively, have not, to date, reported on the outcomes of these populations individually. Thus, their performance with chemoimmunotherapy is not well defined.
This post hoc analysis provides survival data to inform the design, sample size, and feasibility of future systemic therapy studies for eCCA and GBC. The outcomes for patients with iCCA have previously been reported. 

## Patients and methods
A post hoc analysis of individual-patient level data collected as part of the prospective ABC-01,29 ABC-02,2 and ABC-0330 clinical trials was performed. These studies were sponsored by University College, London and coordinated by the Cancer Research UK and UCL Cancer Trials Centre (London, UK) which facilitated access to anonymised individual-patient data. These studies explored the role of CisGem (vs. gemcitabine alone) (ABC-01 and ABC-02) and the potential benefit from adding cediranib to CisGem (over CisGem alone; ABC-03). , , 
Patients diagnosed with eCCA and GBC who had received CisGem in the ABC-01 and ABC-02 clinical trials were included. Given that the addition of cediranib in the ABC-03 clinical trial had no impact on overall survival (OS), patients diagnosed with eCCA and GBC in both arms (CisGem + placebo and CisGem + cediranib) of the ABC-03 trial were also included.
Clinical data, including demographics, baseline tumour markers, complete blood count, renal/liver profile, treatment characteristics, and response/survival data, were retrieved. The best radiological response achieved by each patient was classified based on the version of Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumours (RECIST) used in each study: RECISTv1.0 for ABC-01/-02 and RECISTv1.1 for ABC-03.
The primary aim of this post hoc analysis was to provide reference OS data of patients diagnosed with eCCA (both combined and separately for pCCA and dCCA) and GBC treated with first-line CisGem chemotherapy for future prospective studies. Secondary objectives included description of baseline characteristics of each subgroup, PFS, and response rate.

## Statistical analysis
All eligible patients were included in the analysis. PFS and OS were defined as the time from randomisation to either documented disease progression or death from any cause, respectively. Patients without a PFS or OS event at the time of analysis were censored at their last recorded follow-up date. Descriptive analysis used medians with 95% CI for continues variables and percentages for categorical variables. The Kaplan-Meier method and Cox regression were utilised for survival analysis. Variables significant in the univariate Cox regression (defined as p <0.05) were included in the multivariable analysis. Patients with missing data for the variables included in the multivariable analysis were excluded. Hazard ratio (HR), 95% CI, and p values were reported. Logistic regression was utilised whenever required (i.e. for analysis of ORR data). Wherever the proportional hazards (PH) assumption held by assessing Schoenfeld residuals (whenever p <0.05, this was interpreted as a significant violation of the PH assumption for the variable or the model globally). Stata version 12 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA) was used for analysis. Two-sided p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results
A total of 534 patients were assessed for eligibility (CONSORT diagram is provided in ). Of the 534 patients included in the ABC-01, ABC-02, and ABC-03 trials, 328 were diagnosed with CCA, 188 GBC, and 28 AC. A total of 328 (61.42%) patients had been treated with CisGem. The breakdown by primary tumour type of the CisGem-treated population is shown in . 
In total, eligible patients for this analysis included a total of 117 patients with eCCA treated with CisGem (including 68 with pCCA and 49 with dCCA) and 112 with GBC. They accounted for a total of 35.67 (eCCA) (20.73% (dCCA) and 14.94% (pCCA)) and 34.15% (GBC) of all CisGem-treated BTCs included in these trials, respectively.

## Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: baseline characteristics
Baseline characteristics for the eCCA cohort are provided in (columns 1–3). Those with pCCA were the group with the lowest frequency of previous surgery (4.08%) and the highest rate of biliary stenting (26.53%). Those with dCCA were predominantly metastatic at study entry (77.94%), whereas those with pCCA had a rate of 40.82% and 59.18% for locally advanced and metastatic disease at study entry, respectively. Extrahepatic disease was identified in 22.06% and 18.37% of patients with dCCA and pCCA, respectively. When all eCCA were analysed together, the most frequent site of metastases was the liver (35.0%), followed by peritoneum (11.9%). 

## Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: patient outcomes
A summary of outcomes are provided in (columns 1–3). Median follow-up for the eCCA cohort was 11.89 months (95% CI: 8.76–14.31). At the time of analysis, of the whole eCCA cohort, 86.32% had progressed and 82.05% had died. 
ORR (complete response [CR] + partial response [PR]) was achieved in 28 (23.93%) patients with eCCA: slightly higher for dCCA (19 patients; 27.94%) than for pCCA (nine patients; 18.37%). However, the median PFS and OS were similar across groups. Estimated PFS was 8.28 months (95% CI: 6.31–9.39) for dCCA and 8.37 months (95% CI: 6.53–10.61) for pCCA. Estimated OS was longer for dCCA than for pCCA: 14.25 months (95% CI: 8.80–17.44) vs. 12.18 months (95% CI: 8.31–16.12), despite this group receiving the lowest rate of second-line therapy (5.88% vs. 14.29%).

## Gallbladder cancer: baseline characteristics
Baseline characteristics for the GBC cohort are provided in (column 4). Most patients in the GBC cohort were women (62.50%). A total of 13 patients (11.61%) had previous surgery and only 9.82% had previous stenting. The GBC cohort was predominantly metastatic at study entry (81.25%), with 39.29% having extrahepatic disease. In GBC, liver, peritoneum, and lung metastases were present in 34.82%, 25.89%, and 8.04% of patients, respectively. Median CA125 appeared to be slightly higher in the GBC group (46.5 U/ml; 95% CI: 31.92–68.08) compared with the eCCA cohorts (24 U/ml; 95% CI: 18–38).

## Gallbladder cancer: patient outcomes
A summary of outcomes is provided in (columns 1–3). Median follow-up for the GBC cohort was 8.99 months (95% CI: 8.28–10.71). At the time of analysis, 87.50% and 79.46% had progressed and died, respectively.
In the GBC cohort, ORR was 26.79%, with a 69.64% disease control rate; in 42.86%, this was in the form of disease stabilisation. Median duration of chemotherapy was 4.83 months (95% CI: 3.93–5.06). Estimated median PFS and OS were worse for GBC vs. all the other groups explored: 6.86 months (95% CI: 5.75–8.51) and 10.84 months (95% CI: 8.97–12.41), respectively; with 14.29% of patients receiving second-line therapy.

## Outcomes by disease stage: locally advanced vs. metastatic disease
OS, PFS, and ORR were analysed by disease stage for all subgroups. Patients with locally advanced disease had a numerically longer OS compared with those with metastatic disease, and a similar trend was observed for PFS ( ). In terms of ORR, findings were comparable, regardless of stage. Full details are provided in . 

## Prognostic factors for the eCCA and GBC cohorts
The survival analysis for OS was performed separately for eCCA and GBC ( ). 
For the OS analysis in the eCCA cohort, factors significant in the univariate analysis ( , column 1) included performance status (PS), stage (metastatic disease associated with shorter OS), CA19-9, and CA125. There were no differences by gender. Multivariable survival analysis for OS confirmed worse OS for eCCA with a PS of 2 (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 45.77; 95% CI: 9.55–219.45; p <0.001) and high baseline CA125 (aHR 1.0007; 95% CI: 1.0001–1.001; p = 0.017) ( A, column 2). These two factors remained significant when adjusted for site of eCCA (pCCA and dCCA) ( , column 3), with aHRs of 44.87 (95% CI: 9.34–215.67; p <0.001)and 1.0007 (95% CI: 1.0001–1.001; p = 0.018), respectively. Primary eCCA was not a significant prognostic factor.
In the GBC cohort, both high CA125 and moderate differentiation were associated with OS in the univariate analysis ( , column 1). Again, no differences were identified by gender. In the multivariable analysis, moderate differentiation (aHR 0.11; 95% CI: 0.03–0.43; p = 0.002) and poor differentiation (aHR 0.23; 95% CI: 0.06–0.89; p = 0.033) were independent factors associated with longer OS (vs. well-differentiated tumours; , column 2).

## Factors associated with PFS and ORR in the eCCA and GBC cohorts
Survival analysis for PFS was performed separately for eCCA and GBC ( ). 
When survival analysis was adjusted for eCCA site, eCCA site (dCCA or pCCA) was not a prognostic factor ( , column 1). In the multivariable analysis adjusted for eCCA site, PS [PS1; aHR 2.90; 95% CI: 1.11–7.55; p = 0.029] and PS2 [aHR 13.24; 95% CI: 2.09–83.49; p = 0.006] vs. PS0), metastatic stage (aHR 4.19; 95% CI: 1.20–14.61; p = 0.024) and high CA125 (aHR 1.001; 95% CI: 1.0004–1.01; p = 0.002) were associated with shorter PFS ( , column 3).
For PFS in the GBC cohort, only higher CEA was associated with shorter OS ( , column 1); thus, no multivariable analysis was performed. However, univariate logistic regression identified no factors associated with a higher ORR in either cohort (eCCA or GBC) ( ).

## eCCA and GBC compared with other BTCs
The outcomes of patients with iCCA treated with CisGem have been previously analysed and reported. When iCCA was compared with the other CisGem-treated BTC groups combined, the PFS was similar (8.4 months [95% CI: 5.9–8.9] for iCCA vs. 7.8 months [95% CI: 6.5–8.4] for non-iCCA BTC; log rank p = 0.6467). In terms of iCCA OS was longer (15.4 months; 95% CI: 11.1–17.9) compared with other non-iCCA BTC (11.7 months; 95% CI: 10.2–12.6) (aHR 0.58; 95% CI: 0.35–0.95; p = 0.03). Median OS was even longer for liver-only iCCA (16.7 months; 95% CI: 8.7–20.2; aHR 0.65; 95% CI: 0.36–1.19; p = 0.16).
In the analysis presented here, the CisGem-treated non-iCCA BTC group was divided into cohorts of interest (dCCA, pCCA, and GBC) when compared with CisGem-treated iCCA. Sixteen patients with CCA without primary site specification and 17 patients with AC were excluded; thus, a total of 295 patients were included (68 with dCCA, 49 with pCCA, 66 with iCCA, and 112 with GBC). Based on previous findings that patients with iCCA have a better outcome, this group was utilised for reference.
For OS, patients with GBC had the shortest OS ( ; ); differences were statistically significant in the univariate analysis (unadjusted hazard ratio [uHR] for GBC 1.43; 95% CI: 1.01–2.02; p = 0.044 vs. iCCA). Survival analysis including dCCA, pCCA, iCCA, and GBC identified the primary tumour site, CEA, CA19.9 and CA125 baseline levels, PS, and stage as significant factors impacting OS on univariate analysis ( ). Multivariable analysis confirmed GBC (aHR 2.01; 95% CI: 1.18–3.43; p = 0.011 vs. iCCA), elevated CEA (aHR 1.002; 95% CI: 1.0001–1.001; p = 0.041) and CA125 (aHR 1.001; 95% CI: 1.0001–1.01; p ≤0.001), and PS1 (aHR 1.80; 95% CI: 1.19–2.73; p = 0.005 vs. PS0) and PS2 (aHR 5.44; 95% CI: 2.47–-11.99; p ≤0.001 vs. PS0) as independent prognostic factors associated with worse OS ( ). 
In relation to PFS ( and ), GBC had the worst outcomes (dCCA median PFS 6.63 months; 95% CI: 4.40–8.44; pCCA median PFS 7.55 months; 95% CI: 4.93–8.37; iCCA median PFS 7.55 months; 95% CI: 5.35–8.47; GBC median PFS 6.30 months; 95% CI: 5.71–7.36; ), even though differences did not reach statistical significance (HR 1.25; 95% CI: 0.91–1.73; p = 0.170). For dCCA and pCCA, outcomes were similar to iCCA. Full survival analysis is provided in ).

## Discussion
This post hoc pooled analysis of individual-patient data from the pivotal ABC trials provides one of the largest dedicated prospective evaluations of outcomes for patients diagnosed with advanced eCCA and GBC treated with first-line CisGem. Outcomes of iCCA had been previously reported and there was better OS with a iCCA diagnosis over other non-iCCA BTCs. However, outcome data for eCCA and GBC have often been reported collectively in prospective trials, limiting the ability to design subtype-specific trials. This has become a greater challenge with the arrival of targeted therapies of special interest in these subgroups. Our findings confirm important biological and clinical distinctions between eCCA and GBC, with implications for prognosis, trial design, and emerging therapeutic strategies. This is of particular importance when one realises the high proportion of patients with BTC who have these subgroup primary site diagnoses: in our series, dCCA, pCCA, and GBC represented a total 20.73%, 14.94%, and 34.15% of all CisGem-treated BTCs included in these trials, respectively.
Among patients with eCCA, OS, and PFS were broadly comparable to iCCA. Importantly, no survival differences were observed between pCCA and dCCA, suggesting that anatomical location within the extrahepatic biliary tree does not independently dictate prognosis once disease stage and baseline characteristics are accounted for. As expected, pCCA had a higher rate of stenting and less previous surgical resection. The GBC cohort was predominantly female and metastatic.
Interestingly, prognostic factors identified within these eCCA subgroups were mainly PS and increasing tumour markers, particularly CA125. CA125 was also one of the factors identified in the univariate analysis for GBC. Even though its prognostic role was not confirmed in the multivariable analysis for the GBC cohort, when dCCA, pCCA, iCCA, and GBC data were analysed together, this factor arose again as a strong prognostic marker, together with CEA. Our results confirm previous evidence in this regard and highlights the importance of testing for not only CA19-9 (which had less of a prognostic role), but also CEA and CA125 in BTC. Data from the second-line ABC-06 clinical trial would support their combined use, with shorter survival associated with both markers being increased at baseline. 
Histological differentiation in GBC emerged as a prognostic factor, with moderately and poorly differentiated tumours paradoxically associated with longer OS compared with well-differentiated tumours. Although counterintuitive, this could reflect selection bias or small subgroup sizes, and warrants evaluation in larger datasets.
The impact of stage (locally advanced vs. metastatic) was analysed. This is an important topic in the setting of iCCA and, therefore, was explored also in these non-iCCA BTC cohorts. As previously reported, liver-only iCCA (which could be considered as ‘locally advanced’), had better outcomes. In fact, data from the European Network for the Study of Cholangiocarcinoma registry supports the fact that liver metastases from iCCA have worse outcomes compared with other ‘early stages’, thus supporting adjustment to the current American Joint Committee on Cancer classification, but having better outcomes versus other M1 ‘extrahepatic’ disease. 
Data from the SWOG S1815 randomised phase III clinical trial exploring the role of CisGem with or without nab-paclitaxel for first-line advanced BTC, identified no benefit from the addition of nab-paclitaxel in terms of OS. However, in the exploratory subset analyses, the OS and PFS benefits of the triplet combination were greater (even though not statistically significant) in locally advanced disease compared with metastatic disease. Interestingly, PFS improvement was also numerically higher for GBC. In the setting of our analysis for the eCCA and GBC cohorts, conclusions vary. In our analysis, and as expected, patients with locally advanced disease had longer OS compared with those with metastatic disease for all subgroups. However, its prognostic role was not confirmed in the OS analysis performed either in eCCA or GBC. When the whole survival analysis, including dCCA, pCCA, iCCA, and GBC, was adjusted for stage, results did not hold, and stage was not an independent prognostic factor for OS. In terms of ORR and PFS, findings were comparable regardless of stage, with variability likely the result of sample size. In our series, stage was only associated with longer PFS in the eCCA cohort, with no significant impact on GBC.
Most importantly, patients with GBC had the poorest outcomes of all BTC subtypes, with a median OS of 10.8 months and PFS of 6.9 months, despite similar ORR to eCCA. This is in keeping with previous research. Importantly, compared with iCCA, GBC was independently associated with a twofold higher risk of death (aHR 2.01, p = 0.011). This is in keeping with previous literature and underscores the aggressive natural history of GBC, which is also reflected in the predominance of metastatic presentation (81%) and frequent peritoneal dissemination (26%). This highlights the unmet need that this disease represents, especially in its advanced stages.
Although the ABC-02 trial established CisGem as the standard of care first-line treatment for advanced BTC, subgroup analyses were limited. Our dedicated analysis addressed this knowledge gap, confirming the relative chemosensitivity of eCCA and the particularly poor prognosis of GBC. These findings mirror epidemiological data highlighting the distinct molecular landscapes of these subtypes: FGFR2 fusions and IDH1 mutations predominate in iCCA, whereas HER2 amplification, KRAS mutations, MDM2 amplifications, and MTAP loss are enriched in eCCA and GBC. Therefore, the present survival benchmarks provide crucial context for interpreting outcomes in ongoing and future molecularly targeted or immunotherapy trials, especially in the setting of clinical trials being developed in BTC-specific subgroups, for whom no benchmark reliable data were previously available.
Notably, recent first-line chemoimmunotherapy studies (TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966) have established CisGem plus durvalumab or pembrolizumab as new standards for first-line treatment of advanced BTC. However, subgroup data for eCCA and GBC were not separately reported, limiting their applicability to these populations. Our findings, even though they include patients treated with CisGem in the absence of checkpoint inhibition, fill an important gap, offering reference OS and PFS estimates that can guide sample size calculations and feasibility assessments in dedicated eCCA and GBC trials testing for first-line targeted approaches in the future.
The strengths of this analysis include the use of individual patient-level data from three large prospective randomised trials, standardised treatment exposure, and robust follow-up. These features enable reliable benchmarking of outcomes across subgroups. Limitations include the post hoc design and the absence of molecular data. In addition, some of the missing data reduced the number of observations available in the multivariable survival analysis, thus limiting our statistical power. Somewhat surprising was the low number of patients who were reported to have a biliary stent in situ; this is probably related to an underreporting of such an event taking place before study entry rather than a true lack of presence of biliary stenting and reflects an additional limitation. Our series does not include any patients treated with checkpoint inhibitors, which also represents a relevant limitation. However, taking into account the modest impact seen in OS and PFS in BTC with either durvalumab or pembrolizumab, we believe that the benchmark outcome data provided here are still informative for clinical trial design with immunotherapy. Finally, small sample sizes in certain subgroups, such as those with locally advanced disease, necessitate cautious interpretation of stage-stratified analyses.
In summary, this post hoc pooled analysis provides the most comprehensive assessment to date of outcomes for eCCA and GBC treated with first-line CisGem. Whereas eCCA exhibits outcomes broadly comparable with iCCA, GBC is associated with markedly inferior survival, highlighting its aggressive biology. Performance status and tumour markers (CA125 and CEA) are key prognostic factors across subtypes. These data offer essential benchmarks for designing and powering future prospective studies, particularly in the era of precision medicine for these BTC subgroups.
