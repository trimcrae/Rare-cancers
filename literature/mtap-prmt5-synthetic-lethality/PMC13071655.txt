## Abstract
Thymic carcinoma (TC) is a rare, aggressive subset of thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) with a poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Representing approximately 15–20% of TETs, TC is distinct from thymoma in its histopathologic features, lack of paraneoplastic autoimmune syndromes, and more complex genomic landscape. Surgical resection remains the primary modality for early-stage disease, while platinum-based chemotherapy forms the cornerstone of treatment for advanced or unresectable TC. Advancements in targeted therapies have expanded therapeutic options, resulting in improved clinical efficacy, especially in tumors with high angiogenic activity or specific mutations (e.g., Kit). Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has shown activity in TC, with response rates around 20%, and is being explored in combination with chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic agents, and CTLA-4 blockade. Combinatorial strategies have demonstrated enhanced response rates but require vigilant management of immune-related adverse events. Novel therapeutic approaches are emerging, including PRMT5 inhibitors in MTAP-deficient tumors, TROP-2-directed antibody-drug conjugates (e.g., sacituzumab govitecan), and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies targeting mesothelin. Bispecific agents such as bintrafusp alfa and ivonescimab, which co-target various pathways, offer innovative strategies. Despite these advances, TC remains a challenging malignancy with no standardized treatment algorithm. Collaborative efforts across institutions will be essential to accelerate progress and improve outcomes in this rare disease.
Keywords: Thymic carcinoma (TC), targeted therapies, immunotherapy

## Introduction
Thymic carcinomas (TCs) are rare, aggressive malignancies arising from the epithelial cells of the thymus gland. They account for approximately 15–20% of thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) and represent a distinct clinical and biological entity from thymomas, characterized by more invasive behavior, higher metastatic potential, and poorer prognosis ( ). The annual incidence of TETs in the United States is estimated at 1.5–2.7 cases per million, with TCs comprising a minority of cases, making large-scale prospective studies challenging ( - ).
Historically, treatment paradigms for TC have been extrapolated from thymoma and other thoracic malignancies, but advances in molecular characterization and clinical research have begun to inform more tailored approaches. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for early-stage disease, while multimodal strategies, including chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and emerging targeted and immune-based therapies, are increasingly utilized for advanced or unresectable tumors ( ). However, standardized treatment guidelines are limited, based on expert consensus due to lack of high-level evidence ( , ). Overall outcomes remain suboptimal, with 5-year overall survival (OS) rates ranging from 30–60% depending on stage and resectability ( , , ).

## Staging, pathologic & genomic features
The staging of TETs, including TCs, has evolved since its inception in 1978 with the Masaoka-Koga system being the most used around the world ( , ). However, with the proposal of the tumor, node, metastasis (TNM) system, there has been a transition to use of this system with recent changes as part of the publication of the ninth addition ( ). The recent analysis of across numerous databases continued to support discrimination of outcomes between stage IVa, which includes N1 or M1a tumors [separate pleural or pericardial nodule(s)] versus stage IVb, which includes N2 and M1b tumors [pulmonary intraparenchymal nodule(s) or distant organ metastasis] though the difference was more notable in the thymoma subset ( ). This difference is fundamentally highlighted by the clinical utility of surgery combined with hyperthermic intrathoracic chemotherapy (HITOC) for stage IVa thymomas, while use of surgery with HITOC for stage IVa TC remains unclear ( ).
TCs are histologically distinct from thymomas, exhibiting overt cytologic atypia, increased mitotic activity, necrosis, and a lack of organotypic thymic features such as lobulation or immature T-cell infiltration ( , ). The most common histologic subtype is squamous cell carcinoma, which accounts for approximately 70% of cases, followed by rarer variants such as lymphoepithelioma-like, basaloid, sarcomatoid, mucoepidermoid, and adenocarcinomas ( , ). Unlike thymomas, TCs do not typically harbor medullary differentiation or perivascular spaces and are not associated with paraneoplastic autoimmune syndromes ( ). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining often demonstrates positivity for cytokeratins (CK5/6), p40, p63, and CD5, with CD117 (c-Kit) expressed in up to 80% of tumors, serving as a useful diagnostic marker to distinguish TCs from other mediastinal neoplasms ( , ).
Genomic profiling of TCs has revealed a distinct molecular landscape compared to thymoma. TCs harbor a higher mutational burden and display frequent alterations in tumor suppressor genes such as TP53 and CDKN2A, which are rarely seen in thymomas ( - ). Additional recurrent mutations have been observed in genes involved in cell cycle regulation, DNA repair pathways, chromatin remodeling (e.g., SMARCA4), and receptor tyrosine kinase pathways, including c-Kit and FGFR3 ( , - ). While targetable mutations are infrequent, the identification of c-KIT mutations in a subset of patients has led to the successful use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as imatinib in select cases ( - ). Comprehensive molecular characterization remains limited due to the rarity of TCs, but ongoing studies and prior participation in rare tumor sequencing efforts, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas, continue to enhance our understanding of the biologic drivers of this aggressive disease.

## Management of advanced, unresectable and metastatic TC

## Cytotoxic chemotherapy
Platinum-based chemotherapy remains the cornerstone for the treatment of inoperable TC, with multiple combination strategies demonstrating variable efficacy and toxicity (summarized in ). Regimens including cisplatin, ifosfamide, and etoposide have shown response rates of 25% ( , ). Doxorubicin-containing regimens such as ADOC (cisplatin, doxorubicin, vincristine, and cyclophosphamide) and CODE (cisplatin, vincristine, doxorubicin, and etoposide) have reported higher response rates of 42–75%, with a median progression-free survival (mPFS) ranging from 5.6 to 9.4 months in patients with stage III–IV disease, though with the potential for increased toxicity ( , , ). Cisplatin and irinotecan was evaluated for front-line therapy in a retrospective study reporting an objective response rate (ORR) of 55.6% with an mPFS and median overall survival (mOS) of 7.9 and 33.8 months, respectively ( ). The carboplatin and paclitaxel combination has been evaluated in two studies of advanced disease, yielding response rates of 21.7–36% and mPFS of 5–7.5 months ( , ).
 The addition of anti-angiogenic agents to platinum-based chemotherapy has also been explored. Wang et al. conducted a retrospective analysis comparing gemcitabine/cisplatin with or without Endostar, a recombinant human endostatin targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2) signaling, in 45 patients with invasive metastatic thymic tumors ( ). Among TC patients, Endostar improved ORRs from 46.7% to 71.4%. The RELEVENT trial, a multicenter phase II study, evaluated carboplatin, paclitaxel, and the VEGFR2 inhibitor, ramucirumab, in treatment-naïve advanced TC. Reported ORRs were 57.6% (central review) and 80.0% (investigator-assessed/local review), with an mPFS and OS of 18.1 and 43.8 months, respectively. The study noted the discrepancy in ORR for central vs. local review that was due to two patients deemed ineligible for the efficacy evaluation due to lack of target lesions on baseline radiographs. This highlights the difficulty of performing these studies given the variability of disease assessment with specific disease localization. Common toxicities included peripheral neuropathy (45.7%), fatigue (40.0%), and hematologic toxicities (31.4–42.9%) ( ).
Following progression on first-line therapy, cytotoxic options remain limited. Kanda et al. retrospectively analyzed seven patients treated with irinotecan plus platinum (cisplatin or carboplatin) after ADOC failure, noting an ORR of 28.6% and an mOS of 17.5 months ( ). Palmieri et al. evaluated gemcitabine plus capecitabine in 30 patients with previously treated advanced TETs, including eight with TC; partial responses were observed in three patients (37.5%) ( ). Single-agent therapies have also been explored: S-1, an oral fluoropyrimidine derivative, was studied in 26 patients with platinum-refractory TC, noting an ORR of 30.8% ( ). The mPFS and mOS were 4.3 and 27.4 months, respectively. Amrubicin, a third generation anthracycline and topoisomerase II inhibitor, was evaluated in a phase II study of treatment refractory thymoma and TC ( ). Of the 19 TC patients enrolled on the study, 11% experienced an ORR and 84% had stable disease as the best response. The mPFS and mOS were 7.3 and 18 months, respectively, with the safety analysis noting high frequency of Grade 3 and 4 febrile neutropenia and a Grade 5 febrile neutropenia for which the protocol was amended to reduce the starting dose from 40 to 35 mg/m2 on days 1–3 of a 21-day cycle. Pemetrexed and etoposide showed ORRs of 9% and 13%, with an mPFS of 2.9 and 4 months, respectively, in small cohorts ( , ). Though modest in efficacy, these agents may be appropriate for older or frail patients seeking cancer-directed therapy.

## Target therapies
Several targeted therapies have been evaluated for patients with TC who progress following first-line platinum-based chemotherapy (summarized in ). The proto-oncogene c-Kit, which regulates cell proliferation and survival, is overexpressed in a subset of TCs. C-Kit mutations have been identified in approximately 6–20% of cases ( , , ). While early phase II studies of imatinib showed limited efficacy, subsequent findings clarified that Kit amplification, present in 80–86% of tumors, does not predict sensitivity to TKIs, as most cases lack activating mutations ( , , - ).
 Angiogenesis is another critical pathway implicated in TC progression. Immunohistochemistry studies have demonstrated increased expression of VEGFR and greater microvascular density in more aggressive TET subtypes, including B2/B3 thymomas and TCs ( - ). This led to the evaluation of multi-target TKIs such as sunitinib, which inhibits VEGFR, KIT, and platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) ( ). In a phase II trial of 40 patients (16 thymoma, 24 TC), sunitinib yielded an ORR of 26% and a disease control rate (DCR) of 91% among TC patients, with an mPFS of 7.2 months (95% CI: 3.4–15.2); OS was not reached at the time of publication ( ). Grade 3–4 adverse events (AEs) occurred in 70% of patients, including lymphocytopenia, fatigue, mucositis, and five cases of reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. A modified dosing schedule (2 weeks on, 1 week off) resulted in an 8% response rate and 85% stable disease among 15 additional patients ( ).
The KOSMIC trial further evaluated this modified sunitinib schedule in 25 previously treated TC patients, reporting an ORR of 21.7% and an mPFS of 15.2 months ( ). Notably, exceptional responses were observed in patients harboring Kit mutations or high tumor mutational burden (TMB). The STYLE trial also evaluated sunitinib at 50 mg daily for 4 weeks with a 2-week off period and enrolled 32 TC patients with a 21.7% ORR and a 89.3% DCR ( ). The mPFS and mOS for the TC patients were 8.8 months (95% CI: 5.3–11.1) and 27.8 months (95% CI: 13.2–53.2). Retrospective analysis of a continuous sunitinib schedule, at 37.5 mg daily, was evaluated by Antonarelli et al and colleagues, noting a similar ORR and mPFS of 31.6% and 7.3 months, respectively, across 20 patients with TETs, including 12 with TC ( ).
Another multi-kinase inhibitor, lenvatinib, targeting VEGFR, fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), KIT, and PDGFR, was assessed in the REMORA trial. Among 42 patients, lenvatinib achieved an ORR of 38% and a DCR of 95%, with an mPFS of 9.3 months and an mOS of 28.3 months ( , ). Patients maintaining ≥75% relative dose intensity at 8 weeks had better outcomes, suggesting potential dose-dependence. However, all patients required dose reductions due to AEs, most commonly hypertension and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia. Real-world analyses of lenvatinib in platinum-refractory TC have reported ORRs ranging from 27.3% to 34.9% ( , - ).
The RESOUND trial evaluated regorafenib, another oral TKI targeting VEGFR, PDGFR, FGFR, KIT, CD202b (TIE2), and RAF, in previously treated B2–B3 thymomas and TCs ( , ). In 19 patients (8 with TC), regorafenib showed no responses per the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria, but 86% of TC patients had partial responses by Choi criteria, which account for changes in tumor attenuation. The mPFS was 9.2 months (95% CI: 0.9–34) for TC patients. The observed AEs were consistent with other TKIs, including hand-foot skin reaction, diarrhea, stomatitis, and hypertension with Grade 3–4 AEs occurring in over half of patients.
The KCSG LU17-21 trial investigated the use of palbociclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor in patients with recurrent and metastatic TETs including 23 patients with TC ( ). The study noted two TC patients achieving partial responses with an ORR of 8.7% and DCR of 78.3%. The mPFS and mOS for TC patients was 9.2 and 25.6 months, respectively. Exploratory biomarker analysis in five thymoma and seven TC patients with available tumor tissue revealed six cell cycle or DNA damage repair genes (WEE1, POLE2, TGFB2, IL2R1, FANCC, and MYD88) that were associated with durable clinical benefit.

## Immunotherapy
Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has been increasingly studied in thymic tumors due to notable expression of programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1), particularly in TC (summarized in ). Early trials evaluating single-agent pembrolizumab [200 mg intravenous (IV) every 3 weeks] in pretreated TC reported an ORR of 19.2–22.5% and an mPFS of 4.2–6.1 months ( , , ). Durable responses were observed in some patients, including successful re-challenge after treatment discontinuation due to protocol-defined completion or patient preference ( ).
 Nivolumab, another programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor, demonstrated variable efficacy in TC and mixed cohorts of TC and World Health Organization (WHO) B3 thymoma, with ORRs ranging from 0–14% ( - ). In contrast, studies in predominantly thymoma cohorts showed higher ORRs (38.5–57%), but were associated with high rates of grade ≥3 immune-related adverse events (IRAEs), observed in up to 58.3% of patients in a meta-analysis ( - ). In TC, the safety profile of ICB appears more favorable, with grade ≥3 IRAEs reported in approximately 17.1%, similar to other solid tumors. However, the nature of these IRAEs is notable for their severity, duration, and atypical presentations, including myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis ( , , ).
Dual ICB strategies have also been evaluated. The NIVOTHYM trial investigated nivolumab (240 mg IV every 2 weeks) plus ipilimumab (1 mg/kg every 6 weeks) in 56 patients with pretreated WHO B3 thymoma (n=8) and TC (n=48) ( ). The trial reported an ORR of 17.7% and DCR of 60.8%, with an mPFS of 3.2 months (95% CI: 2.1–3.6) and an mOS of 22.0 months (95% CI: 16.6–not reached). Grade ≥3 IRAEs occurred in 29% of patients, including myocarditis, hepatitis, colitis, myositis, and Sjögren’s syndrome. Despite manageable toxicity, the efficacy of dual ICB did not appear to be superior to single agent PD-1 inhibitor therapies.
ICB combined with chemotherapy has also been investigated in both frontline and refractory settings. In a retrospective study of 77 patients with advanced TC, Wang et al. reported higher response rates with ICB plus chemotherapy (44.4%) compared to ICB alone (17.4%) in the first-line and refractory setting ( ). The mPFS was improved with combination therapy (12.7 vs. 2.1 months), though no OS benefit was observed. Another single-center analysis reported a response rate of 76.9% with chemo-ICB vs. 50% with chemotherapy alone, with mPFS extended to 34.9 vs. 8.8 months ( ). Grade 3 IRAEs occurred in 5.1%, including hepatitis and new-onset type 1 diabetes mellitus. A separate retrospective study comparing taxane-platinum chemotherapy with and without ICB reported no difference in ORR (46.7% vs. 44.7%), but a significant improvement in mPFS (9.4 vs. 6.3 months; P<0.001) in the combination arm ( ).
The MARBLE trial, a prospective multicenter phase II study in Japan, evaluated atezolizumab in combination with carboplatin and paclitaxel as frontline therapy for TC ( ). Forty-eight patients were enrolled and treated with six cycles of combination therapy followed by maintenance atezolizumab. Partial responses were achieved in 56% of patients. The mPFS was 9.6 months (95% CI: 7.7–14.8); mOS was not reached at time of publication. Post hoc analyses showed that higher PD-L1 expression on tumor cells (TC3) or immune cells (IC3) was associated with improved mPFS (17.0 months) compared to intermediate (10.0 months) and low expression groups (7.5 months). Grade 3 IRAEs occurred in 17% of patients, including cases of myositis, hepatitis, skin reactions, and one case of Stevens-Johnson syndrome.
The combination of anti-angiogenic agents and ICB was explored due to their synergistic effects on tumor immunity, including enhanced T cell trafficking, dendritic cell activation, and reduction of immunosuppressive cells ( ). The CAVEATT trial, a single-arm phase II study, evaluated avelumab (PD-L1 inhibitor) and axitinib (VEGFR TKI) in 32 patients with TC or WHO B3 thymoma ( ). The ORR was 34% (95% CI: 21–50%) with a DCR of 91%. In the anti-angiogenic-naïve subgroup, the ORR increased to 47%, while prior exposure was associated with a lower ORR of 15%. The mPFS and mOS were 7.5 and 26.6 months, respectively. Grade ≥3 AEs included hypertension and serious IRAEs (12%), such as interstitial pneumonitis and polymyositis.
The PECATI trial evaluated lenvatinib (20 mg daily) with pembrolizumab (200 mg IV every 3 weeks) in pretreated patients with advanced TC and WHO B3 thymoma ( ). The study reported an ORR of 21% and DCR of 67%. Grade ≥3 AEs occurred in 34.9%, with isolated cases of myocarditis and pneumonitis. An ongoing trial is also investigating sunitinib with pembrolizumab. While the toxicity profile in these combinations is manageable, the incremental efficacy benefit remains modest. The Artemis trial is an ongoing phase II, open-label study of front-line carboplatin, paclitaxel, lenvatinib and pembrolizumab in previously untreated advanced or recurrent TC with the goal of enrolling 35 patients ( ). Future studies are aiming to explore rational combinations of ICB with targeted therapies or novel immunomodulators to improve outcomes in TC.

## Novel treatment strategies
The treatment landscape for solid tumors continues to evolve rapidly, and TC, despite its rarity, is increasingly included in innovative therapeutic strategies. Advances in next-generation sequencing and drug development platforms have enabled the emergence of novel agents targeting pathways previously considered undruggable.
Loss of S-methyl-5'-thioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP), located on chromosome 9p21 and often co-deleted with CDKN2A, is present in approximately 15% of human cancers, including TC with a reported incidence of 14.7–23.1% based on IHC and genomic profiling ( , , , ). MTAP deficiency impairs the metabolism of methylthioadenosine (MTA), leading to the accumulation of MTA and reliance on de novo purine biosynthesis. This vulnerability can be therapeutically exploited through antifolate chemotherapy or protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) inhibition, where MTA acts as a partial endogenous inhibitor.
AMG 193, an MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitor, was evaluated in a first-in-human phase I/Ib trial of pretreated MTAP-deleted solid tumors, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma ( ). Among 80 enrolled patients, active dose cohorts (800 mg daily, 1,200 mg daily, and 600 mg BID) showed an ORR of 21.4% and a DCR of 54.8%. Time to response was 3.9 and 1.8 months at the 800 and 1,200 mg daily doses, respectively. Most AEs, primarily nausea, fatigue, and vomiting, were early-onset and resolved after the first two weeks. Similarly, BMS-986504, a selective PRMT5 inhibitor targeting the PRMT5-MTA complex, demonstrated an ORR of 23% and DCR of 70% in a phase I dose-escalation study ( ). The growing recognition of MTAP loss in TC supports the potential relevance of these agents in future treatment paradigms.
Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have also gained traction as precision-guided chemotherapy platforms. ADCs consist of a monoclonal antibody linked to a cytotoxic payload, enabling selective delivery and intracellular drug release upon binding to tumor-specific antigens ( ). Trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP-2), a transmembrane glycoprotein involved in cell proliferation and survival, is overexpressed in multiple cancers, including TC—especially in squamous subtypes ( - ). Based on this rationale, sacituzumab govitecan, a TROP-2-directed ADC, is being evaluated in an ongoing clinical trial for patients with refractory thymic tumors using a Simon two-stage design (NCT06248515) ( ). Patients receive sacituzumab govitecan at 10 mg/kg IV on days 1 and 8 of a 21-day cycle. The study is assessing efficacy by ORR in parallel cohorts of thymoma and TC.
As described previously, thymic tumors have been shown to express high levels of PD-L1, which may be leveraged to optimize treatments outside of immunotherapies. HLX43 is PD-L1 targeting ADC which has shown preliminary evidence of efficacy in a phase I study across multiple solid tumors with an investigator-assessed ORR of 31.3% with a manageable safety profile ( ). Based on encouraging data of activity in thymic squamous cell carcinoma, a phase I study in TC is planned.
New immunotherapy strategies are also being explored. PT-112, a pyrophosphate-platinum conjugate, has been shown in preclinical models to induce immunogenic cell death and promote T cell infiltration ( ). A phase I study (NCT05104736) is evaluating PT-112 (360 mg/m2 IV on days 1, 8, and 15 of a 28-day cycle) in patients with TETs progressing after platinum therapy ( ). In early results from nine patients, no objective responses were observed, but eight (89%) achieved stable disease, with an mPFS of 6.2 months among TC patients. Correlatives studies from 14 TET patients were recently presented, with peripheral immune cell profiling revealing increased proliferation of CD4+ T cells, activated and proliferative CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells ( ). Moreover, the study noted increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines including, interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor alpha, along with decreases in immunosuppressive factors, VEGF and transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1), among seven patients. Tumor biopsy analysis of three patients revealed increased immune cell density with increased expression of activation markers granzyme B and IFN-γ.
TGF-β is a pleiotropic cytokine that promotes tumor invasion, immune evasion, and stem-like features ( ). A retrospective IHC study of 20 metastatic TC samples revealed co-expression of PD-L1 and TGF-β was associated with poor T cell infiltration and unfavorable survival outcomes ( ). Bintrafusp alfa (M7824), a bifunctional fusion protein targeting PD-L1 and TGF-β via a TGF-β receptor II domain, is under evaluation in a phase II study for relapsed TETs (NCT04417660) ( ).
Ivonescimab, a bispecific antibody targeting both PD-1 and VEGF, has demonstrated promising activity in NSCLC ( , ). A phase II trial is underway to evaluate ivonescimab in refractory TET (NCT06980077), with patients receiving treatment every three weeks ( ). The trial’s primary endpoint is ORR, with secondary endpoints including PFS, duration of response, time to response, and OS. A study (iTHYM) in China will also be evaluating ivonescimab in a prospective, single-arm, phase II trial in combination with chemotherapy as first-line treatment for metastatic TC ( ). The study plans to enroll 24 patients who will receive ivonescimab 20 mg/kg with carboplatin and paclitaxel/nab-paclitaxel. The primary endpoint will be 6-month PFS, while secondary endpoints will include PFS, ORR, DCR, duration of remission and OS.
Mesothelin, a surface glycoprotein with limited normal tissue expression, is highly expressed in several malignancies, including mesothelioma, lung adenocarcinoma, and TC ( - ). Although the anti-mesothelin ADC anetumab ravtansine failed to meet endpoints in mesothelioma and ovarian cancer trials, mesothelin remains a viable target ( , ). A first-in-human phase I trial (NCT06885697) is currently evaluating a novel chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy targeting the membrane-proximal region of mesothelin using hYP218 CAR T cells ( ). These cells are derived from naïve and stem central memory T cells to enhance persistence and reduce exhaustion, potentially improving antitumor efficacy in mesothelin-expressing tumors, including TC.

## Conclusions
TC remains a rare and aggressive malignancy with limited therapeutic options and a historically poor prognosis in advanced stages. Platinum-based cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the mainstay of first-line treatment, with modest efficacy and significant toxicity. The emergence of targeted therapies, particularly multi-kinase inhibitors and anti-angiogenic agents, has offered new avenues for disease control, especially in the relapsed setting. Immunotherapies both alone and in combination with chemotherapy or anti-angiogenic agents, has demonstrated promising activity, although concerns regarding immune-related toxicities, especially in thymoma, necessitate careful patient selection and monitoring. Novel strategies, including PRMT5 inhibition, TROP-2-directed ADCs, bispecific immunotherapies, and mesothelin-targeted CAR T-cell therapies, reflect a growing focus on biomarker-driven and immune-modulating treatments. Continued translational research efforts and clinical trial enrollment are essential to better understand disease biology, identify predictive biomarkers, and develop more effective and tolerable therapies. Multicenter collaboration will be pivotal to accelerate progress and improve outcomes for patients with this rare and challenging cancer.
