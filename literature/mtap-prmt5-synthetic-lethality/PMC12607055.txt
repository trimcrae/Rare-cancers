## Highlights
•Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells drive recurrence and poor outcomes in HNSCC despite modern therapies.
•DTP cells evade treatment via reversible dormancy, metabolic rewiring, enhanced DNA repair, and immune escape.
•Fatty acid oxidation and epigenetic changes support DTP survival and resistance to targeted therapies.
•DTPs reshape the tumor microenvironment, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and immune suppression.
•Emerging strategies target metabolism, DNA repair, and immune evasion to eliminate DTPs and prevent relapse.

Although treatments for HNSCC have improved, the cancer is still challenging because many patients tend to develop the disease again nearby and often have poor recovery. Since drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells display a wide range of types and unique genetic characteristics, nonhormonal endocrine therapies, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy, are ineffective in eliminating these cells. DTP cells evade elimination by entering a reversible dormant state characterized by altered metabolism, enhanced DNA repair capacity, and modified immune escape mechanisms, all of which promote the cells’ survival and treatment failure. Platinum-based chemotherapy, widely prescribed for HNSCC, reduces tumor size but fails to confer long-term benefits because of the strong treatment resistance of DTP cells. These cells also exhibit resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors by activating alternative signaling pathways. Alternative survival pathways are also activated in DTP cells when targeted therapies such as those using epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors are used. Although programmed death 1/programmed death ligand 1 inhibitors are exciting new treatments, their effect is hampered by DTP cells that affect the immune system. This review examines the ways DTP cells make HNSCC tumor cells resistant to treatment by looking at metabolic reprogramming, DNA methylation alterations and the microenvironment of the tumor. This review further explores strategies for targeting metabolic dependencies, inhibiting DNA repair, and combining therapeutic approaches, all of which can mitigate DTP cell-mediated recurrence. Advancing strategies that specifically target DTP cells could enhance HNSCC management by reducing recurrence and improving overall prognosis.

## Introduction
Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is inherently multidisciplinary, comprising surgery, chemoradiation, systemic chemotherapy, and targeted or immune-based therapies, as reflected in updated clinical guidance [ , , ]. Nevertheless, contemporary international data show that long-term outcomes in advanced HNSCC remain unacceptably poor, with most patients experiencing recurrence or progression within five years [ ]. This persists even when chemotherapy is combined with targeted agents and immunotherapy in the recurrent and metastatic settings [ , ]. Although cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the therapeutic backbone, multiple reviews conclude that survival gains are modest and that treatment-related toxicities frequently overshadow the durability of benefit [ ]. A major impediment to durable control is the presence of drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells, a tumor subpopulation capable of surviving exposure to therapeutic agents [ , ]. In contrast to genetically resistant clones, DTP cells exhibit nonheritable resistance by deploying nongenetic stress-response programs that enable survival during initial therapy [ , ]. These cells evade eradication by adopting a reversible, quiescent state that withstands drug-induced stress [ ]. Consequently, DTP cells persist through treatment and contribute to disease relapses and progression. Studies are required to address this form of treatment resistance in HNSCC [ ]. The literature indicates that platinum-based chemotherapy, particularly cisplatin-based chemotherapy, is a standard treatment option for HNSCC. However, therapeutic resistance is increasingly linked to drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells, which survive initial cytotoxic or targeted therapy and later drive tumor relapses [ , ]. Although combining cisplatin or carboplatin with 5-fluorouracil enhances treatment response in HNSCC, the combined therapy causes side effects without improving overall survival [ ]. Furthermore, combining platinum-based therapeutic agents with 5-fluorouracil or cetuximab increases response rates, but these combinations are associated with increased toxicity and only modest survival benefits [ ]. Frizzled-7 exhibits tolerance to platinum-based therapeutic agents, tolerance makes ovarian cancer cells with stem-like features susceptible to ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death through glutathione peroxidase 4 inhibition [ ]. Emerging evidence implicates ferroptosis control in cisplatin tolerance in HNSCC, supporting strategies that target glutathione handling and lipid peroxidation to eradicate persister populations. The Differential Sensitivity Analysis for resistant malignancies tool can be used to identify promising drugs and predictive biomarkers for treating cisplatin-resistant cancers, supporting personalized treatment strategies [ ]. The 5-nucleotidase (NT5E) protein acts as a marker to predict different cancers, including HNSCC. A high level of NT5E is associated with poor prognosis and unsuccessful immunotherapy. NT5E helps cancer-associated fibroblasts transition from epithelial to mesenchymal, which leads to the formation of an immunosuppressive area in the tumor; therefore, NT5E could be a focus for new immunotherapy aimed at cancer-associated fibroblasts [ , , ]. Cetuximab retains a role in selected scenarios; however, adaptive resistance limits durability, and its use is largely in combination regimens [ ]. Unfortunately, DTP cells typically adapt to EGFR-targeted treatment, reducing the duration of therapeutic benefit [ ]. While antibodies against programmed death 1 (PD-1) or programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) provide additional treatment choices, the effects of immune therapy on DTP cells are difficult to predict and vary among HNSCC patients. DTP cells in HNSCC use several adaptive mechanisms to withstand therapeutic pressure [ ]. The present review summarizes recent findings on DTP cells in HNSCC for example, their adaptive mechanisms, the challenges they pose to standard therapies, and potential strategies for targeting them. This review further explores the effects of DTP cells on treatment resistance and relapse in HNSCC to identify suitable pathways for reducing recurrence rates and improving survival outcomes. Nonheritable drug tolerance is increasingly recognized as a principal barrier to durable disease control in HNSCC, enabling small subpopulations of tumor cells to survive initial therapy and drive relapse. A focused synthesis is therefore warranted to consolidate current evidence on the biology of drug-tolerant persister states, define their convergent vulnerabilities across signaling, metabolic, DNA-damage, and immune-evasion programs, and clarify how these insights can be operationalized in the clinic. The objective of this review is to integrate contemporary data, identify actionable targets and biomarkers that enable early recognition of emerging tolerance, and delineate rational combination strategies designed to eradicate persister populations and prolong meaningful clinical benefit.

## Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)
HNSCC arises from the mucosal epithelium of the oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, and larynx; tobacco and alcohol remain established risk factors, while HPV16 is now recognized as a major etiologic driver in oropharyngeal cancer [ , ]. This malignancy remains clinically challenging because of its biological heterogeneity, high rates of locoregional recurrence, and the frequent development of treatment resistance [ ]. A summary of current treatment strategies for HNSCC and their corresponding mechanisms is illustrated in ( ). Current therapeutic approaches include surgery for Resectable disease and, in non-surgical settings, definitive chemoradiation; in the recurrent/metastatic setting, systemic options span platinum-based chemotherapy, EGFR inhibitors, and PD-1–directed immunotherapies [ , , ]. Even with these advances, the prognosis for advanced HNSCC remains poor, with most patients experiencing relapse or progression, underscoring the need for biomarker-guided and multimodal strategies [ , ]. 

## Current treatment approaches for HNSCC
Current treatment regimen for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) follows an escalating care model in which therapy selection depends on disease stage, tumor respectability, response to prior interventions, and evidence of recurrence ( ). Despite technological advances, radiotherapy for head and neck cancer still carries substantial acute/late toxicity and critically 50 % loco-regional recurrence within 3 years in high-risk patients. This relapse pattern aligns with our study’s premise that drug/radiation-tolerant persister (DTP) cells survive initial RT and later repopulate tumors. Targeting persister-cell biology alongside modern RT therefore represents a rational path to improve outcomes and optimize the risk–benefit profile [ ]. Standard first-line treatment typically involves surgery followed by radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT), particularly in respectable cases. For unresectable tumors, chemoradiotherapy remains the primary treatment modality. Integration of 18F-FDG PET-CT imaging significantly improves response assessment, informing clinicians when further intervention or therapy escalation is warranted. Despite aggressive multimodal treatment, many patients experience disease progression and require second-line therapy. While systemic therapies such as targeted inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockade are introduced at this stage, therapeutic resistance frequently emerges, primarily due to the presence of drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells. These cells survive initial treatment by entering a reversible dormant state, exploiting metabolic and epigenetic plasticity, and often serve as a reservoir for subsequent tumor regrowth. As patients advance to third- and fourth-line therapy, options such as taxanes, experimental compounds, and microRNA (miRNA)-based strategies (miR-21 and miR-375) continue to demonstrate limited clinical efficacy. A major limitation of current therapeutic protocols is their focus on eliminating proliferative tumor cells while inadequately addressing the treatment-resistant DTP population. Consequently, heavily pretreated patients often rely on advanced immunotherapies or enrollment in clinical trials, signaling the need for precision strategies tailored to overcome resistance. A growing body of evidence suggests that incorporating DTP-targeted approaches such as inhibition of metabolic dependencies, suppression of DNA-repair pathways, and disruption of immune-evasion mechanisms may enhance therapeutic durability. Understanding how and when DTP cells evade conventional therapies is essential for designing combinatorial regimens that can improve long-term outcomes in HNSCC. 

## First-line treatments
Generally, surgery and radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy are initiated together as the first line of management for head and neck cancers. This approach remains the principal treatment for advanced disease because it achieves effective locoregional control while preserving organ function. Incorporating 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) into treatment planning has improved the accuracy of response assessment after chemoradiotherapy [ ]. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy is especially vital in unresectable cases, where it aims to control local disease and delay systemic progression [ ].

## Second-line treatments
Second-line therapy is commonly initiated after failure of first-line platinum-based chemotherapy. Available options include targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors. In LUX phase III Head & Neck 3 trial, afatinib an oral pan-ErbB inhibitor improved progression free survival and objective response versus methotrexate (Methotrexate) in EGFR therapy naïve patients. Moreover, subsets of patients treated with nivolumab or pembrolizumab (PD-1/PD-L1) directed achieve durable remissions. Nevertheless, donor-cell therapies yield inconsistent benefit because DTP cells can blunt antitumor immunity and develop resistance [ ].

## Third-line treatments
The therapeutic landscape for third-line treatment is narrow, with a focus on rechallenge strategies and investigational approaches. One promising avenue involves targeting tumor-promoting and resistance-mediating microRNAs. For example, miR-21 is associated with chemoresistance and tumor proliferation, while miR-375 exhibits tumor-suppressive properties. Modulating these miRNAs holds potential for overcoming resistance in advanced HNSCC [ ]. Rechallenge with previously effective agents or the introduction of alternative regimens such as taxanes can also be considered based on patient-specific factors and prior responses [ ].

## Fourth-line treatments
Fourth-line therapy is highly individualized and often requires enrollment in clinical trials to access novel immunotherapies or combination regimes. At this stage, biomarker-guided decision-making and DTP-informed therapeutic design are critical. Agents that target stress-response pathways, mitochondrial function, or chromatin remodeling may help sensitize DTP cells. Personalized approaches are essential to manage the cumulative complexity of prior treatments, ongoing tumor evolution, and microenvironmental interactions [ ]. In summary, HNSCC management is a complex, dynamic continuum that demands layered interventions across multiple lines of therapy. Although advances in imaging, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have improved disease control, treatment failure driven by DTP cells remains a formidable barrier. Integrating DTP-specific strategies into current protocols may yield more durable responses and improve survival outcomes in advanced HNSCC.

## Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells
Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells constitute a transient, slow-cycling subpopulation of cancer cells that survive lethal therapy through reversible, nonheritable adaptations. These adaptations include therapy-induced quiescence, activation of stress-response networks, and coordinated chromatin and metabolic reprogramming, thereby sustaining minimal residual disease and seeding relapse when selective pressure abates. The modern oncologic concept was formalized by Sharma et al. (2010), who operationally defined these cells as drug-surviving, reversibly adapted cancer subclones rather than mutation-fixed resistant variants [ ]. summarizes foundational studies delineating DTP biology and its therapeutic implications. 

## DTP cells versus other cellular states
Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells can be distinguished from other tumor cell states, including cancer stem cells (CSCs) and dormant cells by four features: slow proliferation, low drug permeability, a labile cellular phenotype, and a capacity for selective, irreversible genetic change under intense therapeutic pressure [ ]. Collectively, these properties support a self-renewing compartment that can withstand therapy and persist. Unlike CSCs, which are defined by stemness programs, DTP cells display a mobilizable phenotype and function as a reservoir for relapse and drug resistance [ , ]. Clear differentiation of DTP cells from other cancer cell states is therefore essential for effective treatment design, underscoring the need to define their distinct characteristics [ ].

## Quiescence and reversibility of DTP cells
Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells constitute a rare, stress-adapted fraction of tumor cells that enter a reversible slow-cycling/quiescent state under therapeutic pressure, thereby withstanding otherwise lethal exposures to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted/immune agents [ , ]. This tolerance is typically non-genetic and plastic: when treatment pressure is removed, DTPs can exit quiescence, re-enter the cell cycle, and reacquire pharmacologic susceptibility, distinguishing them from fixed, mutation-driven resistance [ , ]. Multiple programs appear to underwrite this transient state, including epigenetic reprogramming (chromatin remodeling that dampens pro-apoptotic transcription), metabolic rewiring (shifts toward lipid and redox homeostasis), augmentation of DNA-damage repair, and activation of pro-survival signaling nodes (JAK/STAT, MAPK, PI3K) often reinforced by stromal cues in the tumor microenvironment. Because these cells are quiescent and frequently display stem-like features, they are well-positioned to seed minimal residual disease and drive relapses after therapy cessation [ , , ]. Accordingly, defining the determinants of DTP entry, maintenance, and exit is pivotal for designing eradication strategies that couple frontline therapy with rational co-targeting of the DTP state. ( ) adapted from our August 2024 publication, “Targeting of FSP1 Regulates Iron Homeostasis in Drug-Tolerant Persister Head and Neck Cancer Cells via Lipid-Metabolism-Driven Ferroptosis” summarizes this trajectory. In our model, a DTP phenotype emerges after 8 days of continuous drug exposure; following a 28-day drug holiday, cells resume proliferation with restored drug sensitivity, confirming reversibility of tolerance in washout assays [ ]. These observations motivate therapeutic concepts that either prevent DTP induction, force exit from quiescence into lethal cycling or exploit DTP-specific liabilities (ferroptotic vulnerability) to eliminate this reservoir before disease re-expansion [ , ]. 

## DTP cells in cancer
Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells confer treatment resistance across multiple malignancies, including HNSCC [ , ]. Unlike “self-determined” cancer cells that acquire fixed genetic alterations and enter classical dormancy [ ], DTP cells display a reversible form of drug tolerance characterized by slow cycling and survival during conventional therapy without stable genetic change [ , ]. DTP cells are therefore a primary source of persistent disease after initial therapy and a frequent driver of relapse and progression [ ]. Their capacity to endure therapeutic pressure arises from multiple adaptive programs [ , ]. A central mechanism is metabolic reprogramming: rather than relying on glycolysis, DTP cells shift toward oxidative phosphorylation to satisfy minimal energetic needs during quiescence [ , ], thereby sustaining survival under treatment and enabling regenerative outgrowth once selective pressure is removed. In parallel, DTP cells engage stress-response signaling that dampens the effects of chemotherapy and targeted agents [ , ], and they augment DNA-damage management, promoting tolerance to platinum-based regimens [ ]. Epigenetic remodeling further tunes therapy-response genes, reinforcing drug tolerance [ , ]. Mitochondrial quality control is also implicated: PINK1-driven mitophagy supports DTP formation, and genetic or pharmacologic disruption of PINK1/mitophagy can facilitate elimination of these cells and improve MAPK-directed therapy [ , ]. Additional evidence indicates that PI3K-pathway deactivation reshapes phenotypic states: JARID1B(high) populations lose stem-like activity, while G0-like cells abandon priming; these shifts intersect with ferroptosis programs such that several agents induce ferroptotic death in HNSCC. Resistance frequently involves activation of the NRF2/ARE axis, and its inhibition can enhance chemotherapy efficacy [ ]. A cancer stem-cell phenotype can be engaged via the KDM5A/MPC1 axis in treatment-refractory HNSCC, increasing susceptibility to ferroptosis; likewise, PGRMC1 activation promotes ferroptosis in paclitaxel-resistant cells by elevating fatty-acid oxidation and sensitizing to system x_c^- (SLC7A11/xCT) inhibition [ , ]. Moreover, epiregulin silencing augments cetuximab sensitivity by inducing ferroptosis, suggesting that epiregulin loss could support combinations of cetuximab with ferroptosis inducers [ ]. Differentiation into a DTP state is accompanied by reversal of pro-apoptotic programs and induction of stress-resistance genes [ ]. Understanding DTP biology in HNSCC is therefore essential, as these cells impede complete and durable responses ( ). Experimentally, DTP populations are generated by prolonged drug exposure of parental lines, yielding slow-cycling, drug-tolerant cells that are subsequently validated by reduced proliferation, characteristic transcriptional changes, and G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest; this in-vitro system enables controlled interrogation of DTP phenotypes and adaptations. Such knowledge informs the design of strategies that selectively target DTP cells, with the dual goals of enhancing efficacy and lowering relapse risk. summarizes mechanisms underlying persistence and therapy resistance, including metabolic rewiring, genetic alterations, alternative survival pathways, chemotherapeutic evasion, and radio-resistance that permit survival, continued proliferation, and tumor re-initiation. The timeline for acquiring DTP phenotypes varies widely by malignancy, from weeks to months: in HNSCC, breast cancer, and NSCLC, DTP populations commonly emerge within 2–4 weeks of exposure, whereas glioblastoma and bladder cancer often require 1–2 months to establish persistence, reflecting distinct adaptive routes under selective pressure ( , ). Consistent with this heterogeneity, sarcomas also exhibit persister-like phenotypes that parallel the biology observed in epithelial malignancies. Recent findings in osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma models reveal the existence of slow-cycling subpopulations with enhanced oxidative phosphorylation, elevated antioxidant defenses, and resistance to conventional cytotoxic agents [ , ]. These cells demonstrate a dependency on iron metabolism and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, rendering them highly susceptible to ferroptosis-inducing agents. The metabolic and redox adaptations observed in these mesenchymal tumors closely mirror the adaptive mechanisms of DTP cells identified in HNSCC and other carcinomas. Such convergence underscores a broader principle of therapy persistence that transcends tissue origin, highlighting the therapeutic potential of ferroptosis induction and metabolic rewiring strategies to overcome relapse and resistance across diverse types [ , , , , ]. Together, these observations underscore the need for tailored interventions that interrupt the specific persistence mechanisms operating within each cancer type. Although drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells have been primarily studied in malignant tumors such as HNSCC, emerging evidence suggests that similar persistence mechanisms may contribute to therapeutic resistance in aggressive benign neoplasms, including ameloblastoma and desmoid-type fibromatosis. These lesions exhibit locally invasive growth and high recurrence rates following conventional therapy, implying the presence of slow-cycling, therapy-tolerant cell subpopulations. Such DTP-like phenotypes have been linked to enhanced oxidative metabolism, DNA-repair activation, and transient stem-like states in non-malignant yet recurrent tumors [ , ]. Therefore, the therapeutic strategies proposed in this review particularly those targeting metabolic reprogramming and DNA-repair vulnerabilities may also hold translational relevance for managing aggressive benign tumors that share persistence-associated biological traits. 

## Mechanisms of drug tolerance in HNSCC

## Signaling pathways supporting the maintenance of DTP cells
Early-response programs including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), NF-κB, and JAK/STAT3 (with FGFR-driven inputs) are rapidly engaged to help cells adapt to initial drug exposure [ , ]. As tolerance persists, major survival cascades MAPK/PI3K/Hippo, and Notch are reactivated to stabilize the DTP state [ , ]. Collectively, these pathways drive metabolic remodeling, epigenetic reprogramming, and apoptosis evasion, identifying them as promising targets for selective elimination of DTP cells [ , , ].

## Metabolic adaptations in DTP cells
In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells undergo marked metabolic reprogramming to withstand therapeutic pressure [ , ]. Rather than relying on glycolysis characteristic of rapidly proliferating tumor cells they transition to oxidative phosphorylation, adopting a low-energy, stress-tolerant state [ ]. This shift reduces vulnerability to agents that preferentially target fast-dividing populations, thereby facilitating persistence [ ]. By maintaining oxidative phosphorylation-driven dormancy, DTP cells evade both cytotoxic and targeted therapies [ ]. Consequently, disrupting these metabolic dependencies represents a rational strategy to re-sensitize tumors to standard treatments.

## DNA damage response and repair mechanisms
Prolonged survival and enhanced DNA-repair capacity are additional hallmarks of DTP cells in HNSCC, particularly under exposure to platinum-based chemotherapeutics that induce DNA damage [ ]. These cells display superior repair of DNA adducts and other therapy-induced lesions, thereby evading apoptosis and subsequent cell death [ , , ]. As illustrated in ( ), DNA-damage tolerance in DTP cells is linked to efficient homologous recombination and nonhomologous ending, the principal components of recombinational and non-recombinational repair pathways [ , ]. Persistent activation of these mechanisms enables DTP cells to survive multiple chemotherapy cycles, facilitating the outgrowth of fully resistant populations [ ]. This augmented repair capacity underscores the limited long-term efficacy of DNA-damaging agents and highlights the importance of therapeutically targeting DNA-repair pathways to eradicate DTP cells [ , ]. 

## Epigenetic reprogramming and transcriptional plasticity
One characteristic feature of DTP cells in HNSCC is their capacity for epigenetic reprogramming, which enables them to modify gene-expression patterns in response to drug exposure [ ]. NRF2-driven metabolic reprogramming, particularly via oxidative phosphorylation, contributes to treatment failure in recurrent HPV-related HNSCC, making NRF2 a promising therapeutic target [ ]. DTP cells exhibit transcriptional plasticity, temporally altering gene expression to activate anti-apoptotic signals and suppress pro-apoptotic programs, thereby enhancing drug tolerance [ , , ]. This epigenetic plasticity enables DTP cells to switch between drug-resistant and proliferative phenotypes, supporting survival during therapy and re-entry into growth after drug withdrawal [ , ]. The transcriptional adaptability of DTP cells poses a major treatment challenge because it allows rapid adjustment to therapeutic stress [ ]. Modulating epigenetic machinery may disrupt this plasticity and enhance the efficacy of standard therapies [ ].

## Effect of the TME on drug tolerance
The TME is central to the survival and adaptation of DTP cells. Interactions among cancer-associated fibroblasts and immune cells within this milieu support DTP-cell persistence during treatment [ , ]. Protective niches foster metabolic reprogramming, management of oxidative stress, and immune evasion [ , ]. In addition, TME-driven redox signaling accelerates epigenetic change and promotes a slow-cycling DTP phenotype, reinforcing drug tolerance [ , ]. Components of the TME therefore represent actionable therapeutic targets, as interrupting these cues can destabilize DTP maintenance and enable durable remission [ , , ]. The TME’s role is integral to both the development and the maintenance of DTP states [ ]. DTP cells exhibit survival advantages metabolic remodeling, anti-apoptotic activity, and epigenetic adaptability that are not purely intrinsic but are acquired and sustained through TME interactions [ , , , ]. Such interactions enable evasion of cytotoxic effects even under continuous drug exposure [ ]. Multiple TME-derived factors thus contribute to treatment failure and relapses through DTP-mediated resistance [ , , ]. The persistence of these cells, supported by the TME, underscores the need for strategies that disrupt adaptive programs and signal cascades sustaining DTP populations [ ]. Targeting TME-DTP crosstalk may reduce drug tolerance and relapse rates, yielding more durable therapeutic responses [ ]. A focus on the TME-DTP axis could ultimately shift treatment paradigms by embedding microenvironmental drivers of resistance into therapeutic planning [ , ]. summarizes mechanisms underlying the development of DTP cells in HNSCC. 

## Drug tolerance of HNSCC in standard therapies

## Chemotherapy-induced development of DTP cells
Platinum-based chemotherapy remains foundational in HNSCC, with cisplatin or carboplatin the most used backbones in current practice ( ) [ , , ]. Across comparative randomized data and subsequent analyses, substituting paclitaxel for 5-fluorouracil in cisplatin-containing regimens has not demonstrated an overall-survival advantage in HNSCC [ ]. Many tumors contain subpopulations of cycling DTP cells that acquire drug tolerance and precipitate relapses [ , ]. In our laboratory testing, FSP1 levels were elevated in patients who relapsed after cisplatin therapy, accompanied by upregulation of lipid-metabolism gene programs, implicating FSP1 as a driver of drug resistance [ ]. Higher FSP1 expression may therefore serve as a predictor of impending relapse and a means to identify patients at greatest risk. Patients with HNSCC could benefit from improved delivery of FSP1 inhibitors. Accordingly, a functional link between FSP1 and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) signaling and drug resistance may be established under cisplatin exposure in HNSCC DTP cells. Ivermectin has also been reported to sensitize cancer cells to cisplatin and other agents. Targeting the balance between FSP1 and ACSL4 may provide a strategy to overcome chemoresistance [ , ]. For appropriately selected patients with locally advanced disease who are not surgical candidates, concurrent cisplatin-based chemoradiation is guideline-recommended and improves outcomes relative to radiotherapy alone, although toxicity remains a concern [ , ]. Both excision repair cross-complementation group 1 (ERCC1) and T-class biomarkers help predict responses to chemotherapy in HNSCC. Robust evidence indicates that ERCC1 may predict outcomes with 5-fluorouracil/cisplatin therapy [ ]. ALDH1A3 (Aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family) functions as a marker of drug-resistant gastric cancer cells; therapies engaging crosstalk with the ALDH1A3/mTOR pathway can eliminate resistant clones in gastric cancer. Cyclin E2 has been identified as a target of miR-30a in oral cancer, nominating miR-30a as a biomarker and potential therapeutic target for resistance to 5-fluorouracil [ ]. Lower PTEN expression appears to indicate resistance to cetuximab in HNSCC, positioning PTEN as a candidate biomarker for personalized therapy in this setting [ ]. Previously, we examined KDM5D as a therapeutic target in HNSCC; KDM5D is overexpressed in tumors from patients with HNSCC, in CSCs, and in cisplatin-resistant cells. Elevated KDM5D expression correlates with poor responses to platinum and earlier relapses [ , ]. One further study reported that PIK3CA and RAS mutations predicted cetuximab resistance in recurrent or metastatic HNSCC, whereas PTEN loss predicted resistance to cetuximab plus radiotherapy [ ]. Definitive radiotherapy cures most patients with non-metastatic HNSCC; nevertheless, 15–30 % experience locoregional failure, and over half of these relapses occur within the first six months after treatment completion, indicating persistence of radiation-tolerant clones that escape initial ablation [ ]. Experimental work in nude-mouse xenografts (FaDu and SCC25) showed that two weeks of clinically relevant fractionated irradiation (≈30 × 2–3 Gy; total ≥48 Gy) eradicated bulk tumor mass but consistently left a slow-cycling, tumor-repopulating reservoir; tumor-control-dose analyses further demonstrated that squamous carcinomas required >50 Gy for 50 % control, underscoring intrinsic radio-tolerance [ ]. Derivative radio-resistant clones (FaDu-R) display an expanded cancer-stem-cell fraction (CD44^high/ALDH1^high), accelerated wound closure, heightened invasiveness, and hyperactivation of PI3K/Akt/MAPK programs long linked to post-irradiation survival [ ]. Multi-omics analyses of patient tumors have additionally shown that NFE2L2 (NRF2) gain of function or KEAP1 loss occurs in roughly one-quarter of HPV-negative HNSCCs and independently predicts radio-resistant disease, in part by rewiring intratumoral myeloid cells and strengthening redox defenses [ ]. Importantly, pharmacologic co-targeting of these adaptations is feasible: glutaminase inhibition with telaglenastat (CB-839) disrupts NRF2-driven metabolic buffering and resensitizes persister cells to ionizing radiation in both in-vitro and in-vivo HNSCC models [ ]. Collectively, current evidence supports the model illustrated here: a 14-day course of radiotherapy ablates the primary HNSCC lesion yet leaves a discrete reservoir of radiation-tolerant persister (RTP) cells that, unless specifically targeted, can repopulate the tumor and drive clinical relapse ( ). Another study associated high dickkopf-1 (DKK1) expression in HNSCC with an immunosuppressive TME and radio-resistance, identifying DKK1 as a potential target for enhancing radioimmunotherapy [ ]. NF-κB plays a central role in promoting radio resistance to cancer. In glioblastoma, the NF-κB-YY1-miR-103 axis drives resistance by enhancing DNA repair and stemness; in HNSCC, NF-κB promotes radio-resistance through senescence-associated cytokines, particularly via the CXCR2 ligand axis [ , ]. In addition, MET (cellular mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor) increases radio-resistance in HNSCC by promoting cellular plasticity and stem-cell features; the MET inhibitor SU11274, when combined with radiotherapy, may mitigate resistance and improve outcomes. Studies have also highlighted miR-9 as a biomarker of response to radiotherapy and cetuximab; high miR-9 levels promote resistance by regulating the Sp1 pathway [ , ]. Radio-resistance remains a major challenge in HNSCC and is driven by mechanisms such as STAT1 activation in HPV-negative disease and NFE2L2 (NRF2) mutations that promote tumor growth and immune evasion. Targeting these pathways particularly with agents such as CB-839 to counter NRF2-driven resistance may enhance radiosensitivity; biomarker-based strategies are therefore needed to improve outcomes [ , , ]. Periostin, acting through ERK/Akt, promotes EMT and radio-resistance in HNSCC and thus represents a biomarker and therapeutic candidate for overcoming radio-resistance [ ]. Cetuximab-resistant HNSCC commonly exhibits compensatory Ras/MAPK activation often with reduced DUSP5/DUSP6 and increased AURKB expression. Pharmacologic ERK1/2 or AURKB blockade can re-sensitize models to EGFR inhibition; resistance is further reinforced by Ras upregulation and heightened EGFR-ligand signaling, and co-targeting Ras prenylation/formylation or PI3K/Akt has restored treatment sensitivity [ , ]. In oral cancer, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome increases IL-1β and enhances survival, contributing to 5-fluorouracil resistance; targeting this inflammasome may improve 5-fluorouracil efficacy [ ]. Initial exposure to cisplatin effectively induces DNA damage in HNSCC cells; however, DTP populations adapt cellular programs to evade drug-induced toxicity [ ]. DTP cells augment DNA-repair capacity and reduce reliance on chemotherapy-sensitive pathways, thereby surviving treatment [ ]. They also undergo metabolic reprogramming and activate stress-signaling pathways to avoid chemotherapy-induced apoptosis [ , ]. Inhibiting IAP family proteins in HNSCC slows growth, promotes apoptosis, reduces invasiveness, and enhances chemosensitivity, nominating IAPs as promising therapeutic targets [ ]. Notch1 upregulation is associated with chemoresistance and poor survival in HNSCC, whereas Notch1 downregulation improves drug sensitivity, supporting Notch1 as a potential biomarker of treatment response [ ]. Multiple adaptive programs including enhanced DNA-damage repair, redox/metabolic rewiring, and phenotypic plasticity sustain DTP survival under platinum pressure and ultimately drive relapse [ ]. In platinum-experienced recurrent/metastatic HNSCC, afatinib has demonstrated improved progression-free survival versus methotrexate (Methotrexate) with acceptable safety, a finding corroborated in subsequent analyses [ ]. Together, these data underscore the limitations of current chemotherapy in eliminating DTP cells and highlight the need to develop synergistic therapeutic strategies that disrupt their survival mechanisms [ , ]. 

## Targeted therapy and DTP cells
EGFR-targeted therapy is effective for HNSCC tumors that harbor DTP cells. EGFR is frequently upregulated in these tumors and is therefore a principal molecular target for agents such as cetuximab [ ]. However, primary resistance to EGFR-directed therapy remains a major concern, because DTP cells often reroute signaling through alternative pathways to ensure survival [ ]. AZD4547, an inhibitor acting on the FGFR/AKT/SOX2 axis, effectively reduces cell viability, cell migration, and colony formation in HNSCC [ ]. Notably, AZD4547 enhances the effects of paclitaxel (Taxol) [ , ]. Macrophage-derived exosomes have been shown to diminish the chemosensitivity of oral cancer cells via the AKT pathway; this observation highlights exotics as potential targets to improve chemosensitivity [ ]. Upon cetuximab exposure, DTP cells frequently shift reliance to PI3K/AKT and ERK cascades, enabling survival despite EGFR blockade and sustaining adaptive resistance [ ]. This signaling plasticity allows DTP cells to persist without EGFR activation and to repopulate when treatment is paused [ , ]. The persistence of these cells under EGFR-targeted therapy remains problematic because their presence can lead to long-term resistance [ , ]. Secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) promotes HNSCC growth and confers cetuximab resistance by activating the KRAS/MEK pathway; thus, targeting SPP1 or using a MEK inhibitor may help overcome cetuximab resistance [ ]. In HPV-negative HNSCC, reduction or loss of SMAD4 is linked to cetuximab resistance and EMT; dual targeting of EGFR together with JNK/MAPK signaling has been proposed to restore responsiveness, and tumors with preserved SMAD4 expression appear more likely to benefit [ , ]. To counter DTP-driven resistance, combinations that pair EGFR inhibition with agents against compensatory nodes such as PI3K/AKT or MEK have been proposed to limit adaptive signaling and improve efficacy [ ]. The aim of these strategies is to reduce the adaptive plasticity of DTP cells and thereby improve the effectiveness of EGFR-targeted therapy in HNSCC [ ]. Ongoing clinical trials are exploring new therapeutic regimens to overcome resistance to EGFR-directed therapy [ ]. Tyrosine 821 of the AXL receptor tyrosine kinase drives radio-resistance and cetuximab resistance in HNSCC by activating ABL1; targeting both EGFR and ABL1 may help overcome resistance and improve treatment response [ ]. Cetuximab resistance in HNSCC is also driven by SNAIL, lymphotoxin-β, and protein arginine methyltransferase 1; this resistance can be mitigated by combining EGFR and NF-κB inhibitors [ ]. We demonstrated that FSP1 confers drug resistance by regulating iron homeostasis and lipid synthesis, which makes FSP1 a promising target for combating chemoresistance. FSP1 supports the survival of cancer cells under chemotherapy, particularly chemotherapy-tolerant DTP cells [ ]. DTP cells isolated from patients with HNSCC show elevated FSP1 levels and upregulation of lipid-metabolism associated genes such as ACSS2, CD36, and FABP4. Therefore, lipid metabolism is critical for drug tolerance. This finding highlights the importance of intracellular lipids in sustaining DTP cells. Deregulation of FSP1 has been observed in drug-resistant HNSCC, supporting the rationale for targeting FSP1 and developing FSP1 inhibitors. Targeting FSP1 may reduce rates of relapse and metastasis among patients with HNSCC [ ].

## Immunotherapy and immune evasion in DTP cells
Currently, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis are effective in the treatment of HNSCC [ ]. As first-line therapy for PD-L1–positive recurrent or metastatic HNSCC, pembrolizumab administered with or without chemotherapy is recommended [ , ]. Compared with standard regimens, pembrolizumab improves overall survival and yields a more favorable severe-toxicity profile in recurrent or metastatic disease [ ]. Sideroflexin-3 expression is upregulated and correlates with poor prognosis, immune suppression, and paclitaxel resistance; accordingly, it may inform therapeutic decision-making [ ]. In HNSCC, lysosomal integral membrane protein 2 has been evaluated as a therapeutic target and associated with increased autophagy, cancer-stem-cell properties, and resistance to immunotherapy. Although immunotherapy offers meaningful benefit, DTP cells exhibit immune features that confer resistance to treatment [ ]. In HNSCC-associated progenitor cells, STAT1 promotes tumor progression and immunosuppression, whereas tripartite motif–containing 24 (TRIM24) a STAT1 repressor emerges as a potential therapeutic target [ , , ]. DTP cells can also inhibit antigen-presentation pathways, hindering immune recognition and clearance [ ]. These cells further downregulate immune checkpoints, creating conditions that favor immune evasion. This immune-evasive behavior is particularly prominent in recurrent or metastatic HNSCC, enabling DTP cells to withstand immune attack [ , ]. Combining T-cell and natural killer (NK) based immunotherapies may improve outcomes by targeting resistant clones within an immunosuppressive microenvironment [ ]. Such combination approaches aim to address limitations of immunotherapy in resistant HNSCC.

## Therapeutic strategies targeting DTP cells in HNSCC

## Targeting unique metabolic characteristics
DTP cells in HNSCC display altered metabolic profiles; accordingly, their unique metabolic features are now being explored as potential therapeutic targets [ ]. DTP cells shift from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation to survive therapeutic stress [ , ]. This metabolic shift presents an opportunity to disrupt DTP-cell survival by using inhibitors that selectively target components of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway [ , ]. Research indicates that combining metabolic inhibitors with standard therapies for ovarian cancer, such as platinum-based chemotherapy, enhances treatment efficacy because DTP cells cannot easily switch to an alternative energy-generation pathway [ , ]. Clinical trials are ongoing to evaluate combination therapies aimed at eliminating DTP cells and reducing recurrence rates [ ].

## Exploiting DNA repair mechanisms and cell cycle checkpoints
HNSCC demonstrates robust DNA-repair capacity, a feature that contributes to the chemoresistance observed in DTP populations [ ]. Consequently, DNA-repair machinery represents a compelling therapeutic target, particularly for agents that inhibit pivotal repair processes. For instance, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors can enforce a non-adaptive state by blocking the resolution of platinum-induced DNA lesions [ , ]. Multiple determinants, including ABC transporters, nucleotide- and base-excision repair complexes, and defined cisplatin and cetuximab resistance modules play central roles in conferring drug tolerance to HNSCC cells [ ]. In parallel, specific cell-cycle programs may prevent DTP cells from entering quiescence and instead drive re-entry into active cycling, nominating discrete cell-cycle regulators as therapeutic points of intervention [ ]. In oral squamous cell carcinoma, MCL1 drives chemoresistance, and combining MCL1 inhibition with standard chemotherapy may improve outcomes [ , ]. Additionally, interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) blockade most notably with tocilizumab reduces BMI1 expression and stem-like properties, thereby mitigating chemoresistance in HNSCC [ ]. Our laboratory has shown that KDM5D controls AURKB, a kinase essential for cell-cycle progression and the DNA-damage response; KDM5D knockdown lowers AURKB and ALDH1A3, diminishes stemness, and increases cisplatin sensitivity, indicating that the KDM5D-AURKB axis sustains drug tolerance in HNSCC cells [ ]. Ongoing investigations suggest that coordinated targeting of DNA-repair and cell-cycle pathways within DTP states can enhance therapeutic efficacy in HNSCC [ , ].

## Epigenetic modulation as a therapeutic approach
Epigenetic remodeling is a central determinant of drug tolerance in DTP cells. These populations remodel chromatin and reprogram transcription to sustain survival under therapeutic pressure [ ]. Accordingly, targeting core epigenetic regulators such as histone-modifying enzymes and DNA-methylation machinery has been shown in recent preclinical and translational studies to re-sensitize DTP cells to standard therapies [ , , ]. Agents under investigation include inhibitors of histone modifications and DNA methylation that directly interfere with DTP maintenance [ ]. By reversing tolerance-promoting chromatin states, epigenetic modulators can prevent re-entry into quiescence and augment the efficacy of conventional regimens; accumulating evidence supports their use within rational combination strategies against DTP cells in HNSCC [ , ].

## Combination therapy and novel agents
Currently, combination therapies that target multiple vulnerabilities and that include US Food and Drug Administration-approved components are being developed to eradicate DTP cells in HNSCC. These regimens are designed to co-target EGFR, DNA-repair pathways, and metabolic dependencies to limit the capacity of DTP cells to adapt to therapeutic pressure [ , ]. Small molecules and monoclonal antibodies that selectively engage DTP populations remain in the experimental stage [ ]. Ongoing trials are determining safe and effective dosing strategies that, when used synergistically, may deliver durable benefit for patients with relapsed or metastatic HNSCC [ , , , ]. Previously, we demonstrated that treatment with the AURKB inhibitor barasertib induces MTC in platinum-tolerant HNSCC cells. In a mouse model, combined administration of cisplatin and barasertib markedly reduced tumor growth and may represent a strategy to overcome platinum resistance in HNSCC. Blockade of the KDM5D-AURKB axis is therefore a plausible approach to sensitize HNSCC to chemotherapy and reduce relapse risk [ ]. In this work, we show that this strategy can be leveraged to optimize single-agent therapies in the setting of DTP-cell drug tolerance.

## HNSCC biomarkers and their functions
Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is biologically heterogeneous, and an expanding repertoire of biomarkers now underpins diagnosis, prognostication, and theragnostic (treatment-guiding) stratification [ , ]. At the molecular level, alterations in EGFR and TP53 inform likelihood of response to systemic and radiation therapies and correlate with risk of progression, while indices of angiogenesis and hypoxia most notably VEGF and HIFs are linked to radio-resistance and adverse outcomes [ , ]. From an immune standpoint, p16 serves as a pragmatic surrogate of HPV-driven disease with distinct prognostic implications, and salivary IL-8 provides a minimally invasive adjunct for identifying HPV-associated and early-stage cancers [ ]. In combination, these readouts capture complementary facets of tumor biology proliferative signaling, genomic instability, vascular and oxygenation status, and immune contexture enabling risk-aligned selection of systemic agents, rational radiation intensification or de-escalation, and definition of measurable endpoints for response assessment [ , , , , ]. Accordingly, integration of molecular and immune biomarkers with standard clinicopathologic variables ( ) refines staging beyond anatomic criteria, supports personalized therapeutic planning, and aims to improve survival and quality of care in HNSCC [ , , ]. 

## Future directions and clinical implications
Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) frequently harbors drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells that survive initial therapy, seed relapse, and worsen prognosis. Their persistence indicates that standard modalities cytotoxic chemotherapy, molecularly targeted agents, and immune checkpoint blockade can be circumvented by adaptive tumor states. Ongoing research continues to elucidate the cellular programs that enable DTP survival under therapeutic pressure including stress-response signaling, metabolic rewiring, and altered DNA-damage management yet cross-study comparisons are constrained by heterogeneous operational definitions of DTP, intratumoral/intertumoral variability, and inconsistent assays. A near-term priority is the development of robust prognostic and predictive biomarkers to detect DTP biology early in the disease course; however, identifying rare, quiescent persister in routine biopsies is technically challenging, longitudinal multi-region sampling is seldom feasible, and standardized pharmacodynamic readouts are not yet established. Establishing reproducible molecular signatures for DTP cells in HNSCC would permit earlier recognition of emergent tolerance and timely therapeutic adaptation, although generalizability across subsites and etiologic contexts (HPV-positive versus HPV-negative disease) remains uncertain. As these signatures mature, opportunities will expand to nominate actionable targets and design agents that selectively eliminate persister populations while sparing normal tissues; nonetheless, co-targeting stress and metabolic pathways may narrow therapeutic windows and raise off-target toxicity concerns. Therapeutic progress is likely to derive from rational combinations that co-target DTP maintenance pathways alongside complementary vulnerabilities, pairing inhibitors of adaptive signaling with agents that disrupt metabolic dependencies, modulate DNA-repair and cell-cycle checkpoints, or counter immune-evasion mechanisms yet preclinical models incompletely recapitulate prior treatment exposures and the immune stromal microenvironment, tempering translational certainty. Therapy failure in HNSCC increasingly reflects reversible cellular adaptation rather than fixed mutation. Under drug or immune pressure, a small fraction of cells shifts into a slow-cycling, quiescent DTP state sustained by enhanced DNA repair (ATR, ERCC1), oxidative-stress control, and epigenetic reprogramming, with support from JAK/STAT3, MAPK, and PI3K signaling and cues from cancer-associated fibroblasts. These strategies aim to deepen responses and prolong their durability and therefore warrant prospective evaluation in biomarker-enriched clinical trials with explicit safety, efficacy, and mechanistic endpoints, while recognizing that many current studies are underpowered for overall survival and rely on surrogate measures. Overall, durable control of recurrent and metastatic HNSCC will require a strategic shift toward DTP-focused intervention, supported by coordinated preclinical and clinical programs centered on early detection of tolerance, mechanism-anchored target selection, and deliberately engineered combinations, alongside rigorous safety monitoring and validation of predictive biomarkers. 

## Conclusion
Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells constitute a transient yet exceptionally resilient subpopulation that enables head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) to survive lethal therapies and subsequently reinitiate tumor growth. These cells persist by engaging reversible survival programs shifting metabolism toward fatty-acid oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation, upregulating DNA-damage response pathways to expedite repair of therapy-induced lesions and adopting immunologic camouflage that dampens cytotoxic immune surveillance. Because conventional chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted agents largely spare this phenotype, disease control is typically short-lived, and relapses are common. Recognizing this challenge, our research group has developed a portfolio of DTP-focused interventions. First, we deploy small-molecule inhibitors and nutrient-restriction strategies that target key metabolic dependencies, including glutamine anaplerosis and peroxisomal fatty-acid oxidation, thereby depriving DTP cells of essential bioenergetic substrates. Second, by pharmacologically unmasking DNA-repair liabilities via PARP and ATR blockade we induce catastrophic genomic instability selectively within persister populations. Finally, we integrate these metabolic and DNA-repair disruptors with immune checkpoint blockades and low-dose chemotherapy, creating synthetic-lethal combinations that both eradicate DTP cells and revive antitumor immunity. Translating these insights to the clinic is the next critical step. Carefully designed early-phase trials that incorporate molecular stratification, longitudinal liquid-biopsy monitoring, and adaptive dosing schedules will be essential to validate DTP-directed regimens. Success in this area has the potential not only to extend progression-free survival but also to transform HNSCC into a more manageable disease with a substantially lower risk of recurrence.
