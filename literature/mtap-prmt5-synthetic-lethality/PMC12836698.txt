## Abstract

## Background
Approximately 10% of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cases are KRAS wild-type (KRAS-wt). Among these, only a small subgroup harbors druggable genomic alterations, raising the question of when to apply broader next-generation DNA sequencing testing. Early-onset (EO) PDAC is increasing globally, with recent evidence suggesting an enrichment of KRAS-wt cases in younger patients.

## Patients and methods
This retrospective observational study analyzed secondary data from a predefined database. Genomic alterations were identified using FoundationOne CDx or Liquid gene panels in patients who had failed standard treatments. Cases were classified by KRAS status, and KRAS-wt patients were stratified into EO (≤52.5 years) and late-onset (LO) (>52.5 years) groups. Alterations were evaluated according to the European Society for Medical Oncology Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESMO ESCAT).

## Results
Next-generation sequencing was carried out on 224 PDAC and 10 pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) patients. Within PDAC patients, 23.2% (52/224) were KRAS-wt. Actionable TIER I-III alterations were more frequent in KRAS-wt cases (23.1% versus 11.6%, P = 0.04), with gene fusion events exclusively found in KRAS-wt (9.6% versus 0%, P = 0.0006). Among KRAS-wt PDACs, significantly higher frequencies of druggable TIER I-III were identified across EO-PDAC (6 versus 6, or 54.5% versus 14.6%, P = 0.006). Notably, actionable gene fusions were found to be significantly enriched in KRAS-wt EO-PDACs compared with LO counterparts (4 versus 1, or 36.4% versus 2.4%, P = 0.005).

## Conclusions
KRAS-wt PDAC diagnosed at a younger age more frequently harbors actionable mutations, highlighting the importance of comprehensive genomic profiling to guide targeted therapeutic interventions in this patient population.

Key words: pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, precision oncology, genomic profiling, early-onset, KRAS, gene fusion

## Highlights
•A small subgroup of PDAC cases hold a KRAS-wt genotype and is particularly enriched in druggable gene alterations.
•Some 36% of early-onset (≤52.5 years) KRAS-wt PDACs are enriched in immediately druggable gene fusions.
•Age is a key clinical factor to identify actionable gene fusion in KRAS-wt PDACs.
•Young patients with KRAS-wt status should be prioritized for comprehensive genomic profiling.

## Introduction
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive and lethal malignancy with a poor prognosis. Currently, PDAC ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide and is projected to become the second within the next decade. While PDAC predominantly affects older adults, with a peak occurrence at ∼70 years of age, there is a rising incidence of early-onset (EO) PDAC, diagnosed in individuals under the age of 50 years. 
The poor prognosis associated with PDAC could be largely attributed to the high rate of patients, ∼85%, presenting with unresectable disease at the time of diagnosis and to the notable resistance of this disease to standard chemotherapeutic regimens. Even with the most recent advancements, such as the NALIRIFOX regimen, which demonstrated superior efficacy over the standard nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine combination in the NAPOLI-3 trial, the median overall survival (mOS) of patients with metastatic disease remains limited to <1 year. Furthermore, unlike other malignancies where targeted therapies and immunotherapies have significantly improved outcomes, PDAC remains largely refractory to these advances. These findings underscore the difficulty of achieving significant survival improvements with chemotherapy alone, highlighting the urgent need for novel targeted therapeutic strategies in this disease.
KRAS is the most commonly mutated oncogene across human cancers, with PDAC exhibiting the highest frequency of KRAS mutations in almost 90% of cases. For nearly four decades, KRAS was considered an undruggable target, primarily due to its structural properties. Recent advancements in drug discovery, however, have led to the development of the first KRAS inhibitors that offer hope for improved therapeutic options in PDAC driven by KRAS mutations. Paradoxically, in the coming years, it is possible to envision a scenario where 10%-15% of patients with KRAS wild-type (KRAS-wt) PDAC may have fewer therapeutic options than those with KRAS-mutated tumors.
The lack of KRAS mutations often leads to distinct molecular drivers that present unique challenges but also potential therapeutic opportunities in patients. KRAS-wt PDAC are, indeed, enriched with actionable alterations, particularly within the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)–RAS–MAPK signaling pathway. This includes point mutations and gene fusions involving BRAF, ALK, ROS1, FGFR2, RET, MET, and RAF1, as well as amplifications of FGFR3, ERBB2, MET, and NTRK. , , These alterations are not only more frequent in KRAS-wt patients but are almost exclusive to this subtype, offering promising avenues for targeted therapies that may not be applicable to KRAS-mutated PDAC.
To identify and target these alterations, comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) by using next-generation sequencing (NGS) would be crucial. NGS allows for the comprehensive profiling of cancer-associated genes, enabling the detection of rare but actionable mutations or gene fusions that may respond to targeted therapies. The application of NGS in routine clinical practice, however, faces challenges due to its technical complexity and high costs. This could also limit its widespread use in KRAS-wt PDAC, which, although representing a relatively small percentage of cases, translates to a significant number of patients annually.
In this regard, it remains of utmost importance to identify clinical characteristics that can help enrich the population of patients with KRAS-wt PDAC for whom NGS would be more appropriately indicated. Tailoring NGS testing to a specific subset of patients could enhance the clinical utility of this approach, ensuring that it is applied where it is most likely to reveal actionable molecular targets.
Here, we retrospectively evaluated KRAS status and related molecular features in 234 patients diagnosed with PDAC or pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) who underwent CGP through NGS. Our analysis revealed molecular differences between EO-PDAC (≤52.5 years) and late-onset (LO) PDAC (>52.5 years), specifically in the KRAS-wt subgroup. These findings indicate a more sustainable strategy, prioritizing younger patients for extensive genomic profiling in routine clinical practice. Focusing on EO KRAS-wt PDAC may improve the likelihood of detecting actionable alterations and subsequently offer these patients more targeted therapeutic options.

## Methods

## Patients and next-generation sequencing
In this study, we retrospectively reviewed the CGP of 224 PDAC and 10 PACC cancer patients, obtained between February 2017 and February 2025 at two leading Italian oncology centers—Azienda Ospedaliera Universitaria Integrata in Verona and Ospedale P. Pederzoli in Peschiera del Garda. The cohort was limited to cases with complete clinical and demographic data. This study was carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration of the World Medical Association.
NGS was conducted exclusively at Foundation Medicine laboratories (Roche®, Penzberg, Germany). For formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded patient samples, the F1CDx assay was carried out on 135 (60.3%) PDAC and 7 (70%) PACC cases, depending on the availability of sufficient tissue material. This advanced NGS technology analyzes 324 cancer-related genes in solid tumors using a high-throughput, hybridization-based capture technique. Extracted tumor DNA was sequenced with uniform depth using the Illumina® NovaSeq 6000 platform. The F1Liquid CDx assay was used to profile 89 (39.7%) PDAC and 3 (30%) PACC cases whose tissue was not sufficient for F1CDx. The assay targets circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) extracted from plasma samples. The cfDNA undergoes whole-genome shotgun library construction, followed by hybridization-based capture of 324 cancer-associated genes. The captured libraries are then sequenced with high deep coverage on the NovaSeq® 6000, and the resulting data are processed through a custom-built analysis pipeline designed to detect genomic alterations across 324 genes. To enhance sensitivity, specific regions within 75 genes are further targeted. 
Additionally, a fraction-based microsatellite instability (MSI) algorithm is employed to classify tumor specimens as either MSI-high or microsatellite stable by analyzing >2000 microsatellite loci. For more detailed information on F1CDx and F1Liquid, refer to the Summary of Safety and Effectiveness Data , and , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100179.

## European Society for Medical Oncology Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets
The clinical actionability of genomic alterations was assessed using the European Society for Medical Oncology Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESMO ESCAT) recommendations. ESCAT classifies gene mutations into six levels based on clinical evidence to guide patient selection for targeted therapy. In this study, only ESCAT TIER I-III classifications were considered.

## Statistical analysis
To establish the age cut-off predictive of gene fusion presence, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was carried out. The optimal cut-off was determined by identifying the age threshold among KRAS-wt PDAC that maximized the balance between sensitivity and specificity. The association between mutational frequency and KRAS status and/or age cut-off was assessed using Fisher’s exact test on 2 × 2 contingency tables to determine non-random relationships between categorical variables. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences between groups evaluated via the log-rank test. Data were censored on the date of last available follow-up. Statistical tests were carried out in GraphPad Prism (version 8) and R-studio. Statistical significance was defined as P value <0.05.

## Results

## KRAS status and patient stratification
Between February 2017 and February 2025, 234 patients diagnosed with pancreatic cancer underwent CGP using the F1CDx or F1Liquid NGS assay. Of these, 224 (95.7%) were histologically confirmed as PDAC. The median age for PDAC patients was 61 years (range 23-89 years) (46.4% female, 53.6% male), while PACC patients had a median age of 56 years (range 43-70 years) (30% female, 70% male). Patients’ demographics are reported in .
 Among PDACs, 172 individuals (76.8%) were found to have KRAS-mutated (KRASMUT) tumors ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100179), while 52 (23.2%) exhibited a KRAS-wt genotype ( ). Among the 10 patients (4.3%) diagnosed with PACC none exhibited KRAS mutations.
 In the PDAC cohort, and consistent with prior studies, loss-of-function alterations in key tumor suppressors, especially TP53, CDKN2A/B, and SMAD4, were significantly more prevalent in KRASMUT tumors than in KRAS-wt counterparts. In detail, TP53 alterations were detected in 140 (81.4%) of KRASMUT cases, compared with only 18 (36.5%) of KRAS-wt PDACs (P < 0.0001). Similarly, CDKN2A/B alterations were detected in 98 cases (57%) of KRASMUT compared with 8 (15.4%) of KRAS-wt PDACs (P < 0.0001). SMAD4 alterations were identified in 48 (27.9%) of KRASMUT cases, whereas only 3 (5.8%) were found in the KRAS-wt PDACs (P = 0.0009) ( A). Conversely, the DNMT3A gene was significantly more frequently altered among KRAS-wt tumors (32.7% versus 10.5%, P = 0.0003).
 Using the ESCAT recommendations, we evaluated the actionability of genetic abnormalities detected in the cohort. Within our PDAC population, we found that ESCAT TIER I-III actionable alterations were more frequent in KRAS-wt cases (23.1% versus 11.6%, P = 0.04) ( B). For example, BRCA1 mutations were detected in two KRAS-wt PDAC cases and one KRASMUT (3.8% versus 0.6%, P = 0.14) case. BRCA2 and ERBB2 mutations, however, were detected at similar frequencies in both KRAS-wt and KRASMUT PDAC groups (BRCA2: 5.8% versus 4.1%, P = 0.70; ERBB2: 1.9% versus 1.2%, P = 0.55). Conversely, PIK3CA actionable mutations were more frequent in KRASMUT, detected in eight cases compared with one case in KRAS-wt cases (4.7% versus 1.9%, P = 0.68). MDM2 alterations were found to be restricted to KRASMUT PDACs, identified in three cases (1.73% versus 0%, P > 0.99)
Notably, TIER III actionable gene fusion events were exclusively observed in KRAS-wt PDAC (9.16% versus 0, P = 0.0006) ( B). BRAF alterations were significantly enriched in KRAS-wt PDACs (7.7% versus 0.58%, P = 0.01), with fusion and V600E point mutations detected exclusively in two KRAS-wt PDAC cases (3.8% versus 0%, P = 0.06) ( C). Similarly, ALK actionable fusions were identified only in two KRAS-wt PDAC (3.8% versus 0%, P = 0.053). RET mutations were identified in two patients, one in each subgroup (1.92% in KRAS-wt versus 0.6% in KRASMUT, P = 0.41). An actionable RET fusion was, however, solely detected in the KRAS-wt group (1.9% versus 0%, P = 0.23) ( C). These findings indicate a unique enrichment of potentially targetable fusions and alterations in KRAS-wt PDAC, underscoring the potential for tailored therapeutic strategies in this subgroup.
Within PACC cases, TIER III oncogenic fusions or rearrangements were identified in 6 of 10 patients (60%), involving the RAF1, ALK, and RET genes ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100179). Additionally, only one PACC patient reported an MSI-high status (10%), and no BRCA1/2 mutations were reported.
Evaluation of mOS between the KRAS-wt and KRASMUT PDAC populations revealed a trend towards improved survival in the KRAS-wt cohort, though this difference was not statistically significant. Specifically, the mOS was 27 months in KRAS-wt patients compared with 19 months in those with KRASMUT tumors [hazard ratio (HR) 0.69, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.46-1.04, P = 0.074] ( D).

## Gene fusion and actionability identification in KRAS wild-type PDAC
This study sought to explore the relationship between age of onset and the presence of potentially actionable somatic alterations in alternative oncogenic drivers within KRAS-wt PDAC tumors.
Assessing the ability of age to discriminate the presence of actionable gene fusions, an age cut-off of 52.5 years (sensitivity 80%, 95% CI 37.55%-98.97%; specificity 85.1%, 95% CI 72.31%-92.59%) was identified by ROC curve analysis (AUC 0.8085, 95% CI 0.5825-1.000, P = 0.0244) among KRAS-wt PDAC. Based on this threshold, KRAS-wt PDAC cases were further distinguished as EO-PDAC (≤52.5 years old) or LO-PDAC (>52.5 years old), based on the age at which diagnosis and/or genomic profiling occurred. Within the KRAS-wt PDAC, 11 patients (21.2%) were classified as having EO-PDAC, with a median age at diagnosis of 47 years (range 23-52 years), and 41 patients (78.8%) were classified as having LO-PDAC, with a median age at diagnosis of 63 years (range 53-86 years) ( B and ).
 Gene variants frequency analysis revealed distinct mutational signatures between EO-PDACs and LO-PDACs patients ( A). Significant higher frequencies of druggable TIER I-III were identified among EO-PDACs (6 versus 6, or 54.5% versus 14.6%, P = 0.006) ( B). ESCAT TIERI BRCA1 mutations were detected in one case for each age subgroup (9.1% versus 2.4%, P = 0.38), while BRCA2 was found in one EO-PDAC and in two LO-PDAC (9.1% versus 4.9%, P = 0.52). MSI-high status was exclusively found in one KRAS-wt LO-PDAC case (2.4% versus 0%, P > 0.99). A significantly higher prevalence of TP53 mutations was observed in KRAS-wt LO-PDAC, being identified in 18 cases, in contrast to only a single case among younger patients (43.9% versus 9.1%, P = 0.04) ( A, , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100179). Conversely, MTAP alterations were significantly enriched in EO individuals among the KRAS-wt PDAC cohort, occurring in four cases (4/4 gene loss) compared with three cases among the LO group (2/3 gene loss and 1/3 gene rearrangement) (36.4% versus 7.3%, P = 0.03).
 Notably, gene fusions were significantly enriched in the EO-PDAC subgroup, accounting for four patients versus one among the LO cases (36.4% versus 2.4%, P = 0.005). Specifically, one EO-PDAC patient (9.1%) carried a fusion involving ALK exon 20 and EML4 exon 13 (EML4::ALK, variant 1) and another case (9.1%) carried gene fusion involving ALK exon 20 and EML4 exon 6 (EML4::ALK, variant 3a/b). A BRAF::TRIMM33 actionable fusion was identified in a 51-year-old KRAS-wt EO-PDAC. Similarly, another EO-PDAC patient exhibited two oncogenic fusions on chromosome 10, specifically RET::KIAA121 and RET::WDFY4 ( C, , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100179). This 36-year-old female was treated with the RET-selective inhibitor selpercatinib at our institution. After 3 months of therapy, she achieved a partial response according to RECIST v.1 criteria, with an evident reduction in target liver metastasis. Treatment was continued for 33 months, during which time the disease remained stable ( D). In contrast, among LO-PDAC patients, only 1 out of 41 (2.4%) had an actionable TIERIII fusion involving ROS1, while 2 others exhibited actionable point mutations in BRAF and PIK3CA (2.4% versus 0% for each) ( C, , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100179).
Survival analysis showed a trend toward improved overall survival in KRAS-wt EO-PDAC patients, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (52 versus 26 months, HR 2.27, 95% CI 0.78-6.66, P = 0.13) ( , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100179).

## Discussion
KRAS mutations, present in ∼90% of PDAC cases, represent an independent negative prognostic factor associated with reduced treatment response, chemotherapy resistance, and shorter overall survival. Only a small subset of patients harbor a KRAS-wt genotype and alternative disease-driver molecular alterations. Accordingly, the KRAS mutational status has emerged as a promising predictive biomarker for guiding personalized treatment approaches in PDAC patients. In this study, we provide evidence supporting the selective identification of patients with KRAS-wt disease who might benefit from CGP, given their increased likelihood of harboring additional actionable mutations. Our findings highlight age as a key clinical factor associated with a higher prevalence of actionable alterations within the KRAS-wt PDAC population. Despite the limited sample size of KRAS-wt PDACs in our study, patients diagnosed at age ≤52.5 years exhibited a significantly higher frequency of potentially druggable gene fusion compared with their older counterparts (36.4% versus 2.4%, P = 0.005). These results suggest that CGP may play a particularly important role in young patients with KRAS-wt PDAC, offering the potential for personalized, targeted therapeutic interventions that could meaningfully improve outcomes in this unique clinical subgroup.
In recent years, the molecular landscape of KRAS-wt PDAC has been increasingly characterized. , Consistent with previous studies, our analysis revealed that key tumor suppressor genes, including TP53, CDKN2A/B, and SMAD4, were significantly less frequent in the KRAS-wt PDAC cohort (P < 0.001). These findings highlight the markedly higher prevalence of tumor suppressor gene disruptions in KRASMUT PDAC, which may contribute to its more aggressive phenotype and poorer clinical outcomes. Moreover, survival analysis in our PDAC cohort suggested a potential advantage in overall survival for KRAS-wt patients compared with their KRASMUT counterparts (27 versus 19 months, P = 0.07), although this difference did not reach statistical significance.
Previous studies have examined the role of age of onset in patients with KRASMUT or KRAS-wt PDAC by comparing individuals younger than 50 years with control groups aged ≥70 years. By excluding patients aged between 50 and 70 years, however, these analyses may have introduced bias and yielded conflicting results. An initial study using a real-world database of DNA sequencing data demonstrated that PDAC patients under 50 years had a significantly lower incidence of KRAS mutations than those aged 70 years or older. Within the KRAS-wt subset, PDACs in patients younger than 50 years were more likely to be driven by gene fusions involving NRG1 and MET. A more recent study, using both an internal and a validation cohort, identified a trend toward a higher prevalence of ESCAT I-IIIA alterations in PDAC patients younger than 50 years compared with those older than 70 years. ESCAT alterations, however, were similarly enriched in KRAS-wt cases across age groups. Our analysis—based on a continuous age spectrum—highlights an enrichment of immediately actionable alterations in KRAS-wt PDACs diagnosed at or before the age of 52.5 years. In particular, ALK, BRAF, and RET gene fusions were exclusively detected in these younger KRAS-wt patients.
Although accounting for <0.5% of the overall PDAC population, gene fusions have significant clinical relevance and play a crucial role in both therapeutic decision-making and diagnostic evaluation. The application of extensive genomic profiling in independent studies reported an incidence of potentially actionable gene fusion in up to 15% of cases of KRAS-wt PDACs, particularly when RNA-based fusion analyses are utilized. Our data suggest that only in patients with KRAS-wt EO-PDAC, the incidence of molecular actionability could increase to as much as 36% of cases, making a strong case for investigating the therapeutic targeting of RTK fusions in these patients. ALK, BRAF and RET gene fusions lead to constitutive kinase activation and are extremely rare in PDAC. ALK fusions account only for 0.16%-1.3% in EO-PDAC cases and are exclusively found in patients not harboring KRAS mutations. Recent studies have reported clinical benefit with the third-generation ALK inhibitor lorlatinib in patients harboring ALK fusions. We recently presented the first documented case of a patient affected by advanced PACC with a novel ALK fusion partner, KN motif and ankyrin repeat domains 4 (KANK4), who achieved a major response under treatment with alectinib. Oncogenic mutations in BRAF drive activation of the MAP kinase signaling pathway and play a pivotal role in promoting tumorigenesis across various cancer types. Though uncommon, BRAF fusions—detected in about 0.4% of PDAC cases—were notably restricted to tumors with a wild-type KRAS genotype. Similarly, RET fusion events in PDAC are sporadic, accounting for ∼0.6% of cases. Data from the LIBRETTO-001 phase I/II clinical trial demonstrated that RET fusion-positive PDACs were sensitive to the selective inhibitor selpercatinib. Among our EO-PDAC KRAS-wt cases, the patient PDAC-031 was treated with selpercatinib after the detection of RET gene fusions. After 3 months of molecularly matched therapy, we measured a partial response according to RECIST v.1 criteria that lasted for 33 months.
MTAP plays a critical role in the methionine salvage pathway by recycling methylthioadenosine, a byproduct of polyamine synthesis, back into methionine. Loss of MTAP function has been implicated in tumorigenesis. In our cohort, MTAP gene loss was significantly more frequent in KRAS-wt EO-PDAC patients (4 versus 2, or 36.4% versus 4.8%, P = 0.014). This finding is of particular interest given recent phase I clinical data showing that selective PRMT5 inhibitors exhibit antitumor activity, specifically in MTAP-deficient PDAC. 
Compared with ductal adenocarcinoma, PACCs have been reported to be enriched in gene fusions, especially involving BRAF and RAF1. In our cohort, 6 out of 10 PACC cases (60%) harbored potentially actionable oncogenic fusions and rearrangements involving RAF1, ALK, and RET. Although the sample size is limited, the high prevalence of actionable alterations observed in our study, consistent with previous reports, supports the recommendation for routine molecular profiling in all PACC patients, regardless of other clinical characteristics.
In the era of personalized medicine, CGP via NGS is increasingly recommended for malignancies enriched in actionable genomic alterations, such as cholangiocarcinoma. , In contrast, the historically low prevalence of actionable mutations in unselected PDAC has limited the routine application of broad genomic testing. Moreover, large-scale NGS testing in all PDAC cases, even if limited to KRAS-wt tumors, would likely be impractical from a cost and resource standpoint. In summary, our findings support a more refined diagnostic algorithm for molecular profiling of PDAC patients ( ). Specifically, we propose initial screening based on KRAS mutation status, followed by prioritization of patients under the age of 52.5 years for CGP. This targeted approach could improve the efficiency of resource utilization and enhance clinical benefit, particularly for younger patients who may be more likely to harbor actionable genetic alterations.
