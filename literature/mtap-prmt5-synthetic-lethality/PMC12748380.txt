## Abstract

## Introduction
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) metabolites are implicated in various neurological disorders, but their relationship with primary brain malignancies remains unclear. This study aims to use a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach to evaluate the role of CSF metabolites in the development of primary brain malignancies.

## Methods
Data on 338 human CSF metabolites were obtained from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) involving 291 participants. The information on primary brain malignancies was derived from a large-scale GWAS summary, which included 816 cases and 314,193 controls of European ancestry. The inverse-variance weighted model was used as the primary analysis method, supplemented by sensitivity analyses such as heterogeneity testing, horizontal pleiotropy testing, and leave-one-out analysis. Linkage disequilibrium score regression was performed for genetic correlation analysis.

## Results
This study identified ten CSF metabolites whose associations with primary brain malignancies are consistent with a potential causal role (0.0001 < P < 0.05), including one unidentified metabolite. Of these, five metabolites were positively correlated with the risk of disease, while four showed a negative correlation. Notably, after FDR correction, the causal association between elevated levels of 5-methylthioadenosine (MTA) and increased risk of primary brain malignancies remained (P = 0.038), and there was no evidence of reverse causality between the two, suggesting that MTA may be an important risk factor for primary brain malignancies.

## Conclusions
The findings provide evidence consistent with potential causal associations between ten CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies, helping us to further understand the pathogenesis of primary brain malignancies and provide clues for the prediction of the disease.

## Introduction
Primary brain malignancies refer to the heterogeneous group of tumors produced by cells in the CNS [ ], mainly including gliomas, primary central nervous system lymphoma, and malignant forms of meningiomas [ ]. Despite advancements in surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, the prognosis for patients remains grim due to the high invasiveness and recurrence rate of tumors, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 40%2. Therefore, early diagnosis and monitoring of treatment are crucial for improving survival rates.
Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of malignant tumors, and detecting alterations of metabolite levels may provide insights into the occurrence and progression of malignancies [ ]. In recent years, metabolites in the biological fluids, such as blood, urine, and saliva, have emerged as potential diagnostic markers for various tumors [ – ]. Unlike other body fluids, CSF is in direct contact with the brain and can accurately reflects the biochemical changes within the CNS, making it a valuable minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic monitoring tool for tumors. However, challenges such as sample collection difficulties, limited sample sizes, and the complexity of metabolites have hindered the study of CSF metabolomics. As a result, current knowledge about the relationship between the CSF metabolites and brain diseases is limited. Recent study has revealed differences in CSF metabolites between glioblastoma (GBM) and control cases, including metabolites related to fatty acid oxidation and gut microbiome, highlighting several metabolites that may serve as valuable biomarkers in GBM patients [ ]. Other studies have also shown that CSF-based metabolomics can identify different types of brain tumors, offering new hope for the diagnosis of these diseases [ ].
Given the potential of CSF metabolites in disease screening and diagnosis, further investigation of the relationship between CSF metabolites and primary brain malignant malignancies is of great significance to improve the diagnosis of tumors. The aim of this study is to investigate the causal relationship between the two by using Mendelian randomization analysis, and provide clues for tumor prediction.
Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical method designed to assess causality. It utilizes single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) associations derived from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as instrumental variables (IVs) or proxy variables for exposure traits. This approach helps to elucidate causal relationships between complex traits while minimizing the influence of confounding factors and reverse causation, thereby addressing the limitations of observational studies [ , ].
In this study, we employed MR analysis to explore the causal relationship between CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies. Among the 338 CSF metabolites analyzed, we found that ten metabolites were significantly associated with the risk of primary brain malignancies, while reverse MR analysis suggested that primary brain malignancies had no potential impact on these ten CSF metabolites. Notably, after FDR correction, there was still a significant positive causal relationship between the levels of 5-methylthioadenosine (MTA) and the risk of primary brain malignancies. Further sensitivity analysis and genetic correlation analysis both confirmed the robustness and credibility of the results.
Compared with previous studies, this study used MR analysis to explore the causal relationship between CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies, which not only successfully identified a series of key metabolites, but more importantly, provided evidence consistent with potential causal links between them. This method effectively avoids the interference of confounding factors in traditional observational studies and makes up for the deficiencies of previous studies in causal inference. We believe that the results of this study contribute to understanding the effects of CSF metabolites on primary brain malignancies, and provide new ideas for tumor prediction.

## Materials and methods

## Study design
A two-sample bidirectional MR design was applied to evaluate the causal relationship between CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies. This method relies on three assumptions: (A) the genetic instruments must be directly associated with the exposure; (B) the genetic instruments should be independent of any confounders; (C) the genetic instruments should influence the outcome only through the exposure. The datasets for CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies recruited in this study were sourced from different GWAS datasets and had no sample overlap. Figure  outlines this two-sample bidirectional MR study.
 

## Source of GWAS data
Table  provides detailed information on the data sources used in this study. The GWAS summary data for CSF metabolites were obtained from a comprehensive metabolic analysis conducted by Panyard et al. 11, which examined 338 CSF metabolites in 291 participants (Table S1). The GWAS dataset related to primary brain malignancies (glioblastoma, lower-grade glioma, lymphoma and ependymoma, etc.) was obtained from the FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi). This dataset sourced from an extensive GWAS study involving a cohort of European descent, comprising 816 individuals diagnosed with primary brain malignancies and 314,193 control participants.
 

## Instrumental variable identification
A series of steps was taken to select eligible genetic variants related to exposure. First, considering that the threshold for screening exposure SNPs based on the standard (P < 5 × 10− 8) was too strict, we used a more relaxed threshold (P < 5 × 10− 5) to extract IVs from the exposure. Using the 1000 Genomes Project as a reference panel, we defined the aggregation functional parameters using r2 < 0.001 and a minimum distance greater than 10,000 kb window to reduce the potential impact of linkage disequilibrium (LD) on the random assignment of alleles. At the same time, we calculated the F statistic using the formula F = beta2/se2 to measure the statistical strength of IVs and excluded IVs with F < 10 to avoid bias due to the use of weak IVs. Then, we extracted exposure-related SNPs from the outcome data and excluded those related to the outcome (P < 5 × 10− 5). Harmonization was conducted to align the alleles of exposure SNPs with those of outcome SNPs, and palindromic SNPs were excluded to prevent distortions in strand orientation and allele coding during this process. Finally, exposures with more than two SNPs were kept for MR analysis.

## Mendelian randomization analysis
The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was selected as the primary approach to estimate the causal relationship between exposure and outcome, with additional support from the MR–Egger, weighted median and weighted mode methods [ – ]. To assess the robustness of the results, the following sensitivity analyses were performed: (A) The Cochran’s Q statistic was applied using both the IVW and MR-Egger methods to detect whether the heterogeneity caused by individual IVs’ correlation heterogeneity violates the assumption [ ]; (B) The MR-Egger intercept was utilized to evaluate the presence of horizontal pleiotropy, ensuring that the genetic variations were independently associated with both exposure and outcome [ ]; (C) The leave-one-out analysis was conducted to explore whether any individual SNPs significantly influenced the associations. The results of the study were presented using odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To correct multiple testing, the false discovery rate (FDR) method was utilized, which is a method implemented by the q-value program. A P-value < 0.05 and q-value < 0.05 was considered significant, while P-value < 0.05 and q-value >0.05 being considered suggestive causal effect.

## Genetic correlation and directionality
MR analysis may compromise causal inferences due to the genetic correlation between the exposure and outcome [ ]. Although SNPs associated with outcome were excluded during the selection of IVs, SNPs without direct relevance may also impact the occurrence of outcome. To account for potential confounding caused by coinheritance, a method called linkage disequilibrium score (LDSC) regression was employed to evaluate the genetic correlation between exposure and outcome. This method utilizes chi-square statistics derived from SNPs to estimate co-inheritance, helping to determine whether the observed causal effects are accurately represented without confounding influences.

## Statistical analysis
All analyses were performed using the “TwoSampleMR” package (version 0.6.0) and “MendelianRandomization” package (version 0.8.0) in R Software 4.3.3. LDSC was conducted by the “LDSC” package [ ].

## Data Availability
The GWAS summary data for CSF metabolites were available from the original studies [ ]. GWAS summary statistics of the primary brain malignancies were obtained from the FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi).

## Results

## MR analysis of the impact of 338 CSF metabolites on the occurrence of primary brain malignancies
We have explored the causal relationship between 338 CSF metabolites and various primary brain malignancies, which mainly include glioblastoma, astrocytoma, ependymoma, and so on. The instruments selected for MR analyses are detailed in Table S2. As presented in Table S3 and Fig.  , ten metabolites were identified as potential causal factors associated with the risk of primary brain malignancies (P < 0.05), including one unidentified metabolite. Among these metabolites, 5-methylthioadenosine (MTA) levels (OR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.16–1.58, P = 0.0001), sphingomyelin (d18:2/16:0, d18:1/16:1) levels (OR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.03–1.19, P = 0.0058), sphingomyelin (d18:1/24:1, d18:2/24:0) levels (OR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.01–1.20, P = 0.0230), phenylalanine levels (OR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.01–1.47, P = 0.0373), and taurine levels (OR = 1.79, 95% CI 1.03–3.11, P = 0.0383) were positively associated with an increased risk of primary brain malignancies. Conversely, higher levels of urate (OR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.58–0.88, P = 0.0016), (N(1) + N(8))-acetylspermidine (OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.87–0.99, P = 0.0149), N1-methylinosine (OR = 0.82, 95% CI 0.70–0.97, P = 0.0201) and 3-methylglutarylcarnitine (2) (OR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.99, P = 0.0380) were negatively associated with the risk of primary brain malignancies. Subsequently, the FDR correction was applied to better characterize these causal associations, revealing a significant causal relationship that between MTA levels and the risk of primary brain malignancies (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). The results of Cochran Q-test and MR-Egger intercept demonstrated no evidence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy in any of these findings (P >0.05) (Table S3).
 

## Investigation of the causal association between MTA levels and the occurrence of primary brain malignancies
Given the significant causal relationship between MTA levels and the risk of primary brain malignancies, we did further analysis. The results of MR analysis using the IVW method, along with MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode approaches, consistently indicate a significant positive causal relationship between MTA levels and primary brain malignancies (Fig.  A). The IVW analysis showed an OR of 1.36 (95% CI 1.16–1.58, P = 0.0001), MR-Egger reported an OR of 1.40 (95% CI 1.08–1.81, P = 0.0117), the weighted median method yielded an OR of 1.50 (95% CI 1.18–1.90, P = 0.0008), and the weighted mode approach produced an OR of 1.69 (95% CI 1.11–2.57, P = 0.0144). These findings collectively suggest that increased levels of MTA are associated with a higher risk of primary brain malignancies, confirming the robustness of the results. To rigorously assess the robustness of these MR findings, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted. The p-values obtained by the Cochran’s Q test exceeded the 0.05, suggesting no significant heterogeneity in our analysis. As for the evaluation of pleiotropy, the intercept of the MR-Egger regression model does not reveal the evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05) (Table  ). In addition, the symmetric funnel plot (Fig.  B) and the leave-one-out method (Fig.  C) further confirm the consistency and reliability of the results.
 To assess the effect of genetic correlations on the findings, we performed LDSC analysis. The results showed a weak genetic correlation between MTA levels and primary brain malignancies (rg = 0.2037, se = 1.1489, P = 0.8593). This suggests that the shared genetic component did not significantly confound the MR estimates.

## MR analysis of the impact of primary brain malignancies on ten CSF metabolites
We performed a reverse MR analysis to assess the potential influence of primary brain malignancies on ten CSF metabolites in a subsequent investigation. By setting a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10− 5, we ultimately included 43 IVs as genetic instruments in the MR analyses (Table S4). As shown in Table S5 and the scatter plots (Fig.  ), the MR analysis consistently indicated no causal relationship between primary brain malignancies and CSF metabolites, with OR close to one. Furthermore, both Cochran’s Q test and MR-Egger intercept (Table S5) indicated no significant heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy in this study. The stability of these findings was further validated by the funnel plots (Fig S1) and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Fig S2). Therefore, these ten CSF metabolites are considered as factors that may contribute to primary brain malignancies, rather than as products of tumor development. This finding provides an important reference for further exploring the metabolite changes involved in the pathogenesis of intracranial malignancies.
 

## Discussion
This study revealed the causal relationship between CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies utilizing MR analysis. Among the 338 CSF metabolites analyzed, ten metabolites were found to be potentially associated with an increased risk of primary brain malignancies, with MTA standing out as particularly significant. Additionally, sensitive analysis and LDSC further bolster the credibility of our findings. These results suggest that these CSF metabolites may be involved in the pathogenesis of primary brain malignancy and have potential role in disease prediction.
MTA is a nucleoside produced from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) during polyamine synthesis and is exclusively metabolized by MTA-phosphorylase (MTAP) [ ]. It exhibits various biological activities, including acting as an agonist of adenosine receptors and inhibiting enzymes such as protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) and protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1) [ – ]. With the increasing focus on tumor metabolism, accumulating evidence suggests that MTA and its related compounds exert significant regulatory effects on the process of tumorigenesis through various mechanisms, including gene expression modulation, signaling pathway regulation, and immune cell function modulation. Many studies have demonstrated that MTA exerts tumor-suppressive effect by inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and invasion, inducing apoptosis, and regulating the inflammatory microenvironment of tumor tissue [ ]. However, in tumors with MTAP deletion, the accumulation of MTA due to disrupted metabolism has been regarded as a potential biomarker for disease progression assessment [ ]. The complex interactions between MTA and tumors highlight the need for further investigation into its role in cancer biology.
In our study, we identified a significant positive correlation between MTA levels and the risk of primary brain malignancies, while reverse MR analysis did not provide evidence that primary brain malignancies influence MTA levels. These findings suggest that MTA may be involved in the pathogenesis of primary brain malignancies. Studies have shown that the MTAP deletion is relatively common in tumors, such as glioblastomas and pancreatic cancers, leading to marked MTA accumulation [ ]. MTA can promote the proliferation and invasion of tumor cells by inducing the transcription of interleukin-8 (IL-8) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), as well as activating the NF-κB signaling pathway [ ]. Additionally, MTA can also profoundly impair T cell function by inhibiting protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) and through adenosine receptor agonism, thereby facilitating immune evasion of tumor cells [ ]. These findings suggest that MTA is associated with the development of tumors and has the potential to serve as a predictive biomarker for cancer.
In this study, we also found that levels of sphingomyelin (d18:2/16:0, d18:1/16:1), sphingomyelin (d18:1/24:1, d18:2/24:0), phenylalanine, and taurine were potentially positively correlated with the risk of primary brain malignancies. Sphingomyelin (d18:2/16:0, d18:1/16:1) and Sphingomyelin (d18:1/24:1, d18:2/24:0) are two specific types of sphingomyelin (SM) that have been less studied in the context of tumor development. Current researches mainly focus on the role of SM in tumorigenesis. SM is a phosphosphingolipid that is integral to the composition of cellular membranes and is synthesized by sphingomyelin synthase (SMS) [ ]. The regulation of SM synthesis in cancer can have a dual role, either promoting or inhibiting tumor development. For example, Burns et al. [ ] found that in chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), the oncogene BCR-ABL can upregulate the expression and activity of SMS1, thereby sustaining tumor cells growth. Conversely, Brandan et al. [ ] demonstrated that inhibiting SMS1 activity can disrupt cell adhesion junctions and promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), potentially inhibiting tumor growth. Phenylalanine is an essential amino acid that can be converted into the non-essential amino acid tyrosine through the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase [ ]. Research has indicated that plasma levels of phenylalanine levels are reduced in patients with gastric and colorectal cancers [ ]. However other studies have reported elevated plasma and urinary levels of phenylalanine in patients with esophageal cancer [ , ], indicating that the metabolic variations of phenylalanine are tumor type specific. Taurine is a natural amino acid that plays a significant role in the development of the CNS, as well as possessing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [ ]. Previous study has found that decreased serum levels of taurine may serve as an early diagnostic biomarker for breast cancer [ ]. Additionally, other researchers have demonstrated that taurine inhibits the process of ferroptosis in prostate cancer by activating the LXRα/SCD1 signaling pathway, thereby promoting tumor growth [ ].
Furthermore, we found that levels of (N (1) + N (8))-acetylspermidine, N1-methylinosine, and 3-methylglutarylcarnitine (2) were negatively correlated with the risk of primary brain malignancies. Urate, the final metabolic breakdown product of purine metabolism, acts as an antioxidant [ ]. However, due to the confounding factors related to urate levels, the relationship between urate and cancer remains controversial. One MR study identified a potential causal relationship between high urate levels and both the incidence and all-cause mortality of various cancers [ ]. In contrast, another MR study found no evidence of a causal relationship between serum urate and lung cancer [ ]. (N (1) + N (8))-acetylspermidine is a polyamine that has been primarily studied in urine samples. Umemor et al. measured the concentration of N1, N8-diacetylspermidine (DiAcSpd) in urine samples from patients with breast and colorectal cancers. They found that while its sensitivity was low at 14.2% in breast cancer, it reached 36.3% in colorectal cancer, suggesting its potential as a tumor marker in certain cancer types [ ]. N1-methylinosine is a modified nucleoside found at position 37 (3’ adjacent to the anticodon) of eukaryotic tRNA and plays a role in various metabolic processes [ ]. Li et al. [ ] identified various modified nucleotides, including N1-methylinosine, in the urine of lymphoma patients using reverse phase liquid chromatography/electrospray tandem mass spectrometry. The presence of these modified nucleotides in the urine of cancer patients may be related to the occurrence and development of tumors. 3-Methylglutarylcarnitine (3MG carnitine) is a specific type of acylcarnitine that is associated with the mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Moore et al. [ ] found that elevated levels of 3MG carnitine, a metabolite related to body mass index (BMI), were independently associated with an increased risk of breast cancer in postmenopausal women.
From the above studies, it is evident that the relationship between metabolites and tumorigenesis largely depends on the tumor type. Different tumors often exhibit distinct metabolic profiles, which not only reflect the biological behavior of tumor cells but may also provide valuable insights for the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of tumors. Therefore, when investigating the relationship between metabolites and tumors, it is essential to consider the specific tumor type and utilize appropriate sources of metabolites to effectively explore the metabolic characteristics of tumors. CSF, as a liquid environment in direct contact with brain tissue, can accurately reflect the metabolic state of the brain through changes in its metabolites. This information may provide valuable insights for diagnosing malignant brain tumors, tracking their progression, and monitoring the effectiveness of treatment.
This study presents several notable advantages. Firstly, previous studies revealed the origin of tumors by drawing the metabolic profile of CSF [ ], but only analyzed the correlation between metabolites and malignant brain tumors, and could not completely exclude the interference of confounding factors and reverse causality. In our study, MR analysis was used to explore the causal relationship between CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies, which can effectively avoid this interference and overcome the limitations of observational studies. And we ensured the robustness of the results through rigorous statistical analysis. Secondly, our rigorous MR analysis effectively ruled out the possibilities of reverse causation and confounding interference, while sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of our results. Thirdly, we assessed the heritability of IVs using LDSC analysis, which reinforced the validity of our MR estimates. However, there are several limitations to our study that warrant consideration. First, the relatively small sample size of GWAS (n = 291) may limit the statistical power to detect strong genetic instruments, which could potentially affect the robustness of our MR estimates. Therefore, future replication in larger cohorts is warranted to validate these findings. Second, our analysis exclusively utilized data from European populations, which may limit the applicability of our findings to other demographic groups. In the future, we still need to conduct research on populations of different ethnicities in order to fully assess and understand the specific differences among various groups. Third, this study did not perform an in-depth analysis of the relationship between CSF and specific types of primary brain malignancies, which restricts our ability to elucidate the unique characteristics and differences among various subtypes of these malignancies. In the future, as more GWAS data for specific types of intracranial malignancies become available, we will be able to conduct more detailed and in-depth stratified analyses to further validate and refine our findings. Finally, we did not explore the potential biological mechanism by which CSF metabolites may affect primary brain malignancies. In subsequent studies, experimental models and clinical samples are needed to further explore the underlying biological mechanisms.

## Conclusion
In summary, this study is the initial MR investigation to comprehensively assess the causal relationship between CSF metabolites and primary brain malignancies. Our extensive analysis of 338 CSF metabolites has successfully identified ten metabolites that could potentially be associated with the risk of primary brain malignancies. Further experimental and clinical studies are needed to validate these results, elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms, and provide new evidence for the occurrence of primary brain malignancies.
