Clustered, regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and their associated proteins (CRISPR-Cas) are an adaptive immune system present in prokaryotic systems, such as bacteria and archaea. It is a relatively new method of editing DNA. Class I and Class II were created based on the structure and targeting mechanism of the effector Cas protein in the CRISPR system, which was then divided into six main categories (I to VI). Class I crRNA-effector complexes consist of three types: type I, type III, and type IV. There is only one crRNA-effector module in Class II, which includes types II, V, and VI. As a result of Cas systems’ ongoing arms race with mobile genetic elements (MGEs), the architecture of CRISPR-Cas loci and the excessive variety of Cas protein sequences are both products of this race. The fast evolution of cas genes causes small changes in how the locus is organized, which opens the door to further subtyping of cas genes in the CRISPR-Cas system. Among the CRISPR-Cas systems that have been found, type II Cas9 and type V Cas12a have garnered the most interest from the genetic engineering community [ ]. While types I, II, and V are able to identify and cleave DNA, types VI and III are able to edit RNA and DNA, respectively. According to research, all CRISPR/Cas systems may have had a common ancestor. Class I, which was expressed by a single-function cas gene, evolved to produce class II by losing part of the extra cas gene. Bacteria have made extensive use of type II and V systems due to their simplicity. More and more, CRISPR/Cas technology is being used in bacteria thanks to the discovery of endogenous type I and III systems [ , ].
Zinck finger nucleases (ZFNs), transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), and RNA-guided CRISPR-Cas systems are the three primary genome editing techniques currently. Molecular biology labs worldwide have flocked to CRISPR-Cas systems as their go-to genome editing tool because of their quick cycle, low cost, high efficiency, strong reproducibility, and simple design [ ]. Genetic disorders, infectious illnesses, malignancies, and immunological diseases have all been studied and treated using CRISPR/Cas systems. These systems have so far been utilized to research target genes involved in genome alteration, splicing, transcription, and epigenetic control. One of the most promising new developments is the possibility of long-term therapy after a single treatment for monogenic human genetic illnesses via the translational application of CRISPR/Cas [ ].
The CRISPR/Cas editing tool has numerous benefits over other engineered nucleases. These include the capacity to synthesize the guiding RNAs in vitro, (A) the ability to edit multiple loci in the mammalian genome simultaneously using various guide RNA sequences, and (B) the ability to direct the nuclease to the designated site using straightforward base-pairing principles. CRISPR/Cas9, the most widely used gene-editing technique in recent years, has been employed to investigate a variety of conditions, including cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease (SCD), Huntington’s chorea condition, Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), chronic granulomatous disease, retinitis pigmentosa, hemophilia, and thalassemia [ ].
About 40% of a vertebrate’s total mass is made up of skeletal muscles, a highly specialized and structured tissue that plays a crucial role in several essential functions, including energy metabolism, oxygen consumption, movement, and the cycle and storage of macromolecules [ ]. Many babies who experience hypotonia and weakness are suffering from muscular disorders. While many myopathic illnesses might appear during the newborn era, most are caused by a few abnormalities. In terms of frequency, myotonic dystrophy ranks first. Myopathic illnesses that lack definitive histology include congenital muscular dystrophy (MD), facioscapulohumeral dystrophy, polymyositis, and minimal change myopathy. Conversely, myopathic illnesses that do result in diagnostic histology include core myopathies, nemaline myopathy, myotubular myopathy, congenital fiber type disproportion, mitochondrial myopathies, and metabolic myopathies [ ].
Thanks to recent developments in gene editing technology, fixing genetic abnormalities that cause fatal monogenic illnesses may be possible. DMD, an exceptionally severe genetic condition, results from gene mutations that produce dystrophin, a membrane-associated protein essential for preserving muscle structure and function. Individuals suffering from DMD eventually lose their ability to move about, which leads to heart and respiratory failure and early death. So far, the illness has resisted every treatment approach. Due to the development of CRISPR gene editing, there are increased opportunities to enhance the condition by eliminating DMD mutations and reestablishing dystrophin expression in the skeletal muscles and heart [ , ]. Mutations in the DMD gene, which encodes dystrophin, cause X-linked monogenic neuromuscular illness, often known as DMD. Through their interactions with actin inside cells and laminin outside of cells, sarcoglycans and dystrophin dystroglycans keep the sarcolemma intact and stable. Patients with DMD have about four thousand known mutations, including minor missense or nonsense substitutions, deletions or duplications of one or more exons, and other variations. Muscle weakness worsens with time in DMD patients, who often die of respiratory and cardiac failure in their mid-twenties [ ].
Chronic usage and DMD-induced skeletal muscle damage activate satellite cells underneath the muscle basal lamina, leading to myofiber regeneration. It is still unknown how much satellite cells help to preserve muscle function and structure after CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. After systemic delivery of gene editing components by adeno-associated virus (AAV), successful gene editing in satellite cells would need both the introduction of gene regulatory elements that enable Cas9 expression in this particular population of stem cells and efficient AAV infectivity [ ]. At the postnatal stage, He et al. [ ] establish strong editing in juvenile SCs by AAV9-mediated short guide RNA (sgRNA) delivery, and they generate mice that express Cas9 in SCs. Furthermore, it was shown that dormant SCs are resistant to editing using CRISPR/Cas9. Using juvenile SCs, researchers proved that the CRISPR/Cas9/AAV9-sgRNA system could efficiently modify the master transcription factor (TF) Myod1 locus. By targeting two important TFs, MYC and BCL6, researchers found that SC activation and muscle regeneration are separate processes. By regulating 3D genomic architecture, MYC orchestrates SC activation, as recently discovered by researchers. The topologically associated domain borders are strengthened upon its depletion, which suggests that it may influence gene expression. Research has laid the groundwork for manipulating endogenous SCs, which can offer light on how key regulators controlling SC activities work [ ] (Fig.  ). 
This article offers a thorough examination of the innovative applications of CRISPR/Cas9 in the modification of muscle-related genes. From structural proteins to signaling networks that control muscle growth, function, and regeneration, it explores the many gene targets that have been the center of CRISPR-based therapies. MDs, myopathies, and neuromuscular diseases (NMDs) are only a few muscle-related ailments in this study. CRISPR-based techniques have shown encouraging outcomes in preclinical and clinical trials for these conditions. In addition, the progress and difficulties of using CRISPR/Cas9 in gene treatments linked to muscles are covered in the study. It delves into methods for minimizing off-target effects, improving target specificity, and efficiently delivering the CRISPR components. The article mentions how CRISPR-based technologies are starting to be used in new ways, such as tissue engineering, muscle regeneration, and creating more tailored treatment plans. To pave the way for more effective and personalized therapies, this review article aims to give a thorough overview of the revolutionary role of CRISPR/Cas9 in improving our understanding and treatment of genetic disorders related to muscles by combining present knowledge with future perspectives.

## Gene therapy in muscle-based disorders
The goal of humankind to eradicate sickness gave rise to GT. There are three aspects to GT: gene silencing using miRNA, shRNA, or siRNA; gene replacement using plasmids or viral vectors to administer the desired gene directly; and gene-editing-based therapy using specific nucleases like zinc-finger nucleases (ZFNs), transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), or CRISPR/Cas-associated nucleases to modify mutations. Transduction is the process of transferring genetic material using a bacteriophage, transformation is the direct absorption of the gene by bacterial cells under specific conditions, and transfection is the forceful delivery of the gene using viral or non-viral vectors. Non-viral transfection methodologies fall into the categories of physical, chemical, and biological procedures. Electroporation, biolistic methods, microinjection, laser treatment, high-temperature exposure, ultrasound, and hydrodynamic gene transfer are a few examples of physical techniques. Chemical approaches use transfection tools such as liposomes, DAE-dextran, calcium phosphate, and nanoparticles (NPs). Viruses that transmit genes are becoming more common in biological systems; stable gene expression may result from the incorporation of certain viruses into the genome of the host cell. Conversely, a small number of non-integrating viruses are episomal, which implies that their expression decreases as the cell divides. Numerous disorders have made use of GT thus far. One of the biggest challenges to implementing this potential treatment is ensuring gene-safe and consistent delivery [ ].
Skeletal muscle’s incredible adaptability allows it to respond functionally to many different signals, including those from the nervous system, the muscles themselves, and even diseases [ ]. Repairing any tissues that may have been harmed after an accident is known as tissue recovery. Physiotherapy is the use of scientifically supported physical techniques, such as exercise or manual therapy, to strengthen the damaged region, restore mobility, reduce discomfort, and maximize healing with the least amount of risk of consequences throughout the rehabilitation phase. Tissue regeneration is a normal, continuous process in living organisms that restores tissue architecture and function after a wound. Its two primary elements are regeneration and repair. In regeneration, injured or destroyed tissue is restored with healthy cells and tissue; in restoration, collagen is deposited to create a scar. Depending on the extent of damage and the tissue’s ability to heal, the process varies. Serious injuries need collagen deposition and scarring, which alters the tissue’s structure permanently. Recovery for superficial wounds involves the regeneration of the surface epithelium. Tissue repair is essential to maintaining overall health since chronic inflammation may lead to severe fibrosis. Despite this tremendous progress, a number of sports-related injuries still occur, such as cartilage lesions, central meniscal tears, muscle tears, and delayed bone fracture healing [ ]. Cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, severe combined immunodeficiency, familial lipoprotein lipase deficiency, homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, DMD, SCD, and other inherited disorders are finding great success with GT, which is based on gene transfection. In GT, defective natural genetic ciphers are corrected by allowing genetic materials encoding therapeutic compounds to access the target cells. One possible benefit of this treatment is the prospect of a steady and long-lasting therapeutic impact with just one dose [ ].
When people with muscle illnesses or disorders are unable to move about as freely as they once did, it takes a heavy toll on their quality of life. When it comes to conditions affecting the muscles, there aren’t many therapy alternatives. The “blood-muscle barrier,” responsible for the slow pace at which medications diffuse from blood to muscle, is one obstacle to creating new therapeutic techniques. Thus, to effectively treat muscle illnesses and disorders, medication delivery methods and tactics that target muscles are crucial [ ].
Due to several molecular processes, existing gene therapies for muscle illnesses are not always practical. One possible approach is to improve the vectors’ cell identification, entrance efficiency, or tissue-specific expression levels to make them more efficient and specific. Because antigen-presenting cells are not targeted for expression, the number of vector copies required to achieve therapeutic effects should be reduced. Additionally, the immunological response to treatment should be reduced. One big problem is that there aren’t enough effective promoters, particularly for tissues. An exciting new approach to treating uncommon disorders is GT. Regarding GT, one major hurdle is the absence of regulatory sequences that guarantee potent and selective expression in cardiac and skeletal muscle. Researchers have identified a new promoter known as muscle hybrid (MH); it is very active in cardiac and skeletal muscle cells. Its modular design allows for easy interchange and includes an intronic module that significantly boosts the gene’s expression. Researchers demonstrated that the MH promoter in cultured myoblasts, myotubes, and cardiomyocytes exhibits comparatively steady expression, more significant activity, and higher protein levels in comparison to the conventional CMV and desmin gene promoters, as well as previously developed synthetic or CKM-based promoters. The MH promoter is combined with AAV2/9, resulting in a significant increase in expression in skeletal muscle and the heart following intramuscular and systemic administration in vivo. It retains the same selectivity as the desmin-encoding gene promoter but is far more efficient. GT in skeletal muscle cells may benefit from this unique promoter. Thanks to its selectivity, it can provide high quantities of therapeutic protein while providing persistent transgene expression with little adverse effects. This has enhanced the efficacy of genetic illness treatment [ ].
One in five males suffers from DMD, a muscle-wasting condition that is X-linked. By the time they are 12 years old, affected people are confined to wheelchairs, and respiratory and heart issues ultimately cause them to pass away in their third decade. The DMD gene mutation, which codes for dystrophin, causes the illness. A structural protein called dystrophin keeps muscle fibers intact and guards against damage brought on by contractions. Muscle degeneration results from the stability and functionality of the muscle fibers being compromised by the lack of dystrophin. For persons with DMD, there is currently no effective therapy for their declining muscular function. Gene transfer utilizing the safe, non-pathogenic AAV vector to restore dystrophin expression is a viable treatment option for this fatal illness. Although the use of AAV vectors for microdystrophin gene transfer has shown remarkable therapeutic efficacy in large animal models of DMD, there is still a need for several optimization stages when bringing this cutting-edge therapeutic product from bench to bedside. Researchers examined the development of AAV-microdystrophin GT for DMD patients and other therapeutic approaches that could be useful for a subgroup of DMD patients based on their mutations [ ].
Typically, research studies examining genotype-targeted therapies for DMD have compared genotype-class-matched treated and untreated individuals. Trial participation is already difficult due to the rarity of this condition, and this measure reduces the pool of eligible controls while preventing treatment effectiveness from being confounded by genetic effects. By measuring the impact of genotype class on changes in motor function endpoints used in clinical trials over a year, researchers were able to assess the adequacy of genotypically mismatched controls in DMD. Approximately 2% of the range in ΔNSAA outcomes at 12 months was explained by the genotype classes that were analyzed. In contrast, other prognostic variables explained over 30% of the variation in significant data sources. The pooled effect estimates for the studied skip-amenable mutation classes were below two units in the ΔNSAA total score in the 1-year follow-up, which is smaller than the clinically essential differences in NSAA. After accounting for nongenotypic prognostic factors, the estimates were accurate, with standard errors below 1 unit, according to a meta-analysis that included all available data. Researchers’ findings supported the feasibility of 12-month motor function outcome trials using genotypically mixed or mismatched controls; this would cut down on the number of patients given placebos and make enrollment easier. To account for the tiny residual genotype effects identified in the study, researchers recommended that trials using these designs—including hybrid designs and multigenotype platform trials—maintain baseline balance between the treatment and control groups for the most relevant prognostic markers [ ].
A thorough meta-analysis of mRNA profiles allowed researchers to discover possible biomarkers and therapeutic targets for DMD. Researchers found a total of 63 genes to have significantly different expression levels across all three datasets with an absolute log fold change greater than 1.5. The genes that showed differential expression were first analyzed for functional enrichment, and then pathways were examined. A number of critical biological processes, molecular activities, and cellular components linked to DMD were identified by the researchers. Eight hub genes were identified as key nodes in the network: COL6A3, COL1A1, COL3A1, COL1A2, POSTN, TIMP1, THBS2, and SPP1. Researchers found two differentially expressed genes with significant absolute log-fold changes to be possible treatments: MYH3, which was upregulated, and DMD, which was downregulated. Investigators showed that the study has contributed to the molecular understanding of DMD and has identified possible treatment targets. As a last step, researchers helped bring about therapeutic approaches that can manage and lessen the effects of DMD [ ].
To boost target affinity and prevent degradation by nucleases, eteplirsen, a first-generation morpholino antisense oligonucleotide (ASO), incorporates changes to the ribose sugar, making it a phosphorodiamidate morpholinooligomer (PMO). In those with fixable mutations (such as deletions of exons 48–50 or exon 52), it restores the reading frame and promotes exon 51 exclusion. Even though there are many variations, exon 51 skipping-amenable mutations are present in 13% of those with DMD. With 8.1% of all DMD mutations occurring at exon 45 and 7.7% at exon 53, respectively, they are the second and third most common locations for single exon skipping. Once a week, patients are given eteplirsen intravenously (IV), usually using a central venous catheter that has been inserted. In clinical studies, researchers discovered that the PMO reduced respiratory decline in most treated individuals by increasing dystrophin expression, improving ambulation, and reducing forced vital capacity compared to historical controls [ ].
Rare inherited muscle disorders recognized as congenital myopathies (CM) are characterized by architectural defects in the muscle fibers. This group of cases is clinically, histopathologically, and genetically diverse. The five groupings established due to the predominating structural pathological alteration on muscle biopsy are core myopathies, nemaline myopathies, centronuclear myopathies, congenital fiber type disproportion myopathy, and myosin storage myopathy. Patients afflicted with CM often have involvement of the respiratory and/or bulbar systems, with little or no cardiac affection being seen. CM seldom manifests with cardiomyopathy except for respiratory insufficiency-related complications such as cor pulmonale. Heart muscle problems may develop in CM as a result of changes in the gene that codes for slow skeletal β-cardiac myosin (MYH7), titin (TTN), or, very rarely, α-skeletal actin (ACTA1). Most individuals born with CM will reach maturity, except in cases where significant weakness and respiratory insufficiency happen throughout infancy. Patients with severe neonatal affection and myotubularin 1 (MTM1) mutations often pass away before their first birthday. At the same time, patients with infantile-onset myotubular myopathy from ACTA1 mutations also tend to die at a young age. Patients with CM have a reduced life expectancy due to (long-term) respiratory and/or cardiac issues in some subtypes of the disorder (e.g., mutations in SEPN1 and TTN). However, precise survival statistics are not yet available. It is generally feasible for pregnant women with CM to give birth and have a baby. However, if the patient has proximal weakness in the lower limbs and pelvic girdle muscles or if anesthesia is required (for example, during a cesarean section), it is essential to use trigger-free anesthetics to avoid dangerous hyperthermia, particularly in cases where the patient has an RYR1 mutation [ ].
Because of the wide variety in the sequence of the RYR1 gene, the enormous size of the protein it encodes, and the rarity of treatment options for hereditary illnesses like myopathies associated with RYR1 variants. Thanks to recent developments in GT, new gene editing technologies allow for targeted manipulation of individual genes or mRNAs and more precise targeting of specific genetic variants. It is possible to draw inspiration from different genetic illnesses while developing cutting-edge gene editing technologies; some have previously undergone cellular and/or in vivo testing [ ]. Mutations in MTM1 cause X-linked myotubular myopathy, an uncommon and potentially fatal congenital muscle condition mainly affecting boys. This illness does not have any authorized therapy. The AAV vector serotype 8 conveying human MTM1, resamirigene bilparvovec, was analyzed for its safety and effectiveness. Researchers demonstrated that more than half of the dosed participants achieved ventilator independence, and some could walk without assistance after receiving MTM1 gene replacement treatment for X-linked myotubular myopathy. Motor function and ventilator dependency were significantly improved in these children. The need to monitor liver function before gene replacement treatment and the danger of underlying hepatobiliary illness in X-linked myotubular myopathy are topics of continuing investigation [ ].
Skeletal muscle dysfunction is a hallmark of neuromuscular disorders, a collection of uncommon and diverse illnesses caused mainly by defects in motoneuron, peripheral nerve, motor end plate, or muscle. There is a vast range of clinical manifestations within this group of diseases, from mild and scarcely noticeable symptoms to severe and potentially fatal ones. Atrophy and weakening in certain muscle groups are typical symptoms; other organs may also be involved, and heart and respiratory failure are the most prevalent complications. Several potential causes of these disorders include autoimmune, inflammation, poisoning, toxic buildup, tumors, environmental agents, neurologic, metabolic, or traumatic syndromes, aging, and hereditary or spontaneous changes in genes necessary for muscles or nerves. There is a wide variety of mutation types and transmission mechanisms. However, the vast majority of mutations are monogenic [ ]. These monogenic neuromuscular illnesses offer great potential for treatment with GT, and several gene treatments have achieved significant progress toward clinical translation. The underlying genetic mutations that underlie many disorders may now be corrected using genome engineering methods, such as gene control and targeted gene editing. In addition to the CRISPR/Cas9 system, meganucleases, zinc finger nucleases, and TALENs are helpful instruments. Gene delivery remains a significant impediment to clinical translation for most GT applications, including genome engineering. Genome editing techniques have been implemented in preclinical models to address genetic disorders. Gene-modified cells or non-viral or viral vectors have been employed to provide these instruments. Several hereditary neuromuscular illnesses are being actively investigated as potential targets for genome engineering treatments because of the encouraging findings of these investigations [ ].
As a viable option for treating DMD, an ASO-mediated exon-skipping may repair the dystrophin gene’s damaged reading frame. Unfortunately, these treatments are mutation-specific and only help certain patients; there are now just four ASOs that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has licensed, and they can only target three exons. If researchers want to focus on developing new therapies that might have the most effect on the DMD community, researchers need to know how widely used exon-skipping methods are. For all variants examined, single and double exon-skipping methods were applicable for 90.3%, 72.4% of duplications, 93.7% of minor lesions, and 92.8% of significant deletions. With an applicability of 10.6% for all mutations and 17.2% for substantial deletions, exon 51 was the most relevant target. For significant deletions (70.6%) and tiny lesions (19.2%), two multi-exon-skipping methods were applicable; these methods targeted exons 45–55 and 3–9. A considerable number of patients do not have access to exon-skipping treatments since just 27% of the UMD-DMD population examined could be treated with current FDA-approved ASOs. Increasing the availability of these treatments for people with DMD requires the clinical translation of alternative techniques [ ].
Researchers improved the effectiveness of the exon-skipping strategy by creating integrated tactics since these different compounds only led to extremely low levels of dystrophin restoration (from 1 to 6%), which is not enough to predict a significant therapeutic impact on patients’ thesis projects. Accordingly, several compounds were delivered in conjunction with the tricyclo-DNA ASOs that were synthesized in lab. In an effort to increase the bioavailability of oligonucleotides, researchers first investigated therapeutic combinations that would decrease their removal from the body via the urine. Researchers looked at treatment combinations with the goal of improving oligonucleotide intracellular transport. An endocytosis process allows oligonucleotides to enter cells. In order to access the pre-messenger RNA in the cell nucleus and execute exon skipping, they must exit the endosomes. Sadly, the cell destroys most of them, and just a tiny fraction survives. Researchers used a molecule known as UNC7938 to facilitate the expulsion of oligonucleotides from endosomes. Oligonucleotides are able to exit the late endosomes because this molecule opens up membrane pores. Restoring dystrophin levels in the heart was enhanced by 31% and in the diaphragm by 58% when the two therapies were administered together. Furthermore, after 12 weeks of treatment, heart function returned to normal. Researchers last tried a therapy combination that would boost the amount of pre-messenger RNA generated, which would expand the number of possible treatment targets. The study examined three different histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi): givinostat, valproic acid, and EX527. The first two were pan HDACis, which had previously been studied in a DMD clinical trial, while the last two were class III inhibitors. By boosting dystrophin synthesis by as much as 74% after 4 weeks of co-treatment, valproic acid and givinostat enhanced the efficacy of oligonucleotides. In addition, the animals’ muscular function was much improved after 12 weeks of co-treatment with valproic acid. In conclusion, the researchers proved in theory that ASOs combined with small molecules might enhance exon-skipping efficiency [ ].
Myofibril atrophy on muscle biopsy has long been used as a diagnostic tool for spinal muscular atrophies (SMAs), disorders affecting lower motor neuronal cells primarily in the spinal cord and brain stem motor nuclei. The survival of the motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene on chromosome 5q13.2 is the site of biallelic pathogenic variations that produce the classic and most frequent type of SMA, one of numerous genetically diverse disorders with multiple loci of inheritance. Classic SMA, which affects an estimated 1 in 6000 to 1 in 10,000 live births annually, is the leading cause of lower motor neuron illness and ranks high among the most prevalent hereditary disorders that cause death in children. To change exon 7 splicing, new medicines targeting post-transcriptional pathways show promise. The ASO Nusinersen (Spinraza, Biogen, Cambridge, MA) was created by Ionis Pharmaceuticals (Carlsbad, CA) and targets the splice site of SMN2 exon 7. This causes exon 7 to be included in the final transcript. It requires intrathecal injection because of its poor transit via the blood–brain barrier. Preclinical and early-stage human studies have shown the product’s great potential. Two phase 3 studies have been finished so far; one was in infants and the other in later-onset SMA. Both studies were halted due to significant interim results; participants in the open-label extension trial were switched to active treatment, and a worldwide exceptional access program was established for patients with type 1 SMA. Over 13 months, the risk of mortality or the need for permanent ventilation was cut in half in the study of babies with type I SMA. Standardized motor scores, electrophysiological biomarkers (ulnar and peroneal compound muscle action potentials), and motor milestones all improved in the group that received the active ingredient compared to the control group [ ].
An autosomal recessive genetic disorder called spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) causes gradual muscle atrophy and weakening. The effectiveness and safety of onasemnogene abeparvovec in SMA patients were investigated by researchers using a systematic review and meta-analysis. Researchers found that increased aminotransferases, thrombocytopenia, vomiting, and pyrexia were frequent side effects. Among SMA patients older than 8 months, thrombocytopenia (79.3%, 95%CI 65.8 ~ 90.5) and high aminotransferases (71.7%, 95%CI 62.5 ~ 80.1) were more prevalent. Researchers’ findings indicated that onasemnogene abeparvovec is a beneficial therapeutic option for people with SMA despite the lack of randomized control trial data and poor quality of evidence to support its safety and effectiveness [ ].
In the treatment of NMDs, Onasemnogene Abeparvovec (Zolgensma) was the first gene substitution medication to get FDA and EMA approval. This is recommended for people with SMA type 1 (EMA) and entails a single intravenous infusion of a recombinant AAV9 virus that contains a functional copy of the SMN1 gene. Zolgensma has shown exceptional effectiveness in extending life and preserving motor, bulbar, and respiratory function since it was first studied in a small cohort of early-onset patients in an open-label, phase I clinical study. Results regarding safety and effectiveness were validated with real-world data and a larger patient group. It is not unexpected that, consistent with other substances, the amount of the action is more significant when patients are treated at a pre-symptomatic stage due to the fast loss of motor neurons. GT’s primary adverse effects are associated with the immune system’s response to the capsid. The larger the dosage administered, the greater this. After infusion, elevated liver enzyme serum levels may be seen. However, they may be managed with steroid conditioning and temporary platelet decrease. Real-world observations have anedoctically documented thrombotic microangiopathy. Because more enormous individuals have a more significant viral load, weight-based dosing still raises safety concerns. Therefore, research is being done to demonstrate the product’s safety and effectiveness in youngsters weighing up to 21 kg (NCT04851873), and intrathecal delivery of the product is being investigated as a means of delivering larger doses of the vector straight to the central nervous system (CNS) [ , ].
Spinal and Bulbar Muscular Atrophy (SBMA) is a hereditary neuromuscular disease that weakens, atrophy, and fasciculated the muscles of the proximal limbs and bulbar regions; it affects only males. Sensory involvement, significantly influencing vibration in the distal legs, may accompany neuromuscular dysfunction in specific individuals. Skeletal myopathic and neurogenic alterations and the degeneration of lower motor neurons in the spinal cord and brainstem are associated with these symptoms. The posterior column of the spinal cord likewise has a less severe loss of myelinated axons. Expanding a CAG/polyglutamine (polyQ) repeat in the first exon of the androgen receptor (AR) gene is the mutagenic alteration that causes SBMA. The unfolding and oligomerization of polyQ AR are essential for toxicity, and the enlarged polyQ tract facilitates these processes. The remarkable sex bias of SBMA is explained by the fact that this protein aggregation is hormone-dependent. This characteristic also sets SBMA apart from eight other neurodegenerative illnesses with the exact cause—CAG repeat expansions in coding areas of unrelated genes. With symptoms usually appearing in midlife and passing away decades later, polyQ diseases are now incurable [ , ] (Fig.  ). 
There are both autosomal dominant (milder) and recessive types of RYR1-related myopathies, which are allelic to the malignant hyperthermia susceptibility (MHS) trait. Increased activity of calcium-activated proteases and aberrant sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium leak is caused by mutations in RYR1, which codes for the type 1 ryanodine receptor, an intracellular calcium release channel. Therefore, preclinical models and a Phase 1 clinical study have been used to investigate stabilizing compounds (NCT04141670). However, in a survey including individuals with RYR1 mutations, the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine did not reach primary goals (NCT02362425). Results on individuals with myopathies associated with SEPN1 (ranging from inflexible spine CMD to multi-mini core CM) are still awaited (NCT NCT02505087) [ , ].

## The CRISPR/Cas9 technology has transformed the science of gene editing
The CRISPR/Cas9 system is regarded as a component of the third generation of gene editing technology, following methods such as transcription activation-like effect nucleases and zinc finger nucleases. Researchers centered on CRISPR/Cas9 because of its efficient and user-friendly properties. This mechanism enables the nuclease activity of Cas9 proteins to break DNA double strands at appropriate sites. This approach has formed the basis for several gene editing methods, such as base editing, transcriptional regulation, gene knock-out, and gene knock-in. The CRISPR/Cas9 system uses a specially engineered single guide RNA (sgRNA) to locate the target gene. The sgRNA directs Cas9 to cleave DNA double strands and create double-strand breaks (DSB) at the target spot to accomplish gene knock-out or knock-in. Then, spontaneous repair of DNA damage takes place [ ]. The system has two primary parts—the Cas9 endonuclease and gRNA. The gRNA is a pair of RNA molecules interacting with and activating Cas9. Half of the molecule is crRNA, responsible for recognizing the target sequence; the other is tracrRNA, responsible for trans-activating crRNA. In a complex with the Cas9 nuclease, the gRNA binds to DNA randomly. An RNA/DNA heteroduplex formation occurs upon detection of the specific recognition site followed by the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) 5′-NGG-3′ (in the case of Cas9 originating from Staphylococcus pyogenes (S. pyogenes). The heteroduplex can only be formed if the gRNA complements the first eight nucleotides after the PAM. Next, the Cas9 endonuclease makes a DSB in the DNA, which the cellular repair systems will fix: (1) indels may be caused by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) at the double-strand break site or (2) donor DNA can be inserted flanked by homologous sequences by homologous recombination-directed repair (HDR) [ ].
There is strong evidence that the CRISPR/Cas9 system could treat various diseases, including cancer, allergies, immune disorders, musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, neurological, liver, blood, ocular, and viral infections. The FDA granted a biological-product license to exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel), a gene-editing treatment for severe SC) and transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia (TDT), on June 28, 2023. The FDA has just green-lit the first commercial CRISPR gene-editing medicine. More people will reap the benefits of CRISPR technology if it works [ ].
Cells use NHEJ and HDR to insert or remove target genes and repair DBS. This allows for targeted and enduring gene-level disease therapy. Two processes are started by the cell’s repair mechanism when the CRISPR/Cas9 system causes DSBs in the genome: HDR and NHEJ. The HDR process usually achieves precise transgene integration when a repair template is present. The kind of cell, the template’s structure, and even the site selection will all inevitably affect how well the foreign gene integrates. HDR mediated by CRISPR/Cas9 has been extensively used in many domains, such as genetic alteration of agriculture and medicinal research [ , ].
In the end, there is still a lack of precision in gene editing tools when doing base conversions. As a result, dCas9 transcriptional modifications have led to the creation of many editing tools. GRNA primers were used to achieve targeted alteration with minimal off-target effects. Clinical trials may be possible with the advent of safer and more effective CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing techniques. ## The efficient delivery method of the CRISPR
With the fast advancements in nanoscience, materials science, and medicine, researchers have lately created a variety of technologies that can deliver CRISPR/Cas9 in different forms for gene editing in vitro and in vivo. Physical delivery, viral vectors, and non-viral vectors are the three primary delivery strategies for the CRISPR/Cas9 system, each with its distinct format. Electroporation, laser, hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HTVI), microinjection, and ultrasonic microbubbles are all examples of physical approaches. Physical methods for in vivo administration of CRISPR/Cas9 have numerous disadvantages despite their frequent use in laboratory investigations and in vitro gene editing. The efficiency of the CRISPR/Cas9 system in editing DNA is a substantial advantage of utilizing viral vectors to disseminate it. Nevertheless, there is a possibility that viral vectors might cause cancer, and insertional mutations can result from immunogenicity. Using viral vectors to provide highly focused delivery is equally challenging. Non-viral vectors are simple to construct, have low immunogenicity, and can encapsulate CRISPR/Cas9 well. Future in vivo CRISPR/Cas9 delivery generations will undoubtedly use them despite their usually lower gene editing effectiveness than viral vectors [ ].
Cas9 and sgRNAs are physically inserted into cells using a microscope and a syringe during microinjection. The molecular weight of Cas9, which is frequently a concern in viral vector-mediated delivery due to its limited cloning capacity, is not a concern in microinjection. The needle directly penetrates the cell membrane to transfer the payloads into the nucleus. Additionally, hand injection makes regulated dosage of payloads into cells possible. Low throughput results from microinjection since the process is tedious and technically challenging. Because injections must be done under a microscope, this method cannot be used on live patients. Most microinjection applications involve transgenic animals derived from zygotes injected into animals. Patients may get therapy by having their hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells modified outside the body and put back into them. Despite the availability of in vivo electroporators and reports of effective gene editing in animals, using electroporation in humans for in vivo gene editing is still not commonly viable. Electroporation-mediated gene editing is also often expensive due to the need for particular electroporation settings for each cell type and the significant adjustment of Cas9-to-sgRNA ratios. Crucially, electroporation’s high cell death rate due to the powerful electrical current it generates suggests it may not be appropriate for stress-sensitive cell types [ , ].
Early tests showed that when applied to grown human cells, Geobacillus stearothermophilus Cas9 (GeoCas9) was essentially inert. In order to get over this inherent restriction, laboratory-evolved versions of GeoCas9 acquire mutations in the wedge (WED) domain that result in genome-editing levels that are more than 100 times higher. Extended interactions between the WED domain of iGeoCas9 and DNA substrates are shown by cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the wild-type (WT) and improved GeoCas9 (iGeoCas9) enzymes. According to their biochemical research, iGeoCas9 speeds up DNA unwinding to grab substrates in the magnesium-restricted environment that is characteristic of human cells but not of bacteria. Researchers suggested a generic approach to editing enzyme development by enabling the rational creation of additional Cas9 orthologs to increase genome-editing levels. Collectively, researchers revealed a novel function for the Cas9 WED domain in DNA unwinding and showed how Cas9-induced genome-editing activity is significantly enhanced by rapid target unwinding [ ].
AAV is a valuable vector because of its unique life cycle and capacity to infect both dividing and nondividing cells continuously. Another attractive feature is the apparent lack of pathogenicity of the WT virus. Because of this, the scientific community has been very interested in AAV, a vector that does not have an envelope and can be altered to transfer DNA to particular cells. This is especially true for experimental treatment approaches still in the clinical stage. The introduction of CRISPR genome editing technology made it easier to create a multimodal approach to treating different kinds of illnesses. Numerous pre-clinical studies and clinical trials have shown encouraging results. Recombinant AAV and CRISPR technologies show great potential for creating therapeutic approaches that may permanently correct genetic flaws that lead to illness. Regardless of the kind of mutation, a desired approach that restores gene function involves the targeted insertion of a typical sequence. Additionally, using AAVs for CRISPR delivery helps decrease pathogenicity and naturally demonstrates tissue selectivity despite the limited packing capacity of AAVs [ ].
Viral vectors are an effective delivery system for CRISPR/Cas9, often leading to a more significant editing percentage than other approaches. Despite the benefits in most situations, a small proportion of cells may be therapeutically edited or reprogrammed to treat ailments, including retinal disorders and spinal cord injuries. Consequently, in many cases, over-editing might lead to safety problems. Each medical situation should be considered when determining gene editing efficiency [ ].
Finally, non-viral delivery techniques use cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), DNA nanoclews, gold NPs based on polymers or lipids, and specific biomaterials. GT now has access to novel delivery mechanisms for DNA, RNA, and proteins because of the fast development of nanotechnology. These methods might replace viral and physical delivery. Because of their unique properties, nanomaterials have attracted much attention for delivering CRISPR/Cas9 system components into cells. Three types of NPs—Cas9/sgRNA plasmid DNA, Cas9 mRNA/sgRNA, and Cas9 protein/sgRNA (RNP)—may introduce the CRISPR/Cas9 components into cells. Positively charged peptides, polymers, and other carriers may improve endosomal escape and delivery efficiency. To improve the Cas9 protein/sgRNA complex’s delivery efficiency, CPPs—tiny, positively charged molecules—have a remarkable capacity to enter cells [ ].
Researchers have come up with a novel concept for future studies that might improve the efficiency of CRISPR-editing in stem cell transplantation for the treatment of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). For the purpose of providing HIV-resistant cells, engraftment support, and the avoidance of heart illness as a side consequence of HIV infection, researchers came up with three study ideas: (1) a double gene knockout and (2) a knockout-knockin approach. It was determined that AAV and Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) delivery were the most efficient methods for delivering CRISPR/Cas9 for knockin purposes. (3) CRISPR/SaCas9 has the potential to take over from CRISPR/Cas9 in editing genes associated with HIV. Further investigation into genes that may enhance stem cell engraftment and HIV resistance is warranted. Optimal engraftment for improved HIV therapy or resistance in individuals may be achieved by using double knockout and knock-in methods. Scientists have recommended more research on RNP delivery with CRISPR/SaCas9 in the future [ ].

A significant challenge to obtaining a complete cure for hepatitis B virus (HBV) is the persistence of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) in hepatocyte nuclei. ## CRISPR/Cas9 in Limb-girdle muscular dystrophies (LGMDs)
Selective atrophy of the muscles in the proximal limbs is the clinical manifestation of the more than thirty subtypes of limb-girdle MDs (LGMDs). There are two types of LGMD, one dominant and the other recessive, based on the mode of heredity. Although the dystrophic phenotype may manifest at any age and in varying degrees among subtypes, it always symmetrically affects the proximal limb girdle musculature. Muscle wasting, which develops from the first weakness, limits mobility and changes posture as the condition worsens. Furthermore, the muscles that control breathing and heart rate are also impacted in more severe forms. Researchers demonstrated iPSC-level fibroblast reprogramming in an LGMD2A patient and establishing an isogenic control line by rescuing the patient’s mutations using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. The second set of studies included differentiating the LGMD2A iPSC into CD82 + skeletal muscle progenitor cells, which the researchers then showed could be rescued from the exon 4 CAPN3 mutation using CRISPR/Cas9. Researchers come up with two separate plans to save these mutations: (i) Scientists used a biallelic selection technique based on FACS and Tet transactivator in conjunction with CRISPR/Cas9 genome targeting to create a novel functional chimeric exon 3–4 in LGMD2A-iPSC that lacked the two CAPN3 mutations. (ii) Researchers showed that the CD82 + /Pax7 + myogenic progenitor cells produced from LGMD2A-iPSC could be rescued from the very common exon 4 CAPN3 mutation using CRISPR/Cas9. Regarding disease modeling, the first way yields iPSCs that are precisely isogenic and LGMD2A corrected; the second strategy may be further developed regarding possible translational approaches. This allows researchers to compare an LGMD2A dystrophic line to its isogenic counterpart, a method that has the potential to provide light on the mechanisms of action of CAPN3 [ ].
A viable therapy does not exist at this time for the uncommon genetic disorder known as limb-girdle MD type D2 (LGMDD2; OMIM: 608,423). Muscle atrophy (proximal and distal), generalized contractures, skeletal abnormalities, and increasing weakening are all symptoms of LGMDD2. Notable characteristics of this inherited condition include a wide variety of possible beginning points (from birth defects to maturity), as well as a wide range of possible intensities and rates of development. In the LGMDD2 gene, a mutation deleted one adenine nucleotide (c.2771delA) from the C-terminal region of the transportin-3 (TNPO3) gene. This deletion caused a frameshift that eliminated the terminal stop codon, resulting in a mutant transportin (TNPO3mut) with a 15-aa extension (CSHSCTVPVTQECLF) that is co-expressed with the WT TNPO3 (TNPO3wt) protein. Extra TNPO3 mutations resulting in extended proteins have been documented, including c.2767delC and c.2757delC. Rather than the particular mutation, this peptide seems responsible for triggering the illness, as all known mutant TNPO3 proteins have a 14-aa C-terminal extension. A rare instance whose phenotype is significantly different from the normal LGMDD2 was discovered to have an extra TNPO3 missense mutation (c.2453G > A) that had a distinct effect on the structure [ – ].
Autosomal dominant illness that is exceedingly uncommon LGMDD2 is the result of a single-nucleotide deletion in the end codon of the nuclear import receptor transportin-3 (TNPO3), which is also involved in the infection of HIV-1. This deletion causes an expansion of the WT protein. An in vitro model of LGMDD2 was developed by scientists who immortalized a myoblast cell line with the TNPO3 mutation. This model was obtained from patients. The cell model replicated several significant molecular changes observed in patients, such as the overexpression of TNPO3, abnormalities in terminal muscle indicators, and the overactivation of autophagy. One of the severe phenotypes that was reversed in edited cells after the TNPO3 mutation was corrected by CRISPR/Cas9 editing was a complete absence of the mutant TNPO3 protein, as identified using a polyclonal antibody specific to the aberrant 15-aa peptide. In model myotubes, transcriptomic studies revealed differential expression of 15% of the transcriptome. 44% of the changes were restored to normal levels in cells repaired with CRISPR/Cas9. According to the studies of miRNAs, the mutant edition restored expression to around half of the miRNAs that had been compromised due to the illness. In conclusion, the researchers demonstrated the feasibility of using CRISPR/Cas9 to alter the TNPO3 gene for therapeutic purposes and outlined the essential reagents needed for LGMDD2 studies [ ].

## CRISPR/Cas9 in myopathies treatment
An uncommon form of myopathy known as myofibrillar disintegration, muscular weakness, and cardiomyopathy may manifest in children as Myofibrillar myopathy 6 (MFM6). Mutation p.Pro209Leu (rs121918312-T) in the BAG3 gene is the dominant gene responsible for MFM6. Most MFM6 cases are caused by de novo mutations since the effects of the BAG3 mutation are severe and develop quickly. Despite MFM6’s well-known genetic etiology, there are currently no viable therapies. Because the p.Pro209Leu mutation is so common, there is hope for regenerative medicine by using orthologous stem cells with the mutant BAG3 gene rendered inactive. Drawing on PAM-altering SNP and PAM-proximal SNP, researchers devised individualized, allele-specific CRISPR/Cas9 methods. Researchers used haplotype phasing in conjunction with cloning-sequencing to determine which chromosome in two patients had the de novo mutation. Scientists used CRISPR/Cas9 strategies based on sequence differences between normal and mutant BAG3 to inactivate the mutant allele selectively. These strategies involved 1) preventing the transcription of mutant BAG3 and 2) inducing nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) of mutant BAG3 mRNA. The following experimental validation in iPSC lines produced from patients demonstrated complete allele specificity of CRISPR/Cas9 methods and molecular effects caused by mutant BAG3 that had been rendered inactive. Moreover, the ability of iPSCs to develop into cardiomyocytes and their properties were unaffected by mutant allele-specific CRISPR/Cas9 targeting. Scientists proved that it is possible to use customized allele-specific CRISPR/Cas9 methods to provide cell resources for MFM6 regenerative medicine by selectively inactivating the mutant BAG3 [ ].
The type 1 ryanodine receptor (RyR1) is the intracellular calcium channel responsible for skeletal muscle contraction. Mutations in the RYR1 gene may lead to a group of inherited disorders called RyR1-related myopathies (RyR1-RM). Irregular calcium homeostasis and weak muscles of varied degrees are symptoms. No treatment exists for these illnesses at the present moment. Scientists demonstrated potential methods for GT. Skeletal muscle cells isolated from individuals suffering from RyR1-related myopathies have been immortalized via CRISPR/Cas9. Developed CRISPR guides target particular areas of the gene, allowing for approach customization depending on the location and sort of mutation in each patient. The researchers examined the anticipated editing’s molecular, cellular, and functional implications. Aiming to improve skeletal muscle contraction and calcium release in patient cells, researchers investigated whether these therapeutic strategies may be effective [ ].
Centronuclear myopathies (CNMs) are highly uncommon CMs affecting the muscles. To find a way to treat or prevent this condition, researchers looked at allele-specific inactivation or repair of a heterozygous mutation in the Dynamin 2 (DNM2) gene. Cells isolated from patients and a mouse model were used to evaluate truncated single-guide RNAs targeting the mutant allele. Clones were generated by precise genome repair or inactivation, and the mutant allele was effectively targeted in patient fibroblasts and Dnm2R465W/+ mice myoblasts. There was a change in transferrin absorption and autophagy in Dnm2R465W/+ myoblasts. These symptoms were restored after the mutant allele was corrected or rendered inactive. These results demonstrate the promise of CRISPR/Cas9 in rescuing symptoms associated with autosomal dominant CNM and treating heterozygous point mutations that have a gain-of-function impact. Many disorders may respond well to this approach if they are brought on by gain-of-function mechanisms brought about by germline or somatic mutations [ ].
To demonstrate CRISPR/CAS9’s potential as a therapeutic tool, Sabha et al. employed it to alter particular mutations in vitro in a muscle disease. Scientists drew their cells from people with a founder mutation in the NEB gene and were discovered to have a homozygous deletion of exon 55, a symptom of Nemaline myopathy. Also, using an animal model of Nemaline Myopathy (NEB-55), researchers were able to repair the exon 55 loss in myotube cells. Using the DNA repair process, researchers used the HITI to reintroduce exon 55 and restore its production at the RNA and protein levels. Researchers added to the growing data suggesting that genome editing using CRISPR/CAS9 technology might be a promising therapy option for neuromuscular illnesses [ ].
The second most prevalent myopathy in adulthood, facioscapulohumeral MD type 1 (FSHD1), is connected to the 4qA161 permissive haplotype and a contraction to less than 11 Repeated Units (RU) of D4Z4 macrosatellite at the 4q35 locus. There is currently no treatment for FSHD1, and early research revealed that phenotypically normal patients do not have the 4q telomeric region, indicating that FSHD is not caused by haploinsufficiency of the area harboring D4Z4 RU. In skeletal muscle cells of people with DMD, researchers investigated several CRISPR/Cas9-based therapeutic strategies. New and alternative CRISPR/Cas9-mediated therapeutic approaches, including gene regulation and duplication elimination, will also be detailed. In addition, research has shown that a modified CRISPR/Cas9 system may boost compensatory utrophin production and restore levels of β-dystroglycan, offering evidence of a functional effect. This technique applies to all cases and does not rely on mutations. Scientists elucidated the far-reaching therapeutic potential of CRISPR/Cas9 in treating DMD and other NMDs [ ].
In a large animal model, the viability of CRISPR-mediated NHEJ has only been explored in one research. Researchers gave young dystrophic pups (1-month-old) a CRISPR platform with an exon 50 deletion that could permanently skip or reframe exon 51 by disrupting an exon splicing enhancer. Administration of low-dose AAV systemically resulted in little dystrophin, in contrast to the single dog that received the high dosage (2 × 1014 vg/kg total virus), which restored high dystrophin levels in some muscles while leaving others with low levels. Researchers demonstrated the feasibility of using CRISPR gene editing to restore dystrophin protein synthesis in a large animal model [ ].
Gene editing by NHEJ-mediated processes will not be effective for all NMDs. It has been proposed that CRISPR might produce functional proteins via NHEJ based on successful exon-skipping experiments in LGMD 2B and C (caused by mutations in dysferlin or gamma-sarcoglycan, respectively). In theory, NHEJ may target other NMDs, such as FSHD, brought on by abnormal expression of harmful proteins. However, NHEJ-based treatment would not benefit many autosomal recessive LGMDs caused by enzyme mutations like LGMD2A (CAPN3) or LGMD2H (TRIM32). Therefore, it will be necessary to carefully consider the possibility of using gene editing for each NMD individually [ – ].
A novel tool that makes genome modification simpler is CRISPR and Cas. Its capacity to modify genes created a new avenue for the development of treatments for neurological monogenic disorders (NMGDs), which are incurable. The path ahead is paved by DMD, as shown by 26 out of 42 research that reveals various gene editing techniques for DMD across multiple disease types. The majority of the methods used CRISPR-cas to skip exons via deletion permanently. The neurotoxic effects in the striatum of mice models of Huntington’s disease were successfully reversed by CRISPR-cas silencing the mHTT gene. Numerous alternative approaches, such as the epigenetic control of gene expression, have been investigated in animal and cellular models of myotonic dystrophy, Fraxile X syndrome, ataxias, and other less common dystrophies. Efficacy, safety, and system delivery are the three main obstacles that must be overcome before even thinking about the therapeutic use of CRISPR-cas. This calls on the scientific community to work together while keeping ethical issues in mind. [ ].

## Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD)
The expression of the gene DUX4, which is usually absent in adult cells but plays a critical role in development, is changed in FSHD. Apoptosis is induced by DUX4 activation in FSHD skeletal muscle. The researchers show how a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function library may be used to find potential therapeutic targets for FSHD, a complicated form of MD that is presently untreated. Misexpression of the FSHD causative gene, DUX4, which codes for the very cytotoxic DUX4 protein, results from both epigenetic and genetic alterations in FSHD. To find the genes whose loss-of-function allowed DUX4-expressing muscle cells to survive, researchers ran a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screen. Researchers found harmful genes connected to the cellular hypoxia response, which is the primary cause of cell death caused by DUX4. The cellular hypoxia response and cell death were reduced when hypoxia signaling inhibitors were applied, increasing DUX4 protein turnover. The chemicals also improved structural and functional characteristics in two zebrafish models of FSHD and reduced FSHD disease indicators in patient myogenic lines. By manipulating DUX4 expression pathways throughout the genome, Researchers have discovered chemicals that may be useful for treating FSHD and provide light on the leading causes of DUX4-induced pathogenesis. Researchers’ experimental technique offers a new way of understanding genetic disorders and finding treatments for them quickly. This paradigm shift might be applied to other diseases with well-established phenotypic selection tests [ ].
Every FSHD clinical trial has used broad strategies to improve muscle homeostasis, such as lowering oxidative stress, increasing muscle mass and strength, or modulating the immune response. The only exception to this rule was the Fulcrum Therapeutics trials (NCT04003974, NCT04004000, NCT04264442). These unfocused experiments were halted before they could achieve their primary goals. Nonetheless, advancements in our understanding of the disease’s origins over the last decade have paved the way for creating several disease-specific treatment approaches, such as those that target DUX4 at various levels (DNA, mRNA, protein, etc.) or its downstream effects. While previous reviews have covered these multiple methods, the current one focuses on genome editing and gene control strategies for FSHD that involve CRISPR/Cas9 and TALEN. Compared to previous methods, CRISPR/Cas9 technology has several benefits. For example, unlike oligonucleotide-based procedures or tiny chemicals, which need regular injections throughout life, a single injection should be enough to fix the genome permanently. Additionally, Cas9 may be coupled to various modulators or editors to suppress the creation of the DUX4 mRNA, which is vital since DUX4 expression is harmful and linked to chromatin relaxation [ ] (Table  ). 

## Challenging in clinical trials
When trying to find treatments for uncommon disorders, the traditional paradigms of small-molecule clinical studies aren’t working. The Orphan Medication Act of 1983 states that a medicine may be designated as an orphan medication if it is intended to treat a rare illness or condition that impacts fewer than 200,000 people in the United States. The discovery of effective drugs is both a challenge and an emergency since many uncommon diseases that might be primarily fatal affect children. Recruiting homogeneous, representative cohorts for clinical trials is challenging due to sparse patient populations with uncommon diseases, illness heterogeneity, and geographical dispersion. Difficulties unique to gene treatments further complicate matters. One example is when patients develop antibodies to the vector after GT. This makes it impossible for them to receive any future gene treatments from that vector and may even prevent them from participating in subsequent clinical trials. Since this might prevent healthy volunteers from getting gene treatments in the future, it could affect the standard practice of dosing them. Because patients may still acquire immunity to subsequent doses of a vector even after receiving subeffective dosages, traditional dose escalation studies should be rethought in the GT setting. There is a solid moral need to ensure that all patients get a dose that has the highest chance of being effective, and there is a disproportionate amount of pressure on preclinical research to determine the safest and most effective dosage range [ ].
Preclinical research has shown promise for GT. Thus, it is time to conduct comprehensive clinical trials to evaluate its safety and effectiveness. Virus transduction, transgenic expression, protein localization, cell stabilization, and functional measurements should all be carefully analyzed and reported in these experiments. The preclinical data should be used to determine the predicted safety signals, including assessments of potential immune responses. Specific cellular and functional characteristics will vary according to the target indication, and the design and target tissues to be evaluated are informed by knowledge about the sickness state and the expected response of gene transfer therapy on those tissues [ ].
The repository website, https://clinicaltrials.gov, now enumerates a total of 94 clinical studies in which the use of CRISPR/Cas9 technology has been sanctioned for patient therapy [ , ]. After careful evaluation of the risks and benefits, a small number of CRISPR GT studies have begun. However, human CRISPR editing is still a hotly contested and contentious subject. Only individuals with severe illnesses, such as malignancies or crippling monogenic disorders, will be considered for the first few authorized studies, which are now in Phase I/II. The studies’ results have improved researchers’ understanding of the technology’s hazards, so researchers are now considering employing this approach to treat less severe disorders more quickly. It is yet unclear if the widespread use of CRISPR/Cas9 editing to treat less severe illnesses will pave the door for non-medical uses of human genome editing, such as modifying embryonic genes to produce children with desirable physical characteristics. Due to the widespread coverage of the modified “CRISPR babies,” the public’s concern about the possibility of artificial selection motivated by immoral motives has undoubtedly grown. The outcomes of the approved clinical trials and the trials’ long-term effects decided the destiny of CRISPR in GT. Some of the most important problems are learning from previous errors, determining the future of CRISPR in medicine, and determining the appropriate ethical limits for its use [ ].
An individual diagnosed with DMD at the age of 27 was administered a bespoke CRISPR-transactivator therapy by Angela Lek et al. in the form of a recombinant AAV (rAAV) serotype 9 transgene containing dSaCas9 (i.e., “dead” S. aureus Cas9, where the Cas9 nuclease activity has been disabled) fused to VP64. The number of vector genomes administered was 1 × 1014 per kilogram of body weight of rAAV. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), cardiac arrest, and mild cardiac dysfunction occurred 6 days after transgene therapy; the patient passed away 2 days later. Pericardial effusion and mild cardiac dysfunction were the following complications. The results of the postmortem investigation revealed extensive damage to the alveoli. The liver showed very low levels of transgene expression, and neither AAV serotype 9 antibodies nor effector T-cell responses were detected in any of the organs. A patient with severe DMD who received high-dose rAAV GT developed ARDS, according to the results of the study (NCT05514249) [ ]. Researchers determined that the adverse event was caused by the large dosage of AAV and not by the CRISPR treatment itself. Despite the great success of GT, high-dose systemic AAV treatment has been associated with deadly immunotoxicity in patients with DMD, SMA, and XLMTM. We can learn a lot from this first incidence of severe ARDS associated with an AAV product, which will aid in the safe translation of gene treatments for patients in the future and serve as a reminder of the obstacles we still have to face [ , ].
The dangerous condition known as transthyretin amyloidosis (also spelled ATTR amyloidosis) develops when misfolded transthyretin (TTR) protein builds up in tissues over time, most noticeably in the nervous system and the heart. One treatment option for ATTR amyloidosis is NTLA-2001, a gene-editing therapeutic drug that works by lowering blood TTR concentrations. The CRISPR-Cas9 system is the basis for this method, which consists of a LNP enclosing messenger RNA for the Cas9 protein and a single guide RNA targeting TTR. One dosage was all it took to silence TTR in preclinical research permanently. There were few serious side effects, and the ones that did happen were rather minor, according to serial safety evaluations conducted in patients during the first 28 days after infusion. There were pharmacodynamic effects that were dose-dependent. On day 28, the serum TTR protein concentration had decreased by 52% (range, 47–56) in the 0.1 mg/kg group and by 87% (range, 80–96) in the 0.3 mg/kg group compared to baseline. Administering NTLA-2001 to a limited group of individuals with familial ATTR amyloidosis and polyneuropathy resulted in reduced blood TTR protein concentrations by targeted deletion of TTR, with only moderate side effects reported (NCT04601051) [ ].
Despite the above explanation suggesting that CRISPR/Cas9 is a promising technique, the hazards and limitations of this editing system make it challenging to utilize in clinical trials. This is because it has just recently been discovered and used in humans. Some of the main issues with the CRISPR/Cas9 system are its immunogenicity, off-targeting, polymorphism, delivery technique, and ethics. To address these problems, a number of solutions have been developed and are available for usage [ ]. The risk of editing the genome in undesirable locations (off-target alterations) is another factor that undermines CRISPR/Cas9-based treatments. When it comes to in vivo editing, this is especially worrisome since it has the potential to cause gene malfunction, epigenetic alterations, or even cancer. The danger of this procedure is modest, according to several studies, and off-target mutations probably won’t cause cancer in muscle tissue as it is thought to be permanently post-mitotic. In addition, there are ways to lessen the likelihood of these adverse effects while still achieving efficient genome editing. For instance, the FokI-dCas9 system or using a pair of nCas9 to create two incisions instead of DSBs are two examples [ , ].
The timing of gene-editing correction is another crucial factor to take into account before introducing DMD gene myoediting into the clinic. Every in vivo DMD editing study that demonstrated good dystrophin restoration used young animals to receive the editing components. This indicated that early intervention is successful and probably preferable to giving older animals AAV CRISPR. Younger patients are actually better off receiving AAV CRISPR because: (A) younger animals require less AAV CRISPR because they are smaller; (B) young patients are less likely to have pre-existing immunity against AAV9; and (C) younger muscles are better preserved and exhibit fewer pathological features of dystrophic muscles. It is necessary to track the gene-editing correction’s lifespan and durability after administration [ ].

## Immunogenicity of CRISPR therapeutics in muscle tissues
Some parts of the CRISPR/Cas9 system may trigger an immune response, and that’s before researchers even talk about the immunogenicity of AAV vectors [ ]. Due to the high prevalence of pre-existing anti-AAV antibodies, AAV GT may not be an option for some DMD patients who have high levels of these antibodies. Before AAV GT can be administered, the patient’s immune system activity must be suppressed, or plasmapheresis must be performed to lower the anti-AAV antibody titer. Due to the bacterial and archaeal origins of Cas proteins, there have been reports of Cas-specific immune responses in humans, dogs, and murine, in addition to the immunogenicity of AAV vectors [ , ]. Humans, for instance, have shown evidence of innate resistance to Cas9. Mice injected with AAV Cas9 vectors have also shown Cas9-specific cellular and humoral responses, albeit whether or not this represents a cytotoxic T-cell response is yet unknown. To sum up, a significant obstacle to AAV-CRISPR treatment in big animals could be Cas9 immunity [ ].
Furthermore, it has been shown that around 78% or 58–67% of healthy human populations contain antibodies and T cells specific to SaCas9 or SpCas9 [ ]. Furthermore, an immunological response is likely to be triggered by the Cas9 enzyme because of its bacterial origin. Cas9 proteins that have had certain epitopes altered to make them less immunologically recognized might help with this. There is some worry that the recovered dystrophin protein would trigger an immunological response; nonetheless, spontaneous “revertant fibers,” in which the reading frame is preserved, occur seldom in individuals with DMD. So, the immune system shouldn’t mistake dystrophin for a foreign substance. Last but not least, sgRNAs may trigger an innate immune response under certain circumstances. But because corticosteroids are already used to many people with DMD to reduce inflammation, this may dampen even a minor immune response, making the problem irrelevant [ ].
Because of the difficulty in correcting neuromuscular abnormalities in vitro, systemic viral delivery of the editing machinery is necessary. However, this approach raises concerns about immunogenicity due to the large AAV dosages needed and the potential long-term expression of CRISPR components. The AAV platform has a number of drawbacks, even if it has a high transduction efficiency, long-lasting therapeutic advantages, and excellent clinical accomplishments. First, because CRISPR/Cas9 components are too big to be produced from a single AAV vector, the delivery choices are restricted due to the cargo capacity. Although dual-AAV systems are often used, there are promising alternatives that are being studied in vivo, such as all-in-one designs with shortened Cas9 nickase. As a manufacturing barrier, the large dosage needed for therapeutic usage has a history of being linked to immunotoxicity in individuals treated with AAV. There has to be meticulous pre- and post-infusion monitoring and intervention due to pre-existing immunity to AAVs and CRISPR components, which can restrict therapy availability. Reducing host immunogenicity is an essential goal of optimizing Cas9 with changed epitopes. Another potential solution to these problems is the creation of novel AAV serotypes that are more effective at lower dosages. Potentially significant future contributions to CRISPR/Cas system delivery might come from non-viral solutions, such as LNPs and gold, which transport messenger RNA to HSPC and other in vivo tissues. Along with all these factors, the long-term efficacy of CRISPR/Cas9 therapy for muscular diseases like DMD is critical. Treatment efficacy in developing patients may depend on the timely and accurate distribution of CRISPR/Cas9 components to satellite cells. Although preliminary research has shown that systemic AAV CRISPR administration may specifically target satellite cells and maintain editing for up to 18 months, there is still room for improvement in terms of efficiency, and additional tuning is necessary to target this specific cell type [ ].
While AAV vectors are often used for in vivo administration, the presence of neutralizing antibodies makes it impossible to administer AAV injections repeatedly. Injecting Cas9 messenger RNA and small gamma RNA into skeletal muscle several times requires a chemically defined LNP technology, which the researchers present here. Researchers showed that the LNP system was able to restore dystrophin protein in a DMD mouse model that has a humanized exon sequence and causes persistent genomic exon skipping, even if the expressions of Cas9 protein and sgRNA were temporary. Plus, researchers may target various muscle groups when researchers administer their LNP using the limb perfusion approach. For skeletal muscle problems, researchers’ LNP system’s low immunogenicity and ability to be administered repeatedly make it an attractive delivery vehicle for CRISPR-Cas9 [ ].

## CRISPR’s long-term safety and off-target effects
Optimal medication timing, dose, delivery route, dispersion, and absorption in susceptible neuronal populations are still challenges for SMN-inducing systems. It is still not apparent how important it is to target tissues and cells outside motor neurons. It is also unclear what the long-term effects will be and whether or not they will be hazardous. It is evident that while some people exhibit significant improvements when treated early during the illness, most patients only show moderate responses. More babies will get treatment for SMA shortly after delivery if the condition is tested for in the womb. Necessary steps toward a cure include developing ways to measure SMN concentrations in specific tissues and finding indicators of illness development. Research on SMN induction using SMN-independent techniques, such as medications that enhance muscular development and contractility and combinatorial treatments that include several SMN-inducing agents, are also active areas of study [ ]. While early findings from CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing techniques bode well for the future of DMD therapies, further research is required to determine how to cure or prevent dystrophy in individuals and how to increase dystrophin production throughout the body. To guarantee the immediate and distant safety of any gene editing technique, it is crucial to examine the consequences of any off-target occurrences thoroughly [ ].
Another obstacle to effective treatment is the immune response that might develop against Cas9. As is typical for S. pyogenes and S. aureus, prior contact with the bacteria that produced the Cas9 enzymes may have provoked this reaction to some extent. For instance, immune effectors might destroy Cas9-containing cells because of this reaction, rendering the treatment ineffective. Many approaches have been suggested to address this, such as modifying Cas9 nucleases so they are less immunogenic in living organisms; however, they are still in their early phases of research and will take some time to provide concrete results. More research is needed to establish the feasibility and safety of utilizing Cas9-reactive regulatory T cells to manage this immune response. However, it has been shown that it may be doable. Additionally, the immunogenicity of viral vectors used in GT is an ongoing concern that needs fixing. Fortunately, efforts are underway to address this critical matter as well, and one such effort is the creation of non-viral delivery methods, such as NPs. However, CRISPR/Cas9 is still preferable to AO therapy since it may permanently fix the genetic abnormality in DMD patients, reducing treatment expenses in the long run. Researchers have seen how CRISPR/Cas9 systems have successfully corrected the dystrophin gene’s reading frame in vitro and in vivo in several ways. Another CRISPR method that has shown promise for DMD treatment uses an enzyme other than Cas9. Reframing dystrophin exons in hiPSCs and mdx mice has been accomplished, for example, using Cpf1 enzymes derived from Prevotella and Francisella bacteria [ ].
Some problems, such as immunogenicity, off-target effects, transfer difficulties, and PAM confinement, remain unresolved. Two major concerns with GT are the off-target impact and the editing efficacy since the former may lead to unwanted changes in healthy genes and the latter to certain diseases. Editing efficiency is a key factor that determines the effect in the end. Essential for CRISPR-based therapies is the transfer of CRISPR/Cas9. An important determinant of the efficacy of Cas9-mediated GT is the transfection efficiency. Two aspects connected to the off-target impact are the targetability and the transfer time [ ]. Previously, researchers covered two methods for minimizing the off-target effect: disposable plans, like transferring the Cas9 protein, and all-in-one plans, like transferring the Cas9 plasmids and sgRNA simultaneously. The off-target impact is also reduced by Cas9 proteins that have been designed or optimized. Base edition and main edition tools avoid creating DSBs while editing genes, in contrast to traditional CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing methods. Fewer off-targets will result from this event. They have tremendous promise for use in medicine, and enhancing chemicals or prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) may boost their editing efficiency even more. Because of its central function in gene targeting, sgRNA design remains crucial. Computer tools and criteria that determine things like the distance from the PAM sequence may be helpful in improving sgRNA design. For treatments like SCDs, the majority of clinical studies investigate gene editing in cells taken from patients outside of the body. Although not applicable to all diseases, this approach lessens the likelihood of off-target impact and transfer difficulties. The clinical use of CRISPR/Cas9 is in its early stages, and there are still significant hurdles with Cas9-based editing technologies, including off-target effects and transfer mechanisms [ ].
The degree and length of the editors’ expression in the cells have a significant impact on the activity and accuracy of gene editing. Consequently, how Cas9/sgRNA is delivered into the target cells has a significant impact on its off-target effect. Cas9/sgRNA may be introduced into cell culture applications via viral transduction, RNP electroporation (purified Cas9/sgRNA complex, or RNP), or plasmid transfection. Compared to the other delivery techniques, RNP electroporation shows better on-target editing efficiency and fewer off-target alterations. To increase the fidelity of genome editing, Cas9 mRNA and sgRNA may also be introduced into cells via liposome-based vectors or electroporation, much as RNP electroporation. In order to prevent off-target consequences from extended editor expression, the goal of all these techniques is to produce a brief peak expression of CRISPR/Cas9 followed by a rapid turnover of these editors [ ].
Following the suggestion of Lee et al., HDR repair restoring WT dystrophin was induced by using gold-conjugated delivery instead of viral delivery vehicles; however, the cytotoxicity of this method is still being monitored. With the use of extracellular nanovesicles, researchers induced permanent exon 45 deletions with little to no off-target cleavage, significantly reducing the potential toxicity of facilitated delivery. Although the technique is not harmful, it should be highlighted that HIV-1 Tat drives the production of its single-directed RNA, which might lead to nonspecific incorporation and pose a danger. It was previously thought that AAV’s low capacity may make it hard to transport all of CRISPR/Cas9’s components. However, it was later shown that utilizing a much smaller protein, such as SaCas9, might overcome this payload constraint [ , ].
The off-target consequences of CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing, which occur at a frequency of 50% or more, are a significant cause for worry. Some proposed changes to address this, such as making the REC3 less sensitive, as seen in Cas9 variations. Furthermore, it was shown that the recognition lobe (REC) is involved in the following conformational activation of the very flexible HNH domain (One of the most essential parts of CRISPR/Cas9 systems is the HNH domain, which is called for the three amino acids that make it up: histidine, asparagine, and histidine), expanding on the previously stated conformational dynamics. Modifications to REC3 inhibit downstream conformation in the HNH domain upon binding to off-targets, therefore avoiding the possibility of off-target genome editing [ ]. There are several ways to do CRISPR/Cas-mediated editing of NMDs, with guide (g)RNAs directed to specific locations like introns, exons, intron/exon borders, or UTRs. Since undesirable tiny indels that emerge at the rejoining site would likely not have a substantial impact, intronic targeting—such as to induce exon deletions or delete pseudoexons—may be beneficial. However, unmapped regulatory regions might be damaged. Although full-length proteins may be generated if mutations were targeted to specific exons, this method requires HDR and pinpoint accuracy. Even though there are continuous efforts to enhance efficiency or use integration routes based on NHEJ, HDR is presently inefficient compared to NHEJ. Furthermore, exons may be used to make deletions or hybrid exons; for instance, a CinDel strategy produced DMD hybrid exons engineered to preserve the correct dystrophin phasing [ ].
Determining if presently available serotypes can effectively target satellite cells is crucial since an ideal treatment for MDs would target these cells. Should that be the case, further research must be conducted to create or develop a unique serotype that can effectively transport gene-editing components to these targets. Other systems, such as virus-like particles or NPs, are potentially viable contenders for this position, although further in vivo research is needed to determine their safety and effectiveness. CRISPR/Cas9 editing efficiency is not as high as intended when targeting muscle tissue. It is necessary to continue developing more effective methods and techniques even while the difficulties in getting editing machinery to cells contribute to less than optimum muscle correction rates. Although promising, tools like Base and Prime Editor must be developed further and validated in vivo. The WT proteins can be restored using knock-in techniques; however, existing methods like HDR are ineffective. Creating systems dependent on integrases and HMEJ may provide new avenues for developing DNA knock-in-based MD treatments. Given their success in vivo research and clinical trials, AAV9s will probably be used in the first generation of CRISPR therapies for medical professionals. While AAV9s could be the most excellent solution, a better delivery system will undoubtedly be needed for future treatments. Since NPs are very modular and may contain a variety of transiently expressed payloads, they could take the form of NPs. NPs might be administered again to reverse the effects of muscle turnover and treat musculoskeletal dystrophies [ ].
For in vivo CRISPR-Cas gene editing, researchers are continuing to refine various viral vectors and non-viral formulations. Vectors based on AAVs are among the most promising platforms for delivering CRISPR-Cas components to many organs in living organisms, including the retina, heart, liver, brain, and muscle. Above all, researchers showed that CRISPR-Cas components delivered to the liver using AAV8 or AAV9 vectors may be effectively targeted in living organisms [ , ]. Despite several findings of genome editing in DMD preclinical animals, questions about the safety and durability of these genome editing techniques remain unanswered. In the mdx animal model of DMD, researchers demonstrated that a single intravenous injection of an AAV-CRISPR may restore dystrophin protein levels and modify the genome for up to a year. Additionally, researchers showed that adult mice fed AAV-CRISPR develop immunogenic responses; however, treating newborn animals prevents both humoral and cellular immunological responses. When designing AAV-CRISPR for therapeutic use, it is essential to keep in mind the unexpected changes to genomes and transcripts that have been seen. Researchers demonstrated the promise of AAV-CRISPR for long-term genome editing and drew attention to areas that need more research, such as the host response and alternate editing results [ ].
Genome editing using CRISPR/Cas9 has successfully corrected juvenile-onset DMD mutations in animal models. But how long CRISPR/Cas9 editing would last was still up in the air. Researchers tackled these problems by delivering AAV9-expressing CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing components systemically into ΔEx44 DMD animals. This allowed for the repair of exon 45 of the dystrophin gene, which allowed for robust dystrophin production and the preservation of muscle structure and function. After correcting the genome using CRISPR/Cas9, researchers discovered that dystrophin is expressed throughout a mouse’s life. Additionally, the restored skeletal muscle exhibited remarkable durability and resistance to myofiber necrosis and fibrosis, and this was even after chronic damage. On the other hand, researchers noticed that gene-edited dystrophin expression was lost when muscle fibers were ablated by injecting barium chloride. Gene correction was found to be stable, and there was no substantial off-target editing at 18 months of age, according to an analysis of on- and off-target editing in elderly mice. Researchers showed that CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing is a long-term effective treatment for DMD that can keep DMD muscles healthy and functioning even when subjected to stressful environments [ ].
Before using CRISPR-mediated editing as a treatment method, it is crucial to know what the long-term impacts and implications will be. Concerning the rate of muscle turnover, there is currently a lack of understanding about the durability of genetic manipulation in muscle cells within the setting of MD patients. It is very improbable that the long-term advantages of a single virally mediated therapy would materialize, according to researchers, unless satellite cell populations are corrected. For MDs, this is a highly pressing matter since the best time to start treatment is in infancy before symptoms get worse. Due to the rapid onset of muscle development at this age, CRISPR treatments targeting non-satellite cells may rapidly become ineffective due to the loss or diluting of corrected nuclei. Despite AAVs’ reputation as a human-friendly vector, their reported side effects might restrict their therapeutic relevance. Integration of the AAV into the genome, immunogenicity against the virus and Cas9 machinery, and genotoxic issues are all amplified [ ]. Researchers are now using capsid gene shuffling to generate viral variants that are resistant to neutralizing antibodies in order to circumvent preexisting immunity to AAV and allow for re-administration. It is more hopeful to modify or replace certain parts of the capsid to develop vectors with reduced host immunological response rather than using different serotypes for a second therapy in conjunction with immune modulatory medicines. Potentially leading to vectors with improved infectivity, stability, toxicity, and increased tropism, selective capsid changes have a wide range of potential applications. Finally, when using dual sgRNA, the probability of undesirable off-target cleavage events by CRISPR/Cas9 is twofold. Researchers also need to think about structural genomic modifications, such as significant deletions and inversions. One major worry is the possibility of unintentional germline alterations. It is possible to target both somatic cells and germ cells in the gonads when treating a multisystem condition like DM1 with a whole-body activation of CRISPR/Cas9. Risks associated with heritable, non-heritable, and accidental heritable genome editing techniques must be carefully considered and managed, as pointed out by Lander et al. and Monckton [ ].

## Conclusion
The clinical world is hopeful about the upcoming uses of CRISPR/Cas9. Genome editing research about CRISPR/Cas9 treatment for genetic illnesses affecting the muscles advances rapidly, generally mirroring genome editing methods’ development. The development of this technology began just a short time ago, and there are now many scientists involved in its advancement. The vast majority of these scientists are investigating the potential of genome editing as a treatment for human illness. There are currently no clinical trials for CRISPR/Cas9 experimental therapies for DMD. Future research into CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing as a treatment for people with advanced DMD might benefit from more investigations in mice exhibiting DMD pathology. CRISPR/Cas9 and its effects may induce genomic exon skipping to endure long. It is also believed that therapy would need several injections because of the difficulty of treating big tissues like skeletal muscle with a single dose. Concerning LNP technology, which inserts an RNA-based CRISPR/Cas9 system into large skeletal muscles, there is still much room for improvement, particularly regarding in vivo safety, injection routes, immunological reactions, and the number of injections needed [ ]. Consequently, research into targeted therapy approaches using various NPs, notably safer and less toxic NPs for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated treatment of muscle genetic disorders, is crucial.
