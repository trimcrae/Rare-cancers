## Abstract
Alternative splicing (AS) is a crucial post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism that is frequently disrupted in cancer, leading to the generation of tumor-specific splice variants. These aberrant splicing events, often driven by mutations in splice sites or splicing factors (SFs), produce abnormal mRNA transcripts and protein isoforms that contribute to tumor initiation, progression, and immune evasion. Recent advancements in cancer immunotherapy have positioned AS-derived neoantigens as a novel and promising class of tumor-specific targets. These neoepitopes significantly expand the pool of immunogenic antigens for mRNA vaccines and adoptive cell transfer therapies, triggering robust and targeted anti-tumor immune responses. This review offers a comprehensive overview of the molecular mechanisms driving the generation of AS-derived neoantigens, their tumorigenic and immunological properties, and the antigen processing and presentation pathways involved. Additionally, we discuss emerging therapeutic strategies that exploit these neoantigens, such as splicing modulation and personalized immunotherapies, while also addressing current challenges and future prospects for translating AS-derived neoantigens into precision cancer immunotherapy.
Keywords: Alternative splicing (AS), Neoantigen, Cancer immunity, Immunotherapy, RNA splicing, Tumor microenvironment

## Introduction
Alternative splicing (AS) is a fundamental post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism in higher eukaryotes that enables a single gene to generate multiple mRNA transcripts through the selective inclusion or exclusion of exons and introns from a precursor mRNA (pre-mRNA) [ ]. Previous research has revealed that over 95% of human multi-exonic genes undergo AS, significantly expanding the coding capacity of the genome and contributing to the remarkable diversity of proteins [ , ]. By enabling the production of various protein isoforms with distinct functions, AS plays a crucial role in a wide range of physiological processes, including neural development, cell differentiation, and immune regulation. Compared with normal tissues, most tumors exhibit extensive AS aberrations, including frequent retention of introns that are normally excised, inappropriate expression of splice variants typically restricted to other cell types or developmental stages, and splicing errors that inactivate tumor suppressor genes or promote the expression of oncogenes [ – ]. These aberrant splicing events may serve as molecular hallmarks of cancer, contributing to malignant proliferation, migration, and invasion [ ]. For instance, cancer cells produce a family of CD44 protein isoforms through AS that are involved in multiple distinct cellular functions, including proliferation, adhesion, and migration [ ]. Similarly, AS of the B-cell lymphoma ×(Bcl-x) pre-mRNA generates the anti-apoptotic Bcl-x(L) and pro-apoptotic Bcl-x(S) protein isoforms [ ].
Meanwhile, with the widespread application of immunotherapy, tumor neoantigens have emerged as a promising frontier in cancer treatment. Neoantigens, generated by tumor cells due to genetic mutations or other alterations, distinguish tumor cells from normal cells in the immune system. Unlike self-antigens that undergo thymic selection, neoantigens escape central immune tolerance mechanisms, thereby eliciting robust immune responses, particularly from CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes [ ]. Because neoantigens are exclusively expressed in tumor cells, they are considered ideal targets for immunotherapy. In recent years, neoantigen-based personalized vaccines and cell therapies have shown significant potential in clinical trials, offering new hope for cancer treatment. For example, in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the objective response rate of personalized neoantigen vaccines combined with pembrolizumab was 30.6%, significantly higher than the 12–18% objective response rate reported for pembrolizumab monotherapy [ ]. While previous research has focused on tumor-specific somatic mutations, emerging evidence suggests that AS represents a substantial reservoir of neoantigens. For instance, 58 tumor-specific antigens were identified in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), mostly resulting from intron-retention events [ ]. These antigens tend to be highly tumor-specific, clonally expressed, and closely associated with therapeutic responses [ , ]. Moreover, studies have shown that some AS-derived neoantigens may be shared across patients or tumor types, highlighting their potential as broadly applicable immunotherapeutic targets [ ]. Advances in high-throughput sequencing, bioinformatics, and mass spectrometry have greatly improved the identification and clinical application of AS-derived neoantigens, opening new avenues for their integration into personalized cancer immunotherapy [ – ].
In this review, we provide a comprehensive summary of the normal mechanisms of AS, the generation of tumor-associated aberrant splicing events, and the tumorigenic effects of these aberrant splicing events. We also delve into the immunogenic characteristics of neoantigens derived from AS, their interactions within the tumor microenvironment, and potential methods for predicting and regulating these neoantigens. Further, we discuss the opportunities and limitations associated with the clinical application of immunotherapies based on AS-derived neoantigens and propose potential solutions.

## Alternative splicing process

## Principles of alternative splicing
In eukaryotic cells, pre-mRNA splicing is catalyzed by the spliceosome, a dynamic ribonucleoprotein complex composed of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) and numerous auxiliary proteins (Fig. A) [ ]. The spliceosome recognizes and binds to specific sequences within the pre-mRNA, which are crucial for accurate splicing. These sequences include the 5’ splice site (donor site), the branch point sequence, the polypyrimidine tract, and the 3’ splice site (acceptor site) [ ]. The 5’ splice site typically features a conserved GU dinucleotide at the exon-intron boundary, while the branch point sequence contains an adenine nucleotide situated upstream of the 3’ splice site. The polypyrimidine tract, rich in cytosine and uracil residues, precedes the 3’ splice site, which usually ends with an AG dinucleotide [ ]. Through two sequential transesterification reactions, introns are excised and adjacent exons are ligated to produce mature mRNA transcripts [ ].
 Splice sites can be classified as “strong” or “weak” based on their similarity to the consensus sequence [ ]. Strong splice sites, which closely resemble the consensus sequence, are more efficiently recognized by spliceosomal components and typically result in constitutive splicing. In contrast, weak or suboptimal splice sites may lead to AS. In such cases, the weak site is only partially utilized in the intron removal and exon ligation reactions, giving rise to different proportions of mature mRNA variants [ ]. The utilization of splice sites is influenced not only by their intrinsic sequences acting in cis, but also by trans-acting proteins known as SFs [ ]. These proteins recognize and bind to specific target sequences located within exons or introns (Fig. B) [ ]. Key regulatory sequences include exonic splicing enhancers (ESEs) and silencers (ESSs), as well as intronic splicing enhancers (ISEs) and silencers (ISSs).

## Canonical types of alternative splicing events
Among the various forms of AS (Fig. C), exon skipping (ES) is the most prevalent in eukaryotes. This event involves the exclusion of one or more exons from the mature mRNA transcript, potentially causing changes in reading frame or disrupting functional protein domains. The therapeutic potential of modulating ES was initially demonstrated in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), where antisense phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers (PMOs) were used to induce ES in the DMD gene. This approach successfully restored the open reading frame (ORF) and partially recovered muscle function in animal models, marking an important milestone in AS-based therapeutics [ ].
Intron retention (IR) refers to the failure to remove intronic sequences from pre-mRNA, often resulting in premature stop codons that trigger nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) or the generation of altered protein isoforms [ ]. In murine neuroblastoma cells, a group of neuron-specific genes exhibit regulated IR that modulates gene expression levels via the NMD pathway and influences neuronal differentiation [ ]. Elevated IR has also been observed in both hematological malignancies and solid tumors, where its level negatively correlates with proliferation and SF expression, suggesting a broader role in cell-fate determination and tumorigenesis [ ].
Alternative 5′ and 3′ splice site selection (A5SS/A3SS) involves the use of non-canonical donor or acceptor sites near the standard splice junctions, resulting in subtle insertions or deletions (indels) of nucleotides within exons or introns [ ]. Genome-wide analyses show that these sites typically lie within ±6 nucleotides of the dominant splice site and often preserve the ORF, reflecting cellular mechanisms to mitigate detrimental outcomes of splicing variability [ ]. Furthermore, tandem NAGNAG acceptor motifs, found in many eukaryotic genes, enable fine-tuning of protein structure through the inclusion or exclusion of three nucleotides at exon boundaries, contributing to proteomic plasticity [ ].
Mutually exclusive exons (MXEs) represent a specialized form of AS wherein only one exon of an exon pair is included in the mature mRNA while the other is excluded [ , ]. This mechanism ensures the production of protein isoforms with distinct functional domains, often influencing tissue-specific functions and developmental processes. For instance, the Drosophila Dscam gene can generate thousands of isoforms through MXE splicing, contributing to neural diversity [ ]. In humans, the TNNT3 gene (encoding fast skeletal muscle troponin T) undergoes MXE splicing in its C-terminal region, resulting in isoforms that modulate muscle contraction properties [ ].
Alternative promoters (APs) contribute to transcript diversity by initiating transcription at different start sites within a gene, leading to mRNA isoforms with variable 5′ untranslated regions (5′UTRs) and, in some cases, distinct coding sequences [ ]. This regulation allows for tissue-specific expression and responsiveness to environmental cues [ ]. For example, the use of alternative promoters in the human MOCS1 gene results in isoforms with different N-terminal sequences, impacting mitochondrial targeting and function [ ].
Alternative polyadenylation (APA), also known as alternative transcriptional termination, involves the selection of different polyadenylation sites within a pre-mRNA, producing transcripts with variable 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTRs) (and sometimes different coding sequence endpoints). APA can influence mRNA stability, localization, and translational efficiency [ , ]. In immune cells, for instance, activation-induced APA leads to shorter 3′UTRs in transcripts, reducing microRNA-mediated repression and enhancing protein expression during immune responses [ , , ].
Collectively, ES, IR, alternative splice site selection, MXEs, alternative promoters, and APA represent the primary mechanisms of AS and transcript diversification. These processes significantly expand the coding potential of the genome and play essential roles in regulating gene expression, contributing to cellular differentiation, adaptation, and disease pathogenesis. Understanding these mechanisms provides insights into the complexity of gene regulation and offers potential avenues for therapeutic intervention targeting aberrant splicing events.

## Alternative splicing of non-coding RNAs
Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), which are functional transcripts without protein-coding capability, also demonstrate an intimate association with AS in addition to coding RNAs. Advances in high-throughput sequencing over the last decades have led to the identification of numerous ncRNAs, including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), which can be generated by AS of precursor lncRNAs (pre-lncRNAs) or pre-mRNAs [ – ].
circRNAs are formed via back-splicing of a pre-mRNA [ – ]. Back-splicing can be considered an additional form of AS. Moreover, the four canonical types of AS described above—cassette exon inclusion/skipping, IR, alternative 5′ splicing, and alternative 3′ splicing—have all been observed in the production of multi-exon circRNAs. Different circRNAs can be produced from the same sequence through alternative back-splicing [ , ]. Compared with linear RNA splicing, the selection of circRNA-specific AS sites has three unique features [ , ]: (1) the biogenesis of circular RNAs can circumvent the canonical splicing sites used for their cognate mRNAs; (2) through selection of AS sites, thousands of previously unannotated cassette exons are incorporated into circRNAs (these exons are typically undetectable in the cognate linear mRNA); and (3) circRNA-specific cassette exons are subject to highly dynamic regulation. The back-splicing process can form new exon-exon junctions and translation reading frames in circRNAs, which may promote the generation of unique peptides or proteins with cancer specificity [ , ].
The AS process of lncRNAs leads to the generation of diverse isoforms. [ , ]. A multi-exon lncRNA PXN-AS1, which is regulated by SFs MBNL3 and DDX17, can be alternatively spliced into multiple isoforms in HCC [ ]. The AS of pre-lncRNAs, mediated by splice factors, facilitates the “L/S” switching mechanism, resulting in the production of long and short isoforms. For example, AS of lncRNA PCAT19 yields two isoforms, PCAT19-short and PCAT19-long, which display reciprocal expression patterns in prostate cancer [ ]. Certain pre-mRNAs exhibit bifunctional characteristics and can serve as precursors for both mRNAs and lncRNAs through AS [ , ]. In breast cancer, the PNUTS mRNA can switch to lncRNA-PNUTS via AS, which functions as a competitive sponge for miR-205, thereby promoting tumor epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. Similarly, a bifunctional PD-L1 pre-mRNA produces PD-L1-lnc, a lncRNA isoform, in addition to PD-L1 mRNA in lung adenocarcinoma [ ]. Furthermore, lncRNAs may also participate in abnormal AS of certain cancer-related genes by interacting with pre-mRNAs, encoding small peptides, or associating with SFs [ ].

## Aberrant alternative splicing in cancer

## Drivers of aberrant alternative splicing
Aberrant splicing in tumors mainly arises from mutations and dysregulation affecting the AS process (Fig. ) and may play crucial roles in tumor development and progression.
 

## Alterations in splice sites
Splice-site-creating mutations (SCMs) directly affect AS. Using the bioinformatics tool MiSplice, researchers found numerous SCMs in 8,656 TCGA tumor samples. Some SCMs not only destroy canonical splice sites but also create alternative ones, producing abnormal splice junctions [ ]. Moreover, in an analysis of 783 cancer cases with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data and 9,494 cases with whole-exome sequencing (WES) data, 562 non-coding mutations causing splice alterations were discovered. These mutations mostly create AS sites, which interact with existing cryptic splice sites and can induce the formation of novel exons [ ]. Additionally, as mobile repetitive nucleotide sequences that integrate into the genome via transposition, transposable elements (TEs) can alter gene expression in diverse ways, such as generating alternatively spliced transcript variants [ ]. In an analysis of 614 matched normal-tumor tissue pairs across 13 cancer types, researchers identified 4,820 TE-derived AS sites [ ]. These splice-site alterations exert significant influence on tumor biology by inducing AS events that disrupt normal gene functions. For instance, TP53 splice site mutations often result in ES or IR, yielding a nonfunctional p53 protein that fails to suppress tumor growth [ ]. Similarly, a PIK3R1 splice-site mutation can dysregulate the PI3K signaling pathway, promoting cell survival and proliferation [ ], while CDKN2A splice-site mutations can lead to loss of p16 function, disrupting cell cycle control [ ].

## Mutations in splicing factors
Recurrent hotspot mutations in core SFs, including SF3B1, SRSF2, and U2AF1, are frequently observed across various malignancies and are known to disrupt pre-mRNA splice site recognition, resulting in widespread splicing dysregulation. The SF3B1 K700E mutation is among the most prevalent of these; it alters U2 snRNP branch-point recognition and leads to aberrant use of 3′ splice sites and misregulated exon inclusion. These events have been reported in both LUAD and hematologic malignancies, where they are implicated in tumor proliferation and modulation of the immune microenvironment [ ]. Notably, SF3B1 K700E disrupts interaction with the splicing factor SUGP1, generating abnormal ANAPC13 isoforms in cancer cells and impairing regulatory T cell function, thus contributing to immune evasion [ ].
Mutations at the P95 position of SRSF2 (e.g., P95H or P95R) are frequently detected in AML and myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS). These mutations alter SRSF2’s RNA-binding specificity toward CCNG motifs, reprogramming the splicing of key hematopoietic genes such as INTS3 and MAP3K7, thereby promoting leukemic cell proliferation and blocking differentiation [ ]. Likewise, U2AF1 S34F/Y and Q157P/R mutations occur in both lung and myeloid cancers. These variants enhance binding to non-canonical 3′ splice sites (e.g., CAG or UAG), inducing aberrant splicing of DNA repair genes such as RAD51. The resultant impairment of homologous recombination sensitizes tumor cells to ATR (ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related) kinase inhibition [ , ].

## Alterations in cis-acting splicing elements
Beyond the well-characterized trans-acting SF mutations, structural alterations and point mutations affecting cis-acting splicing regulatory elements, including ESEs/ISEs and ESSs/ISSs, have emerged as critical contributors to oncogenic splicing dysregulation. These cis-acting element changes can alter the splicing process by changing the binding pattern of splicing factors to exonic splicing enhancer or silencer motifs, resulting in ES, IR, and various other forms of alternative splicing [ , ]. A representative example is the exon 20 insertion in EGFR, which not only constitutively activates the tyrosine kinase domain but also interferes with adjacent ESEs, promoting partial IR. This results in fusion-derived peptides that are presented on major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) and are highly immunogenic [ – ]. Larger-scale structural rearrangements, such as chromosomal translocations or exon duplications, can delete or reposition cis-elements, activating cryptic splice sites and generating tumor-specific isoforms. In prostate cancer, the TMPRSS2-ERG gene fusion eliminates multiple ESSs, leading to aberrant inclusion of ERG exons and production of immunogenic fusion junction peptides [ ]. Collectively, alterations in cis-regulatory splicing elements not only drive the generation of tumor-specific isoforms and resistance mechanisms, but also serve as a rich source of immunogenic neoantigens. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting cis-splicing dysregulation for precision cancer immunotherapy.

## Epigenetic dysregulation of splicing
Epigenetic regulation, such as histone acetylation and DNA methylation, can mediate or prevent interactions with SFs [ , ]. For instance, p300 (a histone acetyltransferase) can acetylate SFs like SRSF2 and SRSF3, influencing their binding to RNA and the selection of splicing sites, thereby regulating AS [ ]. Meanwhile, a complex formed by the splicing factor heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein F (hnRNPF) and the histone demethylase JMJD1A binds to regions of a cryptic exon that contain methylated H3K9, promoting inclusion of this exon [ ].
Recent studies using RNA Polymerase II (Pol II) elongation mutants demonstrate that transcriptional kinetics can influence splicing outcomes: accelerated transcription enhances inclusion of certain alternative exons, whereas slowed elongation promotes ES [ , ]. These findings, validated across diverse tumor models, highlight that the kinetic coupling between transcription and splicing is a key regulatory axis in cancer [ ]. Consequently, epigenetic regulation may play an indirect yet crucial role in AS by modulating chromatin structure and Pol II dynamics. For example, the chromatin architectural protein CTCF has been found to regulate DNA demethylation at a distal exon (exon 64A), leading to Pol II pausing near exon boundaries. This localized stalling facilitates exon inclusion and promotes EMT in breast cancer models [ ]. Additionally, H3K9 acetylation is associated with faster Pol II elongation (favoring ES), whereas H3K36me3 is linked to slower elongation and greater exon inclusion [ ]. R-loop structures (DNA-RNA hybrids formed during transcription) further modulate splicing fidelity. The tumor suppressor MEN1 has been shown to suppress R-loop accumulation by slowing Pol II elongation, thereby maintaining proper AS patterns. Loss of MEN1 leads to excessive R-loop formation, increased IR, and genomic instability, underscoring the essential role of transcription-splicing crosstalk in preserving genome integrity [ ].

## Prevalent as events among common cancers
AS events are ubiquitous in both hematological malignancies and solid tumors such as lung, liver, and breast cancers. Cancer-associated splicing dysregulation can generate pro-tumorigenic protein isoforms that affect critical signaling pathways and biological processes, enhancing tumor proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, augmenting invasion and metastasis, inducing chemoresistance, and helping tumors evade immune surveillance [ , ]. Here, we examine prevalent AS events in several major cancer types, illustrating the diverse roles of AS in their pathogenesis.

## Alternative splicing in hematologic malignancies
Splicing dysregulation is a common feature across many hematological malignancies. In AML, approximately 14–22% of patients harbor mutations in splicing factor genes [ , ]. Moreover, one-third of all genes expressed in AML undergo differential splicing compared to normal cells [ ]. Among 16 types of tumors (and their corresponding healthy tissues), AML showed the greatest divergence in splicing landscape [ ]. Splicing dysregulation is also frequently observed in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), despite a lower prevalence of spliceosome gene mutations in these patients [ ]. For instance, T-ALL cells exhibit a pronounced dependency on certain SFs such as SRSF6 and U2 snRNP components [ , ]. In primary pediatric B-ALL, even without major splicing factor mutations, over 3,000 aberrant splice variants per sample have been reported, and some AS events are linked to drug resistance [ ]. Abnormal splicing of FPGS mRNA [ ]function and methotrexate resistance in leukemia cell lines [ ]. Furthermore, a subset of pediatric B-ALL patients who relapse after chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy have been found to express a CD19 splice variant that skips exon 2, resulting in loss of the epitope recognized by the CD19-specific CAR. This variant allows leukemic cells to escape CAR19-mediated killing [ ]. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), the splicing landscape is substantially altered as well. SF3B1 mutations are present in ~10% of CLL cases [ ], and even mutations in the U1 snRNA have been noted, causing abnormal splicing of cancer driver genes and correlating with poor prognosis [ ]. Other molecular mechanisms (beyond SF mutations) also contribute to splicing dysregulation in CLL. Notably, RNA splicing is the most profoundly impacted molecular pathway in MDS: ~64% of patients have mutations in splicing-related genes [ ]. In MDS, about 60% of patients harbor a mutant SF (commonly SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, or ZRSR2), and in subtypes with ring sideroblasts, > 80% carry splicing mutations, highlighting how integral splicing defects are to MDS pathogenesis [ ]. In multiple myeloma (MM), the incidence of SF3B1 mutations is low ( < 2%), yet myeloma cells show a high reliance on SF3B1 function, indicating that aberrant splicing can be a critical driver even without common spliceosome mutations [ ].

## Alternative splicing in lung cancer
RNA AS plays a crucial role in the development, progression, diagnosis, and treatment of lung cancer [ ]. Recent studies have highlighted the significant roles of SFs and RNA-binding proteins in lung tumorigenesis. In LUAD, MYC directly upregulates transcription of the SF SRSF1, leading to abnormal splicing of downstream targets like the kinase MKNK2 and the transcription factor TEAD1, thereby promoting tumor proliferation [ ]. SRSF1 further facilitates tumorigenesis by modulating AS of VEGF to produce pro-angiogenic isoforms [ ]. Mutations in the splicing regulator RBM10 (often inactivating in LUAD) attenuate its function in skipping exon 9 of the NOTCH-pathway regulator NUMB. Conversely, overexpression of RBM5, RBM6, or RBM10 enhances inclusion of NUMB exon 11, collectively affecting Notch signaling and facilitating non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tumor growth [ ]. Another splicing regulator, Quaking (QKI), is significantly downregulated in lung cancer and has tumor-suppressive effects by influencing cancer-associated splicing patterns (e.g., of ESYT2) [ ]. QKI exists in three major isoforms (QKI-5, −6, −7); loss of QKI-5 specifically reduces inclusion of ADD3 exon 14, which in turn enhances survival signaling in lung cancer cells [ ]. Additionally, the polypyrimidine tract binding protein PTBP1 can induce exclusion of MENA exon 11a, thereby promoting cytoskeletal changes (migration, invasion) and EMT progression in lung cancer [ ].
Several cancer-critical genes in lung tumors are affected by AS. For example, splicing of the EGFR gene can produce different isoforms that impact the sensitivity of lung cancer cells to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors [ ]. MET exon 14 skipping (due to a splice-site mutation or deletion) results in loss of the MET protein’s ubiquitination site, making lung cancers with METΔ14 more responsive to MET inhibitors [ , ]. The apoptosis regulator BCL-X yields two splice isoforms with opposing functions: the shorter Bcl-x(S) promotes apoptosis, whereas the longer Bcl-x(L) inhibits it [ ]. A shift toward the pro-survival Bcl-x(L) isoform is observed not only in lung cancer but also in other malignancies like HCC [ , ], MM, prostate cancer [ ], and breast cancer [ ]. This highlights a common theme where AS of apoptotic regulators contributes to cancer cell survival.

## Alternative splicing in HCC
HCC, the most common type of hepatobiliary malignancy, is characterized by significant molecular heterogeneity, and AS plays a critical role in HCC progression. Kruppel-like factor 6 (KLF6), a tumor-suppressor protein, is expressed at lower levels in HCC; notably, two KLF6 splicing variants (SV1 and SV2) are implicated in HCC development. Elevated KLF6–SV1 expression enhances cell growth via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, while overexpression of the SV2 isoform decreases cell proliferation and apoptosis by affecting CDKN1A and pro-apoptotic BAX gene expression [ , ]. Tumor protein p73 (TP73) functions as a tumor suppressor transcription factor. AS at the TP73 3′ end generates multiple splice isoforms, among which the ΔEx2p73 isoform has been shown to facilitate HCC progression [ , ]. Moreover, specific AS events contribute to HCC’s invasive and metastatic capacity. For example, the RNA helicase DDX17 promotes retention of intron 3 in the PXN antisense transcript PXN-AS1, leading to a novel long noncoding transcript that enhances c-MYC signaling in extrahepatic metastasis [ ]. VEGFA, a pro-angiogenic growth factor, has multiple AS isoforms with distinct functions in angiogenesis [ ]. Among them, one particular isoform is a key driver of pathological angiogenesis in cancer, significantly promoting tumor growth and metastasis [ ]. Targeted therapeutic strategies against specific VEGFA splicing isoforms have shown some efficacy in clinical interventions for HCC, modestly prolonging overall and progression-free survival [ , ]. Another example: an overexpressed PDSS2 splice variant (from exon 2 skipping) promotes HCC metastasis and angiogenesis via activation of EMT and Wnt/β-catenin pathways [ ]. Additionally, upregulation of the RNA helicase MTR4 modulates AS of key glycolytic genes (e.g., GLUT1 and PKM2), contributing to the metabolic reprogramming observed in HCC [ ]. The splicing regulator SLU7 is markedly downregulated in HCC; SLU7 deficiency in mouse liver induces a tumor-like glycolytic phenotype by altering splicing of metabolic regulators (including SRSF3 and HNF4α) [ , ]. These examples illustrate how AS can affect oncogenes, tumor suppressors, metabolism, and signaling pathways in HCC.
HCC’s related malignancy, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), also exhibits notable AS changes. For instance, an inflammatory cytokine gene, IL1RN, produces different isoforms via AS that act as “checkpoints” to attenuate oncogenic KRAS signaling. Upregulation of IL1RN-201/203 isoforms in iCCA dampens KRAS-driven inflammation, eliciting an anti-tumor immune response [ ]. Furthermore, the splicing factor RBM10 functions as a tumor suppressor in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA). Loss-of-function mutations in RBM10 promote the generation of oncogenic isoforms by preventing proper ES of the mitotic regulator ASPM, thus facilitating CCA progression via a Wnt/β-catenin-dependent mechanism [ ].

## Alternative splicing in breast cancer
As one of the most common malignancies in women, breast cancer is also influenced by splicing dysregulation. The aforementioned KLF6 variants (SV1 and SV2) that promote HCC have similar roles in breast cancer, suggesting conserved splicing-based mechanisms across cancers [ ]. Additionally, the cell surface molecule CD44 contains nine variable exons interspersed among its constitutive exons, giving rise to upwards of 20 distinct splicing variants. Notably, the CD44 variant isoform (CD44v) is predominantly expressed in epithelial cells, whereas the standard isoform (CD44s) is mainly found in mesenchymal cells. A shift from CD44v to CD44s via AS plays a pivotal role in driving EMT and metastasis in breast cancer [ ]. Similarly, the BRCA1 gene, a major breast and ovarian cancer susceptibility gene, undergoes AS to produce at least three major isoforms (full-length BRCA1 with all coding exons, BRCA1-Δ11 missing exon 11, and BRCA1-Δ11q with partial deletion of exon 11) [ , ]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the BRCA1-Δ11q isoform correlates with increased tumorigenesis and chemoresistance [ , ]. Another example is Δ16HER2, a splice variant of the HER2 oncogene characterized by deletion of exon 16 (which encodes part of the extracellular domain). Δ16HER2 expression reduces sensitivity to the anti-HER2 antibody trastuzumab and has been associated with enhanced cancer stemness and tumorigenesis in HER2-positive breast cancers [ ], [ ]. Furthermore, an AS event in the glycolytic enzyme gene ALDOA, inclusion of exon 7.2 in the 5′UTR, has been linked to tamoxifen resistance. Inclusion of exon 7.2 increases ALDOA protein levels and activates the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, thereby conferring endocrine therapy resistance [ ].
AS dysregulation is also observed in other cancers like glioblastoma (GBM), prostate carcinoma, and colorectal cancer (CRC), underscoring the broad impact of aberrant splicing in oncology. For instance, in GBM, the splicing factor PTBP1 promotes inclusion of exon 6 of ANXA7, producing the ANXA7–I2 isoform which inhibits EGFR degradation and sustains EGFR signaling [ ]. In prostate carcinoma, the transcription factor FOXA1 interacts with several SFs (PTBP1, U2AF2, HNRNPC, HNRNPK) at specific splice sites, and these interactions are associated with patient survival outcomes, highlighting how splicing regulation can affect disease progression [ ]. In CRC, SRSF6 is often overexpressed and linked to poor prognosis; it drives tumor proliferation and metastasis partly by regulating AS of ZO-1. SRSF6 promotes the nuclear import of TNPO3, which induces inclusion of ZO-1 exon 23, yielding an oncogenic ZO-1 isoform that enhances tumor growth and invasion [ ].

## Alternative splicing as a source of neoantigens

## Generation of novel peptides via alternative splicing
Discovery of AS–derived neoantigens generally starts from RNA-seq profiles of tumor and matched normal tissues. Junction-aware aligners map reads to the reference genome and transcriptome, providing split-read and paired-end evidence for splice usage. STAR delivers ultrafast mapping with accurate junction detection, and HISAT2 applies graph-based alignment to handle polymorphisms and transcript diversity, enabling robust downstream splicing inference [ , ]. Alignment quality strongly influences event calling; multi-mappers, soft-clipped segments, strand specificity and library biases require quality control before quantification.
Event-level callers quantify AS across canonical categories such as exon skipping, intron retention and alternative splice sites. rMATS models replicate variability and read assignment uncertainty to estimate differential inclusion levels [ ]. SUPPA2 computes transcript- and event-level percent spliced-in and propagates uncertainty for statistical testing [ ]. MAJIQ infers local splicing variations on splice graphs to capture complex junction usage [ ], and LeafCutter clusters junctions without relying on existing annotations, which improves detection of tumor-specific junctions in poorly annotated regions [ ]. Together these approaches delineate sample-specific splice graphs and yield effect sizes suited for prioritizing tumor-restricted events.
Transcript reconstruction links local events to full-length isoforms that can be translated in silico. StringTie assembles expressed transcripts and returns isoform-level abundances, enabling open reading frame derivation and peptide generation [ ]. Several AS mechanisms generate neoepitopes. Exon skipping can create novel junctions and frame shifts that expose nonreference peptide segments. Intron retention may introduce premature termination codons; when nonsense-mediated decay escape occurs, truncated proteins with unique C-terminal tails become candidate sources of neoantigens. Alternative splice-site usage can yield microexons or elongated exons that alter peptide context. Translation of the reconstructed isoforms produces a candidate peptide catalog that reflects the observed splicing repertoire rather than reference annotations alone.
Accurate HLA genotyping is required to personalize presentation predictions. OptiType and HLAminer infer class I alleles from next-generation sequencing data and are frequently used in neoantigen studies [ , ]. Peptide–HLA binding is scored with pan-allelic models trained on binding and eluted-ligand data. NetMHCpan integrates large immunopeptidomics resources and antigen-processing features to improve identification of presented ligands, and MHCflurry refines peptide length preference, processing and allele generalization for class I presentation [ , ]. Running more than one predictor and aggregating ranks typically improves robustness when prioritizing hundreds of candidates.
AS-specific, end-to-end pipelines are preferable when the objective is to identify epitopes that arise from splicing rather than from canonical DNA mutations. NeoSplice detects tumor-specific k-mers in RNA-seq, projects them onto splice graphs to infer variant transcripts, then performs presentation prediction; benchmarking on simulated data reported high sensitivity and precision across multiple AS classes, and case studies included immunopeptidomics support [ ]. ASNEO integrates AS calling, peptide generation and class I presentation assessment and reports splicing-derived epitopes supported by mass-spectrometry evidence across patient datasets [ ]. ISOTOPE applies isoform-guided epiTope discovery at cohort scale and systematically enumerates epitopes from tumor-specific isoforms, demonstrating feasibility for large studies [ ]. These pipelines establish practical routes from splicing evidence to candidate pHLA complexes ready for experimental testing.
Evidence integration beyond binding scores strengthens confidence in AS-derived candidates. Proteogenomic analyses indicate that a substantial fraction of tumor-specific antigens originate from noncanonical or aberrantly processed transcripts, emphasizing the need to include AS-generated sequences when building search databases for mass spectrometry [ ]. A recent pan-cancer immunopeptidome atlas reported extensive sets of nonreference peptides detected across tumors and normal tissues, highlighting detectability of noncanonical sources at scale and providing guidance for tissue-matched filtering during validation [ ]. Incorporating junction-spanning peptides into spectral searches, using patient-specific databases derived from observed isoforms, and maintaining rigorous false discovery rate control are recommended practices at this stage [ , , ].
Prioritization benefits from explicit modeling of immunogenicity in addition to presentation. DeepHLApan jointly models presentation and the probability of T-cell activation, producing a composite estimate that ranks pHLA complexes for downstream testing [ ]. IMPROVE aggregates sequence- and physicochemical-level descriptors within a supervised framework validated across multiple cohorts, improving prioritization of epitopes with observed T-cell recognition [ ]. PRIME integrates features associated with T-cell receptor recognition together with presentation markers, improving generalization on cancer epitope datasets [ ]. These methods complement general IEDB utilities and can be applied as a second-stage triage after AS-specific discovery to enrich for candidates with a higher likelihood of productive responses.
Rigorous filtering is important to minimize artifacts. RNA editing, pseudogene transcription and mapping to paralogous regions can mimic tumor-specific junctions; annotation-free clustering helps, but manual inspection in genome browsers and requirement of minimal junction-read support remain advisable [ – ]. Event recurrence across a panel of normal tissues should be evaluated to avoid selecting physiologic isoforms with low basal expression. Differential usage thresholds for inclusion level, minimal read depth around junctions and isoform abundance cut-offs reduce false positives. Tumor purity and stromal content can dilute splicing signals, so joint analysis with purity estimates is useful when prioritizing events for validation.
Best-practice reporting retains genomic and transcriptomic context for each candidate. Recommended elements include event type and genomic coordinates, supporting reads and differential usage statistics, the reconstructed transcript sequence, derived peptide sequences with flanking residues, patient-specific HLA alleles, presentation scores from multiple algorithms, and any corroborating evidence from immunopeptidomics or functional assays [ , – ]. Summaries organized in this manner facilitate cross-study comparison, streamline synthesis of peptide pools for T-cell assays, and align with the analytical flow demonstrated by AS-specific pipelines.
Scope alignment is also important. Pipelines designed for canonical mutation–derived neoantigens that require VCF inputs provide valuable functionality for SNV, InDel and MNV contexts, yet they are not optimized to process AS-derived sequences end-to-end. AS-tailored frameworks are therefore emphasized as primary exemplars within this section [ , , ].

## MHC-I processing and presentation of AS-derived neoantigens
Aberrant proteins (such as those encoded by alternatively spliced transcripts) are processed by the proteasome into short peptides (typically 8–11 amino acids in length) [ ]. Within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), the peptides are loaded onto nascent MHC-I molecules with the assistance of a peptide-loading complex composed of chaperones such as calnexin, ERp57, and tapasin [ ]. Interestingly, splicing-derived neoepitopes are often enriched in anchor residues that confer high MHC-I binding affinity and stability, facilitating preferential loading and prolonged surface expression of the peptide-MHC-I complex [ ] (Fig. A).
 Following assembly, the peptide-MHC-I complex is trafficked through the Golgi apparatus to the cell membrane, where it is presented for surveillance by CD8+ T cells [ ]. In glioma, for example, splicing-derived neoepitopes from the GNAS gene were consistently presented across different tumor regions and correlated with expansion of corresponding T cell receptor (TCR) clones in the peripheral blood, underscoring both the spatial robustness and immunogenic potential of these antigens [ ]. Moreover, some splicing-derived peptides retain their immunogenicity even after trimming by ER-resident aminopeptidases (such as ERAP1 and ERAP2), which further enhances their compatibility with MHC-I presentation and recognition by T cells [ ].

## Tools and datasets for splicing neoantigen discovery
The identification of AS-derived neoantigens has been greatly advanced by high-throughput sequencing and integrative bioinformatics pipelines. For instance, researchers performed RNA sequencing on 37 recurrent GBM samples, delineating the splicing landscape during GBM recurrence and identifying multiple neoantigens derived from novel splicing junctions [ ]. Additionally, the AS Cancer Atlas is a core repository cataloging 17,842 experimentally validated cancer-associated AS events, including splice sites, isoform expression, and prognostic data [ ]. Furthermore, specialized tools have been developed to predict splicing-derived neoantigens. SPLICE-neo combines DNA-level splice-site mutation data with de novo RNA splicing events and prioritizes candidate neoepitopes based on transcript abundance and predicted MHC binding affinity. This approach successfully identified high-affinity neoepitopes in breast, lung, and liver cancers, many of which were experimentally validated [ ]. Similarly, SpliceMutr uses matched normal controls to reduce false positives and has uncovered thousands of recurrent AS-derived neoantigens in TCGA datasets [ ]. Other platforms, including NeoSplice and SpliceIO, apply diverse computational strategies to improve junction reconstruction and HLA-binding predictions, broadening the toolkit for personalized vaccine design [ , ]. A well-characterized example of an AS-derived neoantigen is the exon 14 skipping event in the MET gene (METΔ14), which generates a neoepitope with superior binding to HLA-A*02:01 compared to a conventional EGFR-derived peptide [ , ]. This epitope has demonstrated strong cytotoxic T cell responses in both in vitro assays and co-culture systems using patient-derived cells [ , ]. Moreover, single-cell TCR sequencing in murine models confirmed that METΔ14-specific TCRs exert potent anti-tumor activity, supporting METΔ14 as a “public” neoantigen suitable for broad targeting [ ]. Additionally, studies on the downregulation of the SF HNRNPA1 have identified multiple immunogenic peptides via immunopeptidomics, with corresponding CD8+ T cell responses detected in patients with AML and MDS [ ]. These findings illustrate the therapeutic promise of modulating the splicing machinery to enhance neoantigen exposure and T cell-mediated tumor clearance.
Long-read sequencing is overcoming limitations of short-read sequencing, offering great potential for detecting novel AS events [ ]. As long-read technology advances, several computational tools have been developed for AS isoform detection. Nine cutting-edge tools, including StringTie2, FLAIR, FLAMES, Freddie, TALON, UNAGI, TAMA, Bambu, and IsoQuant, have been published (Table ). Many aberrant splicing isoforms in cancer cells that were previously missed have been identified. These tumor-specific RNA transcripts can serve as sources of immunogenic neoantigens [ , ]. For instance, researchers applied full-length transcriptome (Nanopore) sequencing on 22 NSCLC cell lines and identified 2,021 novel splicing isoforms. Some of these isoforms generate neoantigen candidates which can activate T cell responses through interaction with HLA molecules [ ].
 In addition to the platforms summarized above, NeoSplice and ASNEO provide AS-specific, end-to-end workflows that begin with RNA-seq evidence of splicing and deliver isoform-resolved peptide catalogs linked to HLA presentation; both include cases with immunopeptidomics support for peptide presentation [ , ]. As a complementary example, ISOTOPE demonstrates isoform-guided enumeration of splicing-derived epitopes at cohort scale, facilitating systematic discovery beyond single samples [ ]. After candidate generation, DeepHLApan, IMPROVE, and PRIME can be applied to rank epitopes with an increased probability of inducing CD8+ T-cell responses in vivo, respectively leveraging joint modeling of presentation and immunogenic potential, feature-integrative learning validated across cohorts, and determinants of T-cell receptor recognition [ – ]. Evidence from pan-cancer immunopeptidome resources further supports detectability of nonreference peptides and can guide filtering thresholds during validation and triage [ ]. An additional immunogenicity model, DeepImmuno, offers a deep-learning perspective focused on predicting T-cell response signals and can be used alongside the above methods for consensus ranking [ , ].

## AS-derived neoantigens vs. other neoantigen sources
Tumor neoantigens arise from diverse sources beyond AS, including single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions (indels), gene fusions, aberrant RNAs (such as circRNAs, lncRNAs, and RNA editing events), endogenous retroelements, viral proteins, proteasome-spliced peptides, and post-translational modifications (PTMs) [ ]. In general, SNV/indel-derived neoepitopes are highly tumor-specific and immunogenic, but mostly “private” (patient-unique) and are abundant only in tumors with high mutational burden [ ]. By contrast, AS-derived peptides, which can be generated even in tumors with low tumor mutation burden (TMB), are often entirely novel sequences to the immune system and elicit strong T cell responses. For example, research has identified more AS-derived MHC-I epitopes than mutation-derived ones in breast and ovarian cancers with few mutations [ ], and an estimated 28–43% of predicted AS-neoantigens were immunogenic (versus ~6% of mutation-derived neoantigens) [ , ]. However, it is important to note that most AS events are also patient-specific (similar to mutations) and can involve subtle indels, making the prediction and development of “public” (shared) targets challenging [ – ]. In comparison, gene-fusion neoantigens (e.g., BCR-ABL, EWS-FLI1) are by construction highly tumor-specific, clonally expressed, and often shared among patients with the same fusion [ ], but true recurrent fusions are rare in common solid tumors.
Endogenous retroelements produce shared “virus-like” peptides. For instance, HERV-E peptides in renal cell carcinoma are conserved across tumors and immunogenic [ – ]. These may enhance responses to immunotherapy, but low-level expression of some retroelements in normal tissues (or during development) can induce partial tolerance, complicating target selection. True viral antigens (from oncogenic viruses like HPV, EBV, etc.) are inherently foreign and often elicit very strong T cell immunity, making them ideal vaccine or TCR targets [ , ]. However, their applicability is limited to virus-driven malignancies.
PTMs, such as aberrant glycosylation or phosphorylation, can create neoepitopes. For example, truncated O-glycans on MUC1 generate neoantigens that have been tested in vaccines. Clinical trials have generally shown only modest benefit [ – ], likely reflecting the low abundance of such modified peptides and immune tolerance. Similarly, tumor-specific phosphopeptides, arising from dysregulated signaling pathways, can be presented and recognized by T cells, but their low copy number and the need for mass spectrometry discovery limit current use [ ]. Proteasome-spliced peptides (cryptic epitopes created when the proteasome ligates two distant protein fragments) represent a niche source: proteasomal cis- or trans-splicing can stitch together peptide fragments into novel epitopes. A few spliced peptides have been shown to induce CD8+ T cell responses and even tumor regression in mouse models [ , ], but their actual frequency in the immunopeptidome is debated and predictive algorithms for them are in their infancy.
In summary, AS-derived neoantigens stand out as attractive candidates for immunotherapy (Table ). They share the tumor specificity and strong immunogenic potential of SNV-derived peptides and gene-fusion neoantigens, and they can be abundantly generated even in tumors with few mutations, making them viable targets across a broader range of cancers [ ]. While viral and fusion neoantigens have predictable patterns enabling off-the-shelf therapies, AS-derived neoantigens offer a more diverse and personalized targeting strategy. Their novel sequences help overcome central tolerance, and they have already shown promise as targets for cutting-edge therapies like personalized vaccines and TCR/CAR T-cell approaches [ ]. In particular, AS-derived neoantigens expand the available target space for immunotherapy, benefiting patients with low mutation burden tumors that traditionally have limited treatment options. They represent a powerful addition to the immunotherapeutic arsenal, complementing and potentially surpassing other neoantigen classes in certain contexts [ , ].
 

## Immune characteristics of splicing neoantigens and their roles in the tumor immune microenvironment

## Shared and clonal features of AS-derived neoantigens
Alternative RNA splicing events are often recurrent across tumor subclones and even among different patients, giving rise to a class of broadly shared “public” neoantigens [ ]. Comprehensive multi-regional analyses across 19 cancer types have identified recurrent neojunctions arising from dysregulated splicing of genes such as GNAS and RPL22. These neojunctions generate immunogenic peptides consistently expressed at high frequency in diverse solid tumors, including gastric, prostate, and liver cancers [ , , , ]. Importantly, identical TCR clonotypes capable of recognizing these peptides have been detected in different individuals, highlighting their cross-patient immunogenic clonality [ , ].
Beyond inter-patient sharing, many splicing-derived neoantigens exhibit high intra-patient clonality, meaning they are expressed by a large fraction of tumor cells across spatially distinct lesions in the same patient. For instance, multi-site sampling in glioma patients revealed uniform MHC-I presentation of GNAS-derived neojunction peptides and corresponding expansion of specific TCR clonotypes in T cells at all tumor sites [ , ]. This clonal stability implies that AS-derived neoantigens can be preserved during tumor evolution and are less susceptible to immune escape via antigen loss. Such properties support their potential for “off-the-shelf” vaccine or TCR-T cell therapy strategies targeting conserved tumor antigens.

## Alternative splicing related immune evasion
Despite their immunogenic potential, AS-derived neoantigens can be subject to immune evasion through disruptions in antigen processing and presentation pathways (Fig. B) [ ]. MHC molecules are crucial for presenting neoantigens and inducing antitumor immune responses. However, aberrant AS of MHC components themselves may lead to unstable or non-functional MHC proteins [ , ], which significantly reduces effective neoantigen presentation and facilitates immune escape in tumors [ ]. Tumors harboring SF mutations, such as the SF3B1 K700E hotspot, frequently exhibit downregulation of key antigen-processing components, including TAP1/2 and β2-microglobulin. This leads to reduced surface expression of MHC-I-peptide complexes, thereby limiting CD8+ T cell recognition of tumor cells [ ].
Additionally, AS can generate diverse isoforms of immune checkpoint molecules that exacerbate immune suppression [ ]. A newly identified PD-1 splice variant, preferentially expressed on T cells in HCC, lacks the canonical cytoplasmic signaling domain and correlates with poor clinical outcomes [ ]. This variant likely functions as a dominant-negative receptor, impairing T cell activity. Similarly, a soluble PD-L1 isoform (secPD-L1), generated via AS, has been detected in the serum of patients with various solid tumors [ ]. This soluble isoform can bind PD-1 on T cells and impair their activation, acting as a systemic “immune smokescreen.” Moreover, tumor-derived exosomal PD-L1 (some of which may derive from alternative splicing and secretion) contributes to pre-metastatic niche formation, suppressing T cell responses in distal sites and undermining immunotherapy efficacy [ , ].
The immunoregulatory landscape of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) further complicates matters. “Hot” tumors, characterized by high tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and PD-L1 upregulation, often show initial responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [ , ]. However, these tumors may also mount compensatory increases in immunosuppressive checkpoint isoforms (including alternatively spliced forms), establishing negative feedback loops that dampen long-term treatment efficacy [ , ].

## Time suppressive cells and immune responsiveness
A high burden of splicing-derived neoantigens is generally associated with increased infiltration of CD8+ TILs, as observed in melanoma and lung cancer cohorts [ ]. Quantitative neoantigen prediction using pipelines like SNAF reveals a negative correlation between neoantigen load and TIDE (Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion) scores, suggesting that tumors rich in neoantigens have improved T cell function and reduced immune evasion [ ]. In NSCLC patients, tumors enriched for METΔ14-derived neoantigens show an elevated CD8+ T cell to Treg ratio, which correlates with prolonged progression-free survival following anti-PD-1 therapy [ ].
However, immunosuppressive cell populations within the TIME can dampen these otherwise favorable responses. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), which express high levels of CTLA-4 and consume IL-2, can rapidly expand in neoantigen-rich tumors and competitively inhibit effector T cell activity [ ]. This paradoxical co-occurrence (many neoantigens but also many Tregs) often leads to a disconnect between a tumor’s antigenicity and the clinical response. Similarly, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) contribute to immune resistance by secreting arginase-1 (ARG1), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and reactive oxygen species (ROS), factors which impair T cell proliferation and reshape the tumor stroma to impede immune cell infiltration [ ]. In pancreatic cancer models, for example, even when high splicing-derived neoantigen expression initially recruits T cells, the subsequent expansion of MDSCs rapidly curtails antitumor efficacy [ ].
Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and immunosuppressive cytokines (such as TGF-β and IL-10) further contribute to the formation of a “cold” immune microenvironment [ , ]. Even in the presence of abundant neoantigens, such an environment can induce local immune tolerance [ ]. This underscores the need for combinatorial strategies that both increase neoantigen load/presentation and counteract immunosuppressive cells to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes.

## Targeting alternative splicing for neoantigens
The intricate landscape of cancer-related splicing has unveiled a plethora of therapeutic opportunities. As exploration of cancer splicing mechanisms has expanded, various strategies have emerged to modulate splicing in cancer, thereby generating neoantigens or otherwise enhancing anti-tumor immunity. The main strategies include direct modulation of the core spliceosome, targeting splicing-regulatory proteins (e.g., serine/arginine-rich (SR) proteins), and manipulating factors that indirectly alter the splicing of individual mRNAs [ ]. By altering how tumor cells splice certain transcripts, these approaches can induce the production of new neoantigens or reinstate expression of tumor-suppressive isoforms, thus helping activate antitumor immunity. This provides promising research directions and potential targets for cancer immunotherapy development [ ].

## Modulation of the splicing process and splicing factors
Direct modulation of the splicing process and its factors (Fig. A) has emerged as a powerful strategy for cancer therapy, offering novel ways to generate neoantigens and enhance antitumor immune responses. Multiple approaches have been explored to perturb splicing in tumors, including small-molecule splicing inhibitors, CRISPR/Cas-based methods, and small nucleic acid therapeutics.
 

## Small-molecule splicing modulators
Research has identified several anticancer small molecules that impact AS, often by disrupting spliceosome formation or function. Among the 20 most widely used chemotherapy agents, many were found to incidentally modify specific AS events in cancers [ ]. Notably, 12 of these agents influence splicing of the Bcl-x gene in a way that promotes pro-apoptotic isoform production, tipping the balance toward apoptosis in cancer cells [ ]. Beyond traditional chemotherapy, newer drugs specifically targeting the spliceosome have shown immunomodulatory effects: pharmacologically inhibiting spliceosome components can activate antiviral and immune pathways within tumors [ , , ]. The SF SF3B1 was one of the first to be targeted by small molecules [ , ]. Preclinical studies have shown that compounds like Sudemycin D6 and H3B-8800 disrupt SF3B complex function, leading to accumulation of intron-retained RNAs that form double-stranded RNA structures in the cytoplasm, thereby improving antiviral signaling and adaptive immune recognition in triple-negative breast cancer cells [ ]. H3B-8800, in particular, demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetics and tolerability in early-phase trials in patients with MDS and AML carrying SF3B1 mutations [ ]. This agent partially restored normal splicing patterns and yielded hematologic improvements in a subset of patients [ ]. Preclinical data further revealed that H3B-8800 exhibited selective efficacy in xenograft models with mutant SF3B1, with minimal effect on wild-type SF3B1 tumors, highlighting its mutation-specific therapeutic potential [ ].
Although development of RVT-2001 (formerly H3B-8800) was halted following interim analysis of a Phase I/II trial, insights gained continue to inform the rational design of next-generation splicing modulators [ ]. Beyond SF3B1, numerous other SFs have been identified as potential targets for modulating tumor AS events, including RBM39, PRMT5, SRSF2, and U2AF1 [ , ]. The small molecule indisulam, which induces degradation of RBM39, underwent multiple clinical trials. It showed safety and tolerability but only limited efficacy as a single agent in advanced cancers, with clinical responses or stable disease in ~17–35% of patients [ – ]. Due to these modest results, single-agent development was discontinued. However, combining indisulam with standard chemotherapy (idarubicin plus cytarabine) yielded a 35% response rate in heavily pre-treated AML patients [ ], suggesting combination strategies might unlock its potential, possibly by identifying biomarkers like DCAF15 (indisulam’s target adaptor). Another RBM39-degrading sulfonamide, tasisulam, had an 11.8% partial response rate in second-line metastatic melanoma (Phase II) [ ] and was generally well-tolerated, though a Phase III trial was terminated due to safety concerns. Despite these setbacks, understanding how these drugs modulate splicing and immune responses has been valuable. For example, certain splicing modulators (indisulam and a PRMT5 inhibitor MS023) were found to induce immunogenic neoantigens and slow tumor growth in a CD8+ T cell- and MHC-I-dependent manner, effectively enhancing the efficacy of PD-1 checkpoint blockade [ ]. Similarly, a novel small-molecule splicing modifier called RECTAS (“rectifier of aberrant splicing”) was associated with neoantigen induction [ ]. RECTAS works by targeting CDC-like kinases (CLKs) to enhance the activity of specific SR proteins, thereby correcting some aberrant splicing patterns [ ]. Other known splicing modulators, such as rebecsinib and related compounds, could potentially be screened for similar neoantigen-boosting effects [ – ]. Interestingly, some drugs approved for non-splicing purposes were also found to incidentally modulate AS [ ], these could be repurposed to favor production of neoantigens in tumors.

## CRISPR-based strategies
CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing, adapted from bacterial immune systems, has great potential in immunotherapy applications [ , ]. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating CRISPR/Cas9-modified T cells (for example, knocking out PD-1 in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes) in various cancers [ ]. In terms of splicing, CRISPR/Cas9 can introduce small indels at specific sites to disrupt splice sites or create frameshifts, or larger deletions to remove entire exons, effectively reprogramming how a gene is spliced [ ]. This makes CRISPR a powerful tool to deliberately alter splicing of target genes in cancer cells. It could be used, for instance, to generate known immunogenic splice variants (once those are identified) directly in tumor cells [ ]. Additionally, CRISPR/Cas13 has been adapted to modulate splicing: a catalytically dead Cas13 (dCas13) coupled with a guide RNA can be directed to block binding of SFs on a pre-mRNA [ ]. This direct RNA approach enables specific modulation of virtually any splicing event to promote neoantigen formation [ ]. However, delivering the large CRISPR/Cas machinery into tumors and ensuring a sufficient immune response to a single induced neoantigen remain challenges. These techniques are in early development and currently more suited for laboratory models or ex vivo engineering of cell therapies, rather than direct in vivo tumor editing.

## Small nucleic acid therapeutics
SSOs are short synthetic RNA molecules designed to bind specific sequences in pre-mRNAs and block access of SFs, thereby altering splice site choice [ ]. By masking splice sites or regulatory elements, SSOs can induce ES or prevent inclusion of unwanted exons [ ]. They offer high specificity and can be multiplexed to target multiple splicing events simultaneously. With improved delivery methods (e.g., chemical conjugates or nanoparticles), SSOs have achieved efficient tissue- or tumor-specific delivery [ ]. In the context of immunotherapy, SSOs can be used to manipulate splicing of genes identified as neoantigen sources, essentially forcing cancer cells to produce aberrant peptides that trigger immune responses. SSOs are already in clinical use for genetic diseases (e.g., nusinersen/Spinraza® for spinal muscular atrophy targets SMN2 splicing) [ ], which de-risks their use in cancer. In preclinical tumor models, SSOs targeting oncogenic splice isoforms (such as MCL1 and BCL2L1) have been shown to restore production of pro-apoptotic isoforms, thereby inducing tumor cell apoptosis [ ]. In murine AML models, SSOs designed to correct splicing defects caused by SRSF2 mutations successfully reduced aberrant RUNX1 splicing and prolonged overall survival [ ]. A more recent innovation is bifunctional small molecules that combine an oligonucleotide moiety with a small-molecule splicing effector domain. These compounds can be delivered like traditional drugs but achieve sequence-specific splicing modulation. One prototype targeting both SRSF2 and U2AF1 binding motifs simultaneously restored splicing fidelity in dual-mutant leukemia models and significantly extended survival in vivo [ ]. By reducing the risk of resistance (via dual targeting) and improving tumor selectivity, this bifunctional strategy is a promising direction for future therapeutics.
Although less studied, siRNAs present another approach to induce splicing changes. siRNAs can selectively knock down expression of specific SFs or specific AS isoforms of key genes in cancer cells [ , ]. This can lead to splicing changes and cause the generation of aberrant peptides, regulating tumor growth and serving as a potential source of neoantigens.
A novel strategy in the field is the use of decoy oligonucleotides [ ]. These are chemically modified RNA oligonucleotides that mimic the binding motifs of certain SFs. By flooding the cell with “decoys,” they sequester specific SFs away from their natural pre-mRNA targets. This approach is more precise than broad-spectrum splicing inhibitors because it aims to inhibit only a chosen SFs activity without affecting the entire spliceosome. In proof-of-concept experiments, decoy oligonucleotides targeting SRSF1 or PTBP1 in cancer cell lines successfully suppressed oncogenic properties and induced desired splicing changes in target genes [ ], representing a more targeted modulation of splicing.
Despite the significant progress and promising prospects of small nucleic acid therapies in AS modulation, their delivery systems and techniques continue to pose challenges to further application [ ], and designing small nucleic acids to reliably alter splicing patterns still requires further exploration.

## Modulating nonsense-mediated decay
NMD is a cellular quality-control mechanism that degrades mRNAs harboring premature termination codons (PTCs) [ ]. PTCs can arise from nonsense mutations, frameshift indels, or splicing errors that create an early stop codon. By eliminating these faulty mRNAs, NMD prevents the production of potentially harmful truncated proteins. During NMD, the exon-junction complex (EJC), deposited just upstream of exon-exon junctions during splicing, interacts with NMD factors when a stop codon is encountered more than ~50–55 nt upstream of the final exon junction, triggering mRNA decay [ ]. Many endogenous mRNAs even include “poison exons” (exons containing a PTC) as a regulatory mechanism; the choice to include or skip such exons (via AS) can determine whether an mRNA is destroyed by NMD, thus maintaining the proper balance of protein isoforms [ ].
There is a crucial interplay between NMD and neoantigen production. Frameshift mutations in tumors often generate PTCs; whether the resultant transcripts escape NMD largely determines if the encoded aberrant peptides can be presented as neoantigens. A strong correlation has been observed between a tumor’s neoantigen load and accumulation of frameshift mutations that are not subjected to NMD, underscoring NMD’s impact on immunotherapy efficacy [ ]. Transcripts with PTCs that evade NMD can be translated into truncated proteins or proteins with altered C-termini (from frameshifts), which serve as neoantigens. A trailblazing study proposed that inhibiting NMD in tumor cells could boost anti-tumor immunity. Indeed, siRNA-mediated knockdown of key NMD factors in tumor cells induced the expression of new antigenic peptides, triggering immune responses and slowing tumor growth in mice [ ]. Additionally, the DNA methylation drug 5-azacytidine was found to inhibit NMD (through unclear mechanisms, possibly by downregulating NMD factor expression), thereby stabilizing frameshifted transcripts and enhancing presentation of their peptides on HLA molecules [ ].
Researchers have developed computational tools like NMDetective to predict whether specific PTC-containing transcripts will be targeted by NMD [ ]. Remarkably, the efficacy of checkpoint blockade immunotherapy in patients could be correlated with whether their tumors had frameshift mutations predicted to escape NMD. Pan-cancer analyses indicated that frameshift indels that evade NMD are associated with higher T cell infiltration and improved ICI responses. Complementary studies using allele-specific mutation detection in paired DNA/RNA sequencing have pinpointed tumors with NMD-escaping frameshift mutations that indeed show activated anti-tumor immunity [ ]. Across four melanoma cohorts, patients whose tumors harbored these NMD-escape mutations experienced significantly better outcomes on anti-PD-1 therapy, presumably due to the robust neoantigens generated [ ]. Moreover, in personalized neoantigen vaccine trials, some of the strongest immune responses were against neoantigens originating from frameshift-indel events, suggesting that NMD escape allows those neoantigens to arise [ , ].
Conversely, emerging data indicate that hyperactivation of NMD in tumors can contribute to immune evasion. For example, upregulation of the master NMD kinase SMG1 (often via IL-6/STAT3 signaling) has been noted as a mechanism to diminish antitumor immune responses [ ]. Some cancers ramp up NMD to rid themselves of immunogenic transcripts (like those with frameshifts from aberrant splicing), essentially “cleaning house” of neoantigen sources. Indeed, certain malignancies seem to augment NMD activity to cope with the myriad abnormal RNAs generated by widespread transcriptional and splicing dysregulation, a process contributing to a more aggressive phenotype. Therefore, inhibiting NMD might be effective particularly in tumors that show evidence of NMD hyperactivation [ , ]. In such cases, drugs like NMDI-1, 5-azacytidine, or amlexanox could be deployed to induce neoantigens across various tumors, especially those that are otherwise “cold” (non-immunogenic) and unresponsive to checkpoint inhibitors [ ]. Care must be taken, as broad NMD inhibition could also lead to undesired effects by stabilizing many mutant or off-target transcripts [ ]. However, transient or tumor-specific NMD inhibition, perhaps via nanoparticle delivery of NMD factor siRNAs or small-molecule inhibitors, represents a novel immunotherapeutic strategy to unleash hidden neoantigens [ ].
These treatment methods may have adverse effects on normal tissues. The key to successful treatment lies in achieving an appropriate therapeutic window. Furthermore, by inducing the generation and presentation of tumor neoantigens, targeting mutant splicing factors, and enhancing the delivery of drugs to the tumor site, it is expected to reduce the impact of drugs on normal tissues. As mentioned above, indisulam and MS023 can induce the generation of immunogenic neoantigens by regulating RNA splicing. Researchers have observed T-cell and MHC-I-dependent tumor growth inhibition effects in immune-competent mouse models. Moreover, the presentation of neoantigens can be enhanced and neoantigen-specific T cells can be induced by using CRISPR, SSOs, siRNA and NMD to regulate splicing [ ]. Neoantigen-specific T cells have unique activity against cancer cells and do not have the ability to induce damage to normal tissues [ ]. Splicing factor mutations are present in a variety of cancers, including AML [ ], melanoma [ ], breast cancer [ ], lung cancer [ ] and other solid tumors [ ]. Commonly mutated splicing factors include SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, etc [ ]. These mutations help develop therapeutic strategies that target cancer cells while avoiding damage to normal cells. As previously mentioned, H3B-8800 demonstrated selective efficacy and good tolerability in xenograft models carrying SF3B1 mutations. In addition, the effective targeting of tumors by splicing modulators can also be achieved through conjugates. Conjugation strategies can include attaching splicing modulators to tumor-specific antibodies or ligands, thereby enhancing their delivery specifically to tumor cells and minimizing off-target effects. This targeted delivery can be further refined using advanced technologies such as nanoparticle-based delivery systems, which can encapsulate splicing modulators and release them preferentially within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, leveraging the unique metabolic and vascular properties of tumors can also enhance selectivity. Tumors often exhibit enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects, which can be exploited to preferentially accumulate splicing modulators within the tumor tissue. Additionally, the use of tumor-specific promoters to drive the expression of splicing modulators can ensure that these therapeutic agents are active predominantly in cancer cells. In summary, by combining strategies such as the induction of tumor-specific neoantigens, targeting mutant splicing factors, enhancing drug delivery to tumor sites, utilizing conjugates, and exploiting tumor-specific characteristics, it is possible to achieve tumor selectivity and minimize systemic toxicity. These approaches collectively aim to optimize the therapeutic window and enhance the efficacy of splicing modulators while reducing their adverse effects on normal tissues.

## Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting alternative splicing

## Splice-modulating therapeutics
Among the approaches discussed to alter RNA splicing, both small molecules and SSOs are already in clinical use (or trials) for tumors and other diseases (Table ). In preclinical models, as mentioned, SSOs targeting oncogenic splice isoforms (e.g., MCL1, BCL2L1) restored pro-apoptotic isoform expression and induced tumor cell death [ , ]. In murine AML models, SSOs correcting SRSF2-mutant splicing defects reduced aberrant RUNX1 splicing and prolonged survival [ ]. As for small molecules, H3B-8800 (RVT-2001) showed mutation-specific activity and tolerability in early-phase trials for MDS/AML with SF3B1 mutations [ ]. It partially restored global splicing fidelity and led to hematologic improvements in some patients [ ]. Preclinical data further revealed selective efficacy in xenograft models carrying mutant SF3B1, with minimal impact on tumors expressing wild-type alleles—highlighting its mutation-dependent therapeutic potential. Although development of RVT-2001 (formerly H3B-8800) was halted following interim analysis of a Phase I/II trial, insights gained continue to inform the rational design of next-generation splicing modulators [ ].
 

## AS-derived neoantigens in cancer immunotherapy
As a unique class of tumor-specific antigens, AS-derived neoantigens have increasingly garnered attention for clinical application in immunotherapy. By delivering these neoepitopes (often as long peptides or mRNA) to dendritic cells (DCs) (either in vivo via vaccines or ex vivo via DC culture), robust CD8+ T cell responses can be elicited. A preclinical study found that vaccinating mice with a set of six splicing-derived neoantigens induced a strong T cell response [ ]. Additionally, researchers have identified public neoantigens from aberrant splicing in GNAS and RPL22 in many cancers, which can also trigger strong T cell responses [ ].
Meanwhile, TCR-engineered T cell (TCR-T) therapy offers a highly specific approach to targeting AS-derived neoantigens by using autologous T cells engineered with high-affinity TCRs that recognize peptide-MHC-I complexes from aberrant splicing events. For example, TCRs specific to neoantigens arising from SRSF2 and U2AF1 mutations have been identified and cloned [ , ]. When these TCRs were transduced into patient T cells (creating TCR-T cells), they achieved > 80% tumor clearance in an AML xenograft model, with a significant extension of survival. As mentioned earlier, a TCR library targeting the METΔ14 splice variant enabled rapid identification of multiple reactive TCR candidates; in preclinical NSCLC models, T cells armed with these TCRs induced complete tumor regression [ ]. These results underscore that T cells can be redirected with specificity to splicing-derived neoepitopes and mediate potent anti-tumor effects.
Since single-agent immune responses are often hindered by immunosuppressive factors (like MDSCs and Tregs in the tumor microenvironment), combining splicing modulators with ICIs has emerged as a promising strategy. The idea is that the splicing modulator will increase neoantigen production, while the checkpoint inhibitor frees T cells to attack the tumor. In AML models with SF3B1 K700E mutations, co-administration of H3B-8800 with anti-PD-1 antibodies enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration, reduced immunosuppressive cell populations, and extended median survival by > 30% compared to either therapy alone [ ]. Similarly, inducing RBM39 degradation (with indisulam) in conjunction with anti-PD-1 significantly inhibited tumor growth and reduced MDSC/Treg infiltration in immunocompetent mouse models [ ]. These examples suggest synergy: splicing modulation can convert a “cold” tumor into a “hotter” one immunologically, thereby improving responsiveness to checkpoint blockade.
Growing preclinical and early clinical evidence supports combining neoantigen vaccines or TCR-T therapies with ICIs to overcome immune tolerance, increase response rates, and re-sensitize tumors that were resistant to checkpoint inhibitors [ ]. One novel strategy presented recently is antigen-based immunosensitization: combining indisulam (to spike tumor cells with neoantigens by splicing interference) and chloroquine (to inhibit autophagy and possibly increase antigen presentation). This combination synergistically inhibited tumor growth by promoting neoantigen accumulation and presentation, thereby enhancing tumor immunogenicity in preclinical models [ ].
Despite promising advances in exploiting AS-derived neoantigens, several challenges remain for clinical translation. These include the need to optimize integrated bioinformatics and mass spectrometry pipelines for neoantigen discovery, improving in vivo delivery efficiency of vaccines and TCR-engineered cells, and validating the safety and efficacy of combination strategies in larger trials. Addressing these will be key to moving AS-neoantigen therapies into the mainstream.

## Discussion and conclusion
With the evolution of sequencing technologies and the identification of numerous splicing variants, aberrant RNA splicing is gaining attention for its role in cancer development and progression. Tumors exhibit up to 30% more AS events than normal tissues, leading to cancer-specific splice variants [ , – ]. Targeting abnormal splicing in cancer has become an important research direction in tumor biology.
At present, the clinical strategies to target AS mainly include SSOs and small-molecule splicing modulators. Notably, nusinersen (an SSO) is already FDA-approved for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), proving that splice-correction therapy can be safe and effective in humans [ ]. However, most small-molecule splicing modulators for cancer are still in early clinical stages (Table ), and their safety and efficacy require further evaluation. For patients with advanced tumors, the therapeutic objective of splicing-targeted treatments is mainly to correct oncogenic AS events or induce new vulnerabilities. Combining splicing modulation therapy with immunotherapy, chemotherapy, or other treatments can potentially prolong survival, especially for molecularly defined patient groups. For example, MTAP-deleted tumors show heightened responsiveness to PRMT5 inhibitors, and initial trials combining PRMT5 inhibitors with other agents in MTAP-negative patients are promising [ , , ].
On the other hand, the neoantigens generated by AS provide new targets for immunotherapy [ – ]. These AS-derived neoantigens vastly expand the antigen repertoire for immunotherapies like mRNA vaccines and adoptive T cell therapies (ACT), and have shown great therapeutic potential [ , , ]. Technological advances in high-throughput sequencing and integrated bioinformatics have further accelerated the identification of splicing-derived neoantigens [ , ]. However, given the high intratumoral heterogeneity of cancers, one challenge observed is that not all tumor cells carry the targeted neoantigens—those lacking the neoantigen can evade immune attack and grow out, potentially leading to recurrence even as neoantigen-positive cells are eliminated [ ]. Neoantigens also subject to immunosuppression by the tumor microenvironment—rapid expansion of Tregs and MDSCs in response to the inflammatory stimulus can significantly blunt anti-tumor effects [ ]. Therefore, it is necessary to further enhance the breadth and durability of neoantigen vaccine responses. This might involve optimizing neoantigen selection (to include more clonal and essential targets) and combining vaccines with complementary therapies (e.g., ICIs or tyrosine kinase inhibitors) to counteract tumor escape. Indeed, in published trials, personalized neoantigen vaccines combined with ICIs have shown enhanced anti-tumor activity in solid tumors like HCC, melanoma, and NSCLC (Phase I/II data) [ , – ]. Moreover, the safety profile of adding a vaccine to ICIs was consistent with ICIs alone, indicating this combined approach is feasible. Nonetheless, vaccine efficacy needs further validation in larger trials.
Meanwhile, fully capitalizing on AS-derived neoantigens also requires overcoming several other challenges. Identifying which aberrant splicing events are truly tumor-specific and yield immunogenic peptides remains difficult [ ]. Current neoantigen prediction pipelines can produce many false positives and negatives, underscoring the need for more precise algorithms that integrate multi-omics data and artificial intelligence [ ]. Advanced techniques like single-cell sequencing and new computational tools (e.g., DARTS) are being explored to capture splicing events across all tumor cells and prioritize clonal targets [ ]. Another concern is safety: broad splicing modulators could inadvertently disrupt normal gene splicing, leading to toxic effects such as unintended inflammatory responses [ ]. Balancing anti-tumor efficacy with minimal harm to healthy tissue will likely require tumor-targeted delivery systems or transient, controllable splicing interventions to avoid systemic side effects. Finally, in the context of vaccines or T cell therapies, although AS-derived neoepitopes are by nature tumor-specific (greatly reducing the risk of autoimmunity), any potential cross-reactivity with normal peptides must be carefully evaluated to prevent off-target immune attacks.
As the disease progresses and tumor heterogeneity increases, immune escape becomes more likely. Thus, an important future strategy is early intervention: administering neoantigen-targeted immunotherapy in minimal residual disease or adjuvant settings, when tumor burden and diversity are lowest. Vaccination or TCR-T cell therapy given soon after surgery (or during remission) might eliminate residual tumor cells before they accumulate escape mutations, thereby preventing relapse. Additionally, given that vaccines take time to elicit T cell responses, patients with lower initial tumor burden and slower disease kinetics are more likely to benefit, as they have a window for T cells to expand without the tumor overwhelming the host [ ]. This suggests that patients with earlier-stage disease or after cytoreduction are ideal candidates for neoantigen vaccine trials. On a practical note, the individualized manufacturing of neoantigen vaccines (or TCR-T products) is still costly and logistically complex, limiting widespread use at present [ ].
One way to mitigate this is to develop “public” neoantigen approaches. Unlike fully personalized neoantigens, public neoantigens are shared across patients and tumor types. Also, some well-known “public” tumor antigens like the cancer-testis antigen NY-ESO-1 [ ], or the above-mentioned splicing-derived antigens (NEJ_GNAS and NEJ_RPL22) are expressed in various cancers [ ]. There are a few Phase I/II trials underway targeting such shared neoantigens (e.g., NCT04364230 [ ], NCT04749641 [ ]). Compared with fully individualized vaccines, public neoantigen vaccines could have broader applicability and easier production, though they might not match each patient’s tumor as precisely. It will be important to see if public neoantigen-targeted therapies can achieve strong efficacy. If so, they may represent a more practical near-term path to adopt splicing neoantigen immunotherapy on a larger scale.
Most current studies have focused on MHC-I-restricted CD8+ T cell responses, often neglecting the roles of CD4+ T helper cells and MHC-II in anti-tumor immunity. In reality, peptides from aberrant splicing can also be presented on MHC-II and recruit CD4+ T cells, which help orchestrate and sustain immune responses [ , , ]. For instance, inhibiting PRMT5 was shown to upregulate SFs SRSF1 and SRSF3 in tumor cells, enhancing presentation of MHC-II-restricted neoantigens and promoting CD4+ T cell infiltration [ ]. Future vaccine designs and combination therapies should aim to activate both CD8+ and CD4+ T cell populations to maximize anti-tumor efficacy. A coordinated CD4+ helper response can improve the magnitude and memory of CD8+ responses, and also provide direct anti-tumor effects in some cases.
In addition, aberrant splicing can facilitate immune evasion by disrupting antigen-presentation pathways, as discussed. Dysregulated splicing of MHC, TAP, or β2-microglobulin can cripple antigen presentation, allowing tumor cells to hide from T cells. Splicing variants of immune checkpoints (PD-1/PD-L1, etc.) can further promote immunosuppression [ , , ]. In the future, targeted therapies to fix or compensate for these antigen-presentation defects (for example, drugs that upregulate TAP or stabilize MHC expression) could enhance immunotherapy outcomes. This is an area ripe for exploration—essentially, treating immune evasion mechanisms that originate from splicing errors. AS has a dual effect in tumor immunity. On one hand, it participates in key processes such as tumor occurrence, development, metastasis, formation of drug resistance, and immune evasion by regulating the functions and expression levels of cancer-related genes [ ]. On the other hand, the tumor-specific neoantigens produced by AS can activate T cells to exert anti-tumor effects [ ]. To overcome the paradox of AS generating both immunogenic neoantigens and impairing antigen presentation or upregulating inhibitory isoforms, we propose a multifaceted approach: An in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms and regulatory networks of AS is crucial, as understanding how specific splicing events contribute to immune evasion versus neoantigen generation will identify key regulatory nodes that can be targeted to tilt the balance towards immune activation. Leveraging high-throughput sequencing technology to construct tumor-specific splicing profiles will enable the identification of splicing events that produce immunogenic neoantigens while minimizing immune evasion, aiding in the development of highly selective AS regulators that precisely inhibit immunosuppressive splicing processes and promote neoantigen generation and presentation. Utilizing advanced neoantigen prediction tools can identify candidate targets for immune intervention, aiding in the design of synthetic vaccines or TCR-T cell therapies tailored to splicing-derived neoantigens, thus enhancing the specificity and efficacy of the immune response. The combination of splicing regulators with immunotherapy holds significant promise, for example, using splicing modulators to enhance neoantigen presentation and reduce inhibitory isoforms like soluble PD-L1, while concurrently employing immune checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive T cell therapies to boost the anti-tumor immune response. Designing synthetic vaccines or TCR-T cell therapies based on splicing-derived neoantigens can provide a targeted approach to elicit a robust immune response, with these therapeutic strategies personalized to the specific splicing profiles of individual tumors, thereby enhancing their effectiveness. Continuous monitoring of splicing profiles and immune responses during treatment will be essential, with adaptive therapeutic strategies responding to changes in tumor splicing patterns and immune evasion tactics to maintain therapeutic efficacy over time. In summary, by integrating the detailed molecular understanding of AS with advanced sequencing technologies, neoantigen prediction tools, and combination therapies, we can strategically exploit the dual role of AS in tumor immunity, aiming to achieve precise and effective interventions that enhance tumor-specific immunity while mitigating immune evasion. Furthermore, AS is a crucial post-transcriptional mechanism that significantly contributes to the process of immunoediting, which is the dynamic interaction between the immune system and cancer cells leading to the elimination, equilibrium, and escape of tumor cells. AS can generate novel peptide sequences, creating unique neoantigens that are not present in normal cells, and significantly increase the diversity of neoantigens by producing multiple protein isoforms from a single gene. During the elimination phase of immunoediting, immune cells target and destroy tumor cells expressing these neoantigens. However, tumor cells can develop mechanisms to evade immune detection, one of which is through the modulation of AS to alter the expression of neoantigens. AS can produce splicing variants that lack the exonic regions encoding neoantigens, leading to protein isoforms that do not present the original neoantigenic peptide sequences. This loss or alteration of neoantigenic epitopes can reduce their presentation on MHC molecules, diminishing their recognition by T-cells and contributing to tumor immune escape. Tumors may evolve to favor the production of non-immunogenic isoforms through AS, thereby minimizing the presence of immunogenic neoantigens on the cell surface. The dynamic nature of AS poses challenges for immunotherapies targeting specific neoantigens, as tumor cells may evade such therapies by altering their splicing patterns to “turn off” the targeted neoantigens. To counteract this, novel therapeutic strategies could focus on targeting the splicing machinery itself or using vaccines that target multiple isoforms and splicing variants to ensure a broader and more robust immune response. Understanding the interplay between AS and neoantigen modulation is crucial for developing effective cancer immunotherapies and overcoming immune escape mechanisms. In summary, alternative splicing can indeed lead to the “turning off” of neoantigens that arise from other mechanisms, thereby playing a pivotal role in tumor immune escape and the broader process of immunoediting.
Notably, Tumor stage and disease burden can significantly influence the efficacy of AS-derived neoantigen immunotherapies, particularly when administered in the adjuvant versus metastatic setting. In the adjuvant setting, where the disease burden is typically lower and the immune system is less compromised, the immune response generated by AS-derived neoantigens can be more robust and effective. This is because there are fewer cancer cells to target, allowing the immune system to more efficiently recognize and eliminate residual malignant cells before they can establish new tumors. Additionally, the lower tumor burden in the adjuvant setting may result in fewer immune-evading mechanisms being in place, thereby enhancing the efficacy of the immunotherapy. Conversely, in the metastatic setting, the higher disease burden and more advanced tumor stage can pose significant challenges. The extensive tumor load can lead to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, characterized by the presence of regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and other inhibitory factors that dampen the immune response. Moreover, metastatic tumors often exhibit greater genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity, which can result in a more diverse array of neoantigens and potentially reduce the overall effectiveness of AS-derived neoantigen immunotherapies. The immune system may also be more exhausted in the context of widespread disease, further limiting the potential for a robust therapeutic response. Thus, the timing and context of administering AS-derived neoantigen immunotherapies are critical factors. In the adjuvant setting, where the goal is to prevent recurrence following primary treatment, these therapies may offer a higher likelihood of success. In contrast, in the metastatic setting, combining AS-derived neoantigen immunotherapies with other treatments that modulate the immune environment or reduce tumor burden could be necessary to achieve optimal outcomes.
Looking ahead, multiple strategies could further drive this field forward. For example, the development of “public” splicing-derived neoantigen therapies may accelerate clinical translation [ , , , ]. If recurrent neojunctions like those from GNAS or RPL22 prove immunogenic across patients [ ], they could be leveraged to create off-the-shelf vaccines or T cell products applicable to many individuals, providing a practical complement to fully personalized approaches. Furthermore, integrating splicing research with other biological domains may yield novel anti-cancer strategies. For instance, epigenetic and signaling pathways can influence splicing decisions [ , , , ], thus epigenetic modulators might be used to indirectly promote the expression of favorable splice isoforms (such as those enhancing antigen presentation) or to suppress oncogenic splicing patterns. On the clinical front, as early trials of splicing-targeted treatments show promise [ , , , ], one can envision that truly effective splicing-centric immunotherapies could emerge within the next decade. Achieving this will require resolving key issues like optimal patient selection (e.g. identifying biomarkers for splicing-dependent tumors or high AS-derived neoantigen load) and streamlining personalized treatment manufacturing. Studies have demonstrated that the inhibitory effect of tumor cells carrying neoantigens is dependent on the expression of MHC-I molecules and the functionality of T cells [ ]. Another study has shown that in microsatellite stable (MSS) CRC, low expression levels of neoantigens are associated with poor T-cell infiltration and a diminished response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Insufficient neoantigen stimulation is linked to T-cell dysfunction. Besides direct dysfunction, T cells responding to low neoantigen expression also undergo progressive exhaustion [ ]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that the expression levels of MHC-I molecules and neoantigens in tumor cells could serve as potential biomarkers for patient stratification. Specifically, patients whose tumors exhibit higher expression of MHC-I molecules and neoantigens might be more likely to benefit from therapies targeting AS-derived neoantigens, as these factors are crucial for effective T-cell mediated immune responses. Additionally, the presence of specific transcriptomic signatures, such as high splicing burden or distinct mutation profiles, could further refine patient selection, ensuring that therapies are tailored to those most likely to respond. However, it is important to note that current research in this area remains at the preclinical stage. While these preliminary findings are promising, extensive clinical trials are necessary to validate these biomarkers and their effectiveness in stratifying patients for AS-derived neoantigen-targeted therapies. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to monitor biomarker expression over time and correlate these findings with clinical outcomes. This could ultimately lead to more precise and effective therapeutic strategies for patients with heterogeneous tumor profiles. With continued innovation addressing these challenges, AS-targeted immunotherapy has the potential to become a mainstream component of precision oncology in the near future.
Finally, successful clinical translation of splicing-centric immunotherapy will require a collaborative framework and shared resources. Establishing a public repository of validated AS-derived neoantigens would greatly aid the community. Such a database could support the development of off-the-shelf reagents for commonly shared splicing neoantigens. Regulatory pathways will also need to adapt to the hybrid nature of these therapies. Interdisciplinary alliances, integrating RNA biology, proteomics, immunology, and biomaterials, will be crucial to solving delivery and manufacturing challenges.
In summary, with the broad adoption of immunotherapy in oncology, research on AS has expanded from merely correcting oncogenic splicing to now exploring the immunogenicity of AS-derived neoantigens for therapy. Future research may focus on elucidating the precise mechanisms by which AS influences immune cell activation and function. Integrating splicing-focused approaches into cancer immunotherapy is crucial for bridging current knowledge gaps. Continuous exploration of AS and its interplay with tumor immunity will create new opportunities for precision medicine and pave the way for transformative cancer therapies.
