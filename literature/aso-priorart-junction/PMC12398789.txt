## Graphical abstract
 
Exon skipping therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy is limited by poor drug delivery, reduced muscle regeneration, and excessive fibrosis. By combining dystrophin-restoring and TGF-β-targeting oligonucleotides this preclinical work shows improved enhanced dystrophin restoration, muscle repair, and reduced fibrosis in DMD, offering a safe and more effective combo oligonucleotide therapy.

## Introduction
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked recessive disorder affecting approximately 1 in 5,000 male births. DMD is caused by mutations in the DMD gene encoding the muscle structural protein dystrophin, which is crucial for maintaining muscle fiber integrity. , Patients with DMD present with muscle weakness that manifests by 3–4 years of age, followed by progressive muscle wasting and fibrosis. The pathophysiology of DMD is characterized by myofiber fragility, poor myogenesis, inflammation, and fibrotic replacement of muscle, resulting from the absence of dystrophin. Ultimately, DMD leads to shortened lifespans driven by cardio-respiratory failure. , Dystrophin-restoring genetic therapies offer a cure for DMD and have been a focus of therapeutic research over the past decades.
Gene-based therapeutic strategies approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) include the exon skipping phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers (PMOs), first approved in 2016, as well as the adeno-associated virus (AAV) micro-dystrophin gene replacement vector approved in 2023. , Although AAV vectors target skeletal and cardiac muscles with high efficiency, their use prohibits repeat delivery due to immunological reasons, while producing a partially functional microdystrophin protein. , AAV-microdystrophin therapies are also associated with health risks, including liver injury, myocarditis, and immune-mediated myositis. , In contrast, therapeutic use of PMOs has proven to be free of any safety concerns and is amenable to repeated dosing to produce a minimally truncated dystrophin protein. , , This protein restores myofiber stability, with improved exercise capacity, slowing disease progression, and safely prolonging healthy survival of patients with DMD. , , Despite these growing promises of exon skipping PMO therapies, a paramount challenge is their relatively poor delivery into muscle myofibers and cardiomyocytes. This results in limited dystrophin protein restoration in DMD patient muscles following chronic, systemic dosing. , , , Therefore, development of strategies to enhance PMO bioavailability and myofiber/cardiomyocyte uptake is needed to overcome these barriers and improve patient response and prognosis.
We previously demonstrated that the dystrophic muscles rely on inflammatory macrophages and satellite cell-mediated muscle regeneration to effectively deliver PMOs into dystrophic myofibers. Using the B10-mdx model of DMD, we found macrophages infiltrating the damaged and inflamed regions of the dystrophic muscle sequester the circulating PMO that reaches the muscle interstitium where it serves as a local drug reservoir. The PMOs are thus accessible to the activated satellite cells in these regenerating areas for up to 1 week, well after PMOs in the blood circulation have disappeared. Over this time, PMO transits to the fusing satellite cells for continued delivery to myofibers regenerating in this niche and support local dystrophin expression. , However, in contrast to the robust regeneration and mild fibrosis observed in the B10-mdx model commonly used for preclinical PMO studies, patients with DMD exhibit limited regenerative capacity and significant fibrosis. , As these are barriers to effective PMO delivery to muscle/myofibers in patients with DMD, addressing them would enhance the efficacy of PMO therapy for patients with DMD.
Transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling plays a central role in promoting muscle loss and fibrosis in different skeletal muscle myopathies. In the case of DMD, the expression of TGF-β1 isoform positively correlates with the degree of pathology and clinical severity of disease. , Inversely, inhibition of TGF-β signaling enhances myoblast fusion and regenerative myogenesis, while also reducing muscle atrophy in the mdx model. , , , Thus, improving myogenic potential through TGF-β-targeted approaches in patients with DMD is an attractive therapeutic intervention to enhance regenerative myogenesis and the effectiveness of exon skipping PMO therapies. However, TGF-β signaling controls numerous physiological processes such as angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and innate and adaptive immune responses. Thus, safety of TGF-β-lowering therapy requires that it is delivered locally at sites of injury to minimize detrimental systemic effects.
To address the aforementioned need, we utilized the severe D2-mdx model of DMD, which exhibits heightened TGF-β activity, increased muscle fibrosis, limited regenerative capacity, and, what we observe is a reduced dystrophin exon skipping PMO (DPMO) efficacy compared to the milder B10-mdx model. These deficits are all linked to a polymorphism in latent transforming growth factor binding protein 4 (LTBP4) that diminishes the ability to sequester TGF-β in its latent state. We thus developed TGF-β-targeting PMO (TPMO) and tested the therapeutic potential of a dual PMO therapy (TPMO + DPMO) to address muscle fibrosis, regenerative deficits, and poor dystrophin restoration by DPMO only therapy. Our findings show that local and systemic delivery of TPMO effectively enhances muscle regeneration and reduces fibrotic signaling in the D2-mdx muscle. Dual PMO therapy significantly improved dystrophin restoration in skeletal muscle and enabled detectable dystrophin expression in cardiomyocytes. In addition to restoring dystrophin across multiple tissues, dual PMO therapy also addressed key hallmarks of DMD—poor muscle regeneration and excessive fibrosis—resulting in improved skeletal muscle function in the severe D2-mdx mouse model.

## Results

## D2-mdx exhibits poor DPMO delivery and limited dystrophin restoration in DMD
Our previous work has identified the role of myogenesis in DPMO delivery to muscle fibers and the value of the D2-mdx model for investigating the drivers of myogenic failure in DMD. , , To assess the relevance of the D2-mdx model to study involvement of poor myogenic capacity in dictating the delivery and efficacy of PMO therapies in DMD, we treated D2-mdx and B10-mdx mice with equivalent doses of DPMO (400 mg/kg, intravenous [i.v.]) and compared the resulting dystrophin expression in forelimb (triceps) and hindlimb (quadriceps) skeletal muscles between these models. Analysis of the triceps muscle showed that the number of dystrophin-positive fibers and the corresponding level of Dmd exon skipping were markedly reduced in both of D2-mdx as compared to B10-mdx ( A–1D). The deficit was severe enough that the number and size of clusters containing dystrophin-expressing myofibers in D2-mdx muscles were comparable to the untreated B10-mdx muscle for both the muscles examined—triceps ( E and 1F) and quadriceps ( A–S1E). 
We previously observed that the fewer and smaller clusters of dystrophin-expressing myofibers in PMO-treated muscles are indicative of the extent of actively regeneration in the lesion at the time of PMO administration. , Thus, we next investigated if this reduced DPMO efficacy was the result of poor drug delivery in the D2-mdx muscle or caused by the impaired myogenic capacity of this model. We systemically administered fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated DPMO (F-DPMO; 400 mg/kg) and after 4 days of F-DPMO administration, assessed PMO retention and myofiber uptake in both D2-mdx and B10-mdx muscles. This showed significant F-DPMO retention within the lesions that were inundated with macrophages containing F-DPMO regardless of mdx strain ( A, 2B, A, and S2B). Consistent with our previous report, the extent of macrophage infiltration per damaged lesion was considerably higher in the D2-mdx muscles ( A–2D). Concomitantly, there was heightened F-DPMO signal within these degenerating sites in D2-mdx as compared to B10-mdx muscles ( A and 2B). However, while we observed PMO localized within the myonuclei of regenerating myofibers in B10-mdx muscles, this was rarely observed in D2-mdx muscles ( A and 2B; arrowheads). These results support the importance of inflammatory cells as a reservoir to enable sustained PMO delivery within degenerating/regenerating muscle lesions and suggest poor regenerative myogenesis in D2-mdx is likely responsible for the inefficacy of PMO therapy in D2-mdx model. 
Fibroadipogenic progenitors (FAPs) are skeletal muscle stromal cells that play critical roles in homeostasis and repair of injured, healthy, and dystrophic muscles. , , , , , As TGF-β is produced by the macrophages in the injured and dystrophic muscle and in turn regulates FAP growth and differentiation, we evaluated the abundance of FAPs and their effectors in D2-mdx and B10-mdx muscles. , Expression of Tgfb1, a marker of FAP-mediated muscle fibrosis—fibronectin (Fn1), and abundance of FAP marked by platelet-derived growth factor receptor-α (PDGFRα), were all significantly increased in the D2-mdx as compared to B10-mdx muscle ( E–2H). These increases were correlated with the decreased myogenesis indicated by reduced levels of 5′-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU)-positive centrally nucleated fibers (CNFs) in the D2-mdx muscles ( I and 2J).

## Development and testing of Tgfb1-targeting PMO to lower TGF-β activity
The aforementioned results link the FAP-macrophage interactions driven by elevated TGF-β activity in damaged D2-mdx muscle, in impaired DPMO delivery to myofibers due to reduced myogenesis. , With macrophages being the main source of TGF-β in injured muscles, antisense PMO targeting TGF-β can be an effective tool to downregulate TGF-β in the injured muscle. , , , , With the observed PMO accumulation in D2-mdx macrophages, clearance of FAPs and improved muscle regeneration would enhance local DPMO delivery to the myofibers. , , To assess this we designed and synthesized a series of anti-TGF-β PMOs (TPMOs) targeting various regions of the Tgfb1 gene, including the 5′ UTR (translation blocking PMOs) and various exon-intron junctions (premature termination PMOs) ( A). PMO target sites were selected based on a combination of optimal antisense oligonucleotide (AO) G/C content (<60%), self-complementarity scores, length, and melting temperature (Tm, 90°C–100°C). To examine each TPMO’s inhibitory effect on TGF-β produced by macrophage, we activated cultured mouse macrophages (RAW 264.7) in vitro with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce TGF-β expression and then treated them with individual TPMOs for 48 h. TPMO1, TPMO2, TPMO3, and TPMO6 caused over 3-fold decrease in active TGF-β compared to control (scrambled) PMO ( B), while TPMO4 and TPMO5 reduced active TGF-β by ∼2-fold. These results validated our AO design strategy and identified potential lead candidates for further evaluation. 
Next, we assessed the in vivo efficacy of all TPMOs by local delivery in the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle of D2-WT mice. Muscles were injected with TPMOs (100 μg/TA) or saline for simultaneous needle injury and delivery of the treatment ( C). After 4 days of injury (and knockdown), we evaluated Tgfb1 and downstream targets. , This identified TPMO2 and TPMO6 had the greatest impact on the expression of Tgfb1 and the downstream of Tgfb1-regulated target genes ( D).
Immunofluorescent labeling to monitor SMAD3 phosphorylation (a downstream target of TGF-β activity) and embryonic myosin heavy chain (eMHC; to mark regenerating myofibers), revealed that TPMO2 had the greatest myogenic benefit in vivo. In comparison to control PMO or TPMO6, TPMO2-treated muscles showed reduced pSMAD3 labeling and increased numbers of eMHC+ myofibers, while TPMO6-treated muscles did not significantly differ from the levels observed in control muscles ( E–3G and A–S3D). Together, these results identified TPMO2 (heretofore TPMO) as the most effective PMO for efficient reduction of TGF-β activity in vitro and in vivo, hence used as the therapeutic lead for the subsequent studies.

## Reduction of TGF-β activity enhances D2-mdx muscle regeneration
To assess the in vivo benefit of our lead TPMO, we tested whether its systemic delivery can also address the in vivo myogenic deficit in D2-mdx, which mimics this deficit seen in the patients with DMD. Juvenile (3-week-old) D2-mdx mice were treated with a single i.v. TPMO injection (200 mg/kg) and spontaneous muscle regeneration were examined eight days post-treatment ( A). We found that this systemic TPMO2 dosing strategy safely and effectively decreased TGF-β activity, as monitored by pSMAD3 expression in the spontaneously damaged areas of the D2-mdx muscle ( B and 4D). Consistent with the results of the intramuscular (IM) injection of TPMO in D2-WT, we found that myogenesis (as indicated by the number of eMHC+ regenerated myofibers) was also significantly increased in the TPMO-treated juvenile D2-mdx mice as compared to the control group ( C and 4E). Additionally, TPMO treatment allowed improved growth of the regenerated (eMHC+) myofibers D2-mdx muscles, resulting in reduced frequency of small-sized fibers and an overall increase in the average size of these myofibers in TPMO-treated mice ( C, 4F, and 4G). We observed that the smaller size of the control fibers was in part due to the inability of newly formed (immature) myofibers/myotubes to undergo fusion, leading to unfused eMHC+ myotubes in place of the degenerated myofibers ( ). These results indicate that TPMO-mediated systemic TGF-β inhibition improves myoblast fusion and subsequent myogenesis in vivo to support improved regeneration of the spontaneously damaged dystrophic D2-mdx muscles. 

## Acute dual PMO (TPMO and DPMO) treatment enhances D2-mdx muscle regeneration and dystrophin restoration
As regeneration of injured muscles supports efficient PMO delivery to dystrophic myofibers, we hypothesized that TPMO-mediated TGF-β inhibition will enhance regenerative potential and improve DPMO delivery in D2-mdx muscle. To evaluate this in vivo benefit of a dual PMO therapeutic strategy, we administered TPMO (100 μg) locally by IM injection in the TA muscle of 20-week-old D2-mdx mice, followed by systemic administration of DPMO (200 mg/kg, 2 days later), and assessed dystrophin restoration after 3 weeks ( A). To monitor impact on regenerative myogenesis, mice received BrdU for seven days starting two days after TPMO administration to label myofibers that regenerated following needle injury at the injection site ( A). The benefit of dual TPMO+DPMO therapy was benchmarked against a cohort treated locally (IM) with scrambled PMO and systemically (i.v.) with DPMO. Assessing dystrophin restoration by immunostaining revealed dual TPMO+DPMO therapy yielded increased numbers of dystrophin-expressing fibers compared to DPMO-treated D2-mdx muscles and was associated with a ∼2-fold increase in the numbers of BrdU+ CNFs per TA muscle, increased TA weight, cross-sectional area, and total fibers per TA muscle, when compared to control mice ( B–5D and ). Examination of FAP expansion within damage lesions showed that the inhibitory effect of TPMO on TGF-β signaling is associated with reduced FAP abundance three weeks following a single local dose of TPMO ( B, 5E, and 5F). These results indicate that TGF-β inhibition via TPMO suppresses FAP abundance and promotes muscle regeneration in the damaged lesions, which directly improves DPMO delivery to these regenerating myofibers and the resulting levels of dystrophin restoration in skeletal muscles of D2-mdx mice. 

## Chronic dual PMO therapy of D2-mdx improves dystrophin restoration and muscle health
To assess the therapeutic benefit of the dual PMO strategy for improved function of the severely dystrophic muscle of D2-mdx mice, we systemically treated these mice twice weekly with a TPMO+DPMO cocktail for six weeks starting from the disease onset at 3 weeks of age ( A). After 6 weeks of dual TPMO+DPMO therapy, we examined the extent of dystrophin restoration with chronic TPMO+DPMO treatment, which showed that dystrophin expression far exceeded the saline cohorts, reaching ∼25% dystrophin+ fibers in the treated gastrocnemius muscles ( B, 6C, A, and S6B). These were also CNFs ( D), indicating that DPMO delivery to these dystrophic muscle fibers was facilitated by the improved myogenesis. We also assessed total dystrophin restoration by quantifying protein expression in gastrocnemius muscle lysates using capillary western immunoassay (Wes). This showed dual PMO-treated muscles restored dystrophin protein expression to ∼7% of wild-type (WT) muscle levels ( E and 6F). 
With the aforementioned improvements in dystrophin restoration by dual PMO therapy, we next examined the efficacy of this approach to address muscle fibrosis. Staining the muscle tissue with fluorescent wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) to mark the degenerating areas and Sirius red to specifically mark fibrosis, identified a marked reduction in fibrosis in the dual PMO-treated muscles ( G–6I). This histological evidence of reduced fibrosis was further supported at the level of gene expression by the reduced expression of Tgfb1 and its downstream target genes Col1a1 and Mmp9 ( J–6L).

## Chronic dual PMO therapy of D2-mdx results in long term functional benefits
To assess the impact of the aforementioned improvements in dystrophin restoration and reduced muscle loss by the dual PMO therapy, we investigated the impact of this therapy on muscle strength through in vivo functional measures. Assessment of in situ isometric force (torque) generation by gastrocnemius muscle in response to tetanic stimulation, revealed improved force production by TPMO+DPMO therapy at tetanic stimulations (140–200 Hz), and increased maximal peak isometric force production, as compared to the saline-treated control muscles ( A and 7B). Improved force production by the dual PMO-treated D2-mdx mice also manifested as improved grip strength and a tendency toward greater endurance in inverted wire-mesh hang test, relative to saline-treated D2-mdx mice ( C and 7D). Pertinent to these functional improvements, longitudinal monitoring of body weight or endpoint monitoring of organ weights showed no difference relative to saline-treated controls ( C and S6D). Endpoint monitoring of muscle mass showed mixed results, with no differences observed for the quads and triceps muscles, while TA and gastrocnemius muscles mass was improved relative to saline-treated controls ( E). Further, to assess if TGF-β PMO treatment inhibited TGF-β-mediated gene expression systemically, we monitored TGF-β target genes expression in an unrelated organ—spleen. Unlike the injured muscles ( ), systemic PMO treatment did not alter the expression of TGF-β-regulated genes elsewhere ( ). 
As the dual PMO therapy yielded a significant functional benefit with chronic dosing, we examined if this also restored the localization of the other members of the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC), which is compromised in the absence of dystrophin. Immunostaining serial muscle sections for dystrophin with either β-dystroglycan, dystrobrevin, or α-sarcoglycan revealed the expected low and diffuse expression of these DAPC members, along with their punctate aggregated localization along the sarcolemma in saline-treated muscles. This was reversed in TPMO+DPMO-treated D2-mdx muscles which showed heightened expression of these proteins that were properly localized along the sarcolemma in all fibers where dystrophin had been restored ( E). This suggests that the dual PMO therapy likely results in the improved function of the D2-mdx muscle not only by improving dystrophin expression but also by restoring localization of the DAPC members.
In preclinical models and DMD, PMO therapy fails to restore dystrophin expression in cardiomyocytes , —a major clinical challenge of this therapy. To investigate whether dual TPMO and DPMO therapy could improve dystrophin restoration in D2-mdx hearts, we immunostained cardiac muscle cross section for dystrophin. Dual PMO therapy did allow limited dystrophin expression in cardiomyocyte with expressing clusters scattered throughout the D2-mdx heart ( F, A, and S8B), which we previously did not detect in DPMO monotherapy using the milder B10-mdx model. Dual PMO therapy restored dystrophin expression in ∼150–300 cardiomyocytes per heart cross-section, representing ∼2% of cardiomyocytes and a roughly 10-fold increase compared to revertant cardiomyocyte levels observed in saline-treated hearts ( G and 7H). Unlike skeletal muscle, the sporadic dystrophin restoration seen in dual PMO-treated hearts did not restore the expression of DAPC members ( C), suggesting a need for longer dosing and other optimization to take this initial promising finding toward a functional cardiac benefit for the patients with DMD.

## Discussion
FDA-approved PMO-based exon skipping AO therapies are the most widely used gene-targeting treatments for DMD. While these offer broad therapeutic benefits, their clinical potential is hindered by limited and sporadic uptake in skeletal myofibers and cardiac myocytes, as well as the progressive degeneration of dystrophic muscles. , , , , , Here, we addressed these challenges by employing a dual PMO therapy strategy in the severe DMD mouse model, D2-mdx, which replicates the poor PMO delivery and muscle pathology observed in patients with DMD. Consistent with our previous discovery that PMO delivery is mediated by inflammation, , we found significant amount of PMOs retained in the inflammatory lesions throughout the muscles in the D2-mdx dystrophic model ( ). However, the poor regenerative capacity of the muscle hindered efficient PMO delivery to myofibers, limiting dystrophin restoration ( and ).
We previously demonstrated that excessive TGF-β activity in D2-mdx muscles impacts immune and FAP cells, impairing satellite cell function and muscle regeneration. , This is clinically relevant, as high TGF-β activity is also associated with poor muscle repair and excessive degeneration in patients with DMD, likely contributing to the inefficient PMO delivery in dystrophic muscles. , Macrophages, which are the major producers of TGF-β in dystrophic muscles, also retain PMOs within degenerating lesions. , To harness this biology, we developed novel TPMOs designed to downregulate TGF-β either by blocking mRNA translation—targeting the 5′-UTR and translation start site—or by inducing premature mRNA termination via targeting downstream exon-intron boundaries, promoting “exon skipping” ( ). Initial in vitro screening showed that both types of TPMOs effectively reduced active TGF-β, yielding a list of promising candidates for further in vivo evaluation ( ). Two lead candidates (TPMO2 and TPMO6) were identified, with TPMO2 showing the most promising effects following a single systemic dose, including enhanced regenerative capacity in D2-WT muscles after acute needle injury ( ). This is in contrast to systemic delivery of anti-TGF-β antibody and inhibitory drugs that have offered limited success in treating regenerative deficit. , Improved muscle regeneration by TPMO2 in D2-mdx mice was associated with a local decrease in pSMAD3 levels ( and ). This validates our strategy of reducing TGF-β can help mitigate myogenic deficits in DMD. Importantly, targeting TGF-β provides a therapeutic advantage for all patients with DMD, regardless of the specific dystrophin mutation, as high TGF-β activity is a common feature across patients with DMD and correlates with disease severity and prognosis. , , , , , 
Given TGF-β′s multifunctional roles in embryonic development, cell proliferation and differentiation, immune response, wound healing, and fibrosis, persistent systemic inhibition of TGF-β is undesirable. Unlike our previous efforts to block TGF-β signaling by targeting the type II TGF-β receptor, here we show that local TGF-β inhibition can be achieved through systemic dosing of TPMOs, with macrophages mediating their delivery to local injury sites where they can exert their effects ( and ). Additionally, macrophage-secreted TGF-β inhibition alters the fate of FAPs and SCs, improving myoblast fusion and nascent fiber formation ( ). Chronic systemic administration of the TGF-β-inhibiting TPMO, in combination with DPMO, did not result in notable side effects, organ toxicity, or changes in organ weights, but did improve skeletal muscle weights ( C–S5E).
Exon skipping therapies have been estimated to benefit 55% of DMD-causing mutations and 80% of DMD-causing deletions, as well as some patients with exon duplication. Therefore, co-administering TPMOs with existing PMO-based exon skipping therapies holds the potential to benefit a large cohort of patients with DMD. A major challenge with PMO therapies is their pharmacokinetics; PMOs are rapidly cleared from the blood within hours following systemic administration. , However, PMOs that are sequestered by macrophages within damaged muscle can be retained at the injury site for at least a week, but their efficacy in restoring dystrophin requires efficient myogenesis. The dual DPMO+TPMO approach overcomes this limitation by enhancing myogenesis via TPMO, ensuring greater functional impact of DPMOs sequestered at muscle damage sites. This synergy was observed following acute intramuscular injection of TPMOs or a chronic regimen of combined TPMO and DPMO in D2-mdx mice, where we noted increased myofiber regeneration and higher numbers of dystrophin-expressing fibers ( and ).
PMO-based therapies have had limited success in restoring dystrophin expression in the heart, , with even long-term systemic PMO administration resulting in <2% cardiac dystrophin upregulation and no improvement in cardiac output. Other strategies, such as peptide-conjugated PMOs, have faced safety and toxicity concerns. Our dual TPMO+DPMO approach presents a promising new direction for cardiac dystrophin restoration by potentially overcoming limitations related to cardiomyocyte regeneration. However, with reduced macrophage infiltration and non-regenerative nature of cardiac tissue, the level of dystrophin restoration achieved using dual TPMO and DPMO is unlikely to confer cardioprotective benefits in DMD, hence additional development is still required. Pharmacological inhibition of TGF-β has been shown to enhance cardiomyocyte differentiation and improve cardiac regeneration following myocardial infarction, , suggesting that TPMOs may promote dystrophin expression through similar mechanisms in the heart ( ).
In summary, our novel approach combines PMO-based exon skipping therapy with TGF-β inhibition to enhance the clinical efficacy of DMD therapies. This dual PMO therapy not only holds promise for patients with DMD, regardless of their dystrophin mutation, but also presents a potential monotherapy for a broader range of diseases characterized by inflammation and elevated TGF-β activity. By overcoming current barriers to the clinical impact of PMO-based therapies, this approach also opens new avenues for treating other degenerative diseases.

## Materials and methods

## Animals
The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Children’s National Research Institute (CNRI) reviewed and approved all animal procedures. The C57BL/10ScSn-Dmdmdx/J (B10-mdx) and D2.B10-Dmdmdx/J (D2-mdx) mouse strains were used, which harbor a nonsense point mutation in Dmd exon 23. We also used the DBA/2J WT strain to confirm the efficacy of PMO-mediated Tgfb1 knockdown. Mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and bred in-house for experiments. Mice were housed in a sterile barrier facility and were maintained under normal, ambient conditions (∼21% O2, ∼22 ± 1°C, 12 h light/12 h dark) with continuous access to food and water throughout the studies. For experiments, mice were randomized to cohorts based on sex and body weight. However, in the chronic dual PMO trial, we used strictly 3-week-old D2-mdx male mice to standardize functional testing measures. For the tissue harvesting, mice were euthanized at designated time frames with CO2 inhalation and cervical dislocation, and muscles were collected and mounted on cork in tragacanth gum and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen-chilled isopentane for storage at −80°C.

## BrdU labeling
BrdU (Sigma-Aldrich, B9285) was administered ad libitum in drinking water (0.8 mg/mL) for a period of 7 days after systemic injection of DPMO. , Water containing BrdU was kept protected from light during administration.

## Immunofluorescence
The muscles’ transverse cryosections (8 μm thick) were prepared using a CM3050S cryostat (Leica Biosystems) and stored at −80°C. For immunofluorescence procedures as previously described. , , Sectioned muscle samples were stained with primary and secondary antibodies as described in .

## TGF-β1 ELISA
RAW 264.7 macrophage cell line (ATCC, VA) were cultured in DMEM, high glucose (4,500 mg/L), GlutaMAX supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were plated at 12-well plates with a density of 3.0×105 cells per well, stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) to induce TGF-β1 expression, and treated with each TPMO for 48 h. The amount of active TGF-β1 was determined in whole-cell lysates by a Quantikine ELISA mouse TGF-β1 immunoassay (R&D Systems, Bio-Techne, MB100B) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Final values were normalized to total protein concentration.

## PMO antisense sequences and delivery
Dystrophin-restoring PMO targeting the exon-intron boundary of exon 23 of the Dmd pre-mRNA (+7–18; 5′-GGC CAA ACC TCG GCT TAC CTG AAA T-3′: DPMO) and a modified PMO conjugated to fluorescein (+7–18; 5′-GGC CAA ACC TCG GCT TAC CTG AAA T-3′-fluorescein: F-DPMO) were synthesized (GeneTools, LLC). To suppress the expression of TGF-β1 using PMO, we synthesized PMOs to target six different regions of Tgfb1 pre-mRNA, including the 5′ UTR, the start codon, and the exon-intron junction ( A, TPMO 1–6). PMOs were diluted in saline and incubated at 50°C for 15 min before administration, either by (IM or i.v. delivery as naked oligos. Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, and PMOs were administered IM in the TA muscle or i.v. via the retro-orbital sinus (200 or 400 mg/kg, <100 μL total volume) using an insulin syringe. Saline or random control oligo 25-N (GeneTools, LLC) was administered at equivalent volumes as a control.

## TaqMan RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from sections of muscle samples using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596018) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. cDNA was synthesized from RNA (1,000 ng) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 368813) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA (50 ng) was loaded for each triplicate reaction in 384-well plates and analyzed using mouse-specific TaqMan probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Taqman Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4444557) on the 7900HT Fast Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Taqman probes are listed in . The comparative Ct method (ΔΔCt) quantified relative amounts of mRNA. All mRNA values were normalized to those of Hprt. To calculate the exon skipping of Dmd rate, we used the skipped Dmd product (AIOIXIL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, splice junction spanning Dmd exon 22–24) and non-skipped Dmd product (Mm01216492_m1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) amplifies the region spanning Dmd exons 2–3). Percent exon skipping was calculated as previously described. , 

## Forelimb grip strength
A grip strength meter (Columbus Instruments) was used to assess the forelimb grip strength test daily for three consecutive days according to Treat NMD (Neuromuscular Disease) protocols (DMD_M.2.2.001). Data are represented as averaged grip strength of maximum daily values over three days.

## Timed inverted hang
For the timed wire hanging test, mice were placed in a homemade box covered with wire mesh (1 × 1 cm grid), flipped over, and placed ∼35 cm above a cage with soft bedding. Hang time was recorded, with 600 s used as a cutoff.

## In vivo isometric torque
Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane-mixed O2, and the foot was attached to the dual-mode lever and maintained at 90° for isometric torque assessment (Aurora Scientific) as previously described. , Contraction of the plantarflexor was controlled by stimulation of the tibial nerve. Isometric muscle contractions were stimulated at 10–15 mA using Pt-Ir needle electrodes inserted percutaneously adjacent to the tibial nerve. Peak isometric torque was measured in response to varying tetanic stimulations (20, 40, 80, 100, 120, 140, 160, 180, and 200 Hz), providing a 60 s rest period between stimuli.

## Capillary western immunoassay
Muscles were dissected and frozen in liquid-nitrogen-cooled isopentane. 8 μm sections were lysed in 5% SDS buffer containing 10 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 75 mM Tris-HCL (pH 6.8), and protease inhibitors. Wes analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using 66–440 kDa Separation Modules (ProteinSimple, no. SM-W008). In each capillary, 0.2 mg/mL protein was loaded for analysis with antibodies to dystrophin (Abcam, no. ab154168, dilution 1:15) or alpha-actinin (Abcam, no. ab68167, dilution 1:100), and anti-rabbit secondary (ProteinSimple, no. 042–206). Simple Western (SW) software was used to quantify chemiluminescence data. To measure the expression level of dystrophin after dual PMO treatment to D2-mdx mice, dystrophin in protein lysate of WT gastrocnemius was set to 100% and mixed with protein lysate of D2-mdx gastrocnemius to create a standard curve. A standard curve was generated using the mixture of WT lysate/D2-mdx lysate denoting 20%, 10%, 5%, 2.5%, 1.25%, and 0% WT dystrophin levels. For example, in the case of 5% WT dystrophin, protein lysate for Wes was made by mixing 95% D2-mdx lysate and 5% WT lysate. , 

## Statistical analyses
Data were analyzed using Prism GraphPad software (9.2.0). Data distribution was assessed by Shapiro-Wilks normality test. Statistical analyses were performed using unpaired t test or non-parametric Mann-Whitney test based on whether the data passed the Shapiro-Wilks normality test. All p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Data plots reported as scatterplots with mean and SD.
