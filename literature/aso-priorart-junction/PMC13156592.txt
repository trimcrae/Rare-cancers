Gene fusions, caused by chromosomal rearrangements, may act as drivers of tumorigenesis in glioblastoma. These fusions result from the juxtaposition of two genes, leading to the production of a chimeric protein and, in most cases, constitutive activation of a tyrosine kinase receptor. Despite the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors to block the oncogenic activity of gene fusions, clinical responses in glioblastoma remain poor compared to those in other cancers, highlighting the need for innovative therapeutic strategies. RNA interference, using small interfering RNAs or micro-RNAs, offers a promising approach to target these oncogenic fusions. Through specific silencing, small interfering RNAs spare healthy cells, avoiding the adverse effects associated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Recent advances in biotechnology (e.g., antisense oligonucleotides and aptamers) and delivery systems have improved small interfering RNA stability, specificity, and ability to cross the blood-brain barrier. This review discusses these advances and their potential applications to target oncogenic gene fusions in glioblastoma. RNA interference-based therapy represents a critical area of research that could improve the survival of patients with glioblastoma.

CNS tumors such as GBM may harbor oncogenic tyrosine kinase gene fusions driving tumor cell proliferation. When tyrosine kinase inhibitors fail, RNA-based therapies offer a promising alternative to target these fusions, potentially improving GBM patient survival and expanding treatment options for other refractory fusion-driven cancers.

## Introduction
IDH-wildtype glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary central nervous system (CNS) tumor (WHO grade 4). Despite standard-of-care treatment, GBM remains uncurable, with a median overall survival (OS) of 15–18 months. A defining feature of GBM is marked genomic instability, resulting in extensive genomic rearrangements and the formation of oncogenic gene fusions. The advent of high-throughput sequencing techniques has allowed the identification of gene fusions in GBM. Such fusions mainly involve receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) genes, such as EGFR, FGFR3, MET, or NTRK. Although targeted therapies have demonstrated some efficacy in GBM (e.g., pan-FGFR or pan-NTRK inhibitors), the response rates observed with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are lower than those obtained in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). RNA-based therapeutic strategies may be the key to bridge that gap. The design of fusion-specific sequences would allow the selective targeting of fusion-driven tumor cells while sparing wildtype protein expression. This review discusses innovative RNA-based strategies, especially RNA interference (RNAi) (small interfering RNAs [siRNAs], micro-RNAs [miRNAs]) and antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), that are being developed to target oncogenic gene fusions in GBM. We will address the technical challenges, application modalities, and future therapeutic prospects for these approaches.

## GBM
GBM remains a significant unmet clinical need due to its highly infiltrative nature, intra-tumoral heterogeneity, and location within the blood-brain barrier (BBB)-protected CNS. , The standard-of-care treatment comprises maximum safe surgical resection and radiochemotherapy, followed by six cycles of maintenance chemotherapy (temozolomide). Despite this treatment, most GBMs recur within a few centimeters of the resection cavity, and 5-year survival rates remain below 10%. , , The infiltrative nature of GBM and its involvement of functional brain areas prevent truly complete surgical resection. The development of innovative local adjuvant therapies to control the disease, while bypassing the BBB and minimizing systemic toxicity, represents a major technological challenge. In situ chemotherapy (Gliadel) has shown only modest effects, while local cellular and gene therapies are still experimental treatments. , , , While significant progress has been made in identifying key molecular drivers, the clinical translation of these findings remains a major challenge in GBM due to the previously mentioned limitations. The field should aim to develop more selective agents and combinatorial strategies to overcome the limitations of traditional monotherapies.

## Genomic instability and gene fusions
GBM is one of the most genetically unstable human cancers, harboring extensive chromosome copy number alterations, such as gain of chromosome 7, loss of chromosomes 9p and 10, homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B genes (9p21), and amplification of the EGFR gene (7p11). Whether chromosomal instability is the cause or consequence of GBM development remains to be elucidated.
Although somatic copy number alterations in GBM are well documented, the exact mechanisms driving massive rearrangements involving several, if not all, chromosomes are not yet fully understood. One proposed mechanism is chromothripsis, whereby one or several chromosomes shatter into tens to hundreds of pieces. , Such bursts of genomic rearrangements leads to 1) copy number changes, including deletion of tumor suppressor genes and amplification of oncogenes, and 2) gene fusions. Gene fusions arise from the juxtaposition of two genes during chromosome rearrangements, potentially resulting in the expression of a chimeric protein. The advent of high-throughput DNA and RNA sequencing techniques has enabled the identification of an increasing number of oncogenic gene fusions, thereby highlighting novel therapeutic targets for precision medicine ( ). , The first gene fusion described in GBM was the GOPC::ROS1 fusion, arising from a small intrachromosomal deletion of 240 kb. The most studied gene fusion in GBM is the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion, detected in 3%–4% of cases and conferring sensitivity to FGFR inhibitors. , A recent analysis of 4,800 GBM samples showed that gene fusions occurred in 8.9% of cases. 
FGFR3 (37%), MET (21%), and EGFR (20%) fusions were the most frequent. Rare fusions (<1%) involved NTRK2, PDGFRA, ROS1, and BRAF genes. In a study on 156 GBM cases, fusions involving the ALK gene were reported in 1.9% of the cases. 

## RTK gene fusions
In GBM, as in many other cancers, gene fusions predominantly involve RTK genes, which retain their kinase domains. , These fusions often include partner genes with conserved dimerization domains, leading to constitutive activation of tyrosine kinases and upregulation of downstream signaling pathways, such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/ERK, STAT3, PI3K/AKT, and mTOR pathways, driving tumor cell growth, proliferation, and survival ( ; ). Detection of these fusions in clinical practice is performed through RNA sequencing, often using targeted panels. 
Precision medicine approaches mostly rely on small-molecule TKIs to block signaling pathways downstream of RTKs. , These inhibitors have been improved over time to increase specificity and potency and to overcome drug resistance. For example, various EGFR inhibitors have been investigated in in vitro GBM models. Gefitinib, a first-generation TKI, radiosensitized GBM cell lines in vitro but displayed low specificity and reversible effects. Second-generation TKIs, such as afatinib, offer greater potency and selectivity, helping to overcome resistance to first-generation drugs. Afatinib, in combination with temozolomide, inhibited proliferation and invasion of U87MG and U251 GBM cell lines and delayed tumor growth and progression in mouse models. Third-generation TKIs, including osimertinib, are designed to specifically overcome acquired resistance. Osimertinib demonstrated BBB crossing in a mouse model of NSCLC with brain metastases. In another study, it inhibited the growth of six different GBM cell lines and prolonged the survival of GBM-bearing mice. However, osimertinib yielded no clinical response in a patient with GBM harboring the EGFR T790M mutation. Numerous TKIs have been evaluated for the treatment of GBM; however, to date, none has received approval, either as monotherapy or in combination regimens. Several of these inhibitors are currently under investigation in GBM clinical trials. TKIs currently tested to target recurrent gene fusions in GBM are presented in . Even though EGFR is a key driver in both NSCLC and GBM, the clinical efficacy of TKIs differs significantly between these two malignancies. In NSCLC, oncogenic mutations primarily involve the intracellular kinase domain (e.g., exon 19 deletion or L858R point mutation). In contrast, the most prevalent alteration in GBM is the EGFRvIII variant (found in 25%–30% of patients), characterized by a deletion of exons 2 to 7. , This results in the loss of a significant portion of the extracellular ligand-binding domain, leading to constitutive, ligand-independent activation of the receptor. Unlike EGFR mutations in NSCLC, EGFRvIII variant does not sensitize GBM cells to TKIs.
An attractive alternative to TKIs involves targeting gene fusion products at the messenger RNA (mRNA) or protein level, offering a more precise and potentially more effective therapeutic approach ( ). 

## RNA-based strategies against oncogenic gene fusions
RNA-based strategies offer a promising avenue for targeting oncogenic gene fusions in GBM, addressing the limitations of conventional therapies such as TKIs. These approaches leverage RNAi technologies, including siRNAs, ASOs, miRNAs, and aptamers, to selectively target fusion-specific mRNA sequences, particularly the unique junction (breakpoint) formed between the two partner genes. By specifically silencing these aberrant transcripts, RNA-based therapies can inhibit tumor-promoting signaling pathways while sparing normal cell processes. Challenges such as crossing the BBB and avoiding enzymatic degradation are being addressed through innovative delivery systems like lipid nanoparticles, polymer-lipid hybrids, and aptamer-conjugated carriers. , , Additionally, chemical modifications to RNA molecules, such as backbone or sugar modifications, have improved their stability, specificity, and in vivo efficacy and innocuity. , , , These strategies not only offer a more precise approach for targeting gene fusions but also minimize off-target effects and resistance associated with conventional treatments, making them a highly promising tool against GBM.

## siRNA-based strategies
siRNAs are short double-stranded RNA molecules, typically ∼21 nucleotides in length, that are potent tools for gene silencing. They achieve silencing by leveraging their natural ability to specifically bind and cleave complementary mRNA sequences, a process mediated by the catalytic domain of Argonaute 2 (Ago2). Synthetic siRNAs, often designed as “Dicer-ready” molecules, are engineered to mimic this natural cleavage mechanism. Briefly, the mature siRNA binds to Ago2 to form the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), where Ago2 separates the siRNA into a guide strand and a passenger strand, the latter being degraded. The guide strand directs RISC to the complementary target mRNA, which is then cleaved by Ago2’s endonuclease activity, resulting in gene silencing. 
The design of siRNAs faces several challenges: target identification, the design of the double-stranded RNA to avoid interference with AGO2 activity, the choice and positioning of chemical modifications, and finally, the steps of vectorization and formulation. , With regard to gene fusions, another challenge lies in targeting a very narrow region of the target mRNA corresponding to the junction site of the two partner genes. Modifying the ends of siRNAs by adding G-U base pairs or specific mismatches can increase the structural asymmetry between the two strands. This enhanced asymmetry results in a thermodynamic imbalance that helps guide the correct strand into the RISC complex, leading to improved gene silencing, even at low doses. These strategies have not yet been tested in GBM but have demonstrated robust cell death using siRNAs specifically designed to target cells expressing BCR::ABL or TMPRSS2::ERG fusions in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and prostate cancer, respectively. Many strategies have been tested to optimize siRNA activity using chemical modifications at the level of the sugar, phosphate, and, to a certain extent, the nucleobases of the constituting nucleotides. , , , The 5′ terminal nucleotide of the guide strand influences the interaction between siRNA and Ago2, as well as the efficiency of target cleavage. Chemical modifications at the 5′ end represent an effective approach to enhance siRNA activity. As a proof of concept, incorporation of a chemically modified nucleotide, such as the purine analogue 6-(3-(2-carboxyethyl)phenyl)-purine (6-mCEPh-purine), at the 5′ end of the siRNA guide strand, or incorporation of 2′-deoxythymidine analogues at the 5′ end of the sense and/or antisense strands, increased binding affinity to Ago2 and improved target silencing efficacy and specificity in both in vitro and in vivo models. , 
A major challenge in using siRNAs lies in the necessity to target a very specific junction sequence to avoid targeting native transcripts in both tumor and healthy cells ( ). Several siRNAs can be designed to target overlapping regions spanning the fusion breakpoint, enabling the selection of siRNAs that best silence fusion transcript expression while sparing the wildtype mRNA. Recently, siRNAs targeting the FGFR3::TACC3 breakpoint sequence in a GBM model successively depleted FGFR3::TACC3 transcripts, resulting in tumor cell death while sparing wildtype FGFR3 and TACC3 mRNAs, thereby mitigating toxicity in normal tissues. This strategy may help avoid the side effects associated with the lack of specificity of TKIs, which is, in part, responsible for clinical failures in GBM.

## miRNA-based strategies
miRNAs are short, single-stranded, non-coding RNAs of 18–24 nucleotides without open-reading frames that do not encode proteins. Unlike siRNAs, miRNAs do not have one but multiple (up to 100) different mRNA targets. They play an important role in the regulation of gene expression in cancer by binding to the 3′-UTR region of their target mRNAs to inhibit their translation or induce their degradation ( A). Based on the key roles of miRNAs in many cancers, including GBM, therapeutic approaches can consist either of blocking dysregulated miRNAs that promote GBM progression using complementary sequences (coined “antagomirs”) or employing them similarly to siRNAs to silence one or multiple target genes. , Many studies have focused on fusions that result in the production of chimeric proteins or on fusions that result in promoter switching, leading to dysregulation (overexpression) of an oncogene. , Recent studies suggest that the occurrence of gene fusions could disrupt miRNA regulation and thereby promote tumorigenesis. Moreover, therapeutic strategies using miRNAs have shown encouraging results in cancer ( ). Due to various challenges, including drug synthesis and unresolved safety and efficacy concerns, miRNA-targeted approaches in GBM are still limited to cellular and animal studies, with substantial barriers still to overcome before clinical application. 

## Antisense miRNA oligonucleotides
Antisense miRNA oligonucleotides (antagomirs) are designed to be perfectly complementary to the target miRNA to inhibit its expression ( B). This strategy is particularly suitable for targeting oncomiRNAs (miRNAs involved in tumorigenesis). Interacting with the target miRNA inhibits the formation of the miRISC. As a result, the miRNA is unable to bind to the target mRNA.
The use of antagomirs requires the formation of very stable duplexes with the target mRNA to effectively silence its expression. In addition, antagomirs must have significant extracellular and intracellular stability to resist degradation by nucleases. To overcome these limitations, oligonucleotides can be chemically modified at the level of the sugar and backbone moieties. 
An increasing number of clinical studies, including in oncology, are evaluating the efficacy of antagomirs. , In GBM, antagomirs are widely used to inhibit miR-21. miR-21 is one of the most highly upregulated miRNAs in solid tumors (including GBM), acting as an oncogenic miRNA by inhibiting PDCD4 and PTEN tumor suppressor genes, the latter being a key gene inactivated in GBM. A T7-peptide-decorated exosome was developed for the targeted delivery of antisense miRNA oligonucleotides against miR-21 (AMO-21), which downregulated miR-21 and lead to a reduction in tumor size in GBM models. Similar results were obtained with the delivery of AMO-21 by exosome-mimetic cell membrane nanovesicles decorated with T7 peptides in an orthotopic GBM model. Suppression of miR-21 resulted in the upregulation of PDCD4 and PTEN genes, leading to tumor size reduction in GBM. Furthermore, co-delivery of anti-miR-21 and anti-miR-124 in a polymeric nanoparticle decorated with the Angiopep-2 peptide showed a reduction in tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, leading to tumor growth suppression and increased median OS in orthotopic mouse models of GBM. Finally, in vivo studies using antagomirs coupled to the peptide R3V6 to inhibit miR-21 showed marked induction of cancer cell apoptosis, leading to effective inhibition of tumor growth. 

## miRNA sponges
miRNA sponges are molecules capable of binding and competitively sequestering miRNAs from their natural targets ( C). Endogenous miRNA sponges consist of pseudogenes, long non-coding RNAs, and circular RNAs. Recent reports have shown that some long non-coding circular RNAs sponge miRNA targets and promote GBM progression. , miRNA sponges can be artificially modified to inhibit the activity of oncogenic miRNAs. Circular RNAs against miR-24–3p can be sponged to increase the expression of the UNC5B-AS1 gene, thereby inhibiting GBM cell proliferation in vitro. A circular miR-21 decoy was generated using the tRNA splicing mechanism in GBM cell models to inhibit cell proliferation and migration in vitro and to suppress tumor growth in vivo. 

## miR-masks
miR-masks, also known as blockmiRs, operate differently from the above-mentioned technologies. Indeed, miR-masks can bind complementarily to an mRNA to protect it from degradation by miRNAs ( D). As a result, the miRNA can no longer bind to the 3′ UTR sequence of the target gene and exert its inhibition. In such cases, inhibiting the binding of miRNA to its target mRNA could allow the expression of the target gene. These technologies may be useful for maintaining the expression of a tumor suppressor gene that is downregulated in cancer ( D). This approach was applied to design a miR-mask fully complementary to the DENND2D mRNA target sequence of miR-522, in order to counteract the effects of the latter on NSCLC proliferation and metastases. 

## miR-mimics
In contrast to antagomirs and mRNA blockers, miR-mimics, or miR replacement therapy, allow the restoration of downregulated or completely lost miRNAs, particularly in tumor cells, such as tumor suppressor miRNAs, to promote the degradation of an oncogene ( E). miR-147a mimic can bind to the 3′ UTR of SLC40A1 to inhibit its expression and promote ferroptosis in GBM cells, resulting in decreased cell growth in vitro. miR-147a mimic also enhances the sensitivity of GBM cells to temozolomide. 

## miRNAs and gene fusions: A key relationship in tumorigenesis
As previously described, genomic instability may initiate tumorigenesis and contribute to tumor progression. miRNA genes are often located in genomic regions prone to rearrangements in cancer. , The formation of gene fusions can lead to alterations in miRNA regulation. For example, the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion in GBM leads to the loss of the 3′ UTR, which disrupts miR-99a regulation, leading to increased expression of the fusion protein and exacerbation of GBM progression. 
In most studies, work has focused on fusions that encode functional chimeric proteins. However, other potential functions of gene fusions remain unexplored. In a recent study, 802 pre-miRNA loci within 667 genes were part of expressed gene fusions in breast cancer. These fusions were associated with miRNA dysregulation through complex mechanisms. Fusions can place the sequence of a miRNA under the control of the 5′ promoter of the partner gene, leading to dysregulation of the miRNA. Moreover, many different, low-frequency 5′ partners can deregulate the same miRNA regardless of the coding potential of the fusion transcript. Collectively, these events, referred to as “miRNA-convergent fusions,” can increase miRNA expression and contribute to the aggressive tumor phenotype. Alterations in miRNA regulation due to gene fusions have been demonstrated in several cancers, including chronic lymphocytic leukemia, CML, lymphomas, sarcomas, and other solid tumors, but remain largely unexplored in GBM. Transcriptomic studies to identify deregulated miRNAs should provide a better understanding of the interactions between gene fusions and miRNAs and open new therapeutic avenues in GBM. In this context, antagomirs and miRNA sponges are relevant strategies to inhibit aberrant expression of oncogenic miRNAs following gene fusion, while miR-mimics can restore expression of downregulated or lost tumor suppressor miRNAs. Finally, miRNA-based therapies have already shown promising results in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with the AML1::ETO fusion. Transfection of let-7b miRNA inhibited the proliferation of leukemic cell lines expressing the fusion gene and restored the differentiation of leukemic cells. miRNA let-7b interacted with a negative regulatory site in the 3′ UTR region of the AML1::ETO fusion gene, resulting in downregulation of the fusion. Thus, beyond strategies that focus on targeting fusion breakpoints, the 3′UTRs of fusion transcripts also represent promising therapeutic opportunities. In GBM, such 3′UTR targeting may be particularly relevant in oncogenic fusions such as the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion, where miRNA dysregulation contributes to tumor progression.

## ASO-based therapies
ASOs are short, single-stranded modified nucleotides (DNA or RNA), designed to bind specifically to complementary sequences in target mRNA, thus allowing modulation of gene expression. ASO mechanisms rely on Watson-Crick complementary base pairing, where the ASO binds to the target mRNA and forms a stable ASO-mRNA hybrid, blocking translation into protein. Depending on their design, ASOs can act through different mechanisms: i) ASOs can act by steric blockade of the ribosomal machinery by forming very stable ASO-mRNA duplexes; ii) in a related approach coined “exon-skipping,” ASOs can target exons on pre-mRNA containing disease-causing mutations, which are then “skipped” during splicing ; and iii) depending on the chemistry and sequence composition, ASOs can recruit RNase H, which will specifically hydrolyze the mRNA in the mRNA-ASO duplex , ( ). 
During the development of ASOs, the importance of chemical modifications at the nucleoside backbone was recognized very early. These chemical modifications are pivotal for enhancing stability and specificity and minimizing adverse effects. Other modification strategies, such as uncharged backbones or altered sugar chemistries, are currently being evaluated for their propensity to improve ASO properties. , , , , Typically, ASOs exhibit a backbone structure comprising a phosphorothioate linkage flanked by 2′-modified sugars, which is crucial for protecting the oligonucleotide from exonucleases and improving overall stability. These modifications are sufficient for steric blockade (either of the protein synthesis machinery or in the context of exon skipping), but alternative modification patterns are required for catalytic degradation of mRNA (via recruitment of RNase H). Indeed, ASOs can only recruit RNase H if the nature of the duplex resembles that of a DNA/RNA hybrid duplex. This can only be achieved if modifications are placed at the 3′ and 5′ ends, leaving a DNA-like central section. This design is called “gapmer” and often leads to the activation of RNase H1 to cleave the target RNA when the RNA-DNA duplex is formed. , Moreover, an alternative design for ASOs is the RNase H-independent or occupancy-only pathway. These ASOs are engineered to act as steric hindrances (called steric blocks), spatially inhibiting or preventing the translation or splicing of the target RNA. They can bind to the translation initiation site of mRNAs in the cytoplasm and block ribosomal access, thereby inhibiting translation into protein without degrading the mRNA. Furthermore, if the ASO binds to pre-mRNA, it can modulate splicing to either include or exclude specific exons, leading to a protein altered in structure and function. The precision of ASOs makes them a promising tool to treat a wide range of diseases, including cancer, and specifically target gene fusion products.
Twelve different ASOs have already received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), mainly for liver and rare genetic diseases, , while many ASOs are being tested in clinical trials in different cancer types, including GBM. ASOs are being explored as targeted therapies to suppress oncogenes that drive tumor progression. A notable example is AZD9150 (danvatirsen), a next-generation ASO targeting STAT3, which has shown some efficacy in clinical trials in NSCLC, AML, and lymphoma. 
Many in vitro and in vivo studies have highlighted the potential of ASOs as therapeutic tools in gliomas. TGF-β is highly expressed in GBM and promotes aggressive behavior. A study evaluated two novel phosphorothioate locked nucleic acid (LNA)-modified ASO gapmers, ISTH1047 and ISTH0047, selectively targeting TGF-β1 and TGF-β2. Both compounds efficiently silenced their targets, inhibited SMAD2 phosphorylation, and reduced glioma cell migration and invasiveness in vitro. Systemic administration in orthotopic glioma mouse models suppressed TGF-β signaling and significantly prolonged survival. In another study, antibody-ASO conjugates were engineered to suppress DRR/FAM107A, a key driver of GBM stem cell (GSC) invasion. These targeted conjugates were efficiently internalized and accumulated, leading to reduced DRR/FAM107A expression in patient-derived GSCs.
ASOs were first used in 1995 to target the BCR::ABL gene fusion in CML but failed to reduce BCR::ABL protein levels. Recently, antisense double-stranded DNA oligonucleotides were designed against the BCR::ABL gene fusion. These ASOs reduced the expression of the chimeric protein and inhibited the proliferation of CML cells. However, ASOs have not been widely applied to gene fusions. In the CNS, they were used against the STAT6::NAB2 fusion in solitary fibrous tumors, although this approach targeted only the C-terminal sequence of STAT6 rather than the fusion itself. ASOs offer a vast array of possibilities for treatment; they are able to target previously undruggable oncogenes and gene fusions since they are easily modified to offer better pharmacokinetics. 

## Aptamer-based therapies
Aptamers are small single-stranded oligonucleotides (DNA or RNA) that fold into a variably complex three-dimensional structure. They can bind with high affinity to targets, including cells and small molecules such as proteins. Aptamers are generated by an in vitro selection method called systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX). The latter consists of successive rounds of binding, washing, and PCR amplification, allowing the selection of targeting tools as efficient as antibodies. Aptamers possess numerous advantages compared to antibodies, including longer shelf life, easy chemical functionalization and bioconjugation, lower production costs, low batch-to-batch variation, better delivery due to their small size, low immunogenicity, and low toxicity. 
Due to their unique properties, aptamers hold great promise in cancer therapies, particularly for multi-drug-resistant and difficult-to-access tumors. Aptamers can function as drugs themselves by selective internalization into GBM cells and triggering cell death (e.g., anti-nucleolin aptamer AS1411 or anti-PDGFRβ aptamer Gint4.T) , or can function as drug delivery systems to deliver therapeutic agents to the tumor site. , These aptamer-based delivery systems enable faster and deeper tissue penetration than antibody drug delivery systems, primarily due to their lower molecular weight. Various delivery systems have been developed, including aptamer-siRNA complexes, aptamer-modified nanocarriers, and aptamer-drug conjugates. 
Aptamers and siRNA can be easily conjugated through covalent linkages or by simple Watson-Crick base pairing, since both are nucleic acids. These aptamer-siRNA chimeras offer numerous advantages, including low immunogenicity. An aptamer-siRNA chimera enabled selective STAT3 gene silencing in PDGFRβ-positive GBM cells. This approach significantly reduced cell viability and migration in vitro and suppressed tumor growth and angiogenesis in vivo, offering a novel therapeutic strategy in GBM. Furthermore, chimeras can be encapsulated in nanocarriers to improve their stability, delivery, and therapeutic efficacy. AS1411 conjugated to a (L-γ-glutamyl-glutamine)-paclitaxel (PGG-PTX) nanoconjugate drug delivery system improved the median OS of GBM-bearing mice compared to controls. 
Combination therapy may allow simultaneous delivery of radiotherapy or chemotherapy. An aptamer-based therapy using U2 aptamers radiolabeled with 188RE (188RE -U2), which specifically bound to the EGFRvIII variant without binding to wild-type EGFR in EGFRvIII-expressing U87MG GBM cells, increased radiosensitivity. Exosomes loaded with temozolomide and O6-benzylguanine and decorated with an anti-angiopep-2 peptide and an anti-CD133 RNA aptamer (EXO-An2-Apt) were reported to improve survival by 44% in mice bearing orthotopic GBM xenografts compared with tumor-bearing mice treated with free temozolomide and O6-benzylguanine. Furthermore, aptamer-based therapies targeting gene fusions, such as the AML1::MTG8 fusion in AML, offer a highly specific strategy to inhibit oncogenic chimeric proteins by disrupting the AML1::MTG8/DNA complex and restoring normal AML1 function. Such an approach could be developed in fusion-driven GBM.
In summary, aptamers, especially when combined with modified nucleotides, may be a promising strategy in GBM or other cancers harboring gene fusions. Due to their variable conformation, aptamers can recognize gene fusions, chimeric proteins, or fusion-bearing tumor cells with high affinity and specificity. Fluorophore-labeled aptamers could improve the detection of gene fusions and facilitate the monitoring of chimeric proteins in GBM, while aptamers conjugated to radioisotopes may enhance therapeutic efficacy. 

## Limits of RNA-based technologies in GBM
RNA-based technologies are innovative treatments in GBM; however, several limitations constrain their clinical translation. One major challenge is the BBB, which is a selective barrier that restricts the passage of drugs into the CNS. Even if the BBB is disrupted, some regions of infiltrative tumor maintain an intact barrier, which impedes the distribution of systemically administered RNA-based therapeutics. Alternative strategies include convection-enhanced delivery (CED), the use of focused ultrasound to transiently permeabilize the BBB, and direct intratumoral administration to enhance local delivery.
Beyond the BBB, delivering enough therapeutic molecules to tumor cells remains a major obstacle. RNA molecules are susceptible to nuclease degradation and often exhibit limited cellular uptake without protective carriers. Effective distribution within GBM cells requires strategies that enhance tumor affinity and endosomal escape. Functionalization with targeting ligands (e.g., peptides or aptamers) and the engineering of delivery systems, such as hybrid nanoparticles, have improved the distribution of therapeutic RNAs. , 
Another limitation is the intrinsic heterogeneity and clonal evolution of GBM. Distinct subclonal populations coexist in a spatially heterogeneous microenvironment. This complexity contributes to differential sensitivity to targeted therapies and facilitates resistance. A single-target knockdown can eradicate a subclone while sparing others, ultimately promoting tumor recurrence and resistance. Moreover, clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure can drive compensatory signaling pathways, leading to treatment failures.
Off-target effects and immunogenicity are additional concerns for RNA-based therapeutics. Despite advances in sequence design and chemical modifications, partial complementarity to non-target transcripts can induce unintended gene silencing or dysregulation. This can lead to normal tissue toxicity. Furthermore, some RNA chemistries and delivery vectors can activate innate immune sensors, such as Toll-like receptors and cytosolic RNA sensors. This can trigger unintended immune activation, leading to neurotoxicity or other side effects.

## Conclusion and perspectives
Genomic rearrangements may give rise to gene fusions, which represent novel therapeutic opportunities in GBM. Advances in DNA and RNA sequencing techniques have facilitated the identification of these key drivers. Most oncogenic gene fusions involve a constitutively active RTK, which promotes tumorigenesis through sustained activation of downstream signaling pathways. Although TKIs have shown significant efficacy in some cancers, their effects in GBM remain modest. In this review, we highlighted RNAi-based approaches as an alternative means of targeting gene fusions in GBM. This strategy enables selective silencing of fusion transcripts without interfering with wildtype protein expression. RNAi-based strategies combined with other treatments, such as next-generation TKIs, temozolomide, or cell cycle inhibitors, could offer a more effective approach in GBM.
Despite the therapeutic potential of RNA-based strategies, several limitations must be addressed to bridge the gap between preclinical optimism and clinical success in GBM, including crossing the BBB, intra-tumoral heterogeneity, clonal evolution, and off-target effects. While delivery systems have been developed for most oligonucleotide-based treatments to facilitate BBB crossing, additional work is required to achieve universal and efficient tumor delivery. , , Moreover, it is important to note that RNAi-based therapies for fusion-driven GBM are at the preclinical stage, and their clinical benefits are yet to be proven. RNA sequencing should be performed in more patients with GBM to identify those most likely to benefit from these innovative therapies. The transition to early-phase clinical trials will also be essential. In summary, even though oncogenic gene fusions occur in only a minority of GBM cases, their precise identification and selective targeting using advanced techniques is of tremendous importance to improve patient outcomes. Finally, developing these strategies could extend therapeutic options to other refractory tumors harboring oncogenic gene fusions.
