## Abstract

## Background
The evolution of human genome has been inextricably linked to germ cell infectivity and retrotransposition activity of endogenous retroviruses (ERVs). In result, 8% of the modern human DNA is occupied by human ERVs (HERVs), which although non-infectious and replication incompetent, are transcriptionally active in several cases (such as viral infections and cancer), and are able to produce functional mRNAs, non-coding RNAs and proteins, which on one hand are utilized for cell processes and on the other hand have been reported to be implicated in various pathologies.

## Results
We used Nanopore Direct RNA Sequencing (DRS) technology to study the transcriptome of the transcriptionally active HERV family, HERV-K HML-2 (HK2) in a teratocarcinoma cell line. We developed a unique pipeline of HK2 DRS data analysis, which along with DRS itself, enabled us to investigate the true HK2 transcriptional profile on a single-molecule basis, unveiling alternative and non-canonical splicing patterns, length variants distribution and polyadenine tail length estimation.

## Conclusions
We uncovered a plethora of non-annotated HK2 transcriptional isoforms and read-through transcripts that utilized HK2 splicing sites, promoters and polyadenylation signals to assemble copies of the surrounding genes. This finding supports the observation that HERVs are involved in regulation of cellular transcription.

## Background
Retroviruses infiltrate the animal genomes either horizontally -by infecting somatic cells and leaving their genetic trace in the form of the provirus- or vertically -by infecting germline cells, in which proviral integrations may ultimately lead to endogenization. Since evolutionary pressure acts on the host genome, endogenized viruses are also subjected to changes that aid their persistence within the host’s genetic line. In cases when such endogenized elements confer advantageous genomic functions or protein products, respective sequences are positively selected and remain rather intact. Endogenized viruses that have deleterious effects on the host are rapidly eliminated from the host genome by negative selection, while cases of such viruses with neutral overall effect become subject to sequence degeneration that leads to deprivation of their regulatory or protein-coding capabilities. As a result, approximately 8% of human DNA is occupied by retroviral loci. Although, due to stochastic mutations, human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs) have lost their ability to replicate and infect, some HERV genes encode functional proteins which in many cases have become indispensable for the human host [ – ]. On the other hand, the implication of HERVs in a variety of human pathologies, including cancer, has been vastly reported. Yet it is not fully clarified whether HERV activation is the cause or the consequence of the pathogeny and whether it is beneficial or detrimental to the host [ ].
The persistence of transcriptional activity in some HERV families may be attributed either to insufficient time having passed for their inactivation through the accumulation of mutations, or to the possibility that these insertions confer functional benefits and are therefore evolutionarily conserved [ ].
The most recently active HERV-K HML-2 (HK2) family is of particular interest for having full-length proviral members with almost intact open reading frames (ORFs) and regulatory regions [ ]. This enables some members of the family to produce functional proteins and even form virus-like particles (VLPs) in the placenta, early-stage embryos and in cases of cancer [ – ]. In fact, before the discovery of the first human retrovirus, a hint that human retroviruses might exist came from observations of retrovirus-like particles budding from human placental trophoblasts [ ]. Later, it was discovered that human teratocarcinoma cell lines also produced particles with retrovirus morphology [ – ]. Since then, teratocarcinoma cell lines have been considered as the classical model for HK2 transcription investigation [ ].
Alternative splicing mechanisms due to the existence of multiple splice donor and acceptor sites in HK2 proviruses, are responsible for the production of a variety of HK2 transcripts. In the present study, we utilized the NCCIT teratocarcinoma cell line, which is particularly known to produce HK2 VLPs, to examine the transcriptional profile of HK2 using Oxford Nanopore Direct RNA Sequencing (DRS) technology. Our goal was to study long reads of RNA transcripts by directly sequencing each single RNA molecule without PCR amplification and fragmentation. To enable precise characterization of the HERV transcriptome, we developed a bioinformatics pipeline for analyzing direct RNA sequencing data. Combined, the DRS assay and our analysis workflow, gave us the opportunity to witness the true structure and splicing mechanism(s) of each transcript by detecting exon-exon junctions of single mRNA molecules and to precisely identify alternative splicing products originating from each proviral integration.

## Methods

## Cell lines and cell culture
The NCCIT teratocarcinoma cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, LGC Standards, Teddington, U.K.) and cultured in RPMI 1640 growth medium (PAN Biotech, Cat. No. P04-22100), supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS) (PAN Biotech, Cat. No. P40-37500) and 1% v/v penicillin-streptomycin (Biosera, Cat. No. XC-A4122/100), at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator. Cells were cultured in T-25 flask until 80–90% confluence and subcultured every 3–4 days with trypsin-EDTA (Biosera, Cat. No. LM-T1705/100) to dissociate them from the substratum. Only early passage cells were used for subsequent RNA extraction and sequencing (passages 3–4).

## RNA purification
Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 106 to 3 × 106 cells using Monarch® Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. T2010S). We performed the optional DNase I treatment step that is included in the kit, in order to get rid of the majority of DNA present in the preparation. Subsequently, the quantity and purity of RNA was assessed using the NanoDrop Microvolume Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). RNA was extracted at the same day of the library preparation and sequencing run and only high purity RNA was used.

## Direct RNA sequencing library preparation
The library was prepared using the Direct RNA Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technology – ONT, Cat. No. SQK-RNA002) following the manufacturer’s protocol (Direct RNA Sequencing protocol) with a few modifications: (a) the starting total RNA material was 500ng-2 µg; (b) the addition of RNA Calibrant Standard (RCS) was omitted and substituted by nuclease-free water. RCS is an internal control for the library preparation procedure that comprises the yeast enolase ΙΙ gene to ensure proper strand calibration as a spike-in positive control. In our early trial experiments, we noticed that RCS takes over approximately 50% of the reads and therefore we decided to exclude it from subsequent sequencing runs to increase sequencing throughput of our target, as similarly highlighted in previous work [ ].

## MinION sequencing run
Libraries were loaded on a R9.4.1 flow cell (ONT, Cat. No. FLO-MIN106D) on the MinION Mk1B platform. Sequencing was initiated and handled by the MinKNOW user-platform (23.07.15), which was set to execute base-calling based on the Guppy algorithm (v7.1.4) and the High Accuracy model (HAC). Additional parameters included filtering of reads having an average sequence quality below the Phred Score of 7 (default). In summary, we ran four experiments, of which two were paused to reload the sequencer. As such, Experiment 1 and Experiment 3 generated sequences from a single library per experiment, while Experiment 2 and Experiment 4 generated sequences from two libraries per experiment. All runs were performed with identical configurations.

## Data processing
Data in FASTQ format from all four runs were pooled and quality checked with NanoPlot (v1.42.0) [ ]. We kept every read above the average quality Phred score of 7 and removed the reads with length below 50 nucleotides (nts) with chopper (v0.6.0) [ ]. Next, we mapped the filtered long-reads against the Human Reference Genome GRCh38.p14 (NCBI RefSeq assembly GCF_000001405.40) using minimap2 (v2.26-r1175) [ ]. The splice-aware mode “-ax splice -k 14 -uf”’ was specified, as it is recommended in the case of DRS analysis. Samtools (v1.10) [ ] was used to (a) convert SAM to BAM files with ‘view’, ‘sort’, ‘index’ and (b) calculate alignment statistics. Additionally, we applied a post-alignment filtering step to retain only primary alignments of MAPQ above zero (multi-mappers exclusion) in our dataset.

## HERVs’ expression analysis
Assignment of reads belonging to active HK2 proviral and solo LTR loci was achieved with FeatureCounts (v2.0.6) with the option “-L” to turn on the long-read counting mode [ ]. The database we used to identify the specific proviral loci within the human genome is Telescope’s reference annotation, HERV_rmsk.hg38.v2, which contains 14,968 manually curated HERV loci and has also been previously used for another HERVs’ transcriptome analysis [ ]. The HK2 solo LTR loci were similarly identified based on the Subramanian et al. published annotation consisting of 940 elements [ ], whose coordinates were converted from the hg19 to hg38p14 with the UCSC Lift Genome Annotations Tool [ ]. The bidirectional transcriptomic nature of the HERV loci, and especially of the solo LTRs, was tested by applying both strand-specific and unstranded read counting.

## HK2 poly(A) tail length estimation
FAST5 files and sequencing summaries from each experiment were saved after each run and used later to estimate the poly(A) tail lengths of the assigned HK2 transcripts on the pooled sample. Nanopolish-polya (v0.14.0) allows us to estimate the number of adenine bases of native RNA reads, based on a specific algorithm that incorporates the translocation rate of the molecule passing through the pore [ – ]. As a first step, we created an index with nanopolish’s index subprogram that creates a fast lookup table to facilitate the localization of the raw FAST5 information per base-called read. Then we ran nanopolish-polya with default parameters. Single-read level estimates were grouped based on assigned provirus or solo LTR locus. The output was filtered with grep to take into consideration only the FASTQ entries with assigned ‘qc_tag’ “PASS”, while excluding reads not passing this validity control threshold. When ‘qc_tag’ is set to ADAPTER, SUFFCLIP or any other, estimates are considered unreliable. We applied a minimum threshold of 3 ‘PASS’ reads per locus for poly(A) tail estimation [ – ]. The median tail length per locus was then calculated based on the estimates of the total reads that passed this filtering. At this point of our analysis, we also selected three housekeeping genes – ACTB (B-actin), PPIA (Peptidylprolyl isomerase A), HPRT1 (Hypoxanthine Phosphoribosyltransferase 1) - since those have been previously used in expression analyses of embryonal carcinoma cell lines, including the NCCIT [ – ]. Their transcripts poly(A) tails were likewise calculated to provide a relative distribution of poly(A) tails estimates. We used those distributions as a comparative measure of mRNA stability. Their expression was captured as described above in the ‘ ’ section.

## Investigating non-referenced polymorphic HK2 insertions
To examine the presence of polymorphic HK2 integrations, which are not included in the human reference genome, we revisited two publicly available genomic datasets of NCCIT cells (SRA accession numbers: SRX6777778 [ ] and SRX037077 [ ]). Upon compiling our dataset, we mapped the raw reads against the GRCh38.p14 assembly of the Human Reference Genome using Bowtie2 [ ] and then utilized the Mobile Element Locator Tool (MELT) [ ] with a tailor-made HK2 reference repository built using the HERV-K113 LTR sequence and a list of HK2 solo LTR loci. MELT works by first detecting discordant read pairs or split reads in BAM files, it then defines their precise breakpoints and finally delivers a list of novel HK2-like hits upon comparison with a catalogue of annotated HK2 insertions. HK2 polymorphic integrations were determined upon comparison with lists of catalogued HERV-K and HK2 polymorphic insertion loci using bedtools [ ].

## Results plotting
We used NumPy version 1.24.4 [ ] and RStudio (2024.04.0 + 735) [ ] for descriptive statistics calculation and visualization of results. The following R packages were employed: ggplot2, ggbreak, vctrs, circlize, rtracklayer, tidyverse, hrbrthemes, viridis, readxl, writexl, dplyr, tidyr, cowplot, EnvStats, patchwork, ggpubr.

## Code Availability
All custom scripts, including our protocol’s pipeline and R scripts used for plotting, are publicly available in Github https://github.com/mariaanp/Direct-RNA-seq_NCCIT_pipeline.git. An overview of the bioinformatics workflow is presented in Additional File 1: Figure .

## Results

## Performance of the direct RNA sequencing assay protocol
A total of four poly(A) RNA libraries were prepared using the DRS protocol and sequenced on the MinION platform, while filtering out any produced sequences of quality PHRED score below 7. Each run employed libraries prepared using separate RNA extractions from independent cell cultures. The individual runs of these four biological replicates were pooled, and the merged sample was further filtered to eventually exclude any read under 50 nts of length of an acceptable average read quality (Additional File 1: Figure ). The pooled sample of 3,661,998 reads was mapped against the GRCh38.p14 human reference genome, achieving ~ 1.24x average genome coverage. The aligned reads of MAPQ > 0, comprised 91.92% of the total primary alignments (n = 3,273,295). The median read length was 899 (IQR: 586-1,471) nts. Almost 3% of the total pooled reads were unmapped (n = 111,548), deriving mostly from ribosomal and mitochondrial transcription. Comprehensive quality and alignment metrics for each experiment can be found in Additional File 1: Figure and Additional File 2.

## HERVs expression in NCCITs
Capturing the retrotranscriptome across the human genome was achieved by implementing FeatureCounts [ ]. This enabled us to also distinguish and test a bi-directional counting approach, facilitating the detection in some cases of a two-way transcription activity for some HK2 loci, i.e., transcripts originating from the opposite to the annotated locus direction. From now on, we will refer to these transcription directions as s1, which denotes the annotated transcription direction for each locus and s2, which denotes the opposite to the annotated transcription direction. Additionally, we apply a cutoff of 3 reads per locus, a minimum expression threshold that seems to align with previous studies on long-read transcriptomics [ , ].
In the pooled sample, ~ 1.2% (n = 37,565) of the primary alignments reads with MAPQ > 0 belonged to the HERVs transcriptome, based on our collective database (Fig.  ). Those transcripts were dispersed across all chromosomes (22 autosomes and 2 sex chromosomes) without any noticeable pattern of preferential HERV expression in any particular chromosome. Of interest, when we examined HERVs’ expression in the s2 direction, we observed that only a fraction of transcripts was recovered (n = 3,205) and their clustering across the chromosomes slightly differed compared to the s1 direction. From the total retrotranscriptome, 64% were of HERV-H origin (n = 24,031) (see Additional File 3), which constituted the most actively transcribed endogenous retrovirus group in our sample. Around 25% were HK2 transcripts (n = 9,193), with the rest of ~ 11% (n = 4,341) belonging to other annotated HERV families’ insertions within our database. Figure  shows the chromosomal distribution of all HERV transcripts detected, as well as HK2 transcripts spanning proviral loci in both s1 and s2 orientations.
 There are ~ 1000 HERV-H copies in the human genome [ ]. Half of the full-length HERV-H proviruses in the human genome are bound by pluripotency associated transcription factors (e.g., NANOG, OCT4, SOX2, LBP9) and produce transcripts specific to human Pluripotent Stem Cells (embryonic - ESCs or induced - iPSCs) [ – ]. In fact, HERV-H expression has proven to be indispensable for the maintenance of embryonic stem cell identity as well as induction of pluripotency in somatic cells [ ]. Teratocarcinomas, which are germ-cell tumors, differ from other tumors in that they can potentially differentiate into other cell lineages [ ]. The NCCIT teratocarcinoma cell line was isolated from a patient with embryonal carcinoma. It is a pluripotent permanent cell line that can differentiate into cell lineages belonging to all three embryonic germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm) [ ]. Since HERV-H expression has been associated with pluripotency, the high transcription of HERV-H that we detected in NCCIT cells came as no surprise. It has been shown that HERV-H transcripts in Embryonic Stem Cells lack intact open reading frames (ORFs) [ ]. Instead, these transcripts probably function as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) localized to the nucleus, which in some, yet undeciphered way, aid the preservation of pluripotency [ , , ]. Contrary to HERV-H loci, at least some HK2 proviruses have intact ORFs, able to produce functional proteins in order to shape the HK2 VLPs that have been reported to bud from NCCIT cells [ , , ]. For these and other reasons such as that HK2: (a) is the most recently active HERV group, (b) is linked to human disease, with implications in cancers, neurodegeneration and immunomodulation, and (c) has a comparatively high frequency of polymorphic integrations, which allows population genetic studies of ongoing retroviral endogenization, we prioritized exploring the HK2 loci transcriptional profile, instead of the most highly expressed in the NCCIT cells HERV-H loci.
In principle, the structure of a full-length HK2 provirus follows the common retroviral genome structure, including a set of basic viral genes, in particular gag, pro, pol and env clusters, flanked by long terminal repeat (LTR) sequences (Fig. A-B). However, degeneration has led to the excision of some genes in certain HERV proviruses, while homologous recombination between the LTRs has led to the excision of all genes, leaving the HERV element with a single LTR sequence (solo LTR) [ ].
 LTRs, as the main coordinators of retroviral gene expression, host a variety of regulatory elements, including enhancer and promoter regions, splicing sites and polyadenylation signals [ ]. Following endogenization, these elements may be exploited by the host for transcriptional rewiring, which may lead to the expression of otherwise silenced genes and/or silencing of otherwise constitutively expressed genes under certain circumstances and/or in certain tissues [ , ].
Intact retroviral LTRs contain three functional regions, U3 (unique 3), R (repeat) and U5 (unique 5) (Fig. A). U3 and U5 are unique sequences that come from the 3’ and 5’ end of the viral genomic RNA, while R is a short sequence that is repeated at both ends of the viral RNA [ ]. In exogenous retroviruses, transcription starts between U3 and R, while in endogenous retroviruses the transcription start site is situated at the 3’ end of the R region, thus the R region is left out of the transcripts that are initiated by an LTR [ , ]. The polyadenylation signal (AATAAA) is also located at the very end of the R region, meaning that transcripts that end with a 3’LTR contain the U3 and most of the R region (Fig. B-C) [ ]. Plenty of HERV LTRs have bidirectional promoter activity and therefore can activate transcription of both upstream and downstream genomic sequences. Despite the existence of a TATA box, it is highly possible that LTR promoter activity can be also TATA-independent and is initiated by sequences located at the R/U5 boundary [ ].
According to our pooled sample results, presented in Additional File 4, 17 out of the total 99 annotated HK2 proviral loci showed signs of expression, consisting almost equally of type II (9/17) and type I (8/17) proviruses. The categorization of Type I and II proviruses is based on the presence (type II) or absence (type I) of a 292 bp sequence in the pol-env junction (Fig. B). In some cases, the type of an HK2 provirus cannot be identified due to the complete absence of the surrounding region (e.g. due to env gene deletion). However, the strongest expression signal is emerging from the type I proviruses that produced the majority of reads (~ 90%), while the rest belongs to type II proviruses (~ 10%) and proviruses that are so severely degenerated that cannot be attributed to neither type (Additional File 4). According to the phylogenetic clustering of LTR sequences, which further classifies the viruses into three subgroups (LTR5Hs, LTR5A, LTR5B) [ ], the majority of our sample’s active proviral loci belonged to the LTR5Hs group, which are considered to be the most recently acquired HK2 integrations. Surprisingly, even though younger proviruses seem to govern the expression levels among the active loci with more than 300 read counts per locus, an older integration dating back to an average age estimation of 18.76 MYA on chromosome 11, the HML2_11q23.3 (Additional File 4), was the second most expressed provirus (n = 1,157 transcripts).
Our findings regarding solo LTR expression indicate lower levels of transcriptional activity compared to HK2 proviral expression. Particularly, 194 reads were assigned to only 30 out of the total 941 annotated solo LTR insertions with minimum of 3 supporting reads per locus (~ 3% of total solo LTR sites), which apparently produce read-through transcripts, as partial or complete LTR-derived sequences (Additional File 5). In a similar fashion to the proviral results, most of the produced transcripts of this category come from the LTR5Hs type (Additional File 5). Nevertheless, the second most active group in this case is the LTR5B, in contrast to HK2 proviral expression, wherein all expressed loci belonged to the LTR5Hs group. Concerning the dating of the expressed solo LTRs, we do not observe any specific patterns between their estimated integration age and their expression levels (Additional File 5). Of note, the solo LTR 563 was found to produce 36 reads, but was eventually the 5’ LTR of a provirus that lies in that region (“11q12.3b” at position chr11:62,375,545 − 62,383,091(-)). We decided to exclude it from our downstream analyses to avoid overestimation of counts. Thus, we will be taking into consideration 29 expressed solo LTR loci (≥ 3 reads per locus) with n = 166 total read counts. The results for the solo LTRs, along with notes on their functional characterization can be found in Additional File 5.
Consistent with previous evidence that present HK2 LTRs acting as promoters in both sense and antisense manner [ ], we were able to detect bidirectional activity. In principle, the majority of the produced HK2 transcripts are derived from the s1 direction, which is the annotated transcription direction. The s2 expression of the proviral loci (i.e., antisense to their annotated direction) seems to be limited to 7 proviruses, with 3 of them (1q22, 4p16.1a, 10p14) demonstrating bi-directional derived transcripts (Additional File 4). On the contrary, the s2 activity of the solo LTRs seems broader, with 43 solo LTR loci producing reads in this manner and 7 of them in both directions (Additional File 5). Overall, the s2 transcription in the case of proviral integrations comprises less than 1% of the total reads assigned to HK2 proviral loci, opposed to the s2 solo LTR transcription which comprises almost 18% of total HK2 solo LTR reads. However, for the majority of solo LTR loci we only notice a single read count assigned to the s2 expression, except for the case of solo LTR 383, where n = 3 reads were detected (Additional File 5).

## HK2 expression in non-referenced and centromeric positions
We examined the expression of two additional categories of HK2 integrations, the centromeric and polymorphic insertions, to get a more comprehensive overview of the HK2 expression in NCCIT cells. The activity of these insertions located in the centromeric and pericentromeric regions of many chromosomes was assessed by implementing in our analysis a separate reference genome, as the one used in our initial trial (GRCh38p14), is not complete at the moment. For this purpose, we extracted the reference sequences of the centromeric and pericentromeric insertions and added them to the hg38p14 genome. Reanalysis with the enriched human reference genome did not provide any additional reads mapping to these complementary genomic regions (see Additional File 1). Of note, the incorporation of the recently completed Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) Project [ ], elucidating the remaining 8% of the human genome sequences composed mostly of repetitive regions (centromeres, telomeres, etc.), could potentially aid in the resolution of the aforementioned problem.
In addition, polymorphic HK2 that comprise recent integrations, which might be missing from the human reference genome, could be eventually underestimated in such expression analysis protocols. Examination of NCCIT WGS data using MELT [ ], revealed that this cell line probably carries one known polymorphic site at chromosome 1 (chr1:111,259,969), based on the published list of 36 non-reference polymorphic HERV-K proviruses of Wildschutte et al. [ ]. However, the expression results did not demonstrate any transcripts emerging from this locus.

## Transcripts characterization of the HK2 family
Overall, the generated transcripts originating from the HK2 insertions reached a maximum length of around 8.3 kb, with the most highly expressed provirus detected at locus 5q33.3 (see Additional File 1: Figure ). As already mentioned, in our downstream analyses we filtered out the HK2 loci having less than 3 read counts to focus on the most transcriptionally informative insertions (Table  ) (Total HK2 proviral transcripts = 9,193 and total HK2 Solo LTRs = 194 post filtering). The proviral loci produced transcripts that exceeded a minimum length of 143 nts, with median read length at 1,254 (IQR: 1,177-1,284) nts while many proviral HK2 transcripts seem to lie in the upper range of the read’s length distribution (Fig.  ). As for the solo LTR transcripts’ length distribution, it presents a more variable pattern, with the maximum length being 5,797 nts and the minimum 224 nts. The median read length at this case was 1,231 (IQR: 721.25-2,080.5) nts (Fig.  ).
 

## HK2 proviral transcripts of typical structure
HK2 proviruses produce a primary 9 kb full-length transcript via LTR-driven transcription that covers major viral gene clusters (gag-pro-pol-env). This transcript can be exploited in the production of Gag and Pol polyproteins, as well as protease (Pro) that cleaves the polyproteins into presumably functional proteins (Fig. C) [ ]. The full-length transcript undergoes splicing, generating isoforms like the env transcript for Env polyprotein production and further spliced rec and np9 transcripts in type II and type I proviruses, respectively (Fig. C) [ , – ]. Rec and Np9 are small regulatory/accessory proteins of 14 kDa and 9 kDa, respectively, that have been found upregulated in many tumor types and presumably play an important role in oncogenesis [ , , ]. Another, non-coding transcript, hel, is created by specific splicing patterns (Fig. C) and is highly expressed in teratocarcinoma cells, though its function remains unclear [ – ]. All HK2 transcripts are capped and polyadenylated like typical eukaryotic nuclear protein-coding transcripts [ , ].
Upon manual investigation of the acquired transcripts retrieved from the typically structured HK2 proviral loci (n = 12/17 NCCIT-expressed HK2 proviruses represented by ≥ 3 reads), we assembled Table , where we have classified the observed isoforms per locus according to a canonical or non-canonical spliced manner, and Table , where we provide the abundancy (read counts) of each non-canonically spliced isoform per HK2 locus. The expressed proviruses that do not demonstrate a typical HK2 proviral structure (n = 5/17 of the expressed HK2 proviruses represented by ≥ 3 reads, as described in Table ) were excluded from this evaluation due to the production of transcripts that do not resemble neither canonical nor non-canonical transcripts described in Fig. C, D and Table . The expression patterns of these atypically structured, highly mutated proviral genomes in our sample is thoroughly described in Additional File 1 and depicted in Figure S8. More specifically, in our NCCIT sample the majority of HK2 proviruses of typical proviral structure produced the hel isofom (n = 11/12), which was observed arising from both type I and type II proviruses and seemingly was constituting the most highly transcribed isoform type within each provirus expression profile. This finding is supported by literature, as hel has been found to be highly expressed in teratocarcinoma cell lines [ – ]. This fact, along with the length of hel transcript which is ~ 1500 bp, explains our finding that the median read length of HK2 transcripts is ~ 1200 bp (see previous section). The difference of approximately 300 bp can be explained by possible 5’ end truncations during library preparation (see Discussion). Following, full length or potentially full length (truncated unspliced) transcripts were also observed, all in type I loci. Among the double-spliced transcripts generating either np9 or rec isoforms, the former were more commonly found across the HK2 proviral integrations within our sample, while only two cases were reported to produce rec (type II proviruses 6q14.1 and 12q14.1). Transcripts of the env isoform were the rarest case and product of solely two type I proviruses (11q23.3 and 22q11.21). Lastly, and most significantly, we were able to capture only two proviruses, both type I, exhibiting the full spectrum of expected canonical splicing pattern, including full length, env, np9 and hel transcripts. These proviruses were found to be among the most highly expressed loci (11q23.3 [n = 1,157 reads] and 22q11.21 [n = 318 reads]). Interestingly, among those two, the HK2 provirus 11q23.3, which as previously mentioned, belongs to a very old insertion (average age estimation 18.76 MYA), produced a complete profile covering all possible isoform categories.
 

## Non canonically spliced HK2 proviral transcripts
Extending our manual inspection, we were also able to detect a plethora of non-canonically spliced transcripts (Fig. D, Table , Table ) for almost all typical proviral loci that generated ≥ 3 reads (Table ), where alternative splice donor (SD) and/or splice acceptor (SA) sites were utilized to produce new splicing isoforms. Non-canonical splicing variants have been observed in literature for HERV-H and HERV-K transcripts in various cancer cell lines [ , ]. In canonically spliced HK2 transcripts, an SD (5’-GU-3’) ~ 30 bp upstream from the 5’ end of gag (SD1) acting together with an SA (5’-AG-3’) at the end of pol (SA1) excises the gag-pro-pol region, leaving a transcript composed of the end of 5’ LTR spliced together with the env gene and the 3’LTR (Fig. C) for the production of the Env polyprotein [ ]. For the production of rec and np9 transcripts in type II and type I proviruses respectively, a splice donor site at the end of pol (SD2(I) and SD2(II)) and a splice acceptor site at the end of env (SA2) are utilized in addition to SD1 and SA1 (Fig. C). As a result, the first and last exon of both transcripts has the same length, while the middle exon is shorter in np9 due to the 292 bp deletion in type I proviruses. hel transcript is produced for both types by exploiting the first splice donor just prior to the beginning of gag (SD1) and the last splice acceptor at the end of env (SD2), resulting in the unification of the U5 region of 5’LTR, the last 140 bp of env and the 3’LTR (Fig. C). The majority of SD/SA usage alterations were observed for the second exon of np9 and rec transcripts (Fig. D – example [ ]). For instance, we detected np9 transcripts utilizing SAs located 35 and 21 nucleotides upstream from SDA1 (proviruses 5q33.3 and 11q23.3, respectively). Another non-canonically spliced transcript exhibited a shift in the location of the second exon that started about 220 nucleotides downstream from SA1 and was 92 nucleotides long (in provirus 1q22). We found more transcripts where the second exon of np9 is a bit longer than the typical one, and the same applied for rec transcripts, where we found the second exon to be either a bit longer or a bit shorter (e.g., transcripts of 22q11.21 and 7p22.1, accordingly). We also observed transcripts beginning upstream from the 5’ end of the provirus, having one or more exons outside of the provirus area, one or more exons inside the provirus and then ending inside the 3’LTR region or extending beyond the provirus with additional exon(s) (Fig. D - example [ ]). Other transcript isoforms contained two exons in the 5’LTR region instead of one (Fig. D – example [ ]). Transcript of example [ ] has another peculiarity, as apart from the typical np9 second exon, it may also contain an exon in the pol gene. For both type I and type II proviruses of certain loci, we detected transcripts where the SD1 and SA1 signals were ignored, but the SD2 and SA2 were utilized to produce isoforms that covered the gag-pro-pol region but not the env region (Fig. D - example [ ]). In multiple proviral loci we found transcripts with 5’ ends either inside or before the 5’LTR which ended inside the 5’LTR, utilizing the polyadenylation signal located there (Fig. D - example [ ]). Also, some HK2 transcripts extended beyond the 3’LTR region, having additional exon(s) in non-proviral areas (Fig. D - example [ ]). Finally, we found canonically spliced HK2 transcripts that apparently utilized some poly(A) signal in the U3 region of 3’LTR and therefore ended there (Fig. D - example [ ]). It should be noted that all alternative splicing sites that we found being utilized in non-canonical HK2 transcripts, are of typical sequence (5’-GU-3’ for alternative SDs and 5’-AG-3’ for alternative SAs). This strengthens the possibility of them being the result of genuine splicing events rather than potential artefacts. Alternative splicing events that do not follow the typical splicing patterns of HML-2 proviruses—and resemble the isoforms we categorized as non-canonically spliced (Table )—were identified in the GENCODE database (GENCODE Basic - version 49) for some proviruses. For example, lncRNA transcript ID ENST00000835662.1 mapped to the chromosomal position corresponding to provirus HML2_5q33.3 on GRCh38p14 human reference, resembles non-canonically spliced isoform example 3 from Fig. . As a further confirmation, we conducted orthogonal validation of the slicing junctions of non-canonical splicing isoforms with short-read NCCIT RNA-Seq data, retrieved from SRA, which confirmed the presence of junction-spanning reads for 6 out of 7 non-canonical isoforms (see Additional File 1, section “Orthogonal validation of non-canonically spliced isoforms”). Moreover, representative reads were selected for each canonically and non-canonically spliced transcript isoform of Fig. C and D, annotated, screened for ORFs and deposited to Genbank under accession numbers PZ122232-PZ122243 (see Additional File 1, section “Coding potential of canonically and non-canonically spliced isoforms”).
Along with the canonically and non-canonically spliced transcript isoforms generated from typically structured HK2 proviruses, we discovered some read-through transcripts that were not originally intended to transcribe the proviral information per se, but rather utilized proviral splicing sites and/or polyadenylation signals to assemble a comprehensive and integral copy of the surrounding gene(s) that are located either in the same or in the opposite strand relative to the provirus. For example, the provirus in 4p16.1a is located inside an antisense non-coding RNA (ncRNA) producing region (LOC105369250). The RefSeq database [ ] contains 10 isoforms of this ncRNA, only one of which has an exon spanning a part of the provirus. In our experimental data we observed reads from both strands (Additional File 1: Figure S9A). Two reads followed the direction of the provirus, had a single exon inside the provirus resulting from splicing at the SA2 and ended in the 3’LTR, with one of them having an additional exon approximately 7 kb upstream from the 5’ end of the provirus. One read (possibly truncated from the 5’ end) was generated from the opposite strand and consisted of a single exon spanning ~1 kb of the env region. This read overlaps with part of the aforementioned isoform of the ncRNA registered in RefSeq, which was found to have high expression levels in the brain, endometrium, kidney, ovary and thyroid tissue [ ]. We found read-through transcripts also in the case of the provirus in 1q22 (Additional File 1: Figure S9B), where apart from the canonically structured transcripts, we detected approximately 30 transcripts that had the typical structure of np9 or hel transcripts but did not end in the 3’LTR, instead had an additional exon from the region situated > 25 kb downstream from the 3’ end of the provirus. Some of these transcripts also have additional exons from regions upstream from the 5’ end of the provirus. Moreover, we found one transcript from the opposite strand (possibly 5’ end truncated) covering the first ~ 500 of 5’LTR and extending beyond the 5’ end of the provirus for approximately 600 bp. Since there is no gene or ncRNA recognised in RefSeq for this area, we could only assume that this transcript serves as a ncRNA.
Lastly, we provide a comprehensive examination and discussion regarding some of the expressed HK2 proviruses (with ≥ 3 reads) of our sample that do not show the typical proviral structure due to an extensive number of mutations (Additional File 1: Figure S8). Hence, we are exploring these proviral transcripts separately and present our analysis in Additional File 1.

## HK2 solo LTR transcripts in NCCITs
We classified the transcripts mapped to solo LTRs loci based on the role of each LTR in transcript production (Additional File 5). The solo LTRs were involved in one of the following ways: [ ] they were providing polyadenylation signal (P/A), or [ ] were included in the transcribed read because of read through transcription with the read being initiated or terminated in regions outside of the solo LTR (R/T), or [ ] were the sites of transcripts’ initiation (INI), or [ ] were the site of transcripts’ initiation and termination (INI & P/A). Similarly to the proviral HK2 read analysis, all read counts presented below are from LTR loci represented by at least 3 reads (n = 194 total reads) (Additional File 3).
We were able to demonstrate that the solo LTRs in our sample were mostly used for transcription termination by providing polyadenylation signals (n = 100/194 reads). This can result in the creation of additional transcriptional isoforms of genes, which are terminated earlier than the annotated isoforms and therefore could potentially reduce or alter the activity of the encoded protein product. The most highly transcribed solo LTR locus, locus 65 (n = 29), provides the polyadenylation signal site for all of the 29 transcripts that are being initiated at an upstream HK2 provirus (“1q22”) and terminated at this solo LTR, which lies within the intronic region of an isoform of the gene MSTO1 (Additional File 1: Figure S10A). In another case, we also observe solo LTR loci serving as a polyadenylation site for transcripts of the NBPF9 gene, giving rise to alternative isoforms (Figure S10C). Moreover, in the same case we detected a read that was initiated and terminated within this LTR locus, a frequently observed case in our NCCIT sample (INI and P/A: n = 29/194 reads) (Additional File 3). Referring to LTRs being deployed as transcription initiation sites (INI), we also observed a great proportion of the solo LTRs producing reads in this category (n = 44/194). Namely, we noticed within the CFLAR gene coding region, 3 transcripts that were initiated within the LTR (Additional File 1: Figure S10B), transcribed to the direction of the gene and were depicting isoform structure that was not included in the RefSeq database. Fewer reads demonstrated just read-through transcription (R/T) of solo LTR loci (Additional File 1: Figure S10) that were either in intragenic or intergenic regions (n = 21/194). Again, in the aforementioned case of NBPF9, we can clearly observe that the LTR is transcribed in such a manner for some cases of reads corresponding to that region (Additional File 1: Figure S10C). An extensive characterization for all the expressed solo LTR loci can be found in Additional File 5.
Among the protein-coding genes that host solo LTRs expressed in our study, are four zinc-finger protein genes (ZNF761, ZNF718, ZNF322 and ZNF875, see Additional File 5 – Functional Annotation). In addition, two solo LTRs are located in genomic areas where annotated ZNF-associated ncRNAs are produced. In particular, LTR No 93 (Additional File 5) is situated in chromosome 1 in the region between two ZNF genes on the same strand. This region is intronic to an annotated ZNF670-ZNF695 read-through transcript which is a candidate for nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD). However, the transcript we observed in NCCITs differs as it contains an exon inside the LTR (possibly initiated in the LTR). Perhaps this is a case of solo LTRs playing a role in transcriptional rewiring occurring in response to cancer. The second solo LTR in question (Additional File 5, No 811) is also integrated in the intergenic region between ZNF613 that has the same direction as the LTR and ZNF350 that is in the opposite strand. The said LTR is intronic to an annotated ZNF350-AS1 (antisense RNA 1), possibly also related to a mechanism of negative regulation of ZNF350. Nonetheless, the transcript that we detected in NCCITs contains the whole said LTR and has the opposite direction than the ncRNA. This positional relevance between HERVs and ZNF genes has been reported in literature [ ] and seems to reflect a functional relevance as well. A cohort of zinc-finger factors, the Krüppel-associated box domain zinc finger proteins (KRAB-ZFPs or KZNFs) bind target sequences mainly within repetitive DNA (e.g., transposable elements) thus recruiting KAP1/TRIM28 (KRAB-associated protein 1/tripartite motif-containing 28), a transcriptional repressor which attracts epigenetic modulators to achieve HERV silencing [ , ]. Since many HERV elements are integrated within ZNF genes or in the close vicinity, KZNFs are also involved in an autoregulatory loop with the aid of KAP1, suppressing ZNF gene expression [ , ]. Thus, a repressive chromatin environment is generated near the ZNF genes, which can act as an antiviral barrier, in cases for example if proviral integration of exogenous retroviruses, such as the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), occurs in these regions [ ]. Hence, KZNFs in collaboration with transposable elements are responsible for establishing a big part of epigenetic regulation in humans, one of the aims of which is the restriction of retroviral infection [ , ].

NBP (neuroblastoma breakpoint) gene family is also a group that has five of its members hosting HK2 LTRs that are expressed in NCCITs. NBPF genes consist of at least 23 members that are located primarily within duplication segments of chromosome 1 (1p36, 1p11, 1p12, 1p13 and 1q21) and have been the subject of complete or incomplete gene duplications in human evolution. There is evidence that these loci are still evolving, giving rise to variations in both structure and copy number between different individuals [ ]. The interesting and unique fact about NBPF genes is that they contain the Olduvai (or DUF1220) protein domains, the copy-number of which is directly associated with brain size and cognitive ability. In the evolutionary timeline, the Olduvai domain’s copy number skyrocketed from 1 to 9 copies in non-primate mammals to 250–350 copies in humans. This vast expansion in humans represents the largest human-specific increase in copy-number of any coding region in the genome and there are reasons to believe that Olduvai hyper-amplification in humans is responsible for turning a primate brain into a human brain [ , ]. At some point in the evolution of humans, HK2 LTRs were integrated in one of the duplicated NBPF genes in 1q21 and when further duplications occurred, more NBPF genes with integrated LTRs emerged [ ]. Mutations and copy-number variations of NBP family genes located in 1q21 have been blamed for several mostly neurological pathologies, such as neuroblastoma, schizophrenia, autism as well as cancer predisposition [ ]. Interestingly, 1q21 also hosts the 4 NBPF genes with the largest human-specific Olduvai expansions [ ]. At the same time, six NBPF genes (NBPF8, NBPF10, NBPF11, NBPF12, NBPF13P and NBPF15) have integrated HK2 LTRs [ ], five of which are located in 1q21. It has been previously supposed that only LTR-containing NBPF genes are expressed [ ] but this assumption was quickly disproved [ ] and substituted with findings that LTR-containing NBPF genes showed higher expression levels compared to the LTR-lacking ones [ ]. Thus, it seems that LTRs play a role in NBPF gene expression which as we mentioned above is directly connected with human brain function. In NCCITs, we found 33 transcripts containing parts of five out of the six LTRs (Additional File 5) included in NBPF genes (NBPF8, NBPF9, NBPF11, NBPF12 and NBPF15). All LTRs except for the LTR in NBPF15 are on the same strand as the NBPF gene. In majority of reads, the LTR serves as an alternative polyadenylation signal, resulting in the creation of transcripts that differ from the annotated NBPF transcripts and are prematurely polyadenylated. Therefore, it is possible that LTRs provide some kind of negative regulation of NBPF genes by impeding the creation of full-length transcripts. In a study of LTR activity of NBPF genes in the teratocarcinoma cell line Tera1 it was found that the NBPF LTR displayed high enhancer activity coming from the U3 region [ ].
Another group of protein-coding genes containing solo LTRs that stands out in our dataset is the SLC (solute carrier) gene family, which is a group of membrane transport proteins. We detected expression of solo LTRs within 4 SLC genes (SLC6A20, SLC25A16, SLC3A2, SLC4A8). Moreover, provirus 1p31.1 (Table ) is intronically integrated in an antisense direction inside the SLC44A5 gene. SLC is a superfamily of at least 360 carrier-mediated influx transporters of essential nutrients such as lipids, vitamins, metals, inorganic and organic anions. SLC transporters are highlighted in literature for their role in anti-retroviral drug absorption in the battle against HIV [ , ]. More interestingly, SLC carriers have been utilized by animal exogenous and endogenous retroviruses for cell entry [ – ]. In fact, all infectious animal endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) use SLCs as receptors, by binding to ERVs’ Env proteins. Thus, Env proteins of ERVs are possibly co-opted to modulate nutrient transport [ , ]. The presence of HERVs in SLC genes and the production of transcripts that we spotted in NCCITs could be an attempt to regulate “in cis” SLC expression. Out of the five above mentioned cases of intronic HERVs in SLC genes, four are situated in opposite strands. In two cases the transcript is generated from the strand where the HERV is situated, while in the other two, the transcript corresponds to the SLC strand (Additional File 5). Therefore, perhaps the presence of LTRs in the SLC genes could create transcription initiation sites used in certain circumstances in order to drive transcription away from the SLC production direction. It has been shown that HIV infection results in downregulation of some SLC transporters in the placenta of pregnant women [ ].

## Estimating the polyadenylation of the most recently active HERV family mRNAs
Polyadenylation is employed by cells during transcript maturation for the purpose of mRNA decay inhibition and elongation of half-life. An increase in mRNA stability is directly linked with higher translation yield. Moreover, poly(A) tails participate in interactions with translation initiation factors, thereby boosting initiation of translation [ ]. The length of poly(A) tails (number of adenines) usually determines the extent of transcript stability and translation efficiency [ ], however not linearly [ ]. Despite earlier beliefs that very long poly(A) tails confer greater transcript stability, recent studies have shown that in steady state, highly translated transcripts, have relatively short poly(A) tails (even as short as 30 nts). A typical average poly(A) tail length for human transcripts is now considered at about 50–100 adenines, while tails of > 250 adenines are rarely found and are commonly attached to poorly translated mRNAs [ ]. Most human polyadenylation signals include the AAUAA sequence. This sequence is characteristic also for intact HK2 LTRs, as it is situated in the R region, followed by a polyadenylation site (CA) on the border between R and U5 (Fig. A) [ , ].
During MinION’s DRS, each molecule that passes through the pore is read with a 3’ to 5’ direction, although reported in 5’ to 3’. These transcripts were poly(A)-selected, and therefore we expect any truncations happening during the RNA isolation and library preparation steps to be inevitable for 5’ ends of reads. However, any 3’ end-related truncations would lead to subsequent loss of the poly(A) tail and eventually these would not be selected during the poly(A) selection process. Thus, any 3’ end truncations will go undetected.
Long-read sequencing libraries generated using the DRS protocol enable the assessment of mRNA poly(A) tails by providing estimates of their length, expressed as an estimated number of adenines. More specifically, the ionic current events passing through the pore at the 3’ read end are measured and corrected by the read’s relative velocity [ ]. However, due to potential current abnormalities during the basecalling, we were limited to utilize a part of produced signals effectively. Out of the total 9,352 (filtered) HK2 transcripts, ~ 82% (n = 7,730 transcripts) was used to infer the tails estimations, as the rest did not meet the nanopolish-polya [ ] threshold (see Methods). We calculated the median tail length for each locus, proviral and solo LTR, based on the estimated adenines number of each locus reads. Finally, we applied the same method to the acquired transcripts of the three selected housekeeping genes (ACTB, PPIA, HPRT) so we could eventually observe the relative tail lengths distribution among the expressed loci. The range of the estimated adenines per HK2 derived proviral transcript, as depicted in Fig. , suggests that the median poly(A) tail length estimates overlap with that of the housekeeping genes, and seem independent of sample size and transcript direction. In general, the median tail length lies within the expected range for human transcripts [ ], with many of the estimations however, exceeding 100 adenines. Overall, almost half of the proviruses exhibited tails whose range lingers within the typical poly(A) tail size [ ] (Fig. , Additional File 6). In the case of solo LTRs, we do not observe a similar pattern of polyadenylation across the transcripts (Figs. and ). This group consists of read-through mRNAs transcribed entirely or partially with the polyadenylation region signal lying either within or outside the solo LTR transcriptional unit, as previously mentioned. Thus, the polyadenylation signals could lie in regions that are both gene-dependent and use the gene’s isoform poly(A) signal, as well as solo LTR locus-dependent (Additional File 5).
 A further investigation of the acquired proviral loci tail length estimates, suggests that the type I HK2 proviral tails are centered around 109 adenines, while type II relative estimates are centered around 93 adenines, thus highlighting a relatively significant difference between the two groups of transcripts that were detected within NCCITs (Fig.  , Additional File 6). We could not make any conclusion regarding the correlation between proviral LTR types and polyadenylated tails length as LTR5B and LTR5A types were not represented in our filtered results. However, in HK2 solo LTRs derived reads, where both LTR5_Hs and LTR5B were present, we observed a longer poly(A) tail in transcripts emerging from LTR5B, than LTR5_Hs, with tails around 157 and 100 adenines median length, respectively Fig.  , Additional File 6).

## Discussion
Our goal was to study the transcriptional profile of a human retroviral family not only regarding the genetic content of the transcripts, but also with respect to transcript types and structure, the splicing sites, isoforms and poly(A) tails. This integral transcriptome analysis can be materialized only through the technology provided by Oxford Nanopore DRS, which (a) abolishes the need for RNA conversion to cDNA that may result in loss of information, especially regarding post-transcriptional modifications, (b) does not utilize PCR amplification, in which case, PCR recombination may reduce the accuracy of detection of individual proviruses, as they are very similar to each other, and (c) produces long reads, which makes it possible to follow the sequence of transcripts from the 3’ end, where the sequencing begins, until the 5’ end of the transcript. However, due to inevitable RNA degradation during handling, the 5’ end of many reads (especially the longer ones) is truncated and therefore sequencing stops before reaching the actual 5’ end of the produced transcript. Thus, high-integrity input RNA is required for full-length reads. Lower throughput and yield constitute another limitation of DRS technology. Because DRS sequences native RNA without PCR amplification, it’s less scalable, i.e., fewer reads per run and slower data output. Also, higher error rates in identifying individual nucleotides compared to the short-read technology, may cause base-calling and accuracy issues. Moreover, the standard DRS protocol poses a bias toward polyadenylated RNAs, thereby non-polyadenylated RNAs, such as many noncoding RNAs and histone mRNAs, are excluded or underrepresented.
We targeted the HK2 retroviral subfamily as it is the most transcriptionally active. In general, HERVs are silenced in normal human tissues, or expressed at low levels. However, pathogenic conditions, such as viral infections and cancer, lead to HERV overexpression [ ]. Teratocarcinomas are germ-cell tumors that have retained the ability to differentiate, e.g. into trophoblastic cell lineages [ ]. Thus, it comes as no surprise that VLPs initially found to bud from human placental trophoblasts, were later discovered also in supernatants of teratocarcinoma cell lines, along with the realization that the HERV group responsible for their production, is HK2 [ ]. Thus, mRNA and proteins stemming from some HK2 integrations are highly expressed in teratocarcinomas [ ]. We therefore chose the teratocarcinoma cell line NCCIT as the source of HK2 transcripts [ ]. We found that the most abundantly expressed HERV family was HERV-H, followed by HK2. Earlier phylogenetic studies pointed at members of HERV-K and HERV-H as the most recently active retroviruses in the genomes of primates [ ]. In fact, apart from the HK2 family that carries plenty polymorphic sites [ ], there has been only a single report of another HERV family exhibiting a dimorphic locus: a HERV-H element on chr 1(1q25.3_H3) was shown to exist as proviral and solo LTR alleles in two related individuals [ ]. HK2 transcripts can be divided into two groups, the proviral transcripts and the transcripts emerging from or including solo LTRs. In both cases, transcripts can be LTR-initiated and LTR-terminated at the same time. We have observed such transcripts for relatively intact proviruses (see Results, “HK2 proviral transcripts of typical structure”) and also for solo LTRs that are in proximity with each other (Additional File 5, solo LTRs No 272 and No 273). In case of proviruses, these transcripts copy the information encoded by the provirus, either for HK2 protein synthesis, or for unknown reasons (e.g. in the case of hel transcripts). The transcript produced by solo LTRs flanking a transcribed region, comprised a transcriptional variant of a protein-coding gene (SDHAP3). The rest of the transcripts that we detected in NCCITs can be described as: LTR-initiated only, LTR-terminated only or/and read-through, where the transcript included parts of an HK2 locus, either with simultaneous initiation/termination from another HK2 locus, or not. In the last case, transcripts could have the opposite direction from the HK2 locus. In majority of these cases, the transcript is not a copy of the HK2 genes but rather of the genes surrounding the HK2 locus, and LTRs/proviruses were utilized for providing a promoter or enhancer element for transcription initiation, a polyadenylation signal, or an exon (splicing sites), to an mRNA that carries protein-coding information or a ncRNA. LTR activity may result in the creation of transcriptional variants that are (a) expressed in a specific tissue or under specific pathological (or not) conditions, (b) have an altered exon-intron structure and (c) are prematurely terminated. Creation of these variants may alter gene expression either by diminished expression of other isoforms or through RNA interference [ , ]. In our study, in most transcripts of NCCITs that included parts of solo LTRs, the solo LTR was utilized for the provision of a polyadenylation signal and site (see Additional File 5). In literature, essential human genes have been reported to co-opt polyadenylation signals of HERV loci [ ].
Another interesting aspect of the present study is the observation that HK2 loci expressed in NCCITs inhabit or flank several genes that belong to gene families of high importance for the cell function. For example, NCCIT transcripts of four ZNF genes, five SLC genes and five NBPF genes, all contain parts of HK2 loci (see section “ ”). ZNF genes are known for their role in DNA binding that drives transcriptional repression and restriction of retroviral infections, SLC genes are membrane transporters involved in cell metabolism and exploited for retroviral cell entry, while NBPF genes are responsible for human brain size and cognitive ability that distinguishes humans from other primates. These diverse but crucial genes have one thing in common: their expression is influenced by HK2 LTRs: a group of ZNF factors (KZNF) in cooperation with HK2 elements are responsible for epigenetic regulation to prevent integration of exogenous retroviruses, HK2 Env proteins possibly modulate nutrient exchange through SLC transporters and HK2 LTRs probably enhance the expression of NBPF genes. It is truly remarkable that these ancient remnants of non-human descent can have such an impact on essential gene regulation in humans.
Despite the limitations of DRS listed above, we were able to successfully distinguish the transcript isoforms produced based on their unique splicing patterns. Although in DRS 5’ end sequences of full-length HK2 transcripts are under-detected due to their length (8–9 kb) and the 5’ end truncation bias, the splicing information along with the knowledge provided from the existing literature on HK2 transcripts in teratocarcinoma cell lines [ – ], helped us not only detect and identify a plethora of different isoforms, but also distinguish the reads that are potentially parts of full-length transcripts, despite being shorter than 8-9 kb (see Table ). Moreover, long-read high-throughput sequencing is essentially useful in the case of HK2 identification and mapping due to the polymorphic nature of integrations as well as difficulties in distinguishing closely related proviral loci. Ideally, highly accurate reads exceeding 750 nucleotides are necessary for reliably quantifying HERV transcription at individual integration loci [ ]. Since high-accuracy long-read technology is yet unavailable, combination of Nanopore long-read results with Illumina low error rate results may provide the best HK2 transcripts mapping solution. Additionally, the use of the recently completed Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) reference genome could enable more accurate mapping of HK2 insertions, better discrimination of locus-specific polymorphisms, and more reliable identification of non-reference insertions in future studies since it contains a complete representation of centromeres and satellite arrays in the human genome.
The DRS protocol by Oxford Nanopore that we followed, allows for the study of post-transcriptional modifications, such as polyadenylation. For the first time, we present an estimation of the length of poly(A) tails of transcripts of endogenous retroviruses, which aids the understanding of the stability of HERV transcripts. Since we found that the number of adenines in poly(A) tails of HK2 transcripts follows a similar length distribution to that of poly(A) tails of housekeeping genes transcripts, we can reasonably assume that the stability and, therefore, the availability of HK2 transcripts for repeated translation or other usage might be equally essential/important to that of housekeeping genes. The advantages provided by Nanopore Technology in RNA-seq can be exploited up to the point of HERV epi-transcriptomics studies. Apart from poly(A) tail and cap structure, over 150 epigenetic modifications can be added to mRNAs, including N6,2′-O-dimethyladenosine (m6Am), N6-methyladenosine (m6A), and pseudouridine (Ψ). These modifications play a crucial role in regulating RNA stability, availability, translation and decay.

## Conclusions
To our knowledge, this is the first direct study of transcripts of human endogenous retroviruses that paves the way for similar studies of other HERV families or in other cells and tissues. Since HERVs are implicated in many pathological conditions including cancer, their transcripts and proteins are utilized in attempts to generate therapeutic treatments and even to selectively target cancer cells [ – , , ].

## Supplementary Materials
Supplementary Material 1: Additional File 1: Supporting information in the form of a .docx file that includes the sections: “Identifying the human endogenous retrovirus (HERV)-K HML-2 (HK2) centromeric transcription”, “Functional Annotation Supplementary Database”, “Orthogonal validation of non-canonically spliced isoforms”, “Coding potential of canonically and non-canonically spliced isoforms”, “Transcripts generated from highly mutated proviral HK2 genomes”, as well as supplementary Figures S1 to S10.
Supplementary Material 2: Additional File 2: Per-experiment analysis summary tables. This file includes: (i) per-run quality control and alignment statistics, (ii) detailed HERV read-assignment metrics, and (iii) summaries of poly(A) tail length distributions captured in each experiment using the same computational workflow. The per-run poly(A) tail length distributions are shown together in Figure S3.
Supplementary Material 3: Additional File 3: HERV-H element expression in NCCIT cells. This table lists HERV-H loci detected as transcriptionally active in the NCCIT pooled sample. Sense (s1) and antisense (s2) transcription are reported separately, and only loci supported by at least three reads per direction are included.
Supplementary Material 4: Additional File 4: HK2 proviral expression within the pooled NCCIT sample. We present the read counts of each expressed proviral locus found in our sample, by categorizing the proviral expression activity as “s1” and “s2”, to refer to transcripts that are produced either with the same or opposite orientation to each locus annotated direction, respectively. Entries given in bold are highlighting proviruses with bidirectional activity (transcripts occurring at both sense "s1" and antisense "s2" directionality in regards to the proviral locus annotation). Expression analysis results are accompanied by LTR type information, as well as age estimates and a functional annotation of the region around the HK2 proviral loci, with an extension of 10 kb flanking both proviral sequence boundaries.
Supplementary Material 5: Additional File 5: HK2 solo LTRs expression activity in the pooled NCCIT sample. Expression analysis results are presented based on the direction of the produced transcripts per active solo LTR locus. “s1” results refer to reads produced with the same direction to each locus annotated direction, while “s2” results refer to reads of the opposite orientation. Solo LTR entries in bold highlight bidirectional expression activity (both “s1” and “s2” reads). Also, we are providing LTR type information per locus, and a functional annotation of the region around HK2 solo LTR loci (10 kb extension of the solo LTR genomic coordinates). Each expressed solo LTR is categorized as following: [i] in italics: soloLTRs transcribed from the opposite strand, in bold: the loci that are showing indication of expression in both directions, [ii] based on phylogeny and shared nucleotide features, [iii] for LTRs that are situated inside of a gene or inside of an annotated ncRNA. In parenthesis is the strand where the gene is situated. ncRNAs are denoted with an asterisk, [iv] LTRs used as polyadenylation signals are marked with P/A; R/T (read-through) is used for LTRs that are part of a transcript that begins elsewhere and extends beyond the end of the LTR; INI (initiation) is used for reads putatively originating from the LTR: in Direct RNA Sequencing of Oxford Nanopore, sequencing starts at the 3’ end of a polyadenylated transcript and continues to the 5’ end. However, during the isolation and processing of RNA and also during the library preparation, frequently reads are being truncated and we cannot be sure for the exact 5’ end of a read. Thus, INI transcripts could be in fact R/T transcripts.
Supplementary Material 6: Additional File 6: Summary of the poly(A) tails length estimation results of the HK2 elements. The file lists all tested HK2 elements that passed the filtering threshold described in Methods, based on which median tail length estimates were calculated. For each locus, we provide information transcripts’ orientation (sense[s1]/antisense[s2]), integration position, LTR type for both flanking proviral LTRs and Solo integrations, provirus type (I/II) and the percent (%) of the reads that passed our filtering (based on total reads of each locus). Descriptive statistics include the average, median, Q1, Q3 and IQR of poly(A) tails length. All estimates’ values are given in adenine (‘A’) nucleotides (nts). In addition, results for selected housekeeping genes (ACTB, PPIA, HPRT1) are included for comparison as reference points.
