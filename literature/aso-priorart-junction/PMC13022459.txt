To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/.Human/mouse artificial chromosomes (HACs/MACs) serve as gene vectors for transferring of megabase DNA sequences and multiple gene cassettes into mammalian cells. However, their full potential is hindered by the lack of effective gene-loading methods. In this study, we developed a fragment assembly (FA) system that enables reconstitution of introduced sequences in HACs/MACs by simultaneously loading multiple gene-loading vectors (GLVs) while removing the GLV vector backbone sequences. As a proof of principle, we simultaneously loaded three GLVs, which encode the promoter, open reading frame of a gene, and a poly A signal sequence, onto a MAC carrying a gene-loading pad for the FA system. We successfully obtained clones bearing a reconstituted gene expression cassette on the MAC. Further introduction of three GLVs in two gene-loading steps enabled the reconstitution of another gene expression cassette in the MAC. The gene-loaded MAC was transferable to other cells, and the introduced cells expressed the reconstituted genes. These data demonstrate that the FA system is useful for reconstituting multiple sequences in HACs/MACs.

## Introduction
Human or mouse artificial chromosomes (HACs/MACs) have been used to introduce large genomic sequences, including immunoglobulin genes, dystrophin locus, and Cyp gene cluster – . HACs/MACs are also useful for introducing multiple gene expression cassettes into the target cells. For instance, Hiratsuka et al. introduced multiple copies of Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and Myc expression cassettes and a p53 shRNA expression unit into HAC to construct iPSC-inducing HAC . They employed a P1-derived artificial chromosome (PAC) to load all gene expression cassettes, as only one gene-loading vector (GLV) can be introduced into the HAC using the traditional gene-loading method. Although this strategy successfully constructed an iPSC-inducing HAC, the construction of such a large GLV was technically challenging and time-consuming. Moreover, the insert size that can be introduced into HACs/MACs is limited to approximately 200 kb due to the PAC capacity. To overcome this problem, several gene-loading systems have been developed. Yamaguchi et al.. have reported a multi-integrase system that employed five types of recombinases/integrases for gene loading . This system allows up to five gene-loading events on the HACs/MACs. A drawback of this system is that it requires different drug-resistance genes for each gene-loading step. Since several drug resistance genes are required for the construction of HACs/MACs, it is challenging to allocate five different drug resistance genes for gene loading. Utilization of the double-Flp-in method allows the simultaneous introduction of two GLVs using Flp recombinase . However, this system requires the same number of drug resistance genes as the gene-loading vectors, making it difficult to introduce more GLVs, similar to the multi-integrase system. Tóth et al. developed a gene-loading system that employed Cre/loxP to remove drug-resistance genes . This system enables the repeated use of a drug-resistant gene for gene loading. However, an extra cloning step is required to isolate clones with removed drug-resistant genes. Furthermore, the system leaves a loxP sequence after the removal of the drug-resistance gene, which could cause unintended deletion of the introduced sequences when removing the drug-resistance gene using Cre in the latter gene-loading steps. To overcome these limitations, we previously developed Simultaneous or sequential Integration of Multiple gene-loading vector (SIM) system . The SIM system allows the simultaneous loading of up to three GLVs into HACs/MACs up to two times. Unlimited sequential gene loading is also possible with the SIM system because the integration of the GLVs traps the promoter activity of a drug-resistance gene used in the preceding gene loading. These features greatly enhance the utility of HACs/MACs, and the SIM system has been used in various studies – . The remaining issue with the system is the cointegration of the vector backbone sequences of GLVs with the insert sequences. The vector backbone sequences interfere with the reconstitution of the GLV insert sequences on the HACs/MACs. Thus, the SIM system is not suitable for building large synthetic sequences via the reconstitution of multiple fragments. Utilization of the recombinase-mediated cassette exchange (RMCE) method allows for the integration of only the desired sequences; however, it can only introduce a single gene .
In this study, we have developed an advanced gene-loading system, the fragment-assembly (FA) system, which allows simultaneous gene-loading of up to three GLVs repeatedly, while removing the vector backbone sequences of the GLVs.

## Results
GLVs of the FA system were designed to enable assembly of DNA fragments on HACs/MACs using φC31 and Bxb1 integrases in combination with Cre and Flp recombinases (Figs. and ). The FA system comprised three gene-loading steps. In the first step, φC31 integrase is used for gene loading (Figs. A and B). Previous reports have shown that φC31 and Bxb1 integrases induce recombination between their respective attP and attB sequences when the central dinucleotides of the att sequences are matched , . For instance, attP, which has a TT central dinucleotide, has been shown to selectively recombined with attB, which also has a TT central dinucleotide, resulting in the generation of the attL and attR sequences. Employing this feature, attP and attB pairs with different combinations of central dinucleotides have been used to connect individual GLVs and simultaneously load up to three GLVs onto HACs/MACs. We named the att sites with TT, CT, or CC central dinucleotides attP1/attB1, attP2/attB2, and attP3/attB3, respectively (Fig. C). In the first gene-loading step, mediated by φC31 integrase, the gene of interest (GOI, insert sequence of a GLV) was assembled and the vector backbone sequences of the GLVs were designed to be incorporated into the intron region of the reconstituted neo-resistance genes (Fig. B). The sequences between the 5′ and 3′ portions of the neo-resistance gene are spliced out from the transcript, thus allowing the neo-resistance gene to be expressed. The vector backbone sequences of GLV1s and the 3′ portion of the neo-resistance gene are flanked by FRT sequences after the gene loading. Bxb1 integrase and Flp were used for the second gene loading. The Bxb1 integrase mediates gene loading and GLV connection similarly to φC31 integrase in the first gene loading step, whereas Flp is employed to remove the vector backbone sequence of GLVs used for the initial gene loading. Flp-mediated recombination also deletes the 3′ portion of the neo-resistance gene that allows switching of drug-resistance from G418-resistance to blasticidin (BS)-resistance upon second gene loading (Supplementary Fig. 1). Similarly, φC31 integrase and Cre were used for the third gene-loading step (Supplementary Fig. 2). Subsequently, reciprocal usage of φC31 integrase/Cre and Bxb1 integrase/Flp allowed iterative gene loading (Fig. A).
To establish this system, we first introduced a gene-loading pad for the FA system to a MAC via homologous recombination and generated FAMAC in DT40 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). FAMAC was then transferred to CHO cells for gene loading. Integration of the gene-loading pad and transfer of FAMAC to CHO cells were confirmed using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) (Supplementary Fig. 3).
To test the concept of FA system, we attempted gene loading three times to reconstitute two gene expression cassettes by assembling six fragments (Fig.  ). In the first step, three GLVs carrying the EF1 promoter with an insulator sequence (HS4), coding sequence of the red fluorescence gene mRuby and HA-tagged RNA ligase Rtcb connected with the P2A sequence (designated as R2R), or poly A sequence with HS4, were simultaneously introduced into FAMAC (Figs.  and ). The neo-resistance gene was reconstituted when the three GLVs were correctly assembled on FAMAC. We considered that the 2.4 kb-long plasmid backbone sequence of each GLV, which accumulated in the intron of the neo-resistance gene, may interfere with the expression of the neo-resistance gene. Thus, we also attempted to use in vitro amplified bacterial replication origin (OriC)-based GLVs. with an approximately 400 bp short vector backbone sequence. Consequently, we obtained correctly introduced clones of both GLV types, as verified by genomic PCR (Fig.  A and B; Table  ). More drug-resistant colonies were detected when using OriC-based GLVs compared to plasmid-based GLVs. This could be due to the higher transcription of the neo-resistance gene resulting from the short intron sequence and the higher transfection efficiency attributable to the smaller size of the GLVs. The isolated clones expressed mRuby- and HA-tagged RTCB proteins (Fig.  C and D). The loading of GLVs into FAMAC was further confirmed using FISH (Fig.  E and F). We also analyzed the stability of both gene expression and MAC retention at passage 6. The FISH analysis revealed that 20 of 21 analyzed cells (95.2%) retained their MAC. Consistently, 96.13% of the MACTS-GLV1/CHO cells expressed mRuby, as detected by flow cytometry (Fig.  G). These data demonstrate the stability of both MAC retention and gene expression. We selected a clone carrying the gene cassette reconstituted with the plasmid-based GLVs for further experiments.

 

We next attempted the second gene loading of a GLV carrying a poly A sequence, followed by the third gene loading of GLVs encoding the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) ORF and the EF1 promoter with HS4 to reconstitute an EGFP expression cassette. We successfully isolated clones carrying the inserted sequences on FAMAC, as verified by genomic PCR (Fig. A and B). The precise excision of the vector backbones was confirmed by junction-spanning sequencing of FAMAC-GLV2 (Supplementary Fig. 4). The isolated clone expressed both mRuby and EGFP after the third round of gene loading (Fig. C and D). We also analyzed the switching in drug sensitivity (Fig. E). As expected, the CHO cells carrying empty FAMAC were sensitive to both G418 and BS. CHO cells became G418-resistant/BS-sensitive after introduction of GLV1s. Drug sensitivity was then switched to G418-sensitive/BS-resistant upon introduction of GLV2 and returned to G418-resistant/BS-sensitive after introduction of GLV3s. Taken together, these results have clearly demonstrated that synthetic sequences split into multiple pieces can be reconstituted on the FAMAC using an FA system. To verify that the gene-loaded FAMAC was transferable, FAMAC with two gene expression cassettes (FAMAC-GLV3) was transferred from CHO cells to NIH3T3 cells (Fig. A). We successfully obtained a G418-resistant clone expressing EGFP-, mRuby-, and HA-tagged RTCB (Fig. B-D). FISH analysis revealed that the NIH3T3 clone carried FAMAC-GLV3 (Fig. E). In order to validate whether RTCB expressed from FAMAC-GLV3 is enzymatically functional, we investigated the ER-stress induced generation of spliced Xbp1 mRNA (Xbp1s). Xbp1 mRNA splicing is dependent on the RNA ligase activity of RTCB , . We established endogenous Rtcb-disrupted NIH3T3 cells carrying FAMAC-GLV3 using an endogenous Rtcb gene-specific CRISPR/Cas9 system that did not recognize Rtcb on FAMAC (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B). Rtcb KO NIH3T3 cells harboring FAMAC-GLV3 showed robust generation of Xbp1s mRNA upon tunicamycin-induced ER stress (Supplementary Fig. 5C). These data demonstrated that gene-loaded FAMAC is transferable to other cells and can induce the expression of functional genes.

 

 

 

 

 

## Discussion
In this study, we developed a new gene-loading system, the FA system, which enables the reconstitution of DNA fragments on HACs/MACs, together with the removal of vector backbone sequences of GLVs. Reconstitution of the gene cassettes is not possible with the previously developed multi-integrase system or SIM system because both methods integrate insert sequences along with the vector backbone sequences of GLVs, which remain between the insert sequences of GLVs. This feature is highly advantageous for constructing HACs/MACs carrying long synthetic sequences by reconstituting short fragments. Therefore, the FA system may be useful for constructing artificial genomes to confer special functions to the cells, for example, high protein expression or enhanced cellular functions. Although the FA system leaves small att sequences among the insert sequences, the impact can be minimized by appropriately dividing the introduced sequences, as demonstrated in this study. Even with the advantage of the FA system, the gene-loading efficiency of the plasmid-based GLVs was comparable to that previously reported for the SIM system and simHDR system , . Our GLVs were designed to contain either two attP sites or two attB sites in order to prevent unintended intra-GLV recombination between the attP and attB sites with different central dinucleotides, which could lead to the elimination of insert sequences. Although we did not systematically investigate the impact of specific att site pairings, our successful isolation of correct clones using various combinations, as shown in Table , suggests that the att site assignment is flexible.
We demonstrated that OriC-based GLVs are available for the FA system. OriC-based vectors can accommodate hundreds of kb of DNA fragments, whereas the capacity of the plasmid vector is limited to a sequence of approximately 10 kb. The short backbone sequence of the OriC-based vector is another advantage for reconstituting megabase-sized insert sequences. Bacterial artificial chromosome or PAC vector can also carry hundreds of kb of sequences, but the vector backbone sequences of PAC/BAC are 8–12 kb long, which could interfere with the expression of the drug-resistant gene for selection by extending the intron sequence, especially when multiple PAC/BAC-based GLVs are introduced via the FA system. Moreover, the long stretch of the vector backbone sequences may not be efficiently deleted by Cre or Flp during the gene-loading step of the FA system. Therefore, OriC-based GLV utilization may be advantageous for reconstituting large DNA sequences on HACs/MACs using the FA system for future applications. We recently reported a novel gene-loading system (simHDR) that employs homology-directed repair in homologous recombination-proficient DT40 cells . This system enables simultaneous gene loading of up to four fragments and reconstitution of the insert sequences as the FA system. Thus, simHDR may be particularly advantageous for HAC/MAC construction, which requires the introduction of numerous GLVs through the iterative applications of the method. However, this system requires DT40 cells that exhibit poor transfection efficiency and chromosomal transferability. In contrast, the FA system can be used for any cell type, including CHO and A9 cells, which are known for efficient microcell formation. Moreover, when utilizing the simHDR method for synthetic genome construction requiring simultaneous loading of GLVs, it is essential to ensure that the homologous arms for each GLV lack extensive sequence similarity (e.g., sequences from transposons or repetitive elements) to prevent unintended recombination events. In contrast, the FA system does not require such considerations, because it utilizes integrases rather than homologous recombination for gene loading. Consequently, the FA system offers greater flexibility in the experimental design. These gene-loading systems should be selected according to the application.
Theoretically, the FA system can introduce six fragments simultaneously by using the possible central dinucleotide combinations of att sequences. However, we were unable to introduce more than three GLVs simultaneously using the FA system. This could be attributed to the low recombination efficiency, as we obtained only approximately 15 clones per screening, even with three GLVs (Table ). Improvements of the system are expected to enable the assembly of larger or more complex synthetic sequences. The incorporation of advanced integrases offers significant prospects in order to overcome this problem. Specifically, Durrant et al. recently reported the systematic discovery of recombinases, including Pa01, that exhibit significantly higher activity in mammalian cells . Integrating these integrases into the FA system enables the loading of more than four GLVs in a single round, thereby substantially accelerating the construction of complex artificial genomes.
Although the FA system was developed for gene loading of HACs/MACs, it can be applied to any genomic locus if the gene-loading pad of the FA system is inserted in advance. Therefore, the FA system is valuable for modifying genomic sequences in mammalian cells via the reconstitution of multiple DNA fragments.
The FA system enables the removal of vector backbone sequences of GLVs and the reconstitution of multiple DNA fragments. This crucial capability allows the construction of complex genetic circuits and biosynthetic pathways, thereby advancing the field of synthetic biology. Furthermore, this unique feature enables applications that are challenging with conventional gene delivery methods, such as multigene introduction to manipulate cellular functions and engineering high-performance artificial genomes for pharmaceutical cell factories to produce therapeutic proteins.

## Methods

## Vector construction
The gene-loading pad for the FA system was synthesized and cloned into the pMA vector (Invitrogen). The gene-loading pad sequence digested with SalI and AscI was inserted into the XhoI and AscI sites of the VH21-12 vector (VH21-12-FA) . The splicing donor and acceptor sequences of GLVs and the gene-loading pad were derived from intron 4 of the human DDX1 gene, which has a typical exon-intron organization without any reported splicing variants. 5′ and 3′ neo-cassettes encode 1–115 and 116–267 amino acid residues of the neomycin resistance protein, respectively. 5′ and 3′ BS cassettes encode 1–95 and 96–132 amino acid residues of BS-resistance protein, respectively. Bxb1 and φC31 att sequences and their derivatives used in this study are shown in Fig. C.

## FAMAC construction
DT40 cells harboring MAC were electroporated with linearized VH21-12-FA, as previously described , and selected with 1500 µg/mL hygromycin to make FAMAC. Hygromycin-resistant clones were analyzed by PCR using the primers listed in Table . Nine out of twelve fast-growing clones were PCR-positive. FAMAC was then transferred from DT40 cells to CHO cells via microcell-mediated chromosome transfer (MMCT) as described previously . CHO cells carrying the FAMAC were selected with 500 µg/mL hygromycin and 10 µM ouabain. PCR analysis confirmed that 21 out of 24 rapidly proliferating drug-resistant clones were PCR-positive for FAMAC and negative for the DT40 genome, as determined using primers listed in Table .

## Preparation of OriC-based GLVs
OriC-based GLVs were prepared using the OriCiro Cell-Free Cloning System (OriCiro Genomics, currently renamed Moderna Enzymatics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and a previous study . Briefly, an insert sequence of GLV was PCR-amplified using each GLV plasmid as a template and primer pairs with overlapping ends containing the OriC cassette (378 bp, OriCiro Genomics), and then assembled with the OriC cassette. The assembled circular DNA was subsequently amplified in vitro using replication cycle reaction (RCR).

## Gene-loading via the FA system
GLVs and integrase/recombinase expression vectors were transfected using Polyethylenimine “Max” (Polysciences) to CHO cells harboring FAMAC. Plasmid DNA mixture containing GLV1(3 − 1), GLV1(3 − 2), GLV1(3–3) and ΦC31 integrase expression vector in a ratio of 4.5:3:1.5:1 was transfected for the simultaneous loading of three GLVs. We employed the GLV ratio as previously reported for the SIM system . GLVs were co-transfected with Bxb1 integrase/Flp recombinase and ΦC31 integrase/Cre recombinase expression vectors for the second and third gene loadings, respectively. The loading of GLVs was confirmed via genomic PCR using the primers listed in Table . The expression of fluorescent genes was analyzed using a FACSAria III (BD Bioscience) and an inverted fluorescent microscope IX71 equipped with a CCD camera DP72 (Olympus). The flow cytometry data were processed using FCS Express Flow Cytometry RUO version 6 (DeNovo Software, https://denovosoftware.com/). Protein expression of RTCB with a C-terminal HA tag was detected via western blotting using an anti-HA rat monoclonal antibody (Merck).

## Cell culture
DT40 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% chicken serum, 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol. To maintain MAC without the gene-loading pad of FA system, 0.3 µg/mL puromycin was added to the medium. CHO cells were cultured in Ham’s F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. NIH3T3 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. Chromosome transfer via ecotropic envelope-assisted microcell-mediated chromosome transfer (ecoMMCT) was performed as previously described . DT40 cellsand CHO cells were obtained from RIKEN BioResource Research Center, and NIH3T3 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection.

## Functional analysis of RTCB

Rtcb-disrupted NIH3T3 cells carrying FAMAC-GLV3 were generated by transfection of a modified PX458a CRISPR/Cas9 vector targeting the endogenous Rtcb gene . The gRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table . The transfected cells expressing the plasmid-encoded fluorescent protein ametrine were sorted using FACSAriaIII, and individual clones were randomly isolated. In order to verify Rtcb disruption, the genomic region spanning the CRISPR/Cas9 target site was amplified by PCR and the amplicon sequence was analyzed by sequencing using the primers listed in Table . Xbp1 splicing was induced by treating the cells with 10 µg/ml tunicamycin for 4 h. After treatment, total RNA was purified using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed as described previously using the primers listed in Table . Relative Xbp1s expression was determined using the standard curve method, with Gapdh as the normalization control.

## FISH analysis
FISH analysis was performed as previously described . FAMAC in DT40 cells or CHO cells was detected using digoxigenin-labeled mouse Cot-1 DNA and biotin-labeled pMA-FA. Biotin-labeled insert sequences of GLVs were used to detect GLV1s loading. Cy3-labeled probes for mouse major satellite sequence or mouse Cot-1 DNA were used to identify mouse chromosomes. Chromosomal DNA was visualized by 4´,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining. Images were captured using a microscope AxioImager Z2 (Carl Zeiss) or BZ-X700 (Keyence).
