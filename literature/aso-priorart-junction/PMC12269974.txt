## Summary
The total burden of rare diseases is significant worldwide, with over 300 million people being affected. Many rare diseases have both well-defined clinical phenotypes and established genetic causes. However, a remarkable proportion of patients with high clinical suspicion of a rare disease remain genetically undiagnosed and stuck in the diagnostic odyssey after having a cascade of conventional genetic tests. One of the major factors contributing to this is that many types of variants are technically intractable to whole-exome sequencing (WES). In this study, the added diagnostic power of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) for patients with clinically suspected rare diseases was assessed by detecting technically challenging variants. 3,169 patients from the Hong Kong Genome Project (HKGP) were reviewed, identifying 322 individuals having high clinical suspicion of a rare disorder with well-established genetic etiology. Notably, 180 patients have performed at least one previous genetic test. Through PCR-free short-read WGS and a comprehensive in-house analytic pipeline, causative variants were found in 138 patients (138 of 322, 42.9%), 30 of which (30 of 138, 21.7%) are attributed to technically challenging variants. These included 6 variants in low-coverage regions with PCR bias, 2 deep intronic variants, 2 repeat expansions, 19 structural variants, and 2 variants in genes with a homologous pseudogene. The study demonstrated the indispensable diagnostic power of WGS in detecting technically challenging variants and the capability to serve as an all-in-one test for patients with high clinical suspicion of rare diseases.

This study evaluates the added diagnostic yield of WGS in a diverse cohort of individuals with a high clinical suspicion of a rare disorder. Our findings demonstrate how WGS enables the detection of variants that are difficult for conventional genetic tests, underscoring its role as an all-in-one diagnostic tool.

## Introduction
Rare diseases impose a huge burden on global healthcare, collectively affecting over 300 million individuals worldwide. Despite rapid medical advancement, patients with rare diseases often face a prolonged diagnostic journey, with many remaining undiagnosed for decades. This challenge stems partly from an incomplete understanding of genetic mechanisms and technological limitations in detecting certain variants. With the broad spectrum of genetic variation in the human genome, some variants pose challenges in detection, such as structural variants, repeat expansion, and deep intronic variants. , 
Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) offers a promising solution by overcoming these limitations through enhanced sensitivity and specificity in identifying technically challenging variants. , , Multiple studies have shown the superiority of WGS over conventional methods, such as karyotyping, chromosome microarray analysis (CMA), multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), and even whole-exome sequencing (WES), in detecting technically challenging variants. , , , , , Furthermore, meta-analyses have also revealed that WGS achieved the highest pooled diagnostic yield compared to CMA and WES. , 
Recognizing the potential and importance of WGS in the development and advancement of genomic medicine, the Hong Kong Genome Project (HKGP) was launched in 2021 to cater to the diagnostic needs of patients. The HKGP employs Illumina PCR-free short-read WGS coupled with multiple bioinformatics tools, including DRAGEN and Exomiser, for variant identification and prioritization. In the pilot phase analysis of the first 520 patients with undiagnosed diseases or hereditary cancer, the HKGP has achieved a 28% diagnostic yield, in which many of the identified variants were non-coding variants, copy-number variants (CNVs), and structural variants (SVs). In parallel to the genome sequencing study of the HKGP, several studies have been published to showcase the added diagnostic yield using WGS. Most of them have focused on specific disorders (e.g., inherited retinal disorder) to show the ability of WGS to detect variants missed by conventional genetic tests. , , , However, large-scale investigations into the added diagnostic utility of WGS on technically difficult-to-detect variants for clinically heterogeneous cohorts remain limited. , , , 
This study aimed to evaluate the added diagnostic yield of WGS in a diverse cohort of patients with clinically suspected rare diseases, focusing on variants that are technically difficult to detect. From 3,169 HKGP patients, 322 patients with strong clinical suspicion of rare diseases were selected. The diagnostic efficacy of WGS was systemically assessed.

## Subjects and methods

## Patient selection
Patients were recruited through three partnering centers of the Hong Kong Genome Institute (HKGI) into the HKGP, as described previously. A total of 3,169 HKGP affected individuals covering undiagnosed rare diseases and hereditary cancers were reviewed, and 322 individuals were selected based on the presence of high clinical suspicion of a rare disorder with well-established genetic etiology at the time of referral in which the patient’s clinical findings, imaging results, biochemical profile, histological findings, and/or family histories were compatible with the clinical diagnostic criteria of the disease. Patients suspected of rare diseases with high genetic heterogeneity (e.g., developmental delay, epilepsy, and retinitis pigmentosa) or those lacking known genetic associations were excluded ( ). 
Human phenotype ontology (HPO) terms were used to record patients’ clinical phenotypes. To standardize the clinical input of the patients, the HKGI offers a set of alignment-of-practice and training sessions to genetic counselors in the three partnering centers. Detailed clinical information, including the clinical characteristics, family history, previous genetic tests, and other investigations done by the patients, was summarized using HPO terms by the well-trained genetic counselors. These HPO terms were used as the input for Exomiser in the variant prioritization process.

## Ethics statement
Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at pre-test counseling sessions and ethics approvals were obtained from the Central Institutional Review Board (IRB) (HKGP-2021-001 and HKGP2022-001) and the IRBs of the Department of Health (L/M 257/2021), Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong New Territories East Cluster (2021·423 and 2023·120), and the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (UW 21-413 and UW 23-289).

## WGS
WGS was performed according to the HKGI optimized protocol as previously described. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted, and 1 μg DNA was enzymatically fragmented. PCR-free libraries were constructed using the KAPA HyperPlus Kit (Kapa Biosystems) following the protocol from the manufacturer. Size selection was performed using KAPA HyperPure Beads (Roche) to obtain a final library range of 400–700 bp. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 or NovaSeq X Plus sequencer, achieving ≥30× mean coverage, >400 bp mean insert size, and >15× depth for ≥95% of the GRCh38 reference genome.

## Variant calling and prioritization
Base calling, sequence mapping, and variant calling were performed as previously described. In brief, base calling was performed using DRAGEN (v.4.1.5), and reads were aligned against Homo sapiens (human) genome assembly GRCh38 (hg38) using BWA (v.0.7.17). Duplicate reads were removed using Picard (v.2.27.4). 
Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions or deletions (indels) were detected using GATK (v.4.2.6.1). CNVs larger than 25 kb were called with CNVkit (v.0.9.9). Small deletions, duplications, and insertions were detected using Manta (v.1.6.0), SurVIndel, and INSurveyor (v.1.1.0). In addition, inversions and translocations were called using Manta (v.1.6.0). Expansions of short tandem repeats (STRs) in 31 targeted loci were identified via ExpansionHunter (v.3.1.2) ( ). For genes with high-homology pseudogenes, targeted callers in DRAGEN, such as the CYP21A2 caller, were used to detect SNVs and indels.
The resulting VCF files were annotated and filtered using an in-house bioinformatics pipeline. SNVs and indels with a minor-allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 1% in any of the control populations in Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD: v.2.1.1, v.3.1.2, and v.4.1.0) were filtered. For CNVs/SVs, the allele frequencies were determined and filtered based on the Database of Genomic Variants (DGV), gnomAD SV (v.2.1), and an in-house Hong Kong healthy population cohort, with an MAF ≥ 1% considered as benign.
Retained SNVs were prioritized by Exomiser (v.12.1.0) according to the similarity between the phenotypes of the patient and the diseases associated with the affected genes. They were then filtered by gene panels related to the clinical suspicion in the Genomics England PanelApp and the PanelApp Australia. Missense variants with a REVEL score ≥ 0.644 and intronic variants with at least one SpliceAI delta score ≥ 0.2, in which PP3 can be applied, were further prioritized for evaluation. SVs/CNVs, on the other hand, were prioritized based on their potential for disrupting the expression of target genes mentioned in the above gene panels. Deletions, duplications, and insertions affecting only promoter regions, 5′ UTRs, or 3′ UTRs were removed. Deletions and duplications larger than 25 kb that were only called by Manta but not by CNVkit were removed. For inversion and translocation events, the breakpoint junctions were annotated, and only those having at least one breakpoint junction residing in the transcribed region (exons and introns) of target genes were retained. For repeat expansions, events that failed the LowDepth filter with an overall locus depth below 10× or a number of reads that span one or both breakends below 5 were removed. Only repeat calls with a repeat size larger than the normal range and that passed the LowDepth filter were analyzed.
SNVs, small indels, and intragenic CNVs were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines and recommendations from the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) Working Group. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings were organized to discuss and refine the classification of complex variants.

## Variant confirmation
Details of the validation methods were described previously. In brief, pathogenic SNVs and indels were validated by Sanger sequencing, while CNVs and SVs were validated by long-read sequencing.

## Results

## Cohort characteristics
Among 3,169 Hong Kong patients recruited to the HKGP between July 2021 and December 2023, a cohort of 322 probands (149 females and 173 males) was selected based on strong clinical suspicion of rare diseases with well-established genetic etiologies. Primary clinical indications were diverse, with renal disorders (20.8%), neurodevelopmental disorders (17.4%), and neurological disorders (10.9%) being the most common ( ). On average, pediatric and adult patients in the selected cohort had 6.2 ± 4.4 and 12.6 ± 10.6 years of diagnostic odyssey before joining the HKGP, respectively. Notably, 55.9% of patients (180 of 322) have undergone at least one prior genetic test. In this study, disease-causing variants that are difficult or impossible to detect by WES were defined as technically challenging. They include SNVs/indels located within poorly covered regions in WES, intronic variants located >50 bp from the exon-intron junction, repeat expansions, structural variants with lengths larger than 50 bp, and variants in genes with pseudogenes sharing high sequence similarity ( ). Following the abovementioned selection criteria, we identified that 30 of 138 positive results (21.7%) harbored technically challenging variants ( ). 

## Identification of diseasing-causing variants
A robust WGS workflow was developed to address technically challenging variants. PCR-free libraries were used to minimize PCR bias, ensure uniform coverage, and improve read alignment. Short-read WGS was performed with a mean insert size of >400 bp and a mean coverage of ≥30× to allow better detection of CNVs and SVs. Multiple callers were integrated to maximize the detection sensitivity of different types of variants. Phenotype-driven variant prioritization was performed using Exomiser, followed by further filtering using gene panels. All candidate variants were classified according to the most updated ACMG guidelines and ClinGen recommendations by well-trained HKGI curators. The genetic causes in 138 of 322 patients were successfully identified, and a diagnostic yield of 42.9% was achieved. The majority of these 30 patients (20 of 30, 66.7%) had prior inconclusive genetic testing. The median number of tests performed was 2, with targeted sequencing (14 of 30, 46.7%) and WES (11 of 30, 36.7%) being the most common ( ). 

## Overview of technically challenging variants
Among the 31 technically challenging variants detected in the 30 patients, 6 resided in low-coverage regions of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), PKD1, and PKD2. Two deep intronic variants in NF1 and two pathogenic repeat expansions in ATXN2 and HTT were identified. Most of the technically challenging variants (19/31, 64.5%) were CNVs/SVs affecting a variety of genes. In addition, two variants in CYP21A2 were uniquely resolved by WGS due to the existence of the highly similar pseudogene CYP21A1P.

## Enhanced detection of variants in low-coverage regions with PCR bias
SNVs were prioritized by comparing the similarity between the patient’s phenotype and the known phenotype of diseased genes using Exomiser. Variants affecting genes associated with the patient’s clinical suspicion were additionally screened for curation if they were in the top 10 of the ranked list. The algorithm prioritized three variants in NF1, two variants in PKD1, and one variant in PKD2 as the top candidates of disease-causing variants in six affected individuals. These variants were located in a low-coverage region with PCR bias in which five variants were in exonic regions and one was in intronic region of PKD2. Of note, 80% (4 of 5) of the exonic variants were located within 15 bases from the exon boundary. For instance, in patient P5, the PKD1 variant c.341_345del (GenBank: NM_001009944.3) (p.Leu114Ter) identified was in a region where coverage over 20× was only observed in less than 50% of individuals in the exome data from the gnomAD v.4.1.0 ( A). The PCR-free library construction protocol removed genomic coverage bias associated with PCR amplification , ( B). 

## Detection of deep intronic variants
Deep intronic variants that are predicted to affect splicing by SpliceAI (any delta score ≥ 0.2) in the disease-associated genes were also prioritized for analysis. Two deep intronic variants in NF1 were detected.

## NF1
Patient P7 had a clinical diagnosis of NF1 (MIM: 162200), and WGS detected a heterozygous variant c.5812+332A>G (GenBank: NM_001042492.3) (p.?) in intron 39 of NF1. It was also confirmed to occur de novo in the patient. The variant was reported as pathogenic by multiple submitters in ClinVar (VCV000216065). RNA study revealed that the variant created a strong cryptic splice donor, which led to the inclusion of a cryptic exon that disrupted the reading frame and reduced the expression of NF1. , 
Patient P8 was also clinically diagnosed with NF1. He had multiple café-au-lait spots over his body and limbs, bilateral axillary freckles, and a strong family history ( A). WGS analysis identified a monoallelic variant c.1392+751T>G (GenBank: NM_001042492.3) (p.?) in the patient and his affected father and brother ( B). This variant has not been reported on ClinVar or in the literature. SpliceAI predicted it to activate a cryptic splice acceptor in intron 12, which leads to the inclusion of 750 bp of intronic 12 ( C). This leads to the introduction of a premature stop codon ( C). The co-segregation of the variant with the disease further supported its classification as likely pathogenic (PP4_Strong, PP1_Moderate, PP3, and PM2_Supporting). 

## Repeat expansion called by ExpansionHunter
In parallel to the SNV analysis, the STR called by ExpansionHunter was analyzed, and two pathogenic expansions in ATXN2 and HTT were identified.

## ATXN2
Patient P9 had a diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) (MIM: 183090) and has gone through a 5-year diagnostic odyssey since his initial onset of muscle weakness. An expanded allele of 30 CAG repeats (95% confidence interval [CI], 30–30) in ATXN2 was identified ( ). The heterozygous repeat expansion is associated with an elevated risk of ALS, confirming the molecular diagnosis. It should be noted that STR expansion in ATXN2 is not a common cause of ALS, and ATXN2 is generally not included in the gene panel of clinical genetic tests for ALS. 

## Structural variants ascertained from WGS
The genomic rearrangement identified in this study covered four major types of structural variants, encompassing 13 deletion, 2 translocations, 2 inversions, and 1 case of mobile element insertion. A combination of a general SV caller (Manta) with callers specified in insertion (Insurveyor) and CNV detection (CNVkit and SurVIndel) , was used to maximize the sensitivity of SV calling. Manta detected almost all variants (18/19) with a size range from 126 to 479,668 bp ( ). Insurveyor allowed the detection of all mobile element insertions reported (2/2), including the one missed by Manta. In terms of CNV detection, CNVkit and SurVIndel complement Manta. CNVkit, a coverage-based CNV-detecting tool, could pick up all SVs larger than 60 kb in P17 and P24 and an SV of only about 10 kb in P16. SurVIndel, on the other hand, detected most of the deletions (12/13) except one in P26. 
Importantly, 83.3% (15 of 18) of probands with structural variants have undergone previous genetic test(s). However, they remained genetically undiagnosed at the time of referral despite focused examination of the associated genetic loci by conventional targeted sequencing (73.3%, 11 of 15) and WES (53.3%, 8 of 15) ( ).

## SLC25A13
Patient P11 had a clinical diagnosis of citrullinemia type II (MIM: 605814) by biochemical test and has experienced a 7-year diagnostic odyssey since birth. Further investigation of his elder sister also revealed suspected citrin deficiency. Initial targeted sequencing identified only one maternally inherited heterozygous variant in intron 16 of SLC25A13 c.1754-21_1754-5dupinsNM_138459.3 (GenBank: NM_001160210.1) in the proband and his affected sister. This insertion of around 2.7 kb processed cDNA of NUS1 (GenBank: NM_138459.3) in an antisense orientation, flanked by 17 bp of target site duplication, in intron 16 of SLC25A13 is a well-documented recurrent pathogenic variant in East Asia, which created a cryptic exon with a premature stop codon and leads to nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). , Subsequent RNA study only detected a naturally occurring in-frame exon 4 skipping isoform in the proband without identifying the second pathogenic variant. WGS analysis in the HKGP detected an additional insertion of the LINE1 transposable element in intron 4 of SLC25A13, which was inherited from the father and further validated by long-read sequencing ( A and ). It is also a well-reported recurrent variant in East Asia that results in multiple alternatively spliced mRNAs predicted to undergo NMD ( B). The exon 4 skipping isoform was among the multiple alternatively spliced mRNAs induced by the insertion of LINE1, which potentially explained the result of RNA sequencing in the proband. 

## PAX3
Patient P13 was clinically diagnosed with Waardenburg syndrome (MIM: 193500 and 148820) with a strong paternal family history. WGS analysis revealed a paternally inherited inversion event inv(2)(q33.1q36.1) in chromosome 2 in the proband, his father, and his paternal grandmother ( A–7C). WGS analysis enabled precise mapping of the breakpoints of the inversion event (seq[GRCh38] inv(2)(q33.1q36.1)pat, g.[201236834delinsTGAGCCCAGGGCTCT; 201236835_222273459inv] (GenBank: NC_000002.12)) ( B). The 3ʹ (telomeric) breakpoint at chr2:22273459, GRCh38 (hg38) is located in intron 4 of the PAX3 gene, which likely disrupted PAX3 expression. Notably, the change in the pattern of cytoband brought by this inversion is hardly distinguishable from the normal band pattern ( C). Therefore, it could not be validated by conventional karyotyping and was confirmed using Nanopore long-read sequencing. 

## HSD17B3
Patient P28 was a 10-year-old who had ambiguous genitalia at birth and is being raised as a girl. The patient is genetically male with a 46 XY karyotype. Her hormone profile leads to clinical suspicion of 17-beta hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase deficiency (MIM: 264300). However, previous targeted gene testing and trio-based WES have identified only one paternally inherited monoallelic missense variant (c.389A>G [GenBank: NM_000197.2] [p.Asn130Ser]) in HSD17B3 in the proband. A focused analysis of the HSD17B3 locus in WGS data revealed a 1.5 kb deletion covering the 5′ UTR, exon 1, and intron 1 of HSD17B3 in the proband and the mother ( D). The diagnosis was confirmed by the bi-allelic variants in HSD17B3, which provided valuable information for genetic counseling since the parents had another ongoing pregnancy with similar findings of sexual development disorder in the fetus.

## Overcoming the high sequence homology by DRAGEN
Targeted callers in DRAGEN were used to detect variants in genes with highly homologous pseudogenes. It calculates copy-number estimations of both the gene targets and their paralogous copies for each haplotype to more reliably call variants in the target genes. In addition, it identifies abnormal read pairs and reports potential recombination events. In this cohort, one SNV and one gene conversion in CYP21A2 were detected using the DRAGEN targeted caller.

## CYP21A2
Both patients P29 and P30 had clinical diagnoses of congenital adrenal hyperplasia due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency (21-OHD CAH) (MIM: 201910), which is caused by the bi-allelic mutation of CYP21A2. Sequence alignment of CYP21A2 is influenced by its pseudogene CYP21A1P in the RCCX module, which shares 98% sequence homology. The region is also prone to unequal crossing over during meiosis and gene conversion events, which further complicates variant detection. In both P29 and P30, only one monoallelic variant, c.923dup (GenBank: NM_000500.9) (p.Leu308PhefsTer6), and c.293-13C>G (GenBank: NM_000500.9) (p.?) were initially detected by the clinical laboratory, respectively. Utilizing the newly developed DRAGEN CYP21A2 caller, it additionally detected the other pathogenic variants c.92C>T (GenBank: NM_000500.9) (p.Pro31Leu) in P29 and c.1069C>T (GenBank: NM_000500.9) (p.Arg357Trp) in P30. An in-depth analysis also revealed that the c.92C>T variant in P29 arose from a gene conversion event between CYP21A2 and CYP21A1P, harboring the promoter to the intron 2 region, which created an attenuated chimera CH4/CH9 and led to nonclassical CAH in P29.

## Discussion
Technically challenging variants, including structural and non-coding variants, significantly impede molecular diagnosis in genetic diseases, as evidenced by their substantial contribution to unresolved patients across studies. , , , , Depending on the disease of interest, sequencing methodologies, and analytic frameworks, the additional diagnostic yield of WGS could vary widely from 12.7% to 40%. , , , , This study selected 322 patients (from 3,169 HKGP participants) with highly clinically suspected diseases lacking prior molecular diagnoses. WGS resolved 30 affected individuals (21.7% of positively diagnosed patients) harboring technically challenging variants and ended their diagnostic odysseys. These patients have received a median of two prior inconclusive genetic tests before WGS, indicating that most of the technically challenging variants cannot be identified by conventional methods.
The HKGP implemented a PCR-free library preparation workflow together with the use of the phenotype-driven prioritization tool Exomiser to enhance the detection of SNVs and indels in regions prone to PCR bias or low WES coverage, especially those near exon/intron junctions. PCR-free WGS avoided PCR bias and improved the uniformity of coverage, , , , uncovering 6 variants (20%, 6/30) in previously poorly covered regions. Of note, these variants were also prioritized as the top-1 candidates in Exomiser based on phenotypic concordance, population frequency, and predicted pathogenicity. This result highlights the utility of Exomiser in variant prioritization in clinical genomics. , 
Beyond coding regions, an increasing number of intronic variants have been reported to cause human diseases through disruption of splicing. In response to the potential deleterious impact of non-coding variants, new recommendations were also specifically designed by expert panels and the ClinGen SVI Splicing Subgroup for the clinical interpretation of non-coding variants that can only be detected using WGS. , Moreover, the development of splicing prediction tools such as SpliceAI helped with the prioritization and interpretation of pathogenic deep intronic variants and enabled the identification of two disease-causing deep intronic variants in NF1 (P7 and P8). In addition, new variant prioritization tools, such as Genomiser, can help prioritize intronic variants. , To fully unlock the diagnostic power of WGS, the HKGI has transited from using Exomiser to Genomiser and is currently conducting a comparison study to evaluate their performances.
ExpansionHunter, a sequence graph-based tool for the calling of repeat expansions in WGS, was acquired to extend the detection power. It can accurately detect the repeat sizes in a number of genes, including the STRs in ATXN2 of patient P9 and HTT of patient P10. Nevertheless, the actual sizes of detectable STRs are still limited by the 150 bp read length of short-read WGS. For large STRs with over hundreds and thousands of trinucleotide repeats, such as FMR1, they are usually underestimated using Illumina short-read data. Therefore, long-read sequencing is required to precisely determine the repeat sizes in these patients.
For SV/CNV analysis, many studies have shown that the careful selection of bioinformatic tools and the establishment of a robust filtering and prioritization workflow were critical for effective detection. , , The HKGI applied multiple calling algorithms to handle different types of SVs/CNVs. Manta, which calls SVs based on supporting paired and split-read evidence, performed the best in the detection of all types of SVs/CNVs in the current cohort. The application of additional callers such as CNVkit, SurVIndel, and InSurVeyor helped to refine the calling of specific SVs/CNVs. CNVkit, using a coverage-based approach, can detect large CNVs that may not have resolvable breakpoint junctions for Manta to process, while SurVIndel was specifically designed to handle deletions and tandem duplications in repetitive regions. InSurVeyor, on the other hand, was used to improve the calling of insertions.
The combined SV/CNV candidates were then prioritized according to their potential to disrupt the expression of target genes associated with the patient’s suspected disease (e.g., deletions overlapping with coding exons). This approach performed well for deletions, duplications, and insertions in exonic regions, even in diseases with high genetic heterogeneity, such as developmental delays. For insertions, inversions, and translocations with the breakpoint junctions located in intronic regions, this method left considerable false positive calls. Hence, tentative diagnosis provided by referring clinicians was essential for variant filtering and improving specificity. In this study, the majority (60%, 18/30) of challenging variants were SVs/CNVs, with most affected individuals having undergone at least one prior genetic test, demonstrating the robustness of our approach. This also revealed that many CNVs/SVs identified in this study are undetectable by standard genetic tests, often due to their small sizes. In some circumstances, large genomic rearrangements, such as the inversion detected in P13, can also be hard for conventional genetic tests such as karyotyping because the rearrangements create visually indistinguishable changes in chromosome banding patterns.
To overcome the challenge in high-homology sequences, an integrated approach considering the estimated copy numbers of both genes and pseudogenes, haplotyping information, and the possibility of recombination events was used to increase the accuracy of variant calling by Illumina’s DRAGEN, which currently supports SMN1, GBA, and CYP21A2. , In this study, the DRAGEN’s CYP21A2 caller has also accurately picked all four variants in the patients with CAH (P29 and P30). More targeted callers and bioinformatic methods handling homologous regions have been developed for PCR-free WGS. , , They can also be easily incorporated into our current pipeline.
The detection of the abovementioned difficult-to-detect variants demonstrated the robustness of the integrated workflow in the HKGI. A strong collaboration between the clinical team at the referring centers and the HKGI ensured an in-depth review of medical and family history and results of previous genetic investigations. The use of PCR-free sequencing together with strict quality controls (QCs) on read quality and insertion length guaranteed the high sensitivity of detecting SVs/CNVs. The variant calling and prioritization pipelines were also effective, as illustrated. Furthermore, the HKGI provided well-organized training for curators to learn the most updated gene-specific guidelines and recommendations. For complicated cases, MDT meetings were arranged to facilitate the discussion on the classification of the detected variants. This optimized workflow successfully ended the long diagnostic odyssey of patients. Notably, some pathogenic variants remain undetectable when using short-read WGS due to its short read length and inability to directly detect epigenetic changes. This limitation in the detection of complex SVs, large repeats, and methylation disorders can be overcome by introducing long-read sequencing. Currently, the HKGI is conducting a study to directly compare the performance of short-read and long-read WGS.
Altogether, the results of this study demonstrate the diagnostic power of WGS as an all-in-one test to detect nearly all types of technically challenging variants, including SNVs in low-coverage regions with PCR bias, deep intronic variants, SVs, and variants in genes with highly similar pseudogenes. Since the proportion of these technically challenging variants is significant, WGS should be used when the standard targeted sequencing fails to detect the causative variants.
