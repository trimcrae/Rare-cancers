Cleavage can occur from the RNA ends (exonucleolytic cleavage) or from within (endonucleolytic cleavage) an RNA molecule depending on the processing enzyme. For example, exonucleolytic cleavage of RNAs can occur in a 5′–3′ direction, mediated by EXORIBONUCLEASES (XRNs) [ ], or 3′–5′ direction, mediated by the multisubunit RNA exosome complex [ ] or the exonuclease SUPPRESSOR OF VARICOSE [ ]. Alternatively, endonucleolytic RNA cleavage can be carried out within an mRNA, for example, by the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), composed of a mature miRNA bound to an ARGONAUTE (AGO) protein [ ]. Given the complexity of RNA cleavage, the ability to accurately quantify a variety of cleavage types is foundational to our understanding of RNA metabolism.
Multiple methods exist to profile RNA cleavage based on the capture of genome-wide degradation intermediates (the degradome), such as global mapping of uncapped and cleaved transcripts (GMUCT) [ ], parallel analysis of RNA ends (PARE) [ ], and degradome sequencing [ ]. Such methods utilize T4 RNA ligase 1 to ligate an RNA adapter onto 5′-monophosphorylated RNAs. These RNAs harbour a terminal 5′-monophosphate (5′-P end), which can arise from cap hydrolysis (e.g. by the decapping enzyme DCP2) or endonucleolytic cleavage (e.g. by RISC). Degradome profiling was conceived to study decapped RNAs and small RNA cleavage, since it captures the 5′-P end of the 3′ cleaved fragments [ – ]. But the degradome also retains signatures of mRNAs harbouring terminating ribosomes and exon-junction complexes (EJCs) [ – ]. While the former is considered to reflect co-translational RNA decay (CTRD) by XRN4 [ , ], the latter represents degradation before completion of the pioneering round of translation, including nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) [ ]. It is unclear the extent to which EJC footprints reflect cytoplasmic cleavage. For example, a nuclear scanning model of RNA surveillance has been proposed in which EJC-triggered NMD can occur after ribosome proofreading in the nucleus or during nucleocytoplasmic export [ , ]. Cleavage also occurs at noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), especially in the nucleus (e.g. miRNA biogenesis), which can be missed in bulk RNA degradome analysis [ – , , ], meaning that nuclear-localized steps may be overlooked and understudied.
In plants, miRNAs are a class of small (∼21 nucleotides) noncoding RNAs that control morphogenic processes by post-transcriptionally regulating gene expression [ ]. Their biogenesis and regulatory activity represent processes involving distinct cleavage reactions in the nucleus and cytoplasm, respectively. Mature miRNAs are generated from a single-stranded, polyadenylated, hairpin-containing primary transcript (primary miRNA, pri-miRNA), which are encoded by endogenous MIRNA (MIR) genes that are transcribed by DNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RNAP) II [ ]. Pri-miRNA processing occurs in the nucleus in either a co- or post-transcriptional manner, which varies between pri-miRNAs and growth conditions [ ]. This processing entails sequential cleavage by DICER-LIKE1 (DCL1), which can initiate from the base of the hairpin structure and operate in a base-to-loop (BTL) manner or from the terminal loop and proceed in a loop-to-base (LTB) direction [ – ]. This cleavage generates miRNA duplexes consisting of a strand derived from the 5′ (miRNA-5p) and 3′ (miRNA-3p) arms of the hairpin precursor [ ]. Either strand may form the mature, biologically functional miRNA while the other (often denoted miRNA*) is degraded [ ]. The mature miRNA strand is loaded onto ARGONAUTE1 (AGO1) to form the RISC, which is exported to the cytosol where it binds to complementary mRNA [ , ]. Upon mRNA-binding, AGO1 will mediate mRNA endonucleolytic cleavage [ – , ] or repress translation [ , ]. While miRNA-mediated cytoplasmic cleavage is readily detectable, it is challenging to observe the nucleoplasmic pri-mRNA processing steps. This often requires tailored methodology, which can be technically challenging and only captures one type of nuclear RNA cleavage [ , , ].
The eukaryotic nuclear cap-binding complex (CBC) is a heterodimer of CAP-BINDING PROTEIN80/ABA HYPERSENSITIVE1 (CBP80/ABH1) and CBP20 and binds to the 7-methylguanosine (m7G) cap at the 5′-termini of RNAP II transcripts [ , ]. The CBC was originally shown to facilitate pre-mRNA splicing [ ]. Further research has implicated its function in alternative splicing, mRNA stabilization, nucleocytoplasmic export, the pioneer round of translation, NMD, and CTRD [ , – ]. The CBC is also considered important for miRNA biogenesis [ , , , ]. Given these diverse and critical functions, it may be surprising that cbp20 and cbp80 single and double null mutants remain perfectly viable [ ]. Disruption of the CBC can have transcript-specific effects. For instance, the biogenesis of some miRNAs is impacted but not others [ , ]. Furthermore, it remains unclear how a nuclear-localized complex [ ] contributes to CTRD, which should occur in the cytoplasm [ ]. These observations suggest that there is an incomplete view of the extent to which the CBC guides nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA processing and cleavage events.
In this study, we address the aforementioned gaps by coupling subcellular RNA fractionation with GMUCT (subcellular GMUCT) to enhance the detection of compartmentalized RNA cleavage. Systematic comparison between fractions revealed a diverse array of nucleoplasm-enriched RNA cleavage, including pre-mRNA cleavage and ncRNA processing, including for miRNAs, rRNAs, and snoRNAs, and RNAs from retrotransposons and predicted enhancers. We then show that ABH1 impacts nucleoplasmic EJC and CTRD footprints, likely reflecting impaired RNA surveillance in the perinuclear cytoplasm. Beyond RNA surveillance, a stabilizing role for ABH1 was evident in the nucleus for hundreds of mRNAs, antisense RNAs, and retrotransposon-derived RNAs, including many associated with epigenetically activated small-interfering RNAs (easiRNAs) [ ]. In the cytoplasm of abh1 flower buds was a plethora of sites of increased and decreased cleavage within mRNA exons, highlighting its role in shaping cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage. Many of these were predicted to harbour miRNA binding sites, suggesting that its impacts on cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage occur partly through its role in miRNA biogenesis and function. In summary, we present a method to profile compartment-enriched degradomes and highlight the genome-wide importance of ABH1 in shaping nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic RNA processing and surveillance.

## Materials and methods

## Plant material and growth
All experiments were carried out with Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. (Arabidopsis) germplasm in the Columbia (Col)-0 background and included wild-type (WT) Col-0 and abh1 (abh1-8, SALK_117 039) lines. Homozygous abh1 plants were identified based on polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of SALK_117 039 (and lack of amplification with primers flanking the insertion site). Progeny collected by single-seed descent from a selfed homozygous parent were used for all experiments, after confirming that there was no segregation of the expected phenotypes [ ]. Primers used for genotyping are listed in .
Seeds were sown onto moist soil (treated overnight with diatomaceous earth), supplemented with 10 g/l Jack’s Professional 15-16-17 Peat-Lite water-soluble fertilizer (JR Peters, Allentown, PA, USA) and NemAttack (Arbico Organics, Tucson, AZ, USA), and stratified in the dark at 4°C for 48 h. Plants were grown at 22°C under a 16-h-light (100–150 µmol m−2 s−1)/8-h-dark photoperiod. Approximately 20–30 mg of early stage flower buds (stages 1–12) [ ] were harvested from 10-week old plants. Early stage flower buds were used owing to their numerosity, harbouring many detectable small RNAs [ , ], and being cell dense structures [ ]. Excised buds were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Biological replicates represent pooled flower buds collected from independently grown plants.

## Subcellular RNA fractionation
Nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic RNA-enriched fractions were isolated using the Cytoplasmic and Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek, Thorold, ON, CAN), including on-column DNA nuclease treatment for nuclear RNA (RNase-Free DNase I Kit, Norgen Biotek, Thorold, ON, CAN). Briefly, flower buds were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Ground material was removed from liquid nitrogen and immediately dissolved in 200 μl Lysis Buffer J containing 0.1% (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol with gentle mixing. After 3 min of incubation, the lysate was centrifuged at 20 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant (cytoplasm-enriched RNAs) was separated from the pellet (nucleoplasm-enriched RNAs). To the cytoplasmic and nucleoplasmic RNA, 200 and 400 μl of Buffer SK, containing 0.1% (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol, was added, respectively, followed by 10 s of thorough mixing. Then, 200 μl of ethanol was added followed by thorough mixing. The resulting solutions were transferred onto a column, centrifuged at 18 000 × g for 1 min, and the flow-through was discarded. 400 μl Wash Solution A was added to each fraction followed by centrifugation for 2 min at 18 000 × g and the flow-through was discarded. On-column DNase I treatment was performed to each nucleoplasmic fraction using the RNase-Free DNase I kit (Norgen Biotek, Thorold, ON, CAN) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. All fractions underwent an additional two rounds of washing with Wash Solution A. After discarding the final flow-through, one final centrifugation was performed (2 min at 18 000 × g) to dry the resin. RNA from each fraction was then eluted using 50 μl of nuclease free water. This was repeated twice for each column to maximize RNA recovery, such that a total volume of 100 μl was obtained for each fraction. This was concentrated using ethanol precipitation and resuspended in 20 μl of Tris–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; 10 mM Tris–Cl, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 6.5). The quantity and purity of RNA was assessed using a Nanodrop ND-1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Integrity (and presence of genomic DNA) was assessed by visualizing heat-treated (65°C for 5 min) aliquots of purified RNA, mixed with an equal volume of 2 × RNA Loading Dye (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA), on a 1.2% agarose gel. A detailed protocol is available on Protocols.io (dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.kqdg3x2nqg25/v1).

## Immunoblotting
Immunoblotting was performed to assess the purity of the nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic fractions. Crude lysates were prepared by lysing snap-frozen, ground flower buds in 200 μl of Lysis Buffer J containing 0.1% (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol. After 3 min of incubation, lysates were centrifuged at 20 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant (cytoplasmic fraction) was separated from the pellet (nucleoplasmic fraction), and an additional 200 μl of Lysis Buffer J containing 0.1% (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol was added to the pellet and mixed. An aliquot of each crude lysate was diluted 1:2 (v/v) in water, mixed 3:1 (v/v) with 4× NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and boiled at 95°C for 10 min. The denatured proteins were separated on a NuPAGE Bis–Tris 4%–12% polyacrylamide gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 100 V for 90 min in NuPAGE MES SDS running buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The separated proteins were then transferred onto a Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore) via wet electroblotting in Tris–glycine transfer buffer at 100 mA for 90 min at 4°C. The membrane was blocked in 2.5% milk in phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20 (PBST) overnight at 4°C. The membrane was then blotted with antihistone H3 (1:5000, #ab1791, Abcam) in 2.5% skim milk in PBST for 1–2 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. The membrane was washed thrice in PBST for 10 min, then blotted with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antirabbit IgG (1:10 000, Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. After another three washes in PBST for 10 min with gentle agitation, SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher) was applied to the membrane then, after 1 min, chemiluminescence was detected with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera. Ponceau S staining was used as a loading control (Cell Signaling Technology).

## RT-qPCR analysis
First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed on 500 ng RNA using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). PCR reactions were performed with 50 ng cDNA in 20 μl reactions including 0.4 μM of each forward and reverse primer and 1 × Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Each reaction was run in technical duplicate. PCR reactions were run on a QuantStudio3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and entailed an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, 30 s at 60°C, and 30 s at 72°C (temperature rate change at each step = 1.6°C s−1). This was followed by melt curve analysis (15 s at 95°C, 60 s at 60°C, 15 s at 95°C with a rate change of 0.1°C s−1) to ensure that all reactions amplified a single, consistent product for each primer pair. Raw fluorescence data were analyzed using LinRegPCR to perform background subtraction, determine PCR efficiency, and calculate starting concentration (N0) [ , ]. N0 values, based on the mean PCR efficiency for each amplicon, were used to determine relative abundance. Primer sequences are provided in .

## GMUCT library preparation
For each sample, individual preparations of each fraction were pooled, separately, and concentrated via ethanol precipitation. Individual preparations were chosen for pooling based on the enrichment of nucleoplasmic RNA markers (LNCCOBRA3, LNCCOBRA5, and unspliced PP2AA3) and depletion of cytoplasmic RNA markers (spliced PP2AA3) in the nucleoplasmic fraction relative to the cytoplasmic fraction. The concentrated RNA samples were used to produce GMUCT libraries with an adapted protocol [ ]. Briefly, for cytoplasm-enriched RNA samples, ∼20 µg of total RNA was used for poly(A) RNA isolation using the Dynabeads mRNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions except that reactions were scaled down by one-half. Poly(A) RNA was used for ligation of the TruSeq RA5 5′-adapter onto monophosphorylated RNAs using T4 RNA ligase (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). For nucleoplasm-enriched RNA fractions, 2–5 µg of total RNA was used directly for 5′-adapter ligation without the initial poly(A) RNA isolation. Subsequently, the products from both 5′-adapter ligation reactions underwent poly(A) RNA isolation using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions. Then, first-strand synthesis was performed using SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a random hexamer primer that incorporates the TruSeq RA3 3′-adapter sequence ( ). Nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic RNA-enriched libraries were amplified by 14 and 12 cycles of PCR, respectively, using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) and TruSeq Small RNA indexing primers (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). PCR products were concentrated using ethanol precipitation then separated by PAGE (6% TBE polyacrylamide gel, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for size selection (150–500 bp) by gel purification. Gel slices were excised and passed through gel breaker tubes (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA), then incubated in 300 µl of NEB Buffer 2 for at least 2 h with rotation at ambient temperature. The mixture was passed through a Spin-X centrifuge tube filter (Sigma-Aldrich). A final ethanol precipitation was performed to purify and concentrate libraries. Library concentration was determined using a Qubit fluorometer (double-stranded DNA high sensitivity kit; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Libraries were sequenced paired-end (150 cycles) on the HiSeq X Ten or NovaSeq X Plus at Novogene (Sacramento, CA, USA). Sample information and sequencing statistics are summarized in . A detailed protocol is also available on Protocols.io: dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.yxmvm2jjbg3p/v1.

## mRNA sequencing
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a ratio of 1 ml solution per 50 mg ground tissue. Residual phenol was removed through two chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (24:1) extractions at a ratio of 1:5 (v/v), followed by overnight precipitation in 1:1 (v/v) isopropanol at −20°C. The precipitated RNA was washed with 75% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. Resuspended RNA was then treated with RNase-free DNase (Qiagen), before precipitation with 3:1 (v/v) ethanol, 300 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.5) and 150 µg ml−1 glycogen for 2 h at −20°C. DNase-treated RNA was washed with 75% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free Tris–EDTA (10 mM Tris–Cl, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5). Purified RNA was quantified using the Nanodrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer, and inspected for DNA contamination and integrity using semi-denaturing 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Full details are available on Protocols.io: dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.36wgq4enyvk5/v2. Poly(A) RNA was isolated from 4 µg total RNA using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA magnetic isolation module (NEB). This was used to generate stranded mRNA sequencing libraries with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB).

## Bioinformatic analyses
Raw reads from GMUCT, PARE, mRNA sequencing, and small RNA sequencing libraries were trimmed using TrimGalore (Babraham Bioinformatics) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality base calls (PHRED < 20), and sequences shorter than 10 nucleotides (–length 10). For small RNA libraries, sequences longer than 30 nucleotides were also removed. However, to focus on 20–21 nucleotide small RNAs in abh1 and ddm1 small RNA libraries, only the first 21 nucleotides of reads were used for mapping (fastx_trimmer -l 21). Trimmed reads for all libraries were mapped to the Arabidopsis TAIR10 genome assembly using STAR [ ]. For mRNA sequencing libraries, this was performed using default settings except “–alignIntronMax 3000″. For GMUCT and PARE libraries, STAR was run with the parameters “–outFilterMismatchNmax 0 –outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.75 –outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.75 –outFilterMultimapNmax 1″. This was modified for PARE libraries, where only the first 20 nucleotides of the trimmed reads were used for mapping (fastx_trimmer -l 20). For small RNA libraries, the following flags were supplied to STAR: “–outFilterMismatchNmax 0 –outFilterMatchNmin 20 –outFilterMultimapNmax 4 –alignEndsType EndToEnd”. For libraries sequenced across multiple lanes, reads from each lane were processed individually before merging the aligned reads with samtools [ ].
Transcript quantification was performed using featureCounts [ ] (flags -C -M -s 2) on gene-level features defined in the TAIR10 annotation (gene transfer format (GTF)) retrieved from EnsemblPlants [ ]. Raw counts were imported into edgeR for loci detected at counts per million (CPM) >1 in at least three samples [ ]. Counts were normalized between samples using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method [ ]. Normalized counts were used for multidimensional scaling analysis (MSA) using plotMDS (gene.selection = “common”) in edgeR [ ]. Normalized counts were also used to fit generalized linear models to estimate dispersion (glmQLFit) followed by quasi-likelihood F-tests (glmQLFTest) to identify significant differences with correction for multiple hypothesis testing (false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P-value <.05). This process was performed for fragments derived from sense (featureCounts -s 2 for reverse-stranded libraries) and antisense (featureCounts -s 1) transcripts. Small RNA sequencing was used to quantify miRNA levels using featureCounts (flags -s 1 -M) with miRBase annotations [ ]. Raw counts, for miRNAs detected at >1 CPM, were converted into TMM-normalized CPM in edgeR before calculating relative abundance. Alternate splicing (AS) was assessed using rMATs [ ] on aligned mRNA sequencing reads with parameters “-t paired –readLength 150 –cstat 0.001 –libType fr-firststrand”. Significant AS events were defined as those with inclusion level change (Δψ) > 5% and FDR < 0.05 based on junction reads only. Since Δψ is unreliable at low coverage, alternate splicing events were filtered to those with ≥ 8 total junction-spanning reads in at least two replicates per genotype.

Transcripts with TSICTRD or TSIEJC ≥2 and 5′-P end count ≥10, were defined as CTRD-regulated or EJC-bound mRNAs, respectively. Enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms were determined using the statistical overrepresentation test (Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction) from PANTHER [ ]. Only GO terms with a fold enrichment >1 were considered and redundancy was removed using the rrvgo package [ ]. Protein subcellular localization was based on SUBA5 consensus (SUBAcon) predictions [ ]. NMD targets were defined based on previous alternate splicing analysis in upf1upf3 mutants [ ]. Those transcripts with upregulated or downregulated splice variants in upf1upf3 mutants (FDR < 0.1) were defined as NMD-suppressed or NMD-enhanced, respectively.
DiffSegR was used to identify differentially expressed regions from GMUCT data, which we refer to as differentially cleaved regions (DCRs) [ ]. Mapped reads were loaded into DiffSegR [version 1, install_github(“aLiehrmann/DiffSegR@f657435”)] to compute coverage profiles across the nuclear chromosomes using loadData (isPairedEnd = TRUE, readLength = 150, stranded = FALSE, strandSpecific = 0) based on 5′ (coverageType = “fivePrime”) and 3′ (coverageType = “threePrime”) read ends, separately. Coverage profiles were segmented into expressed regions based on a changepoint detection algorithm [ ] initiated by the segmentation function [isPairedEnd = TRUE, featureCountsOtherParams = list(allowMultiOverlap = FALSE), strandSpecific = 0, accordingly]. For this step, reads were reduced to their terminal 5′ (read2pos = 5) or 3′ (read2pos = 3) end corresponding with the coverage profile computed in the previous step (e.g. if coverageType = “fivePrime” then read2pos = 5). To capture distinct cleavage sites, segments longer than 10 nucleotides were excluded. The remaining segments were then supplied for differential expression analysis, which utilizes the DESeq2 framework [ ] to detect statistically significant differences with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing (significanceLevel = 0.01, predicate = NULL). Only DCRs with average expression (baseMean) > 10 were retained. DCRs were assigned genomic coordinates with intersectBed from the Bedtools suite [ ]. At least 50% of a DCR (-f 0.5) had to overlap with another DCR or TAIR10 feature to be considered overlapping. This process was repeated for antisense cleavage analysis, except that strand information was retained using the flags stranded = TRUE and strandSpecific = 1 when computing coverage profiles. To identify antisense DCRs, intersectBed was executed with flag -S to find overlaps with genomic features on the opposing strand.
Sequences of mature miRNAs were predicted based on: (i) the nucleotide sequence between two nucleoplasm-enriched 5′-P end peaks (aided by small RNA expression if detected), or (ii) the 10 nucleotides upstream and 11 nucleotides downstream from a cytoplasm-enriched 5′-P end peak occurring within an annotated mRNA [ ]. Predicted (pri-)miRNA sequences were cross-referenced with miRBase [ ]. Binding sites of miRNAs were predicted using psRNATarget with default parameters (Schema Version 2) [ ] and cross-referenced to degradome supported miRNA targets from TarDB [ ]. RNAfold was used to predict RNA secondary structure, selecting the conformation with minimum free energy, using default settings except that energy parameters were re-scaled to 23°C to reflect the intracellular temperature relative to ambient conditions [ ].

## Results

## Subcellular GMUCT captures compartment-specific RNA cleavage including pri-miRNA processing and miRNA-guided mRNA slicing
We hypothesized that subcellular GMUCT would improve the detection of nucleoplasmic RNA cleavage events ( ). Previous degradome profiling was performed on Arabidopsis flowers owing to the large number of detectable small RNAs [ , ]. Since early stage flower buds are also cell dense structures [ ], and relatively depleted of photosynthetic chloroplasts [ ], we reasoned that subcellular RNA fractionation with this material should maximize RNA yields to facilitate biological insights. Therefore, we performed subcellular RNA fractionation on early stage flower buds from WT Col-0 plants using the Cytoplasmic and Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek). Initial protein-level assessment, via immunoblotting on crude lysates for each fraction, highlighted an enrichment of histone H3 in the nucleoplasmic fraction with concomitant depletion in the cytoplasmic fraction ( ). With this enrichment, our approach yielded between 1–5 µg and 15–25 µg of RNA in the nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic fraction, respectively, which is amenable to constructing GMUCT libraries [ ]. Additional quality control was conducted using mRNA-level measurements via reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). We measured the relative abundance of nucleoplasmic markers (unspliced PP2AA3, LNCCOBRA3, and LNCCOBRA5) [ ], and a cytoplasmic marker (spliced PP2AA3) between fractions ( ). We observed an increased abundance of LNCCOBRA3, LNCCOBRA5, and unspliced PP2AA3 in the nucleoplasmic fraction relative to cytoplasm. The opposite was true for spliced PP2AA3, which had an increased abundance in the cytoplasmic fraction compared to the nucleoplasm. Taken together, the protein- and mRNA-level markers confirmed the isolation of nucleoplasmic and cytoplasm-enriched RNA fractions.
We performed GMUCT on the nucleoplasmic- and cytoplasmic RNA-enriched fractions derived from WT flower buds. MSA confirmed that fractionation was the primary source of variation between these samples (PC1 = 94%; ). We sought to ground-truth our dataset based on well-studied molecular phenomena. MiRNA biogenesis and function entails nuclear pri-miRNA processing followed by cytosolic mRNA cleavage, respectively [ ]. One of the earliest regulatory miRNAs discovered was miR165, which is highly expressed in developing ovules [ , ]. Therefore, we reasoned that we should detect cleavage events for pri-miR165a processing in nucleoplasm-enriched RNA and target mRNA cleavage in the cytoplasm-enriched RNA. We assembled data encompassing mRNA sequencing, small RNA sequencing, and degradome sequencing, all performed on bulk RNA derived from flower buds ( ) [ , , ]. We included two degradome datasets in order to compare our method to those with modest (GMUCT, GSE47121) [ ] and deep (PARE, GSE118215) [ ] sequencing coverage. When viewing MIR165A, we observed pronounced 5′-P ends flanking the region corresponding to miR165a-3p as well as modest, yet detectable, signals flanking miR165a-5p (Fig. and  ). We infer that this decreased signal might reflect rapid decay of the miRNA* strand (miR165a-5p). We also ensured that these 5′-P ends were consistent across replicates, further highlighting the reproducibility of our datasets ( ). Strikingly, these peaks perfectly bound regions consistent with small RNA abundance and directly corresponding to the annotated miR165a-5p and miR165a-3p sequences. Furthermore, they were consistent with peaks previously obtained using an orthogonal method to detect pri-miRNA processing intermediates [ ]. This suggests that we are detecting pri-miRNA processing intermediates arising from DCL1 cleavage. Additionally, these products were far more abundant in our nucleoplasmic RNA fractions compared to bulk RNA samples. However, the cytoplasmic fraction still harbours 5′-P ends, at the same genomic coordinates as miR165a-3p and miR165a-5p. This may partly reflect some cross-contamination, but also AGO1-bound miRNAs in the cytoplasm [ ].
 A developmentally important target of miR165 are PHAVOLUTA (PHV) transcripts [ ]. We could identify four cleavage sites across PHV transcripts based on cytoplasm-enriched 5′-P ends (Fig.  ). One of these occurred within the predicted binding site of miR165a-3p, between the 11th and 12th nucleotides relative to the 5′-end (Fig.  , left). Three additional cleavage sites were also observed, two of which were predominantly detected in the cytoplasmic fraction (Fig.  ). We were able to predict miRNA binding for these two sites but focused on the pronounced cleavage detected in the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR, Fig.  ). The sequence surrounding this cleavage site demonstrated complementarity to multiple miR169 members. Out of these candidates, we could detect processing of pri-miR169a that corresponded with miR169a-5p in the nucleoplasmic fraction (Fig. and  ). The observed cleavage in the 3′ UTR of PHV occurred between the fifth and sixth nucleotides of the predicted binding site of miR169a-5p (Fig. , right). This does not align with the expected cleavage site for a 21-nucleotide miRNA (between the 10th and 11th nucleotides) [ ] nor was this site previously identified in a catalogue of degradome-based miRNA targets in Arabidopsis [ ]. Therefore, this may reflect a novel site with imprecise miRNA binding (predicted binding site had 12/21 sites with perfect complementarity) leading to AGO1-independent cleavage of a RISC-bound mRNA [ ]. However, additional genetic analyses are required to rule out the possibility of independent nucleolytic activity.
To expand our survey of pri-miRNA processing, we considered pri-miRNAs with distinct processing modes—co- or post-transcriptional processing in a BTL or LTB manner—as defined in prior work [ , , ]. We already highlighted cleavage at MIR165A (co-transcriptional BTL) and MIR169A (co- and post-transcriptional BTL) (Fig.  ). Therefore, we compiled a suite of pri-miRNAs encompassing distinct processing modes, including pri-miR165b (post-transcriptional BTL), pri-miR156b (co- and post-transcriptional LTB), pri-miR156h (post-transcriptional LTB), and pri-miR161 (dual BTL and LTB), and pri-miR472 (sequential BTL; ). Examination of the corresponding MIR loci revealed distinct cleavage patterns flanking the mature miRNA sequence. In most cases, cleavage was evident on both arms, however, as with MIR165A, a clear bias was observed toward one arm. This likely reflects disparity between the biologically active mature miRNA (increased signal) and degraded miRNA* strand (reduced signal). This pattern is clearest at MIR156B and MIR156H, each annotated with a single mature miRNA in miRBase ( ). MIR161 was an exception as it generates two overlapping miRNAs—miR161.1 and miR161.2—from the same precursor, depending on whether processing occurs in a BTL (miR161.2) or LTB (miR161.1) manner. As opposed to two-step cleavage at the majority of these pri-miRNAs, MIR472 undergoes sequential LTB processing ( ). Curiously, there were still prominent cytoplasmic 5′-P ends corresponding to miR472-3p alongside negligible signal (< 0.5 RPM, nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic 5′-P ends) at miR472-5p. It is possible this represents a feature of sequential cleavage or coordinated processing and export at the nuclear periphery [ ]. Thus, we visualized cleavage at MIR156A, which undergoes two-step LTB processing at the nuclear periphery [ , ]. In this case, cleavage was more evident in the cytoplasmic samples (and bulk degradomes), possibly indicating that the ratio of detection between compartments may partly reflect the speed of export ( ), which warrants further investigation. Nevertheless, these observations suggest that subcellular GMUCT can capture pri-miRNA processing of all modalities. Collectively, this data highlights the enrichment of 5′-P signals emanating from compartment-specific RNA cleavage, which has the potential to facilitate novel insights for miRNA processing and function, as well as other RNA classes.

## Nucleoplasm-enriched degradomes capture a diverse array of nuclear RNA cleavage
To catalogue the diversity of RNA cleavage enriched in the nucleoplasm and cytoplasm, we employed DiffSegR [ ] to identify short (≤10 nucleotides) DCRs between fractions. To increase the diversity of sites captured from paired-end sequencing data, such as those arising from endonucleolytic cleavage or regions protected from 5′-to-3′ decay, we implemented DiffSegR based on 5′ and 3′ coverage pileups, separately (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). Indeed, each implementation detected distinct DCRs with little overlap ( ). In total, we identified 6504 DCRs comprised of 2243 occurring at protein-coding mRNAs (848 loci), 1016 at annotated ncRNAs (215 loci), and 3245 in intergenic space ( ). Strikingly, the vast majority of DCRs had increased abundance in the nucleoplasmic fraction compared to the cytoplasmic fraction (Fig.  ). Overall, this analysis suggests that we effectively captured compartment-enriched cleavage events with a pronounced bias for the nucleoplasm.
 We first investigated DCRs occurring at protein-coding mRNAs (Fig.  ). We inspected the position of nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs occurring within 5′ UTRs (390), introns (490), exons (580), and 3′ UTRs (438) relative to TSS, PAS, exon–intron junctions or splice sites, and start and stop codons (Fig.  – , , and ). The most striking observation was the increased number of nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs at the 3′ end of exons (Fig.  , right) and at 3′ splice sites (Fig.  , right). There were also distinct peaks at the TSS and PAS, however, additional peaks could also be seen ∼50 nucleotides downstream and upstream from the TSS and PAS, respectively (Fig. ). Furthermore, inspection of nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs adjacent to mRNA features suggests that many are highly specific to the nucleoplasmic fraction (Fig.  and ). Taken together, this suggests that subcellular GMUCT is effective at capturing nuclear RNA cleavage likely associated with mRNA processing and surveillance.
We next characterized nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs associated with ncRNAs and intergenic regions. Quantifying the proportion of annotated ncRNAs harbouring a DCR revealed that nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage was largely detected at snoRNAs and rRNAs (Fig.  ). Many nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs (243) also occurred at long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), however, this reflected a small proportion relative to the number annotated in the genome. With respect to rRNA, we observed nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs within the subtelomeric region of chromosome 2 reflecting Nucleolar Organizer Region 2 (NOR2). Here, we observed pronounced nucleoplasmic 5′-P ends likely reflecting endonucleolytic processing of 18S and 5.8S rRNA ( ). We also noticed a pronounced small RNA peak within 5.8S rRNA, which coincided with a modest nucleoplasmic 5′-P signal. This may reflect rRNA-derived small RNAs, snoRNAs in association with an rRNA processing intermediate for 5′-end modification, or a combination thereof [ , ]. We further investigated lncRNA- and snoRNA-associated cleavage by considering the previously characterized snoRNA containing lncRNA, LNCCOBRA1 (Fig.  , left) [ ]. A pronounced 5′-P end signal was evident towards the 5′ end of SNO-COBRA1 along with multiple 5′-P ends at the 3′ end. There were also distinct 5′-P end signals at the borders of SNO-COBRA1B, albeit at lower intensity. Together, this suggests that subcellular GMUCT captures snoRNA ends. Since 5′-P ends were observed in both fractions, this suggests that these snoRNAs are present in both compartments, possibly due to their association to a lncRNA. To find nuclear-retained snoRNAs, we considered those harbouring nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs. Indeed, multiple examples could be found with predominantly nucleoplasm-enriched 5′-P ends (Fig. , middle, right). Therefore, subcellular GMUCT may distinguish snoRNAs (and sno-lncRNAs) that are nuclear retained from those that are exported. Furthermore, in the case of LNCCOBRA5, 5′-P ends were observed in an arrangement resembling those at SNO-COBRA1A/1B within LNCCOBRA1, possibly reflecting the presence of a novel snoRNA. This data suggests that subcellular GMUCT can be used to study nuclear processing events in ncRNA maturation.
 Interestingly, the majority of nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs were observed in intergenic regions lacking any annotated mRNAs or ncRNAs. Therefore, we considered the possibility that these DCRs reflected cleavage associated with transposons. In fact, we identified 836 nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs within annotated transposons (Fig.  and ). While the largest number of DCRs were found at Gypsy elements (425), the nonautonomous Sadhu and RathE1 elements were most enriched when considered relative to the total annotated in the genome (Fig. ). There was also a clear bias in nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs towards class I transposons. This suggests that subcellular GMUCT captures nuclear cleavage of RNAs associated with retroelements. One source of transposon-associated RNA cleavage is that of miRNA-mediated production of easiRNAs [ ]. These are a class of 21-nucleotide small RNAs generated from transposons through the activity of RNA-DEPENDENT RNA POLYMERASE6 (RDR6). Utilizing a published dataset [ ], we defined 3293 RDR6-dependent 21-nucleotide easiRNA generating loci ( ). Of these, 308 coincided with nucleoplasm-enriched 5′-P ends (Fig.  , top, and ). However, these did not always exactly align with 21-nucleotide easiRNAs or 5′-P ends from ddm1 mutant flowers ( ). We also considered whether these DCRs reflect 5′-P transcripts generated by RNAP IV [ , ]. Of the 3245 intergenic nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs, 275 and 160 were detected at regions associated with RNAP IV-dependent [ ] and RNAP IV- and RDR2-dependent RNAs [ ], respectively ( ). Collectively, these examples suggest that subcellular GMUCT detects nucleoplasmic cleavage of intergenic RNAs derived from a variety of sources, especially retrotransposons.
We then interrogated whether other nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs located in intergenic space reflected unstable RNAs associated with enhancer regions, so called enhancer RNA (eRNAs) [ ]. To do this, we compared the coordinates of these DCRs to those of 4844 DNase I hypersensitivity-based predictions of accessible enhancers in early stage Arabidopsis flowers [ ]. We found 1020 intergenic nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs occurred at predicted enhancer regions (Fig. , bottom, and ). Upon individual inspection, these DCRs were highly specific to the nucleoplasm and showed a combination of distinct peaks as well as spreading across the predicted enhancer region (Fig.  ). A subset of these also coincided with predicted enhancers within annotated transposons, possibly reflecting those elements harbouring enhancers. These examples suggest that we are capturing the nuclear processing of RNAs originating from enhancers. Collectively, these results highlight that subcellular GMUCT captures the cleavage of a variety of RNAs from noncoding regions of the Arabidopsis genome.

## ABH1 maintains EJC and CTRD footprints in the nucleoplasmic fraction
To investigate the impacts of the nuclear CBC on transcript-specific RNA cleavage in nucleoplasm- and cytoplasm-enriched fractions, we performed subcellular GMUCT on abh1 early stage flower buds. We achieved comparable levels of nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic RNA enrichment in abh1 flowers buds as for WT ( ). MSA across all degradomes revealed that subcellular RNA fractionation was still the primary source of variation (PC1 = 93%), however, a subtle, yet distinct, amount of variation could be associated with genotype (PC2 = 2%;  ). To further validate that we could detect ABH1-dependent impacts on RNA cleavage, we observed whether our data reflected previous reports that ABH1 is involved in pri-miR165 processing [ ]. Unexpectedly, we observed enhanced cleavage at miR165a-3p alongside loss of miR165a-5p processing, accompanied by reduced abundance of each corresponding miRNA ( ). While this is partly consistent with prior reports that ABH1 impacts 5′-arm processing at pri-miR165a (i.e. miR165a-5p) [ ], it also suggests that pri-miRNA processing is not completely abolished in abh1 flower buds. Regardless, miRNA function was retained as cleavage was still evident at the canonical miR165-3p binding site within PHV transcripts in abh1 flower buds ( ). Although, a reduction in cleavage was evident in comparison to WT flower buds, which aligns with reduced mature miR165a-3p levels. Therefore, along with new biological insights into miRNA processing and function, these observations suggest that we had subcellular degradomes with which to probe for compartment-specific effects of the nuclear CBC on RNA processing and surveillance.
Based on our prior findings that ABH1 is involved in CTRD [ ], we hypothesized compartment-specific changes in EJC and CTRD footprints would be evident between abh1 and WT samples. Within WT samples, we observed distinct EJC and CTRD footprints in both the nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic RNA-enriched fractions (Fig. and  ). Stronger footprints were observed in the cytoplasm, likely reflecting greater activity in this compartment. Furthermore, we observed attenuated EJC footprints and CTRD in abh1 flower buds, specifically in the nucleoplasmic fraction but not the cytoplasm. This suggests that ABH1 facilitates nucleoplasm-localized RNA surveillance and CTRD.
 We then tested whether the nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic footprints were derived from different transcript pools. We used the TSI metric to identify mRNAs harbouring EJCs (TSIEJC > 2) or undergoing CTRD (TSICTRD > 2) in each fraction and genotype ( and ) [ ]. Comparisons between fractions and genotypes revealed that each contained largely distinct mRNAs (Fig. and  ). Although, there appeared to be greater overlap in the cytoplasmic RNA-enriched fractions, between genotypes, than the nucleoplasmic fractions. Nonetheless, a comparable number of mRNAs harbouring EJC or undergoing CTRD were identified in the nucleoplasmic RNA-enriched fractions in WT and abh1 flower buds. This prompted us to explore transcript-specific measures of TSIEJC and TSICTRD, both of which demonstrated considerable variation in both genotypes, especially in the cytoplasmic RNA-enriched fraction (Fig. and  , left panel). Nonetheless, when we consider transcript-specific differences between genotypes in TSIEJC (∆TSIEJC) and TSICTRD (∆TSICTRD), we still observe that mRNAs had statistically larger reductions, in abh1 relative to WT, in the nucleoplasmic compared to the cytoplasm, which is consistent with the global trend (Fig. and  , right panel). Interestingly, there are some cytoplasmic mRNAs that show a large increase in abh1 relative to WT, especially in TSICTRD, which may reflect compensatory activity in the cytoplasm when ABH1 function in nucleoplasm-localized CTRD is abolished. Overall, these results support that the nuclear CBC functions in CTRD- and NMD-mediated RNA surveillance within the nucleoplasm (or perinuclear cytoplasm, see the ‘Discussion’ section) in eukaryotic cells.
We then considered the biological function and cellular localization of the encoded proteins in each category of mRNAs through GO term analysis. While there appeared to be consistent over-representation in specific GO terms across all categories, relative to the genome, they were much weaker amongst nucleoplasmic mRNAs in abh1 flower buds (Fig. and  ). Interestingly, there were specific GO terms related to biological function (e.g. response to stress, reproductive development, protein folding) and cellular compartments (e.g. endoplasmic reticulum, nucleus) that were lost in nucleoplasmic EJC- and CTRD-regulated mRNAs in abh1 flower buds, hinting at ABH1 target specificity. To explore whether this was related to protein localization, we used SUBA5 [ ] to assign a subcellular localization for each protein encoded by EJC- and CTRD-regulated mRNAs. We then evaluated ∆TSIEJC and ∆TSICTRD in nucleoplasmic- and cytoplasmic RNA-enriched fractions across cellular compartments ( ). In general, we observed distributions of ∆TSIEJC and ∆TSICTRD in each fraction and subcellular localization that resembled global trends. However, statistically significant differences could only be established for specific compartments, such as the endoplasmic reticulum (∆TSICTRD), plastid (∆TSICTRD), nucleus (∆TSIEJC and ∆TSICTRD), and extracellular space (∆TSIEJC). It was previously conjectured that target specificity of ABH1 may be guided by NMD [ ]. To investigate this, we utilized a previous study identifying splicing variants that were either suppressed or enhanced when NMD activity was attenuated as defined by being upregulated or downregulated (FDR < 0.1) with mutations in two core NMD factors, UP-FRAMESHIFT (UPF)1 and UPF3 [ ]. We then considered ∆TSIEJC and ∆TSICTRD for EJC and CTRD-regulated mRNAs in each fraction based on whether they generated NMD-suppressed, NMD-enhanced, or no NMD-triggered splice variants. Indeed, transcripts that are NMD targets, harbouring either NMD-enhanced or NMD-suppressed splice variants, showed lower TSIEJC in abh1, compared to WT, than non-NMD targets (n.d., ). On the other hand, there were no significant differences observed in TSICTRD, likely reflecting that transcripts harbouring EJC footprints are enriched for being NMD targets [ ]. Taken together, these analyses reveal that ABH1 contributes to EJC footprints and CTRD for nucleoplasm-enriched mRNAs, especially those encoding proteins involved in stress response, protein folding, and reproduction. Furthermore, there is some evidence that this ABH1 target specificity could be guided by mRNA and protein localization, as well as NMD activity.

## ABH1 stabilizes nuclear RNA and impacts miRNA-mediated cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage
To explore additional nucleoplasm- and cytoplasm-enriched ABH1-associated differences in RNA cleavage, we implemented DiffSegR on 5′ and 3′ coverage pile ups between WT and abh1 samples for each fraction. This identified 4067 and 5338 DCRs in the nucleoplasmic and cytoplasmic fractions, respectively ( ). There were thousands of sites of increased cleavage in abh1 relative to WT, especially in the nucleoplasmic fraction (Fig.  ), which is consistent with ABH1 playing a stabilizing role for RNAs in the nucleus. We investigated whether these sites of increased cleavage at mRNAs might reflect events at the TSS, PAS, exon–intron junctions, or 5′/3′ splice sites. Indeed, we mapped 925 DCRs across 356 transcripts that reflected sites of increased cleavage at, or within proximity to, these mRNA features in the nucleoplasmic fraction of abh1 flower buds relative to WT (Fig. – and ). We observed comparable patterns at each feature as was observed in nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage in WT flower buds (Fig. – ), suggesting that ABH1 is involved in guiding, or protecting from, various nuclear cleavage events. Notably, there were observed differences at the PAS, where cleavage events appeared to occur specifically within 50 nucleotides of the end of transcript annotations. This suggests some contribution for ABH1 towards PAS choice, although this could only be detected for 42 transcripts. There was also an enrichment of increased cleavage at 5′ and 3′ splice sites, as well as at 3′ exon–intron junctions. In combination, these results are consistent with ABH1 protecting nuclear pre-mRNA from cleavage during maturation and export.
 Increased cleavage in abh1 was also evident at many intergenic regions and some ncRNAs, consistent with roles of ABH1 beyond mRNA processing. With respect to ncRNA, we observed an enrichment for altered cleavage, mostly decreases, in the nucleoplasm for rRNAs ( ). Although this enrichment partly reflects relatively few annotated loci, we could observe examples of altered cleavage that suggests some contribution of ABH1 towards rRNA processing ( ). Further exploration of intergenic DCRs revealed many to be occurring at transposons, especially retroelements and those that are associated with easiRNA production (Fig. and  , and ). Examples at such loci highlighted that those 5′-P ends were predominantly detected in the nucleoplasm, however, they did not necessarily align with cleavage associated with easiRNAs based on data from ddm1 flowers ( ). In total, these results suggest that ABH1 partially contributes to easiRNA processing.
A more complex picture is evident in the cytoplasm where we observe comparable numbers of sites with increased and decreased cleavage in abh1, predominantly within exons (Fig.  ). We hypothesized that these sites should include miRNA binding sites. To test this, we used psRNATarget [ ] to predict whether a miRNA binding site was present in the surrounding 50-nucleotide region of each exonic DCR. This revealed 520 (25%) and 216 (15%) DCRs with increased and decreased abundance, respectively, in abh1 harbouring predicted miRNA binding sites, corresponding to 177 miRNAs across 218 transcripts (Fig.  and ). These sites appear to represent a combination of AGO1-dependent and independent cleavage, as the predicted binding sites were associated with both mRNA cleavage and translational repression. Interestingly, the largest number of predicted miRNA binding sites showed increased cleavage in abh1, suggesting that ABH1 may play a greater negative role in miRNA function. However, while individual examples could be found where an enriched cytoplasmic 5′-P end signal was observed within the predicted miRNA binding site (Fig.  ), there was not always a clear small RNA signal. Nevertheless, these analyses indicate that ABH1 can positively and negatively impact cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage, including at many predicted miRNA binding sites.
To investigate whether cytoplasmic DCRs in abh1 flowers correlated with perturbed miRNA levels, we quantified changes in miRNA abundance using small RNA sequencing in WT and abh1 flowers buds [ ]. We could profile 268 miRNAs in both genotypes ( ), of which 92 were associated with newly predicted miRNA binding sites across 95 mRNAs ( ). This revealed that ABH1 both positively and negatively impacts miRNA levels, which did not always align with the directionality of cleavage observed. To further investigate this, we directly compared the change in miRNA abundance with cleavage at mRNAs with predicted binding sites (Fig.  ). This revealed that in the majority of miRNA–mRNA pairs (57.2%), the change in miRNA abundance was positively correlated with the change in cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage between abh1 and WT. However, there was still a reasonable fraction (42.8%) of miRNA–mRNA pairs demonstrating an anticorrelated relationship. These analyses indicate that changes in miRNA abundance due to loss of ABH1 partially explains the changes in cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage at predicted miRNA binding sites. Whether the anticorrelated sites represent feedback regulation remains to be investigated.

## ABH1 prevents nucleoplasmic antisense degradation at mRNA-encoding loci
Since GMUCT preserves strand information, we considered the possibility of degradation emanating from the antisense strand of annotated protein-coding genes (i.e. natural antisense transcripts, NATs) [ ]. Indeed, we could observe some examples of antisense DCRs in abh1 nucleoplasmic samples relative to WT (Fig.  ). Therefore, we repeated DiffSegR analysis between WT and abh1 samples using strand-specific coverage profiles. In the nucleoplasm, we identified 947 DCRs that overlapped with features (5′ UTRs, introns, exons, and 3′ UTRs) from 412 mRNAs but were associated with the opposite strand (Fig.  and ). These antisense DCRs largely overlapped exons but many introns also. Critically, all except two represented increased cleavage in abh1 flower buds, implicating ABH1 in the prevention of antisense RNA degradation. We overlapped these antisense DCRs to annotated lncRNAs to see whether these reflected known NATs. However, only 15 nucleoplasmic antisense DCRs were mapped to 7 lncRNAs, predominantly at the 5′ UTR of mRNAs (Fig.  and ). In the cytoplasmic samples, however, only 79 DCRs in abh1 overlapped with 39 mRNA features on the opposite strand (Fig.  and ). These occurred mostly in the 3′ UTR and were also biased towards increased 5′-P ends in abh1, albeit a greater number of decreased 5′-P ends were observed here compared to the nucleoplasm. Of these, 15 antisense DCRs were mapped to 3 lncRNAs ( ). One of these lncRNAs harboured 13 of the cytoplasmic antisense DCRs, suggesting that these are relatively rare events (Fig.  ). This suggests that ABH1 prevents antisense RNA degradation, including for some NATs, primarily in the nucleus. Whether those antisense DCRs occurring at NATs (Fig. and  ) represent a distinct phenomenon from those at introns with nucleoplasm-specific cleavage (Fig. ) remains to be elucidated. However, our analysis suggests that ABH1 regulates antisense RNA decay at protein-coding mRNAs in the plant nucleoplasm.
 

## ABH1-associated cytoplasmic RNA cleavage has subtle impacts on mRNA turnover
To assess the functional significance of ABH1-associated changes in nuclear and cytosolic mRNA cleavage, we incorporated strand-specific mRNA sequencing in WT and abh1 flower buds. MSA confirmed that genotype was the primary source of variation in steady state mRNA abundance between samples (PC1 = 76%;  ). Although some within-group variation was observed along PC2 (18%), Pearson’s correlation analysis confirmed high concordance between replicates (Pearson’s r: WT = 0.95–0.99; abh1 = 0.92–0.98). We employed negative binomial generalized linear models with quasi-likelihood F-tests in edgeR [ ] to statistically assess changes in mRNA abundance (referred to as differentially expressed genes, DEGs). This revealed 596 and 530 upregulated and downregulated genes, respectively, in abh1 flower buds relative to WT ( and ). Inspection of DEGs with the largest increase (NITRILASE 2, NIT2) and decrease (DEHYDRIN LEA, LEA) hinted at a link between cleavage dynamics and steady-state mRNA levels (Fig.  ). Cross-referencing DEGs to genes harbouring DCRs—either nucleoplasm- (N-DCR) or cytoplasm-enriched cleavage (C-DCR), or nucleoplasm-enriched antisense cleavage (N-asDCR)—showed that altered cytoplasm-enriched cleavage was most often observed at DEGs (92 genes, Fig.  ). Interestingly, many mRNAs (265/412, 64%) had both nucleoplasm-enriched sense and antisense RNA cleavage in the absence of ABH1. However, most cleavage sites did not occur at DEGs, including those with the most extreme changes (NIT2, LEA), suggestive of a more subtle impact on transcript levels. We also considered whether transcripts displaying altered EJC or CTRD footprints (Fig.  ) were the result of differential mRNA abundance. However, only a small fraction of mRNAs that were EJC-bound (TSIEJC > 2) in abh1 but not WT—405 in the nucleoplasm and 545 in the cytoplasm—were also differentially expressed (18, 4.4%, and 31, 7.7%, respectively). Similarly, of the 275 nucleoplasmic and 380 cytoplasmic CTRD-regulated mRNAs (TSICTRD > 2) in abh1 but not WT, only 10 (3.6%) and 18 (4.7%) were differentially expressed. We also compared, separately, the detection of antisense mRNA with antisense cleavage ( ). In a similar manner, nucleoplasm-enriched sites of altered antisense-cleavage in abh1 flower buds rarely coincided with differentially expressed antisense mRNAs ( ). However, more than half (53%) of the nucleoplasm-enriched sites of differential antisense cleavage were from higher abundance antisense mRNAs in our dataset (top 50% abundance, ). Together, these observations are consistent with a role for ABH1 in mRNA surveillance, including regulating antisense mRNA stability independently of changes in transcription.
 We further interrogated the relationship between changes in mRNA abundance (mean fold-change in mRNA sequencing) and mRNA cleavage (mean fold-change in GMUCT) using linear regression, separately, for each category of cleavage site identified (Fig.  ). This revealed that nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage had only a weak relationship with mRNA changes, while nucleoplasm-enriched antisense cleavage had almost none. However, ABH1-associated cytoplasm-enriched cleavage changes demonstrated a modest positive relationship to mRNA abundance, accounting for ∼26% of the variation in mRNA levels in this dataset. This positive relationship likely reflects that increased transcript abundance enables greater detection of cleavage changes, which alone cannot overpower transcriptional changes. Although, clearly, this is not the case for many transcripts. To more directly interrogate impacts on mRNA stability, we combined our cytosolic GMUCT and mRNA sequencing samples to calculate the proportion of uncapped transcripts (pun) as a measure of mRNA turnover [ ], where an increase in pun reflects increased turnover normalized by changes in mRNA abundance (Fig.  ). At transcripts with nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage sites, we did not observe a statistically significant shift in pun with increased or decreased cleavage between WT and abh1 flower buds. However, at cytoplasm-enriched cleavage sites, we observed a statistically significant decrease in pun at sites of decreased cleavage and the converse-increased pun at sites of increased cleavage. Effect size estimation using Vargha-Delaney’s A suggests a similar magnitude of change in pun between nucleoplasm- and cytoplasm-enriched sites, indicating that statistical significance may be partially driven by a greater number of observed cytosolic cleavage sites. Nevertheless, collectively, these analyses suggest that ABH1-associated cleavage in the cytosol can impact mRNA turnover.
Since ABH1 is implicated in pre-mRNA splicing [ , , ], and because of the prevalence of nucleoplasm-enriched ABH1-associated DCRs occurring at splice sites, we considered whether there was an association with alternative splicing (AS). Analysis with rMATs [ ] identified 625 significant AS events, across 503 genes, of which the majority (64%) involved intron retention followed by alternative 5′ splice site selection (18%, Fig.  and ). A comparison of genes with AS in abh1 flower buds showed a limited overlap with those harbouring nucleoplasm-enriched ABH1-associated DCRs ( ). At most, 17 protein-coding mRNAs were in common, representing a small fraction of all intron-retained transcripts (17/368, 4.6%) and transcripts with differential cleavage (17/951, 1.8%). This suggests that nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage is not occurring due to AS resulting from loss of ABH1.

## Discussion
In this study, we perform subcellular GMUCT to detect genome-wide and compartment-enriched RNA cleavage in Arabidopsis. We captured extensive nucleoplasm-enriched 5′-P ends, otherwise lost in GMUCT performed on bulk RNA, at numerous classes of RNA. Furthermore, subcellular GMUCT on abh1 flower buds revealed extensive roles for the eukaryotic nuclear CBC in RNA processing and surveillance. Our observations are consistent with a role for ABH1 in stabilizing nuclear RNAs, including hundreds of mRNAs and antisense RNAs, and both positively and negatively regulating cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage, including at newly predicted miRNA binding sites (Fig.  ).
 We observed a plethora of sites with increased nucleoplasmic 5′-P ends (Fig. and  ). It is tempting to suggest that our data reflect greater RNA cleavage in the nucleus, the site of RNA synthesis. Indeed, nearly all RNA classes undergo processing in the nucleus involving at least one hydrolytic cleavage resulting in a 5′-P end [ – ]. Although, it should be noted that it is difficult to account for differences in size and composition between subcellular RNA pools. Indeed, despite nuclear RNA yields being low, they appear to represent a greater diversity of detectable RNA classes at modest sequencing depth. Nevertheless, we estimate that thousands of nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage events occur at mRNAs and ncRNAs (Fig.  ). These may reflect a variety of nuclear phenomena, including uncapped nascent transcripts and polyadenylation associated cleavage (Fig.  ), pri-miRNA processing (Fig.  ), rRNA processing (Fig.  and ), and snoRNA processing (Fig. and  ). Those uncapped nascent transcripts may represent nuclear mRNA decapping or premature RNAP II termination. Many nucleoplasm-enriched 5′-P ends arose from introns, with an enrichment at the 3′ splice site (Fig. and  ). It is possible that these represent linearized lariat RNA decay, which could be verified with subcellular GMUCT on plants lacking DEBRANCHING ENZYME1 [ ]. Nucleoplasmic 5′-P ends were also prominent from RNAs derived from intergenic regions, such as retroelement-derived RNA (Fig.  ). Although translation-dependent post-transcriptional gene silencing of retroelement-derived mRNAs has been reported in the cytoplasm [ ], we observed predominantly nucleoplasmic 5′-P ends without cytoplasmic signal (Fig.  and ). This posits nuclear cleavage of retroelement-derived RNAs as a DNA methylation-independent form of transcriptional gene silencing [ ], such as via easiRNA activity [ ]. While relatively few nucleoplasmic cleavage events occurred at easiRNA generating transposons (Fig.  ), this does not omit the possibility of regulation in trans. Furthermore, nucleoplasmic 5′-P ends did not necessarily align with easiRNA abundance ( ), consistent with easiRNAs only partially resulting in cleavage [ ]. Similarly, only a small portion of nucleoplasm-enriched DCRs were associated with RNAP IV transcription [ , ]. Nonetheless, mutant analysis by subcellular GMUCT paired with evaluation of transposition activity [ ] should clarify these unknowns.
There was also a greater occurrence of nucleoplasmic 5′-P ends from predicted enhancer regions (Fig.  ), possibly reflecting unstable eRNAs. Vertebrate-like eRNAs are thought to be rare in plants, based on the capture of gene-distal sites of bidirectional transcription of 5′-m7G-capped mRNAs [ ]. Here, the direct capture of RNA 5′-P ends suggests that eRNAs may be more prevalent, although we only detect ∼1000 such instances in flower buds. The inability to capture them as capped RNAs is consistent with rapid degradation in the nucleus, and our data suggests that the nuclear CBC contributes to this for some potential eRNAs (Fig. ). Together, this suggests that further work is warranted to establish whether plants truly harbour eRNAs that are distinct from those in vertebrates.
The observation of nucleoplasmic EJC footprints (Fig.  ) appears to support a nuclear scanning model of RNA surveillance, whereby a pioneering round of translation can occur in or at the periphery of the nucleus [ , , , ]. A less controversial interpretation of our data, and our preferred model, is that this occurs within the perinuclear cytoplasm, likely retained in our nucleoplasmic fractions, upon nuclear RNA export [ , ]. Since miRNA cleavage can occur prior to the pioneer round of translation [ ], this may also explain why 5′-P ends of some miRNA targets could be detected in the nucleoplasmic fraction (Fig. and  ). Furthermore, we observed CTRD footprints in the nucleoplasm-enriched fraction, which is dependent on the cytoplasmic XRN4 [ , ]. This suggests that CTRD might occur on newly exported RNAs bound by perinuclear ribosomes. Both EJC footprints and CTRD were impaired specifically in the nucleoplasm of Arabidopsis flower buds lacking ABH1. This is consistent with its roles in RNA surveillance and CTRD [ , ]. That nucleoplasmic CTRD footprints are attenuated in abh1 suggests that nuclear CBC-bound mRNAs can directly undergo CTRD, likely in the perinuclear cytoplasm. Additionally, cytoplasmic EJC turnover and CTRD remains intact in abh1 flower buds, possibly representing eIF4E-bound mRNAs [ , ]. Overall, these findings suggest that ABH1-dependent RNA surveillance occurs within the perinuclear region, but does not abrogate cytoplasmic RNA surveillance that may be mediated by other cap-binding proteins. This is consistent with work in human cell lines where silencing CBP80/ABH1 did not disrupt eIF4E binding [ ]. That cytoplasmic ribosome-dependent RNA surveillance remains intact suggests that translation is not completely inhibited, which would explain the viability of plants lacking CBC components [ , ]. Our data also suggests that ABH1 surveys distinct RNAs, based on the localization of the encoded protein or whether they are NMD targets (Fig. – and ). Similar observations were made in human cell lines where the nuclear CBC guides ribosome-nascent chain complexes, harbouring a signal recognition particle, to the endoplasmic reticulum for co-translational translocation [ ]. Mechanistic investigation that combines co-immunoprecipitation, or proximity labelling, with co-localization of ABH1, EJC components, and XRN4 at the nuclear periphery would further substantiate our model for perinuclear RNA surveillance by the CBC.
Loss of ABH1 resulted in increased nucleoplasmic 5′-P ends, especially at 5′ and 3′ splice sites in abh1 flower buds (Fig. – ), possibly reflecting defects in pre-mRNA splicing [ , , ]. We observed a limited overlap between AS transcripts and nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage. Since these AS events represent transcripts that reached maturity, it is plausible that differential cleavage is detected at improperly spliced pre-mRNAs that are rapidly decayed within the nucleus. Consistent with this hypothesis, nucleoplasm-enriched cleavage of sense and antisense RNA did not correlate with mRNA abundance (Fig.  ) or differential expression of sense or antisense mRNAs (Fig.  and ), which suggests that this is an active form of surveillance and not just a consequence of transcriptional changes in the absence of ABH1. However, the nature and function of these hydrolytic cleavage events remain unclear and might be further elucidated through analysis of nuclear RNA decay mutants. Our analysis posits some coordination between ABH1 and NMD factors ( ). In humans, CBP80/ABH1 can guide NMD via an interaction with UPF1 [ ]. However, this interaction was not captured in plants [ ]. Therefore, while coordinated activity is possible, a plant-specific mechanism remains unknown. Additionally, CBP20 was shown to interact with a spliceosome component, U1 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (snRNP), which itself interacts with mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation factors [ ]. Future investigations may consider whether this interaction can guide nuclear mRNA cleavage.
It was also clear that ABH1 shapes cytoplasmic cleavage of mRNAs (Fig.  ). Here, both increased and decreased cleavage was observed, indicating that ABH1 can positively and negatively shape RNA cleavage [ ]. This combination could represent confounded direct and indirect impacts of ABH1 loss, and further genetic analyses, for example by incorporating mutants lacking miRNA processing machinery, should disentangle these. We also observed that these changes in cytoplasm-enriched cleavage could impact mRNA stability, whereby increased cleavage correlated with increased turnover (Fig. ). Many of the cleavage sites were within, or near, miRNA binding sites, highlighting the role of ABH1 in miRNA function [ , ]. Unexpectedly, we did not observe widespread impairment of pri-miRNA processing in abh1 flower buds (Fig.  and ). Instead, loss of ABH1 appears to have more localized impacts on pri-miRNA processing, such as loss of cleavage at miR165a-5p as observed here and previously [ ]. Similar observations were made in Drosophila ovaries whereby CBP80 silencing did not abrogate piwi-interacting RNA precursor levels [ ]. But we still saw evidence of altered miRNA-mediated cleavage (Fig.  and ), suggesting that ABH1 could aid AGO1-miRNA loading and/or export [ ]. This is also consistent with reports that ABH1 aids co-transcriptional processing of pri-miRNAs [ ], which is tightly coordinated with export mediated by interaction with nuclear pore components [ ]. A combination of fluorescence in situ hybridization of pri-miRNAs and measuring AGO1-bound small RNA levels would substantiate these hypotheses.
Our data also suggests that ABH1 stabilizes retroelement-derived RNAs (Fig. and  , and ), implicating this protein in transposon defense as was observed in Drosophila [ ]. But it may also reflect co-option of the plant’s cellular machinery to facilitate copy-and-paste retrotransposition or, conversely, co-option of retroviral elements for host cell regulation [ , ]. Finally, we also reveal a role for the nuclear CBC in regulating the degradation of nuclear antisense RNAs at hundreds of protein-coding loci (Fig.  ). The overlap between nucleoplasm-enriched sense and antisense RNA cleavage in abh1 flower buds (Fig.  ) may also represent a loss of transcriptional fidelity thereby necessitating degradation of both RNA strands. Nevertheless, we cannot discern the functional significance of this new class of ncRNAs, but their characterization is an intriguing future research direction. Overall, subcellular GMUCT has revealed multiple new functions of the nuclear CBC in plant cells. Although the nuclear CBC subunits are largely conserved across eukaryotes, species-specific functions are observed as discussed here and previously [ ]. Therefore, future investigation into the extent to which our observations are conserved in other eukaryotic systems is warranted.
In conclusion, we present our strategy, subcellular GMUCT, to profile compartmentalized RNA cleavage across the genome. We catalogue a range of nucleoplasm- and cytoplasm-enriched cleavage sites as a resource to the community, many of which may reflect novel snoRNAs and miRNA binding sites. Subcellular GMUCT also reveals a direct involvement of ABH1 in RNA surveillance, likely during the pioneer round of translation on CBC-bound mRNAs exported into the perinuclear cytoplasm (Fig.  ). Furthermore, we catalogue that ABH1 has a widespread, protective role for nuclear RNAs while also guiding cytoplasmic mRNA cleavage for hundreds of transcripts, where the latter partly reflects its role in small RNA biogenesis and function.
