## Introduction
Transcriptomic studies have revealed that over 95 % of the mammalian transcriptome is composed of non-coding RNA sequences, including circular RNAs (circRNAs), a diverse and abundant class lacking protein-coding capacity [ ]. In 1971, the first circular RNA molecule was identified in a potato affected by spindle tuber “virus” [ ]. In 1976, Kolakofsky et al. [ ] first observed circular defective interfering RNAs by electron microscopy, and Sanger et al. [ ] identified viroids, which were described as covalently closed circRNA molecules. Several years later, circRNAs were observed in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cell lines [ ]. However, human endogenous circRNAs were not discovered until 1991 [ ]. Due to their specific structure, low abundance, and unknown functions [ ], circRNAs were initially considered aberrant splicing artifacts, arousing little interest. The advent of high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) combined with circRNA-specific bioinformatics algorithms has revealed their ubiquitous presence and functional importance.
Natural circRNAs are endogenous RNA transcripts predominantly produced by the back-splicing of pre-messenger RNAs in eukaryotic cells. Conversely, synthetic circRNAs are generated in vitro through chemical or enzymatic methods for research and therapeutic applications [ ]. The vast majority of circRNAs are expressed from known protein-coding genes. CircRNAs exhibit the following characteristics: structural stability, conservation, broad presence and expression, and tissue- and developmental stage-specific expression. Notably enriched in brain tissues and detectable in biofluids such as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood-derived exosomes, circRNAs have rapidly emerged as pivotal regulators in central nervous system (CNS) disorders [ , ]. Furthermore, the covalently closed circular structure of circRNAs confers exceptional resistance to RNase degradation, leading to extended half-lives compared with their linear counterparts [ , ]. This intrinsic stability, combined with their highly specific base-pairing interactions with target miRNAs or proteins (e.g., as molecular sponges), results in minimal off-target effects [ ]. These attributes—prolonged persistence and high target specificity—make circRNAs particularly attractive as therapeutic agents, molecular sponges, and diagnostic biomarkers [ ]. Therefore, circRNAs may represent an emerging avenue for the treatment, diagnosis, and prediction of neurologic disorders.

## The biogenesis and function of circRNAs
2.1

## The biogenesis of circRNAs
Although considerable progress has been made in elucidating the biogenesis of circRNAs, certain aspects of the mechanisms involved in this process remain incompletely understood. CircRNAs are mainly classified into three categories: exonic circular RNAs (ecircRNAs), which are formed by precursor messenger RNA back-splicing; circular intronic RNAs (ciRNAs), which typically originate from intron lariats that escape debranching; and exon–intron circular RNAs (EIciRNAs) and tRNA intronic circRNAs (tricRNAs), which are formed through a different splicing mechanism [ , , , ]. Among them, ecircRNAs are the most studied, accounting for approximately 80 % of the total intracellular circRNAs [ ]. CircRNAs are formed via back-splicing, where a 3′ splice site joins a 5′ splice site from the same or an upstream exon, creating a covalently closed loop [ , ]. The cis-regulatory elements and splicing factors, particularly spliceosomes, play essential roles in this process [ ]. Although back-splicing occurs in the nucleus, most mature intronless circRNAs are subsequently exported to and function in the cytoplasm [ , ]. The mechanisms of circRNA biogenesis mainly include: ① RNA-binding protein (RBP)-pairing; ② intron-pairing; ③ spliceosome-dependent lariat-driven splicing; and ④ tricRNA splicing, which appears in cytoplasm [ , ]. The back-splicing driven by RBPs, such as Muscleblind (MBL) [ , ], Quaking (QKI) [ ], Fused in Sarcoma [ ], and adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 1 (ADAR1) [ ], might play an essential role in regulating circRNAs biogenesis. The flanking inverted repeat elements in primates, such as Alu elements, bring the intronic regions and exon ends into close apposition through the action of proteins such as MBL and QKI. This spatial rearrangement promotes the formation of both ecircRNAs and EIciRNAs ( ). MBL regulates its own expression by promoting the formation of circMbl, a circRNA derived from its second intron. CircMbl sequesters MBL protein, creating a negative feedback loop that fine-tunes MBL availability [ , ]. QKI mediates the biogenesis of circPDIA4 via binding to introns 2 and 4 of PDIA4 pre-mRNA and back-splicing of exons 3 and 4 in gastric cancer [ ]. Alternatively, ADAR can bind to double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) and disrupt the stem-loop RNA secondary structure [ ]. Other mechanisms are shown in , and the detailed mechanisms by which RBPs regulate circRNA biogenesis are illustrated in . 

2.2

## Functions of circRNAs
The exceptional stability of circRNAs is attributed to their covalently closed circular structure, which lacks free 5′ caps and 3′ poly-A tails, thereby rendering them resistant to exonucleolytic degradation [ ]. Considering their high stability, circRNAs have significant non-coding functions. New research related to the functions of circRNAs has yielded promising discoveries in recent years.
2.2.1

## miRNA sponges or competing endogenous RNAs
Extensive studies have demonstrated that circRNAs function as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) or microRNA sponges to regulate gene expression [ , ]. According to the ceRNA hypothesis, certain RNAs function as decoys that sequester microRNAs, effectively titrating them away from their mRNA targets and integrating individual transcripts into a cohesive regulatory circuit [ , ]. Despite having fewer miRNA-binding sites than their linear counterparts, circRNAs exhibit miRNA sponging properties and act as miRNA “sponges” to perform several functions [ ]. For example, circGalntl6 alleviates oxidative stress by sequestering miR-335 and suppressing its activity [ ]. The extent to which circRNAs achieve biologically relevant miRNA sponging effects requires further investigation.

2.2.2

## Regulation of transcription and alternative splicing
Emerging evidence has revealed that circRNAs exert bidirectional transcriptional control over their host genes through diverse molecular strategies. These encompass: ① interaction with Pol II to modulate its activity; ② scaffolding of transcriptional activators or repressors; and ③ generation of R-loop structures at critical regulatory elements, thereby influencing transcriptional initiation or elongation [ , ]. Yu et al. [ ] found that circMETTL6 suppresses ovarian cancer progression by sequestering the non‐POU domain-containing octamer binding protein, thereby inhibiting growth differentiation factor 15 transcription. Another study revealed that circSMARCA5 mediates transcriptional pausing via R-loop formation at the SWI/SNF-related matrix-associated actin-dependent regulator of chromatin subfamily A member 5 (SMARCA5) locus, producing a truncated, nonfunctional protein [ ]. All the above-mentioned studies demonstrated that several circRNAs could regulate gene expression at the levels of transcription and splicing.

2.2.3

## Interactions with proteins
Under different circumstances, one circRNA may either bind to a single protein or interact with multiple proteins [ ]. For example, by activating the RBP PC4 and SFRS1-interacting protein 1 (PSIP1)/c‐Myc signaling pathway, circRNA–mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) promotes hepatocellular carcinoma cell stemness, which in turn drives tumor progression and imparts lenvatinib resistance [ ]. Ectopic circ-Foxo3 expression formed a ternary complex with cyclin-dependent kinase 2 and p21 in NIH3T3 cells, thereby repressing cell cycle progression [ ]. Numerous studies have reported that circRNAs act as protein baits or antagonists to suppress protein activity [ ]. Based on manners of function, circRNA–protein interactions can be categorized as follows: ① Altering interactions between proteins, including cementation and dissociation [ , ]: for example, circFARP1 binds directly to caveolin 1 (CAV1) in cancer-associated fibroblasts, thereby blocking its interaction with the E3 ligase zinc and ring finger 1.This inhibition stabilizes CAV1 by preventing its degradation, consequently enhancing leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) secretion [ ]. ② Blocking proteins from nucleic acid and protein interactions [ , , ]: for example, circPABPN1 blocks HuR from binding to autophagy-related gene Atg16l1 mRNA in the intestinal epithelium, thereby suppressing ATG16L1 production and autophagy [ ]. ③ Recruiting proteins (i.e., transcription factors, chromatin-modifying enzymes, and chromatin remodelers) to chromatin [ , ]: for example, circEsyt2 was found to interact with the RNA splicing factor polyC-binding protein 1 and regulate its intracellular localization [ ]. ④ Forming circRNA–protein–mRNA ternary complexes to regulate RNA stability and translation [ , ]. The functional outcome of a circRNA is largely determined by its abundance and its stoichiometric ratio with target molecules. Thus, the circRNA–protein stoichiometry must be carefully evaluated, especially given the typically low abundance of most circRNAs [ ].

2.2.4

## Other functions
With ongoing exploration of circRNAs, an increasing number of functions have been continually reported. Beyond the functions mentioned above, several circRNAs may be translated into proteins or peptides [ , ] and translocate or redistribute proteins [ , ]. A recent study revealed that the 12 → 7 circRNA, which harbors a start codon, undergoes rolling circle translation, yielding a protein product composed of multimers of the microtubule-binding repeats R1–R4 54. Synthetic circRNAs also generate many functions of natural circRNAs, such as miRNA or protein sponges [ , ]. Technologically optimized, synthetic circRNAs exhibit superior stability and translational capacity by mimicking natural circRNAs, although their immunogenic potential must be carefully evaluated [ ]. CircRNAs have multiple biological functions; however, there is a considerable gap between basic research and clinical practice, including delivery challenges and off-target effects, hindering their clinical transformation.

## Central nervous system diseases
3.1

## CircRNAs and stroke
Stroke, as defined by the World Health Organization, is a sudden onset of neurological deficits lasting 24 h or more or resulting in death, caused by brain cell death due to interrupted blood flow, either ischemic or hemorrhagic [ ]. Stroke, the third most prevalent Global Burden of Disease Level 3 cause of mortality worldwide (7·3 million), is a leading global health burden. Globally in 2021, the prevalence and incidence of stroke reached 93.8 million and 11.9 million cases, respectively [ ]. Generally, stroke cases include ischemic stroke (IS) and hemorrhagic stroke [ ]. IS is the most common type (approximately 87 % of cases) [ ], primarily resulting from arterial occlusion. IS, especially acute IS (AIS), is currently the main focus of stroke research [ ]. The ischemic penumbra, defined as functionally impaired yet structurally preserved tissue adjacent to the infarct core, is a concept central to acute ischemic stroke therapy [ ]. Salvaging this region through the restoration of blood flow within a narrow time frame is the cornerstone of treatment [ ]. Many circRNAs have been detected at higher abundances in the mammalian brain than in other analyzed tissues. CircRNAs play an essential role in modulating pathophysiological processes in AIS (see ). Thus, circRNAs are deemed potential therapeutic targets of AIS, encouraging many researchers to investigate the roles of circRNAs in AIS [ ]. 
Yang et al. [ ] found that circSCMH1 bound to the transcription factor methyl-CpG binding protein 2 (MeCP2) and then released the inhibition of MeCP2 target gene transcription, thereby contributing to neuroprotection and mitochondrial function preservation. A subsequent study revealed that CircSCMH1 facilitated vascular repair after stroke via fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO)-dependent N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation [ ]. Wang et al. [ ] revealed that circSCMH1 bound signal transducer and activator of transcription 5B to prevent its nuclear entry and downregulate kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO) and thereby enhanced mitochondrial fusion and inhibited mitophagy. Hence, circSCMH1 might serve as a novel therapeutic target in AIS. Wu et al. [ ] indicated that upregulated circTLK1 after stroke functions as a ceRNA by sequestering miR-335-3p. This sequestration derepresses the miR-335-3p target TCDD-inducible poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (TIPARP), and the subsequent TIPARP upregulation ultimately promotes neuronal injury, cerebral infarction, and neurological deficits. A compromised blood–brain barrier (BBB) facilitates the infiltration of peripheral immune cells (i.e., leukocytes) into the brain. This exacerbates the local inflammatory response, releasing cytotoxic cytokines and reactive oxygen species that amplify ischemic damage [ ]. Recent research has revealed that circ-FoxO3 suppressed mTOR complex 1(mTORC1) activity by sequestering mTOR and E2F transcription factor 1 (E2F1), thereby enhancing autophagy-mediated clearance of cytotoxic aggregates and improving BBB integrity [ ]. Moreover, other circRNAs also play important roles in the progress of AIS. Circ-HECTD1 silencing protected against ischemic brain injury in middle cerebral artery occlusion mice [ , ]. CircFOXP1 also represents a potential therapeutic target for AIS, as its protective effect against ischemic brain injury is achieved by modulating the STAT3/apoptosis signaling axis [ ]. CircOGDH acted as a ceRNA by sponging miR-5112, thereby derepressing collagen type IV α 4 chain (COL4A4) and promoting neuronal injury [ ].
CircRNAs play dual roles in stroke pathogenesis, either exacerbating neuronal injury or promoting functional recovery through diverse molecular mechanisms. Furthermore, circRNAs participate in critical signaling pathways, including nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and hypoxia-inducible factor 1α, to regulate neuroinflammation and hypoxia responses. This functional duality makes circRNAs central to stroke pathology and promising therapeutic targets, with their ultimate effects depending on specific circRNA species, cellular context, and disease stage. Although circRNAs share high sequence similarity with their linear RNA counterparts, recent advances in long-read and single-cell RNA sequencing technologies, coupled with the application of artificial intelligence, have provided new tools for investigating the spatiotemporal regulatory mechanisms of circRNAs in disease and development [ ].

3.2

## CircRNAs and Alzheimer's disease
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a common and chronic neurodegenerative disorder characterized by deficits in memory and cognitive functions [ ]. As the aging population increases, AD has become a growing burden on individuals, families, healthcare systems, and the social economy. It is widely recognized that the major pathological features of AD are the extracellular aggregation of amyloid β (Aβ) peptides originating from amyloid precursor protein (APP) and the intracellular enrichment of hyperphosphorylated tau protein in AD brains, which lead to impairments in synaptic plasticity, neurofibrillary degeneration, and neuronal loss [ , ]. Meanwhile, neuroinflammation aggravates the pathology of Aβ and the microtubule-associated protein tau [ ]. Although AD accounts for approximately 60 %–80 % of cases of dementia, curative treatment for AD remains to be explored. Strikingly, it was recently demonstrated that circRNAs were selectively expressed in the brain and differentially regulated in AD [ ], introducing new avenues into the early diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of AD.
Recent research revealed that circRNAs participated in multiple pathophysiological processes of AD, including Aβ metabolism, tau aggregation, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, autophagy, apoptosis, and neurotrophin modification (see ) [ , ]. CircRNA expression profiles, modulated by AD-related neuronal stress, exhibited significant variation across different dementia subtypes and distinct brain regions [ ]. Genetic background [ ] and sex [ ] represent additional determinants of circRNA expression profiles in patients with AD. Mo et al. [ ] revealed that circAβ-a, a circRNA harboring the Aβ-coding region of the APP gene, was translated and then processed into Aβ peptides. Subsequently, Amaya Urdánoz-Casado et al. [ ] demonstrated that circAPP (hsa_circ_0007556) expression was reduced in the entorhinal cortex of AD samples and negatively correlated with Aβ deposits. By binding to and influencing miR-1906 expression, circAPP regulates both chloride intracellular channel 1 (CLIC1) expression and its channel function, thereby controlling microglial polarization in AD [ ]. ADAR activation stimulates pathological translation products from both 12 → 10 and 12 → 7 tau circRNAs, generating aggregation-prone tau protein that may contribute to neurofibrillary tangle development in AD pathogenesis [ ]. Moreover, a plasma circRNA profile has recently been proposed to distinguish AD or mild cognitive impairment with healthy status [ ]. 
CircRNAs exert bidirectional regulatory effects in AD pathogenesis through multiple molecular mechanisms. This multifaceted regulatory capacity positions circRNAs as crucial players in AD progression, with different circRNA species either exacerbating or ameliorating neurodegenerative processes depending on their specific targets and cellular contexts. The complexity of these interactions highlights the need for further research to fully elucidate circRNA networks in AD and explore their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

3.3

## CircRNAs and Parkinson's disease
Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder with Lewy body pathology that affects multiple neurotransmitter systems, resulting in both motor impairment and frequently early non-motor dysfunction [ ]. PD is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after AD; global PD cases are projected to reach 25.2 million by 2050 [ ]. However, pathological heterogeneity and a lack of biomarkers hinder early diagnosis; moreover, widespread neurodegeneration limits treatment efficacy in later stages [ , ]. Thus, it is imperative to find more potential drugs and biomarkers for diagnosing and treating PD.
Recent studies have increasingly investigated the potential involvement of circRNAs in the pathogenesis and progression of PD, owing to their high stability and disease-specific expression patterns (see ). Wang et al. [ ] revealed that targeting the circPRDM5/miR-433-3p/HDAC6 axis may provide therapeutic benefits in PD, as this pathway critically regulates neuronal survival by modulating both apoptosis and autophagy processes. Bao et al. [ ] used RNA immunoprecipitation or RNA pull-down to explore the binding of eukaryotic initiation factor 4A3 (EIF4A3) to circDLGAP4 and high mobility group AT-hook 2 (HMGA2). Previous research revealed that the loss of circzip-2 resulted in an increase in the activity of miR-60, resulting in decreases in the expression levels of protective genes [ ]. Wang et al. [ ] indicated that hSA-SCMH1_0001 declined in brains of patients with PD and might impact its occurrence and pathogenesis, possibly positioning it as a novel biomarker for the assessment of PD severity. 
CircRNAs contribute to PD pathogenesis by regulating α-synuclein aggregation (e.g., circSNCA), dopaminergic neuron apoptosis [ ], and neuroinflammation. Their stability and brain-specific expression highlight their diagnostic/therapeutic potential.

3.4

## CircRNAs and other CNS diseases
In addition to the above diseases, circRNAs are also promising therapeutic targets and biomarkers for other CNS diseases, such as epilepsy (EP), Huntington's disease (HD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).
Recent research revealed that circRNA–miRNA–mRNA networks played pathological and functional roles in EP [ ]. Zhao et al. [ ] indicated that the expression of circSRRM4 was upregulated and had specific spatiotemporal features of EP. CircSRRM4 bound with and upregulated serine- and arginine-rich splicing factor 3 through suppressing the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, regulating pyruvate kinase M1/2 alternative splicing and ultimately evaluating aerobic glycolysis [ ]. Recent research found for the first time that the downregulated circRNAs mainly originated from the Rere gene and enriched the mitogen-activated protein kinase, dopaminergic synapse, and long-term depression pathways, affecting the development of HD [ ]. Recent research engineered circRNA-based scaffolds designed to recruit the E3 ubiquitin ligase RC3H2 into proximity with ALS-associated mutant proteins (hnRNPA1-P288S), leading to a moderate yet statistically significant reduction in their steady-state levels [ ].

## CircRNA delivery
CircRNAs exhibit durable expression, enhanced stability, and lower immunogenicity compared with mRNA delivery strategies, with no risk of DNA integration [ , ]. Thus, circRNAs represent an emerging frontier in the treatment of CNS diseases due to their highly conserved structure, specific expression in the CNS, and unique functions [ ] and are poised to become novel potential biomarkers in therapeutic applications. However, the delivery of circRNA therapeutics to the CNS is impeded by the BBB [ ]. Currently, the efficacy of circRNA delivery approaches can be enhanced via synergistic chemical modifications. Actively delivering circRNAs to regulate their expression levels is crucial for clinical application. The choice of delivery vector is dictated by the functional objective of the genetic cargo. Although established viral and non-viral platforms can deliver circRNAs, viral vectors are predominantly engineered for DNA delivery. Consequently, non-viral vectors have become the predominant focus for circRNA delivery [ ]. This preference is driven by the therapeutic utility of circRNAs, which leverages non-viral systems for efficient, stable, yet transient protein production with low immunogenicity. In contrast, DNA delivery remains a foundational research tool for persistent gene expression. However, its clinical translation faces significant challenges, including genotoxicity risks such as insertional mutagenesis and persistent off-target effects [ , , ].
4.1

## Exosomes
Exosomes, typically ranging from 30 to 150 nm in diameter, are endosome-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) [ ]. Some advantages of exosomes include their high blood stability, targeted therapy, easy absorption and fusion with recipient cells, and effective drug protection [ , , ]. Furthermore, exosomes may increase bioavailability and decrease adverse reactions due to their high targeting efficiency and biocompatibility [ , ]. Existing research has outlined the dynamics of mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosome migration and accumulation across diverse cerebral pathologies for up to 96 h [ ]. Chitosan-modified tumor exosomes not only significantly alleviated the hallmark cardiotoxicity of doxorubicin, but also maintained its potent anti-tumor effects [ ]. Thus, exosomes can potentially be utilized as diagnostic biomarkers and in therapeutic interventions for CNS diseases.
Emerging evidence underscores the therapeutic potential of exosome-delivered circRNAs for CNS disorders. For instance, engineered EVs have been developed to selectively deliver functional circRNAs, such as circSCMH1, promoting recovery from AIS in both rodent and nonhuman primate models [ ]. Similarly, exosomes from hypoxia-pretreated adipose-derived stem cells delivered circ-Rps5 or circ-Epc1 to reduced neuronal injuries and shift microglial M1/M2 polarization, thus ameliorating brain damage in AIS and AD-induced cognition impairment, respectively [ , ]. Exosomal circCMTM3 facilitates vasculogenic mimicry by inhibiting the CNOT4-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of STAT5A/B, leading to their stabilization. Furthermore, circCMTM3 acts as a molecular scaffold for activated STAT5A, which initiates a transcriptional program and establishes a positive feedback loop with SRSF1 to sustain tumor progression [ ]. CircOGDH acts as a ceRNA by binding to and sequestering miR-5112. This sponging action leads to the upregulation of the miR-5112 target gene collagen type IV alpha 4 chain (COL4A4), which subsequently enhances neuronal damage following ischemic stroke ( ) [ , ]. The clinical development of exosome-based therapeutics faces several key challenges, including the lack of standardized isolation methods, inherent heterogeneity, low drug loading efficiency, as well as limitations in scalable manufacturing [ ]. Although challenges in production cost and loading efficiency persist for exosome-based delivery [ ], they have not precluded its significant potential, driving extensive research aimed at addressing these issues. 

4.2

## Lipid nanoparticles
Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), smaller than 100 nm, primarily comprise the following components: ionizable cationic lipids, cholesterol, phospholipids, and polyethylene glycol-lipids [ ]. The types of LNPs include solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), cationic lipid amphiphiles, and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) [ ]. SLNs provide a solid lipid matrix for controlled release, whereas NLCs utilize a mixed lipid core to optimize drug loading and stability. Cationic lipid amphiphiles, which are essential for gene therapy, facilitate nucleic acid complexation and cellular uptake through their positive charges [ , ]. The mRNA COVID-19 vaccines served as a monumental proof-of-concept and a technology accelerator. They demonstrated that non-viral delivery of exogenous RNA for in vivo protein production is feasible, safe, and effective on a global scale [ ]. After being used for mRNA-1273 and BNT162b2 severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 mRNA vaccines [ , ], LNPs are increasing in popularity and being employed in the delivery of multiple circRNA therapeutics and vaccines. The US Food and Drug Administration has approved more than ten drugs delivered by LNPs, such as a small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting transthyretin and doxorubicin [ ]. The characteristics of LNPs include their stability, lipophilic nature, transfection efficacy, and lower toxicity, which are beneficial to targeting the brain [ ]. LNP drug delivery systems are an emerging method of circRNA delivery owing to their protection from nucleases; easy cellular uptake, as they bypass the phospholipid membrane; low immunogenicity; endosomal trapping avoidance; and avoidance of the mononuclear phagocyte system [ ].
A recent study demonstrated that a lyophilized, mannose-modified circRNA-LNP vaccine enhances stability and induces potent immune responses by driving continuous antigen availability, promoting a robust humoral immune response including the generation of T follicular helper cells, germinal center B cells, and long-lived plasma and memory B cells in mice [ ]. Li et al. [ ] revealed that circRNA-LNP therapy could exert potent antitumor effects by inducing innate and adaptive immune responses in vivo. The LNP-based delivery of circPRKD3 effectively suppresses glioblastoma (GBM) by inhibiting tumor cell growth and reprogramming macrophages via the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein C (HNRNPC)/interleukin 6 signal transducer (IL6ST)/STAT3 axis. This RNA immunotherapy remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and potently enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment [ ]. In vivo studies demonstrated that LNP-circRNARBD elicits innate immune responses comparable to those of 1mΨ-mRNA-LNP but notably weaker than the responses induced by transfected RNAs ( ) [ ]. As the most effective and preferred nanocarriers for circRNAs, LNP-encapsulated circRNAs target the CNS and thus possess the potential utility for diagnosing and treating CNS diseases.

4.3

## Other strategies
In addition to the above methods, some studies have investigated other methods, such as circRNA vaccines. CircRNA vaccines are more efficient in expressing antigens and eliciting strong immune responses than are mRNA vaccines [ ]. Moreover, compared with mRNA vaccines, circRNA vaccines offer superior stability for storage and delivery, a more favorable safety profile with lower inherent immunogenicity and cytotoxicity, and prolonged efficacy due to sustained antigen production [ , ]. Considering that circRNAs are RNA-based and have mRNA-like encapsulation properties, other approaches to mRNA delivery have reference value for circRNA delivery [ ]. For example, virus-like particles (VLPs) are non-infectious nanospheres composed of viral structural proteins but devoid of viral genetic material. In a delivery system, VLPs package circRNA as a pseudoviral genomic cargo, leveraging intrinsic viral machinery for efficient cell entry and transgene expression [ , ].

4.4

## BBB penetration strategies
Current strategies for circRNA delivery to the brain primarily focus on overcoming the BBB through several approaches, such as the use of exosomes and LNPs. Exosome-based delivery systems harness inherent targeting capabilities and demonstrate favorable biosafety profiles with enhanced tissue retention. However, their clinical translation faces substantial challenges in scalable production and efficient therapeutic loading [ , ]. Surface modification and parent cell preconditioning enhances the brain targeting of exosomes, such as rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) [ ] and T7 peptide-decorated exosome [ ]. Based on multiple research findings, EVs can be functionalized with RVG peptides that specifically bind to acetylcholine receptors. This surface modification enables the targeted delivery of circSCMH1 to diverse cell types in the brain. Furthermore, RVG-decorated EVs demonstrate significantly enhanced BBB penetration capability and improved brain tissue retention [ , ]. LNPs constitute a major delivery platform characterized by high circRNA loading capacity and scalable production. However, their application is constrained by a short circulation half-life (typically 2–6 h in vivo), dose-dependent hepatotoxicity, and the need for targeting ligands (e.g., Angiopep-2) or BBB modulation techniques to achieve effective brain delivery [ , , , ]. The rational design of LNP formulations combined with surface modifications significantly enhances brain-targeted delivery and prolongs the retention time of nanoparticles [ , ]. A therapeutically engineered extracellular vesicle platform, M-Ang2-EVs, was developed through the integration of dual bioengineering strategies. This construct combined Angiopep-2 functionalization for precise injury site targeting with melatonin preconditioning of M2 microglia to enhance therapeutic payload, resulting in superior targeting specificity and amplified regenerative potential [ ]. Moreover, several innovative approaches facilitate CNS drug delivery, such as physical disruption (e.g., electrical stimulation, photo-stimulation, and focused ultrasound (FUS)), biological vectors (e.g., receptor-mediated transport and cell-penetrating peptides), and cell-based carriers (e.g., using cells in vivo to design cell membrane-based biomimetic nanoparticles). Whereas physical disruption non-selectively enhances permeability for high-dose delivery, receptor-mediated transcytosis facilitates the targeted transport of specific therapeutics, and biomimetic nanoparticles provide a versatile platform for diverse cargo with improved pharmacokinetics. Physical methods primarily risk localized, procedure-dependent damage, such as inflammation or hemorrhage. In contrast, biological and biomimetic approaches are associated with more systemic risks, including immune responses and off-target effects due to their inherent biological interactions [ ].
Exosomes excel in long-term safety, LNPs in high payload delivery, and FUS in localized BBB opening. The choice depends on the therapeutic goal (e.g., gene editing/protein replacement) and disease type (e.g., tumors/neurodegeneration).

## Discussion and future perspectives
CircRNAs are intimately involved in the progression of CNS diseases (e.g., AIS, AD, and PD) via numerous molecular mechanisms. Their structural stability, coupled with tissue- and developmental stage-specific expression, positions circRNAs as promising candidates for novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in CNS diseases, potentially revolutionizing modern medicine and pharmaceutics [ ]. Below, we discuss key advances and challenges in basic research, technological development, and clinical translation.
5.1

## Basic research
Recent advances have further identified novel circRNA subtypes—including those derived from previously overlooked genomic regions (e.g., intronic circRNAs) and cell-type-specific circular isoforms—opening unprecedented avenues for precision medicine target discovery [ ]. CircRNAs can be specifically quantified using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) with back-splice junction-spanning primers. However, potential false positives from alignment artifacts, repeat sequences, or template switching during reverse transcription require pretreatment with RNase R to degrade linear RNAs prior to analysis [ , ]. The integration of RNA-seq with qRT-PCR and Northern blot quantification represents a robust strategy for comprehensive circRNA validation [ ]. Moreover, to enhance endogenous circRNA biogenesis, circular RNA-promoting RNAs can be employed to optimize exon circularization by supplementing reverse complementary matches flanking target pre-mRNAs, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for diseases associated with deficient circRNA expression [ ].

5.2

## Technological development: detection and delivery
Despite progress in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, circRNA detection remains challenging due to their high sequence similarity to linear RNAs and the low abundance of brain-derived circRNAs in biofluids (e.g., blood). Although short-read sequencing struggles to distinguish circRNAs from linear RNAs due to the presence of exons (approximately 150 nt average length), long-read sequencing technologies, especially Nanopore long-read sequencing (up to 1000 nt) have enabled comprehensive profiling of full-length circRNA isoforms, significantly improving reconstruction efficiency and accuracy [ , , ]. Benchmarking studies have indicated that although circRNA detection methods achieve robust accuracy, their sensitivity varies widely across tools [ ]. To bridge this gap, CIRI-deep was developed as a deep learning framework for robust circRNA regulatory prediction across diverse RNA-seq datasets [ ]. Therefore, integrating high-sensitivity detection tools with pseudo-references or comparative filtration algorithms provides a balanced strategy for precise circRNA identification and quantification [ ].
Despite advancements in circRNA synthesis and purification methods, inefficient output and complex purification increase the costs of circRNA-based therapeutics [ ]. Moreover, the self-circularization efficiency of pre-circRNA is negatively correlated with transcript length, structural complexity, and concentration [ ]. Persistent challenges in circRNA synthesis span scalable manufacturing, tissue-specific delivery, and translational control—where auto-circularizing RNA [ ] and complete self-splicing intron for RNA circularization [ ] have demonstrated preclinical efficacy.
The development of effective delivery vehicles for circRNAs has also encountered some dilemmas, including extracellular degradation, BBB permeability, intracellular trafficking, and target specificity. Moreover, the quality control methods and storage and transport conditions of exosomes have not been standardized [ ]. Although LNPs have high encapsulation efficiency and can be stored long-term, their hepatocyte propensity and low rate of endosomal escape limit their application [ , ]. Current strategies focus on multifunctional/smart carrier design, integrated delivery strategies, and advanced evaluation protocols. FUS-mediated BBB opening combined with long-circulating NPs enables precise brain genome editing, achieving targeted CRISPR-Cas9/sgRNA delivery via systemic administration [ ]. Peptide-functionalized LNPs enhance mRNA delivery to the brain while reducing hepatic uptake following systemic administration in mice [ ].

5.3

## Clinical translation: from bench to bedside
Clinical research serves as a critical bridge in translating fundamental discoveries into therapeutic applications. Circular genomics has pioneered the first blood-based biomarker test leveraging brain-enriched circRNAs to predict patient responses to antidepressant therapies. Xu et al. [ ] developed a surface-enhanced Rahman scattering-chiral anisotropy nanobiosensor for ultrasensitive blood-based detection of the lung cancer biomarker circSATB2, enabling early diagnosis. Due to the limited availability of brain tissue biopsies in CNS disorders, circRNA detection in blood (plasma/serum) and CSF has emerged as a promising alternative. Liquid biopsy provides an effective and minimally invasive approach for noninvasive detection and monitoring of CNS diseases [ , ]. However, preanalytical variability (e.g., degradation, contamination) and low abundance of brain-derived circRNAs in biofluids pose significant challenges. Emerging evidence reveals multiple degradation pathways, including Argonaute 2 (AGO2)-mediated cleavage guided by perfectly complementary miRNAs, m6A-dependent decay via YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA-binding protein F2-recruited RNase P/MRP complex, and stress-induced degradation through the regulator of nonsense transcripts 1 (UPF1)–G3BP stress granule assembly factor 1 (G3BP1) complex during stress granule formation [ , ]. Reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography analysis demonstrated temperature-dependent degradation of circRNA samples, with detectable circular structure breakdown observed after (1) 7-day storage at 4 °C and 25 °C and (2) 24-h incubation at 37 °C when compared with freshly prepared controls [ ]. For optimal circRNA stability, samples should be transported on dry ice and stored at −80 °C. Plasma should be collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes. Current studies demonstrate that patient-derived organoids serve as physiologically relevant platforms for evaluating circRNA mechanisms and therapeutic efficacy [ , ]. By integrating rigorous pre-analytical controls with CNS-mimicking models (organoids), circRNA biomarker pipelines can overcome biofluid limitations. Immediate priorities include standardizing CSF collection protocols and validating organoid–CSF circRNA correlations in multicenter studies. Notably, a novel circRNA delivery system (circSCMH1@LNP1) was engineered for direct intranasal administration, demonstrating targeted delivery of circSCMH1 to ischemic brain regions with enhanced efficiency and safety profiles [ ]. It has entered the clinical trial preparation and industrial translation phase, with anticipated market approval within 3–5 years to benefit stroke patients. Feng et al. [ ] revealed that intrahippocampal adeno-associated virus (AAV)-delivered ds-cRNAs effectively suppress protein kinase R overactivation, reducing neuroinflammation and Aβ plaques with minimal toxicity. Systemic AAV-PHP.eB administration achieves whole-brain ds-cRNA delivery across the BBB in AD mice. However, whether the dosages of these methods meet the treatment requirements needs to be considered and tested. It is also crucial to reduce circRNA expression in non-target tissues and cells. With the development of basic research and biotechnology, more circRNA functions and safe therapeutic strategies will be discovered and formulated, facilitating circRNA clinical transformation in diagnosing and treating CNS diseases.
