## ABSTRACT
The aggregation and transmission of SNCA/α-synuclein (synuclein, alpha) is a hallmark pathology of Parkinson disease (PD). PLK2 (polo like kinase 2) is an evolutionarily conserved serine/threonine kinase that is more abundant in the brains of all family members, is highly expressed in PD, and is linked to SNCA deposition. However, in addition to its role in phosphorylating SNCA, the role of PLK2 in PD and the mechanisms involved in triggering neurodegeneration remain unclear. Here, we found that PLK2 regulated SNCA pathology independently of S129. Overexpression of PLK2 promoted SNCA preformed fibril (PFF)-induced aggregation of wild-type SNCA and mutant SNCAS129A. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of PLK2 attenuated SNCA deposition and neurotoxicity. Mechanistically, PLK2 exacerbated the propagation of SNCA pathology by impeding the clearance of SNCA aggregates by blocking macroautophagic/autophagic flux. We further showed that PLK2 phosphorylated S1098 of DCTN1 (dynactin 1), a protein that controls the movement of organelles, leading to impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion. Furthermore, genetic suppression of PLK2 alleviated SNCA aggregation and motor dysfunction in vivo. Our findings suggest that PLK2 negatively regulates autophagy, promoting SNCA pathology, suggesting a role for PLK2 in PD.

## Introduction
Parkinson disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease following Alzheimer disease (AD), with clinical symptoms of motor, nonmotor, and cognitive deficits, affecting approximately 10 million people worldwide [ ]. Clinicopathologically, the aggregation of SNCA to form Lewy bodies (LBs) and Lewy neurites is recognized as the diagnostic gold standard [ , ]. Moreover, abnormal SNCA aggregates are found in the brains of most patients with sporadic PD, multiple system atrophy and dementia with LBs, as well as in those with familial PD [ ], suggesting that the association of SNCA with neurodegenerative diseases extends beyond a rare gene variant.
There is increasing evidence that SNCA spreads along anatomical networks in the brain, further facilitating SNCA pathology and toxicity [ ]. Indeed, increased concentrations of SNCA oligomers have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid and blood of PD patients [ ], suggesting that SNCA oligomers may be secreted from neurons. In addition, neurons transplanted into the brains of PD patients presented the same SNCA pathology, suggesting that the pathology travels from the host neurons to the transplanted neurons [ ]. Importantly, the injection of brain homogenates from PD patients into mouse brains also produced pathological aggregation of SNCA and neuronal degeneration [ , ]. Pathological SNCA seeds in the form of preformed fibril (PFF) produced from recombinant SNCA monomers can generate and propagate pathological LBs and trigger neuronal death, leading to motor dysfunction in mice [ , ]. Therefore, it is currently believed that the accumulation of SNCA to form oligomers in pathological states, further fibrotic assembly into LBs and subsequent propagation of LBs pathology are important causes of neuronal insults.
PLK2 (polo like kinase 2), a serine/threonine kinase containing a substrate-bound C-terminal Polo-Box domain and an N-terminal kinase catalytic domain [ , ], plays a critical role in the regulation of the cell cycle [ ], meiosis [ ], cell differentiation [ ], and neuronal growth [ ]. In the central nervous system, PLK2 facilitates the degradation of the rap guanosine triphosphatase-activating protein SIPA1L/SPAR during epileptiform activity in hippocampal neurons, leading to the loss of dendritic spines [ , ]. Importantly, PLK2 is expressed at much higher levels in the central nervous system than other members of the family and is one of the major kinases that phosphorylates SNCA S129 [ , ]. Functionally, S129 phosphorylation strengthens the affinity of SNCA protofibrils for the receptor and further promotes SNCA pathology [ ]. The overexpression of PLK2 in yeast and mammalian cells enhances SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity [ ]. In addition, increased PLK2 protein levels and SNCA oligomer formation have been detected in a variety of rodent and primate PD animal models [ ]. Interestingly, PLK2 expression is also elevated in PD, AD, Lewy body disease, and aged primate brains [ , , ]. However, in addition to the phosphorylation of SNCA, the role of PLK2 and the mechanisms of PLK2-mediated neuronal insult in PD remain unclear.
In the present study, we demonstrated that PLK2 is able to mediate SNCA aggregation and propagation in an SNCA S129-independent manner. The overexpression of PLK2 promoted but the knockdown of Plk2 alleviated PFF-induced SNCA pathology in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, PLK2 phosphorylated the motor protein DCTN1 (dynactin 1) at the S1098 site to suppress the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, leading to autophagy dysfunction. Our study provides evidence that PLK2 impairs the autophagic degradation of SNCA via the phosphorylation of DCTN1, which contributes to the promoting effects of PLK2 on SNCA pathology.

## Results

## PLK2 promotes SNCA PFF seeding independent of S129
Administration of exogenous PFF induces endogenous SNCA aggregation and propagation to form pathological SNCA, leading to neuronal death, which is recognized as a seeding effect of SNCA [ , ]. Pathological SNCA is characterized by the phosphorylation of SNCA at S129 (p-SNCA) [ ]. We overexpressed PLK2 with a pCDH-PLK2-Flag-ZsGreen nonfusion vector carrying green fluorescent protein and packaged it for lentiviral neuronal infection (LV PLK2-Flag). Immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that elevated PLK2 protein levels exacerbated pathological SNCA formation in primary cortical neurons 10 days after PFF exposure ( , Data S1). In addition, overexpression of PLK2 increased the formation of Triton X-100 (TX)-insoluble (TX-insoluble) SNCA and p-SNCA ( , Data S1). p-SNCA protofibrils are more susceptible to receptor binding to amplify SNCA pathology [ ]. To investigate whether this process is dependent on the effect of PLK2 on SNCA phosphorylation, we constructed a cell line that stably expresses EGFP-SNCAS129A in HEK293 cells to monitor pathological SNCA aggregation. The overexpression of PLK2 promoted PFF-induced SNCA aggregation via S129A mutants ( , Data S1), suggesting that PLK2 mediates SNCA aggregation via an SNCA S129-independent mechanism. In addition, PLK2 overexpression induced more cell death in primary cortical neurons 14 days after PFF treatment, as determined by propidium iodide (PI) staining ( , Data S1). These data suggest that PLK2 acts independently of S129 to promote pathological SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity.

 

## PLK2 deficiency attenuates PFF-induced pathological SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity
Since elevated PLK2 protein promotes SNCA pathology, we next investigated the effects of suppressing Plk2 expression on SNCA pathology. We designed three small hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) to silence Plk2 expression. shPlk2-2# and shPlk2-3# efficiently decreased PLK2 protein levels (Figure S1A, B, Data S11). Accordingly, we selected these two shRNAs for subsequent experiments. Pretreatment with Plk2 shRNAs for 3 days effectively reduced PFF-induced pathological SNCA, as determined by anti-p-SNCA immunostaining in primary cortical neurons 10 days after PFF treatment ( , Data S2). Consistent with previous studies [ ], PFF induced the formation of filamentous-like aggregates, as shown by thioflavine S (ThS) staining (Figure S2A). PLK2 deficiency significantly restrained the formation of fibrillar SNCA aggregates induced by PFF (Figure S2B, C, Data S12). Similarly, the suppression of Plk2 expression decreased the formation of TX-insoluble p-SNCA and SNCA ( , Data S2). The number and morphology of neuronal dendritic branches were determined via MAP2 (microtubule associated protein 2) staining [ , ]. Fourteen days after PFF treatment, the number of dendritic branches in primary cortical neurons decreased significantly, and a large number of pathological p-SNCA aggregates formed ( , Data S2). Moreover, silencing Plk2 expression partially restored the dendrite number and decreased the number of pathological SNCA aggregates ( , Data S2). To further determine the protective effect of PLK2 deficiency on PFF-treated neurons, we examined the generation of cleaved CASP3 (caspase 3) and the release of LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) 14 days after PFF administration. Decreases in PLK2 expression reduced PFF-induced CASP3 activation as well as LDH release (Figure S1C-E, Data S11). Finally, we statistically determined the number of dead neurons via PI staining and showed that PLK2 deficiency reduced neuronal death ( , Data S2). Taken together, these data demonstrate that PLK2 deficiency can reduce PFF-induced SNCA aggregation as well as neurotoxicity.

 

## TC-S7005, an inhibitor of PLK2, reduces PFF-induced pathological SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity
PLK2 is an evolutionarily conserved serine/threonine kinase that plays an important role in the regulation of mitosis, cell differentiation and neurodevelopment [ ]. TC-S7005 is a potent and selective PLK2 inhibitor (IC50, 4 nM) with selectivity for PLK3 and PLK1 (IC50 values are 24 and 214 nM, respectively) [ ]. We used a 10 nM concentration for the subsequent assays. In primary cortical neurons that were treated with PFF, pre- and continuous treatment with TC-S7005 significantly reduced the levels of filamentous-like SNCA aggregates, as determined by immunostaining ( and S2D, E, Data S3 and S12) and immunoblot analysis ( , Data S3) 10 days after PFF administration. Morphological analysis of primary cortical neurons revealed that TC-S7005 pretreatment alleviated the reduction in the number of dendrites 14 days after PFF treatment ( , Data S3). Moreover, TC-S7005 pretreatment decreased the activation of CASP3 ( , H, Data S3) and reduced LDH release ( , Data S3). In addition, TC-S7005 reduced PFF-induced neuronal death ( , Data S3). These data suggest that the PLK2 inhibitor TC-S7005 reduces pathological SNCA formation and alleviates PFF-induced neurotoxicity.

 

## PLK2 deficiency prevents DA neuronal deficits in the SNCA PFF mouse model
We demonstrated that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of PLK2 attenuates PFF-induced neurotoxicity in vitro. We wondered whether PLK2 deficiency could prevent PFF-induced dopaminergic (DA) neuronal degeneration. We stereotactically injected SNCA PFF into the striatum of the mice [ , , ]. One month prior to the injection of PFF, rAAV-U6-shPlk2-CMV-EGFP-WPRE-PA (AAV-shPlk2) or rAAV-U6-scr. shRNA-CMV-EGFP-WPRE-PA (AAV scr. shRNA) was stereotaxically injected into the striatum ( ). Striatal injection of AAV-shPlk2 (AAV scr. shRNA was used as a control), which was highly efficient and highly expressed 1 month after injection, as indicated by EGFP fluorescence (Figure S3A). Additionally, one month after adeno-associated virus injection, i.e., at the start of SNCA PFF (t = 0) injection, immunoblot analysis with anti-PLK2 and anti-SNCA antibodies in striatal tissues revealed that, compared with the control, AAV-shPlk2 significantly inhibited PLK2 expression without affecting the protein levels of SNCA (Figure S3B-E, Data S13). Six months after the injection of PFF, the mice were subjected to behavioral tests. Compared with those in the PBS group, PFF-injected mice presented locomotor defects and reduced limb grip strength; however, the injection of AAV shPlk2 significantly improved locomotor function and potentiated grip strength in SNCA PFF-injected mice ( , Data S4). Pathological SNCA, which is induced by PFF propagates from the striatum to the substantia nigra and causes degenerative death of DA neurons [ ]. In the mice that were injected with PFF presented a large number of p-SNCA-positive aggregates that were widespread in the brain, whereas presilencing Plk2 expression limited the formation of p-SNCA (Figure S3F, G, Data S13). Immunoblot analysis also revealed that knockdown of Plk2 reduced TX-insoluble p-SNCA and SNCA aggregation (Figure S3H, I, Data S13). We next quantified the number of DAergic neurons via TH (tyrosine hydroxylase) staining. As previously reported by other investigators [ ], a significant loss of DA neurons was observed 6 months after the injection of PFF; however, AAV-shPlk2 strongly protected DA neurons from PFF-induced neuronal death ( , Data S5). In SNCA PFF-treated mice, a large number of p-SNCA-positive aggregates were observed in TH neurons, whereas the injection of AAV-shPlk2 significantly attenuated the number of p-SNCA-positive aggregates ( , Data S5). Immunoblot analysis also revealed that PLK2 deficiency alleviated pathological SNCA aggregation in the midbrain and decreased TH protein levels ( , Data S5). Thus, our data suggest that silencing Plk2 represses pathological SNCA aggregation and DA neuron loss in the PFF mouse model.

 

## PLK2 contributes to SNCA pathology by negatively regulating autophagy
We have shown that PLK2 promotes SNCA PFF-induced aggregation of S129A mutants ( ), suggesting that, in addition to phosphorylation, another pathway by which PLK2 regulates SNCA pathology exists. Autophagy is the most prominent way to clear SNCA aggregates [ ]. In primary cortical neurons, overexpression of PLK2 did not affect soluble SNCA monomer levels (Figure S4A, B, Data S14), suggesting that PLK2 is involved mainly in the regulation of SNCA aggregation.
In HEK293 (Figure S4C, D, Data S14) or SH-SY5Y ( , Data S6) cells, overexpression of PLK2 increased the protein levels of LC3-II compared with those in the control cells that were transfected with empty vectors. However, after the cells were treated with the V-ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (Baf A1), which inhibits autophagosome degradation [ ], the protein levels of LC3-II were similarly high in the PLK2-overexpressing cells ( and S4C, D, Data S6 and S14), suggesting that overexpression of PLK2 leads to autophagy dysfunction without affecting the synthesis of autophagosomes. Interestingly, SQSTM1/p62 (sequestosome 1), an autophagy receptor that interacts with cargo and phagophores (precursors to autophagosomes), ultimately being delivered to the lysosome for degradation, exhibited the same changes as LC3-II did ( ), suggesting an impairment of autophagic flux. Overexpression of PLK2 resulted in the accumulation of cytoplasmic undegraded autophagosomes, which were detected via transmission electron microscopy ( , Data S6). Moreover, there was greater accumulation of autophagosomes in neurons in the striatum of mice striatally injected with rAAV-hsyn-PLK2-3×Flag-EGFP (AAV PLK2) than in those striatally injected with control rAAV-hsyn-EGFP (AAV EGFP), according to immunohistochemical staining ( , Data S6) or immunoblot analysis (Figure S4E, F, Data S14), suggesting that PLK2 negatively regulates autophagy. Importantly, one month after striatal injection of AAV PLK2, subsequent injection of SNCA PFF into the striatum induced more pathological SNCA ( , Data S6) two months after injection of PFF.

 

## PLK2 deficiency enhances autophagy to facilitate the clearance of SNCA aggregates
We showed that PLK2 impairs autophagic flux and promotes the formation of pathological SNCA aggregates. We wondered whether Plk2 knockdown can improve autophagy to clear SNCA aggregates.
As SQSTM1 is a substrate of autophagy, we used SQSTM1 to monitor autophagy levels in SNCA PFF-treated primary cortical neurons. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that SQSTM1 formed massive puncta in primary cortical neurons 7 days after PFF treatment, whereas Plk2 knockdown decreased PFF-induced SQSTM1 aggregation ( , Data S7). Immunoblot analysis also revealed that Plk2 knockdown decreased the accumulation of the autophagosome marker LC3-II and the autophagic substrate SQSTM1 ( , Data S7). To further verify that Plk2 knockdown promotes the degradation of pathological SNCA via autophagy, we used the lysosomal inhibitor ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) to assess the efficacy of shPlk2 on SNCA degradation [ ]. In primary cortical neurons that were treated with PFF, PFF induced SNCA pathology, as indicated by p-SNCA staining ( , Data S7). Moreover, Plk2 knockdown decreased SNCA pathology ( ). However, NH4Cl treatment greatly increased SNCA pathology ( ). Similar results were obtained via immunoblot analysis, which revealed that shPlk2 decreased the levels of insoluble SNCA and p-SNCA oligomers, but the inhibition of lysosomal activity by NH4Cl blocked the shPlk2-induced decrease in SNCA aggregation ( , Data S7). Taken together, our data suggest that Plk2 knockdown potentiates lysosomal degradation of pathologic SNCA.

 

## PLK2 impedes autophagosome-lysosome fusion to disrupt autophagic flux
The process of autophagy involves autophagy initiation, vesicle encapsulation of substrate material to form autophagosomes, autophagosome-lysosome fusion and substrate degradation [ ]. To further determine the impact of PLK2 on the process of autophagy, we examined the PRKAA/AMPK (protein kinase AMP-activated catalytic subunit alpha), MTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase) and ULK1 (unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1) signaling pathways, which play important roles in regulating autophagy initiation [ , ]. Overexpression of PLK2 did not alter the phosphorylation of PRKAA, ULK1, MTOR or the MTOR substrate RPS6KB/p70S6K (ribosomal protein S6 kinase B), suggesting that PLK2 does not influence the initiation of autophagy regulated by PRKAA, ULK1 or MTOR (Figure S5A, B, Data S15). However, immunostaining revealed that overexpression of PLK2 induced the colocalization of SQSTM1- and LC3-positive puncta ( , Data S8), suggesting that PLK2 does not affect the recognition of the autophagosome cargo. We next used mCherry-EGFP-LC3 as a reporter to monitor autophagic flux, as EGFP fluorescence is quenched under acidic conditions in lysosomes after the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes [ ]. More yellow dots formed in PLK2-overexpressing cells than in empty vector-transfected cells ( , Data S8), suggesting that the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes was impaired. In cells subjected to starvation with Earle’s balanced salt solution (EBSS), which induces autophagy, more yellow and red dots were formed, indicating increased formation of autophagosomes (yellow dots containing both mCherry and EGFP signals) and autolysosomes (red dots) upon autophagy induction ( ). Moreover, more red dots were observed, suggesting complete autophagic flux after starvation in cells that were transfected with the Flag vector. However, after starvation, more yellow dots were observed in cells overexpressing PLK2 ( ), suggesting that PLK2 impaired the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes. Moreover, in cells transfected with PLK2, the colocalization of the lysosomal protein LAMP1 (lysosomal associated membrane protein 1) and autophagosomal LC3 decreased during starvation, further suggesting that autophagosome-lysosome fusion was impaired ( , Data S8). Furthermore, in the cells that were transfected with the kinase-activated mutant Flag-PLK2T239D, the accumulation of SQSTM1 and LC3 was observed, similar to that in the cells transfected with wild-type PLK2 ( ). However, overexpression of the kinase-dead Flag-PLK2D223N mutant did not repress autophagy ( ), suggesting that the negative regulation of autophagy by PLK2 depends on its kinase activity. Thus, our results suggest that PLK2 negatively regulates autophagy by restraining autophagosome-lysosome fusion.

 

## PLK2 phosphorylates DCTN1 at T481 and S1098
We have shown that PLK2 promotes SNCA pathology by impairing autophagic flux, which depends on its kinase activity. We wondered which substrate (s) of PLK2 are involved in this process. We performed binding assays to identify potential PLK2-binding proteins via immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry/IP-MS. Among the candidates ( ), one of the proteins, DCTN1 (dynactin 1), was verified to interact with PLK2 in cells expressing EGFP-DCTN1 and Flag-PLK2 via coimmunoprecipitation assays with either anti-Flag or anti-GFP antibodies ( , Data S9). Furthermore, we carried out in vitro affinity-isolation assays in a cell-free system using purified recombinant GST-PLK2 and His-DCTN1 fusion proteins produced from E. coli. PLK2 directly bound to DCTN1 ( , Data S9). Cellular experiments also revealed that mCherry-PLK2 was codistributed with EGFP-DCTN1 (Figure S6).

 To determine whether PLK2 phosphorylates DCTN1, we captured EGFP-DCTN1 from HEK293T cell lysates with an anti-DCTN1 antibody in the presence or absence of a Flag-PLK2 overexpression plasmid and examined the immunoprecipitants via a phospho-(Ser/Thr) antibody. The phosphorylation of DCTN1 was increased with PLK2 expression ( , Data S9). However, the phosphorylation of DCTN1 was abolished when the immunoprecipitates were treated with lambda protein phosphatase ( ). Using MS, we identified 6 potential sites after comparing the phosphorylation sites between cells that were transfected with or without PLK2 ( ). To further identify the specific sites at which DCTN1 is phosphorylated by PLK2, we mutated each S or T to an A of those 6 potential sites and cotransfected each mutant with EGFP-DCTN1 into cells. Among them, the phosphorylation of the T481A or S1098A mutant of DCTN1 greatly decreased, suggesting that T481A and S1098A are phosphorylation sites of PLK2 ( , Data S9).

## DCTN1S1098A promotes autophagosome-lysosome fusion to alleviate SNCA pathology
To further determine the effects of the phosphorylation of DCTN1 by PLK2, we constructed a DCTN1-knockout (KO) HeLa cell line via CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, in which the DCTN1 protein was eliminated (Figure S7A, B, Data S16). In DCTN1-wild-type cells, starvation with EBSS for 1 h induced the colocalization of LAMP1 and LC3, suggesting the induction of autophagosome-lysosome fusion (Figure S7C, D, Data S16). However, much less colocalization of LAMP1 and LC3 was observed in DCTN1-KO cells after starvation, suggesting that autophagosome-lysosome fusion was impaired (Figure S7C, D). In DCTN1-KO cells that were reintroduced with the wild-type, S1098D, S1098A, T481D and T481 mutants of DCTN1, except for the S1098D mutant, the wild-type or the other mutants recovered autophagosome-lysosome fusion (Figure S7C, D), suggesting that the phosphorylation of S1098 on DCTN1 May be involved in the regulation of autolysosome formation. Furthermore, in DCTN1-KO cells transfected with PLK2, only DCTN1S1098A restored autophagosome-lysosome fusion, whereas the other mutants did not ( , Data S10), further suggesting the involvement of S1098 on DCTN1 in autophagosome-lysosome fusion. In addition, infection of primary cortical neurons with the LV-ZsGreen-DCTN1S1098A mutant attenuated SNCA PFF-induced pathological SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity ( , Data S10).

 

## Discussion
The formation of substantial SNCA aggregates within residual neurons in the brainstem and cortex, accompanied by phosphorylation of the S129 site, which is a pathological hallmark of PD, has been identified in postmortem autopsies of PD patients as an important cause of neuronal death [ ]. The role of p-SNCA in PD pathology has long been of interest. Some studies have suggested little to no role [ ] or even beneficial effects [ ] due to phosphorylation. In contrast, some studies have suggested that phosphorylation of S129 promotes pathological SNCA aggregation and spreading to accelerate neurodegeneration [ , ], whereas reducing the phosphorylation of S129 alleviates neurodegeneration [ ]. Interestingly, however, S129 phosphorylation in the SNCA PFF model occurs after protein aggregation [ ], suggesting that other factors are involved in the assembly of SNCA aggregates. PLK2, one of the kinases of S129, is highly expressed in Lewy body dementia (LBD) and PD [ , ], suggesting its possible participation in SNCA pathogenesis. In our study, PLK2 deficiency attenuated pathological SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity in PFF-treated primary cortical neurons. Importantly, the manipulation of SNCA pathology by PLK2 is independent of the phosphorylation of S129. Furthermore, PLK2 deficiency suppressed pathological SNCA transmission, protected dopamine neurons and improved motor function in SNCA PFF-treated mice.
Altered gene expression patterns, structural changes, and altered interactions with other cellular proteins usually trigger the aggregation of SNCA, yet the autophagy-lysosomal pathway is the most essential manner of removing SNCA [ , , ]. Autophagy is a lysosomal degradation process utilized to recycle outdated cellular elements and eliminate damaged organelles and misfolded proteins [ ]. Lysosomal dysfunction exacerbates the pathological spread of extracellular SNCA [ ], as opposed to the potentiation of lysosomal capacity, which degrades toxic SNCA seeds [ ]. However, in aging-related disorders such as AD and PD, defects occur at different phases of the autophagic pathway and have different consequences for pathogenesis [ ]. Targeting autophagy has also emerged as an important strategy for the management of neurodegenerative diseases [ ]. The overexpression of SNCA compromises the RAB1A-specific secretory pathway and interferes with the location of the autophagy protein ATG9 in the trans-Golgi network, hindering autophagosome formation [ ]. Upregulation of the MTOR signaling axis inhibits autophagy initiation in the SNCAA53T cell model [ ]. SNCA aggregation inhibits transcription factor EB (TFEB), an important regulator of autophagy because it is responsible for autophagy and lysosomal gene expression [ ]. All of the above evidence strongly implicates autophagy as playing an essential role in SNCA pathology. In our study, we demonstrated that PLK2 negatively regulates autophagy and promotes SNCA aggregation by inhibiting autophagic flux.
In our study, overexpression of PLK2 induced the accumulation of autophagosomes as well as the autophagy receptor SQSTM1, suggesting an impairment of autophagic flux. The LC3-II level is regulated by both synthesis and degradation [ ]. As LC3-II levels are not further increased by overexpression of PLK2 when lysosomal degradation is inhibited by Baf A1 and AMPK, MTOR and ULK1 signaling, the key component regulating autophagy initiation [ ], is not affected by overexpression of PLK2, these findings suggest that PLK2 is not involved in the initiation of autophagy. However, we observed an accumulation of autophagosomes, as indicated by the use of mCherry-EGFP-LC3 as a reporter of autophagic flux. Thus, we hypothesized that the reduction in the formation of mature autolysosomes may be due to impaired fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes. In support of our hypothesis, overexpression of PLK2 leads to more LC3 puncta being distributed in the cell periphery, where the LC3 puncta are unable to colocalize with LAMP1 under starvation conditions. Thus, our data suggest that PLK2 negatively regulates autophagy by interfering with autophagosome-lysosome fusion. Interestingly, Oueslati, Abid et al reported that PLK2-induced SNCA S129 phosphorylation mediates its degradation by autophagy through a specific recognition of PLK2 in an SNCA complex-dependent manner [ ]. In their study, co-infections of AAVs that overexpress PLK2 and SNCA showed a repressive effect of PLK2 on SNCA aggregation. It is possible that AAV-expressed SNCA forms soluble S129-phosphorylated monomeric SNCA, which forms a complex with PLK2 that is recognized by autophagic receptors for autophagic degradation, specifically in an S129 phosphorylation-dependent manner. In our PFF model, PFF induces oligomeric SNCA to form aggregates, which is aggravated by PLK2-induced impairment of autophagy. Meanwhile, the effects of PLK2 on PFF-induced SNCA aggregation are S129-independent. It is possible that there are different pathways between overexpressed monomeric SNCA and PFF-induced oligomeric SNCA for autophagic degradation. We cannot exclude the possibility that the soluble fraction of S129-phosphorylated monomeric SNCA is also degraded by PLK2 complex-mediated autophagy in our system.
DCTN1 is a member of the DCTN family that binds to dynein to mediate dynein-driven retrograde transport of autophagosomes and lysosomes [ ]. DCTN1 deficiency impairs autophagosome-lysosome fusion [ ]. In our study, we identified DCTN1 as a substrate of PLK2, which is phosphorylated at multiple sites by PLK2. In DCTN1-KO cells overexpressing PLK2, only DCTN1S1098A, but not DCTN1S1098D or wild-type DCNT1, restored autophagosome-lysosome fusion, suggesting that the DCTN1 S1098 site is involved in the regulation of autophagosome-lysosome fusion, although how DCTN1S1098A functions in this regulatory process is still unknown.
Although we characterized the effects of the phosphorylation of DCTN1 by PLK2 on autophagy, several limitations require further study. First, we did not identify the impact of PLK2 on SNARE proteins that mediate autophagosome-lysosome fusion in the final step of the autophagy pathway [ ]; thus, we cannot exclude an indirect effect of PLK2 on SNARE proteins. Second, how the phosphorylation of S1098 on DCTN1 disturbs the maturation of autolysosomes is unclear. RILP (Rab interacting lysosomal protein) regulates autophagosome formation, transport, and turnover by associating with the dynein-dynactin complex to recruit downstream RAB7 and LC3 via ATG5 [ , ]. It will be interesting to determine whether the phosphorylation of S1098 on DCTN1 affects the dynein-dynactin complex or whether S1098 binds to RILP to recruit downstream effector proteins.
Interestingly, PLK2 is highly expressed in the LBD and PD [ , ]. We propose a possible link between PLK2 and autophagy in PD. PLK2 negatively regulates autophagic flux to accelerate SNCA pathology. In our study, PLK2 directly bound to DCTN1 and phosphorylated its S1098 site, leading to impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion. In primary cortical neurons, overexpression of DCTN1S1098A, but not wild-type DCTN1, represses SNCA PFF-induced pathological SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity. We hypothesized that wild-type DCTN1 is phosphorylated by endogenous PLK2 in primary cortical neurons, leading to the absence of suppressive effects. PLK2 is recognized as a potential target for ameliorating the pathology of PD, as kinase inhibitors are already used to treat many diseases [ ]. Here, we found that TC-S7005 ( ), a PLK2-selective inhibitor, at a low concentration of 10 nM suppressed the neurotoxicity induced by the administration of SNCA PFF to primary cortical neurons. As the pharmacokinetics and permeability of the blood-brain barrier of TC-S7005 are still unknown, we did not identify the effects of TC-S7005 in vivo. Thus, the development of inhibitors specifically targeting PLK2 has substantial potential for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases. However, some limitations of PLK2 as a viable target are of concern. PLK2 maintains neuronal homeostatic plasticity by preventing abnormal hippocampal neuronal activity, and PLK2 inhibition causes excessive neuronal activation [ , ]. Furthermore, learning activity-induced PLK2 expression participates in synaptic modifications encoding memory [ ]. Thus, the function of PLK2 in the maintenance of synaptic homeostasis is a concern for PLK2 as a target.
It has been reported that autophagy mainly degrades oligomeric SNCA but not monomeric SNCA [ ]. Consistent with the finding that PLK2 promotes the aggregation of polyubiquitinated proteins [ ], it is possible that the impairment of autophagy by PLK2 influences the autophagic degradation of SNCA oligomers, leading to an increase in SNCA aggregation and the development of SNCA pathology. In addition, a number of autophagy-related proteins, which are associated with aggregated SNCA, organelles, and lipid membranes, including the structures of lysosomes and autophagosomes, have been identified in the LBs of PD patients [ , ]. Similarly, most LBs are immunoreactive for LC3, and LC3-II levels are significantly increased in the SNc in PD [ ], further suggesting a failure of autophagy in PD. It has been reported that the inhibition of PLK2 ameliorates Aβ pathology and synaptic damage in AD mice [ ]. As PLK2 expression is elevated not only in PD and Lewy body disease, but also in AD and aged primate brains [ , ], it is possible that PLK2 May also affect the pathology in other neurodegenerative disease.
In summary, our study provides a mechanistic explanation for how PLK2 regulates SNCA pathology ( ). PLK2 negatively regulates autophagy to repress the clearance of pathological SNCA. Furthermore, DCTN1, a substrate of PLK2, is phosphorylated at S1098 to restrain autophagosome-lysosome fusion. Pharmacological or genetic depression of PLK2 represses PFF-induced pathological SNCA aggregation and neurotoxicity in primary cortical neurons. In addition, PLK2 deficiency protects against PFF-induced dopaminergic neuron loss and motor dysfunction in vivo.

 

## Materials and methods

## Preparation of SNCA PFF
The purification of recombinant full-length mouse SNCA monomers has been described elsewhere [ ]. Briefly, SNCA monomers were shaken in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes at 1500 ×g for 7 days at 37°C in an incubator. After incubation, SNCA PFF was diluted to 2.5 mg/mL with PBS (Sangon Bioteh, B548117), sonicated for 15 min in an ice bath, and stored at −80°C. SNCA PFF was characterized via transmission electron microscopy as described previously [ ].

## Experimental animals
Two-month-old male C57BL/6J mice were acquired from Shanghai Laboratory Animal Company (Shanghai, China). All the animal experiments were performed in accordance with the regulations of Soochow University for the management of laboratory animals, and all the procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Soochow University (202306A0206). The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups of 15 mice each: (i) the rAAV-U6-scr group. shRNA-CMV-EGFP+PBS group; (ii) rAAV-U6-scr. shRNA-CMV-EGFP+PFF group; (iii) rAAV-U6-shPlk2-CMV-EGFP+PBS group; (iv) rAAV-U6-shPlk2-CMV-EGFP+PFF group. Adeno-associated virus injections were administered one month prior to PFF injection into the striatum. The method of injection was described previously [ ]. Behavioral tests and immunohistochemical and immunoblot analyses were performed after 6 months of PFF treatment.

## Behavioral assessment
Behavioral changes in the mice (12 male mice per group) were assessed via the rotarod test, pole test, grip test and open field test, which are the same approaches as those described previously for the assessment of locomotor performance [ , ]. The experimental environment was kept quiet to maintain the spontaneous activity of the mice. The mice were acclimatized 1 h before the trial, and the test apparatus was washed with 30% ethanol to reduce odor interference.

## Cell culture and treatment
HEK293 (ATCC, CRL-1573), HEK293T (ATCC, CRL-3216), HeLa (National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, TCHu187), and SH-SY5Y (National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, TCHu97) cells were incubated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, F7524), penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). Primary cortical neurons were prepared and cultured as previously reported [ , ]. The neurons were cultured to maturity, and lentivirus was added three days prior to PFF treatment. Ten days after PFF treatment, the neurons were subjected to immunofluorescence and biochemical assays, and 14 days after treatment, they were subjected to neurotoxicity assays. Triton X-100 (Sangon Bioteh, A110694) soluble and insoluble SNCA were prepared as previously described [ , ]. TC-S 7005 (MedChemExpress, HY-108597) was pretreated for one day and administered at 3-day intervals, followed by the incorporation of SNCA PFF as described above. For the experiments with NH4Cl-treated neurons, SNCA PFF was added for two days, followed by the addition of NH4Cl (Sigma-Aldrich, 254134) for three days, and the results were subsequently analyzed by immunofluorescence and immunoblotting.

## Cell death assay
Neurons that were treated with SNCA PFF for 14 days were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Sigma-Aldrich, 14533) and propidium iodide (Invitrogen, P3566). Images were taken via a microscope, and dead cells were automatically counted via ImageJ.

## Cell lysis and immunoblotting
After the abovementioned treatments, the neurons were lysed with 1% Triton X-100 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, 4906845001) and 2% SDS lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) to collect protein fractions. The samples were boiled in loading buffer for 10 min and then separated by SDS-PAGE. Primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were as follows: anti-ACTB/β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A1978; 1:50,000), anti-cleaved CASP3/caspase 3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9661S; 1:1,000), anti‐Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804; 1:10,000), anti-GFP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-9996; 1:2,000), anti-LC3 (ABclonal, A19665; 1:1,000), anti-PLK2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 14812S; 1:1,000), anti-SQSTM1/p62 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-28359; 1:2,000), anti-p-Ser129 SNCA/α-synuclein (Abcam, ab51253; 1:500), anti‐phospho‐Ser/Thr (Cell Signaling Technology, 9631S; 1:1,000), anti-SNCA/α-synuclein (BD, 610787; 1:1,000), anti-TH (Proteintech, 25859–1-AP; 1:1,000). After the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody, they were incubated with goat anti-mouse (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 115-035-008; 1:100,000) or anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G-horseradish peroxidase secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111-035-003; 1:100,000). Finally, the labeled proteins were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence detection kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34580).

## Immunohistochemistry and immunocytochemistry
The experimental animals were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium and then cardiac perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). After perfusion, the brains were separated and fixed with 4% PFA and then dehydrated with a gradient sucrose solution. The sections were cut into cryosections (Leica CM1950, Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, DEU) and subjected to immunostaining. Cultured cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for five min, and then washed with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for five min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-LC3 (ABclonal, A19665; 1:500), anti-LAMP1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc -20,011; 1:500), anti-p-Ser129 SNCA/α-synuclein (Abcam, ab51253; 1:1,000), anti-SQSTM1/p62 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-28359; 1:1,000), and anti-TH (Cell Signaling Technology, 25648S; 1:1,000). Then, the cells were labeled with an Alexa Fluor secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A10037; 1:500), and images were captured via a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) and a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## Plasmid construction and lentiviral package
EGFP-DCTN1 was generated by excising full-length DCTN1 cDNA from myc-DCTN1 [ ] and inserting it into the pEGFP-N1 vector (Clontech, 6085–1) at the HindIII and EcoRI sites. Point mutants of EGFP-SNCA [ ], Flag-PLK2 (Youbio, G121393) and EGFP-DCTN1 were generated via site-directed mutagenesis via a MutanBEST kit (Takara, R401) with the primers listed in Table S1. LV-ZsGreen-PLK2 was generated by excising full-length Plk2 cDNA from Flag-PLK2 and inserting it into the pCDH-ECMV-MCS-3×FLAG-EF1α-ZsGreen1-T2A-Puro vector (NoroPro, V013238) at the XbaI and BamHI sites. LV-ZsGreen-DCTN1S1098A was generated by excising full-length DCTN1S1098A cDNA from EGFP-DCTN1S1098A and inserting it into the pCDH-ECMV-MCS-3×FLAG-EF1α-ZsGreen1-T2A-Puro vector at the EcoRI and XbaI sites.
The DCTN1 sgRNA sequence CACCGGAATCCTCAGGCAGCAGAG and the anti-sequence AAACCTCTGCTGCCTGAGGATTCC were cloned and inserted into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (Youbio, VT8107) for the construction of the DCTN1-KO cell line.
Hairpin RNA was annealed and amplified and then cloned and inserted into the PLKO.1 vector (Addgene, 10878; deposited by David Root). The small hairpin RNA sequences used are listed in Table S2.
The target plasmids were cotransfected with the lentiviral packaging plasmids pSPAX2 (Youbio, VT1444) and pMD2.G (Youbio, VT1443) in HEK293T cells to obtain lentiviral particles. The viral supernatant was concentrated with a Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara, Japan), diluted with PBS and stored at −80°C.

## LDH assay
LDH release was assessed via the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega, G1780). Briefly, 50 μL of growth medium collected from primary cortical neurons after 14 days of SNCA PFF incubation was mixed with 50 μL of CellTiter-Go and shaken for 10 min at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm by a microplate reader.

## Co-IP
293T cells that were transfected with different plasmids were collected and lysed on ice with IP lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, followed by sonication 10 times and centrifugation at 15,000 ×g or 20 min at 4°C. GFP or Flag antibodies in PBST (8 mM Na2HPO4, 0.136 M NaCl, 2 mM KH2PO4, 2.6 mM KCL, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.2–7.4) solution were incubated with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10004D) for 4 h at 4°C, followed by the addition of cell lysate and incubation overnight. Coprecipitated proteins were collected and analyzed by immunoblot analysis after three washes with IP lysis buffer.

## GST affinity-isolation
Equilibrated Glutathione Sepharose 4B GST-tagged protein purification resin (G4B) (Cytiva, 17075601) was equilibrated by washing out anhydrous ethanol from the G4B homogenate with PBS. Subsequently, appropriate amounts of GST or GST-PLK2 recombinant proteins were added and incubated on ice in a shaker for 6 h. Next, the unbound proteins were washed away with PBS, and His-DCTN1 was added, after which the mixture was incubated for 3 h. The proteins were washed 3 to 5 times with PBS, and protein binding was detected by immunoblotting.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Transfection of EGFP and EGFP-PLK2 plasmids in HEK293 cells for 24 h. The cells were harvested and washed with 1× PBS three times, and then, the cells were analyzed at 488 nm on a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, FC500, USA). The cells were centrifuged at 1,500 ×g for 10 min at 4°C and then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.2 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h. The collected cells were subsequently subjected to sample processing and image acquisition.

## Statistical analysis
Quantitative analysis of the immunoblots was performed via ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). GraphPad Prism 7.00 (GraphPad Software, USA) was used for statistical analysis and plotting. Significant differences were evaluated via an unpaired t test or one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The criterion of significance was set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001; ns indicates no significant difference. The values are shown as the means ± SDs.
