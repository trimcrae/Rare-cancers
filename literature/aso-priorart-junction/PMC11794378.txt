Visualization of individual mRNA molecules can reveal new details of transcript variation within understudied portions of mRNA, such as the 5′ untranslated region (UTR). Differential 5′ UTRs may contain motifs, upstream open reading frames (uORFs), and secondary structures that can serve to regulate translation or further indicate changes in promoter usage, where transcriptional control may impact protein expression levels. To begin to explore isoform variation during T-cell activation, we generated the first Iso-Seq reference transcriptome of activated human CD4 T cells. Within this dataset, we discovered many novel splice- and end-variant transcripts. Remarkably, one in every eight genes expressed in our dataset was found to have a notable proportion of transcripts with 5′ UTR lengthened by over 100 bp compared to the longest corresponding UTR within the Gencode dataset. Among these end-variant transcripts, two novel isoforms were identified for CXCR5, a chemokine receptor associated with T follicular helper cell (Tfh) function and differentiation. When investigated in a model cell system, these lengthened UTR conferred reduced transcript stability and, for one of these isoforms, short uORFs introduced by the added length altered protein expression kinetics. This study highlights instances in which current reference databases are incomplete relative to the information obtained by long-read sequencing of intact mRNA. Iso-Seq is thus a promising approach to better understanding the plasticity of promoter usage, alternative splicing, and UTR sequences that influence RNA stability and translation efficiency.

## Introduction
Though a majority of mRNA sequencing methods focus on quantifying changes in transcript amounts, only about 40% of the variation in human protein expression can be explained by changes to mRNA levels alone (Schwanhäusser et al. ). To fully comprehend differential expression across both physiologic and disease states, we must recognize mRNA-intrinsic mechanisms of regulation occurring among isoform variants. Pacific Biosciences Isoform Sequencing (Iso-Seq) has revolutionized our understanding of isoform variation by utilizing single-molecule real-time (SMRT) technology to generate full-length reads of mRNA. In this method, circularized cDNA allows multiple sequencing passes that are ultimately collapsed into highly accurate consensus reads (Leung et al. ; Xie et al. ). After primary analysis and cleanup, Iso-Seq can achieve > 99.9% sequencing accuracy of entire mRNA molecules (Leung et al. ; Sharon et al. ; Wegner et al. ).
Full-length transcriptomes generated by bulk Iso-Seq can be useful tools to provide isoform references across different cell types and conditions. A comprehensive reference may be especially beneficial in mapping short-read data for isoform prediction, as in the recently developed Scasa method (Pan et al. ). Though single-cell isoform sequencing is also available, its lower sequencing depth may neglect low-expressed isoforms (Al’Khafaji et al. 2021; Gupta et al. ; Lähnemann et al. ; Volden and Vollmers ). In comparison, bulk Iso-Seq provides the depth necessary to generate a sufficient roadmap of transcript variants and remains unsurpassed in its ability to broadly identify novel isoforms, particularly end-variants (Deng et al. ; Magrini et al. ; Tardaguila et al. ). End-variant isoforms, which differ from canonical isoforms through alteration of their 5′ or 3′ ends, are often overlooked by RNA-Seq de novo reconstruction algorithms that focus primarily on estimating alternatively spliced transcripts (Dimopoulos et al. ; Liu et al. ; Merino and Fernandez ; Sims et al. ). Yet, end-variants may possess important regulatory elements within differential untranslated regions (UTRs) that can drastically affect protein expression (Grillo et al. ; Leppek et al. ; Lo Giudice et al. ; Wang et al. ).
For human immune cells, regulation of both internal splicing and transcript UTR are thought to play important roles in controlling expression during anti-cancer immunity, pathogenic response, inflammation, and autoimmune disease (Amin et al. ; Bernard et al. ; Chatterjee and Pal ; Chen et al. ; Gruber et al. ; Kandhari et al. ; Nicolet and Wolkers ; Nicolet et al. ; Oliveira et al. ; Su and Huang ; Surani et al. ; Weber et al. ; Yabas et al. ; Zhang et al. ). However, few studies have attempted to investigate the global isoform landscape of activated human CD4 T cells, and no bulk Iso-Seq reference for this cell state subset yet exists (Ip et al. ; Nguyen Quang et al. ; Singh et al. ). To begin to address this gap, we activated human CD4 T cells from a female donor and purified RNA (RIN > 8) at four time points (4, 16, 48, and 120 h) of activation for parallel Iso-Seq and RNA-Seq analysis.

## Methods

## Human blood collection and CD4 T cell isolation
Using standard phlebotomy, 100 mL venous blood from a healthy, consented 24-year-old female donor was collected and adjusted to 6 mM K3EDTA. Blood collection was approved by the University of Louisville Institutional Review Board under expedited review (IRB 14.0661).
PBMCs were isolated from blood using Sepmate™ PBMC isolation tubes (StemCell Technologies, cat no. 85450) as directed by the manufacturer. Cell yield was determined using the count per µL feature of a Cytek® Northern Lights flow cytometer as previously described (Woolley et al. ), immediately after which the PBMCs were further processed to isolate CD4 T cells.
CD4 T cells were purified from freshly prepared PBMC via negative magnetic selection using EasySep™ Human CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies, cat no. 17952) per manufacturer’s instructions. A fraction of these cells were used for flow cytometric staining of markers to assess cell viability and purity as described in Supplementary Methods and below. The remaining isolated cells were immediately plated for activation.

## CD4 T cell culture and activation
Isolated CD4 T cells were plated in a 96-well U-bottom plate at 0.3 million (for 4, 16, or 48-h activation) or 0.1 million (for 120-h activation) cells per well in complete RPMI. Cells were activated for the indicated times using Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat no. 11161D) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. No additional cytokines or stimulants were added during activation. Additional details on cell culture and activation technique are available in Supplementary Methods.
At the indicated time points, approximately 3 million (4, 16, and 120 h) or 1.8 million (48 h) activated cells were lysed in-well with Buffer RLT Plus (RNeasy® Plus Mini Kit, Qiagen, cat no. 74134), per the manufacturer’s protocol. Lysates were then transferred to a microfuge tube and Dynabeads were removed before immediately proceeding with RNA extraction. One well of cells per time point was also used for flow cytometric staining to assess viability and activation state as described in Supplementary Methods and below.

## CXCR5 plasmid preparations
All plasmids were produced through the services of BlueHeron Bio (Bothell, WA) using standard synthesis and cloning techniques. A plasmid expressing CXCR5 in the pCMV6-XL4 backbone (OriGene, cat no. SC309454) was used as a base. Three plasmids expressing a canonical CXCR5 mRNA isoform or either of two novel 5′ end-variant isoforms were created (Fig. ). Insert sequences differed only in their 5′ UTR region and used a standard CXCR5 coding sequence and 3′ UTR (Table , CDS and 3′ UTR from NM_001716.2). Later, two additional mutant variants of the Novel Isoform 2 were generated in the same backbone, again differing only from their parent in the 5′ UTR region (Table ). All inserts were sequence-verified by BlueHeron Bio. Plasmid maintenance and purification are further described in Supplementary Methods. Purified plasmid DNA was stored in DNA-ase-free water at − 20 °C until use in transfection experiments.

## HEK293T cell culture and plasmid transfection
HEK293T cells were maintained in complete DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat no. 10569–010) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 0.1 mg/mL Normocin (InvivoGen, cat no. ant-nr-1). Cells were passaged to achieve 50–80% confluency on the day of transfection, and directly prior to transfection media was replaced with Opti-MEM® reduced serum media (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat no. 31985–070). All transfections were performed using the Lipofectamine 3000 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat no. L3000-008) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

## RNA stability assay
For RNA stability experiments, transfection was performed with pooled plasmid preps and allowed to proceed for 20 h to achieve a standardized maximum transfection. RNA stability in the transfected cells was assessed using an actinomycin D (ActD) transcription inhibition assay based on a previously described protocol (Ratnadiwakara and Änkö ). In this assay, relative quantification of RNA was performed at select time points after transcription inhibition to assess decay.
Briefly, after 20 h of transfection, HEK293T culture media was replaced with complete DMEM containing 5 µg/mL of ActD. At 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-ActD addition, cells were lysed in-well with Buffer RLT Plus (RNeasy® Plus Mini Kit, Qiagen, cat no. 74134), per the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell lysates were then transferred to a microfuge tube and kept at − 80 °C until same-day RNA extraction as described below.
Immediately following RNA extraction, 1 µg of RNA was used as input to cDNA synthesis. cDNA synthesis was performed using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription kit (Qiagen, cat no. 205313) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples with low-quality RNA were excluded based on a low A260/A280 ratio, leading to the exclusion of the 12-h time point for all preps in one experiment and a single experiment 24-h time point for Isoform 1. cDNA was aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C until qPCR.
qPCR was performed using Quantinova SYBR® Green PCR kit (Qiagen, cat no. 208054) on a Quantstudio 3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) instrument following the manufacturer’s protocol. All independent experimental replicates were analyzed with four technical replicates across two qPCR runs. All primer pairs (Bio-Rad PrimePCR Assay™, cat no. 10025636, Table S2) were certified as wet lab validated and optimized for qPCR. An untransfected HEK293T cDNA control was used to assess off-target amplification of the CXCR5 primer pair, and none was found. No template controls and controls that had no reverse transcriptase added during cDNA synthesis were also run in parallel to further screen for the quality of each experiment.
The qPCR data were analyzed using the QuantStudio 3 Design and Analysis software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the Relative Quantification app (v4.3). Only wells with a raw Ct between 15 and 35 were kept, and those with low-quality amplification curves were excluded. Endogenous controls SDHA, ACTB, and B2M were used for initial normalization. The average gene stability score across experiments for these controls was 0.002, with no single score greater than 0.008, suggesting highly stable expression. ΔCt values were calculated based on these controls, and relative quantification of CXCR5 and FOXO3 was performed using ΔΔCt normalized to 1 h post-ActD addition. Relative quantification values of CXCR5 or FOXO3 at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-ActD addition were then graphed using GraphPad Prism. For each condition, one phase decay nonlinear regression was performed with the constraints of plateau = 0 and shared Yo. Curves generated were used to estimate the 95% confidence interval (CI) of RNA half-life (t1/2). The 1-h time point was consistently the peak of CXCR5 expression, so 0-h data were excluded from these analyses and instead inferred by the shared Yo restriction. Analyses were performed for three independent experiments of the Canonical Isoform or four independent experiments for each of Novel Isoform 1 or 2. One experimental outlier at the 4-h time point for Isoform 1 was detected by GraphPad Prism and thus excluded.

## Protein expression assay
For protein expression experiments, transfections were performed with independent plasmid preps and allowed to proceed for 3 h prior to the replacement of media with complete DMEM. This synchronized transfection allowed a clearer assessment of surface expression kinetics while maintaining a minimum time of transfection to preserve relative efficiency (Fuge et al. ; McLenachan et al. ). At the indicated times, cells were stained for CXCR5, and flow cytometric analysis was performed as described below.

## Flow cytometric staining and analysis
All flow cytometric staining was performed in 96-well plates as previously described (Woolley et al. ) with minor modifications. Complete methods of flow cytometric staining are described in Supplementary Methods.
Flow cytometry was performed with a Cytek® Northern Lights three-laser flow cytometer, and spectral profiles of each fluorophore were unmixed using SpectroFlo® software (Cytek Biosciences) and appropriate single-stain and unstained controls to account for autofluorescence. The count per µL feature of the cytometer was also used to calculate cells per staining well and estimate cell expansion during activation. For activation staining, Dynabead fluorescence was accounted for per the advice of Cytek Biosciences technical specialists by independently unmixing the autofluorescence of Dynabeads from activated cells. To do this, the software was instructed to consider Dynabeads as a cell marker and was provided with a Dynabead-only control in addition to the other unmixing controls. All processed data files were analyzed in FlowJo™ (BD Biosciences).
For the CD4 T-cell experiment, initial purity and viability are reported as the average of three technical replicates calculated using a fraction of isolated cells prior to plating for activation. Quality metrics of activated cells are reported as values for a single parallel well stained at each time point of RNA extraction.
For analyses of HEK293T cells in synchronized transfection experiments assessing protein expression kinetics, total CXCR5 MFI was calculated across two technical replicates for each independent transfection. These values were then normalized per experiment by dividing by the average 21-h MFI of Canonical plasmid transfections. Per the figure legend, between 6 and 13 independent transfections per condition were averaged across experiments. Significance is reported based on the adjusted p-value of multiple t-test analyses using the Holm-Sidak method to account for multiple comparisons. Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.4.3).

## RNA extraction
RNA was purified from corresponding cell lysates using RNeasy® Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat no. 74134) following the manufacturer’s protocol with the addition of on-column DNA-ase digestion (Qiagen, cat no. 79254). RNA from each sample was eluted in 30 µL of RNA-ase-free water. For activated CD4 T cell RNA, samples were aliquoted and kept at − 80 °C until Iso-Seq library preparation or until shipping to the HudsonAlpha Institute for Biotechnology (Huntsville, AL) for RNA-Seq library preparation. For HEK293T RNA, the quality and amount of RNA were assessed using nanodrop, and 1 µg of eluted RNA was immediately used as input for cDNA synthesis and subsequent qPCR as described above.

## RNA-Seq library preparation and sequencing
RNA-Seq libraries were prepared by the HudsonAlpha Institute for Biotechnology (Huntsville, AL) according to an in-house protocol. Approximately 500 ng of high-quality RNA (all RIN > 8 measured by HudsonAlpha directly prior to library construction) was used as input for an NEB PolyA enrichment and library prep. The resulting cDNA libraries possessed fragment sizes ranging from 616 to 628 base pairs and passed all metrics of quality per company standards assessed prior to sequencing using Kapa qPCR. Samples were pooled and paired-end sequencing (50 M) with strand specificity was performed for each sample on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument. The average read length for all runs was 101 base pairs.

## Iso-Seq library preparation, sequencing, and initial data analysis
Iso-Seq libraries were generated as previously described (Woolley et al. ) and further detailed in Supplementary Methods. In total, 500 ng of high-quality RNA (RIN ≥ 9.7) was used as input to generate SMRTbell libraries. Sequencing was then performed using one SMRTcell 8 M per pair of barcoded samples on a Sequel IIe system in the UofL Sequencing Technology Center. Following data generation, multiple, iterative sequences covering a single molecule were collapsed to generate highly accurate circular consensus sequence (CCS) reads. These reads were demultiplexed and used as input into the IsoSeq 3 pipeline within SMRTLink (v10.1). CCS reads were further filtered on those reads with quality > 99%, producing “HiFi” reads. The IsoSeq 3 pipeline demultiplexed HiFi reads per individual sample from the pooled sequencing data, filtered out amplification artifacts, trimmed primers and polyA tails, and produced de novo FLNC transcripts for downstream mapping and annotation.

## RNA-Seq data analysis
For newly generated RNA-Seq data, the quality of the sequenced reads was first assessed using FastQC v.0.10.1 (Andrews ) which indicated high-quality reads such that no sequence trimming was necessary. The sequences were then directly aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome assembly (hg38) using the STAR aligner v2.6 (Dobin et al. ) two-pass method with Gencode (v39) annotations (Frankish et al. ). The STAR option –outFilterMultimapNmax was set to 1 to allow only uniquely mapped reads. Read counts for gene regions were obtained with HTSeq v.0.10.0 (Putri et al. ) using Gencode annotations.
To analyze previously deposited RNA-Seq data (Table S3) (Bauer et al. ; Bediaga et al. ; Blake et al. ; Calderon et al. ; Cook et al. ; Gao et al. ; Giles et al. ; Herati et al. ; Hertweck et al. ; Hill et al. ; Hinrichs et al. ; Martino et al. ; Schmidt et al. ; The ENCODE Project Consortium ; Vella et al. ; Weinstein et al. ), a custom script was written to pull FASTQ files from the NIH Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (Leinonen et al. ) and directly align sequences to the reference genome assembly (hg38) using STAR aligner. The resulting SJ.out.tab files generated for each sample were used to assess uniquely mapped read coverage across CXCR5 junctions. Studies selected contained human samples with an average read length ≥ 85, and all samples used for analysis were pre-screened within their respective study to include only those without known autoimmune conditions. Corresponding study data and associated publications were used to assign each sample to a cell subset category (Table S4). After primary analysis, only samples with > 5 reads mapping to the previously annotated internal CXCR5 junction (Chr11:118,883,993,118,893,595) were recognized as expressing CXCR5 and included in calculations. This resulted in samples from 216 unique donors considered as expressing CXCR5, with some donors repeated across samples and cell types (Table S4). The proportion of novel isoform expression was estimated per sample by dividing the coverage of novel end-variant isoform junctions (Chr11:118,882,935,118,883,748 and Chr11:118,882,935,118,883,851) by the total coverage at the shared internal CXCR5 junction (Chr11:118,883,993,118,893,595). The average of this calculation per subset category was reported. For all calculations, two or more supporting reads across either novel junction were required to be considered as true expressions.

## Tertiary Iso-Seq data analyses and isoform characterization
A modified version of a previously described custom pipeline (Woolley et al. ) was used to integrate steps in the cDNA Cupcake protocol for post-processing of Iso-Seq v3 FLNC reads (https://github.com/Magdoll/cDNA_Cupcake). The alignment files for the FLNC reads were converted to fastq (Cock et al. ) and fasta format. Fasta files were aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome assembly (hg38) using minimap2 v2.18-r1015 (Li ) with the option secondary = no. The resulting alignment files were collapsed into isoforms based on sequence similarity using the cDNA Cupcake Python script collapse_isoforms_by_sam.py. Isoform abundance was calculated using get_abundance_post_collapse.py, and isoforms were filtered to include those with at least two supporting reads with filter_by_count.py. Isoforms were also filtered to remove possible non-full length reads by removing any with indications of a degraded 5′ prime region, i.e., apparent 5′ shortened isoforms with otherwise equivalent long reads (filter_away_subset.py). For chained analyses, the samples were chained together using chain_samples.py. SQANTI3 v4.0 (Tardaguila et al. ) was used to summarize the results for each of the individual samples and the chained dataset. SQANTI3 input included the cDNA Cupcake GTF, Gencode (v39) annotations (Frankish et al. ), the hg38 assembly, long-read transcript abundance, a polyA motif list, and a CAGE peak (transcription start sites, TSS) BED file for human and mouse. UCSC Genome Browser tracks were created using guidelines and utilities available on the UCSC Genome Browser website (Kent et al. ). Custom tracks for the individual sample Iso-Seq and matched RNA-Seq data are available to view at http://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-fbin/hgHubConnect?hgHub_do_redirect=on&hgHubConnect.remakeTrackHub=on&hgHub_do_firstDb=on&position=chr1:206,903,317-206,921,941&hubUrl=http://162.215.210.70/~tracks/Mitchell_IsoSeq_Stim/hub.txt.

## Calculating and comparing TSS versus 5′ UTR length
SQANTI3 calculates the distance of the observed 5′ ends to the nearest annotated transcription start sites and reports the values as diff_to_gene_TSS. These values were used to tabulate genes whose transcripts were reported to be at least 100-bp upstream of known TSS, with each considered to be a potentially novel promoter usage not attributable to “broad” promoter activity (Bernardini et al. ; Carninci et al. ). The proportion of each isoform among a gene’s transcripts with this property was calculated using FLNC counts and reported as “notable” (> 10% of total reads) or “majority” (> 50% of total reads).
A custom script was written to evaluate 5′ UTR length variability by comparing annotated TSS to SQANTI3-identified coding sequence (CDS) start sites. The longest 5′ UTR length among annotated transcripts for each gene was calculated from transcripts within Gencode (v39, comprehensive) or Matched annotations between NCBI and EMBL-EBI (MANE Select) (Morales et al. ). Only predicted protein-coding isoforms that mapped to a known gene were compared in this analysis. Publicly available R packages ggplot2 and dplyr were used to visualize distributions of UTR differences across transcripts.
Where indicated, novel 5′ UTR discovered for immune-important genes were aligned to each other and to previously annotated 5′ UTR of their parent gene using CLUSTAL Multiple Sequence Alignment by Muscle 3.8 (Goujon et al. ; McWilliam et al. ; Sievers et al. ). Additionally, upstream elements within novel 5′ UTR were identified using independent observation, UTRscan (Grillo et al. ), or the NCBI ORF finder (Sayers et al. ; Wheeler ).

## Results

## Quality of purification and metrics of activation
 Isolated CD4 T cells to be used as input for activation were sampled and found to be viable (99.9%) and highly pure (98.2%) with contaminating cells primarily consisting of CD4−CD8− T cells (0.82%) (Fig.  ). In accordance with our goal of creating broadly representative references, the negative selection kit catered to enriching diverse circulating subsets. Of the CD4 T cells present, most were naïve (58.3%) or memory (41.4% total Tcm and Tem), with a low fraction of effector (0.31%) and NK T cells (0.007%) (Fig.  ). This allowed our activated references to reflect both new and re-activated CD4 T cells. 
Once cells were isolated, they were cultured with anti-CD3/CD28 beads to various time points of activation. Each time point succeeded in achieving nearly complete activation, supported by almost all sampled CD4+ cells expressing CD69 and/or CD25 (Fig.  ). Throughout stimulation, cells also remained viable (viability ≥ 95.9%, Fig.  ) and demonstrated expected kinetics for the primary stages of T-cell activation (Fig.  ). Cells initiated upregulation of the activation marker CD69 by 4 h and fully transitioned to a blasting phenotype with increased FSC and SSC by 48 h (Fig.  ). Based on comparative calculations of cells per staining well, we further found that cells had not yet doubled at 48 h but by 120 h had expanded to over six times the estimated input cell count. These metrics of cellular phenotype and behavior suggested that the references presented are high-quality representations of early activated (4 and 16 h), blasting (48 h), and proliferating (120 h) CD4 T cells. 

## Iso-Seq initial sequencing data metrics
Isoform sequencing was performed with two pooled samples per 8 M SMRTcell (4 and 16 h, 48 and 120 h). Loading, read length, and yield metrics fell within the expected ranges for both SMRTcells (Table  ). For all sequencing data, 50% of bases were in reads > 146,000 bp (read length N50), a length allowing > 48 passes of the average 3-kb mRNA. This multi-pass sequencing resulted in highly accurate HiFi reads (≥ Q40, 99.99%) that were further processed using primary Iso-Seq analysis to generate ~ 1 to 2 million full-length non-concatemer reads (FLNCs) per sample (Table  ). These FLNCs could then be viewed independently as processed sequence reads or used as input for further downstream analyses.
 

## RNA-Seq read metrics and genomic mapping
Short-read RNA sequencing was performed with each sample to generate ~ 58 to 70 million reads per activation time point with alignment rates > 92.9% when considering reads that were uniquely mapped to a single locus (Table  ). Between 91.5 and 92.0% of sequenced reads were at or above Q30, and sample data mean quality scores were all above Q35 (> 99.97%). High alignment and good quality metrics suggest these short-read data may serve as a useful counterpart to each long-read dataset in reference.
 

## Transcript annotation and classification of isoforms
To further analyze only the highest confidence and truly unique reads, FLNCs were collapsed and filtered for each sample, and a combined collapse of all samples was used to generate a chained analysis group. The chained group was created to assess the sum of unique expression across CD4 T-cell activation, representing a broader, combined transcriptome for activated human T cells. Resultant transcripts for the chained and per-sample groups were summarized using SQANTI 3 (Table  ). Unique genes were identified based on the mapped genomic locations of expressed reads, while unique isoforms were categorized as collapsed reads possessing distinct splice junctions and/or distinct transcription start and stop boundaries.
 
At each activation time point, ~ 11 to 15 thousand unique genes and over 39 thousand distinct isoforms were expressed (Table  ). When all four samples were considered together, ~ 18 thousand unique genes were found with over 118 thousand unique isoforms, which is less than the raw total across samples suggesting some overlap of both genes and isoforms throughout CD4 T-cell activation (Table  ). Most genes expressed were previously annotated within Gencode v39. However, between 601 and 3311 novel genes were found across samples, totaling 4970 novel genes within the chained sample (Table  ).
SQANTI3 was utilized to further categorize transcript isoforms into various structural classifications based on their alignment to previously reported reference transcripts (Gencode v39) and their utilization of known donor and acceptor splice sites. These were either full splice match (FSM), incomplete splice match (ISM), novel in catalog (NIC), novel not in catalog (NNC), intergenic (between annotated genes), antisense (anti-sense to an annotated gene), fusion (fusion of two annotated genes), genic genomic, or genic intron (Fig.  ). Unexpectedly, transcripts matching their reference completely (FSM) represented only 36–40% of transcripts per sample and were less than a third of unique transcripts identified within the chained analysis group (Table S5). Rather, a larger percentage of transcripts were novel (e.g., NIC and NNC representing 51% of transcripts in the chained sample, Table S5), supporting the notion that the isoform landscape of activated CD4 T cells has many nuances that remain to be illuminated. Most of these novel transcripts were also predicted to be protein-coding (Fig.  ), further suggesting that, if valid, these transcripts may hold relevant expression-level consequences. 

## Quality metrics of isoform discovery
The top four isoform categories (FSM, ISM, NIC, NNC) were used to assess metrics of quality via SQANTI 3, with most transcripts reflecting attributes of good quality and few demonstrating potential for bad quality (Fig.  a–b, respectively). A majority of transcripts mapped to known genes and possessed transcription start and termination sites within annotated cap analysis gene expression (CAGE) peaks or polyadenylation (polyA) motifs, respectively (Fig.  a). These transcripts also appeared to primarily use canonical splice junction motifs (Fig.  a), maintaining a high biological capacity for expression. Genuine expression was also supported by the low percentages of transcripts predicted to have undergone reverse transcriptase (RT) switching, a common cause of artifactual novelty (Fig.  b). For both full and incomplete splice match categories, there were also very few transcripts predicted to have undergone nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) which would result in incomplete reads (Fig.  b). 
Though still relatively minimal, a fraction of novel transcripts (NIC and NNC) was marked as possible NMD (Fig.  b). It is important to consider that this NMD flag appears any time an isoform’s predicted coding sequence ends at least 50 base pairs before the last junction identified for a transcript (Tardaguila et al. ). Thus, NMD prediction may be artificially inflated by instances in which splicing occurred within 3′ UTRs (Chan et al. ; Hong and Jeong ) and may not necessarily indicate true decay within these novel categories.
Considered in combination with the quality of purification and subsequent activation achieved, these metrics demonstrate that the datasets provided here consist of representative and high-quality polyadenylated transcripts, a large proportion of which are entirely novel and previously unannotated.

## End-variant isoforms within the Iso-Seq reference dataset
As we examined the novelty of transcripts in our datasets, we noticed that many isoforms were end-variants, meaning they differed from previously annotated references only on their 5′ or 3′ ends. SQANTI 3 classified some of these end-variant isoforms as subcategories of full splice match transcripts, with alternative 5′ or 3′ transcripts having novel transcription start sites or termination sites, respectively (Fig.  ). Interestingly, end-variant transcripts made up the majority of the full splice match transcripts identified (Table  ), suggesting a significant gap in the current annotation of end-variation. While some of these proportions may be inflated due to collapse, particularly for 5′ end variants where collapse skews toward longer reads, many of these transcripts were still found within annotated regions of CAGE Peaks or PolyA motifs, supporting the potential for true expression (Table  ). It is therefore likely that many of these transcripts represent real events. 
End-variant isoforms can alter the regulation of protein expression by modifying elements within the 5′ or 3′ UTR. While the 3′ UTR primarily regulates mRNA stability, the 5′ UTR plays an important role in regulating both stability and translational efficiency (Grillo et al. ; Leppek et al. ; Lo Giudice et al. ; Wang et al. ). Therefore, we focused on analyzing 5′ end-variants of protein-coding isoforms that possessed longer 5′ UTRs than previously annotated for their gene. Longer UTRs are more likely to introduce new regulatory mechanisms and less likely to be sequencing artifacts of processivity or degradation. To classify these isoforms in categories beyond FSM, we developed a custom script that compared the calculated 5′ UTR length of each sample isoform to the longest known UTR of its parent gene within MANE or Gencode (v39, comprehensive) annotations. Only isoforms that were predicted to be protein-coding, mapped to known genes, and were within the FSM, ISM, NNC, or NIC categories were considered for this analysis.
To validate the use of this method for 5′ UTR comparisons, we first analyzed the distribution of lengths compared to MANE Select transcripts (Morales et al. ). Based on the assumption that most transcripts expressed are MANE and that MANE 5′ annotations are representative, we would expect the UTR length differences to cluster around zero with a relatively normal distribution of UTR lengths. Yet, like the findings of SQANTI-classified end-variants, large proportions of the isoforms analyzed within every sample were found to possess 5′ UTR ≥ 10 bp longer than the MANE transcript of their parent gene (Table  ). Far fewer transcripts were predicted to possess UTR lengthened by ≥ 100 bp compared to MANE transcripts (Table  ), which was further visualized in the overall distribution of lengths compared to MANE UTR where the modes centered around zero with only a slight skewing toward lengthened reads (Fig. S2). Minor skewing may, in part, reflect an artifact of the 5′ collapsing to longer reads, as processed by cDNA Cupcake to minimize the impact of 5′ degraded reads. However, as previously mentioned for the SQANTI classified end variants, this does not mean these reads were not real transcripts but instead cautions that the proportion of these reads may be overrepresented in our processed datasets. It is also possible that skewing toward longer reads is a true reflection of the UTRs present in our transcripts, as MANE transcripts are not always predominant in every cell state, and current annotations for 5′ UTRs, even in the MANE set, may be incomplete. We thus considered these results to validate this method with the aforementioned caveats and continued to compare our transcript 5′ UTRs to the longest previously annotated UTRs in the Gencode transcript dataset.
 
As expected, far fewer reads had 5′ UTR longer than the longest known UTR of any Gencode annotated transcript for their parent gene (Table  ). In further classifying these lengthened UTR for the chained sample, all structural categories appeared to retain similar proportions of transcripts with novel UTR ≥ 10 bp longer, while the NIC and NNC categories had a higher proportion of transcripts with UTR ≥ 100 bp longer (Table S6). An even higher proportion of intron retention events within the ISM, NIC, and NNC categories resulted in lengthened 5′ UTR (Table S6), though these events did not dominate because the sum of intron retention events was far less than the sum of events within their parent categories. Ultimately, these results suggest novel lengthened UTR are represented in a diverse set of transcripts generated from multiple mechanisms of isoform variation.
To further evaluate the potential for an impact of the novel 5′ UTR-lengthened transcripts in the activated CD4 T-cell state, we assessed the percentages of the genes identified within our chained sample with a notable (≥ 10%), majority (≥ 50%), or high (≥ 90%) proportion of transcripts possessing novel lengthened 5′ UTR (Table  ). Remarkably, over one in every eight genes identified in our sample (13.0%, Table  ) had at least a notable proportion of expressed transcripts with novel 5′ UTR lengthened by at least 100 bp. As expected, there were fewer genes with a majority or high proportion of lengthened transcripts, and still fewer when considering greater base pair amounts lengthened. Still, close to one in every 40 genes identified (2.6%, Table  ) had a majority (≥ 50%) of greatly lengthened transcripts (UTR lengthened ≥ 300 bp). Similar proportions were seen at each time point of T-cell activation (Table S7). Interestingly, only 5.3% of transcripts identified as having novel extensions of 5′ UTR ≥ 100 bases also scored as having alternate TSS that were ≥ 100-bp upstream of a known promoter. This discordance suggests that novel promoter usage was not a common source of 5′ end-variation in this reference dataset.
 

## Immune-important genes with novel end-variant isoforms
To evaluate the relevance of these lengthened 5′ UTRs, we looked for 5′ variant transcripts expressed at a notable level within immune-important genes and discovered, among other examples, variants of CXCR3, CXCR5, and IL7R with lengthened 5′ UTR (Fig.  a–c). Both end-variants of CXCR5 and two of the three variants of CXCR3 retained all canonical junctions and the canonical CDS (Fig.  a–b), suggesting the added UTR length could serve a predominantly regulatory role. For IL7R, the dominant end-variant transcripts did not retain the canonical CDS and instead skipped canonical exon 1, causing a frameshift and a predicted N-terminal truncation of the IL7R protein (Fig.  c). UTRscan of these exon-skipping transcripts suggested the translation start of this truncated CDS was downstream of a predicted internal ribosomal entry (IRES) site (Grillo et al. ), supporting potential for protein production. These examples, though unvalidated, demonstrate instances where 5′ UTR variation could hold relevance for genes important to the activated CD4 T cell state. 
Of these examples, we became particularly interested in the two CXCR5 isoform variants due to the importance of this gene in the differentiation of T helper cells into T follicular helper cells (Crotty , ; Locci et al. ). Furthermore, we noticed an apparent increase in the frequency of FLNC matching the novel CXCR5 end-variants with later activation while relative expression of CXCR5 appeared to decrease (Fig.  a). Although FLNC counts are not directly quantitative, the pattern of expression throughout activation for these novel isoforms did not follow that of the canonical isoform, suggesting the canonical and novel isoforms are regulated differently. The transcription start sites (TSS) of these novel isoforms mapped to a known promoter that had not previously been associated with any CXCR5 transcripts in the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (v006) (212) (Fig.  b). These isoforms also appeared to have a high potential to confer intrinsic regulation of CXCR5 expression through novel 5′ UTR elements (Fig.  b). As only one 5′ UTR had been previously documented for CXCR5 (Grillo et al. ; Lo Giudice et al. ), and given the potential importance of these novel CXCR5 end-variants, we decided to continue validation with these isoforms as an example of the significance of discovering such variants using Iso-Seq. 

## Evidence of novel CXCR5 end-variants in other donors and datasets
In order to assess the presence of these isoforms in other donors, and investigate for CD4 T-cell subset preference, we probed for evidence of these isoforms in previously deposited RNA-Seq datasets (Table S3). Using the isoforms present in our activated references as a guide, we determined the genomic location of the novel 5′ junctions of our isoforms (Novel Isoform 1, Chr11:118,882,935,118,883,851 and Novel Isoform 2, Chr11:118,882,935,118,883,748) and used these to assess junctional coverage, or uniquely mapped reads spanning each junction, within previous RNA-Seq data. The coverage of the shared internal CXCR5 junction (reads spanning Chr11:118,883,993,118,893,595) was used as a metric of total CXCR5 expression. The total junctional coverage across the two novel junctions was then divided by coverage across this shared internal junction to estimate per sample fractions of expressed CXCR5 corresponding to our novel isoforms. The average of approximated isoform expression could then be calculated for samples across different cell subsets and states (Table S4).
 Of the 216 unique donors considered to express CXCR5, 166 were found to possess two or more junction-spanning reads across our novel CXCR5 isoforms. Interestingly, our novel isoforms were primarily found among activated subsets of CD4 T cells, particularly those that would be classified as early proliferating (72–120 h activation) (Fig.  a–b). This aligns with our own observations of novel isoform frequency increasing with activation time to 120 h and relatively aligns with a pattern of transient CXCR5 protein expression known to occur during early CD4 T-cell activation (Nakayamada et al. ; Schaerli et al. ). This correlation with activation appeared in all CD4 T-cell subsets except in resting and activated T regulatory cells (Fig.  b). 
It is further interesting to note that novel isoform junctions were not found at high frequencies in resting T follicular helper (Tfh) cell subsets (Fig.  c). Tfh cells express high levels of CXCR5 protein for the receptor’s capacity to encourage homing to germinal centers (Crotty , ; Locci et al. ). Lower detection of our isoforms in these stable CXCR5 expressing cells suggests that the novel isoforms could perhaps play a part in CXCR5 regulation prior to Tfh commitment. This is supported by an observation of higher novel isoform frequencies in ex vivo polarized Tfh precursors (Fig.  c). To investigate the regulatory potential of these isoforms, we next explored the effects of isoform-specific variation on mRNA stability and protein expression.

## A novel CXCR5 transcript is an unstable, low-expressing, protein-invariant isoform
Plasmids expressing each isoform variant were generated with a standardized CXCR5 CDS and 3′ UTR (Fig. and Table ). These plasmids were then transfected into HEK293T cells to force the unique expression of each CXCR5 mRNA variant.
To investigate the mRNA stability of each variant and calculate an estimated half-life in this idealized system, we performed an ActD transcription inhibition assay of transfected HEK293T. FOXO3 was used as an internal control representing an unstable RNA transcript (Lugowski et al. ), confirming assay success (Fig.  a). Using this assay, we observed that both variants of CXCR5 had altered decay curves compared to their canonical counterpart, and for Isoform 2 the half-life of its transcripts was significantly decreased compared to the canonical transcripts (Fig.  b–c, t1/2 95% CI shown on graph). This suggested that the 5′ UTR additions were sufficient to confer decreased transcript stability. 
We next investigated if either novel variant exhibited altered kinetics of protein expression. We did this using a synchronized transfection system where transfection was allowed to proceed unhindered for 3 h prior to the removal of transfection reagents followed by flow cytometric measurement of CXCR5 expression at various time points thereafter (Fig.  a–b). This synchronized transfection approach allowed the continued use of the CMV overexpression system with preservation of relative efficiency (Fuge et al. ; McLenachan et al. ) while simultaneously preventing an overload of the system with CXCR5 mRNA that would hinder the observation of more nuanced differences in expression. We observed significantly altered CXCR5 expression at all time points for Isoform 2 compared to the Canonical Isoform (Fig.  b), suggesting novel mechanisms of mRNA-intrinsic regulation occurring. 
Interestingly, Isoform 1 did not show a difference in protein expression compared to the Canonical Isoform (Fig.  a), contrasting apparent differences in mRNA stability (Fig.  b). We believe this may be in part due to the CMV overexpression system allowing for sustained availability of mRNA which masked changes in expression caused by lower RNA stability. However, Isoform 2 revealed significantly lower protein expression even when overexpressed. Thus, we hypothesized that additional elements present in the 5′ UTR of Novel Isoform 2 could contribute to its decreased ability to express CXCR5 protein.

## Specific elements of the novel CXCR5 5′ UTR decrease the efficiency of protein expression
In previously scanning the 5′ UTR of each novel isoform for regulatory elements, we found Novel Isoform 2 contained multiple upstream open reading frames (uORFs) and an upstream adenine-rich (A-rich) region (Fig.  b). To test whether these elements caused differences in expression, we generated plasmids expressing mutant variants of Isoform 2 lacking uORFs or the A-rich region. To ensure the complete negation of uORF activity, we altered the sequence of all three upstream start codons possessing an in-frame stop (Isoform 2 w/o uORFs, Table ). Separately, in removing the A-rich region, we kept only the minimum A-repeat necessary to retain the frame of upstream elements present with respect to the downstream CXCR5 coding sequence (Isoform 2 w/o A-rich, Table ). This ultimately produced two separate mutants with minimally altered sequences. We then tested these mutants’ expression kinetics against the Canonical Isoform and unaltered Novel Isoform 2.
When the A-rich region of Isoform 2 was removed, we observed a late but nonsignificant divergence of CXCR5 expression from the unaltered isoform which trended toward a similar expression to the Canonical Isoform (Fig.  c). This suggests that the A-rich element does not play a primary role in inhibiting CXCR5 translation. However, this variant may still play a small role in mRNA intrinsic regulation that remains to be further investigated, perhaps only in altering mRNA stability which seems less easily visualized in this system.
Removal of all uORF regions, on the other hand, rescued CXCR5 expression by Isoform 2-derived constructs to levels mirroring those of the Canonical Isoform (Fig.  d). CXCR5 expression was significantly increased compared to unaltered Isoform 2 at all but the 3-h time points (Fig.  d). This pattern suggests that intact uORF regions are the primary elements responsible for decreased expression of CXCR5 protein by Novel Isoform 2.
Previous studies have shown the presence of uORFs can decrease transcript expression by 30–80% (Calvo et al. ) through mechanisms such as ribosomal stalling or by encoding small regulatory peptides that independently affect translation (Bottorff et al. ; Chen and Tarn ; Jayaram et al. ; Leppek et al. ; Moro et al. ; Wang and Rothnagel ). Though we confirmed expression hindrance from the uORFs within Novel Isoform 2, further investigation is needed to explore the impact of the single uORF within Novel Isoform 1. Novel Isoform 1 did not appear to directly regulate CXCR5 protein expression in our study, but our investigations were conducted in an overexpression system that likely masks some effects. While we may not have observed the full range of expression differences that would be present in an activated CD4 T-cell system where the isoforms are naturally expressed, the experiments with Novel Isoform 2-derived constructs demonstrated a functional consequence for protein expression was conferred by uORF regions which had not been previously associated with CXCR5 coding transcripts.

## Frequency of novel promoter usage
As was observed for CXCR3, CXCR5, and IL7R transcripts, above, 5′ end-variants can be generated by one or more overlapping mechanisms such as changes in promoter usage, novel splice events within UTR, and frameshifting splice events that generate downstream translation start sites thus altering the 5′ UTR length calculation. To estimate how often promoter usage was sufficient to explain 5′ end-variants we investigated the chained dataset further. For promoters with inherently variable TSS, such as TATAA-less and other “broad” promoters, variability is limited to a 100-bp window of the primary start site (Bernardini et al. ; Carninci et al. ). Hence, we considered transcripts with TSS that were at least 100 bp from annotated TSS as unlikely to be generated by a broad promoter. We grouped the transcripts by their associated genes and calculated their relative abundances based on FLNC values. With this approach, we estimate that 25% of genes expressed a notably frequent (≥ 10%) isoform variant, from a potentially novel promoter element, that was unlikely to be due to “broad” transcription. Fully 14% of genes, for which an isoform representing ≥ 50% of sequenced transcripts had a novel TSS of at least 100 bp from an annotated TSS, may be regulated by novel promoter elements in activated T cells.

## Discussion
Using Iso-Seq, we generated isoform-aware transcriptomes corresponding to four representative points in human CD4 T-cell activation and revealed many novel isoforms. Even with some amount of 5′ collapse artifact, Iso-Seq appears to have revealed both novel promoter usage and novel additions to 5′ UTR that, for a substantial fraction of genes, make up a majority of transcripts expressed in our activated CD4 T cell dataset. Changes in frequencies of these novel UTR-lengthened transcripts could be important to further characterize activated CD4 T cells as well as in additional cell types and states, particularly when attempting to enhance understanding of UTR deviation in instances of pathology or disease response (Chatterjee and Pal ; Nicolet and Wolkers ; Nicolet et al. ; Surani et al. ; Weber et al. ).
While a notable limitation of this data is the single-donor, single-sex nature of the sample, the isoforms identified within this dataset are intended to serve as a reference for future characterization of isoform variation within activated CD4 T cells. In this state, when protein expression and function must be labile to accommodate blasting and proliferation, isoform variation via mechanisms like alternative splicing is thought to play a vital role in promoting protein expression-level and functional changes (Ip et al. ). However, this mechanism of regulation remains understudied for its role in the immune response. There have been many short-read mRNA sequencing studies performed on various activated CD4 T-cell states prior to our study (Bauer et al. ; Bediaga et al. ; Blake et al. ; Calderon et al. ; Cook et al. ; Gao et al. ; Giles et al. ; Herati et al. ; Hertweck et al. ; Hill et al. ; Hinrichs et al. ; Martino et al. ; Schmidt et al. ; The ENCODE Project Consortium ; Vella et al. ; Weinstein et al. ), yet a majority of the isoforms identified in our chained sample were still categorized as novel splice variants (NNC or NIC) that must have been previously overlooked. Thus, in addition to our findings presented, our data provides a base for further exploration and validation of these novel splice variants and their potential biologic consequences.
Aside from the novelty of the splice variants present in our dataset, remarkably, one in every eight genes expressed in our dataset was found to have a notable proportion of transcripts with 5′ UTR lengthened by ≥ 100 bp compared to the longest known UTR for corresponding transcripts within the Gencode dataset. We further validated two novel isoforms of CXCR5 and, for one of these isoforms, demonstrated a significant ability to confer decreased transcript stability and altered protein expression kinetics in a model mammalian cell system. Though this is but one example, it is known that mRNA levels and protein expression are not directly equivalent, and 5′ end-variant regulation may be an under-explored but important mechanism contributing to this disassociation (Lichti et al. ; Nicolet et al. ; Schwanhäusser et al. ).
Though arguably less studied than 3′ UTR counterparts, components within the 5′ UTR are important mediators of post-transcriptional regulation. Elements such as RNA binding protein sites, differences in mRNA secondary structure, upstream start codons, or upstream open reading frames (uORFs) can regulate translation outcomes and may simultaneously confer both differential translational efficiency and differential transcript stability (Bottorff et al. ; Calvo et al. ; Grillo et al. ; Leppek et al. ; Liu et al. ; Lo Giudice et al. ; Moro et al. ; Wang et al. ; Zhang et al. ). For example, upstream start codons, both alone and as a part of uORFs, can stall ribosomes during scanning and force re-initiation prior to translation of the main ORF (Bottorff et al. ; Leppek et al. ; Wang and Rothnagel ). Because of their short length, the uORF stop codon may also be recognized as premature by RNA surveillance mechanisms and trigger nonsense-mediated decay of a transcript (Chen and Tarn ; Leppek et al. ; Russell et al. ). Outside of these cis-regulatory mechanisms, a uORF may further encode for a small peptide that can independently interact and regulate the expression or activity of its associated gene (Chen and Tarn ; Jayaram et al. ; Moro et al. ).
Considering the novel isoforms of CXCR5, multiple uORFs were identified that had not been previously associated with coding transcripts, and the presence of these uORFs was found to confer differential transcript expression for Novel Isoform 2 when in a model cell system. This model system was chosen for the absence of any possibility of intrinsic CXCR5 expression that could have complicated measurement of expression kinetics. However, the system was likely also lacking native regulatory elements that might be present in activated CD4 T cells where these CXCR5 mRNA variants are expressed. Though expression differences conferred by the novel isoforms remain to be further investigated in the native activated CD4 T cell state, the example still highlights how traditional mRNA sequencing and analysis methods may overlook end variants with novel regulatory potential. While the median length for uORFs is thought to be only 48 base pairs (Calvo et al. ), we found thousands of transcripts within our reference possessing 5′ UTR over 100 bp longer than the longest known within the Gencode reference dataset, suggesting a likelihood for a large number of regulatory regions within novel end-variant transcripts which remain to be identified and explored. With few human immune cell types and states well-annotated with isoform-aware sequencing, there is a continued need for Iso-Seq studies cataloging the end-variant landscape during the human immune response.
In addition to enhancing our understanding of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms located within the 5′ UTR region, a comprehensive understanding of end-variation and transcription start loci among isoforms would also add to our ability to adequately annotate transcriptionally relevant regions such as promoters and enhancers. Many aspects of promoter and enhancer regions have been shown to overlap, including the ability to start transcription near the element’s locus (Andersson ; Tippens et al. ). Recent models have also proposed that promoter and enhancer elements might exist on a spectrum rather than as independent categories (Andersson ; Tippens et al. ). Isoform-aware data may provide additional insight into distinguishing promoter and enhancer elements by providing valuable information on novel TSS and associated transcript characteristics, as in visualizing bidirectional starts that would be typical of more enhancer-like TSS (Andersson ; Tippens et al. ). The information on mapped TSS with linked full-length transcripts provided by bulk Iso-Seq may further be helpful in corroborating previous CAGE Peak and chromatin accessibility data. This is particularly relevant where specific regulatory loci are predicted but have yet to be associated with a corresponding transcript, as was previously the case for the TSS corresponding to our novel CXCR5 isoforms (Andersson ; Giles et al. ; Schmidl et al. ).
Adequate mapping of TSS corresponding to functional transcripts is particularly relevant for continued mapping of cell-state-specific transcriptional control of immune-important genes. For example, the novel CXCR5 end-variant isoforms we discovered appear to map to a known promoter that not only previously lacked a linked transcript but whose role in regulating Tfh differentiation had not yet been cataloged much less studied (Dreos et al. ). Further, the variants comprised a larger proportion of CXCR5 mRNA as time progressed and did not follow the same expression pattern as their canonical counterpart, suggesting the locus may be differentially controlled during CD4 T-cell activation compared to the canonical CXCR5 start. Future studies should investigate the mechanisms of regulation occurring at this locus, particularly as CXCR5 expression at the time point that the novel end-variants appeared during unpolarized T-cell activation is thought to be independent of the transcription factor BCL6, which is intriguing because BCL6 is canonically considered vital in controlling CXCR5 transcript expression (Choi and Crotty ; Crotty ; Hart and Laufer ; Liu et al. , ). Low-level expression of these mRNA may serve to keep the primary locus available for quick fluctuations in expression that may be needed at a time when the cells are primed for further secondary signaling toward differentiation. In this unpolarized state, it is also possible the two novel variants represent a transitional state of expression of CXCR5 that could help promote homing to areas where cells could receive additional signals thought to be needed to complete Tfh differentiation or that could allow rapid return to a non-Tfh path. Additional characterization of these isoforms in pre-Tfh and fully differentiated Tfh cells is needed to determine the potential for such a role.
Without appropriate end-variant characterization, current annotations of transcriptional and translational regulation for many genes are likely incomplete. It is probable many TSSs and intrinsic transcript 5′ UTR regulatory regions remain to be discovered. Further, as demonstrated for our novel CXCR5 isoforms, potentially important variants of the transcripts produced at even recognized TSS may remain undocumented. While we chose to focus on end-variant transcripts, Iso-Seq cell-state references are equally important for defining novel splice variant transcripts, as evidenced by the large number of NNC and NIC novel splice variants identified but not further characterized within our data. The high-quality isoform references provided here will help fill gaps in the known transcriptome of activated CD4 T cells and enhance our understanding of the isoform landscape to guide future differential expression analyses and better define the complete transcriptional and translational regulatory atlas of immune-important genes.
