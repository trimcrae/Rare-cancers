## SUMMARY
Emerging evidence suggests that long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play diverse and critical roles in neural development, function, and disease. Here, we examine neuronal lncRNAs in a model system that offers enormous advantages for deciphering their functions: the Drosophila olfactory system. This system is numerically simple, its neurons are exquisitely well defined, and it drives multiple complex behaviors. We undertake a comprehensive survey of linear and circular lncRNAs in the Drosophila antenna and identify a wealth of lncRNAs enriched in it. We generate an unprecedented lncRNA-to-neuron map, which reveals that olfactory receptor neurons are defined not only by their receptors but also by the combination of lncRNAs they express. We identify species-specific lncRNAs, including many that are expressed primarily in pheromone-sensing neurons and that may act in modulation of pheromonal responses or in speciation. This resource opens many new opportunities for investigating the roles of lncRNAs in the nervous system.

## INTRODUCTION
There is a growing body of evidence that long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) act in the regulation and evolution of a broad range of neuronal processes. – An increasing number of lncRNAs have been found to influence neural processes or to be influenced by them. – It has become clear that an understanding of many aspects of the nervous system will require examination of its lncRNAs.
lncRNAs are, by definition, greater than 200 nt in length and lack the potential to encode stable proteins. lncRNAs are typically expressed at lower levels than mRNAs, are more highly restricted in their spatiotemporal expression, and show lower levels of sequence conservation. lncRNAs are in many ways extremely well suited to roles in the regulation of neural activity. They can be rapidly synthesized or degraded and can carry out many functions: they can act as catalysts, scaffolds, regulators, sponges, or ligands. They can fold into multiple stable structures and can evolve rapidly. Finally, they can be extensively altered, e.g., spliced, tailed, capped, or chemically modified. – 
There is precedent for lncRNAs that regulate neuronal development and activity. One antisense RNA, the lncRNA Kcna AS, prevents the transcription and accumulation of its corresponding mRNA, Kcna, in dorsal root ganglion neurons in rats and is important for the desensitization of these neurons. One sense-overlapping lncRNA, ADEPTR, is transported to synapses, where it anchors actin-folding regulators to modulate the plasticity of dendritic spines in mice. One intergenic lncRNA, Silc1, promotes the expression of a nearby coding gene, Sox11, and thereby acts in neuronal development and regeneration in mice. One circular RNA, Cdr1as, modulates the availability of the microRNA miR-7 by direct binding and thus contributes to the regulation of neuronal activity in mice. Other lncRNAs regulate genes essential for neurotransmitter release or synapse organization. – Many have been associated with human neural degeneration, and lncRNAs have been proposed as potential targets for the treatment of neural disease. – 
There is enormous potential for studying the roles of lncRNAs in a well-defined, numerically simple sensory system of a genetic model organism. One such system is the olfactory system of Drosophila. The antenna of the fly has been characterized in great detail. Its odor receptors have been defined, the neurons that express them have been analyzed extensively, and the behaviors it drives have been well characterized. – The genome of Drosophila melanogaster is relatively small, and the genomes of many related species are available for comparative purposes. The system is ideal for examining the roles of lncRNAs in neural development and function.
One particular question of outstanding interest concerns the coding of olfactory information by the ensemble of olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs). Physiological analysis of these neurons has shown great diversity in their functional properties. Recent high-resolution morphological analysis has revealed surprising diversity in their anatomy. Different ORNs express different odor receptors (Ors), and the construction of a receptor-to-neuron map has provided a foundation for a wide range of studies over the past 20 years. However, while Ors confer some of the properties of ORNs, they do not explain all of their functional or morphological diversity.
Here we describe a rich lncRNA landscape in the Drosophila antenna. We provide a compendium of antisense lncRNAs, sense-overlapping lncRNAs, intergenic lncRNAs, circular RNAs, and exceptionally abundant intronic RNAs. Many of these RNAs were previously unannotated. A number of the lncRNAs are highly enriched in the antenna. Included in this atlas are several antisense or circular lncRNAs that are excellent candidates for regulators of receptor genes or other olfactory genes. We take advantage of a recent single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset to map lncRNAs to cell types. In particular, we create an lncRNA-to-neuron map. This map reveals that ORNs differ in their expression not only of Ors but also of lncRNAs. Our study generates a wealth of hypotheses about the molecular basis of olfactory function and provides a foundation for testing them.

## RESULTS

## Hundreds of linear long non-coding RNAs are detected in the Drosophila antenna
Many lncRNAs are detected in RNA-seq studies that begin with the selection of poly(A)+ RNAs. However, many lncRNAs are polyadenylated inefficiently, have no poly(A) tails at their ends, or have no ends at all, i.e., they are circular. – Consequently, many lncRNAs are underrepresented in datasets derived from poly(A)+-selected libraries. Therefore, to obtain a broad view of the Drosophila antennal lncRNA transcriptome, we began by profiling both poly(A)− and poly(A)+ RNAs following the removal of rRNA; we will refer henceforth to this RNA as “rRNA-depleted” RNA.
We manually dissected the third antennal segments, which contain the olfactory sensilla, carefully avoiding the second antennal segments to which they are attached. We obtained four biological replicates, each prepared from the antennae of 150 animals and each with 30–60 million reads, of which ~90% mapped to the fly genome. In all replicates we detected transcripts of the expected Or, Ir, Gr, and Obp genes previously identified in the third antennal segment by high-throughput sequencing. By contrast, abundant transcripts enriched in the second antennal segment were barely detectable, confirming the purity of our samples ( ).
We first examined the linear long noncoding transcriptome of the fly antenna ( ). We detected transcripts in the antenna of 355 genes that had been annotated as non-coding (BDGP6.48), using 1 transcript per million (TPM; averaged across the four replicates) as a threshold as a means of increasing the robustness and reproducibility of our analysis. Of these genes, 198 were antisense to a coding gene ( ), 147 were intergenic ( ), and 10 overlapped partially with a coding gene in the same orientation ( ). We identified an additional 119 putative lncRNAs that overlapped a coding gene in the same orientation, but we excluded them from the analysis because we could not determine with confidence that they represent lncRNAs as opposed to alternative splice forms of mRNAs.
lncRNAs can act as transcriptional regulators in cis. Intriguingly, we detected lncRNAs antisense to two chemoreceptor genes, Gr64e and Ir21a (CR45042 and CR43609, respectively), opening the possibility that sensory receptor transcription may be regulated by lncRNAs in the antenna. IR21a responds to environmental changes in temperature. 
We also detected five lncRNAs that overlap, or are near to (<1 kb), seven genes that encode odorant-degrading enzymes (alpha-Est1, alpha-Est2, Cyp4ae1, Cyp4d2, Cyp4d14, GstD2, and GstT1). Genes of this class show great differences in expression when exposed to various conditions or when compared across closely related Drosophila species. It is possible that lncRNAs contribute to their differential expression.

## Identification of previously unannotated lncRNAs
The lncRNAs identified above had been annotated in the Drosophila genome, even if not shown previously to be expressed in the antenna. We next looked for lncRNAs that had not been annotated (BDGP6.48), perhaps because their expression was limited to the antenna. For each of our four biological replicates, we assembled a de novo transcriptome and considered only transcripts that were expressed at a level ≥1 fragment per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM) in all replicates and contained minimal repetitive sequences (<20% of the total length). We identified 74 previously unannotated putative genes that had clear 5′ and 3′ ends, as judged by manual inspection of their read coverage ( and ; ). Hereafter, we refer to these previously unannotated transcripts as “antennal RNAs” (aRs). In support of their identification as previously unannotated transcripts, as opposed to artifacts of library preparation, 68 of these transcripts could be detected in at least one publicly available antennal RNA-seq dataset. – Importantly, this analysis provides the most complete antennal transcriptome of D. melanogaster to date.
Six of the 74 previously unannotated putative genes, including one expressed at 30 TPM in our dataset, aR13, were not detected in any of the publicly available RNA-seq datasets. In addition to these six, nine others were overrepresented in our library, compared with poly(A)+-selected datasets, suggesting that some of the aRs can accumulate without a poly(A) tail. Consistent with this notion, several well-defined lncRNAs that are not polyadenylated (7SK, 7SL, RNaseP, and RNaseMRP) were enriched in our dataset by more than 20-fold.
Most of the 74 previously unannotated genes share several characteristics with the previously annotated non-coding genes and pseudogenes. On average, the previously unannotated genes are characterized by low transcript levels, low GC content, low sequence conservation, and low exon number relative to coding genes ( ; p < 0.0001 in all four cases; Kruskal-Wallis test). Their mean length is 2 kb, ranging up to 23 kb (aR35).
As a class, the 74 aRs showed a much lower coding potential compared with mRNA in our dataset using each of three programs: Coding Potential Calculator (CPC2), Coding-Potential Assessment Tool (CPAT), and Coding-Non-Coding Index (CNCI) (p < 0.0001 in each case; Kruskal-Wallis test). Of the 74 genes, 59 were classified as non-coding by all three programs ( ), consistent with the analysis shown in . Of these, 26 are antisense and 33 are intergenic. They are encoded throughout the fly genome and overlap or lie near genes of diverse functions. Strikingly, three of the previously unidentified genes lie near chemosensory genes: one is ~200 bp upstream of the polycistronic Or10a/Gr10a gene, one is antisense to ppk19 and Obp99a ( and ), and one is antisense to the second intron of Ir41a ( ).
Of the remaining 15 genes, the putative open reading frames (ORFs) of most were truncated in other species and poorly conserved ( ), and only one was conserved in Drosophila erecta, which diverged from D. melanogaster ~13 million years ago ( ). Among the four putative ORFs that were conserved in Drosophila sechellia, which diverged ~3 million years ago, all are in a transcript antisense to a previously known coding gene, and three overlap a coding exon ( ). One conserved putative ORF shows no such overlap and merits further investigation ( ). Most putative peptides showed little similarity to other known proteins, and we did not find a consensus on the predicted localization of these putative proteins ( ). We conclude that most if not all of these 15 transcripts are likely to be lncRNAs as well.

## Hundreds of circular RNAs are detected in the antenna
We next searched for circular lncRNAs, known as “circRNAs,” that are produced by backsplicing, i.e., splicing of the 5′ end of one segment to the 3′ end of the segment that is downstream of it in the genome ( ). circRNAs can regulate a broad range of biological processes. – To identify such circRNAs, we searched our four rRNA-depleted RNA-seq datasets and found chimeric reads, in which two segments were joined in reverse order ( ), consistent with circularization via such a backsplicing event. We considered a potential circRNA further only if at least five cases of such an abnormal, chimeric junction were identified; this criterion was imposed to prevent misinterpretation of artifacts of library preparation. In this manner, 206 genes were identified that may harbor one or more circRNAs, suggesting that the antenna may host many circRNAs.
To validate the existence of these circRNAs and further characterize the circular transcriptome of the fly antenna, we treated aRs with RNase R, an exonuclease that degrades most linear RNAs, prior to preparing libraries for high-throughput sequencing. By nature, circular RNAs are resistant to exonucleases such as RNase R. We confirmed that 158 of the 206 genes host at least one antennal circRNA, and these were considered for further analysis ( and ; ). In addition, by depleting linear RNAs from our sample, we were able to increase the sensitivity of the analysis and thereby identify more circR-NAs, again imposing the criterion that each circRNA be identified via at least five chimeric reads. In total, we identified antennal circRNAs encoded within 338 genes ( ). Many of the host genes are associated with the GO terms “synapse organization,” “nervous system development,” “signaling,” or a combination of these terms ( ). Some of these circles are likely to exceed 10 kb in length.
Interestingly, one circRNA derives from Ir40a, which encodes a receptor that responds to changes in humidity ( ). Three circRNAs are derived from previously unreported splicing events ( ), suggesting the possibility that these circRNAs play roles uniquely suited to olfaction.

## Identification of exceptionally abundant intronic RNAs
The enrichment for circular RNAs described above also allowed us to identify a different class of RNAs that are produced by a different mechanism than that producing circRNAs. After splicing, a typical intronic RNA is released as a circular RNA and, within minutes, is linearized and then degraded rapidly. Some of these circular intronic RNAs, however, escape linearization and persist for hours or even days. These circular intronic RNAs, referred to as ciRNAs, have been proposed to regulate transcription or processing of other intronic RNAs. – 
In our antennal RNase R high-throughput dataset, 523 introns were detected at a level of ≥10 reads per 100 bp across 70%–95% of their length (example in ; ). These 523 introns represent only 1.6% of the introns in our antennal transcriptome. Manual inspection revealed that the read coverage generally began near the 5′ splice site and ended a few nucleotides upstream of the 3′ splice site, as is typical for a lariat without a tail. Of the introns from which these ciRNAs derived, 87% were 150–500 bp in length.
We were able to identify chimeric reads at the junctions of eight ciRNAs ( ; ). Some of these chimeric reads showed sequencing errors, as expected of a lariat structure: sequencing the junction of a lariat is difficult (because of the 2′-5′ covalent bond that forms between the 5′ splice site and the branchpoint) and prone to error at the branchpoint.
Are these ciRNAs stable intronic sequences, i.e., sisRNAs, or unstable byproducts of splicing? If they were only byproducts, we might expect them to have the same read count as other introns of the same length in mRNAs of the same abundance. However, among introns of length 150–500 bp in aRs expressed at 30 to 300 TPM, only 15% gave rise to a putative ciRNA, i.e., with ≥10 reads per 100 bp. Moreover, the abundance of the ciRNA and its corresponding mRNA did not correlate well, despite deriving from the same transcriptional event ( ). The simplest interpretation of these results is that these ciRNAs are unusually stable.

## lncRNAs preferentially expressed in the antenna
We wondered if any of the lncRNAs we characterized were preferentially expressed in the antenna. To determine their abundance across fly tissues, we first took advantage of the large dataset in FlyAtlas 2. This dataset was compiled from rRNA-depleted libraries constructed from 15 fly tissues ( ). We quantified the number of reads of each of our 74 previously unannotated aRs, as well as the ~3,000 previously identified linear lncRNAs in the fly transcriptome (BDGP6.48). Most tissues contained 200–400 linear lncRNAs, although the testis contained >1,000, as previously reported ( ).
We performed a hierarchical clustering of lncRNA abundance in the 15 tissues, using log-transformed TPM as a value for each transcript and evaluating two samples from each tissue. The antennal samples clustered with other neuronal samples, eye, brain, and thoracicoabdominal ganglion, suggesting that the antennal non-coding transcriptome is most similar to neuronal non-coding transcriptomes ( ). Two clusters contained 17 abundant lncRNAs expressed primarily in these four tissues (“N” in ); they included three lncRNAs previously shown to be expressed in embryonic cells that expressed the neural marker elav+, consistent with a neural identity. 
Most interesting, we identified a cluster of 38 lncRNAs that were readily detected in the antenna but displayed little or no expression in the other tissues (“A1” in and ). In a pairwise differential expression analysis between the antennal transcriptome and the other tissues, 15 of the 38 lncRNAs in cluster A1 were significantly enriched in the antenna compared with all of the other 14 tissues examined ( , red dots; log2 fold change ≥1; adjusted p < 0.05; and ). Notably, 11 of these 15 lncRNAs were among the 74 aRs.
Additional lncRNAs were also found enriched in the antenna to a somewhat lesser extent: (1) another 9 lncRNAs of the A1 cluster were enriched by at least 2-fold in the antenna compared with 70% of the tissues analyzed ( ; log2 fold change ≥1; adjusted p < 0.05; ). (2) A cluster of 41 lncRNAs showed preferential expression in the fly antenna (“A2” in and ), and of the genes in this cluster, 18, all aRs, were enriched in the antenna compared with 70% of the tissues analyzed (log2 fold change ≥1; adjusted p value < 0.05; ). We note that more lncRNAs may be enriched in the antenna but did not meet statistical significance in our analysis, likely due to low read counts ( , gray dots).
To identify circular RNAs enriched in the antenna from the FlyAtlas 2 dataset, we recovered chimeric reads. This analysis focused on putative circRNAs detectable in the rRNA-depleted libraries; the antennal circRNAs detected only in our RNase R datasets were not included, as they were not abundant enough to be detected in the rRNA-depleted libraries. We found that the largest pools of circRNAs were in neuronal tissues: the brain, thoracicoabdominal ganglion, eye, and antenna ( ), consistent with a previous study. 
We identified 15 circRNAs that are expressed in the antenna, but whose abundance in all other tissues fell below our detection threshold (1 chimeric read/million mapped reads). We confirmed the circularity of all 15 by analysis with RNase R. Most of these antennal circRNAs derive from host genes that are preferentially expressed in the antenna ( ).
To confirm the expression pattern of the lncRNAs most highly enriched in the antenna and to extend the analysis to other chemosensory organs, we carried out RT-PCR analysis. We dissected antennae and maxillary palps, which are olfactory organs, as well as labella, legs, and wings, which contain taste neurons. We also dissected heads, from which the antennae, maxillary palps, and labella had been removed, as well as three other tissues: gut, ovary, and testis. The quality of the dissected tissue was validated by amplification of tissue-specific products ( , dissection markers). As a positive control, we analyzed a ubiquitously expressed lncRNA, CR34335, and detected it in all tissues. In addition, a neuronal lncRNA, CR44922, was detected in all chemosensory organs and in heads from which chemosensory organs had been removed.
Numerous antennal lncRNAs were confirmed to be expressed in the antenna ( ). Some of these, such as aR5, were not detected in other tissues; some, such as aR10, were also expressed in the maxillary palp; some, such as aR19, were expressed in all chemosensory tissues tested but showed little, if any, expression in non-neuronal tissues. In summary, a number of lncRNAs are highly restricted to olfactory tissue, suggesting the possibility of an olfactory-specific function.

## Mapping of lncRNAs to cell types
We wondered if any of the antennal lncRNAs were preferentially expressed in olfactory sensilla, particular cell types, or perhaps even specific classes of ORNs. To address this question, we took advantage of two resources: (1) an antennal transcriptome of amos mutant flies, which lack trichoid and basiconic sensilla, i.e., sensilla that detect pheromones and food odors, respectively, and (2) a recent single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset. 
From the amos dataset we found that 23 lncRNAs were reduced in amos antennae (log2 fold change ≤ −2; adjusted p value < 0.05). Among these reduced lncRNAs, 13 belong to the clusters A1 or A2 shown in , 2 belong to cluster N (e.g., CR44317, ), and 8 were detected in the antenna and one other tissue. Remarkably, among the latter 8 lncRNAs, 4 exist in the antenna as a different isoform than that previously annotated (e.g., CR45259, “de novo annotation” vs. “BDGP6 annotation”). These results suggest that these lncRNAs are expressed in olfactory sensilla that respond to pheromones or food.
The amos library was constructed from poly(A)+-selected RNA, thereby precluding direct identification of circRNAs, but we found that two genes that host circRNAs show reduced expression in the amos antenna (log2 fold change ≤ −2): CG42540 and 5-HT2A, which encodes a serotonin receptor. This finding suggests the possibility that the corresponding circRNAs may be enriched in pheromone- or food-sensing sensilla.
To identify the expression patterns of abundant lncRNAs at the single-cell level, we analyzed the antennal single-nucleus RNA-seq datasets from the Fly Cell Atlas, which includes transcripts from actively transcribed genes and RNAs that are enriched in the nucleus. We found 75 ncRNAs that were detected in at least one ORN cluster; the criterion for detection was that the mean expression level of the gene averaged among all cells of the neuronal cluster was >0.3 ( ). The expression patterns of previously annotated lncRNAs were consistent with the Fly Cell Atlas database ( and ). Twenty-seven lncRNAs are expressed in more than 50 clusters and showed a broad expression pattern ( ; e.g., CR34335, , was detected in all 170 clusters); 16 were detected in fewer clusters, including non-ORN clusters. Of particular interest, 30 lncRNAs were expressed primarily in ORNs: some of these genes were detected only in ORN clusters; some were detected in other clusters but were highly enriched in an ORN cluster (log2 fold change >5, p < 0.05) ( ; e.g., CR44024, ). Conversely, 53 lncRNAs were detected in at least one cluster, but not in ORN clusters ( ; e.g., glial lncRNA CR30009, 
 ).
Among the 30 neuronal lncRNAs, 24 were detected in five different ORN classes or fewer. One of these lncRNAs (CR45259) was highly enriched in one specific ORN type, the ab8A neuron ( ). This ORN expresses Or43b, which responds to a number of alcohols and acetate esters. , Another lncRNA (CR44317) was detected in five ORN types, but was particularly abundant in one of them, which expresses Or13a ( ).

## An ncRNA-to-ORN map
To capture the full extent of neuronal lncRNA diversity, we generated an lncRNA-to-neuron map for 30 neuronal ncRNAs we identified ( ). We identified several classes of lncRNAs: those that are (1) expressed in most ORNs; (2) expressed in a smaller subset of ORNs in sensilla of multiple morphological types; (3) expressed specifically in coeloconic sensilla or the sacculus, a multichambered pit on the antennal surface that contains coeloconic and other sensilla; (4) expressed specifically in basiconic sensilla; or (5) expressed specifically in trichoid sensilla.
Intriguingly, neurons of trichoid sensilla, which respond to pheromones, host the greatest number of lncRNAs (8–11 lncRNAs/neuron). Moreover, six of these lncRNAs are restricted to trichoid sensilla, as opposed to other sensilla. One lncRNA was most enriched in neurons expressing either Or47b or Or65a/b; its level in these clusters exceeded that in all other clusters by a large factor (log2 fold change >10; p < 0.05). This lncRNA, aR65, lies near two genes encoding extracellular antimicrobial peptides, one of which, BomT3, is highly expressed in ORNs expressing Or47b (Fly Cell Atlas database). This finding suggests the possibility that aR65 regulates the expression of these proteins and invites investigation into the role of these peptides in the antenna.
Finally, we asked whether any of these 30 antennal lncRNAs were unique to D. melanogaster. We purified antennal RNA from Drosophila simulans, a closely related species, and generated rRNA-depleted libraries for high-throughput sequencing. Poly(A)+-selected publicly available datasets were used for further confirmation of lncRNA expression in D. simulans. Ten of the 30 lncRNAs that mapped to ORNs showed little or no expression in D. simulans antennae (<0.1 reads per million mapped reads in D. simulans, or log2 fold change >4, adjusted p value < 0.05, relative to D. melanogaster; ; , black solid outlined shapes; ). Of these 10 lncRNAs, 7 were detected in neurons of trichoid sensilla. Among the other 20 lncRNAs that were detected in D. simulans, 8 showed poor conservation between the two species (<60% identity in nucleotide sequence and/or truncated in D. simulans by >50% compared with the D. melanogaster ortholog; ).
In summary, the lncRNA-to-neuron map shows that different functional types of ORNs are defined by a combinatorial code of lncRNAs, with very little degeneracy to the code. These results suggest that lncRNAs may play roles uniquely suited to each ORN functional type and each species.

## DISCUSSION
lncRNAs have recently received increasing attention as molecular components of the nervous system. They are ideally suited for roles in the modulation of neuronal activity across a variety of timescales, ranging from adaptation to evolution. However, direct evidence of such roles remains relatively scarce. In this study we examined lncRNAs in a model sensory system that offers enormous advantages for their analysis.
Our study provides a comprehensive examination of lncRNAs in the Drosophila antenna. We have depicted the rich antennal lncRNA landscape of this well-defined sensory organ by combining the sensitivity of bulk RNA-seq with the cellular resolution of single-nucleus RNA-seq. Through different screens we detected 429 linear lncRNAs and 861 circRNAs, many previously unannotated. We found that dozens of these lncRNAs are highly enriched in the antenna relative to 14 other tissues, as expected of lncRNAs that have evolved an olfactory function. Some of these lncRNAs lie near genes with defined roles in chemosensation.
We have generated an lncRNA-to-ORN map, which demonstrates that ORNs differ not only in the Ors they express, but also in the lncRNAs they express. Pheromone-sensing neurons in particular contain a number of lncRNAs that show little or no expression in other antennal cells. These results suggest roles for lncRNAs in the modulation of pheromone response.

## Specificity of lncRNA expression
We identified lncRNAs in this study initially by virtue of their expression in the antenna. Further analysis revealed different levels of specificity. Many lncRNAs were highly enriched in the antenna, which may explain in part why they had not been annotated. Some antennal lncRNAs were found predominantly in a particular morphological class of the sensillum, such as basiconic sensilla that sense food odors or trichoid sensilla that sense pheromones. Among these lncRNAs, some were detected in a single class of ORN, such as the ab8A ORN that expresses the Or43b receptor.
We suspect that these narrowly expressed lncRNAs are early examples of a larger class of specific lncRNAs awaiting discovery. We focused our efforts primarily on abundant lncRNAs (>1 TPM in bulk RNA-seq). It is likely that some lncRNAs are expressed in single ORN classes, or other small subsets of antennal cells, at low levels that fall below our threshold in bulk RNA-seq. We note that lncRNAs can play critical roles even when expressed at low abundance, for example, in biomolecular condensates or when recycled to act on multiple targets. Our study invites examination of the lncRNA landscape using RNA-seq on fluorescence-activated cell-sorted cells or using a targeted approach such as an RT-PCR assay for lncRNAs antisense to chemosensory genes.
Another kind of specificity concerns the environment and the internal state of the fly. lncRNA expression can be regulated by intrinsic or extrinsic signals. Our study was based on flies cultured under standard laboratory conditions, but provides a foundation for profiling of the non-coding antennal transcriptome in flies raised under a wide variety of conditions, including starvation or exposure to odorants, mating partners, or predators.
Finally, the species specificity of lncRNAs may provide insight into the evolution of the olfactory system. Of the 30 lncRNAs mapped to ORNs of D. melanogaster, 10 were not found in the sibling species D. simulans ( ). The evolutionary dynamics of the antennal lncRNA profile is consistent with the rapid emergence and disappearance of lncRNAs observed in other systems. Such rapid dynamics in the olfactory system suggests the possibility of roles for lncRNAs in the adaptation to new ecological niches that contain different olfactory cues. In addition, it is striking that most of the lncRNAs that mapped to pheromone-sensing neurons of D. melanogaster are not found in D. simulans, since pheromone production and detection undergo evolutionary changes during speciation. – It will be interesting to determine whether the lncRNAs in pheromone-sensing neurons contribute to alterations in the recognition of mating partners that occurs during speciation.

## A resource for analysis of lncRNAs in olfactory function
Our work provides a resource for examining the function of lncRNAs in a well-studied genetic model sensory system. The fly olfactory system has been defined at high resolution, most notably in terms of the expression of receptor genes, the functions of its ORNs, and its connectivity in the CNS. It provides an excellent laboratory for the functional analysis of lncRNAs in olfactory response, modulation, and evolution.
We identified lncRNAs that are antisense to Gr64e, Ir21a, and Ir41a, inviting genetic analysis of whether these lncRNAs regulate the transcription or RNA levels of these receptor genes in the antenna. Likewise, five lncRNAs overlap or lie near genes encoding seven odorant-degrading enzymes, a class of olfactory genes whose expression levels are affected by exposure to odors, a form of modulation that lncRNAs could underlie. A circRNA is encoded by a neuropeptide receptor gene, sNPF-R (short-neuropeptide F receptor), which is upregulated and facilitates ORN post-synaptic facilitation in fasted animals. We note also that an lncRNA detected primarily in ORNs expressing Or65a/b, CR45332, lies upstream and on the same strand as Cad-N2, a cell adhesion protein that is also detected primarily in Or65a/b-expressing ORNs. 
Some of these lncRNAs may affect splicing of antennal RNAs. An intriguing example is provided by the aR13 lncRNA, which is enriched in the antenna and lacks a poly(A) tail, consistent with a nuclear localization. aR13 contains sequences complementary to the Charlatan (Chn) gene, which encodes a transcription factor required for antennal development. 
Chn encodes multiple isoforms, of which one is enigmatically localized in axons ( ). Interestingly, two regions of aR13 are predicted to target either side of an alternatively spliced exon included in the axonal form ( ), suggesting a role for aR13 in Chn splicing. A genetic analysis of the role of aR13 in antennal development could be highly informative.
There is ample precedent for lncRNAs that regulate mRNA turnover or translation, , in some cases by binding to mRNAs and promoting their translation. One of the lncRNAs that is broadly expressed in ORNs, CR44024, contains regions complementary to an mRNA encoding G-protein-coupled receptor kinase 2 (Gprk2), which enhances olfactory response ( ). These observations invite a genetic investigation of whether CR44024 acts in olfactory modulation via regulation of Gprk2.
Many lncRNAs are interpreted as non-coding because they have no ORF longer than 100 codons. However, there is growing evidence that numerous cytoplasmic RNAs classified as lncRNAs in fact encode stable micropeptides. – There is also precedent for lncRNAs with dual functions, acting in gene regulation in the nucleus and also encoding a micropeptide that is translated in the cytoplasm. , In this regard, we note that an lncRNA that is expressed in five ORNs, CR44317, is translated during embryogenesis. These observations suggest the possibility that micropeptides could add yet another layer of complexity to ORNs. We note, finally, that our study has focused on lncRNAs (≥200 nucleotides), but a variety of small ncRNAs may also provide insights into ORN functional diversity.
In summary, our study has elaborated a rich repertoire of lncRNAs in the Drosophila olfactory system. We have revealed that ORNs differ in their composition of lncRNAs as well as in the receptors they express. These differences in lncRNAs among ORNs, along with other reports of lncRNAs in olfactory tissues, – suggest interesting directions for research in mammalian olfactory sensory neurons, which have recently been found to have intriguing differences in transcriptional profiles. Our study identifies a wealth of candidates for future functional characterization, opening opportunities for investigating the mechanisms of neuronal modulation. We anticipate that work on the lncRNAs in the Drosophila olfactory system will provide precedent for deciphering the role of lncRNAs in sensory biology writ large.

## Limitations of the study
This study suggests that certain lncRNAs in fruit flies may have evolved an olfactory function, as evidenced by their enrichment in the antenna and in specific sets of ORNs. Further genetic studies are necessary to confirm this function for individual lncRNAs. It is worth noting that among the lncRNAs that are enriched in the antenna (A1 and A2 clusters, ), some may be more abundant in tissues that were not analyzed in this study. Moreover, it is possible that lncRNAs enriched in specific ORNs may also be expressed at low levels in other antennal cells. In situ hybridization will be a valuable tool to further characterize individual lncRNAs and their functions in olfaction.

## EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

## Fly strains
Flies were reared on corn syrup and soy flour culture medium (Archon Scientific) at 25°C and 60% relative humidity in a 12:12-hour light–dark cycle. All experiments used 5-day old flies of both sexes. D. melanogaster strain Canton-S was used. D. simulans (14021–0251.001) were obtained from the Drosophila Species Stock Center.

## METHOD DETAILS

## RNA purification, library preparation, and high-throughput sequencing
Third antennal segments, with the arista still attached, were carefully hand-dissected from 5-day old flies of both sexes and fell immediately into 1.5 ml microfuge tubes containing liquid nitrogen. Four independent biological replicates were collected, from ~150 animals each. The tissues were mechanically crushed with disposable plastic pestles in liquid nitrogen and lysed in RTL lysis buffer (Qiagen) on ice. For RNA extraction, acid phenol was added to the lysate (1:1) and the samples were heated at 65°C 3 times for 2 min, with vortexing in between, before cooling on ice for 5 min. Samples were spin at 15k rpm for 1 min to collect the aqueous phase that contains the RNA. Chloroform was used to remove any residual of phenol. DNA was removed using DNase-Zero (LGC, Biosearch Technologies).
Libraries were prepared using Zymo-Seq RiboFree Total RNA library Kit (Zymo), using a 5-hr rRNA depletion step, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer by the Yale Center for Genome Analysis. Thirty to sixty million 75 bp paired-end or single-end reads were obtained per sample. Raw reads are accessible at the Genbank SRA database.

## Bioinformatics
Reads obtained from the rRNA-depleted libraries were aligned to the D. melanogaster genome (BDGP6) using TopHat (version 2.1.2) with the following options: tophat –library-type fr-firststrand -g 1 –no-discordant –no-mixed. The mapped reads were analyzed with Cufflinks (version 2.2.2) to generate a de novo annotation with the following options: cufflinks –library-type fr-firststrand #no GTF file provided. IGV, Integrative genomics viewer (version 2.8.9), was used to inspect (i) the read coverage of lncRNAs that overlapped a coding gene, (ii) aRs, and (iii) other lncRNAs of interest ( , , , , and – ).
The coding potential of the putative 74 genes was assessed using Coding Potential Calculator (CPC2) (version2), Coding-Potential Assessment Tool (CPAT) (version 3.0.4), and Coding-Non-Coding Index (CNCI) (version 2). All putative peptides were further analyzed with web based software: signalP (version 6), interPro (version 88.0), DeepTMHMM (version 1.0.15), and DeepLoc (version 2). All programs were used with default settings. The gene ontology (GO) analyses were carried out with Flymine (version 53). 
Reads from all bulk RNA-Seq libraries were aligned to the D. melanogaster genome (BDGP6) using TopHat (version 2.1.12) and quantified using HTseq (0.13.5) with the following options: htseq-count -q -f bam -s reverse -r pos -m intersection-nonempty and using a custom annotation file containing known genes (BDGP6.48) and the 74 genes identified in this study.
