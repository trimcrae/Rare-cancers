## Abstract
Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is a heterogeneous multisystemic disease caused by a CTG repeat expansion in DMPK. Transcription of the expanded allele produces toxic CUG repeat RNA that sequesters the MBNL family of alternative splicing (AS) regulators into ribonuclear foci, leading to pathogenic mis-splicing. To identify genetic modifiers of toxic CUG RNA levels and the spliceopathy, we performed a genome-scale siRNA screen using an established HeLa DM1 repeat-selective screening platform. We unexpectedly identified core spliceosomal proteins as a new class of modifiers that rescue the spliceopathy in DM1. Modest knockdown of one of our top hits, SNRPD2, in DM1 fibroblasts and myoblasts, significantly reduces DMPK expression and partially rescues MBNL-regulated AS dysfunction. While the focus on the DM1 spliceopathy has centered around the MBNL proteins, our work reveals an unappreciated role for MBNL:spliceosomal protein stoichiometry in modulating the spliceopathy, revealing new biological and therapeutic avenues for DM1.
Keywords: repeat expansion diseases, myotonic dystrophy, genetic modifiers, genome screen, alternative splicing

## Introduction
Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is a complex multisystem disorder and the most common adult-onset form of muscular dystrophy [ ]. Symptoms are wide-ranging and reflect the multisystemic nature of disease, and include myotonia, myopathy, cardiac conduction abnormalities, insulin resistance, gastrointestinal issues, sleep disturbance and cognitive disruption [ ]. DM1 is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a CTG repeat expansion in the dystrophia myotonica protein kinase (DMPK) gene [ ]. The unaffected population typically have fewer than 50 repeats within DMPK, whereas affected individuals may have several hundred to thousands of CTG repeats in the expanded allele [ ]. While global prevalence for DM1 is often cited as ~ 1 in 8000 individuals [ ], a recent newborn bloodspot screening study reported a prevalence of expanded DMPK CTG alleles (≥ 50 repeats) within New York State of ~1 in 2100 births, indicating that DM1 is likely underdiagnosed [ ].
A major pathogenic mechanism in DM1 involves transcription of the expanded DMPK allele, producing toxic gain-of-function CUG expansion RNA that sequesters the muscleblind-like (MBNL) family of RNA binding proteins into ribonuclear foci [ , , ]. This sequestration results in mis-splicing of MBNL-dependent developmentally regulated cassette exons, leading to failure in the transition from a fetal to adult alternative splicing (AS) program that directly causes many disease symptoms [ , , ]. For example, mis-splicing of the insulin receptor (INSR), muscle-specific chloride channel (CLCN1), and sodium voltage-gated channel alpha subunit 5 (SCN5A) pre-mRNA leads to insulin resistance, myotonia and cardiac conduction defects, respectively [ ]. Thus, MBNL-mediated mis-splicing is a central pathogenic feature in DM1 and serves as an important biomarker of disease [ , ].
There are currently no disease-modifying therapies available for DM1. The recent DM1 phase 1/2a clinical trial (NCT02312011) using antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) to target and degrade the DMPK transcript, concluded without achieving sufficient skeletal muscle drug concentrations predicted to substantially reduce overall DMPK RNA levels [ ]. In addition to the urgent need for therapeutics, the scarcity of data addressing the mechanisms of disease progression, clinical heterogeneity, and response to therapeutics in DM1 has been a direct impediment to prognosis, effectively conducting trials and for measuring therapeutic efficacy of candidate interventions. While the CTG repeat tract length generally correlates with age-at-onset and disease severity [ , ], this association is largely true for the extremes of repeat length. As such, there is considerable heterogeneity in the spliceopathy for DM1 which is not attributable to CTG repeat length [ ], thus indicating the likely existence of modifiers of toxic CUG RNA mediated pathogenesis in DM1.
In this study, we performed a genome-scale siRNA knock-down screen using our previously established CTG repeat-selective HeLa DM1 screening cell line [ ]. We identified core spliceosomal genes that when knocked down, selectively reduce CUG RNA levels and promote MBNL-regulated AS. We validated in-depth, one of our top hits, SNRPD2, a spliceosomal Sm complex protein, and observed a positive correlation between SNRPD2 levels and DMPK expression in RNA sequencing data from DM1 patients. Modest knock-down of SNRPD2 in primary patient-derived fibroblasts and myoblasts significantly reduced DMPK transcript levels and promoted MBNL-regulated AS. Based on these findings, our model is that in DM1 cells, where the MBNL:spliceosome equilibrium is disrupted due to sequestration of MBNL proteins by the toxic CUG expansion RNA, modulating the levels of select core spliceosomal proteins functionally counteracts this pathogenic imbalance. Thus, the spliceosome represents a new class of modifiers of toxic RNA pathogenesis in DM1.

## Results

## A genome-scale siRNA screen in HeLa DM1 cells identifies core spliceosome proteins that selectively modulate CUG expansion RNA levels
To identify novel regulators of toxic CUG RNA in DM1, we conducted a genome-scale knock-down screen ( ) in our previously established HeLa DM1 CTG repeat-selective screening cell line [ ]. This cell line expresses a (CUG)480 RNA and a separate (CUG)0 control RNA, both in the context of DMPK exons 11–15 [ ], allowing rapid CTG repeat-selective screening. It should be noted that this screen is repeat selective but not necessarily expansion selective as it utilizes a (CUG)0 control, instead of an unexpanded CUG repeat. This system has previously been used to identify hits that modulate CUG RNA transcript levels, which have implications for toxic CUG RNA pathogenesis in DM1 [ ]. We prioritized a knock-down screen over knock-out screening to allow for the identification of both essential and non-essential genes whose expression correlate with (CUG)480 RNA levels. We utilized the Sigma-Aldrich MISSION enzymatically prepared siRNA (esiRNA) human genome-scale library containing 16 744 esiRNA targeting 16 432 genomic targets arrayed across 44 384-well plates. DM1 HeLa cells were reverse transfected (~2000 cells per well) in 384-well plates containing 1 target esiRNA per well (~25 ng) which was sufficient to reduce RNA levels of a test target (PAF1) by approximately 50% (Supplementary Material, ). Each 384-well plate also contained 2 wells of esiLuciferase as a negative control and 2 wells of a (CAG)7 siRNA to target the (CUG)480 RNA as a positive control for knock-down during the screen. Following 24 h of transfection, media was replaced with fresh media and cells were cultured for an additional 48 hours for a total of 72 h following esiRNA treatment. Cells were then permeabilized and the “lysate” containing the RNA was directly subjected to multiplex RT-qPCR using probes to measure r(CUG)480 levels relative to r(CUG)0 as done previously [ ] ( ). Treatment with the (CAG)7 siRNA positive control across the screen revealed a relative reduction in r(CUG)480 levels of ~50% ( ). Applying a threshold of < 0.5 relative r(CUG)480 levels, identified 380 hits with a hit rate of ~2.27% from the 16 744 esiRNA targets ( ). We also applied a threshold of > 1.55 relative r(CUG)480 levels to identify a comparable number of factors (348, ~2.08% hit rate) that may potentially stabilize or upregulate CUG expansion RNA ( ).
 To identify common pathways and biological functions underlying the hits, we performed functional annotation using the DAVID Functional Annotation tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [ , ]. The top 5 significantly enriched terms were all related to RNA metabolism and splicing ( ) and unexpectedly, there was enrichment for the spliceosome ( ). We conducted a secondary screen (Horizon Discovery siGENOME smartpool custom library) of all 34 hits from the top 5 enriched terms ( ). The secondary screen was performed in 2 independent HeLa DM1 clonal lines to avoid effects of genomic context from the randomly integrated transgenes [ ]. While none of the hits that increased r(CUG)480 levels were recapitulated in both cell lines, we identified 17 hits that consistently reduced relative r(CUG)480 levels in both clonal lines ( , ). These hits included factors previously associated with CUG expansion RNA and myotonic dystrophy, including DDX17 [ ] and HNRNPA1 [ ] as well as novel factors not previously associated with myotonic dystrophy. Grouping hits by the level of selective r(CUG)480 reduction, revealed that the hits with consistently < 0.5 reduction in both HeLa DM1 clonal lines and from 2 independent (e)siRNA screens, were almost all (7 out of 8 hits, ) core protein components of the spliceosome. The remaining hit was EIF4A3, a component of the exon junction complex associated with the spliceosome during splicing.

## Expression of spliceosomal hits in DM1 patient muscle tissue significantly correlates with DMPK expression and ankle dorsiflexion strength
Given that knockdown of core spliceosomal proteins selectively reduced r(CUG)480 levels in our screen, we wondered if expression of any of our spliceosomal hits correlated with expression of DMPK transcript in DM1 patients. We compared RNA levels of DMPK and our top 8 spliceosomal hits from the secondary screen in RNA sequencing data from DM1 patient tibialis anterior muscle biopsies (DMseq.org) [ ] ( ). There was a significant positive correlation between all 8 hits and DMPK RNA levels in DM1 patients ( ). Furthermore, with the exception of SF3B5 and SNRPD2, all remaining hits displayed a significant negative correlation with normalized ankle dorsiflexion strength ( ) and were significantly upregulated in DM1 patients compared to unaffected controls ( ). To determine if expression of these factors in DM1 patients correlates with other CTG repeat containing genes, we evaluated 3 additional genes containing a CTG repeat tract in an intron (TCF4), 3′UTR like DMPK (CASK) or in the ORF (RPL14) (Supplementary Material, ). The spliceosomal components that were found to be upregulated in DM1 patients all showed a significant positive correlation with expression of CASK and TCF4 but either lacked a correlation with RPL14 expression or had a more modest correlation (Supplementary Material, ). Both SNRPD2 and SF3B5 which were positively correlated with DMPK but did not show significant upregulation in DM1 patients, did not show significant correlations with these other genes. Thus, several core components of the spliceosome display differential expression in DM1 patients and correlate with expression of multiple genes containing a CTG repeat tract. This includes DMPK containing the expansion, which is predicted to differentially impact toxic RNA pathogenesis through its altered levels and therefore clinical outcome, supporting the role of core spliceosomal components as potential modifiers in DM1.
 

## Knocking down select components of the spliceosome partially rescues MBNL-dependent mis-splicing
A reduction in expanded CUG RNA levels is predicted to lead to reduced MBNL sequestration and thus less MBNL-dependent mis-splicing. To determine the effects of modulating our lead hits on MBNL-dependent splicing, we evaluated 2 well-characterized and representative MBNL-regulated mis-splicing events, the MBNL1 exon 5 autoregulatory event (exclusion) and NUMA1 exon 16 (inclusion) following siRNA knockdown of our 17 secondary screen hits (Supplementary Material, ). Hits were ranked based on percent spliced in (PSI) values for both events (Supplementary Material, ). While several hits that reduced r(CUG)480 levels, did not rescue mis-splicing, including EIF4A3, the majority of hits (9/10) that reduced r(CUG)480 levels and showed the greatest reversal of mis-splicing for both events when knocked down, were core components of the spliceosome (Supplementary Material, ).
The spliceosome is a dynamic ribonucleoprotein assembly involving over 100 proteins [ ]. To evaluate the regulatory potential of spliceosomal components, we conducted an additional targeted siRNA screen of a compact panel of 12 selected core splicing factors. This panel included hits from our screen (SNRPD2, SF3B5, SNRNP200, BCAS2, LSM3) and additional splicing factor candidates to represent U1, U2, U4/U6, U5, nineteen complex (NTC), Sm and LSm complexes, as well as the auxiliary components U2AF1, involved in 3′ splice site interaction, and SF1, involved in branch point sequence interaction ( ). These factors were knocked-down (Supplementary Material, ) and evaluated for selective reduction of r(CUG)480 levels and reversal of MBNL-regulated mis-splicing in the DM1 HeLa screening cell line ( ). While knock-down of most spliceosomal factors reduced relative r(CUG)480 levels to varying degrees ( ), only knock-down of 5 components from our list (SNRPD2, SNRNP200, BCAS2, SNRPA & SNRPC) significantly rescued mis-splicing ( ). Interestingly, knock-down of U2AF1 significantly exacerbated mis-splicing ( ). Thus, modulating the expression of select protein components of the spliceosome rescues mis-splicing of MBNL-regulated AS events, but not proportional to the reduction in r(CUG)480 levels ( ). In fact, knock-down of SNRPD2 in the HeLa parental cells without the CTG expansion, promoted MBNL-regulated splicing of MBNL1 exon 5 and NUMA1 exon 16 (Supplementary Material, ). These results indicate that the effects on r(CUG)480 levels and MBNL-regulated mis-splicing, while both mediated by the levels of specific spliceosomal components, are not necessarily directly dependent.
 

## SNRPD2 and SNRNP200 levels modulate DMPK expression and MBNL-regulated alternative splicing in primary fibroblasts
While the spliceosome is necessary to catalyze basal splicing in eukaryotes, differential expression of specific protein and RNA components of the spliceosome has been found to modulate alternative splicing outcome without inducing general splicing failure [ ]. Much of the focus on alternative splicing dysregulation in DM1 has centered on the MBNL protein family, however the vast AS regulatory potential conferred by varied spliceosomal protein expression has been largely unexplored in the context of DM1. To investigate the role of core spliceosomal proteins in modifying the DM1-associated spliceopathy in greater detail, we focused our subsequent analyses in primary cell lines on SNRPD2 and SNRNP200, two of the top overall hits from the HeLa DM1 screen ( ), and the targeted spliceosomal screen ( ), which also showed significant correlations with DMPK expression (SNRPD2 and SNRNP200) and normalized ankle dorsiflexion strength (SNRNP200) in DM1 patients ( ). We first quantified SNRPD2 RNA and protein levels following siRNA-mediated knock-down in DM1 patient and unaffected control dermal fibroblasts ( ). This analysis showed a modest level of reduction at the protein level allowing us to probe the effects of SNRPD2 variability rather than depletion. With 70-80% reduction in SNRPD2 RNA ( ), there was approximately a 40% reduction in protein ( ). This level of knock-down resulted in a modest but significant reduction in DMPK RNA in DM1 patient fibroblasts and in unaffected control fibroblasts ( ). SNRPD2 knock-down in the HeLa DM1 cells led to a selective reduction in r(CUG)480 levels compared to the r(CUG)0 control. That we observed an effect on DMPK levels in both control and DM1 fibroblasts supports a mechanism involving the CTG repeat tract, but independent of expansion status since control fibroblasts contain unexpanded CTG repeats in DMPK. In DM1 patient fibroblasts, there was a significant rescue of the PSI of INSR exon 11 ( ), an MBNL-regulated AS event [ ] that is excluded more frequently in DM1 patients [ , ] due to sequestration of MBNL proteins. Interestingly, the effects on INSR exon 11 splicing were also evident in control fibroblasts treated with siSNRPD2 ( ). Similar effects were observed from knock-down of another top hit, SNRNP200 (Supplementary Material, ). These results validate a role for SNRPD2 and SNRNP200 levels in modulating DMPK expression and MBNL-mediated mis-splicing, two key molecular determinants of pathogenesis, in DM1 patient cells. While variable expression of these factors in unaffected individuals may also modulate DMPK expression and MBNL1 AS, their expression levels in DM1 patients could modulate the molecular pathology and thus clinical severity of pathogenesis.
 To ensure that the effects of SNRPD2 and SNRNP200 knock-down were not due to general splicing failure, we evaluated DMPK expression and INSR exon 11 splicing following treatment of control and DM1 fibroblasts with the general splicing inhibitor isoginkgetin [ ] (Supplementary Material, ). We confirmed splicing inhibition through accumulation of DNAJB1 pre-mRNA containing intron 2 across a dose range that is known to induce pre-mRNA splicing failure as reported previously [ ] (Supplementary Material, ). Treatment with isoginkgetin did not reduce DMPK expression nor was INSR exon 11 inclusion enhanced in either control or DM1 fibroblasts (Supplementary Material, ), in contrast to SNRPD2 and SNRNP200 knock-down. These results indicate that general splicing failure was not the cause of the splicing rescue of DM1 events following SNRPD2 and SNRNP200 knock-down.
In DM1 cells, SNRPD2 and SNRNP200 knockdown shifted splicing levels towards those in unaffected control cells ( , Supplementary Material, ). However, the increase in splicing in unaffected control cells suggests that knock-down of spliceosome components may affect MBNL-regulated AS in DM1 cells through reduction in DMPK transcripts containing the CUG expansion and through additional mechanisms. To test our hypothesis, we performed compound knock-down of MBNL1 and SNRPD2 in control fibroblasts (Supplementary Material, ) and evaluated MBNL-regulated AS events that were previously characterized in this cell line [ ] ( , Supplementary Material, ). Consistent with mis-splicing of INSR exon 11 due to reduced MBNL1 levels, siRNA mediated knockdown of MBNL1 in unaffected cells resulted in a significant decrease in PSI ( ). Furthermore, compound knock-down of SNRPD2 and MBNL1 resulted in a significant increase in PSI compared to MBNL1 knock-down alone ( ). These results were confirmed for an independent MBNL1-regulated inclusion event (FLNB exon 31), with a non-significant trend observed for another independent MBNL1-regulated exclusion event (SYNE1 exon 65; Supplementary Material, ). These results highlight the sensitivity of MBNL1 and spliceosomal protein stoichiometry in regulating alternative splicing.
To investigate the transcriptomic effects of SNRPD2 knockdown more broadly, we carried out RNA sequencing (RNAseq) on control and DM1 fibroblasts treated with siControl or siSNRPD2 from 3 biological replicates. Analysis of gene expression revealed largely similar profiles for unaffected control and DM1 fibroblasts treated with siSNRPD2 compared to siControls using a significance cut-off of < 0.05 adjusted P value and > 0.5 log2 fold change ( ). As expected SNRPD2 expression levels were reduced in both unaffected (control) and DM1 fibroblasts treated with siSNRPD2. Additionally, there were a comparable number of both unique and overlapping genes in the siSNRPD2 treated control and DM1 fibroblasts ( , ). A notable overlapping gene was MBNL1 which was upregulated in both control and DM1 fibroblasts following SNRPD2 knockdown ( , ). RT-qPCR confirmed significant MBNL1 upregulation at the RNA level following siSNRPD2 treatment ( ). However, evaluation of MBNL1 protein levels following SNRPD2 knock-down, did not reveal a significant corresponding upregulation (Supplementary Material, ).
 Analysis of alternative splicing using a change in percent spliced in (ΔPSI) cut-off > 0.1, FDR < 0.1 and a minimum 25× read depth across junctions revealed comparable global effects in DM1 and control fibroblasts treated with siSNRPD2 compared to siControl ( , and ). Consistent with previous studies on the effect of knocking down core spliceosomal components [ ], knock-down of SNRPD2 largely affected skipped exon (SE) events without inducing general splicing failure through widespread intron retention (RI, ). The median |ΔPSI| for each AS category was roughly the same for both unaffected and DM1 cells ( ). Evaluation of selected previously characterized MBNL-regulated AS events in DM [ , , ], revealed significant reversal of mis-splicing in the DM1 cells with modest effects in unaffected control cells ( ). This result is consistent with the increased sensitivity of DM1 cells to MBNL-mediated AS due to toxic CUG expansion RNA. Interestingly, DMPK exons 13 and 14, which were previously identified as alternatively spliced [ , ], were significantly spliced out at a greater frequency when SNRPD2 was knocked down (Supplementary Material, ). Taken together, our analysis of DM1 and control fibroblasts indicate that DMPK expression and MBNL-regulated AS are sensitive to SNRPD2 levels. Thus, in DM1 cells where MBNL-regulated AS is disrupted by the toxic CUG expansion DMPK transcripts, modulating SNRPD2, a core spliceosomal protein, appears to functionally counteract this pathogenic effect by downregulating DMPK levels and by reversing MBNL-mediated mis-splicing, though this effect does not appear to be simply due to elevated MBNL1 protein levels.

## Knockdown of SNRPD2 mitigates RNA toxicity in primary DM1 myoblasts
Muscle dysfunction is a central symptom of DM, thus we evaluated the effect of modulating SNRPD2 levels on DMPK expression and MBNL-mediated AS in primary patient-derived myoblasts. Myoblasts were previously derived and characterized from an unaffected control individual and a DM1 patient containing ~1900–3000 CTG repeats in DMPK [ ]. Myoblasts were treated with siRNA targeting SNRPD2 or non-targeting control siRNA followed by immunological detection and quantification of SNRPD2 protein levels. We observed a mean reduction of ~25% for SNRPD2 protein in both unaffected and DM1 myoblasts ( ). This modest decrease in SNRPD2 protein was sufficient to significantly reduce overall DMPK transcript levels by over 50% in both control and DM1 myoblasts ( ). Inclusion of DMPK exons 13 and 14 was also significantly reduced following SNRPD2 knock-down in myoblasts (Supplementary Material, ) as was observed in fibroblasts (Supplementary Material, ). The reduced inclusion levels of exons 13 and 14 could in part be responsible for the overall decrease in DMPK transcript levels if this isoform is less stable compared to the DMPK isoform including exons 13 and 14.
 To determine if DMPK reduction resulting from reduced SNRPD2 levels resulted in a decrease in toxic CUG ribonuclear foci in DM1 cells, we performed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) following treatment of DM1 myoblasts with siControl and siSNRPD2 ( ). While the overall percentage of foci-positive nuclei was not significantly altered following SNRPD2 knockdown, the percentage of nuclei containing more than 2 foci was significantly reduced ( ).
Furthermore, SNRPD2 knockdown rescued MBNL-regulated mis-splicing in DM1 myoblast cells without significantly altering the splicing of the same events in control cells ( ). Mis-splicing of both exclusion (MBNL1 exon 5, MBNL2 exon 5) and inclusion (SYNE1 exon 137) AS events were significantly rescued by reducing SNRPD2 levels in DM1 cells. Expression of MBNL1 RNA was elevated in control and DM1 myoblasts upon SNRPD2 knockdown (Supplementary Material, ), however protein levels displayed a more variable response (Supplementary Material, ), indicating that rescue of MBNL-mediated AS may not be simply due to upregulating MBNL1 protein. Taken together, these data demonstrate that modest reductions in SNRPD2 protein levels in primary DM1 patient muscle cells, significantly modulate key molecular hallmarks of DM1 pathogenesis, including DMPK expression, toxic CUG ribonuclear foci, and MBNL-regulated alternative splicing.

## Discussion
To identify genetic factors that modulate toxic RNA levels and the MBNL-mediated spliceopathy in DM1, we conducted a genome-scale siRNA knockdown screen utilizing the HeLa DM1 toxic CUG RNA screening platform [ ]. This screen and subsequent characterization identified core components of the spliceosome as a new class of modifiers in DM1. Modest knockdown of select core components of the spliceosome significantly reduces DMPK expression and promotes MBNL regulated AS in our HeLa DM1 cell model and in primary DM1 patient-derived fibroblasts and myoblasts. While MBNL mediated mis-splicing is a well-known contributor to pathogenesis in DM1, the contribution of MBNL:spliceosome stoichiometry has been overlooked. Our discovery has implications for biological, biomarker and therapeutic development in DM1.
The discovery of MBNL protein sequestration to CUG expansion DMPK transcripts [ , ] and the resulting mis-splicing of pre-mRNA targets that causes symptoms in DM1 [ , ] has firmly established a therapeutic target towards disease-modifying therapies. While toxic RNA gain-of-function plays a central role in DM1, the contribution of DMPK deletion to DM1 pathogenic hallmarks are very minor [ ], with loss of DMPK well tolerated in mice with minimal impact to tissues (i.e. cardiac and muscle) where DMPK expression is particularly high [ ]. Leveraging these findings, Ionis Pharmaceuticals conducted a phase 1/2a clinical trial (NCT02312011) using antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) to target and degrade the DMPK transcript through an RNase H1 mediated mechanism [ ]. While providing proof-of-concept for the safe utilization of ASOs targeting DMPK in DM1 and establishing a path forward for therapeutic development, limitations identified in the clinical trial included insufficient drug delivery and a variable therapeutic response [ ]. It is noteworthy that this variability, measured by an MBNL-dependent splicing panel, could not be accounted for simply by the concentration of drug present. Though limited by the small sample size of the trial (6–10 participants per treatment group), this finding reiterates the extreme clinical heterogeneity observed in DM1 [ ], and emphasizes the need for a better understanding of the underlying molecular determinants of the variable spliceopathy. In this regard, our unbiased genetic screen identifying spliceosomal factors as modifiers of DMPK expression and MBNL-mediated mis-splicing, provides insight into the variability observed in the DM1 spliceopathy and could offer important insight for the utilization of splicing as a clinical trial biomarker in DM1.
The reduced expression of core spliceosomal proteins led to selective reduction of r(CUG)480 levels in the HeLa DM1 cell line, in which r(CUG)0 serves as the control but a generalized downregulation of DMPK in primary fibroblasts and myoblasts. This difference suggests that the presence of a repeat tract, whether it is unexpanded or expanded, serves as a cis-regulatory element that contributes to DMPK regulation mediated through the spliceosome. RNAseq and RT-PCR analysis of fibroblasts treated with siSNRPD2 revealed significantly lower inclusion of DMPK exons 13 and 14 (Supplementary Material, ), which has been reported to result in a transcript isoform that fuses exons 12 and 15 leading to a frameshift that causes early translation termination [ , ]. It is not clear however, if alternative splicing of DMPK exons 13 and 14 contribute to DMPK levels. Future studies addressing the precise mechanism of DMPK modulation by the spliceosome will be of considerable interest.
Our findings demonstrate that compound knock-down of MBNL1 and SNRPD2 in unaffected control fibroblasts lacking the CTG expansion shifts MBNL-regulated splicing ( , Supplementary Material, ), suggesting that some of the effect in correcting splicing abnormalities in DM1 could be mediated through modulating MBNL:spliceosome stoichiometry. Based on these findings, our working model postulates that a functional imbalance in MBNL:spliceosome equilibrium is created in DM1 due to the sequestration of MBNL proteins to the toxic CUG expansion RNA, and up-regulation of core spliceosomal components ( ). This imbalance can be restored, at least partially, by modulating select core spliceosomal proteins ( ). AS regulators such as MBNL proteins bind their pre-mRNA targets which directs spliceosome recruitment appropriately to promote exon skipping or inclusion [ ]. In DM1, MBNL proteins are titrated away to toxic CUG expansion RNA and thus spliceosomal interaction with pre-mRNA is mis-regulated at the level of AS. A modest reduction in particular spliceosomal proteins within a regulatory window that does not induce splicing failure may partially restore MBNL AS regulation by functionally re-balancing stoichiometry ( ). In the unaffected population, variable expression of certain spliceosomal components could contribute to variability in DMPK expression and AS differences without clinical significance, however in DM1, due to the production of pathogenic CUG expansion RNA from DMPK and the resulting MBNL sequestration and pathogenic mis-splicing, variable spliceosomal protein expression would be an important biological determinant underlying the clinical heterogeneity. This point is underscored by our observation that some of our spliceosomal hits were found to be upregulated in DM1 patients ( ), and thus it is tempting to speculate that reducing expression levels of one or more of those factors to that observed in unaffected controls could have significant therapeutic potential.
 It will be informative to delineate the precise mechanism through which spliceosomal gene expression may be altered in DM1 and how modulating expression of select core spliceosome components affects DMPK expression and reverses MBNL-regulated mis-splicing. The expression correlation of our top spliceosomal hits and multiple CTG repeat containing genes ( , Supplementary Material, ) is consistent with a potential cis-regulatory role of CTG repeats acting through the spliceosome. However, it is currently unclear if this effect may be directly mediated through splice site selection influenced by the CTG repeat tract or through an indirect role where spliceosome expression levels modulate expression of one or more transcription or RNA processing factors that in-turn act on the CTG/CUG repeat-containing genes/transcripts. Likewise, the effect of spliceosome modulation in promoting MBNL-mediated AS remains to be elucidated. While analysis of MBNL1 RNA levels revealed consistent up-regulation following SNRPD2 knock-down, MBNL1 protein levels do not appear to follow suit ( , Supplementary Material, and ). While we cannot entirely rule out this effect to be due to MBNL1 protein levels, other mechanisms should be considered. For example, because AS regulation generally proceeds co-transcriptionally, kinetic coupling between transcription rate and the splicing machinery is necessary to ensure proper splicing [ ]. By this model, in DM1, MBNL AS regulators are sequestered to toxic CUG repeats and hence the kinetics of splice site selection and spliceosome recruitment to the appropriate sites on the pre-mRNA during transcription may be biased against MBNL proteins due to low free MBNL concentrations, but when spliceosome factors are knocked-down, spliceosome rates may be sufficiently slowed to enable the reduced concentration of MBNL proteins to bind their target sites during the splicing cycle. Future studies are necessary to explore these speculative models and build upon our current findings.
While we studied DM1 here, the regulatory potential of modulating spliceosomal components to counteract pathogenic mis-splicing may also apply to other repeat expansion diseases involving sequestration of MBNL such as myotonic dystrophy type 2 [ , ] and Fuch’s endothelial corneal dystrophy [ ]. Furthermore, repeat expansion diseases involving AS dysregulation independent of MBNL protein sequestration but potentially associated with other AS regulators, such as C9orf72 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis—frontotemporal dementia [ ] and Huntington disease [ ], may also be impacted by spliceosomal protein expression. Future studies to directly test these hypotheses may shed light on an underappreciated role of AS regulator:spliceosomal dynamics that may be relevant to a multitude of developmental and disease contexts.

## Materials and methods

## esiRNA screen in HeLa DM1 cells
The HeLa DM1 cell model was previously characterized and described in detail [ ]. Briefly, these clonally derived dual expression cells contain randomly integrated constructs expressing an interrupted (CTG)480 repeat tract composed of 24 units of [(CTG)20(CTCGA)] and a separate (CTG)0 control tract both within the genomic context of exons 11–15 of human DMPK with the introns [ ]. Multiplex RT-qPCR is performed using the same reverse transcriptase primer and PCR primers downstream of the repeat in the 3′UTR, but with different fluorescent probes targeting a unique sequence in the 3′UTR downstream of the repeat tract position to distinguish the 2 transcripts produced.
DM1 HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) and reverse-transfected into 384-well plates. Each well contained 25 ng of esiRNA (Sigma-Aldrich MISSION® human genome-scale) complexed with INTERFERin® transfection reagent (Polyplus) in Gibco Opti-MEM™ reduced serum media. A (CUG)7 siRNA (Horizon Discovery) was used as a positive control for knock-down throughout the screen at a final concentration of 10 nM. Media was replaced with fresh DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% P/S following ~24 h of treatment and the cells cultured for an additional 48 h under standard conditions of 37°C and 5% CO2. Following ∼72 h of total culture, cells were washed with 1× Phosphate Buffered Saline (Corning), and the plates were stored at −80°C until processing.
Direct, multiplexed RT-qPCR from the treatment plates to determine r(CUG)480 levels relative to r(CUG)0 was performed as described previously [ ]. Briefly, each well was incubated with 5 μl of 0.25% Igepal buffer containing 10 mM Tris·HCl (pH 7.5) and 150 mM NaCl on an orbital shaker for 5 min at room temperature. One microliter of the “lysate” was used for cDNA generation with HT_RTprimer (IDT) [ ] and SuperScript IV RT (Thermo Fisher). qPCR using the cDNA template was carried out on a Bio-Rad C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler using Hot Start Taq 2× Master Mix (NEB) with HT_Forward and HT_Reverse primers and custom HT_Probe1 and HT_Probe2 fluorescent probes (IDT) (Supplementary Material, ) [ ]. Data were analyzed using the comparative (ΔΔCT) method for r(CUG)480 expression relative to r(CUG)0 levels for each siRNA treatment, normalized to non-targeting esiLuciferase control treatment.

## Cell culture and siRNA treatment of primary fibroblasts and myoblasts
Primary patient and control fibroblast and myoblast cell lines were previously derived from skin and muscle biopsies under a University of Florida-approved IRB protocol with informed consent from all subjects and characterized [ ]. Fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% P/S to ~50%–60% confluence. For knock downs, siRNA was complexed with Polyplus INTERFERin® transfection reagent in Gibco Opti-MEM™ reduced serum media and added to each well for a final siRNA concentration of 10 nM. Following ~24 h of treatment, media was replaced with fresh DMEM with 15% FBS and 1% P/S and cells were cultured for an additional 48 h under standard conditions of 37°C and 5% CO2. Following ∼72 h of total culture, cells were washed with 1× Phosphate Buffered Saline (Corning), and the plates were stored at −80°C until processing. Myoblasts were cultured using the Lonza SkGM™-2 Skeletal Muscle Cell Growth Medium-2 BulletKit™ and siRNA treatments were carried out as described above for fibroblasts.

## RT-PCR splicing analysis and RT-qPCR
RT-PCR analysis was carried out as previously described with minor modifications [ ]. Total cellular RNA was isolated using the Quick-RNA Midiprep Kit (Zymo Research Corporation) with on-column DNase I according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed with SuperScript IV (Thermo Fisher) with random hexamer (IDT) priming. The cDNA template was used for semi-quantitative PCR using specific primer sets listed (Supplementary Material, ). PCR products were analyzed using capillary electrophoresis on the Advanced Analytical Fragment Analyzer with the 1- to 500-bp DNF-905 kit. ## Immunoblotting
The protocol was performed as described previously [ ]. Cells were washed with 1× PBS (Corning) and lysed in 200 μl of radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (ThermoScientific) with 1× cOmplete Protease Inhibitors (Roche) for 15 min on ice. DNA was sheared by passage through a 21-gauge needle, lysates were centrifuged at 21 000 × g for 15 min at 4°C and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was measured from the lysate using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoScientific). A minimum of 10 μg of soluble protein lysate was separated on 4%–12% Bis–Tris gel (BioRad) and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in Intercept® (TBS) Blocking Buffer (LI-COR) and probed with anti-SNRPD2 antibody (Abcam, ab198296, 1:2000), anti-MBNL1 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, MABE70, 1:400) and anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) antibody (Abcam, ab8245, 1:10000) overnight at 4°C in blocking solution. Following 3 × 5 min washes in 1× PBST, membranes were incubated with species-specific IRDye-680 (SNRPD2/MBNL1) and IRDye-800 (GAPDH) secondary antibodies (Li-COR) in blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature and washed 3 × 5 min in 1× PBST. Bands were imaged on the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Quantification of protein levels was performed using Image J. All protein levels were normalized within each lane to GAPDH which served as the loading control.

## RNA sequencing library preparation
The protocol was carried out as previously described with minor modifications [ , ]. RNA was isolated from cells using the Aurum Total RNA mini kit (Bio-Rad) with on-column DNase I treatment as outlined in the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) following rRNA Depletion (NEBNext Kit), with a total of 500 ng of input RNA. The protocol was carried out following the manufacturer’s instructions with the following exceptions: 40× adaptor dilutions; bead incubations were performed at room temperature and 10 cycles of library amplification. Libraries were quantified using the NEBNext® Library Quant Kit for Illumina, inspected on the Advanced Analytical Fragment Analyzer using the NGS Analysis DNF-474 kit, pooled at equimolar concentrations and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 sequencer at The RNA Institute, University at Albany.

## RNA sequencing analysis
The protocol was carried out as previously described with minor modifications [ , ]. Raw reads were checked for quality using FASTQC (Version 0.11.9) and aligned to GRCh38.p13 human genome using STAR (Version 2.7.10b) [ ] and Ensembl GRCh38.100 gene annotation. STAR (Version 2.7.10b) was used to generate gene counts files. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (Version 1.38.3) [ ]. MA plots were generated using ggpubr R package (Version 0.6.0) and Venn diagram was made with ggvenn R package (Version 0.1.10). For splicing analysis, rMATS (Version 4.1.1) [ ] and maseR R package (Version 1.16.0) were used to quantitate isoform abundances using a false discovery rate (FDR) cut-off < 0.1 with a minimum read depth of 25 across junctions and filtered for events with changes in percent spliced in (ΔPSI) > 10%.

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization
Myoblasts were seeded in either 4- or 8-chamber slides (Corning BioCoat®) for siRNA treatment at ~50%-60% confluence as described above. Cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, permeabilized using 70% ethanol, and prehybridized in the presence of tRNA (Thermo Fisher catalog no. AM7119) for 30 min at 37°C. Cells were incubated with a Cy3-(CAG)8 probe (IDT) overnight at 42°C. Slides were washed with prewarmed (42°C) 40% (vol/vol) formamide in 2× saline sodium citrate and mounted by using ProLong Diamond Antifade mountant with DAPI (Life Technologies). Nuclei were imaged on a Zeiss Axioscope 5.
