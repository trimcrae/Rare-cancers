## Introduction
Lymphoma represents a diverse group of hematologic malignancies, broadly classified into Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [ ]. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common and aggressive subtype, of NHL [ , ]. Clinically, lymphoma exhibits significant biological heterogeneity, with molecular subtypes and genetic mutations contributing to variations in treatment response and prognosis [ ]. Despite advances in frontline therapies, standard treatment user remission in only 60 %–70 % of patients [ ]. However, a substantial proportion of patients experience relapse or are refractory to treatment, and treatment options for these cases remain limited, often resulting in poor outcomes [ ]. This challenge, coupled with the toxicity of standard chemotherapy, underscores the need for innovative therapies that can specifically target tumor cells while minimizing systemic side effects [ ].
Phototherapy is a promising alternative in cancer treatment owing to its precision, minimal invasiveness, and ability to induce targeted cytotoxicity [ , ]. Among the various phototherapeutic approaches, photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) have garnered significant attention for their potential to enhance tumor specificity and immune activation in lymphoma [ ]. PDT, through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon light activation of a photosensitizer (PS), induces localized cell death while triggering immunogenic responses [ ]. Meanwhile, PTT, uses nanoparticles to absorb near-infrared (NIR) light and generate heat, leading to hyperthermia and direct tumor destruction [ ]. Both modalities offer localized treatment and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling, facilitating immune recognition and tumor cell destruction [ ]. For patients with lymphoma resistant to conventional treatment, phototherapy holds promise as a complementary or stand-alone option that could improve tumor control and patient outcomes [ ].
Nanotechnology enhances the efficacy of phototherapy in treating lymphoma by improving the delivery, specificity, and bioavailability of PSs and photothermal agents [ ]. Nanocarriers such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and antibody-conjugated nanoplatforms enable tumor-targeted delivery, controlled drug release, and deep tissue penetration. These advances address the limitations of conventional light-based therapies and support the development of synergistic treatment strategies that integrate phototherapy with immune modulation and systemic chemotherapy.
In this review, we systematically explore the application of phototherapy, specifically PDT and PTT, in lymphoma treatment ( ). We discuss the current state of research, underlying mechanisms, and therapeutic potential of these approaches in lymphoma management. Furthermore, we address the challenges associated with integrating phototherapy into clinical practice, including issues of light penetration, nanoparticle delivery, and immune system modulation. Finally, we provide insights into future directions for enhancing the efficacy of phototherapy in treating lymphoma, particularly when combined with immunotherapy and nanotechnology.

## Heterogeneity and therapeutic challenges in lymphoma
2.1

## Biological heterogeneity in lymphoma
Lymphoma encompasses a diverse group of hematologic malignancies originating from lymphocytes, and characterized by marked biological heterogeneity at the genetic, molecular, and cellular levels [ ]. This heterogeneity significantly influences disease classification, prognosis, and therapeutic responses [ ]. Lymphomas are broadly categorized into HL and NHL, with NHL, with NHL encompassing various subtypes, such as DLBCL, follicular lymphoma (FL), and mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), each defined by distinct molecular and clinical characteristics [ ].
Lymphoma is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous disease, characterized by significant variation in molecular features, clinical behavior, and treatment response [ ].The World Health Organization classification of lymphoma is summarized in [ , ]. Genomic alterations play a crucial role in the pathogenesis and classification of lymphoma [ ]. High-throughput sequencing studies has identified a spectrum of mutations, chromosomal translocations, and copy number variations that contribute to tumor initiation and progression [ ]. For instance, DLBCL can be broadly classified into two major molecular subtypes: germinal center B-cell-like (GCB) and activated B-cell-like (ABC) DLBCL, each defined by distinct gene expression profiles and signaling pathway activation patterns [ ]. GCB-DLBCL is commonly associated with EZH2, BCL2, and CREBBP mutations, which influence epigenetic regulation and apoptosis resistance, whereas ABC-DLBCL often exhibits mutations in MYD88, CD79B, and CARD11, leading to constitutive activation of NF-κB signaling. In general, GCB-DLBCL is associated with a more favourable prognosis and greater responsiveness to standard therapies, whereas ABC-DLBCL tends to follow a more aggressive clinical course, with higher rates of treatment failure and poorer overall survival [ ]. The genetic subtypes of DLBCL are summarized in . 
FL is characterized by the t(14; 18)(q32; q21) translocation, leading to BCL2 overexpression and prolonged cell survival [ ]. These genetic variations not only serve as prognosis biomarkers but also provide potential therapeutic targets for precision medicine [ ]. Additional genetic aberrations, such as MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 rearrangements, contribute further to the heterogeneity, resulting in “double-hit” or “triple-hit” lymphomas, which are resistant to conventional treatments [ ]. This molecular diversity complicates treatment strategies because a one-size-fits-all approach is often ineffective, leading to variability in patient outcomes [ ].

2.2

## Limitations of existing therapies for lymphoma
Lymphoma treatment has evolved significantly with the advent of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, improving patient outcomes across various subtypes [ ]. However, several limitations persist, including drug resistance, treatment-related toxicity, and the challenge of effectively managing relapsed and refractory disease [ ].
Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of lymphoma treatment, particularly for aggressive subtypes such as DLBCL and HL [ ]. However, its efficacy is significantly reduced in indolent forms such as FL, which are prone to relapse and often require long-term management rather than curative therapy [ ]. Radiotherapy is another standard modality that is effective in early-stage HL and localized NHL, but its clinical utility is limited by its inability to treat systemic or advanced disease [ , ]. In recent years, targeted therapies, including Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitors, BCL-2 inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs; e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors), offer novel treatment avenues, particularly for patients with defined molecular profiles [ , ]. Nonetheless, immune checkpoint blockade has variable efficacy in DLBCL, typically benefiting only select subgroups [ ].
CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies, such as axicabtagene ciloleucel, and tisagenlecleucel, have revolutionized lymphoma treatment by engineering T cells to target malignant B cells, marking a breakthrough for treating relapsed or refractory aggressive B-cell lymphomas [ , ]. However, despite its success, CAR-T therapy is associated with substantial toxicity, such as cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, and not all patients are eligible or respond to this therapy; only a subset of patients achieves long-term remission, with relapse occurring in approximately 60 %–70 % of treated individuals [ , ]. Additionally, the complexity of CAR-T manufacturing, along with its high cost, further limits accessibility and widespread implementation in clinical practice [ ].
A particularly challenging aspect of lymphoma treatment is management of high-risk and refractory subtypes [ ]. Double-hit and triple-hit lymphomas, which harbor rearrangements involving MYC, BCL2, and/or BCL6, respond poorly to standard regimens such as R-CHOP, and often require more intensive chemotherapy such as DA-EPOCH-R, which carries a higher toxicity burden [ ]. Primary central nervous system lymphoma presents another unique challenge, as the blood–brain barrier limits the penetration of systemic therapies, necessitating the use of high-dose methotrexate-based regimens that are often poorly tolerated [ ]. T-cell lymphomas, which exhibit greater molecular heterogeneity than their B-cell counterparts, lack effective targeted therapies and frequently have worse prognoses due to resistance to conventional treatments [ ].
The available treatment strategies for lymphoma are discussed in detail ( ). Although current therapies have significantly improved the prognosis of many patients with lymphoma, challenges remain in addressing resistance, toxicity, and disease relapse [ ]. These limitations highlight the pressing need for new treatment modalities, particularly for patients with refractory or high-risk lymphoma who have exhausted conventional therapeutic options [ ]. Compared with traditional therapies, phototherapy offers several advantages that address the aforementioned limitations. Unlike chemotherapy and radiotherapy, phototherapy enables spatially and temporally controlled tumor ablation with minimal systemic toxicity, thereby reducing damage to healthy tissues [ ]. Moreover, the ability of PDT and PTT to induce immunogenic cell death provides a unique mechanism to enhance anti-tumor immunity, which conventional treatments often lack [ ]. By leveraging targeted nanoparticle delivery, phototherapy can achieve high tumor specificity and circumvent drug resistance mechanisms that are prevalent in standard chemotherapy and targeted therapies [ ]. These features highlight phototherapy as a promising strategy to overcome therapeutic bottlenecks in lymphoma management. 

2.3

## Phototherapy as an alternative strategy
Given the limitations of current lymphoma therapies, including drug resistance, insufficient tumor specificity, and poor immune modulation, novel treatment strategies that can overcome these challenges have attracted growing interest [ ]. Phototherapy, particularly PDT and PTT, has emerged as a promising alternative, especially when used in combination with conventional treatments, where it exhibits strong synergistic potential [ ]. Lymphoma, unlike many solid tumors, often involves lymphoid tissues that are accessible to external light or interstitial fiber-optic illumination, such as superficial nodes, tonsils, and cutaneous or mucosal sites [ ]. This anatomical accessibility makes it more amenable to light-based therapies. Moreover, lymphoma is a hematologic malignancy characterized by high immunogenicity and systemic immune interactions [ ]. Since both PDT and PTT can induce ICD and enhance antigen presentation, phototherapy aligns well with the immunologically responsive nature of lymphoma [ ].
Additionally, nanotechnology allows for the targeted delivery of PSs or photothermal agents to lymphoma cells expressing CD19 or CD20, minimizing systemic toxicity [ ]. These characteristics together make phototherapy a compelling and precise alternative or adjunct to conventional lymphoma treatments. These characteristics together make phototherapy a compelling and precise alternative or adjunct to conventional lymphoma treatments. In studies of PDT-induced ICD, the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) in response to ICD promotes the recruitment and maturation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), leading to the activation of CD8+ T-cells and induction of antitumor immunity ( A) [ ]. PTT is another strategy to ablate tumor cells with high precision through the use of nanoparticles to generate heat upon NIR light exposure [ ]. PTT nanocomposites are personalized to take advantage of TME-activated NIR absorption ( B), allowing for enhanced photothermal conversion and ultimately non-invasive and effective tumor ablation [ ]. 
The unique ability of phototherapy to induce tumor-specific cytotoxicity while sparing surrounding healthy tissues makes it an attractive option for patients with lymphoma who are not candidates for aggressive therapies or who have developed resistance to standard treatments [ ]. Additionally, the capacity of phototherapy to modulate the TME, particularly by inducing ICD, suggests that it may exert a synergistic effect when combined with immunotherapy approaches, such as CAR-T cells or ICIs [ ]. An approach combining CAR-T cell immunotherapy and a chemo-photothermal therapy nanoplat form (FA-Gd-GERTs@Ibrutinib) based on gadolinium-loaded gap-enhanced Raman tags (Gd-GERTs) allows for more precise CT/MRI/SERS bioimaging of tumors through active targeting of folate receptors (FRs). The photothermal effect of this nanoplatform not only directly kills lymphoma cells and destroys the tumor microenvironment, but also promotes the recruitment of immune cells and the secretion of multiple chemokines and cytokines without inhibiting CD19 CAR-T cell activity, which greatly improves the therapeutic efficacy ( C) [ ].Although most phototherapy studies focused on solid tumors, relevant insights can still be drawn for lymphoma. For example, in a hepatocellular carcinoma model, a nanomedicine-based PTT system has been used to activate antitumor immune responses and promote macrophage M1 polarization in the TME. When combined with an anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody, this system further enhances the cytotoxic activity of T lymphocytes, demonstrating strong therapeutic synergy ( D) [ ]. Although this example is derived from a non-lymphoma model, it provides a conceptual basis for exploring similar phototherapy-immune strategies in lymphoma. Therefore, incorporating phototherapy into the lymphoma treatment paradigm could offer novel opportunities to improve the outcomes of patients with this challenging and heterogeneous disease.
PDT primarily induces oxidative stress pathways by generating ROS, leading to mitochondrial damage, caspase-dependent apoptosis, and DNA fragmentation [ ]. Key molecular pathways involved include the activation of the p53-Bax-caspase cascade, autophagy through Beclin-1, and endoplasmic reticulum stress via PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 [ ]. In contrast, PTT induces rapid protein denaturation and membrane rupture through hyperthermia, leading to necrosis or thermal apoptosis via the MAPK/JNK and HSP-mediated stress pathways [ ]. The dominant mechanism is distinguished using temperature monitoring (>45 °C favors PTT), ROS detection assays (e.g., DCFH-DA staining), and pathway-specific markers (e.g., cleaved caspase-3 vs. HSP70) [ ]. In animal models, thermal imaging combined with ROS probes can indicate whether tumor ablation results primarily from photothermal or photodynamic effects [ ].

## Mechanisms of phototherapy in lymphoma
3.1

## Basic principles of PDT
PDT is a minimally invasive treatment that relies on the activation of a PS by light at a specific wavelength, typically in the visible or near-infrared range [ ]. Several examples of these distinguishing characteristics of PSs are summarized in . Upon activation, the PS transfers energy to molecular oxygen, generating ROS, such as singlet oxygen. These ROS trigger oxidative damage in tumor cells, leading to cell death through apoptosis or necrosis, depending on the level of ROS produced and the cellular context ( A). The localized effect of PDT ensures minimal damage to surrounding healthy tissues because the generation of ROS is confined to areas exposed to light [ ]. The primary mechanism involves direct tumor cell killing, damaging tumor vasculature, and stimulating immune system [ ]. To enhance the antitumor effect of PDT, researchers used the carrier system to increase the concentration of PSs in tumor tissues, thereby specifically killing tumor cells and damaging tumor blood vessels. 
PDT combined with nitric oxide (NO) therapy can be targeted to kill cancer cells ( B) [ ]. On the one hand, NO can damage the DNA of cancer cells, leading to cell death; on the other hand, ROS further react with NO to produce active nitrogen and activate matrix metalloproteinases, thus destroying the tumor extracellular matrix and increasing the phototoxicity of cancer cells. In addition, PDT combined with novel nanoparticles disrupts intracellular REDOX balance by efficiently producing ROS ( C), induces mitochondrial and nuclear DNA damage, and significantly kills tumor cells ( D). This PDT nanodelivery system can lead to targeted accumulation of nanoparticles at the tumor site, activate the cGAS-STING pathway under laser irradiation, and finally trigger antitumor immune response ( E). This phenomenon has been confirmed in mouse tumor models pretreated with Ac4ManNAz ( F) [ ]. PDT offers the advantage of a dual-action approach: direct cytotoxicity and immune activation, making it a promising option for lymphoma treatment [ ].

3.2

## PDT-induced ICD
One of the most significant advantages of PDT in cancer treatment is its ability to induce ICD, which transforms dying tumor cells into sources of tumor antigens that stimulate the immune system [ , ]. ROS generated during PDT causes oxidative stress, leading to the release of DAMPs from dying tumor cells [ ]. These DAMPs, including calreticulin, ATP, and high-mobility group box 1, signal immune cells to recognize and attack the tumor [ ]. ICD can promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation, increase tumor T cell infiltration, and form an immune responsive TME, thus significantly improving the response rate of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies [ ]. The chemotherapy drug paclitaxel (PTX) can induce ICD. Researchers designed a nano-carrier to load PTX and anti-PD-1 antibodies, deliver them to the TME for release, and promote the infiltration of CD8+ T cells; anti-PD-1 antibodies block the pd-1/PD-L1 axis, thereby inhibiting immune escape and promoting ICD ( G). This approach has shown a strong antitumor immune effect in mouse melanoma models ( H) [ ].
In lymphoma, PDT-induced ICD can enhance antitumor immunity by stimulating DC maturation and promoting the presentation of tumor antigens to cytotoxic T cells [ ]. This immune activation may complement existing immunotherapies, such as CAR-T cell therapy or ICIs, by boosting the immune system's ability to recognize and destroy residual lymphoma cells[ ]. Furthermore, the combination of PDT with immunotherapy holds the potential to overcome immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME, making it a powerful approach for refractory or relapsed lymphoma cases [ ]. Given its dual role in inducing direct tumor cell death and enhancing immune system activation, PDT is a promising therapeutic strategy for lymphoma, particularly when combined with other modalities [ ].

3.3

## PTT for localized hyperthermia and immune enhancement in lymphoma
Beyond its direct cytotoxic effects, phototherapy synergizes with chemotherapy and targeted therapies at multiple mechanistic levels. For instance, PDT-induced oxidative stress can sensitize tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents by downregulating anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL-2 and MCL1 [ ]. In triple-negative breast cancer, NADPH-selective depletion nanomedicine not only enhances radiotherapy efficacy by disrupting redox homeostasis but also reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby augmenting the response to anti-PD-L1 therapy through immunometabolic regulation [ ]. In B-cell lymphomas, phototherapy may also potentiate the effects of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies by upregulating CD20 expression or modulating Fc receptor pathways [ ]. These combinatorial effects suggest a rational basis for integrating phototherapy into multi-modality treatment regimens to enhance therapeutic efficacy and overcome resistance in refractory lymphoma.
One of the unique advantages of PTT is its ability to induce localized hyperthermia, which not only directly destroys tumor cells but also potentially modulates the TME and enhances antitumor immunity [ ]. The PS in PTT is usually administered intravenously to the patient and then distributed throughout the body, eventually accumulating in tumor tissue. The classification of photothermal agents is summarized in . The specific wavelength of light is specifically focused on the tumor tissue, resulting in selective PS excitation and tumor ablation ( A). Various nanoparticle structures, including liposomes, micelles, carbon nanomaterials, and gold nanoparticles, are being explored as carriers of PTT to target tumor tissue precisely. Local hyperthermia has been achieved by adjusting the size and surface chemistry of nanoparticle (or macromolecule) drugs and by using high-affinity ligands to improve the effectiveness of targeting tumors ( B) [ ]. The heat generated during PTT can cause tumor cell stress and damage, leading to the release of TAAs and DAMPs [ ]. These molecular signals can then recruit and activate immune cells, including DCs, which play a crucial role in presenting tumor antigens to cytotoxic T cells [ ]. 
This immunogenic effect of PTT complements immunotherapies, such as ICIs or CAR-T cell therapies, by amplifying the immune response against lymphoma [ ]. Moreover, the ability of PTT to induce local tumor destruction while sparing the immune system offers a valuable opportunity for combining it with existing immunotherapies [ ]. Moreover, PTT's ability to induce local tumor destruction while sparing the immune system offers a valuable opportunity for combining it with existing immunotherapies [ ]. Researchers have designed IMQ@IONs/ICG as a drug delivery vector for imiquimox (IMQ) and ICG to guide interventional PTT for in situ pancreatic tumors via ion-assisted MRI. PTT can cause dying tumor cells to release DAMPs, such as calreticulin, to induce an adaptive antitumor immune response ( C). IMQ can promote the expression of CD80 and CD86 on DC surface and induce DC maturation ( D), thus enhancing ICD-initiated antitumor immunity. IMQ@IONs/ICG can also induce a systemic immune response in vivo, producing effective anti-metastatic effects ( E and F) [ ].
The synergistic interaction between localized hyperthermia and immune activation holds promise for overcoming immune escape mechanisms that contribute to treatment resistance in lymphoma, thereby offering a potential therapeutic strategy for refractory or relapsed cases [ ]. Comparative Analysis of PDT and PTT in Lymphoma, PDT induces tumor death through ROS-mediated oxidative damage, whereas PTT relies on localized thermal ablation [ ]. PDT is more effective in oxygen-rich tumors but limited by light and oxygen availability [ ]. In contrast, PTT can overcome hypoxia and offers more robust thermal killing, but carries risk of nonspecific heating. PDT more strongly promotes ICD and antigen presentation, making it better suited for immune synergy. Therefore, therapy choice depends on tumor location, oxygenation, and desired immune modulation.

3.4

## Phototherapy-induced cell death pathways and immune activation in lymphoma
Phototherapy exerts its anti-tumor effect by inducing various forms of cell death, including apoptosis, necrosis, necroptosis, and autophagy, each involving distinct subcellular organelles and signaling pathways ( ) [ , , ]. Upon light activation, photosensitizers or photothermal agents generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) or local hyperthermia that damage key cellular structures such as the mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), plasma membrane, and DNA. Mitochondrial dysfunction triggers intrinsic apoptosis via cytochrome c release, while membrane damage may lead to necrosis. ROS-mediated ER stress also initiates apoptotic signaling cascades through the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 axis [ ].
Notably, necroptosis—a regulated form of necrosis involving the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL necrosome complex—can be activated when apoptosis is inhibited. Phototherapy has also been shown to modulate autophagic pathways, particularly via photothermal-induced autophagosome formation. These diverse forms of cell death not only eliminate tumor cells directly but also reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) by releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), and inflammatory cytokines [ ].
Importantly, phototherapy-induced DNA damage and mitochondrial stress can activate the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway. Activation of the SGAS-STING axis promotes type I interferon production and enhances antigen presentation, thereby bridging innate and adaptive immunity. This immune-stimulatory effect synergizes with phototherapy to convert immunologically "cold" lymphomas into "hot" tumors, sensitizing them to checkpoint inhibitors and CAR-T cell therapies. Therefore, the integration of cell death mechanisms and immune activation pathways, especially SGAS-STING, forms the foundation of phototherapy's immunomodulatory potential in lymphoma treatment [ ].

## Synergistic effects of phototherapy and immunotherapy in lymphoma
4.1

## Combination of PDT/ICD and immunotherapy in lymphoma
PDT induces a unique form of cell death known as ICD, which is characterized by the release of DAMPs and TAAs [ ]. These signals enhance the recruitment and activation of APCs such as DCs, which consequently prime T cells to recognize and attack tumor cells [ ]. PDT brings hope to patients with immunosuppression [ ]. Spontaneous ferroptosis of neutrophils in the TME results in the release of oxygen-containing lipids that restrict T cell activity. To overcome this phenomenon, an immunomodulatory nanomedical drug was designed, and the ICD inducer Icy7 and ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1 (LLI) were co-encapsulated by liposomes, effectively triggering ICD in vitro ( A). When injected into mice, neutrophil ferroptosis is inhibited in mouse cancer tissue ( B). Meanwhile, in vivo fluorescence imaging showed that the fluorescence intensity of LLI gradually increases in the tumor, reaches the peak 24 h after tail vein injection, and remains strong 48 h later. Moreover, the in vitro fluorescence intensity of the tumor is much higher than those of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, indicating that LLI-specific targeting of tumor tissues does not cause obvious toxic effects on internal organs ( C). Co-culture with CD8+ T cells significantly enhances the killing activity of CD8+ T cells ( D), counteracting the immunosuppressive effect on the TME. In addition, PDT combined with anti-PD-1 antibody exerts a more significant inhibitory effect on neutrophil ferroptosis, further increasing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells ( E), reversing the immunosuppressive TME, and thus promoting tumor immunotherapy ( F) [ ]. This process offers a powerful way to boost the effectiveness of immunotherapies, particularly ICIs such as PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors [ ]. 
In lymphoma, the combination of PDT with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has the potential to overcome immune suppression within the TME [ ]. By increasing the release of TAAs and enhancing immune cell infiltration, PDT may improve the overall immune response against lymphoma cells [ ]. Furthermore, PDT-induced ICD can enhance the expression of immune checkpoint molecules on T cells, making them more susceptible to inhibition by PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [ ]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the combination of PDT and ICIs can improve outcomes in various cancer models, and this approach is now being explored for hematologic malignancies such as lymphoma [ ]. IR700DX-6T-PDT is a mitochondria-targeted PDT designed for microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors that do not respond to ICIs. Results showed that IR700DX-6T-PDT combined with anti-PD-1 inhibits tumor growth more significantly than anti-PD-1 alone ( G). This treatment does not reduce the weight of mice and has almost no side effects ( H). Compared with the control treatment, the combination treatment promotes DC maturation and cytotoxic T cell proliferation ( I), enhancing the sensitivity of MSS to PD-1 therapy [ ]. This phenomenon creates a synergistic effect that not only targets the tumor directly through PDT-induced cell death but also modulates the immune system to sustain a prolonged antitumor response [ ]. The ability of PDT to activate the immune system in this context may be valuable for patients with relapsed or refractory lymphoma who have limited treatment options [ ].

4.2

## Phototherapy and CAR-T cell therapy in lymphoma
CAR-T cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment of lymphoma, especially for patients with refractory or relapsed disease [ ]. Despite its success, several challenges remain, including limited CAR-T cell infiltration into the tumor, poor persistence, and resistance due to an immunosuppressive TME [ ]. Phototherapy, particularly PDT and PTT, offers a potential strategy to modulate the TME and enhance CAR-T cell activity [ ].
Phototherapy can synergize with CAR-T cell therapy by disrupting the immunosuppressive barriers within the TME [ ]. PTT generates localized heat that can enhance tumor permeability and increase immune cell infiltration [ ]. To break the immune barrier of tumors, a nano-PS (indocyanine green nanoparticles, INPs) engineered CAR-T biohybrid (CT-INP) was constructed, which not only retains the original activity and function of CAR-T cells ( A) but also reshapes the TME. In terms of biosafety, CT-INPs are well tolerated in mice and do not cause significant changes in the body weight of mice ( B). The concentrations of liver and kidney function indices do not significantly differ between the CT-INP and PBS control groups; moreover, these biohybrid has good biocompatibility in vivo ( C). Under laser irradiation, CT-INPs promote the invasion of endogenous monocytes/NK ( D) and the expression of antitumor cytokines ( E), ultimately enhancing CAR-T cell immunotherapy [ ]. By improving the accessibility of the tumor to CAR-T cells, PTT can potentially boost their antitumor efficacy. However, whether the immune activation effect of this nano-adjuvant can persist in vivo warrants further exploration. PVP-MPDA@R837 was constructed by loading the nano-adjuvant imiquimod (R837), a TLR 7 agonist, into mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) nanocarriers with high efficiency and modifying its surface with polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP). When it was injected into mice, PP-MPDA NPs could be effectively accumulated in the drainage lymph nodes (LNs) ( F). Flow cytometry was used to detect effective DC activation and CD8+ T cell response in the drainage proximal LNs and spleen of tumor-bearing mice ( G). These results indicate that the nano-adjuvants not only have certain transport and retention capabilities in LNs but also demonstrate strong anti-solid tumor efficacy by combining the photothermal conversion characteristics of MPDA with lymphatic focused immune activation [ ]. In addition, PDT-induced ICD can release DAMPs and cytokines that promote the recruitment of CAR-T cells and increase their persistence within the tumor site ( H). A nano-catalyst (ACSP@TCM) was constructed by coating core/shell Au/Cu2-xSe and 3-bromopyruvate with CAR-T cell membrane, which not only induces ICD but also inhibits lactic acid effluence and regulates TME acidity, making it more favourable for the infiltration and differentiation of CAR-T cells. Treatment with ACSP@TCM + NIR decreases the number of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and increases the number of CAR-T cells, consequently enhancing the number of CD4+ and CD8+ cells and promoting the antitumor effect ( I and J). In addition, the activated CAR-T cells after treatment become memory T cells, which possess durable antitumor potential ( K) [ ]. 
Moreover, phototherapy can help reshape the immune landscape of the tumor by reducing the abundance of immunosuppressive cells, such as Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, which often hinder CAR-T cell function [ ]. By decreasing the suppressive influence of these cells, phototherapy creates a favourable environment for CAR-T cells to exert their cytotoxic effects, ultimately improving the overall therapeutic outcome [ ]. Notably, several preclinical studies suggest that phototherapy, particularly in combination with CAR-T or checkpoint blockade, can induce long-term immune memory [ ]. Mice treated with combined PDT and CAR-T exhibited resistance to tumor rechallenge, accompanied by increased memory T cell populations [ ]. This result implies the potential of phototherapy to induce acute tumor control and promote durable remission via immune memory establishment.

## Nanomedicine in phototherapy for lymphoma
5.1

## Nanocarriers in phototherapy
Nanocarriers have become an integral part of phototherapy owing to their capacity to encapsulate and deliver PSs effectively [ ]. Traditional PSs often suffer from issues such as poor solubility, photodegradation, and nonspecific distribution, which can limit their therapeutic potential [ ]. Nanocarriers, such as liposomes, polymer nanoparticles, and nanocapsules, can effectively load and protect PSs, extending their circulation time in the body and preventing accumulation in non-target tissues while enabling efficient drug release at tumor sites [ ]. Liposomes and polymer-based nanoparticles can shield PSs from early degradation, ensuring that therapeutic agents reach the tumor site in optimal condition [ ]. This nanotechnology-based strategy significantly enhances the therapeutic efficacy of photodynamic and photothermal therapies [ ].
Moreover, the surface of nanocarriers can be modified with targeting ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, or small molecules, to achieve enhanced specificity for lymphoma cells [ ]. Nanoparticles modified with CD20 or CD19 antibodies can precisely recognize and target lymphoma cells, minimizing phototoxicity to non-tumor tissues and enhancing the precision and safety of the treatment [ ]. This targeted delivery reduces the risk of damage to healthy tissues and enhances therapeutic efficacy [ ]. These advances have allowed nanocarrier-based phototherapy to overcome many of the limitations associated with traditional approaches, paving the way for more effective and safer treatments for patients with lymphoma [ ].
PSs such as porphyrins (e.g., Photofrin), chlorins, and ICG vary in their ROS yield, tissue penetration, and clinical track record. Photofrin, though FDA-approved, suffers from prolonged skin photosensitivity. ICG provides deeper NIR penetration but is unstable in circulation without encapsulation. Gold nanorods offer excellent photothermal efficiency but lack biodegradability. Carbon-based materials present functional versatility but raise concerns over long-term accumulation. The characteristics of the different nanocarriers are summarized in . Therefore, translational efforts must balance efficacy, safety, and manufacturability to ensure clinical viability. 

5.2

## Nanotechnology in PDT
Nanotechnology has significantly enhanced the efficacy of PDT by improving the delivery and performance of PSs [ ]. PSs loaded into nanocarriers benefit from improved pharmacokinetics and biodistribution, ensuring greater accumulation in tumor tissues [ ]. This is particularly advantageous in lymphoma, where the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect can be exploited to localize PSs to the TME [ ]. Once activated by light of an appropriate wavelength, these PSs generate ROS that induce tumor cell death while sparing surrounding healthy tissue [ ]. The development of a pH-sensitive hydrazone bond to conjugate carrying pyrophospho-A, which is dormant when it enters the body, enables specific activation and release in the acidic TME. The nanomaterials enable the accumulation of PSs in tumor tissues, achieving targeted antitumor effects through light activation while reducing the concentration of PSs in the liver and spleen to avoid damage to normal tissues. This treatment shows high levels of selective tumor accumulation and good antitumor PDT effects in a mouse sarcoma S180 tumor model ( A) [ ]. 
Nanotechnology has enhanced the potential of PDT for treating lymphoma; it not only ensures efficient PS delivery but also enables controlled release, enhancing the selectivity and therapeutic index of the treatment [ ]. A nanodrug can deliver PSs to tumor blood vessels in hyperoxic areas to produce ROS and hypoxic-activated prodrugs (HAPs) to the tumor for internal activation to achieve the controlled release of PSs and HAPs. The nanoparticles can effectively produce ROS under laser irradiation, lyse ROS-responsive thioketone crosslinker, release small polyaminamine dendrimer, bind HAP, promote tumor penetration into the anoxic zone, and finally enhance the antitumor effect of PDT combined with anoxic activation ( B) [ ]. Furthermore, some nanomaterials possess intrinsic properties that can amplify ROS generation, further increasing the efficacy of PDT [ ]. The Cas9 ribonnucleeprotein in a multifunctional DNA/upconversion nanoparticle complex can precisely cleave the gene associated with factor 2 (Nrf2) of antioxidant regulatory nuclear factor e2, increasing cancer cell sensitivity to ROS and enhancing synergistic PDT effect ( C) [ ]. These advancements underscore the potential of nanotechnology to revolutionize PDT applications in lymphoma treatment.

5.3

## Nanotechnology in PTT
PTT relies on the conversion of light energy into heat to induce localized tumor ablation [ ]. Nanotechnology has promoted the development of advanced photothermal agents, such as gold nanoparticles, carbon-based nanomaterials, and metal oxide nanoparticles, all of which have a tetrapyridine structure. This large-ring structure is related to effective light absorption and singlet oxygen generation, and has a high photothermal conversion efficiency ( A) [ ]. Improving the selectivity and efficacy of photothermal drug delivery to tumor tissues is a widespread concern. Thus, passive aiming, active aiming, and the use of physical force and other methods gradually attracted attention. With the rapid growth of cancer cells and their high consumption of local nutrients, the neovascularization in tumors is not perfect. The leakage pores in tumors can enhance the penetration of circulating nanoparticles into the TME. This phenomenon does not occur in normal tissues with complete vascular barrier protection. In addition, the lymphatic drainage system of the tumor tissue is damaged, and nanoparticles selectively remain in the tumor tissue. At present, the permeability and retention of nanoparticle (or macromolecule) drugs can be enhanced by adjusting their inherent properties, such as size, surface chemistry, shape, charge, hydrophilicity, and blood circulation time, thereby achieving passive tumor targeting [ ]. Although this method has been confirmed in preclinical tumor models, its application in clinical trials still has a long way to go. Conversely, active targeting employs high-affinity ligands such as peptides [ ], monoclonal antibodies [ ], folic acid [ ], and vitamins [ ], which bind to specific surface molecules mainly expressed by cancer cells or tumor epithelial cells. Photoimmunotherapy, a type of PTT with monoclonal antibodies as PS carriers, has entered Phase III trials for patients with recurrent head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (NCT037695060) [ ]. Physical forces (such as magnetic or electric fields) can also be used to enhance the targeting of PTT drugs to tumors. Magnetic nanoparticles such as superparamagnetic Fe3O4) carry PSs in the blood and can be redirected to tumor tissues under the action of an external magnetic field and accumulate there, thereby enhancing the selectivity and efficiency of PTT [ ]. This magnetic field-oriented PTT has been successfully applied to preclinical models. 
Therefore, these materials can be functionally designed to achieve specific targeting of tumors [ ]. A novel photosensitizer (1-oxo-1H-benzoo[de]anthracene-2,3-dicarbonitrile-triphenylamine (OBADC-TPA)) was embedded in an amphiphilic polymer (DSPE PEG2K-NHS) and further functionalized with a sialic acid (SA) derivative (9-NH2-SA) for further functionalization, OBADC-TPA-based multifunctional nano particles (NPs-SA) were synthesized. This nanoparticle had significant photothermal conversion efficiency under the irradiation of NIR-II fluorescence, generated a large amount of ROS, and effectively inhibited tumor growth. Meanwhile, SA improves lymphoma targeting providing a promising phototherapeutic regimen for targeted lymphoma ( B) [ ]. This ensures that the heat generated is focused on the lymphoma cells, thereby minimizing damage to healthy tissue [ ].
In addition to their direct therapeutic effects, nanomaterials used in PTT have shown potential in modulating the TME to enhance antitumor immune responses [ ]. This synergistic interaction with immunotherapy has gained increasing attention in recent years [ ]. A STING agonist-based photoimmunothernostic nanomedicine (SAPTN) targeting tumor extracellular matrix (ECM) has been proposed to induce neutrophils to secrete inflammatory factors in the TME by second-window near-infrared (NIL-II) laser stimulation. The ECM on the surface of nanomedical drugs makes inflammatory factors slowly quenchable, thus enhancing the accumulation of SAPTNs in the TME, continuously releasing STING agonist diABZIs, and initiating strong and long-term anticancer immunity ( C) [ ]. Dynamic adaptive nanoparticles can complete the conversion of PTT to PDT consistent with the change in the TME. Adaptive nanoparticles (TPASIC-PFH@PLGA NPs) are composed of AIEgen TPASIC with ROS generation and photothermal capabilities, perfluorohexane (PFH) as a TME enhancer and photoactivity regulator, and biocompatible polymer poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) as a biocompatible coating substrate. When TPASIC is in the dispersed state, the time-thermal effect is dominant, and the liquid PFH is added to promote the photothermal conversion. Under laser irradiation, the increase in temperature within the tumor induces liquid–gas phase transition and oxygen release in PFH, reversing the hypoxic state of the TME. After the evaporation of PFH, the nanoparticles collapse to form tight TPASIC aggregates, promoting ROS generation efficiency, and PDT is dominant at this time, maximizing the phototherapy performance. Dynamic adaptive nanoparticles enhance the antitumor effect of light therapeutic agents ( D) [ ]. Nanotechnology, by integrating PTT with other therapeutic modalities such as PDT or immunotherapy, holds promise for achieving superior therapeutic outcomes in patients with lymphoma [ ].

5.4

## Development direction of nanotechnology for lymphoma phototherapy
A key limitation of phototherapy is limited light penetration, especially in deep-seated or disseminated lymphoma [ ]. CNS lymphoma presents additional barriers due to the blood–brain barrier and limited optical accessibility [ ]. Approaches such as fiber-optic light delivery, NIR-II window activation, and the use of upconversion nanoparticles are under development to address these issues. Intrathecal delivery of nanophotosensitizers combined with minimally invasive light probes may expand phototherapy applicability in CNS-involved lymphoma in the future [ ]. In addition, gas-assisted phototherapy, such as systems employing oxygen-generating or nitric oxide releasing nanoparticles, has shown promise in overcoming tumor hypoxia and enhancing the therapeutic depth of PDT [ ]. A recent study has reported a UCST polymeric H2S-donor-fabricated nanomedicine that enables photothermal-activated gas therapy to manage atherosclerosis through a “turning waste into treasure” strategy, where photothermal energy is converted into therapeutic gas release and localized immunomodulation [ ]. These gas-generating nanoplatforms can amplify ROS production or improve vascular perfusion under light activation, thereby compensating for inadequate light and oxygen penetration in solid or hematologic tumors. Such strategies represent a novel direction to extend phototherapy efficacy beyond superficial lesions, including applications in deep lymphatic tissues or CNS-involved lymphoma.
The future of nanotechnology in phototherapy for lymphoma lies in the development of innovative and multifunctional nanoplatforms [ ]. Stimuli-responsive systems, which release therapeutic agents in response to specific environmental cues, such as pH or temperature, can potentially improve treatment specificity and efficacy [ ]. SR780, a new PS based on croconaine dye, has the characteristics of pH-responsive NIR-II imaging. It is inactivated when combined with Fe3+(SR780@Fe) and is released and activated only under weakly acidic conditions, realizing the precise treatment of cancer cells. In situ pH activation treatment of nanometer platform SR780@Fe-PAE-GP in vivo and in vitro has powerful antitumor action ( A) [ ]. Moreover, multifunctional nanosystems can deliver therapeutic and diagnostic drugs to the tumor site and activate their respective functions through TME recognition. A pH and redox dual-activated self-assembled nanotherapeutic drug (DA-SNs) can be activated in a TME with low pH and high glutathione (GSH) levels, demonstrating superior photodynamic therapeutic efficiency. At the same time, by visualizing the different pH and redox sensitive T1MR double contrast in tumor targeting diagnosis and fluorescence imaging ( B) [ ]. Combining diagnostic and therapeutic functions into a single nanoplatform, termed theranostics, could enable the real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy, providing a personalized approach to lymphoma therapy [ ]. 
Another promising direction involves integrating nanotechnology with other treatment modalities, such as immunotherapy and chemotherapy, to create synergistic effects [ ]. Nanocarriers can be designed to co-deliver PSs and ICIs, amplifying the therapeutic benefits of both treatments [ ]. IR780-M-APP NPs were assembled from anti-PD-L1 peptide (APP) and PS IR780 by molecular engineering. The nanoparticles exert PDT effects and trigger ICD, and the APP fragments block the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway to promote the immune therapy, thereby effectively killing tumor cells ( C) [ ]. The combination of nanotechnology and chemotherapeutic drugs significantly improves the antitumor effect. For instance, a bacterial nanodrug prepared from Porphyromonas gingivalis (nmPg) has shown potent PDT and immune-activating properties against oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). Meanwhile, nmPg/DOX, prepared by encapsulating doxorubicin into nmPg, can synergistically inhibit OSCC growth and metastasis in vitro and in vivo via photodynamic immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy ( D) [ ].
Nanocarriers are functionalized to enhance specificity, biodistribution, and therapeutic efficacy in lymphoma-targeted phototherapy [ , ]. Active targeting approaches utilize ligands such as antibodies (e.g., anti-CD19, CD20), peptides (e.g., RGD), and aptamers to recognize surface markers uniquely expressed on malignant B cells [ ]. Surface modification with PEG improves circulation half-life and minimizes clearance by the reticuloendothelial system [ ]. Additionally, pH-sensitive and redox-responsive linkers can facilitate controlled drug release in the acidic and oxidative TME [ ]. A recent study has developed a carbon-based nanoenzyme with dual-catalytic activity that can sensitize the TME by elevating oxidative stress and depleting intracellular GSH, thereby amplifying the ROS generation during PDT [ ]. This dual-catalytic strategy not only improves therapeutic selectivity but also overcomes redox-associated treatment resistance. Engineering dual-targeted nanoparticles capable of simultaneously engaging immune checkpoint pathways and B-cell antigens also show promise in combined photoimmunotherapy [ ]. As research progresses, these strategies are expected to bridge the gap between preclinical findings and clinical implementation, establishing nanotechnology as a cornerstone of personalized medicine in lymphoma [ ].

## Preclinical and clinical research progress of phototherapy in lymphoma
6.1

## Preclinical studies of phototherapy in lymphoma
Preclinical research on phototherapy, including PDT and PTT, has demonstrated promising results in animal models of lymphomas [ ]. In these studies, phototherapy has shown significant tumor growth inhibition and enhanced immune responses [ ]. Vascular targeted PDT has a good therapeutic effect on malignant lymphoma. Nanoparticles with catalase and HMME-nanoparticles, EGFP-EGF1-modified PEG-PLGA (CENPs), are constructed with tissue factor (TF) as the tumor vascular target. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells can internalize the modified EGFP-EGF1 nanoparticles well ( A). This result was confirmed in xenograft mouse models. The increase in local nanoparticles was found to be most pronounced in the CENPs + PDT group ( B). In the CENPs + PDT group, nanoparticles were enriched in blood vessels, and TF expression in vascular endothelium was significantly increased ( C). In addition, CENPs entering cells can decompose endogenous ROS to produce O2 through catalase ( D), reversing the hypoxic microenvironment, which can convert oxygen molecules into more ROS, thus damaging blood vessels and killing tumor cells. Significant tumor suppression was observed in the ENPs/CENPs plus PDT group after light therapy in a mouse model ( E) [ ],but no obvious toxic effect was produced on the major organs ( F). 
PTT has proven effective in inducing hyperthermia to selectively kill lymphoma cells while promoting an immunostimulatory TME [ ]. High-density lipoprotein-like magnetic nanostructures (HDL-MNSs) can bind to scavenger receptor type B1 (SR-B1) to cut off SR-B1 receptor-positive lymphoma cells and maintain pro-proliferation and pro-survival signaling pathways dependent on cholesterol. The researchers incubated cells with different concentrations of HDL-MNSs and confirmed that HDL-MNSs show dose-dependent cytotoxicity only to SR-B1-positive cell lines ( A). When treating lymphoma cells with HDL-MNSs, thermal activation made the therapeutic effect more pronounced ( B). Further experiments showed that thermal activation of MNS can induce the expression of heat shock proteins in immune cells. Treatment of SU-DHL4 cells with conditioned media of HDL-MNS and RF promotes the migration and differentiation of dendritic cells ( C). Further studies found that HDL-MNS + RF-conditioned medium induces the expression of CD80 and CD86, two markers of mature DCs, and activation of T cells ( D, E), leading to innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses [ ]. These results indicate that HDL-MNS-induced heat activation-mediated antitumor immune response is promising for lymphoma therapy. 

6.2

## Early-stage clinical investigations
Although phototherapy remains primarily preclinical in the context of lymphoma treatment, early research on its combination with other therapeutic modalities demonstrates promising potential. Several preclinical investigations have explored how phototherapy synergizes with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and nanotechnology in hematolymphoid tumors.
For instance, Lin et al. employed the AuroLase system in Balb/c mice bearing A20 lymphoma cells and demonstrated that PTT combined with anti-PD-1 antibody and a CD20xCD3 bispecific antibody significantly enhances tumor regression and prolongs survival compared with monotherapy [ ]. Similarly, Shi et al. developed multifunctional FA-GdGERTs@Ibrutinib nanoparticles for chemo-PTT and CAR-T combinatorial therapy in NOD/SCID mice and found that these nanoparticles exhibit robust antitumor effects without significant side effects [ ].
Tang et al. constructed LY/ICG@HES-PCL nanoparticles and combined them with CD19-CAR-T cell therapy and PTT in NSG mice. This approach enhances tumor inhibition and reduces relapse more effectively than CAR-T therapy alon [ ]. Meanwhile, Wang et al. reported that using Ag2S nanoparticles for PDT inhibits the proliferation and increases the apoptosis and necrosis of Raji lymphoma cells possibly by disrupting glycolytic metabolism and elevating ROS levels [ ].
Additionally, Abd El-Kaream et al. introduced sono-photodynamic therapy by using CPL-micelle nanoparticles in in vitro U-937 cells and in vivo DMBA-induced lymphoma mice [ ]. Molecular, biochemical, and histological evaluations revealed that the treatment achieves substantial anti-lymphoma effects via a combination of laser and ultrasound irradiation.
However, a disconnect exists between preclinical and clinical PTT research. In specific, preclinical efforts emphasized on photothermal agents, whereas clinical trials focused on device-based ablation systems, with limited integration. A phase I trial using silica–gold nanoshells demonstrated the feasibility of nanomaterial-based focal PTT. Sixteen patients received a single nanoshell infusion followed by MRI- and ultrasound-guided laser ablation (6.5 W, 808 nm) via up to 21 optical fibers. Tumor ablation was achieved in 94 % of cases without serious complications [ ]. This result highlights the clinical potential of PTT for targeted tumor therapy. With continued advances in photothermal materials and their capacity to enhance immune responses via localized heating, PTT may emerge as a promising treatment modality for lymphoma.
Collectively, these studies highlight multiple mechanisms through which phototherapy may be integrated into combination treatment strategies—such as vascular disruption, immune modulation, metabolic interference, and improved drug delivery—to enhance the efficacy of lymphoma therapies and potentially overcome therapeutic resistance (see ).

## Conclusion and prospect
Phototherapy has emerged as a promising and innovative modality for the treatment of lymphoma, offering unique advantages over conventional therapeutic approaches. Unlike conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which exert systemic toxicity and non-specific cell killing, phototherapy offers a spatiotemporally controlled approach that can selectively ablate lymphoma cells while preserving healthy tissues. This precision is further enhanced by nanomedicine, which allows for the tumor-specific delivery of PSs or photothermal agents through EPR effects and ligand-mediated targeting (e.g., CD19/CD20 antibodies). Additionally, PDT and PTT can reshape the immunosuppressive TME and induce ICD, a property rarely achieved by standard cytotoxic agents. These advantages make phototherapy-nanomedicine platforms uniquely positioned to improve efficacy and reduce toxicity in treating heterogeneous and refractory lymphomas. By leveraging the mechanisms of PDT and PTT, phototherapy enables precise, light-triggered ablation of malignant cells, while largely sparing surrounding healthy tissues. Importantly, beyond its direct cytotoxicity, phototherapy can elicit ICD, promoting the release of tumor-associated antigens and DAMPs. These processes lead to the activation of innate and adaptive immune responses and reprogramming of the TME, laying the groundwork for synergistic interactions when combined with immunotherapeutic agents, molecularly targeted drugs, or standard chemotherapy.
Recent preclinical studies have highlighted the potential of phototherapy to enhance antitumor efficacy when integrated with other therapeutic strategies. This combinatorial approach may help overcome key limitations of current lymphoma treatments, including suboptimal response rates and high relapse risks. Notably, phototherapy exhibits a dual capacity: it can directly ablate tumor cells and simultaneously stimulate antitumor immunity. This dual functionality is particularly advantageous in lymphoma, where the immune microenvironment critically influences disease progression and treatment outcomes. Despite these advantages, the clinical application of phototherapy in lymphoma remains constrained by the limited tissue penetration of light, especially in cases involving deep-seated lesions or widespread nodal dissemination. Most conventional PSs are activated within the visible or NIR-I (650–900 nm) spectrum, where light penetration is typically restricted to a few millimeters in biological tissues. This shallow penetration poses substantial challenges for targeting malignant B cells in deep lymph nodes, bone marrow, or central nervous system compartments.
Several strategies have emerged to address these limitations. These strategies include the development of upconversion nanoparticles and NIR-II (1000–1700 nm) responsive agents, which enable deeper light penetration, as well as the use of fiber-optic delivery systems for localized illumination. Within B-cell-targeted therapies, such as anti-CD19 or anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, phototherapy can serve as a complementary tool, selectively ablating antigen-expressing cells in accessible areas while enhancing immune priming. However, fully realizing this synergy will require the integration of deeper-penetrating photonic platforms with systemic immunomodulatory strategies. Looking ahead, several key areas are expected to drive the advancement of phototherapy in lymphoma. First, the rational design of next-generation PSs with superior tumor selectivity, photostability, and deeper tissue responsiveness will be critical to improving therapeutic precision and minimizing off-target effects. Concurrently, breakthroughs in nanotechnology are creating opportunities to refine drug delivery through prolonged systemic circulation, controlled release, and the integration of diagnostic and therapeutic functions into multifunctional platforms.
The development of nanocarriers capable of co-delivering PSs, immune adjuvants, and chemotherapeutic agents presents a promising path toward synergistic, multimodal treatment regimens. At the same time, innovations in light delivery, such as implantable fiber optics, NIR-II illumination, and upconversion nanomaterials, are actively being explored to overcome depth-related limitations. Crucially, the successful clinical translation of phototherapy will depend on well-structured, multicenter trials that rigorously evaluate safety, efficacy, and durability in patients with lymphoma. These trials should prioritize assessments of long-term clinical outcomes, immune memory generation, and the potential for integration into personalized therapeutic protocols.
As technological innovations and clinical research progress in tandem, phototherapy is poised to play an increasingly prominent role in lymphoma management. With its capacity to deliver minimally invasive, immune-responsive, and personalized treatment, phototherapy holds the potential to reshape the therapeutic paradigm and significantly improve outcomes for patients with newly diagnosed and relapsed/refractory disease. Ultimately, it may become a cornerstone of precision oncology in hematologic malignancies.
