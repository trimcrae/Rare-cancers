## Abstract

## Objective
Theta-burst stimulation, including intermittent (iTBS) and continuous (cTBS) protocols, is a promising neuromodulatory intervention for autism spectrum disorder (ASD). This study aims to elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of iTBS and cTBS for ASD.

## Methods
Prenatal valproic acid-induced ASD rats were established and were randomized into VPA, VPA + iTBS, and VPA + cTBS groups, with a saline group as control. Core and comorbid ASD behaviors in rats were assessed. Multi-omics analyses included 16 S rRNA sequencing of cecal contents, non-targeted fecal metabolomics, and prefrontal cortex transcriptomics. Key pathways were validated via Western blot, ELISA, and immunofluorescence. Integrative analyses correlated multi-omics data with neuroendocrine findings.

## Results
Behavioral assessments demonstrated that both iTBS and cTBS significantly ameliorated social deficits and repetitive behaviors in VPA-exposed rats. However, protocol-specific effects on comorbidities were observed: cTBS, but not iTBS, effectively alleviated anxiety-like behaviors, whereas iTBS, but not cTBS, significantly improved learning and memory. The multi-omics approach demonstrated that iTBS primarily modulated inflammatory immune responses and energy metabolism, while cTBS predominantly regulated oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism. Both interventions suppressed the hyperactivated PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, an effect potentially linked to the normalization of hypothalamic-pituitary axis function. Furthermore, we identified a potential interplay between the GH/IGF-1 axis and the gut microbiome in ASD, which was differentially modulated by iTBS and cTBS.

## Conclusion
iTBS modulated inflammatory-immune responses and energy metabolism, while cTBS regulated oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism. The inhibition of the central GH/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway by both protocols may involve their specific regulation of distinct gut microbiota communities.

## Introduction
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurobehavioral developmental disorder characterized by core symptoms including social communication deficits and restricted, repetitive behaviors. In 2021, approximately 61.8 million individuals were affected by ASD globally, equating to 1 in 127 people, with a significantly higher prevalence observed in males compared to females [ ]. Current treatment approaches for ASD primarily rely on behavioral interventions, though these may impose significant time and resource burdens on families [ ]. While pharmacological treatments have demonstrated efficacy in managing co-occurring mental health symptoms associated with ASD, such as irritability, anxiety, and inattention or hyperactivity, their effectiveness is limited in addressing the core symptoms of ASD [ ].
Over the recent years, theta-burst stimulation (TBS), a specialized transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) protocol employing high-frequency burst patterns, has emerged as a promising neuromodulatory intervention for ASD due to its efficient cortical plasticity modulation [ ]. TBS comprises two main types: intermittent TBS (iTBS), which induces long-term potentiation-like effects, and continuous TBS (cTBS), which induces long-term depression-like effects [ ]. Evidence from clinical trials indicates iTBS effectively enhances social cognition and emotion recognition capacity in ASD individuals, with favorable tolerability and safety observed in children and adolescents [ – ]. In addition, iTBS targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex yields significant efficacy in improving neurocognitive performance and alleviating depressive symptoms in individuals with ASD [ , ]. In a double-blind randomized controlled trial, four weeks of personalized cTBS targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex enhanced social and communication skills in minimally verbal children with ASD [ ]. Beyond core symptoms, cTBS treatment yields significant improvements in common ASD comorbidities, including improvements in eating behavior problems, motor dysfunction, and sleep disturbances [ – ]. Collectively, accumulating evidence supports both iTBS and cTBS as effective neuromodulatory interventions for core and comorbid symptoms in ASD, representing valuable clinical neuromodulation approaches.
Current understanding of the mechanisms underlying rTMS effects in ASD remains limited. Recent studies indicate that low-frequency rTMS upregulates expression of GAD67, vGAT, and NR2B while downregulating PSD95 and vGlut1 in the hippocampus of VPA-induced rodent ASD models, thereby restoring synaptic plasticity and ameliorating the excitation/inhibition imbalance of ASD [ , ]. High-frequency rTMS improved VPA-induced ASD symptoms in rats, possibly due to its antioxidant properties and ability to increase BDNF, SYN levels, and the density of hippocampal dendritic spines [ ]. In summary, current findings provide preliminary mechanistic insights into how rTMS ameliorates core symptoms of ASD. Nevertheless, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying both iTBS and cTBS treatment effects in ASD remain unexplored to date.
In this study, we systematically evaluated the therapeutic potential and mechanistic differences between iTBS and cTBS for ASD symptoms. Using a prenatal valproic acid (VPA)-induced ASD rat model, we first assessed behavioral and histopathological improvements following iTBS/cTBS interventions. To elucidate the distinct biological mechanisms, we then conducted integrated multi-omics analyses encompassing gut microbiome profiling, metabolomics, and transcriptomics. Furthermore, we investigated the impacts of iTBS and cTBS on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and hypothalamic-pituitary axis in ASD rats. Finally, this study provides novel insight into the potential interplay between the GH/IGF-1 axis and the gut microbiome in ASD, highlighting the influence of iTBS and cTBS on this pathway. Our preclinical findings identify distinct neurobiological mechanisms underlying iTBS and cTBS effects in ASD, revealing protocol-specific molecular signatures. These results provide mechanistic insights that may inform the development of targeted rTMS strategies for ASD intervention.

## Methods

## Animal study and experimental procedure
The adult Wistar rats (purchased from Charles River Laboratories, Beijing, China) were housed in individually ventilated cages under specific pathogen-free conditions at the China Rehabilitation Science Institute. The temperature was kept at 22 ± 1 °C and the relative humidity at 50 ± 5%, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The animals had ad libitum access to water and food. This study was approved by the Animal Experiments and Experimental Animal Welfare Committee of Capital Medical University (AEEI-2024-073) and complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.
Adult males and females were mated overnight. If vaginal plugs or sperm in vaginal smears were found the next morning, it indicated that the female rat is pregnant. This time point was designated as embryonic day 0.25. After 12.5 days of confirmed mating, pregnant rats received a single dose of 600 mg/kg of sodium salt of valproic acid (VPA) (P4543, Sigma, Germany) or an equal amount of 0.9% saline [ ]. Pregnant rats were kept individually near the time of delivery until the pups were born. The rat offspring were weaned at postnatal week 3, and only male rats were retained for the experiment. Male rats exposed to VPA were randomly assigned to one of three groups: VPA, VPA + iTBS, and VPA + cTBS. The offspring of rats that received an intraperitoneal injection of saline only were designated as the control group (Fig. ).
 

## rTMS intervention
A figure-of-eight coil (25 mm in inner diameter, MagStim, UK) and a rTMS device (Rapid2, MagStim, UK) were utilized for brain stimulation. Rats were restrained in a plastic bag when treated with rTMS. The center of the coil was positioned between the rat’s eye and ear, in the middle of the head with the handle perpendicular to the body. The cTBS protocol consisted of 600 pulses delivered at 5 Hz over 48 s. For iTBS, a 5 Hz stimulation was applied in 10-second trains separated by 8-second intervals, totaling 600 pulses within a 3-minute session. The stimulation intensity was set at 100% of the average resting motor threshold, which was determined as 30% of the maximum stimulator output. For both the VPA group and the control group, the coil was positioned upside down at a sufficient distance to ensure that no magnetic pulses were delivered, while maintaining a sound level equivalent to that of the treatment group. To reduce stress and acclimate the rats, they underwent a one-week habituation period involving restraint and exposure to rTMS noise before treatment.

## Behavioral tests
All behavioral tests were carried out in a sound/light-attenuated testing room. In the three-chamber social test, open field test, Morris water maze, and novel object recognition test, rat behaviors were captured using video tracking systems and subsequently analyzed with the ANY-maze software (v7.3, Stoelting Co., USA). Each test was followed by cleaning with 75% alcohol to remove any olfactory cues that could influence animal behavior.

## Marble burying test
A transparent cage (40 × 25 × 20 cm), containing 5 cm of clean bedding and 20 glass marbles arranged in 5 rows, was used. Rats were placed in the cage for 30 min to freely explore, and the number of buried marbles was counted, representing repetitive and compulsive-like behavior [ ].

## Three-chamber social test
The three-chamber social test was used to assess the social interaction of rats. A rectangular acrylic case was divided into three interconnected chambers (30 cm × 45 cm × 35 cm) with dividing walls containing small doorways. Two cylindrical wire cages (12 cm in diameter) were used either as inanimate objects or to house a stranger rat. During the habituation phase, the test rat was placed in the central chamber with the two lateral chambers containing two empty cages, permitting free exploration of all three empty chambers for 10 min. During the sociability test, an age- and sex-matched unfamiliar rat was placed in one of the wire cages, while the other wire cage remained empty. The test rat was then allowed to freely explore all chambers for 10 min. During the social novelty test, a second unfamiliar rat was placed in the previously empty wire cage, and the test rat was again allowed to freely explore all chambers for 10 min. ## Open field test
The open field test was used to detect exploratory behavior, locomotor activity, and anxiety-related behaviors in rats. Each rat was placed individually in the center of a quadrangular apparatus (50 × 50 × 50 cm) and allowed to freely explore for 5 min. The following parameters were calculated: number of entries to the center, time in the center, and latency to first entry to the center [ ].

## Y maze
A Y-shaped maze with three identical arms (52 × 18 × 35 cm) at 120 degrees from each other was used to assess the short-term spatial working memory of rats. The rat was placed at the end of one starting arm and allowed to freely explore for 8 min. The researchers sat several feet away from the maze, avoiding making any noise, and recorded the alternating sequence of the rats in the maze. The spontaneous alternation rate was calculated as the number of successful alternations divided by (the total number of entries – 2) × 100% [ ].

## Morris water maze
The Morris water maze test was used to assess the cognitive spatial learning and memory functions of rats. The test was conducted in a circular pool (160 cm in diameter) filled with opaque water (19–22 °C) to a depth of 40 cm. A platform (10 cm in diameter) located in the center of the southeast quadrant was submerged 1 cm under the water surface. The Morris water tank was divided into four equal quadrants by two lines that intersect each other right in the middle of the tank. Patterns of different colors and shapes were placed on the walls of the water tank in the four quadrants as cues. During the five-day training period, rats were placed in the pool facing the wall from each of the four quadrants each day. If rats failed to find the platform within 60 s, they were placed on the hidden platform and allowed to observe the environment for 15 s. On the 6th day, the platform was removed from the tank, and rats were allowed to swim freely for 60 s [ ]. The distance traveled in the target quadrant, the number of entries to the target quadrant, and the average speed were analyzed.

## Novel object recognition test
The novel object recognition test was conducted in a box with dimensions of 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm, which was used to evaluate the cognitive function of rats. The test consisted of three phases: habituation (5 min in an empty arena), familiarization (10 min exploration of two identical objects), and test phase (10 min exploration of one familiar and one novel object after a 1-h inter-trial interval). The discrimination index was calculated using the formula [(Time exploring the novel object – Time exploring the familiar object)/Total time] [ ].

## Sample collection
Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation. Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta, with an approximate volume of 4 mL per rat. The entire brain was extracted from the severed head, and the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and pituitary gland were rapidly dissected on ice. The isolated tissues were then aliquoted and immediately transferred to liquid nitrogen for preservation. Rats were also perfused through the left cardiac apex with 100 ml of pre-cooled normal saline, followed by perfusion with 100 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde. The obtained brain tissue was placed in 4% paraformaldehyde for Hematoxylin-eosin staining, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence. At least three fecal pellets were collected from the rectum, and at least 3 mL of cecal contents were collected. Blood was centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min, and the serum was collected. All samples were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

## Hematoxylin-eosin staining
The brain tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution overnight at 4 °C. The paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned to 5 μm thick sections after dehydration and vitrification. The tissue sections were deparaffined with xylene, rehydrated with gradient ethanol, and stained with HE dye in proper order. HE-stained slides of prefrontal cortex and hippocampus were digitized and analyzed using CaseViewer (v2.4, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Hungary). A high-power field (×40) with equal areas was selected from the same location in the prefrontal cortex of each rat to count the number of pyknotic neurons [ ]. From the hippocampal tissue of each rat, high-power fields (×40) with equal areas were selected at the corresponding locations in CA1, CA3H, and DG regions to count the number of normal neurons [ ].

## DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing analysis of cecal contents
Genomic DNA was extracted from the cecal contents of each group (n = 3 animals per group). DNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). The V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was PCR-amplified using the primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). PCR products were recovered by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, quantified using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and subsequently sequenced on an Illumina Nextseq2000 platform (Illumina, USA) following the protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 16 S reads were processed by filtering, trimming, merging, and analyzing using the DADA2 pipeline, and an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table was constructed. All samples were rarefied to 30,136 sequence reads before downstream analyses. The detailed information regarding the bioinformatic analysis is provided in Method .

## Non-targeted fecal metabolomic profiling analysis
Samples of rat feces (100 mg each, n = 3 animals per group) were homogenized in centrifuge tubes by adding 800 µL of extraction solution (methanol: water, 4:1 v/v; containing an internal standard at 2 µg/mL). The mixture was ground for 6 min at −10℃ and 50 Hz, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 min at 5℃ and 40 kHz. The mixture was then incubated at −20℃ for 30 min and subsequently centrifuged at 4℃ and 13,000 g for 15 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected for analysis. Quality control samples were prepared using a pooled sample consisting of aliquots from each individual sample. The LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on a SCIEX UPLC-Triple TOF 6600 system (SCIEX, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, USA). Detailed elution and mass spectrometry conditions were provided in Method . The differential metabolites were identified based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) value greater than 1 (obtained by the OPLS-DA model) and a p-value less than 0.05 (Student’s t-test). Based on the KEGG database, the related metabolic pathways of the differential metabolites were determined.

## RNA sequencing of the prefrontal cortex
Total RNA was isolated from rat prefrontal cortex (n = 3 animals per group) with TRIzol Reagent following the standard protocol. Sequencing libraries were prepared exclusively from high-quality RNA samples meeting stringent criteria (A260/A280 ratio: 1.8–2.2; A260/A230 ≥ 2.0; RNA Quality Number ≥ 6.5; 28 S:18 S ratio ≥ 1.0; total RNA > 1 µg). Detailed information regarding library preparation and sequencing is provided in Method . To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two experimental groups, gene expression levels were quantified using transcripts per million (TPM) normalization. Differential expression analysis was performed with a threshold of p-value < 0.05 and a minimum fold change > 1.5. GO enrichment analysis, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were conducted by Goatools and by Python scipy software, respectively.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) assays
Total RNA was isolated from the prefrontal cortex using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity was assessed by measuring the A260/A280 ratio. mRNA levels were quantified using GAPDH as the amplification reference, and relative expression was analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table . All reagents and consumables were purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.

## Western blot assay
Prefrontal cortex tissues were weighed and homogenized in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, followed by lysis on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (4 °C, 10 min), the supernatant was collected and quantified using the BCA assay. Protein samples were then denatured by boiling at 95 °C for 10 min after adding loading buffer. The denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using freshly prepared gels (stacking and resolving layers) under constant voltage until the bromophenol blue dye reached the bottom. The prestained protein molecular weight markers were utilized for visual tracking. For immunoblotting, proteins were transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system with a “sandwich” assembly (sponge/filter paper/gel/membrane). Membranes were briefly blocked with protein-free blocking buffer (5 min) and incubated with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight: anti-AKT (1:1000, rabbit, GB15689-50, Servicebio), anti-phospho-AKT (1:500, rabbit, WLP001a, WANLEIBIO), anti-PI3K (1:500, rabbit, GB11525-50, Servicebio), anti-phospho-PI3K (1:500, rabbit, bs-5582R, Bioss), anti-mTOR (1:500, rabbit, GB11405-50, Servicebio), and anti-phospho-mTOR (1:500, rabbit, GB114489, Servicebio). On the second day, membranes were washed with TBST (3 × 5 min), and then probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min. Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate (1:1 mixture of A/B solutions, 1 min incubation) and detected by chemiluminescence imaging (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software after background subtraction and normalized for relative expression analysis.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Serum levels of IGF-1 and GH, as well as pituitary GH content, were measured using commercial ELISA kits. IGF-1 was assayed with a kit from Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd. (MM-0197R2, 48 T). GH was assayed with a kit from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (E-EL-R3003, 96 T). Pituitary tissues were homogenized in PBS containing protease inhibitors, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay prior to ELISA. The data were normalized to the total protein concentration and expressed as GH per total protein (ng/mg). All procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each group included five biological replicates, and each sample was assayed in duplicate.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Following transcardial perfusion with saline and 4% paraformaldehyde, brain tissues were post-fixed, cryoprotected, and sectioned coronally. For immunofluorescence staining, free-floating hypothalamic sections were co-incubated with primary antibodies against somatostatin (SST) (1:100, rabbit, bs-37040R, Bioss) and growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) (1:100, rabbit, bs-0205R, Bioss), while prefrontal cortical sections were incubated with primary antibodies targeting growth hormone (GH) (1:1000, rabbit, GB113303-100, Servicebio), phospho-AKT (1:100, rabbit, bs-0876R, Bioss), phospho-mTOR (1:100, rabbit, bs-3495R, Bioss), growth hormone receptor (GHR) (1:100, rabbit, bs-0654R, Bioss), and somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) (1:100, rabbit, bs-10986R, Bioss). After incubation, all sections were treated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies to visualize the localization and co-expression of the target proteins. Images were acquired under standardized exposure settings using a fluorescence microscope. Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified from three randomly selected fields per section using CaseViewer and ImageJ software by an investigator blinded to sample identity.

## Integrative analysis of multi-omics
Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between the gut microbiome and the metabolome, between DEGs and the metabolome, as well as between the gut microbiome and GH and IGF-1 levels, using a correlation coefficient threshold of |0.5| and a significance cutoff of p < 0.05. We performed joint pathway analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data using MetaboAnalyst (https://dev.metaboanalyst.ca/ModuleView.xhtml), integrating both metabolites and metabolic genes into the pathways. The enrichment analysis was conducted using the hypergeometric test, with a topology measure based on degree centrality. The integration method applied was ‘Combine queries’ to merge the multi-omics results.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Developmental status of the VPA rat model of ASD
The morphological features of VPA-exposed and control male rats from postnatal week 1 to postnatal week 5 are shown in Fig. A. VPA-exposed male offspring exhibited tail kinks and significantly shorter tails (p < 0.001) compared to controls (Fig. B). In addition, the VPA group exhibited significantly lower body weights than the control group from postnatal weeks 3 to 5 (p < 0.001) (Fig.  C).
 

## iTBS and cTBS significantly attenuate repetitive behavior and social impairment in ASD rats
One-way ANOVA revealed significant differences in the number of buried marbles (F = 15.8, p < 0.0001, Fig.  A). Post-hoc analysis indicated that VPA-exposed rats exhibited a significant preference for burying marbles (p < 0.001), which was significantly attenuated by both iTBS (p < 0.01) and cTBS (p < 0.001). One-way ANOVA showed significant main effects of treatments on the sociability index (F = 36.02, p < 0.0001) and social novelty index (F = 13.16, p < 0.0001). The VPA group exhibited significantly reduced sociability and social novelty indices (p < 0.001) compared to the control group. In both the iTBS and cTBS groups, the sociability and social novelty indices were significantly higher than in the VPA group (p < 0.01) (Fig. B-K).
 

## cTBS, but not iTBS, effectively and comprehensively ameliorated anxiety-like behavior in ASD rats
As depicted in Fig. A-C, one-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of treatments in the open field test (F = 13.24, p < 0.0001; F = 15.82, p < 0.0001; F = 23.67, p < 0.0001 for time in the center, latency to first entry to the center, and number of entries to the center, respectively). The cTBS treatment group significantly increased time in the center compared to the VPA group (p < 0.001, Fig.  A), while iTBS did not generate such effects. The average locomotor speed did not differ significantly among the four groups (F = 0.2794, p = 0.840), suggesting that the observed behavioral differences were not attributable to changes in general motor activity (Fig. ). Both iTBS and cTBS groups showed reduced latency to first center entry (p < 0.001) and increased center entries (iTBS: p < 0.05; cTBS: p < 0.001) (Fig. B-C, E-H). The spontaneous alternation rate in the Y maze revealed significant differences among groups (F = 11.10, p < 0.0001, Fig. D). VPA-exposed rats exhibited a significantly reduced spontaneous alternation rate (p < 0.001), and both iTBS and cTBS significantly reversed this change compared to VPA-exposed rats (p < 0.01).
 

## iTBS, but not cTBS, significantly improves learning and memory ability in ASD rats
One-way ANOVA showed a significant difference in the distance travelled in the target quadrant (F = 4.143, p = 0.015) and the number of entries to the target quadrant (F = 6.096, p = 0.0025) during the formal testing phase of the Morris water maze test. The model group traveled a shorter distance in the target quadrant (p < 0.05) and had fewer crossings of the target quadrant (p < 0.01) compared to the control group. Compared with the VPA group, iTBS significantly increased the traveled distance (p < 0.05) and crossing times (p < 0.01) in the target quadrant, whereas cTBS did not show a significant effect (Fig. A-B, E-H). No significant differences were found in swimming speed among the groups (Fig.  C). The discrimination index in the novel object recognition test revealed significant differences among groups (F = 10.25, p = 0.0001). VPA-exposed rats showed a significantly reduced discrimination index (p < 0.001). Both iTBS and cTBS significantly reversed this reduction compared to VPA-exposed rats (iTBS: p < 0.001; cTBS: p < 0.05) (Fig. D, I-L).
 

## iTBS and cTBS protect against prefrontal and hippocampal neuronal injury in ASD
Representative micrographs of HE staining are shown in Fig. . In the control group, the prefrontal and hippocampal neurons were neatly aligned, with normal morphology and structural integrity. By contrast, the neurons in the VPA group exhibited irregular shape, loose arrangement, pyknosis, and karyolysis. In the group treated with iTBS or cTBS, hyperchromatic degenerated neurons were still present, in addition to neurons with a normal morphology. Quantitative analysis revealed that the VPA group showed a significant increase in pyknotic cells in the prefrontal cortex compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Both iTBS and cTBS groups exhibited a significant decrease in pyknotic cells compared to the VPA group (p < 0.001) (Fig. E). The VPA group exhibited a significantly lower number of normal neurons in the CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus regions of the hippocampus compared to the control group (p < 0.001). iTBS significantly increased the number of neurons with normal morphology in the dentate gyrus (p < 0.01), and cTBS increased it in both the CA3 and dentate gyrus regions (Fig.  J, O, T).
 

## iTBS and cTBS exert divergent effects on gut microbiota in VPA-induced ASD rats
Numerous studies show that gut microbial dysbiosis contributes to ASD pathogenesis, and rTMS may treat ASD by modulating the gut–brain axis [ , ]. Using 16 S rRNA sequencing, we investigated whether iTBS and cTBS alter gut microbiota composition in VPA-exposed ASD rats. The ASV rarefaction curves indicated that, with increasing sequencing depth, the species richness (Sobs index) in each group reached a plateau, suggesting that the sequencing depth was adequate to capture nearly all species of the rat gut microbiota (Fig. A). In the α-diversity analysis, the Shannon index was employed to evaluate the diversity of bacterial communities, while the Simpson index quantified the evenness of bacterial communities. No significant differences in Shannon index and Simpson index were observed between groups (Fig. B-C, p > 0.05, one-way ANOVA). The PCoA plots analysis (β-diversity) demonstrated distinct clustering of the four groups at the ASV level (Fig.  A, R = 0.6605, p = 0.001). As shown in Figs. B and 273 ASVs were shared across all four groups. The control, model, iTBS, and cTBS groups contained 1,202, 1,203, 822, and 1,340 unique ASVs, respectively. The histograms display the gut microbiota composition, showing differences in the relative abundance of dominant phyla, genera, and species among the groups (Fig. C-E). At the phylum level, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio was higher in ASD rats (19.36 ± 16.94) than in control rats (5.03 ± 1.94), and it was partially restored after cTBS treatment (5.59 ± 1.86), whereas iTBS had no such effect (20.24 ± 19.52) (Fig.  C). We evaluated the overall differences between the four groups at the family and genus levels by one-way ANOVA (Fig. ). At the family level, iTBS significantly reduced the relative abundance of Christensenellaceae, whereas cTBS significantly increased it. At the genus level, iTBS reversed the increased relative abundance of UCG-007 and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, whereas cTBS reversed the increased relative abundance of Papillibacter in the model group. We also compared the significant between-group differences in bacterial taxa at the genus level (Tables - ). iTBS significantly reduced the relative abundance of several genera, including g__Eubacterium_ruminantium_group, g__unclassified_f__Peptostreptococcaceae, g__Rodentibacter,, g__Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, and g__Family_XIII_AD3011_group, while enriching g__Bacillus and g__Intestinimonas. ## The effects of iTBS and cTBS on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis of ASD rats
Transcriptomic results revealed that GH1 was the most up-regulated gene in the VPA rat prefrontal cortex. Conversely, it was the most down-regulated gene following iTBS and cTBS. The qRT-PCR analysis confirmed these observations (Fig.  A). Additionally, qRT-PCR results revealed a significant decrease in SSTR2 levels in the prefrontal cortex of the VPA group, while both iTBS and cTBS treatments significantly increased SSTR2 expression (Fig. ). These findings prompted our investigations into alterations in the hypothalamic-pituitary axis in the ASD rat models, as well as the potential effects of TBS on this axis.
Serum ELISA revealed significantly decreased GH levels in VPA-exposed rats compared to controls (p < 0.01), and neither iTBS nor cTBS significantly changed serum GH levels (Fig. B). However, pituitary GH levels were significantly elevated in VPA-exposed rats (p < 0.05), and iTBS intervention significantly reduced pituitary GH levels (p < 0.05) (Fig.  C). Since GH secretion patterns regulate insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) production, we further measured serum IGF-1 levels across groups. VPA-exposed rats showed significantly lower IGF-1 relative to controls (p < 0.05), and neither iTBS nor cTBS significantly changed serum IGF-1 levels (Fig. D).
 Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that SST was predominantly distributed around the third ventricle and localized in the cytoplasm across all groups, whereas GHRH was widely distributed with a higher density in the arcuate nucleus and primarily localized in the nucleus (Fig. ; Fig. E). Compared with the control group, rats in the VPA group exhibited a significant decrease in SST expression and a significant increase in GHRH expression in the hypothalamus (p < 0.001). Both iTBS and cTBS interventions significantly reversed these alterations (p < 0.001) (Fig. F-G).

## iTBS and cTBS may exert therapeutic effects for ASD via the GH/PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis
GH is a significant upstream activator of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (Fig.  A). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that VPA-exposed rats exhibited a significant increase in GHR expression (p < 0.001) and a significant decrease in SSTR2 expression (p < 0.001) in the prefrontal cortex. Both iTBS and cTBS interventions significantly ameliorated these alterations (p < 0.001) (Fig. B-C). The immunofluorescence results for SSTR2 were consistent with our PCR data.
 Immunofluorescence staining similarly showed that, compared to the control group, VPA-exposed rats exhibited increased expression levels of GH, p-AKT, and p-mTOR in the prefrontal cortex. A subsequent co-localization analysis demonstrated a significant increase in the co-localization between GH and p-AKT, as well as between GH and p-mTOR, in VPA rats (p < 0.001). Both iTBS and cTBS interventions effectively reduced this enhanced co-localization (p < 0.001) (Fig. A-D). In summary, these findings indicate that both iTBS and cTBS interventions significantly suppressed the activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, accompanied by reduced GH expression and restored hypothalamic-pituitary axis function. It can be inferred that the therapeutic effects of iTBS and cTBS may be mediated, at least in part, through the GH/PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling axis.
 

## Impact of iTBS and cTBS on the interplay between the GH/IGF-1 axis and the gut microbiome
Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the associations between the top 20 most abundant genera and serum GH/IGF-1 as well as pituitary GH levels in the model, iTBS, and cTBS groups. In the iTBS group, significant positive correlations were observed between serum GH levels and Desulfovibrio, Bacillus, and Lactobacillus, while Turicibacter showed a negative correlation with pituitary GH, and Ruminococcus was positively correlated with serum IGF-1 -- associations that were absent in the model group. ## Discussion

## iTBS and cTBS differentially ameliorate core and comorbid ASD symptoms
In this study, VPA-exposed rats displayed characteristic tail curvature and shortened tails, indicative of underlying neurodevelopmental defects [ ]. Behavioral assessments revealed that VPA-exposed rats exhibit core autistic features, including impaired social interaction and increased repetitive/stereotypic behaviors, as well as common comorbid symptoms, including deficits in learning and spatial memory, and elevated anxiety-like behaviors. The HE staining results indicated that these behavioral abnormalities were accompanied by signs of neuronal degeneration in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. The prefrontal cortex plays a pivotal role in regulating core ASD behaviors, with circuit dysfunctions directly contributing to social deficits and repetitive behaviors [ , ]. Similarly, the hippocampus is critically involved in ASD-associated learning and memory impairments and emotional disturbance [ ]. In summary, this study successfully established a VPA-induced rat model of ASD that recapitulates key behavioral features and neuropathological manifestations of human ASD.
Behavioral results demonstrated that both iTBS and cTBS significantly improved core symptoms of ASD in rats, namely social deficits and repetitive behaviors. These observations are consistent with findings from clinical trials involving ASD subjects [ – , ]. In addition, we found that cTBS, but not iTBS, effectively ameliorated anxiety-like behavior in ASD rats, as evidenced by a significant increase in time spent in the open field center. A substantial body of evidence indicates that cTBS may serve as a viable treatment option for patients with anxiety disorders, demonstrating significant efficacy, particularly in generalized anxiety disorder [ – ]. In contrast, iTBS has shown limited effectiveness in alleviating anxiety symptoms [ , ]. A randomized controlled trial involving patients with treatment-resistant depression revealed that cTBS was significantly superior to iTBS in improving anxiety-related symptoms [ ]. Furthermore, we found that iTBS, but not cTBS, significantly improved learning and memory ability in ASD rats. Similarly, Mix et al. [ ] reported that iTBS, but not cTBS, significantly enhanced learning performance in a tactile discrimination task in rats. iTBS also improved the performance of offspring from maternally immune-activated rats (another established ASD model) in the Morris water maze task [ ]. The improvement in learning ability induced by iTBS has been attributed to the promotion of learning-related dendritic spine formation and stabilization [ ], the enhancement of hippocampal theta oscillation [ ], and the modulation of hippocampal mossy fiber plasticity [ ]. Clinical evidence demonstrates that iTBS effectively mitigates learning and memory deficits associated with various neurological and psychiatric disorders, including bipolar disorder [ ], post-stroke cognitive impairment [ ], and Alzheimer’s disease [ ]. In summary, our behavioral findings are supported by both clinical and preclinical evidence, indicating that both iTBS and cTBS can effectively improve the core symptoms of ASD, while exerting distinct modulatory effects on its associated comorbid symptoms.

## Divergent modulation of the microbiota-gut-brain axis by iTBS and cTBS
ASD is linked to significant alterations in gut microbiota composition and impaired microbiota-gut-brain axis communication. Research indicates a prevalent state of gut dysbiosis in individuals with ASD, characterized by changes in the composition and diversity of intestinal bacteria. These disruptions are hypothesized to modulate neurodevelopment and behavior via endocrine, immunological, metabolic, and neurological systems, contributing to the core symptoms of ASD [ , ]. Emerging evidence suggests that rTMS may modulate the composition and function of the gut microbiota through its effects on neurotransmitters and the immune system. The regulation of the microbiota-gut-brain axis is suggested to be one of the underlying mechanisms, highlighting the potential of rTMS as a novel therapeutic strategy for ASD [ , ]. Consistent with recent reports in diverse ASD rodent models [ – ], the present study found no significant difference in gut microbial α-diversity but a significant change in β-diversity between VPA-exposed ASD rats and controls. Furthermore, an increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio was observed in ASD rats, aligning with earlier findings [ , ]. Both iTBS and cTBS altered β-diversity in VPA-exposed rats, with cTBS additionally reversing the elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio. Christensenellaceae is a recently described family within the phylum Firmicutes that is closely associated with host metabolic health [ ]. We found that although the abundance of Christensenellaceae (family) and Christensenellaceae R-7 (genus) was elevated in VPA-exposed rats compared to controls, the two rTMS protocols exerted opposite effects: iTBS significantly decreased their abundances, while cTBS further increased them. iTBS enhances cortical excitability, whereas cTBS exerts inhibitory effects, which may partly explain the differential outcomes. Quoilin et al. [ ] suggested that both excitatory and inhibitory brain processes are linked to gut microbiota composition, reporting that higher relative abundances of Christensenellaceae and the Christensenellaceae R-7 were correlated with stronger inhibitory influences.
The pathology of ASD is associated with a neuroinflammatory cascade and abnormal energy and lipid metabolism [ – ]. The dominant genera in the iTBS group included Intestinimonas, Parvibacter, and Faecalibaculum, and in the cTBS group, the dominant genera included Christensenellaceae_R-7, norank_f__Peptococcaceae, and Frisingicoccus. Both Intestinimonas and Faecalibaculum produce butyrate from sugars and amino acids. This compound can enhance intestinal barrier function and mucosal immunity and exert anti-inflammatory properties in ASD [ – ]. In addition, Faecalibaculum is involved in energy metabolism through carbohydrate fermentation, butyrate production, and by driving an age-related metabolic shift towards increased short-chain fatty acid synthesis [ ]. As an anti-inflammatory probiotic, Parvibacter can improve social behavior in ASD mice by ameliorating inflammatory responses [ ]. Peptococcaceae exhibits a significant negative correlation with serum and hepatic lipid profiles, as well as the expression levels of lipogenic genes, which exert a crucial role in alleviating lipid metabolism disorders [ – ]. Frisingicoccus is a butyrate-producing genus associated with metabolic health; its abundance is positively correlated with ketogenesis and lipid metabolic pathways [ ]. In summary, the above evidence suggests that iTBS and cTBS exert distinct roles in modulating the gut microbial structure and function in ASD rats. The genera enriched by iTBS are primarily associated with inflammatory responses and energy metabolism, whereas those promoted by cTBS are mainly linked to lipid metabolism.
Gut metabolomic KEGG analysis revealed that iTBS reversed the up-regulation of the arachidonic acid metabolism pathway induced by VPA exposure, with the downregulation of prostaglandin E2. Substantial evidence indicates that arachidonic acid is metabolized via the cyclooxygenase pathway to synthesize prostaglandin E2, a pro-inflammatory mediator that contributes to neuroinflammation in ASD [ , ]. Additionally, iTBS reversed VPA-induced down-regulation in alkaloid biosynthesis pathways (e.g., histidine, purine, ornithine, lysine, nicotinic acid, terpenoid, and polyketide), central carbon metabolism in cancer, and the TCA cycle. Metabolomic analyses of feces, urine, and blood from children with ASD consistently show significantly reduced levels of histidine, ornithine, and lysine compared to healthy controls, suggesting their potential role as biomarkers for ASD [ – ]. Biosynthesis of histidine and ornithine from Faecalibaculum (dominant genera in iTBS group) alleviates intestinal inflammation and induces alterations in energy metabolism of intestinal epithelial cells [ , ]. Lysine deficiency impairs energy efficiency, leading to retarded growth and body compartments development in rats, along with upregulated liver C-reactive protein expression that mediates inflammation [ ]. Consistently, VPA-exposed offspring exhibited reduced body weight in our study. Bioactive terpenoids and polyketides produced by gut microbiota have been well-documented for their anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and antibacterial activities [ , ]. Disruptions in nicotinic acid and purine metabolism represent characteristic features of the gut metabolome in ASD [ , ]. Nicotinic acid has demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties across various experimental models of inflammatory diseases, such as hepatic inflammation and vascular inflammation [ , ]. Furthermore, nicotinic acid serves as a primary precursor for the biosynthesis of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, and adenine is a key component of both nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide and adenosine triphosphate. Hence, iTBS may enhance cellular energy supply and signaling by upregulating purine metabolism to promote nucleotide synthesis. The coordinated upregulation of the TCA cycle and central carbon metabolism further indicates a fundamental enhancement of mitochondrial energy production induced by iTBS.
In contrast to iTBS, cTBS reversed the up-regulation of nucleotide metabolism, purine metabolism, glutathione metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and steroid biosynthesis in VPA-exposed rats. A recent gut metabolomic study in VPA-induced ASD mice also identified enhanced nucleotide metabolism [ ]. DNA replication and repair rely on the supply of deoxynucleoside triphosphate building blocks, which are produced and maintained by nucleotide metabolic pathways [ ]. VPA-induced DNA damage, including DNA double-strand breaks, underlies the autistic characteristics in VPA-exposed rats [ ] and may compensatorily increase DNA synthesis and cellular proliferation [ ], thus resulting in enhanced nucleotide metabolism. Our functional predictions of gut flora also showed that cTBS inhibited nucleotide synthesis and cell proliferation activated in VPA-exposed rats. Therefore, cTBS reversing the up-regulation of nucleotide and purine metabolism indicates that it may alleviate the DNA damage and cellular stress state caused by VPA exposure. Analyses of gut microbiota function, urinary proteomics and metabolomics, and plasma metabolomics have consistently revealed significant disruptions in glutathione metabolism in individuals with ASD, associated with the redox imbalance and oxidative stress damage observed in ASD [ – ]. Oxidative stress acts as a key driver of arachidonic acid metabolism by enhancing the release of arachidonic acid via cytosolic phospholipase A2. The metabolites of arachidonic acid, in turn, directly or indirectly contribute to the production of more reactive oxygen species, thereby creating a vicious cycle of lipid peroxidation in ASD [ , ]. In addition, as a major polyunsaturated fatty acid in brain membrane phospholipids, the depletion of arachidonic acid contributes to neuronal damage in ASD through the deregulation of lipid metabolism [ , ]. Disrupted cholesterol homeostasis, linked to ASD, is supported by the upregulated primary bile acid and steroid biosynthesis in our VPA-exposed rats, consistent with observations of elevated total cholesterol and lipid network dysregulation in ASD children [ – ]. The levels of peripheral steroid hormones, including cortisol, testosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone, androstenedione, androsterone, pregnenolone, and progesterone, are significantly elevated in individuals with ASD relative to healthy control subjects [ – ]. rTMS can modulate steroid hormone levels, as evidenced by reduced cortisol, by targeting the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [ , ]. In sum, the above evidence supports the regulatory effects of cTBS on nucleotide metabolism, oxidative stress, and lipid metabolism in VPA-exposed rats. Furthermore, predictive functional profiling of the gut microbiota was substantiated by our metabolomics data. Integrated analysis further revealed strong correlations between key differential microbial taxa and intestinal metabolites. The high concordance between our 16 S rRNA sequencing and metabolomics results underscores that iTBS and cTBS may alleviate ASD by influencing distinct gut microbiota communities and their associated metabolic profiles.
KEGG enrichment analysis of the prefrontal cortex transcriptome revealed that iTBS primarily modulates inflammation- and immune-related pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, intestinal immune network for IgA production, etc. The cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction provides a framework for the binding of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukins, interferons, and tumor necrosis factor) to their receptors, thereby serving as the foundational basis for neuroinflammatory signaling [ ]. A recent multi-omics study revealed dysregulated TNF signaling in circulating NK and T-cell subsets from ASD children, marked by upregulated levels of TNFSF10, TNFSF11, and TNFSF12, pointing to an enhanced inflammatory response in these cells [ ]. Metabolomic studies of ASD children and VPA-exposed mice consistently identify significantly altered taurine and hypotaurine metabolism [ , , ]. Taurine exerts anti-inflammatory effects during neurodevelopment by reducing microglial activation, and its deficiency is linked to increased prevalence of ASD [ ]. The identification of the intestinal immune network for IgA production further underscores that iTBS may alleviate intestinal inflammation by regulating gut immunity via the microbiota-gut-brain axis [ ]. The integrated analysis of metabolomics and transcriptomics also identified altered taurine and arachidonic acid metabolism, further supporting that iTBS acts through regulating inflammatory responses.
The significantly altered pathway in transcriptome induced by cTBS included the ECM-receptor interaction, Focal adhesion, TGF-beta signaling pathway, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, and Protein digestion and absorption. The ECM-receptor interaction is critical for cell adhesion, differentiation, and proliferation. For instance, this pathway mediates the differentiation of mesenchymal stromal cells into adipocytes and regulates lipid metabolism in subcutaneous fat [ , ]. Impaired ECM-receptor interaction is also a primary target of hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress [ ]. Post-mortem frontal cortex tissue, cerebrospinal fluid, and plasma from ASD subjects showed significant increases in TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 levels compared to controls [ , ]. TGF-β signaling stimulates the production of reactive oxygen species and suppresses cell proliferation by inhibiting nucleotide metabolism [ , ]. In addition, TGF-β signaling is closely linked to lipid metabolism and can promote hepatic lipid accumulation, thereby contributing to hepatic steatosis [ – ]. Elevated oxidative stress in ASD is also evidenced by blood accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which is positively linked to co-occurring gastrointestinal problems in ASD children [ , ]. Thus, the above findings provide support for the potential regulatory role of cTBS in oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism. The identification of the protein digestion and absorption suggests that cTBS may directly regulate gastrointestinal function via the microbiota-gut-brain axis. Furthermore, integrated analysis of the cTBS group revealed significant alterations in glutathione and arachidonic acid metabolism, providing further evidence for cTBS’s impact on oxidative stress.
The identified close associations among key differential molecules, microbial taxa, and metabolites reinforce the discovery that iTBS and cTBS modulate the microbiota-gut-brain axis in ASD. For instance, iTBS significantly downregulated the expression of Ptgfr in the prefrontal cortex, while its ligand, prostaglandin F2-alpha, was found to be markedly increased in the gut of VPA model rats. Prostaglandin F2-alpha, known for its pro-inflammatory properties, can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and induce systemic as well as central nervous inflammation [ , ]. Furthermore, iTBS induced upregulation of 5-Aminosalicylic acid, Lactic acid, Succinic acid, and the microbial genera g__Faecalibaculum and g__Bacillus. 5-Aminosalicylic acid can promote the growth of g__Faecalibaculum, and together they exert protective effects by alleviating colitis and reinforcing intestinal barrier function [ ]. g__Bacillus, capable of producing lactic acid and succinic acid, plays a critical role in energy metabolism [ , ]. In contrast, cTBS significantly downregulated intestinal levels of L-Valine, L-Glycine, and L-Alanine, along with the abundance of g__Corynebacterium. g__Corynebacterium, a pathogenic bacterium, can produce L-Valine, L-Glycine, and L-Alanine [ – ]. Our correlation analysis also showed a significant positive correlation between g__Corynebacterium and L-valine. Notably, elevated levels of L-Valine, L-Glycine, and L-Alanine have been observed in individuals with ASD, contributing to disruptions in glutathione metabolism and energy metabolism [ , , ].

## Potential downregulation of the central GH/PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis by TBS
PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling is a key regulator of cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation, and is upregulated in the ASD pathogenesis [ ]. Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses revealed that both iTBS and cTBS interventions effectively inhibited the activated PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling in VPA-exposed rats. Strikingly, transcriptomics identified a significant increase in prefrontal GH1 expression following VPA exposure, which was markedly downregulated by both interventions. This key finding directed our attention to the role of iTBS and cTBS in modulating the upstream GH of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling. We found that both iTBS and cTBS significantly lowered pituitary GH levels and reduced its colocalization with p-AKT/p-mTOR in the prefrontal cortex of VPA-rats, suggesting a potential GH-dependent mechanism for PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibition. This hypothesis was further supported by immunofluorescence analyses of hypothalamic SST and GHRH, as well as prefrontal GHR expression. Transcriptomics revealed that both iTBS and cTBS significantly downregulate Lepr. Leptin, a peptide involved in appetite regulation, lipogenesis, and metabolism, has been shown to increase GH production [ ]. Additionally, the upregulation of prefrontal SSTR2 by iTBS and cTBS, as confirmed by immunofluorescence and qPCR, suggests a potential mechanism for behavioral improvement, given the established role of prefrontal SST signaling in reducing anxiety and promoting exploration [ ].
Dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis may underlie the ASD pathogenesis [ – ]. Our results preliminarily support this hypothesis, suggesting that hypothalamic-pituitary axis dysregulation may lead to excessive central growth hormone levels, further activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Notably, we observed significantly reduced serum GH and IGF-1 levels in VPA-exposed rats, which were not significantly altered by iTBS or cTBS. The growth-promoting effects of GH are primarily mediated by stimulating the production of IGF-1 in tissues like the liver, which in turn negatively inhibit GH secretion [ ]. Consistent with previous studies, this study found that VPA-exposed offspring had significantly lower body weight than healthy controls [ – ], reflecting potential developmental delay, which may be related to the decreased serum GH and IGF-1 levels. Furthermore, IGF-1 plays a critical role in the development and function of neurons and glial cells [ ]. Our recent meta-analysis also suggests that individuals with severe ASD may have lower blood IGF-1 levels due to hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction [ ]. Under physiological conditions, the growth hormone axis is tightly regulated by negative feedback. For instance, GH acts at the hypothalamus to stimulate somatostatin release and suppress GHRH secretion. Liver-derived IGF-1 can promote SST and suppress GHRH and GH secretion [ ]. The precise mechanisms underlying the dissociation between central and peripheral GH levels observed in VPA-exposed rats remain unclear. Plausible hypotheses include a potential dysregulation of the GH axis negative feedback and/or an impaired transport of centrally produced GH to the systemic circulation. BBB dysfunction is a key pathogenic mechanism in ASD [ , ], a phenomenon also observed in prenatal VPA rodent models [ – ]. By impairing the transport of GH from the central nervous system to the peripheral circulation, BBB dysfunction may disrupt the negative feedback regulation of GH secretion that is normally responsive to peripheral IGF-1 levels [ , ]. Both iTBS and cTBS effectively reversed the central GH abnormalities and partially restored neuroendocrine homeostasis, which may underlie their therapeutic effect on core ASD behaviors. GO enrichment analysis also indicated that both iTBS and cTBS are closely associated with the regulation of developmental functions.

## Interplay between the GH/IGF-1 axis and the gut microbiome
A growing body of research highlights a bidirectional communication between the GH/IGF-1 axis and the gut microbiome [ ]. For instance, certain gut bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus) can elevate IGF-1 levels [ , ], while short-chain fatty acids can suppress GH secretion via the cAMP pathway [ ]. In return, IGF-1 contributes to weight restoration and helps remodel immature and dysbiotic microbial communities [ ], whereas GH has been demonstrated to inhibit gut bacterial translocation and reduce intestinal permeability [ , ]. Although neither iTBS nor cTBS significantly altered serum GH and IGF-1 levels in VPA rats, both interventions showed a trend toward reversing the abnormalities. In the iTBS group, the genera Desulfovibrio, Lactobacillus, and Bacillus exhibited significant positive correlations with serum GH levels. This finding is mechanistically supported by existing literature, which identifies Desulfovibrio as a participant in ghrelin and GH activation [ ], and notes that Bacillus and Lactobacillus can elevate GH/IGF-1 levels to promote growth performance [ – ]. In the cTBS group, Bacillus and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group were significantly positively correlated with serum GH, serum IGF-1, and pituitary GH levels, while Romboutsia was significantly negatively correlated with these measures. These patterns are opposite to the correlation results observed in the model group. Enrichment of gut Christensenellaceae_R-7_group is linked to promoted growth, increased body weight, and higher GH/IGF-1 levels [ , ], while reduced Romboutsia abundance is associated with elevated GH and IGF-1 release [ ]. These observations suggest that both iTBS and cTBS may exert their regulatory effects on growth and metabolism in ASD by specifically enhancing or altering the correlative relationships between specific gut microbiota and GH/IGF-1.

## Limitations and future perspectives
Several limitations of our study should be considered. First, a key limitation of this study is the sole inclusion of male offspring in the experiments. Given the well-established sexual dimorphism in ASD, wherein males are diagnosed approximately four times more frequently than females, our findings are inherently limited to the male context. Second, current correlation analysis cannot establish a causal relationship between changes in gut microbiota and behavioral improvements induced by TBS. Alterations in the gut microbiota may either be a key mediator of TBS therapeutic effects or an accompanying phenomenon. Third, we have not directly demonstrated that the modulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway by TBS is specifically dependent on changes in GH levels or GHR activity.
Future studies need to employ fecal microbiota transplantation from TBS-treated donors and functional validation via colonization of key bacterial taxa. While diet was carefully controlled in this study, its complex interplay with lifestyle factors in human ASD represents a critical confounding variable for the gut microbiota that must be addressed in future clinical translation research. Moreover, future studies using GHR antagonists are needed to definitively establish the causal dependency of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway regulation on alterations in GH signaling in this context.

## Conclusion
To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first multi-omics-based comparison of the mechanisms underlying iTBS and cTBS in the treatment of ASD. By integrating prefrontal transcriptome, gut microbiota analysis, and fecal metabolomics, we found that both iTBS and cTBS could regulate the intestinal flora structure and metabolic function of ASD through the microbiota-gut-brain axis, thereby exerting different therapeutic mechanisms: iTBS mainly regulates inflammatory immune response and energy metabolism, while cTBS is more focused on regulating oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism. Furthermore, our results indicate that the modulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis by iTBS and cTBS in ASD implicates the downregulation of the GH/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway as a potential mechanism. These findings provide valuable insights into the neurobiological basis of TBS therapies in ASD and offer a scientific foundation for developing targeted interventions for ASD.
