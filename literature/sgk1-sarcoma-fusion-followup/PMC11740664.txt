## Abstract
Gynecologic cancers (GCs), including cervical cancer (CC), ovarian cancer (OC), endometrial cancer (EC), as well as vulvar and vaginal cancers, represent major health threats to women, with increasing incidence rates observed globally. Conventional treatments, such as surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, are often hindered by challenges such as drug resistance and recurrence, contributing to high mortality rates. Organoid technology has emerged as a transformative tool in cancer research, offering in vitro models that closely replicate the tumor cell architecture and heterogeneity of primary cancers. Tumor-derived organoids preserve the histological and molecular characteristics of the original tumors, making them invaluable for studying tumor biology, molecular pathways, and the tumor immune microenvironment. Furthermore, organoids play a crucial role in biomarker discovery, drug screening, and the development of personalized therapeutic strategies. In contrast to traditional cell lines and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, gynecologic cancer organoids accurately mirror the genetic mutations and specific gene expression profiles of primary tumors. This review provides an overview of recent advancements in the development of gynecologic cancer organoid models, highlighting their contributions to understanding disease mechanisms, facilitating drug discovery, and advancing precision medicine. It also addresses the potential and challenges of organoid technology, with a focus on its role in advancing personalized treatment approaches for GCs.

## Introduction
Gynecological cancers (GCs) include ovarian cancer (OC), cervical cancer (CC), endometrial cancer (EC), vulvar and vaginal cancers, and fallopian tube cancer [ ]. Among these, OC, CC, and EC are the most common malignancies of the female reproductive system, posing significant threats to women's lives and health and representing a global public health concern [ – ]. Particularly, OC is difficult to diagnose early due to its hidden location, with over half of patients presenting with distant metastasis at diagnosis [ – ]. While early detection techniques have improved cure rates for EC and CC, treatment options remain limited for cases of recurrence and metastasis [ – ]. Currently, the treatment of GCs primarily involves a comprehensive approach, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy [ , ]. However, due to the unique anatomical relationships of GCs, complete surgical resection is challenging, leading to high recurrence rates and often impacting patients' quality of life post-surgery. Radiotherapy mainly utilizes intracavitary brachytherapy, but its effectiveness is compromised in cases of poor anatomical conditions, retroperitoneal lymph node metastasis, or extensive pelvic adhesions, resulting in incomplete target coverage and uneven dose distribution [ , ]. Additionally, traditional animal models and two-dimensional (2D) cell culture systems have significant limitations in simulating tumor biology and drug responsiveness, failing to fully replicate the complex microenvironment of human tumors. Therefore, finding more precise and effective treatment methods is urgent. For example, three-dimensional cell culture technology and organoid models can better mimic the biological characteristics and microenvironment of tumors to some extent, providing new means for drug screeningand personalized treatment [ , ]. Simultaneously, the application of immunotherapy and gene editing technologies brings new hope for the treatment of GCs. In summary, the main challenge in gynecologic tumor research is to overcome the limitations of existing technologies and find more precise and effective treatment methods.
Organoids are three-dimensional (3D) culture systems derived from stem cells, which replicate the architecture and function of human organs. They retain stem cell properties like continuous division and differentiation, enabling them to closely resemble the original tissues in terms of spatial structure, genetics, and function. Organoids can be derived from various stem cell sources, including adult stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and embryonic stem cells (ESCs), each offering unique advantages for cancer research [ ] (Fig.  ). In cancer studies, organoids derived from adult stem cells are valuable for precision oncology because they retain the genomic integrity and tumor microenvironment (TME) of the original tumor. However, most organoid platforms primarily focus on epithelial (tumor) cells and lack the other critical cell types typically found in the TME, which limits their ability to fully replicate the complexity of cancer biology. These patient-specific models are ideal for studying drug resistance and pharmacodynamics [ ]. In contrast, iPSC and ESC-derived organoids can be genetically engineered to mimic oncogenic mutations, making them versatile tools for investigating cancer mechanisms, screening drugs, and conducting functional genomics studies [ ]. The integration of organoids with the TME, through both reconstituted and holistic native models, further enhances their ability to model tumor-immune interactions, immune evasion, and therapy responses [ ] (Fig.  ). The summary of organoid types and their applications in cancer research was showed in Table  , while TME models and their applications were displayed in Table  . Overall, organoid models offer a robust and innovative approach to understanding tumor biology and developing personalized cancer therapies [ ].
 In GCs research, organoid technology has shown substantial potential for application. For instance, by culturing human-derived OC tumor cells under appropriate conditions within a three-dimensional cell culture system, individualized OC organoids models can be generated [ ]. These OC organoids preserve the histomorphology, genomics, and transcriptomics characteristics of their source tumors. Research indicates that the in vitro drug sensitivity test results of OC organoids align with patient treatment responses, providing a valuable platform for investigating the origin, progression, and resistance mechanisms of OC [ ]. Moreover, individualized OC organoids models facilitate the screening of personalized treatment plans, offering considerable clinical translational value [ , ]. In summary, organoids technology offers promising prospects for both fundamental research and clinical applications in gynecologic malignancies. By further refining organoids culture techniques and application strategies, it is anticipated that more effective treatment methods for gynecologic tumor patients will be developed, thereby enhancing patient prognosis, improving quality of life, and ultimately advancing precision medicine for GCs.

## Application of organoids of different tissues in benign and malignant gynecological diseases

## Organoids derived from normal and benign gynecologic condition
Organoids can closely mimic the physiological structure and function of in situ tissues while maintaining genetic stability over long-term passaging. They have emerged as crucial tools in biomedical research, offering a novel and superior in vitro model compared to traditional methods. Recent studies have successfully constructed organoids with normal physiological functions by extracting cells from healthy gynecologic tissues, such as the fallopian tubes, ovaries, and endometrium. These organoids serve as an ideal experimental platform for further investigating the development and treatment of various gynecologic diseases (Table  , Fig.  , ).
 

## Normal gynecologic condition
In a recent study, researchers isolated endometrial gland organoids from term placental membranes. These organoids exhibit typical epithelial markers, including E-cadherin, laminin, and cytokeratin 7. The study demonstrated that these Organoids can be passaged through multiple generations in vitro, and they generate individual-specific organoids relevant to pregnancy outcomes. RNA sequencing analysis confirmed the epithelial origin of these organoids and ruled out contamination from trophoblastic cells or endometrial stromal fibroblasts. Furthermore, these organoids respond to stimuli from estrogen, progesterone, and cAMP, underscoring their potential for investigating endometrial biology and pathology [ ].
Wu et al. developed an ovarian organoid model with reproductive and endocrine functions using a three-dimensional culture system derived from female germline stem cells. This model is capable of producing oocytes that can generate offspring. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that the model comprises six ovarian cell types, including germ cells, granulosa cells, and theca cells, with gene expression profiles that align with their respective functions. The model can be utilized to study the effects of drugs, such as salinomycin, on follicle development and germ cell maintenance, providing a new platform for drug screening and toxicological testing. Oocytes derived from this model, after in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer, successfully produce healthy offspring with no significant differences from wild-type mice, demonstrating that the organoids can generate functionally normal oocytes. In summary, this female germline stem cells derived ovarian organoid model offers a novel research platform for reproductive medicine, drug screening, and toxicological testing [ ]. Ding et al. investigated the stemness, self-renewal capacity, and Wnt-related organoid formation of human fallopian tube epithelial cells (FTECs). Their findings revealed that FTECs exhibit a cuboidal cell morphology and a constant proliferation rate, and can form colonies from single cells with an efficiency of 4%. Flow cytometry analyses demonstrated that FTECs express both normal and cancer stem cell markers, form spheres and colonies on low-adhesion culture plates, and show enhanced stemness and colony-forming ability in the presence of matrigel. Additionally, FTECs co-cultured with fallopian tube stromal cells and endothelial cells are capable of forming organoids, though this process can be inhibited by the Wnt pathway inhibitor DKK1 [ ]. Moreover, Ding et al. compared the cost and effectiveness of traditional and composite media for culturing FTEC organoids. The study found that traditional media were more conducive to organoid proliferation, both in terms of quantity and size, whereas composite media enhanced the expression of PAX8 and stemness genes. Although composite media require additional growth factors, their costs are comparable to traditional media, and the benefits of composite media warrant further investigation [ ].
Research on endometrial organoid models has revealed significant advancements in replicating the characteristics of endometrial epithelium. Long-term expansion of organoid culture systems derived from mouse and human endometrium can replicate the characteristics of endometrial epithelium, including glandular-like structures and specific marker expression, and respond to key regulatory hormones such as estrogen and progesterone, thereby simulating the physiological changes of the menstrual cycle. Under estrogenic influence, the organoids exhibit proliferative phase characteristics, whereas progesterone induces secretory phase features, including epithelial cell differentiation, cilia formation, and mucus secretion. This endometrial organoid model offers a robust platform for studying endometrial biology and pathology, overcoming the limitations inherent in two-dimensional cell cultures. By co-culturing with endometrial stromal cells, the model can be further refined to simulate epithelial-stromal interactions [ ]. Research demonstrates that when endometrial organoids are transplanted into the basal layer of an intrauterine adhesion mouse model, they integrate fully and grow normally, restoring the integrity of the endometrial epithelial structure and achieving functional repair. Endometrial organoids exhibit proliferative and secretory functions akin to those of the menstrual cycle, facilitating the repair of damaged endometrium and restoring fertility [ ]. Furthermore, a standardized method for isolating and culturing endometrial organoids from mouse uteri has been established, providing a valuable tool for investigating endometrial biology and diseases. This method enables exploration of the regeneration and differentiation processes of endometrial epithelial cellsand their gene expression responses to estrogen, which is pertinent to research on endometriosis, EC, and implantation failure. Gene-engineered mouse models with endometrial organoids aid in identifying growth factors and signaling pathways that regulate endometrial cell renewal and differentiation [ ].
Numerous studies have elucidated the key factors involved in establishing endometrial epithelial organoid systems. Vankelecom et al. developed long-term organoid models for endometriosis, endometrial hyperplasia, and EC, which replicated the genetic background, signaling pathway abnormalities, and gene mutations of primary lesions and recapitulated clinical features upon transplantation. These models serve as powerful tools for studying the pathogenesis of endometrial diseases and for drug screening, revealing that organoids from different stages and types of EC exhibit varying drug responses, thereby facilitating personalized drug screening [ ]. Another study established an endometrial organoids model that maintains endometrial tissue structure, expression patterns, secretory characteristics, and responsiveness to estrogen. This research identified estrogen signaling as the primary driver of ciliated cell differentiation, while NOTCH signaling provides a supportive environment. Although inhibition of NOTCH signaling enhances estrogen -induced cilia formation, it alone is insufficient to trigger cilia formation, offering insights into the dynamic balance between secretory and ciliated cells in the endometrial epithelium [ ]. Spencer et al. utilized human endometrial epithelial organoid systems to analyze transcriptomic and proteomic responses to estrogen, progesterone, and prostaglandin estrogen. They discovered that progesterone and prostaglandin estrogen regulate the basal secretion of Organoids, particularly CST3, SERPINA3, and STC1. CST3 was found to inhibit stromal cell shedding and membrane-like reactions in vitro, indicating that endometrial gland-derived factors like CST3 are significant for establishing pregnancy and female fertility [ ]. Further research identified that the miR-3194-5p/AQP-1 and AQP-9/S100A9 axes are critical regulators of early pregnancy trophoblast invasion, providing a foundation for understanding early human placental development [ ]. Gargett et al. addressed the limitations of existing models by constructing and comparing organoid models derived from menstrual fluid and hormonally treated endometrium. They found that organoid models derived from menstrual fluid and hormonally treated endometrium exhibited similar cell proliferation rates, endometrial marker expressions, and gene expression profiles to standard endometrial organoids. This non-invasive sampling method and new approach for studying hormonally treated endometrium hold promise as valuable tools for gynecological research and personalized medicine [ ]. Torrance et al. explored the feasibility of establishing organoids from cryopreserved endometrial tissue, demonstrating that the freezing process does not affect organoid characteristics.
Many key molecules and signaling pathways are integral to the biological effects observed in endometrial epithelial organoid (EEO). These organoids can self-renew, be expanded long-term, cryopreserved, and maintain genetic stability, expressing the epithelial marker FOXA2 and responding appropriately to E2 and progesterone regulation. Treatment with E2 or a combination of E2 and progesterone induces gene expression changes in endometrial epithelial organoids that mimic the proliferative and secretory phases of the endometrium [ ]. Single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed that endometrial epithelial organoid encompass various epithelial cell types, with their proportions and gene expression profiles modulated by hormonal treatments. This endometrial epithelial organoid model is a valuable platform for investigating the physiology and pathology of the human endometrium [ ]. Research indicates that endometrial epithelial organoids form a biochemical selective barrier, with qualitative metabolomics showing asymmetric apical and basal secretion in cultured endometrial epithelium [ ]. The endometrium undergoes dynamic changes during the menstrual cycle and pregnancy; however, the role of endometrial glands in tissue remodeling remains unclear. Organoids cultured from human endometrial biopsies can be stably maintained for over 30 months, reproducing glandular features, including the secretion of “lactation-like” components and the ability to differentiate into ciliated cells, while displaying stem cell characteristics. Long-term cultured organoids exhibit a transition from mature stages to progenitor stages, with FUT4, AXIN2, and POU5F1 (OCT4) emerging as potential markers. They show an increasing proportion of epithelial stem cells and a decreasing proportion of glandular stem cells, respond to hormonal stimuli, and reflect various stages of the menstrual cycle and early pregnancy [ ]. DeMayo et al. investigated the impact of progesterone signaling on endometrial epithelial cell organoids, finding that progesterone attenuates E2-dependent gene responses but has a minimal overall effect on the transcriptome. PGR binding sites overlap with estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) binding sites, predominantly located in inactive chromatin regions. HiC analysis demonstrated that more ESR1 peaks are associated with E2-regulated genes. This organoid model contributes to defining chromatin regulatory components in EECs [ ]. Huntsman et al. treated organoids with the Notch inhibitor DBZ, resulting in an increased proportion of ciliated cells. RNA sequencing revealed that secretory cells can differentiate into ciliated cells, and single-cell RNA sequencing identified new markers for both cell types, validated by immunohistochemistry. These markers correlate with better survival rates in endometrial and OCs, with MPST markers refining EC risk stratification [ ]. DeMayo et al. also integrated ESR1 cistrome and transcriptome data from endometrial biopsies and organoids, finding that ESR1 binding sites are more widely distributed outside gene promoter regions in EMOs, similar to the proliferative endometrium [ ]. Clevers et al. used base editors to introduce PTENQ245 and PTENR130 mutations into EMOs, leading to the downregulation of PTEN protein and activation of the PTEN/PI3K pathway’s mTOR signaling. Further introduction of PIK3CAE545K mutations enhanced mTORC1 signaling, and the double-mutant organoids exhibited reduced sensitivity to the mTOR inhibitor Everolimus [ ]. Another study found that PTEN knockout alone or combined with KRASG12D activation could not induce tumors in mouse endometrial organoids. However, KRASG12D combined with Cdkn2a knockout or Trp53 deletion could trigger sarcoma-like cancer, displaying epithelial-mesenchymal transition. The KRASWT allele may act as a tumor suppressor in sarcoma-like cancer cells, with its loss potentially leading to excessive activation of the KRAS pathway [ ].

## Benign gynecologic condition
The study employed genetic engineering to reconstruct gene abnormalities associated with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) in fallopian tube organoids to explore tumorigenic mechanisms in FTECs. The results indicated that the deletion of the p53 gene alone is insufficient to induce tumors; suppression of PTEN and induction of mutant PIK3CA are necessary for the development of in situ cancer and HGSOC-like tumors. Additionally, deletion of the APC gene leads to the formation of benign cysts, emphasizing the critical role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in HGSOC, while the Wnt pathway has a lesser role. KRAS gene mutations combined with p53 deletion can result in tumors with both sarcomatous and carcinomatous components, suggesting a tissue-specific epithelial-mesenchymal transition program [ ]. Furthermore, researchers utilized CRISPR-Cas9 technology to introduce HGSOC-associated gene mutations (e.g., Trp53, Brca1, Pten, and Nf1) into epithelial cells of mouse fallopian tubes and ovaries, establishing an HGSOC organoid model. These organoids exhibited varying tumorigenic potentials both in vitro and upon transplantation, with mutated fallopian tube organoids demonstrating faster proliferation and a higher tendency to form malignant tumors. Organoids derived from tumors with different morphologies displayed distinct gene expression patterns and biological characteristics. Mesenchymal-like tumor organoids upregulated epithelial-mesenchymal transition-related genes, while epithelial-like tumor organoids more closely resembled the original clones. Gene expression analysis revealed that fallopian tube tumor organoids partially reflect the molecular subtypes of human HGSOC, such as differentiated and immune-responsive types, thereby simulating the molecular heterogeneity of HGSOC. These findings suggest that this organoid model provides a valuable experimental platform for studying the pathogenesis and treatment of HGSOC [ ].
Organoids derived from both fresh and frozen tissues showed no significant differences in morphology, expandability, endometrial marker gene expression, or hormonal responsiveness, providing a valuable tool for studying diseases such as infertility [ ]. Kim et al. reported a novel scaffold-free endometrial organoid containing both epithelial and stromal cells to investigate the effects of androgen excess related to polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) on the endometrium. These organoids express estrogen, androgen, and progesterone receptors (PGRs) and exhibit proliferative activity, forming distinct structures under normal hormone levels with epithelial cells on the outer layer and stromal cells on the inner layer. Without hormonal stimulation, the structure becomes disorganized. Under high androgen levels, the organoids show increased proliferation and gene expression dysregulation, suggesting that elevated androgen levels may contribute to increased EC risk in PCOS patients [ ].

## Biomaterials-enhanced organoids
Additionally, biomaterials can enhance the functionality of organoids. The generation of organoids has revolutionized biomedical research by providing sophisticated in vitro models that mimic the structure and function of human organs (Fig.  ). The success of organoids technology relies heavily on advanced culture techniques and bioengineering innovations [ , ]. organoids culture techniques typically begin with the isolation of stem cells, which can be either ASCs or PSCs, such as ESCs and iPSCs. These stem cells are then embedded in an extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffold, such as Matrigel, which provides a three-dimensional environment conducive to organoid formation and differentiation [ – ]. The culture medium is supplemented with specific growth factors and signaling molecules to guide the stem cells through self-organization into complex structures that resemble native tissues. Recent bioengineering advancements have significantly enhanced the complexity and functionality of organoids [ , ]. One such advancement is the development of synthetic hydrogels as ECM substitutes [ , ]. These hydrogels can be precisely tuned in terms of their biochemical and mechanical properties, allowing for better control over organoid development. Additionally, microfluidic devices, also known as organ-on-a-chip systems, have been integrated with organoid cultures to provide dynamic flow conditions and more closely replicate the in vivo environment. These devices enable the study of nutrient and waste exchange, as well as the interaction between organoids and circulating cells, such as immune cells. Another notable advancement is the application of CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology, which allows for the introduction of specific genetic modifications into stem cells before organoid formation [ – ]. This technique has been used to create disease models by introducing mutations associated with various genetic disorders, providing valuable insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing and high-resolution imaging techniques have provided deeper insights into the cellular composition and spatial organization of organoids, enabling researchers to better understand their development and function. In conclusion, the generation of organoids has been greatly advanced by sophisticated culture techniques and bioengineering innovations. These advancements not only enhance the fidelity of organoid models but also expand their applications in disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative medicine, heralding a new era in biomedical research. Chen et al. developed multi-lineage endometrial organoids (MLEOs) by co-culturing endometrial epithelial organoid units with endometrial mesenchymal stem cells (eMSCs) and subsequently seeding them onto a human acellular amniotic membrane (HAAM) scaffold to create a biological graft patch (MLEO-HAAM). In a rat model of endometrial injury, the MLEO-HAAM patch promoted endometrial angiogenesis and regeneration and improved pregnancy outcomes [ ]. Vriens et al. identified that the mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1 is expressed in primary human and mouse EECs as well as in EMOs. They demonstrated that PIEZO1 activation, through mechanical and chemical stimuli such as the PIEZO1 agonist Yoda1, increases calcium ion concentrations in EECs and EMOs, highlighting the functional role of PIEZO1 in these cells. Endometrial organoids provide an in vitro model to study embryo-endometrial interactions and investigate the role of PIEZO1 in embryo implantation. Additionally, EECs also express other mechanosensitive ion channels, such as TRP channels, which may play a role in regulating endometrial physiological functions [ ].
 

## Gynecologic tumor tissue-derived organoids
Organoids offer superior preservation of genetic heterogeneity and morphological characteristics of primary tumors compared to traditional cell lines, thus more accurately reflecting the features of the original tumor. Consequently, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) are constructed from primary tumor tissues obtained directly from patients. PDOs enable a more profound understanding of the mechanisms underlying gynecological tumors and serve as valuable platforms for preclinical DS and personalized medication evaluation. These models provide a closer approximation to clinical conditions, enhancing their utility in both research and therapeutic development (Table  ).
 

## CC
Clevers et al. utilized Pap smear-derived cervical tumor tissues to establish CC organoids, which were successfully transplanted into immunocompromised mice. These organoids not only formed tumors but also accurately replicated the characteristics of the primary cancers, demonstrating that the organoid model preserves the tumorigenic potential of the original tumor. The study reported that these tumor organoids can be stably cultured for extended periods exceeding 1.5 years and exhibit cancer-related features, including chromosomal instability. Additionally, comparative analysis revealed that the transcriptome of these CC organoids underwent substantial alterations relative to organoids derived from normal cervical epithelial cells, notably including the upregulation of the viral infection marker p16INK4a [ ].

## EC
EC is categorized into type I (E2-dependent with a more favorable prognosis) and type II (E2-independent with a less favorable prognosis). Cheng et al. enhanced the EC organoid model by incorporating cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), demonstrating that CAFs significantly increased the proliferation of EC-derived spheroids and organoids [ ]. Beyaz et al. developed organoid models from patient surgical specimens of both normal endometrium and EC, successfully preserving the histological and genetic features of the tumors. Low-grade cancers formed large, hollow structures, while high-grade cancers were smaller and denser. These organoids can be cultured long-term while retaining their phenotypic characteristics [ ]. Furthermore, Yao et al. established a biobank comprising 67 PDOs of CC, which recapitulate the pathological and genetic diversity of the primary tumors. These PDOs can be co-cultured with tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes to simulate individual responses to passive T-cell therapies [ ].

## OC
OC is the most lethal gynecological malignancy, often associated with recurrence and drug resistance. Wennerberg et al. found that using M1/M2 culture media significantly improved the success rate of OC organoid cultivation. M1 macrophages are polarized using IFN-γ and LPS, which promote a pro-inflammatory, tumor-suppressive phenotype. M2 macrophages, on the other hand, are polarized using IL-4 and IL-13, driving them toward an immunosuppressive phenotype that supports tumor progression. The OC organoids were highly consistent with the original tumors in terms of histology and immunohistochemistry, exhibiting increased cell proliferation activity [ ]. Weiswald et al. reported that PDOs and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models outperform traditional 2D cultures in reproducing primary tumor characteristics and treatment responses, with PDOs and PDX models reflecting clinical drug resistance [ ]. The Hippo team enhanced the modified MBOC protocol, increasing the organoid cultivation success rate from 45 to 90% and preserving the original tumor characteristics. Some organoids could form xenograft tumors in immunocompromised mice and were used for drug response testing [ ]. Pan et al. established the iOVCAR-3-OSKM model, which demonstrates strong spheroid and tumor-forming abilities, as well as higher drug resistance and migratory potential. This model retains ovarian lineage differentiation capabilities and can develop into organoids mimicking poor prognosis features of OC, offering strategies for personalized treatment. In OC patients, co-expression of OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG is associated with poorer overall survival [ ]. As 3D dynamic tumor models, organoids can be cultured from patient ovarian tumor tissues, ascites, or pleural effusions, replicating tumor clonal heterogeneity, TME, and cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions. They match the primary tumors in morphology, cytology, immunohistochemistry, and genetics, making them more suitable for OC research than cell lines. A study presents a method for generating HGSOC organoids from patient samples with a success rate of up to 97%, suitable for long-term culture and drug sensitivity testing. However, attention must be paid to sample homogenization and avoiding bubble formation, with batch differences in matrix gel being a limitation [ ]. Kessler et al. established long-term stable HGSOC organoids from primary tumor deposits, finding that low Wnt and active BMP signaling are required for maintaining growth. Oviduct organoids with knocked-down p53, PTEN, and RB also required a low Wnt environment for growth, indicating that early regulation of the stem cell microenvironment is crucial for HGSOC development [ ]. The Brenton team created 15 HGSOC-derived PDO models, replicating patient mutation profiles and p53 status, covering mechanisms of chromosomal instability such as homologous recombination deficiency and chromosomal fragmentation. These models are used to study gene expression, copy number variations, and compound sensitivity, providing important tools for precision therapy of HGSOC [ ].

## Application of organoids in the study of molecular mechanisms of gynecologic cancers
The emergence of organoid technology has transformed cancer research, especially for gynecologic cancers like ovarian, endometrial, and cervical cancers. organoids, which are 3D culture systems derived from stem cells, mimic the structure and function of their original tissues. This advanced model provides a more relevant platform than traditional 2D cell cultures. For gynecologic cancers, organoids allow researchers to explore the complex molecular mechanisms behind tumor development, progression, and spread. They maintain the genetic and phenotypic diversity of the original tumors, making them ideal for studying cancer cell interactions with their microenvironment. This is essential for understanding the roles of various signaling pathways, gene mutations, and epigenetic changes in cancer. Additionally, organoid models enable high-throughput drug screening and personalized medicine. By creating patient-derived organoids, researchers can test different therapies' effectiveness and toxicity, leading to tailored treatment strategies. This personalized approach improves treatment outcomes by increasing efficacy and reducing side effects. ## EC
Zhao et al. discovered that SNORD14E is aberrantly overexpressed in EC tissues, correlating with reduced disease-free survival and relapse-free survival in patients, though it is not associated with specific molecular subtypes. In vitro experiments demonstrated that SNORD14E promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion of EC cells while inhibiting apoptosis. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting SNORD14E can suppress these malignant behaviors and inhibit tumor growth in nude mouse xenograft models. Furthermore, ASOs also inhibit proliferation in PDO models from high-risk EC patients. Mechanistic studies reveal that SNORD14E advances EC progression by promoting the expression of the malignant FOXM1 subtype and inducing β-catenin nuclear accumulation [ ].

## OC
Sun et al. found that FBN1 is significantly upregulated in cisplatin-resistant OC organoids and cell lines. Knockout of FBN1 reduces glycolysis, impairs angiogenesis, and increases sensitivity to cisplatin. Mechanistically, FBN1 activates the FAK/AKT pathway through phosphorylation of VEGFR2, which induces phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of STAT2, thereby altering the expression of related genes [ ]. Wang et al. investigated the role of Aurora-A in chemotherapy resistance in OC and found that its expression is higher in resistant PDOs compared to sensitive PDOs. Overexpression of Aurora-A reduces cisplatin sensitivity, whereas inhibition of Aurora-A increases sensitivity and apoptosis. Aurora-A promotes resistance by phosphorylating SOX8 to activate FOXK1, affecting cellular aging and glycolysis genes [ ]. The study also observed that Akt-activated endothelial cells co-cultured with ovarian cancer cells form resistant vascular spheroids, similar structures were found in patient ascites. FGF2, Pentraxin 3, PD-ECGF, and TIMP-1 are involved, and the Notch3/Jagged1 pathway participates in ovarian cancer cells -EC interactions, influencing ovarian cancer cells proliferation and peritoneal invasion [ ]. Kang et al. explored the effects of TP53 and RAD51D gene knockdown on early lesions in FTECs using a human-derived fallopian tube organoid model. Knockdown of TP53 reversed the adverse effects of RAD51D knockdown on organoid proliferation, stemness, cellular aging, and apoptosis, alleviating G0/G1 phase arrest and promoting DNA replication. Dual knockdown of TP53 and RAD51D downregulates ciliary assembly and motility but upregulates pathways associated with high-grade serous OC, including the IL-17 signaling pathway, making organoids more sensitive to platinum-based drugs, PARP inhibitors, and cell cycle-related drugs [ ]. Overexpression of circRAD23B in organoids promotes OC cell proliferation and chemotherapy resistance, while knockdown of circRAD23B inhibits these phenotypes, offering a valuable preclinical model [ ]. Organoid models with tumor cells mixed with matrix gel reflect patient tumor heterogeneity and aid in studying individual responses to chemotherapy. The study found that miR-3622b-5p induces apoptosis and inhibits migration by targeting Bcl-xL and EGFR. The combined use of EGFR inhibitors and BH3 mimetics significantly reduces the viability of OC cell lines and patient-derived organoids, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy [ ]. Sun et al. assessed the sensitivity of three PDOs to cisplatin treatment through ATP activity assays and found that SGK1 gene expression was high in the cisplatin-resistant group, while VEGFA gene expression was high in the cisplatin-sensitive group, consistent with TCGA database analysis. The PPAR nuclear receptor family (including PPARα, PPARβ/δ, and PPARγ) plays a crucial role in energy metabolism, cell dynamics, inflammation, and tumorigenesis. Activation of the PPAR pathway is associated with poor prognosis in OC patients, inhibiting apoptosis through the Bcl-2/Caspase3 pathway and promoting tumor progression. Additionally, the PPAR pathway regulates the release of inflammatory factors, creating a microenvironment conducive to tumor development [ ].

## Application of organoids in the screening of gynecologic tumor drugs
Organoids have emerged as a transformative tool in the screening of drugs for gynecologic tumors. These three-dimensional cell culture systems closely mimic the architecture and functionality of human tissues, providing a more accurate model for drug testing compared to traditional cell lines. By using patient-derived organoids from ovarian, endometrial, and cervical cancers, researchers can evaluate the efficacy and toxicity of potential therapeutic agents in a personalized manner. This approach enhances the prediction of clinical responses, facilitates the identification of effective treatments, and reduces the risk of adverse effects, ultimately improving patient outcomes in gynecologic oncology. ## Ovarian marginal tumors
Borderline ovarian tumors represent approximately 15% of all ovarian tumors. Cheng et al. developed 13 PDO models from BOTs, which can be stably cultured in vitro for over three months while accurately replicating the genetic and morphological characteristics of the primary tumors. Bractoppin, a BRCA1 C-terminal domain inhibitor, effectively suppresses the growth of BOT-derived PDOs and induces apoptosis. Its mechanism of action includes the inhibition of cell cycle progression, impairment of DNA repair processes, and promotion of apoptosis. Furthermore, Bractoppin exhibits anti-tumor activity in OC cell lines by inhibiting homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining repair pathways. This study underscores the utility of BOT-derived PDOs in assessing targeted therapies and validates Bractoppin's potential as a therapeutic agent [ ].

## Uterine sarcoma
The Dahl research team established PDO models from tumor samples of a patient with uterine carcinosarcoma and performed comprehensive phenotypic analyses. The PDOs expressed both epithelial and mesenchymal markers, such as E-cadherin and N-cadherin, and displayed a range of morphologies, including columnar and squamous epithelium. The study evaluated the effects of six single-agent treatments and nine combination therapies, finding that gemcitabine, both as a monotherapy and in combination with carboplatin or paclitaxel, significantly induced apoptosis, surpassing the efficacy of the carboplatin + paclitaxel regimen [ ]. Hippo et al. established three PDOs from late-stage uterine carcinosarcoma patients, sourced from surgical tumor resection, peritoneal lavage fluid, and endometrial curettage. The three PDOs exhibited variations in morphology, proliferation rates, and genetic characteristics, reflecting tumor heterogeneity, and showed differing sensitivities to HER2 inhibitors and standard cytotoxic drugs. These variations were consistent with the heterogeneous expression of HER2 within the tumors. Multiple PDOs more accurately represent spatial tumor heterogeneity, offering valuable resources for uterine carcinosarcoma research. However, the sensitivity of PDOs to drugs may not always align with in vivo treatment responses, potentially due to non-cell-autonomous factors influencing in vivo conditions [ ].

## CC
Cervical clear cell carcinoma (cCCC) is a rare subtype of CC, and its pathogenesis remains largely unexplored due to the scarcity of cell lines and preclinical models. Researchers established cell lines from biopsy samples of cCCC patients and developed organoids that were cultured for over six months. Genomic analysis of these organoids provided new insights, revealing their sensitivity to major chemotherapeutic agents and MET inhibitors, thereby indicating their potential as novel preclinical models for studying cCCC [ ]. Nam et al. generated organoid models from four patients with various types of CC, accurately replicating the primary features of the original tumors, such as DNA copy number and mutation profiles. They identified KMT2C gene mutations across all types of CC PDOs, which may represent potential therapeutic targets. Screening of 171 FDA-approved drugs identified seven that had growth-inhibitory effects on CC organoids. The study observed differential responses of CC PDOs to drugs and radiation therapy; specifically, adenocarcinoma and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma were resistant to radiation, whereas squamous cell carcinoma and papillary adenocarcinoma exhibited greater sensitivity [ ]. Another study employed PDOs derived from surgical specimens of three CC patients to assess the antitumor effects of Trabectedin alone and in combination with the β-blocker Propranolol. Trabectedin inhibited the proliferation of CC cell lines and patient-derived CC tumor organoids by inducing DNA double-strand breaks and S-phase cell cycle arrest. Propranolol augmented the efficacy of Trabectedin through mechanisms involving mitochondrial involvement, ERK1/2 activation, and increased COX-2 expression, thereby counteracting Trabectedin resistance caused by β-adrenergic receptor activation [ ]. Liao et al. developed a long-term 3D organ culture system, establishing HSIL and SqCa organoid libraries. These models preserved the genomic, transcriptomic, and HPV characteristics of the original tissues and demonstrated variable responses to chemotherapeutic agents and mouse xenograft assays. Co-culturing the organoids with immune cells stimulated by HPV antigens elicited virus-specific T cell responses, supporting their utility in HPV vaccine screening [ ].

## EC
Researchers have developed a primary mouse model of EC driven by mutations in the Trp53, Pten, and Pik3r1 genes and subsequently cultured EC organoids. Screening of a small molecule compound library targeting epigenetic factors revealed that MI-136, an inhibitor of the Menin-MLL complex, significantly inhibited organoid growth, potentially through modulation of the HIF pathway, thereby proposing MI-136 as a novel therapeutic strategy for EC [ ]. Pollock et al. established 16 PDX organoids from various EC subtypes, demonstrating the morphological and molecular characteristics of the primary tumors. PDX organoids with high or medium FGFR2 expression were significantly sensitive to the FGFR inhibitor BGJ398. In vivo experiments showed that PDXs with high or medium FGFR2 expression exhibited substantial tumor growth inhibition and prolonged survival when treated with FGFR inhibitors. In PDX models with abnormal p53, Pemigatinib in combination with cisplatin demonstrated notable efficacy [ ]. Smith et al. created PDOs from tissues of 15 EC patients, including both primary EC and lymph node metastasis (LNM) PDOs. They found that the STAT3 inhibitor BBI608 strongly inhibited PDO growth, emphasizing the importance of stem cell characteristics. Additionally, various tyrosine kinase inhibitors were effective in inhibiting the growth of certain PDOs, highlighting variability in growth factor signaling pathways among PDOs. Notably, four PDOs were sensitive to anti-E2 drugs, underscoring the significance of E2 receptor signaling [ ]. Researchers also established 53 Fukushima organoids from different tumor tissues and conducted detailed analyses on three EC-derived Fukushima organoids (REME9, 11, and 16). These Fukushima organoids were capable of long-term culture, preserving morphological and gene expression characteristics similar to the original tumor tissues. Transplantation of these three Fukushima organoids into mice confirmed their tumorigenic capabilities, though with varying growth rates. High-throughput screening using REME9 and 11 assessed the efficacy of various antitumor drugs, revealing notable resistance to certain drugs such as paclitaxel and carboplatin. This study provides valuable research tools for personalized tumor treatment [ ].

## OC
Leslie et al. established organoid models from OC cells isolated from the ascites of patients with advanced ovarian sarcoma and identified a rare N131 deletion mutation in the tumor suppressor gene TP53. The cultured organoid models exhibited overexpression of the p53 protein, and computational modeling suggested that this residue is crucial for maintaining protein conformation. Drug screening revealed that a combination of protease inhibitors and histone deacetylase inhibitors was particularly effective against these organoid models, highlighting the potential of PDOs in cancer modeling and therapeutic evaluation [ ]. Researchers created OC organoid models from HGSOC patient tissues and tested various culture medium components. The addition of neuregulin-1 significantly increased both the number and expandability of organoids, which exhibited morphologies and characteristics akin to the patient's tumors, replicating markers and mutation profiles of the primary tumors and showing tumor-specific sensitivity to clinical chemotherapy drugs [ ]. Smith et al. utilized multicellular spheres from malignant effusions of HGSOC patients to construct short-term organoid models. RNA sequencing analysis revealed significant upregulation of genes related to proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and KRAS signaling pathways within six days of culture, identifying several potential therapeutic drugs for HGSOC [ ]. Han et al. cultured OC-derived PDOs and treated them with the PARP inhibitor olaparib, which significantly inhibited organoid growth and reduced the proportion of Ki67-positive cells. However, some cells survived at high PARP inhibitor concentrations, indicating resistance. Gene expression analysis provided insights into the mechanisms of PARP inhibitor resistance, affecting several key genes, including some previously unreported ones [ ]. A quantitative study using γH2AX markers investigated the response of four OC organoid models to etoposide, finding that one organoid model was unresponsive, confirming its resistance. This imaging and analysis method can be applied to other 3D organoid and spheroid models for high-throughput screening, offering new tools for TT research [ ]. Stelloo et al. evaluated the use of OC PDOs as preclinical models by constructing 36 PDOs from 23 patients and conducting whole-genome sequencing. Results demonstrated that PDOs retained the genomic characteristics of the original tumors. Drug responses of PDOs were significantly correlated with clinical responses, with PDO responses to carboplatin and paclitaxel aligning with patients' clinical response scores, CA-125 levels, and RECIST criteria. PDOs exhibited inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity in drug responses, with most showing poor responses to carboplatin and paclitaxel but better responses to gemcitabine; LGSOC-derived PDOs often exhibited resistance to carboplatin and paclitaxel [ ]. In another study, researchers successfully established OC organoids from HGSOC, cCCC, and EC with an 80% success rate and a culture period of within three weeks. organoids retained the genomic features of the primary tumors, with 59.5% of gene mutations consistent with those in the primary tumors, and replicated copy number variations. HGSOC organoids with pathogenic BRCA1 mutations were more sensitive to PARP inhibitors and platinum-based drugs, whereas cCCC organoids showed resistance to conventional drugs [ ]. Kolesar et al. established six HGSOC organoids from debulking surgery tissues, finding that UK1254 was resistant to carboplatin. Gene expression analysis indicated an association with the NF-κB signaling pathway, cell differentiation, and B cell receptor signaling and PI3K-Akt pathways [ ]. Rizzolio et al. developed a passive microfluidic platform for culturing 3D cancer organoids from HGSOC patients. This platform improved organoid growth, reduced cell death, and enhanced drug penetration and efficacy. Testing standard chemotherapy drugs (carboplatin, paclitaxel, doxorubicin) and targeted drugs on the platform showed lower IC50 values, demonstrating its potential for simulating treatment and personalizing therapy [ ]. Several cases have highlighted the significant responses of serous OC patients to targeted therapies. One patient with low-grade serous OC responded exceptionally well to ibrutinib despite previous ineffective standard chemotherapy and two surgeries, with drug sensitivity testing showing high efficacy of ibrutinib and resulting in notable clinical improvement with monotherapy [ ]. Another 59-year-old female patient with recurrent HGSOC relapsed after surgery and chemotherapy; tumor models established from ascites demonstrated the effectiveness of topotecan and niraparib, with topotecan treatment significantly lowering CA-125 levels and alleviating symptoms [ ]. Additionally, a stage IV low-grade serous OC patient, after failing multiple lines of treatment, responded effectively to a combination of fulvestrant and everolimus through in vitro drug sensitivity testing, stabilizing the disease for seven months [ ]. These cases underscore the potential value of in vitro drug sensitivity testing for personalized treatment. Sette et al. confirmed the strong anticancer activity of CDK12/13 inhibitors using HGSOC cell lines and PDO models. High-throughput RNA sequencing revealed that CDK12/13 inhibition induced extensive transcriptome reprogramming, including intron retention and premature transcriptional termination, affecting DNA repair genes, EGFR, and mTORC1 regulatory proteins. Combining CDK12/13 inhibitors with EGFR inhibitors, mTORC1 inhibitors, paclitaxel, PARP inhibitors, and ATR/CHK1 inhibitors significantly enhanced sensitivity in HGSOC cells and PDOs, showing clinical relevance. CDK12/13 inhibition can improve the efficacy of existing drugs, with PDO models providing an important platform for personalized medicine [ ].

## Breast cancer (BC) organoid models and comparison with GCs
Organoids derived from BC tissues have become an essential tool for cancer research due to their ability to preserve the architectural and molecular characteristics of the original tumors. Similar to GC organoids, BC organoids are used to study tumor heterogeneity, genetic mutations, and drug responses, thereby providing insights into the biology of breast tumors that are difficult to capture in conventional 2D cell cultures. Recent studies have shown that BC organoids closely recapitulate the histological features of the original tumors, including receptor expression (e.g., estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2), which is crucial for understanding tumor biology and guiding therapeutic decisions in clinical practice [ ]. Moreover, organoid models are increasingly being employed in the development of precision medicine for BC, as they allow for high-throughput drug screening and the testing of personalized treatment regimens. A key advantage of BC organoids, similar to those from gynecological cancers, is their ability to maintain the genomic and transcriptomic features of the primary tumor, making them ideal for the testing of novel drugs and identifying potential biomarkers for targeted therapies [ ].
However, it is important to note several critical differences between BC and GC organoids. While both organoid types generally maintain tumor-specific genetic mutations and molecular signatures, the TME plays a more prominent role in BC research, particularly in the context of stromal interactions and immune microenvironment. BC organoids have been frequently co-cultured with fibroblasts, immune cells, and endothelial cells to study the influence of these components on tumor progression and drug resistance [ ]. In contrast, GC organoids are typically studied in isolation, with fewer studies incorporating the TME components, though this trend is starting to change as co-culture techniques become more widely adopted. In terms of drug screening, BC organoids have been shown to be particularly effective in evaluating responses to chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapies such as PARP inhibitors and CDK4/6 inhibitors, which have emerged as promising treatments for various BC subtypes [ ]. This is similar to the use of GC organoids, where drug screening has also played a significant role in identifying novel therapeutic options for cancers such as OC and EC.
Overall, while BC organoids offer significant potential for personalized medicine and drug development, they also highlight some key differences in TME composition and stromal interactions when compared to GC organoids. This comparison underlines the versatility of organoid models across different cancer types and their potential to revolutionize cancer research and treatment strategies.

## Discussion and prospects
Organoids technology is rapidly advancing, with future priorities centered on tumor model establishment, personalized medicine and new drug development [ ]. Organoids simulate the structure and cell interactions of tumor tissues within three-dimensional culture systems, offering significant advantages over traditional animal models and two-dimensional cancer cell lines. This approach facilitates a better understanding of tumor mechanisms and provides more accurate assessments of drug efficacy and safety [ – ]. Organoids have emerged as transformative tools in biology and biomedical sciences, offering unprecedented opportunities to study human physiology and disease (Fig.  ). These three-dimensional cell culture systems closely mimic the architecture and function of human organs, making them invaluable for a wide range of applications across various scientific disciplines. Besides, traditional organoid models predominantly replicate the properties of tumor cells, and the tumor microenvironment is largely absent. The influence of the TME is typically studied through co-culture experiments rather than relying solely on organoid cultures themselves [ ]. While organoids are valuable for studying tumor cell biology, they do not fully recreate the complex cellular interactions present in the native tumor environment. For example, without the presence of immune cells or stromal cells, organoids cannot accurately mimic the regulatory effects of the TME on tumor cell behavior. To overcome this limitation, an increasing number of studies have utilized co-culture systems, combining organoids with various cell types, such as immune cells or fibroblasts, to more fully reconstruct the tumor microenvironment and study its impact on cancer progression [ ]. These co-culture models not only enhance the clinical relevance of organoid research but also provide a valuable platform for studying cancer immunotherapy.
 In GC research, organoids derived from patient tumors—often referred to as tumor organoids—have proven invaluable for studying tumor biology and testing therapeutic interventions. Unlike conventional 2D models, tumor organoids preserve the complex genetic and phenotypic characteristics of the original tumors, which is crucial for understanding tumor heterogeneity, drug resistance, and metastasis. These models are increasingly used in the development of personalized medicine approaches, where different treatment regimens can be tested on patient-specific organoid models to identify the most effective therapies [ ]. This ability to accurately mirror in vivo tumor biology helps researchers better predict treatment outcomes and side effects, providing a more reliable platform for drug testing compared to traditional cancer models. For example, recent studies have utilized EC organoids to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor growth and metastasis. These models have enabled the identification of novel therapeutic targets and the testing of targeted therapies, contributing to personalized treatment plans [ ]. Additionally, in OC, organoid models derived from patient tumors have been instrumental in exploring the mechanisms of drug resistance, offering new avenues for overcoming therapeutic challenges in treatment-resistant cancers [ ].
Organoids have shown great promise in drug screening, particularly in gynecological cancers. The three-dimensional structure of organoids better replicates the complexity of tumor tissues, including interactions between tumor cells, stromal cells, and the extracellular matrix. This improved mimicry of the tumor microenvironment enhances the predictive power of drug response assays, which is critical for evaluating the efficacy and safety of potential treatments [ ]. For instance, ECOs have been used to screen for compounds that target specific mutations found in patient tumors, helping identify the most promising drugs for individual patients [ ]. In OC, researchers have developed patient-specific organoid models to predict responses to chemotherapeutic agents, improving the accuracy of drug sensitivity testing. These models are not only important for evaluating existing drugs but also for discovering new therapeutic agents. The ability to test multiple drugs in a high-throughput fashion using patient-derived organoids allows for the identification of treatment regimens that could lead to better clinical outcomes with fewer side effects [ ].  In addition to the more commonly studied gynecological cancers such as ovarian, cervical, and endometrial cancers, vulvar and vaginal cancers are also significant contributors to the global burden of gynecological malignancies. However, unlike the better-characterized cancers, there is limited research on the development of organoid models for these types of tumors. This lack of detailed models hampers our ability to understand the molecular mechanisms driving vulvar and vaginal cancers, as well as to explore targeted treatment options for these rare and often aggressive cancers. One of the challenges in studying these cancers using organoid models is the relative scarcity of patient-derived tissues, which makes it difficult to establish comprehensive organoid biobanks. Further efforts are needed to develop organoid models for vulvar and vaginal cancers to better understand their unique biological characteristics and improve treatment options. This represents a notable gap in the current landscape of gynecological cancer research, and addressing this issue could significantly enhance the applicability of organoid technology for these underserved cancer types.
Despite their potential, the use of organoids in gynecological malignancy research faces several challenges. One key issue is the limited recapitulation of the full complexity of the tumor microenvironment in organoid models. Organoids typically lack a complete stromal component and immune cell interactions, which limits their capacity to fully mimic the immune response to treatment [ ]. While co-culture systems are being developed to address this gap, these models are still in the early stages of optimization. Additionally, organoids derived from extended passages may develop a monoclonal population of cells, potentially losing the heterogeneity present in the original tumors. This loss of diversity could affect the accuracy of drug sensitivity assays and reduce the reliability of these models for predicting treatment outcomes in patients [ ]. Standardization of culture protocols and drug testing procedures is needed to overcome this challenge and ensure the reproducibility of results across different research groups and clinical settings. Ethical concerns also arise with the use of organoids, particularly those derived from human tissues and stem cells. Issues related to the ownership of biological samples, the use of gene editing technologies, and the potential for exploitation of patient data must be carefully addressed through clear legal and ethical guidelines. These concerns are particularly pertinent in gynecological research, where the use of sensitive patient data and tissues for organoid generation requires strict oversight to ensure ethical compliance [ ].
Despite the significant advancements in the use of organoids for gynecological cancer research, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while organoid models replicate the genetic and phenotypic characteristics of primary tumors, they do not fully mimic the complexity of theTME, particularly the interactions with stromal components and immune cells. This limits their ability to capture the multifactorial nature of tumor progression and response to therapies, especially in the context of immunotherapy. Additionally, organoids often lack the full representation of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic processes, such as drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, which are essential for predicting drug efficacy and safety in vivo. The limited availability of patient-derived tissue, especially for rarer cancers like vulvar and vaginal cancers, further restricts the development of comprehensive organoid models for these malignancies. Furthermore, prolonged culture and passaging of organoids can lead to the loss of tumor heterogeneity, affecting their ability to represent diverse tumor subpopulations and influencing the accuracy of drug sensitivity assays. There are also challenges in maintaining the stability of genetic and phenotypic traits over time, which may impact the long-term relevance of organoid models. Lastly, the lack of an integrated immune system in organoid models hinders the study of immune-tumor interactions, which are crucial for the development of effective immunotherapies. Addressing these limitations through advanced co-culture systems, improved tissue procurement, and better standardization of protocols will be essential for enhancing the applicability and predictive power of organoids in gynecological cancer research.
Looking forward, organoid technology holds great promise for advancing research and treatment in gynecological malignancies. As these models continue to evolve, they will provide more accurate representations of the TME, offering unprecedented opportunities to study the molecular mechanisms driving cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis in GCs [ ]. Moreover, by identifying critical signaling pathways within these models, researchers can discover novel therapeutic targets that could lead to the development of targeted therapies specifically tailored to gynecological cancers. Personalized treatment strategies will be a key focus, with patient-specific organoid models providing insights into the most effective drug regimens. For example, by developing organoid models from ovarian cancer patients, researchers can screen multiple treatment options and identify regimens that are most likely to improve clinical outcomes and minimize adverse side effects [ ]. Additionally, organoid-based research will continue to improve our understanding of chemoresistance, particularly in cancers like endometrial and ovarian cancer, where resistance to standard therapies is a major challenge. Overall, while organoid technology has already proven to be a valuable tool in the study of gynecological malignancies, continued innovation in culture techniques, drug screening, and genetic engineering will further enhance its clinical utility. Overcoming current limitations and addressing ethical concerns will be essential for fully realizing the potential of organoids in personalized medicine and improving treatment outcomes for patients with gynecological cancers.

## Conclusion
GC organoids preserve the histological architecture, genetic profiles, and tumor heterogeneity of the original tumors, thus facilitating the development and application of personalized treatment strategies. In the context of understanding the pathogenesis of female reproductive system malignancies, as well as drug screening and efficacy prediction, organoid-based preclinical tumor models enable precise selection of therapeutic approaches. While organoid technology holds significant promise in biomedical research, further optimization of culture protocols is essential. This includes the incorporation of both tumor parenchymal cells and stromal components to more faithfully replicate the in vivo TME. Ultimately, organoid models are poised to establish comprehensive cancer modeling systems, providing an efficient platform for tumor prevention research, personalized treatment design, and patient survival prediction.
