## Abstract
Acute pain is a transient sensation that typically serves as part of the body's defense mechanism. However, in certain patients, acute pain can evolve into chronic pain, which persists for months or even longer. Neuroplasticity refers to the capacity for variation and adaptive alterations in the morphology and functionality of neurons and synapses, and it plays a significant role in the transmission and modulation of pain. In this paper, we explore the molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways underlying neuroplasticity during the transition of pain. We also examine the effects of neurotransmitters, inflammatory mediators, and central sensitization on neuroplasticity, as well as the potential of neuroplasticity as a therapeutic strategy for preventing chronic pain. The aims of this article is to clarify the role of neuroplasticity in the transformation from acute pain to chronic pain, with the hope of providing a novel theoretical basis for unraveling the pathogenesis of chronic pain and offering more effective strategies and approaches for its diagnosis and treatment.
Keywords: Synaptic plasticity, Pain transition, Neurotransmitter, Inflammatory mediators, Central sensitization

## Introduction
Pain is a complex physiological and psychological phenomenon that is typically defined as an unpleasant physical or mental experience caused by actual or potential tissue damage, accompanied by physiological and biochemical responses to tissue damage [ ]. Based on the duration and type of pain, it can be classified into different types. Acute pain is typically a part of physiological pain and is temporary, short-term pain that gradually diminishes or disappears as tissue damage heals. Chronic pain, on the other hand, is a condition that persists for an extended period, usually beyond 3 months, and often continues to cause discomfort and interfere with the patient's life [ ]. There is a close relationship between acute pain and chronic pain, with acute pain potentially serving as the starting point for chronic pain. Acute pain is usually a normal physiological reaction, representing the body's natural response to trauma or injury and facilitating recovery. However, if acute pain is not appropriately managed or if pain is extremely sensitive, it may gradually progress to chronic pain. The mechanism underlying this transformation involves alterations in several molecules and neurotransmitters, including neuroplasticity, inflammatory response, and neurotransmitter changes [ , , ]. Neuroplasticity plays a crucial role in the maintenance of chronic pain, leading to neurons gradually becoming overly sensitive to pain stimuli.
Neuroplasticity plays a key role in the maintenance of chronic pain, resulting in neurons gradually becoming over-sensitive to pain stimuli.
Neuroplasticity refers to the nervous system's ability to adapt to changes in the internal and external environment [ ]. During the process of pain transmission and processing, neuroplasticity can modify the connections between neurons, the efficiency of synaptic transmission, and the functional state of neural pathways, resulting in the intensification and persistence of pain. Acute pain enhances the synaptic connections and synaptic transmission of neurons, while chronic pain leads to the weakening or loss of these synaptic connections. These changes are closely related to neuroplasticity [ , ]. Comprehending the role of neuroplasticity in the transition from acute pain to chronic pain is crucial for understanding the pathogenesis of pain and developing strategies for the prevention and treatment of chronic pain.
Pain is a prevalent health issue that imposes a significant impact on an individual's quality of life, extending beyond the physical realm to the psychological and social aspects. The objective of this review is to elucidate the function of neuroplasticity in the transition from acute pain to chronic pain, along with the associated molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways. This understanding will aid us in better comprehending the onset and persistence of chronic pain. It offers a novel theoretical foundation for unveiling the pathogenesis of chronic pain and provides a fresh perspective and theoretical basis for devising individualized treatment strategies and preventing chronic pain in the future, thereby alleviating the suffering of pain patients.

## Neuroplasticity and Its Regulatory Factors

## Basic principles of synaptic plasticity
Synaptic plasticity refers to the modifiable alterations in synaptic morphology, structure, and number of synapses that result from actual or potential tissue damage and other stimulus information, leading to varying degrees of enhancement or attenuation of synaptic transmission efficiency [ , ]. These changes include both structural and functional synaptic plasticity [ ]. Within the nervous system, synapses are the connections between neurons through which neural signals are transmitted. The principal forms of synaptic plasticity encompass long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) [ , ]. The regulation of synaptic plasticity is intimately linked to the signaling pathways of the pre-synaptic and post-synaptic membranes, calcium ion signals, and neurotransmitters. The modulation of these mechanisms may have significant effects on the transmission of neural signals and synaptic plasticity. LTP is a sustained increase in synaptic transmission efficiency induced by short-term high-frequency tetanic stimulation.
LTP is widely present in the central nervous system and was initially discovered in the hippocampus, which is considered the neural basis for learning and memory [ , ]. In addition to the hippocampus, the majority of current studies on spinal cord LTP focus on spinal dorsal horn sensory neurons. LTP of the spinal dorsal horn is regarded as a form of “pain memory,” which serves as the neurological basis for chronic pain, pain memory, and hyperalgesia [ , ]. High-frequency tetanic stimulation promotes the release of presynaptic membrane transmitters, causing postsynaptic membrane depolarization and eliminating the blocking effect of Mg2+ on the coupling pathway of NMDA receptors. This facilitates the opening of NMDA receptor channels activated by glutamate, and a large influx of Ca2+ occurs into the postsynaptic membrane. It further acts as a second messenger to activate calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase Ⅱ (Ca/CaMK Ⅱ) and CAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA), among others, thereby triggering a series of intracellular biochemical reactions and resulting in a persistent increase in synaptic efficacy [ , ]. These events lead to an augmented release of neurotransmitters within the synapse, thereby increasing the excitability of subsequent neurons. Additionally, LTP can also modulate neuronal excitability by increasing the number of postsynaptic membrane receptors and enhancing synaptic transmission function [ , ]. Apart from synaptic enhancement, studies have reported that long-term disinhibition may contribute to persistent pain caused by nerve damage [ ]. Injury triggers a postsynaptic decrease in the expression of KCC2, a potassium chloride transporter, accompanied by a subsequent significant increase in sensory excitability of the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. These alterations may occur in the later stages of chronic pain and play a crucial role in the enhancement of spinal excitability following injury. In LTD, when synapses are stimulated with low frequency or weak stimulation, the intracellular calcium ion concentration declines, and ca2+/CAM-dependent protein phosphatase calcineurin is activated at low ca2+/CaM concentrations. This initiates a series of signal transmission events [ ]. These events lead to a decreased release of neurotransmitters within the synapse, resulting in reduced excitability of subsequent neurons. Concurrently, LTD can also diminish the excitability of neurons by reducing the number of postsynaptic membrane receptors and inhibiting synaptic transmission function [ , ].
The modulators of synaptic plasticity include calcium ion signaling, cytokines, and neurotransmitters. Signaling pathways and calcium ion signals on the postsynaptic membrane can also participate in the regulation of synaptic plasticity, and Ca2+ plays a crucial role in the induction of LTP. Florian et al. discovered that the increase of intracellular Ca2+ is regulated by NMDA receptors and L-type voltage-dependent calcium channels (VDCC-L) [ ]. In addition to VDCC-L, By using specific VDCC antagonists, they determined that P-type voltage-dependent calcium channels (VDCC-P) and Q-type voltage-dependent calcium channels (VDCC-Q) play a significant role in the induction of LTP, while N-type voltage-dependent calcium channels (VDCC-N) are involved in the maintenance of LTP, different subtypes of VDCC have distinct roles in pain afferent [ , ]. High-voltage activated calcium channels (HVACCs) have also been shown to play a role in pain transmission. HVACCs are composed of α1, β(Cavβ), and α2δ subunits, with Cavβ playing an important role in membrane expression and the formation of pores in the α1 subunit. Cavβ3 and Cavβ4 are the most commonly expressed subtypes. Some studies have found that by spinal nerve ligation, the expression of Cavβ3-related mRNA and protein increases in dorsal root ganglia, while the HVACC current of dorsal root neurons and excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSCs) induced by dorsal horn neurons of the spinal cord increase. However, the amplitude of HVACC currents in dorsal root ganglion neurons and the excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) in spinal dorsal horn neurons decreased after the injection of Cavβ3-specific siRNA, confirming that the pain input of spinal dorsal horn neurons is related to the upregulation of calcium channel subunit expression [ , ]. Berra-Romani et al. [ ] demonstrated that Ca2+ signals not only enter through plasma membrane channels but also affect synaptic plasticity through Ca2+ signals generated by intracellular organelles. The regulation of synaptic plasticity is also influenced by the action of neurotransmitters. Turovskaya et al. [ ] found that BDNF can induce BDNF-LTP in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord by activating extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase (ERK), P38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (P38MAPK), and nuclear factor-KB (NF-kB) signaling pathways. In addition, it has been discovered that tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) can increase the current of NMDA, and this effect is abolished in mice with TNF-α receptor 1 (TNFR1) gene knockout, while TNF-α receptor 2 (TNFR2) gene knockout still exists in mice, suggesting that TNF-α is also involved in NDMA mediated neuropathic pain. This influence may be exerted through TNFR [ ]. LTP in the spinal dorsal horn was associated with increased gene expression of glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GNDF) and interleukin1β (IL-1β) [ , ]. Neurotransmitters affect synaptic plasticity by altering synaptic transmission effects [ , ]. The effect of different neurotransmitters can enhance or weaken the transmission effect of synapses, thereby regulating synaptic plasticity.

## Changes in neurotransmitter and synaptic transmission
Neurotransmitters are a category of chemicals that convey signals within the nervous system, and they are capable of altering the efficiency and functionality of synaptic transmission, thereby influencing the conduction and intensification of pain signals. After spinal cord injury or the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, glial cells can be activated and central sensitization takes place [ , ]. Spinal microglia are the inherent immune cells of the CNS, playing a crucial role in the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain. After nerve injury, the activated microglia release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, resulting in the hypersensitivity reaction of dorsal horn neurons and the emergence of neuropathic pain [ ]. Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) is released by injured dorsal horn neurons. High concentrations of ATP can activate members of the ionotropic P2X7R–P2X purine receptor family, leading to microglia proliferation and neuropathic pain. P2X7R antagonists can retard neuropathic pain [ ]. Jorge et al. [ ] discovered that P2X3 receptors expressed on the dorsal horn of the spinal cord contribute to the transition from acute muscle pain to chronic muscle pain. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), synthesized by nociceptive receptor neurons, is a neurotrophic factor and synaptic modulator that is released into the spinal cord in an activity-dependent manner to induce central sensitization. BDNF can enhance the NMDA receptor-mediated C-fiber induction response after binding to its high-affinity TrkB receptor. Activation of the ERK and PKC signaling pathways in spinothalamic tract neurons, while generating inflammatory mediators in the spinal cord in response to strong externally-modulated harmful stimuli, acting through G-protein-coupled B2 receptors expressed in the dorsal ganglia, and activation of the PKC, PKA, and ERK signaling pathways. ERK is also activated by 5-HT through ionotropic 5-HT3 receptors and 5-HT7 GS-coupled receptors [ , ]. Neurotransmitters activate relevant pathways in SCI to induce neuropathic pain, transforming acute pain into chronic pain.
In the course of pain transition, a multiplicity of neurotransmitters are implicated in the processing and regulation of pain signals. Some excitatory neurotransmitters, such as glutamate, serve as neurotransmitters in the dmPFC/vlPAG neural pathway to act on neurons containing VGLUT2 in vlPAG. The potential mechanism underlying chronic pain might be related to mechanical hyperalgesia induced by the inactivation of glutamate [ ]. Changes in synaptic transmission occur during pain transition. By binding to specific receptors on the neurons, the excitability of the neurons and the efficiency of synaptic transmission are altered, thereby affecting the transmission of pain signals [ , , ]. Inhibitory neurotransmitters like gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) can typically suppress the transmission of pain signals. However, in chronic pain, the capacity of GABA may be weakened. This could be due to alterations in the synthesis or release of GABA, as well as the reduced sensitivity of GABA receptors [ ]. This imbalance leads to a diminution of inhibitory control mechanisms, permitting pain signals to persist and intensify. Additionally, certain inflammatory factors also exert a significant role in neuroplasticity and pain transition [ ]. Alvarez [ ] and Li [ ] et al. discovered that tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-1β play a regulatory role in neuroplasticity, and they affect the enhancement and conduction of pain signals by altering the excitability and synaptic transmission of neurons. Neurotransmitters play a crucial role in the process of pain transition by modifying the efficiency and function of synaptic transmission, thereby influencing the conduction and augmentation of pain signals.

## Inflammatory mediators in neuroplasticity
Inflammation assumes a significant role in acute pain. However, the inflammatory process can also promote the occurrence and transformation of neural plasticity, leading to the transformation from acute pain to chronic pain [ , ]. Inflammatory mediators encompass cytokines, chemical mediators, and other inflammatory regulatory molecules, which perform diverse functions in the nervous system and participate in the regulation of synaptic function through multiple aspects by influencing the plasticity of synapses, the alteration of synaptic structure and function, as well as the perception and response to pain in the central nervous system.
The release of inflammatory mediators can directly impact the plasticity of synapses. Numerous studies have confirmed that inflammatory mediators and cytokines such as interleukin-1β can mediate the loss of neuronal cells [ , ] and promote related molecular and synaptic plasticity [ , ], and affect synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability [ , ], which in turn reduces the binding of receptors existing on the surface of neurons to neurotransmitters. Immune cells like astrocytes are not only present in all regions of the brain, but these cells are located in close proximity to neuronal structures [ , ], ensuring direct communication between cells. Studies in mice by Andoh and Costa et al. [ , ] have demonstrated that inflammatory mediators can regulate the expression of synaptic plasticity-related proteins by facilitating the activation of microglia and the release of neurotrophic factors, thereby influencing the alterations in synaptic structure and function. In a pathological state, astrocytes release pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and chemokines like CCL2, CCL3, CCL5, and CCL8. When the levels of these molecules are overly high, they can all cause damage to neurons. In fact, receptors on the surface of microglia and glutamatergic neurons can also be interacted with by these factors, inducing a series of neuroinflammatory responses [ , ]. Experimental studies have shown that inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) can inhibit long-term potentiation (LTP) by influencing neurons and postsynaptic signaling pathways and altering the efficacy of synaptic connections [ , , , , , ]. Additionally, inflammatory mediators can also affect synaptic transmission pathways and increase the postsynaptic calcium concentration, thereby enhancing the plasticity of synaptic transmission [ ]. Inflammatory mediators further influence the occurrence of pain transition by regulating the structure and function of neuronal synapses.
The role of inflammatory mediators in the nervous system is not merely confined to altering the structure and function of neuronal synapses, but can also further modulate the perception and response of the central nervous system to pain. The inflammatory response can lead to an augmented sensitivity of neurons to pain information, namely central sensitization [ , ]. In chronic pain, activated microglia result in increased calcium flux, induce the translocation of NF-κB and activate the MAPK pathway, and promote the synthesis and secretion of various neuroinflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-18, IL-6, TNF-α, COX2, PE2, and so on [ , ]. On one hand, these inflammatory mediators further activate and release inflammatory factors through autocrine or paracrine by the surrounding glial cells, forming an inflammatory cascade. On the other hand, it can directly enhance excitatory synaptic transmission and/or weaken inhibitory synaptic efficacy, thereby altering the polarization characteristics of nociceptive afferent neurons, induce central sensitization, and give rise to chronic pain. Pain is frequently accompanied by tissue damage, infection, or autoimmune disorders. In such cases, immune cells respond by releasing inflammatory factors that can increase neuronal excitability by activating peripheral nerve terminal receptors, resulting in an enhanced perception of pain. TLRs are a category of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the cell surface that recognize pathogenic or injury-related molecular patterns (DAMPs, PAMPs) and sense the pathogenic or injury-related cells in the surrounding environment. In the CNS, microglia trigger immune responses through TLRs, and its family members TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4 play significant roles in NP [ ]. After spinal nerve ligation (SNL), the expression of TLR4 in microglia was elevated, accompanied by the formation of pain sensitivity. Studies have discovered that in neuropathic pain model mice HMGB1 may be released during the early stages of paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain. This molecule activates TLR4 receptors, leading to the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines that may contribute to neuropathic pain [ , ]. TLR2 and TLR3 have a similar mechanism of TLR4, TLR2 mediates microglial activation and the secretion of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 and contributes to central sensitization in chronic migraine model [ , , , ]. Inflammatory mediators can also regulate pain regulatory pathways in the central nervous system by activating NMDA receptors, AMPA receptors, and inflammation-related receptors, etc., to further enhance the transmission and persistence of pain [ , ]. By utilizing central nervous system regulatory pathways to bring about the transition from acute to chronic pain, new targeting mechanisms are being investigated and implemented to take advantage of these fundamental processes that are known to occur.

## Neuroplasticity in Pain Transition

## LTP, LTD in pain transition
Neuroplasticity refers to the ability to reshape cells and networks within the nervous system, which assumes a crucial role in physiological and pathological processes such as neurodevelopment, learning and memory, and pain perception. In acute pain, long-term potentiation (LTP) can strengthen the connection between neurons and raise the transmission efficiency of pain signals. It has been reported that spinal cord SGK-1 is implicated in post-inflammatory pain, and inhibiting the SGK-1 gene can reduce neuropathic pain hypersensitivity in mice [ , ]. Behavioral stress can give rise to SGK phosphorylation. SGK-1 in the spinal cord participates in central sensitization and enhances the nociceptive response of pain models [ , ]. Sai et al. [ ] discovered that after mild stimulation of the sciatic nerve, the phosphorylation of SGK-1 on spinal dorsal horn neurons increased. Inhibiting SGK-1 with GSK-650394 could alleviate persistent pain sensitivity. SGK-1 is involved in the regulation of a variety of transcription factors, including NF-κB. The activation of NF-κB in the spinal cord is not only associated with the synthesis and release of various pro-inflammatory cytokines but also closely related to the activation of spinal glial cells [ ]. Previous animals studies have indicated that tetanic stimulation of the sciatic nerve can induce persistent touch-induced pain in both hindlimbs of rats and an up-regulation of phosphorylated NF-κB expression in bilateral dorsal root ganglia, by inhibiting neuroinflammation and promoting nerve repair through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway [ , ], suggesting that the phosphorylation of SGK-1 on spinal dorsal horn neurons and astrocytes plays a significant role in the generation of chronic pain. Additionally, Zanos et al. [ ] found that blocking the activation of NMDA receptors and AMPA receptors and increasing the concentration of calcium ions after synapses can suppress LTP. NMDARs functions as a membrane calcium channel, and its activation leads to calcium ion influx, thereby triggering a signaling cascade. These alterations provide continuous impulses that are more susceptible to painful stimuli [ , , , , ]. This process can enhance the synaptic transmission effect, strengthening the connections between neurons and enabling pain signals to become more sensitive and long-lasting. These data suggest that LTP is involved in the development of pain via synapses.
When the duration of acute pain is protracted, LTD may exert an influence in the transition of the pain state. Adeline et al. [ ] discovered that the glycine mediated by the AMPA receptor subunit GluR2 on the membrane of glycinergic neurons might be one of the mechanisms for the opening of the “pain hypersensitivity gate” under neuropathic pain conditions caused by peripheral nerve injury. Glycine neurons and PKCγ neurons exhibit pathway-specific LTD, and the glutamate uptake in the postsynaptic membrane of glycinergic neurons could be the cause of the formation of LTD. The inhibitory pathway LTD diminishes the inhibitory function of glycinergic neurons, and then leads to the loss of control of PKCγ neurons by glycinergic neurons, and activates the excitatory pathway that is “silent” among pain pathways. Yang et al. [ ] demonstrated that when the concentration of neurotransmitters alters and synaptic connections are reorganized, LTD would be generated, converting long-term acute pain into chronic pain. Under chronic pain conditions, the postsynaptic activity may result in a reduction in the presynaptic synaptic binding force, thereby weakening the synaptic connections [ ]. This weakening might cause the signaling between neurons to become attenuated, reducing the perception of pain. Studies have found that inflammatory mediators can exert a regulatory impact on synaptic plasticity by influencing the postsynaptic signal transduction pathway, the presynaptic amino acid release, and the expression level of neurotransmitter receptors [ , ], which can further enhance or weaken the connections between neurons, thereby influencing the transmission of pain signals.
The two key mechanisms of neuroplasticity, LTP and LTD, play distinct roles in the process of pain transition. Through the regulation of synaptic connections, they affect the transmission efficiency of pain signals, resulting in LTP or LTD, which alters neuroplasticity and leads to the pain transition.

## Synaptic rearrangement and remodeling on pain persistence
In the process of pain transition, synaptic rearrangement and remodeling serves as an important mechanism and plays a crucial role in the persistence of pain. The synapse is a significant connection point for the transmission of signals between neurons. Synaptic rearrangement and reconstruction refer to the adjustment and restoration of synaptic morphology and function. In the state of acute pain, synaptic connections may be damaged or eliminated, resulting in alterations in the morphology and function of synapses, thereby changing the information transmission between neurons. Studies [ ] have indicated that repetitive or persistent painful stimuli can induce synaptic rearrangement. Such synaptic rearrangement can give rise to abnormal signaling between neurons, which in turn leads to the continuous transmission and intensification of pain signals, evolving into chronic pain. Nevertheless, Jensen et al. [ ] demonstrated that inhibiting pain transmission fails to prevent the synapse strengthening associated with chronic pain, and drugs that inhibit synapse strengthening do not have an impact on acute or physiological pain.
The rearrangement of the composition and structure of synaptic proteins is the fundamental mechanism for regulating synaptic plasticity. As postsynaptic organizers, PSD-95/Dlg proteins have been intensively studied as substrates mediating synaptic plasticity [ , ]. Kang et al. [ ] discovered that P-synaptic formation and synaptic transmission increased, while overexpression of PAR-1 had the opposite effect. PAR-1 kinase, a conserved regulator of cell polarity, plays a key role in controlling the postsynaptic localization of Dlg. Activation of PAR-1 causes chronic pain via suppressing spinal glial glutamate transporter function and enhancing glutamatergic synaptic activity. The loss of PAR-1 function is achieved by phosphorylating Dlg at Ser residues in the GUK domain, [ , ]. The preservation of this serine residue in all members of the MAGUK protein suggests that this phosphorylation event may represent a general mechanism by which MAGUK proteins are regulated. The PAR-1 family plays a significant role in synaptic development and function. Correa et al. [ ] indicate that chronic neural network overactivity induced by PiTx leads to synaptic rearrangement, which is conducive to inducing long-term synaptic enhancement. Hence, synaptic rearrangement is beneficial for the regulation of synaptic plasticity, thereby achieving the transition of pain.
Synaptic rearrangement and remodeling can also alter the expression and distribution of synaptic proteins at the synapses. Ma et al. [ ] discovered that inducing the over-expression of alpha-nuclide can suppress the function of presynaptic neurons; Xu et al. [ ] demonstrated that promoting the accumulation of dysfunctional synaptic vesicles and hippocampal synaptic toxicity, and the mutated Huntington protein can reduce presynaptic inhibitory synaptic vesicles, resulting in impaired inhibitory synaptic transmission. Changes in these synaptic proteins can give rise to abnormal synaptic transmission, which in turn leads to the sustained transmission and enhancement of pain signals. Additionally, inflammatory mediators also exert an important role in synaptic rearrangement and remodeling. Inflammatory mediators may affect synaptic rearrangement and remodeling by activating postsynaptic signaling pathways and regulating protein expression associated with synaptic plasticity, thereby altering the transmission and persistence of pain signaling [ , , ]. Through the synaptic plasticity mechanism, the morphology and function of synapses are modified, leading to the continuous transmission and enhancement of pain signals. Comprehending the mechanism and influencing factors of synaptic rearrangement and remodeling holds great significance for uncovering the pathogenesis of pain and developing corresponding therapeutic strategies, and further exploring the molecular mechanism and regulatory factors of synaptic rearrangement and remodeling and their interrelationship with other pain mechanisms, thereby providing more effective strategies for the treatment of chronic pain.

## Central sensitization and neuroplasticity
Central sensitization is a crucial process in the transformation from acute pain to chronic pain and has a close association with neuroplasticity [ ]. Central sensitization pertains to an overly excited state of the central nervous system. Central sensitization takes place when nociceptive stimuli enhance the capacity of nociceptive receptors in the central nervous system to respond to the normal subthreshold incoming information. The progression of chronic pain is influenced by the central sensitization process, which is also a significant characteristic of many chronic pain sufferers [ , , ]. Central sensitization-mediated pain is related to the activation of NMDA receptors, altered neuroplasticity, and the dysfunction of the descending regulatory system.
The primary substrate for central sensitization is the N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor that is activated by glutamate. Under typical circumstances, the NMDA receptor channels are blocked and activated by magnesium ions, and the blocking effect of magnesium ions will be removed by the membrane depolarization resulting from the introduction of nociceptive stimuli. The activation of NMDA receptors can enhance synaptic efficiency and cause calcium ion influx, thereby activating intracellular signaling pathways and ultimately initiating and maintaining central sensitization [ , , ]. In the disease state, the central and peripheral neural networks associated with pain exhibit significant plasticity. Neuroplasticity is a compensatory change in the function of adaptive neurons after nerve injury, which is crucial for maintaining normal physiological functions. Central sensitization can lead to alterations in membrane excitability, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal’ inhibition, and the neurotransmission efficiency of synapses is changed, which is manifested as LTP and LTD [ , , ]. It can also modify neuronal function, and neurons stimulated by long-term pain may undergo structural rearrangement of axons and dendrites, resulting in changes in synaptic connections [ , ]. During central sensitization, this structural change may alter normal synaptic transmission, further enhancing the transmission and perception of pain signals. Central sensitization can also create loops in pain delivery pathways that can give rise to pain. In addition to the damaged area, central sensitization will further cause the area around the damaged tissue to respond to harmless stimuli, thereby causing pain [ ]. In brief, central sensitization alters neuroplasticity through NMDA receptor activation, generating circuits, damaging tissues, and so on, thereby influencing the generation and transformation of pain.
The relationship between central sensitization and neuroplasticity is also associated with the descending regulatory system. The brain coordinates a pain-modulating system that can either promote or suppress nociceptive sensations from the periphery. Chronic pain may be linked to the dysfunction of the descending nociceptive modulation system, and the imbalance of the descending regulatory system may activate the descending promoting system to facilitate and maintain chronic pain. The dysfunction of the descending inhibitory system is a significant factor in determining whether pain progresses to chronic pain [ , ]. The antinociceptive effect of the descending regulatory system is mainly achieved through descending serotonin (5-HT) and norepinephrine projection fibers at the spinal cord level [ , ]. The central and peripheral nervous systems, particularly the regions involved in pain processing such as the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and thalamus, are characterized by high expression of α and β adrenergic receptors that mediate the action of norepinephrine. When nociceptive stimuli activate the endogenous descending regulatory system, 5-HT serves as the primary neurotransmitter in this system. It is released in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord to reduce pain levels [ , , , ]. Central sensitization may determine the transformation of pain via the descending regulatory system.
Furthermore, central sensitization is also intimately related to pain-related inflammatory mediators, which can activate glial cells and release a variety of neuroregulatory factors, further influencing the occurrence and transduction of synaptic plasticity, thereby impacting the persistence and aggravation of pain, neuroinflammation contributes to central sensitization in chronic models [ , , , ]. Additionally, studies have shown [ ] that the accumulation of lactic acid in the synaptic microenvironment also contributes to synaptic remodeling and central sensitization. The glucose metabolism of inflammatory cells in neuropathic pain is characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria and enhanced glycolysis, and these alterations induce the phenotypic transformation of inflammatory cells and promote neuroinflammation in the peripheral and central nervous systems. Central sensitization is closely tied to neuroplasticity. Further exploration of the relationship between central sensitization and neuroplasticity will assist in comprehending the pathogenesis of chronic pain and offer new targets and strategies for the treatment and prevention of chronic pain.

## The Regulatory Mechanism of Neuroplasticity in Pain Transformation

## Regulation of NMDA receptor, AMPA receptor and calcium ion signal
The NMDA receptor, an ion channel receptor, is a tetramer constituted by several homologous subunits of GluN1-, GluN2-, or GluN3-types. The composition of NMDA receptor subunits is stringently regulated during development and activity-related synaptic plasticity [ ]. In the central nervous system, the CXCL1/CXCR2 signaling boosts the current of NMDA receptors in neurons and promotes the expression of genes associated with neuroplasticity, giving rise to long-term chronic pain, spinal NF-κB-CXCL1/CXCR2 in rats with induced radiating pain [ , ]. The AMPA receptor is another category of ion channel receptor, which, in combination with the NMDA receptor, can further enhance the efficacy of synaptic transmission and thereby enhance the transmission of persistent pain signals [ ]. The calcium ion signal is a crucial link in the regulation of the NMDA receptor and the AMPA receptor [ , ]. CaMKⅡ is expressed in nociceptive neurons in the superficial layer of the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and the dorsal root ganglion, and is involved in the regulation of synaptic remodeling and pain information transmission, mediating synaptic genesis, dendritic axon formation, and cytoskeletal interaction [ ]. It has been reported in relevant literature that CaMKⅡ mutation can lead to a reduction in dendrite spine density and excitatory synaptic transmission to mediate inhibitory synaptic transmission and plasticity [ , ]. Gao et al. [ ] found that activation of the NMDA receptor-CaMKIIα signal in rACC is associated with pain in rats with NP. Additionally, opening voltage-gated Ca2+ channels near the resting membrane potential and activating NMDA receptors without depolarization or the release of intracellular stored Ca2+ can drive neurotransmitter release, and manipulating the resting neuronal Ca2+ signal results in significant steady-state synaptic plasticity [ ]. These studies suggest that calcium ion signals play a vital role in the normal development and transmission efficiency of excitatory synapses. Synapses are distributed throughout the dendrite spine of neurons, typically hundreds of microns away from the cell body. Upon neuronal activation, CaMKⅡ is induced to transfer from the cytoplasm to the active region of the presynaptic membrane and the postsynaptic membrane density (PSD), participating in the maintenance of synaptic plasticity [ , ]. Glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in spinal cord neurons, and CaMKⅡ in the presynaptic membrane can regulate the phosphorylation of different molecular targets to regulate the synthesis and release of the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate [ ]. When the body is subjected to injurious stimuli, glutamate is released from the presynaptic membrane of primary afferent sensory neurons and acts on ionic NMDA receptors and AMPA receptors in the postsynaptic membrane. The NMDA receptor is accountable for nearly all synaptic plasticity changes in the central nervous system [ , ]. The activation of the NMDA receptor leads to a substantial influx of Ca2+ into the cell, and Ca2+ binds to CaM, resulting in autophosphorylation at Thr286 of CaMKⅡ. After activation, CaMKⅡ migrates to the PSD, inducing long-term potentiation (LTP) of the postsynaptic membrane, LTP induction by structural rather than enzymatic functions of CaMKII [ , , , ]. If the self-phosphorylation of CaMKⅡ Thr286 is inhibited, synaptic LTP is severely damaged [ ]. When glutamate acts on the postsynaptic membrane AMPA receptor, Ca2+ influx and CaM binding promote the activation of CaMKⅡ, prompting the AMPA receptor subunit GluR1 to anchor to the postsynaptic membrane, increasing the amount of GluR1 on the postsynaptic membrane, enhancing the synaptic transmission efficiency, inducing synaptic remodeling [ ], resulting in changes in neuroplasticity and pain persistence. The regulation of the NMDA receptor, the AMPA receptor, and Ca2+ can lead to changes in neuroplasticity and pain persistence [ ], thereby transforming acute pain into chronic pain.

## Nerve growth factor and synaptic development factor
Nerve growth factor (NGF) is a type of protein that is capable of promoting the growth, development, and differentiation of nerve cells. It is primarily secreted by target organs and mediates the growth, survival, and functional regulation of nerve cells through binding with receptors on the surface of nerve cells [ ]. Nerve growth factor can not only facilitate axon regeneration and synaptic plasticity by binding to receptors, but also plays a role in pain perception through regulating the release of inflammatory agents [ ]. The inflammatory response is one of the crucial characteristics associated with acute pain, and the release of nerve growth factor can influence the synthesis and release of inflammatory mediators and participate in the regulation of neuronal activity. Baassiri et al. [ ] indicated that tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) can promote the synthesis and release of inflammatory mediators by regulating the release of nerve growth factor, thereby resulting in the persistence of pain sensation. The pain caused by incision ​+ ​formaldehyde in rats can be treated by inhibiting the SDF-1/CXCR4 pathway and generating pro-inflammatory agents [ ]. Additionally, Wang et al. [ ] pointed out that BDNF and tyrosine kinase receptor B (tyrosine kinase receptor B, TrkB) combine, activating the Ras protein (rat sarcoma, Ras) - mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, and activating cyclic adenosine monophosphate response element binding protein (cAMP response element binding protein, CREB) at a serine site. CREB can increase the expression of the BDNF gene and the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2, promoting the survival of nerve cells and enhancing synaptic plasticity. Nerve growth factor plays a significant role in promoting pain transition and neuroplasticity.
Synaptic growth factor is a sort of proteins that govern synaptic structure and function, encompassing the neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM) and synaptic adhesion molecules (SAM) [ , , ]. These factors can directly impact synaptic structure and neuronal connectivity, and modulate the efficiency and function of synaptic transmission. Baldwin et al. [ ] noted that the conditional deletion of HepaCAM from developing astrocytes would significantly impair the coupling of gap connections between astrocytes and disrupt the balance between synaptic excitation and inhibition. The imbalance of synaptic development factors might give rise to alterations in synaptic structure and synaptic transmission, thereby resulting in the persistence of pain sensation. Trans-synaptic cell adhesion molecules, such as neuronal adhesins (Nrx) and neuronal adhesins (Nlg), can directly regulate the assembly of presynaptic and postsynaptic molecules, including synaptic vesicles, active region proteins, and receptors. Li et al. [ ] indicated that the trans-synaptic adhesion mechanism mediated by the Nrx-Nlg interaction might lead to chronic pain and other neurological disorders, and the dysfunction of Nrx-Nlg causes changes in synaptic plasticity, leading to the persistence of pain. In conclusion, by regulating the expression and function of synaptic development factors, synaptic structure and neuronal connectivity can be affected, and synaptic plasticity can be enhanced, thereby regulating the persistence of pain.

## Glial cells in the regulation of neuroplasticity
Glial cells are a type of non-neuronal cells in the central nervous system, such as astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and microglia. Previously, these cells were considered to merely offer mechanical and nutritional support; however, recent research has demonstrated that glial cells can affects neuronal plasticity in the central nervous system [ , , ]. Glial cells are implicated in the process of neuroplasticity during the transition from acute pain to chronic pain through the release of neurotransmitters, immune regulation, and synaptic formation and repair.
Glial cells are implicated in the regulation of pain signaling through the release of neurotransmitters and receptors. Astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes actively facilitate intercellular communication by releasing a variety of neurotransmitters and the expression of their membrane receptors [ ]. In a pathological context, the swift response of glial cells significantly alters their activity and function. Verkhratsky et al. [ ] discovered that glial cells can enhance the sensitivity of neurons to pain stimuli. Microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes all express members of the P2X receptor (P2XRs) family that are selectively engaged by ATP [ ]. With the increase in extracellular ATP concentration under neuropathological conditions, rapid ionotropic P2X channels are activated to promote neuronal firing. Due to the activity of a metabolic enzyme, the ectonucleoside triphosphate diphosphate hydrolase 1 (CD39 or E-NTPDase1), which successively degrades ATP to ADP and AMP, and the ecto-5′-nucleotidase (CD73), which dephosphorylates AMP [ , ], ATP is rapidly hydrolyzed into its decomposition product adenosine within a matter of seconds. The role of adenosine in the regulation of pain signaling is well established. Neuroinflammation in the central nervous system (CNS) is characterized by the breakdown of the blood-brain barrier integrity, increased vascular permeability resulting from immune cell extravasation, and the activation of microglia and astrocytes. The latter cell type releases a variety of cytokines, chemokines, neuropeptides, and other substances (including ATP) that automatically amplify and sustain glial cell activation over time, while regulating synaptic plasticity and increasing excitatory neuronal firing, thereby driving the transition to pathologically persistent chronic pain [ , ]. The most widely studied purinergic receptor involved in pain transmission is the P2X4 subtype, which has been found to be both necessary and sufficient to promote the activation of spinal microglia. In response to nerve injury or inflammatory conditions, its recruitment triggers a series of biochemical events, leading to neuronal sensitization and ultimately to chronic pain, blocking Cx43 can alleviates neuropathic pain in rats with chronic constriction injury via the P2X4 [ , ]. Hence, glial cells release a variety of neurotransmitters, and the release of these neurotransmitters exerts an important regulatory role in the alteration of synaptic transmission between neurons and can directly or indirectly regulate pain signaling.
Inflammatory factors are also implicated in the regulation of synaptic plasticity. The inflammatory response activates glial cells, leading glial cells to generate and release a variety of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [ , , ], which regulate the conduction and processing of pain signals by interacting with neurons and other glial cells. Chen et al. [ ] demonstrated that peripheral monocytes mediate changes in some neurons and synapses in the motor cortex via NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent IL-1β production. IL-1β has been shown to induce synaptic alterations that directly impact how neurons perceive and conduct pain signals. Glial cells are also involved in the formation and restoration of synapses. Astrocytes can secrete various nerve growth factors such as neurotrophic factor and brain-derived neurotrophic factor. Lindholm et al. [ ] indicated that astrocytes contribute to the synaptic uptake and recycling of BDNF/proBDNF in synaptic plasticity. Albini et al. [ ] discovered that glial cell-derived neurotrophic factors (GDNF) promote the survival of neurons and regulate the plasticity of adult neurons during mammalian development, and these factors can facilitate the formation and repair of synapses. Additionally, astrocyte-secreted neuro can control inhibitory synapse formation and function to support the reconstruction of synapses [ , ]. Through a variety of mechanisms, including the release of neurotransmitters, immune regulation, and participation in synaptic formation and repair, glial cells govern the transmission and processing of pain signals, as well as synaptic plasticity.

## Treatment Strategies and Interventions

## Therapeutic strategies targeting neuroplasticity
Pain serves as a protective response of the human body; however, when pain transforms into a chronic state, it can significantly impact the patient's quality of life as well as their physical and mental well-being. Hence, finding effective treatment strategies has become an urgent necessity to alleviate chronic pain. Neuroplasticity is a crucial mechanism influencing the transition of pain, and its regulation represents a potential therapeutic approach. In recent years, therapeutic strategies targeting neuroplasticity have garnered extensive attention in clinical pain management.
A prevalent therapeutic strategy is to interfere with neuroplasticity through regulating the neurotransmitter system. Glutamate is the predominant excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system and is of great significance in the process of synaptic transmission and neuroplasticity [ , ]. A substantial amount of evidence has indicated that the neuroplasticity changes mediated by glutamate signaling pathways and glutamate receptors are important neurobiological mechanisms in the occurrence and progression of chronic pain [ ]. In the streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) rat model, the radiofrequency ablation (PRF) can alleviate the cold hyperalgesia and mechanical hyperalgesia in DPN rats. The release of glutamate from the afferent nerve endings of DRG neurons to spinal dorsal horn neurons in DPN rats increased, and the level of glutamate in the cerebrospinal fluid of DPN rats decreased after PRF treatment [ , ], suggesting that the analgesic mechanism of PRF may be related to inhibiting the release of glutamate and thereby enhancing the synaptic plasticity of the spinal cord. Additionally, by regulating the release of endorphins, gamma-aminobutyric acid or norepinephrine, the inhibition of central nervous system activation can also reduce pain perception [ , , ], which utilizes the neurotransmitter system to intervene in neuroplasticity and thereby alleviate pain.
Besides invasive therapy, non-invasive therapy is also among the treatment strategies that target neuroplasticity. Utilizing the CCI model, Hu et al. [ ] verified that repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) treatment can facilitate sciatic nerve repair in CCI rats and relieve the pain symptoms in CCI rats, and the higher the frequency, the more favorable the effect. The primary mechanism might be associated with reducing the expression levels of glutamate and aspartic acid receptors in the brain and regulating neuroinflammation. Nitric oxide (NO) is mainly generated by neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) in neurons and plays a crucial role in the formation of pain [ , ]. Bai et al. [ ] discovered that rTMS could alleviate neuropathic pain by down-regulating the expression of nNOS in the spinal dorsal root ganglion and inhibiting the activation and proliferation of astrocytes. In the temporomandibular joint disorder (temporomandibular joints disorder, TMD) model, De Freitas Rodrigues [ ] found that after laser irradiation on the rat temporomandibular joints, PBM can down-regulate the expression of pain-inducing substances (TRPV1, SP and CGRP) in the trigeminal ganglion. In the spinal cord injury (SCI) model, Janzadeh et al. [ ] revealed that laser irradiation of the injured site could relieve neuropathic pain, and the mechanism might be related to the decreased expression levels of IL-4, IL-10, IL-1α, P2X3 and P2Y4 in the spinal cord. The employment of rTMS therapy and phototherapy to modulate the neurotransmitter system has altered synaptic connections and neuroplasticity, thereby alleviating chronic pain [ , , ].
Furthermore, neuroplasticity can improve the motor function and self-care abilities of patients experiencing pain through rehabilitation, while also concurrently reducing their sensitivity to pain [ , ]. Individualized treatment strategies are also a crucial aspect of pain management based on neuroplasticity. Each patient varies in terms of pain perception, the cause and progression of the disease, thereby necessitating the development of an individualized treatment plan. By evaluating the patient's neuroplasticity status, such as long-term potentiation or sensitization, more targeted treatments can be selected to achieve better pain relief [ , ]. Through the development of drugs that target neuroplasticity, key processes like the neurotransmitter system, synaptic transmission, and neurodevelopment can be interfered with to regulate the transition to pain more effectively. Nevertheless, the current treatment strategies for neuroplasticity are still in the early stages, and numerous challenges remain, such as the optimization of drug selection and dosage, the timing and duration of treatment. Further research and optimization of treatment strategies are still requisite to achieve more effective pain management and to provide a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of chronic pain.

## Neuroplasticity as a potential target for translational medicine research in chronic pain
Neuroplasticity plays a pivotal role in transitioning from acute to chronic pain, making it a crucial focus of translational medicine research. This approach seeks to translate fundamental scientific discoveries into effective clinical strategies aimed at personalized treatment and chronic pain prevention. By understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying neuroplasticity, researchers can identify key therapeutic targets.
For instance, Fukata et al. [ ] have demonstrated that the Membrane-Associated Guanylate Kinase (MAGUK) protein family, which includes PSD-93, PSD-95, SAP-102, and SAP-97, has been shown to significantly impact NMDA receptor activity in spinal neurons. PSD-93 and PSD-95 are particularly essential for maintaining NMDA receptor function, and their absence has been linked to the alleviation of chronic pain following nerve injury and inflammation. These findings suggest that MAGUK proteins are potential targets for therapeutic intervention. Additionally, Bagdas et al. [ ] have noted that chlorogenic acid can reverse the hyperphosphorylation of AMPA receptor subunit GluA1, providing another avenue for pain management. Long-term treatments, such as tacrolimus (FK506) [ ], can lead to alterations in AMPA receptor activity, by inhibiting the activity of the calmodulin-dependent phosphatase (CaN), and eventually lead to pain. These findings suggest that targeting NMDA receptors and AMPA receptors can assist in blocking the transmission and persistence of pain signals, thereby alleviating pain symptoms in patients.
Other potential targets include nerve growth factor and synaptic adhesion factors, which are implicated in pain sensitization and synaptic transmission. Peach et al. discovered [ ] that subsequent to nerve injury or inflammation, local immune cells are activated and release a variety of cytokines to facilitate the generation of nerve growth factor, along with intracellular cascade reactions taking place, leading to hyperalgesia and hypersensitivity. In vertebrate brains, synaptic cell adhesion molecules (SAM) direct synaptic formation, specialization, and plasticity [ ]. The genetic deletion of the same SAMs from neurons typically has no impact on the number of synapses, but frequently significantly impairs synaptic transmission, suggesting that most SAMs govern the function and plasticity of synapses. Understanding how local immune cells release cytokines following nerve injury may help identify new treatment options. Synaptic adhesion molecules, crucial for synaptic specialization and transmission, could be leveraged to influence neuroplasticity and pain perception positively.
Research into neuroplasticity not only reveals mechanisms underpinning chronic pain development but also informs preventative strategies. By targeting the transition from acute to chronic pain through therapies such as physical rehabilitation，drug therapy or psychological support [ , , ], we can enhance pain management outcomes. These research findings will furnish clinicians and patients with more efficacious treatment alternatives, enhance patients' quality of life, and present new directions and challenges for the future development of pain research and translational medicine.

## Individualized treatment strategies for chronic pain prevention
The increasing awareness of neuroplasticity's role in pain transition underscores the importance of individualized treatment strategies for chronic pain prevention. Traditional approaches often fail to account for the unique factors affecting each patient's pain experience. Each patient's pain condition may be influenced by diverse factors, traditional treatments might not suffice to meet the patient's requirements. By understanding the neuroplastic mechanisms at play, clinicians can tailor treatment plans that address individual pain profiles more effectively.
An individualized approach prioritizes understanding a patient's medical history, clinical manifestations, and neuroplasticity biomarkers. For instance, patients with central sensitization may benefit from neuromodulating drugs or neurostimulation therapies [ , , ], while those experiencing inflammation may respond better to anti-inflammatory agents [ , ]. Recent studies [ , ] have demonstrated that alterations in brain functional connectivity patterns indicate the transformation of acute pain into chronic pain, implies that the development of chronic pain is closely associated with changes in brain functional connectivity. Moreover, advances in imaging techniques, like PET scans [ , ], enhance our ability to observe changes in brain connectivity and neurotransmitter systems associated with chronic pain. Such insights are invaluable for creating personalized analgesic protocols, particularly noting how central opioid receptors vary among pain types [ ]. Preventative strategies are equally critical. Early interventions during acute pain episodes can hinder the neuroplastic changes that lead to chronic pain. For example, electroacupuncture [ ]has demonstrated potential in reverting hyperalgesic thresholds, and timely use of analgesics post-surgery can mitigate adverse neuroplastic effects. Carley and Siracusa et al. [ , ] indicated that the early utilization of analgesic drugs and neuroprotectants after surgery could reduce the impact of neurotransmitters and inflammatory mediators on neuroplasticity, thereby lowering the prevalence of postoperative chronic pain. By expanding our understanding of neuroplasticity in pain transition, we can offer targeted and effective treatments, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and quality of life while alleviating the socio-economic burden of chronic pain.
However, challenges remain in individualized treatment strategies. Further exploration of the neuroplasticity-pain perception relationship is essential, alongside the development of precise biomarkers [ ]. Multi-disciplinary collaboration is necessary to integrate expertise from multiple fields, ensuring comprehensive patient care.
The purpose of this review is to delve into the role of neuroplasticity in the transition from acute pain to chronic pain. Through an in-depth comprehension of neuroplasticity, it is ascertained that neuroplasticity exerts a crucial role in pain perception. Acute pain and chronic pain are distinct types of pain, with acute pain typically emerging rapidly subsequent to trauma or pathological irritation, while chronic pain persists for a longer duration and is challenging to treat. Neuroplasticity refers to the adjustability of the connections and functions among neurons and synapses, which plays a significant role in the regulation of pain sensation. Neuroplasticity is regulated by a variety of factors, encompassing the fundamentals of synaptic plasticity, the role of neurotransmitters and alterations in synaptic transmission, as well as the involvement of inflammatory mediators. During the transition from acute pain to chronic pain, neuroplasticity has undergone a series of alterations, including the mechanism of long-term potentiation and long-term depression, the influence of synaptic rearrangement and remodeling on the persistence of pain, and the relationship between central sensitization and neuroplasticity. Additionally, studies on molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways have disclosed the regulation of NMDA receptors, AMPA receptors and calcium ion signals, the engagement of nerve growth factors and synaptic development factors, and the role of glial cells in the regulation of neuroplasticity. These investigations hold significant importance for deepening the understanding of the pathogenesis of chronic pain and developing corresponding treatment strategies. According to preceding studies, neuroplasticity can serve as a target for the transition from acute pain to chronic pain. By regulating the process of neuroplasticity, chronic pain can be effectively treated and the pain perception of patients can be mitigated. Furthermore, the study of neuroplasticity also provides an important potential target for translational medicine research, which can be utilized to develop more efficacious treatment strategies. The application of individualized treatment strategies and the prevention of chronic pain can also be devised based on the findings of neuroplasticity to achieve more favorable treatment outcomes.
In conclusion, this paper highlights that neuroplasticity assumes a crucial role in the transition from acute pain to chronic pain, and underlines the significance of neuroplasticity research for uncovering the pathogenesis of chronic pain and formulating treatment strategies. Nevertheless, in order to further probe the role of neuroplasticity in the pain transition, there are still certain challenges and unexplored areas. Future research ought to focus on further delving into the mechanism of action of molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways to identify more precise targets, and at the same time, more clinical studies are also requisite to apply neuroplasticity research in clinical practice to offer patients individualized treatment strategies and superior preventive measures.
