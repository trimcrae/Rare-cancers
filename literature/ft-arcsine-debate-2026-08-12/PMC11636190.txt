## ABSTRACT

## Background
High placebo responses have limited drug development in eosinophilic oesophagitis. The optimal configuration of trial outcomes is uncertain.

## Aims
To inform more efficient future trial designs, to characterise clinical, endoscopic and histologic placebo responses in eosinophilic oesophagitis randomised controlled trials (RCTs).

## Methods
We updated a Cochrane systematic review and meta‐analysis, searching multiple databases to January 1, 2024, to identify placebo‐controlled RCTs evaluating medical therapies for patients with eosinophilic oesophagitis. The primary outcome was the pooled proportion of study‐defined clinical, endoscopic and histologic responders and remitters randomised to placebo, using an intention‐to‐treat approach and random‐effects model. Sources of heterogeneity were explored using meta‐regression.

## Results
We included 25 RCTs. The pooled proportion of clinical response was 41.0% [95% CI: 29.7%–52.8%] with substantial heterogeneity (I
2 = 74.9%). On meta‐regression, older age and a higher probability of being randomised to placebo reduced the likelihood of clinical response to placebo. The pooled proportion of histologic remission defined as a peak eosinophil count [PEC] ≤ 6 eosinophils per high power field [HPF] or ≤ 1 eosinophil/HPF was 4.3% [95% CI: 2.6%–6.2%] (I
2 = 23.6%) and 1.3% [95% CI: 0.5%–2.5%] (I
2 = 0%), respectively. The standardised mean difference in the Eosinophilic Oesophagitis Endoscopic Reference Score to placebo was −0.25 [95% CI: −0.41, −0.10].

## Conclusions
Over 40% of patients in eosinophilic oesophagitis trials respond clinically to placebo, and this is associated with trial design factors such as randomisation ratio and trial population. Objective endoscopic and histologic measures are associated with very low placebo responses.

This meta‐analysis evaluates the response to placebo across multiple outcome domains in eosinophilic oesophagitis trials. While > 40% of patients improve clinically on placebo (influenced by patient age and randomisation ratio), most patients do not improve histologically or endoscopically, highlighting the importance of objective disease assessment.

## Introduction
Eosinophilic oesophagitis (EoE) is a chronic, immune‐mediated, Type 2 inflammatory and stricturing disease of the oesophagus, characterised histologically by eosinophilic infiltration and clinically by symptoms of oesophageal dysfunction [ , ]. Globally, the incidence and prevalence of EoE has increased, with recent pooled estimates of approximately five incident cases per 100,000 person‐years of follow‐up and 40 prevalent cases per 100,000 population, respectively [ ]. The clinical spectrum of EoE is broad, ranging from mild, intermittent symptoms to severe, progressive dysphagia and food impaction requiring urgent endoscopy [ ]. This variability highlights the complexity of EoE management and the importance of timely diagnosis and initiation of appropriate treatment to improve quality of life and minimise the risk of disease progression [ ].
Historically, medical therapies have included off‐label use of proton pump inhibitors and/or swallowed topical corticosteroids [ , , , ]. In the past 5 years, two oesophagus‐specific corticosteroid formulations (budesonide oral dispersible tablet and budesonide oral suspension) and the first biologic therapy, dupilumab, have received regulatory approval for the treatment of EoE. Despite these advances, there have also been several disappointing setbacks, with many promising therapies targeting different components of the immune response failing to deliver positive results in Phase 3 trials [ ]. A major hurdle in drug development for EoE is the current regulatory requirement to achieve both histologic and patient‐reported outcome (PRO)‐defined remission as coprimary outcomes [ ]. The symptoms of EoE are only modestly correlated with endoscopic and histologic disease features [ ] and although the peak eosinophil count (PEC) is used for diagnosis, it inadequately captures the spectrum of histologic involvement in EoE [ ]. In prior randomised controlled trials (RCT), high rates of response to placebo have been observed, which can dilute treatment effects and decrease statistical efficiency for demonstrating therapeutic efficacy [ ]. A prior review primarily focused on defining endpoints in EoE trials but did not pool responses to placebo and could not evaluate different threshold definitions for endoscopic and histologic outcomes [ ]. Furthermore, that study did not assess factors associated with response to placebo, which will help inform future study designs and the optimal configuration of RCT outcomes.
Here, we conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of all randomised, placebo‐controlled trials of pharmacological interventions in EoE to estimate clinical, endoscopic and histologic responses to placebo and the factors influencing these rates.

2.

## Methods
A systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations [ ]. The protocol for the review can be accessed upon request.
2.1.

## Search Strategy
We updated a Cochrane systematic review and meta‐analysis by Francosi et al. that summarised the efficacy and safety of medical interventions for patients with EoE [ ]. All data were re‐extracted in duplicate by two authors to ensure accuracy of all analysed data. We then performed an updated literature search of MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane CENTRAL (via Ovid) from March 2023 to January 1, 2024, without language restriction. We also searched Clinicaltrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trial Registry Platform (ICTRP). Terms related to EoE were searched applying validated RCTs filters for MEDLINE and Embase. The search strategy is detailed in Data .

2.2.

## Study Selection and Eligibility Criteria
Eligible studies included placebo‐controlled RCTs, assessing the efficacy of any pharmacological intervention for the treatment of adult or paediatric patients with EoE. Trials of endoscopic dilation or complementary therapies, trials of dietary modification and trials without a placebo arm were excluded. All studies (including those previously identified in the Cochrane review) were independently assessed by two authors (A.R. and N.S.A.) to evaluate their eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved by a third author (Y.Y. and C.M.).

2.3.

## Data Extraction and Outcome Measures
All data from the included studies were extracted independently by two authors (A.R. and N.S.A.) using a standardised extraction form. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus or in consultation with a third reviewer (Y.Y. and C.M.). Primary outcomes were the proportion of patients randomised to placebo with clinical, endoscopic and histologic response and remission were evaluated on an intention‐to‐treat basis. Clinical and endoscopic outcomes were defined using the original study authors' definitions. Generally, clinical remission definitions correlated with the absence of symptoms whereas clinical response was based on improvement in symptoms compared to baseline; definitions varied by individual study and based on the instrument used to evaluate symptoms and therefore, study prespecified definitions of response and remission were used in analysis. Histologic response was defined as achieving a PEC ≤ 15 eosinophils per high power field (eos/HPF); histologic remission was defined as PEC ≤ 6 eos/HPF and a secondary analysis was conducted using a more stringent threshold of PEC ≤ 1 eos/HPF. To facilitate valid comparisons between studies, some outcome definitions were grouped according to the closest category (e.g., pooling data for ≤ 5 eos/HPF and < 6 eos/HPF, pooling data from ≤ 1 eos/HPF and 0 eos/HPF).
The secondary outcome was the change in endoscopy using the EoE Endoscopic Reference Score (EREFS) recognising that this is the most frequently used and standardised method for scoring endoscopy. All included studies scored the same component items of the EREFS (oedema, rings, exudates, furrows and stricture); however, there were differences in how individual items of the EREFS were assessed and the range of the total EREFS score across trials (ranging from 0–8, 0–9 and 0–20). Therefore, we pooled the standardised mean difference to account for differences in the range of the score.
Other extracted covariables included trial design and participant characteristics (number of treatment arms, randomisation ratio, trial phase, publication year, study location, sample size, study duration, number and frequency of follow‐up visits, number of participants analysed under treatment and placebo arms, mean age, sex ratio), as well as comparator drug, dosing, route and frequency of administration. The methodological quality of each included study was independently assessed by two authors (A.R. and N.S.A.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB2) tool, with disagreements resolved by consensus or consultation with a third author.

2.4.

## Data Synthesis and Analysis
Proportions of placebo response and remission rates were pooled using the DerSimonian‐Laird random‐effects model. The Stuart‐Ord inverse double arcsine square root transformation method was used to compute 95% confidence intervals (CI) for point estimates of the proportion of responders [ ]. The mean change in EREFS posttreatment among participants randomised to placebo was quantified as the standardised mean difference using a random‐effects model with the baseline and posttreatment standardised mean EREFS scores and standard deviation (SD), because different scaling of the EREFS was used by the primary studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I
2 statistic and interpreted according to the Cochrane benchmarks: 0%–40% (may not be important), 30%–60% (moderate heterogeneity), 50%–90% (substantial heterogeneity), 75%–100% (considerable heterogeneity) [ ]. Potential publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger's test when at least 10 studies were included. When there was substantial to considerable heterogeneity and at least 10 studies were available for analysis, univariable meta‐regression using the restricted maximum likelihood method was performed to evaluate potential sources of heterogeneity. All analyses were performed using StatsDirect Version 3.3.6 (England: StatsDirect Ltd. 2024).

## Results
3.1.

## Search Results and Included Studies
We identified 208 reports from the previous Cochrane review and 392 additional records from the updated literature search. After removing duplicates, we screened 538 records and excluded 300 records based on title and abstract review; another 85 were excluded on full‐text assessment (Figure  ). In total, 25 placebo‐controlled RCTs (153 records) were included in quantitative analysis [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]. Characteristics of these trials is summarised in Table  . Most trials were at low risk for bias (Data ).
 
3.2.

## Clinical Response and Remission
Definitions of clinical response and remission varied among RCTs. Multiple different instruments were used to assess patient‐reported response or remission (Table  ), including the Mayo Dysphagia Questionnaire, the EoE Activity Index (EesAI PRO), the Dysphagia Symptom Questionnaire (DSQ) and the Straumann Dysphagia Index, as well as patient or physician global assessments of disease activity. Varying recall periods from 1 to 14 days were used.
A total of 11 studies reported the proportion of patients experiencing clinical response, and 9 studies evaluated clinical remission (Figure  ). The pooled placebo clinical response rate was 41.0% [95% CI 29.7% to 52.8%] with substantial heterogeneity (I
2 = 74.9%), and the pooled placebo clinical remission rate was 13.8% [95% CI 5.9%, 24.3%] with considerable heterogeneity (I
2 = 66.2%). Meta‐regression was performed to evaluate potential sources of heterogeneity in clinical response to placebo. Older mean age (−14.4% per decade of age [95% CI: −26.3%, −2.5%], p = 0.02) and increased probability of being randomised to placebo (−15.1% per 10% increased likelihood of receiving placebo [95% CI: −29.1%, −1.1%], p = 0.04) were significantly associated with a reduction in the rate of clinical response (Figure  ). Factors not associated with clinical response on meta‐regression included male sex, baseline PEC, number of study visits, time to the primary endpoint, multicentre vs. single‐centre design, biologic vs. swallowed topical corticosteroid comparator and daily versus nondaily recall period for symptoms (Table  ). There was no evidence of publication bias (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## Endoscopic Response and Remission
In total, three studies reported the proportion of endoscopic responders and remitters, with two studies reporting no patients achieving endoscopic remission on placebo. The pooled placebo endoscopic response rate was 25.1% [95% CI: 12.4%, 40.5%] (I
2 = 0%), and the pooled placebo endoscopic remission was 2.8% [95% CI 0.1%, 9.6%] (I
2 = 9.1%). The EREFS was the most used scoring system for evaluating endoscopic changes. A total of nine studies assessed the quantitative change in EREFS posttreatment compared to baseline. The standardised mean difference in EREFS compared with baseline among participants randomised to placebo was −0.25 [95% CI: −0.41, −0.10] (I
2 = 10.3%) (Figure  ).
 
3.4.

## Histologic Response and Remission
In total, 15 studies evaluated histological response, and 24 studies evaluated histologic remission. Most studies defined histologic response as a PEC ≤ 15 eos/HPF. Histologic remission was defined predominantly as either ≤ 1 eos/HPF or 0 eos/HPF, or less stringently as ≤ 5–6 eos/HPF. These groupings were used for pooled analysis. The pooled histologic response rate to placebo was 6.2% [95% CI: 3.3%, 10.0%], with substantial heterogeneity demonstrated (I
2 = 50.6%). The pooled histologic remission rate was 4.3% [95% CI 2.6%, 6.2%] (I
2 = 23.6%) using the less stringent definition, and 1.3% [95% CI: 0.5%, 2.5%] (I
2 = 0%) using the more stringent definition (Figure  ). There was no evidence of publication bias (Figure  ).
 Five studies reported data for a composite outcome of combined clinical and histological response or remission. The pooled placebo clinical‐histologic response/remission rate was 4.9% [95% CI 0.01%, 17.7%] (I
2 = 87.8%) (Table  ).

## Discussion
As the epidemiologic burden of EoE continues to grow, there is an increasing emphasis on addressing the unmet medical needs of patients who require effective and safe therapeutic options, which can improve both subjective symptom‐based and objective endoscopic and histologic disease measures. However, drug development for this indication has been challenging, and high clinical response rates to placebo in late phase trials have resulted in several promising therapies failing to demonstrate sufficient efficacy for regulatory approval. Our study builds on previous work by pooling placebo response rates across trials, evaluating response rates across different outcome domains and across different thresholds for defining response and remission, and by assessing trial‐ and patient‐level factors associated with clinical response to placebo. In this systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluating all placebo‐controlled RCTs of pharmacologic therapies for EoE, we demonstrate that 41% of patients clinically respond to placebo but substantial heterogeneity. On meta‐regression, this response rate is statistically highest in studies enrolling younger patients and where the randomisation ratio is skewed away from placebo. Conversely, fewer than 1 in 10 participants randomised to placebo will respond histologically or endoscopically.
The current guidance from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for developing drugs for EoE recommends the use of a coprimary configuration, assessing signs and symptoms of EoE using a well‐defined, reliable clinical outcome assessment (COA) instrument, which can include either patient‐reported or observer‐reported outcomes [ ]. While this framework aims to reduce the burden of active disease and provide relief for patients, the clinical features in EoE vary widely, depend on age, are often episodic even without treatment, and do not always correlate with disease severity or other measures of activity [ ]. Consequently, even using validated instruments such as the DSQ or EEsAI that have undergone psychometric validation [ , ], symptom assessments in EoE remain subjective. For example, the DSQ is the most used PRO measure in EoE trials [ ]. The DSQ relies of dysphagia assessment in patients ‘eating solid food’, but does not account for differences in food consistencies or food avoidances, specifically used to prevent dysphagia. The DSQ queries ‘food going down slowly or getting stuck’, both of which are subjective measures without clear anchors for these sensations. The DSQ requires assessment of having to drink liquids, cough or vomit to achieve relief of dysphagia, recognising that different patients will have different thresholds for these behaviours. Finally, PROs that focus on dysphagia may not capture the spectrum of symptoms related to EoE.
This is highlighted by two important observations in our meta‐regression. First, we showed that studies enrolling younger patients had higher clinical response rates to placebo. Assessing symptoms in younger patients is challenging: paediatric patients with EoE often present with non‐specific findings such as nausea, food refusal and abdominal pain, and a recent analysis showed that patients with non‐EoE‐related oesophageal dysfunction actually had higher Paediatric EoE Symptom Scores compared to patients with EoE [ ]. Second, we showed that expectation bias influences placebo responses in EoE RCTs: in trials where patients were less likely to receive placebo, the proportion of patients clinically improving on placebo increased [ ]. Indeed, this expectation bias was more powerful on a study‐level than the frequency of trial visits, duration of the RCT, comparator drug and recall period for symptom assessment. Accordingly, future clinical trial may want to preserve a 1:1 randomisation ratio when possible, recognising that this may be associated with lower placebo response rates and is statistically generally more efficient compared to imbalanced randomisation.
Higher rates of placebo response reduce trial efficiency as it becomes statistically more challenging to prove therapeutic efficacy and requires recruitment of substantively larger studies [ ]. We showed in this analysis that while over 40% of patients respond clinically to placebo, fewer than 1 in 20 patients will achieve histologic remission. Histology has been used as a coprimary endpoint in prior RCTS; however, this has been limited by assessing only the PEC. The PEC is used for diagnostic purposes in EoE, but eosinophils likely represent only one of many pathological mechanisms of inflammation and fibrosis in EoE [ ]. The limitations of defining histologic response using PEC alone were highlighted in two recent RCTs. In the MESSINA trial, Rothenberg et al. showed that 87.4% of participants randomised to benralizumab, an eosinophil‐depleting anti‐IL5 receptor α monoclonal antibody, achieved histologic response (≤ 6 eos/HPF), yet there was no significant improvement in symptoms or endoscopy [ ]. Similarly, Dellon et al. showed in the KRYPTOS phase 2/3 trial that while 88%–92% of patients receiving lirentelimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting Siglec‐8 expressed on the surface of eosinophils, achieved histologic response, there was also no significant improvement in the DSQ or EREFS [ ]. These negative Phase 3 trials demonstrate that eosinophil depletion alone does not represent histologic remission, and that the PEC is insensitive to structural mucosal changes and inflammation driven by other inflammatory cell types. Using a more representative histologic endpoint will be particularly relevant for future trials that target other mechanisms of action beyond eosinophilic inflammation, such as mast cell stabilisers and antifibrotic agents [ ].
Endoscopic evaluation may be an alternative primary endpoint for consideration in EoE, although it has traditionally been reported as a secondary outcome. In other disease areas in gastroenterology, such as reflux oesophagitis and inflammatory bowel disease, endoscopy features as either a coprimary or composite primary endpoint. The EREFS is a validated instrument for assessing endoscopic findings in EoE and includes both inflammatory and fibrostenotic subcomponents [ ]. The EREFS has been demonstrated to be reliable between and within observers, and responsive to change after effective treatment [ ]. In our analysis, we demonstrated that the mean change in EREFS was −0.5 points after receiving placebo: while this was statistically significant (tested against the null hypothesis of no change), the clinical significance of a half‐point reduction in EREFS score is unclear. Previously, Dellon et al. showed that the mean EREFS score decreases by approximately two points (3.88 to 2.01, p < 0.001) in patients with EoE prospectively treated with topical corticosteroids or dietary elimination, and this difference was even more pronounced in histologic responders [ ]. Although endoscopy may be more objective for defining EoE disease activity, this measure is still prone to potential observation biases in a clinical trial, as local investigators performing endoscopy will be aware of the trial time point and may not be blinded to patient symptoms if they also performed the clinical evaluation. Therefore, using measures such as blinded central reading may reduce the risk of bias for endoscopic evaluation in the RCT setting.
Our study has several strengths. We performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis to comprehensively evaluate the response to placebo in EoE RCTs across the range of relevant endpoints, and in meta‐regression, we evaluated key design features that may be associated with clinical response. However, we acknowledge some important limitations. First, our study only included placebo‐controlled RCTs, and our findings may not be generalisable to patients with EoE who do not meet relatively stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria required for trial enrolment. This is especially relevant for patients with stricturing phenotypes who may require endoscopic dilation, which has been shown to dissociate the correlation between symptoms and histology, and is generally not permitted within 8 weeks of enrolment in most EoE trials [ ]. Second, we recognise that substantial observed heterogeneity may be related to differences in trial designs. Although we attempted to evaluate the role of some of these factors in meta‐regression, this methodology is limited by the risk of ecological fallacy arising from the use of study‐level rather than individual patient‐level data.
In conclusion, we demonstrated that over 40% of patients with EoE will respond clinically to placebo and that key design features within a clinical trial including study population and randomisation ratio, are associated with potential biases that influence this clinical response rate. Future research priorities include development of more discriminatory clinical disease measures, and consideration for implementing alternative primary trial endpoint configurations that feature endoscopic evaluation or more representative histologic measures of disease activity that are responsive to treatment.
