## ABSTRACT

## Objectives
(1) Assess current methods for the collection of patient‐ and surgeon‐reported outcomes after superficial parotidectomy reconstruction. (2) Determine which post‐parotidectomy reconstructive method provides the highest degree of patient satisfaction.

## Methods
PRISMA guidelines were followed for article identification and selection. A total of 17 peer‐reviewed articles including 898 patients were included in the study. Outcome data was mathematically harmonized across studies to allow statistical analysis. Random effects meta‐analysis of proportions was performed. The Freeman‐Tukey Double Arcsine method was used to calculate the pooled estimate, or rate for each analyzed outcome.

## Results
No standardized or previously validated instrument was used to collect patient satisfaction data following superficial parotidectomy reconstruction in any of the included studies. After data harmonization, overall patient satisfaction was 93% when no reconstruction was performed, compared to 97% for sternocleidomastoid flap (SCM), and 100% for superficial muscular aponeurotic system flap (SMAS). Patient satisfaction of symmetry was 41% when no reconstruction was performed, compared to 65% for SMAS, 95% for free fat graft, and 96% for SCM.

## Conclusions
Reporting methods for patient satisfaction with appearance and symmetry after superficial parotidectomy and reconstruction are heterogeneous and non‐standardized. Artificially harmonized data demonstrate that patient‐reported outcomes of satisfaction and symmetry were improved following any form of reconstruction after parotidectomy, and patients reported symmetry‐specific satisfaction was less when SMAS reconstruction was performed. Validated and objective instruments should be utilized to determine the optimal approach to facial reconstruction after superficial parotidectomy to maximize patient‐centered outcomes.

Level of Evidence: 3.

## Introduction
Evidence‐based outcomes research continues to be a major area of interest in head and neck surgery [ ]. However, traditional metrics used to assess surgical outcomes such as mortality rates, length of stay, and complication rates do not always capture the entire picture of the patient experience. Increasingly, patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) and surgeon‐reported outcomes (SROs) have been recognized as important measures in evaluating the success of surgical interventions [ ].
In head and neck surgery, the importance of PROs and SROs is particularly relevant given the potential impact of surgical interventions on functional, esthetic, and social aspects of patients' lives. Esthetic outcomes are of particular importance in reconstructive procedures, as in addition to function, these surgeries often aim to enhance or restore patients' physical appearance and self‐esteem. Hence, in addition to understanding the technical success of surgery, assessing patient satisfaction is essential to understanding the effectiveness of these procedures [ ].
The increasing importance of PROs and SROs has been recognized by both researchers and policymakers, and there has been a growing emphasis on incorporating these measures into clinical practice. The use of PROs and SROs has been shown to improve quality of care in part by objectively measuring outcomes to identify successful and unsuccessful interventions which may lead to improved and tailored treatments to meet individual patient needs [ ]. Additionally, incorporating these measures can enhance patient‐centered approaches to treatment by allowing patients to play an active role in their own care [ ].
Superficial parotidectomy is a common procedure, often indicated for benign or malignant lesions within the parotid gland. Common side effects and sequelae of surgery may include asymmetric facial concavity, sialocele, and Frey's syndrome. Reconstruction following parotidectomy aims to restore facial volume, contour, and symmetry as well as help to prevent other post‐parotidectomy complications. While treatment guidelines and outcomes for tumor extirpation are well described, the optimal reconstructive technique for a given resulting defect has not been established [ ] despite evidence that facial asymmetry and contour deformities following parotidectomy can negatively impact the patient's quality of life [ , ]. Additionally, reconstruction after parotidectomy improves patient and surgeon satisfaction and reduces post‐operative complications [ ].
The most common techniques utilized for superficial parotid reconstruction include acellular dermal matrix (Alloderm), sternocleidomastoid (SCM) flaps, superficial muscular aponeurotic system (SMAS) flaps, and fat grafts [ ]. Each of these methods has both benefits and drawbacks [ , , ]. With these issues in mind, it has been previously suggested that the specific reconstructive technique chosen should be tailored to the parotid defect or patient. Unfortunately, there is no standardized, universally accepted protocol to aid in the planning or evaluation for the appropriate reconstructive technique. To our knowledge, no study has consolidated the existing literature to identify the preferred reconstructive technique for superficial parotid defects with respect to appearance and patient reported satisfaction.
The objective of this systematic review and meta‐analysis is to assess current methods for the collection of patient and surgeon‐reported esthetic outcomes after superficial parotidectomy reconstruction and compare esthetic outcomes among the superficial parotidectomy reconstructive techniques reported in the published literature.

2.

## Methods
This systematic review and meta‐analysis were performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplement ) [ ]. Institutional Review Board approval was not required for this study per the University of Washington policies as it used only previously reported, de‐identified data. This study protocol was not registered prior to initiation, and extracted data and analyzed data are available upon request of the authors.
2.1.

## Study Selection
Using a standardized population, intervention, comparator, and outcome (PICO) format, the population of interest was anyone undergoing parotidectomy. Patients undergoing total parotidectomy, deep lobe parotidectomy, radical parotidectomy, or temporal bone resection were excluded. Additionally, patients who had facial nerve sacrifice ± facial nerve reanimation and free flap reconstruction were also excluded as the pre‐operative expectations and post‐operative course and assessment may be highly variable when compared to superficial parotidectomy and non‐free flap reconstruction alone. Pediatric patients were excluded as this represents a distinct cohort due to the challenges in self‐reported measures of symmetry and satisfaction in this age group as well as the ongoing facial growth into childhood which may impact the perception of symmetry as the child grows. The intervention of interest was post‐parotidectomy reconstruction with any vascularized or non‐vascularized tissue or material. Outcomes included overall esthetic appearance, contour, morbidity, volume, resorption, and revision rate. We were interested in surveying how these outcomes were collected and reported in the included studies, then in performing a meta‐analysis on the domains as able.
The search strategy was created by a medical librarian using the above PICO question. A fully reproducible outline of search strategies and terms/strings can be found in Supplement . Searches were executed in Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov. Results were exported to EndNote, and duplicate citations were removed. Non‐English language studies were not included.
Three authors (J.S.B., Z.H.R., and N.L.) independently reviewed research abstracts and studies using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp). Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Overall, 358 abstracts were identified, 104 full manuscripts were reviewed in full, and 17 studies with 898 patients were included in the systematic review and meta‐analysis. Studies were excluded if they were not focused on parotidectomy, reconstruction, and contour outcomes. Additionally, letters, studies including pediatric populations, technique‐only reports, review articles, studies on obsolete techniques, and studies with less than three cases were excluded (Figure  ).
 
2.2.

## Data Extraction
Studies meeting inclusion criteria were first assessed for the method in which patient‐ and SROs measures of symmetry, contour, volume, and satisfaction were measured. Reconstruction methods identified included free fat grafts, SMAS or facelift flaps, platysma flaps, SCM flaps, and acellular dermal matrix (Alloderm). There were also several studies with combined procedures. Studies were classified into comparator groups based on reconstruction type and the dominant reconstruction method. Final groups analyzed included no reconstruction, SCM flaps, SMAS‐Type flaps (SMAS flaps, platysma flaps, facelift‐flaps), free fat grafts, and Alloderm. We did not make a distinction between autologous fat grafting and dermal‐fat grafting.
Primary outcomes collected were overall patient satisfaction and assessment of symmetry. Secondary outcomes collected included surgeon satisfaction, resorption, Frey's Syndrome, and fistula/sialocele. When reporting satisfaction, included studies used one of two general methods, either a binary “Satisfied” or “Unsatisfied” designation, or a Likert Scale, of which there were many varieties (0–10, 1–10, 1–5, etc.). For studies using a Likert Scale, linear transformation was used to standardize these data to a 0–5 scale. A score of 3.5 or greater (70%) were interpreted as a “satisfied” result. Study design, type of surgery, number of patients, and length of follow‐up were also collected.

2.3.

## Statistical Analysis
Random effects meta‐analysis of proportions was performed. We used the Freeman‐Tukey Double Arcsine method to calculate the pooled estimate, or rate. All analyses were performed in Stata version 14.2 (College Station, TX) and Microsoft Excel version 16.16 (Redmond, WA). The primary meta‐analyses performed were reported rates of patient satisfaction (defined as the percentage of patients reporting being satisfied after surgery), patient assessment of symmetry (defined as the percentage of patients reporting that their postoperative result was symmetric), surgeon satisfaction (defined as the percentage of patients that had a satisfactory result as assessed by the surgeon), resorption (defined as loss of volume after surgery), Frey's syndrome (defined as clinically symptomatic Frey's syndrome, including facial sweating or flushing), asymptomatic Frey's syndrome (defined as a positive starch iodine test), and fistula. Facial weakness was specifically excluded as an outcome measure as it would be difficult to ascertain if this was a sequela of the initial parotid surgery or related to the reconstruction of choice and may confound an assessment of symmetry. Surgeon assessment of symmetry was also not included in the analysis as no studies specifically documented results for this domain.

3.

## Results
The search strategy resulted in 358 unique citations. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 17 studies were included in our systematic review and meta‐analysis (Figure  ), of which six were retrospective cohort studies, and 11 were prospective cohort studies. The included studies comprised 898 patients that underwent four different reconstructive techniques, including a SMAS/platysma flap (n = 295), SCM flap (n = 195), fat graft (n = 52), Alloderm (n = 10), or no reconstruction (n = 346). Studies were published between 2001 and 2019; the mean sample size was 53 patients (range 8–172), and were from countries around the world, including the United States (n = 3), Canada (n = 2), Italy (n = 2), Spain (n = 2), China (n = 1), Germany (n = 1), India (n = 1), United Kingdom (n = 1), Greece (n = 1), Japan (n = 1), Korea (n = 1), and Turkey (n = 1). Mean follow‐up time ranged from 6 months to 44.4 months of the studies that reported follow‐up, with six studies not reporting follow‐up. A summary of all included studies can be found in Table  .
 Most commonly, assessments included patient and surgeon ratings of appearance and symmetry. These assessments were generally survey‐based with either binary (satisfied/unsatisfied) or Likert‐scale rating schemes. Specifically, five studies had a binary outcome measure, simply asking patients or surgeons if they were satisfied or not with appearance, contour, etc. [ , , , , ]. Eight studies utilized forward‐direction Likert Scales (i.e., higher number equates to more positive outcome) with scales of 1–10, 0–10, 0–5, and 1–3 [ , , , , , , , ]. One study used a 1–0, reverse‐direction Likert Scale [ ]. Finally, three studies utilized text‐based Likert Scales with descriptors. Curry et al. [ ] asked patients how severe their cosmetic concerns were on a 4‐level scheme ranging from slight to severe enough to seek treatment. Fasolis et al. [ ] used a 3‐level scheme ranging from slight to severe, and Gooden et al. [ ] used a 4‐level scheme ranging from unsatisfied to very satisfied. Studies using objective assessments of symmetry, such as MRI or CT‐based volumetric analysis, were uncommon and not included in this meta‐analysis as they were not PROs.
When comparing the various reconstructive techniques, we observed several differences between groups in various outcomes. Table  and Figure  show a summary of the meta‐analyses for overall patient satisfaction. This demonstrated that the pooled rate of patient satisfaction was 93% (95% CI 59–100) when no reconstruction was performed compared to 97% (95% CI 82–100) for SCM, and 100% (95% CI 97–100) for SMAS.
 The meta‐analysis for patient assessment of symmetry is shown in Table  and Figure  . This analysis shows that the pooled rate of patient assessment of satisfaction of symmetry was 41% (95% CI 6–81) when no reconstruction was performed compared to 65% (95% CI 17–99) for SMAS, 95% (95% CI 86–100) for free fat graft, and 96% (95% CI 77–100) for SCM.
 Next, SROs were examined. Overall surgeon satisfaction is shown in Table  and Figure  . This meta‐analysis shows that the pooled rate of overall surgeon satisfaction was 76% (95% CI 20–100) when no reconstruction was performed, compared to 93% (95% CI 59–100) for SCM, 100% for SMAS (95% CI 98–100), and alloderm (95% CI 72–100).
 From the included studies, secondary outcome data regarding complications was examined. Complications such as hematoma and infection were unable to be included due to infrequent and incomplete reporting. The meta‐analyses for fistula or sialocele formation are shown in Table  and Figure  . This analysis shows that the pooled rate of fistula or sialocele was 3% (95% CI 1–6) when no reconstruction was used, 2% (95% CI 0–8) for SMAS, and 0% (95% CI 0–4) for SCM. Table  and Figure  show the summary of the meta‐analyses for symptomatic Frey's syndrome. This analysis shows that the pooled rate of symptomatic Frey's syndrome was 14% (95% CI 2–32) when SMAS reconstruction was performed, compared to 13% (95% CI 6–22) for no reconstruction, 9% (95% CI 1–23) for SCM, and 8% (95% CI 3–20) for free fat graft. The summary of meta‐analyses for asymptomatic Frey's syndrome can be found in Table  and Figure  . This analysis shows that the pooled rate of asymptomatic Frey's syndrome was 34% (95% CI 4–72) when SMAS reconstruction was performed, compared to 30% (95% CI 13–49) for no reconstruction, 25% (95% CI 2–58) for SCM, and 5% (95% CI 0–30) for free fat graft. Finally, the summary of meta‐analyses for resorption can be found in Table  and Figure  . This analysis included only SCM reconstruction and fat grafting, with both techniques showing a pooled event rate of 0% and a 95% CI of 0–16.
 The risk of bias was rated using the Cochran Collaboration Schema. Bias was high for seven studies and low for 10 studies. The primary reason for the high risk of bias rating in those studies was due to incomplete outcome reporting. A more detailed listing of the risk of bias assessment can be found in Supplement .

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta‐analysis sought to appraise the literature of methods used to assess patient and surgeon reported esthetic outcomes after superficial parotidectomy and reconstruction and compare these published esthetic outcomes stratified by reconstruction technique.
PROs are increasingly recognized as an essential measure of the success of facial reconstructive and cosmetic surgery [ ]. The use of PROs allows patients to provide feedback on their quality of life, function, and satisfaction with the outcome of their surgery. In facial surgery, studies have utilized a variety of PRO measures (PROM) to evaluate patient‐centered outcomes as they relate to appearance, quality of life, and facial function, including the FACE‐Q questionnaires, Facial Clinimetric Evaluation (FaCE) Scale, the Short Form‐36 (SF‐36), the Facial Disability Index (FDI), and the University of Washington Quality of Life (UW‐QOL) questionnaire, among others [ ].
Although none of the reviewed studies, nor any literature to our knowledge, have used the above mentioned PROMSs to evaluate parotidectomy reconstruction, the FACE‐Q questionnaires have been commonly employed in research to assess the outcomes of a range of facial esthetic procedures, including rhinoplasty, facelift, and brow lift surgeries. The FACE‐Q consists of multiple scales that assess various aspects of PROs, including satisfaction with facial appearance, quality of life, psychosocial function, and surgical recovery [ ]. A recent addition to FACE‐Q is a head and neck cancer module which strives to determine QOL outcomes as they relate to head and neck cancer patients broadly, and has been used to evaluate appearance and psychosocial QOL after microvascular reconstruction [ , ]. There are however no studies that utilize this module for parotid reconstruction which may be more appropriate than the other FACE‐Q modules, as patient expectations may be different when undergoing reconstruction following tumor extirpation vs. elective cosmetic procedures. Furthermore, the included appearance domains related to contour, symmetry, proportion, and overall satisfaction with appearance are particularly relevant to parotid reconstruction [ ].
Overall, the lack of standardized use of PROMs for parotidectomy reconstruction was broadly illustrated in the current study by the heterogeneous methods PROs were collected and reported. This limited the statistical analysis that could be performed, requiring elaborate data harmonization procedures to draw conclusions. This highlights the need for the development of a specific tool to evaluate patient‐reported appearance outcomes following parotid surgery or the widespread implementation of previously developed instruments such as the FACE‐Q Head and Neck Cancer Module to help guide interventions toward improved satisfaction and quality of life.
When examining patient reported satisfaction, both overall and symmetry‐specific satisfaction were improved with all methods of reconstruction. Interestingly, while overall satisfaction with appearance remained high (93%) without any reconstruction, patient reported symmetry‐specific satisfaction was 41%. This discrepancy suggests that the domain of symmetry‐specific satisfaction includes unique factors that are not accounted for or may be missed when simply assessing patient satisfaction following surgery. This further emphasizes the need for detailed and validated tools that comprehensively evaluate domains related to parotid and facial reconstruction to understand the best or most appropriate patient‐centered interventions.
In the pooled meta‐analysis, no single method of reconstruction was clearly superior for any of the examined satisfaction domains, though SCM reconstruction did show slightly lower rates of overall patient satisfaction. This could be due to the resultant depression deformity of the neck that is created when transposing muscle into the parotid bed [ ], or inadequate bulk of muscle compared as SCM thickness can be variable depending on patient sex, age, and overall muscularity [ ]. Additionally, the SCM can have variable blood supply, which may lead to differential rates of flap atrophy and/or survivial [ ]. SMAS flaps showed lower rates of symmetry‐specific satisfaction. This symmetry discrepancy seen in SMAS flap parotid reconstruction could be due to a “lifted appearance” of the operated side, and this effect could be more pronounced in an aging face [ ]. It is interesting to note that while SMAS flaps had a lower symmetry score, satisfaction was still quite high. This likely speaks to a deeper question regarding the components of “satisfaction,” highlighting the potential different domains that “symmetry” and “satisfaction” capture. Satisfaction may be the result of a combination of factors, including incision design, bulk and contour, symmetry, pre‐operative expectations, and pre‐operative satisfaction with appearance. More work is needed to delineate the objective measures which contribute to this subjective impression.
Analysis of surgeon‐reported satisfaction revealed high levels of satisfaction across all reconstructive techniques, with only SCM flaps showing a pooled rate less than 100. Conversely, surgeon satisfaction was much lower when no reconstruction was performed. Interestingly, surgeon‐ and patient‐reported satisfaction rates were similar. While not specific to parotid or facial reconstruction, previous work has shown high discordance between surgeon and patient perception of quality of life after anterior skull base surgery, and surgeons tended to rate their patients' quality of life higher than the patients' own assessments [ ]. In contrast, analysis in rhinoplasty has shown the opposite trend, finding surgeons to be more critical of their outcomes when compared to patients [ ]. Though the current study found patient‐ and SROs are similar, inherent bias in the methods used for surgeon and patient ratings requires more stringent, blinded techniques to verify this finding.
Although not the primary endpoint of this study, rates of common complications were also evaluated. As study inclusion criteria mandated a focus on the esthetic outcome from reconstruction, this does not represent an exhaustive collection of studies specifically focused on complications. As such, there is some inherent bias in this data, though it does represent complications in the context of parotid reconstruction. Composite data regarding flap resorption was available for four of the relevant procedures. Rates of resorption were essentially zero for SCM flap and fat grafts. Resorption of avascular free fat grafts is a known phenomenon, and literature recommends overcorrecting defects by 20%–30% when using free fat to account for this loss of bulk [ , , ]. Thus, the zero resorption rate for fat grafts may be a result of an intended initial overcorrection. Unfortunately, the included studies did not report any quantitative measures of the fat grafts utilized. Rates of symptomatic and asymptomatic Frey's syndrome were dramatically reduced when reconstruction was employed except when SMAS flaps were utilized. The mechanism behind this is unclear but may be due to the relatively thinness of the SMAS flap. Sialocele formation was uniformly low across all methods of reconstruction analyzed. Other complications such as infection or hematoma were unable to be studied due to infrequent and/or inconsistent reporting. Overall, this suggests that reconstruction may be employed to not only improve esthetic outcome but also reduce the risk of common post‐operative sequelae associated with parotidectomy. Furthermore, the overall risk of these complications may be impacted more so with thicker reconstruction options such as SCM or fat grafts when compared to the SMAS flap.
A discussion of heterogeneity is key to understanding and contextualizing the results to draw overall conclusions. As a measure, heterogeneity, or I
2, is a mathematical representation of the variation in outcomes between studies. Overall, the heterogeneity was high for almost all outcomes evaluated. This heterogeneity is likely due to several factors including inconsistency among studies in the outcomes reported and the need to transform Likert scale‐based assessments into binary assessments of satisfaction. The persistently high heterogeneity despite an exhaustive review of the current literature reflects the lack of use of any standard measure or scoring system for facial contour, symmetry, or satisfaction following parotidectomy. This provides a strong argument for the use of a validated scale to assess these domains in a systematic and objective way [ ]. There was, however, a notable exception to the otherwise high heterogeneity. In the evaluation of patient assessment of symmetry, studies concerning fat grafts had an I
2 of 0.0. This suggests that fat grafting has shown more consistent positive results with regard to patient symmetry. However, this result cannot be directly compared to other methods of reconstruction, making it difficult to provide clinical recommendations.
4.1.

## Limitations
Unfortunately, there were only two randomized controlled trials [ , ] and three non‐randomized controlled trials [ , , ] included in this analysis that met our inclusion criteria. Therefore, patient selection for the various reconstruction groups was likely based on patient factors including lesion location, size, histology, individual anatomy, comorbidities, and surgeon preference. Furthermore, there will be some technique variability within a given reconstruction method: SCM flaps may be full or partial thickness and may also be superior or inferiorly based [ , , , , , , ]; SMAS flaps may be harvested and inset using various methods [ , , , , , , , , ]. However, we feel that comparing multiple techniques in the included studies allowed us to generate meaningful conclusions despite not having randomized data, which is a very difficult study design in this clinical scenario.
It should be noted that no distinction between free fat grafting and dermal‐fat grafting was made. One of the included studies, Fasolis et al., used dermal‐fat grafting. We elected to include this paper in the analysis given the number of patients and quality of the study. As there is some evidence that dermal‐fat grafts have less resorption than traditional fat grafts, this may bias our results [ , , ]. Additionally, there is no standardized measure of surgeon skill or years of experience that can be analyzed using the included studies, which may also bias our results. Mean follow‐up time, although a relevant and critical point of consideration in short‐ and long‐term reconstruction results, was inconsistently reported and therefore not included, although the reported follow‐up times are included in Supplement . Finally, there is no consistent data within the included studies that details the specific location within the parotid (tail vs. lateral cheek, etc.). It is possible that variation in lesion location plays a role both in the reconstructive option chosen and patient satisfaction post‐operatively.
While there are limitations in the study, the findings give meaningful insight into the esthetic outcomes and secondarily, complications, following parotid reconstruction. Equally important, these limitations ultimately inform the conclusion that a validated instrument to assess patient‐reported perception of symmetry, contour, and satisfaction after parotidectomy and reconstruction is critical to give surgeons the ability to compare techniques to optimize outcomes.

4.2.

## Recommendations and Future Work
Further work should focus on deploying an existing validated PROM or developing a new PROM for the assessment of symmetry, contour, volume, and patient satisfaction after parotid reconstruction. A prospective study should pay particular attention to the differences between various reconstructive techniques as well as the extent and indication for parotid surgery. This will help delineate which reconstructive method provides the best patient‐centered outcome for a given defect.

5.

## Conclusion
Overall, there is significant disparity among the non‐validated methods used to assess satisfaction and symmetry following parotid reconstruction. Validated, objective, and non‐biased tools must be used to determine the optimal approach to facial reconstruction after parotidectomy as they relate to patient‐reported satisfaction, symmetry, and contour.
This study demonstrates that though patients may report high levels of overall satisfaction following parotidectomy without reconstruction, symmetry‐specific satisfaction is reduced in comparison. Although this meta‐analysis cannot recommend a specific method, it is clear that patient‐ and surgeon‐reported satisfaction and symmetry can be improved, and the incidence of Frey's syndrome reduced, by utilizing some form of reconstruction following superficial parotidectomy. There is evidence that while fat grafting shows more consistently high levels of reported satisfaction and symmetry, SMAS reconstruction may result in lower symmetry‐specific satisfaction and higher rates of Frey's syndrome compared to other techniques.
