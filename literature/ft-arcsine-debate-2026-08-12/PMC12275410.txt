## Background & aims
The epidemiological characteristics of malnutrition in cancer patients remain unclear. The Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria, published in 2019, were established to compare the prevalence of malnutrition globally, drive the development of standardized care practices, and promote improved clinical outcomes. This study aimed to systematically assess the prevalence and related factors of malnutrition in cancer patients based on the GLIM criteria, and to evaluate its association with clinical outcomes.

## Methods
Two authors independently searched PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Embase up to July 20, 2024. Eligible studies were observational (cross-sectional or cohort), involved adult cancer patients, and assessed nutritional status using the GLIM criteria. Studies were required to report at least one of the following outcomes: prevalence of malnutrition, associated factors, or relevant clinical outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² and Q tests. A random-effects model was used when significant heterogeneity was present (I²>50%, P < 0.05); otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied. PROSPERO registration number: CRD420251053345.

## Results
A total of 20 cross-sectional studies and 45 cohort studies were included, all of which reported the prevalence of malnutrition and together covered over 30,000 cancer patients. Three studies examined factors influencing malnutrition. Nine studies evaluated its impact on overall survival (OS), while three assessed its effect on postoperative complications. Overall, the prevalence of malnutrition was 41% (95% CI: 36-45%), with moderate malnutrition at 19% (95% CI: 16-23%) and severe malnutrition at 20% (95% CI: 17-23%). The main factors influencing malnutrition in cancer patients were tumor-related symptoms. Malnutrition in cancer patients is significantly associated with OS (HR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.56–2.67). Specifically, the risk is significantly increased in patients with moderate malnutrition (HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.07–1.72), and even higher in those with severe malnutrition (HR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.55–2.39). Additionally, malnutrition in cancer patients is also significantly associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications (OR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.41–2.55).

## Conclusion
Malnutrition prevalence is high among cancer patients, primarily due to tumor-related symptoms. It significantly increases the risk of poor clinical outcomes, with a more pronounced effect in patients suffering from severe malnutrition.

## Introduction
Malnutrition is prevalent in cancer patients, which directly affects their prognosis, incidence of adverse reactions, and quality of life [ , ]. Although various tools exist to assess malnutrition, there is currently no universally accepted gold standard. To establish an international consensus for diagnosing malnutrition, compare its global prevalence, drive the development of standardized care practices, and promote improved clinical outcomes, the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria were published in 2019 [ ]. The GLIM criteria consist of two steps: risk screening and diagnosis. A diagnosis of malnutrition requires meeting at least one phenotypic criterion and one etiologic criterion. Phenotypic criteria include weight loss, low body mass index (BMI), and reduced muscle mass; etiologic criteria include reduced food intake or assimilation and inflammation. Furthermore, the severity of malnutrition was classified based on the phenotypic criteria proposed by the GLIM. Moderate malnutrition (Stage 1) was defined as a 5–10% weight loss within the past 6 months, a BMI < 20 kg/m² for individuals under 70 years or < 22 kg/m² for those aged 70 years and older, or a mild-to-moderate reduction in muscle mass. Severe malnutrition (Stage 2) was defined as a weight loss exceeding 10% within 6 months, a BMI < 18.5 kg/m² for individuals under 70 years or < 20 kg/m² for those aged 70 years and older, or a severe reduction in muscle mass.
The GLIM criteria provide a standardized method for diagnosing malnutrition. However, the epidemiological characteristics of malnutrition in cancer patients, particularly its prevalence and related factors, remain unclear. Current systematic reviews primarily focus on the impact of malnutrition on clinical outcomes in cancer patients as assessed by the GLIM criteria, while detailed epidemiological data are still lacking and require further investigation. According to the results of four systematic reviews [ , , , ], malnutrition in cancer patients is significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) (HR = 1.56–1.90). Subgroup analysis indicated that the HR for patients with severe malnutrition ranged from 1.73 to 1.79. However, these findings differ significantly from those of a recent systematic review using the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) tool, which reported an HR of 2.65 (95% CI: 1.90, 3.70) [ ]. Regarding disease-free survival (DFS), the results of two systematic reviews were relatively consistent, with HRs ranging from 1.51 to 1.63 [ , ]. Additionally, the results of two systematic reviews on the relationship between malnutrition and postoperative complications showed significant differences: one study reported an RR of 1.82 [ ], while another reported an OR of 5.94 [ ]. Building on these findings, this study aims to determine the prevalence of malnutrition among cancer patients, analyze its related factors, and further explore the impact of malnutrition on OS and postoperative complications. The goal is to provide more comprehensive insights for clinical practice and contribute to the development of more effective management strategies.

## Materials & methods
This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. To enhance transparency and reproducibility, the review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251053345).

## Search strategy
Searches were conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Embase databases, covering the period from January 1, 2019, to March 6, 2024. Subsequently, an additional search was performed for literature published between January 1, 2024, and July 20, 2024, to update the data. A combination of free terms and MeSH terms was used for the search, including keywords such as ‘cancer,’ ‘GLIM,’ and ‘malnutrition.’ For example, the detailed search strategy for PubMed is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

## Eligibility criteria
Inclusion criteria comprised cross-sectional and cohort studies that assessed malnutrition in cancer patients using the GLIM criteria and reported at least one outcome related to the prevalence of malnutrition, related factors, OS, or postoperative complications. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicate publications; (2) studies with inaccessible full texts; (3) letters to the editor, conference abstracts, reviews, etc.; and (4) studies involving pregnant women and patients under 18 years of age.

## Data extraction and quality assessment
Two researchers independently screened the literature based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Extracted information included: author, year, type of study, region, malnutrition risk screening tool, methods for assessing muscle mass, type of cancer, sample size, malnutrition, moderate malnutrition, severe malnutrition, age, adjustments, data on related factors, OS, and postoperative complications comparing patients with and without malnutrition. For studies that published data on the same population multiple times, we prioritized including the publication that provided the most comprehensive data on malnutrition prevalence.

The quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two authors using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist. The NOS was primarily used for evaluating cohort studies, while the JBI checklist was applied to assess the risk of bias in cross-sectional studies. Any discrepancies in screening, data extraction, or quality assessment were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

## Statistical analyses

The primary objective of this study is to determine the prevalence of malnutrition in cancer patients and identify its related factors and clinical outcomes. The effect sizes of relevant factors were combined using odds ratios (OR) and hazard ratios (HR) from multivariable analyses. Qualitative or quantitative analysis was selected as appropriate. The pooled prevalence of malnutrition was calculated using the metaprop command in Stata, applying a random- or fixed-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird method. A Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to stabilize the variance of proportions before pooling. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to assess the statistical differences between effect sizes, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² and Q tests, with I² > 50% and P < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity. For results with high heterogeneity (I² > 50%, P < 0.05), a random effects model was used; otherwise, a fixed effects model was applied. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we initially considered conducting meta-regression analyses. If substantial heterogeneity persisted within subgroups stratified by identified covariates, we planned to perform pre-specified subgroup analyses informed by previous studies and clinical relevance. The subgroup factors included type of cancer, region, malnutrition risk screening tool, and methods for assessing muscle mass. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each study to determine the stability of the results. All statistical analyses for this meta-analysis were performed using Stata 15.0.

## Results

## Search results
The initial search yielded the following number of studies: PubMed (n = 131), Web of Science (n = 192), Cochrane Library (n = 7), and Embase (n = 267). After step-by-step screening based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 57 studies remained. An additional search conducted on July 20, 2024, updated the data with 8 more studies, bringing the total to 65 included studies Fig. .

 

## Characteristics of studies and quality assessment
A total of 45 cohort studies [ – ] and 20 cross-sectional studies [ – ] were included, covering four regions: Asia, Europe, Australia, and Brazil. Sample sizes ranged from 36 to 4,122 participants, with ages primarily between 50 and 60 years. Two studies explored the impact of using different screening tools or not conducting screening on the outcomes [ , ]. Forty-one studies did not conduct nutritional risk screening [ , – , – , , , , – , – , – , – ] while 21 studies did [ , , , , – , , – ], primarily using tools such as NRS2002, MST, and MUST; one study did not specify the screening tool used [ ]. Six studies compared the effects of different muscle assessment methods on the results [ , , , , , ]. Six studies did not assess muscle mass [ , , ]– [ , , ], while 45 studies did, mainly using indicators such as FFMI, L3-SMI, and anthropometry [ – , , – , , , , – , , , – , – , , , – , , , , , , ]; eight studies did not specify the methods used for muscle mass assessment [ , , , , , , , ]. Three studies discussed factors related to malnutrition [ , , ]. Nine studies reported multivariate analysis results on the impact of malnutrition on OS [ – ], while 3 studies reported multivariate analysis results on the impact of malnutrition on postoperative complications [ – ]. The quality of the included cohort studies was assessed using the NOS. Most studies scored between 6 and 9 stars, indicating moderate to high quality, with strengths in representativeness, exposure ascertainment, and comparability. The quality of the cross-sectional studies was evaluated using the JBI checklist. Most of these studies clearly defined inclusion criteria and applied valid outcome measures, although reporting of confounding adjustment was often lacking. Further details on the included studies and their quality assessments are provided in Supplementary Tables 2–4.

## Prevalence of malnutrition
Overall, the prevalence of malnutrition was 41% (95% CI: 36–45%), based on 65 studies involving 31,330 cancer patients. The prevalence of moderate malnutrition was 19% (95% CI: 16–23%), derived from 23 studies including 11,710 patients, while the prevalence of severe malnutrition was 20% (95% CI: 17–23%), also based on 23 studies. Subgroup analysis by tumor type revealed that pancreatic cancer had the highest prevalence of malnutrition at 51% (95% CI: 24-78%), followed by esophageal cancer at 46% (95% CI: 25-68%), while breast cancer had the lowest prevalence at 13% (95% CI: 6-21%). Subgroup analysis by region showed that Brazil had a slightly higher prevalence of malnutrition at 65% (95% CI: 44-86%), whereas the rates in Asia, Europe, and Australia were similar, at 34% (95% CI: 29-39%), 44% (95% CI: 36-52%), and 31% (95% CI: 17-44%), respectively. Nutritional risk screening appeared to be associated with differences in the outcomes, although no direct statistical comparison was performed. The prevalence of malnutrition among patients who did not undergo nutritional risk screening was 47% (95% CI: 39-54%), which is notably higher. The NRS2002 was the most commonly used nutritional risk screening tool, with a malnutrition prevalence of 38% (95% CI: 32-43%) after screening. Except for using anthropometry to assess muscle mass, which showed a higher prevalence of malnutrition 68% (95% CI: 57-80%), whether muscle mass assessment was conducted and the method used seemed to have little impact on the results. ## Factors related to malnutrition

Three studies investigated the factors related to malnutrition in patients with esophageal cancer, nasopharyngeal cancer, and liver cancer, respectively [ , , ]. Due to the limited number of included studies, this analysis was qualitative. The main factors related to malnutrition in cancer patients were tumor-related symptoms. For esophageal cancer patients, dysphagia (OR = 7.39, 95% CI: 1.46–37.52) and esophageal obstruction (OR = 10.49, 95% CI: 3.47–31.70) were key factors leading to severe malnutrition [ ]. In nasopharyngeal cancer patients, reduced appetite (OR = 2.839, 95% CI: 1.269–6.353) was an important factor [ ]. Additionally, increased radiation dose (OR = 1.046, 95% CI: 1.023–1.069) significantly increased the risk of malnutrition. For liver cancer patients, the presence of ascites (OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.10–3.72) was a significant factor for malnutrition [ ]. Moreover, liver cancer patients aged 65 and above had a 1.04 times higher risk of malnutrition compared to those under 65 (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.07). Anemia (OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.01–4.23) also significantly increased the risk of malnutrition in liver cancer patients.

## Impact of malnutrition on OS and postoperative complications in cancer patients
The results of this study showed that malnutrition in cancer patients was significantly associated with poorer OS, with a pooled HR of 2.04 (95% CI: 1.56–2.67), based on 5 studies involving 866 patients (Fig. ). Specifically, the risk of mortality increased progressively with the severity of malnutrition. The pooled HR for moderate malnutrition was 1.34 (95% CI: 1.07–1.72), derived from 3 studies including 1,427 patients, while the HR for severe malnutrition was 1.92 (95% CI: 1.55–2.39), based on 5 studies with 1,653 patients (Fig. ). This apparent discrepancy may reflect differences in study inclusion and population characteristics across analyses. Furthermore, the findings indicate that malnutrition is significantly associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications (OR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.41–2.55) (Fig. ). All of the above results show I² < 50% and P > 0.05, and were analyzed using a fixed-effects model.

 

 

 

## Publication bias and sensitivity analysis
A trim-and-fill analysis was conducted for results showing publication bias. The pooled effect size for the prevalence of malnutrition after the trim-and-fill adjustment is presented in Table . As an example, results for the overall population are reported here. Egger’s test revealed a significant intercept (bias = 11.66, 95% CI: 8.01–15.31; P < 0.001). The corresponding funnel plot is shown in Supplementary Figure 1. After applying the trim-and-fill method, the adjusted effect size was 0.18 (95% CI: 0.13–0.23). Additionally, the relationship between malnutrition and postoperative complications in cancer patients also indicated publication bias; after correction using the trim-and-fill method, the pooled effect size was (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.35–2.31). For the other meta-analyses, the funnel plots were largely symmetrical, and Egger’s test (P > 0.05) indicated no significant publication bias. Furthermore, excluding each included study one by one from the meta-analyses did not significantly alter the pooled effect sizes, suggesting that the findings are robust.

## Discussion
The results of this study indicate that the prevalence of malnutrition in cancer patients is approximately 41%. Tumor-related symptoms are the primary factors related to malnutrition in these patients. Compared to cancer patients without malnutrition, those with malnutrition are more likely to experience adverse clinical outcomes, such as increased mortality and postoperative complications.
The results of this systematic review indicate that the prevalence of malnutrition is highest among patients with pancreatic cancer, followed by those with esophageal cancer, and lowest in those with breast cancer. Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency is a common complication in pancreatic cancer patients [ ], while esophageal cancer patients often experience varying degrees of dysphagia [ ]. These patients typically present with subtle early symptoms and persistent nutritional depletion, leading to a high prevalence of malnutrition at the time of diagnosis. The standard surgical procedure for pancreatic cancer is the Whipple procedure, which involves the resection of the pancreatic head, gallbladder, duodenum, distal stomach, and part of the common bile duct. This extensive surgery can trigger a stress response and metabolic disturbances [ ]. Although minimally invasive esophagectomy has become a widely accepted alternative to traditional open surgery [ ], early oral feeding remains uncommon [ ]. In contrast, breast cancer treatment has a relatively minimal impact on the digestive system, resulting in a lower prevalence of malnutrition.
The prevalence of malnutrition in Brazil is 65% (95% CI: 44-86%), significantly higher than in other regions. This may be due to the inclusion of five studies from Brazil [ , , , , ]two of which specifically targeted high-risk groups among cancer patients—one focusing on patients over 60 years old [ ] and the other on patients in the ICU [ ]. Additionally, the different muscle mass assessment methods used for diagnosis according to the GLIM criteria might contribute to this variation. For example, one study using L3-SMI as the muscle mass assessment method reported a malnutrition prevalence of 23% [ ], whereas another study using AMC or a combination of AMC and HGS reported prevalences of 83% and 90% [ ], respectively. Subgroup analysis of studies using anthropometry showed a reported malnutrition prevalence of 68% (95% CI: 57-80%) [ – , , , ], which is significantly higher than that reported by studies using other methods of muscle mass assessment. This difference may be attributed not only to the inclusion of three studies focused specifically on high-risk populations [ , , ] but also to the inconsistency in the cutoff values used for the anthropometry.
The PG-SGA tool is a comprehensive assessment instrument that integrates nutrition screening, assessment, classification, and monitoring. It is recommended by the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) for use in cancer patients [ ]. According to the GLIM criteria, the first step of malnutrition risk screening can be performed using any existing validated screening tool [ ]. Currently, there is still debate regarding this step in existing research. Some studies have compared the consistency of different screening tools with the PG-SGA in diagnosing malnutrition, as well as the consistency when no screening is conducted [ , ]. Results have shown higher consistency when no screening was performed. Henriksen et al. found that without screening, sensitivity was 69% and specificity was 70% [ ]; Wang et al. reported a sensitivity of 81.6% and specificity of 97.7% [ ]. Our study found that the prevalence of malnutrition was 47% (95% CI: 39-54%) when no malnutrition risk screening was conducted. Among the screening tools, NRS2002 is the most commonly used, with a malnutrition prevalence of 38% (95% CI: 32-43%) after screening, which is the closest to the unscreened result.

According to the GLIM criteria, accurately assessing muscle mass is a crucial component in diagnosing malnutrition [ ]. The results of this study indicate that, apart from when anthropometry are used—which report a higher prevalence of malnutrition at 68% (95% CI: 57-80%)—the prevalence of malnutrition reported using other muscle mass assessment methods is fairly consistent, ranging from 33 to 47%. Notably, even in the absence of muscle mass evaluation, the reported prevalence of malnutrition remains relatively high at 40% (95% CI: 14-67%) [ , , , , , , ]. This suggests that cancer patients experiencing weight loss and reduced BMI may also have decreased muscle mass. However, it is important to note that the subgroup without muscle mass assessment included only seven studies. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution, and further research is needed to validate these findings. A meta-analysis has shown that 41.6% of hospitalized elderly patients have both malnutrition and sarcopenia [ ]. Cachexia is characterized primarily by the reduction of skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [ ]. A systematic review of non-small cell lung cancer patients found a cachexia prevalence of 39% (95% CI: 33-46%) [ ]. We attempt to explain these findings through their shared mechanisms. Tumors are described as ‘unhealing wounds,’ a description derived from the similarity between the inflammatory components of tumor stroma and wound tissue. Systemic inflammation is at the root of complex metabolic alterations, including impacts on the central nervous system (anorexia, fatigue), muscle protein breakdown (skeletal muscle atrophy), fat storage (lipolysis), hepatic synthesis of acute-phase proteins, and metabolic endocrine disruptions (insulin resistance) [ , ].
A systematic review using various nutritional assessment tools found that cancer patients with malnutrition had a significantly increased risk of mortality (HR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.62, 2.17) [ ]. Another systematic review focusing on the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) as an assessment tool found that this index was associated with OS in cancer patients (HR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.49, 2.56) [ ]. Similarly, a systematic review using the PG-SGA found that malnourished cancer patients had poorer OS rates (HR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.77, 2.21) and a higher risk of postoperative complications (OR = 4.65, 95% CI: 1.61, 13.44) [ ]. In terms of OS, the study results were generally consistent. However, there were significant differences between studies regarding the risk of postoperative complications. Our study found an OR of 1.90 (95% CI: 1.41, 2.55), while two previous systematic reviews based on the GLIM criteria reported an RR of 1.82 (95% CI: 1.28, 2.60) [ ] and an OR of 5.94 (95% CI: 3.58, 9.85) [ ]. These discrepancies are primarily related to the characteristics of the original studies included. Additionally, the small number of studies included in these systematic reviews may introduce a higher risk of bias. Therefore, further high-quality original research is needed to validate these findings. It is noteworthy that, like GLIM, PG-SGA can grade patients when used as a malnutrition assessment tool. Subgroup analysis revealed that cancer patients with moderate malnutrition had an OS (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.06), while those with severe malnutrition had a significantly poorer OS (HR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.90, 3.70) [ ]. The results of this study indicate that patients with severe malnutrition have a significantly increased risk of poorer OS (HR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.55–2.39). This finding shows a considerable difference compared to the PG-SGA assessment tool, but it is consistent with the results of four previous systematic reviews based on the GLIM criteria [ , , , ]. This suggests that further original research is needed to compare the accuracy of these two assessment tools in predicting adverse OS and postoperative complications.
Tumor-related symptoms, age, anemia, and other factors are related factors for malnutrition in cancer patients. Early identification of high-risk individuals and timely initiation of nutritional support are crucial. A multicenter prospective randomized trial in Switzerland (EFFORT) evaluated the impact of early nutritional support on hospitalized patients with malnutrition. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either individualized nutritional support guided by a protocol to meet protein and caloric goals (intervention group) or the standard hospital diet (control group) [ ]. The results showed that by 30 days, 23% of patients in the intervention group experienced adverse clinical outcomes, compared to 27% in the control group (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.64, 0.97). The mortality rate was 7% in the intervention group, compared to 10% in the control group (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.47–0.91). A secondary analysis focusing solely on cancer patients revealed similar survival benefits [ ]. Physical activity has been included in the ESPEN guidelines for nutritional management [ ]. Recently, two systematic reviews analyzed the impact of exercise on cancer patients [ , ]. One systematic review, which included three randomized controlled trials, indicated improvements in muscle strength, and three studies showed positive effects on skeletal muscle loss, skeletal muscle index, and fat-free mass index [ ]. Another systematic review explored the impact of home-based exercise on cancer patients and found that most included studies did not observe significant improvements in muscle strength and body composition [ ]. Future efforts should emphasize multidisciplinary collaboration to optimize exercise programs and further validate the effects of exercise on cancer patients.

To our knowledge, this systematic review is the first to clearly define the prevalence of malnutrition and its related factors among cancer patients. Additionally, this systematic review further compared the differences between GLIM criteria and other assessment tools in predicting adverse OS and postoperative complications, providing important insights for future research. The strength of our study lies in our systematic search across multiple databases, ultimately including 65 studies and covering over 30,000 patients. We provide the most recent and accurate estimates of malnutrition prevalence in cancer patients, laying the groundwork for future intervention strategies. Furthermore, we summarize the related factors for malnutrition and its impact on OS and postoperative complications, aiming to aid in the early identification of high-risk individuals and the prevention of malnutrition.It should be noted that ECOG performance status and cancer stage are clinically important factors that may influence the prevalence of malnutrition. However, most of the original studies included in this review did not report data on their association with nutritional status. We recommend that future research systematically include and report these variables in relation to malnutrition outcomes to facilitate more nuanced and informative analyses.

Like most studies, this systematic review has some limitations. First, some results exhibit significant heterogeneity due to factors such as sample size and the baseline characteristics of cancer patients, although we have addressed this issue as much as possible from a statistical perspective. In addition, heterogeneity may also arise from variations in the application of the GLIM criteria across studies, especially in the methods used for assessing muscle mass. Second, only three studies reported related factors for malnutrition, and some subgroup analyses involved a limited number of studies, warranting cautious interpretation of these results. Future studies are warranted to investigate malnutrition-related factors and clinical outcomes in underrepresented cancer subtypes, such as breast cancer. Lastly, although this review includes studies from Europe, Asia, Oceania, and South America, the lack of data from North America and Africa somewhat limits the generalizability of our conclusions.

## Conclusion
Malnutrition prevalence is high among cancer patients, primarily due to tumor-related symptoms. It significantly increases the risk of poor clinical outcomes, with a more pronounced effect in patients suffering from severe malnutrition.
