## Abstract

## Purpose
Inguinal hernias are commonly encountered during robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP), either preoperatively or intraoperatively. Performing concurrent inguinal hernia repair (IHR) at the time of RARP may prevent the morbidity, cost, and inconvenience associated with a second operation. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of concurrent IHR during RARP compared with RARP alone.

## Methods
This study followed PRISMA guidelines and was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42025646245). Comprehensive searches of PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane CENTRAL were conducted to identify studies comparing outcomes of RARP with and without concurrent IHR. Primary outcomes included operative time, length of hospital stay, blood loss, and postoperative complications. A proportional meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model, and heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic.

## Results
Twenty studies comprising 1,402 patients who underwent concurrent IHR and 20,405 patients who underwent RARP alone were included. Concurrent IHR was associated with a statistically significant increase in operative time (mean difference 30.45 min; 95% CI 13.71–77.38) but showed no significant differences in postoperative complications, blood loss, or hospital stay. The pooled hernia recurrence rate was low (mean 1.9%), and Clavien-Dindo I–III complication rates were comparable between groups.

## Conclusion
Concurrent IHR during RARP may be feasible, with perioperative outcomes broadly comparable to RARP alone. Concurrent repair may be considered in selected patients and experienced centres however given study heterogeneity and the predominance of retrospective evidence, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

The trial was prospectively registered on PROSPERO on 03/02/2025 under ID: CRD42025646245.

## Introduction
Prostate cancer is amongst the most prevalent malignancies affecting men worldwide, particularly in aging populations [ ]. Robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) has been widely adopted as part of the treatment algorithm for localized prostate cancer due to its advantages in nerve sparing, reduced blood loss, and shorter length of stay when compared to open techniques [ ]. Concurrently, inguinal hernia (IH) is a frequently encountered comorbidity in this patient population, with studies estimating that 20% to 33% of men undergoing RARP also present with IH, either diagnosed preoperatively or incidentally during surgery [ , ].
Simultaneous management of IH during RARP presents an opportunity to improve patient outcomes by avoiding the morbidity, cost, and inconvenience associated with a second surgical intervention. However, the safety and efficacy of concurrent inguinal hernia repair (IHR) during RARP remain subjects of clinical debate, primarily due to concerns about potential mesh-related complications such as infection, seroma formation, or interference with the urethrovesical anastomosis [ , ]. These concerns have led to variability in surgical practice and the need for a comprehensive evaluation of outcomes.
Minimally invasive hernia repair techniques, particularly robotic transabdominal preperitoneal (rTAPP) and total extraperitoneal (TEP) approaches, have provided feasible options for performing IHR during RARP with minimal added operative time and complication risk. Multiple institutional reports and case series have demonstrated promising results, citing low complication and recurrence rates, as well as acceptable increases in operative time [ – ]. Moreover, no significant differences in estimated blood loss, hospital length of stay, or Clavien-Dindo classified complications have been consistently reported when comparing patients undergoing RARP alone versus those undergoing concurrent IHR [ , , – ].
A previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Melhem et al. consolidated evidence from thirteen studies and concluded that concurrent RARP and IHR is both safe and feasible, though limited by the heterogeneity of surgical techniques and relatively small sample sizes in the included studies [ ]. Since the publication of that review, several additional studies have reported outcomes from larger cohorts, multicentre settings, and with longer follow-up, warranting an updated and comprehensive evaluation [ – ].
The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to assess the safety and efficacy of concurrent IHR at the time of RARP. By synthesizing outcomes from the current evidence, we aim to offer a recommendation for the management of these commonly concurrent issues.

## Methods

## Registration and search strategy
The search was performed in concordance with the most recent Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations [ ]. The study was prospectively registered on 03/02/2025 on PROSPERO under the registration ID CRD42025646245. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from database inception to 15/02/2025. Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH] in PubMed, EMTREE in EMBASE) with free-text keywords related to robotic prostatectomy and inguinal hernia repair. Boolean operators, truncation, and field tags were applied as appropriate for each database. No language or publication status restrictions were applied.
In addition, grey literature sources were searched, including Google Scholar, ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global, and conference proceedings from the American Urological Association (AUA), European Association of Urology (EAU), and Society of International Urology (SIU). Clinical trial registries were searched via ClinicalTrials.gov, the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP), and the EU Clinical Trials Register.
The reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews were screened to identify additional eligible studies. The complete search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table .
A complete breakdown of analysed studies can be viewed in the PRISMA diagram.

 

## PRISMA flowchart
Inclusion criteria:

Retrospective or prospective study describing outcomes of patients undergoing RARP and concurrent IHR.
Adult patients.
All studies regardless of year were considered.

Exclusion criteria:

Case reports.
Review articles.
Studies describing outcomes of inguinal hernia repair procedures after previous prostatectomy, i.e. non-concurrent repair.
Studies not describing post-operative outcomes or not containing follow up.

## Identification of studies and outcomes of interest
All studies that met the selection criteria were included in this review. The studies were selected based on the following PICO criteria [ ]:

Population: Patients undergoing RARP.
Intervention: Concurrent IHR at time of RARP.
Comparison: RARP alone
Outcome: Safety outcomes and efficacy outcomes.

All studies were independently reviewed by three separate authors (JB, ME, EO) using Rayyan [ ]. If there was any disagreement amongst reviewers, then an alternate author (BMC) mediated discussion until consensus on suitability was reached. Rayyan’s auto resolver was used to assist with reviewing duplicate studies with studies having > 97% similarity excluded.
The primary outcome of interest was safety of concurrent repair assessed via length of stay, estimated blood loss and operative duration.
Secondary outcomes of interest included efficacy of hernia repairs (e.g. recurrence rates and rates of revision surgery) and post-operative complications.
Postoperative complications were categorised according to the Clavien–Dindo (CD) classification system: Grade I – deviation from the normal postoperative course not requiring pharmacological, surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention; Grade II – requiring pharmacological treatment; Grade III – requiring surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention; Grade IV – life-threatening complication requiring intensive care management [ ].

## Data extraction
Patient demographics and variables of interest were extracted from each study and directly transcribed using Google Sheets (Mountain View, California, United States). Seven independent authors (N.C., W.Q., M.E., A.D., R.N., I.H., and H.T.) were involved in data extraction.

## Study selection
This systematic review and meta-analysis included all retrospective and prospective studies that met the described inclusion and exclusion criteria.

## Risk of bias assessment
The risk of bias in non-randomised cohort studies was evaluated using a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale [ ]. This instrument appraises studies across multiple domains, categorising each as either ‘satisfactory’ or ‘unsatisfactory’, and applies a star-based scoring system to reflect methodological quality. Studies were assigned a total score from 0 to 9, with scores of 0–2 indicating low quality, 3–5 moderate quality, and 6–9 high quality. Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series. Each study was evaluated across 10 domains, including clarity of inclusion criteria, reliability of measurement, completeness of case inclusion, reporting of patient characteristics, clinical information, outcome reporting, and appropriateness of statistical analysis [ ]. The assessment was independently performed by two reviewers (RN and JB), and any discrepancies in scoring were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (BMC). The risk of bias assessment is available in Supplementary Tables and .

## Statistical analysis
A proportional meta-analysis was conducted for this review [ ]. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). The Freeman-Tukey transformation method was applied for the proportional meta-analysis. Continuous variables were analysed using weighted mean differences [ ]. Only comparative studies with two arms (RARP vs. RARP + IHR) were included in the meta-analysis with non-comparative case series excluded from all pooled analyses and summarised narratively. Results were presented with 95% confidence intervals, and a P-value of ≤ 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [ , ]. An I² value exceeding 50% was interpreted as significant heterogeneity. Given the variability in study designs, a random effects model was selected to account for this heterogeneity [ ]. Additionally, qualitative bias assessment was performed following the approach described by Barker et al., appropriate for proportional meta-analyses [ ]. Publication bias was assessed with funnel plots, constructed using the effect size and standard error for each study from the meta-analysis to visually evaluate symmetry around the pooled effect estimate [ ].

## Results

## Literature search
Following review of the literature and selection process application as outlined in Figs. and 20 papers were selected for this systematic review [ – , – , – , – ].
 

## Study characteristics
The included studies were published between 2007 and 2025 and included 1,402 patients undergoing concurrent IHR at time of RARP and 20,405 control patients. The largest study had 375 concurrent IHR and the smallest had 4 [ , ]. Visual inspection of the funnel plots demonstrated mild asymmetry, with a relative clustering of smaller studies on one side of the pooled effect estimate for certain outcomes, raising the possibility of small-study effects or publication bias (Supplementary to ).

## Patient characteristics
The average age of the patients in each study undergoing RARP alone was 62.39 ± 1.54 years and for those undergoing concurrent IHR was 63.89 ± 2.14 years. The average BMI in the RARP cohort was 27.41 ± 0.90 kg/m2 and in the concurrent IHR it was 26.74 ± 1.21 kg/m2. There was no statistically significant difference in the post-operative length of stay with a mean duration of 2.42 ± 1.46 days for RARP and 2.83 ± 1.99 days for concurrent IHR (Table  ; Fig.  ).
 

## Primary outcomes
Operative details were also analysed within these papers where data was available. There was a statistically significant increase in operative time of 30.45 min (95% CI 13.71–77.38 min) in the concurrent IHR group over RARP alone (Fig.  .) Blood loss was not significantly different between groups, with 138.00 ± 49.72 mL for RARP and 140.08 ± 59.42 mL for concurrent IHR (Fig.  ). Average time to follow-up was also similar between both groups, with 35.92 ± 2.58 months for RARP and 24.32 ± 22.52 months for concurrent IHR. This data is demonstrated in in Table  .
 

## Secondary outcomes
Hernia recurrence within the concurrent IHR group occurred in a mean of 0.9 ± 2.1 patients or 1.9% of the cohort. Complications were grouped based on Clavien-Dindo classification for both groups [ ]. Patients in the RARP group had a total of 440 Clavien-Dindo I complications with an average of 44 ± 93.5 (4.9%) while patients undergoing concurrent IHR had a total of 77 with an average of 4.1 ± 6.7 (7.6%). There were a total of 1309 Clavien-Dindo II complications in the RARP group with an average of 130.9 ± 3.05 (1.65%) while the concurrent IHR group had a total of 38 Clavien-Dindo II complications with an average of 1.9 ± 5.27 (7.9%). There was a total of 18 Clavien-Dindo III complications in the RARP group with an average of 1.8 ± 3.19 (0.8%) while the concurrent IHR group had a total of 8 Clavien-Dindo III complications with an average of 0.4 ± 1.11 (0.4%). ## Sensitivity analysis
Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses demonstrated that pooled estimates for all outcomes were robust to the exclusion of any single study. For the composite complication outcomes CD1 and CD2, there was no measurable heterogeneity (I² = 0%), and omission of any trial produced only minor fluctuations in effect size, with confidence intervals consistently crossing the null. CD3 showed moderate heterogeneity (I² = 45%), which decreased to < 5% when either the Nakamura or Xia studies were excluded [ , ].
Operative time exhibited extreme heterogeneity (I² = 99.5%), with the largest change observed on exclusion of the Rogers study (mean difference reduced from 30.5 to 24.2 min) [ ]. Estimated blood loss was also highly heterogeneous (I² = 95.1%), and exclusion of the Rogers study reduced heterogeneity to 0% and narrowed the confidence interval. Length of stay demonstrated minimal heterogeneity overall (I² ≈ 0%), with stable pooled estimates except for transient increases when Ludwig, Mourmouris, Peixoto, or Xia were omitted [ , , , ].

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the safety and efficacy of concurrent IHR performed at the time of RARP. Across 20 studies comprising 1,402 patients undergoing concurrent RARP and IHR, the results suggest that this combined approach is safe, effective, and does not significantly increase perioperative morbidity. These findings support the consideration of concurrent IHR as a standard surgical strategy in appropriately selected patients.
A key finding of this analysis was a statistically significant increase in operative time for patients undergoing concurrent IHR, with a mean difference of approximately 30 min.
This difference is largely attributable to the additional procedural steps required for hernia repair, including dissection and reduction of the hernia sac followed by mesh shaping and fixation [ , ]. Operative duration is further influenced by the laterality of repair, with bilateral repairs requiring substantially more time than unilateral procedures, and by variations in approach, as open or laparoscopic techniques generally take longer than robotic repairs [ , ]. In some series, logistical factors such as the involvement of an additional surgeon for the hernia portion and inter-surgeon handover have also contributed to prolonged operative time [ ]. While this increase is measurable, its clinical relevance appears limited given the lack of associated increase in blood loss, postoperative complications, or length of hospital stay. From a surgical planning perspective, this additional time may be acceptable, particularly when weighed against the broader benefits of avoiding a second surgical event as abdominal adhesions and subsequent adhesiolysis increase operative time and risk of gastrointestinal complications [ ].
Importantly, the length of stay and estimated blood loss were not significantly different between the RARP-only and RARP + IHR groups. These findings are clinically reassuring and suggest that the addition of a hernia repair does not result in extended recovery or greater perioperative risk. The similarity in post-operative outcomes further reinforces the feasibility of this approach, especially when performed in experienced hands and within centres familiar with minimally invasive hernia repair techniques.
Although no significant difference in hospital length of stay was observed between the cohorts in this review, contemporary perioperative care practices have likely influenced this outcome. In recent years, advances in anaesthetic techniques — including optimised multimodal analgesia, minimally sedating anaesthetic regimens, and improved intraoperative fluid management — have facilitated earlier mobilisation and discharge. Furthermore, the adoption of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols across urological procedures has standardised measures such as early oral intake, proactive catheter and drain removal, and structured postoperative mobilisation, all of which contribute to shorter inpatient stays. As highlighted by Li et al. in their meta-analysis of perioperative outcomes for minimally invasive versus open radical nephrectomy with venous tumour thrombectomy, such perioperative and anaesthetic advancements can substantially reduce hospitalisation durations, potentially attenuating any incremental effect of concurrent hernia repair on length of stay [ ].
The hernia recurrence rate observed in this review was low, averaging 1.9%. This aligns with recurrence rates reported in elective laparoscopic or robotic IHR series, underscoring the effectiveness of hernia repair performed in the setting of concurrent pelvic surgery [ ]. Additionally, the rate of Clavien-Dindo I–III complications did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that performing hernia repair with mesh during RARP does not increase the risk of early post-operative morbidity. This is particularly notable given the longstanding concerns about potential mesh-related complications in urological procedures, including infection, seroma formation, or interference with the urethrovesical anastomosis. In the current review, several series explicitly stated that no mesh-related complications occurred [ , , , – , , ]. There was only one mesh-associated event identified across all included studies was a self-limiting seroma [ ]. Taken together, these findings suggest that while theoretical concerns regarding mesh contamination during RARP persist, the available evidence indicates that clinically significant mesh-related morbidity in this setting is exceedingly rare.
Concurrent repair offers advantages over a staged approach, including avoidance of a second procedure, reduced overall healthcare utilisation, and lower patient inconvenience. Staged repairs may also expose patients to interim hernia-related symptoms or complications. While direct comparisons between concurrent and staged repairs were not performed in the included studies, the overall complication and recurrence profile of concurrent IHR in this meta-analysis compares similarly to historical data on elective hernia repairs, further supporting this combined approach [ ].
The cost-effectiveness of concurrent IHR, although not directly evaluated in this review, is another compelling consideration. The avoidance of an additional hospital admission, anaesthesia, and surgical event would likely translate into a lower cumulative healthcare cost. This is particularly relevant in health systems under increasing resource strain and aligns with contemporary principles of surgical efficiency and value-based care [ ].
The leave-one-out sensitivity analyses indicate that the pooled estimates for all outcomes are stable and not materially influenced by the exclusion of any single study. The lack of heterogeneity in CD1 and CD2 underscores the consistency of findings across these outcomes. In contrast, the moderate heterogeneity in CD3 was largely attributable to the Nakamura and Xia studies, suggesting these trials had methodological or population differences influencing variability. Additionally, several studies lacked baseline data for the control cohorts (e.g., BMI, ASA status, comorbidity indices), which may have introduced residual confounding and contributed to between-study heterogeneity.
For operative time and estimated blood loss, the Rogers study emerged as a key driver of heterogeneity, and its exclusion substantially reduced between-study variability—especially for blood loss, where I² fell to zero. This may be attributed to the delays resulting from a general surgeon not being immediately available to perform the hernia repair. This pattern highlights the potential influence of high-weight or outlier studies on meta-analytic results. The generally low heterogeneity for length of stay supports the reliability of this pooled estimate, though small studies appeared to cause temporary increases in variability when excluded. The inconsistent reporting of baseline data such as BMI and ASA also contributed to the overall heterogeneity. Collectively, these findings suggest that while the magnitude and direction of effects are robust, heterogeneity in certain outcomes is disproportionately driven by a small subset of studies, warranting cautious interpretation.
The funnel plots raise the possibility of small-study effects or publication bias. However, this observation should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of included studies for several outcomes, which reduces the reliability of visual assessment [ ].
One limitation of this study is the heterogeneity in use of and reporting of hernia repair technique across the included studies. Most series reported use of either robotic transabdominal preperitoneal (rTAPP), total extraperitoneal (TEP), or open approaches, with varying mesh materials and fixation methods. While all techniques appeared broadly safe and effective, this variability precluded a robust comparison to determine the superiority of one method over another. Standardisation in technique and reporting will be necessary to better inform best practices moving forward.
The strength of evidence included in this study is limited by the retrospective nature of all included studies. Additionally, long-term outcomes were underreported in many studies, limiting our ability to draw conclusions on late hernia recurrence or chronic mesh-related complications such as groin pain. Only a minority of studies reported follow-up beyond two years, highlighting a need for future longitudinal data.
Only studies evaluating concurrent inguinal hernia repair in the setting of robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy were identified for inclusion. While the clinical principles are likely to be similar for patients undergoing robotic-assisted simple prostatectomy; where a concomitant inguinal hernia may also be encountered - there is currently no high-level evidence addressing this scenario. Extrapolation of our findings to the benign disease setting should therefore be made with caution, and dedicated studies in the robotic simple prostatectomy population are warranted.
Despite these limitations, the current evidence suggests that concurrent IHR during RARP is a safe and effective strategy when performed by experienced surgical teams. Given the high prevalence of occult or pre-existing inguinal hernias in men undergoing radical prostatectomy, routine consideration of simultaneous repair is warranted. Routine reporting of the presence of inguinal hernia on multiparametric prostate MRI may assist surgeons with their pre-operative counselling of their patients.
Future research should focus on prospective studies comparing concurrent and staged repair, evaluating long-term outcomes including hernia recurrence, chronic pain, and functional parameters such as continence and quality of life. Randomised studies assessing the impact of repair technique and mesh type would also be valuable to inform standardisation and optimise patient outcomes.

## Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis signals that concurrent IHR during RARP may represent a feasible approach, with perioperative outcomes that appear broadly comparable to RARP alone. While a modest increase in operative time was observed, the available data does not indicate a clear rise in perioperative morbidity or length of stay. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the heterogeneity of the included studies, the predominance of retrospective designs, and limited long-term follow-up. Concurrent repair may be considered in appropriately selected patients and in centres with relevant expertise, but further high-quality prospective studies are required before definitive recommendations regarding routine adoption can be made.
