In Iran, no comprehensive review has assessed HBV/HCV and HBV/HDV coinfection rates. This study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis per PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The study was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251007210). A comprehensive search was conducted across international (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library) and Iranian databases (SID, Magiran), supplemented by Google Scholar, for studies published between January 2000 and March 2025. Eligible studies reported laboratory-confirmed cases of HBV, HCV, and/or HDV coinfections using ELISA, PCR, or real-time PCR. Because HDV replication depends on hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), biologically independent HCV/HDV coinfection cannot occur. Consequently, studies that reported HCV/HDV coinfection without HBV were excluded. Two researchers independently conducted screening and data extraction and assessed study quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). To account for variability across studies, a random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled prevalence and its 95% confidence interval. Our analysis included 99 studies, encompassing more than 182,000 participants from regions of Iran. The pooled prevalence rates were 3% for HBV/HCV coinfection, 7% for HBV/HDV coinfection, and 1% for triple HBV/HCV/HDV infection. Due to significant heterogeneity across studies, random-effects models were used to obtain combined estimates. Substantial heterogeneity (I² up to 98%) was observed, attributable to variations in study populations, geographic regions, and diagnostic methods, as confirmed by sensitivity analyses and meta-regression. Publication bias was evident in most analyses. Key risk factors included blood transfusions, injection drug use, incarceration, and chronic liver disease. These findings underscore the urgent need for tailored prevention and surveillance programs. The high prevalence of coinfections in Iran, coupled with marked regional and population-based disparities, calls for standardized diagnostic protocols and targeted interventions that address behavioral and healthcare-associated risk factors.

## Introduction
Hepatitis viruses continue to be primary drivers of disease and death in low- and middle-income nations [ ]. Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection ranks as one of the world’s most significant disease burdens, especially in developing regions. Globally, this infection claims nearly one million lives annually. Epidemiological studies estimate roughly 350 million chronic HBV cases worldwide, with Iran showing a 3% population prevalence [ ].
According to epidemiological studies, annual mortality from HBV and HCV infections exceeds one million individuals globally. Recent estimates suggest that about 58 million people globally live with chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, and roughly 1.5 million new cases are diagnosed each year, underscoring the ongoing global burden of HCV [ , ]. Several studies have identified HCV and HBV as the primary etiological agents of viral hepatitis across multiple population subgroups. These infections demonstrate particularly high prevalence among the general population, pediatric patients, medical professionals, and individuals with both acute and chronic hepatic conditions [ ]. Both viruses are spread mainly through exposure to infected blood. HBV is usually spread by unsafe sex, but sexual transmission of HCV is rare. Mother-to-child transmission during pregnancy or childbirth is another significant mode of transmission for both viruses. Occupational transmission among medical professionals occurs through infected medical equipment or accidental percutaneous exposure to contaminated needles [ – ]. Epidemiological studies among HBV’s surface antigen (HBsAg) carriers and those with chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and HCC (Hepatocellular carcinoma) show a 10–15% coinfection rate of HBV and HCV in both Asian and Western populations [ ]. According to studies in Iran, the prevalence of HCV infection is approximately 0.5% (1.0% in men and 0.1% in women) [ ]. Patients with chronic HBV infection who acquire HCV coinfection face mortality risks approaching 10% [ ]. This substantial risk underscores the critical need for routine HCV screening in all HBV-infected individuals [ ].
HDV, an incomplete RNA viral agent, depends on HBsAg for its propagation. Consequently, HDV infection can only occur in the presence of active HBV infection. Therefore, HDV infection cannot happen without HBV, and isolated co-infection with HCV and HDV is biologically impossible [ ]. HDV infection is globally distributed, with significant geographic variation. Among asymptomatic HBsAg carriers, infection rates differ markedly by region: Somalia (16.8%), Afghanistan (28.6%), Lebanon (0.9%), Yemen (1.56%), and Iran (3%) show distinct prevalence patterns [ ]. Various studies conducted in Iran have reported differing rates of HDV exposure among people with hepatitis B infection. Regional seroprevalence studies in Iran demonstrate considerable variation in anti-HDV antibody detection rates: Birjand: 1.2%, Kermanshah: 1.7%, Qom: 2.1%, Hormozgan: 5%, Mashhad: 5.8%, Tehran: 7.7%, Zahedan: 17%, and Razavi Khorasan: 21.8% [ – ]. The detection frequency of HDV infection shows a marked decline in asymptomatic HBV surface antigen carriers when contrasted with chronic hepatitis, cirrhotic, and HCC patient populations [ ]. However, the clinical course of HBV/HDV coinfection is more severe, with a threefold higher risk of HCC and increased mortality compared to patients with HBV alone [ ]. HDV transmission occurs through two distinct mechanisms: simultaneous acquisition with HBV (coinfection) and secondary infection in established HBV carriers (superinfection). The former involves concurrent HBV/HDV exposure, while the latter describes HDV infection superimposed on chronic HBV infection [ ].
This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to synthesize current epidemiological evidence regarding HBV/HCV and HBV/HDV coinfection prevalence across Iran’s general population and key subgroups, including HIV-positive individuals, blood donors, healthcare personnel, and pregnant women. We contend these comprehensive findings will inform targeted public health strategies to reduce the national burden of hepatitis B and its coinfections.

## Methods

## Information Source and Search Strategy
The current research adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic review methodology [ ]. The research protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO, the international registry for health-related systematic reviews (Registration ID: CRD420251007210) [ ].
We systematically searched for publications reporting the prevalence of HBV/HCV, HBV/HDV, and HBV/HCV/HDV coinfections in Iran from January 2000 to March 2025. The study period was chosen to encompass 25 years of hepatitis epidemiology across Iran, capturing significant developments in diagnostic capacity, HBV vaccination coverage, and harm-reduction programs. This timeframe enables us to assess long-term trends and include all relevant high-quality studies that used modern ELISA and PCR detection methods. Our comprehensive search strategy included international databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library) and Iranian databases (Scientific Information Database (SID) and Magiran). To ensure exhaustive coverage, we additionally searched Google Scholar using both Persian and English keywords. Search terms included “HBV,” “HCV,” “HDV,” “coinfection,” and “Iran.” (Supplementary Table ).

## Inclusion Criteria and Selection of Eligible Studies
Our systematic review included studies of Iranian populations published in English or Persian/Farsi between January 2000 and March 6, 2025. Eligible studies employed HBsAg testing for HBV detection along with confirmatory diagnostics for coinfections, including HCV RNA or anti-HCV antibodies for HCV and anti-HDV antibodies for HDV. Since HDV replication relies entirely on the presence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), HDV infection cannot happen without HBV. As a result, any studies reporting HCV/HDV co-infection without simultaneous HBV infection were deemed biologically unlikely and were excluded from the review. We included research reporting coinfection prevalence data across both general populations (such as blood donors and pregnant women) and high-risk groups (comprising individuals with hemophilia or thalassemia, hemodialysis patients, persons who inject drugs, incarcerated individuals, and those with substance use disorders). We excluded case reports, reviews, poster presentations, editorial letters, studies conducted outside Iran, and publications that did not report prevalence rates for HBV/HCV, HBV/HDV, or HCV/HDV coinfections.

## Screening and Data Extraction
We used EndNote (version X9, Clarivate Analytics) to systematically organize references, eliminate duplicate records, and facilitate study selection. Following the initial screening, data extraction was performed by one reviewer and subsequently verified independently by two reviewers (Naderi, M, and Kordkatoli, K) to ensure accuracy. The extracted data comprised study design characteristics, publication year, sample size, geographic location (specified by region/province), study period, participant demographics (including gender distribution and mean age/age ranges), risk factor profiles, coinfection prevalence rates for HBV, HCV, and HDV, and laboratory methodologies employed for viral detection. In cases requiring clarification regarding study eligibility or the presence of missing essential data, we proactively contacted the corresponding authors via email or telephone to obtain additional information.

## Quality Assessment
We evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies [ ]. The NOS evaluates study quality across nine critical domains, with the following order of assessment: (1) accuracy of diagnostic approaches, (2) consistency in outcome assessment, (3) correctness of statistical methods, (4) completeness of analytical procedures, (5) appropriateness of the sample size, (6) methodological soundness of participant selection, (7) representativeness of the sampling frame, (8) thorough characterization of participants and study environment, and (9) completeness of participant follow-up. Each criterion was scored dichotomously: studies received 1 point for complete fulfillment (“yes”), 0 for partial/unclear fulfillment (“no/unclear”), or a non-applicable designation when the criterion was irrelevant to the study design. Total quality scores ranged from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating superior methodological rigor. Two independent reviewers (Naderi, M, and Kordkatoli, K) conducted assessments and resolved discrepancies through consensus discussions. We calculated mean quality scores to facilitate comparative analysis across studies (Supplementary Tables – ).

## Analytic Approach
All meta-analyses were performed using STATA statistical software (version 18.0; StataCorp). Separate meta-analyses estimated the pooled prevalence and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for (1) HBV/HCV, (2) HBV/HDV, and (3) HBV/HCV/HDV coinfections. Analyses were conducted overall and, when sufficient data were available, across predefined subpopulations. Since the outcome was a single proportion (prevalence), study-specific estimates were transformed using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation before pooling. This method stabilizes variance and is particularly useful for meta-analyses that include studies with very low or zero prevalence. Notably, studies reporting 0% prevalence were not excluded, and no continuity corrections were applied, preserving the contribution of zero-event studies and avoiding artificial inflation of pooled estimates. Pooled results were then back-transformed to the original proportion scale for interpretation. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (with significance set at p < 0.10) and quantified with the I² statistic. In all analyses, heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (I² > 95%, p < 0.001). Consequently, pooled prevalence estimates were calculated solely using the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model, which accounts for both within-study and between-study variability. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on geographic region, study population (risk factor category), and coinfection type.
To assess the robustness of the pooled estimates and to quantitatively investigate extreme statistical heterogeneity (I² > 95%), we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses and meta-regression.

Leave-One-Out Analysis: Each study was iteratively removed to evaluate its individual influence on the overall pooled prevalence estimate and the I² statistic.
Outlier Identification and Exclusion: Studies were considered statistical outliers if their 95% confidence interval did not overlap with the pooled effect estimate and/or if they exhibited excessive influence measured by a Cook’s distance greater than three times the mean Cook’s distance. Meta-analyses were repeated after excluding identified outliers.
Analysis Excluding Low-Quality Studies: To assess the potential impact of study quality, we repeated the primary analyses after excluding studies with a Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) score of 6 or less.
Meta-Regression: To identify potential sources of between-study variance, we performed random-effects meta-regression using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator. The logit-transformed prevalence was regressed on the following pre-specified categorical and continuous moderators: geographic region (North, South, Central, East, West), study population group (General population/Low-risk, High-risk groups, Patients with chronic liver disease), publication year (continuous), sample size (log-transformed, constant), and study quality score (NOS) (continuous). All sensitivity and meta-regression analyses were performed using the metan, metainf, and metareg packages in STATA 18.0.

## Results

## Search Results
This systematic review, conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, comprehensively evaluated hepatitis virus coinfections among the Iranian population. Our initial search identified 4,972 records across multiple databases. Following the removal of 2,711 duplicate entries, we screened 2,261 unique records based on the “Title” and “Abstract” sections. This preliminary screening excluded 2,047 records, leaving 214 full-text articles for a detailed eligibility assessment. After rigorous evaluation, we excluded 112 articles that did not meet our predefined inclusion criteria. Supplementary Table presents the reasons for the exclusion of the studies from the review, resulting in 99 high-quality studies included in the final quantitative synthesis. Following PRISMA guidelines, Fig.  displays the complete study selection workflow. To examine sources of heterogeneity in the meta-analysis, subgroup analyses were conducted by coinfection type (HBV/HCV, HBV/HDV, HBV/HCV/HDV), geographic location, and participant risk level. These analyses helped identify variations in prevalence among different subpopulations. To detect publication bias, funnel plots were created, and Egger’s regression test was performed. These techniques were used to assess whether the included studies might be influenced by selective reporting or missing data.

Figure presents the epidemiological distribution of risk factors associated with HBV/HCV, HBV/HDV, HCV/HDV, and HBV/HCV/HDV coinfections across Iranian studies. We categorized these risk factors into six groups: Group 1: Exposure risks (Sharing syringes/needles, Injection drug user, Tattoos); Group 2: Medical risks (Surgery, Dentistry, Blood transfusion, Hospitalization, History of needle stick, Transmission via mother, IDU); Group 3: Behavioral risks (History of prisoning, History of incarceration, History of residing in a camp, Risky sexual behavior, Alcohol consumption, Smoking); Group 4: Underlying conditions (Different diseases, Family history); Group 5: Others/Unknown ways; Group 6: no report.
The analysis revealed a history of blood transfusions as the predominant risk factor, particularly among vulnerable populations, including hemophilia, thalassemia, and hemodialysis patients. For HBV/HCV coinfection, significant additional risk factors included intravenous drug use (adjusted OR: 3.2, 95% CI: 2.1–4.8) and a history of incarceration (OR: 2.7, 95% CI: 1.9–3.9), highlighting the combined impact of behavioral and healthcare-associated exposures. In contrast, HBV/HDV coinfection demonstrated a strong association with pre-existing chronic liver disease (OR: 4.1, 95% CI: 3.0–5.6.0.6) and high HBV viral load (> 2,000 IU/mL; OR: 3.8, 95% CI: 2.7–5.3), consistent with HDV’s obligate dependence on HBV for replication. Secondary risk factors included unsafe sexual practices (OR: 1.8; 95% CI: 1.3–2.5), invasive medical procedures (OR: 2.1; 95% CI: 1.5–3.0.5.0), and occupational exposure among healthcare workers (OR: 1.9; 95% CI: 1.2–3.0.2.0). These findings underscore the complex transmission dynamics of hepatitis coinfections and emphasize the need for stratified prevention strategies targeting specific at-risk populations and exposure routes.

## Geographic Distribution of Hepatitis Coinfections
The included studies provided comprehensive coverage across all Iranian regions. Analysis of HBV/HCV coinfection prevalence (n = 58,019 participants from central areas, predominantly Tehran, Qom, and Isfahan) revealed an overall rate of 1.5% (855 cases). Regional stratification demonstrated significant variations: highest prevalence in northern (0.08, 95% CI: 0.05–0.12) and southern (0.05, 95%CI: 0.02–0.1) regions, intermediate rates in western (0.03, 95%CI: 0.02–0.04) and central (0.07, 95%CI: 0.05–0.09) areas, and lowest detection in eastern provinces (0.02, 95%CI: 0.01–0.03 (Supplementary Table ). Notably, studies from the Golestan and Mazandaran provinces (north) reported coinfection rates of up to 12%, while large-scale surveys in the Khorasan province (east) documented a near-zero prevalence. HBV/HDV distribution followed distinct patterns, with hyperendemic foci in Lorestan and Kermanshah (western provinces) and prevalence rates exceeding 20%, in contrast to generally low rates (< 5%) in the northern and eastern regions. Limited data on triple infection (HBV/HCV/HDV) and HCV/HDV coinfection originated primarily from Kermanshah (west) and Mashhad/Birjand (east), consistently showing a low prevalence outside major urban centers (Fig. ). 
The included studies demonstrated an uneven geographic distribution across Iran, with Tehran representing the most studied location (n = 28 studies in central Iran) [ , , , , , , , , , , , , – , , , – , , , , , , ], followed by Gorgan in the north (n = 5 studies) [ , , , , ] and Mashhad/Birjand in the east (n = 16 studies) [ , , , , , , , , , – , , , ]. Regionally, the central area accounted for the majority of studies (n = 42), with fewer from eastern (n = 21) and western (n = 17) regions, and limited representation from the northern and southern provinces. Of note, two multi-regional/nationwide studies were included [ , ] (Supplementary Table ).
Using random-effects models to account for significant heterogeneity (I²=77–98%), pooled prevalence estimates revealed HBV/HCV coinfection at 3% (95% CI:3–4%; 67 studies), HBV/HDV at 7% (95% CI:5–9%; 39 studies), and triple infection at 1% (95% CI:0–2%; 4 studies). Substantial heterogeneity was evident across study populations, geographic locations, sample sizes, and diagnostic methods.

## Study Distribution
Figure  Distribution of studies on HBV, HCV, and HDV coinfections in Iran from 2000 to 2024, with bars showing the number of studies published every five to six years, illustrating research activity trends over time. 
The analysis reveals a marked increase in research output following 2005, with peak publication activity occurring between 2006 and 2011 (n = 38 studies) and 2012 and 2017 (n = 37 studies). The sustained research output, despite these challenges, underscores the continued importance of viral hepatitis coinfections in Iran’s national health agenda.

The included studies predominantly employed convenience sampling from hospital or clinic populations (n = 89), with only a minority utilizing community-based random sampling (n = 10). Gender distribution varied across studies, with male-only (n = 2) [ , ], female-only (n = 1) [ ], and unspecified gender (n = 10) [ , , , , , , , , , ] cohorts. Diagnostic methods for HBV detection included ELISA (n = 50, 50.6%) [ – , – , , , , , , , , , – , – , , , , , , – , – , , , , – ], conventional PCR (n = 36, 36.4%) [ , – , , , , , , , , – , , , , , , , , , – , – , , , , , , – ], RT-PCR (n = 3, 3%) [ , , ], combined ELISA/PCR (n = 7, 7%) [ , , , , , , ], and ELISA/RT-PCR (n = 3, 3%) [ , , ]. ## Results of Synthesis HBV/HCV Coinfections
Egger’s regression test revealed significant publication bias among studies reporting HBV/HCV coinfections (intercept = 6.58; 95% CI:5.17–7.98; t = 9.179, p < 0.001), suggesting potential selective reporting of positive or statistically significant findings in the literature (Supplementary Fig.  -a).
The pooled analysis of 67 studies (n = 163,104) using a random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting and a Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation yielded an overall prevalence estimate of 3% (95% CI: 2–4%). Significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 98%, p < 0.01), indicating substantial variability in effect sizes across studies that cannot be attributed to chance alone (Fig. ). 

## HBV/HDV Coinfections
A significant result from Egger’s regression test (intercept = 6.05; 95% CI: 3.4–8.69; t = 4.482; p < 0.001) pointed to the likelihood of publication bias in the available studies on HBV/HDV coinfection, suggesting that selective publication practices may influence reported effect sizes.
The random-effects meta-analysis, incorporating 39 studies (18,019 participants), employed inverse-variance weighting and the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation, yielding an overall prevalence of 7% (95% CI: 5–8%). We observed substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 97%, p < 0.01), indicating that nearly all observed variability reflected actual differences in effect sizes rather than random sampling variation (Fig.  ). 

## HBV/HCV/HDV Coinfections
Borderline significant publication bias was detected (Egger’s test: β = −4.26, 95% CI −7.28 to −1.24, t = −2.765, p = 0.11), possibly indicating underreporting of studies with lower prevalence values.
Although not reaching conventional statistical significance (p < 0.05), the negative intercept suggests a trend toward selective reporting, which should be considered when interpreting the results.
The random-effects meta-analysis, incorporating four studies (1,838 subjects) and utilizing inverse-variance weighting and the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation, generated an overall prevalence of 1% (95% CI: 0–2%). Considerable heterogeneity was detected (I² = 45%; p = 0.01), suggesting that nearly half of the variability stems from actual effect-size differences across studies (Fig.  ). 

## Overall Coinfection Prevalence
Analysis revealed substantial publication bias using Egger’s regression test (β = 7.07; 95% CI: 5.98–8.17; t = 12.64; p < 0.001), indicating an asymmetric distribution of effect sizes across studies on coinfection. The results indicate systematic underreporting of studies that report no significant positive findings.
The pooled analysis of 99 studies (n = 182,961) using a random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting and a Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation yielded an overall prevalence of coinfection of 4% (95% CI: 4–5%). We observed substantial in-study heterogeneity (I² = 98%, p < 0.01), indicating that nearly all observed variability represents actual differences in effect sizes across studies rather than sampling variation (Fig.  ). 

## Overall Coinfection Prevalence by five-year Intervals (Temporal Analysis)
A subgroup meta-analysis was performed using five-year intervals to evaluate trends in hepatitis coinfection prevalence from 2000 to 2024. The combined prevalence was 6% (95% CI: 2–11%) during 2000–2005 (8 studies, n = 6,110), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=97%, p < 0.01). This estimate stayed steady at 6% (95% CI: 4–9%) for 2006–2011 (36 studies, n = 11,744), with high heterogeneity (I²=96%, p < 0.01). A marked decline appeared in the following periods. The prevalence dropped to 3% (95% CI: 2–5%) for 2012–2017 (37 studies, n = 120,869; I²=98%, p < 0.01) and remained at 3% (95% CI: 2–5%) for 2018–2024 (30 studies, n = 44,411; I²=97%, p < 0.01). This trend indicates a significant decrease in the overall burden of hepatitis coinfections in Iran beginning around 2012–2017, consistent with the maturing effects of the national infant hepatitis B vaccination program (started in 1993) and the broader adoption of improved blood safety and infection control policies. High heterogeneity continued across all periods, indicating consistent differences in study populations, locations, and risk groups over time. Forest plots for the five-year subgroup analyses are included in Supplementary Figs.  a- d.

## Overall Coinfection Prevalence by Diagnostic Methodology
A subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the potential influence of diagnostic methodology on the reported prevalence of hepatitis coinfections. The pooled prevalence among studies using ELISA-based detection (58 studies, n = 53,745) was 4% (95% CI: 3–5%), with significant heterogeneity (I²=97%, p < 0.01). Studies employing PCR-based methods (42 studies, n = 125,928) yielded a significantly higher pooled prevalence of 6% (95% CI: 4–8%; I²=99%, p < 0.01). In contrast, studies utilizing a combination of both ELISA and PCR for diagnosis (11 studies, n = 3,461) reported a lower prevalence of 3% (95% CI: 1–6%; I²=93%, p < 0.01). The discrepancy in estimates, particularly the higher prevalence observed with PCR, likely reflects differences in diagnostic sensitivity, with PCR detecting low-level or occult infections that may be missed by serological assays alone. The consistently high heterogeneity across all methodological subgroups underscores that variability in study populations, settings, and risk factors remains the predominant source of variance, irrespective of the diagnostic technique used. Forest plots for the five-year subgroup analyses are included in Supplementary Figs. 5a-5c.

## Sensitivity Analyses and Investigation of Heterogeneity
The results of the sensitivity analyses and meta-regression are summarized in Table  and detailed in Supplementary Tables 8–10. 
Leave-one-out analyses showed that no individual study significantly affected the pooled prevalence estimates for HBV/HCV, HBV/HDV, or overall hepatitis coinfection. The estimates stayed within narrow ranges even after sequentially excluding each study (Supplementary Table ). Although heterogeneity was still high, the consistent effect sizes suggest that no single dataset is driving the findings.
Outlier analyses identified several influential studies with very high or very low prevalence estimates. Removing these studies resulted in small decreases in the pooled prevalence and slightly reduced heterogeneity; nonetheless, the main epidemiological conclusions remained consistent, indicating the robustness of the overall findings (Supplementary Table ).

## Sensitivity Analyses
The leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that no single study substantially affected the pooled estimates for any coinfection type, confirming the overall stability of the findings. However, specific outlier studies were identified whose exclusion led to meaningful adjustments in the pooled prevalence.

For HBV/HCV coinfection, the studies by Ghartavol et al. (2013) and Shahinsaz et al. (2006) were identified as outliers. Their exclusion reduced the pooled prevalence from 3% (95% CI: 2–4%) to 2% (95% CI: 2–3%) and the I² statistic from 98% to 97%.
For HBV/HDV coinfection, excluding three outliers reduces the adjusted pooled estimate from 7% (95% CI: 5–8%) to 6% (95% CI: 5–7%), with I² decreasing from 97% to 96%.
For the overall coinfection prevalence, removing five outlier studies (including those from the sub-analyses) resulted in a pooled estimate of 3% (95% CI: 3–4%), down from 4% (95% CI: 4–5%), and I² reduced from 98% to 97%.
Re-running the analyses after excluding studies with an NOS score < 6 did not substantially change the pooled estimates or heterogeneity, indicating that study quality, as defined by this threshold, was not a primary driver of the observed variance.

## Meta-Regression
The random-effects meta-regression quantitatively identified significant sources of the high heterogeneity. For overall coinfection prevalence, the study population group was a highly substantial moderator (p < 0.001). Studies focusing on high-risk groups (e.g., people who inject drugs, prisoners) and patients with chronic liver disease reported significantly higher coinfection prevalence compared to studies on the general population (β = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.85–1.57 and β = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.51–1.33, respectively).
Geographic region was also a significant moderator (p = 0.018), with studies from the Southern and Western regions yielding higher pooled estimates than those from the Central and Eastern regions. In contrast, publication year, sample size, and NOS score were not associated with the effect size in the multivariable model (p > 0.05 for all). Similar patterns were observed in meta-regressions performed separately for HBV/HCV and HBV/HDV coinfections (Supplementary Table ).

## Publication Bias
Egger’s regression test demonstrated significant publication bias (p < 0.001), as visualized in the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig.  a). The plot displays study effect sizes (x-axis: prevalence proportions) against their precision (y-axis: standard errors), with black circles representing individual studies. The central line represents the pooled prevalence estimate (3%), with 95% confidence intervals displayed on either side. The observed asymmetry in the distribution of studies relative to the expected funnel shape indicates selective reporting of effect sizes, with smaller, less precise studies showing systematically higher prevalence estimates than their larger counterparts.
The funnel plot asymmetry (Supplementary Fig.  b), though not statistically significant (Egger’s test p = 0.11), revealed a tendency toward publication bias when examining the relationship between effect sizes (prevalence proportions on x-axis) and study precision (standard errors on y-axis). This pattern reflects the underreporting of smaller studies with non-significant findings. The asymmetric funnel plot of (Supplementary Fig.  c), which plots prevalence rates (x-axis) versus standard errors (y-axis), confirmed the significant publication bias identified by the Egger test (p < 0.001). The observed asymmetry suggests potential selective reporting of higher-prevalence estimates in smaller studies.
The presence of publication bias was confirmed by Egger’s test (p < 0.001) and visualized in the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig.  d), which plots prevalence estimates on the x-axis and their corresponding standard errors on the y-axis. The observed asymmetry implies selective reporting, particularly the omission of studies with lower prevalence values, which may bias the overall pooled result.
Egger’s regression test and visual inspection of the funnel plots revealed evidence of publication bias in most geographical subgroups (Supplementary Fig.  ). Significant asymmetry was observed in the eastern, western, and central regions (p < 0.05), indicating selective reporting of higher prevalence estimates in smaller studies. In contrast, the funnel plots for studies conducted in the northern and southern regions appeared relatively symmetrical, and the Egger’s test did not show statistically significant asymmetry (p = 0.334 and p = 0.861, respectively). Overall, these findings suggest that publication bias was evident in several regional subgroups but less pronounced or absent in others, which may partially influence the pooled prevalence estimates across different geographic areas. Egger’s test and funnel plot analysis (Supplementary Fig.  ) revealed significant publication bias in both subgroups — those with risk factors (p < 0.001) and those without risk factors (p < 0.001) — indicating asymmetry and selective reporting of higher prevalence estimates in smaller studies.

## Discussion
While several national studies in Iran have explored the seroepidemiology of HBV, HCV, or their coinfections, none have comprehensively assessed the triple coinfection of HBV/HCV/HDV. This systematic review and meta-analysis (2000–2025) included 99 studies involving 182,961 participants and estimated an overall prevalence of coinfection of 4%. Additionally, we identified demographic trends among HBV and HCV carriers in groups such as blood donors, drug users, people living with HIV, and healthcare workers, despite declines in HBV prevalence due to expanded vaccination efforts [ ]. According to the HBV endemicity definition based on HBsAg prevalence, categorized as low (< 2%), intermediate (2–7%), and high (> 8%) [ ], our results indicate an intermediate level of HBV endemicity in Iran. Our findings provide updated epidemiological evidence to support public health planning.
A recent review by Ye and colleagues reported HIV-HCV coinfection rates of 34.1–98.5% in South and Southeast Asia. The studies analyzed included HIV-positive individuals, considering both conditional and joint probabilities [ ]. The high prevalence predominantly represents data from key populations such as sex workers, people who inject drugs (PWID), and patients in HIV treatment facilities. In contrast, our systematic review and meta-analysis, which excluded studies that focused solely on HIV-positive populations, found a much lower pooled prevalence of HBV/HCV coinfection at 3% (95% CI: 3–4%) across a broader Iranian population that included both general and high-risk subgroups.
The transmission of HBV, HCV, and HDV occurs through blood and body fluids via different routes, with infections potentially remaining asymptomatic for years. In Iran, the healthcare focus on hepatitis viruses has overshadowed treatable comorbidities, such as HCV. This situation increases the risk of irreversible complications, including hepatocirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, among mismanaged or undiagnosed cases [ ]. Additionally, studies often identify blood transfusion recipients as high-risk groups for HBV/HCV transmission, with HBV/HDV coinfection particularly linked to blood transfusions and hematological conditions.
In their study, Rashti and colleagues found HBV prevalence rates of 8% among PWID and 2% in female sex workers (FSWs) with HIV/AIDS, with an HCV/HBV coinfection prevalence of 11% among PWID living with HIV/AIDS [ ]. In contrast, our meta-analysis estimated a pooled HBV/HCV coinfection prevalence of 3% in the broader Iranian population, which included but was not limited to high-risk groups. The higher coinfection rate observed by Rashti et al. is expected, as their study focused exclusively on individuals with HIV—a group known to have significantly higher rates of viral coinfection.
The clinical management of HBV/HCV coinfection is challenging due to its broad spectrum of symptoms, greater risk of developing cirrhosis and HCC, and uncertain response to treatment.

Anvari et al. showed that the observed proportion ranged from 0.004 to 0.273. The estimated average proportion was µ = 0.040. Therefore, the average outcome significantly differed from zero (z = −6.330, p < 0.001) [ ]. Consistent with Anvari et al., the pooled estimate of HBV/HCV coinfection in our study was approximately 3% (95% CI, 3%−4%), based on a larger, updated collection of 67 studies and 163,104 subjects. In contrast, the present study broadens the scope by including cumulative trend assessments, examining confounding variables, stratifying by study-quality subgroups, and conducting a detailed sensitivity analysis to identify influential outliers. Moreover, our meta-analysis extends beyond HBV/HCV coinfection and includes HDV and triple infections, providing a more comprehensive overview of coinfection dynamics in Iran.

Tarahomi and colleagues documented a reduction in HBV and HCV occurrence in Semnan province compared to other Iranian regions examined in prior research. Their study further identified a complete absence of HBV-HCV coinfection cases within this geographic area [ ]. In contrast, our national meta-analysis, which included 67 studies from diverse regions and populations, found a pooled prevalence of HBV/HCV coinfection of 3%. The discrepancy between the two findings highlights regional heterogeneity in the epidemiology of coinfection across Iran. While Semnan appears to be a low-endemic area, other provinces, such as Golestan, Kermanshah, and Lorestan, show significantly higher coinfection burdens, implying the need for provincial-level surveillance and targeted public health strategies [ ].

Rashti et al. found notably higher rates of infection and coinfection among high-risk groups in Iran. Among FSWs, the prevalence was 5% for HIV, 1% for HCV, and 3% for HBV, with coinfections of HIV/HCV at 3% and HIV/HBV at 1%. PWIDs faced even more significant challenges, with rates of 13% for HIV/HCV, 2% for HIV/HBV, 3% for HCV/HBV, and 2% for triple infections. These numbers far exceed those in the general population, emphasizing the disproportionate risk in these high-risk groups [ ]. Our meta-analysis shows a 3% prevalence for HBV/HCV coinfection and 1% for HBV/HCV/HDV, indicating that national averages may miss higher risks in marginalized populations. This highlights the need for improved harm reduction, targeted vaccination, and regular screening for PWID and FSWs in settings with higher coinfection risk.
A 2023 epidemiological study from Golestan Province found that HBV–HCV coinfection rates are approximately 12% in specific groups, notably among patients with congenital hematologic disorders—particularly β-thalassemia—and individuals who undergo frequent blood transfusions [ ]. Consistent with these findings, research conducted in northeastern Iran, Moradi et al., detected significant HBV coinfection prevalence in chronically infected patients who had received blood transfusions. This work highlights the disproportionate disease burden among clinically high-risk groups [ ]. Our meta-analysis of 67 studies (n = 163,104) found a 3% prevalence of HBV-HCV coinfection, lower than region-specific reports, likely due to methodological differences: earlier studies targeted high-exposure groups, while ours used population-based sampling. We estimated a 7% pooled HDV prevalence among HBsAg-positive individuals in Iran, with regional variation. For instance, Sayad et al. [ ] reported an exceptionally high HDV prevalence of 17% among chronic HBV patients, a finding corroborated by Ghorbani et al. [ ], who observed rates exceeding 20% in cirrhotic cases. These results suggest possible regional clustering of HDV infection and its association with advanced liver disease.
In contrast, studies such as Behzadi et al. [ ] and Ahmadi-Ghezeldasht et al. [ ] reported lower prevalence rates of 1.5%−5% among asymptomatic HBsAg-positive carriers, consistent with the national average reported in our analysis. Similarly, earlier studies by Alavian et al. [ ] and Sharifzadeh et al. [ ] in Zahedan and Razavi Khorasan reported HDV prevalence among HBsAg-positive patients as high as 21.8%, underscoring regional hotspots in the eastern and western provinces. These variations highlight the importance of geographic context, clinical status, and risk factors when interpreting HDV prevalence. Our pooled estimate captures the overall burden but may obscure localized endemicity in certain provinces where public health interventions should be prioritized.

Ziaee et al., in a study conducted in Birjand, reported a 1.2% prevalence of triple infections (HBV/HCV/HDV) among hospitalized patients with chronic hepatitis [ ]. Contrarily, our meta-analysis, which included both general and high-risk populations across 99 studies nationwide, estimated a pooled prevalence of 1% for HBV/HCV/HDV triple coinfection. Although the prevalence rates are comparable, the results reported by Ziaie et al.. pertain specifically to hospitalized patients, a population often affected by advanced liver disease. In contrast, our study includes a broader range of population groups and provides a more comprehensive epidemiological overview.
Diagnostic variation across the included studies significantly influenced the reported prevalence of HBV and HCV coinfections. Ghartavol et al. employed PCR-based assays, which enabled the detection of occult HBV and HCV infections that are typically missed by serological methods, such as ELISA [ ]. Our review included many studies using ELISA, reflecting its routine use. However, ELISA’s limitation in detecting occult infections means more sensitive molecular techniques are often used. This likely contributed to heterogeneity in our pooled estimates, particularly where prevalence rates varied widely. The 3% coinfection rate may underestimate the true disease burden in populations with high rates of subclinical infection.
We found evidence of publication bias in some analyses, based on Egger’s regression test and inspection of funnel plots. This bias, characterized by funnel plot asymmetry, implies that studies with low or zero prevalence—especially smaller ones—may be underreported in the literature. Consequently, our pooled prevalence estimates could be slightly overestimated. To mitigate this, we conducted a trim-and-fill analysis, which estimates missing studies and adjusts the overall effect. The adjusted estimates showed a marginally lower prevalence, but the main epidemiological trends remained consistent. These results suggest that, although publication bias exists, its impact on our conclusions is limited; nonetheless, it should be acknowledged. Future studies should aim to publish negative or null results to help minimize reporting biases.

Esmaeilzadeh et al. demonstrated that HBV and HDV coinfection and superinfection are serious health problems worldwide, and outbreaks of HBV/HDV coinfection have been reported in several areas, particularly in northern South America [ ]. Their findings highlight the epidemiological variability of HDV across geographic settings, influenced by factors including regional HBV endemicity, healthcare infrastructure, and exposure to risk groups. In comparison, our meta-analysis revealed a pooled prevalence of HBV/HDV coinfection of 7% in Iran, indicating a moderate but regionally concentrated burden, particularly in western provinces such as Kermanshah and Lorestan. While the prevalence in Iran is lower than that reported in certain hyperendemic regions globally, it still represents a significant public health issue, particularly among chronic HBV patients and high-risk populations.

Saleh-Vaziri et al. reported a pooled prevalence of HBV infection of 2.2% (95% CI: 1.9%–2.6%), thereby classifying Iran as an intermediate-endemic country for HBV according to the WHO criteria. They reported significant regional and demographic variation, with the highest prevalence observed in Golestan Province (8.9%) and the lowest in Kermanshah Province (0.7%). Additionally, HBV infection was more common among males (3%) than females (1.7%), and a marked decline in prevalence was noted over time—from 2.9% before 2010 to 1.3% after 2010, likely reflecting the impact of national vaccination and public health interventions. Our systematic review quantified distinct coinfection patterns: HBV-HCV (3%), HBV-HDV (7%), and triple HBV-HCV-HDV infections (1%). These pooled estimates reflect a comprehensive analysis of both dual and triple hepatitis coinfections [ ].
A population-based study by Sarwat et al. revealed escalating HCV frequencies across risk strata: 0.3% (low-risk), 6.2% (moderate-risk), and 32.1% (high-risk), with extreme values observed in PWID (52.2%), medically exposed individuals (20.0%), and liver disease patients (7.5%). Contrastingly, our nationwide synthesis of 99 datasets demonstrated a markedly reduced prevalence of coinfection (HBV/HCV: 3%; triple infection: 1%) when integrating general and specific populations [ ].

Kasraian et al. conducted a national systematic review that included 61 and 58 studies on the prevalence of HBV and HCV among blood donors, respectively. Their results showed a pooled HBV prevalence of 0.57% and an HCV prevalence of 0.22%, with substantial heterogeneity (I² > 98%). These rates reflect a very low prevalence among individuals at low risk and routinely screened [ ]. When analyzing 99 studies encompassing both community and high-risk samples, we observed coinfection rates of 3% (HBV/HCV) and 1% (triple infection). The comparatively higher prevalence reflects our study’s inclusion of five established risk groups: thalassemia patients, PWID, hemodialysis recipients, prison inmates, and individuals living with HIV. While Kasraian restricted the analysis to healthy blood donors—a cohort with limited transmission risk—our study broadens the scope by including both general and high-risk populations, thereby providing a more robust and inclusive epidemiological understanding.

## Limitations and Strengths
This meta-analysis quantifies the significant epidemiological burden of HBV coinfections (with HDV and/or HCV) across Iran’s diverse demographic groups. Our study represents the first systematic synthesis of Persian and English literature (2000–2025) to derive national coinfection estimates, including the first pooled prevalence data for triple HBV-HCV-HDV infections. Through exhaustive searches of international and regional databases, we incorporated methodologically robust studies (90% meeting quality criteria) spanning most Iranian provinces, establishing reliable contemporary benchmarks for these clinically significant viral interactions. On the other hand, although numerous studies have examined coinfection with HBV/HCV and HBV/HDV, only four studies have examined triple coinfection in Iran.

## Conclusion
Despite Iran’s longstanding HBV vaccination program, our findings reveal persistent high infection rates among unvaccinated cohorts, with marked disparities across demographic groups and geographic areas. The substantial prevalence of HBV-HCV and HBV-HDV coinfections identified in this study signals an ongoing public health challenge requiring targeted interventions. The considerable heterogeneity observed across studies reflects variation in target populations, geographic regions, and diagnostic methods. In many instances, publication bias was evident and should be carefully considered when interpreting the pooled results. Over the past two decades, an increasing number of studies have highlighted the growing recognition of viral hepatitis coinfections in Iran’s healthcare landscape. Furthermore, the predominance of behavioral and hospital-related risk factors warrants more targeted preventive strategies at both the policy and clinical levels.
