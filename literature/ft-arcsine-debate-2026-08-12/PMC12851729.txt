## Abstract

## Background:
The aim of this systematic review was to evaluate the survival rates of computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing ceramic dental restorations.

## Methods:
The study followed PRISMA guidelines and the study protocol was pre-registered on the PROSPERO platform (registration number: CRD42024599053). Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies with a minimum follow-up period of 1-year were included. The search period was set from 2000 to May 2024. A Comprehensive Literature Search was Performed in MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. Additionally, references list of relevant reviews and full-text articles were hand-searched to identify potentially eligible studies.

## Results:
A total of 714 articles were identified in the initial search. After screening titles and abstracts, 31 studies were selected for full-text review. Following the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 25 studies-comprising 13 RCTs and 12 cohort studies-were incorporated into the systematic review. Quality assessment and risk of bias were conducted using 2 established scales. Regarding survival rates, the overall heterogeneity (I2) among the RCTs was calculated at 23% (P = .17). The pooled survival rate across these trials was determined to be 96% (95% confidence interval: 94–98%), with a mean follow-up period extending from 12 to 64 months.

## Conclusion:
Ceramic restorations with computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing have shown high survival rates in either randomized controlled studies or cohort studies. Meta-analysis results showed high heterogeneity in cohort studies, so randomized controlled studies with a larger number of participants are still needed to prove their clinical effectiveness.

Keywords: CAD/CAM, ceramic dental restorations, dentistry, meta-analysis, survival rates

## 1. Introduction
With the continuous advancement of dental restoration technology, the performance of various restorative materials in clinical applications has garnered widespread attention. Selecting the appropriate restorative material is crucial for ensuring the long-term stability and aesthetic appeal of dental restorations. A wide range of materials are utilized in dental restorations, including ceramic materials, metal alloys, and composite resin materials.[ ] However, resin materials usually face problems such as poor wear resistance, discoloration, and shrinkage.[ ] All-metal dental restorations usually have a high survival rate; however, the biocompatibility of metal materials is relatively poor, which may cause allergic reactions, infections, and other problems that affect oral health.[ , ]
Traditionally, ceramic materials have been considered brittle, but modern high-strength ceramics such as zirconia ceramics possess high strength and durability, making them suitable for posterior tooth restorations that withstand high occlusal forces. Ceramic restorations typically require less tooth wear, which is an important consideration for patients who wish to preserve more of their natural tooth structure.[ ] Survival rates of ceramic restorations vary. For instance, lithium disilicate restorations have demonstrated high survival rates, with an average follow-up time of 3 years and the longest observation period reaching 6 years.[ ] Zirconia ceramic implants also show promising results, with a survival rate of 92% after 1 year of functional use reported in a clinical study review.[ ] However, there may be some less desirable factors when patients choose ceramic restorations: compared to traditional metal restorations, ceramic restorations may be more costly. The bonding of ceramic restorations requires higher technical and operational precision, necessitating accurate marginal sealing and good adhesive techniques.[ , ] To achieve good aesthetic results, ceramic restorations may require a certain thickness, which to some extent increases the requirements for tooth preparation.[ ]
Computer-aided design (CAD)/computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) technology has revolutionized the field of dentistry, enabling the production of highly precise dental restorations using ceramic materials.[ ] By optimizing material composition and structure, CAD/CAM ceramics significantly enhance material strength and fatigue resistance. During processing, they allow for highly accurate restoration design and fabrication, reducing human error and simplifying the manufacturing process by eliminating complex steps associated with traditional manual techniques. Due to their excellent aesthetics, strength, and biocompatibility, CAD/CAM ceramics are increasingly popular in dental restorations.
However, despite some studies demonstrating good short-term clinical outcomes for CAD/CAM resin-ceramic composite materials, their long-term clinical efficacy remains a matter of debate.[ ] Several systematic reviews have assessed the clinical outcomes of CAD/CAM ceramic restorations, but few have focused specifically on the survival rates of these restorations. Therefore, a systematic evaluation of the available evidence is necessary to determine the effectiveness of these restorations. Consequently, the objective of this systematic review is to evaluate and compare the survival rates related to the clinical performance of CAD/CAM ceramic restorations.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Protocol registration and search strategy
This systematic review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO, the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (ID: CRD42024599053), and was conducted in strict adherence to the guidelines set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).[ ] Further details can be found in Appendix 1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161. Ethical approval was not required under the Institutional Review Board guidelines, as no new human or animal data were collected or analyzed in this work.

## 2.2. Eligibility criteria
The search strategy for literature retrieval was meticulously crafted using MeSH terms and free keywords, all of which were selected based on the PICOS framework: population, intervention/comparison, outcome, and study design. The criteria for inclusion and exclusion of studies were also meticulously established in accordance with the PICOS framework, as detailed below:

## 2.2.1. Population
Patients who have received at least one dental restoration in CAD/CAM ceramic material.

## 2.2.2. Intervention/comparison
The intervention group consisted of ceramic restorations using nonmetallic, including veneers/crowns/abutments/dentures that were designed and machined using CAD/CAM, and we excluded studies that did not use CAD/CAM at all to fabricate restorations.

## 2.2.3. Outcome
The primary outcomes of interest was survival rate, with a focus on studies that reported the respective numbers of survival, failure rates.

## 2.2.4. Study design
We included clinical follow-up studies, such as controlled clinical trials and cohort studies, with a minimum mean observation period of over 2 years. We excluded literature reviews, systematic reviews, protocols, in vitro studies, and case reports from our analysis.

## 2.3. Study selection
Literature searches were conducted using the strategy detailed in Appendix 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161 across 3 key electronic databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). All database queries were finalized in January 2025, focusing on English-language articles published between 1998 and 2025. The identified articles were imported into EndNote X9 software (Thomson Reuters, Philadelphia) to eliminate duplicates. Two independent reviewers carried out the initial screening of article titles and abstracts, followed by an assessment of eligibility. Full-text articles of all potentially relevant studies were obtained for further evaluation. Any discrepancies regarding study eligibility were addressed through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted if consensus could not be reached. Additionally, references cited in related reviews and the full-text articles under consideration were manually searched to uncover any other potentially eligible studies.

## 2.4. Data collection
Data extraction was performed independently by 2 reviewers, who compiled their findings into a standardized collection spreadsheet. Any discrepancies between the spreadsheets were resolved through consensus. The data extracted encompassed various details, including the authors’ names, year of publication, study methodology (such as country of setting, inclusion period, study design, follow-up period), participant demographics (number of patients, number of restorations, patient age, type of restorations, restoration materials), outcome measures (evaluation criteria), study results, conclusions, and the corresponding event numbers or proportions (survival rates). In instances where data was incomplete, the corresponding authors were contacted to seek the missing information.

## 2.5. Risk of bias and quality assessment
The potential biases and the quality of the included studies were evaluated independently by 2 reviewers. Any disagreements between the reviewers were addressed through discussion, and a third reviewer, was consulted to provide additional perspective when required to reach a consensus. To ensure the scientific validity and reliability of the included studies, this Meta-analysis conducted a rigorous quality assessment of all studies. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we used the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for assessment (ROB).[ ] The tool meticulously analyzed the risk of bias for each study in terms of the following key areas: randomization of sequence generation, allocation concealment, blind implementation, incomplete data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. For observational studies, both retrospective and prospective, we assessed study quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale,[ ] which scores studies on 3 main dimensions: study selection, comparability, and assessment of outcomes/exposures.

## 2.6. Statistical analysis
In this systematic review, we conducted a narrative synthesis of the findings from the included studies, categorizing the data based on the studies’ characteristics. This synthesis encompassed various aspects: research methodologies, participant demographics (including patient details and restorations), outcome measures (utilized evaluation criteria), and study conclusions. The synthesis also detailed the results, such as survival rates. However, the scope for conducting a meta-analysis was constrained, with only a few studies providing comparative data between intervention and control groups. Given this limitation, we employed a frequentist random-effects meta-analysis to pool the event prevalence or proportion. This approach involved the application of the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize the variance, followed by back-transformation to obtain the pooled estimate. The meta-analysis was performed using R 4.3.3 (University of Auckland, New Zealand), which facilitated the calculation and interpretation of the pooled results. Thus, the confidence intervals (CIs) derived from this method were consistently within the acceptable range of 0 to 1. We extracted the number of events and the total sample size from each individual study to calculate the event prevalence or proportion. Utilizing these figures, we estimated the 95% CIs for the survival rates associated with restorations made of ceramic materials. In this review, the survival of restorations made of ceramic materials is defined as the absence of any absolute failure. An absolute failure is deemed to have occurred if the restorations necessitate intervention or significant repair, or if it has been partially or wholly lost due to fracture or detachment. The pooled estimate was determined as the weighted average of the estimates derived from each individual study. Heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using the I² statistic, with the following interpretations: 0% to 40%, indicating no significant inconsistency; 30% to 60%, suggesting moderate heterogeneity; 50% to 90%, indicating substantial heterogeneity; and >90%, denoting considerable heterogeneity. I² is >50%, analyzed using a random effects model, and <50% the results are summarized using a fixed effects model. Data from nonrandomized clinical studies are more susceptible to bias and often exhibit high levels of heterogeneity. Consequently, separate meta-analyses were conducted for RCTs and non-RCTs, with results stratified according to study design. Subgroup analyses for survival were conducted for types of restorations and types of materials to explore sources of heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis was performed by comparing fixed- and random-effects meta-analyses to assess the robustness of the intervention effect. Funnel plots and Egger tests were used to check for publication bias.

## 2.7. Certainty of evidence
In this systematic review, we utilized the grading of recommendations, assessment, development, and evaluation (GRADE) approach to assess the confidence in the findings, rate the quality of the evidence from the included studies, and summarize these findings using the GRADEpro GDT tool.[ ] The certainty assessment incorporated the following domains: study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other considerations. The evidence was categorized into 4 levels of certainty: very low, low, moderate, or high, reflecting the confidence in the effect estimate and its adequacy to inform a specific recommendation. Any disagreements regarding the certainty level of the evidence between the reviewers were addressed through consensus discussions, with a third reviewer consulted if necessary to reach a unanimous decision. This rigorous approach ensured a transparent and robust evaluation of the evidence, facilitating an informed interpretation of the review’s conclusions.

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Study selection
A comprehensive electronic database search identified a total of 714 articles. Following the screening of titles and abstracts, 31 studies were selected for a full-text review. Additionally, a manual search uncovered one more article. Upon applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 25 studies[ - ] were deemed eligible for both quantitative and qualitative analysis, as depicted in Figure . The Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ) for the title and abstract screening stage was 0.83, and for the full-text articles screening, it was 0.89, indicating a high level of agreement between the reviewers.
 

## 3.2. Risk of bias and quality assessment
5 recent RCTs were identified with an overall bias rating of “some concerns.” Specifically, Montaser et al’s study was assigned a high overall bias due to deviations from the intended interventions, issues in the measurement of outcomes, and selection bias in reported results (Appendix 3.1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161). In terms of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale quality assessment, 12 retrospective studies received 8 stars each, indicating good quality (Appendix 3.2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161).

## 3.3. Study characteristics
Among the 25 studies included in this review, there were 13 RCTs and 12 cohort studies, with the latter comprising 9 retrospective and 3 prospective investigations. The number of restorations evaluated in these studies varied widely, from 10 to 426, and the mean follow-up period ranged from 12 to 120 months. Most of these studies were published within the last decade. The clinical criteria used to evaluate the restorations in these studies included modified US Public Health Service (USPHS) criteria, Fédération Dentaire Internationale World Dental Federation criteria, and a combination of clinical and radiographic examination. In these studies, different types of ceramic materials were used, including zirconia, resin, lithium disilicate, and others. Table details the characteristics of these studies.
 

## 3.4. Survival rates analysis
25 studies were included for quantitative analysis. Two meta-analyses were performed according to the study designs (RCT and non-RCT) to reduce the bias and high heterogeneity. The pooled estimated survival rate across all RCTs was 96% (95% CI: 94 to 98%), with the mean follow-up time spanning from 12 to 64 months, the heterogeneity was I2 = 23.0% (P = .166). For the non-RCT studies, the estimated survival rate was 96% (95% CI: 93–98%), with the mean follow-up period ranging from 24 to 67 months, the heterogeneity was I2 = 52.0% (P = .011).
In addition, the types of restorations and the materials used in the restorations were analyzed in subgroups. For the RCTs analysis, the survival rates of the types of restorations were in descending order: veneer (100%, 95% CI: 96–100%); crown (96%, 95% CI: 92–99%); partial dentures (96%, 95% CI: 90–100%); inlay/onlay restorations (92%, 95% CI: 86–97%), the survival rates of the types of materials were in descending order: other (mixed materials or other; 99%, 95% CI: 95–100%); lithium disilicate (98%, 95% CI: 94–100%); zirconia (95%, 95% CI: 92–98%); resin (92%, 95% CI: 86–97%). For the non-RCTs analysis, the survival rates of the types of restorations were in descending order: crown (95%, 95% CI: 92–96%), other types were not pooled due to small number of studies, the survival rates of the types of materials were in descending order: zirconia (99%, 95% CI: 95–100%); lithium disilicate (95%, 95% CI: 93–96%); other (mixed materials or other; 93%, 95% CI: 82–100%); resin (92%, 95% CI: 71–100%; Fig. , Appendices 4.1 and 4.2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161).
 To investigate potential factors that may impact survival rates, subgroup analyses were performed, with a focus on evaluating the influence of follow-up period and average patient age on survival outcomes. A subgroup analysis was conducted with 32-month follow-up time as the threshold. In the RCTs, the pooled survival rate of patients with a follow-up time >32 months was 97% (93–99%), and that of patients with a follow-up time <32 months was 96% (94–98%). The difference between the 2 groups was not significant (P = .63). The pooled survival rate of patients with an average age >50 years was 97% (91–100%), and that of patients with an average age <50 years was 96% (92–99%). The difference between these 2 groups was also not significant (P = .59). In the cohort studies, the pooled survival rate of patients with a follow-up time >32 months was 95% (91–97%), and that of patients with a follow-up time <32 months was 96% (94–98%). The difference between the 2 groups was not significant (P = .08). The pooled survival rate of patients with an average age >50 years was 96% (94–98%), and that of patients with an average age <50 years was 96% (94–98%). The difference between these 2 groups was not significant (P = .49; Fig. , Appendices 4.3 and 4.4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161).
 

## 3.5. Complicatio
The present study performed a meta-analysis to assess complications related to CAD/CAM ceramic dental restorations. Owing to the paucity of studies reporting complications, data from RCTs and non-RCTs were combined for analysis. Key complications examined encompass color alterations, marginal defects, and recurrent caries. Appendix 4.5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161 presents a forest plot illustrating the estimated color retention rates of CAD/CAM ceramic restorations from RCTs and cohort studies. The overall color retention rate for CAD/CAM ceramic restorations appears to be relatively high, with a pooled estimate of 98% (96–100%). Appendix 4.6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161 illustrated a forest plot of the estimated edge integrity rates for CAD/CAM ceramic restorations derived from randomized controlled clinical trials and cohort studies. The pooled effect size for edge integrity is 94% (94–98%). Appendix 4.7, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161 presents a forest plot of the estimated caries-free rates for CAD/CAM ceramic restorations from RCTs and cohort studies. The pooled results from the random-effects model indicate a caries-free rate of 98% (96–99%).

## 3.6. Sensitivity analysis
Sensitivity analyses were performed separately for RCT and non-RCT studies using the 1-by-1 exclusion method, and it was observed that the effect sizes remained consistent after excluding each study, indicating that the results of this meta-analysis were relatively robust (Appendix 4.8, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161).

## 3.7. Publication bias analysis
In this Meta-analysis, we used funnel plots and Egger’s test to assess publication bias for RCTs and non-RCTs, respectively. For RCT studies, the funnel plot did not show significant asymmetry, whereas the P value of the Egger test was .778, indicating that there was no statistical evidence to support the presence of publication bias. For non-RCT studies, the funnel plot also did not show significant asymmetry, and the Egger’s test P-value was .200 (Appendix 4.9, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R161).

## 4. Discussion
This study provides an in-depth insight into the application of CAD/CAM ceramic restorations in dental rehabilitation by systematically evaluating their survival rates and complications. After an extensive electronic database search yielding 714 articles, 25 eligible studies were selected for qualitative and quantitative analysis, including 13 RCTs and 12 cohort studies. Quantitative analysis across these studies revealed a pooled estimated survival rate of 96% (95% CI: 94–98%) for all RCTs and a similar rate for non-RCT studies. Subgroup analyses of restoration types and materials showed higher survival rates for zirconia and lithium disilicate in both RCTs and non-RCTs. These findings align with previous studies, indicating the potential of CAD/CAM technology to enhance restoration survival rates.[ , ]
For different types of study designs, we used different quality and risk of bias assessment scales (NOS and ROB2 for cohort and RCT studies, respectively), and meta-analysis was performed to summarize the studies separately for the 2 designs. The results revealed that the heterogeneity of RCT studies was much lower compared with non-RCT studies. However, based on the pooled results, the differences in the results of effect sizes pooled through the fixed-effects model or the random-effects model were small, representing a more stable result in this study, and the results of the sensitivity analyses supported our conclusions. By analyzing the subgroups of restorations by category and material, the heterogeneity of some subgroups in the non-RCT study was reduced, representing that the category and material of restorations may be the source of heterogeneity.
Some results showed that In-Ceram ceramics had a lower survival rate (85.3–94.8%) than In-ceram zirconia and IPS e.maxZirCAD.[ ] The authors concluded that longer-term follow-up and higher sample sizes in clinical studies are needed for more reliable results to evaluate the clinical success of the various all-ceramic resin-bonded fixed dental prosthesis designs. one study included 13 studies reporting 1598 restorations in 1161 patients, and the results showed that more biological, technical, and aesthetic complications were observed in the CAD/CAM group than in the conventional group when compared to conventional restoration fabrication. Zirconia and CAD/CAM techniques must be further developed to surpass conventional techniques. Additional RCTs and prospective cohort studies are still needed to strengthen the findings.[ ]
The survival rate of ceramic restorations is influenced by a multitude of clinical factors. Ceramic restorations made from different materials may exhibit varying longevity. For instance, while all-ceramic materials may offer superior aesthetics compared to metal-ceramic restorations, they might compromise on mechanical strength and durability.[ ] The design of the restoration margin significantly impacts the service life of the restoration. Research indicates that distinct margin types can alter the thickness of the marginal porcelain and the distribution of stress at the margins, thereby affecting the restoration’s survival rate.[ ] Previous meta-analyses have shown that ceramic partial coverage restorations outperform resin partial coverage restorations at both the 5-year and 10-year follow-ups, which is consistent with the findings of this study.[ ] The selection of luting agents and the proper application of bonding techniques are crucial for the longevity of ceramic restorations. Effective bonding can enhance the retention rate of the restorations.[ ] Patient-specific factors such as age, periodontal health, caries risk, parafunctional habits (e.g., bruxism and clenching), and the vitality of the teeth can all impact the long-term success of the restoration. The position of the restoration within the dental arch also affects its survival rate, with different occlusal forces exerted on anterior and posterior teeth potentially leading to varying degrees of influence on the restoration.[ ] Proper tooth preparation is key to ensuring the success of a restoration; improper preparation may result in stress concentration, increasing the risk of restoration damage. Correct occlusal adjustment can prevent undue pressure on the restoration, reducing failures due to occlusal issues. Regular maintenance and follow-up can help identify and address issues that may lead to restoration failure in a timely manner.[ ]
In this systematic review, the certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach, yielding a moderate rating for RCTs and a moderate rating for cohort studies. This review faced limitations due to incomplete data reporting in the included studies, particularly concerning parafunctional habits and bruxism, occlusal schemes, and cementation protocols. These factors could significantly influence restorations failure rates. However, accounting for all potential confounding variables is inherently complex. To mitigate these distortions, subgroup analysis was conducted to enhance the clarity and reliability of the findings. Funnel plots, egger tests, and sensitivity analyses suggest the reliability of the results of the current study, but due to the small sample size of the RCTs in the currently included studies and the still high heterogeneity of the cohort studies, a randomized trial with a larger sample size and a longer follow-up period in a woodland randomized trial is needed for the assessment of the clinical outcomes of ceramic material restorations for cad to overcome the existing evidence of the limitations. The present report focused on evaluating survival rates; however, further studies are required to additionally investigate other covariates such as bacterial adhesion,[ ] fatigue[ ] and flexural strength.[ ]
