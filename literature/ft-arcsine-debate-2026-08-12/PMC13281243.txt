## Abstract

## Background
Nigeria is among the leading countries contributing to the burden of cerebral palsy. Cerebral palsy (CP) is the commonest physical disability in childhood, accounting for 16.2% of child neurology referrals. Many cases of CP are preventable with simple interventions such as neonatal resuscitation and effective phototherapy. Lack of reliable data on the burden of disease limits comprehensive national policy directed to the prevention, holistic care and rehabilitation of patients with cerebral palsy in Nigeria. Therefore, to effectively address this problem, this study aimed to describe the pooled prevalence of CP in Nigeria, its potential causes and types.

## Methods
This was a systematic review and meta-analysis of published articles on Nigerian children less than 18 years with CP. A combination of controlled vocabulary and keyword searching was used to reflect the core concepts of CP in Nigeria. Searches were conducted in nine databases and articles were screened using Rayyan. Data extraction forms were developed and refined by the team. Risk of Bias Tool was derived from the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) 2018.

## Results
One hundred and twelve articles were initially included following title/abstract screening from 330 distinct articles. Thirty-nine studies involving 194,831 children were eventually included in the review with about half (18/39) from the South-West. The pooled prevalence of CP in Nigeria was 32.5% (95%CI 25.5–39.9%) in neurology/physiotherapy clinics, 1.7% in the general Paediatric clinic/admissions, and 0.22% (95%CI 0.20–0.24%) in community settings. A higher proportion of individuals with CP were male (59.4%) compared to female (40.6%). Asphyxia and neonatal jaundice were the leading causes of CP accounting for 37.1% (95%CI 31.3–43.2%) and 24.8% (95%CI 20.4–29.5%), respectively. There was a high degree of heterogeneity in most of the data on prevalence, types and causes of CP (I2 >80%). Univariable meta-regression showed that the prevalence of CP was higher in studies that included younger children while study quality accounted for 20% of heterogeneity. However, this significance was lost with multivariable meta-regression analysis.

## Conclusion
This review highlights the high prevalence of CP in Nigeria. Tackling the leading preventable causes of CP calls for urgent public health interventions such as improved obstetric and neonatal care, in addition to improved rehabilitation services for children with CP.

## Introduction
Cerebral palsy (CP) includes a group of permanent disorders that occur as a result of non-progressive injury to the developing fetal or infant brain, affecting movement and posture leading to activity limitation(s).[ , ] A diagnosis of CP can be accompanied by disturbances of sensation, cognition, communication, perception, behavior or seizure disorders.[ ] It is said to be the commonest motor disability in childhood[ ] especially in the low and middle-income countries (LMICs) where Nigeria is among the three leading countries contributing to the burden of this disability.[ ] India, China and Nigeria are the top three leading contributors to the global prevalence of developmental disabilities which increased from 8.6 million to 14.7 million from 1990 to 2016.[ ].
Cerebral palsy accounts for about 16.2% of new referrals to a child neurology clinic in Nigeria[ ] while it contributes to 36.5–50.3% of neurologic disorders seen in the pediatric neurology clinic.[ , ] The reported prevalence in the community in Nigeria is about 0.2%, though the actual prevalence may vary due to geographical disparities in access to specialty care.[ ].
About 63% of the aetiologies of CP are preventable.[ ] It is often caused by perinatal asphyxia, acute bilirubin encephalopathy, prematurity, intracranial infection and trauma.[ – ] These preventable causes are still prevalent in LMICs due to a variety of problems including poor healthcare systems, low socioeconomic factors, high birth rate, late presentation to the hospital, lack of effective phototherapy, paucity of subspecialists such as paediatric physiotherapists, neurologists and a host of other barriers to care.[ ].
Cerebral Palsy is traditionally classified based on predominant motor phenotype, with 70–80% people with CP having a spastic phenotype. While other phenotypes, including dyskinetic, ataxic, and hypotonic are considered less prevalent, in reality, most people with CP have a mixed movement pattern.[ , ] CP can also be classified by distribution as hemiplegic, diplegic, monoplegic or quadriplegic.
Children with cerebral palsy often suffer from comorbidities such as epilepsy, visual and hearing impairment, learning disabilities and malnutrition. In addition to these medical problems, these children also encounter social difficulties such as stigma, child abuse, abandonment, and bullying. Both the children and their caregivers often have a reduced quality of life.[ , ].
The comprehensive management of children with CP is usually multidisciplinary including physiotherapist, speech therapist, pediatric neurologist, and nutritionist. However, these are not fully available in many health facilities in Nigeria and when they are, patients are unable to cope with the financial burden and multiple hospital/clinic visits.[ ].
Due to the significant burden CP places on the child, family, community and nation, a comprehensive national policy directed at the holistic care and rehabilitation of affected children cannot be overemphasized. No such policy is currently available, perhaps due to lack of data on the true prevalence, causes, clinical presentation and other missing information needed to develop such policies. Most reports in Nigeria are from single centre studies which do not represent a significant proportion of the population of the country. This systematic review and meta-analysis provides critical data on prevalence, causes and types of CP which is more representative of the population of Nigeria. It is hoped that the outcome of this review will greatly enhance the development of a comprehensive national policy directed specifically at the holistic care and rehabilitation of children/individuals with CP.

## Materials and methods
A combination of controlled vocabulary and keyword searching was used to reflect the core concepts of CP in Nigeria. A complete search strategy is available in the appendix. Searches were conducted in nine databases: Africa Journals Online, Cochrane Library (via Wiley), Embase (via Ovid), Global Index Medicus, Google Scholar, Medline (via Ovid), PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science SCI-Expanded and Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI). No limitations were placed on study design, language of publication, or publication date. Searches were first conducted on April 23, 2023, and updated on January 2, 2024 and December 18, 2025.
Articles were screened using Rayyan software for systematic review.[ ] One author (CB) conducted the literature search and uploaded all eligible articles onto the software. Authors were paired into four groups for the review: group A (UMD and AU-C), group B (KG and PI), group C (FA and AOO) and group D (DDS and RGM). Screening was conducted in two phases: title-abstract and full-text. During the screening, each member independently assessed all allocated articles for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved via group calls, and where necessary, a third author (TS and GEO) acted as an arbitrator. This was followed by full text screening with reasons for exclusion recorded in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Articles were included if they studied Nigerian children aged less than18 years and included number of children seen with CP. Articles were excluded if they had no clear age definition for the children or were case reports, case series, qualitative studies or review articles. For studies with missing data on the total number of children seen, we excluded them from analysis of pooled prevalence after corresponding authors were contacted but all failed to provide this information and/or did not respond. However, the affected articles were included in data analysis of types and causes of CP if they contained the data.
Data extraction forms were developed and refined by the team. This data included citation information, year of publication, geo- political zone of the country, study setting, age ranges of patients, mean age of studied children, total number of participants, sex distribution, number with CP, etiology and types of CP. In addition, we looked at how CP was defined with reference to the 2006 consensus definition which states: “CP is a group of permanent disorders of the development of movement and posture, causing activity limitation, that are attributed to nonprogressive disturbances that occurred in the developing fetal or infant brain. The motor disorders of cerebral palsy are often accompanied by disturbances of sensation, perception, cognition, communication, and behaviour, by epilepsy, and by secondary musculoskeletal problems.”[ ] This classification was not applicable to studies that were carried out before 2006. The primary outcome was pooled prevalence of CP in Nigeria. Secondary outcomes were causes, types and sex of children with CP. Data were extracted using Qualtrics and exported into an Excel sheet for further analysis. This dataset was reviewed, cleaned up, discrepancies resolved and completeness assured by the entire group via virtual meetings.
Risk of bias was assessed using a tool derived from the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) 2018.[ ] The tool evaluates five parameters with a score of 0–2 each and maximum score of 10. The parameters included sampling technique, representativeness of the target population, appropriateness of the measurements, non-response bias, and statistical analysis to answer each research question. Studies were scored as follows: high quality (≥ 7), moderate quality (4–6) and low quality (0–3). (Supplementary Table 1)

## Statistical analysis
Analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Prevalence/proportion of CP was the summary estimate for this meta-analysis. To ensure that these estimates from binomial data corresponded to probabilities ranging from 0 to 1, they were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation.[ ] The DerSimonian and Laird’s random-effects method, which weighs individual study estimates by the inverse of the variance of their transformed proportion as study weight, was used to pool estimates. To estimate the precision of the calculated estimates, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using the Clopper-Pearson exact binomial method.[ ] Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, and I2. The presence of publication bias was evaluated graphically using funnel plot and Doi plots and quantitatively using Egger’ regression asymmetry tests and LFK index values.[ ] LFK values more extreme than ± 2 indicates the presence of publication bias.[ ] To evaluate whether study-level covariates like mean age, year of publication, region and study quality explained some of the observed between-study heterogeneity, univariable and multivariable meta-regression analyses were performed.

## Ethical considerations
No patients or members of the public were involved in any way, and data were from published sources; therefore, ethical approval was not needed.

## Results
Eight hundred and forty-three (843) items were retrieved through the searches, of which 513 were duplicates. This resulted in 330 unique items. Following titles and abstract review, 218 articles were excluded and 112 articles underwent full-text review. (Fig.  ) Thirty-nine studies were included in the review. (Table  ) All the 6 geo-political regions of Nigeria were represented with nearly half (18/39) of the included studies coming from the South-western region. Twenty-one studies (21/36) were determined to be of fair quality. (Table  ). Only 23 studies had information about the total number of children seen and were therefore included in the meta-analysis for pooled prevalence of CP. (Table  ). The pooled prevalence included 7593 with CP out of a total of 194,497.
 This study included articles that were published from 1971 to 2025 and Fig.  shows the time trend in reported prevalence of CP. The prevalence of CP in Nigeria varied significantly across study settings, ranging from 33.2% (95% CI 35.9–40.9%) in specialist Neurology and physiotherapy clinics, 1.7% (95% CI 0.4–3.8%) in the general pediatric clinics/admissions and 0.22% (95% CI 0.20–0.24%) in community/immunization clinics (p < 0.001). (Fig.  ). Prevalence of CP significantly varied with how CP was defined with reference to the 2006 consensus definition (p < 0.001). (Table  .)
 There was a statistically significant preponderance of CP in males (59.5%, 95%CI 57.3- 61.7%) compared to females (40.5%, 95%CI 38.3–42.7.3.7%) (p<0.001). Regarding the types of CP, the spastic type was the most common, accounting for 72.7% (95%CI 65.8–79.2.8.2%) of the reported cases of CP. This was followed by mixed (9.5%, 95%CI 5.9–13.8.9.8%) and dyskinetic types (7.3%, 95%CI 4.8–10.1.8.1%). Asphyxia and neonatal jaundice were the leading causes of CP in Nigeria accounting for 37.1% (95%CI 31.3–43.2.3.2%) and 24.3% (95%CI 20.4–29.5.4.5%), respectively. There was a high degree of heterogeneity in most of the data on prevalence, types and causes of CP (I2 >80%). (Table .)
 

## Publication bias
There was significant publication bias shown in an asymmetrical funnel plot with studies reporting a high prevalence of CP being more likely to be published. (Fig.  ). This finding is supported by Egger’s test(t = 6.5, Bias estimate: 19.6 (standard error = 3.2) p value of < 0.001 and Doi plot, as well as a LFK index of 10.1. (Supplementary Fig. 1).
 

## Meta-regression analysis of potential factors associated with between-study heterogeneity in the prevalence of CP in Nigeria
In univariable meta-regression analysis, the prevalence of CP tended to be less in more recent studies. However, this was not statistically significant (p = 0.4391). Accordingly, year of publication did not account for any of the heterogeneity in the prevalence of CP (0%). (Fig.  ). Similarly, region was not significantly related to the heterogeneity in the prevalence of CP.
 Only age and study quality were associated with the prevalence of CP. Specifically, the prevalence of CP tended to be less in studies with higher mean age of participants. A 1-month increase in mean age was associated with a 0.0035% decrease in the prevalence of CP (p = 0.0161). Variation in the mean age of participants across studies explained 34.71% of the heterogeneity in the prevalence of CP. (Fig.  ). Study quality also explained 20% of the heterogeneity in the prevalence of CP (p = 0.0434). When the significant factors in the univariable models were all included in the multivariable model, none of the factors were statistically significantly related to the between-study heterogeneity in the prevalence of CP.
 

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis offer the most comprehensive synthesis to date on the burden, types, and causes of CP among Nigerian children. Drawing on 39 studies from all six geopolitical zones, our findings confirm that CP remains a major and persistent public health challenge, particularly within the context of pediatric neurology in Nigeria.
Prevalence varied widely depending on the study setting of specialized neurology and physiotherapy centers, the general paediatric population, and community/immunization clinics, mainly because the denominators are not comparable. This review included only two (2) community-based studies which reported a pooled prevalence of 0.2%. However, the numbers of studies are too few to be a representative estimate of the general population. On the other hand, more studies were conducted in clinical settings with a pooled prevalence of 32.5% in neurology/physiotherapy clinics. However, the high prevalence estimates in the different settings underscores the substantial burden of CP among children accessing healthcare services, especially in specialized clinics. From our study, it was noted that prevalence estimates have been declining in more recent studies, which is consistent with reports from a recent systematic review on the global prevalence of CP that found that prevalence in high-income countries has declined from 2.1 to 1.5 per 1000 live births in the 2000s. That review however highlighted the gap as a result of insufficient information to determine prevalence trends over time.[ ] In addition, the systematic review noted a much higher prevalence of CP in LMICs- up to 3.4 per 1000 live births compared to high-income countries.[ ] Two recent reviews, one a scoping review by Murugasen et al. [ ] and another systematic review by Abate et al. [ ], looking at CP in children in Africa further underscore that most of the data and knowledge regarding CP come from high-income countries. They highlight the paucity and variability of data coming from African countries.[ , ] As in our study, both studies noted that the most common causes of CP in Africa were perinatal including asphyxia, kernicterus, and infection.[ , ] The reported prevalence ranged from 0.8 to 10 per 1000 children with higher numbers of premature death and more severe disability as compared to high-income countries.[ ].
Consistent with global literature, a preponderance of males (59.6%) compared to females (40.4%) was observed.[ ] This sex disparity is not well understood but the following reasons have been adduced: biological vulnerabilities in male infants, including greater susceptibility to perinatal brain injuries, differences in central nervous system maturation, and sex-specific responses to hypoxic-ischemic events.[ , ] In many African countries, the higher prevalence of glucose-6-phosphate-dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and manifestation of severe jaundice in male infants also contribute.[ ].
Based on motor classification, spastic CP emerged as 72.7% accounting for close to three-quarters of cases. Second is dyskinetic CP, sometimes called chorea-athetoid, which is commonly a sequalae of severe neonatal jaundice causing acute bilirubin encephalopathy (ABE).[ ] The relatively low prevalence of dyskinetic CP (7.3%) is notable, given the significant burden of neonatal jaundice in Nigeria—a known risk factor for this subtype.[ , ] Several factors may explain this discrepancy, including diagnostic challenges, underreporting, or symptom overlap with more dominant spastic features. Severe cases of jaundice may lead to Kernicterus Spectrum Disorder (KSD) beyond the neonatal period.[ ] Severe cases of KSD may result in early childhood mortality, leading to an under-representation of dyskinetic CP in clinical populations.[ ] This underscores the urgent need for enhanced detection, timely referral, and effective treatment of neonatal jaundice to prevent irreversible brain damage.[ ].
Preventable perinatal neurological insults remain the primary drivers of CP in Nigeria. Perinatal asphyxia and ABE accounted for 37.1% and 24.8% of cases, respectively, in the studies reviewed. These findings support the widely held view that the majority of CP cases in LMICs—up to 63%—stem from preventable causes.[ , ] Persistent system-level weaknesses—such as poor access to skilled birth attendants, delayed emergency obstetric care, inadequate neonatal resuscitation, and limited effective phototherapy and exchange transfusion services—contribute significantly to this burden.[ ] The Essential Newborn Care and Helping Babies Breathe courses [ , ]that are being cascaded to health care providers in primary and secondary care facilities in addition to the comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn programs/guidelines such as the National Guidelines for Comprehensive Newborn Care [ ] are laudable initiatives by the Nigerian Federal Ministry of Health to improve access to quality obstetric care and reduce the burden of severe birth asphyxia. Similarly, the prominence of ABE as a leading cause highlights the relevance of national efforts by bodies and collaborations such as the Nigerian Society for Neonatal Medicine (NISONM) and Stop Kernicterus and Infection in Northern Nigeria Plus (SKIINN+). Addressing ABE as a modifiable risk factor demands targeted interventions, including improving caregiver awareness, discouraging harmful traditional practices, promoting early healthcare-seeking behaviors, and ensuring the availability of affordable, effective phototherapy units at all levels of care.[ , ].
Despite nationwide study inclusion, data were disproportionately drawn from the South-West region, which accounted for half of the studies. This geographic imbalance not only reflects disparities in reporting but also likely mirrors inequalities in service availability, infrastructure, and workforce distribution. Under-representation from Northern regions signals a critical need for capacity building in pediatric neurology services and research across the country.
Marked heterogeneity (I² > 80%) in the pooled estimates is common in studies of this nature and suggests variations in diagnostic criteria, study populations, and methodologies which poses significant challenges to the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. The effect of differences in study methods on reported prevalence is supported by the results of univariate regression that showed that study bias score accounted for 20% of the heterogeneity. This underscores the urgent need for standardized national surveillance systems and consensus diagnostic guidelines to enable reliable comparisons and guide planning. Furthermore, evidence of publication bias suggests a tendency toward reporting higher prevalence figures, which may overstate the true burden and points to the need for more prospective, community-based research.[ , ] Another significant gap is the lack of accessible, multidisciplinary care for children with CP in Nigeria. There is a pressing need for policy reforms that expand health financing, encourage task-shifting, and integrate CP care into primary and secondary healthcare systems to ensure equitable access and long-term support for affected families.[ ].

## Limitations
This review is subject to several limitations. First, there was unequal geographic representation, with almost half (50%) of the included studies originating from the South-West region of Nigeria. This differential representation, however, did not significantly influence the overall findings, especially regarding the prevalence of CP. Nonetheless, the inclusion of 39 studies spanning all six geopolitical zones provided a broad enough national picture of the burden of CP. Secondly, as expected for prevalence studies, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies in terms of design and diagnostic criteria, which likely contributed to the wide variation in reported prevalence rates. Many studies were hospital-based, often in tertiary care or specialized clinics, potentially inflating estimates due to referral bias. The limited number of community-based studies makes it difficult to generalize findings to the broader Nigerian pediatric population. Though it has increased implications on cost and other resources, there is a need for more population-based studies to generate more representative estimates of the prevalence of CP in Nigeria. In addition, while this review identified the leading causes and subtypes of CP, variations in classification systems and the non-uniformity of study methods across studies may have introduced misclassification bias. This was particularly evident in the reporting of dyskinetic CP, which may be underdiagnosed or conflated with spastic subtypes due to overlapping clinical features. Finally, important clinical and contextual variables such as socioeconomic status, access to rehabilitative care, and family-level determinants were inconsistently reported, limiting our ability to assess the full spectrum of factors influencing CP outcomes in Nigeria. Future research should prioritize the use of standardized diagnostic criteria and include under-represented regions and community-based populations. Longitudinal cohort studies that assess risk factors, access to care, and long-term outcomes are particularly needed to inform targeted policy and health system responses.

## Conclusion
Cerebral palsy remains a leading cause of chronic childhood disability in Nigeria, with a high burden in clinical populations and a predominance of preventable causes such as birth asphyxia and neonatal jaundice. Spastic CP is the most common subtype, and there is a consistent male predominance across studies. Findings from this review call for urgent public health interventions, including improved maternal and neonatal care, national jaundice prevention programs, and investment in rehabilitative and supportive services. Our study further provides strong and current evidence for prioritizing emergency obstetric and neonatal jaundice care as key entry points for reducing CP burden in Nigeria. Larger, population-based studies are needed to provide more accurate prevalence estimates and to guide evidence-based policies aimed at improving the lives of children with CP and their families.
