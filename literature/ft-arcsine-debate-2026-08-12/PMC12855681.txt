## Abstract

## Introduction
There is a gap in the understanding of the burden of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) among adults in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries, particularly with regard to up-to-date epidemiological data. Meta-analyses were conducted to determine pooled estimates of RSV prevalence among high-risk 18–59-year-old adults and ≥ 50-year-old adults with or without risk factors or comorbidities, who present with respiratory illnesses in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries.

## Methods
Using studies identified from a previously described systematic literature review, a random-effects model was used to determine pooled prevalence for each study population. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression were conducted using the moderator variables deemed most relevant a priori and a mixed-effects approach.

## Results
Pooled RSV prevalence estimates were 5.1% (95% confidence interval [CI] 3.9–6.6%) and 3.9% (95% CI 3.3–4.7%) across 33 studies in 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses and 66 studies in ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses, respectively. Subgroup analyses for 18–59-year-old high-risk adults found geography, study setting, and respiratory illness to collectively explain ~ 54% of the variation in RSV prevalence estimates. The diagnostic method for RSV was found to explain ~ 8% of the variation in RSV prevalence estimates in ≥ 50-year-old adults; no other factors explored via subgroup analyses for ≥ 50-year-old adults had a notable effect on variation. For both populations, a substantial level of residual heterogeneity was observed using Higgin’s I2 when studies were split for subgroup analyses.

## Conclusions
Overall, there is a considerable disease burden associated with RSV among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries, highlighting the need for improved prevention programs for RSV in these populations.

## Introduction
Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of acute respiratory illness (ARI) [ ], with its clinical presentation ranging from a mild cold to serious illness requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission and mechanical ventilation [ , ]. Associated complications include pneumonia [ , ], cardiopulmonary complications [ , – ], and sometimes death [ , , ]. Treatment of RSV infection is typically limited to symptomatic treatment of these associated complications [ , ].
The burden of RSV among children aged < 5 years has been well-documented through global studies [ – ]. However, RSV also has important clinical significance in adults, particularly high-risk adults and those in older age groups, as these attributes predispose them to comorbidities, which affect the severity of disease [ , , ].
Despite this clinical significance, there is a gap in the understanding of the burden of RSV in the adult population in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries, such as those in North Africa, Latin America, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean [ , ]. In particular, although there have been some systematic literature reviews (SLRs) exploring RSV epidemiology in certain developing regions, these are often restricted in geographical scope and do not focus on lower-income geographies [ , ]. Furthermore, currently published SLRs capture data up to 2019 and thus do not reflect the most recent RSV prevalence data available [ , , ]. Therefore, there are limited synthesized, up-to-date epidemiological data available for RSV infection among adults across low- to middle-income geographies.
To help develop strategies to improve disease awareness of RSV in the general population and among healthcare workers, an SLR of the epidemiology, patient diagnosis and treatment pathway, and clinical, humanistic, and economic burden of RSV in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries was undertaken. The epidemiological results of this SLR have been previously reported and indicate a substantial impact of RSV among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses [ ].
The objective of the meta-analyses presented herein was to synthesize results from the aforementioned SLR to determine pooled estimates of RSV prevalence in the two populations of interest: high-risk 18–59-year-old adults and ≥ 50-year-old adults with or without risk factors or comorbidities, who present with respiratory illnesses. This study also explored the role of potential moderators in explaining variations in prevalence estimates across studies.

## Methods
Two separate meta-analyses—one for each study population—of observational studies reporting RSV prevalence in patients presenting with respiratory illnesses, such as influenza-like illness (ILI), ARI, lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD), and/or upper respiratory tract disease (URTD), were conducted. The aggregate-level prevalence estimates reported across studies were quantitatively synthesized for each of the populations of interest: 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses (the first meta-analysis) and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses (the second meta-analysis). Patient-level data for individual studies were not sought.
The full methodology for the SLR that formed the main evidence base for these meta-analyses has been previously described [ ], and is summarized below, in addition to the methodology used for the meta-analyses reported in this article. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

## Search Strategy and Study Selection
The SLR was conducted to identify prevalence literature using Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE®) and Excerpta Medica DataBASE (EMBASE®). Searches were conducted on December 8, 2023 and were restricted to studies published after the year 2000. Animal studies, in vitro studies, and clinical trials were excluded.
Studies were included if they provided data for 18–59-year-old high-risk adults or ≥ 50-year-old adults, both presenting with respiratory illnesses, and if studies were conducted in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries, predominantly in Latin America, Southeast Asia, the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean, and Africa. The definition for ‘high-risk’ and list of the included countries used have been published previously [ ].
From those eligible for full-text screening, selected studies were included to form the main evidence base for determining relevant studies for the meta-analyses (as described in Figure ). Methodological heterogeneity across studies occurs due to the use of different study designs, effect size measures, or analysis methods, whereas clinical heterogeneity arises as a result of different inclusion and exclusion criteria between studies. The full feasibility assessment included a detailed examination of the studies with respect to the amount of methodological and clinical heterogeneity, and an assessment of possible biases. To ensure that the pooled estimates would be valid and interpretable, only studies that were sufficiently comparable in methodology and quality, and that reported data in a useable format, were considered for qualitative synthesis.

## Quality Assessment
The included studies were critically appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), adapted for cross-sectional studies [ , ]. NOS scores range from 0 to 9, with higher ratings indicating better quality. The quality thresholds for NOS are: ≤ 5 for low quality; 6–7 for medium quality; 8–9 for high quality [ ].

## Data Extraction
For the purpose of the meta-analyses, relevant data from the selected studies were extracted in an Excel-based data extraction grid by two independent reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion.
The following parameters were extracted for each study, as applicable: author last name, year of publication, geographical region, country income-level (low, lower-middle, or upper-middle, based on the World Bank classification for the 2025 fiscal year [ ]), study setting (inpatient, outpatient, or both), study period, age group, respiratory illness (e.g., ARI, severe ARI [SARI], ILI), high-risk condition (e.g., immunosuppression, cancer, cardiopulmonary conditions), sampling method (e.g., nasopharyngeal or any other type of swab), and diagnostic method (e.g., polymerase chain reaction [PCR], real-time reverse-transcriptase PCR [rRT-PCR]), number of RSV cases (numerator), population size with respiratory illness (denominator), and quality rating.

## Statistical Analysis

## Calculation of Pooled Effect Size
A random-effects model was used to calculate a pooled effect size for each study population (18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses), rather than a fixed-effect model.
As the effect size index for these meta-analyses was prevalence (i.e., a proportion), all effect size values were first transformed into logit values, using formulas derived from Barker et al. [ ] and Schwarzer and Rücker [ ], to maintain normality and variance stabilization [ , ]. After performing the analysis, the results were back-transformed to the original units for presentation and reporting.
The summary effect size was estimated as the weighted average of the observed effect sizes using the classic inverse variance method. The confidence interval (CI) of the overall effect size was calculated using the Knapp–Hartung method [ , ].

## Assessment of Heterogeneity
To understand the magnitude of dispersion or heterogeneity in the effect sizes across studies, the following statistics were calculated: Cochran’s Q [ ]; Higgin’s I2 [ ]; tau, and tau-squared (estimated using the method of moments or Der Simonian and Laird [DL] method) [ ]; and 95% prediction interval (using a formula derived from previous studies) [ , , ].

## Outlier and Influence Analyses
Potential outliers were defined as estimates with 95% CIs that did not overlap with the CI of the summary effect size and were examined using forest plots.
An influence analysis was conducted to investigate the influence of a single study on the overall estimate by omitting one study in each turn. Each study was removed sequentially, and the summary effect was re-estimated based on the remaining studies to evaluate if the results changed in a practically meaningful way.

## Subgroup Analyses and Meta-Regression
To understand the potential sources of variation in the reported estimates across studies, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were conducted using the moderator variables deemed most relevant a priori (e.g., geographic location, study setting) and a mixed-effects approach [ ].
An exploratory (post hoc) analysis was also conducted to evaluate other potential moderators of interest, such as respiratory illness, high-risk condition, income level, sampling method, RSV diagnostic method, and age.
Q-tests were used to assess the dispersion of the summary effects across the subgroups. A p value of  ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
To evaluate which moderator variables best explained the variation in the reported estimates across studies, a random-effects meta-regression was conducted. Linear relationships between the effect size and the explanatory variables were determined based on the statistical significance (set at ≤ 0.05) of the regression coefficients. The Knapp–Hartung [ ] method was used to modify the standard errors of the regression coefficients to produce more conservative estimates for these.
R2 analogs were calculated to estimate the proportion of true variance in the effect size explained by the covariates [ ].

## Sensitivity Analyses
To evaluate how the results of the meta-analyses changed when key assumptions or methodological choices were varied, a sensitivity analysis was conducted based on the choice of pooling method (generalized linear mixed model [GLMM] versus the classic random-effects inverse variance [IV] model) [ – ], choice of transformation method (logit versus Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation) [ ], and choice of between-study variance estimator (DL method versus Paule–Mandel [PM] method versus Restricted Maximum Likelihood [REML] method) [ , ].

## Publication Bias Assessment
Publication bias assessment was not applicable to these meta-analyses, as the common procedures used to address publication bias are based on the concept that studies that are statistically significant are more likely to be published than studies that are not statistically significant. This framework does not apply to studies that report the prevalence of a condition [ , ].
That said, a qualitative assessment of publication bias was conducted using a cumulative meta-analysis starting with larger studies at the top and sequentially adding smaller studies to evaluate if the weight of the evidence shifted as a function of sample size.

## Software
These meta-analyses were performed using a combination of R [ , ] and Comprehensive Meta Analysis Software (Version 4) [ ].

## Results

## Study Characteristics
From a total of 511 studies eligible for full-text screening, 162 studies were included to form the main evidence base for selecting relevant studies for the meta-analyses (Figure ).
Overall, 33 studies (providing 42 unique estimates of prevalence) and 66 studies (providing 124 unique estimates of prevalence) were included for meta-analyses in 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses, respectively (Table  ). This included two abstracts for the 18–59-year-old high-risk population, and two abstracts and six weblinks for the ≥ 50-year-old population. For 18–59-year-old high-risk adults, the total number of patients across the 33 studies was 55,890. For ≥ 50-year-old adults, the total number across the 66 studies was 80,214 (Table  ).
 

## 18–59-Year-Old High-Risk Adults with Respiratory Illnesses
Of the 33 studies reporting prevalence data for the 18–59-year-old high-risk population, 14 were from Latin America, while seven studies each were from Africa and Southeast Asia. Fewer studies were available from the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean (n = 4) and one study reported data for multiple regions. Studies reporting estimates for 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses were either cross-sectional (n = 16), prospective cohort (n = 10), retrospective cohort (n = 6), or retrospective and prospective cohort (n = 1) studies.
A summary of further study characteristics of the included articles, including study setting, respiratory illness, RSV diagnostic method, and high-risk condition, is provided in Table .

## ≥ 50-Year-Old Adults with Respiratory Illnesses
Of the 66 studies reporting prevalence data for the ≥ 50-year-old population, most were from Latin America (n = 27) or Southeast Asia (n = 19), while fewer were from the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean (n = 10) or Africa (n = 9). One study reported data from multiple regions. Estimates for ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses were derived from cross-sectional (n = 38), prospective cohort (n = 22), retrospective cohort (n = 5), or retrospective and prospective cohort (n = 1) studies.
A summary of further study characteristics of the included articles, including study setting, respiratory illness, RSV diagnostic method, and age group is provided in Table .

## Prevalence of RSV Among 18–59-Year-Old High-Risk Adults with Respiratory Illnesses

## Pooled Estimate
The pooled estimate for RSV prevalence among 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses was 5.1% (95% CI 3.9–6.6%; Fig.  A). The Q value was 892, with 41 degrees of freedom (p < 0.01), suggesting significant heterogeneity across studies. The I2 value was 95%. The prediction interval indicated that, in 95% of similar populations (or future studies), the RSV prevalence was likely to fall between 1.1% and 20.0%.
 

## Influence Analysis
Removal of any one study did not appear to affect the overall pooled estimate in a meaningful way (i.e., the pooled prevalence estimate did not change by greater than ± 10%). However, removing the two estimates from Luna et al. [ ] reduced tau-squared from 0.57 to 0.51 for each estimate, whereas removing the Piralam et al. [ ] and the Moyes et al. [ ] studies increased tau-squared from 0.57 to 0.66 and 0.65, respectively, demonstrating an impact on the overall heterogeneity of effect sizes.

## Subgroup Analysis

## By Geography
The overall pooled prevalence of RSV was 7.8% (95% CI 5.5–10.8) in Latin America, 3.0% (95% CI 1.8–4.9) in Africa, 2.9% (95% CI 1.6–5.1) in Southeast Asia, and 8.0% (95% CI 3.3–18.3) in Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean (Figure ). The difference between the four subgroups was statistically significant (p < 0.01). The level of heterogeneity (I2) within regions ranged from 57% in the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean to 93% in Africa.
Studies from Africa comprised the largest sample size (n = 37,290), followed by Southeast Asia (n = 12,060), Latin America (n = 6036), and the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean (n = 504).
For 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses, studies from the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean were conducted exclusively in Turkey, and reported the highest pooled prevalence (8.0%) among all the regions. The small sample size in this subgroup resulted in wider CIs compared to other regions. R2 analog was 53.3%.

## By Study Setting
The overall pooled prevalence of RSV was 15.4% (95% CI 1.3–71.4) in studies reporting data from both inpatient and outpatient settings, 4.8% (95% CI 3.5–6.6) in inpatient settings, and 3.6% (95% CI 2.5–5.0) in outpatient settings (Figure S3). The difference among the three subgroups was statistically significant (p = 0.03). The level of heterogeneity (I2) was 77% in the outpatient subgroup, 95% in the inpatient subgroup, and 89% in the combined inpatient and outpatient subgroup.
The sample size was much larger in the inpatient subgroup (n = 46,030) compared to the outpatient subgroup (n = 9279) and the combined inpatient and outpatient subgroup (n = 542).
The inpatient and outpatient subgroup exhibited notably wider CIs (95% CI 1.3–71.4) due to its small sample size of 542. After excluding the three studies reporting data from both inpatient and outpatient settings, the difference between the inpatient and outpatient subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.174). R2 analog was 18.7%.

## By Respiratory Illness
The overall pooled prevalence of RSV was 9.4% (95% CI 3.8–21.1) in patients with ARI, 4.8% (95% CI 3.4–6.7) in patients with SARI, 2.8% (95% CI 2.0–3.9) in patients with ILI, and 5.0% (95% CI 1.1–19.5) in patients with pneumonia-related illnesses (Figure S4). The difference between the four subgroups was statistically significant (p < 0.01). The level of heterogeneity (I2) ranged from 65% in the ILI subgroup to 95% in the SARI subgroup.
The sample size was notably larger in the SARI subgroup (n = 44,978) compared to the ILI (n = 8411), ARI (n = 1325), and pneumonia-related (n = 1176) subgroups. R2 analog was 20.2%.

## Additional Subgroup Analyses
The results of additional subgroup analyses by publication quality, country income level, sampling technique, high-risk condition, and diagnostic method are presented in full in the Supplementary Results.

## Predictors of RSV Prevalence (Meta-Regression)
Studies from Latin America had 2.83 times greater odds of reporting RSV compared to studies from Southeast Asia after controlling the effects of study setting and respiratory illness (p = 0.006; Table  ).
 Studies reporting data from both inpatient and outpatient settings had 4.37 times greater odds of reporting RSV compared to studies that only included outpatient data, after controlling for the effects of geography and respiratory illness (p = 0.022; Table  ).
Study geography, study setting, and respiratory illness together accounted for ~ 54% of variation in the RSV prevalence estimates across studies included in the analysis.

## Sensitivity Analysis
Compared to random-effects IV models, which reported pooled estimates ranging from 4.89% to 5.26%, the GLMM produced a slightly lower pooled estimate (4.84%; Table S3).
With other statistical parameters held constant, the Freeman–Tukey transformation yielded marginally different prevalence estimates compared to the logit transformation. The difference in pooled prevalence estimates using different between-study variance estimators was negligible when applying the logit transformation method (range 5.07–5.08%). However, when applying the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine method, there was a slight difference in pooled estimates (range 4.89–5.26%), but this was of little practical significance.

## Publication Bias
In the cumulative meta-analysis by sample size, the 14 smallest studies (sample size ≤ 150) reported a high pooled RSV prevalence of 8.7%. As larger studies were included, the pooled prevalence estimates decreased and became more stable, approaching a pooled value of 5.1%. Seven out of the 14 smaller studies were rated as low quality.

## Prevalence of RSV Among ≥ 50-Year-Old Adults with Respiratory Illnesses

## Pooled Estimate
The pooled estimate for RSV prevalence among ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses was 3.9% (95% CI 3.3–4.7%; Fig.  B). The Q value was 1738, with 123 degrees of freedom (p < 0.01), suggesting significant heterogeneity between studies. The I2 value was 93%. The prediction interval indicated that, in 95% of similar populations (or future studies), the RSV prevalence was likely to fall between 0.7 and 18.7%.

## Influence Analysis
Removal of any one study did not appear to affect the overall pooled estimate in a meaningful way (i.e., the pooled prevalence estimate did not change by greater than ± 10%). However, removing the first Mexico Ministry of Health [ ] and fifth World Health Organization (WHO) Global RSV [ ] Argentinian estimates reduced tau-squared from 0.75 to 0.70 and 0.67, respectively, whereas removing the Al-Romaihi et al. [ ] study increased tau-squared from 0.75 to 0.88, demonstrating an impact on the overall heterogeneity of effect sizes.

## Subgroup Analysis

## By Geography
The overall pooled prevalence of RSV was 3.3% (95% CI 2.4–4.6) in Southeast Asia, 4.6% (95% CI 3.3–6.4) in Latin America, 3.7% (95% CI 2.8–4.8) in Africa, and 4.2% (95% CI 2.7–6.2) in the Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean (Figure S5). The difference between the four subgroups was not statistically significant (p < 0.47). The level of heterogeneity (I2) within regions ranged from 79% in Africa to 96% in Latin America.
Studies from Latin America comprised the largest sample size (n = 37,115), followed by Southeast Asia (n = 16,111), Middle East/Eastern Mediterranean (n = 15,871), and Africa (n = 11,117). R2 analog was 0%.

## By Study Setting
The overall pooled prevalence of RSV was 3.8% (95% CI 3.0–4.7) in studies reporting data from inpatient settings, 3.7% (95% CI 2.4–5.8) in outpatient settings, and 4.2% (95% CI 2.7–6.3) in both inpatient and outpatient settings (Figure S6). The difference among the three subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.91). The level of heterogeneity (I2) was 89% in the inpatient subgroup, 78% in the outpatient subgroup, and 96% in the combined inpatient and outpatient subgroup.
The sample size was comparable between the inpatient subgroup (n = 28,908) and the combined inpatient and outpatient subgroup (n = 28,141), whereas it was notably smaller in the outpatient subgroup (n = 3729). R2 analog was 0%.

## By Respiratory Illness
The overall pooled prevalence of RSV was 4.4% (95% CI 3.2–6.0) in patients with ILI, 3.9% (95% CI 3.1–4.8) in patients with SARI, 4.6% (95% CI 2.1–9.8) in patients with ARI, 3.7% (95% CI 1.8–7.2) in patients with pneumonia-related illnesses, and 3.2% (95% CI 1.7–6.0) in patients with multiple respiratory illnesses (Figure S7). The difference between the five subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.88). The level of heterogeneity (I2) ranged from 84% in the ARI subgroup to 97% in the multiple subgroup.
The sample size was considerably larger in the SARI (n = 29,647), ILI (n = 21,630), and multiple (n = 21,268) subgroups compared to the ARI (n = 2692) and pneumonia-related (n = 4977) subgroups. R2 analog was 0%.

## By Diagnostic Method
The overall pooled prevalence of RSV was 3.0% (95% CI 2.2–4.0) in studies reporting multiplex-based diagnosis, 1.6% (95% CI 0.2–15.3) in multiple diagnosis, 3.9% (95% CI 3.1–4.9) in PCR-based diagnosis, and 7.2% (95% CI 3.8–13.4) in antigen-based diagnosis (Figure S8). The difference between the four subgroups was statistically significant (p = 0.03). The level of heterogeneity (I2) was 61% in the multiplex-based diagnosis subgroup, 83% in the multiple diagnosis subgroup, and 94% in both PCR-based and antigen-based diagnosis subgroups.
The sample size was considerably larger in the PCR-based diagnosis subgroup (n = 59,746), compared to the multiplex-based subgroup (n = 5774), multiple subgroup (n = 2931), and the antigen-based subgroup (n = 4567).
After excluding the studies from the multiplex-based diagnosis and multiple diagnosis subgroups, the difference between the PCR-based and antigen-based diagnosis subgroups was statistically significant (p = 0.024). R2 analog was 7.9%.

## Additional Subgroup Analyses
The results of additional subgroup analyses by publication quality, country income level, sampling technique, and age group (50–65 years and > 65 years) are presented in full in the Supplementary Results.

## Predictors of RSV Prevalence (Meta-Regression)
Studies using antigen-based diagnosis had 2.08 times greater odds of reporting RSV compared to studies using PCR-based diagnosis (p = 0.024; Table  ). Diagnosis accounted for ~ 8% of variation in the RSV prevalence estimates across studies included in the analysis.
 

## Sensitivity Analysis
Compared to random-effects IV models, which reported pooled estimates ranging from 3.43% to 3.92%, the GLMM model produced a slightly lower pooled estimate of 3.41% (Table S4).
With other statistical parameters held constant, the Freeman–Tukey transformation yielded marginally lower prevalence estimates compared to the logit transformation. The difference in pooled prevalence estimates using different between-study variance estimators was negligible when applying the logit transformation method (range 3.91–3.92%). However, when applying the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine method, there was a slight difference in pooled estimates (range 3.43–3.57%).
With other statistical parameters held constant, removing imputed studies from the dataset did not have any material impact on the pooled prevalences.

## Publication Bias
In the cumulative meta-analysis by sample size, as studies were added in an ascending order of sample size, the pooled prevalence estimates gradually decreased and stabilized, converging to a pooled value of 3.9%.

## Discussion
There are limited synthesized and up-to-date epidemiological data on RSV among adult populations in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries [ , ]. Despite recent SLRs investigating the burden of RSV in adults [ , , ], these often have small sample sizes, limited geographical scope, or a focus on high-income countries. To improve knowledge about the burden of RSV, these meta-analyses provide a comprehensive and precise range of estimates for the prevalence of RSV among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses across a broad range of low- to middle-income geographies, using up-to-date data from 33 and 66 studies, respectively.
The meta-analyses produced pooled estimates for RSV prevalence of 5.1% (95% CI 3.9–6.6%) and 3.9% (95% CI 3.3–4.7%) among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses, respectively. Influence analyses demonstrated that the removal of any one study did not affect these overall pooled estimates in a meaningful way. Sensitivity analyses indicated that the pooled estimates were generally robust across various methodological approaches, regardless of whether a random-effects IV model or GLMM was used, whether logit or Freeman–Tukey transformation was applied, or whether the tau-squared estimator was calculated using DL, PM, or REML methods. Moreover, in ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses, removing imputed studies from the dataset did not lead to any material impact on the pooled prevalences.
Subgroup analyses for 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses showed significant differences based on geography, study setting, and respiratory illness, with these three predictors collectively explaining ~ 54% of the variation in RSV prevalence estimates across the included studies. Studies from Latin America had 2.83 times greater odds of reporting RSV compared to those from Southeast Asia, while studies from both inpatient and outpatient settings had 4.37 times greater odds of reporting RSV compared to studies from outpatient settings alone. R2 analogs for additional subgroups (publication quality, country income level, sampling technique, high-risk condition, diagnostic method) ranged from ~ 0–12%, indicating that these factors could only explain a small proportion of the variation in estimates observed.
Subgroup analyses for ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses showed significant differences based on the diagnostic method for RSV used, with studies using antigen-based diagnosis having 2.08 times greater odds of reporting RSV compared to those using PCR-based diagnosis, and diagnostic methods explaining ~ 8% of the variation in prevalence estimates across the included studies. R2 analogs for all other additional subgroups investigated—including geography, study setting, and respiratory illness—were ~ 0%, indicating that these factors could explain approximately none of the variation in estimates observed.
Across both the 18–59-year-old high-risk and the ≥ 50-year-old adult populations, a substantial level of residual heterogeneity was observed using Higgin’s I2 when studies were split for subgroup analyses. This could be a result of synthesizing data for these meta-analyses from observational studies, which carry an increased risk of bias and confounding factors, as well as a high degree of heterogeneity in terms of study design and populations compared with randomized control trials [ , ]. Therefore, the observed degree of heterogeneity is not unexpected.
The results of the current study generally align with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RSV burden among adults with respiratory illnesses, although these were predominantly conducted in higher-income countries. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of RSV among adults in developed countries, RSV was estimated to cause 7.0% (95% CI 5.2–9.5%) and 4.7% (95% CI 3.3–6.5%) of symptomatic respiratory infections in annual studies for high-risk adults and ≥ 60-year-old adults, respectively [ ]. As in the current study, this previous study identified a high level of heterogeneity between estimates across studies, with I2 values of 92.1% and 97.5% for high-risk adults and ≥ 60-year-old adults, respectively [ ]. It should be noted that this study also synthesized data from observational studies, which can introduce heterogeneity, as discussed above. Nonetheless, these reported prevalences in developed countries are generally higher than the pooled estimates reported herein in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries, despite overlapping 95% CIs. This is unlikely to reflect a true difference in RSV infection rates between higher- and lower-income countries, but rather to reflect inequities in access to healthcare and the availability of surveillance for respiratory infections. A review of medically-attended RSV in the United States (US) also identified a considerable burden of RSV among adults, particularly for adults ≥ 65 years, for whom pooled estimates for annual hospitalization were 178 per 100,000 [ ]. This US study also found the rates of RSV reported in the included articles depended on the diagnostic method used [ ]. This aligns with the findings for the ≥ 50-year-old population in the current study, where diagnostic method was the only factor investigated that significantly impacted the variation in RSV prevalence estimates. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis of RSV studies among ≥ 50-year-old adults worldwide estimated RSV to be responsible for 1–10% of ILI/ARI cases, and this study again noted a substantial level of heterogeneity between included studies (I2 generally > 85%) and a considerable variation in sample size [ ]. Additionally, a systematic review of RSV epidemiology in adults in Latin America reported RSV detection rate among adults with respiratory infections to range between 0% and 77.9%, although no meta-analysis was conducted to establish a pooled estimate [ ]. Finally, a meta-analysis of RSV infection among different age groups in Latin America estimated RSV prevalence to be 12.6% among adults > 65 years with lower respiratory tract infection, although this estimate was pooled from a low number of studies (n = 2) [ ].
It is possible to speculate on the reasons behind the variation in prevalence reported across geographies and settings. For instance, the more consistent prevalence reported in studies from Latin America might be explained by the well-established and widely available ILI and SARI surveillance system (the Severe Acute Respiratory Infections Network [SARInet]) in countries like Mexico, Chile and Brazil, consisting of sentinel sites, National Influenza Centers and reference laboratories [ – ]. Such a surveillance system increases the likelihood of detecting RSV and other respiratory viruses. While studies from most geographies reported on retrospective analyses of surveillance databases, those in Turkey also included prospective epidemiological studies [ – ]. In the present analysis, studies conducted in Turkey reported considerably higher prevalence of RSV in 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with respiratory illnesses compared to some other regions. Prospective studies are particularly robust by design for identifying outcomes of interest, which could have resulted in higher reported prevalence. Interestingly, recent results from a prospective study of ≥ 60-year-old adults with an ARI episode across five European countries reported an overall rate of PCR-confirmed RSV-ARI of 9.4% [ ], more consistent with the overall rate of 8.0% among 18–59-year-old high-risk adults in Turkey from the present meta-analysis. Additionally, studies reporting data on ≥ 50-year-old adults conducted in other countries in the Middle East, including Qatar, United Arab Emirates, and Saudi Arabia, also reported higher prevalence compared with Asia and Africa. Middle Eastern countries included in the meta-analyses were considered upper-middle income and therefore may have more resources allocated to healthcare than other lower-income geographies. For instance, healthcare spending in Saudi Arabia (an upper-middle-income country) in 2022 was reportedly $1593 US dollars (USD) per capita, whereas India (a lower-middle-income country) spent only $80 USD per capita in the same year [ ]. Moreover, differences in the reported prevalence of RSV across geographies could reflect inequalities in access to and funding for healthcare [ ], or difficulties in accurate surveillance in rural versus urban areas [ , ]. For instance, there is a reported difference in the quality of surveillance data between the Northern and Southern regions of Brazil, with decreased quality in the North attributed to a lack of technological resources [ ]. Furthermore, no studies from low-income countries were identified in the SLR, which could be due to a lack of resources dedicated to research and surveillance of RSV.
Regarding the variation between study settings, studies reporting data from hospitalized patients are likely to yield higher estimates of RSV prevalence due to the increased likelihood of patients undergoing proper diagnostic testing. This reduces the chances of mis- or underdiagnosis of RSV in hospitalized patients presenting with respiratory symptoms, potentially leading to higher reported prevalence. In contrast, studies performed in outpatient or community settings may underestimate the prevalence of RSV. Interestingly, recent data in children aged < 5 years in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia indicated that RSV was detected more frequently in cases of community mortality compared to cases of deaths within healthcare facilities, suggesting a large burden of RSV in the community setting that might have gone unnoticed without post-mortem testing [ ]. Epidemiological data on RSV infection in low- to middle-income countries are limited, and recent work suggests that the burden of RSV infection is underestimated across these regions [ , ]. There are likely a number of factors underlying this scarcity of data, including lack of resources for surveillance and diagnostic testing [ ], difficulties in accessing healthcare [ ], and a lack of formalized archiving and reporting pathways for healthcare-related data [ , ]. The available epidemiological data for adults may therefore not capture cases of RSV that are not medically attended, and the number of undiagnosed cases of RSV in low- to middle-income countries could be substantial, leading to considerable underestimation of the pooled prevalence of RSV. In the present study, no significant difference was observed when comparing inpatient and outpatient settings directly, across both populations of interest. However, the lack of a statistically significant difference between inpatient and outpatient settings should be interpreted with caution, given the large imbalance in subgroup sample sizes. Both subgroups also showed substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 75%), which could mask true differences. Finally, residual confounding by other study-level covariates, such as geography, respiratory illness type, or diagnostic approach, may have further attenuated any underlying effects.
The results of this study must be interpreted within the context of its limitations, particularly in the realm of missing data, data imputation, generalizability, and publication bias. As mentioned, no studies from low-income countries were identified in the SLR, limiting the generalizability of the study findings to these regions. Conference abstracts and weblinks included in the SLR were not evaluated for study quality, resulting in this information being missing for the estimates derived from these types of articles. Data were also missing for study setting, diagnostic method, and sampling technique for some studies. Furthermore, as previously mentioned, prevalence data do not lend themselves to formal assessment of publication bias, limiting the ability to assess this for the studies included in the SLR. However, cumulative meta-analyses suggested that as larger studies were added, the pooled RSV prevalence estimates became smaller and more stable. This pattern suggests that smaller studies reported higher RSV prevalence (and with lower precision) compared to larger studies, reflecting an inverse association between effect size and sample size.
Additionally, as the possibility of confounding by other study-level characteristics cannot be ruled out, the potential predictors of RSV prevalence identified in this study can only be considered observational, rather than causal. Furthermore, as the statistical power for detecting differences between subgroups or relationships between moderators and effect sizes is often low [ ], the failure to obtain statistically significant relationships for other potential predictors and RSV prevalence cannot be interpreted as evidence of no relationship existing between the two.
Some studies on RSV prevalence among ≥ 50-year-old adults (providing data for 12 estimates) did not provide the actual number of crude RSV cases or total sample size, meaning an imputed total sample size of 181 (median sample size across the remaining estimates) was used to calculate the number of RSV cases based on the RSV prevalence reported. Although bootstrapping could help quantify the uncertainty around imputed sample sizes, the lack of raw data in the affected studies precluded its reliable application in this analysis.
Notwithstanding these limitations, the current meta-analyses enabled estimation of the prevalence of RSV among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses with considerable precision and power, using a large sample size for each population. The meta-analyses also provided insight into the potential moderators that could explain variation in RSV prevalence estimates observed across studies. The pooled estimates obtained in this study provide a better understanding of the burden of RSV in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries, where data for RSV are often scarce [ , ]. Given this scarcity, it is possible that the pooled prevalence of RSV infection in 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses is considerably higher than estimated in the present analysis. This is not unexpected, since the burden of RSV infections is underreported even in high-income countries [ ]. Furthermore, more severe RSV and associated outcomes are more likely to be recorded in hospital settings compared with community settings. Therefore, cases of less severe RSV that did not require inpatient or outpatient care may have been missed in the literature informing this analysis. This underscores the importance of robust surveillance and detection of RSV, and the data presented herein may be used in future health economic and budget impact models to inform health policy decisions.

## Conclusions
In low- to middle-income countries, approximately 4–5% of 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with respiratory illnesses were found to test positive for RSV, with geography, diagnostic method, respiratory illness, and study setting all identified as potential moderators of RSV prevalence estimates. These findings highlight a considerable disease burden associated with RSV, and the need for improved RSV prevention programs in these populations, which may be guided by the data generated in this study.
