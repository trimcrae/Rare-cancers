## Abstract

## Objective
To estimate the prevalence of technical errors in the total testing process of pathology tissue specimens.

## Design
A systematic review and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology was performed. Extracted data were pooled using a random-effects model and Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation approaches.

## Data sources
PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane Library and Science Direct databases were searched on 29 May 2025 for eligible studies.

## Study selection
Peer-reviewed studies published after 2003 that reported technical error prevalence in any aspect of routine tissue collection and processing.

## Results
The search returned 30 articles (31 studies), including data from 2 794 987 surgical pathology cases. Pooled effect sizes were calculated for pre-analytic errors at 16.71 per 1000 cases (95% CI 6.42 to 31.67), actively identified errors in the laboratory at 40.17 per 1000 (95% CI 7.04 to 97.61) and reported surgical pathology specimen errors at 4.94 per 1000 (95% CI 1.33 to 10.80). Subgroup analysis showed an occult error prevalence of 2.54 per 1000 cases (95% CI 1.63 to 3.64), and surgical pathology case contamination was 62.23 per 1000 (95% CI 8.22 to 159.33).

## Conclusion and relevance
The analysis suggests that tissue processing technical errors are more prevalent than reported. A lack of standardised methods for defining and detecting errors contributes to undocumented and unidentified errors, leaving the true error prevalence unknown. The majority of clinical decisions are based on laboratory results; however, without reliable detection and reporting, errors in vulnerable processes risk compromising patient diagnoses.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Prior research has established that technical errors occur across the tissue specimen total testing process (TTP); unfortunately, fragmented definitions, heterogeneous detection methods and reliance on self-reporting have obscured the true error burden.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This study provides the first standardised, cross-phase comparison of active versus passive detection, demonstrating that active surveillance identifies substantially more errors and revealing previously under-quantified workflow vulnerabilities. By harmonising definitions and measurement units, the analysis yields the first robust, phase-specific and aggregated prevalence estimates, clarifying the magnitude of under-reporting across the TTP.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
These findings support the need for standardised terminology, structured and automated error-tracking systems and non-punitive reporting frameworks to improve quality assurance, inform policy and reduce preventable harm in surgical pathology.

## Introduction
 Specimen misidentification is the most significant error in laboratory medicine, responsible for an estimated 160 000 adverse events annually. Surgical pathology produces high-impact diagnostic results critical to patient treatment decisions in many clinical disciplines. However, pathology tissue specimen collection, handling and processing have remained predominantly unchanged for decades, relying heavily on manual techniques and labour-intensive workflows that are vulnerable to errors. Unrecognised errors and tissue contamination pose serious challenges, often going unnoticed or unreported in routine workflows. Consequently, the true prevalence of errors is likely underestimated, hindering accurate risk assessment and process improvement. Due to the complex yet critical nature of tissue specimen collection and processing, increased standardisation and quality assurance are necessary to minimise errors and costs while safeguarding patient care. Previous research lacks a comprehensive assessment of technical errors spanning the total testing process (TTP).
Potential sources of error in the tissue TTP include mislabelling during collection or accessioning, and mislabelling or contamination during macroscopic analysis, tissue processing, embedding, microtomy and staining. Human mistakes are the most common source of error, and while challenging to detect, they are likely preventable. A comprehensive analysis of the TTP and its vulnerabilities is essential to prevent harmful errors, inform high-quality care, improve patient safety, reduce costs and strengthen public confidence in the healthcare system. Prior research has not comprehensively compared the sensitivity of active versus passive error reporting methods, nor has it standardised or fully captured the range of error definitions used. Additionally, no studies to date have quantified and compared error prevalence across individual stages of the pathology workflow or synthesised them into an overall error prevalence. Subgroup analyses stratified by error types are also lacking, limiting insight into stage-specific vulnerabilities. This study addresses these gaps by systematically evaluating reporting sensitivity, harmonising error definitions and presenting both stage-specific and aggregated error prevalence, including subtype-level analysis. A meta-analysis estimated the cumulative TTP error prevalence and compared error rates across testing phases. Accurate assessment of the prevalence of technical errors in tissue specimen processing is essential for enhancing clinical vigilance when faced with inconsistent findings and guiding the development of process improvements and quality control initiatives.

## Methods
This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines, and the checklist for prevalence studies is included as part of the critical appraisal tools for JBI (formerly the Joanna Briggs Institute) Systematic Reviews. The CoCoPop framework for systematic reviews of prevalence, defining the condition, context and population, was applied to guide the inclusion criteria and ensure conceptual clarity and consistency used in this study design. This study was a systematic review that analysed previously published, de-identified data and did not involve interaction with human participants; therefore, it did not require Institutional Review Board oversight.

## Patient and public involvement
Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this systematic review and meta-analysis, as the study relied exclusively on previously published data.

## Population
The population comprised surgical tissue cases processed for standard histopathology testing. The inclusion criteria were any (1) solid tissue type and (2) specimen complexity, including biopsies and complex resections. Exclusion criteria were populations consisting of (1) cytopathology, (2) blood samples, (3) microbiology and (4) samples other than surgical pathology specimens.

## Scope of review
This systematic review considered technical errors from all work phases of the TTP: (1) pre-analytic errors, (2) actively identified laboratory errors and (3) reported surgical pathology errors. The TTP begins with tissue collection in a procedure or operating room, placement into a labelled container and completion of a test requisition form. Errors occurring before laboratory receipt are classified as pre-analytic. Upon arrival, laboratoryaccessioning in a histology is the critical first laboratory step, during which tissue specimens are formally received, documented, and uniquely identified before processing. Next, the specimen is macroscopically analysed, described, dissected as needed and placed into one or more cassettes. These cassettes proceed to histology, where tissue is paraffinised, embedded and sectioned into 3–7 µm-thick slices (microtomy). Sections are floated on the surface of a water bath, mounted onto labelled slides, stained and sent for pathologist review. A single specimen may yield multiple cassettes and slides. In this study, errors during accessioning, grossing, embedding, microtomy and staining are categorised as laboratory errors. Surgical pathology errors could have occurred anywhere in the TTP workflow ( ).
 

## Error definition
In this review, errors are events affecting specimens that may lead to inaccurate results. This study is limited to technical errors in the handling and processing of surgical specimens. We distinguish between reported and actively identified errors. Reported errors are those formally documented during routine practice, typically captured in error and quality logs, amended reports, adverse events, root cause analysis and near-miss databases. By contrast, actively identified errors are detected through direct observation, investigations, audits or error-tracking efforts outside routine documentation (see for additional information).

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The inclusion criteria encompassed technical errors during all phases of the TTP, from specimen collection and processing to result reporting, and included discrete technical errors, such as misidentification, sample loss, contamination and typographical errors. Exclusion criteria were professional judgement errors, ambiguous errors such as fixation, equipment malfunctions and errors occurring outside the scope of routine testing. Equipment and technology that may be related to technical errors are described in .
All contexts and settings for routine surgical pathology testing were included: (1) all specimen collection settings, including operating rooms, outpatient clinics, doctors’ offices and emergency rooms; (2) all histology laboratory types, small community laboratories, medium and large laboratories, reference laboratories, teaching laboratories and (3) all countries.
The study design inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) peer-reviewed, (2) prospective or retrospective and (3) randomised clinical trials, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies and observational prevalence studies. Excluded study designs were (1) case reports, (2) case series studies, (3) editorials, (4) reviews without primary prevalence data and (5) studies using data already represented in an included study. Articles published before 1 January 2004 were excluded from this review due to technological and practice changes before that date.

## Outcomes
The primary outcome of interest was the cumulative technical error prevalence across the TTP reported per 1000 cases. All included data were standardised to the patient-case level, representing the complete set of specimens, cassettes and slides associated with a single patient during a single clinical encounter. Secondary outcomes included the distribution of errors by phase (pre-analytic, laboratory or surgical pathology) and by detection method (actively observed or reported).

## Search strategy
From 28 May to 29 May 2025, two authors (AK and KL) conducted independent searches of PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane Library and Science Direct databases using the following search terms: defect, error, discordance, surgical specimen, surgical pathology specimen and anatomic pathology specimen. The search terms were used both singularly and in the plural, separately and combined with Boolean operators such as ‘OR’ or ‘AND’. The specific search strings and search numbers are available in . Additionally, reference and citation searching of included articles was performed using ResearchRabbit, and authors of the included articles were consulted ( ). To enhance completeness beyond database searching, we used ResearchRabbit, a publicly available citation-mapping tool, to perform backward citation screening and forward citation tracking for all included studies. This approach facilitated the identification of relevant referenced and citing articles not captured in initial searches. All records identified through citation tracking were screened using the same eligibility criteria as those from database searches. Resulting articles were imported into Covidence (2024) software, and duplicate articles were removed. All search articles were available in English. At least two of the reviewers (AK, JMJ, PG) independently screened the titles and abstracts of search result studies. One full article could not be retrieved despite contacting the author and extensive searching.
Two reviewers considered the full text of the articles for inclusion according to the eligibility criteria. Reviewer discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consultation with the third reviewer. contain a complete list of included study characteristics and references, author contributions and credentials.

## Data extraction
Two of the three reviewers (AK, JMJ, PG) independently extracted data from the included studies using Covidence. Data on error prevalence and types, processing steps and study design were extracted using a prespecified extraction template ( ). The data were compared, and notes were combined. Data were standardised prior to analysis ( ).

## Risk of bias assessment
Two of the three reviewers (AK, JMJ, PG) independently conducted the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data to assess the quality of included studies ( ). A quantitative scoring approach was used, with 1 point for ‘Yes’ and 0 points for ‘No’ or ‘Unclear’. Disagreement between reviewers was resolved by consulting the third reviewer and discussing to reach a consensus.
Studies were required to have a minimum score of five to be included in the meta-analysis, as previously described in similar published studies. No studies in this systematic review were excluded due to low quality.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were completed using STATA/SE 18.0 (StataCorp). Meta-analysis with a random effects Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) model using the Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine-transformation for proportions method was performed to calculate the prevalence rates and 95% CI of TTP errors for workflow phases. The Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine-transformed proportions method is a variance-stabilising transformation that is appropriate when proportions are close to 0. Forest plots were generated for pre-analytic errors, actively identified laboratory errors and reported surgical pathology errors to estimate the TTP’s cumulative error prevalence. Subgroup analyses of study scope and types of errors were performed to identify factors contributing to the heterogeneity of the results ( ). Heterogeneity was measured using I2 and prediction intervals (95% PIs). Leave-one-out analysis, meta-regression and forest plots by publication year were performed ( ). 

## Publication bias
Funnel plots were constructed to evaluate publication bias qualitatively ( ). Using a random-effects model, a regression-based Egger test for small-study effects was performed ( ). A non-parametric trim-and-fill analysis of publication bias was also performed ( ).

## Certainty of evidence
The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework was used to rate the certainty of the evidence as low, moderate or high ( ).

## Results

## Study selection
Literature searches returned 818 unique studies. After assessing studies based on titles and abstracts, the full text of 87 studies was reviewed for eligibility. The meta-analysis included 30 articles published between 2005 and 2024 using predetermined criteria. A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow chart is shown in . All included studies had a quality assessment rating between 6 and 9 ( ).

## Descriptive statistics
The review’s total sample size was 2 794 987 surgical pathology cases. Teaching institutions were heavily represented, with only four studies representing laboratories without residency programmes; however, non-teaching hospitals may have been represented in the 11 multi-institutional studies. The included studies represented 10 countries within North America, Europe and Asia. The smallest sample size was 200, and the largest was 1 283 412. The studies were predominantly retrospective observational prevalence studies. The error detection methods included reported errors from error logs, amended reports and adverse events databases, as well as active error detection, such as direct observation, comparative DNA testing, specimen tracking methods (RFID tags or colour-coding specimens), rotational inking and secondary review of slides and pathology reports.

## Pre-analytic errors
The pre-analytic phase analysis included 16 studies (n=760 740), which resulted in a pooled prevalence of 16.71 errors per 1000 surgical pathology cases (95% CI 6.42 to 31.67; I2=99.92%, 95% PI 0 to 118.20) ( ). The meta-analysis I2 showed high heterogeneity with stable pooled estimates in leave-one-out sensitivity analyses ( ), suggesting that variability reflects dispersed study-level differences rather than influence from a single outlier study. Funnel plot inspection ( ) and statistical tests (Egger’s p=0.1162; trim-and-fill with 0 imputed studies) provided no evidence of publication bias ( ). Meta-regression revealed no significant publication year trend (p=0.914), suggesting that error rates have remained relatively stable over the past two decades despite technological and procedural advances ( ). Subgroup analysis revealed minimal differences between single-institution and multi-institution studies (p=0.92) ( ).
 

## Actively identified laboratory errors
Actively identified (observed, contaminated and occult) laboratory error rates were reported in 11 studies (n=1 736 663). The pooled error prevalence of these studies was 40.17 errors per 1000 cases (95% CI 7.04 to 97.61; with high heterogeneity (I²=99.99%) and a wide PI (0 to 377.75) ( ). Error type subgroup analyses demonstrated an occult error prevalence of 2.54 errors per 1000 (95% CI 1.63 to 3.64), and a contamination prevalence of 62.23 per 1000 (95% CI 8.22 to 159.33) ( ). Sensitivity testing through leave-one-out analyses confirmed stable pooled estimates despite high heterogeneity ( ). Bias assessment indicated minimal funnel plot asymmetry ( ) with the Egger test showing a borderline association with small-study effects (p=0.0723), though not statistically significant ( ). Trim-and-fill analysis imputed zero missing studies, suggesting publication bias is unlikely ( ). Meta-regression revealed no association between publication year and error prevalence (p=0.961), with substantial residual heterogeneity persisting (I²=99.89%; Q_res p≤0.0000) ( ). Subgroup analysis comparing multi-institution versus single-institution studies showed no significant differences (p=0.21) ( ).
 

## Reported surgical pathology errors
Six studies (n=726 529) included rates of reported surgical pathology technical errors ranging from 0.52 to 21.26 per 1000 cases, with a pooled prevalence of 4.94 (95% CI 1.33 to 10.80) ( ). Heterogeneity was high (I²=99.85%), though leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed that no single study disproportionately influenced the summary estimate ( ). The funnel plot ( ) and Egger test (p=0.0008) ( ) indicated small-study effects and possible publication bias; however, trim-and-fill did not impute missing studies ( ). Meta-regression revealed no significant trend by publication year (p=0.495), with residual heterogeneity remaining substantial (I²=99.79%; Q_res p≤0.000) ( ).
 

## TTP errors
Combining the pooled pre-analytic estimate (16.71/1000) with the actively identified laboratory estimate (40.17/1000) suggests an approximate composite burden of ~56.9 errors per 1000 cases (5.7%) across the pre-analytic and laboratory phases ( ).
 

## Certainty of evidence
Across non-randomised studies of surgical pathology specimens, the prevalence of pre-analytical technical errors was estimated at 16.7 per 1000 specimens, 40.2 per 1000 actively identified laboratory errors, and 4.9 per 1000 reported laboratory errors, with overall low certainty of evidence due to inconsistency and imprecision ( ).

## Discussion
This study is a comprehensive review and meta-analysis investigating errors across the pathology tissue specimen TTP. By synthesising evidence from multiple studies, we evaluated the cumulative error rate of the TTP, compared frequencies between errors occurring during collection, actively identified laboratory errors and formally reported surgical pathology errors. Previous research has focused on isolated workflow steps, particularly pre-analytical or diagnostic errors, and has often relied on incidental error reporting through amended or adverse event reports. Despite the low certainty of the evidence, this study identified a substantial discrepancy between actively identified errors and reported errors, suggesting that amended reports and documented error rates underestimate the true burden of errors within the tissue specimen TTP.
Considerable heterogeneity exists across studies of surgical specimen processing errors, often driven by variability in study design, error definitions, detection methods, institutional practices, specimen processing methods and outcome assessment, hindering cross-study comparisons. Studies using active surveillance methods, such as real-time monitoring or systematic secondary review, capture a broader range of errors, including minor events and near misses. The reliance on passive reporting systems, such as voluntary self-reporting or institutional databases, may lead to underestimation due to unreported or informally corrected incidents. Further complicating comparability, fear of blame, time constraints and cultural barriers can deter reporting in some settings. In contrast, institutions with a strong quality culture may report more errors, not because they perform worse, but due to greater transparency. These methodological and cultural differences contribute to divergent error-prevalence estimates, which may reflect differences in detection capacity rather than true performance disparities.
In addition to methodological variation, workflows in low- and middle-income countries introduce context-specific factors that further limit the generalisability of pooled estimates. Laboratories in these settings may rely more heavily on manual processes due to limited access to automation, resulting in different error types and frequencies than in high-resource environments. Staff training variability, high workload intensity and limited opportunities for continuing professional development also shape how and where errors emerge. Often, resources are directed toward essential service delivery at the expense of quality assurance, reducing the feasibility of systematic error detection. Moreover, differences in patient demographics, case complexity and disease burden can alter diagnostic workflows and influence the types of errors observed. These factors underscore the need to interpret error data within operational and regional contexts, as estimates from well-resourced institutions may not reflect conditions in resource-constrained environments.
The cumulative estimated TTP technical error prevalence based on the reviewed literature was 56.88 errors per 1000 cases, which stands in marked contrast to the 4.94 technical errors per 1000 cases described in studies relying on formally documented surgical pathology errors. This discrepancy is concerning and suggests that some errors remain undetected or are not considered reportable errors. The true error prevalence has widespread implications for all clinical disciplines that use pathology results to guide treatment decisions. Underestimating the prevalence of errors reduces the urgency and resources dedicated to improving processes and clinician vigilance when interpreting unanticipated results.
The most information was available regarding specimen collection, handling and submission during the pre-analytic phase; however, the least data was identified analysing the highly complex processes that occur in the laboratory. Contrary to the prevailing consensus that most specimen errors arise during the pre-analytical phase, our meta‐analysis demonstrated that the laboratory processes were the most error-prone component of the TTP. Each time a specimen is relabelled or manually manipulated, opportunities for misidentification, contamination and tissue loss are introduced. Although laboratory processing is central to transforming a tissue specimen into diagnostic information, relatively little is known about the types, prevalence and consequences of errors occurring during this aspect of processing. This knowledge gap highlights the laboratory as a critical target for systematic quality improvement efforts.
In our analysis, reported surgical pathology errors were approximately eightfold lower than the actively identified laboratory errors. Inconsistent definitions of error can partly explain this discordance. For example, although contamination is among the most frequent laboratory errors, Lamothe et al found that only 6.1% of surveyed pathology laboratories had written reporting protocols, and few tracked the rates, indicating contamination is significantly underestimated. Direct observation studies have recorded up to 5.5 near misses per specimen that required correction to provide the appropriate diagnosis during the accessioning and grossing steps alone. Retrospective studies that rely on self-reporting systems are particularly susceptible to reporting bias, since quality issues are documented only when individuals voluntarily report them. Under-reporting is common and may stem from concerns about blame, time pressure, competing workload demands or organisational cultures that discourage disclosure. Conversely, departments with strong quality assurance systems and a non-punitive environment may report more errors, not due to higher failure rates, but because detection is more systematic and transparency is actively encouraged.
Many surgical pathology studies included only errors that required amended reports. In the 2007 D’Angelo study, 414 observed technical errors only resulted in 12 amended reports; based on this data, using the amended report rate as a quality indicator underestimates the error prevalence by 97.1%.
Failure to detect, report, analyse and address all errors impairs the ability to improve the process and increases the likelihood of error recurrence that may result in misdiagnosis and patient harm. A survey by Kaldjian suggested that errors are frequently under-reported. Physicians responded that reporting errors is essential, but only about half knew how to report them. 
Studies of occult errors confirm that specimen misidentifications happen and remain undetected during routine processing. Demetrick compared the DNA of two different specimens from the same patient and discovered unexplained DNA mismatches in one of the 942 specimen pairs. Similarly, Marberger used DNA analysis to discover that 26 of 6455 specimens were misidentified, and an additional 859 specimens were contaminated with foreign DNA. Pfeifer’s DNA analysis of 12 947 specimens revealed 34 specimens identified as the wrong patient, and an additional 87 were contaminated with foreign DNA. Findings from these studies illustrate that technical errors create serious risks to patient safety, including misdiagnoses and inappropriate treatments.
To bridge the gap between actively identified and formally reported errors, the development of automated detection and reporting systems is essential. Such systems can reduce reliance on inconsistent self-reporting and counteract cultural reluctance to disclose errors. Fostering a blameless culture of quality, supported by cross-departmental training and shared accountability, can improve both transparency and proactive quality improvement. Implementing standardised protocols for error detection, routine staff training and regular monitoring, using tools such as case reviews or audits, can collectively improve diagnostic reliability and reduce preventable harm. Future research should focus on resolving methodological inconsistencies and creating reliable performance benchmarks adaptable across diverse laboratory contexts.

## Limitations
This review has several limitations. The included studies exhibited substantial heterogeneity in study design, error definitions and error detection methods. This variability likely contributed to the high I² values observed and reduced the precision of pooled prevalence estimates. Pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they synthesise data from heterogeneous studies and may therefore not reflect a single underlying truth about the prevalence or nature of these errors. Of note, high heterogeneity in prevalence studies is common and can reflect true variability. Second, many of the studies were observational and retrospective, which could introduce reporting bias. Although a comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases, it remains possible that some relevant studies were missed. Third, this review intentionally focused on procedural and technical errors, excluding cognitive and diagnostic errors. However, this exclusion likely underestimates the total burden of TTP errors. Fourth, error prevalence alone does not capture the clinical severity or consequences of errors, which can range from negligible patient impact to serious adverse consequences.

## Strengths
Despite the limitations, this study has important strengths. It is, to our knowledge, the first systematic review and meta-analysis to examine errors across the entire surgical pathology TTP, encompassing more than 2.7 million surgical pathology cases. By synthesising data across workflow phases, the review provides a comprehensive and structured understanding of where and how errors arise within the testing process. Our findings demonstrate that errors in surgical specimen processing are substantially more prevalent than reported by quality metrics, and that active error-detection methods are far more sensitive than passive, self-reporting methods traditionally used.

## Conclusion
This review advances our understanding of technical error prevalence and offers actionable implications for patient safety. Findings underscore a critical need for standardised error definitions, detection strategies, consistent error tracking across all phases of specimen processing, supported by proactive quality improvement initiatives. The substantial heterogeneity across included studies reflects wide variation in institutional workflows, error definitions and detection practices. As a result, the pooled estimates presented here should be interpreted as approximations of overall trends rather than precise indicators of underlying error rates. Addressing the persistent gap between actively identified and formally reported errors will require investment in automated detection systems, standardised protocols, routine staff training and a non-punitive culture of quality. Strengthening these systems-level safeguards can improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce costs and ultimately improve patient outcomes and overall laboratory quality.
