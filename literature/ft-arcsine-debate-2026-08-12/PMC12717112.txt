## Abstract
Graft versus host disease (GVHD) is a major drawback for haematopoietic stem cell transplant (SCT). Steroids are the main therapy. However, steroid resistance (SR) has poor outcome. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) had been assessed in several clinical trials but no consistent results. Here we present a systematic review and meta-analysis to explore the potential role of MSCs in steroid refractory acute GVHD (SR-aGVHD). We analysed 16 trials with total of 1301 patients. Primary end points included the safety of MSCs intravenous infusion (IVI), overall response rate (ORR) and overall survival (OS). Secondary end points included complete response rate (CR), relapse rate (RR) and organ specific response. Also, we analysed effect of other variables on outcome such as conditioning intensity, GVHD severity, age of recipients and MSCs related variables. ORR was 0.61 with 95% CI 0.54–0.67, CR rate was 0.29 with a 95% CI 0.23–0.36. Regarding organ specific response, Skin-ORR was 0.73, 95% CI 0.6–0.84, Gut-ORR was 0.62, 95% CI 0.54–0.7 and Liver-ORR was 0.55, 95% CI: 0.37–0.73. No statistically significant difference between the 3 organs ORR. At a median of 12 months, OS was 0.34 with 95% CI 0.29–0.4. However, in the controlled trials OS post MSCs was comparable with the control and ratio was 1.15, 95% CI of 0.81–1.62 and P = 0.4. RR was 0.10, 95% CI 0.06–0.15. Also, RR in controlled trials was comparable between MSCs recepients and control, risk ratio was 0.62, 95% CI of 0.3–1.25 and P = 0.18. Regarding heterogeniety, we assessed effect of conditioning intensity, recepients’ age, GVHD severity and MSCs related variables. ORR post “MAC” was comparable with “nMAC”, P = 0.7. GVHD grade IV cohorts had ORR of 0.51 while less severe GVHD had ORR 0.66 and P = 0.006. Young age (< 50 ys) was associtaed with better ORR and OS. ORR in “young” was 0.64 while older age had ORR of 0.5 and P = 0.04. And OS in “young” was 0.41 while older cohort had OS 0.25 and P = 0.04. Regarding MSCs related variables, dose of 1 × 106 cell/kg was comparable with 2 × 106 cell/kg. However, frequent MSCs infusions “ 4 IVIs in first 28 days” was associated with ORR 0.64, 95% CI 0.56–0.72 while ORR in the infrequent infusions cohort was 0.47, 95% CI 0.34 - o.62 and P = 0.04. This was also translated to better survival. OS in the “Frequent” cohort was 0.4, 95% CI 0.35–0.45 while OS in the “Infrequent” cohort was 0.22, 95% CI 0.17–0.28 and P < 0.0001. Otherwise, once weekly MSCs infusion was equivalent to twice weekly and ORR post cryopreserved MSCs was comparable with fresh cells. MSCs IVI is a safe effective therapeutic modality for SR-aGVHD. Dose of 1 × 106 cell/kg and frequency of 4 or more IVI within first 4 weeks seem the optimal conditions.

## Introduction
 Over about 70 years, haematopoietic stem cell transplant (SCT) represented an important therapeutic modality for many hematologic disorders . With advancement of patient care, the pace of utilisation of SCT is increasing significantly. However, GVHD remains a major limitation and a main cause of non-relapse mortality .
Currently, steroids are the first line therapy . Response rate is about 50% while non-responders have significantly lower overall survival (OS) , .
Other lines of therapy include extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP), Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), T-cell depletion, anti-cytokine antibodies and JAK inhibitors . Nevertheless, these therapies showed variable rates of success in addition to risk of infection, toxicity, graft failure and relapse .
Given the fact that acute GVHD is an inflammatory process driven by activated T-lymphocytes with subsequent inflammatory cytokine release. Therefore, anti-inflammatory potential of MSCs represents an attractive therapeutic modality.
MSCs have immune modulatory properties that could potentially reverse GVHD pathogenesis and augment tissue regeneration. They have anti-inflammatory properties such as priming pro-inflammatory TH1 cells into anti-inflammatory TH2 cells, repressing inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-8, TNF and IFNγ while up-regulating the anti-inflammatory IL-10 .
Also, MSCs support tissue regeneration via several mechanisms such as resuming integrity of endothelial barrier, activating macrophages as well as antiapoptotic and antifibrotic properties .
One of the interesting properties is that MSCs weakly express HLA-I and do not express HLA-II antigens. So, they do not trigger immune response by cytotoxic lymphocytes or NK cells. Therefore, MSCs are effectively non-immunogenic and can be transplanted across the HLA barriers , .
Furthermore, pre-clinical studies demonstrated that MSCs supported progenitor haematopoietic cell proliferation, differentiation and enhanced engraftment post allo SCT. In addition, MSCs delayed rejection of non-matched skin grafts and renal transplants as well .
In clinical practice, utilisation of MSCs for SR-GVHD goes back to the landmark report in 2004 by Le Blanc et al. who successfully treated severe GVHD in a 9 years old boy using haplo identical MSCs from his mother . Afterwards, many trials investigated utilisation of MSCs in SR-GVHD and it is currently approved as a standard of care in Japan, New Zeeland and Canada – .
Nevertheless, clinical trials showed inconsistent results . This is likely due to lack of consensus about “ Clinical grade MSCs” among other factors such as the optimum dose of cells and frequency of infusions.
We demonstrated previously safety and efficacy of MSCs in sepsis . Here, we aimed to systematically assess MSCs utilisation in SR-aGVHD in view of the available evidence. Also, analyse effect of different variables to conclude the best conditions for MSCs utilisation.

## Methodology

## Search and selection process
We followed the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidance . Database search included PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library and clinical trials starting from inception until September 2024. In addition to citation tracking and manual screening. We used the key words: mesenchymal stem cells, mesenchymal stromal cells, steroid refractory graft versus host disease and GVHD.
The search resulted in 773 articles. Using automated search 599 were excluded as they did not meet inclusion criteria. 174 articles were screened, 129 were further excluded and 45 were screened in detail. Then, 29 were further excluded as did meet exclusion criteria. Finally 16 studies with total of 1301 patients were taken forward for analysis, Fig.  .
 Inclusion criteria included: phase II and/or III for prospective trials, utilised MSCs for SR-aGVHD. Retro-spective studies were included and we did not specify randomisation as conditions for study selection. Other criteria included full article available, English language and data available in an analysable format.
Exclusion criteria included MSCs in non-SR-aGVHD indications, chronic GVHD, phase I trials, pilot studies, case series (defined as volume 15 patients), pure pediatric studies, non-English, commentaries, conference abstracts, pre-clinical and in-vitro studies.
Primary end points included (i) safety of MSCs intravenous infusion (IVI), (ii) overall response rate (ORR) and (iii) overall survival (OS). Secondary endpoints : complete response rate (CR), relapse rate of underlying malignancy, organ specific response and effect of other variables such as recipient’s age, GVHD severity, cell dose and frequency of IVI.
Data were extracted manually by two investigators. If discrepancy is encountered then discussion was done to achieve consensus.
Data collected included basic characteristics of study participants such as age, gender, underlying disease/indication for SCT and pre-transplant conditioning. Also, GVHD characteristics such as organs involved, severity, prophylaxis and therapies utilised. In addition to MSCs related variables such as tissue source, dose, frequency of infusion, preparations (fresh vs. cryopreserved).
Data were tabulated for further analysis purposes.
The selection process flow chart is summarised in Fig.  .

## Statistical analysis
Since the analysed studies had different variables such as age, gender ratio, disease distribution, conditioning, GVHD prophylaxis and treatment strategies among other variables. So, we could not assume same effect size across all studies. Therefore, the random effects model was utilised , .
Across the whole meta-analysis, apriori random effects model with the inverse variance method and Freeman-Tukey Double arcsine was utilised to calculate the mean effect size as it would consider within and in-between studies heterogeneity and Mantel-Haenszel method to compare the rate ratio. P value < 0.05 (two sided) was considered statistically significant.
For heterogeneity assessment, a criterion alpha of 0.1 was used to test the null hypothesis that the true effect size is the same in all studies. Tau-squared (T2) was expressed in logit units and was used to express the variance of true effect sizes . The I -squared statistic (I2) was utilised to assess the true variance in effect size between studies rather than sampling error.
The prediction interval (PI) was calculated with the assumption that true effects are normally distributed (in logit units) and the true effect size in 95% of all comparable populations would fall in this interval.
To explore potential causes for heterogeneity, sub-group analysis was utilised. We analysed effect of different variables such as age, GVHD severity and cells related variables.
The online meta-analysis calculator https://metaanalysisonline.com was used for analysis .
Across studies, overall survival (OS) was assessed from time of first MSCs IVI to death from any cause or end of observation period for alive subjects that are treated as censored data.

## Risk of bias (RoB) assessment
The Cochrane ROBINS-I tool, revised version (V2) for risk of bias assessment in non-randomised interventions was used. And the online software RobV tool was utilised to generate visual representation.
The ROBINS-I tool addresses seven domain: “1. Risk of bias due to confounding factors, 2. Risk of bias in classification of interventions, 3. Risk of bias in selection of participants into the study (or into the analysis), 4. Risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions, 5. Risk of bias due to missing data, 6. Risk of bias arising from measurement of the outcome, 7. Risk of bias in selection of the reported result” .
The RoB in each domain was determined following the algorithm in the ROBINS-I tool. The final study RoB was determined by the highest score in any of the domains.
The RoB was categorised as per the ROBINS-I classification into: 1. Low risk, 2. Moderate risk, 3. Serious risk and 4. Critical risk of bias.
Overall, we noted only one trial had moderate RoB, while the rest of trials had low RoB.
(supplementary material 1).

## Definitions
Definition of steroid resistant acute GVHD (SR-aGVHD) varied across studies. Most trials defined steroid refractoriness (SR) as: (i) Progress of 1 grade after 72 h or (ii) No response after 7 days or (iii) No partial response after 14 days.
While Muroi et al., Dotoli et al. and Zhao et al. 2022 report used the 5 days no response as the cut-off point , , and Salmenniemi et al. added recurrence on weaning steroids as an indicator of SR .
Most studies used steroid dose of 2 mg/kg Methyl prednisolone (MP) while Dotoli et al. used dose of 1 mg/kg MP and Murata et al. used steroid dose equivalent to prednisolone at ≥0.5 mg/kg .
Nevertheless, analysis based on these variables (cut-off point to define SR or steroid dose) did not reveal any significant difference (data not shown).

## Results

## Characteristics of studies
The analysis included 16 trials that met the inclusion criteria. Total of 1301 patients, 1102 received MSCs for SR-aGVHD and 199 in the control group. Basic characteristics of trials included in the analysis are summarised in Table  .
 

## Safety of MSCs intra-venous infusion (IVI)
Due to limited number of placebo controlled trials, meta-analysis of adverse events (AEs) related to MSCs IVI was not possible. Nevertheless, most studies reported safe MSCs IVI , , , .
Across studies, adverse events (AEs) were quite limited including nausea and vomiting in 1 case and headache in 1 case presumed to be due to DMSO and cold IVI respectively and no limiting toxicity was reported . Serious AEs and AEs of grade III were reported, however, they were statistically comparable with the control cohorts , , .
Notably, major haemorrhagic events were reported such as fatal haemorrhagic cytitis, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, brain stem bleeding, cerebral bleeding and other major bleeds , , , , . Nevertheless, it was not possible to draw clear conclusion about causality versus co-incidence but it is worth further assessment.

## Response

## Overall response rate (ORR)
Effect size was ORR. All together 16 studies were analyzed with a total of 1102 subjects. ORR was 0.61 with 95% CI 0.54–0.67, PI 0.34–0.85 and I2 78.5%. A significant heterogeneity was detected (p < 0.01), suggesting inconsistent effects in magnitude and/or direction. I2 indicates that 78% of variability is due to heterogeneity rather than random chance. Across studies, weight was homogenous with no single study dominating the results, Fig.  .
 ORR: overall response rate and PI: prediction interval.

## Complete response rate (CR)
Then, we assessed CR rate across evaluable studies. All together 15 studies were analyzed with a total of 1017 subjects. CR rate was 0.29 with a 95% CI 0.23–0.36, PI 0.08–0.56 and I2 77.7%. Significant heterogeneity was detected (p < 0.01), indicating inconsistent effects in magnitude and/or direction. I2 value indicates that 78% of variability arises from heterogeneity rather than random chance, Fig.  .
 

## Organ specific response
Then, we assessed differential effect of MSCs at organ level. Here, number of subjects in each study was reduced to the number of patients affected at every particular organ. All together 7 studies were analyzed with a total of 840 subjects. For skin SR-aGVHD response to MSCs, the mean effect size (Skin-ORR) was 0.73, 95% CI 0.6–0.84, Tau2 = 0.018 and I2 = 70.5%. For gut SR-aGVHD response to MSCs, the mean effect size (Gut-ORR) was 0.62 with 95% CI 0.54–0.7, Tau2 = 0.006 and I2 = 59.8%. Then, for liver SR-aGVHD response to MSCs IVI, mean effect size (Liver-ORR) was 0.55 with 95% CI: 0.37–0.73, Tau2 = 0.03 and I2 = 71.2%, Fig.  .
 Significant heterogeneity was noted in all organs’ ORR while no statistically significant difference between the 3 organs response rate, p = 0.2.

## A. Overall survival (OS)
Then, we assessed OS post MSCs in SR-aGVHD cases. All together 15 studies were analyzed with a total of 1092 subjects. OS at a median of 12 months was 0.34 with 95% CI of 0.29–0.4 and PI 0.17–0.54 and I2 65.5%. A significant heterogeneity was detected (p < 0.01), suggesting inconsistent effects in magnitude and/or direction. I2 value indicates that 66% of variability arises from heterogeneity rather than random chance, Fig.  .
 

## B. Survival post MSCs vs. control
Then, we assessed survival in the controlled trials. Three trials were placebo controlled and one utilised historical control . A total of 348 subjects receievd MSCs and 234 subjects in the control cohort. Overall ratio is 1.15, 95% CI 0.81–1.62, PI 0.44–3.01 and P = 0.4.
Heterogenity was detected with I2 = 0.54% indicating about 54% of variability among studies is due to true heterogeneity rather than random chance, Fig.  .
 

## Relapse rate of underlying malignancy (RR)
Then, we assessed the relapse rate (RR). All together 10 studies were analyzed with a total of 828 subjects. The RR was 0.10 with a 95% CI of 0.06–0.15, PI 0.00–0.28 and I2 73.9%. A significant heterogeneity was detected (p < 0.01), suggesting inconsistent effects in magnitude and/or direction. The I2 value indicates that 74% of the variability among studies arises from heterogeneity rather than random chance, Fig.  .
 Nevertheless, given that most trials did not report full details about disease status, so data about malignancy relapse should be interpreted with caution.

## Relapse rate (RR) in CTs
Then, RR in controlled trials was assessed but no significant difference was detected. All together 4 studies were analyzed with a total of 348 subjects in the Experimental cohort and 234 subjects in the Control cohort. Overall risk ratio is 0.62 with a 95% CI of 0.31–1.25 and I2 34.1%. No significant heterogeneity was detected which indicates consistent effect sizes across studies in both magnitude and direction.
However, same limitation applies as missing crucial data about disease status and other lines of therapies applied prior to MSCs utilisation Fig. .
 

## Heterogeneity assessment
Heterogeneity was notable across studies. I2 of 78% in ORR indicated significant difference in mean effect size in magnitude and/or direction. This seems a multifactorial phenomenon.
Part of the heterogeneity spectrum could go to the differences in the cohorts recruited across studies, treatments utilised prior to MSCs infusion as well as cells related variables.
So, we started to analyse effect of different potential variables.

## Conditioning intensity does not affect responsiveness
Studies were categorised per conditioning intensity into “MAC” if > 50% of cases received myeloablative conditioning and “nMAC” if < 50% had myeloablative conditioning. All together 11 studies were analyzed with a total of 685 subjects. The “MAC” cohort had ORR of 0.59, 95% CI 0.43–0.74 while ORR in the “nMAC” cohort was 0.56 with 95% CI 0.51–0.6. The difference was non-significant, P = 0.7, Fig.  .
 This analysis was extended to effect of conditioning intensity on survival including subgroup analysis within the cohorts that receieved frequent and infrequent MSCs infusions. However, all results were comparable with no significant superiority for any subgroup (data not shown).

## Effect of GVHD severity on ORR
We divided studies into “Advanced” when 50% or more of the cohort had grade IV SR-aGVHD, and “Not-advanced” if percent of grade IV SR-aGVHD was < 50%. All together 13 studies were analyzed with a total of 1005 subjects. The “Not-advanced” cohort had ORR of 0.66, 95% CI 0.57–0.75 while the “Advanced” cohort had ORR of 0.51, 95% CI 0.44–0.57 and P = 0.006 in keeping with significant difference in favour of the less severe aGVHD, Fig.  .
 

## Young age is associated with better response (ORR) to MSCs
Then, we assessed effect of recipients’ age on ORR post MSCs. We categorised patients per age into “Young” for median age < 50 years and “Old” for median age > 50 years old. All together 14 studies were analyzed with a total of 914 subjects. The “Young” cohort had ORR of 0.64, 95% CI 0.54–0.74 while the “Old” cohort had ORR of 0.5, 95% CI 0.41–0.59 and P = 0.04 indicating significant difference in favour of the young age, Fig.  .
 

## Young age is associated with better overall survival (OS)
Then, we assessed effect of age on survival. We extended same categorisation into “Young” for median age < 50 years and “Old” for median age 50 years and effect size was the OS.
All together 14 studies were analyzed with a total of 1003 subjects. The “Young” cohort had OS of 0.41, 95% CI 0.31–0.50 while OS in the “Old"cohort was 0.25, 95% CI 0.14–0.37 and P = 0.04 which indicates statistical significance, Fig.  .
 

## Mesenchymal stromal cells’ variables
MSCs related variables.
Across studies, MSCs were mostly extracted from the BM , , , , – , – , , , . However, Keklik and Macias-Sanchez utilised MSCs from adipose tissue and umbilical cord , .
Median dose across studies ranged from 0.99 to 4 × 106 cell/kg , . And frequency of MSCs IVI also varied from 1 to 8 IVIs , , while extra IVI were allowed in some trials to boost the initial effect , , in cases with PR at day 28. Also, Muroi et al. allowed additional MSCs IVIs of same dose weekly up to 52 weeks .
Most studies utilised cryopreserved cells while Zhao 2015, Zhao 2022 reports and Dotoli et al. utilised fresh cells and Macias-Sanchez thawed the cells a week before IVI , , , .

## Effect of MSCs dose on overall response rate (ORR)
We assessed effect of MSCs dose on response rate. Mean effect size was ORR. Based on median MSCs dose, subjects were categorised into those who utilised 1 × 106 cell/kg as “One” and those who utilised 2 × 106 cell/kg as “Two”. All together 15 studies were analyzed with a total of 1084 subjects. ORR in the “One” cohort was 0.59 with 95% CI 0.46–0.7, while the “Two” cohort had ORR of 0.57 with 95% CI 0.52–0.61 and P= 0.7 which indicates no significant difference, Fig.  .
 

## Frequent MSCs IVI is associated with better overall response rate (ORR)
Based on frequency of MSCs IVIs, we categorised patients across studies into “Frequent” for those received 4 IVIs and “Infrequent” if < 4 IVIs within first 28 days. All together 16 studies were analyzed with a total of 1089 subjects. The “Frequent” cohort had ORR 0.64 with 95% CI 0.56–0.72 while the ORR in the “Infrequent” cohort was 0.47 with 95% CI 0.34 - o.62 and P = 0.04. This result signals clear superiority of frequent IVIs “ 4 IVIs in first 28 days”, Fig.  .
 

## Frequent MSCs IVI is associated with better overall survival (OS)
After promising results of superiority of the frequent IVIs on ORR, we assessed its effect on OS at a median of 12 months. We continued same categorisation based on frequency of MSCs IVIs as “Frequent” for those received 4 IVIs and “Infrequent” if < 4 IVIs. All together 15 studies were analyzed with a total of 1092 subjects. The “Frequent” cohort had OS of 0.4 with 95% CI 0.35–0.45 while OS in the “Infrequent” cohort was 0.22 with 95% CI 0.17–0.28 and P < 0.0001, which indicates superior OS with frequent MSCs IVI relative to infrequent, Fig.  .
 

## ORR post once weekly is equivalent to twice weekly IVI
Then we did further subgroup analysis to find out if there is any difference within the “frequent” group between once weekly IVI or more. We divided the frequent IVI group into “Once” for once weekly IVI and “Two” for more frequent IVI. All together 11 studies were analyzed with a total of 1009 subjects. There was no significant difference between the two subgroups. The once weekly IVI had mean ORR of 0.65 with 95% CI 0.49–0.8 while the more frequent weekly IVI had mean ORR 0.64, 95% CI 0.51–0.76 and p = 0.9. This result indicates equivalence between once or more frequent weekly MSCs IVI, Fig.  .
 Indeed, this effect was also translated to equal effect on OS (data not shown).

## Effect of cryopreservation of MSCs on ORR
Looking into other variables that may affect the response rate, we assessed if usage of MSCs fresh vs. cryopreserved would incur any difference. All together 14 studies were analyzed with a total of 976 subjects. The “Cryo” cohort had ORR of 0.57 with 95% CI 0.48–0.65 while ORR in the “fresh” cohort was 0.65 with 95% CI 0.48–0.8 and P = 0.4, which indicates comparable results, Fig.  .
 Then we utilised same approach to assess effect of cryopreservation vs. fresh MSCs on overall survival and results were still statistically comparable (data not shown).

## Discussion
Steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease (SR-aGVHD) remains a life-threatening complication post-allogeneic stem cell transplantation. A substantial proportion of patients fail to respond to multiple lines of immunosuppressive therapies, often with dismal outcomes. Indeed, overall survival (OS) in this population is consistently reported at only 10–20% , .Therefore, any therapeutic intervention demonstrating a positive impact would be of high clinical value.
Here, we analysed 16 trials that experimented MSCs in SR-aGVHD. MSCs were extracted from 3rd party non-HLA matched healthy volunteers in the majority of trials. However, Le Blanc et al. used MSCs from HLA matching siblings, haplo-identical and 3rd party volunteers as well . They reported no effect of HLA matching on response rate . Also, no reports about cytokine release syndrome or graft versus host reactions, in keeping with the non-immunogenic nature of MSCs .
We started analysis with safety of MSCs intra-venous infusion (IVI) in SR-aGVHD. Most studies reported favorable safety profiles of MSCs , , , . Boberg et al. reported no immediate side effects related to MSC IVI. However, there were 5 cases of grade III infections, 2 instances of dysplasia (skin and cervix) and a patient had rising M-protein level .
Zhao et al. reported 83 AEs including hematologic toxicities (thrombocytopenia and neutropenia), infections, skin, GI, cardiac, liver, renal and vascular events. Importantly, these AEs were comparable with the control group.
Kebriaei et al. similarly found no significant difference in the rates of probable and serious AEs between MSCs recepients and the control cohort . No reports of secondary malignancies . Also, long term safety was reported during follow up period of median 16 months .
Sanchez et al. reported no AEs related to MSCs IVIs. In this cohort, ischemic heart event was experienced twice in a known IHD patient . In addition, they reported CMV reactivation rate of 40% and other infections including fungal and bacterial in 12% and 32% respectively. However, this study was conducted before Letermovir era which changed the landscape of CMV reactivation. So these results would likely differ with current practice. In addition, bacterial infections and concerns around fungal infections are quite common post allogenic SCT and cannot conclude causality with MSCs IVI.
Zhao et al. reported good MSCs tolerance in about 125 IVIs. No AEs grade III/IV were reported. After a median of 115 days, only 1 case of EBV induced post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD). Also, rates of CMV and EBV activation were comparable between MSCs and control group. Importantly, a significantly reduced incidence and severity of chronic GVHD (cGVHD) were reported post MSC .
Previously, some safety concerns about MSCs IVI were raised. For instance, Wu et al. reported thromboembolic events in two patients after MSCs IVI caused by thrombo-inflammation , .
The other safety concern was about potential carcinogenic effects. Nevertheless, studies that raised concern about MSCs potential malignant transformation declared that results were not reproducible and the identified malignant cells were likely contamination . Therefore, current regulations indicate Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) adherence during MSCs handling for clinical applications.
Regarding response to MSCs. Day 28 was utilised as the cut point for response assessment and predictability of overall responsiveness , , . However, some reported progressively better response with time. Also, some of the PR cases at day 28 achieved CR over the course of 6 months follow up. Therefore, CR increased gradually while most non-responders continued to be refractory with high mortality .
Our data demonstrate overall response rate (ORR) was 61% and CR was 30%. Nonetheless, significant heterogeneity was noted. This was evidenced by ORR prediction interval (PI) of 0.34–0.85 with I2 78% and the CR had PI of 0.08–0.56 with I2 77%.
There was notable heterogeneity in this analysis at multiple levels, in keeping with the inconsistent results of clinical trials. This is likely a multifactorial phenomenon due to overlapping influential factors such as patients’ age, GVHD severity and MSCs related variables.
To assess sources of heterogeneity, we analysed effect of conditioning intensity, GVHD severity, recepients’ age and MSCs related variables.
Conditioning intensity did not demonstrate a significant effect on treatment response or survival following MSC infusion. This neutral effect persisted even when stratified by frequency of MSC administration. Overall survival (OS) outcomes were similar across groups receiving either intensive or reduced-intensity conditioning, suggesting that pre-transplant conditioning regimens do not substantially influence MSC efficacy in the SR-aGVHD setting.
Age was a significant determinant of treatment outcome. Patients under 50 years of age demonstrated a superior overall response rate (ORR) of 64%, compared to 50% in patients over 50 years (p = 0.04). This translated into improved survival outcomes: at a median follow-up of 12 months, OS was 41% in the younger cohort versus 25% in the older group (p = 0.04).These findings suggest younger patients mount a more robust response to MSCs based therapies.
GVHD severity also significantly influenced MSC responsiveness. Patients with Grade IV SR-aGVHD had an ORR of 51%, while those with less severe forms of disease demonstrated an ORR of 66% (p = 0.006). This highlights the challenge of treating advanced disease, where irreversible tissue injury and inflammation may overwhelm the immunomodulatory effects of MSCs.
Interestingly, no significant difference in response or survival was observed between patients who received MSCs as a second-line therapy (immediately after steroid failure) versus those treated after multiple lines of prior therapy. This contrasts with findings from our earlier analysis of Ruxolitinib, which demonstrated improved outcomes when used later in the treatment algorithm . This suggests that, unlike some pharmacologic agents, MSCs may be effective across different treatment timepoints without a clear advantage in earlier use.
A key limitation in our analysis was the lack of detailed reporting on prior therapies before MSC infusion.However, it was notable that trials utilised T cell depletion +/- anti-TNFa had low OS. Remarkably, these studies reported infections as the most common cause of death – . Lowest OS 18% was reported by Servais et al. The cohort had infrequent MSCs infusions, T cell depletion and old age .
Further analysis shed light on potential cells related variables that could affect the outcome.
Our data revealed significant effect of frequency of MSCs IVI on ORR which translated clearly into better OS. Frequent IVI defined as 4 or more IVIs over first 28 days achieved significantly better ORR. Frequent MSCs IVI had ORR of 65% which seems close to the JAK inhibitor Ruxolitinib effect of 71% . However, Ruxolitinib had 50% incidence of cytopenias (all grades) mainly anaemia and thrombocytopenia . Obviously, we cannot conclude statistical difference between MSCs and Ruxolitinib. Nevertheless, utilisation of either modality should be considered carefully against the patient condition, potential side effects and availability.
As regard MSCs dose effect, the dose of 1 × 106 cell/kg effect on ORR was comparable with the higher doses. This is consistent with our previous report about differential dose effect of MSCs . These data address the uncertainity about differential dose efficacy that was raised in previous reviews .
While other factors such as once weekly infusions or more and use of cryopreserved versus fresh cells did not show significant difference.
Interestingly, in our meta-analysis of MSCs efficacy in sepsis, the best outcome also correlated with frequent MSCs IVI and the dose of 1 × 106 cell/kg . So, these are the MSCs conditions that proved consistently effective to improve outcome in the inflammatory conditions of sepsis and SR-aGVHD.
This is likely due to short in-vivo life span of MSCs due to enhanced apotosis , . Therefore repeated IVI would enhance durability of MSCs’ anti-inflammatory and regenerative effects.
Regarding organ specific response. Our data show statistically comparable effect. ORR was 74%, 66% and 59% for skin, gut and liver respectively. Some trend to better skin responsiveness relative to liver was noted but results were not statistically significant (data not shown).
Regarding overall survival (OS). Our data show OS of 35% at a median of 12 months. However, analysis of OS in controlled trials showed neutral effect. OS was comparable between MSCs receipients and the control. Indeed, this is in keeping with the comparable relapse rate between MSCs receipients and the control. Also, in keeping with other reports that demonstrated limited effect on long term OS .
This could be expalined by the hypothesis that MSCs act via transient interruption of aGVHD inflammatory cascade rather than actual induction of immune tolerance . Another hypothesis is that limited long term effect could be related to short in-vivo durability of MSCs . Therefore, repeated infusions were associated with better OS .
Through this perspective, MSCs could be considered as pharmacologic category of cellular therapy that maintains effects with continuous exposure rather than CAR-T like phenomenon that holds self expansion property.
It is noteworthy that patients who achieved CR within first 28 days had better OS. For instance, Le Blanc et al. reported the 2 years OS in complete responders 52% but only 16% in PR and NR cases and P = 0.01 . Also, Sanchez et al. reported OS 11/25. Within the survivor group, 8/11 had CR while only 3/14 of the cohort with PR or NR were alive at 12 months follow up .
Across studies, GVHD, infection and malignancy relapse were the most common causes of death.
Le Blanc et al. reported aGVHD as the main cause for transplant related mortality (TRM). Muroi et al. reported 12 deaths with 7 mortalities were TRM within 2 months post first MSCs .
Also, Zhao et al. reported 7 mortalities over the period of aGVHD treatments. Progressive aGVHD was the cause in 3 cases, infections in 2 and bleeding in 2 cases. During same period, the control group had 11 mortalities, progressive aGVHD was the cause in 7 cases and infections in 4 cases .
Regarding relapse (RR) of underlying malignancy, our data showed overall relapse rate of 10% at a median of 12 months. Controlled studies showed neutral effect and RR was comparable between MSCs recipients and the control. However, most studies did not report detailed information about disease status and treatments prior to MSCs. Therefore, these data should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, our data are in line with previous reports about neutral effect of MSCs on relapse rate and long term survival .
Considering previous systematic reviews, our analysis had different end points.They reported survival at 6 months and assessed complete response rate as well as effect of MSCs dose and culture media . Also, included prophyalxis studies and chronic GVHD . Furthermore, some reviews did not specify steroid refractoriness and included different end points such as engraftment . Notably, they had limited volume of patients, early phase trials and short duration of follow up.
Here, we focused on SR-aGVHD therapy and analysed higher volume of patients. Nevertheless, our data are in line with previous systematic reviews regarding neutral effect of MSCs dose (within the spectrum of 1 × 106 cell/kg and above) on outcome with limited effect of MSCs on relapse rate and long term survival , , , .
Further to previous reports, here we could conclude influential factors that translated into better response rate and survival including GVHD severity, recepients’ age and MSCs frequency.
To the best of knowledge, our meta-analysis has the largest volume of patients and is the first to highlight the importance of MSCs infusion frequency as the only modifiable influential factor.

## Conclusion
MSCs represent a safe therapeutic modality in SR-aGVHD. The dose of 1 × 106 cell/kg, once weekly IV infusion for at least 28 days and 3rd party healthy volunteer as donor are the most cost effective conditions of MSCs utilisation.

## Limitations
Our work has some limitations. First, data among studies were not consistent in terms of presentation. This led to some limited areas of analysis. Also, most studies were single arm which makes it difficult to draw statistical significance. However, this is likely on ethical grounds given the severe life-threatening condition of steroid refractory GVHD and difficulty to placebo control such condition.
