## Background
This study aimed to investigate the incidence of contrast‐associated acute kidney injury (CA‐AKI) and renal replacement therapy (RRT) after endovascular therapy (EVT) for acute ischemic stroke.

## Methods
PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and the Web of Science were searched to identify all relevant studies regarding the incidence of CA‐AKI and RRT in patients with acute ischemic stroke receiving EVT until July 2021. To pool the incidence of CA‐AKI and RRT, a random‐effects model with a double arcsine transformation was used. Subgroup analysis and meta‐regression analysis were used to investigate the relationship of individual study‐level covariates with CA‐AKI and RRT.

## Results
It was found that 15 studies involving 27 246 patients with acute ischemic stroke receiving EVT met the inclusion criteria. The incidence of CA‐AKI was 5.0% (95% CI, 2.1%–8.9%), and the incidence of RRT was 0.2% (95% CI, 0.0%–0.4%). Subgroup analysis showed that the incidence of CA‐AKI was higher when using the European Society of Urogenital Radiology's criteria (6.6%, 95% CI, 3.8%–10.1%) than when using the Kidney Disease: The Improving Global Outcomes criteria (3.0%, 95% CI, 1.0%–6.1%). Meta‐regression analysis confirmed that the presence of diabetes was associated with increased rates of CA‐AKI (P=0.002); however, the rates of CA‐AKI were not elevated because of impaired baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (P=0.518), chronic kidney disease (P=0.860), or the volume of contrast (P=0.536).

## Conclusions
The risk of CA‐AKI and RRT secondary to EVT is low for patients with acute ischemic stroke; therefore, treatment should not be delayed for the patients eligible for EVT by waiting for the results of renal function analysis.

## Meta‐Regression Analysis

## Meta‐Regression Analysis for the Comorbidity of Diabetes
Diabetes was significantly associated with greater rates of CA‐AKI (coefficient, 0.0138; 95% CI, 0.0053–0.0224; P=0.0016). Figure  depicts the meta‐regression analysis of the proportion of diabetes versus the incidence of CA‐AKI on a study‐level basis, which revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between the rate of diabetes and the incidence of CA‐AKI.
 

## Meta‐Regression Analysis for the Comorbidity of CKD
There was no significant association between the proportion of CKD and the rates of CA‐AKI (coefficient, −0.0016; 95% CI, −0.0191–0.0160; P=0.8601). Figure  depicts the meta‐regression analysis of the proportion of CKD versus the incidence of CA‐AKI, which revealed that the proportion of CKD was not associated with the incidence of CA‐AKI.

## Meta‐Regression Analysis for the Amount (mL) of Contrast Exposure
The contrast exposure amount had no difference in association with the rates of CA‐AKI (coefficient, −0.0003; 95% CI, −0.0011 to 0.0005; P=0.5358). Meta‐regression analysis of the contrast exposure amount versus the incidence of CA‐AKI on a study‐level basis is shown in Figure  , which indicated that the contrast exposure amount was not significantly associated with the incidence of CA‐AKI.

## Meta‐Regression Analysis for the Baseline Impaired eGFR
There was no significant difference in the association between the impaired baseline eGFR and the rates of CA‐AKI (coefficient, −0.0019; 95% CI, −0.0075 to 0.0038; P=0.5177). Meta‐regression analysis of baseline impaired eGFR versus the incidence of CA‐AKI on a study‐level basis is shown in Figure  , which indicated that the impaired baseline eGFR level was not significantly associated with the incidence of CA‐AKI.

## Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta‐analysis involving ≈27 000 patients to report the proportion of patients with CA‐AKI following EVT for AIS. The risks for CA‐AKI and RRT were 5.0% and 0.2%, respectively. Our findings revealed that baseline renal function and the contrast volume have no significant impact on the rate of development of CA‐AKI; however, we found that diabetes may be a risk factor in patients with EVT. These findings are crucial, as they suggest that it might be unnecessary to withhold or delay acute stroke vascular/perfusion imaging and EVT because of concerns about renal function deterioration in the future.
Based on the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association 2019 guidelines, mechanical thrombectomy is a level A/class I evidence‐based treatment for patients with large‐vessel occlusion. However, these techniques requiring microcatheter manipulation and microguidewire guidance should be assisted by the administration of iodinated contrast. Although the mechanism of CA‐AKI is unknown, the major ones appear to be medullary hypoxia caused by contrast‐induced medullary vasoconstriction and direct damage to renal tubular cells. , , It is well known that the third most common etiology of AKI is the use of iodinated contrast in hospitalized patients. However, several studies have questioned whether the use of iodinated contrast might cause AKI. In a meta‐analysis of 6708 patients with AIS after CTA/computed tomography perfusion, the incidence rates of CA‐AKI and hemodialysis were 3% and 0.07%, respectively. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in AKI, dialysis, or mortality rates between patients undergoing CTA/computed tomography perfusion and those undergoing non–contrast‐enhanced computed tomography. Patients with ischemic stroke undergoing EVT may have higher severity and more contrast exposure than those receiving only CTA/computed tomography perfusion; however, as per the results of previous studies, brain imaging followed by EVT does not deteriorate renal function. The results of our meta‐analysis agree well with the results of the aforementioned reports. The overall rates of AKI and RRT were relatively low.
Whether the use of iodinated contrast poses a burden on renal function among patients with AIS must be clarified. Many studies have indicated that poor outcomes of AIS are associated with renal function deterioration. AKI has also been reported to be associated with a higher risk of disability, a longer hospital stay, increased intracranial hemorrhage, and increased in‐hospital mortality. , Given that poorer outcomes were noted in patients with AIS following AKI, risk factors for AKI must be identified and eliminated. In our study, there was no significant association between the incidence of AKI and the volume of contrast exposure or the severity of AIS. Thus, EVT should not be delayed in patients with AIS when facing the dilemma of losing neurons or nephrons because these contrast exposures may not significantly reduce the renal function.
There are strong associations between CKD and cerebrovascular disease that further strengthen with declining kidney function. CKD also results in worse stroke prognosis, a greater probability of hospitalization, and a higher short‐ and long‐term risk of mortality. In our study, approximately one‐third of the patients with EVT had CKD; however, there was no significant relationship between CKD or impaired baseline eGFR and the occurence of AKI. In a previous study, Hu et al found that the risk of developing AKI was increased 5‐fold when eGFR was <30 mL/min per 1.73 m2; however, there was no relationship when the eGRF was >30 mL/min per 1.73 m2. In another study, patients with present CKD had a higher incidence of AKI; however, CKD was not an independent risk factor for AKI or in‐hospital mortality. , These contradictory findings could be attributed to patients with ischemic stroke and CKD having multiple risk factors such as advanced age and diabetes, as well as the amount of contrast used during the procedure. , , , , , , In our study, most coexisting morbidities were not significantly associated with increased AKI after EVT in patients with ischemic stroke, except for diabetes. In a previous CTA or EVT study on ischemic stroke, a history of diabetes was also found to be strongly associated with CA‐AKI. , Diabetes, the most frequent cause of end‐stage renal disease, might induce transient renal ischemia from renal vasoconstrictors and decrease nitric oxide–dependent vasodilatation. Thus, patients with diabetes are more vulnerable to renal hypoperfusion caused by iodinated contrast.
The pathophysiology of CA‐AKI involves a complex interplay between vascular and tubular effects; hence, a single strategy for prevention and treatment may not always be successful. First, despite the lack of well‐designed randomized trials demonstrating benefit, prophylactic hydration may be considered for high‐risk patients undergoing procedures requiring intraarterial contrast administration. Second, the safe dose of contrast remained unclear. In our analysis, we observed that the incidence of AKI did not increase with the increasing volume of contrast exposure (mean volume, 184.7 mL; patient‐level range, 71.2–251.5 mL). The optimal contrast dose to ensure EVT performance and minimize the incidence of AKI is unknown. Hence, it would be prudent for the physicians to use the temperance amount of contrast agent necessary to perform EVT for patients with AIS. Finally, in the acute stage of ischemic stroke, there is no good evidence to guide the clinical practice regarding RRT. Only one of the included studies showed that 0.4% (4/1017) of the patients needed transient hemodialysis. Other studies did not mention the type of RRT. Continuous forms of RRT were recommended to prevent increased intracranial pressure because of the advantages of greater intracranial stability and lower blood pressure and volume fluctuations compared with intermittent hemodialysis. 

## Strengths and Limitations
The results of the study should be elaborated in light of either its strengths or its potential limitations. To stabilize variances, we constructed a double arcsine transformation on the incidence and prevalence values in the meta‐analysis. We used a validated tool to assess the quality of the study, and we used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessments, Developments, and Evaluations to determine the certainty of evidence. Despite our best efforts, there is some bias in our study. Most of the included studies were cohort studies or secondary analyses of randomized controlled trials; consequently, the results were likely to be affected by selection bias. Another limitation is that there was significant heterogeneity in the reported estimated incidence values. We used subgroup analysis and meta‐regression analysis to reduce confounders by identifying factors that correlate with AKI rates. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis revealed that heterogeneity was reduced for each outcome, but no discernible change was observed for the pooled estimates. Therefore, study heterogeneity did not influence the conclusions of this meta‐analysis. Another limitation was the differences in the definitions of AKI. We found an ≈2‐fold increased risk of AKI based on the European Society of Urogenital Radiology criteria compared with that found using the the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria, possibly reflecting the increased sensitivity of this definition. Additionally, the amount of contrast, the brand of contrast, the contrast medium osmolality, the route of drug administration, and the renal protection strategy might have influenced the results. Finally, the quality of the evidence might be low because of the study design.

## Conclusions
Our meta‐analysis provides evidence that the risk of CA‐AKI and RRT secondary to EVT for patients with AIS is low. Diabetes increases the risk of developing CA‐AKI; however, the baseline impaired eGFR, CKD, and the amount of contrast exposure do not significantly increase the rates of CA‐AKI. Hence, treatment for the patients with AIS who are eligible for EVT should not be delayed by waiting for the results of renal function analysis.
