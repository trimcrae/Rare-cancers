## Highlights
•First meta-analysis on nonmotor symptoms in
Parkinson’s disease from South Asia.
•Nocturia, depression, and memory issues were the
most common symptoms.
•Validated tools (NMSQuest and NMSS) ensured reliable
symptom assessment.
•Large variability in symptom prevalence highlights
the need for standardization.
•Urgent need for tailored strategies to manage
nonmotor symptoms in South Asia.

## Background
The most common movement disorder and neurodegenerative
disorder that progresses over time, Parkinson’s disease, has both motor and
nonmotor symptoms. Asia is home to half of the world’s elderly population, and
Parkinson’s disease is a common condition in this demographic.

## Objectives
We aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of nonmotor
symptoms among Parkinson’s disease patients in South Asia based on the available
literature.

## Methods
The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO with
reference number CRD42024579956. Databases were searched for observational
studies from 2000 to 2024 in English that have assessed the prevalence of
nonmotor symptoms among Parkinson’s disease patients in South Asia via validated
tools. High-quality studies were included after quality assessment. A
random-effects model was used to calculate the pooled prevalence with a 95 %
confidence interval.

## Results
Among the 50 articles (4931 participants) included in
the review, 9 (862 participants) used the Nonmotor Symptoms Questionnaire, 9
(803 participants) used the Nonmotor Symptoms Scale, 2 (171 participants) used
both, and the remaining 30 (3095 participants) used other validated tools to
assess nonmotor symptoms in South Asian patients with Parkinson’s disease. The
prevalence of nonmotor symptoms was high, with nocturia being the most
prevalent. Psychological symptoms were frequently explained via other
tools.

## Conclusions
Our research is among the few clinical accounts in the
literature of the prevalence of nonmotor stmptoms in South Asian Parkinson’s
disease patients. Although the heterogeneity of the results could not be fully
explained, our study could significantly enhance the understanding of symptoms,
leading to more tailored therapeutic and diagnostic strategies.

## Introduction
The most prevalent movement problem and the second most common
neurodegenerative illness associated with aging is Parkinson's disease
[ ]. It is a
neurodegenerative condition that progresses over time and has both motor and
nonmotor symptoms (NMSs) [ ].
While generalized slowness of movement (bradykinesia) with at
least one additional symptom of rigidity or tremor at rest is an example of a
motor symptom [ ], it is
now generally acknowledged that Parkinson's disease is defined not only by its
motor characteristics but also by a wide range of nonmotor symptoms (NMSs),
which include sensory abnormalities, behavioural alterations, autonomic
dysfunction, sleep difficulties, and other more elusive symptoms, such as
fatigue [ ].
Sporadic and familial Parkinson’s disease are the two types of
Parkinson’s disease. Genetic anomalies are what lead to the family form
[ ]. It is likely that
genetic factors play a role in Parkinson's disease; however, the precise
mechanism is yet unknown [ ]. We are aware of 28 specific chromosomal areas that are
either strongly or weakly associated with Parkinson’s disease [ ].
Braak's concept proposes that sporadic Parkinson's disease is
triggered by a pathogen that enters the body via the nasal canal and then
becomes ingested and reaches the stomach [ ]. When microbial products contact enteric and/or olfactory
neurons, α-Syn aggregation occurs [ ]. The neurons in the stomach and nasal cavity are the two
initial sites of sporadic Parkinson's disease [ , ]. The vagus nerve and the olfactory bulb facilitate the
propagation of aggregated α-Syn toward the central nervous system. The
accumulated α-Synuclein eventually reaches the substantia nigra.
Nonmotor anomalies in Parkinson's disease have poorly understood
neuroanatomical and neuropharmacological causes [ ]. Nonmotor symptoms (NMSs) can often precede
many of the motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease, sometimes for years
[ ]. As the existence
of NMS in Parkinson's disease has come to light, it has become clear that these
nonmotor characteristics are crucial—even dominating at times—to the treatment
and diagnosis of the condition [ ]. According to Shulman et al. [ ], neurologists fail to identify nonmotor
complaints in approximately 50% of visits.
Moreover, NMS exacerbate disability severity, increase the
likelihood of institutionalization, and lower quality of life [ ]. Two instruments have been
developed to improve the recognition of NMS in Parkinson's disease patients: the
Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQuest), which is used to detect the
presence of NMS, and the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS), which is used to
assess the frequency and severity of different dimensions of NMS [ ].
A multidisciplinary team of professionals, including members
from patient groups, called the Parkinson’s Disease nonmotor group, created and
validated the 30-item Non Motor Symptoms screening questionnaire (NMSQuest)
[ ]. The Non-Motor
Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQuest) is a self-administered screening instrument
intended to highlight the existence of NMS [ ]. A new 30-item Non-Motor Symptoms in
Parkinson’s Disease assessment Scale (NMSS) was created [ ]. The nine dimensions of the
Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) are as follows: cardiovascular, mood/cognition,
sleep/fatigue, perceptual issues, attention/memory, gastrointestinal, urine,
sexual function, and miscellaneous [ ]. Both tools are recommended by the MDS and other learned
societies for holistic assessment of NMS and their burden.
Asia is home to half of the world's elderly population, and
Parkinson’s disease is a common condition in this demographic. However, little
is known about the epidemiology of Parkinson's disease in Asian nations
[ ]. The objective of
this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate
the prevalence of premotor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease patients within South
Asia. This study aims to provide a thorough analysis of NMS among studies
utilizing a variety of validated assessment tools, including NMSQuest and NMSS.
By aggregating and analyzing data from the selected studies, this review intends
to determine the most prevalent NMS reported across different populations and
assessment methods, thereby offering insights into the prevalence and impact of
these symptoms in the Parkinson’s disease cohort in South Asia.

## Methods
2.1

## Design
This systematic review and
meta-analysis followed the principles of the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses
[ ]. The PRISMA
diagram detailing the selection process is shown in . 
The study protocol was registered at PROSPERO with reference
No. CRD42024579956 before beginning the study. The full checklist and
protocol for this study are included in S3_Appendix and
S4_Appendix.

2.2

## Search strategy
The PubMed, Embase and Google Scholar databases were
searched up to 10th August 2024. Studies were also obtained from
supplementary sources, manual searches, and other repositories. Cross
references from the published articles were manually searched to retrieve
additional literature.
To create an extensive search strategy that encompassed all
fields in the records as well as Medical Subject Headings (MeSH words) for
broadening the search in an advanced PubMed search, the predefined search
phrases were identified. For PubMed, meshes of related terms and keywords
combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used for the systematic
identification of records. The names of South Asian countries—Afghanistan,
Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka—were
combined with search terms. For Google Scholar, the keywords “Non-Motor” and
“Parkinson” were used along with each South Asian country. To ensure that
relevant records were included in Embase, we used a combination of Emtree
terms, keywords, and Boolean operators (AND, OR). Search phrases that
combined “Non-Motor,” “Parkinson,” and the names of South Asian
countries—Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan,
and Sri Lanka—were used. The preliminary search strategy is described in
S1_Appendix.

2.3

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Studies from South Asian countries in English from the year
2000 involving patients with Parkinson’s disease diagnosed according to the
UK Parkinson's Disease Society Brain Bank Clinical Diagnostic Criteria that
reported NMS based on validated questionnaires were included.
The exclusion criteria were parkinsonism in addition to
Parkinson’s disease, such as atypical parkinsonism and secondary causes;
animal studies; case reports; and pharmaceutical trials.

2.4

## Selection of studies
The literature search was performed by PP. The included
studies were exported to Google Sheets in compatible format. Duplicate
articles were screened manually. Duplicates were then recorded and removed.
After removing duplicates, two independent authors, PP and AS, screened the
title and abstract of every article that remained. Full-text articles were
obtained for the relevant studies satisfying the inclusion criteria. The
data were extracted by the two authors, PP and AS, independently.

2.5

## Data extraction
The following data were extracted from the studies: name of
the author, year of publication, country of study, study design, tool used,
sample size, mean age of population, percentage of male population, mean
duration of Parkinson’s disease and percentage of Parkinson’s disease
patients having NMS as per the NMSQuest and NMSS separately. Similarly, data
are also extracted from articles that analyze Parkinson’s disease and some
specific NMS only.

2.6

## Quality assessment of the
studies
The quality assessments of each study identified were
conducted by two authors (PP and AS) via an 11-item instrument recommended
by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) for cross-sectional
studies. Three of these studies were evaluated as high quality, and all
other studies were evaluated as medium quality. Overall, the studies
included in this study demonstrated a relatively good level of quality.
Details are given in S2_Appendix.

2.7

## Statistical analysis
The data collected from the Google Sheets were exported, and
analysis was performed via Jamovi 2.3.28. Prevalence estimates of NMS were
calculated by pooling the study-specific estimates with 95 % confidence
intervals via the random effects model via the “Freeman–Tukey transformation
of inverse hyperbolic sine function”. Heterogeneity was evaluated via the
χ2 test on Cochrane’s Q statistics and then quantified
by calculating the I2. The heterogeneity test was
considered statistically significant when p ≤ 0.05. In this case, the data
were analysed via a random effects model. In contrast, if p > 0.05, a
fixed effects model was used to analyze the data.

2.8

## Sensitivity analysis and publication
bias
A sensitivity test was performed by sequentially excluding
one study at a time and recalculating the summary effect size to examine the
stability of the analysis. To verify whether publication bias might
influence the validity of the incidence, a linear regression method was
used. All p values were two-sided, and the cutoff for statistical
significance was set at 0.05.

2.9

## Data availability statement
The data that support the findings of this study are
available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

## Results
3.1

## Literature search
A total of 540 articles were retrieved via our search
strategy, and 26 records were discarded owing to duplication. The titles and
abstracts of the remaining 514 articles were screened. We excluded 435
articles and retained the remaining 79 articles for further evaluation by
reading the full texts. Eight reports were not retrieved. Therefore, a total
of 71 reports were assessed for eligibility. Among them, 9 [ , , , , , , , , ] reports had irrelevant data, 6 [ , , , , , ] were from regions other than South Asia, and 6
[ , , , , , ]were reviews. Thus, 50 eligible articles were
ultimately included in this study.

3.2

## Characteristics of eligible
articles
Fifty articles involving 4931 participants described and
analyzed the prevalence of premotor symptoms in patients with Parkinson’s
disease in South Asia. A variety of methods have been used in the assessment
of NMS across studies and symptoms. Nine articles of the 50 eligible
articles involving 862 participants described and analyzed NMS via NMSQuest;
9 articles involving 803 participants described and analyzed NMS via NMSS;
and 2 articles involving 171 participants used both NMSQuest and NMSS to
assess NMS. The remaining 30 articles involving 3095 participants analyzed
and described NMS via other validated tools. The detailed characteristics of
the included studies, including Author/Year, Country, Sample Size, Mean Age
of Population (year), Male (%), and Mean Disease Duration, along with the
three most and least prevalent NMSs, as mentioned by various studies, are
summarized in . 

3.3

## Tool-based analysis of NMS
Studies using NMSQuest and NMSS were included in the
quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis). We calculated
the combined prevalence of NMS across studies using the same tool
only.

## Discussion
The presence of cardinal motor symptoms and the progressive
development of a wide range of NMS are both part of the highly heterogeneous
clinical phenotype of Parkinson's disease, and they both negatively affect the
functional independence of individuals with the disease [ ]; nevertheless, the precise
frequency and prevalence are yet unknown. To the best of our knowledge, this is
the first comprehensive meta-analysis on the prevalence
of several important NMS in Parkinson’s disease patients in South
Asia.
Our review focused on data exclusively from South Asian
countries. The articles that utilized NMSQuest and NMSS as tools, both of which
are recommended by the Movement Disorder Society and other learned societies for
the holistic assessment of NMS and their burden, were involved in quantitative
synthesis. Additionally, we reviewed studies that used other specific
questionnaires to assess specific NMS, providing a more tailored understanding
of these symptoms in the context of the study population. However, these
articles were excluded from the quantitative synthesis because of the
considerable variability in assessment methods across different questionnaires,
even for identical symptoms.
This approach of including articles that used NMSQuest and NMSS
only in the meta-analysis ensured consistency in symptom
definition and assessment methods, thereby minimizing variability in the
evaluation of nonmotor symptom burden. However, substantial heterogeneity across
studies still exists given the large variability in patient characteristics,
stage and duration of disease, and treatment modalities. Owing to the lack of
previous meta-analyses of NMS among Parkinson’s disease
patients in South Asia and the limited number of studies as well as limited data
in available research, we decided to include these studies rather than being
exclusive despite the heterogeneity.
With the introduction of self-reported tools such as the NMS
Quest and health professional-administered tools such as the NMSS, it is now
possible to conduct a systematic and comprehensive assessment of NMS
internationally rather than relying on individual evaluations [ ]. Our
meta-analysis of studies using NMSQuest and NMSS provided
valuable insights into the prevalence of NMS in Parkinson's disease patients.
The NMSQuest results revealed that the most commonly experienced symptoms were
nocturia (64.1 %), depression (56.6 %), and memory alterations (54.2 %),
emphasizing their significance in clinical care. Dimensionwise analysis via the
NMSS revealed that the most common symptoms were related to miscellaneous
symptoms (68.5 %), sleep/fatigue (62.4 %) and mood/cognition (60 %), indicating
the need for comprehensive management in these areas. The NMSS highlighted
nocturia (54 %), insomnia (50.3 %), and worry/nervousness (50.3 %) as
particularly prevalent symptoms affecting patients' quality of life.
Additionally, a review of studies utilizing other validated tools highlighted
psychological symptoms, sleep disturbances, and gastrointestinal issues as
commonly reported NMS. Overall, our findings underscore the importance of
addressing a broad range of NMS to ensure effective patient care.
Our meta-analysis revealed that nocturia
is among the top three NMSs. Both Chaudhuri et al. [ ] and Martinez-Martin et al. [ ] reported that more than 60 % of
patients reported experiencing nocturia. This finding aligns closely with the
South Asian data included in this analysis. Rana et al. [ ] proposed that immigrant Asian
patients might have a greater risk of developing nocturia, although their study
did not consider indigenous populations in Asia. Nocturia seems to be frequently
reported by individuals regardless of their ethnic or cultural background.
Despite this high prevalence, nocturia is still not well studied or managed as a
nonmotor symptom, highlighting the need for treatment-based trials to address it
globally. In addition to nocturia, depression is among the three most common
NMSs. The NMSQuest findings indicated that the prevalence of depression was
56.6 %, which is considerably higher than the 35 % reported by the systematic
review of Reijnders et al. [ ], who reported clinically significant depressive symptoms
in Parkinson's disease patients regardless of whether they met the criteria for
a DSM-defined depressive disorder. The difference in prevalence rates is likely
due to the review considering various settings and different diagnostic methods
used in the studies to determine average rates of depressive
disorders.
Self-reported memory problems are challenging to quantify, and
compared with the NMSS cognitive dimension, studies have also indicated issues
in the cognitive and apathy dimensions. All the studies identified
attention/memory as one of the most commonly reported nonmotor symptom
dimensions. However, this does not indicate a higher prevalence of mild
cognitive impairment or dementia in these populations, as such conclusions can
only be drawn from systematic examinations using specific validated tools, which
were lacking in the reported studies [ ]. Numerous factors, including genetics and lifestyle,
affect memory and cognition in Parkinson's disease patients, and these factors
should be taken into account in future systematic studies.
In addition to South Asia, few studies have specifically
evaluated the prevalence of NMS among Parkinson’s disease patients in other
regions or worldwide. A systematic review by Sauerbier et al. 2017 revealed that
self-reported NMS among Parkinson's disease patients in Asia is high and similar
to that reported in studies of Western/White Caucasian PD patients, with
nocturia, constipation, and memory impairment being the most frequently reported
symptoms on the NMS Quest, whereas sleep/fatigue, attention/memory, and
mood/apathy dimensions were the most prevalent on the NMSS, and bowel
incontinence, delusions, and impaired sex drive were reported the least
frequently by Asian patients with Parkinson’s disease [ ]. These findings align closely
with the results of our meta-analysis. A
meta-analysis by Noyce et al. investigated NMS and
other factors associated with the risk of Parkinson's disease [ ], but it did not offer any
information regarding the prevalence of these symptoms. In a
meta-analysis by Honglei Chen et al.
(2015)[ ], a random
effects model was applied to investigate the occurrence of specific NMS both
before and after the diagnosis of Parkinson's disease in patients and controls.
The symptoms most often recognized were hyposmia (75.5 % in patients vs. 19.1 %
in controls), constipation (50 % vs 17.7 %) and anxiety (39.9 % vs 19.1 %). The
variation with our meta-analysis signifies that the
priority of symptoms changes with the intervention and the design of the study.
However, both studies noted that mood and sleep disorders are significant
burdens in patients with Parkinson’s disease. Our analysis also included a range
of symptoms, such as gastrointestinal and autonomic symptoms, because the nature
of NMS in Parkinson’s disease is multifaceted. Both
meta-analyses address the high NMS burden in Parkinson’s
disease patients, which calls for the need for integrated approaches for
management, particularly in the South Asian context, around which this review
has focused.
There are various limits to our analysis. We restricted our
search to English-language literature from PubMed, Embase and Google Scholar.
There were significant differences in the source population, study design,
sample size, and symptom evaluation, and many studies were not initially
intended to investigate the prevalence of NMS, resulting in heterogeneity. We
calculated the pooled prevalence of NMS without considering the effects of
interventions such as medication or deep brain stimulation. This approach
provides a broad overview of the prevalence of NMS across various studies,
regardless of the treatment modality used. Additionally, we did not compare the
prevalence of NMS with that of motor symptoms, focusing solely on nonmotor
aspects.
Despite these drawbacks, our research constitutes one of the
rare clinical accounts in the literature of the prevalence of NMS manifestation
in South Asian Parkinson's disease patients. The information from our study
could significantly enhance our understanding of NMS Parkinson’s disease
patients, potentially leading to more tailored therapeutic and diagnostic
strategies for different Parkinson’s disease patient populations.
