## Background
A comprehensive understanding of the prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) among patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is lacking. To fill this knowledge gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the prevalence of CKD and its associated factors among CHB patients.

## Methods
Following the PRISMA guidelines, we conducted a comprehensive search for literature published between January 1, 2010, and January 5, 2025, in databases including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang. The prevalence of CKD among CHB individuals was used as the statistical effect size, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated. Additionally, we compared the risk of CKD between CHB and non-HBV-infected populations, expressed as odds ratios (OR). Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed based on factors such as year of publication, study sample size, age, CKD diagnostic basis, country, WHO region, economic status, presence of cirrhosis, diabetes, and antiviral treatment status to further explore the influencing factors of CKD among CHB individuals. Subgroup results were corrected using Holm–Bonferroni correction, and meta-regression was used to explore sources of heterogeneity.

## Results
A total of 41 studies were included in our analysis, of which 30 were deemed high-quality. These studies collectively encompassed 1,066,919 CHB patients, among whom 44,021 were diagnosed with CKD. The meta-analysis demonstrated a significantly elevated CKD risk in CHB populations versus non-hepatitis B controls (OR = 2.30, 95%CI: 2.01–2.63). Due to substantial between-study heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses which revealed: the highest prevalence in studies published during 2021–2024 (11.48%, 95%CI: 6.08%-21.61%); numerically but non-significantly higher prevalence in patients aged ≥ 50 years (21.87%, 95%CI:0.41%-64.78%) versus younger patients (4.68%, 95%CI: 0.50%-32.57%, p > 0.05) and in diabetic (17.78%, 95%CI: 1.91%-70.58%) versus non-diabetic patients (1.52%, 95%CI: 0.61%-3.69%, p > 0.05). CKD prevalence was significantly higher when diagnosed by persistent eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (12.20%, 95%CI: 6.04%-23.09%) versus other criteria. Antiviral-treated patients showed higher prevalence (11.37%, 95%CI:8.62%-14.86%) than untreated counterparts (4.09%, 95%CI:2.63%-6.31%). Meta-regression identified age, diabetes status and antiviral treatment as heterogeneity sources.

## Conclusions
This study reveals a higher prevalence of CKD in patients with CHB. Subgroup analysis indicated that groups with cirrhosis, diabetes, and a history of antiviral therapy exhibited even higher prevalence rates. These findings suggest that individualized management strategies tailored to specific subgroups of CHB patients may enhance the prevention and management of comorbid conditions. However, due to age, region, and other natural differences in populations, the observed prevalence shows great heterogeneity. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution given the high degree of heterogeneity.

## Introduction
Hepatitis B virus (HBV), a hepatotropic DNA virus, is the causative agent of chronic HBV infection (CHB). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 296 million people worldwide are living with CHB [ ]. The clinical implications of CHB extend beyond hepatic complications to include various extrahepatic manifestations [ ]. Studies indicate that approximately 20% of HBV-infected individuals develop extrahepatic symptoms, with chronic kidney disease (CKD) being one of the most prevalent extrahepatic complications [ ]. A large-scale prospective cohort study of 500,000 Chinese adults investigating the association between CHB and CKD risk demonstrated that HBsAg positivity increases CKD incidence by 37%, with particularly pronounced effects in male patients showing a 77% higher risk [ ]. The coexistence of CHB and CKD creates a dangerous bidirectional relationship. On one hand, persistent hepatic inflammation accelerates renal function deterioration through multiple mechanisms [ ]. Conversely, renal impairment limits the clinical use of essential antiviral therapies due to altered pharmacokinetics [ ]. Additionally, immunosuppressant treatment for CKD may exacerbate hepatitis B virus replication [ ]. When comorbidity progresses to the point of requiring renal replacement therapy, it not only imposes substantial medical costs but also severely compromises the patient’s quality of life [ ]– [ ].
The association between HBV infection and CKD remains controversial in current research. While some meta-analyses have reported an elevated risk of CKD in HBV-infected adult populations [ ], other studies have found no significant association, particularly in Asian cohorts [ , ]. Although increasing attention has been paid to the co-occurrence of CHB and CKD, systematic evaluations of CKD prevalence specifically in CHB populations remain limited. To address this knowledge gap, we performed a comprehensive literature review and systematic analysis to better characterize the CKD burden among CHB patients. These findings may offer initial evidence to inform future investigations into CKD management strategies for the CHB population.

## Methods

## Literature sources and search strategy
This study adhered to the PRISMA guidelines (Supplementary Table 1) and was registered with PROSPERO under the identifier CRD42024554024. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we searched Pubmed, Embase, Cochrane library, Web of science, CNKI and Wanfang databases for articles evaluating the development of CKD among CHB patients published between January 1, 2010 and January 5, 2025. The literature search was conducted using both subject headings and free-text terms, including “hepatitis B” and “chronic kidney disease” and “prevalence” (search strategy in Supplementary Table 2, List of references in Supplementary Table 3).

## Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

## Literature inclusion criteria

(I)The type of study was a cross-sectional study or a cohort study;
(II)Patients greater than or equal to 18 years of age;
(III)CHB was defined as HBsAg-positive >6 months with evidence of chronic hepatitis [ ], or based on clinical diagnosis using standardized disease classification codes (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revisions; Korean Classification of Diseases version-7);
(IV)CKD was defined according to KDIGO guidelines as either an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or the presence of kidney damage markers lasting ≥ 3 months [ ], or based on clinical diagnosis using standardized disease classification codes (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revisions; Korean Classification of Diseases version-7).

## Literature exclusion criteria

(I)The type of literature was conference abstracts, review studies, meta-analysis, case-control studies, and case reports;
(II)Literature from which prevalence could not be extracted, or the survey sample was duplicated with other studies;
(III)Studies with sample size < 30;
(IV)Studies involving patients with associated malignant tumors;
(V)Persons younger than 18 years of age, pregnant, or breastfeeding women.

## Literature screening and data extraction
Literature screening and data extraction were conducted independently by two researchers, JC and WZ, with disagreements discussed and resolved with CC and SY. EndNote software was used to manage the literature obtained from the search. After removing duplicates, titles were read for primary screening, abstracts for secondary exclusion, and the remaining full texts for further screening. Information extracted from the literature that met the criteria, including author, year, study population, study area, study design, sampling method, sample size, and outcome indicators.

## Quality assessment
Quality assessment in cohort studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Scale (NOS), which consists of 8 entries in 3 dimensions, with a total score of 9. Studies scoring 0–3 were classified as low quality, 4–6 as moderate quality, and 7–9 as high quality [ ]. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) evaluation criteria were used to evaluate the quality of cross-sectional studies, which consisted of 11 evaluation entries with a total score of 11 points. Scores of 0–3, 4–7, and 8–11 indicated low, moderate, and high quality, respectively [ ]. Higher NOS and AHRQ scores indicate higher quality and lower risk of bias; lower scores indicate worse quality and higher risk of bias. The quality assessment was done independently by two researchers (JC and WZ), and studies with inconsistent scores were judged by a third researcher (CC).

## Evidence evaluation and testing for publication bias
The overall certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) evidence profile, which was generated using the online GRADEpro software [ ]. The assessment was conducted independently by JC and WZ. In cases where discrepancies arose, discussions were held with CC to reach a consensus. Publication bias was assessed through funnel plots, supplemented by Egger’s and Begg’s statistical tests [ ].

## Statistical analysis
The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess data normality, with non-normally distributed data undergoing appropriate transformations (logarithmic, logit, arcsine, or double arcsine). Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic, with I² values of 30%−60%, 50%−90%, and >75% indicating moderate, substantial, and considerable heterogeneity, respectively. A random-effects model was employed when heterogeneity exceeded 50% or when significant differences between models were observed, to better account for between-study variability [ ]. The prevalence of CKD in the CHB population served as the primary effect size, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated. Subgroup analyses were conducted across 10 predefined categories: publication year, sample size (stratified into tertiles as low, medium, or high), age, country, CKD diagnostic criteria, WHO region, economic status, cirrhosis status, diabetes mellitus status, and antiviral treatment status, to further characterize CKD in CHB patients. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni method to control for type I error. Additionally, we compared CKD risk between CHB and non-HBV-infected populations using odds ratios (ORs). Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding moderate-quality studies to assess result robustness. Forest plots were generated using R’s forestplot package to visualize effect sizes with 95% CIs. Potential sources of heterogeneity were examined through univariate meta-regression. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1) utilizing the metaprop and forestplot packages.

## Results

## Results of literature search
A total of 5,372 articles were retrieved from the literature, and after a step-by-step screening process, 41 articles were included, encompassing a total of 1,066,919 patients with CHB, of whom 44,021 suffered from CKD. The process of literature screening is shown in Fig. .

 

## Basic characteristics of included studies and quality assessment
The included studies consisted of 8 cross-sectional studies and 33 cohort studies. 2 studies were published between 2010 and 2015, 20 studies between 2016 and 2020, and 19 studies between 2021 and 2024. The literature quality was predominantly high, with 30 studies rated as high quality, 11 as medium quality, and none as low quality. There was no study of high-risk bias included in the original article (The NOS assessment was shown in Supplementary Tables 4, and the AHRQ evaluation in Supplementary Table 5). Among the 35 studies reporting age data, participant ages showed a distinct distribution pattern: twenty-five studies (71.4%) focused on middle-aged cohorts (45–59 years), seven studies (20.0%) examined young adult populations (≤ 44 years), and three studies (8.6%) included elderly participants (≥ 60 years). The basic characteristics of these studies are shown in Supplementary Table 6.

## Meta-analysis results

## Prevalence of CKD among CHB patients
Based on the data from the 41 studies we included, as I2 > 50%, we used a random-effects model to pool the effect sizes. The results showed that the prevalence of CKD in patients with CHB was 8.70% (95% CI: 6.71%–11.21%) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

## Subgroup analysis
Six studies reported the prevalence of CKD in both CHB and non-hepatitis B populations. Because the I2 value was greater than 50%, combined odds ratios (ORs) were calculated using the random effects model. The meta-analysis revealed a significantly higher risk of CKD in CHB populations compared to non-hepatitis B controls (OR = 2.30, 95% CI 2.01–2.63) (Figure ).

 

The prevalence of CKD in different CHB populations was analyzed in subgroups according to year of study publication, country, sample size, age, CKD diagnostic basis, WHO regional distribution, income level as defined by the World Bank, presence of cirrhosis, presence of diabetes mellitus, presence of antiviral treatment status, and different antiviral drugs. Since the I2 values were all greater than 50%, a random-effects model was used to pool the effect sizes.
Comparing the prevalence of CKD among CHB patients in different countries, the highest prevalence rate was 50.00% (95% CI: 40.84%−59.16%) in Oman and the lowest in South Korea at 3.33% (95% CI: 2.72%−4.08%). The results were shown in Supplementary Fig. .

(I)Differences between years of publication showed that the highest prevalence of 11.48% (95% CI: 6.08%−20.61%) was found in the literature published between 2021 and 2024; The prevalence in studies with low sample size was 16.13% (95% CI: 10.07%−24.83%) higher than in studies with high sample size 6.13% (95% CI: 3.98%−9.35%).
(II)There was a significant difference between age groups, with a higher prevalence of CKD in those older than 50 years (21.87%, 95% CI: 0.41%−64.78%) compared to those younger than 50 years (4.68%, 95% CI: 0.50-32.57). However, there was no statistical difference between the groups.
(III)The diagnostic criteria for CKD were unified into the following three categories: eGFR alone < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (22 studies), Markers of kidney injury or eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for ≥ 3 months (7 studies), diagnostic codes (12 studies). The prevalence of CKD diagnosed based on the criterion of eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² persisting for three months was 12.20% (95% CI: 6.04%−23.09%), which was significantly higher than that determined by other diagnostic criteria (such as kidney damage or eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for three months (9.27%, 95% CI: 4.02%−19.94%), or using diagnostic codes alone (4.48%, 95% CI: 3.40%−5.88%)).
(IV)According to the WHO regional distribution, the prevalence rates vary among different countries. The South-East Asia region had the highest prevalence of CKD at 36.96% (95% CI: 24.36%−51.61%), followed by the Eastern Mediterranean region with 19.97% (95% CI: 1.62%−79.06%), and the Europe region had the lowest prevalence at 7.12% (95% CI: 5.15%−9.76%).
(V)Subgroup analysis based on income levels of different economies, as classified by the World Bank [ ]. The prevalence rate in upper-middle-income countries (9.69%, 95% CI: 5.47%−16.58%)) is higher than that in high-income (8.07%, 95% CI: 5.97%−10.82%).
(VI)In addition, patients with cirrhosis (7.62%, 95% CI: 1.91%−25.92%) have a higher prevalence of CKD than those without cirrhosis (4.35%, 95% CI: 0.81%−20.28%). The prevalence rate was higher among those with diabetes (17.78%, 95% CI: 1.91%−70.58%) compared to those without diabetes (1.52%, 95% CI: 0.61%−3.69%). The prevalence rate among patients who received antiviral therapy was 11.37% (95% CI: 8.62%−14.86%), which was higher than the rate of 4.09% (95% CI: 2.63%−6.31%) among those without treatment experience.

The results of subgroup analyses were presented in Fig. (forest plot). Notably, significant between-group differences (p < 0.05) were observed for the following unadjusted subgroups: year of publication, sample size, CKD diagnostic criteria, WHO region, diabetes comorbidity status, antiviral treatment status, and antiviral treatment type. After Holm-Bonferroni correction, only three factors maintained statistical significance (p < 0.05): year of publication, WHO region, and antiviral treatment status. Complete results are provided in Supplementary Table 7.

 

Meta-analysis of risk factors for developing CKD in patients with CHB. Analysis of cross-sectional studies showed that age OR=1.04 (95% CI: 1.03-1.06), diabetes OR=1.82 (95% CI: 1.39-2.39) were strongly associated with CHB co-morbidities CKD. Analyses of the cohort studies showed that diabetes HR=.2.82 (95% CI: 2.42-3.29) was strongly associated with CHB co-morbidities CKD (cross-sectional results in Supplementary Table 8, cohort study results in Supplementary Table 9).

## Sensitivity analysis
After excluding studies with moderate risk of bias, the prevalence rate was 9.02% (95% CI: 6.60%−12.22%), which roughly overlaps with the confidence interval of the overall prevalence rate, indicating robust results (Supplementary Fig. 3).

## Evidence assessment and publication bias
The results of the GRADE assessment indicate that the quality of evidence from the meta-analyses was very low (Supplementary Table 10). Given that all included studies were observational in design, the initial evidence quality was already expected to be low. Additionally, the significant heterogeneity between studies, which could not be fully explained, further reduced the credibility of the evidence.
Although the funnel plot showed slight asymmetry, both Begg’s test (z = −0.74, p = 0.46) and Egger’s test (t = 1.98, p = 0.06) indicated no statistically significant publication bias, with the observed asymmetry likely attributable to the substantial between-study heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 4).

## Meta regression‌
To explore the sources of heterogeneity, this study employed meta-regression analysis to systematically evaluate the effects of covariates including mean age, year of publication, sample size, study design, CKD diagnostic criteria, income, diabetes status, and antiviral treatment status. The results demonstrated that age (β = 0.073, p = 0.015), diabetes status (β = 2.650, p = 0.050), and antiviral treatment status (β = 1.153, p = 0.001) may be contributors to heterogeneity, whereas the remaining covariates showed no statistically significant associations (Table ). 

## Discussion
HBV viruses are pan-hepatophilic viruses, not purely hepatophilic, and can also directly infect kidney cells [ ]. Epidemiologic [ ], clinical, and immunologic [ ] evidence suggests a causal relationship between HBV infection and the development of kidney disease [ ]. However, there are fewer studies on the prevalence of CKD in patients with CHB. This meta-analysis and systematic review included 1,066,919 participants in 41 studies between January 1, 2010 and January 15, 2025, and provides a comprehensive overview of the prevalence, risk factors, and progression of CKD in patients with CHB. Based on the available data, while acknowledging the uncertainties in the findings and the inherent limitations of the study, the prevalence of CKD among patients with CHB was estimated to be 8.70%, a figure that was slightly lower than the results of previous studies in the Western Pacific region [ ], with the discrepancy likely stemming from the varying status of the hepatitis B-infected patients included and regional differences. Additionally, this study found that the risk of CKD was higher in the CHB population compared to the non-HBV population. Due to differences in the search time range and the types of studies included, the comprehensiveness of the results of this study is not as good as that of Chen Danjing et al. [ ]. However, this study focuses more on the epidemiological characteristics of CKD in the CHB patient population, as well as the differences in the prevalence of CKD in the CHB patient population under specific disease conditions.
The CHB population is gradually aging. Data from a territory-wide study conducted in Hong Kong showed that the percentage of CHB patients aged 60 and above was 11.6% between 2000 and 2004, while this percentage increased significantly to 35.4% between 2014 and 2017 [ ]. The kidneys, similar to other parts of the body, undergo corresponding changes with age, and these changes can lead to a gradual decrease in kidney function, and this natural physiologic decline may make older patients with hepatitis B more likely to suffer from impaired kidney function [ ]. In a cohort study using a combined model, researchers found that eGFR declined by an average of 0.82 ml/min/1.73 m² per year, while urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) increased by an average of 0.05 mg/g per year [ ]. In addition, the incidence of these common co-morbidities, which are potential risk factors for CKD, increases progressively with the age of CHB patients [ ]. The results of this study showed that the prevalence of CKD in patients over 50 years of age was about 4 times that of patients under 50 years of age, and the prevalence of CKD in patients with diabetes was 11 times that of non-diabetic patients. Although these associations did not reach a statistically significant level (p >0.05) due to the limited number of included studies, the observed trends were highly consistent with the known mechanisms of age-related renal decline and the pathophysiology of diabetic nephropathy. These findings suggest that age and diabetes may be important risk factors for the occurrence of CKD in CHB patients, but larger sample sizes are needed for further validation. Meta-regression also showed that age and diabetes status were sources of heterogeneity. Although international clinical practice guidelines provide guidance on treatment [ , ], they do not set clear therapeutic endpoints specifically for older patients with CHB or those with co-morbid diabetes mellitus [ ]. Physiological changes associated with aging and multimorbidity complicate the treatment and management of these patients and require more individualized treatment strategies [ ]. Therefore, careful and rigorous monitoring of renal function in elderly and diabetic patients with CHB was particularly important.
In addition, the prevalence of CKD in CHB shows variability among different countries and regions, which may also be related to some extent to the differences in muscle levels and creatinine levels among different populations, most of the formulas for eGFR are closely related to creatinine level, so it is necessary to pay attention to the specificity of CKD risk in different physical populations [ ]. At the same time, dietary habits, genetic background, and the distribution of CKD-related underlying diseases such as diabetes and hypertension vary between countries, and these factors together contribute to geographic differences in CKD prevalence [ ]. Therefore, it is particularly important to develop personalized prevention and intervention strategies that address the specific risk factors of different populations.
Global modeling data from 2022 estimated a 3.2% prevalence of HBV infection worldwide, corresponding to approximately 250 million HBsAg-positive individuals. Among these, an estimated 83.3 million met treatment eligibility criteria based on either country-specific guidelines or EASL recommendations (where national guidelines were unavailable). However, only 6.8 million eligible patients received antiviral therapy [ ]. While antiviral therapy effectively suppresses HBV DNA replication, the current low treatment coverage may significantly accelerate disease progression to cirrhosis and other complications. Study has shown that baseline HBV DNA levels in patients with CHB have a significant dose-response relationship with the progression of cirrhosis. Meta-analysis data showed that the incidence of cirrhosis increased stepwise with the increase in viral load: 0.301 cases/100 person-years in the < 2,000 IU/mL group, 0.719 cases/100 person-years in the 2,000–20,000 IU/mL group, and 1.461 cases/100 person-years in the 20,000-200,000 IU/mL group, while the ≥ 200,000 IU/mL group had the highest risk of 2.236 cases/100 person-years [ ]. In addition, the occurrence of cirrhosis is closely related to kidney damage. Study has shown that cirrhosis can significantly increase mortality in patients with renal failure by 7 times, and 50% of patients die within 1 month of diagnosis [ ]. And our study showed that the prevalence of CKD was higher in cirrhotic patients than in non-cirrhotic patients. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited study numbers in subgroup comparison. A study of universal HBsAg screening for CHB in the general population in the United States showed that universal screening reduced compensated cirrhosis by 30% and decompensated cirrhosis by 43% [ ], and a study of the cost-effectiveness of universal screening for CHB in China also showed that universal screening for HBV in adults aged 18–70 years was cost-effective, with a variety of strategies applicable to different willingness-to-pay thresholds [ ]. Therefore, screening for hepatitis B in the population improves diagnosis and treatment rates, and early diagnosis and treatment reduces the incidence of cirrhosis and the risk of CKD.
In addition, The results of this study demonstrate significant variations in the reported prevalence of CKD among CHB patients across different publication periods, with the highest prevalence observed during 2021–2024. This phenomenon may be closely associated with the following factors: First, the widespread application of antiviral medications has significantly prolonged the survival of CHB patients, leading to progressive aging of this patient population. Second, the cumulative nephrotoxic effects resulting from long-term medication use cannot be overlooked [ ]. Most notably, our data analysis revealed that the CKD prevalence was significantly higher in patients receiving antiviral therapy compared to untreated groups, providing strong evidence supporting these observations. Antiviral drugs, such as adefovir (ADV) and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) [ ], which are mainly metabolized and excreted by the kidneys and may lead to significantly increased rates of nephrotoxicity when used in long-term or high-dose use [ , ], especially ADV, which is no longer recommended by current guidelines due to its high drug resistance rate [ ], but significant tubular damage has been observed in patients with historical use. Some patients continue to progress with renal impairment even after drug discontinuation. Systematic subgroup analysis of antiviral therapies for CHB revealed that while the tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) group exhibited the highest CKD prevalence (16.74%), this finding requires careful interpretation. During the period from 2010 to 2025, the evolution of clinical practice guidelines for CHB treatment has witnessed significant transitions in recommended antiviral regimens, from ADV to TDF and subsequently to TAF. Concurrently, the treated population has demonstrated progressive aging. These changes have collectively influenced renal function in CHB patients through distinct mechanisms across different therapeutic eras: A subgroup analysis of five CHB patients treated with TAF found that three of the pivotal studies (60%) had specific enrollment criteria: these studies specifically included renal impairment during prior nucleos(t)ide analog therapy—including TDF, ADV, and entecavir (ETV)—and were subsequently switched to TAF. Notably, these patients demonstrated significant improvement in renal parameters (e.g., increased eGFR) after switching to TAF, indicating that the observed “elevated prevalence” reflects residual baseline renal injury from prior agents rather than intrinsic nephrotoxicity of TAF. TAF demonstrates renal protective effects attributable to its optimized molecular structure, extended plasma half-life, enhanced metabolic stability, and hepatocyte-selective delivery mechanism. These characteristics enable TAF to achieve antiviral efficacy comparable to TDF at a lower dose, while reducing systemic drug exposure and thereby minimizing potential toxicity to the kidneys and bones [ , ]. In 2024, the Expert Consensus on the Management of Hepatitis B in the Whole Population, published by the Chinese Medical Association, emphasized that the use of TAF or ETV should be prioritized in the treatment of first-treatment patients, especially in those with pre-existing renal abnormalities or bone disease, or those at high risk of developing these diseases. This recommendation aims to minimize the renal and skeletal side effects that may be associated with the use of TDF. Our study also found that CHB using TDF had a higher prevalence of CKD [ ]. Therefore, it is particularly important to choose antiviral drugs with a low impact on the kidneys in the treatment of CHB, especially if the patient is at risk of renal impairment. It also emphasizes the importance of regular monitoring and assessment of renal function to ensure that patients receive treatment safely and effectively [ ].
This study has several strengths. Firstly it is the most recent meta-analysis designed to examine the prevalence of CKD in CHB patients from 2010 to 2025. Secondly, we also conducted detailed specific analyses of various subgroups such as age, country, region, cirrhosis status, medication use, co-morbidities, etc., thus providing an in-depth estimation and analysis of the prevalence of CKD in patients with CHB, which may be useful for policy makers. However, our study has some limitations. Firstly, the included studies exhibited significant heterogeneity, and the pooled results represent an average across highly diverse studies. Although meta-regression analysis confirmed that patient age, diabetes status, and antiviral treatment status were the main sources of heterogeneity, while factors such as publication year, sample size, study design, and diagnostic criteria for CKD did not contribute significantly to heterogeneity (both p >0.05), there are still some unmeasured confounders who may contribute to residual heterogeneity. Therefore, caution should be exercised in interpreting the results of this study, as its conclusions may not be applicable to certain specific subpopulations or clinical contexts. Secondly, although our systematic search encompassed observational studies worldwide, the majority of publications meeting methodological quality standards originated from the Western Pacific region. As the epicenter of HBV endemicity, this region bears approximately 60% of the global disease burden [ ], with consequently greater research investment and clinical study resources. In contrast, while sub-Saharan Africa similarly carries high HBV prevalence, limited research infrastructure and funding may have resulted in fewer high-quality studies satisfying our inclusion criteria. Although this geographical concentration may affect the generalizability of the findings in regions such as Africa, the available evidence does represent the most important HBV disease burden regions globally. It is also expected that more multi-center clinical studies that meet international quality standards can be carried out in Africa and other areas with high HBV disease burden in the future. Thirdly, the GRADE assessment indicates that the certainty of the evidence was very low, which means this estimate should be interpreted with caution. According to the GRADE evidence classification system, observational studies inherently carry methodological limitations that necessitate a two-level downgrade from the initial high-quality rating to low-quality. Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) represent the gold standard for interventional research, the implementation of RCT design for assessing CKD prevalence raises both ethical concerns and practical challenges. Our study systematically incorporated 41 observational studies, constituting the most representative real-world evidence currently available. Meta-analysis revealed significant heterogeneity, leading to further downgrading of the evidence level. In-depth analysis demonstrated that this heterogeneity likely stems from unavoidable between-study variations, particularly in age distribution and antiviral treatment status. Therefore, the low certainty rating assigned by the GRADE system primarily reflects the intrinsic characteristics of chronic disease epidemiological research rather than deficiencies in study quality. Finally, it was important to note that our analysis did not fully control for the impact of key confounders such as hypertension and diabetes severity because the original study did not universally report detailed data on key confounders such as hypertension. This may affect the accuracy of prevalence estimates, and future studies should enhance the collection and reporting of these clinically important variables. In summary, despite the limitations of this study, the latest meta-analysis provides data on the prevalence of CKD in CHB patients, and may provide assistance in policy development.

## Conclusion
In this meta-analysis, we estimated that 8.70% of patients with CHB also had CKD. Due to natural variability across different populations, regions, or time points, as well as differences in study design, it is inevitable that the prevalence rates exhibit high heterogeneity. The analysis revealed a higher prevalence among the following groups: individuals over 50 years of age, those from the Southeast Asia, diabetic patients, individuals with cirrhosis, and those with a history of antiviral therapy. These findings may help us to better understand the prevalence of CKD in patients with CHB.
