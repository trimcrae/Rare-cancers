YuZhixin LiZichun ChenPing ZhouYixin – ] The treatment strategy for NPC is relatively independent and differs from that for other head and neck malignancies in clinical practice.[ – ] Intensity-modulated radiotherapy is currently the main treatment for locoregional NPC.[ ] However, for recurrent or metastatic NPC (RM-NPC), palliative systemic therapy is the leading treatment for prolonging overall survival (OS). Zhang et al[ ] and Hong et al[ ] demonstrated the advantages of gemcitabine combined with cisplatin/carboplatin (GP) over conventional fluorouracil plus platinum (FP) in terms of the objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), and OS, which enables GP to be the standard of care in the first-line setting and provides patients with more regimen options for second-line treatment. However, it is still difficult to achieve a median OS beyond 30 months.
EBV-associated NPC is widely regarded as a “hot tumor”, since previous studies have indicated the enrichment of a substantial immune cell population within the tumor microenvironment of most NPCs,[ – ] suggesting that immunotherapy may be an efficacious treatment for RM-NPC. Previous explorations of immunotherapy in NPC have focused on evaluating the efficacy and safety of immunotherapy alone in second- or later-line settings, which has led to an ORR of approximately 20–40%. Nevertheless, head-to-head trials of second-line treatment have failed to reveal the therapeutic benefit of immunotherapy over chemotherapy in terms of PFS and ORR. Recently, the results of three randomized controlled clinical studies including CAPTAIN-1st,[ ] JUPITER-02,[ ] and RATIONALE 309[ ] have been reported, confirming that programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) inhibitor plus GP (ICI + Chemo) could further improve PFS for treatment-naïve patients without significantly increasing treatment-related adverse events (AEs). Therefore, in the first-line setting, ICI + Chemo has become the recommendation of the current Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines for NPC.[ ]
However, due to the low morbidity of NPC worldwide,[ ] the sample size of the mentioned trials was relatively small. Thus, individual trials comparing ICI + Chemo vs. chemotherapy in a first-line setting were not powerful enough to explore differences in treatment efficacy between patient subgroups. In addition, the results of trials evaluating the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) alone as second- or later-line treatment fluctuate greatly due to the small sample size.
Therefore, we performed this meta-analysis to pool the three first-line trials for RM-NPC patients, seeking potential clinical or molecular predictive factors; then, the pooled results of ICIs alone in subsequent-line treatment can be used to provide clinicians with more comprehensive data and guidance.

## Methods

## Study eligibility and identification
Eligible randomized controlled trials investigating RM-NPC that compared ICI + Chemo with chemotherapy in the first-line setting, or ICIs alone in the second- or later-line setting were identified from PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. We searched for studies that were published in English from inception to December 13, 2022, using the keywords pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, camrelizumab, toripalimab, tislelizumab, penpilimab, spartalizumab, ipilimumab, PD-1, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and randomized controlled trial [Supplementary Method, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C205]. We also reviewed abstracts from major conference proceedings of the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO), the American Association for Cancer Research (AACR), and the World Conference on Lung Cancer (WCLC). When duplicate studies were identified, only the most complete and updated data of the studies were included.

## Data extraction
Two authors (Zhixin Yu and Hui Yu) independently extracted the data with a predefined information sheet. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. We extracted the following information for each included trial: acronym and design of the trial, number of patients enrolled, year of publication or conference presentation, clinicopathological characteristics of the patients, and treatment outcomes including PFS, OS, ORR, disease control rate (DCR), and AEs. The quality of the included randomized controlled trials and single-arm studies was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration tool[ ] and the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS),[ ] respectively.

## Statistical analysis
The PFS outcomes were measured with hazard ratios (HRs) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), which were extracted from each study or calculated using other available statistics. Additionally, interaction tests were used to assess the differences in treatment effects across the subgroups. The ORR and AEs outcomes were measured with risk ratios (RRs) and the corresponding 95% CIs.
The secondary outcome was the pooled result of ICIs alone in the subsequent-line treatment of RM-NPC patients. ORR and AE data were pooled per regimen and are presented as percentages. Additionally, we compared the efficacy and safety of ICIs and chemotherapy in the second-line treatment of RM-NPC patients.
We used Cochrane’s Q statistic to assess between-study heterogeneity and calculated the I2 statistic, which estimates the percentage of total variation across studies due to heterogeneity rather than chance. The pooled estimates for PFS and OS are presented with HRs, 95% CIs, and P values calculated using the inverse-variance-weighted method, while the measures for dichotomous data are pooled with RRs, 95% CIs, and P values using the Mantel–Haenszel method. Moreover, double arcsine transformation was performed when pooling the non-normally distributed data. If significant heterogeneity (I2 >50%) were detected, we utilized the random effects model to calculate the combined proportion and its corresponding 95% CIs; otherwise, the fixed effects model was applied.
We used Stata version 15.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) for all of the analyses. All tests were two-sided, and P <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

## Results
A total of 316 studies were identified through the initial search strategy. After screening the abstracts and reviewing the full texts, a total of 15 trials involving 1928 patients were included in the final analyses [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C205].
The main characteristics of the included trials are summarized in Table . Three trials including CAPTAIN-1st,[ ] JUPITER-02[ ] and RATIONALE 309[ ] compared ICI + Chemo with chemotherapy alone (a GP regimen) in the first-line setting; eleven trials[ – ] evaluated the efficacy and safety of PD-1 inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, camrelizumab, toripalimab, tislelizumab, penpilimab, and spartalizumab) as monotherapy in the second- or later-line treatment of RM-NPC; and one trial[ ] investigated nivolumab plus ipilimumab in later-line treatment. The first-line regimen of ICI + Chemo exhibited remarkable efficacy with an ORR of 69.5–87.3%, a median PFS of 9.6–21.4 months, and an incidence rate of AEs of grade 3 or greater of 80.9–92.5%. However, in subsequent-line therapy, ICIs alone had an ORR of 17.1–43.0%, a median PFS of 1.9–10.4 months, and relatively lower toxicity (incidence rate of AEs of grade 3 or more, 10.0–29.6%).
 

## ICI + Chemo vs. chemotherapy in a first-line setting
The pooled results showed that compared with chemotherapy, ICI + Chemo significantly reduced the risk of disease progression (HR = 0.52, 95% CI, 0.43–0.63; Z = 6.87, P <0.001) [Figure A]. There was no significant heterogeneity in the overall treatment effect in terms of PFS across the three trials (I2 = 0, P = 0.946).
 The results of the subgroup analyses for PFS are shown in Figure B. Compared with chemotherapy, ICI + Chemo led to significantly longer PFS across all the tested subgroups in terms of age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS), pathological type, metastatic site, EBV DNA status, disease state, and PD-L1 expression. The magnitude of the benefit of ICI + Chemo vs. chemotherapy was similar within each subgroup, indicating that none of these factors could predict PFS. Notably, for patients whose PD-L1 expression was 10% or greater, the pooled HR for PFS was 0.53 (95% CI, 0.35–0.79), compared with the HR of 0.38 (95% CI, 0.20–0.72), HR of 0.59 (95% CI, 0.39–0.89), and HR of 0.35 (95% CI, 0.15–0.81) in those with PD-L1 expression of less than 10%, 1% or greater, and less than 1%, respectively. No statistically significant difference in PFS was found across the PD-L1 subgroups (P = 0.553).
The ICI + Chemo regimen also had a greater ORR than did chemotherapy (RR = 1.14, 95% CI, 1.05–1.24; P = 0.001) [Figure A]. No statistically significant heterogeneity was observed in the analysis of ORR (I2 = 0, P = 0.371).
 In terms of AEs [Figure B], the addition of ICIs to first-line chemotherapy tended to increase the risk of treatment discontinuation (RR = 1.63, 95% CI, 0.93–2.88; P = 0.090), but did not significantly increase the incidence rates of AEs of any grade (RR = 1.01, 95% CI, 0.99–1.03; P = 0.481), grade 3 or higher (RR = 1.00, 95% CI, 0.94–1.06; P = 0.905), serious AEs (RR = 0.99, 95% CI, 0.82–1.19; P = 0.907), or death-related AEs (RR = 1.75, 95% CI, 0.59–5.17; P = 0.311). In the ICI + Chemo group, the pooled incidence rates of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) of any grade, grade 3 or higher, leading to death were 59% (95% CI, 34–85%), 8% (95% CI, 1–15%), and 1% (95% CI, 0–5%), respectively.

## ICIs in second- or later-line settings
The treatment efficacy and safety of ICIs in subsequent-line settings are presented in Figure , including the ORR [Figure A], DCR [Figure B], PFS [Figure C], and AEs [Figure D]. The pooled ORR was 24% (95% CI, 20–28%), the DCR was 52% (95% CI, 45–59%), and the median PFS was 4.12 months (95% CI, 2.93–5.31 months). Heterogeneity was found in these analyses across the included trials, with one showing borderline significance (ORR: I2 = 41%, P = 0.07; DCR: I2 = 67%, P <0.01; PFS: I2 = 83%, P <0.01), and random models were applied. In terms of toxicity, the incidence rates of grade 1–5/grade 3–5 AEs were 79% (95% CI, 71–88%)/14% (95% CI, 12–17%), while the incidence rates of serious AEs/AEs leading to treatment discontinuation were 12% (95% CI, 9–15%)/2% (95% CI, 1–3%).
 

## ICIs vs. chemotherapy in a second-line setting
Two trials[ , ] investigated ICIs alone vs. chemotherapy as a second-line treatment. Compared with chemotherapy, ICIs alone were associated with significantly shorter PFS (HR = 1.31, 95% CI, 1.01–1.68; P = 0.040) [Figure A] and a numerically lower ORR (RR = 0.69, 95% CI, 0.37–1.29; P = 0.250) [Figure B]. No statistically significant heterogeneities were found in these two trials.
 

## Pooled results of PFS, ORR, and incidence rates of AEs
To enhance the integration of the meta-analytical findings, we present the combined results of PFS, ORR, and AEs across different treatment regimens in various settings.
In the first-line setting, ICI + Chemo demonstrated a median PFS of 14.05 (95% CI, 13.07–15.03) months and an ORR of 79% (95% CI, 74–82%), accompanied by an incidence rate of grade 1–5 AEs at 100% (95% CI, 99–100%) and grade 3–5 AEs rate of 88% (95% CI, 84–91%). In contrast, chemotherapy alone yielded an inferior median PFS of 7.20 (95% CI, 6.67–7.73) months and an ORR of 68% (95% CI, 63–72%), with a similar incidence rate of grade 1–5 AEs at 100% (95% CI, 99–100%) and a grade 3–5 AEs rate of 87% (95% CI, 84–90%). In the subsequent-line treatment setting, ICIs alone demonstrated previously mentioned results, while chemotherapy alone resulted in a median PFS of 5.88 (95% CI, 4.23–7.54) months, an ORR of 26% (95% CI, 20–34%), an incidence rate of grade 1–5 AEs of 88% (95% CI, 82–92%), and a grade 3–5 AE rates of 43% (95% CI, 35–51%).

## Quality assessment
Details of the quality assessment for each study are summarized in Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C205. The randomized controlled trials were of high quality, despite the two trials in the second-line setting having a high risk of performance bias due to their open-label design. Additionally, most single-arm studies presented a high risk in terms of unbiased assessment of the study endpoint and prospective calculation of the study size.

## Discussion
In this study, we comprehensively analyzed clinical trials of immunotherapy for RM-NPC patients. We demonstrated that ICI + Chemo was significantly associated with a 48% reduction in the risk of disease progression, and a 14% increase in the probability of achieving an objective response compared with chemotherapy (GP) for first-line treatment of RM-NPC, without significantly increasing AEs. Neither PD-L1 nor other clinicopathologic factors were found to predict the treatment efficacy of ICI + Chemo vs. chemotherapy. This study provides a greater level of evidence for adding ICIs to chemotherapy in the first-line treatment of RM-NPC patients. In addition, the pooled ORR, DCR, and median PFS for patients treated with ICIs alone in the second- or later-line setting were 24%, 52%, and 4.12 months, respectively, and ICIs alone failed to exhibit a treatment advantage over chemotherapy alone in the second-line setting.
In the first-line treatment, despite conferring a significant prolongation of PFS over chemotherapy, ICI + Chemo only exhibited a limited improvement in ORR (14%), highlighting the indispensable role of chemotherapy for the treatment of RM-NPC. Future studies should focus on how this encouraging increase in PFS translates to improved OS outcomes and whether a combination of other therapies is required to improve treatment efficacy. In addition, this finding underscores the imperative of screening appropriate individuals for the addition of immunotherapy to frontline chemotherapy.
However, in our study, none of the clinicopathological factors were found to be predictive of response to first-line treatment. The results of subgroup analyses for PFS demonstrated that ICI + Chemo was superior to chemotherapy alone across multiple subgroups including age, sex, ECOG PS, pathological type, metastatic site, EBV DNA status, disease state, and PD-L1 expression level. One important concern was that patients from the three trials included in our analysis were exclusively from endemic areas; thus, it remains uncertain whether these results can be generalized to non-endemic regions where patients are primarily characterized by the keratinizing subtype and negative EBV DNA status. According to our subgroup analysis, neither pathological subtype nor EBV DNA status had a significant impact on the difference in therapeutic efficacy between ICI + Chemo and chemotherapy. Moreover, previous studies have shown that survival outcomes are very similar across different histological or racial/ethnic groups.[ , ] These findings suggest that frontline ICI + Chemo therapy in non-endemic areas may be efficacious; therefore, conducting a clinical trial to confirm its therapeutic benefits is warranted.
Another research hotspot is PD-L1, an established biomarker for predicting treatment efficacy in multiple malignancies[ – ] and guiding clinicians in selecting patients for first-line treatment with pembrolizumab monotherapy in non-small cell lung cancer[ – ] and squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck.[ ] However, the prognostic and predictive value of PD-L1 in RM-NPC patients remains controversial.[ , – ] Recently, a meta-analysis indicated that higher/positive PD-L1/PD-1 expression may not serve as a suitable biomarker for the prognosis of NPC patients.[ ] In our study, the expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells failed to predict the treatment efficacy of ICI + Chemo, neither with a cut-off value of 10% nor a cut-off value of 1%. One possible explanation is that the expression of PD-L1 in NPC is different from that in other malignancies, which may result from EBV-induced latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1). In an in vitro study, EBV-negative cell lines exhibited decreased PD-L1 expression.[ ] Moreover, neither a uniform antibody nor a validated PD-L1 scoring algorithm is recommended for NPC; therefore, interpretations of the results must be performed with caution.[ ] However, biomarkers for immunotherapy in NPC remain to be identified. Tumor mutational burden (TMB), another pan-cancer predictive marker for immunotherapy efficacy, was also found to have no predictive value in NPC patients.[ ] Potential biomarkers were the early decrease in plasma EBV DNA copy number, genomic amplification in the 11q13 region or ETS-variant gene 6 (ETV6) genomic alterations,[ ] dynamic changes in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)/ alanine aminotransferase (ALT),[ ] expression of lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3) and PD-1 on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs),[ ] and copy number loss in granzyme genes.[ ]
Going forward, exploring predictive biomarkers is particularly important for second- or later-line immunotherapy in NPC patients. For these unselected patients, our pooled results demonstrated that the treatment efficacy of ICIs alone was limited with an ORR of 24%, a DCR of 52%, and a median PFS of 4.12 months, which was consistent with the findings of a previous meta-analysis.[ ] These pooled outcomes indicated that ICIs benefited only a small subset of patients in the later-line treatment of RM-NPC. We further performed comparisons between ICIs and chemotherapy in a second-line setting (patients who were platinum-pretreated). In the KEYNOTE-122 trial, compared with chemotherapy, pembrolizumab monotherapy failed to improve OS in RM-NPC patients. Based on the pooled results of our study, patients treated with ICIs alone exhibited significantly shorter PFS (HR = 1.31; P = 0.04) and numerically lower ORR (RR = 0.69; P = 0.25), which raised more concerns about the limited efficacy of ICIs alone in the second-line treatment. Thus, predictors are warranted to distinguish patients with primary resistance to immunotherapy.
Therefore, a potentially effective strategy is immunotherapy-based combinatorial treatment. Recently, a clinical trial showed that toripalimab combined with gemcitabine and apatinib exhibits promising survival benefits in patients with RM-NPC, both in first-line and subsequent-line treatment.[ ] However, there is still a lack of prospective clinical studies investigating the efficacy of ICI-combinatorial treatment in later-line NPC. In addition, since immunotherapy has recently been applied more often as a first-line treatment, whether immunotherapy can continue to be used after disease progression remains controversial. Although efficacy of ICI + Chemo in subsequent-line treatment of RM-NPC is still unclear, this study demonstrated that the safety profile of ICI + Chemo was manageable. In the first-line setting, the incidence rates of grade 1–5 AEs (100%) and 3–5 AEs (88%) in the ICI + Chemo group were similar to those in the chemotherapy group (100% and 87%, respectively). One possibility is that adding ICIs to chemotherapy may enhance efficacy and reduce tumor burden, thus improving overall health.[ ] Furthermore, the mild irAEs resulting from immunotherapy may be overshadowed by chemotherapy-induced, since there are already numerous AEs in chemotherapy alone group. Intriguingly, we found that compared with a PD-(L)1 inhibitor alone, a combination with chemotherapy for the treatment of NPC decreased the rate of grade 3–5 irAEs (8% vs. 14%), which is consistent with the findings of a previous study,[ ] suggesting that chemotherapy might also inhibit an overactive immune response and thereby reduce irAEs.
In the first-line treatment group, source data were obtained from three randomized controlled high-quality trials without observed heterogeneity, which made these pooled results reliable to some extent. However, there are still some limitations. First, the OS data regarding ICI + Chemo vs. chemotherapy were not presented since the OS of patients in the included trials has not yet been reported. Furthermore, the results of ICI + Chemo vs. chemotherapy raises the clinically important question of whether the treatment advantage remains when the chemotherapy regimen shifts from GP to TP (taxanes plus platinum) or other regimens. Another limitation is that most trials in the second- or later-line setting were in phase 1/2; therefore, the sample sizes were limited and significant heterogeneities were found in these trials. Hence, we pooled all the data using the random effects model to minimize the impact of heterogeneity and thus provide clinicians with a more reliable pooled treatment efficacy. However, randomized controlled studies with larger sample sizes are still needed to validate the results.
In conclusion, ICI + Chemo treatment has advantages over chemotherapy as a first-line treatment for RM-NPC in terms of PFS and ORR, without significantly increasing toxicity. However, ICIs alone exhibited manageable safety but failed to achieve a significant survival benefit over chemotherapy in subsequent-line treatment. Neither PD-L1 expression nor other clinicopathologic factors showed predictive value in comparing the treatment efficacy between first-line ICI + Chemo and chemotherapy. Further research is necessary to investigate additional predictive factors related to immunotherapy in RM-NPC patients to identify the optimal population.
