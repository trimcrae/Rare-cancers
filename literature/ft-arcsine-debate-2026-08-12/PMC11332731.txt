Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is one of the most common infectious diseases in humans, affecting approximately half of the world's population.[ , ] Persistent H. pylori infection can cause various conditions, including chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer, gastric adenocarcinoma, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma.[ – ] In 1994, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (a branch of the World Health Organization) classified H. pylori as a class I carcinogen.[ ] Thus far, H. pylori infection has been identified as the strongest independent risk factor for gastric cancer[ ]; it is present in approximately 90% of patients with new non-cardia gastric cancer worldwide.[ ] Moreover, H. pylori eradication significantly reduces the incidence of gastric cancer;[ , ] Consequently, H. pylori eradication is an effective strategy to prevent gastric cancer onset in high-risk populations. However, there is concern that extensive use of antibiotics has led to increased antibiotic resistance, particularly to clarithromycin and metronidazole, hindering the management of H. pylori infection.[ , ]
Most H. pylori infections occur during childhood and adolescence.[ ] Moreover, intra-familial transmission of H. pylori is an important mechanism of bacterial dissemination.[ ] The presence of similar cultural and dietary habits in East Asia, including sharing utensils and food, may increase the frequency of H. pylori infection. Therefore, comprehending the antibiotic resistance of H. pylori among children and adolescents in East Asia is essential.
Thus far, few meta-analyses have evaluated the rates of antibiotic resistance in H. pylori among children and adolescents. This systematic literature review and meta-analysis was performed to explore the rates of primary antibiotic resistance in H. pylori among East Asian children and adolescents.

## Methods

## Search strategy and study selection
The search approach is outlined in Supplementary Material, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915. We searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for observational studies and randomized controlled trials published in East Asia in any language up to February 23, 2023. We also reviewed the reference lists of relevant articles to identify additional sources. The East Asia region was delineated using the internationally recognized United Nations geoscheme developed by the United Nations Statistics Division.[ ] According to this geoscheme, East Asia consists of countries and territories such as China, Japan, South Korea, North Korea, and Mongolia. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42023402510) and the study was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for systematic reviews.[ ]

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Studies included in this review diagnosed H. pylori infection using at least one standard diagnostic method (rapid urease test, histology, bacterial culture, 13C urea breath test, or fecal H. pylori antigen test); determined antibiotic resistance in H. pylori by E-test, agar dilution, Kirby–Bauer method, or polymerase chain reaction; and analyzed patients aged ≤18 years who had neither history of H. pylori eradication nor use of antibiotics within 2 weeks before the study.
Studies were excluded from this review if they focused on special populations (e.g., patients with diabetes or patients on hemodialysis); described resistance rates using fewer than 50 isolates; reported resistance rates solely in percentage format; did not mention total isolate number; comprised guidelines, conference reports, published letters, reviews, or letters to the editor; and/or displayed poor quality.

## Data extraction
Two researchers (Zhou and Zhang) independently assessed the retrieved article titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant studies, removing articles that were obviously irrelevant or duplicates. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer. If data were provided regarding at least one type of antibiotic resistance, the full text of the article was retrieved for further assessment. Studies that may have been missed during assessment or omitted from database searches were identified by reviewing the references of retrieved articles.
From each article, the following information was extracted: first author, year of publication, study area (country or region), year of sample collection, age of participants (range or mean), sex, history of antibiotic treatment for H. pylori, number of patients, histological diagnosis, number of samples (gastric biopsies or H. pylori isolates), antibiotic resistance rate, and resistance detection method (e.g., agar dilution, E-test, paper diffusion, or point mutation detection by polymerase chain reaction).

## Quality assessment
The qualities of the included studies were independently assessed by two reviewers (Zhou and Zhang) using a tool proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute for critical appraisal of prevalence studies[ ] and an adapted version of the tool[ ] [Supplementary Material, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915] based on previous literature regarding quality assessment, with scores ranging from 0 to 8 (>5, high quality; 4–5 moderate quality; <4, low quality). Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (Du).

## Statistical analysis
A meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the rates of primary antibiotic resistance in H. pylori. Because some studies showed no resistance to a subset of antibiotics, the data were transformed using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine method [ , ] to avoid excluding studies in which resistance rates were 0. Because antibiotic resistance rates can substantially vary across East Asia, heterogeneity was addressed using a random effects model; pooled estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained by the DerSimonian and Laird method.[ ] Cochran's Q test and the I2 statistic were used to assess heterogeneity among the included studies (<25%, 25–75%, and >75% I2 were assumed to reflect low, moderate, and high rates of inter-study heterogeneity, respectively).[ ] Funnel plots and Egger's test[ ] were utilized to examine possible bias in small studies. All tests were two-sided and the significance threshold was 0.05.
To investigate potential sources of variability, subgroup analyses were conducted, using geographic region, sample collection period, and H. pylori resistance testing technique as variables of interest. For subgroup analyses, sample collection intervals were separated into three periods: before 2005, 2005–2015, and after 2015. Studies with a sample collection interval that spanned two periods were grouped within the period during which the majority of the study was conducted. If the sample collection interval was not specified, the 2 years preceding the year of publication were regarded as the sample collection interval.[ ] For articles with ≥2 non-consecutive sample collection intervals, each sample collection interval was considered a separate study in the meta-analysis; for articles that provided both primary and secondary resistance data, only primary resistance data were analyzed. Stata software (version 17.0, StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) was used for statistical analysis.

## Results
As shown in Figure , the initial search yielded 182 articles: 41 from PubMed, 6 from the Cochrane Library, 132 from Embase, and 3 from the reference lists of retrieved articles. After the exclusion of 31 duplicates and 132 irrelevant articles, the full texts of 19 articles were subjected to further review. One article was excluded because it contained data that overlapped with subsequent reports; two articles were excluded because they were not identified in the original database search and their full texts were not widely accessible; and one article was excluded because of poor quality. Thus, 15 articles (12 in English and 3 in Chinese, involving 4831 H. pylori isolates) described samples that had been collected between 1990 and 2020 to assess resistance to clarithromycin, metronidazole, amoxicillin, tetracycline, furazolidone, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, moxifloxacin, and azithromycin. One study[ ] contained two separate non-consecutive sample collection intervals and was therefore divided into two studies for meta-analysis. Two studies[ , ] analyzed both primary and secondary resistance; however, we only examined primary antibiotic resistance data. Only two studies examined resistance rates for gentamicin, erythromycin, and rifampicin in children and adolescents; therefore, resistance rates for these three antibiotics were not included in the meta-analysis. In all studies, H. pylori infection was detected by histological methods. Basic information about each study is provided in Table .
 Among the 15 studies, 12, 2, and 1 were conducted in China, Japan, and South Korea, respectively; the South Korean study was regarded as 2 separate studies in the meta-analysis because its sample collection interval covered 2 distinct time periods. Although only 33 H. pylori isolates were included in one of the South Korean studies after the division of the original study, it was included in the analysis because the total number of isolates involved was >50, which satisfied the inclusion criteria.

## Study quality assessment
Sixteen articles were included in the initial quality evaluation; however, one study was excluded because it displayed low quality. Among the remaining 15 studies, 6 displayed high quality (score >5) and 9 displayed moderate quality (score 4–5). Quality was evaluated using established assessment tools, as shown in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915.

## Primary resistance to metronidazole in H. pylori
Fourteen studies (4668 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to metronidazole in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 9% to 88% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 51% (95% CI: 40–62%); there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 98.46%) [Figure A].
 In subgroup analysis according to sample collection interval, the metronidazole resistance rates were 18% (95% CI: 6–30%) before 2005 (three studies [i.e., n = 3]), 53% (95% CI: 41–64%) from 2005 to 2015 (n = 8), and 82% (95% CI: 76–89%) after 2015 (n = 3). There was a significant increase (P <0.01) in metronidazole resistance over time among H. pylori isolates from children and adolescents [Figure B].
In subgroup analysis according to country or region, the metronidazole resistance rates were 69% (95% CI: 63–75%) in the Chinese mainland (n = 9) and 20% (95% CI: 11–28%) in other regions of China (n = 5); these rates significantly differed (P <0.01) [Figure C]. Upon further subdivision according to geographic region, the metronidazole resistance rates were 59% (95% CI: 53–65%) in northern China (n = 2), 74% (95% CI: 55–93%) in southwestern China (n = 3), and 70% (95% CI: 62–77%) in southeastern China (n = 4). Within-group heterogeneity was low in northern China (I2 = 0); heterogeneity within the other two subgroups was lower than before subgroup analysis, but it remained high (I2 >75%) [Figure D].
In subgroup analysis according to resistance detection method, E-test and agar dilution showed resistance rates of 45% (95% CI: 24–66%) and 57% (95% CI: 47–68%), respectively; thus, metronidazole resistance did not significantly differ according to detection method (P = 0.29) [Figure E].

## Primary resistance to clarithromycin in H. pylori
Sixteen studies (4831 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to clarithromycin in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 7% to 97% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 37% (95% CI: 20–53%); there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 99.49%) [Figure A].
 In subgroup analysis according to sample collection interval, the clarithromycin resistance rates were 19% (95% CI: 10–27%) before 2005 (n = 5), 46% (95% CI: 16–76%) from 2005 to 2015 (n = 8), and 41% (95% CI: 29–53%) after 2015 (n = 3). There were significant differences among subgroups (P = 0.01) [Figure B], such that the rate of clarithromycin resistance in children and adolescents increased between the 2005–2015 and pre-2005 periods, but it did not significantly increase between the 2005–2015 and post-2015 periods.
In subgroup analysis according to country or region, the clarithromycin resistance rates were 46% (95% CI: 25–68%) in the Chinese mainland (n = 10) and 20% (95% CI: 12–28%) in other regions of China (n = 6) [Figure C]; these rates significantly differed (P = 0.02), but the within-group heterogeneity remained high. Upon further subdivision according to geographic region, the clarithromycin resistance rates were 91% (95% CI: 80–100%) in northern China (n = 2), 60% (95% CI: 32–87%) in southwestern China (n = 3), 21% (95% CI: 14–29%) in southeastern China (n = 5), 20% (95% CI: 14–27%) in Taiwan (China) area (n = 2), 29% (95% CI: 15–43%) in Japan (n = 2), and 11% (95% CI: 0–22%) in South Korea (n = 2). Within-group heterogeneity in each region of the Chinese mainland remained high (I2 >75%), although it decreased in Taiwan (China) area, Japan, and South Korea [Figure D].
In subgroup analysis according to the resistance detection method, E-test and agar dilution showed resistance rates of 45% and 33%, respectively; in one study, both E-test and agar dilution were used, revealing a resistance rate of 15%. The detection method had a significant effect on the clarithromycin resistance rate (P = 0.01) [Figure E].

## Primary resistance to amoxicillin in H. pylori
Twelve studies (4548 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to amoxicillin in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 0 to 38% across regions. Notably, six studies showed no amoxicillin resistance (0); after data transformation, the average resistance rate was 2% (95% CI: 0–5%). Furthermore, there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 94.13%) [Supplementary Figure 1A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. In subgroup analysis according to country or region, amoxicillin resistance rates were generally low. High resistance rates of 19–38% were only detected in small studies from South Korea and Guiyang (China);[ , ] extremely low or no amoxicillin resistance was present among H. pylori isolates from children and adolescents in other regions.

## Primary resistance to tetracycline in H. pylori
Seven studies (2033 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to tetracycline in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 0 to 12% across regions, including 0 in four studies; the average resistance rate was 1% (95% CI: 0–5%) after data transformation, and there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 88.48%) [Supplementary Figure 1B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. Similar to amoxicillin, subgroup analysis according to country or region showed that tetracycline resistance rates were generally low. High resistance rates of 7–12% were only detected in a small study from South Korea[ ]; very low or no tetracycline resistance was present among H. pylori isolates from children and adolescents in other regions.

## Primary resistance to furazolidone in H. pylori
Six studies (4007 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to furazolidone in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 0 to 12% across regions, including 0 in three studies; the average resistance rate was 0 (95% CI: 0–1%) after data transformation, and there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 86.18%) [Supplementary Figure 1C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. Furazolidone resistance rates were generally modest.

## Primary resistance to levofloxacin in H. pylori
Eleven studies (4311 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to levofloxacin in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 5% to 90%, and the average resistance rate was 19% (95% CI: 11–28%); there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 98.44%) [Figure A].
 In subgroup analysis according to sample collection interval, the levofloxacin resistance rates were 8% (95% CI: 2–14%) before 2005 (n = 2), 23% (95% CI: 8–38%) from 2005 to 2015 (n = 6), and 21% (95% CI: 17–25%) after 2015 (n = 3). There were significant differences among subgroups (P <0.01), such that the rate of levofloxacin resistance in children and adolescents increased between the 2005–2015 and pre-2005 periods. There was lower within-group heterogeneity between the pre-2005 and post-2015 periods (I2 <50%); most heterogeneity was present within the 2005–2015 period [Figure B].
In subgroup analysis according to country or region, the levofloxacin resistance rates were 25% (95% CI: 13–37%) in the Chinese mainland (n = 7) and 8% (95% CI: 5–12%) in other regions of China (n = 4); these rates significantly differed (P = 0.01). The within-group heterogeneity remained high in the Chinese mainland (I2 = 99.06%), but the intra-group heterogeneity was low in other regions (I2 = 0) [Figure C]. Further analysis revealed that the heterogeneity in levofloxacin resistance rates was primarily attributable to a 2015 study from Guiyang (China),[ ] which reported a resistance rate of 90.5%; studies from other locations reported resistance rates of <24% [Figure D].

## Primary resistance to ciprofloxacin, moxifloxacin, and azithromycin in H. pylori
Three studies (146 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to ciprofloxacin in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 5% to 15% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 10% (95% CI: 4–17%); there was moderate heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 41.72%) [Supplementary Figure 2A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915].
Three studies (164 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to ciprofloxacin in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 7% to 15% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 11% (95% CI: 5–16%); there was low heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 18.15%) [Supplementary Figure 2B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915].
Three studies (164 H. pylori isolates) reported rates of primary resistance to azithromycin in H. pylori among children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 14% to 88% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 41%; there was high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 98.86%) [Supplementary Figure 2C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. In particular, resistance rates of 14% and 21% were reported in two studies from South Korea, whereas one study from Beijing (China), reported a resistance rate of 88%.

## Dual resistance in H. pylori
Some studies reported the rates of dual or multiple antibiotic resistance among H. pylori isolates. After the elimination of poorly presented data, 12 studies (4501 H. pylori isolates) reported dual resistance to antibiotics. Dual resistance rates ranged from 7% to 54% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 28% (95% CI: 21–35%); there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 96.39%) [Supplementary Figure 3A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915].
In subgroup analysis according to sample collection interval, the rates of dual resistance were 12% (95% CI: 0–24%) before 2005 (n = 2), 28% (95% CI: 19–38%) from 2005 to 2015 (n = 7), and 38% (95% CI: 28–48%) after 2015 (n = 3). There was a significant increase (P = 0.01) in dual resistance over time among H. pylori isolates from children and adolescents. There was lower within-group heterogeneity between the pre-2005 and post-2015 periods; most heterogeneity was present within the 2005–2015 period [Supplementary Figure 3B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915].
In subgroup analysis according to country or region, the dual resistance rates were 35% (95% CI: 25–44%) in the Chinese mainland (n = 8) and 14% (95% CI: 7–21%) in other regions of China (n = 4); these rates significantly differed (P <0.01). The within-group heterogeneity remained high in the Chinese mainland (I2 = 97.52%), but the intra-group heterogeneity was lower in other regions of China (I2 = 55.55%) [Supplementary Figure 3C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. Upon further subdivision according to geographic region, the dual resistance rates were 52% (95% CI: 45–58%) in northern China (n = 2), 40% (95% CI: 31–49%) in southwestern China (n = 3), and 21% (95% CI: 9–32%) in southeastern China (n = 3). Within-group heterogeneity was significantly lower (compared with heterogeneity before subgroup analysis) in both northern and southwestern China (I2 = 12.81% and 50.48%, respectively), but it remained high in studies from southeastern China (I2 >98.59%) [Supplementary Figure 3D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. The resistance detection method did not have a significant effect on the dual resistance rate (P = 0.09) [Supplementary Figure 3E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915].

## Multiple resistance in H. pylori
Ten studies (4440 H. pylori isolates) reported the rates of primary resistance to ≥3 antibiotics among H. pylori isolates from children and adolescents. Resistance rates ranged from 2% to 57% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 10% (95% CI: 7–14%); there was a high degree of heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 95.26%) [Supplementary Figure 4A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915].
In subgroup analysis according to sample collection interval, the rates of multiple resistance were 9% (95% CI: 5–12%) up to 2015 (n = 7) and 11% (95% CI: 7–15%) after 2015 (n = 3). There was no significant difference among subgroups (P = 0.32) [Supplementary Figure 4B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915], indicating that the rates of multiple resistance among H. pylori isolates from children and adolescents did not significantly increase over time.
In subgroup analysis according to country or region, the multiple resistance rates were 11% (95% CI: 7–15%) in the Chinese mainland (n = 8) and 7% (95% CI: 0–16%) in other regions of China (n = 2); these rates did not significantly differ (P = 0.43), and within-group heterogeneity in the Chinese mainland remained high (I2 = 96.19%) [Supplementary Figure 4C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. Further analysis revealed that the heterogeneity in multiple resistance rates was primarily attributable to a 2015 study from Guiyang (China),[ ] which reported a resistance rate of 57%; studies from other regions reported resistance rates of <22% (mostly <13%) [Supplementary Figure 4D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915]. The resistance detection method did not have a significant effect on the multiple resistance rate (P = 0.56) [Supplementary Figure 4E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B915].

## Discussion
Antibiotic resistance is a key factor in the treatment of H. pylori infection, and an understanding of antibiotic resistance patterns can facilitate the development of more effective H. pylori eradication regimens.[ ] Previous systematic reviews have examined antibiotic resistance in H. pylori isolates from various regions,[ , , , ] but most reviews were focused on adults. Thus far, few systematic reviews have specifically examined antibiotic resistance in H. pylori among children and adolescents. This systematic review concerns the rates of antibiotic resistance in H. pylori among children and adolescents within a region (East Asia) displaying the highest incidence of gastric cancer worldwide.
Previous studies have shown that regions with high rates of gastric cancer also have very high rates of antibiotic resistance; regions with lower rates of antibiotic resistance also have lower rates of gastric cancer.[ , ] Although a causal relationship has not been conclusively established, this correlation remains concerning. Additionally, H. pylori is primarily transmitted within families,[ ] and most H. pylori infections occur during childhood and adolescence.[ ] Furthermore, the common practice of sharing meals (i.e., utensils and food) in East Asia may increase the risk of transmission and promote antibiotic resistance.[ ]
In the present systematic review, metronidazole had the highest rate of resistance (51%) in H. pylori isolates from children and adolescents in East Asia. Resistance rates gradually increased from 18% (pre-2005) to 53% (2005–2015), and then 82% (post-2015); the Chinese mainland had a significantly higher resistance rate, compared with other regions. A meta-analysis by Kuo et al[ ] revealed that metronidazole resistance did not significantly increase (P = 0.085) among adults in the Asia-Pacific region between 2000 (36%) and 2011–2015 (45%). Another meta-analysis revealed that metronidazole resistance rates in the Western Pacific region ranged from 52% to 57% between 2006 and 2016,[ ] without a substantial increase. Our systematic review revealed a large increase in metronidazole resistance over time, in contrast to previous findings in adults; consequently, there is a need to closely monitor the increase in metronidazole resistance in H. pylori among East Asian children and adolescents (primarily in the Chinese mainland).
Resistance to clarithromycin is equally concerning. The present systematic review revealed that the resistance rate ranged from 7% to 97% across regions, and the average resistance rate was 37%. Clarithromycin resistance in children and adolescents gradually increased between the 2005–2015 and pre-2005 periods, but it did not significantly increase between the 2005–2015 and post-2015 periods. This phenomenon may be related to the recent decline in clarithromycin triple therapy because of increased clarithromycin resistance. Furthermore, the rate of clarithromycin resistance was significantly higher in the Chinese mainland than in other parts of East Asia; in northern China, the rate was 91%, which is considerably higher than the rate among adults in northern China (9–53%) reported in a previous study.[ ] In regions where clarithromycin resistance exceeds 20%, the rate of eradication with clarithromycin-based triple therapy is below 80%.[ ] Most current guidelines recommend selecting treatments for H. pylori eradication in children and adolescents according to the results of antibiotic susceptibility testing or bismuth-based quadruple therapy.[ – ]
Levofloxacin is a quinolone antibacterial antibiotic that can cause arthropathy and bone/chondral lesions in younger members of certain animal species; accordingly, its safety in children has not been explored, and it is typically contraindicated in children or adolescents aged <18 years.[ , ] However, the present systematic review showed that the levofloxacin resistance rate in H. pylori was substantial (19%) and tended to gradually increase over time, similar to findings in adults across various populations. This similarity may be related to intrafamilial transmission of quinolone-resistant H. pylori strains from adults, reflecting resistance among the infecting strains.[ ] A 2015 study in Guiyang (China), revealed that the rate of levofloxacin resistance in children or adolescents was 90.5%[ ]; this study was the main source of heterogeneity regarding levofloxacin resistance in the present systematic review. Overall, the evidence indicates that levofloxacin should be used cautiously when treating infections among adults in East Asia.
Amoxicillin, tetracycline, and furazolidone are important antibiotics in the management of H. pylori infection; the present systematic review revealed low average resistance rates for all three antibiotics, consistent with previous studies involving adults from various regions.[ , , ] These findings suggest that it remains appropriate to include these three antibiotics in empirical eradication regimens. However, amoxicillin resistance rates were significantly greater in Guiyang, China, and Jinju, South Korea; tetracycline resistance rates were higher in Jinju, South Korea.
Some treatment regimens have failed to eradicate H. pylori because of increasing resistance to clarithromycin and metronidazole; thus, H. pylori has gained resistance to other antibiotics, and dual- or multi-resistant strains have emerged. In the present systematic review, we analyzed the rates of dual or multiple resistance among strains of H. pylori. Notably, the dual resistance rate of 28% (95% CI: 21–36%) gradually increased over time; it was higher in the Chinese mainland than in other East Asian regions. In contrast, the multiple resistance rate of 10% (95% CI: 7–14%) did not change over time or differ among East Asian regions. Additional attention is needed regarding the phenomenon of multiple resistance to antibiotics in Guiyang, China[ ]; greater caution is essential when selecting treatment regimens. Because some studies had fragmented data and were not clearly described, the characteristics of specific antibiotic classes with dual or multiple resistance could not be analyzed. However, dual resistance to clarithromycin and metronidazole should be carefully monitored, considering its potentially wide-ranging impact on treatment.
Surveillance systems for antibiotic resistance in H. pylori can help physicians to select empirical treatment regimens.[ , ] Such surveillance systems should be established in areas where socioeconomic conditions permit, with the goal of eradicating existing antibiotic-resistant strains of H. pylori and reducing the emergence of new antibiotic-resistant phenotypes. Additionally, H. pylori is primarily transmitted between family members during childhood and adolescence[ , ]; prevention and management of intrafamilial transmission is a practical approach for interrupting the transmission chain and reducing rates of social infection.[ , ] Consequently, screening and concurrent treatment of other family members should be strengthened to reduce the likelihood of H. pylori re-infection after eradication in children and adolescents.[ , ]
A key strength of this systematic review is that a detailed search strategy was used to collect data regarding a large number (4831) of antibiotic-resistant H. pylori isolates, yielding the pioneering summary of antibiotic resistance rates in H. pylori isolates from East Asian children and adolescents, and providing insights regarding the management of H. pylori infections among those children and adolescents.
This systematic review had some limitations. For example, most included studies were focused on the Chinese mainland and few studies from Japan and South Korea were published after 2015, which may indicate publication bias. Substantial heterogeneity was detected after data from different studies had been combined and analyzed; most heterogeneity could not be explained by differences in region, sample collection interval, or resistance detection method. The heterogeneity may be related to patient age, sex, diagnostic method, and H. pylori strain, but there were insufficient data to analyze the impacts of these factors. Heterogeneity may also arise from intrinsic regional differences caused by various local infectious illnesses, which result in distinct patterns of antibiotic use.[ ]
In summary, this meta-analysis revealed surprising metronidazole and clarithromycin resistance rates among children and adolescents in East Asia, particularly in the Chinese mainland. Although levofloxacin is not routinely administered to children and adolescents aged <18 years, the high rate of resistance detected among H. pylori isolates remains concerning. When socioeconomic conditions permit, antibiotic resistance surveillance systems (including assessment and treatment of family members) should be established to improve the efficacy of H. pylori eradication treatment in children and adolescents. These systems can also limit the development of antibiotic-resistant strains, thereby reducing the rate of H. pylori infection in adults.
