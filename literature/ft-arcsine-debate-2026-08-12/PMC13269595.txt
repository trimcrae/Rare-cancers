## Background
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) represents a significant and growing public health challenge in Pakistan, due to antibiotic misuse, weak infection control measures and rapid bacterial evolution. Determining the overall prevalence of MRSA in the country is crucial for informing targeted treatment protocols and effective management strategies. This study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to establish the pooled prevalence of MRSA in Pakistan.

## Methods
We searched electronic databases (Google Scholar, PubMed, Embase) for studies published between 2015 and 2025. The PRISMA guidelines were followed to conduct this systematic review. Meta-analyses were performed using R Studio software, applying both fixed- and random-effects models to calculate pooled prevalence. The heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistics. Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s and Egger’s tests. Most studies used cefoxitin disk diffusion to characterize MRSA, while some studies used PCR to detect the mecA gene.

## Results
Sixty-eight studies meeting the inclusion criteria were analyzed. The overall pooled MRSA prevalence was estimated at 50% (95% CI: 45–54%), with I² = 98.9). Year-wise subgroup analysis revealed significant variation from 2015 to 2025, with estimates ranging from 64% (95% CI: 26–90%), with I² = 96.68 in 2022 to a low of 34% (95% CI: 17–56%), with I² = 99.16 in 2020, which may be due to reduced healthcare access and infection control measures implemented during the COVID-19 lockdowns. The pooled estimate among general patients and healthcare personnel was 50% (95% CI: 44–57%), with I² = 98.5 and 44% (95% CI: 30–60%), with I² = 95.4, respectively. The subgroup differences test (p = 0.54) for population type revealed no statistically significant difference. The Begg’s test (p = 0.2378) and Egger’s test (p = 0.8893) showed no significant evidence of bias. The significant variation between the two diagnostic methods (p = 0.03) showed that the observed heterogeneity may be due to diagnostic methods used across the studies.

## Conclusion
The high pooled prevalence of MRSA in Pakistan highlights an urgent need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship, standardized surveillance systems, and integrated infection prevention strategies.

## Introduction
Staphylococcus aureus is a major human pathogen that causes a wide range of infections, from minor superficial skin conditions like impetigo, pimples and boils to serious, invasive infections such as cellulitis, folliculitis, abscesses, carbuncles, and life-threatening toxic shock syndrome [ ]. S. aureus exhibits various mechanisms of antibiotic resistance. For example, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains pose a particular challenge in managing infections, as they limits the effectiveness of β-lactam antibiotics and complicate infection management [ ].
MRSA strains acquire a mobile genetic element known as the staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec), that contains the mecA gene (or its homologs mecB or mecC). The mecA gene encodes a modified penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a) that has a low affinity for most of beta-lactams, but exceptions (e.g., ceftaroline) exist [ ]. Beyond this core resistance, MRSA strains often exhibit multidrug resistance to other antibiotic classes, such as aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides [ ]. Additionally, MRSA’s ability to colonize asymptomatically, especially in the anterior nares, generates a substantial reservoir of potential infection and transmission [ ]. The bacteria can persist on surfaces for weeks to months, making decolonization techniques and environmental cleaning are essential to prevent the spread [ ].
MRSA remains a major global health burden, infecting over 50 million people annually [ ]. A comprehensive meta-meta-analysis reported a worldwide MRSA prevalence of 14.58% (95% CI: 6.61–29.14%) out of 322,269 samples [ ]. The burden of MRSA is particularly high in South Asia (9.23% out of 5423 samples), with an increasing incidence in both community and healthcare settings [ ]. Despite this regional concern, the epidemiological landscape of MRSA in Pakistan remains poorly characterized. The currently available data are frequently fragmented, limited to single-center studies, and report highly variable prevalence rates, ranging from 13% (out of 713) to 94% (out of 18) across various reports [ , ].
This lack of a clear, consolidated overview creates a crucial knowledge gap, hindering the development of efficient, evidence-based national infection control and treatment protocols. The present study was conducted to address this gap by providing a comprehensive synthesis of data from multiple centers across Pakistan. The study aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of Methicillin-resistant among clinical S. aureus isolates across different regions of Pakistan from 2015 to 2025, and to estimate the pooled prevalence, with subgroup analyses by population, year, diagnostic method, and province. We hypothesized that the pooled MRSA prevalence would exceed 40% and would vary significantly by region and year, reflecting differential antimicrobial stewardship and infection control practices.

## Methodology

## Literature Search
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) standards were followed to conduct this systematic review. Using the electronic databases Google scholar (July 8, 2025), PubMed (July 13, 2025) and EMBASE (July 15, 2025), a thorough literature search was conducted until July 2025. Additionally, a manual search was conducted through the reference lists of each relevant studies. The search strategy employed key terms including ((“Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus“[Mesh] OR “Staphylococcus aureus“[Mesh] OR “MRSA”) AND (“Prevalence“[Mesh] OR “Epidemiological Studies“[Mesh] OR “Cross-Sectional Studies“[Mesh]) AND (“Healthcare workers“[Mesh] OR “Health personnel“[Mesh] OR “Patients“[Mesh])). The review included all English-language articles published from 2015 to 2025. The complete search strategy for each database is given in (Table  ). 

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Two reviewers separately conducted the initial screening and full-text evaluation of the retrieved articles in order to minimize bias. The conflicts were resolved via discussion with a third reviewer. Studies that reported the prevalence of MRSA in hospitalized patients or healthcare workers from 2015 to 2025 were considered eligible for the analysis. Following the initial search, the titles and abstracts of identified records were screened for relevance. Subsequently, the complete texts of studies were thoroughly examined to extract specific data, such as: (a) the number of positive events, (b) the sample size, (c) study region (d) study site (e) diagnostic tests used for detection of MRSA and (f) the year the study was carried out. The study excluded articles that did not meet the aforementioned screening criteria. Additionally, articles having very large (> 6000) or very low (< 10) number of events or samples (insufficient data) were excluded from the analysis. Exclusion criteria also included: articles did not satisfy time period criteria (published before 2015), review papers and conference abstracts that did not report the prevalence of MRSA, duplicates studies, other than human and studies with no clear diagnostic criteria.

## Data extraction and Quality Assessment
The two investigators independently extracted data from each included study. The records extracted from each eligible study were: (a) authors name, (b) publication year, (c) sample type (d) total S. aureus (e) total MRSA (f) region and site of study (g) study design (h) diagnostic criteria. We were also interested in the type of population type whether it is obtained from patients, medical personnel, or clinical settings to categorize the included studies based on the type of population type. The quality of the included studies was assessed by following the protocols of Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for prevalence studies. Studies were categorized as low (≥ 7), moderate [ , ], or high (≤ 4) risk of bias based on predefined criteria [ ].

## Meta-Analysis
The meta-analysis was performed using the R Studio (5897.5.1) statistical software. We used the meta (metaprop function), and metafor packages to compute Tau2 and Higgins’ I2 statistics and associated p-values to estimate the percentage of overall variation due to heterogeneity among the included studies. The pooled prevalence was calculated by both fixed and random effects models, and only the results of random effects models were interpreted due to high heterogeneity. This approach generates a weighted average percentage of prevalence in several studies. The results are presented graphically through forest plots. To determine the between-study variance (τ²) the restricted maximum-likelihood estimator was used. The pooled prevalence of MRSA was computed and presented as a percentage with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Prior to pooling the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was performed to stabilize variances and approximate normal distribution for proportion data. This transformation handles zero and small numerators more effectively, without adjustments that could bias the pooled estimate. To assess the effect of individual studies on the overall pooled prevalence, we performed the Leave-One-Out sensitivity analysis.
The publication bias was analyzed by both Egger’s linear regression and Begg’s tests to detect small study effect. The potential impact of missing studies on pooled prevalence was estimated by trim and fill analysis and visualized through funnel plot. The studies were categorized by year of publication, region and population for subgroup analyses to investigate the potential of heterogeneity and the univariable meta-regression was used to calculate the p value.

## Results

## Search and Studies Selection
A comprehensive search of the literature identified 368 studies. After removal of eight duplicates, 360 records remained for the title and abstract screening. Title and abstract screening excluded 168 records. The remaining 192 articles were assessed for full text review. After a thorough review 124 articles were excluded due to the following reasons: [ ] Articles did not satisfy time period criteria (n = 42) [ ], unclear MRSA diagnostic methodology (n = 34) [ ], other than human (n = 25), and [ ] insufficient data (n = 23). A total of 68 publications were included in the meta-analysis after meeting the predetermined inclusion criteria (Fig.  ). Of the 68 studies, 56 scored nine, nine studies scored eight and three studies scored seven out of nine on the JBI 9-item checklist. All the studies were included based on quality, as all met the predetermined inclusion criteria (Supplementary file. ). Cohen’s Kappa was used to measure inter-rater agreement for the study selection process. The (κ = 0.88) value indicates the agreement between the reviewers was ‘almost perfect’ (Table  ). 

## Characteristics of the Included Studies
The studies were conducted in 15 different regions of Pakistan i.e.: Faisalabad (2 studies), Islamabad (6 studies), Peshawar (12 studies), Rawalpindi (7 studies), Karachi (10 studies), Bahawalpur (2 studies), Lahore (20 studies) and Abbottabad (2 studies). Sialkot, Wah Cantt, Narowal, Hazara, Muzaffarabad, Rahim Yar Khan and Multan have one study each. Lahore contributed the largest number of studies (n = 20) and sample (n = 2742). Regarding source of sample type, 52 studies examined patients, 12 healthcare workers, 2 cancer patients and 2 clinical setups. Furthermore, the studies included were published from 2015 to 2025. The majority of the studies were conducted in 2019 (n = 10) and 2021 (n = 10) followed by 2015 (n = 7) and 2016 (n = 7). The most common samples were blood, pus, wound swabs, nasal swabs and urine. The predetermined diagnostic criteria were used to confirm MRSA isolates: including culture of samples on MacConkey, Mannitol Salt, blood and chocolate agars, Gram staining, Biochemical tests (catalase, coagulase, and DNase), Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method with Cefoxitin (30 µg) and multiplex PCR for mecA/mecC/nuc genes. All the characteristics of the 68 included studies are summarized in (Table  ) (Supplementary file. ). The mean number of the MRSA isolates per study was 155.82 (range: 12–2742), and median size is 65.5. This discrepancy may probably be due to different patient demographics, clinical settings and the geographic distribution of studies. 

## Pooled prevalence of MRSA
This meta-analysis reported data from 68 studies. A total of 10,596 MRSA strains were identified out of 23,023 strains of S. aureus. The overall estimate of MRSA in Pakistan exhibited considerable variation, ranging from 13% (95% CI: 0.11–0.16) to 94% (95% CI: 0.73- 1.0). The pooled prevalence was estimated by the random-effects model to be 50% (95% CI: 0.45–0.54). The study estimates showed significant heterogeneity (I² = 98.9%, τ2 = 0.053 and p = 0) (Fig.  ). The pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity, and greater emphasis should be placed on subgroup analysis. The Leave-One-Out sensitivity analysis estimated the pooled prevalence mostly between 45% and 51%, suggesting that after omitting each individual study does not affect the overall pooled prevalence (Fig.  ). 

## Year-Wise Prevalence of MRSA in Pakistan
The year-wise subgroup meta-analysis revealed significant variations in the pooled prevalence of MRSA in Pakistan, with high heterogeneity between studies each year (Fig.  ; Table  ). The pooled estimate varied significantly from 2015 to 2025, ranging from high of 64% (95% CI: 0.26–0.90) in 2022 to a low of 34% (95% CI: 0.17–0.56) in 2020. In 2023, a second peak of 62% (95% CI: 0.42–0.79) was also observed. The analysis of seven studies in 2015 revealed the estimate of 60%, with remarkable wide confidence interval (0.31–0.83) and the highest heterogeneity with (I² = 99.3%, τ² = 0.0732, p < 0.001). In 2016, seven studies revealed the estimate as 34% (95% CI: 0.25–0.46) with I² = 92.8%, τ² = 0.0141, p < 0.001. In 2017, three studies revealed the estimate as 42% (95% CI: 0.22–0.65) with I² = 87.28%, τ² = 0.0077, p < 0.001. In 2018, five studies revealed the prevalence as 52% (95% CI: 0.40–0.67) with I² = 88.84%, τ² = 0.0097, p < 0.001. In 2019, 10 studies revealed the estimate as 47% (95% CI: 0.31–0.64) with I² = 97.2%, τ² = 0.0411, p < 0.001. In 2020, six studies revealed the estimate as 34% (95% CI: 0.17–56) with I² = 99.16%, τ² = 0.0338, p < 0.001. In 2021, 10 studies revealed the estimate as 48% (95% CI: 0.31–0.65) with I² = 95.84%, τ² = 0.0441, p < 0.001. In 2022, four studies revealed the estimate as 64% (95% CI: 0.26–0.90) with I² = 96.68%, τ² = 0.0418, p < 0.001. In 2023, seven studies revealed the estimate as 62% (95% CI: 0.42–0.79) with I² = 96%, τ² = 0.0357, p < 0.001. In 2024, three studies revealed the estimate as 40% (95% CI: 0.35–0.92) with I² = 98.68%, τ² = 0.0601, p < 0.001. In 2025, six studies revealed the estimate as 55% (95% CI: 0.38–0.72) with I² = 93.03%, τ² = 0.0253, p < 0.001. 

## Region-wise pooled prevalence of MRSA
The included studies were categorized by provinces base on their study locations. Including Punjab (Faisalabad, Lahore, Sialkot, Narowal, Bahawalpur, Multan and Rahim Yar Khan), Sindh (Karachi), Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (Peshawar, Hazara and Abbottabad) and Islamabad Capital Territory (I.C.T). (Islamabad, Rawalpindi and Wah Cantt). Azad Kashmir (Muzaffarabad) is excluded from region-wise meta analysis due to only one study. We performed inverse-variance weighted random-effects models for meta-analysis within each province. In region-wise analysis the Sindh with ten studies revealed the prevalence of 55% (95% CI: 0.46–0.64) with I² = 97.7%, τ² = 0.3508, p < 0.0001 and the Punjab having 28 studies showed the MRSA estimate of 48% (95% CI: 0.40–0.56) with I² = 95.2%, τ² = 0.7505, p < 0.0001. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (K.P.), having 15 articles showed the MRSA prevalence of 49% (95% CI: 0.33–0.66) with I² = 99.1%, τ² = 1.7897, p < 0.0001. Islamabad Capital Territory (I.C.T), with 14 studies revealed prevalence as 50% (95% CI: 0.39–0.61), I² = 97.4%, τ² = 0.6846, p < 0.0001. The subgroup differences test revealed no statistically significant difference in MRSA prevalence across provinces (p = 0.6). (Fig.  ). 

## Prevalence of MRSA by Population Type, Clinical vs. Colonization Studies and Specimen Type
The sub-group analysis of population type showed that colonization or infection of MRSA is extremely prevalent across all populations, with pooled estimate ranging from 43% (95% CI: 0.27–0.61), with I²= 89.3%, τ² =0.2447, p < 0.0001 in cancer patients to 53% (95% CI: 0.48–0.58). However, estimates consistency varies significantly between these subgroups. The patient sample with 52 studies showed prevalence as 50% (95% CI: 0.44–0.57), with I²= 98.5%, τ² =0.8530, p = 0 and health care sample with 12 studies showed the prevalence as 44% (95% CI: 0.30–0.60) with I²= 95.4%, τ²= 1.1511, p < 0.0001. The overall pooled prevalence revealed a substantial heterogeneity with I²= 98.1%, τ² =0.82 and p = 0 among the included studies. The subgroup differences test showed that no significant variations were found between subgroups by using random-effect model (χ2 = 2.21, df = 3, p = 0.54) (Table  ; Fig.  ). The subgroup meta-analysis by clinical vs. colonization studies revealed the pooled estimates of 51% (95% CI: 0.46–0.57) with I²= 98.7%, p < 0.001 for clinical and 39% (95% CI: 0.27–0.52) with I²= 94.5%, p < 0.001 for colonization studies. The subgroup differences test (p = 0.06) showed no significant variations, but suggest a trend that should be confirmed with further studies. The analysis of specimen type showed the pooled estimates of 54% (95% CI: 0.46–0.61) with I²= 98.4% for blood, and 48% (95% CI: 0.37–0.59) with I²= 97.7% for pus, and 42% (95% CI: 0.32–0.52) with I²= 94.9% for swabs. No significant difference was observed between subgroup (p = 0.15) (Supplementary file. ). 

## Prevalence of MRSA by Diagnostic Methods
In the subgroup analysis by diagnostic method, the molecular method showed the estimate as 60% (95% CI: 0.49–0.71) with I²= 95%, and p < 0.001, and the phenotypic method revealed estimate as 47% (95% CI: 0.41–0.53) with I²= 98.7%, and p < 0.0001. The subgroup differences test (χ2 = 5.36, df = 3, p = 0.02) showed significant variation between the two diagnostic method (Fig.  ). 

## Meta regression for Region and Sample Type
The meta regression was performed to determine if geographic region and sample type moderated MRSA prevalence. The analysis revealed no significant effect of region (p = 0.94, I²= 98.03%) and sample type (p = 0.49, I²=98.07%), suggesting that the observed variation among studies could not be explained by regional factors and sample (Figs.  and ). 

## Publication Bias Analysis
Both the Begg’s test (z = 1.18, p = 0.2378) and Egger’s test (t = 0.14, df = 66, p = 0.8893) showed no significant evidence of bias; however, adjusted estimates suggest a modest potential impact (46.5%) (Fig.  ). Although, meta regression analysis showed variation in estimates over time, with no significant temporal trend (p = 0.3219). The non-significant p-values indicate that the pooled estimate is substantial and unlikely to be affected by the selective publishing of research depending on the direction or magnitude of their findings. 

## Discussion
Antibiotic resistance is a serious public health concern in Pakistan, including the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant pathogens, such as MRSA Uncontrolled sales of over-the-counter antibiotics, widespread misuse of medicine in human, inadequate diagnostic stewardship and poor hospital infection control are some of the contributing factors. This led to increased mortality rate, healthcare costs and treatment failures. The study of Hayat et al. (2022) [ ] found that the development of multidrug-resistant infections was strongly correlated with past antibiotic misuse, including self-medication by using leftover medications.
The MRSA pooled prevalence in Pakistan was found to be 50% (95% CI: 0.44–0.55), with I² = 98.9%, according to the meta-analysis conducted from 2015 to 2025, which is considerably higher than the studies conducted in India 37% (95% CI: 0.32–0.41) and Nepal 38.2% (95% CI: 0.31–0.45) [ , ]. However, given the extreme heterogeneity (I² = 98.9%), this figure should be interpreted as an average of highly variable settings rather than a uniform national prevalence. The significant variation between the two diagnostic methods (p = 0.03) showed that the observed heterogeneity may be due to diagnostic methods used across the studies. The study of Tariq et al. (2024) [ ] revealed that the identification of MRSA by using Cefoxitin disc diffusion missed 37.5% of cases detected by PCR. This suggest that the diagnostic methods may affect the pooled estimates of MRSA and should be considered when comparing and analyzing studies.
Moreover, the high pooled prevalence rates among general patients 50% (95% CI: 0.44–0.57) and healthcare personnel 44% (95% CI: 0.30–0.60), suggests that healthcare facilities are both active transmission reservoirs and treatment centers for MRSA infections. The high prevalence in both healthcare workers and patients highlights an ongoing transmission cycle. Colonized healthcare personnel may unintentionally spread MRSA to patients, and both are constantly at risk due to high environmental contamination in clinical setup [ ]. Effective interventions must simultaneously target healthcare personnel, patients and the clinical settings through decontamination processes, strict infection prevention control (IPC) measures and strong antimicrobial stewardship to break this transmission chain [ ]. The cancer patients with two studies showed prevalence as 43% (95% CI: 0.27–0.61), indicating a wide confidence interval due to small number of studies, which reduce the accuracy of this estimate. Further studies in cancer populations are required to obtain more precise results. Furthermore, low heterogeneity (I² =0%) was observed for clinical setup with only two studies, which is likely underpowered, and may be result in the instability of the pooled estimate. The clinical vs. colonization subgroup analysis revealed the pooled estimates of 51% (95% CI: 0.46–0.57) with I²= 98.7%, for clinical and 39% (95% CI: 0.27–0.52) with I²= 94.5%, for colonization studies, with a borderline significant variation (p = 0.06), which suggested a trend toward significant that should be confirmed with further studies.
The year-wise meta-analysis reveals a significant difference in the MRSA pooled prevalence between 2015 and 2025, with estimates ranging from high of 64% (95% CI: 0.26–0.90) in 2022 and 62% (95% CI: 0.42–0.79) in 2023 to a low of 34% (95% CI: 0.17–0.56) in 2020. The decreased in MRSA prevalence in 2020 may be due to the implementation of strict infection control measures during the COVID-19 pandemic, such as improved cleaning procedures in healthcare settings, use of personal protective equipment (PPE), and increased hand hygiene. The study of Madden et al.(2021) [ ] showed 28% of reduction in MRSA cases during COVID-19 pandemic. The most crucial and persistent finding throughout all the years is the consistently high and statistically significant heterogeneity (I² persistently > 85%, p < 0.001 for each year). The I² values, which frequently exceeds 95%, reveal that majority of the observed variance in prevalence estimates is likely associated with actual variations in true effect sizes rather than sampling error [ ]. This high heterogeneity reflects the significant variations between studies conducted within each year. Studies conducted in distinct regions of the same year probably had different prevalence rates, which may be due to regional approaches to antibiotic prescription, and infection control measures [ ].
The region-wise subgroup analysis reveals a crucial epidemiological finding across Pakistan’s largest cities. MRSA has a highly variable but significant impact with pooled prevalence estimates ranging from 48% (95% CI: 0.40–0.56) in Punjab to 55% (95% CI: 0.46–0.64) in Sindh. The notable geographical variation emphasizes that MRSA is highly localized public health issue. The high statistically significant heterogeneity (I² > 90%, p < 0.0001) was recorded in other major provinces, like Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (99.1%) and Islamabad Capital Territory (ICT) (I²=97.4%). This reveals a wide range of prevalence rates within various regions reported by different studies. The high incidence in several places is alarming and requires targeted investigation to validate these findings. Decentralized AMR surveillance system may result in under reporting and delayed identification of emerging regional hotspots [ ]. Ultimately, providing strong surveillance tools to provincial and district health officials is crucial to create targeted and efficient responses for preventing the covert spread of MRSA throughout Pakistan.
In this meta-analysis, there is no statistically significant evidence of publication bias, according to the results of both Egger’s linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry and Begg’s. A bias estimate of 0.2096 and a non-significant p-value of 0.8893, which are minor in comparison to its large standard error (SE = 1.5000). However, the reliability of these tests in proportion meta-analyses is limited. Therefore, the results of these tests were interpreted with caution. There are limitations to Egger’s test, especially when there are large number of studies and high heterogeneity, as in the case in this analysis a significant finding may indicate not only potential publication bias but actual clinical or methodological difference among studies [ , ]. Therefore, rather than being conclusive evidence of publication bias, the asymmetry seen here is described as evidence of small-study effects. The adjusted estimates by trim and fill analysis, suggested a modest potential impact (46.5%). The limitation of trim and fill method is it assumes that the primary source of variation in studies is due to sampling error, which are often varied due to methodological differences in primary research [ ]. The meta regression analysis showed no potential temporal trend (p = 0.3219), however the cumulative analysis revealed fluctuation in estimates over time. These fluctuations should be interpreted as observed variability rather than a confirmed temporal trend. The study of Jaglal et al. (2025) [ ] also revealed a significant variation in temporal trend of MRSA prevalence over time.

## Conclusion
The overall MRSA pooled prevalence in Pakistan is estimated to be 50%, indicating a significant public health burden. The high heterogeneity in the results, shows that prevalence changed significantly among study periods, geographic locations and patient demographics. The unstable prevalence rates over time, may cause by the emergence of particular MRSA clones and local infection control measures. The study highlights that MRSA is a common and resilient pathogen, and its stability is caused by various factors including excessive use of antibiotics and inequities in healthcare settings. These results indicated the critical need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship, enhanced worldwide surveillance and robust infection prevention strategies to reduce this persistent threat.
