## ABSTRACT

## Background and Aims
The study's goal was to assess the global prevalence of HIV/AIDS infection in transgender people and to provide light on regional variances.

## Methods
A systematic search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed (Medline), Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, Ovid, and PsycINFO, from inception until September 2023. Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q and I‐squared statistics. A meta‐analysis was performed using the random‐effects model in Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, Texas, USA). A total of 3125 articles were identified, of which 37 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta‐analysis.

## Results
The global prevalence of HIV/AIDS among transgender populations was 24% (CI: 12%–39%). Regional analysis revealed higher rates of HIV/AIDS among male‐to‐female (MTF) transgender individuals in Asia (30%, CI: 5%–54%) and Africa (35%, CI: 13%–57%) compared to other regions. In contrast, female‐to‐male (FTM) transgender individuals in Asia had a higher prevalence (23%, CI: 9%–31%) than those in the Americas (11%, CI: 3%–23%).

## Conclusion
Given the high prevalence of HIV/AIDS in the TG community worldwide and the significant variance within communities, it is critical to execute a comprehensive set of interventions to successfully prevent HIV/AIDS in transgender people.

## Introduction
The definition of transgender people varies in different parts of the world and is constantly evolving [ , ]. The sex formally assigned to human beings from birth (male and female) is based on physical characteristics [ ]. Trans people are individuals whose current identity or sexual expression differs from the sex assigned at birth [ ]. Trans people are divided into two main groups; some have male biological sex and female sexual identity, and others have female biological sex and male sexual identity [ ]. The population of trans people worldwide is unknown. Still, an estimated 25 million trans people in the world, of whom about 1.4 million live in the United States [ , , ]. A national survey of transgender people done in 2008, it was found that 90% of respondents had experienced sexual harassment, abuse, or job discrimination in their lifetime [ ]. In trans people, individual factors such as depression, substance abuse, illegal hormones and silicone injections, social factors such as lack of social support and social deprivation, and socio‐cultural factors such as limited economic opportunities, limited access to services, stigma, and discrimination make these people have less social protection and to be exposed to discrimination and social stigma. Many of these people are forced to resort to sex for survival because of low self‐esteem, depression, position abuse, biological differences and different sexual tendencies, and economic necessity [ , , , , ]. Various studies worldwide showed that 7%–69% of sexual intercourse in transgender people was unprotected [ ]. To earn their livelihood and pay for medical treatment, they have unprotected sex with many different sexual partners for money, exposing them to infectious diseases such as HIV [ ]. Sex for money, particularly in trans women, is a risk factor for transmitting HIV, mainly if unprotected sex is associated with paying more [ ]. Trans people are considered one of the most at‐risk groups for HIV owing to substance, and sexual abuse, unprotected sex, having multiple sexual partners, experiencing spousal and family violence, engaging in sexual activities, and lack of access to testing [ , ]. The prevalence of HIV in trans women was 21.6% in high‐income and 18.7% in middle‐ and low‐income countries [ ]. In total, 25%–28% of trans people in the United States are HIV‐positive [ ]. Trans people are 49 times more likely to be infected with HIV than adults in the general population worldwide [ ]. In several studies evaluating the prevalence of HIV among trans people, the proportion of HIV‐positive people ranged from 16% to 68%. Identifying the prevalence of HIV among trans people is essential to develop prevention strategies among this population and prohibiting the increasing prevalence of HIV [ ]. Of course, a few studies have been done in some parts of the world, but given their contradictory results, conducting a systematic review and meta‐analysis to estimate the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people was deemed necessary. Therefore, the aim of this study was to estimate the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender individuals.

2.

## Methods
This article was based on the standards of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyzes (PRISMA) [ ].
2.1.

## Eligibility Criteria
This study focused on cross‐sectional or retrospective studies that primarily investigated transgender individuals, specifically Male to Female (MTF) or Female to Male (FTM), and presented data on the prevalence of HIV/AIDS. The inclusion criteria required these studies to specifically report on HIV/AIDS prevalence in transgender populations. Any studies that did not provide information on HIV/AIDS prevalence in transgender individuals were excluded. Additionally, studies published in languages other than English, cohort studies, case‐control studies, clinical trials, letters to editors, and systematic reviews were also excluded. Furthermore, studies that focused on non‐transgender populations were not included.

2.2.

## Information Sources
The study employed a thorough and comprehensive search methodology, encompassing various databases such as PubMed (Medline), Embase, Ovid, and PsycINFO. The search was last updated in September 2023. Furthermore, the authors also explored gray literature sources, including UNAIDS and affiliated organizations.

2.3.

## Search Strategy
This study used a systematic review and meta‐analysis to provide an estimate of the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people. The following keywords were utilized in the search and evaluation process.
“Female to Male (FTM),” “Male to Female (MTF),” Gender Dysphonia,” “Gender Incongruence,” “Gender Identity Disorder,” “HIV,” “AIDS,” “Human Immunodeficiency Virus,” “Acquired Immunodeficiency Disorder Syndrome.”
In each electronic database, the articles were selected using Mesh and EMTREE. The search was only limited to the English language. The reporting checklist of the search strategy was based on PRISMA (the reporting checklist for the search strategy would be based on PRISMA). For gray literature, the databases related to HIV/AIDS, such as UNAIDS, the World Health Organization (WHO), and the Global AIDS Monitoring (GAM) platform, were searched. The search strategy was done and developed by two independent authors (Y. M. and M. S.), and the existing differences were resolved with the opinion of the third, more experienced researcher (G. M.). Search syntaxes are provided in File  .

2.4.

## Selection Process and Data Extraction
An Endnote library of version 8 was utilized to compile articles, remove any duplicates, and analyze titles and abstracts. In the initial stage, the primary reviewer (referred to as M. S.) conducted independent screenings of titles and abstracts. In order to ensure accuracy and consistency, 25% of the screened articles were also assessed by a secondary reviewer (identified as Y. M.), in accordance with established guidelines for stringent review procedures. Inconsistencies or disagreements between the primary and secondary reviewers were addressed with input from a tertiary reviewer (referred to as G. M.), with the aim of achieving a consensus. This methodology was employed to ensure a comprehensive and impartial process for selecting articles. In the event of any disagreements, the more experienced third reviewer (G. M.) facilitated discussions to effectively resolve them. To extract the data, a checklist was prepared with the opinion of experts according to the information gained from the articles, and then the data extraction was performed. Required information included the author's name, the year of publication, HIV/AIDS prevalence rates in transgender people, the study type, the sample size, the country or region of the publisher, the study population, the method of measuring HIV/AIDS prevalence and the age of transgender people.

2.5.

## Study Quality Assessment
Two authors evaluated the studies qualitatively, using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) checklist [ ]. This checklist assessed the quality of observational studies, mainly cross‐sectional ones. This tool evaluated each study with six items in three groups: how to select study samples, compare and analyze study groups, and measure and analyze the desired outcome. Each item was given a score of 1 if observed in the studies, and the maximum score for each survey was 9 points.

2.6.

## Data Synthesis
Prevalence in this meta‐analysis was defined as the percentage of individuals with HIV/AIDS among transgender people. To accurately calculate the pooled prevalence, the Freeman–Tukey double‐arcsine transformation was employed to stabilize variances and normalize data for proportions [ ]. The pooled prevalence was estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird random effects model. Prediction intervals were calculated to indicate the range within which future study estimates were likely to fall [ ]. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I‐squared (I
2) statistic to quantify the percentage of variation due to heterogeneity rather than chance, and the tau‐squared (τ
2) statistic was used to present the between‐study variance [ ]. Funnel plots were used to visually inspect for publication bias, with the understanding that they provided an indication rather than a definitive assessment of bias [ ]. Since publication bias could not be formally assessed with fewer than ten studies, this limitation was noted. Meta‐regression analysis was conducted to explore the influence of study‐level covariates on the pooled prevalence estimates, helping to identify sources of heterogeneity [ ]. Sensitivity analyses were also performed to test the robustness of the results, including evaluating the impact of individual studies on the overall findings using forest plots. The methods for meta‐regression and sensitivity analyses were clearly described, with careful interpretation of the results in light of these analyses [ , ].

3.

## Results
After completing the search strategy, 3125 articles in international databases and 31 papers in gray literature were found. After eliminating duplicates and screening based on article titles, remained 818 articles were entered into the screening phase according to their abstracts. In the next step, 502 articles were deleted due to the irrelevance of their abstracts and types of study with their objectives, and 316 papers were reviewed based on their full texts. After a complete review and full‐text evaluation, 37 articles were entered into the meta‐analysis (Figure  ). The general characteristics of the articles are presented in Table  .
 3.1.

## The Prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Males to Females
Twenty articles reported the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in MTF transgender people. The domain and range of the HIV/AIDS prevalence in MTF transgender people in the results of these studies ranged from 5% to 84%, with the lowest one in the research done by Brito and colleagues in 2015 in the Dominican Republic and the highest one in the study conducted by Clark and colleagues in 2017 in Peru. After combining these studies, the overall prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people changed from MTF, was 24% with a CI of 12%–39%. Heterogeneity in this study was high (99.66%) and statistically meaningful (Figure  ). The bias publication analysis was performed using the Eggers test. The results indicated the absence of diffusion bias in the studies included in the meta‐analysis (B = −3.01, SE = 4.64, p = 0.517). The meta‐regression analysis was also performed to investigate the relationship between the age variables and the article sample size with the calculated cumulative prevalence of HIV in transgender people. The results expressed that age was not meaningfully associated with the cumulative prevalence of HIV (the coefficient = 0.0012, SE = 0.021, T = −0.40, p = 0.694). The sample size also did not have a meaningful relationship with the cumulative prevalence of HIV (the coefficient = 0.013, SE = 0.073, T = 1.85, p = 0.082) (Figure  ).
 
3.2.

## The Prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Females to Males
Seven cross‐sectional studies and one retrospective study reported the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in FTM transgender individuals. The sample size in these studies was 3617 people. Four studies were conducted in the United States and others in Israel, Pakistan, Australia, and Italy. Also, one of these eight studies, excluded from the meta‐analysis because of heterogeneity in its results, reported a zero percent prevalence of HIV/AIDS in FTM transgender individuals. After combining the results of these seven studies, the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in FTM transgender individuals was 11%, with a CI of 3%–23% (Figure  ). The bias publication analysis was performed using the Eggers Test, showing diffusion bias in the studies included in the meta‐analysis (B = 10.23, SE = 4.40, p = 0.020). The meta‐regression analysis was also performed to investigate the relationship between the age variables and the article sample size with the calculated cumulative prevalence of HIV in transgender people. The results showed that age was not meaningfully associated with the cumulative prevalence of HIV (the coefficient = −0.031, SE = 0.034, T = −0.09, p = 0.929). Also, the study sample size was not meaningfully associated with the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS (the coefficient = 0.027, SE = 0.0001, T = 0.30, p = 0.777) (Figure  ).

3.3.

## The Prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Transgender Populations (Both FTM and MTF)
The prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people was reported in 10 studies without separating men and women. A total of 3497 people were assessed for HIV/AIDS in these studies, whose prevalence of HIV/AIDS ranged from 4% in the study of Weissman and colleagues to 60% in Bockting and colleagues. After combining the results of these studies, the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people was 21%, with a CI of 11%–32%. The heterogeneity rate in this estimate was 98.14% (Figure  ). The bias publication analysis was performed using the Eggers Test, representing the diffusion bias in the studies included in the meta‐analysis (B = 6.96, SE = 2.67, p = 0.009).
The meta‐regression analysis was also performed to check the relationship between the age variables and the article sample size with the calculated cumulative prevalence of HIV in transgender people. The results indicated that age was not meaningfully associated with the cumulative prevalence of HIV (the coefficient = −0.003, SE = 0.004, T = −0.84, p = 0.439). Also, the sample size did not have a meaningful relationship with the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS (the coefficient = −0.002, SE = 0.0001, T = −1.04, p = 0.328) (Figure  ).

3.4.

## The Prevalence of HIV/AIDS in LGBT Populations (Lesbians, Gays, Bisexuals, and Trans)
After the screening, four studies reported HIV prevalence in LGBT individuals without the separation of minorities. The lowest and highest prevalence were 5% with a CI of 4%–7% in the study of Gutierrez and colleagues and 26% with a CI of 23%–30% in the study of Valles and colleagues, respectively. After combining these results, the cumulative prevalence of HIV in the LGBT population was 12%, with a CI of 6%–18%. The rate of heterogeneity in this estimation was 97.74% (Figure  ). The bias publication analysis was performed using the Eggers Test. The results expressed the diffusion bias in the studies included in the meta‐analysis (B = 4.03, SE = 1.01, p = 0.005).

3.5.

## Sensitivity Analysis
The sensitivity analysis results determining the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people who had changed their gender from MTF showed that if all studies except the one of Clark and colleagues were omitted, the main result would not change so much. Still, if the survey of Clark and colleagues was considered, the result would change a little (File  ).
In determining the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people changed from FTM, Callander et al. [ ] had different results than other meta‐analyses. Therefore, as to the sensitivity analysis, the results were calculated without this study. It indicated that the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people with gender reassignment from FTM was 9%, with a CI of 2%–16% (File  ).
If the study of Bockting and colleagues was omitted, the overall result of the prevalence would be reduced relative to the general prevalence. This deletion was performed to determine the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people (MTF or FTM). Also, in assessing the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in LGBT people, if the study of Valles and colleagues was omitted, the result of the study would change (File  ).

3.6.

## Subgroup Analysis
The results of the subgroup analysis are also shown in Table  . The cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in male and female transgender people in Asia and Africa was higher than in other parts of the world. Three studies with a sample size of 1936 people reported the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in African men to women transgender people, and seven studies with a sample size of 3722 people reported the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Asian ones. The prevalence of HIV/AIDS in African MTF transgender people was 35%, with a CI of 13%–57% (Figure  ). The heterogeneity in this estimate was 98.94%, which was also meaningfully significant. Also, the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Asian MTF transgender people was 30%, with a CI of 5%–54% (Figure  ). The heterogeneity in this estimate was high and equal to 90.37%. The prevalence of HIV/AIDS in MTF transgender people also varied according to the HIV/AIDS diagnostic methods, so after combining the studies which had identified HIV/AIDS in MTF transgender people, using the ELISA method, the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS was 25% with a CI of 6%–44%.
 Moreover, nine studies used the rapid test method to identify HIV/AIDS in MTF transgender people. The cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS after combining these studies was 22%, with a CI of 11%–33% (Table  ). The cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Asian FTM transgender people was higher than that in Americans. According to the results of the subgroup analysis, the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Asian FTM transgender people was 23% with a CI of 9%–31% (Figure  ). Based on the HIV/AIDS diagnosis methods, three studies used the ELISA method to identify HIV/AIDS in FTM transgender people. After combining their results, the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS was 8% with a CI of 1%–17% (Table  ).

4.

## Discussion
The results of the present meta‐analysis expressed that the cumulative prevalence of HIV/AIDS in transgender people who had changed from MTF was higher than in those who had switched from FTM (24% and 11%, respectively). Approximately 1 million people in the United States have been identified as transgender. Transgender men and women are at the risk of HIV. The prevalence of HIV in transgender people was 9.2% (men to women 14.1% and women to men 3.2%) [ , , , , , ]. According to the WHO, the risk of HIV in MTF transgender people is ~49 times higher (up to 80 times higher in some countries) than that in the general population of adults of reproductive age (with an approximate prevalence of 19% worldwide) because this group of transgender people is more prone to risky behaviors such as unprotected sex with different sexual partners, sex trade, injectable substance, and alcohol abuse than other sexual minorities. Discrimination, social stigma, social pressures, and rejection from families in these groups are higher than those in other sexual minorities. In many societies, the male gender is more accepted than the female gender owing to cultural limitations. So, transgender people who switch from FTM have more support from families and people around them than transgender individuals who change gender from MTF. This creates a significant social and cultural strain on MTF transgender people, pushing them toward the sex trade, drug and alcohol abuse, and establishing unprotected sex in exchange for money [ , , , , , ]. The results of various studies worldwide showed that the prevalence of HIV in MTF transgender people varied between 11% and 78% [ , , , , ]. The results of a meta‐analysis study conducted on transgender women in five high‐income countries (especially the United States) indicated that the prevalence of HIV in transgender people was 22% [ , , , , ], confirming the results of the current study that the prevalence of HIV in transgender people (regardless of gender reassignment) was 21%. So far, few studies have been conducted in some parts of the world regarding the prevalence of AIDS/HIV infection in transgender people.
The first systematic review and meta‐analysis of HIV prevalence among transgender women in the United States was published by Herbst and colleagues in 2008. In this comprehensive survey, data from 13 years were collected, and the results showed that HIV infection rates were higher among African–American transgender people (MTF). Also, the prevalence of risky behaviors among MTF transgender people was more elevated. They stated that the prevalence of HIV and high‐risk behaviors among FTM transgender people was low too. Also, a previous systematic review study in the United States indicated that the highest prevalence of HIV was in black transgender women [ , , , , ].
In a study conducted by Clements and colleagues on 392 MTF and 123 FTM individuals, the prevalence of HIV in MTF and FTM subjects was 35% and 2%, respectively. Moreover, the African–American race, the history of drug injection, having multiple sexual partners, and low education level were independently associated with HIV [ , ]. The results of the present meta‐analysis also expressed that the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in MTF Asian and African transgender people was higher than in other parts of the world. On the other hand, the prevalence of HIV/AIDS in Asian FTM transgender people was higher than in American ones. The high prevalence of HIV in Asian transgender people could be attributed to limited access to HIV‐related educational, counseling, prevention, and treatment services and facilities. In addition, having different sexual contacts and many sexual partners were other factors associated with the high prevalence of HIV in transgender people living in Asian and African countries [ , , ]. Also, a cross‐sectional study in sub‐Saharan Africa showed that the chance of HIV infection in transgender women was 2.2 times higher than that in CIS‐MSM people. Trans women were more prone to HIV because of having multiple sexual partners and insufficient power to negotiate for protected sex than other trans people [ ]. Stigma and discrimination, lack of social and legal recognition of their verified gender, and deprivation of employment and educational opportunities were assigned as fundamental stimulators of the HIV risk in transgender women around the world [ ]. The present meta‐analysis showed that the MTF trans community was more at risk for HIV/AIDS. This trans community in Asia and Africa faced more sociocultural constraints, such as exclusion from families and relatives, stigma and discrimination, and more significant economic problems than the trans community in America and Europe [ ]. These social factors have led MTF trans people to engage in high‐risk sexual and asexual behaviors. High‐risk sexual behaviors such as not using condoms and other contraceptives, alcohol consumption during sex, and having sex with multiple sexual partners were more in MTF trans people in Asia and Africa than other trans people in different areas due to the above factors [ ].
Other reasons included that access to HIV/AIDS prevention and care services in Asian and African countries was more difficult than in other countries, especially developed countries. In addition, providing educational services to raise awareness of the transgender community regarding HIV/AIDS was incomplete in Asian and African countries. As a result, their information and knowledge on how HIV/AIDS is transmitted in these countries were less than transgender people in developed countries. Therefore, transgenders in these countries are much more vulnerable to HIV/AIDS [ , ].
It is essential to acknowledge the limitations of this study. The meta‐analysis revealed a significant amount of unexplained heterogeneity, even after considering geographic regions and diagnostic methods for HIV/AIDS. This suggests that additional unmeasured factors may be contributing to the observed differences in HIV/AIDS prevalence across the studies. To better understand these disparities, future research should prioritize the collection and reporting of a broader range of demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic factors that could influence HIV/AIDS prevalence. Consistent documentation of these factors in primary studies will enhance the depth of subgroup analyses and improve the interpretability of the findings. Furthermore, it is recommended that primary studies adhere to standardized reporting guidelines, such as Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE), to ensure thorough reporting of potential confounders and methodological details. Adopting such practices will not only improve the quality and comparability of future meta‐analyses but also contribute to more informed public health interventions and policies aimed at reducing HIV/AIDS transmission among transgender populations.

5.

## Conclusion
Finally, the study emphasizes the significance of implementing a comprehensive set of interventions for effectively preventing HIV/AIDS among transgender people. These interventions include improving access to high‐quality and affordable HIV prevention, control, and treatment services, encouraging community participation and education, verifying transgender people's identities, and educating and training healthcare professionals and providers about transgender identity and sexual orientation. Implementing these interventions can greatly lower the risk of HIV/AIDS in transgender communities, resulting in better overall health and well‐being for this disenfranchised community.
