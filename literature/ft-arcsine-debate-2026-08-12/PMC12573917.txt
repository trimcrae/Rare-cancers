## Abstract

## Objective
Following an earthquake, differences in antibiotic resistance of pathogens may arise due to environmental changes, human activities, and the inherent characteristics of pathogens. To systematically analyze the incidence and resistance profiles of pathogens following earthquakes, aiming to provide a basis for the rational use of antimicrobial agents aimed at drug-resistent pathogens in earthquake-affected patients.

## Methods
A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CNKI, and Wanfang databases from January 1, 2002, to May 10, 2025. Data were extracted on earthquake locations, mean age of patients, number of isolated strains, and types of strains. Two researchers independently conducted the literature search and data extraction. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the JBI Case Series Appraisal Tool. Data analysis was performed using Stata 17.0 software to calculate the incidence rates of pathogens and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), as well as the resistance rates and their 95% CIs for the five most prevalent pathogens.

## Results
Sixteen single-arm studies involving 2974 pathogen strains were included. The meta-analysis revealed that the five most prevalent pathogens were Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter cloacae, and Staphylococcus aureus, with detection rates of 14.5% [95% CI (10.8%, 18.9%)], 12.1% [95% CI (6.4%, 19.7%)], 10.5% [95% CI (7.6%, 13.8%)], 9.6% [95% CI (4.6%, 16.3%)], and 7.4% [95% CI (5.2%, 9.9%)] respectively.The highest resistance rates were found against Imipenem in Escherichia coli (79.8% [95% CI (29.6%, 99.5%)]), Cefazolin in Acinetobacter baumannii (85.1% [95% CI (30.0%, 95.9%)]), Piperacillin in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (45.5% [95% CI (25.1%, 66.2%)]), Cefazolin in Enterobacter cloacae (85.8% [95% CI (30.0%, 95.5%)]), and Ceftriaxone in Staphylococcus aureus(87.9% [95% CI (55.0%, 99.9%)]).

## Conclusion
This study shows that there is limited research on the pathogens and their drug resistance after the earthquake disaster, and the data is concentrated in one region. The distribution of pathogens in earthquake-affected patients shows specific patterns, with a higher incidence and common resistance observed in Gram-negative rods. These pathogens exhibited the lowest resistance to Imipenem/Cilastatin and were less likely to develop resistance to quinolone antibiotics such as Netilmicin and Amikacin.

## Introduction
Natural disasters have long been precursors to public health crises, including those related to infectious diseases [ ]. Tsunamis, floods, and earthquakes expose humans to microbes that they would not normally encounter, increasing the risk of wound infections and bloodborne diseases.
Among all natural disasters, earthquakes occur frequently and are highly destructive. For instance, the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake affected over 46 million people, with an estimated 87,000 fatalities [ ].
Beyond the physical damage and casualties caused by collapsed structures, one of the primary causes of mortality following earthquakes is the outbreak of infectious diseases [ ]. Post-earthquake infections, predominantly wound infections, have mortality rates ranging from 19.0–35.0% [ ]. Other common infections include acute respiratory infections, enteritis, and dermatological conditions, typically manifesting within two weeks after the disaster [ ].
Earthquake victims are particularly susceptible to bacterial infections due to deep wounds, necrotic tissue decay, and poor blood supply to injuries, compounded by unsanitary conditions in the post-disaster environment. The influx of patients and the high turnover in emergency shelters or hospitals further complicate the situation [ ]. Many patients require prolonged bed rest, significantly increasing the likelihood of various infections. Moreover, the emergency nature of earthquakes often limits medical facilities’ ability to promptly identify pathogens in wounds, leading to empirical treatment. The inappropriate use of antibiotics frequently results in treatment failures, either due to ineffectiveness against the pathogen or subtherapeutic levels of antibiotics, leading to extended hospital stays, secondary infections with multi-drug resistant organisms, and increased mortality among earthquake victims [ ].
In recent years, with frequent earthquakes in China, there has been growing recognition of the importance of identifying disaster-related microbes and preventing infections for effective medical intervention. To reduce the incidence of earthquake-related traumatic infections and improve recovery rates, extensive research has been conducted on open wound bacterial cultures and antibiotic susceptibility testing. However, the results are inconsistent, and even within the same region, findings can vary between earthquakes [ ]. Thus, this study aims to conduct a meta-analysis on the detection rates of pathogens in post-earthquake traumatic wounds, establish a spectrum of these pathogens, and analyze the antibiotic resistance rates of the most frequently detected pathogens. This will provide a scientific basis for the treatment and early warning of traumatic infections in disaster scenarios.

## Materials and methods 

## Literature search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted using both computerized databases and manual retrieval to identify relevant articles up to May 10, 2025. The databases searched included PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), and Wanfang Data. Search terms were used in both English and Chinese to maximize the retrieval of pertinent studies. The search terms included: “Earthquakes,” “earthquake,” “Drug Resistance,” “Resistance, Drug,” “Bacteria,” and “Eubacteria.” The search strategy combined both MeSH terms and free-text terms to ensure comprehensive coverage of the literature.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

## Inclusion criteria
Studies that analyze the distribution of pathogens or their resistance to drugs following earthquakes. The article can extract data on pathogens and drug resistance related to human patients, injured individuals, and rescue personnel in earthquake disaster areas. For studies in the same hospital in the same area, only the one with the largest sample size was retained.

## Exclusion criteria
Studies with incomplete pathogen or resistance data; duplicate data published in different journals by the same research team; and studies for which antibiotic susceptibility testing methods did not meet standards; duplicate publications, news articles, reports, studies available only in abstract form, publications from which data cannot be extracted, conference abstracts, reviews, commentaries, and case reports.

## Literature screening and data extraction
Two researchers independently screened the literature and extracted data according to the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through consultation with a third researcher. Extracted data included: titles of the included studies, first author, year of publication, country, antibiotic susceptibility test methods, names of pathogens detected, quantities of pathogens detected, tested drugs, and their resistance rates.

## Assessment of literature quality
The quality of the included studies was assessed using the quasi-experimental study appraisal tool from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) of Evidence-Based Healthcare in Australia [ ]. This tool includes ten criteria:
 Are there clear criteria for inclusion of cases?
Are the methods for identifying the disease or health issue standard and reliable?
Are the interventions and other measures provided to the groups being compared other than the intervention of interest applied in a comparable manner?
Is the inclusion of subjects into the study cohort consistent?
Is the inclusion of subjects comprehensive within the study cohort?
Is demographic information of the study population clearly reported?
Are the outcome measures for each group measured in the same way?
Is clinical information of the study subjects clearly reported?
Is geographical/socio-demographic information of the cases clearly reported?
Are the statistical analysis methods appropriate?
Each criterion is evaluated with a response of ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’, or ‘not applicable’.

## Statistical methods
This study involved meta-analysis of single-arm studies using Stata 17.0. Given that all included studies reported pathogen incidence rates greater than 70%, traditional meta-analytical methods like the inverse variance method can be problematic when rates approach 0 or 1. To address this, the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to stabilize variances. This transformation was used to calculate the pooled incidence rates and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from transformed proportions (tp). The final incidence rates (p) and their 95% CIs were then back-transformed from the transformed rates.
Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and the Chi-squared test. If P > 0.10 and I2 < 50%, indicating low heterogeneity, a fixed-effect model was employed for the meta-analysis. Conversely, if P ≤ 0.10 and I2 ≥ 50%, indicating significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used to aggregate the effect sizes.

## Results

## Systematic review and characteristics of included studies
A total of 692 records were retrieved from databases in both English and Chinese. Among them, 12 studies were retrieved by forward and backward citations. At the same time, 14 studies conducted in the same hospital were deleted. After screening abstracts for duplicates and irrelevant articles, 16 studies were ultimately included in the analysis [ , – ] (Fig. ). Of these, 13 were in Chinese and 3 in English.
 The studies conducted microbial assessments on various samples from earthquake victims, including wound swabs, respiratory secretions, necrotic tissue, blood, urine, and feces, yielding a total of 2974 bacterial isolates. In this study, the JBI method was conducted to evaluate the quality of the literature, and the quality of the included literature was above 50 points. Of the 16 studies included in the review, all met the criteria for items 1, 2, 3, 9, and 10. Ten studies met the criteria for item 4, two studies satisfied item 5, and 8 studies consistently met the criteria for item 6. ## Distribution of pathogens in earthquake victims
Meta-analysis was conducted using Stata software to assess the detection rates of pathogens in earthquake victims. The results of the pathogen detection rates are summarized in Table . The table also presents the heterogeneity of the studies included in the analysis. For cases where heterogeneity exceeded 50%, a random-effects model was utilized to account for the variability across studies. Conversely, a fixed-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was less than 50%.
 The meta-analysis identified the five most frequently detected pathogens among earthquake victims. The top five pathogens and their respective detection rates are as follows: Escherichia coli: 14.5% [95% CI (10.8%, 18.9%)], Acinetobacter baumannii: 12.1% [95% CI (6.4%, 19.7%)], Pseudomonas aeruginosa: 10.5% [95% CI (7.6%, 13.8%)], Enterobacter cloacae: 9.6% [95% CI (4.6%, 16.3%)], Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive): 7.4% [95% CI (5.2%, 9.9%)]. Among these, Staphylococcus aureus is the only Gram-positive bacterium, while Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter cloacae are Gram-negative bacteria.

## Analysis of pathogen distribution and resistance in earthquake victims
The analysis focused on the antibiotic resistance profiles of the top five detected pathogens, with detailed results presented in Tables , , , and . Specifically, Escherichia coli showed the highest resistance rates. The resistance to Imipenem was notably severe, with an overall resistance rate of 79.8% [95% CI (29.6%, 99.5%)]. This was followed by resistance to Piperacillin at 70.8% [95% CI (28.7%, 98.2%)], Cefazolin at 65.2% [95% CI (39.0%, 87.2%)], Tetracycline at 59.9% [95% CI (0.2%, 97.8%)], and Meropenem at 59.4% [95% CI (0.0%, 97.2%)]. The lowest resistance rate for Escherichia coli was against Nitrofurantoin, with an overall rate of 12.1% [95% CI (1.9%, 28.7%)], followed by Ceftriaxone at 19.3% [95% CI (0.7%, 53.5%)] and Aztreonam at 21.7% [95% CI (3.0%, 51.5%)].
 For pathogens with heterogeneity above 50%, as detailed in Table , a random-effects model was employed to analyze the data. Conversely, a fixed-effects model was used when heterogeneity was below 50%.
Acinetobacter baumannii demonstrated significant antibiotic resistance, with the most severe resistance observed against Cefazolin, exhibiting a total resistance rate of 85.1% [95% CI (30.0%, 95.9%)]. Other notable resistance rates include Ceftriaxone at 62.3% [95% CI (7.9%, 99.9%)], Meropenem at 59.4% [95% CI (23.4%, 90.1%)], Ceftazidime at 58.9% [95% CI (18.5%, 93.1%)], and Piperacillin at 57.4% [95% CI (6.9%, 98.7%)]. The lowest resistance rate was found against Cefoperazone/Sulbactam at only 19.7% [95% CI (3.0%, 79.4%)], followed by Ciprofloxacin at 31.0% [95% CI (1.3%, 76.1%)] and Ampicillin/Sulbactam alone at 32.8% [95% CI (0.1%, 90.4%)].
Given the heterogeneity observed in these findings, as detailed in Table , a random-effects model was applied for analyses with heterogeneity greater than 50%, whereas a fixed-effects model was used otherwise.
Pseudomonas aeruginosa demonstrated significant resistance across various antibiotics. The most pronounced resistance was observed against Piperacillin, with an overall resistance rate of 45.5% [95% CI (25.1%, 66.2%)]. Other antibiotics with high resistance rates included Piperacillin/Tazobactam at 42.0% [95% CI (14.8%, 71.7%)], Imipenem at 37.6% [95% CI (1.9%, 85.5%)], Levofloxacin at 35.7% [95% CI (14.5%, 60.9%)], and Nitrofurantoin at 35.2% [95% CI (23.8%, 95.5%)]. Conversely, the lowest resistance were observed against Cefoxitin, Cefuroxime sodium and Ampicillin, both at 0.5% [95% CI (0.2%, 3.4%)].
As detailed in Table , heterogeneity analysis indicated that for pathogens with heterogeneity greater than 50%, a random-effects model was applied, whereas a fixed-effects model was used for lesser heterogeneity, ensuring the reliability and validity of the analysis outcomes.
Enterobacter cloacae exhibited considerable antibiotic resistance, with the highest resistance observed against Cefazolin, showing a total resistance rate of 85.8% [95% CI (30.0%, 95.5%)]. Notably high resistance was also found to Ciprofloxacin at 82.9% [95% CI (51.0%, 99.4%)], Levofloxacin at 64.3% [95% CI (30.5%, 91.8%)], Piperacillin at 63.8% [95% CI (20.9%, 96.4%)], and Ceftriaxone at 61.8% [95% CI (27.8%, 90.1%)]. The lowest resistance rates was observed against Tetracycline at 17.8% [95% CI (0.9%, 49.5%)], followed by Meropenem at 31.4% [95% CI (18.1%, 99.6%)] and Ampicillin at 31.9% [95% CI (2.9%, 96.0%)].
Due to the high heterogeneity as documented in Table , a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis of pathogens with heterogeneity exceeding 50%. Conversely, a fixed-effects model was applied where heterogeneity was below this threshold.
Staphylococcus aureus showed the highest resistance rates to Ceftriaxone, with an overall rate of 87.9% [95% CI (55.0%, 99.9%)]. This was followed closely by resistance to Linezolid at 69.4% [95% CI (4.3%, 96.3%)], Cefotaxime at 69.4% [95% CI (43.0%, 90.6%)], Clindamycin at 67.1% [95% CI (55.4%, 77.8%)], and Ampicillin at 65.7% [95% CI (10.5%, 100.0%)]. The lowest observed resistance was against Tetracycline at 23.0% [95% CI (6.4%, 46.0%)], followed by Levofloxacin at 25.1% [95% CI (15.2%, 36.6%)] and Gatifloxacin at 28.2% [95% CI (2.4%, 67.6%)].

## Discussion
To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to systematically assess pathogen distributions and resistance profiles of frequently detected pathogens following earthquake-induced injuries, providing evidence-based support for managing post-disaster traumatic infections.

The pathogens most commonly identified in post-earthquake infections include Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter cloacae, and Staphylococcus aureus, with the first four being Gram-negative rods.
For Escherichia coli, high resistance rates exceeding 65% were observed for Imipenem, Piperacillin, and Cefazolin, whereas lower resistance rates below 25% were noted for Aztreonam, Ceftriaxone, and Nitrofurantoin.
Acinetobacter baumannii demonstrated over 60% resistance to Cefazolin and Ceftriaxone, with resistance below 35% to Ampicillin/Sulbactam, Ciprofloxacin, and Cefoperazone/Sulbactam.
Pseudomonas aeruginosa showed more than 40% resistance to Piperacillin, and Piperacillin/Tazobactam, but less than 1.0% to Ampicillin, Cefuroxime sodium, and Cefoxitin.
Resistance in Enterobacter cloacae exceeded 80% for Cefazolin and Ciprofloxacin, but was less than 20% for Tetracycline.
Staphylococcus aureus exhibited over 85% resistance to Ceftriaxone, but below 25% for Tetracycline.

Our research findings have revealed that several pathogens frequently detected post - earthquake, namely Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter cloacae, and Staphylococcus aureus, all exhibit high rates of drug resistance. This situation is likely to have far-reaching impacts on the health of disaster-affected patients, hospital infection control, and public health. Drug-resistant bacteria tend to be insensitive to conventional antibiotics, resulting in poor therapeutic outcomes [ , ]. They are more prone to cause wound deterioration and even systemic infections. Moreover, they can negatively affect the recovery of function in injured areas and increase the risk of disability. For instance, Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections can lead to local tissue necrosis at wound sites and may even induce systemic inflammatory response syndrome, which can be life-threatening [ ]. Additionally, after an earthquake, there is a surge in the number of patients in hospitals [ ]. This not only puts a strain on medical resources but also increases the risk of spreading drug-resistant bacteria, thereby making prevention and control more challenging.
Our analysis reveals significant heterogeneity in antibiotic resistance among the same species, such as the heterogeneity index for Escherichia coli against Imipenem reaching 0.9502 [ ]. This indicates that pathogens found in hospital environments related to earthquakes consistently exhibit multidrug resistance, yet their resistance profiles vary. Pathogens associated with post-earthquake infections are complex, often involving opportunistic and unusual bacterial infections that increase with severe injuries [ ]. Gram-negative rods are prevalent in external environments like water and soil and in the human body, undergoing an adaptation, colonization, and proliferation process lasting 6 to 12 h, consistent with our findings of these bacteria being most commonly detected [ ]. Due to difficult rescue conditions, many victims trapped under rubble for more than six hours, and wounds exposed directly to soil, create conducive conditions for pathogenic infections and antibiotic resistance. Initial limited medical facilities in disaster areas prevent adequate microbiological culturing and sensitivity testing, often leading to the empirical use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, which may further contribute to the emergence of resistant strains [ , ].
Therefore, we need some measures to enhance antibiotic stewardship and infection control in the post-earthquake settings. In terms of antibiotic stewardship, it’s essential to develop clear antibiotic usage guidelines based on the common infection types and pathogen spectrum after an earthquake. This can guide healthcare workers to select and use antibiotics properly, thus avoiding misuse and overuse. Meanwhile, regular surveillance of antibiotic resistance among pathogens in the post-earthquake environment is a must. It can reveal the development trend and prevalence of antibiotic resistance, offering a basis for adjusting antibiotic-use strategies [ ]. When it comes to infection control, priority should be given to clearing debris and rubbish in the disaster area, eliminating stagnant sewage water, ensuring drinking water safety, and providing adequate sanitary facilities. These actions can reduce the breeding of pathogens and interrupt their transmission routes, lowering the risk of infectious diseases. For trauma patients affected by the earthquake, debridement should be done promptly and thoroughly. Strict implementation of disinfection, isolation, and hand hygiene measures in hospitals is crucial, along with isolating patients with invasive infections in separate wards when possible, and using personal protective equipment during bedside dressings to prevent nosocomial infections.
China is mainly located in the Eurasian seismic belt and the circum-Pacific seismic belt, and the internal fault activity of the Chinese mainland is more active, so the seismic activity in China is more active, and therefore there are more studies on earthquakes in China. Despite the valuable insights from this study, limitations exist. Firstly, the inclusion of 16 studies stemming from six different earthquakes primarily in Asia may not reflect conditions found elsewhere due to variations in environment, healthcare quality, and ethnic differences. Secondly, methodological differences across studies and variability in pathogen detection and resistance testing techniques among medical institutions might affect the consistency and comparability of results. Lastly, the absence of antibiotic sensitivity data from some regions and potential changes in antibiotic usage patterns, which vary over time and location, might not fully reflect the actual resistance profiles of some pathogens. Future research should include more geographic areas to increase the data’s generality and applicability and long-term studies to monitor the dynamic changes and trends in pathogen resistance following earthquakes.

## Conclusion
Our results suggest that Gram-negative bacteria are more resistant to pathogens often detected after earthquakes, with Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter claus, and Staphylococcus aureus all showing high resistance rates. These pathogens have low resistance to Imipenem/Cilastatin and are unlikely to become resistant to quinolone antibiotics such as Netimicin and Amikacin. However, we included a limited number of studies that may not reflect the situation in other regions, so long-term studies are needed to broaden the scope of the data.
