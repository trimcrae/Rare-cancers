## Abstract

## Background
Adherence to 24-h movement guidelines that include physical activity (PA), sedentary behaviour (SB), and sleep is linked to improved health outcomes, yet its impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in older adults remains underexplored.

## Objective
This study investigated the relationship between adherence to guidelines for 24-h movement behaviours and HRQoL in older adults.

## Methods
A systematic search of four databases was conducted to identify studies published from the date of inception to January 2025. The Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies was used to assess the risk of bias among the included studies. Random-effects models were used to conduct a meta-analysis involving the extracted proportions and odds ratios (OR) from each study. The Freeman–Tukey transformation was applied to stabilise the variance of proportional data before meta-analysis, and pooled proportions were back-transformed to their original scale for reporting. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Publication bias was evaluated using Begg’s test. A sensitivity analysis was conducted, which included changing the random-effects model to a fixed-effects model and adopting a leave-one-out analysis.

## Results
Twenty-five cross-sectional articles were included, covering a total of 170,670 older adults aged ≥ 60 years. We found that 24.3% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 22.1%–26.5%, p < 0.01) of older adults adhered to at least one movement guideline, among whom 23.0% (95% CI: 20.8%–25.2%, p < 0.01, n = 18) adhered to PA guidelines, 18.5% (95% CI: 15.3%–21.7%, p < 0.01, n = 7) to SB guidelines, and 40.0% (95% CI: 37.5%–42.5%, p < 0.01, n = 7) to sleep guidelines. Our meta-analysis suggests that compared with non-compliance, adherence to at least one 24-h movement guideline was associated with higher HRQoL among older adults (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.44–1.89, p < 0.01, I^2 = 95.5%, n = 15). Small-study effects were examined with Begg’s test; all subgroup analyses of individual guidelines were underpowered due to a small number of included studies (n < 10: PA n = 7, SB n = 5 and SL n = 4).

## Conclusion
Adherence to at least one 24-h movement guideline is associated with better HRQoL in older adults. However, the lack of available research—primarily due to an insufficient number of studies reporting data on multi-component adherence—limits our ability to conduct further analyses on the association between adherence to a combination of two to three movement guidelines and HRQoL.

## Introduction
Health-related quality of life (HRQoL), as a core indicator measuring an individual’s health status and life satisfaction, has long been a focus in the global public health field—it not only reflects the integrity of physical functions but also covers multidimensional aspects such as psychological state and social participation, directly embodying the practical impact of health on daily life [ ]. With the acceleration of the global aging wave, this focus is gradually shifting to the elderly population:According to data from the United Nations Population Division, the proportion of people aged 60 and above worldwide will exceed 20% by 2025 [ ]. Against this backdrop, safeguarding the health of older adults and enhancing their HRQoL has become a central priority in public health policies across nations.
From the perspective of older adults’ health status, the aging process brings about a series of health challenges that directly threaten the HRQoL of older adults. Data show that approximately 50% of community-dwelling older people experience physical decline and chronic illnesses as they age, with mental health issues like depression and loneliness often co-occurring [ ]. These conditions severely degrade HRQoL through multiple pathways: for instance, physical decline limits older adults’ ability to perform activities of daily living and persistent discomfort caused by chronic diseases reduces their life comfort—all of which may be linked to decreased quality of life [ ].
Specific to the behavioral status of older adults: only 25% of adults aged 60 years and older meet the minimum daily PA requirements set by the World Health Organization (WHO) [ ]. This indicates that the majority of the elderly are at risk of muscle atrophy and decline in heart and lung function - which will ultimately indirectly reduce their quality of life. For SB, a systematic review of 349,698 participants found older adults spend an average of 9.4 hours daily in sedentary states (65%–80% of waking hours) [ ]; this prolonged SB links to metabolic abnormalities, higher cardiovascular disease risk, reduced social interaction, and increased loneliness, impairing HRQoL physiologically and psychologically [ , ]. Regarding sleep, older adults with sleep duration outside the 7–8-hour/night range show significantly decreased cognitive function and a 30% higher risk of fatal coronary heart disease, with such decline and disease distress further undermining daily living ability and life satisfaction, leading to reduced HRQoL [ ].
It is important to note that PA, SB, and sleep are inherently time-constrained within a 24-hour cycle—more time spent on one behavior inevitably reduces time available for the other two. This critical interaction has been overlooked in traditional single-behavior research [ ]. Thus, the 24-hour movement paradigm—integrating time-use research, compositional data analysis, and scholars including Buman’s integration theory—examines these behaviors as a holistic system to assess health associations [ ]. This study takes this theory as the core framework and combines it with the WHO 2020 Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour (the guidelines recommend that older adults aged 65 years and older complete at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise per week, limit daily sedentary time to less than 8 hours, and maintain 7–8 hours of quality sleep) as the joint standard for determining older adults’ adherence to 24-hour movement behaviours [ ].
As age increases, maintaining HRQoL becomes more challenging for older adults, with 24-hour activity patterns may play a crucial role in being associated with their HRQoL outcomes [ , ]. While individual associations between PA, SB, sleep, and HRQoL are well-documented [ – ], few studies have explored how comprehensive adherence to these three behaviors can provide information for the design of health-related quality of life interventions [ – ]. Therefore, this study employed the meta-analysis method, aiming to unify the inconsistent definitions of comprehensive compliance across various studies, quantify the overall proportion of each component of the World Health Organization 24-hour guidelines for the elderly, and calculate the combined odds ratio (OR) for the association between comprehensive compliance and health-related quality of life. The specific objective of this study is to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to: 1) estimate pooled adherence prevalence to 0/1/2/3 WHO 24-hour guideline components among adults aged ≥60; 2) quantify pooled OR (95% CI) for combined adherence-HRQoL associations.

## Methods
This study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines, and its protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251177503). The PRISMA checklist is included in Appendix .

## Search strategy
Four databases (Medline, PubMed, Proquest, and Web of Science) were used to search for studies published from inception to January 2025. The following keywords were used: (1) population-related (i.e., elderly OR ‘elderly people’ OR ‘old people’ OR ‘older adults’); (2) behaviour-related (i.e., sleep OR sleep duration OR sleep time OR sedentary behaviour OR sedentary OR sedentary time OR screen time OR physical activity OR light physical activity OR moderate physical activity OR exercise OR moderate to vigorous physical activity OR PA OR MVPA OR LPA OR SB OR 24 hours movement recommendation OR 24hrs movement recommendation OR 24-h movement recommendation OR 24 hours movement guidelines OR 24hrs movement guidelines OR 24-h movement guidelines OR 24-HMG); and (3) HRQoL-related (i.e., health OR quality of life OR QoL OR HRQoL OR HRQL ‘Health Status’ OR ‘Value of Life’ OR value of life OR life quality).The detailed search strategy can be found in Appendix 2.

## Study selection
The inclusion criteria for studies were: (1) a study population with a mean age of 60 years or older [ ]; (2) reporting on at least one 24-hour movement guideline behaviour; (3) reporting on at least one measurement of individuals’ health status and life satisfaction (as components of HRQoL); and (4) published in an English peer-reviewed journal. Review articles and on-going studies were excluded. After duplicates were deleted, title, abstract, and full-text screenings were conducted according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The reference lists of the included studies and relevant reviews were also manually checked to identify additional studies. WT and ZZ selected articles in parallel. YGS made the final decisions when there were any disagreements.

## Risk of bias assessment
This study utilised the Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies scoring scale to assess the quality of the included publications [ ]. This assessment is mainly divided into three dimensions: reporting quality, design quality, and risk of bias, covering a total of 20 items. Referring to the scoring criteria of the AXIS tool proposed by Downes et al. (2016) [ ], this study scores each item of the tool on a scale of 0 to 1. A "Yes" response (indicating compliance with methodological standards or clear reporting) earns 1 point, while a "No" response (indicating non-compliance with standards) or "Unclear" response (indicating ambiguous reporting or insufficient information) earns 0 points. A score of 16–20 indicated high research quality with rigorous and standardised methodologies; 11–15 indicated moderate quality, with some minor issues or areas needing further improvement; and 0–10 suggested low research quality, possibly indicating significant methodological flaws or incomplete reporting. Researchers LL and WJQ independently reviewed each included study and scored every item of the AXIS tool in accordance with the aforementioned uniform criteria. In case of any discrepancies in their scores, WAW made the final decision.

## Data extraction
Three researchers (WT, YPY, and WJQ) randomly assigned the included articles and extracted the following relevant information from each study: names of authors; date of publication; country; study design; characteristics of the study population (number and age); instruments used to measure quality of life, PA, SB, sleep, and outcome indicators; and the number of people meeting the three behaviours recommended in the 24-hour activity behavioural guidelines. For each study, researcher TW extracted the data, and the other two researchers cross-checked the existing data. In case of any disagreement, WAW made the final decision. In addition, we have pre-defined the analysis unit rules, including setting an effect size for each queue/cycle; giving priority to using the adjusted effect size rather than the unadjusted one; and for multi-outcome studies, extracting only the overall health-related quality of life data; for multi-behavior studies, marking the effect size of specific behaviors.

## Data analysis
The meta-analysis of the included studies was performed using Stata version 18.0. To assess the compliance rate, a random-effects model was used to conduct a meta-analysis of proportions (the ratio of individuals meeting each movement guideline to the total sample size in each study) that estimated the adherence of older adults to the 24-hour movement guidelines.Given that proportional data are inherently susceptible to variance instability the Freeman–Tukey transformation was applied to stabilise the variance of these proportional data points prior to analysis. For associations between meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines and HRQoL, given that the measures and scales for HRQoL in different studies varied, the odds ratio (OR) was chosen as the combined effect size for this study. The I2 statistic was applied to test the heterogeneity of the included studies, with an I2 < 25% indicating low heterogeneity, 25–50% indicating moderate heterogeneity, and > 50% suggesting high heterogeneity [ ]. For publication bias assessment, Begg’s test was planned to evaluate potential small-study effects [ ], based on the number of included studies for specific behavioural dimensions. Finally, to assess the robustness of the pooled results, two complementary sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, a leave-one-out analysis was performed: each included study was sequentially excluded to evaluate the impact of individual studies on the statistical significance of the overall pooled effect, and studies that altered the statistical significance of the overall results were defined as sensitive studies. Second, the random-effects model (used in the primary analysis) was switched to a fixed-effects model for re-analysis.
Grading the evidence
This study adopted the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) framework to evaluate the overall quality of the outcome evidence in two core research directions [ – ]. The quality grading was independently completed by two researchers (TTZ and LC). An expert (YGS) reviewed and arbitrated any discrepancies until a consensus was reached [ ].

## Results

## Literature search
A systematic database search retrieved 22,560 studies. After removing 7,579 duplicate records, 14,981 studies remained. Following a review of the titles and abstracts, 14,897 irrelevant studies were excluded, leaving 66 studies for full-text review. Ultimately, 25 studies were included in this review according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Figure provides a flowchart documenting the detailed literature search.
 

## Study characteristics
The main characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table . The 25 studies were published between 2008 and 2025, with a notable concentration in recent years (i.e., 17 studies from 2020 to 2025) [ – ]. Geographically, the studies spanned 13 countries, including South Korea (n = 4), China (n = 4), the United States of America (n = 3), Brazil (n = 3), Poland (n = 2), and one study each from Sweden, Croatia, Japan, Greece, Portugal, Spain, India, and Australia.
 All studies were cross-sectional. In this review, the HRQoL assessments involved the use of the following. The EuroQol 5-Dimension Questionnaire (EQ-5D) was utilised in eight studies, the Short Form Health Survey was employed in eight studies (five using SF-36, two using SF-12, and one using SF-8), and the WHO Quality of Life–Brief Form was used in four studies. Additionally, one study applied a mental and physical health measurement questionnaire, while four studies relied on participants’ self-reported responses in a question-and-answer format. Among the included studies, 24-hour movement behaviours were measured using accelerometers in three studies, telephone interviews in one study, and participants’ self-reported responses in the remaining 21 studies.

## Participant characteristics
The combined study population of the 25 included studies totalled 170,670 older adults. Among the 25 studies, 10 involved 47,524 Asian older adults, while the remaining 15 studies comprised 123,146 non-Asian older adults. Among the 25 studies, three (combined n = 652) specifically reported the HRQoL of older adults with chronic diseases, while 21 studies (combined n = 170,018) analysed HRQoL among older adults with and without chronic conditions.In this study, all the ‘elderly’ individuals included in the analysis were those aged 60 years and above. There are 10 studies focusing exclusively on participants aged 60 years and above, with a total sample size of 41,063. Among these, 2 studies further narrow their focus to the 60–80 years age range, involving 4,233 individuals.Separately, 10 studies target only participants aged 65 years and above, with a total sample size of 125,154. One of these studies specifically includes those aged 65–74 years, with a sample size of 1,580.Additionally, 5 independent studies involve only participants aged 70 years and above, with a total sample size of 4,463.

## Quality assessment and certainty of evidence
Table shows the results of the methodological quality assessment. After the assessment, 76.0% of the studies were categorised as high quality and 24.0% as medium quality. All the included studies met the requirements of the study report in terms of the purpose of the study (item 1), the fit of the study design (item 2), and the definition of the target reference population (item 4). Regarding the description of the sample size (item 3), 76.0% of the studies were able to clearly describe the sample size, while 24.0% of the studies had unclear descriptions. Only 24.0% of the studies provided measures for handling non-responders (item 7), indicating a general lack of addressing sample integrity among the included studies. Among the 25 studies, 92.0% met the criteria for the key items related to variables, such as appropriateness of measurement of risk factors and outcome variables (item 8), accuracy of measurement (item 9), and reproducibility of methodology (item 10), indicating that the quality of variable treatment and methodology elaboration was high. Respectively, 84.0% and 92.% of the studies met the criteria for non-response bias (item 12) and description of non-respondents’ information (item 13), indicating that sample quality control needed to be improved.
 The overall degree of certainty of each result and its detailed evidence can be found in Appendix 3. The grading of the certainty of the evidence was generally very low. The main reason for downgrading the evidence was imprecision in outcome estimates: subgroup analyses of individual guidelines (PA k=7, SB k=5, SL k=4) had low statistical power due to few included studies, and 95% prediction intervals for PA (0.93–3.87) and sleep (0.87–4.44) adherence-HRQoL ORs crossed the null value (1.0), creating uncertainty about association consistency.

## Synthesis of results of the meta-analysis
This study identified 25 articles reporting on adherence to at least one movement behaviour guideline. Among these, two articles reported adherence to any two movement behaviour guidelines, and one article reported adherence to all three movement behaviour guidelines. Among the articles reporting adherence to at least one movement behaviour guideline, 18, nine, and seven considered the PA guideline, SB guideline, and sleep guideline, respectively. After extracting the data from the 25 articles included in this systematic review and obtaining the relevant data from the authors, only the data from 13 articles could be used for the meta-analysis. The remaining 12 articles did not provide the effect size of 'the association between adherence and HRQoL' as they only reported the adherence rates of 24-hour physical activity (PA, SB, SL) for the elderly. These 13 articles included 6 studies on physical activity, 5 studies on sleep behavior, and 3 studies on sleep quality [ – ].

## Compliance proportion
Across the included studies, 24.3% of older adults met at least one of the 24-hour behavioural activity movement guidelines (95% confidence interval [CI]: 22.1%–26.5%). Among those meeting any movement guideline, 23.0% adhered to the PA guideline (95% CI: 20.8%–25.2%, n=18), 18.5% complied with the SB guideline (95% CI: 15.3%–21.7%, n=7), and 40.0% met the sleep guideline(95% CI: 37.5%–42.5%, n=7). ## Associations between meeting 24-hour movement guidelines and HRQoL
Thirteen studies examining the association between adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines and HRQoL were included in the meta-analysis. Twelve studies were excluded due to a failure to extract effect sizes. Due to the limited number of included studies, meta-analyses were only conducted for studies meeting the criteria for PA, SB, sleep, or any one of these factors. No meta-analyses were performed to examine the association between meeting any combination of two or three movement guidelines and HRQoL.
Compared with non-compliance, compliance with moderate to vigorous PA guidelines was associated with a significantly higher HRQoL (OR = 1.90, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.42–2.55, 95% prediction interval [PI]: 0.93–3.87, p < 0.001; I2 = 88.6%). Adherence to SB time guidelines was also associated with a better HRQoL (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.11–1.62, 95% PI: 0.86–2.09, p < 0.001; I2 = 97.4%). Similarly, meeting the sleep duration guideline (7–8 hours) was linked to a higher HRQoL (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.34–2.88, 95% PI: 0.87–4.44, p < 0.001; I2 = 91.3%). Overall, adherence to at least one of the 24-hour movement guidelines was associated with improved HRQoL (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.44–1.89, 95% PI: 1.01–2.69, p < 0.001; I2 = 95.5%). For details, please see Figures , , and .
 

## Publication bias assessment
Small-study effects were assessed using Begg’s test, with p-values for PA (n=6), SB (n=5), and SL (n=4) being 0.1329, 0.4624, and 0.086, respectively. No clear evidence of small-study effects was observed.

## Sensitivity analysis
To assess the robustness of the pooled results, First, a leave - one - out analysis was performed. As shown in Figure , when each included study was sequentially excluded, the effect sizes (with 95% confidence intervals) for the association between adherence to 24 - hour movement guidelines and HRQoL remained statistically significant (all p values = 0.000). This indicates that the overall positive association is robust, and the exclusion of any single study, including those that might appear influential, does not undermine the statistical significance of the pooled effect.
 Second, after changing the random - effects model to a fixed - effects model, the results were essentially consistent with the main results of the study (see Figures , and ). Collectively, these sensitivity analyses confirm that neither the exclusion of individual studies nor changes in statistical models undermine the core conclusion about the positive association between adherence to 24 - hour movement guidelines and higher HRQoL in older adults.
 

## Discussion
This study is among the first to examine the relationship between 24-hour movement behaviours and HRQoL in older adults via meta-analysis. While a prior systematic review (Hakimi et al., 2022) explored associations between individual behaviours (PA, SB, sleep) and HRQoL [ ], it lacked quantitative synthesis of combined 24-hour movement guideline adherence. By reviewing relevant research, we identified 25 studies (involving a combined total of 170,670 participants) that met our broad inclusion criteria. The results indicate that older adults who adhere to at least one component of the 24-hour movement guidelines exhibit significantly higher HRQoL than those who do not. The findings were consistent across random- and fixed-effects models, indicating a consistent association between guideline adherence and higher HRQoL, despite substantial between-study heterogeneity.
Our findings on compliance with 24-hour movement behaviours show that only 24.3% of older adults met at least one of the 24-hour movement behaviour guidelines, which is comparable to the adherence rate (34.3%) reported by Duarte Junior et al. (2025), but notably lower than the rate (51.2%) based on the United States (U.S.) National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data and another study reporting a 94.7% adherence rate among U.S. older adults [ , ]. These substantial differences may stem from variations in culture, environment, and lifestyle among older adults in the U.S. [ ].
Regarding specific behaviours, the adherence rate for PA guidelines in this study was 23.0%, which is slightly higher than the 17.4% reported by Yilmaz et al. (2018). This modest increase may be attributed to factors such as differences in regional health policies, variations in older individuals’ health awareness, the accessibility of community sports facilities, and the influence of local traditional exercise habits [ ].
For SB, the adherence rate to guidelines in this study was 18.5%, which is consistent with the high SB rate (59.4%) observed among older adults in Europe from 2002 to 2017 [ ]. The primary contributor to sedentary time was leisure-time sitting [ ]. Future efforts should focus on promoting healthy lifestyles and increasing social activities to reduce sedentary time and improve adherence to SB guidelines among older adults.
Adherence to sleep guidelines was the highest among the three behaviours at 40.0%, which is similar to the 66.4% rate reported by Liang et al. (2024) but still relatively low, indicating that more than half of older adults failed to achieve the recommended 7–8 hours of high-quality sleep per night [ ]. Future interventions, such as melatonin supplementation and establishing regular daily routines, could enhance adherence to sleep guidelines among older adults.
We assessed the association between compliance with 24-hour movement guidelines and HRQoL. Systematic reviews by Marquez et al. (2020) and Soong et al. (2025) highlight that regular physical activity substantially enhances HRQoL in older adults, particularly in domains such as mental health, social interaction, and independence. Group-based activities, such as square dancing, foster interpersonal communication, strengthen social bonds, and enhance a sense of belonging, thereby mitigating loneliness. Additionally, regular exercise has been associated with better muscle strength, cardiorespiratory fitness, and balance, which in turn correlate with lower fall risk and greater functional independence in older adults [ , ]. However, our analysis revealed that only 39.3% of older adults met the WHO PA recommendations (150 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week) [ ]. This low adherence underscores the potential value of targeted interventions to promote PA among older adults, such as community-based exercise programmes tailored to their preferences and capabilities. Such interventions may help increase PA duration and intensity and enhance guideline adherence, though their impact on HRQoL would need to be verified by interventional studies.
Despite the benefits of PA, the independent risks associated with SB cannot be overlooked. Fanning et al. (2022) and Asiamah et al. (2023) report that prolonged sedentary time in older adults is associated with muscle atrophy, metabolic dysfunction, and increased cardiovascular disease risk [ , ]. Furthermore, Rezende et SB is also correlated with restricted mobility and reduced social interactions with family and friends, which are further linked to feelings of loneliness [ ]. Even in the presence of PA, excessive SB may attenuate health benefits. Therefore, future interventions should not only focus on increasing PA but also incorporate strategies to reduce sedentary time. For instance, promoting social activities (e.g., community interest groups), designing individualised exercise programmes (e.g., light stretching or gardening), or utilising wearable devices to prompt regular movement could effectively decrease SB and enhance social engagement, thereby supporting HRQoL.
Our findings indicate that 7–8 hours of sleep per night is associated with higher HRQoL. Adequate sleep (7–8 hours/night) is associated with better cognitive clarity and emotional stability, which are in turn linked to higher HRQoL in older adults [ ]. Conversely, a sleep duration of less than 6 hours is linked to lower HRQoL, potentially due to reduced energy levels and mood disturbances [ ]. Excessive sleep (over 9 hours) may also pose risks, such as cognitive decline or poor health status [ ]. However, inconsistencies in the literature regarding the sleep–HRQoL relationship may stem from variations in sample characteristics (e.g., age or health status) or measurement methods (e.g., self-reported vs. objective assessments) [ ]. Standardised sleep assessment tools, such as actigraphy, could help clarify these associations in future studies.
To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine the relationship between 24-hour movement behaviours and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in older adults via meta-analysis. Previous research has largely focused on the isolated effects of individual behaviours (PA, SB, or sleep) on HRQoL [ , , ], with limited exploration of their combined impact. While 24-hour movement behaviour patterns have been shown to significantly influence HRQoL in children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder [ – ], research in older adults is still in its infancy. Although some studies have investigated the relationships between PA, SB, or sleep and HRQoL in older adults [ ], a comprehensive analysis of the integrated effects of 24-hour movement behaviours has been lacking. A prior systematic review explored this topic but did not conduct a meta-analysis [ ]. By combining systematic review and meta-analytic techniques, this study provides preliminary evidence supporting an association between adhering to the 24-hour movement guidelines and better HRQoL in older adults, with considerations for study limitations.
Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the high heterogeneity in our results, potentially due to differences in assessment tools (e.g., SF-36, EQ-5D), age among the included populations, and measurement methods for PA, SB, and sleep (e.g., questionnaires vs. accelerometers, self-reports vs. objective devices), limited the precision of our conclusions [ , ]. Notably, these factors may also explain the extreme effect size estimates observed in some analyses, they may not reliably reflect the true association in diverse older adult populations. To address this, future studies should use objective measurement tools (e.g., accelerometers, polysomnography) and standardised HRQoL instruments to reduce heterogeneity and enhance the comparability of results. Additionally, the scarcity of studies simultaneously examining two or three components of the 24-hour movement behaviour guidelines restricted our ability to assess their combined effects, with most studies focusing on a single behaviour, thus limiting insights into the complex interactions among PA, SB, and sleep and their collective impact on HRQoL. Furthermore, due to the limited number of studies included, the resulting lack of statistical power precluded the conduct of subgroup analyses. Future research should consider enriching the baseline characteristics of study samples by including older adults with diverse socioeconomic backgrounds or health conditions to facilitate more robust subgroup analyses and enhance the generalisability of findings.
To advance this field, future studies should consider the following directions: (1) systematically evaluate the combined effects of 24-hour movement guideline behaviours, particularly the synergistic impact of meeting two or three behavioural guidelines on HRQoL in older adults; (2) use objective measurement tools (e.g., accelerometers, polysomnography) and standardised HRQoL instruments to reduce study heterogeneity [ ]; (3) incorporate more longitudinal studies to strengthen causal inferences regarding the relationship between adherence to 24-hour movement guideline behaviours and HRQoL in older adults [ ]; and (4) include older adult populations with diverse characteristics (e.g., varying socioeconomic backgrounds or health statuses) to enhance the generalisability of research findings.

## Conclusions
This systematic review and meta-analysis yields two key findings: first, adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines among older adults is low, with only 24.3% meeting at least one of the guidelines; second, such adherence is associated with better HRQoL in this population. Notably, the majority of included studies are cross-sectional and substantial between-study heterogeneity limits result interpretation. Future research should focus on two priorities: (1) well-designed intervention trials to evaluate whether promoting guideline adherence could serve as a potential strategy for supporting older adults’ HRQoL; (2) longitudinal studies with standardized measurement methods to clarify temporal dynamics between 24-hour movement behaviors and HRQoL, addressing the gap in data on combined adherence to multiple guidelines identified in this review.
