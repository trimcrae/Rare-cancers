## Background
Data extraction is an essential step in evidence synthesis but remains time-consuming and prone to human error. Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus may offer partial automation solutions. This proof-of-concept study evaluated their preliminary performance in extracting data from full-text randomized controlled trial (RCT) reports within systematic reviews.

## Methods
Two previously validated systematic reviews published in European Urology (105 trials in total) were selected. Standardized prompts were created and optimized with ChatGPT-4 and tested independently across trials using both ChatGPT-4 (paid version) and Claude 3 Opus via the standard web interface. Each prompt was executed three times, and only the first output was used to calculate the proportion of correctly extracted items (Pacc). Extracted data were compared with verified gold-standard datasets.

## Results
For binary outcomes, ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus showed high accuracy in group size extraction (Pacc: 91%-94%) and moderate accuracy for event counts (Pacc: 57%-71%). For continuous outcomes, group size accuracy was moderate (Pacc: 59%-69%), while mean and standard deviation extraction was poor (Pacc: 24%-56%). Test-retest reliability was substantial to almost perfect.

## Conclusions
Current LLMs can assist in automating data extraction for binary outcomes but remain inconsistent for continuous outcomes. These preliminary results should be interpreted with caution. Further research using larger datasets and iterative prompt refinement is needed before LLMs can be reliably integrated into systematic review workflows.

## Introduction
Systematic reviews (SRs) and other evidence syntheses are critical for evaluating the effectiveness and risks of interventions, treatments, diagnostic tests, and technologies [ , ]. This process involves standardized steps such as formulating research questions, conducting literature searches, appraising study methods, extracting data, and synthesizing evidence [ ]. Data extraction, although essential, is labor-intensive and prone to human error [ ]. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT), single-investigator data extraction with verification took an average of 107 min per study, while dual-independent extraction took 172 min [ ]. Reproducibility studies revealed frequent data extraction errors, with a 17.0% error rate at the study level and 66.8% at the meta-analysis level and 85.1% at systematic review level [ ].
The emergence of LLMs [ , ] in AI offers a potential solution through automation. Contemporary AI models utilizing advanced machine learning techniques and extensive data repositories promise to transform the process of data extraction [ – ]. By employing tailored input prompts, these Large Language Models (LLMs) can streamline the data extraction process, thereby saving time and resources. However, the current literature lacks empirical evidence to prove the efficacy of LLMs in data extraction.
This analysis aims to evaluate the performance of an LLM in extracting prespecified data elements from PDF versions of full-text study reports published in scientific journals, compared to human-extracted data. The study investigates the preliminary accuracy and reliability of AI-driven data extraction for commonly used elements in SRs and other evidence syntheses, contributing to the integration of AI tools in evidence-based medicine.

## Methods

## Identification of ‘cases’
We began by selecting two systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials published in European Urology. These reviews were chosen for several reasons. First, previous reproducibility studies have documented pervasive data extraction errors, with error rates of 17.0% at the study level, 66.8% at the meta-analysis level, and 85.1% at the systematic review level [ ]. To minimize the influence of such errors, we selected two reviews from our prior reproducibility projects, for which all extracted data had already undergone independent verification for accuracy [ ]. This provided a reliable gold standard for evaluating AI-assisted data extraction. The two reviews were also selected because they were published in a leading specialty journal, and represented complementary data extraction types—one focusing on binary outcomes and the other on continuous outcomes. Both offered moderate sample sizes with accessible full-text PDFs, making them suitable for proof-of-concept testing.
The first review evaluated adverse events of gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonists versus agonists (41 eligible trials, 9 binary outcomes), while the second compared robot-assisted versus open radical cystectomy (64 trials, 12 continuous outcomes). Metadata from both reviews were initially extracted by the original authors and subsequently reverified by our team through multiple training and validation rounds to ensure accuracy.

## Data extraction tasks
For binary outcomes, the extraction focused on the 2 × 2 table data, which included event counts and group sizes in each trial arm. For continuous outcomes, the extraction involved 3 × 2 table data, consisting of mean, standard deviation, and group size in each trial arm. Each table cell represented a distinct task, resulting in four tasks for binary outcomes and six tasks for continuous outcomes per trial (Table  ). 

## Setting
The AI-assisted data extraction process involved standardized prompts developed for each task. Prompts were created using few-shot prompting and optimized with the assistance of ChatGPT-4 (paid ChatGPT Plus subscription) and Claude 3 Opus (paid professional tier). All interactions were conducted via the standard web interface; neither API services nor free versions were used. Data collection occurred in mid-May 2024, and each prompt was executed in a new session to prevent memory retention bias.
Only the primary full-text PDF publication for each trial was included; supplementary materials and multiple reports per trial were excluded. Although three outputs were generated for each prompt, only the first output was used to calculate the proportion of correct extractions (Pacc). Extracted values were validated against our verified gold standard dataset: exact numerical matches were required for continuous data and exact count matches for binary data; any missing or incorrect extraction was coded as “incorrect.” Prompts were not iteratively adjusted based on incorrect outputs—each version was tested independently across all trials. We acknowledge that excluding supplementary tables and not performing error-based prompt refinement may have contributed to lower accuracy for continuous outcomes. We report Pacc as the proportion of correctly extracted items, not a correlation coefficient, to avoid misinterpretation.

## Outcomes
The primary outcome measured was the percentage of correct extractions (Pacc = ) for each task, calculated as the number of correctly extracted RCTs divided by the total number of RCTs. For example, if the task involved extracting death events in the intervention group from 20 RCTs and the number of deaths was correctly extracted in 10 RCTs, the Pacc would be 50%. Any failure to identify the required information was considered an incorrect extraction.
The secondary outcomes included the percentage of incorrect extractions (Pinc= ) and the percentage of extractions that failed to identify the information (Pfail= )). Additionally, the test-retest reliability of each question, based on three separate runs, was evaluated as a measure of robustness. This reliability was categorized into the following agreement levels: slight (0.00–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), and almost perfect (0.81–1.00).

## Statistical analysis
The proportions of correctly, incompletely, and incorrectly extracted items (Pacc, Pinc, and Pfail) were computed for each task. Because group sizes were trial-dependent, they were evaluated directly rather than by specific outcomes. For each outcome, these proportions were summarized using the inverse variance heterogeneity (IVhet) method with Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation to account for between-task heterogeneity; pooled estimates are reported as exploratory summaries. To indicate precision, Pacc values are also presented with binomial 95% confidence intervals. Test–retest reliability was assessed separately using Cohen’s Kappa to account for agreement due to chance. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 27 and MetaXL 5.3 (EpiGear International Pty Ltd.).

## Binary outcomes

## Group size information
The Pacc for extracting group size information in the treatment group (Task 2) and control group (Task 4) across 41 trials using ChatGPT-4 was 94% (95% CI: 85%, 100%) and 92% (95% CI: 81%, 99%), respectively. When using Claude 3 Opus, the Pacc for the same tasks across trials were both 91% (95% CI: 80%, 98%) (Fig.  ). The Pinc and Pfail were lower for both ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus across all tasks (see Figure S3). 

## Binary outcome information
Figure presents the performance on Task 1 (extracting the event count in the treatment group) and Task 3 (extracting the event count in the control group). The Pacc of ChatGPT-4 for extracting event counts ranged from 29% to 100% across 9 outcomes, with a weighted mean Pacc of 57% (95% CI: 48%, 69%, I² = 26%). For Claude 3 Opus, the Pacc for extracting event counts ranged from 33% to 100%, with a weighted mean Pacc of 71% (95% CI: 61%, 81%, I² = 60%). Again, Pinc and Pfail were lower for both ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus across all tasks (see Figure S4 and Figure S5). 

## Continuous outcomes

## Group size information
The Pacc for extracting group size information in the treatment group (Task 3) and control group (Task 6) across 64 trials using ChatGPT-4 was 61% (95% CI: 49%, 73%) and 69% (95% CI: 57%, 80%), respectively. When using Claude 3 Opus for the same tasks across trials, the Pacc were both 59% (95% CI: 47%, 71%) (Fig.  ). The Pinc and Pfail were lower for both ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus across all tasks (see Figure S6).

The Pacc of ChatGPT-4 for extracting the mean value ranged from 0% to 47% across 12 outcomes, with a weighted mean Pacc of 30% (95% CI: 22%, 38%, I² = 65%). For Claude 3 Opus, the Pacc for extracting the mean value ranged from 0% to 100%, with a weighted mean Pacc of 56% (95% CI: 48%, 65%, I² = 79%).

The Pacc of ChatGPT-4 for extracting the standard deviation ranged from 0% to 43% across 12 outcomes, with a weighted mean Pacc of 24% (95% CI: 17%, 31%, I² = 58%). For Claude 3 Opus, the Pacc for extracting the standard deviation ranged from 0% to 100%, with a weighted mean Pacc of 44% (95% CI: 36%, 53%, I² = 83%). 
Pinc and Pfail were different for both ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus across all tasks (see Figure S7-10).

## Test-retest reliability analysis of (semi) automation numerical data extraction
Cohen’s kappa coefficients, presented in Table  , indicate the level of agreement among responses across three runs for each prompt. For binary outcomes, the Kappa coefficients of the responses from the three runs of each prompt are 0.69 and 0.74 for ChatGPT-4, indicating a substantial but not perfect level of agreement. The Kappa coefficients of the responses from the three runs of each prompt are 0.84 and 0.96 for Claude 3 Opus, indicating a nearly perfect level of agreement. For continuous outcomes, the Kappa coefficients of the responses from the three runs of each prompt ranged from 0.67 to 0.68 for ChatGPT-4, indicating a substantial but not perfect level of agreement. For Claude 3 Opus, the Kappa coefficients of the responses from the three runs of each prompt are 0.91 and 0.93, indicating an almost perfect level of agreement. 

## Discussion

## Key findings
This study demonstrated that large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus can achieve reasonable accuracy in extracting binary outcomes from randomized trial reports. Extraction accuracy for binary event counts and group sizes reached up to 71–94%, approaching or exceeding human single-extraction accuracy (typically ~ 65%) and nearing double-extraction performance (~ 75%) for safety outcomes [ ]. Moreover, test–retest reliability was satisfactory, with Kappa values ranging from 0.67 to 0.96, indicating moderate-to-excellent agreement across repeated extractions. These findings suggest that AI-assisted data extraction could substantially reduce manual effort in systematic reviews, particularly for straightforward binary data.
In contrast, performance for continuous outcomes (mean and standard deviation) was poor, with accuracy ranging from 24% to 56% and high failure rates (~ 40–50%). The main failure modes included (i) complex table layouts, (ii) nonstandard or incomplete reporting (e.g., median and IQR instead of mean/SD), and (iii) missing locator words within PDF texts [ ]. Both models also showed sensitivity to prompt variation, producing inconsistent outputs across repeated runs. Furthermore, their training on general internet-scale corpora—rather than biomedical literature—likely limited their domain-specific precision. Claude’s lack of live retrieval capability further restricted access to contextual trial registry data, while ChatGPT’s default PDF-only setup prevented retrieval-augmented inference. These factors collectively reduced extraction robustness and highlight the gap between general-purpose LLMs and domain-optimized models for evidence synthesis.
Several factors may contribute to the unsatisfactory accuracy of ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus in automated data extraction. First, these models are primarily designed to summarize key findings of articles. Although the latest versions can identify specific information within pages, they can misidentify data from text and tables and struggle with complex tables, leading to inaccurate extractions or no response. This limitation is evident in RCTs with continuous outcomes, where data are often presented in tables. Second, both models are sensitive to prompt variations, resulting in different responses to the same prompt across multiple attempts (see the varied Pacc in Figure S11) [ , ]. Reformulating prompts can also lead to different outcomes, as reflected in the test-retest reliability results (Table ). Third, ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus use an internet-scale corpus and curated knowledge bases for training, but they are not specifically trained in biomedical corpora. Additionally, some literature comes from registered RCTs with information on registration sites. Claude 3 Opus, being non-networked, cannot access these sites [ ]. In the case of ChatGPT-4, additional instructions are required to request this, but this paper has only explored the ability of the AI to extract directly from the pdf, and ChatGPT-4 has not actively accessed and obtained information about registered clinical trials. Prompt engineering techniques such as RAG may be needed to compensate for this disadvantage [ ].
Comparing ChatGPT and Claude, we find that ChatGPT-4 generally has a slightly higher extraction accuracy than Claude 3 Opus, particularly in prompt 1, when extracting sample sizes for both binary and continuous outcomes. This difference in accuracy may be related to the fact that prompt 1 does not explicitly specify the control and experimental groups, a detail that Claude 3 Opus may handle less effectively than ChatGPT-4. However, Claude’s extraction accuracy surpasses that of ChatGPT-4 when extracting the number of event occurrences at the binary ending, as well as for the mean value and standard deviation in continuous data. This suggests that different models may vary in their extraction capabilities for different content types. In the future, combining the capabilities of different models for different content to achieve the goal may be a promising avenue [ , ]. For the level of concordance, the kappa values obtained from repeated measurements of Claude’s extracted data are significantly higher compared to ChatGPT. Kappa statistics were calculated across all trial–outcome combinations within each task type to quantify agreement across three runs, rather than per individual cell.
AI-assisted data extraction has been explored across multiple domains [ – ], including evidence synthesis, biomedical databases, and clinical research. Prior studies, such as that by Yun et al. [ ], reported similar findings but were largely limited to small-scale or conceptual validations. Larger systematic evaluations are still needed to establish the reliability of these tools in real-world applications.

## Limitation
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was based on a relatively small sample—105 trials from two systematic reviews in a single journal—which may limit generalizability. Second, we observed variability in responses from ChatGPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus to identical prompts, resulting in inconsistent outputs (see Figure S11). Future studies should systematically examine prompt variability, explore other AI tools (e.g., Deepseek reasoning model, Gemini) [ ], and use integrated frameworks such as LangChain to optimize extraction pipelines [ ]. Third, our evaluation focused mainly on accuracy (Pacc) and reliability (Kappa), without assessing other performance metrics such as F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, hallucination rate, and workload reduction [ ]. Future research should employ a more comprehensive framework encompassing these complementary metrics. Finally, this study compared AI outputs with verified gold-standard data but did not assess the potential of AI to detect or correct human extraction errors—an important avenue for future investigation that may reveal AI’s corrective capabilities [ ].

## Conclusions
In summary, this proof-of-concept study demonstrates that current LLM-powered AI tools can assist with data extraction for binary outcomes but remain inconsistent, particularly for continuous outcomes. Given the limited sample size and scope, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. At present, AI tools cannot replace human reviewers but may serve as complementary aids to improve efficiency when used under supervision. Future research should involve larger datasets, broader outcome types, and iterative prompt optimization to establish the reproducibility, generalizability, and practical utility of AI-assisted data extraction in systematic reviews.
