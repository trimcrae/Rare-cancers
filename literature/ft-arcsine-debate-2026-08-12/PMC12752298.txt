ZhangJiayi ]. Norovirus (NoV) is now recognized as a foremost cause of acute gastroenteritis around the world [ ]. It is responsible for about 685 million cases of diarrhea each year, including 200 million among children, and leads to nearly 50,000 fatalities [ ]. Clinical symptoms of NoV infection typically include acute diarrhea and/or vomiting, and may involve nausea, abdominal cramps, fever, headache, and muscle pain [ ]. Symptoms usually start 12–48 h after exposure and commonly resolve within 1–3 days [ ]. Although most infections are mild and self-limiting, severe cases can result in dehydration, hospital admission, and death. High-risk groups comprise children under five—especially those below two years of age—older adults, and immunocompromised persons [ – ]. There are currently no approved antiviral treatments for NoV, partly due to the absence of reliable in vitro culture methods and animal models for vaccine testing [ ]. Therefore, treatment is primarily supportive, focusing on rehydration. No licensed vaccines against NoV are yet available. Vaccine candidates currently in clinical trials include bivalent virus-like particle intramuscular vaccines (Hillevax Inc., USA) [ – ], oral adenovirus-vectored vaccines (Vaxart Inc., USA) [ ], and mRNA-based virus-like particle intramuscular vaccines (Moderna Inc., USA) [ ]. Furthermore, a bivalent vaccine (National Vaccine and Serum Institute, China) and a quadrivalent vaccine (Anhui Zhifei Longcom Biopharmacy, China) are in Phase II clinical trials in China [ ].
Within China’s passive surveillance system, the National Notifiable Infectious Disease Surveillance System (NNIDSS) categorizes NoV-associated diarrhea as “Other Infectious Diarrhea (OID).” Since etiologic confirmation is not mandatory for reporting, cases are frequently grouped with diarrhea caused by other pathogens, such as rotavirus, resulting in substantial underreporting and systematic underestimation of NoV incidence. The China CaliciNet Surveillance Network [ ], launched in October 2016, employs molecular tools to monitor NoV genotypes and outbreaks, offering valuable information on viral evolution and transmission, its capacity to estimate disease burden is limited. Constraints include its narrow coverage, emphasis on clustered outbreaks over sporadic community cases, and inability to produce population-level burden estimates. In light of the consistent underreporting of NoV-related diarrhea in China, this meta-analysis sought to conduct a thorough evaluation of the disease burden of NoV-associated diarrhea among children under five years old in mainland China from 2020 to 2024. This meta-analysis provide the critical inputs required to quantify the burden of norovirus related diarrhea in China, furnishing robust evidence for surveillance prioritization, disease control strategies, and the forthcoming development and assessment of Norovirus vaccines.

## Methods
This meta-analysis was registered prospectively in the PROSPERO registry (CRD420251018912) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The PRISMA checklist is included in the supporting information ( Checklist).

## Search strategy and selection criteria
A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang databases to identify Chinese and English articles published from January 2020 to December 2024 that addressed NoV-associated diarrhea in children under five years old in mainland China. The eligibility criteria were developed within the CoCoPop framework for observational meta analysis [ , ]. For Chinese-language publications, only studies indexed in nationally recognized core journal databases—Peking University Core Journals, China Science and Technology Paper Citation Database, and China Science Citation Database [ ]—were included. The search strategy incorporated the following Boolean operators: (Norovirus OR “Norwalk Virus”) AND (Diarrhea OR “Acute Gastroenteritis” OR Epidemiology OR Incidence OR Prevalence OR Mortality OR severity OR complication) (Supporting Information: File). References of selected articles were hand-searched. Inclusion criteria were: (1) studies reporting laboratory-confirmed NoV diarrhea cases in children under five; (2) studies reporting at least one clinically relevant outcome (e.g., incidence, genotype distribution, detection rate, seasonal variation, demographic traits, clinical features); and (3) studies using standard laboratory methods for NoV detection (e.g., ELISA, RT-PCR, viral culture, IFA, electron microscopy, latex agglutination, immunochromatography, or PAGE). Excluded were: (1) reviews, meta-analyses, vaccine studies, non-human research, duplicate publications, and outbreak reports; (2) studies from Hong Kong, Macao, or Taiwan; (3) studies limited to genetic analysis of individual strains; (4) articles focused on comparing or validating diagnostic methods; (5) studies involving non-representative groups (e.g., children with chronic illness, immunocompromised individuals); and (6) articles where quantitative data could not be extracted due to missing or unclear key parameters. After initial screening of titles and abstracts, two researchers (J.Z. and L.C.) independently examined the full text of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third researcher (YM.Z.).

## Data extraction and quality assessment
Literature management was carried out using EndNote 21 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA). Two researchers independently performed data extraction and quality evaluation. The following data were collected from eligible studies: number of NoV-positive cases, detection rate, incidence, age category, sex distribution, seasonal trends, genotypes, hospitalization status, study design, and complexity. Study quality was assessed based on the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [ ]. Key criteria included participant selection, measurement of exposure and outcome, and control of confounding factors, with secondary criteria covering study-specific biases and statistical methods. Overall risk of bias was evaluated using a custom algorithm (Supporting Information: File) implemented in Microsoft Excel. Each criterion was rated as high, medium, low, or unclear risk of bias. Studies were classified as having high risk of bias if at least one primary criterion indicated clear bias or at least two primary criteria suggested potential bias or uncertainty. Medium risk was assigned if two or more criteria indicated possible bias or uncertainty. Studies were rated as low risk if no major criteria indicated bias and only minor criteria suggested possible bias or uncertainty. Two reviewers (J.Z. and L.C.) underwent training to align their understanding of the assessment criteria to calibrate their judgments. During the formal phase, standardized forms were independently completed in duplicate under double-blind conditions. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus; if agreement could not be reached, a third reviewer (YM.Z.)—also blinded to the previous ratings—made the final decision, thereby minimizing subjectivity in the risk-of-bias evaluation.

## Statistical analysis
The test positivity rate of norovirus (NoV) diarrhea, defined in each included study as the number of NoV-positive diarrheal cases among children aged < 5 years divided by the total number of children tested. Pooled proportions were estimated with the Metafor package in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).The raw positive rates from individual studies were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation. The inverse-variance weighting method was applied, assigning weights to each study proportional to the reciprocal of its variance (weight ∝ 1/variance). The weighted effect sizes were then pooled using the Mantel-Haenszel method. The result was back-transformed to the original proportion scale and reported as the pooled positive rate with its 95% confidence interval. We prespecified a decision algorithm for model selection: if substantial heterogeneity was detected (I² ≥ 50% and Cochran`s Q p < 0.05), a random-effects model with the DerSimonian-Laird estimator was applied [ ]; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was employed. Publication bias was appraised visually with funnel plots and quantitatively with Egger’s linear regression test (significance set at p < 0.05) [ ]. All descriptive statistics were performed in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

## Results

## Literature selection
The initial database search yielded 3,419 articles, and 26 additional records were identified through manual searching. After removing 1,243 duplicates, 234 articles published before 2020, and 844 articles from non-core journals, 1,124 articles underwent title and abstract screening. Following exclusion of 759 articles, 365 were selected for full-text review. Ultimately, 60 articles meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the meta-analysis. Among these, 59 reported only NoV test positivity rates, and one provided data on both detection rates and incidence. One additional article on incidence was included for qualitative synthesis (Fig.  ). Of the 60 included articles, 12 were in English and 48 in Chinese. Detailed search strategy is available in Supporting Information: File; information on authors, publication year, study location, participant age, specimen type, diagnostic methods, sample size, and outcomes is provided in Supporting Information: and File. Quality assessment for studies reporting detection rates is presented in Supporting Information: File.

 

## Characteristics of included articles
53/60 (88.33%) were hospital-based, 6/60 (10.00%) used NNIDSS data, and 1/60 (1.67%) was a cross-sectional study in kindergartens and schools. 25/60 (41.67%) tested only for NoV, while 35/60 (58.33%) tested for multiple pathogens, including rotavirus and adenovirus. Regarding healthcare settings, 16/60 (26.67%) involved outpatients, 12/60 (20.00%) inpatients, 8/60 (13.33%) both inpatients and outpatients, and 24/60 (40.00%) did not specify the setting. Further details are available in Supporting Information: File.

## Incidence of NoV-associated diarrhea
Only one population-based study reported incidence rates of NoV-associated diarrhea in children under five. Fan et al. [ ] used a pyramid model integrating sentinel surveillance, community surveys, and census data to estimate the incidence of five common foodborne pathogens (Salmonella spp., Shigella spp., Vibrio parahaemolyticus, diarrheagenic Escherichia coli, and NoV). The study indicated that in 2023, NoV-associated diarrhea had the highest incidence at 5133.68 per 100,000 (95% Uncertainty Interval, UI: 4047.24–6229.35) in China. Additional details are provided in Supporting Information: File.

## NoV test positivity rates
This meta-analysis included 60 studies reporting NoV test positivity rates, encompassing 169,558 pediatric diarrhea cases in China. The pooled test positivity rates for children under five was 15.76% (95% CI: 13.93–17.59). The highest rate was found in children aged 1–3 years (21.29% [95% CI: 17.44–25.14]), followed by those aged 3–5 years (15.19% [95% CI: 12.02–18.36]) and 0–1 year (13.08% [95% CI: 11.06–15.10]). Hospitalized patients had a test positivity rate of 16.61% (95% CI: 12.23–20.98), while outpatients had a rate of 15.85% (95% CI: 13.15–18.56). Studies including both inpatients and outpatients reported the highest pooled rate at 17.37% (95% CI: 9.17–25.56). Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the test positivity rate of norovirus-associated diarrhea fluctuated between 13.53% and 17.99%. With the onset of COVID-19, positivity fell to 15.74% (95% CI: 7.71–23.78), and once pandemic restrictions were lifted, it rapidly rebounded to 20.75% (95% CI: 10.07–31.43). Seasonal analysis of eight studies indicated the highest test positivity rate in autumn (20.91% [95% CI: 12.95–28.88]), followed by spring (19.95% [95% CI: 10.57–29.33]), with the lowest rate in summer (11.85% [95% CI: 5.00–18.69]). Test positivity rates across eastern, northern, southern, and central China are shown in Fig.  . Geographic analysis revealed the highest rate in Northwest China (17.24% [95% CI: 13.79–20.70]), followed by North China (16.57% [95% CI: 12.27–20.87]) and East China (15.97% [95% CI: 11.80–20.13]). Central China had the lowest rate (11.08% [95% CI: 7.37–14.80]). Temporal analysis showed a peak during 2022–2023 (20.75% [95% CI: 10.07–31.43]) and 2018–2019 (17.99% [95% CI: 13.24–22.73]), with the lowest rate during 2014–2015 (13.53% [95% CI: 8.62–18.44]) (Tables  and ).

 

 

 

## Distribution of NoV genotypes
GII genotypes were tested at significantly higher rates (89.98%, 95% CI: 84.45–95.50) than GI genotypes (4.09%, 95% CI: 2.52–5.65). Among VP1-based genotypes, GII.4 Sydney was most prevalent (34.74%, 95% CI: 13.78–55.71), followed by GII.3 (20.63%, 95% CI: 16.22–25.03). For RdRp region genotyping, GII.P31 was dominant (24.91%, 95% CI: 1.91–47.91), with GII.P12 second (19.45%, 95% CI: 9.10–29.79). Dual-gene sequencing (VP1/RdRp) indicated that GII.4 Sydney [P31] was the most common recombinant strain (33.15%, 95% CI: 20.19–46.14), followed by GII.3 [P12] (16.88%, 95% CI: 9.84–23.91) (Table  ).

 

## Publication bias
Egger’s regression test indicated no significant publication bias when studies were stratified by geographic region (North and Northwest China: P = 0.576; East China: P = 0.498; combined South/Southwest/Central China: P = 0.134). Funnel plots are provided in Supporting Information: File.

## Discussion
This meta-analysis revealed a critical data gap: only one Chinese study has estimated the incidence of norovirus related diarrhea, whereas reports on norovirus test positivity are plentiful. Pooled data show the virus in 15.76% (95% CI: 13.93–17.59) of diarrheal episodes, with clear seasonality—peaks in autumn and spring and a nadir in summer. The positivity rate dipped modestly during the COVID-19 pandemic but has been rebounding steadily since public-health restrictions were relaxed after the COVID-19 pandemic.
The highest test positivity rate was found in children aged 1–3 years. Factors such as waning maternal immunity, exploratory behavior, and variations in sanitation may contribute to the high rates in this age group [ – ]. Similar test positivity rates were observed among outpatients (15.85%) and inpatients (16.61%), likely reflecting the generally mild and self-limiting nature of NoV infection, which usually requires only supportive care [ ], resulting in limited differentiation by healthcare setting. Infections occurred year-round but showed seasonal peaks in autumn and spring. This pattern differs from previous reports, where higher rates are typically seen in colder months [ – ]. The observed seasonal increase may be due to increased close contact among children under five following kindergarten enrollment in September (autumn) and March (spring). Geographic variation was evident, with the highest test positivity rate in Northwest China (17.24%) and the lowest in Central China (11.08%), possibly due to differences in climate, socioeconomic factors, and healthcare access [ , ].
Genotyping confirmed GII as the predominant genotype, consistent with global studies reporting GII detection rates of 70–93.6% in pediatric NoV diarrhea cases [ – ]. In China, the GII.4 Sydney variant (34.74%) and its recombinant form GII.4 Sydney [P31] (33.15%) were most prevalent. This aligns with a multinational meta-analysis (2015–2020) involving 45 countries [ ], which identified GII.4 as the leading cause of childhood NoV gastroenteritis. The analysis also noted a recurring pattern of novel GII.4 variants emerging and replacing prior dominant strains every 2–3 years [ ].
Longitudinal trends showed a more noticeable reduction from 17.99% in 2017–2018 to 15.77% in 2019–2021. This decline is probably attributed to improved hygiene, strict public health measures, and increased awareness of infection prevention during the COVID-19 pandemic [ ], consistent with reports from Australia, England, and Japan [ – ]. Notably, rates rebounded to 20.75% in 2022–2023, reaching the highest level in the study period. This resurgence is likely due to the relaxation of non-pharmaceutical interventions in China since December 2022, similar to trends observed in Kenya [ ], the United States [ ], and England [ ]. The periodic emergence of novel NoV variants with immune-escape properties may also play a role [ , ]. With the conclusion of the COVID-19 pandemic and the increasing number of susceptible individuals, the incidence of diarrhea and gastroenteritis caused by norovirus is expected to rise further.
Substantial heterogeneity (I²≥ 50%) was observed in most subgroup analyses, indicating that the variability observed exceeds what would be expected from random sampling error and reflects genuine, systematic differences among studies. Several factors may account for this heterogeneity: (i) the studies encompassed widely separated regions of China, which differ in climate, socioeconomic development, and sanitation—each of which can influence viral transmission and detection; (ii) differences in study design, sampling strategies, case definitions, and surveillance durations among the studies; and (iii) the inclusion of data collected before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, during which public health interventions further altered the natural circulation patterns of the virus. Due to the high heterogeneity, the pooled positive rate reported in this meta-analysis may not accurately reflect the actual situation in any single specific setting (such as a single hospital, region, or season). Simultaneously, the presence of heterogeneity reduces the precision of our estimate of the ‘true’ pooled effect size. This is intuitively evident from the wider 95% confidence interval. Given the significant heterogeneity, this study employed a random-effects model for the pooled analysis. This model allows for the existence of real differences in effect sizes across studies, and its pooled result represents the best estimate of the average of these differences. In summary, the high heterogeneity observed among the included literature indicates that the test positive rate for Norovirus associated diarrhea is significantly influenced by various factors such as geographic distribution, case definition, age group, and season, etc. Therefore, the core value of this meta-analysis lies not only in providing a single estimate of the positive rate but, more importantly, in systematically delineating a comprehensive picture of its distribution across different contexts nationwide and in identifying the key factors contributing to its variation.
This study has several limitations. First, there is substantial heterogeneity among the included studies, likely due to differences in population sampling, geographic distribution, and methods of norovirus detection. This heterogeneity is an objective reality that must be carefully considered when interpreting the results. Second, the strict inclusion criteria applied to Chinese publications may have excluded some regionally relevant data, potentially leading to underrepresentation bias. Third, studies related to norovirus outbreaks were excluded from this analysis; while this exclusion helps to provide a more accurate estimate of the positivity rate, it may also underestimate the overall disease burden of norovirus-associated diarrhea in specific populations and time periods [ ].

## Conclusions
Our meta-analysis documents an increasing burden of norovirus related diarrhea in Chinese children < 5 years and delineates a bimodal, spring–autumn peak that differs with the winter dominance described in temperate regions. The most pressing evidence gap is the paucity of high-quality, community-based incidence data. We therefore propose following research agendas: (i) accelerate the development and deployment of safe, effective norovirus vaccines as the cornerstone of population-level control; (ii) institute sustained, genotype-resolved genomic surveillance to detect emerging variants and provide real-time intelligence for precision prevention; and (iii) launch standardized, methodologically rigorous epidemiological studies to generate precise estimates of incidence, disability, and economic burden that can inform evidence-based policy.
