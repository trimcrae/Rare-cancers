## Abstract

## Purpose
Os Odontoideum (OO) is an anatomical anomaly of the craniocervical junction, which may lead to severe instability, resulting in potentially fatal spinal cord compression. Considering clinical improtance of OO, the aim of this study was to provide a thorough synthesis of data on its epidemiology, specifically regarding its prevalence with neurosurgical considerations.

## Methods
The major electronic databases (Pubmed/Medline, Embase, Scopus, ScienceDirect) were thoroughly searched up to July 18th, 2025. Studies reporting original data on the prevalence of OO were deemed eligible for inclusion. The extracted data subsequently underwent qualitative assessment. A random effects model based on the DerSimonian-Laird approach was employed. The AQUA tool was used to evaluate the quality and risk of bias of the included studies.

## Results
Of the initial 1,638 records, 19 studies (n = 18,521 patients) met the inclusion criteria. Most of the studies revealed “low” risk of bias. The lowest prevalence of OO was found in the general population (0.21%, 95%CI: 0.00-0.72, I2 = 50%). OO was more commonly found in subjects with craniofacial malformations (1.9%, 95%CI: 0.90–3.24, I2 = 0%) and among patients undergoing craniocervical junction fixation (10.84%, 95%CI: 5.19–18.11, I2 = 83%; random-effects meta-regression with Knapp-Hartung adjustment, p = 0.0006). The highest prevalence of OO was observed in a subgroup of patients with both 21 trisomy and craniocervical junction symptoms (30.86%, 95%CI: 14.82–49.49, I2 = 42%).

## Conclusions
OO is rare among the general population. However, its prevalence is significantly higher in individuals with craniofacial malformations or 21 trisomy. Knowledge of the epidemiology of OO may facilitate earlier and more accurate diagnosis, particularly in high-risk patient populations. However, substantial heterogeneity warrants cautious interpretation. In pursuance of establishing the odds ratio, a greater number of case-control studies should be conducted.

## Introduction
Os odontoideum (OO) is a rare anatomical variation of a cervical spine that was first described by an Italian anatomist, Carlo Giacomini, in 1886 [ ]. In radiological imaging, it is considered a spherical-shaped dens being disconnected from the vertebral body C2 (Fig. ). In the majority of cases, it is half the size of the correctly developed process. Moreover, a process developed this way makes it much more common to move out of the expected localization. Etiology remains controversial—there are two main theories explaining this phenomenon: congenital or acquired [ ]. Patients may remain asymptomatic for years, often discovering the condition incidentally following presentation to the emergency department for minor trauma. OO needs to be differentiated from other conditions, the main ones being the odontoid type II fracture, ossiculum terminale, the third occipital condyle, and the ossification of the apical ligament (Fig. ). A critical clinical implication of OO is its strong predisposition to craniocervical instability, which can lead to severe spinobulbar compression.
 Although numerous research articles published in recent decades have substantially advanced our understanding of this variation, contributing significantly to the current knowledge of the clinical aspects of os odontoideum, no consensus has yet been reached regarding its relative frequency and epidemiology [ , , ]. Thus, in regard to the relevancy of this condition, more precise awareness about the epidemiological patterns of OO could potentially serve as a significant aid for physicians to make earlier diagnoses and therefore ensure better care for the patients. Therefore, the aim of this study was to systematically gather and synthesize all available evidence on the prevalence of OO across different clinical and anatomical subpopulations. By addressing the central research question—“What is the prevalence of os odontoideum in the general population and in clinically meaningful subgroups?”, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive epidemiological overview that may support earlier diagnosis and guide clinical vigilance in high-risk groups.

## Materials and methods

## Study design and search strategy
This paper is a systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence, anatomical, and surgical implications of OO. As this was a retrospective analysis of the studies that had been published in the past, consent to participate was not necessary and ethics approval was not required. The structure of this study closely adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) requirements [ ]. In addition, the creation of the flowchart also followed the standards proposed by the PRISMA guidelines (Supplementary Material ). The following databases: Pubmed/Medline, Embase, Scopus, and ScienceDirect have been thoroughly searched to identify studies reporting original data on OO from inception up to July 18th, 2025. The first Boolean string consisting of the search term “os odontoideum” was employed, without any modifiers so as not to miss any potentially relevant articles. The search techniques for the databases followed the same general design, considering the specific syntactic needs of the search engine. A full initial search strategy and the second complementary wave of search following peer review with the broader combinations of MeSH and free-text terms can be viewed as Supplementary Material . Regarding the original language and the year of publication, there were no restrictions. Medical experts who were proficient in the languages were contacted for papers written in languages other than English. After the entire texts were retrieved, a manual review of the articles’ reference lists was done in order to find more records. When information was unclear or the complete text was unavailable, the appropriate author was emailed for more details.

## Study selection
Full-text articles, abstracts, and research titles were evaluated by two independent researchers (W.G. and T.K.). In case of any disagreements, help from a third experienced senior investigator (L.S.) was sought for data validation and final conclusion until a unified consensus had been reached. Because disagreements were few, qualitative in nature, and resolved quickly through consensus, inter-rater reliability statistics (such as Cohen’s κ) were not calculated. The researchers subsequently took responsibility for the study selection and data extraction process. To avoid duplication of data, potential overlaps between study populations were carefully assessed. When studies originated from the same institution, research group, or clinical registry, their study periods, age ranges (adult vs. pediatric cohorts), imaging modalities, and eligibility criteria were compared to determine whether they represented independent samples. In cases where overlap could not be ruled out, we included only the study with the largest dataset.

## Eligibility criteria and data extraction process
Any study that reported original data regarding the epidemiology of OO was deemed eligible for inclusion. A qualitative and quantitative synthesis of the collected data was then performed. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table . N > 5 threshold was selected to reduce the smallest-study effects, minimize the influence of observation-level noise on prevalence estimates, and align with established methodological recommendations in evidence-based anatomical prevalence reviews [ ]. Data extraction was likewise performed independently by the same two reviewers. The information listed below was extracted from every study that satisfied all inclusion requirements into the spreadsheet of Microsoft Excel 2016 (Redmond, USA): (1) type of study, (2) number of patients in the general population, (3) number of patients with abnormal imaging, (4) number of patients with craniofacial malformations, (5) number of patients with Trisomy 21, (6) number of patients undergoing craniocervical fusion, (7) number of patients with OO, and (8) mean age of the entire subgroup. Mendeley Reference Manager version 2.93.0 (Elsevier Ltd., Amsterdam, Netherlands) was used for the entire study selection process. All extracted variables are presented in a structured format in Table , which summarizes the characteristics of each eligible study and the availability of specific parameters across studies.
 

## Risk of bias assessment
A quality check and risk of bias assessment were conducted on the anatomical studies included in the present meta-analysis using the anatomical quality assessment (AQUA) tool [ ]. The AQUA was selected because our review primary focus is the anatomical and radiologic identification of os odontoideum prevalence, rather than the evaluation of clinical outcomes, prognostic factors, or comparative exposures. Two independent investigators (T.K. and W.G.) used the anatomical quality assessment tool separately for all analyzed studies. We consulted a senior coauthor (L.S.) to reach a consensus when we encountered discordance. Each study was assessed for 5 domains: (1) objectives and subject characterization; (2) study design; (3) methodology characterization; (4) descriptive anatomy; and (5) reporting of the results. Next, the risk of bias within the domains was recorded as low, high, or unclear. If the answer to a significant question within a domain was “no,” consensus was reached regarding whether the entire domain should be considered high risk or low risk. However, if two questions had been answered “no,” that domain was instantly recorded as high risk.

## Statistics
The primary endpoint of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to calculate the pooled prevalence estimate (PPE) of OO in the general population. The secondary endpoints of this study were to estimate the PPEs of OO among subgroups of individuals with craniofacial malformations, 21 trisomy, cranio-cervical junction (CCJ) symptoms, and both CCJ symptoms and 21 trisomy.
Formal statistical estimation of PPEs was executed using software MetaXL 5.3, EpiGear International Pty Ltd. (Brisbane, Australia). Each of the PPEs was estimated with corresponding confidence intervals of 95%; a difference was considered not statistically significant if the two ranges overlapped. To deal with missing data, the list-wise deletion technique was applied. A random effects model based on the DerSimonian-Laird estimator was used to determine the PPE for the previously stated analyses. Because several included studies reported very low event proportions, including values close to 0%, we applied the Freeman–Tukey double–arcsine transformation to stabilize variances before pooling. This transformation reduces the influence of studies with extreme prevalence values, prevents variance from approaching zero when proportions are near 0 or 1, and is widely recommended for meta-analyses of rare anatomical and radiologic conditions. After computation of the pooled transformed estimates, we applied the standard inverse (back-transformation) to express the final pooled values as interpretable prevalence proportions. The heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using the χ2 test and the I2 statistic. The I2 values were interpreted as follows: 0–40%: not important, 30–60% moderate heterogeneity; 50–90%: substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100%: considerable heterogeneity. An arbitrary p value of < 10% was set for Cochrane Q significance (< 0.10). Conversely, the significance of comparative tests was agreed to standard < 5% (< 0.05). Random-effects model was used for all calculations, as studies were gathered from the literature and included different populations [ ]. Subgroup differences in prevalence were compared using a test of homogeneity based on χ2 (Statistica 13.3.0, TIBCO Software Inc, Palo Alto, USA). We used a Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index, a quantitative indicator of Doi plot asymmetry, for the detection of publication bias [ ]. Minor asymmetry and a potential small-study effect are indicated by values between ± 1 and ± 2, which reflect a slight degree of publication bias; significant asymmetry is indicated by an index greater than ± 2, which strongly suggests the presence of major publication bias and a pronounced small-study effect. An LFK index between 0 and ± 1 indicates no asymmetry and no publication bias, suggesting the absence of small-study effect [ ]. Consistent with methodological standards, subgroup meta-analyses were performed only when at least two independent studies were available. For the isolated trisomy 21 subgroup, only a single eligible study was identified; therefore, no statistical pooling was conducted, and its prevalence was reported descriptively. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using leave-one-out method. To explore sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression on the subset with the highest number of included studies was performed with geographical region and imaging modality as moderators and Knapp-Hartung adjustment.

## Results

## Search
The initial search of the databases produced a total of 2,481 results. Following the removal of 843 duplicates, a total of 1,638 unique entries have been identified. In addition, 32 articles that could potentially meet our eligibility criteria were recognized through the reference searching. Overall, after abstract screening, 115 articles have been assessed and evaluated in full text, of which 19 were found to be eligible for inclusion in this meta-analysis (Fig. ) [ , – , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ].
 

## Study characteristics
In total, 19 studies including 18,521 participants were included. Ten of those were conducted in the North America, five in Asia, and four in Europe (Table  ). The median age of the entire cohort was 27.8 years with interquartile range of 36.9 years. Three studies reported prevalence in general population. Two studies reported prevalence in subjects with craniofacial malformations. Prevalence of OO in patients with trisomy 21 was documented in four studies; however, three of these included subjects with symptoms suggesting craniocervical instability, not general subpopulation with Down syndrome. Therefore, it was divided into two subgroups: general subpopulation with Down syndrome (n = 1) and subpopulation with Down syndrome and simultaneous symptoms suggesting CCJ pathology (n = 3). Ten studies reported prevalence among consecutive surgical patients undergoing craniocervical fixation. Tabular summary of risk of bias analysis by means of AQUA tool can be viewed as Supplementary Material .

## Epidemiology of Os odontoideum
Results for each group were as follows (see Fig.  ): group A of general population (n = 16,580) included 3 studies, pooled prevalence = 0.21% (95% CI 0.00–0.72.00.72 I2 = 50% p = 0.14, LFK index = 7.21, a meta-analysis in random effects). Group B: patients with craniofacial malformations (n = 546) included 2 studies, pooled prevalence = 1.9% (95% CI 0.90–3.24 I2 = 0% p = 0,52, a meta-analysis in random effects). Meta-analysis was impossible to conduct in group C (patients with trisomy 21 n = 160) because in this category there was only one eligible study presenting the prevalence of 4.38%. Group D: patients with trisomy 21 and CCJ symptoms (n = 59) included 3 studies, pooled prevalence = 30,86% (95% CI 14.82–49.49 I2 = 42% p = 0.18, LFK index = 1.16, a meta-analysis in random effects). Group E: patients with CCJ fixation (n = 657) included 10 studies, pooled prevalence = 10.84% (95% CI 5.19–18.11 I2 = 83% p < 0.01, LFK index = 2.30, a meta-analysis in random effects). The Doi plots and LFK indices for each subgroup are presented in Fig.  . Sensitivity analysis can be viewed as Supplementary Material . Results of meta-regression with geographical region and imaging modality utilizing Knapp-Hartung adjustment are included as Supplementary Material . To formally evaluate whether the prevalence of OO differed between the general population and surgical cohorts, we performed a random-effects meta-regression using a Knapp–Hartung adjustment and a logit event-rate model. Subgroup membership (general population vs. surgical cohort) was entered as a moderator. The analysis demonstrated that subgroup significantly predicted OO prevalence (p = 0.0006; Supplementary Material ).
 

## Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this systematic review and meta-analysis comprising 18,521 individuals is the first study to provide a comprehensive synthesis of data about the epidemiology of OO. Not only did we establish that this anatomical variation is relatively rare in the general population, occurring in approximately 1 out of every 500 individuals (0.21%), but we also demonstrated that its relative frequency is significantly higher within certain specific subgroups, such as craniofacial malformations, craniocervical junction fixation, and 21 trisomy.

## Etiology and relevance of vertebral artery anomalies
The nature of C1–2 instability in OO differs in several important biomechanical and radiographic aspects from other atlantoaxial pathologies. In OO, the detached odontoid fragment exhibits smooth, corticated margins and chronic remodeling of the anterior arch of C1 and the superior aspect of C2, reflecting long-standing, typically reducible hypermobility. By contrast, instability due to type II odontoid fractures or acute ligamentous injuries is usually rigid or poorly reducible, with irregular fracture surfaces, comminution, and associated soft-tissue swelling, whereas rheumatoid atlantoaxial subluxation is characterized by progressive erosions, pannus formation, and fixed deformity.
According to the congenital theory, OO is a developmental abnormality brought on by non-traumatic avascular necrosis, imperfect migration, segmentation failure, or a failed union of the odontoid with the axis [ , , ]. Cases in trauma-free identical twins, familial events, and genetic expression research all lend credence to this theory [ ]. This viewpoint is further supported by its correlation with congenital cervical abnormalities such as Klippel-Feil syndrome, trisomy 21, and Morquio syndrome [ ]. Furthermore, OO may coexist with congenital craniovertebral junction dysplasia, osseous anomalies, and vertebral artery (VA) variants [ , , , , ] – see Table . Three-dimensional CT angiography series have shown [ , ] that extraosseous V3-segment anomalies such as persistent first intersegmental artery, fenestration, and early PICA origin, as well as high-riding or asymmetric VAs are significantly more prevalent in patients with atlantoaxial subluxation and congenital bony anomalies (including OO and related defects) than in controls or those with purely degenerative changes [ , ]. These findings have been confirmed in larger craniovertebral fixation cohorts, where congenital skeletal anomalies at the CVJ strongly predict the presence of VA anomalies and high-riding VA [ ], which is defined as C2 isthmus height of ≤ 5 mm and/or C2 internal height of ≤ 2 mm measured 3 mm lateral to the lateral border of the spinal canal [ , ]. In addition, unilateral high-riding VA has been associated with asymmetric C1–2 joint morphology and atlantoaxial osteoarthritis, suggesting a close interaction between VA anatomy, joint loading, and secondary degenerative change [ ].
 To prevent false-positive diagnoses, it is crucial to comprehend the normal embryology and ossification of the odontoid process, particularly in children [ ]. The first cervical sclerotome gives rise to the odontoid process and anterior arch of C1, the second sclerotome to the axis body, and the fourth occipital sclerotome to the odontoid apex [ , , ]. Around ages 10–12, the ossiculum terminale, a secondary ossification center, merges with the odontoid body after ossifying between ages 3–6. Similarly, between the ages of 3 and 8, the subdental synchondrosis, which is situated between the odontoid and C2 body, usually unites.

## Os odontoideum in down syndrome
In our analysis, we demonstrated that in patients with trisomy 21 OO could be much more prevalent than in general population, reaching over 30% in individuals with symptoms suggestive of craniocervical disease. In 1987 Pueschel and Scola [ ] showed that subjects with Down syndrome are more prone to develop atlantoaxial instability in comparison to healthy children. Moreover, the authors [ ] also stated that those with trisomy 21 are more likely to have OO than those without chromosome disorder. Similar results can be found in a study by M. El-Khouri et al. [ ], where out of 80 adult patients with Down syndrome, 11.2% were found to have atlanto-axial instability. Although this meta-analysis could not estimate the prevalence of OO among individuals suffering solely from 21 trisomy (the meta-analysis was conducted with data from ≥ 2 studies available), we managed to perform the estimation in the subgroup of patients with Down syndrome who present CCJ symptoms, which proved that OO is significantly more common when compared to the healthy general population. This observation is particularly important due to the widespread ligamentous laxity, joint hypermobility, and hypotonia that are linked to the majority of Down syndrome’s musculoskeletal symptoms; these conditions can then result in various spinal presentations, such as scoliosis, spondylolisthesis, atlantoaxial instability, and atlanto-occipital instability [ ]. The causal relationship with atlanto-axial instability in particular, which occurs when there is excessive movement between the atlas (C1) and axis (C2) vertebrae, can subsequently lead to serious neurological complications, including spinal cord compression associated with a risk of spinal cord injury and paralysis.
In individuals with Down syndrome, the increased risk of atlanto-axial instability necessitates heightened clinical awareness among healthcare professionals. Early recognition and screening for atlanto-axial instability are crucial to prevent severe neurological complications, particularly before procedures involving neck manipulation or anesthesia [ ]. Proactive monitoring and timely intervention can significantly improve patient outcomes and reduce the risk of spinal cord injury in this vulnerable population. Further studies involving larger groups of patients and using modern imaging techniques have the potential to help us better understand this pathology and treat people with Down’s syndrome more effectively.

## Physical examination and diagnosis
This condition’s clinical manifestation ranges from asymptomatic to severe neurological impairments [ ]. Headaches, torticollis, upper limb paresthesia, and neck and shoulder pain are examples of the most common initial symptoms that are nonspecific. Because of spinal cord compression or recurrent mild trauma, progressive instances may manifest with lower limb paralysis and abnormalities in gait [ ]. Cerebral artery occlusion brought on by increased C1-C2 motion might result in brainstem ischemia, convulsions, syncope, vertigo, visual abnormalities, or even unexpected death [ ]. A physical examination may show indications of myelopathy, including spastic gait and decreased hand dexterity, as well as neurological impairments, hyperreflexia, and aberrant reflexes [ ]. Given the nonspecific nature of these findings, other causes of myelopathy should be first ruled out.
Accurate imaging assessment of OO is crucial for proper diagnosis, especially in cases of articular instability, which may help to prevent further neurological complications. Plain radiography of the anteroposterior, lateral, and open-mouth odontoid structures are used in the initial evaluation to detect the potential presence of the ossicle and posterior widening of the anterior atlantoaxial distance [ ]. Typically, atlanto-axial instability is measured by flexion-extension radiographs, and joint subluxation is further evaluated by preoperative skull traction radiographs [ ]. In addition to evaluating congenital abnormalities like incomplete union of the C1 posterior arch or abnormal vertebral arteries, CT scans also confirm the corticated margins of the ossicle and show a shortened odontoid process [ ]. MRI plays an essential role in assessing spinal cord involvement because it can reveal spinal alterations that are suggestive of myelomalacia [ ].

## Treatment and surgical considerations
Management of asymptomatic patients with OO in the majority of cases highly depends on the presence of radiographic instability [ ]. To lower the risk of neurological injury, conservative therapy with consequent routine clinical and radiological follow-up is advised for individuals without evidence of C1-C2 instability [ , ]. Avoiding contact sports should also be advised [ ]. However, because undiagnosed spinal cord compression may result in neurological degeneration, asymptomatic individuals with radiographic evidence of atlanto-axial instability should be considered for surgical stabilization [ ]. Although C1-C2 fusion may considerably lessen neck rotation, in some circumstances, surgical intervention may be a good alternative since the advantages of avoiding neurological impairment exceed the hypothetical functional restrictions [ ].
The degree of atlantoaxial instability, the reducibility of subluxation, overall bone quality, and the existence of spinal cord compression play a role in choosing the most suitable surgical management of OO [ , ]. One successful method is posterior C1-C2 fusion, which uses either C1 lateral mass-C2 pedicle screws (Harms technique) or transarticular screws [ ]. These methods maintain a certain amount of cervical mobility while offering robust fixation [ ]. Occipito-cervical fusion is necessary to guarantee appropriate stabilization in cases of dystopic OO or occipitocervical instability [ , ]. In patients with poor bone density, occipitocervical fusion is often preferred due to the increased risk of screw pull-out and hardware failure in standalone C1-C2 fixation [ , ].
An anterior transoral approach may be preferred in cases of anterior spinal cord compression or irreducible atlantoaxial subluxation [ ]. Although the odontoid process can be directly decompressed with this surgery, it is technically challenging, requires surgeon’s experience, and can result in complications such as wound infection, dysphagia, and cerebrospinal fluid leakage [ ]. Because of such risks, the transoral approach is usually only used when posterior fixation is insufficient to enable decompression [ ].
Recent advancements in segmental atlantoaxial fixation without transoral decompression or foramen magnum approaches have demonstrated efficacy in stabilizing the CCJ with reduced surgical morbidity [ ]. Goel et al. reported a series of 190 patients who underwent direct C1-C2 fixation without the need for anterior decompression or occipitocervical fusion, highlighting that rigid posterior fixation alone can achieve sufficient decompression by restoring anatomical alignment [ ]. This reduces the risk associated with anterior approaches while maintaining a more physiological range of motion [ ].
Our study highlighted the clinical relevance of OO in surgical practice, demonstrating that over 10% of all CCJ fixation procedures could be performed in patients with OO. This is a striking contrast to the general population prevalence of OO, estimated at only 0.21%, indicating that, overall, OO cases represent a substantial portion of surgical candidates despite being relatively rare. These findings emphasize the need for greater familiarity with surgical strategies among spine surgeons to ensure optimal management of these patients.
Additionally, the American Association of Neurological Surgeons and the Congress of Neurological Surgeons have acknowledged the lack of high-quality studies defining an optimal surgical approach for OO [ , ]. They recommend clinical and radiographic monitoring for asymptomatic patients without spinal cord compression but advocate for posterior fixation and fusion in cases with neurological symptoms or radiographic instability [ , ]. When irreducible cervicomedullary compression is present, an anterior transoral release followed by posterior fixation may be required, though this remains a controversial and technically demanding procedure [ , ].
Ultimately, surgical management of OO aims to prevent neurological deterioration, stabilize the cervical spine, and improve long-term functional outcomes, with technique selection guided by individual anatomical and clinical considerations. Spinal fusion has been proven to eliminate neurological deficits in children with symptomatic disease [ , ]. Despite the risk of complications such as abnormal adhesions, cerebrospinal fluid leaks, vertebral artery injury, and new neurological deficits, the benefits of surgery in preventing further neurological deterioration and improving quality of life outweigh these risks [ ].

## Limitations
Despite the fact that high heterogeneity values are expected in this meta-analysis due to the intrinsic variability of anatomical studies (methodology, setting, expertise, timing, and many others), most of the estimated values presented in our study were low, which subsequently plays in favor of the reliability of our calculations [ ]. However, we suggest examining our results with caution, as several other aspects may account for the certain degree of uncertainty of the meta-analysis. First, heterogeneity in imaging modalities across the included studies, spanning plain radiography, CT, MRI, and angiographic techniques, may have limited consistent identification of associated craniovertebral junction anomalies, potentially leading to underestimation of true OO prevalence (Table ). Second, there were significant differences in sample sizes across the included studies, and the majority of subjects were reported in a single study [ ]. Also, the information used to synthesize estimated outcomes is derived from studies with a range of geographic origins, genetic backgrounds, and ethnicities; as a result, it naturally incorporates the impact of anatomical variations among different patient subgroups on outcomes reported in the included articles. Finally, dichotomized analyses such as odds ratios could not be performed due to the absence of study designs allowing between-group comparisons.
In this meta-analysis, the estimated prevalence of OO in the general population was 0.21%. In this general cohort, the LFK index (7.21) indicated marked asymmetry. Such extreme value likely reflects a combination of small-study effects and selection bias, as two of three included studies in this subgroup had modest sample sizes and heterogeneous imaging methodologies. In addition, the very low underlying prevalence of OO increases the susceptibility of proportion-based analyses to instability in smaller datasets, further amplifying apparent asymmetry. Such a high LFK index also raises concerns about the reliability of the PPE, as it may be inflated or biased due to selective reporting or underrepresentation of larger studies. Finally, since the analysis was based on data taken from only three studies, it is crucial to interpret this result cautiously, as such a limited number of studies may not provide a stable or accurate representation of publication bias, and the LFK index value may be disproportionately influenced by individual study characteristics. Therefore, while the observed prevalence offers an initial estimate, it should be considered preliminary and interpreted in light of these methodological limitations. In the CCJ subgroup, the PPE of OO was 10.84%. The LFK index was 2.30, suggesting possible publication bias or small-study effects. Overall, the actual prevalence in this symptomatic population could possibly be lower than what was estimated in our study.
Also, the generalizability of our findings may be restricted by the fact that no articles included in our study were performed on African, South American, and Australian populations. Lastly, the study protocol wasn’t registered before this systematic review and meta-analysis since PROSPERO (as of August 2025) does not allow for registration of prevalence meta-analyses and, despite being advised, the worldwide survey revealed that it was not a standard practice [ ].
Future research should aim to overcome the limitations of the current evidence base by establishing large-scale, multicenter, prospective imaging cohorts capable of estimating the true population prevalence of os odontoideum with greater precision. Standardization of diagnostic criteria, imaging protocols, and reporting frameworks will be essential to reduce inter-study variability. Additionally, coordinated international efforts, particularly involving regions currently unrepresented in the literature, such as Africa, South America, and Oceania, are needed to generate globally representative epidemiological data and to clarify prevalence patterns across diverse genetic and demographic backgrounds.

## Conclusions
Our study proved that OO is a rare anatomical variation of the cervical spine in the general population. However, it is significantly more frequently observed in individuals with specific comorbidities, such as trisomy 21, craniofacial deformities, and CCJ fixation. A more precise understanding of the epidemiology of os odontoideum may facilitate earlier diagnosis in patients belonging to high-risk groups. To ensure a personalized approach that promotes CCJ stability and long-term functional outcomes, the surgical technique implemented is case-dependent and considers the degree of atlantoaxial instability, the severity of the symptoms, and specific anatomical characteristics.
