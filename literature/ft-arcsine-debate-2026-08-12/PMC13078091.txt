## Background
The burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is rising rapidly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM), defined as myocardial dysfunction occurring independently of coronary artery disease, hypertension, or significant valvular disease, is an important complication of T2DM and may progress to overt heart failure if undiagnosed. While well characterised in high-income settings, DbCM prevalence and distribution in LMICs remain poorly defined. This systematic review aims to estimate the pooled prevalence of DbCM among adults with T2DM in LMICs.

## Methods
This protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis. A comprehensive search will be conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, AJOL, and EBSCOhost, supplemented by regional databases and grey literature sources. Observational studies conducted in LMICs that report the prevalence of DbCM among adults with T2DM will be considered. DbCM must be diagnosed using objective cardiac imaging after exclusion of alternative causes of myocardial dysfunction. Two reviewers will independently screen, extract data, and assess risk of bias using a tool adapted for prevalence studies.

## Data synthesis and analysis
Where data are sufficiently comparable, pooled prevalence estimates will be calculated using random-effects meta-analysis. Prevalence proportions will be modelled using generalised linear mixed models with a logit link function to account for within- and between-study variability. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I2 statistic and explored through subgroup analyses by demographic, clinical, and regional characteristics. Where meta-analysis is not appropriate, findings will be synthesised using a structured narrative approach.

## Expected outcomes
This review is expected to report pooled prevalence estimates of DbCM across LMICs and to describe regional variation. Differences in diagnostic approaches, glycaemic control, diabetes duration, age, sex, and geographical region are anticipated to contribute to heterogeneity and will be explored where data permit.

## Conclusion
By synthesising available evidence on the prevalence of DbCM in LMICs, this review will clarify the burden of disease and provide evidence to inform health system planning and guide future research and prevention efforts aimed at reducing diabetes-related heart failure.

## Strengths and limitations

Comprehensive, multi-database search strategy, including grey literature.
Use of a pre-registered protocol, ensuring methodological transparency and reproducibility.
Flexibility to conduct both meta-analysis and narrative synthesis, depending on data heterogeneity.
Focus on low- and middle-income countries addresses a critical gap in the epidemiology of diabetic cardiomyopathy.
Variability in diagnostic criteria may affect the comparability of prevalence estimates across studies.
Publication bias cannot be excluded, as studies reporting null or low prevalence estimates may be underrepresented.

## Background
Diabetes mellitus is a major global public health challenge, affecting over 500 million adults worldwide, with most cases attributable to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and the largest burden (81%) now seen in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to demographic transitions and lifestyle changes [ – ]. As the prevalence of T2DM continues to rise, so does the burden of diabetes-associated morbidity, disability, and health-system costs [ – ].
Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality and morbidity among individuals with T2DM, accounting for a large proportion of disease burden in both resource-rich and resource-limited settings [ – ]. Beyond atherosclerotic disease, diabetes is increasingly recognised to exert a direct deleterious effect on the myocardium, contributing to the development of heart failure even in the absence of coronary artery disease or hypertension [ , , ]. Heart failure associated with diabetes is linked to high rates of hospitalisation, poor quality of life, and increased mortality, highlighting the need for early detection and prevention [ – ].
Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) is a myocardial disorder defined as left ventricular systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction occurring in patients with diabetes in the absence of coronary artery disease, hypertension, or other conventional causes of myocardial dysfunction [ – ]. DbCM typically develops insidiously, with subclinical myocardial changes detectable only by imaging or tissue characterisation long before overt heart failure manifests [ – ]. Consequently, it is regarded as an intermediate stage in the natural history of diabetes-related cardiac disease, preceding clinically apparent systolic dysfunction and symptomatic heart failure [ , – ]. The development of DbCM is closely linked to the duration and severity of diabetes [ ], with poor glycaemic control, obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidaemia serving as key drivers [ , ]. Genetic susceptibility and ethnic variation may further influence risk, potentially leading to heterogeneity in prevalence across different populations [ ].
Despite the rising prevalence of T2DM and cardiovascular complications in LMICs, epidemiological data on DbCM in these regions remain sparse and fragmented [ , , , ]. Studies from high-income countries (HICs) demonstrate that DbCM is relatively common among individuals with T2DM, with prevalence estimates varying widely (11–67%) depending on study population and diagnostic criteria used [ – ]. However, these estimates may not be directly applicable to LMICs, where healthcare access, disease patterns, and genetic backgrounds differ substantially [ , ]. In many LMIC settings, diagnostic capacity for echocardiography or advanced imaging is limited, resulting in subclinical myocardial dysfunction likely remaining undetected and leading to a significant underestimation of its true public health impact [ , , ].
To our knowledge, no previous systematic review has comprehensively synthesised the prevalence and distribution of diabetic cardiomyopathy specifically in LMICs. Without reliable prevalence estimates from these regions, efforts to design targeted interventions, guide healthcare planning, and allocate resources for prevention and management remain severely limited [ , , ]. Therefore, this systematic review aims to synthesise available evidence on the prevalence of diabetic cardiomyopathy among adults with T2DM in LMICs and to describe variation in prevalence across populations and study characteristics.

## Objectives
The primary objective of this systematic review protocol is to estimate the pooled prevalence and distribution of DbCM in adults with T2DM in LMICs.
The secondary objective is to descriptively examine factors associated with variation in the reported prevalence and distribution of DbCM in LMICs, including glycaemic control (haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)), duration of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), sex, and age.

## Research question
This review follows the CoCoPop mnemonic (Condition: DbCM; Context: LMICs; Population: adults (≥18 years old) with T2DM).
The specific research question is: What is the prevalence and distribution of DbCM in adults with T2DM in LMICs?

## Methods

## Protocol and registration
This protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines [ – ] (Appendix 1).
Given that the primary objective of this review is to estimate disease prevalence, methodological procedures follow established guidance for systematic reviews of prevalence and incidence data, as outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis [ , ]. This guidance is specifically designed to address challenges inherent to prevalence reviews, including heterogeneous diagnostic criteria, variable population denominators, and differences in study design.
The protocol is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (ID: CRD42024586860; registration date: 17 September 2024). Any protocol amendments will be documented in PROSPERO and reported in the final publication.

## Eligibility criteria

## Inclusion criteria

## Population
Studies conducted among adults aged ≥18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) will be included. The adult age threshold is applied to ensure clinical comparability and minimise biological heterogeneity, since the disease trajectory, pathophysiology, and complications of adult T2DM differ from paediatric and adolescent forms [ , ].

## Condition and diagnosis
The condition of interest is diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM). As there is no single definitive diagnostic test, DbCM is recognised as a diagnosis of exclusion [ ]. In this review, DbCM will be defined as the presence of T2DM, with objective evidence of myocardial dysfunction, in the absence of alternative causes such as significant coronary artery disease, severe or uncontrolled hypertension (as defined by the primary study authors), or primary cardiomyopathies. To ensure a comprehensive assessment of the burden of disease, we will include studies reporting DbCM across its clinical spectrum, ranging from subclinical (asymptomatic) structural changes described in the “ ” to symptomatic presentations, provided that the diagnosis is confirmed by cardiac imaging.
Eligible studies must report objective evidence of myocardial dysfunction identified using accepted cardiac imaging modalities, such as echocardiography or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, based on criteria defined in prior studies or professional society guidelines [ , – ]. At least one imaging-based marker of myocardial structural or functional abnormality must be reported. Detailed diagnostic definitions and measurement thresholds are provided in Appendix 2.

## Context
This review will be restricted to studies conducted in LMICs, as defined by the World Bank classification at the time the study was conducted [ ].

## Study design
Observational studies reporting prevalence data, including cross-sectional and cohort studies, will be included. Studies must be published from 1972 onwards to be considered, reflecting the year DbCM was first described [ ]. No language restrictions will be applied.

## Exclusion criteria

## Population
Studies focusing exclusively on type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), gestational diabetes, paediatric populations (<18 years), or mixed populations where data specific to adults with T2DM cannot be disaggregated will be excluded.

## Condition and diagnosis
Given that DbCM is recognised as a diagnosis of exclusion, studies will be excluded if myocardial dysfunction is attributable to alternative cardiac or systemic conditions. These include coronary artery disease, severe or uncontrolled hypertension (as defined by the primary study authors), moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease, congenital heart disease, or other known primary cardiomyopathies (e.g. hypertrophic, restrictive, alcoholic, or viral). Studies in which DbCM is diagnosed solely on the basis of circulating biomarkers (e.g. BNP or NT-proBNP), without confirmation via cardiac imaging, will also be excluded.

## Context
Studies conducted in HICs or multinational studies in which data specific to LMICs cannot be isolated.

## Study design
Editorials, letters, narrative reviews, case reports, case series, case-control studies, and interventional studies that do not report baseline prevalence data. Conference abstracts where insufficient methodological detail or outcome data are available to assess eligibility or extract prevalence estimates.

## Information sources and grey literature
A comprehensive electronic search will be conducted in the following bibliographic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase (via OVID). Searches via the EBSCOhost platform will include MEDLINE Complete, CINAHL Plus, and Academic Search Complete.
To enhance coverage of studies from LMICs, additional regionally relevant and institutional sources will be searched, including African Journals Online (AJOL) and the WHO Global Index Medicus (via the World Health Organization Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (WHO IRIS)).
Grey literature will be searched to minimise publication bias, including conference abstracts and proceedings, dissertations and theses, and institutional reports. Specifically, ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global, OpenGrey, and WorldCat will be searched. Where relevant, conference proceedings and reports from international health organisations will also be considered.
To further identify potentially eligible studies, the reference lists of included articles and relevant systematic reviews will be manually screened (via backward citation searching), and forward citation tracking of key studies will be performed where feasible.

## Search strategy
The search strategy will be designed to identify all potentially eligible studies using a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), informed by the CoCoPop framework. The strategy will integrate controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to diabetic cardiomyopathy, cardiac imaging, prevalence, and low- and middle-income countries.
An illustrative PubMed search strategy is provided in Appendix 3 and will be adapted appropriately for use across other databases. Prior to implementation, the draft search strategy will be peer-reviewed by an experienced information specialist using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist.

## Selection of studies
The selection of eligible studies will be conducted independently by at least two authors. All retrieved studies will be imported into and managed in the Rayyan software, where articles will be deduplicated and screened for inclusion [ ].
The screening process will be conducted in two phases. During the first phase, we will screen articles for inclusion based on title and abstract; the second phase will involve full-text screening.
The selection process will be documented using the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram adapted from Page et al. [ ] (Fig.  ), recording the number of records identified, screened, included, and excluded. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be tabulated. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion and, where no consensus is reached, through consultation with a senior reviewer. 

## Data extraction and quality assurance
At least two reviewers will independently extract data from included studies using a standardised data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel. The form will be piloted by all reviewers prior to full extraction to ensure consistency and clarity. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a senior reviewer.
Data items to be extracted will include:Study characteristics: author(s), year, country/region, setting (urban/rural), design, and sample size.
Population characteristics: age, sex, race/ethnicity, and relevant contextual factors.
DbCM data: duration and type of diabetes, diagnostic criteria (specifically imaging modality and cut-offs used), and reported risk factors.
Outcome data: numerator (number of participants with DbCM) and denominator (total participants assessed) to permit prevalence estimation.

## Data synthesis and analysis
The characteristics of the included studies will be summarised descriptively and presented in tabular form detailing study setting, population characteristics, diagnostic criteria, and reported prevalence estimates.

## Quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis)
When studies are sufficiently comparable with respect to population, diagnostic definitions, and outcome measures, a meta-analysis will be conducted. Synthesis will be restricted to studies meeting minimum assumptions for pooling [ , , ].
Pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be calculated using random-effects models to account for between-study variability. Prevalence proportions will be analysed using generalised linear mixed models (GLMM) with a logit link function, which appropriately model binomial data while accounting for within- and between-study variation [ ]. This approach was selected to avoid potential biases associated with variance-stabilising transformations, such as the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation, particularly in prevalence meta-analyses involving extreme proportions or substantial heterogeneity [ ].
Between-study variance will be estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, which has been shown to provide more accurate and less biased estimates of heterogeneity compared with the DerSimonian-Laird estimator [ ].
Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed through visual inspection of forest plots, Cochran’s χ2 test (using a 10% level of significance due to its low statistical power), and the I2 statistic, with values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [ , ].

## Subgroup and sensitivity analyses
Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Planned subgroup analyses include geographical region (e.g. Africa versus non-Africa), study setting (urban versus rural), age group, sex, diagnostic classification of diabetic cardiomyopathy, and study design. Sensitivity analyses will be performed by excluding studies assessed as having a high risk of bias.

## Narrative synthesis
If meta-analysis is not feasible due to clinical or methodological heterogeneity, such as variation in diagnostic criteria, imaging modalities, or population characteristics, findings will be synthesised using a structured narrative approach. This narrative synthesis will summarise prevalence estimates by region and compare study-level characteristics and diagnostic approaches, consistent with PRISMA and JBI guidance [ , , ].
All statistical analyses will be conducted using StataSE software (version 18; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) [ – ]. A p value of less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

## Publication bias
If ≥10 studies are included in a meta-analysis, publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test [ ].

## Risk of bias assessment for included studies
At least two reviewers will independently assess the methodological quality and risk of bias (RoB) of included studies using an adapted tool developed by Hoy et al. [ ] and further modified by Werfalli and colleagues [ ] (Appendix 4). This tool is specifically designed for prevalence studies and evaluates both internal and external validity, thereby supporting assessment of the representativeness and methodological rigour of included studies.
The tool comprises a 10-item rating system covering key aspects of study design and conduct, including sampling methodology, case definition, measurement reliability, and data collection procedures. Each item will be scored as 1 (“yes”, indicating low risk of bias) or 0 (“no”, indicating high risk of bias). An overall quality score ranging from 0 to 10 will be calculated for each study.
Based on the aggregate score, studies will be classified as having low risk of bias (≥8 points), moderate risk of bias (6–7 points), or high risk of bias (≤5 points), consistent with previous applications of the tool in prevalence reviews [ ].
Discrepancies between reviewers will be resolved through discussion, and where consensus cannot be reached, a senior reviewer will be consulted.

## Missing data
Corresponding authors will be contacted to request missing or unclear data. If data remain unavailable, this will be documented and addressed during sensitivity analyses and interpretation.

## Confidence in cumulative evidence
The certainty of the evidence will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach adapted for prevalence studies [ ]. We will evaluate the quality of evidence across domains, including risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

## Expected outcomes and implications

## Anticipated impact
This systematic review is expected to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the prevalence and distribution of diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where epidemiological evidence remains limited despite the rapidly increasing burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [ , ]. By consolidating available data, this review aims to generate more robust prevalence estimates and highlight regional patterns that may reflect differences in demographics, healthcare access, and diagnostic practices.
Evidence from high-income settings suggests that DbCM is common among individuals with T2DM, with large community-based and hospital-based studies demonstrating both a substantial burden and prognostic significance [ – ]. However, reported prevalence varies widely depending on the diagnostic approach and population characteristics. The extent to which these findings translate to LMIC settings remains unclear and warrants systematic evaluation. Without reliable prevalence estimates from these regions, efforts to design targeted interventions, guide healthcare planning, and allocate resources for prevention and management remain severely limited [ , ]. This review will provide a structured comparison between regions and contribute to understanding contextual differences in disease detection and reporting.

## Exploration of heterogeneity
In addition to estimating overall prevalence, the review is expected to examine variation in reported prevalence across key demographic and clinical factors. Where data permit, planned subgroup analyses (as outlined in the “ ”) will examine potential sources of heterogeneity, including glycaemic control, duration of diabetes, body mass index, sex, age, and geographical region. These analyses may help identify subpopulations at higher risk and inform targeted screening and prevention strategies.

## Methodological considerations
As anticipated in prevalence reviews, variability in diagnostic criteria, imaging modalities, and case definitions is expected across studies and may influence comparability of estimates. In LMICs, limited access to advanced cardiac imaging and systematic screening may further contribute to underdiagnosis of subclinical disease. These considerations will be explicitly addressed through stratified analyses and narrative synthesis where quantitative pooling is not appropriate, in accordance with established methodological guidance.

## Conclusion
By systematically synthesising available evidence on the prevalence of diabetic cardiomyopathy in LMICs, this review will address a critical gap in the global literature on diabetes-related myocardial disease. The findings are expected to inform clinical practice, support health system planning, and guide future epidemiological research in settings disproportionately affected by the diabetes epidemic. Ultimately, improved understanding of the burden of DbCM may contribute to earlier detection and more effective prevention of diabetes-related heart failure.
