## Abstract

## Background
Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma in situ (Bowen's disease) is a precancerous condition confined to the epidermis of the skin. Despite the critical need for effective interventions to halt its progression, there remains a notable shortage of comprehensive data comparing the efficacy of various therapeutic approaches.

## Objectives
This systematic review and meta‐analysis endeavour to compare the different efficacies of interventions by investigating and synthesizing data from numerous trials.

## Methods
A pre‐defined protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021242224, registration date: 16 April 2021). Systematic searches in Medline, Embase and Central, along with manual trial register searches, identified studies reporting lesion clearance rates (LCR), participant clearance rates (PCR) or recurrence rates (date of last search: 12 June 2024). Quality assessment followed guidelines from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI). After the study arms were categorized into treatment groups and groups of study quality, the proportions were pooled using the generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) as meta‐analytical method.

## Results
A comprehensive inclusion of 71 studies facilitated an evaluation of 3783 lesions for LCR, 1225 patients for PCR, 4073 lesions for lesion recurrence rates (LRR) and 740 patients for participant recurrence rates (PRR). Surgery demonstrated the highest LCR and PCR (0.97, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.90–0.99) and the lowest LRR (0.04, 95% CI: 0.02–0.07).

## Conclusions
This study provides a thorough overview of reported efficacy outcomes for practice‐relevant interventions for Bowen's disease. Surgery outperformed other treatments for Bowen's disease. For the other intervention groups, it was not possible to show clear differences in effectiveness: LCR, PCR, LRR and PRR showed various treatment rankings, and the comparability was restricted by different numbers of studies between treatment groups and outcome measures, methodical and clinical heterogeneity. Further high‐quality studies are needed to investigate practice‐relevant interventions for Bowen's disease.

## Data collection, synthesis and management
Information for each included study regarding study design, baseline characteristics, intervention, treatment procedure, as well as the outcomes LCR, PCR, LRR and PRR were collected and summarized by two authors independently (TS and AP). Data were extracted to an internally piloted data extraction spreadsheet using Microsoft Excel 2016. As the literature search did not yield a sufficient number of comparative studies, it was not feasible to conduct a network meta‐analysis or two‐armed meta‐analysis. Therefore, we extracted the available clearance and recurrence rates from each treatment arm in the included studies and performed a random‐effects meta‐analysis of proportions for each outcome using the generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). For each outcome, two meta‐analyses were conducted: one that considered studies of any quality and another that included only studies that were rated as good. For both analyses, the different treatment modalities were described in subgroups. The methodical approach is visualized in Figure  . Based on the resulting pooled proportions from the meta‐analysis, we established a treatment ranking and conducted pairwise chi‐squared tests against placebo whenever possible. We used the Bonferroni correction to adjust the significance level when conducting multiple tests to control the risk of type‐I errors. Missing data due to participant dropout was dealt with through intention‐to‐treat analysis: we included all participants in the analysis irrespective of whether their outcomes were collected. The analyses were conducted in RStudio (https://www.rstudio.com/, Version 2022.07.2+576) using the packages ‘meta’ and ‘metafor’. Outcomes were stated with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

## Meta‐analysis of proportions using GLMM

For the herein‐performed random‐effects meta‐analysis of proportions, we decided to use a one‐step procedure offered by GLMM instead of using a conventional two‐step method (log, logit, arcsine or Freeman–Tukey double‐arcsine transformation and backtransformation). It fully accounts for within‐study uncertainties, which are especially critical for small sample sizes and small or high proportions, such as those present in our study. Furthermore, it does require neither a normal assumption being probably inappropriate herein nor corrections for zero counts. 

In GLMM, the normal within‐study likelihood is replaced by the binomial likelihood
Liθi=expθiYi1+expθini,
where Yi is the event count, ni the sample size, and θi is the latent true proportion of study i.
 After directly modelling event counts with binomial likelihoods, a specific link function is used to transform latent true proportions to a linear scale. Here we used the logit transformation gθi^=logθi^1−θi^ with θi^ being the estimated θi, as it is the default link function for GLMM.

## Assessment of study quality, heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis and risk of bias
To assess the methodological quality of studies, one author (AP) used the quality assessment tool for (1) before‐after (pre‐post) studies with no control group, (2) controlled intervention studies and (3) observational cohort and cross‐sectional studies, as described by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) (available from https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health‐topics/study‐quality‐assessment‐tools). Each assessment tool contains 12–14 questions. The specific questions and criteria may vary depending on the type of study, but generally include aspects such as sample selection, exposure as well as outcome definition and measurement, control of confounding factors, data analysis and result reporting. The NHLBI tool does not have any set threshold for quality scores but depending on how well the included studies performed in the respective assessment tool, we rated them as good (≥9 criteria fulfilled), fair (5–8 criteria fulfilled) or poor (≤4 criteria fulfilled). We assessed variability between studies by using the I
2 statistic. I
2 statistic describes the percentage of the variability in effect estimates that is due to variability in true effects rather than chance and is defined as low (25%–50%), moderate (50%–75%) or high (>75%). Sensitivity analysis was not undertaken in this study because of lacking functions available for the GLMM meta‐analytical approach, which may be possible in future updates. We assessed small study bias (that can include publication bias) for each outcome by creating a funnel plot.

## RESULTS

## Evidence identification and study characteristics
Our initial literature search identified 3375 references. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, full texts of 107 records were reviewed of which 36 references were excluded for reasons described in Figure  . Finally, 71 studies were included in the analyses. There were 39 before‐after studies with no control group, 18 controlled intervention studies and 14 observational cohort studies. The methodological study quality was rated as good in 20 cases, 42 studies were rated as fair and nine studies achieved only poor quality in the rating with the respective assessment tool. Patient and study characteristics of all included studies listed by treatment modality are summarized in Table  . One study was subsequently excluded from the meta‐analyses as an outlier because the reported clearance rate (0 out of 5 lesions cleared in the 5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU) treatment) was likely to distort the overall result. The poor clearance rate herein may be because only organ transplant recipients were included. Additionally, another study was excluded from the MAL‐PDT group because it used white light instead of red light, a likely moderating factor that could bias the overall result. These study arms were mapped in the forest plots, but not included in the sum and pooled rates. Finally, 3783 lesions were evaluable for LCR, 1225 patients for PCR, 4073 lesions for LRR and 740 patients for PRR calculations.

## DISCUSSION
Treatment patterns for Bowen's disease vary widely, and comparative effectiveness data are limited. The Cochrane review on Bowen's disease treatments highlighted the lack of high‐quality data, including only nine RCTs comparing various non‐invasive treatments. Thus, giving a complete overview of all treatment options was not possible here. Recently, Ahmady et al. filled a significant knowledge gap with the first RCT comparing excision, 5‐FU and MAL‐PDT, showing higher sustained clearance rates at 12 months for excision compared to the other treatments, aligning with our findings. This systematic review and meta‐analysis provides an extensive and in our opinion complete overview of published studies reporting LCR, PCR, LRR and PRR for Bowen's disease patients treated with practice‐relevant interventions. For reasons of completeness, it was also necessary to include non‐randomized studies. In addition, IMB is still included, although it is no longer available in the EU since 2020 due to safety concerns. For each outcome, we performed a one‐armed meta‐analysis of (1) only good‐quality studies and (2) any‐quality studies to show both the best possible approach combining different study qualities into one analysis and the highest possible sample size and information given in the literature. We ranked treatments for each outcome, finding surgery to have the highest LCR and PCR (0.97), while placebo had the lowest. Surgery also showed the lowest LRR (0.04), while ablative laser showed the highest values for this outcome (LRR = 0.32). Data for PRR were limited. These rankings should be interpreted cautiously due to overlapping confidence intervals, varying sample sizes and high heterogeneity. The GLMM approach used for the herein performed single‐arm meta‐analysis replaces the approximate normal within‐study likelihood by the appropriate exact likelihood, overcoming many problems of conventional two‐step methods: GLMM does not need critical normal approximations, avoids the use of (arbitrary) continuity correction in case of zero events and avoids the bias caused by the correlation between estimate and standard error. However, the single‐arm design prevents direct treatment comparisons. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were not performed due to limitations in RStudio functions for GLMM. Although there is less risk of bias on the analytical level, we still have a risk of bias because of limitations in the database used herein: first, there was significant funnel plot asymmetry detectable for all outcomes warning against a remarkable risk of small study bias that can include publication bias. Results might be biased towards better outcomes because it is likely that especially small studies with poor outcomes were not published. High‐quality studies generally showed lower LCR and PCR and higher LRR, suggesting less bias.
Second, the classification into good, fair and poor quality remains approximate and study features still differ within each quality group limiting the comparability of the study arms. As we also included non‐randomized studies, study features vary widely and it is difficult to group studies for different features. The NHLBI quality assessment tool was used instead of the more detailed ROBINS‐I or RoB 2 tools recommended by the Cochrane Handbook , which was impractical for the large number of studies included. Analyses should be interpreted with caution because of potential bias arising from, for example, worse attrition, rarely conducted intervention and outcome assessment according to standardized protocols, confounding on expectation arising due to missing randomization, and typically little protection against selection of the reported result. For example, as we handle missing data by intention‐to‐treat analysis, an (unknown) loss to follow‐up could lead to potential underestimation of recurrence rates (bias due to worse attrition).
Third, the clinical heterogeneity within intervention groups was high because of varying treatment procedures, patient characteristics and measurement time points between studies. Differences were noted in light sources, dosages and treatment sessions for PDT; safety margins and freeze–thaw cycles for cryotherapy; application durations for 5‐FU and imiquimod (e.g. 5‐FU cream was applied twice daily for 4 weeks in Ahmady et al. versus once daily for 1 week, followed by twice daily for 3 weeks in Morton et al. ); surgical methods (including also immuno‐cryosurgery in Gaitanis et al. ); and placebo encompassing placebo cream only as well as placebo cream combined with red light. Also, differences in sex distribution, age and localization of lesions can be noticed between studies and there may be other (unknown) variables influencing the outcome. Table  details these differences. In this study, it was not possible to adjust for these clinical variations or to do subgroup analysis due to a lack of individual patient data. Therefore, both methodical and clinical heterogeneity is only addressed by calculating the I2 statistic for each outcome measure and treatment group. In particular, treatment groups with high statistical heterogeneity (I
2 > 75%) or not applicable I
2 have to be interpreted with caution. The interventions imiquimod, ablative laser, placebo, vertepofin PDT, CLL442 and phenol peels are seldom studied, necessitating further high‐quality trials.
Another limitation of this study is the lack of assessment of adverse events and cosmetic outcomes being crucial for clinical decisions, particularly as Bowen's disease mainly affects the elderly and is predominantly slow‐growing with a good prognosis.

## CONCLUSIONS
This study provides a thorough overview of reported efficacy outcomes for practice‐relevant interventions for Bowen's disease. Overall, our results indicate that surgery might be the most effective treatment option for patients with Bowen's disease compared to other available treatment options. For the other interventions, it was not possible to show clear demarcations regarding the effectiveness. However, as there was only limited quality data available, these analyses are limited by the single‐arm design of the meta‐analysis, highly varying numbers of study arms and sample sizes between treatment groups and potential bias due to methodical and clinical heterogeneity. Thus, clinicians need to carefully consider the needs of individuals, patient characteristics, sites of the lesions, patient preferences and any comorbidities when choosing an appropriate treatment. Further high‐quality trials are desirable.
