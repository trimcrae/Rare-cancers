## Background
For patients with perioperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) after noncardiac surgery, earlier conversion to sinus rhythm might improve outcomes. The efficacy of a rhythm vs rate control strategy for the acute management of POAF remains uncertain.

## Methods
We searched databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies that included patients with POAF after noncardiac surgery and reported outcomes for patients acutely treated with a rhythm control strategy vs either a rate control or no treatment strategy. Studies were pooled using random effects models.

## Results
Of the observational studies, a rhythm control strategy was associated with higher conversion rates to sinus rhythm compared with a rate control or no treatment strategy (risk ratio [RR], 1.93; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.25-2.97; 9 studies; N = 591). Compared with a rate control or no treatment strategy, a rhythm control strategy was not associated with differences in length of hospital stay (mean difference, -1.67 days; 95% CI, -7.10 to 3.76; 2 studies), length of intensive care stay (mean difference, -1.90 days; 95% CI, -7.62 to 3.82; 1 study), or all-cause mortality (RR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.62-2.00; 5 studies). In an RCT that compared amiodarone vs magnesium, the RR was 0.56 for conversion to sinus rhythm (95% CI, 0.31-1.03; N = 34).

## Conclusions
A rhythm control strategy was associated with greater success rates for conversion to sinus rhythm compared with a rate control or no treatment strategy. However, the observational studies were of low quality and only 1 small RCT was identified, and few data were available for other outcomes.

## Résumé

## Contexte
Chez les patients présentant une fibrillation auriculaire périopératoire (FAPO) après une intervention chirurgicale non cardiaque, une conversion plus précoce en rythme sinusal pourrait améliorer les résultats cliniques. L'efficacité d'une stratégie de contrôle du rythme par rapport au contrôle de la fréquence pour la prise en charge aiguë de la FAPO reste incertaine.

## Méthodes
Nous avons recherché dans les bases de données les essais contrôlés randomisés (ECR) et les études observationnelles qui incluaient des patients atteints de FAPO après une chirurgie non cardiaque et qui rapportaient les résultats pour les patients traités en phase aiguë avec une stratégie de contrôle du rythme par rapport à une stratégie de contrôle de la fréquence ou à l'absence de traitement. Les études ont été regroupées à l'aide de modèles à effets aléatoires.

## Résultats
Parmi les études observationnelles, une stratégie de contrôle du rythme était associée à des taux de conversion en rythme sinusal plus élevés par rapport à une stratégie de contrôle de la fréquence ou d'absence de traitement (risque relatif [RR], 1,93; intervalle de confiance [IC] à 95 %, 1,25-2,97; 9 études ; N = 591). Par rapport à une stratégie de contrôle de la fréquence ou d'absence de traitement, une stratégie de contrôle du rythme n'a pas été associée à des différences en termes de durée d'hospitalisation (différence moyenne, -1,67 jours; IC à 95 %, -7,10 à 3,76; 2 études), de durée de séjour en soins intensifs (différence moyenne, -1,90 jours; IC à 95 %, -7,62 à 3,82; 1 étude) ou de mortalité toutes causes confondues (RR, 1,12; IC à 95 %, 0,62 à 2,00; 5 études). Dans un ECR comparant l'amiodarone au magnésium, le RR était de 0,56 pour la conversion en rythme sinusal (IC à 95 %, 0,31-1,03; N = 34).

## Conclusions
Une stratégie de contrôle du rythme a été associée à de meilleurs taux de réussite pour la conversion en rythme sinusal par rapport à une stratégie de contrôle de la fréquence ou d'absence de traitement. Cependant, les études d'observation étaient de faible qualité et seul un ECR de petite taille a été identifié, tandis que les données disponibles pour d’autres critères d'évaluation étaient limitées.

Uncontrolled POAF after noncardiac surgery should be promptly identified and treated, because failure to achieve early control can lead to worsening of symptoms (eg, chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations), acute cardiovascular complications (eg, heart failure, myocardial injury), and hemodynamic instability. These might result in longer lengths of hospital stay, greater use of critical care resources, and higher costs of care. , , 
Uncontrolled POAF can be managed using either a rhythm or rate control strategy. A rhythm control strategy is aimed to achieve conversion to sinus rhythm via pharmacological or electrical cardioversion. In contrast, a rate control strategy is aimed to lower the ventricular heart rate of atrial fibrillation (AF) using β-blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and/or digoxin. 
It is unclear whether one strategy is superior to the other for managing uncontrolled POAF. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the efficacy of a rhythm control strategy vs a rate control or no treatment strategy in patients with POAF after noncardiac surgery. We also assessed success rates of individual rhythm control therapies for achieving conversion to sinus rhythm in this population.

## Methods
We reported this systematic review and meta-analysis according to the Meta-analyses Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) reporting guidelines. We registered the study protocol on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42022374830). Differences in the registered protocol and the final protocol are provided in .

## Search methods
We identified relevant studies using a systematic literature search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CENTRAL from the time of database inception until February 28, 2024, using Covidence software (covidence.org). Our search strategy combined keywords and terms related to surgery, AF, and either rhythm control or rate control ( ).

## Study selection and outcome assessment
We considered observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to be eligible for inclusion. We included studies if they: (1) had patients who underwent noncardiac surgery; (2) included ≥ 10 patients with POAF; (3) included patients 18 years of age or older; and (4) reported either: ≥ 1 of the comparative study outcomes (ie, conversion to sinus rhythm, length of hospital stay, length of intensive care stay, all-cause mortality) in patients using a rhythm control strategy vs either a rate control or no treatment strategy for POAF; and/or the success rate of ≥ 1 rhythm control therapies (ie, antiarrhythmic drug or electric cardioversion) for achieving conversion of POAF to sinus rhythm. We excluded studies published only as meeting abstracts. We did not exclude studies on the basis of publication language. We included studies that described mixed arrhythmias if at least 50% of the arrhythmias were AF. We included studies of mixed populations if at least 50% of the patients had undergone noncardiac surgery. We conducted screening and full-text review independently and in duplicate and resolved discrepancies through consensus. If agreement could not be reached, discrepancies were resolved by a third independent reviewer.
We defined POAF to be any AF that occurred in the intraoperative and/or postoperative setting. We defined the use of a rhythm control strategy as the use of ≥ 1 class I or class III antiarrhythmic drugs and/or electric cardioversion for the management of POAF, with or without the use of rate control therapies. We defined the use of a rate control strategy as the use of ≥ 1 rate control therapies (ie, β-blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and cardiac glycosides) for the management of POAF without the use of antiarrhythmic drugs or electric cardioversion.
For the comparisons of a rhythm control strategy vs either a rate control or no treatment strategy, the main outcome of interest was conversion to sinus rhythm, before or at the time of hospital discharge. Other outcomes included length of hospital stay, length of intensive care stay, all-cause mortality until discharge or 30 days after surgery, and recurrence of AF after hospital discharge. For the outcome of conversion to sinus rhythm, we used the longest follow-up duration available for each study.
For the success rates of individual rhythm control therapies, the outcomes of interest were conversion to sinus rhythm for: (1) class I antiarrhythmic drugs; (2) amiodarone; and (3) electric cardioversion. We used the longest follow-up duration available for each study, up until the date of hospital discharge, unless otherwise specified.

## Data extraction
We performed data extraction independently and in duplicate using standardized forms. We collected information on the study design, sample size, types of surgical procedures, baseline demographic characteristics, study definitions (ie, POAF, treatment strategies used, study outcomes), number of POAF patients treated with a rhythm control strategy vs a rate control or no treatment strategy, associations between strategies and outcomes, and success rates for conversion to sinus rhythm for individual rhythm control therapies. For the main study outcome, we contacted study authors to obtain additional unpublished outcomes data and for other study clarifications.

## Assessment of quality and certainty of the evidence
For the comparative analyses, we used the Newcastle Ottawa Scale and the Risk of Bias 2 tools to assess the quality of nonrandomized and randomized studies, respectively. , Nonrandomized studies that received a score of ≥ 7 of 9 on the Newcastle Ottawa Scale and reported multivariable adjusted results were deemed to be high quality. We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) framework to assess the certainty of the evidence. The major domains of GRADE are risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. Evidence was graded as very low, low, moderate, or high certainty.

## Statistical analysis
For the comparative meta-analyses, we separately pooled nonrandomized and randomized studies. We estimated pooled risk ratios (RRs) for nonrandomized studies using the inverse variance method and RRs for randomized studies using the Mantel-Haenszel method, along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We used random-effects models for all analyses. We quantified between-study statistical heterogeneity using the I2 value. Heterogeneity was considered to be important when I2 was > 30%. For the outcome of conversion to sinus rhythm, we conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis with the exclusion of a single large observational study that recruited patients only from the intensive care unit. 
For the individual rhythm control therapies, we used meta-analyses of proportions to determine pooled estimates and their corresponding 95% CIs for the outcome of conversion to sinus rhythm. We used Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine-transformed proportions for variance stabilization.
We conducted analyses using Review Manager version 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration; London, United Kingdom), and STATA 18/BE (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Analyses were 2-tailed with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

## Observational studies of rhythm control strategies compared with rate control or no treatment strategies
Sixteen observational studies including 1991 patients reported outcomes for the comparison of a rhythm control strategy vs a rate or no control strategy for POAF occurring after surgery ( ). , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , Studies included patients who underwent all noncardiac surgeries (8 studies), lung transplantation (2 studies), thoracic surgery (2 studies), thoracic surgery and esophagectomy (2 studies), esophagectomy (1 study), and head and neck surgery (1 study). Rhythm control strategies used included class III antiarrhythmics alone (8 studies), class I antiarrhythmics alone (2 studies), electric cardioversion alone (1 study), and various combinations of antiarrhythmic drugs with or without electric cardioversion (5 studies). Outcomes were reported for conversion to sinus rhythm in 14 studies, mortality in 5 studies, length of hospital stay in 2 studies, length of intensive care stay in 1 study, and AF recurrence in 2 studies. All of the studies reported on the use of rhythm vs rate control or no treatment strategies for the acute management of POAF. None of the studies reported on the long-term use of strategies. None of the studies reported on the use of outpatient rhythm monitoring after surgery. Because none of the studies reported multivariable adjusted results, all of the studies were considered to be of low quality ( ). Additional study characteristics are shown in . 
Of the 14 studies that reported conversion to sinus rhythm, the longest durations of follow-up were during the post-anaesthesia care unit stay for 1 study, 6 hours for 1 study, 24 hours for 3 studies, 48 hours for 2 studies, 6 days for 1 study, at discharge from the intensive care unit for 1 study, and at hospital discharge for 5 studies. At the time of hospital discharge, 95% of patients treated with a rhythm control strategy and 95% treated with a rate control or no treatment strategy were in sinus rhythm (5 studies). Four of the studies reported all patients to be in sinus rhythm at the end of the follow-up period and were excluded from the meta-analysis. Two of the 14 studies reported on patients with intraoperative AF and had patients overlapping from the same study population. , Because the study with the longer follow-up duration reported that all patients were in sinus rhythm at the end of the follow-up, both studies were excluded from the main meta-analysis and were reported descriptively. For the 5 studies that reported short-term all-cause mortality, outcomes were reported at hospital discharge for 2 studies, at discharge from the intensive care unit for 1 study, and at 30 days after surgery for 2 studies.
A rhythm control strategy was associated with increased rates of conversion to sinus rhythm compared with a rate control or no treatment strategy (RR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.25-2.97; I2 = 0%; N = 591; 9 studies; very low certainty; ). The sensitivity analysis had similar findings. There was no association between using a rhythm control strategy compared with a rate control or no treatment strategy for short-term all-cause mortality (RR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.62-2.00; N = 495; I2 = 0%; 5 studies; very low certainty; ), length of hospital stay (mean difference -1.67 days; 95% CI, -7.10 to 3.76; N = 73; I2 = 0%; 2 studies; very low certainty) or length of intensive care stay (mean difference, -1.90 days; 95% CI, -7.62 to 3.82; N = 62; 1 study; very low certainty; and ). 
Two of the larger observational studies consisted of thoracic surgery and esophagectomy patients from overlapping populations who had intraoperative AF alone. In 1 study, a rhythm control strategy using first-line amiodarone therapy was associated with increased conversion to sinus rhythm at 48 hours of follow-up (RR, 8.11; 95% CI, 3.92-16.76; N = 416) compared with either β-blocker or no treatment, but after 6 days of follow-up all patients reverted to sinus rhythm. In the other study, patients with intraoperative AF treated with amiodarone with or without β-blockers) achieved sinus rhythm intraoperatively in 14% (16/113) of cases while β-blocker use alone led to sinus rhythm intraoperatively in 36% (21/59) of cases. Treatment of AF that persisted into the post-anaesthesia care unit with electric cardioversion was associated with greater conversion rates to sinus rhythm compared with no treatment (98% [155/158] vs 18% [63/355]; RR, 293.5; 95% CI, 74.0-775.0; N = 513). 
Two of the observational studies reported on AF recurrence. The first study showed that all 51 patients were in sinus rhythm at their follow-up visit with electrophysiology at 30 days after discharge. The second study showed recurrence of AF in 2 of 9 patients who had received rhythm control and 0 of 3 patients who had received rate control over a median follow-up period of 19 months. 

## RCTs of rhythm control strategies compared with rate control or no treatment strategies
In a single RCT consisting of 34 medical and surgical patients with supraventricular arrhythmias in the intensive care unit that compared intravenous amiodarone vs magnesium, the RR was 0.56 (95% CI, 0.31-1.03; very low certainty) after 24 hours of follow-up for conversion to sinus rhythm ( ). The study was determined to have a high risk of bias.

## Conversion success rates for specific rhythm control therapies
Thirty-two observational and randomized studies reported the success rates for conversion to sinus rhythm for an antiarrhythmic drug and/or electric cardioversion ( ). The overall success rate for conversion to sinus rhythm using amiodarone was 91% (95% CI, 82-97; 25 studies, N = 1035) when the longest duration of follow-up available was used ( ). The success rates according to follow-up duration were 54% after 12 hours (95% CI, 48-61; 5 studies; N = 263), 71% after 24 hours (95% CI, 55-86; 10 studies; N = 542), 83% after 48 hours (95% CI, 71-92; 5 studies; N = 449), 96% after 72 hours (95% CI, 73-100; 3 studies; N = 287), and 96% at discharge (95% CI, 90-99; 6 studies; N = 376). The overall success rates for conversion to sinus rhythm using the longest duration of follow-up available were 81% using electric cardioversion (95% CI, 60-97; 13 studies; N = 311; ) and 76% using class I antiarrhythmic drugs (95% CI, 58-91; 5 studies; N = 248; ). 

## Discussion
In this meta-analysis consisting mostly of observational studies, the use of a rhythm control strategy was associated with higher conversion rates to sinus rhythm compared with the use of a rate control or no treatment strategy for patients with POAF after noncardiac surgery. However, there were few data available pertaining to other postoperative outcomes; almost no RCTs were identified, and the overall certainty of the evidence was very low.
POAF is associated with more admissions to the intensive care unit, longer hospital lengths of stay, and greater care costs. , , , Although there are several potential benefits of using a rhythm control strategy in patients with POAF, we found almost no high-quality studies to inform this issue. Despite this, temporal trends suggest that there has been an increase in the use of rhythm control over the past 2 decades for this population. Studies of other populations with new or recent onset AF have shown mixed findings regarding the effect of strategy choice on patient outcomes. For example, a study of 54 patients with new onset AF in the emergency department (ED) showed that patients treated with procainamide had fewer hospital admissions and shorter lengths of ED stay compared with historical controls. However, a randomized trial of 523 patients with POAF after cardiac surgery showed no significant difference in the total number of hospital days or freedom from AF at discharge when patients were treated with an amiodarone-based strategy compared with a rate control strategy. Similarly, a meta-analysis of 15 observational and randomized studies that compared the effects of rhythm control vs rate control in patients with POAF after cardiac surgery showed no differences in lengths of stay, AF recurrence, or mortality. Importantly, it is unclear whether such findings should be extrapolated to patients with POAF after noncardiac surgery, because the pathophysiology of AF in this population significantly differs from those who experience POAF after cardiac surgery (eg, direct cardiac manipulation) and from those who present to the ED with recent onset AF (eg, potential absence of a physiologic stressor). Moreover, patients with POAF after noncardiac surgery tend be older and have a high burden of cardiovascular disease, and might therefore respond differently to rhythm control. Finally, it is important to highlight that most patients who do not receive antiarrhythmic therapy or electric cardioversion experience spontaneous conversion to sinus rhythm before hospital discharge. Therefore, achieving earlier conversion times might not necessarily affect outcomes in the longer term.

## Limitations
Our study findings have limitations. First, we identified no high-quality data to inform our outcomes. Almost no RCTs have been previously conducted, and none of the observational studies reported multivariable adjusted results, which likely led to residual confounding. For example, rhythm control strategies tend to be favoured in those who are either hemodynamically unstable or located in a highly monitored setting (eg, the intensive care unit), and it is possible that the efficacy of rhythm and rate control therapies differ among these populations. Surgical characteristics including the type and duration of procedures might have also contributed to residual confounding. Second, there was significant heterogeneity in the rhythm and rate control strategies used in the included studies. Moreover, many of these studies did not report the dose or duration of therapies used, the order in which the therapies were given, or whether therapies were used concomitantly. Third, the methods used for the ascertainment of conversion to sinus rhythm were inconsistently reported across studies, and only some studies systematically used continuous rhythm monitoring and/or obtained electrocardiograms to detect conversion. Fourth, most of the studies that compared rhythm vs rate control strategies only reported on success rates for conversion to sinus rhythm, with few studies reporting on patient outcomes such as length of stay and mortality. Fifth, our review did not address whether long-term use of rhythm vs rate control strategies affects outcomes in patients with POAF. Whether treatment strategy affects long-term outcomes in this population has not been investigated. Finally, most studies did not specify the frequency at which POAF was assessed throughout the follow-up time period. It is plausible that some of the patients who were in sinus rhythm when assessed at single instances in time (ie, as opposed to continuous rhythm monitoring) continued to experience transient episodes of clinically silent POAF.

## Conclusion
In patients with POAF after noncardiac surgery, a rhythm control strategy was associated with greater success rates for conversion to sinus rhythm compared with a rate control strategy. However, the observational studies were of low quality, only 1 small RCT was identified, and the studied populations were extremely heterogeneous. Moreover, few data were available for patient-important postoperative outcomes including length of stay and mortality. The potential value for each of these strategies therefore remains largely unknown, because the existing data fail to support any specific management strategy for POAF. High-quality randomized trials are needed to demonstrate the safety and efficacy of rhythm and rate control strategies in this population.
