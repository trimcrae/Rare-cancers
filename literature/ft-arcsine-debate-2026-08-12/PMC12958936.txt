## Abstract

## Abstract

## Objective
To determine the prevalence of potentially inappropriate prescribing (PIP), potentially inappropriate medication (PIM), potential prescription omission (PPO), potentially harmful drug–drug interactions (PDDI) and identify associated factors among older Ethiopians.

## Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis

## Data source
We searched PubMed, HINARI, Scopus and Web of Science databases to identify eligible studies published up to 31 October 2025.

## Study selection
Observational studies reported the prevalence of PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI among older adults from any healthcare settings were screened.

## Data extraction and synthesis
Two independent reviewers selected studies, extracted data and assessed the risk of bias. The quality and risk of bias of the studies were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale and Hoy risk of bias tool, respectively, while the certainty of evidence of outcomes was assessed using Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation based on Cochrane recommendations. We used a random-effects model for analyses to estimate the pooled prevalence and associated factors. All data analyses were done using Stata V.17 software.

## Main outcomes and measures
The national prevalence of PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI was estimated as main outcomes. Variations were estimated based on regions, age groups, outcome evaluation tool, disease type and healthcare setting.

## Results
The review included 25 studies (n=5662 participants) for PIP or PIM, 14 studies (n=2706 participants) for PDDI and 6 studies (n=1342 participants) for PPO. The pooled prevalence estimate was 41% (95% CI 33% to 48%), I2=96.87% for PIP, 37% (95% CI 31% to 44%), I2=96.33% for PIM, 55% (95% CI 36% to 73%), I2=99.00% for PDDI and 14% (95% CI 6% to 24%), I2=95.07% for PPO. The majority of the studies have very good quality (very good=13, good=1, satisfactory=11 for PIP and PIM; very good=11, satisfactory=3 for PDDI; very good=6 for PPO) and low risk of bias (low risk=18, moderate risk=7 for PIP and PIM; low risk=12, moderate risk=2 for PDDI and low risk=6 for PPO), while all studies for each outcome have low certainty of evidence. Subgroup analyses revealed significant regional and contextual variations. Polypharmacy was significantly associated with PIP (OR=3.72, 95% CI 2.53 to 5.46, p<0.01, I2=69.56%), PIM (OR=4.20, 95% CI 2.91 to 6.06, p<0.01, I2=57.83%) and PDDI (OR=4.51, 95% CI 3.05 to 6.69, p<0.01, I2=0.00%), while hypertension (OR=2.46, 95% CI 1.38 to 4.36, p<0.01 I2=0.00%) was associated with PIP.

## Conclusions and relevance
This review found a high prevalence of PIP, PIM, PDDI and PPO among older adults in Ethiopia, with notable heterogeneity across regions. Polypharmacy was associated with PIP, PIM and PDDI, while hypertension showed association with PIP. Despite generally good study quality, the certainty of evidence was low for the included studies due to the cross-sectional design nature, with high heterogeneity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. This study indicates a high burden of inappropriate medication prescribing and its associated factors, underscoring the importance of further robust studies to clarify prescribing practices and associated factors.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
A comprehensive and systematic search strategy was used to identify all relevant studies on potentially inappropriate prescribing, potentially inappropriate medication, potential prescription omission and potentially harmful drug–drug interactions.
We used clear eligibility criteria to select studies and applied established tools to assess study quality.
Subgroup analyses and various sensitivity analyses were done to strengthen the validity of the findings.
Subgroup analysis between males and females was not performed due to insufficient data.
High heterogeneity was detected among included studies, highlighting careful interpretation of our findings.

## Introduction
 The number of older people (aged 60 years and above) has been increasing across the globe to around 81 million by 2012; this number is projected to reach 1 billion in less than 10 years and double by 2050, reaching two billion. The fastest growth is occurring in developing countries. In Ethiopia, 5.6% of the population was older people (aged over 60 years) in 2020, making it the country with the largest older population in East and Southern Africa. This proportion is expected to increase to 9.1% by 2050. 
Ageing is associated with an increasing number of comorbidities, which in turn leads to the use of multiple medications. The use of multiple medications (polypharmacy) can contribute to inappropriate prescribing, potential inappropriate medication use, prescription omission and drug–drug interactions. , 
Potentially inappropriate prescribing (PIP) refers to the act of prescribing medications where the risk outweighs benefit, or the omission of clinically indicated drugs to treat or prevent diseases. PIP encompasses the use of potentially inappropriate medication when its risk outweighs its benefit (PIM) and potential prescription omission (PPO). It is a major public healthcare concern because of its association with healthcare resource utilisation, adverse drug events, potentially harmful drug–drug interactions (PDDI), hospitalisation and mortality. PDDI occurs when the coadministration of drugs results in clinically meaningful alteration of the effects or safety of one or more drugs as a result of coadministration with other drugs. Older people are more vulnerable to experiencing PIP and its associated adverse outcomes, often receiving medications with more risk than benefit. Although researchers have assessed PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI using standard treatment guidelines, American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria and the Screening Tool of Older People’s Prescriptions/Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment (STOPP/START) criteria are the most widely used criteria worldwide.
The prevalence of potential inappropriate prescribing (PIP) varies widely, ranging from 7% to 80% across different studies. PPOs are reported in 4% to 32% of patients, while potential drug–drug interactions (PDDI) occur in 6% to 90% of participants. The prevalence of PIMs varies globally, with the higher prevalence found in Africa and low-income countries, including Ethiopia. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a pooled PIM prevalence of 37% among older adults, with Africa having the highest regional prevalence of 47%. In Ethiopia, a meta-analysis including studies conducted up to 2020 reported a prevalence of PIM of 37%, whereas a more recent meta-analysis revealed a prevalence of 42.11%. However, these meta-analyses limited to PIM included a small number of studies and did not provide comprehensive estimates of PIP, PDDI and PPO, nor their associated factors. Therefore, the present review addresses this gap by including a larger number of studies and by determining the pooled estimate of PIP, PDDI and PPO, as well as their associated factors.
The global prevalence of PIM has changed over time, displaying an increasing trend. Various factors are associated with PIM, such as older age, polypharmacy and health deterioration. 
As the prevalence of PIMs has been increasing over time, influenced by various factors such as ageing, polypharmacy and healthcare system limitations, determining the pooled prevalence of PIM, PIP, PPO and PDDI, along with their associated factors, is of paramount relevance. A comprehensive meta-analysis on this issue would provide valuable insights for policy makers, healthcare providers and researchers to develop targeted interventions aimed at optimising medication use and safety, particularly in resource-limited settings such as Ethiopia. The prevalence of PIP, PIP, PPO and PDDI may be high due to a fragmented healthcare system, formulary limitations and the ongoing internal armed conflict in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aims at determining the pooled prevalence of PIM, PIP, PPO, PDDI and their associated factors among older adults in Ethiopia.

## Methods

## Search strategy
We conducted this systematic review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis guidelines ( ). 
The review protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO (CRD42024556744). Research questions were framed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Reviewer’s Manual for prevalence studies and condition, context and population as follows: (1) What is the prevalence of PIM, PIP, PPO and PDDI among older adults in Ethiopia? (2) What are the factors associated with PIM, PIP, PPO and PDDI in older adults?
The search strategy was developed in accordance with the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines for systematic reviews. Initially, the primary investigator developed search terms, and then all coauthors reviewed and approved the comprehensive search terms. The initial key words used were inappropriate prescribing, drug interaction and geriatrics. Then, independent search terms were developed for each key word. For potential inappropriate medication included “drug therapy” OR “inappropriate prescribing” OR “drug interactions” OR “pharmaceutical preparations” OR “drug utilization” OR “inappropriate drug” OR “inappropriate drugs” OR “inappropriate medication” OR “inappropriate medications” OR “inappropriate medicines” OR “inappropriate prescription” OR “inappropriate prescriptions” OR “inadequate medication” OR “suboptimal therapy” OR “suboptimal prescribing”, while search terms for geriatrics included “Aged” OR “elderly” OR “geriatrics” OR “old age”. The full search terms are included in supplementary table ( ).
Articles published in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and HINARI up to 31 October 2025 were searched. Additionally, studies were searched from Google Scholar using key words, and reference lists were checked for relevant information.

## Eligibility criteria
We included studies if they met the following criteria: (1) condition: studies reported prevalence of PIM, PIP, PPO and PDDI in any health conditions, along with their associated factors; (2) context: we included studies conducted in inpatient or outpatient or both settings; (3) population: older adults (age 60 years and above) who received medications for any condition; and (4) study designs: observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort, case–control). The following exclusion criteria were used: (1) non-observational studies (eg, clinical trials), reviews, case reports, case series, editorials and commentaries; (2) studies not focused on the target age group (older age) or country (Ethiopia); and (3) studies not published in English.

## Quality assessment
We assessed the overall methodological quality of the included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) tool for non-randomised studies. Each included study was assessed for representativeness, response rate, method, comparability of the subject and the appropriateness of the statistical analysis used. Two reviewers scored the quality of the studies individually. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. Additionally, we determined the risk of bias of the included studies using risk-of-bias assessment tool for prevalence studies. This risk-of-bias assessment tool focuses on confounding factors, risk of bias in selection and measurement and analysis quality. Additionally, certainty of evidence for studies was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) Working Group. 

## Study selection
Two independent reviewers (AMB and TSY) screened titles and abstracts of all identified studies to check their eligibility for full-text review. Studies meeting the criteria for full-text evaluation were assessed against predefined criteria for inclusion in the final synthesis. Any disagreements between reviewers were settled through discussion and, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (ZDA).

## Data extraction
The JBI data extraction form for prevalence and incidence studies was used to extract relevant data from the included studies. From each included study, the following data were extracted: first author, publication year, study design, region, setting context, tools, patient number, gender, age, proportion of PIM, PIP, PPO and PDDI, as well as associated factors. Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers (MAA and WSZ), and any disagreements were resolved by consulting a third reviewer (ZAE).

## Statistical analysis
Prior to data analysis, data were transformed to approximate a normal distribution. Proportions were transformed using logit and Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation methods, while ORs were transformed using the natural logarithm. The pooled prevalence of PIM, PIP, PPO and PDDI, along with their corresponding 95% CI, was calculated using a random-effects model. Weighting of studies was determined using the DerSimonian and Laird method. The pooled OR with 95% CI was calculated using a random-effects model to determine factors associated with the occurrence of PIM, PIP, PPO and PDDI. Both χ2-based Q statistics and the I2 test analysis were performed to determine heterogeneity. Significant heterogeneity was considered when either p<0.1 for Q statistics or I2 value>50%. Additionally, meta-regression was performed to identify the potential sources of heterogeneity of studies. Funnel plot and Egger’s test were used to estimate publication bias. Furthermore, trim and fill analysis was performed to assess potential publication bias and its impact on the pooled estimates.
Sensitivity analysis was done by reanalysing the data using a fixed-effects model to assess the pooled prevalences. Additionally, a leave-one-out approach analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the pooled effect estimate by removing one study at a time, turn by turn, and analysing the remaining studies. A bad robustness was considered when the removed study results in a significant change in the pooled effect estimate, that is, p<0.05. Subgroup analysis was done based on study setting, region, age group, outcome assessment tool, publication year, methodological quality of studies, risk of bias of studies and health condition group. Statistical analysis was done using Stata V.17 software. Statistical significance was considered at p<0.05 for all statistical tests.

## Patient and public involvement
There was no patient and public involvement in this study.

## Results

## Study selection
A total of 4894 studies identified through the searching strategy, including 4773 from databases and 121 from Google Scholar. Following the removal of the duplicates, 3411 studies were excluded based on titles and abstracts screening. Then, in total, 126 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility; 2 articles originated from a single study reporting different outcomes, resulting in 125 unique studies assessed. Of these, 31 studies were included in the final analysis, and the remaining 94 were excluded for various reasons ( ).
 

## Characteristics of included studies
Overall, 31 studies were included from which 25 studies (n=5662) reported the prevalence of PIM , and PIP , ( ), while 14 (n=2702) and 6 studies (n=1342) reported the prevalence of PDDI , and PPO, respectively ( ). All studies used a cross-sectional study design. All studies reported origins of the region where the study was conducted; 16 studies from the Amhara region, 7 from Addis Ababa, 5 from Oromia, 2 from Harar and 1 from Tigray ( ).
 

## Prevalence of PIP
25 studies were included in the meta-analysis of PIP, and the pooled prevalence of PIP was found 41% (33%–48%). We found a significant study heterogeneity among studies with I2=96.87%, p<0.01. The lowest prevalence of PIP among included studies was 7% (4%–13%) while the highest prevalence was 80% (68%–88%) ( ). Subgroup analyses by publication year showed a significant increase in the pooled prevalence over time, from 24% in studies published before 2015 to 38% in studies published between 2015 and 2020, and 48% in studies published after 2020, but high heterogeneity persists within subgroups (I2>95%). Additionally, the pooled estimates were significantly different across different regions, ranging from 23% to 56%. However, substantial heterogeneity persists within subgroups (I2>88%) ( ).
 

## Prevalence of PIM
25 studies were used to determine the prevalence of PIM. The pooled prevalence of PIM was 37% (31%–44%). We found high heterogeneity of studies evidenced with I2 value of 96.33%, p<0.001 ( ).
 In subgroup analyses, we found that the pooled prevalence of PIM in the Tigray region was 29% (21%–36%), and in the Harar region 19% (15%–23%), which were significantly different from the overall pooled prevalence of PIM. Additionally, subgroup analysis revealed that the prevalence of PIM increases over time, from 24% in studies published before 2015 to 32% in studies published between 2015 and 2020 and 34% in studies published after 2020. The test of heterogeneity between subgroups was high (I2>95%, p<0.01) ( ).

## Prevalence of PPO
Six studies were included in meta-analyses of PPO and we found 14% (6%–24%) pooled prevalence of PPO. High heterogeneity was detected among the included studies (I2=95.71%, p<0.001) ( ).
 Our subgroup analyses showed a PPO prevalence of 27% (95% CI 23% to 32%) in the inpatient setting, which was higher than 8% (95% CI 4% to 13%) observed in the outpatient setting. Moreover, the prevalence of PPO varied across different regions of Ethiopia, ranging from 4% (95% CI 3% to 7%) in Harar, the lowest, to 18% (95% CI 5% to 38%) in the Amhara region, the highest. Differences were also noted by sample size and publication year. Studies with smaller sample sizes reported a prevalence of 16% (95% CI 7% to 27%), whereas those with larger sample sizes reported a prevalence of 4% (95% CI 3% to 7%). Studies published between 2015 and 2020 showed a prevalence of 27% (95% CI 23% to 32%), which is higher than a prevalence of 8% (95% CI 4% to 13%), which was reported from studies published after 2020. For all cases, the heterogeneity test between subgroups was significant ( ).
 

## Prevalence of PDDI
The pooled prevalence of potential drug–drug interactions was 55% (95% CI 36% to 73%) from 14 included studies. We detected high heterogeneity among included studies (I2=99.00%, p<0.001) ( ).
 In subgroup analyses, we found that the prevalence of PDDI checked by drug interaction checkers such as Beer’s criteria 6% (4%–9%), detection method not mentioned 11% (8%–15%), Micromedex 54% (95% CI 36% to 72%), Medscape 69% (95% CI 43% to 90%) and other 90% (95% CI 86% to 92%) were significantly different from the overall pooled prevalence of PDDI. Furthermore, studies conducted in the Oromia region reported a prevalence of 73% (95% CI 65% to 79%), and studies involving participants aged 65 years and above reported a prevalence of 29% (95% CI 11% to 50%), both of which were significantly different from the overall pooled prevalence of PDDI. Additionally, subgroup analysis based on study quality showed that studies classified as having a moderate risk of bias according to the Hoy tool reported a higher prevalence of PDDI than those studies with a low risk of bias (p<0.01) ( ).

## Factors associated with PIP, PIM and PDDI
Concerning factors associated with the prevalence of PIP, we found that the presence of hypertension (adjusted odds ratio (AOR)=2.46, 95% CI 1.38 to 4.36) and polypharmacy (AOR=3.76, 95% CI 2.53 to 5.46) is significantly associated with the prevalence of PIP. However, age was not significantly associated with the prevalence of PIP ( ).
Regarding meta-analyses of factors associated with prevalence of PIM, polypharmacy (AOR=4.20, 95% CI 2.91 to 6.06) was significantly associated with PIM, whereas presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus and older age were not significantly associated in our meta-analyses ( ).
Concerning associated factors, only polypharmacy (AOR=4.51, 95% CI 3.05 to 6.69) was significantly associated with the prevalence of PDDI ( ).

## Meta-regression
Sources of heterogeneity were further explored by identifying relevant study-level covariates and conducting meta-regression analyses. Random-effects meta-regression was performed using the D-L method to estimate between-study variance. The following covariates were examined for PIP and PIM: sample size, publication year, aetiology of the health condition and study region.
The publication year showed a statistically significant association with the prevalence of both PIP and PIM and was therefore considered a study-level determinant of prevalence. In regional analyses, studies conducted in the Harar region demonstrated a significant association with PIM prevalence, whereas no significant association was observed for PIP. Studies from other regions did not significantly moderate PIM prevalence.
For PDDIs, sample size, the drug-interaction screening tool used and the Hoy risk-of-bias level were significantly associated with prevalence estimates. In contrast, publication year showed a significant association with the prevalence of PPOs. Detailed meta-regression results are presented in .
 

## Quality assessment score
The risk of bias of the included studies was shown in ). The NOS was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies, and the quality scores were very good in eighteen studies, good in one study and satisfactory in thirteen studies. We also assessed the risk of bias of the included studies using Hoy et al risk-of-bias assessment tool for prevalence studies. According to this tool, we found 23 studies have low risk of bias, while 9 studies have moderate risk of bias. On the other hand, the certainty of evidence was done using the GRADE approach, based on Cochrane recommendation for certainty of evidence evaluation for each outcome and was found to be low ( ).

## Sensitivity analysis
The robustness of the results of the meta-analyses was checked by performing further sensitivity analyses using two methods. In these analyses, there were no significant differences between the fixed-effect model results and random-effect model results ( ). We also performed ‘leave-one-out’ procedure analyses, and the analysis results showed that the pooled prevalence of PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI was not significantly influenced by removing each study ( ).

## Publication bias
In our meta-analysis, we assessed publication bias using funnel plots ( ), which did not indicate significant asymmetry. Additionally, Egger’s test for PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI showed no statistically significant publication bias (p=0.223, 0.073, 0.1623 and 0.5089, respectively). Furthermore, we performed trim and fill analysis to assess potential missing studies, adjusting prevalence estimates for publication bias. Although the Egger’s test did not detect publication bias, we found asymmetry of studies in the trim and fill funnel plot, which shows a moderate publication bias ( ).

## Discussion
PIP is a major public health concern, as it increases the risk of adverse drug reactions and health complications, particularly among older adults. It includes prescribing medications that pose a higher risk than benefits, particularly when safer or more effective alternatives exist. PIP also encompasses inappropriate dosages or durations, drug–drug and drug–disease interactions that increase the likelihood of harmful effects, and the omission of potentially beneficial prescription of medications. The burden of PIP varies across studies, necessitating a comprehensive analysis to quantify the overall prevalence of PIP and identify associated factors. Beers and STOP/START criteria of PIP and PIM screening tools are among the key factors that influence PIP or PIM prevalence. Beer’s criteria are medication oriented and consist of a list of medications to be avoided in older people without detailed clinical contexts, in contrast to the STOP criteria. Therefore, Beer’s criteria tend to report a higher prevalence of PIM than the STOP criteria when used alone. On the other hand, when STOP and START criteria are used together, as it assesses under prescription/prescription omissions, it reports more PIP prevalence than Beer’s criteria. The findings of the present review help to develop targeted strategy and clinical guidelines to minimise the occurrence of PIP and to improve health outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first comprehensive meta-analysis to determine the pooled prevalence of PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI, as well as their associated factors in Ethiopia.
In our review, we found a pooled PIP prevalence of 41% (95% CI 33% to 48%) and it varied across regions of Ethiopia where the highest was found in Oromia region (56%, 95% CI 22% to 87%), while the lowest was found in Harar (23%, 95% CI 19% to 27%) (p<0.01). Additionally, in subgroup analysis, we found a significant difference between prevalence of PIP among studies published in different publication year categories (p<0.01). Studies published before 2015, between 2015 and 2020 and studies published after 2020, reported 24% (22%–26%), 38% (25%–53%) and 46% (37%–56%), respectively. Concerning the overall burden of PIP, our finding was higher than the finding of the meta-analysis by Liew et al (33.3%, 95% CI 29.7% to 37.0%), and Amrouch et al (35%, 95% CI 30% to 40%). 
We found PIM pooled prevalence of 37% (95% CI 31% to 44%) from studies included for quantitative synthesis. In subgroup analysis, the highest pooled prevalence of PIM was observed in Oromia region (50%, 95% CI 18% to 81%), while the lowest was in Harar region (19%, 95% CI 15% to 23%). Additionally, we found that the prevalence of PIM in Tigray (29%, 95% CI 22% to 37%) and Harar region (19%, 95% CI 15% to 23%) was significantly different from the overall pooled prevalence of PIM. We also showed that the pooled prevalence of PIM from studies published before 2015 (24%, 95% CI 22% to 26%) was significantly different from the overall estimate of PIM. Our point estimate of PIM is in line with the findings of previous studies, which reported 36.7% (33.4%–40%) and 37% (23%–51%). However, our finding is lower than the findings from other previous studies by Mekonnen et al reported 47% (95% CI 37% to 56%) and Praxedes et al reported 65% (95% CI 28.7% to 95.3%). 
Point estimate of PPO was 14% (95% CI 6% to 24%) in our meta-analysis and in subgroup analyses, we observed significant variation in point estimate of PPO accross care settings and regions, where findings from inpatient setting (27%, 95% CI 23% to 32%), and Harar (4%, 95% CI 3% to 7%) differed from the overall pooled estimate of PPO. Additionally, pooled estimates varied over various publication years and sample sizes (p<0.01). Our pooled estimate of PPO is lower than the finding of a previous study where 55% (95% CI 46% to 64%) PPO was reported. 
Regarding PDDI, 55% (95% CI 36% to 73%) pooled prevalence was found from the included primary studies. Pooled estimates of PDDI based on outcome assessment tools, study regions, publication years, Hoy risk-of-bias status and age subgroups analyses showed significant differences. Point estimates of PDDI prevalence varied significantly by the assessment tool used, with higher prevalence reported using guidelines and Drugs.com (90%, 95% CI 86% to 92%) compared with the AGS Beers Criteria (6%, 95% CI 4% to 9%) and unspecified tools (11%, 95% CI 8% to 15%), differing significantly from the overall pooled prevalence. Concerning finding based on regions, only the point estimate of PDDI from the Oromia region (73%, 95% CI 65% to 79%) was significantly different from the overall estimate, while in the age group of 65 years or more, the prevalence of PDDI (29%, 95% CI 11% to 50%) showed significant difference from the overall prevalence of PDDI. Our point estimate of PDDI is higher than the finding of the study by Hughes et al reported 28.8% (95% CI 19.3% to 40.7%), whereas it was lower than the study result reported 50.69% (95% CI 18.77% to 82.63%) by Alemayehu et al. 
Variation in pooled prevalence of PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI in our study from previous meta-analyses can arise from differences in methodology, study population characteristics and healthcare system. The prevalence of PIP is expected to be lower in healthcare systems where there is strong pharmacovigilance, deprescribing initiatives and practice of prescription review. Additionally, PIP may be lower in community settings, outpatient care settings and primary care settings compared with inpatient care and tertiary care settings. Moreover, the extent of comorbidities and polypharmacy can increase the likelihood of PIP. 
Various factors are known to influence the occurrence of PIP. In the included studies, some reported that factors such as length of hospital stay, presence of comorbidities, heart failure, acute coronary syndrome as the reason for admission and the use of antiplatelet only antithrombotic therapy were associated with PIP. However, these factors were not pooled in the meta-analysis, as insufficient number of studies reported them. In the meta-analysis, hypertension and polypharmacy maintained their association with PIP. This finding is in line with findings of previous meta-analyses. 
Regarding the meta-analysis of factors associated with PIM, we analysed advanced age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and polypharmacy, but only polypharmacy showed a significant association with PIM. While this finding supports the previous meta-analysis, which reported the significant association of polypharmacy with PIM, it contradicts the same study’s finding for advanced age. Our systematic review identified several additional factors associated with PIM in individual studies, including heart failure, thrombocytopenia, ischaemic stroke, peripheral neuropathy, deep vein thrombosis, lack of interest in activities, cognitive impairment, health deterioration and the use of mobility aids. However, we did not pool these factors as each was reported by only a single study.
Our study also showed that polypharmacy had a significant association with PDDI. This finding aligns with previous meta-analysis reporting that polypharmacy increases the risk of PDDI. Moreover, our systematic review found several additional factors associated with PDDI in individual studies, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, occupational status of retired, and length of hospital stay of greater than 7 days. However, a pooled OR was not conducted on these factors as each was reported only in a single study.
The variations among meta-analyses of factors associated with PIP, PIM and PDDI are multifactorial. These may arise from differences in study selection criteria, population characteristics (eg, age distribution, presence of comorbidities) and healthcare settings (hospital vs community-based studies, outpatient vs inpatient-based studies). Additionally, variations in outcome definitions (such as differing criteria for PIP), regional prescribing practices, medication availability and local disease burden can substantially influence results. Such methodological and contextual differences may limit the direct comparison of findings and may bring inconsistencies in the factors identified as significantly associated across meta-analyses.
The prevalence of PIM was found to be high in Africa and lower-income countries. It has been reported to cause more healthcare utilisation, medication non-adherence, drug addictions, drug interactions, adverse drug reactions, hospitalisation, morbidity and mortality. Therefore, it is essential to strengthen the medication review system, educational strategy and deprescribing initiatives to minimise PIM and improve patient outcomes. 
To our knowledge, this review is the first to provide a comprehensive national analysis of PIP, PIM, PPO and PDDI in Ethiopia. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the included studies were cross-sectional, limiting causal inference. Second, sex-based analysis was not performed because primary studies did not report outcomes separately for females and males. Third, the absence of studies from some regions of Ethiopia may limit the generalisability of the findings at the national level. Finally, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies; however, we conducted sensitivity analyses, including subgroup analyses, leave-one-out analyses and comparison of random-effects and fixed-effects models, to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. Furthermore, funnel plot, Egger’s test and Trim and Fill analyses were performed to assess publication bias where Egger’s tests were not significant despite visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry and missed studies imputed using Trim and Fill methods showed publication bias. Still, the high heterogeneity is being maintained, but we found the findings are consistent via leave-one-out analysis and using fixed and random-effect models. The source of heterogeneity of each outcome was examined using meta-regression analysis and in part heterogeneity for PIP, PIM, PPO was explained by publication years, while heterogeneity of PDDI was explained by sample size, drug interaction screening tools and Hoy risk-of-bias level. Moreover, to some extent, PIP and PIM heterogeneity was explained by study regions. However, in all outcomes, heterogeneity persists even after adjusting for covariates in meta regression, indicating the presence of unexplained sources of heterogeneity of the findings. The existence of high heterogeneity and low certainty of evidence for each outcome of the included studies warrants careful interpretation of the findings of this review.

## Conclusion
This meta-analysis revealed a high pooled prevalence of PIP, PIP and PDDI among older adults in Ethiopia, with a comparatively lower prevalence of PPO. Polypharmacy was significantly associated with PIP, PIM and PDDI, while hypertension also showed a significant association with PIP in our meta-analysis. Although most included studies showed good methodological quality and low risk of bias, the overall certainty of evidence was low, as findings derived from cross-sectional designs with substantial heterogeneity. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted with caution. This review underscores the considerable burden of inappropriate medication prescribing and its key associated factors in older adults, highlighting the need for further high-quality and context-specific study to better characterise the prescribing patterns and their determinants in Ethiopia.
