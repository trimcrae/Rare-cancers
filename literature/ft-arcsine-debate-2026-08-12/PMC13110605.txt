## Abstract

## Objectives
To evaluate the effects of nurse-led shared decision-making (SDM) on lung cancer screening outcomes, including low-dose CT (LDCT) uptake, benign findings, early cancer detection and willingness to participate among high-risk populations.

## Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis.

## Data sources
PubMed, Medline via OvidSP, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, EMBASE via OvidSP, Web of Science, Scopus, grey literature databases and clinical trial registries were searched from inception to March 2025.

## Eligibility criteria
Studies evaluating nurse-led SDM interventions in high-risk lung cancer populations, reporting outcomes including LDCT uptake rates, screening results (Lung-RADS (Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System) classifications), early-stage cancer detection or willingness to participate. Randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies and observational studies were included.

## Data extraction and synthesis
Two reviewers independently extracted data and assessed risk of bias using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (for non-randomised studies) and Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (for randomised controlled trials). Meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models. Meta-regression explored sources of heterogeneity.

## Results
13 studies (n=13 608 participants) were included, comprising 10 single-arm studies and three comparative studies. In single-arm studies without control groups, nurse-led SDM programmes achieved a pooled LDCT uptake rate of 98% (95% CI 28% to 100%; I²=99%), and willingness to participate was 68% (95% CI 24% to 93%; I²=98%). In comparative studies, nurse-led SDM showed no significant difference in LDCT uptake compared with usual care (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.02; I²=0%), suggesting non-inferiority rather than superiority. Among individuals who completed screening, 81% (95% CI 77% to 85%) had benign or low-risk findings (Lung-RADS [Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System] I/II), and 2% (95% CI 1% to 3%) were diagnosed with early-stage lung cancer, rates consistent with benchmark screening trials. Meta-regression identified female sex as positively associated with uptake (β=0.54, p<0.001), while current tobacco use was negatively associated (β=−0.37, p=0.033). The risk of bias was moderate to serious across studies.

## Conclusions
Comparative evidence suggests that nurse-led SDM achieves equivalent LDCT uptake to standard care approaches, indicating feasibility as an alternative service delivery model. However, the predominance of single-arm studies, high heterogeneity and moderate-to-serious risk of bias limit causal inference. High uptake rates in single-arm studies likely reflect selection bias rather than intervention effectiveness. Current evidence supports the feasibility but not the superiority of nurse-led SDM. Well-designed randomised controlled trials are needed to establish comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness before recommending widespread integration of nurse-led SDM into lung cancer screening programmes.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
Comprehensive search of six databases and grey literature sources with no language or date restrictions minimised the risk of missing relevant studies.
Rigorous dual-reviewer processes for study selection, data extraction and risk of bias assessment enhanced methodological reliability.
Meta-regression analysis systematically explored sources of heterogeneity and identified key factors influencing screening outcomes.
The predominance of single-arm studies and quasi-experimental designs limited the ability to draw causal inferences about intervention effectiveness.
High heterogeneity in intervention components and outcome measurements across studies restricted the comparability and generalisability of pooled estimates.

## Introduction
 Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with approximately 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths reported in 2022. 5-year survival rates vary dramatically by stage at diagnosis, exceeding 90% for stage I disease but falling below 10% for stage IV, underscoring the critical importance of early detection. Low-dose CT (LDCT) screening has demonstrated 20%–24% mortality reductions in high-risk populations in landmark trials, including the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and the Dutch-Belgian Nederlands–Leuvens Longkanker Screenings Onderzoek (NELSON) trial. However, despite robust evidence supporting its efficacy, real-world uptake remains disappointingly low, ranging from 10% to 33% globally (approximately 18% in the USA vs 10% in China). This substantial gap between screening efficacy and implementation effectiveness represents a major missed opportunity for lung cancer prevention.
Multiple barriers at the system, clinician and patient levels contribute to suboptimal LDCT uptake. System-level barriers include complex referral pathways, limited access to screening facilities (particularly in rural areas), inadequate reimbursement mechanisms and a lack of standardised protocols for follow-up of screen-detected nodules. At the clinician level, knowledge gaps regarding eligibility criteria and screening guidelines, time constraints during clinical encounters, competing clinical priorities and uncertainty about how to communicate benefits and harms of screening limit effective engagement with eligible patients. Patient-level barriers are equally substantial and include low awareness of screening availability, inadequate health literacy, psychological factors such as fear of cancer diagnosis and fatalism, smoking-related stigma and self-blame, concerns about radiation exposure and logistical challenges including cost, transportation and time off work. Importantly, these barriers operate interactively rather than in isolation: system-level constraints shape provider behaviour, while patient-level factors reflect not merely knowledge gaps but psychological, social and structural constraints strongly patterned by race, socioeconomic status and geography. Addressing this complex web of barriers requires multifaceted interventions that extend beyond simple awareness campaigns.
Shared decision-making (SDM) is a patient-centred approach that facilitates collaborative decision-making between clinicians and patients. This process ensures that informed choices are made based on clinical evidence, patient preferences and values. Given the complexity of LDCT screening, encompassing both potential benefits (mortality reduction) and harms (false positives, overdiagnosis and radiation exposure), the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) issued a grade B recommendation for lung cancer screening (LCS) with LDCT in older adults with significant smoking histories and emphasised the necessity of SDM counselling prior to screening. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) initiated coverage of LCS in 2015 and mandated that all referred patients receive SDM counselling from a physician or qualified non-physician practitioner, using at least one decision aid (DA). 
However, empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that SDM as envisioned by guidelines is rare, low-fidelity and poorly documented in real-world LDCT programmes, even a decade after CMS coverage. The reasons behind this gap are complex and multifaceted. Time constraints in clinical settings represent a primary barrier, with short visits and competing preventive and chronic care priorities limiting opportunities for meaningful SDM conversations. Fragmented care pathways further exacerbate this problem: primary care physicians often order LDCT, yet centralised programmes or radiology departments manage follow-up, complicating SDM coordination across care settings. Additionally, underserved populations face compounded barriers, including lower awareness, limited health literacy, cost and transportation challenges and distrust of health systems; DAs and SDM workflows are rarely codesigned with these communities. Decision quality metrics (knowledge thresholds, values-choice concordance and decisional regret) are used inconsistently across studies, and few investigations report how SDM affects long-term adherence, downstream testing rates or net benefit in a risk-stratified manner. Standalone DAs improve knowledge but do not guarantee values-congruent decisions; pairing DAs with patient navigators, telehealth sessions and electronic health record (EHR)-embedded workflows shows greater promise. Overall, the field has evolved from DAs’ development to system-level implementation research, but the central challenge remains how to deliver truly informed, values-congruent decisions about a complex, risk-sensitive screening test within constrained, heterogeneous real-world settings.
Patient navigators are individuals who assist patients, families and caregivers in navigating the health system and overcoming its barriers. Navigators play a vital role in promoting LCS, particularly among high-risk patients, by facilitating procedures, appropriate management and follow-up of screen-detected nodules. Evidence has demonstrated that the implementation of patient navigation constitutes a strategy for surmounting patient-level and system-level barriers within LCS. Navigation services can be delivered by various healthcare professionals, including nurses, social workers, lay health workers and peer supporters. Saab et al report that nurse navigation, SDM interventions and electronic prompts improve LCS referral and related outcomes; however, in their systematic review, nurse navigators were components of multifaceted interventions, and their specific impact was not isolated. Similarly, Lin et al found that interventions incorporating navigation elements achieved wide-ranging screening completion rates (3.6%–98.8%), but the specific effects of nurse navigators were not separately measured. Recent qualitative and descriptive studies have identified nurses as uniquely positioned to facilitate SDM, given their extended patient interaction time, expertise in patient education and counselling and inherent role as trusted patient advocates. Nurse-led initiatives employ diverse methodologies, including education, patient reminders, counselling, navigation and integrated multicomponent approaches and have been shown to enhance cancer detection outcomes, improve cancer knowledge, strengthen screening-related beliefs and increase detection of precancerous lesions. The multifaceted roles that nurses assume as coordinators, consultants, educators, navigators, practitioners and researchers position them well to deliver comprehensive SDM for LCS.  The evidence demonstrating that SDM tools and navigation improve decisions and behaviours is robust; however, nurse-specific effects remain largely invisible in aggregated data. A substantial body of SDM-focused LCS research encompassing DA, decision coaching and navigation clearly demonstrates benefits for knowledge, decisional conflict and screening behaviour but rarely isolates nurse-specific contributions. Studies exploring nurse-led SDM vary considerably in target population, intervention modality, DAs employed and intervention duration, yet no comprehensive synthesis of this evidence exists. Critically, no identified studies directly compare nurse-led SDM to physician-led SDM on key outcomes, including screening uptake, values-choice concordance or decisional conflict. This gap highlights the field’s lack of evidence regarding whether nurse-delivered SDM is equally effective, more acceptable or more efficient than physician-delivered SDM in LCS and under what conditions such models may be most beneficial. By systematically evaluating existing evidence, this review aims to address these knowledge gaps and inform evidence-based guidelines regarding the role of nurses in LCS programmes. summarises study types, nursing centrality and SDM specificity in previous nurse-led programmes.
 

## Methods

## Protocol registration
This systematic review was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420251033595). The full protocol is available at the registry.

## Search strategy
Comprehensive search strategies were developed for each database and executed from inception to March 2025. Six electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Medline via OvidSP, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, EMBASE via OvidSP, Web of Science and Scopus. Grey literature was searched using OpenGrey (http://www.opengrey.eu), ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov) and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (https://www.who.int/ictrp/search/en/). Reference lists of included studies were manually searched for additional relevant publications. No date or language restrictions were applied. Search terms are presented in , and detailed search strategies with results for each database are provided in .

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Studies were included if they evaluated the effects of nurse-led SDM on LCS outcomes. Eligibility was determined using the PICOS framework: (i) Participants: adults at high risk for lung cancer, as defined by the eligibility criteria referenced in each study. These typically included: USPSTF criteria (aged 50–80 years with ≥20 pack-year smoking history, currently smoking or quit within the past 15 years); NLST criteria (aged 55–74 years with ≥30 pack-year smoking history) or CMS criteria (aged 55–77 years meeting similar smoking requirements). Studies involving patients with a prior lung cancer diagnosis were excluded. (ii) Interventions: nurse-led SDM interventions were defined as programmes in which nurses (including registered nurses, nurse navigators, nurse practitioners or clinical nurse specialists) served as the primary facilitator of SDM discussions with autonomous decision-making authority. Interventions in which nurses played only a supportive or assisting role were excluded. (iii) Comparators: studies comparing nurse-led SDM to usual care (standard clinical practice without structured SDM), physician-led SDM, no intervention or historical controls were included. Single-arm studies without comparators were also included for descriptive synthesis. (iv) Outcomes: studies reporting at least one of the following outcomes were included. The primary outcome was LDCT uptake rate. Secondary outcomes included knowledge of LCS, level of decisional conflict, willingness to participate in screening, proportion of benign findings (Lung-RADS I/II) and early-stage lung cancer detection rate. (v) Study designs: randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies (including pre-post designs), prospective and retrospective cohort studies and other interventional studies were eligible. Case reports, editorials, protocols, conference abstracts without full data and qualitative studies were excluded.

## Study selection
Study selection was conducted in three stages. First, all retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Boston, USA), and duplicates were removed. Second, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against inclusion criteria. Third, full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed by both reviewers. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer.

## Data extraction
Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardised extraction form developed for this review and pilot-tested on a random sample of included studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The following information was extracted: author name; publication year; study design (prospective vs retrospective, interventional vs observational and type of control group); country and study setting (community-based, hospital-based or hybrid); study period; participant characteristics (sample size, eligibility criteria, mean age, proportion of women, proportion of current tobacco users, proportion of racial/ethnic minorities and insurance coverage); types of nurses involved; intervention characteristics (type, content, format, duration and delivery process) and outcome data. For studies reporting multiple intervention arms, data from the nurse-led intervention arm were extracted.

## Risk of bias assessment
Two reviewers independently assessed risk of bias. For randomised controlled trials, the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool was used, which evaluates five domains: randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of reported results. Each domain was judged as low risk, some concerns or high risk of bias. An overall risk of bias judgement was derived according to the RoB 2.0 algorithm. The Cochrane-recommended Excel tool was used for implementation. For non-randomised studies, the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used, which evaluates seven domains: confounding, classification of interventions, selection of participants, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes and selection of reported results. Each domain was judged as low, moderate, serious or critical risk of bias. An overall judgement was derived according to the ROBINS-I algorithm: low risk (low risk in all domains except confounding, which may be moderate); moderate risk (at least one domain at moderate risk but none at serious or critical risk); serious risk (at least one domain at serious risk or multiple domains at moderate risk) or critical risk (at least one domain at critical risk or multiple domains at serious risk). Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer.

## Data synthesis
Meta-analyses were conducted using STATA 22.0 software. The DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was employed to account for between-study heterogeneity. For proportion data (uptake rates, Lung-RADS I/II proportions, cancer detection rates), we applied the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilise variances before pooling. Meta-regression analyses were conducted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Studies reporting zero events in one arm were handled using a continuity correction of 0.5 added to all cells.
Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and quantified using the I² statistic. A Q test p value <0.10 indicated significant heterogeneity. I² values of <25%, 25–50% and >50% were considered to represent low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively. Given anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity, the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was used for all pooled analyses. Studies with zero events in one arm were handled using a continuity correction of 0.5 added to all cells. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding each study to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates. Outcomes that could not be quantitatively pooled were summarised descriptively.
Meta-regression analyses were conducted using REML estimation to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The following covariates were pre-specified based on clinical relevance and data availability: (i) current tobacco use: proportion of participants identified as current tobacco users at baseline, coded as a continuous variable (0–1). (ii) female proportion: proportion of female participants, coded as a continuous variable (0–1). (iii) Guideline used for eligibility: categorised as USPSTF (coded 1), NLST (coded 2) or CMS (coded 3). Studies using modified or combined criteria were coded according to the primary referenced guideline. (iv) Racial/ethnic minority proportion: proportion of participants self-identifying as non-White, coded as a continuous variable (0–1). Studies not reporting race/ethnicity data were excluded from analyses involving this covariate. (v) Insurance coverage: proportion of participants with health insurance at baseline, coded as a continuous variable (0–1). Studies conducted in universal healthcare systems (eg, UK) were coded as 1.0. For covariates reported as ranges, the midpoint was used. For covariates reported only for subgroups, weighted averages were calculated where possible.

## Patient and public involvement
Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

## Results

## Search results
A total of 589 records were identified through database searching (n=559), trial registers (n=29) and citation searching (n=1). After removing 511 duplicates, 18 records flagged by automation tools and 16 records excluded for other reasons, 44 records underwent title and abstract screening. Following this initial screening, 26 records were excluded, leaving 18 reports for full-text assessment. Of these, five were excluded for the following reasons: LCS was only part of a broader intervention (n=2), full text was unavailable (n=1), and nurses did not serve in a leadership role (n=2). Ultimately, 13 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the review ( ).
 

## Study characteristics
13 studies evaluating nurse-led LCS programmes were included. Six studies employed pretest/post-test designs, one was a randomised controlled trial, one was an observational study, one used a mixed-methods design, and three were classified as interventional studies. With regard to comparison, one compared outcomes with the NLST trial, one used general population data, and four were single-arm studies without comparators. 12 studies were conducted in the USA and one in the UK.

Study periods ranged from 2013 to 2024. Four studies were conducted in community-based settings (including primary care clinics, community health centres and mobile screening units), while the remaining nine were conducted in hospital-based academic medical centres. Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from 40 participants (HIV-positive population) to 2012 participants (UK community trial). Participants had a median age range of 55–77 years, with female proportions ranging from 4% to 54%, current tobacco use proportions from 61% to 88% and insurance coverage from 0% to 100%. Several studies specifically included ethnic minorities (African American, Hispanic and other groups).
Study populations varied across included studies, encompassing underserved communities, veterans, rural residents and individuals living with HIV. Most programmes aligned with established screening guidelines: NLST criteria (n=5), USPSTF criteria (n=5) or CMS criteria (n=3), though specific inclusion criteria varied. For example, Hoffman et al adopted the updated USPSTF age range (50–80 years) and reduced smoking threshold (≥20 pack-years), reflecting evolving evidence on risk stratification. Conversely, Raghavan et al excluded adults aged ≥65 years due to Medicare coverage limitations, highlighting systemic barriers in underserved populations.

## Characteristics of intervention
Nurses assumed multifaceted roles across programmes, including nurse navigators (n=5), programme coordinators (n=2), nurse practitioners (n=4) and registered nurses without specified advanced roles (n=2). Core responsibilities typically included: (i) patient identification through EHR queries to flag eligible individuals; (ii) education and SDM through structured counselling sessions using DAs (eg, VA-developed booklets, HIV-tailored tools); (iii) care coordination including scheduling LDCT scans, tracking results and facilitating multidisciplinary follow-up and (iv) smoking cessation support through integrated counselling or referrals to quitlines. Interventions typically combined proactive outreach (telephone calls, mailed materials) with process optimisation. Digital tools enhanced programme scalability and precision. 12 of 13 studies incorporated smoking cessation support, recognising its dual role in risk reduction and screening adherence. Strategies included direct counselling by nurse practitioners or navigators during SDM visits, referrals to external services such as VA Quit Lines or pharmacists and provision of tailored educational materials addressing stigmatisation and fatalism. Rosario et al and Strong et al highlighted the navigator’s role in reinforcing cessation messages during follow-up, suggesting that sustained engagement may improve quit rates.

## Risk of bias of included studies
Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool for the 12 non-randomised studies and RoB 2.0 for the single randomised trial. Assessment results revealed variability across studies ( ). For Domain 1 (confounding), three studies exhibited serious risk due to inadequate control for confounding variables, while four studies had moderate risk. For Domain 2 (classification of interventions), one study was rated as serious risk due to potential misclassification, one as low risk and nine as moderate risk. For Domain 3 (selection of participants), moderate risk was observed in two studies, while all others were rated low risk. For Domain 4 (deviations from intended interventions), five studies demonstrated moderate risk, often linked to protocol deviations. For Domain 5 (missing data), one study was rated serious risk due to high attrition, two were low risk and eight were moderate risk. For Domain 6 (measurement of outcomes), moderate risk was identified in two studies due to reliance on non-validated instruments, while all others were low risk. For Domain 7 (selection of reported results), five studies showed moderate risk due to incomplete reporting of prespecified outcomes, and one was assessed as serious risk. 
 Overall, three studies were classified as having a serious risk of bias and were classified as having a serious overall risk of bias, while the remaining nine demonstrated moderate risk. No studies achieved a low overall risk. ## Meta-analysis of the uptake of LDCT screening
Five single-arm studies, encompassing 1279 participants, evaluated LDCT uptake among eligible individuals. Using a random-effects model, the pooled uptake rate was 0.98 (95% CI 0.28 to 1.00), indicating high adherence to screening in these programme contexts ( ). However, significant heterogeneity was observed (I²=99%, p<0.001), necessitating further exploration.
 Sensitivity analyses demonstrated stable results (range: 0.57–1.04). Meta-regression analysis, adjusted for current tobacco use, female proportion, screening guideline used and proportion of racial/ethnic minorities, explained 83.2% of heterogeneity (R²=0.832). Female proportion was positively associated with LDCT uptake (β=0.543, 95% CI 0.253 to 0.833, p<0.001), while current tobacco use demonstrated a negative association (β=−0.372, 95% CI −0.713 to −0.031, p=0.033). Other covariates were not significantly associated with uptake (p>0.05). presents complete meta-regression results.
Three comparative studies compared LDCT uptake between nurse-led SDM interventions (n=755) and control conditions (n=678). Control conditions included usual care without structured SDM, standard clinic-based screening pathways and historical cohort comparisons. Using a random-effects model, the pooled relative risk was 1.00 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.03), indicating no significant difference in uptake between groups ( ). Heterogeneity was negligible (I²=0%, p=0.73).

## Screening outcomes: proportion of Lung-RADS I and II findings
Five studies comprising 913 participants evaluated the proportion of individuals classified as Lung-RADS I or II (benign or low-risk findings) following LDCT. The pooled proportion was 0.81 (95% CI: 0.77 to 0.85), consistent with benchmark rates from major screening trials ( ). Significant heterogeneity was observed (I²=82.7%, p<0.001). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated stable results.
 Meta-regression analysis explained 67.8% of between-study variance (R²=0.678). Female proportion was significantly associated with higher Lung-RADS I/II rates (β=0.174, 95% CI 0.021 to 0.327, p=0.026). Three studies evaluated willingness to participate in LDCT screening. The pooled effect size was 0.66 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.86), with extreme heterogeneity (I²=97.7%, p<0.001) ( ). This substantial heterogeneity likely reflects variability in how willingness was operationalised and measured across studies, including differences in assessment timing (pre- vs post-SDM), measurement instruments (validated scales vs single-item questions) and response formats. Results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated stable results (range: 0.36–1.03) ( ).

## Discussion
Our key findings can be summarised in three points. First, comparative studies demonstrated that nurse-led SDM achieved equivalent LDCT uptake to standard approaches (RR=1.00), representing the most robust evidence from this review. Second, single-arm studies showed high uptake rates, though these estimates are likely inflated by selection bias and multicomponent intervention designs. Third, meta-regression consistently identified female sex and current smoking status as significant predictors of screening participation across outcomes.
The implementation of SDM in LCS varies considerably across healthcare systems, reflecting differences in policy frameworks, healthcare financing and cultural contexts. In the USA, CMS reimbursement mandates have incentivised SDM implementation, although compliance and the quality of SDM encounters remain variable. European countries are increasingly incorporating SDM principles into LCS pilot programmes; for instance, the UK’s Lung Health Check programmes employ targeted invitation strategies with embedded SDM components, adapting communication materials for diverse population groups. In contrast, SDM implementation in many Asian healthcare systems is generally considered less developed. In China, where lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer mortality with over 700 000 annual deaths, national screening programmes are expanding through initiatives such as the cancer screening programme in Urban China and rural cancer screening initiatives. However, systematic SDM is largely absent from Chinese clinical guidelines and routine practice. A scoping review including 60 studies concluded that SDM in mainland China is ‘in its infancy’, with research and practice heavily skewed towards decisional needs and small decision-aid projects rather than routine implementation or outcome evaluation. Furthermore, newer empirical work indicates that full, multi-step SDM (encompassing clear options, balanced presentation of benefits and harms, preference elicitation, deliberation and joint agreement) is rare, with most practice resembling enhanced informed consent rather than comprehensive SDM. By 2021, mainland China had very few feasibility or implementation studies, no standardised DA development process and limited theory-guided interventions. This contrasts sharply with the US requirement for SDM in LDCT reimbursement, as Chinese LDCT practice shows little SDM awareness or institutionalisation. SDM is seldom incorporated into required training or quality metrics, unlike in many Western programmes where it is at least nominally embedded in guidelines, curricula and payment schemes. Cultural factors in Asian contexts present both challenges and opportunities for SDM adaptation. The traditional model of physician authority in clinical decision-making, preferences for family-centred over individual-centred health decisions and communication norms favouring indirect expression may necessitate culturally tailored SDM approaches rather than direct adoption of Western frameworks. Taken together, China appears to be approximately a decade behind leading Western countries in transitioning SDM from an imported ideal to a routinely supported, system-level expectation. 
SDM is defined as a multistep process that includes balanced information on benefits, harms, deliberation and explicit integration of patient values and preferences, not mere education or consent. Clinician SDM skills are often weak, and there can be tension between CMS documentation requirements and genuinely patient-centred conversations. Nurse-led telehealth models and NP-led programmes that explicitly integrate preference elicitation and structured DAs are closer to this higher SDM standard. Nurses’ communication time and relational continuity make them good candidates to bridge the gap between mandated SDM checklists and substantive, patient-centred deliberation. 
The most robust and clinically meaningful finding of our review is that nurse-led SDM demonstrates non-inferiority compared with standard care approaches in comparative studies (RR=1.00, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.03). This finding has important practical implications: healthcare systems may implement nurse-led SDM programmes without compromising screening uptake, potentially allowing reallocation of physician time to other clinical activities while maintaining programme effectiveness. In comparative studies, control conditions varied and included usual care without structured SDM counselling (Watson et al), physician-led or clinic-based standard screening pathways (Thuppal et al) and historical cohort comparisons using preintervention screening rates (Quaife et al). Despite this heterogeneity in comparators, the consistently null findings (I²=0%) strengthen confidence that nurse-led approaches achieve equivalent outcomes across different comparison conditions. Possible reasons are as follows: (1) longitudinal patient–nurse relationships can reduce procedural anxiety when compared with episodic physician interactions. In our included studies, most consultation durations are more than 30 min, while the consultation duration in clinician visits is usually 10 min or less. Besides, nurses perceived that a non-judgmental approach that counteracts smoking-related shame can relieve stigma mitigation, which is critical for individuals who currently smoke.
Furthermore, our study found a consistent influence of female proportion across all three primary outcomes, representing a novel finding not extensively explored in previous meta-analyses. Female participants exhibited higher screening adherence, potentially linked to gender-specific health-seeking behaviours or greater responsiveness to patient-centred communication. Conversely, current smokers demonstrated lower uptake, likely due to fatalism, stigma or competing priorities, emphasising the need for tailored interventions addressing psychological barriers. This concerning finding suggests that those who might benefit most from screening are least likely to participate, creating a potential ‘inverse care law’ scenario. Unexpectedly, meta-regression did not identify significant associations between screening uptake and several hypothesised predictors, including guideline adherence in eligibility determination, insurance coverage and proportion of racial/ethnic minorities. This null finding may reflect insufficient statistical power due to the limited number of studies, heterogeneity in how these variables were defined and measured across studies or the possibility that these factors operate through more complex pathways not captured in our analysis. Future research with larger, more diverse populations and standardised covariate definitions is needed to more comprehensively explore facilitators and barriers to nurse-led SDM implementation.
A study of Ruparel et al. shows that screening-eligible adults hold fatalistic beliefs about lung cancer, have limited understanding of LCS harms (especially overdiagnosis) and worry about information overload. They desire information that allows an informed decision, with optional deeper layers, and these are precisely the kinds of communication challenges that nursing training is well suited to handle, particularly regarding tailoring detail to literacy and numeracy, addressing stigma and fatalism around smoking and lung cancer, and also integrating discussions of comorbidities and life context into risk and benefit deliberation. Nurses’ multifaceted roles, as educators, navigators and coordinators, position them uniquely to address barriers to LCS. Their extended patient interactions, compared with physicians, allow for deeper rapport-building and personalised counselling, particularly in underserved populations (eg, rural residents, veterans and HIV-positive individuals). For example, telehealth SDM sessions led by nurse practitioners improved accessibility for geographically isolated veterans, while HIV-tailored DAs enhanced comprehension in immunocompromised cohorts. These findings align with studies emphasising nurses’ capacity to bridge health literacy gaps and mitigate systemic inequities. Integration of digital tools, such as EHRs and clinical decision support systems, further amplified programme scalability. Automated eligibility alerts and standardised workflows reduced administrative burdens, ensuring adherence to evolving guidelines (eg, USPSTF 2021 criteria). However, variability in intervention design, such as the use of lay navigators versus advanced practice nurses, introduced heterogeneity, necessitating clearer frameworks for role delineation and competency assessment.
Persistent disparities in LCS participation, particularly among individuals with significant smoking histories, low-income populations and ethnic minorities, were partially mitigated through nurse-led initiatives. Mobile screening units and community-based programmes extended services to marginalised groups, achieving uptake rates comparable to hospital-based cohorts. For instance, Raghavan et al reported a 78% screening completion rate in underserved communities using a mobile LDCT unit, coupled with navigator-led education. Such models highlight the importance of decentralising screening infrastructure and addressing logistical barriers (eg, transportation, cost). Smoking cessation support, integrated into 12 of 13 programmes, emerged as a dual facilitator, enhancing screening adherence while directly reducing cancer risk. Nurses’ iterative counselling during follow-up visits reinforced cessation efforts, aligning with evidence that sustained engagement improves quit rates. However, the lack of standardised cessation protocols across studies limited comparability, underscoring the need for evidence-based tobacco treatment frameworks.
Our findings regarding LDCT uptake rates align with some previous research while differing from others. The 98% pooled uptake rate exceeds those reported in many real-world implementation studies (11%–87%). This discrepancy likely reflects multiple sources of bias inherent to single-arm designs: (1) selection bias, where participants enrolled in nurse-led programmes may have been more motivated, faced fewer barriers or had pre-existing healthcare engagement compared with the general eligible population; (2) intensive support provided in research settings that may not be replicable in routine practice and (3) the multicomponent nature of interventions, which typically combined nurse counselling with proactive outreach, telehealth capabilities, EHR-based identification and structured DAs.
However, the heterogeneity of nursing roles across included studies may differentially impact screening outcomes. Nurse practitioners (n=4 studies) function as independent providers with prescribing authority who can autonomously order LDCT scans, interpret results and manage follow-up care, essentially serving as primary care providers for screening, while by contrast, nurse navigators (n=5 studies) primarily facilitate coordination, patient education and barrier reduction without prescriptive authority, while programme coordinators (n=2 studies) focus on workflow optimisation and eligibility identification. These distinct scopes of practice have different implications for programme design. NP-led models may reduce referral delays through direct ordering capabilities and provide continuity when abnormalities require follow-up but require substantial investment in the advanced practice nursing workforce. Navigator-led models may be more scalable and cost-effective for high-volume programmes but depend on physician oversight for clinical decisions. The limited number of studies in each category precluded meaningful subgroup analysis in our review; however, future research should explicitly compare outcomes by nursing role to inform optimal programme staffing and scope of practice considerations.
This meta-analysis contributes to the field of LCS implementation by: (1) establishing that nurse-led SDM achieves non-inferior uptake compared with standard approaches, supporting its consideration as an alternative service delivery model; (2) identifying consistent associations of female sex (positive) and current smoking status (negative) with screening uptake, providing direction for targeted intervention design; (3) synthesising descriptive benchmarks from implementation studies that can inform programme expectations and (4) highlighting the substantial heterogeneity in intervention components and nursing roles that must be addressed in future research.

## Limitations
These findings must be interpreted within the broader international context of SDM implementation in LCS programmes. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the predominance of single-arm studies and quasi-experimental designs (11 of 13 studies) substantially restricts causal inference. The absence of randomised comparisons means observed outcomes cannot be definitively attributed to nurse-led SDM, and the heterogeneity of intervention components makes it challenging to isolate the specific contribution of nursing roles. The remarkably high pooled uptake rate (98%) likely reflects multicomponent programme effects and selection bias rather than nurse involvement per se. Second, risk of bias assessments revealed moderate-to-serious concerns across all studies, with confounding and missing data as the most prevalent sources. Most studies were conducted in USA academic medical centres (12 of 13), limiting generalisability to community settings and non-US healthcare systems. These biases suggest our pooled estimates represent upper-bound estimates of programme performance under favourable conditions. Third, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed for LDCT uptake (I²=99%) and willingness to participate (I²=97.7%), indicating that the true effect likely differs substantially across settings. Meta-regression explained only 67.8%–83.2% of between-study variance, and the limited number of comparative studies (n=3) precluded definitive conclusions about comparative effectiveness. The diversity of study designs also precluded formal GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) assessment, and publication bias favouring positive findings cannot be excluded.

## Future direction
Nurses are functionally central to SDM in many LCS programmes, particularly in centralised, telehealth and NP-led models. However, the empirical literature is only beginning to quantify their specific impact and theorise nurse-distinctive contributions. For instance, McDonnell et al surveyed primary care NPs regarding LDCT screening, finding that while most were aware of guidelines and perceived SDM as within their scope of practice, time constraints were prominent. Their willingness to engage in SDM decreased significantly when encounters exceeded 8 min (from 92% willing at ≤8 min to 37% beyond that). 
Systemic issues, such as a lack of EHR support, inadequate educational materials and insufficient follow-up resources, alongside patient barriers (cost, transportation and follow-through), further limited SDM practice. McDonnell et al’s study confirms NPs’ willingness and conceptual alignment with SDM but highlights that routine primary care visits are often poorly suited for high-quality SDM. Thus, future studies should focus on strategies to facilitate SDM and address the barriers faced by both nurses and patients.
Furthermore, the theoretical framing of SDM in LCS predominantly draws on generic models (eg, three-talk models, decision coaching frameworks), often omitting nursing-specific constructs such as relational autonomy, advocacy, holistic assessment and emotional and moral labour. These nursing-specific elements are not explicitly incorporated into study designs, qualitative analyses of nurse roles or measures of SDM quality or patient experience. This presents an opportunity to design research that explicitly tests how nursing frameworks shape SDM processes and outcomes and examines longitudinal nurse–patient relationships across repeated annual LDCT decisions and nodule follow-up. 
Recognising that rural and underserved populations experience lower access to LCS, telehealth or navigator models may offer potential solutions to mitigate this disparity in access. However, few studies stratify outcomes by race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status or rurality, meaning the equitable reach and impact of nurse-led SDM remain inadequately evaluated. This highlights the need for future nurse-led SDM interventions to incorporate equity as an explicit design and evaluation dimension. Such research should assess whether nurse navigator SDM improves reach, comprehension and value-concordant decisions, particularly among populations that currently underuse LCS. Crucially, most available data on nurse-linked SDM are cross-sectional, typically examining a one-time SDM encounter and immediate decisions or 12-month LDCT completion. Evidence regarding the impact of nurse involvement on adherence to repeat annual screening is limited. Similarly, evidence is scarce concerning SDM for decisions such as stopping screening, managing incidental findings or choosing follow-up pathways where nurses often play significant roles. Given nurses’ continuity functions in patient care, there is a strong rationale for longitudinal studies investigating multidecision SDM trajectories anchored by nursing roles. 

## Conclusions
Comparative studies provide provisional evidence that nurse-led SDM achieves equivalent LDCT uptake to standard care approaches, supporting its consideration as a potentially viable alternative service delivery model. However, well-designed randomised controlled trials are essential before recommending widespread integration into LCS programmes. Future research should prioritise methodological rigour, standardised outcome measurement and explicit attention to implementation context to build a more robust evidence base for policy and practice decisions.
