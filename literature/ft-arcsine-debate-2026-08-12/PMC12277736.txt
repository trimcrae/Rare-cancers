## Background
Vitamin D deficiency is prevalent among the older generation who are also prone to developing degenerative rotator cuff tears. Vitamin D is found to be critical in tendon-to-bone healing and is potentially a modifiable risk factor for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (RCR) outcomes. However, there are few studies evaluating the effects of vitamin D levels on the latter. This study thus aims to evaluate the relationship between preoperative serum vitamin D levels and rotator cuff tears as well as arthroscopic RCR outcomes.

## Methods
A systematic search of four databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane Library) was performed. The inclusion criteria were studies with (a) preoperative serum vitamin D levels, (b) patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR, and (c) preoperative characteristics and postoperative outcomes. Meta-analysis of correlation coefficients was performed to compare the relationship between vitamin D levels and preoperative factors, including (a) fatty degeneration (FD) of supraspinatus and (b) tear size and retraction size, as well as postoperative outcomes, including (a) patient-reported outcome measures and (b) retear rates or failure requiring revision surgery.

## Results
Five articles were included with a sample size of 1436. The pooled mean serum vitamin D level was 23.66 ng/ml. Higher vitamin D levels were significantly associated with lower FD (R = −0.24, P = .05), but not with tear or retraction size. There was no association between vitamin D levels and postoperative patient-reported outcome measures. There were insufficient studies for meta-analysis of retear rates, but individual results from included studies showed lower rates of retear for groups with sufficient vitamin D levels.

## Conclusion
While higher vitamin D levels are associated with lower rates of rotator cuff FD and possible lower retear rates, it does not necessarily lead to better clinical outcomes post-repair. This study's findings do not justify routine testing and supplementation of vitamin D in patients undergoing RCR.

Hence, prognostic factors for rotator cuff repair (RCR), including tear size, tear shape, extent of retraction, degree of fatty infiltration, and muscle atrophy have been well-established in the existing literature. , , , Recently, several studies have identified preoperative risk factors for arthroscopic RCR failure that may affect a patient's rotator cuff biology, such as lower bone mineral density, female gender, hyperlipidemia, and advanced age. , , Hence, there has been growing interest in modifiable, preoperative risk factors that could be optimized perioperatively to improve outcomes following arthroscopic RCR.
One area of interest is the effect of preoperative vitamin D level on the extent of rotator cuff disease and postoperative outcomes. Globally, vitamin D deficiency has remained prevalent, with 47.9% of the population having vitamin D levels of less than 50 nmol/L or 20 ng/ml. Unsurprisingly, recent studies have also reported that 44.3% of patients with rotator cuff tears and 54% of patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty have vitamin D deficiency. , Many factors predispose the elderly population to vitamin D deficiency, including deprivation of sunlight, avoidance of dairy, intestinal malabsorption, and decrease in concentration of 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin required for the activation of vitamin D. Given that both vitamin D deficiency and rotator cuff pathologies profoundly affect the elderly population, it is important to establish whether vitamin D deficiency is a negative prognostic factor following RCR. Moreover, it is a highly modifiable preoperative risk factor that can be corrected easily at low costs.
The regeneration of the bone-tendon interface is critical in RCR surgery. Following RCR, the usual tendon footprint, which consist of 4 layers, disappears and is replaced with fibrovascular scar tissue. Vitamin D is postulated to induce an increase in the content of type 1 collagen fibers and decrease in content of type 3 collagen fibers, making the healing tendon similar to its original structure. A laboratory study by Angeline et al likewise reported that low vitamin D levels in rats may negatively affect early healing at the RCR site. However, whether these histological changes translate into clinical outcomes remain uncertain. Hence, further clinical studies on humans are required to validate this relationship.
In the current literature, there are a few cohort studies investigating the relationship between perioperative serum vitamin D levels and postarthroscopic RCR outcomes. , However, the evidence remains inconclusive. Hence, this study sought to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the effects of preoperative serum vitamin D levels on rotator cuff fatty degeneration (FD) and outcomes post-arthroscopic RCR. This study's hypothesis is that lower levels of preoperative serum vitamin D is associated with poorer rotator cuff quality and thus poorer outcomes after arthroscopic RCR.

## Materials and methods

## Search strategy
This review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was performed in 4 different databases, PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, from inception to January 18, 2024. This study utilized the following search terms: RCR-related terms (‘rotator cuff,’ ‘rotator cuff repair,’ ‘arthroscopic,’ ‘rotator cuff surgery’) and vitamin D-related terms (‘vitamin D,’ ‘25-hydroxyvitamin D,’ ‘25-hydroxy∗,’ ‘25(OH)D,’ ‘calcifediol,’ ‘bone health,’ ‘calcium’). Grey literature searching from references of existing articles was also done to identify additional studies. The search strategy is available in .

## Selection criteria
This study included articles with: (a) preoperative serum vitamin D levels; (b) Patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR; and (c) preoperative characteristics as well as postoperative outcomes. The exclusion criteria included the following: (a) non-English; (b) non-human study (eg, biomechanical or animal study); (c) review, conference abstract or editorials; (d) studies without RCR outcomes; (e) studies without preoperative serum vitamin D levels; and (f) studies with overlapping cohorts or duplicates.
Articles were screened by title or abstract and subsequently full text by two independent authors (M.Y and S.S), using the web-based platform Rayyan (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Qatar). Any disagreement was resolved via discussion with a third author (H.W.T).

## Data extraction
Using a predefined Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA), data from each individual study was independently extracted by the same authors (M.Y and S.S). Any disagreement in data extraction was arbitrated after discussion with a third author (H.W.T).
In terms of study and patient characteristics, the following data were extracted: (a) sample size of groups (control vs. vitamin D insufficiency vs. vitamin D deficiency) and corresponding preoperative serum vitamin D levels (ng/ml); (b) follow-up duration (months); (c) recruitment period; (d) study design; (e) hospital or database and country; (d) baseline clinical characteristics, including mean serum vitamin D level (ng/ml), FD of supraspinatus (SSP) (mean grade from Goutallier classification), retraction size and tear size (cm); and (e) baseline patient demographics, including gender (number of males), age (years), duration of symptoms, or time to operation (months) ( ). 
In terms of outcomes, correlation coefficients between preoperative serum vitamin D levels and the following preoperative characteristics were extracted: (a) FD of SSP and (b) tear and retraction size. Correlation coefficient between vitamin D and the following postoperative patient-related outcome measures (PROMs) were extracted, where available: (a) visual analog scale (VAS) pain score; (b) American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score; (c) constant shoulder score; and (d) University of California-Los Angeles shoulder score. Lastly, data on retear rates or failure requiring revision surgery was extracted. Retear was defined as Sugaya type IV/V on magnetic resonance imaging. To standardize the reporting of FD, only correlation coefficient with FD of SSP was included in the analysis ( ). 
Data from continuous variables were collected as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Raw data reported as median (interquartile range) or mean (confidence interval) were converted to mean ± SD using validated methods described by Wan et al and the Cochrane handbook. , Any raw data from plots or graphs were extracted using an online-based plot digitizer software PlotDigitizer (PlotDigitizer, USA). , 

## Risk of bias assessment
The methodological quality of included articles was evaluated by two independent authors (M.Y and S.S) using the Cochrane-validated risk of bias assessment tool for non-randomized studies, Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool, of which articles were graded as ‘low risk,’ ‘moderate risk,’ ‘serious risk,’ or ‘critical risk.’ 

## Statistical analysis
Data for baseline clinical characteristics and patient demographics across the individual studies were pooled via meta-analysis, using random effects models. Raw means (for continuous variables) and Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformed proportions (for categorical variables) were pooled using the inverse variance method, via metamean and metaprop functions respectively, as described by Harrer et al. 
Meta-analysis of correlations was conducted to estimate the pooled effect size of association between preoperative serum vitamin D levels and 1) preoperative characteristics as well as 2) postoperative outcomes. Meta-analysis was conducted for outcomes with minimum two studies reporting them. Using established methods, Pearson correlation coefficients (rs) were converted to Fisher's z transformation, which were then pooled to obtained effect size estimates in forest plots. , , Where Spearman correlation coefficients (rp) were reported, the same transformation was applied, as rp is equivalent to rs between ranks, as recommended by Hunter et al. , Random effects models were utilized in view of heterogeneity in study population or methods. The forest plot displayed Cochrane heterogeneity statistic and Higgins I2 coefficient. Two-tailed statistical significance was established at P ≤ .05, while I2 was used to quantify statistical heterogeneity, of which I2 > 50% represented moderate-to-high heterogeneity. This study was unable to conduct sensitivity analysis, such as subgroup analysis, due to the paucity of data. All analyses were performed using R 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) via the dmetar 0.09, meta 4.19-2, and metafor 3.0-2 packages.

## Results

## Literature retrieval
This study's database search yielded a total of 1846 articles. After removal of duplicates, 1250 articles were screened based on title and abstract, of which 44 full-text articles were sought for retrieval. All 44 articles were retrieved and screened, of which 5 articles were included based on this study's eligibility criteria. , , , , Two of these articles were from the same institution but their recruitment period differed; hence, their cohorts did not overlap and both were eligible for inclusion. , In contrast, 2 other articles , were excluded as their cohorts overlapped with another one of our included articles. An additional relevant article was identified via grey literature search but was excluded as it was a conference abstract. The study selection process and reasons for exclusion are summarized in the Preferred Reporting items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 flowchart ( ).

## Study characteristics
All study and baseline characteristics are summarized in . The 5 included studies originated from 3 countries (Korea, United States of America, and China). All studies were non-randomized, of which 3 studies were prospective, , , while 2 were retrospective cohort studies. , 
The total sample size across all 5 studies was 1436 patients. In Oh et al, only the group with rotator cuff tear injuries only (reported as group 1 in the study) was included. The definition of vitamin D insufficiency and deficiency differed across the studies with 2 studies reporting vitamin D deficiency as levels <10 ng/ml , and 3 studies reporting it as levels <20 ng/ml. , , As the optimal level of vitamin D is found to be 25-80 ng/mL, vitamin D deficiency will be defined as <20 ng/ml in this study. 292 patients had vitamin D deficiency, while 1144 patients had replete vitamin D levels.

## Baseline patient demographics and clinical characteristics
In terms of patient demographics, the mean age (years) and gender (number of males) were pooled to obtain summary estimates across the studies. The pooled mean age reported across 4 studies was 60.1 years (95% CI: 56.9-63.2), , , , while the pooled proportion of male gender across all 5 studies was 40% (95% CI: 32-48). , , , , 
In terms of baseline clinical characteristics, the mean preoperative serum vitamin D level (ng/ml), tear and retraction size (cm), and FD of SSP (mean grade via Goutallier's classification) were pooled. The mean serum vitamin D level across 4 studies was 23.66 ng/ml (95% CI: 1.58-45.73). , , , The mean tear and retraction size across 2 studies were 1.90 cm (95% CI: 0.91-2.89) and 1.87 cm (95% CI: −0.02 to 3.76), respectively. , The mean grade of FD of SSP was 1.58 (95% CI: 0.57-2.57) across 4 studies. , , ,

## Vitamin D and preoperative FD of SSP
Three studies involving a total of 362 patients reported correlation coefficients between preoperative serum vitamin D levels and mean FD grade of SSP (via Goutallier's classification). , , Ryu et al was excluded from this analysis as it reported correlation coefficient of vitamin D and both SSP or infraspinatus (ISP) FD, but not SSP alone. Meta-analysis of correlations found significant negative correlation (R) between vitamin D and FD of SSP (R = −0.24, 95% CI: −0.48 to 0.01, P = .05) ( ).

Only 2 studies, involving 135 patients, reported correlation coefficients between preoperative serum vitamin D levels and postoperative VAS, ASES, and Constant scores. ## Retear rates
In terms of retear rates, meta-analysis was not conducted due to the paucity of data and heterogeneity in reporting of the outcome. However, 3 included studies that evaluated either retear rate or revision surgery risk reported outcomes that largely favored control groups or groups with sufficient vitamin D levels. , , Chen et al compared retear rates (defined as Sugaya type IV/V) between control vs. vitamin D deficiency groups and found significant difference favoring the control group (9.09% vs. 26.67%, P = .031). Meanwhile, Cancienne et al stratified risk of revision surgery based on the vitamin D level and found significantly greater risk in the vitamin D deficiency group compared with the control group (5.88% vs. 3.70%, OR = 3.1, P = .007) as well as insufficiency group (5.88% vs. 4.97%, OR = 2.4, P = .011). Lastly, Ryu et al compared preoperative serum vitamin D levels between different postoperative Sugaya tear types. Although no significant difference was found (P = .66), it was observed that lower vitamin D levels tended to be observed in Sugaya types IV/V. ## Discussion
Degenerative rotator cuff tears are potentially debilitating, and the introduction of RCR has led to estimated socioeconomic savings of $3.44 billion yearly. , However, given the variability in long-term outcomes and relatively high revision rates in the existing literature, , careful selection of suitable surgical candidates and sufficient perioperative optimization are crucial to improving outcomes. In particular, optimization of serum vitamin D levels in patients with muscle or tendon injuries has been increasingly studied due to reported physiologic benefits through muscle proliferation and improved tendon-to-bone healing. , In terms of postoperative outcomes for rotator cuff tears, the evidence behind the association between serum vitamin D levels and PROMs remains conflicting. , Therefore, establishing the association between preoperative serum vitamin D levels and outcomes post-arthroscopic RCR is essential before recommendations for routine testing and even supplementation are made. In the present study, meta-analysis found a significant negative correlation between serum vitamin D levels and FD of the SSP. However, there was no relationship between vitamin D levels and other tear characteristics like tear size and retraction size. Similarly, no relationship was found between PROMs and serum vitamin D levels.

## Fatty degeneration
This study found a negative correlation between serum vitamin D levels and degree of FD of the SSP muscle. FD of the rotator cuff refers to fibro-adipogenic changes in the rotator cuff muscles, and it is unequivocally linked to poor outcomes. Apart from being an important independent prognostic factor for bone-tendon interface healing after RCR, FD is also found to be associated with poorer functional outcomes, including weaker postoperative muscle strength. Across our included studies, only Rhee et al did not find any correlation between serum vitamin D levels and level of FD. However, their study population had a relatively small sample size of patients in the vitamin D-sufficient arm (11 patients, 12%), thus having a high risk of running a type II error. On the other hand, both Oh et al and Chen et al had a more balanced distribution of patients. , Furthermore, Oh et al conducted a multivariate analysis correcting for other known risk factors, such as age, tear size, and retraction size, , and concluded that serum levels of vitamin D is an independent risk factor of FD. One explanation supported by several animal studies and in-vitro experiments is that vitamin D blocks matrix metalloproteinases, a substance that inhibits cuff healing and promotes adipogenesis, thereby promoting accumulation of fibrocartilage and collagen to improve tendon healing. , 

## Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs)
Despite the above, this study found no correlation between vitamin D levels and PROMs (VAS, ASES, and Constant score). One reason could be the lack of relationship between fatty infiltration and quality of life. Fermont et al found that although fatty infiltration could predict results from the RAND 36-Item Health Survey and shoulder hindrance score, it was not a negative prognostic factor for quality of life post cuff repair. Nonetheless, the evidence regarding the relationship between vitamin D and function is debatable. Vitamin D deficiency is linked to proximal muscle weakness, which is reversible with supplementation. , , Bischoff et al found that a course of vitamin D plus calcium over a period of 3 months reduced the risk of falling by 49% compared to calcium alone. In terms of pain, observational studies have found that low vitamin D levels is associated with increased pain as vitamin D is thought to decrease inflammatory response by reducing proinflammatory cytokines. , Chen et al found a significant correlation between vitamin D levels and improved VAS pain scores at 1 and 3 months postoperatively, but with no significant correlation preoperatively and at 6-24 months postoperatively. Moreover, with the availability of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in the postoperative phase, with evidence highlighting its safety, the utility of vitamin D supplementation for its analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects could be negligible. Finally, since vitamin D deficiency is associated with reduced participation in physical activities, patients with vitamin D deficiency may have lower function demands. As a result, they may report similar subjective PROMs post-arthroscopic RCR compared to individuals with sufficient vitamin D levels.

## Retear rates
Ultimately, given the relationship between FD and tendon healing, one of the most concerning implications of FD of the rotator cuff tendon is increased retear rates and need for revision surgery. While the current study did not evaluate the relationship between serum vitamin D levels and retear rates via meta-analysis due to a lack of data, a review of the included studies highlighted an association between retear or revision surgery rates and vitamin D deficiency. Cancienne et al found that the rate of revision was significantly higher in the vitamin D-deficient group (5.88%) compared to vitamin-sufficient patients (3.7%). In a multivariate logistic regression performed by Harada et al, vitamin D deficient patients were also more likely to undergo future revision surgery (OR 1.54, P < .05). Chen et al also found a significantly higher retear rate in the vitamin D-deficient group (26.67% vs. 9.09% in control group). This is aligned with recent evidence reporting increased RCR retear rates in proportion to preoperative Goutallier stage. Besides FD, other mechanisms by which vitamin D reduces retear rates and improves bone-tendon healing include promotion of myocyte proliferation, increase in BMD and, interestingly, exertion of anti-inflammatory effects. 

## Clinical implications
Finally, while vitamin D is found to have a significant negative correlation with FD, the main clinical implication of this study is whether routine testing and supplementation of vitamin D affects outcomes post-surgical repair. Cancienne et al found that the absolute risk reduction of revision rate of 2.2% in vitamin D-replete patients corresponded to a number needed to treat of 46 patients. While our present study found a significant correlation coefficient (0.25) between vitamin D levels and level of FD, its value might be underpowered and not high enough to establish true clinical significance. Additionally, vitamin D levels appear to have no correlation with PROMs including pain. Therefore, based on the results from the current study, there might be limited benefit for routine vitamin D testing, and supplementation for patients with rotator cuff tears. Further well-powered prospective studies are required to verify if supplementation with vitamin D affects functional and anatomical outcomes post RCR.

## Limitations
This study has its limitations. Firstly, majority of our included studies (5/6) measured preoperative vitamin D levels, and only Rhee et al measured both pre and postoperative vitamin D levels. It is unknown if patients, especially vitamin D-deficient ones, had received perioperative vitamin D supplementation, and it may be inaccurate to correlate preoperative vitamin D levels with postoperative structural and function outcomes. Secondly, there is some degree of selection bias present as most of the included studies were retrospective in nature. Thirdly, majority of the studies did not account for heterogeneity in demographic factors affecting vitamin D, such as amount of sunlight exposure, geographic location of the subject, season when the level is measured, diet, and patient age, , , , and it did not control for other factors that may affect rotator cuff tissue quality, such as smoking and patient comorbidities (eg, diabetes mellitus). Moreover, while FD of the ISP was found to be a stronger predictor of cuff repair failure and poorer outcomes than other cuff muscles, this study did not include FD of the ISP as only 2 of 4 studies reported it. , , Nonetheless, FD in SSP and ISP are equally prevalent and common (29.5%) and thus either was determined to be a fair surrogate for FD of the rotator cuff tendon. Lastly, this study was limited by the small sample size, due to the paucity of existing evidence in the literature.

## Conclusion
This meta-analysis found a significant statistical association between preoperative serum vitamin D levels and degree of FD of the rotator cuff muscles but not with PROMs. However, the current evidence is still not enough to justify routine testing and supplementation of vitamin D in patients undergoing RCR, highlighting the need for more prospective studies comparing outcomes in vitamin D-deficient vs. sufficient populations.
