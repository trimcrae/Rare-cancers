## Abstract

## BACKGROUND:
Acute respiratory infections are a major trigger of asthma exacerbations. This study sought to estimate the overall proportion of viruses, atypical pathogens, and bacteria detected in adults with asthma exacerbations.

## METHODS:
PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases and all related studies from the reviews and references were searched from inception to February 13, 2020. Two authors independently performed study selection, data extraction, as well as quality evaluation. Subsequently, meta-analysis, between-study heterogeneity, and publication bias assessment were conducted on RStudio.

## RESULTS:
Forty-three eligible studies comprising 3511 adults were included, of which 21 publications mentioned multiple infections during acute asthma attacks. Meta-analysis showed an acute infection prevalence of 40.19% (95% confidence interval [CI] 34.53%–45.99%). Viruses, atypical pathogens, and bacteria were detected in 38.76% (95% CI 32.02%–45.71%), 8.29% (95% CI 2.64%–16.27%), and 7.05% (95% CI 3.34%–11.81%) of asthmatics during exacerbations, respectively. Rhinovirus infections are always the dominant trigger for exacerbations with a proportion of 20.02% (95% CI 14.84%–25.73%). Substantial heterogeneity across studies (Cochran Q test: 479.43, P < 0.0001, I2 = 91.2%) was explained by subgroup analysis, indicating that year, region, population, respiratory secretion, detection method, pathogen, and study quality were all influencing factors.

## CONCLUSION:
This meta-analysis provided the first quantitative epidemiological data for adults, and in the future, more research and health-care supports are necessary in this area.

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic respiratory disease with variable underlying molecular mechanisms resulting in protean manifestations, treatment responsiveness, and clinical outcomes. Asthma may be broadly classified into two main endotypes.[ ] The type 2 airway inflammatory network is characterized by a high level of type 2 T helper (Th2) cells and innate lymphoid cells of type 2, which seems to be prone to virus-induced exacerbation,[ , ] and refractory type 2 asthma has been acknowledged as an independent risk factor for exacerbation. The non-type 2 asthma, of which exact molecular pathways have not been well-established, is observed associated with frequent infective exacerbations.[ ] With the efforts of the global initiative for asthma (GINA) in the past 20 years, great progress has been made in the prevention and management of asthma. However, asthma still affected 272.68 million people worldwide, causing approximately 490,000 deaths in 2017.[ ] Asthma exacerbation (AE), also known as asthma attack, is characterized by worsening of asthma symptoms, deterioration of lung function, and requiring the use of corticosteroids and health-care utilization, remains a major public health concern and is the main reason for asthma-related school absences, emergency department presentations, hospitalizations, systematic corticosteroid use, or even death.[ , ]
Despite optimal therapy, 53.8% of children and 43% of adults suffering from asthma in the USA experienced at least one acute exacerbation of symptoms in the preceding 12 months in 2018.[ ] In addition, 15.5% of Chinese adult asthmatics had at least one emergency room visit due to an asthma attack, where asthma was largely undiagnosed and undertreated.[ ] Respiratory infections, and viral infections in particular, are common triggers of asthma exacerbation.[ , , ] Knowing the prevalence and distribution of these pathogens among asthmatics could help clinicians avoid misuse of antibiotics and generate certain novel therapies and strategies so that improving the quality of daily life and releasing the financial burden could be achieved. In previous studies, Papadopoulos et al.[ ] summarized the infection epidemiology of asthma exacerbation in children as well as adults and reviewed known effects for each pathogen. Zheng et al.[ ] quantified virus-specific prevalence in all age groups and explored possible determinants of variation. Feddema and Claassen[ ] reported that viral infections are responsible for approximately 55%–72% and 38%–57% of exacerbations in children and adults, respectively. The causative entity of virus in asthma exacerbation has been agreed on, and a meta-analysis in 2005 included 12 clinical studies, 9 of which supported the correlation between chlamydia or mycoplasma infections and acute asthma attacks.[ ] Compared with viruses or atypical bacteria, few studies have evaluated the frequency and pathogenic role of bacteria during AE. Furthermore, the improvement of detection methods such as Reverse transcription-Polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)[ ] and 16S rRNA sequencing[ ] might have led to a higher detection rate among asthmatics with exacerbations worldwide. Thus, a comprehensive epidemiologic analysis of infectious pathogens is urgently needed.
Although children seem to endure more frequent attacks than adults, the mortality among adults with asthma is about five times that of children in the USA (12.8 vs. 2.6 deaths per million population).[ ] Moreover, the year from 2001 to 2017 has seen a 50% increase in people aged over 75 years dying from asthma in England and Wales.[ ] Adults who account for the majority of mortality and medical expenses do require further investigation and improved management. Owing to the difference in terms of exposure to viruses, viral loads in airway, immune defence, and respiratory tract secretions detected, viral prevalence among adults is distinct from those in children and adolescents. Since previous epidemiological studies and reviews have primarily included children, the burden of the disease and to what degree respiratory infections contribute to asthma exacerbations and disease severity in adults are largely unknown. To provide a particular focus on adults with more evidence, we performed a systematic meta-analysis by synthesizing high-quality studies and discussed potential leading variations with subgroup analysis in terms of publication year, geographic regions, populations, detection technique, kind of sample, and type of pathogen. To assess the time trend and geographic distribution of specific pathogens, we also performed a subgroup analysis stratified by geographic region and year of population.

## Methods
We followed the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement in this study [ ].[ ] The protocol of this study was previously registered on the PROSPERO (CRD42022186992).

## Data sources and search strategy
A systematic search was performed in the PubMed/Medline, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases without language limitation from inception to February 13, 2020. To identify studies detailing the prevalence of respiratory infections in adults with asthma exacerbations, the following MESH terms and keywords in PubMed were applied: (((Adult[MeSH Terms]) OR (adults[Title/Abstract])) AND ((((((Infection* [Title/Abstract]) OR (Pathogen* [Title/Abstract])) OR (Virus[Title/Abstract])) OR (Viruses[Title/Abstract])) OR (bacteria* [Title/Abstract])) OR (Influ* [Title/Abstract]))) AND ((((asthma exacerbation* [Title/Abstract]) OR (asthma control[Title/Abstract])) OR (asthma attack* [Title/Abstract])) OR (flare-ups[Title/Abstract])). Additional articles were manually searched through review articles and reference list of selected full-text papers. Detailed search strategy is given in .

## Eligibility criteria
The main inclusion criteria were: (1) origin articles irrespective of language published up to February 2020; (2) studies with a clear diagnosis of asthma exacerbation and acute infection; (3) individual pathogen and detection methods were elucidated; and (4) participants were restricted to adults (age ≥ 16) only.

## Exclusion criteria
Studies met one of the following criteria were ruled out: studies included children and adults without presenting separate results; studies with insufficient data despite repeated contacts with the corresponding authors; animal studies, abstracts, unpublished studies, conference proceedings, review articles, meta-analysis or systematic reviews, case reports, and duplicate publications.

## Quality assessment and data extraction
Although the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) checklist[ ] is a validated tool to assess the quality of observational studies in epidemiology, especially for case–control and cohort studies, much less attention has been given to cross-sectional and prospective studies. Since there was no research type limitation in our review, we developed a revised NOS checklist which contains 11 items to evaluate each study on the representativeness of disease, diagnostic accuracy, and outcome assessment [ ]. A study was considered to be of high quality when it scored ≥7 out of 11. Two authors (XC and PAZ) independently assessed the enrolled studies, and any dispute was resolved through discussion or consulting a senior author (YLM).
The following data were extracted: authors, year of publication, country, sample size, study population (hospitalized, emergency room, or clinic visitors), age, definition of exacerbation, study design and duration, detection specimen and method, the number of patients with acute infections, and individual pathogen included during asthma exacerbations.

## Statistical analysis
Pooled prevalence, together with its corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were stabilized by applying the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation and then results were converted back to the raw proportions for presentation.[ ] Heterogeneity among studies was quantified by using Cochran’s Q test and I2, respectively, where I2 > 50% as well as P < 0.1 were considered statistically significant. The pooled point estimations were done using a random-effects model while heterogeneity existed. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was performed. In addition, a funnel plot was used for checking the possibility of publication bias, and the Egger’s linear regression test and Begg’s test (P < 0.05 for evident publication bias) were applied to measure any asymmetry. Finally, we did a subgroup analysis to explore potential heterogeneity with respect to publication year, geographic region, population, detection measurement, kind of sample, type of pathogen, and study quality score. All statistical analyses were conducted by RStudio.

## Results

## Study characteristics
The systematic search yielded 4948 publications. After screening title/abstract and removing duplicates, 158 full-text papers remained to be assessed for eligibility. Additional 13 studies were included through reference checking. Three studies were excluded from this meta-analysis because of low quality. Overall, 3511 patients from 43 studies[ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] were ultimately selected for quantitative analysis [ ].
 Of the final 43 studies, forty were published in English, one was in Turkish, one was in Spanish, and another was Chinese. There were 40 observational studies (12 case–control studies, 7 cohort studies, 3 cross-sectional studies, 10 longitudinal studies, and 8 prospective studies) and 3 randomized trials. Seven studies (16%) were published before 2000 and others (84%) were later, with sample sizes ranging from 19 to 278 asthmatics. Most studies were conducted in Asia and Europe, while only one study provided Rhinovirus prevalence during a severe asthma attack in South Africa.
Twenty-one out of the 43 eligible studies mentioned coinfections during acute wheezing episodes. Eight studies had more than one virus detected. Three studies identified dual infections with Chlamydophila and Mycoplasma, and 4 studies observed a few asthmatics with viral infections were positive for the bacterium or Chlamydophila. Features of the included studies are shown in .
 RT–PCR and multiplex PCR were the most widely chosen techniques to confirm viral infection of an exacerbation. However, the laboratory diagnosis of underlying atypical bacterial infection is mainly based on microimmunofluorescence test for Chlamydophila pneumonia (CP), and ELISA for Mycoplasma pneumonia (MP). The molecular method targeting the DNA of pathogens is not yet routinely recommended because it yields a relatively low-positive rate compared with serologic methods and is unable to distinguish between acute infection and colonization.

## Overall prevalence
Pooled analysis [ ] found an overall prevalence of infection among adults with asthma exacerbations of 40.19% (95% CI 34.53%–0.45.99%). Significant heterogeneity was observed across studies (Cochran Q test: 479.43, P < 0.0001, I2 = 91.2%).
 

## Subgroup analysis
As shown in , a higher detection rate was noted in studies published after 2010 (44.73%; 95% CI 37.01%–52.58%) and emergency settings (41.08%; 95% CI 26.13%–56.90%). European countries seemed to share less infection-induced asthma exacerbations (34.56%; 95% CI 24.28%–45.59%). After stratification by pathogen, viruses were the most frequently detected infectious agents (38.76%; 95% CI 32.02%–45.71%), followed by atypical pathogens (8.29%; 95% CI 2.64%–16.27%), and bacteria (7.05%; 95% CI 3.34%–11.81%). Of these virus-induced acute attacks, Rhinovirus was always dominant (20.02%; 95% CI 14.84%–25.73%), followed by the influenza virus, coronavirus, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza virus, metapneumovirus, adenovirus, Enterovirus, and Boca virus, which were 6.93%, 4.14%, 3.55%, 2.27%, 2.09%, 0.98%, 0.39%, 0.05%, respectively. Chlamydia pneumoniae, MP, and Legionella were identified in 4.33%, 4.24%, and 0.23% of the samples, respectively. As for Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Moraxella catarrhalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, the detection rate was 2.48%, 1.65%, 0.42%, 0.24%, 0.03%, and 0%, respectively.
 When each pathogen detected was stratified by geographic region, rhinovirus was seen most in Africa (35.14%; 95% CI 20.44%–51.35%) and least in Asia (12.27%; 95% CI 8.88%–16.08%). The respiratory syncytial virus was frequently reported in studies conducted in Oceania (14.83%; 95% CI 0%–64.43%) whereas other regions were < 5%. In Asia, parainfluenza virus and metapneumovirus were more prevalent compared with America and Europe. In terms of bacteria and atypical pathogens, no data was available for America and Africa. When stratified by year of publication, the overall prevalence of infection was increased by the year of publication and then decreased during 2016–2020. The period of 2006–2010 has seen the highest detection rate of coronavirus (19.05%; 95% CI 4.6%–39.06%), respiratory syncytial virus (14.29%; 95% CI 2.01%–33.08%), rhinovirus (31.73%; 95% CI 9.13%–59.87%), C. pneumoniae (19.83%; 95% CI 0.26%–55.16%), and MP (16.43%; 95% CI 0%–53.72%). Influenza virus (12.62%; 95% CI 2.78%–27.09%) was prevalent in 2011–2015 [ ].
 

## Publication bias
According to Egger’s regression test and Begg’s test, symmetrical funnel plots suggested no evident publication bias [ ].
 

## Discussion
In this study, we provide the first quantitative pooled estimates of viruses, atypical pathogens, and bacteria detected among adults presenting with asthma exacerbations. With the largest number of included studies and patients to our knowledge, we were able to report a relatively robust prevalence among adults.
Viral infections were attributed to major infection-associated exacerbations in adults. Our analysis yields a lower rate (32.02%–45.71%) compared with the previous meta-analysis (51.52%–65.19%)[ ] since children were outside the scope of this systematic review. Rhinovirus was the most common virus detected in people experiencing acute wheezing episodes of asthma as well as Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.[ ] However, individuals with asthma are not at increased risk of rhinovirus infection but have more severe and long-lasting lower respiratory tract symptoms.[ ] Impaired innate antiviral immunity and augmented TH2 inflammation in people with asthma are 2 potential mechanisms linked to increased severity of virus-induced AE pathogenesis.[ ] In accordance with previous findings in children,[ ] coronaviruses made a small contribution to AE in adults with a proportion of 1.51%–7.73%. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the incidence of asthmatic patients infected with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pneumonia varied widely across different countries, ranging from less than 1% to 21.7%.[ , , , ] Although early researches suggested that SARS-CoV-2 might not induce severe asthma exacerbation, emerging evidence highlights that people with severe asthma are a high-risk group for SARS-CoV-2 infection, and COVID-19 severity[ ] and distinct asthma endotypes may have a differential risk of COVID-19 severity. The UK Biobank data reported that nonallergic asthma was a risk factor for severe COVID-19 but atopy was associated with a decreased duration of COVID-19 hospitalization. This may be related to reduced angiotensin-converting-enzyme-2 expression levels in atopy.[ ]
In this meta-analysis, the detection rate of atypical pathogens during AE was 8.29% (2.64%, 16.27%). CP 4.49% (0.74%, 10.43%) and MP 4.24% (1.18%, 8.69%) accounted for similar proportions, and a much lower prevalence was observed in Legionella Pneumophila 0.23% (0, 1.17%). However, whether atypical pathogens play a causative role in asthma attacks is debated. In several case–control studies, CP and MP infections were not detected significantly between adults with AE and stable asthma[ , , ] or healthy controls.[ , ] On the other hand, two studies[ , ] found contrary results, and a prospective study conducted in Italy reported that 38% of asthmatics admitted due to severe asthma exacerbation had serologic evidence of CP or MP infection and suffered a higher degree of lung function decline.[ ] Webley and Hahn[ ] have justified that CP is closely involved in asthma pathogenesis and severity with emerging evidence in clinical and molecular aspects. Even if we detected samples for several times, especially at acute and convalescent stages, to confirm acute rather than chronic infection. It remains to be determined whether atypical pathogens act as cofactors for viral infections[ , ] or as one of the causes of asthma exacerbation. Moreover, full elucidation of the etiologic link between MP and AE is awaited.
Although a causal link between bacterial infection and asthma exacerbation was brought up in the 1940s,[ ] it was not until recently that this view has gained increasing focus thanks to the application of new molecular methods with higher sensitivity. Previous studies have relied on culture as the gold standard to identify bacterial infections during acute asthma attacks, which easily underestimates the real prevalence. Metagenomics researches[ , , , , ] have confirmed the increased abundance of airborne pathogenic bacteria in asthma patients, indicating lower respiratory tract is colonized by Proteobacteria such as H. influenzae, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and M. catarrhalis, especially in neutrophilic asthma.[ ] Moreover, staphylococcus can also be found in the lower airway of children with refractory asthma. In addition, there is evidence that adult patients with asthma have an increased chance of suffering from the severe coccal infection,[ ] and the immunity to lipopolysaccharide produced by bacteria wanes at the same time.[ ] Thus, it is likely asthma patients are more susceptible to bacterial respiratory tract infections. In our review, bacterial infections were observed in 7% of asthmatics with exacerbations, which is surprisingly similar to that of atypical bacteria. In line with previous metagenomics analysis,[ , ] the bacterial genera detected among included studies belong to Proteobacteria and Gram-positive cocci. Underlying mechanisms of bacteria-induced asthma exacerbation and the association between bacterial infection and disease severity deserve further exploration. And now, early detection and prescription of antibiotics in these patients may help minimize health-care costs and deterioration of lung function.
Among the studies that found polymicrobial infections, there were not enough data to reach a statistical conclusion when it comes to association with specific bacteria and viruses in adults. A multivariate logistic regression conducted by Wark and his colleagues suggests that coinfection with viruses and bacteria worsens AE prognosis measured by readmission within 60 days.[ ] While children with acute wheezing episodes, early studies have reported that combined RV with bacterial infections are fairly frequent.[ ] Furthermore, Duenas Meza et al.[ ] speculated that viral infections may increase the susceptibility of further bacterial infections and altogether trigger exacerbated airway inflammation as all of the children were admitted to emergency rooms because AE tested positive for Mycoplasma presenting coinfection with some type of virus. A possible mechanism behind this might be that viral infection, with proven evidence from RV,[ ] influenza virus,[ , ] and RSV[ ] in in vitro models, increases bacterial adherence to infected epithelial cells, and RV impairs the antibacterial defence mediated by alveolar macrophages.[ ]
Several limitations of the present study should be addressed. First, as the included studies were nonrandomized and observational, the heterogeneity among studies was pretty high and unavoidable because of lacking control of bias. Although we excluded children’s isolation rate from this study to meet our needs and age are one of the major sources of heterogeneity at the same time. Then subgroup analyses identified that the differences in publication year, region, population, respiratory secretion, detection method, and study quality were all confounding factors for heterogeneity. Other factors, such as gender and country income may partially affect the heterogeneity. However, we failed to do further analysis owing to insufficient data. Consequently, we should interpret these estimated results in a critical way and extend the pooled prevalence to the general population with caution.
Secondly, we made our own checklist for quality assessment according to NOS checklist; therefore, it is not standardized and verified for validity. Moreover, the choice of quality scale can largely shape or even reverse the conclusions of subsequent meta-analyses. Given that we aim to obtain a pooled estimate of observational prevalence proportions instead of the effect of interventions, we weakened the comparability of the groups of NOS checklists and took factors such as seasonality and administration of vaccines into consideration. As viral infection presented a seasonal prevalence among adults, seasonality predicts epidemics of an asthma attack.[ ] And evidence from a systematic review[ ] and an observational study[ ] found that influenza vaccination might be effective in both reducing influenza infection and exacerbations of asthma. It turns out that studies with full marks showing no significant heterogeneity, indicating this might be an appropriate and reliable tool to evaluate the risk of bias.
Finally, we identified only one eligible study conducted in Africa, and the distribution and patterns of infectious agents in this area remain obscure. Considering asthma is more prevalent in low- and middle-income countries, this meta-analysis is likely to underestimate the real infection-induced AE prevalence. More researches would be desirable to confirm the observational differences between geographic regions in the future.

## Conclusion
Although asthma attacks were mainly associated with viral infections, bacterial and atypical pathogenic infections also require attention. In the future, more well-designed, large research with a comprehensive analysis of microbial exposures should be conducted to confirm epidemiology and causality. Moreover, more studies on the relative contribution of COVID-19 in asthma exacerbations are desirable to fill our gap.
