## Background
Gastroparesis is a delay in gastric emptying without mechanical obstruction, lacking a clear pathophysiological mechanism, but with multiple histological abnormalities including loss of interstitial cells of Cajal, which may alter slow waves. We can assess slow waves with electrogastrography.

## Objectives
To determine the prevalence and range of abnormalities in gastric slow waves in adults with gastroparesis using electrogastrography.

## Methods
We systematically searched Medline, Embase, LILACS, Web of Science, and Cochrane Register of Controlled Trials. We included studies with patients older than 18 years with gastroparesis, assessed using electrogastrography. We evaluated the percentage of duration of the recording in which the dominant power was in normogastria, tachygastria, and bradygastria; dominant frequency; power ratio; change in post-stimulus dominant power; and dominant frequency instability coefficient. Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute tool. Data were synthesized using narrative summary and meta-analysis.

## Results
A total of 3730 articles were reviewed, including 31 articles with 1545 patients and 340 controls. Compared to controls, gastroparetics patients had less normogastria (fasting: 50.3% versus 65.8%) (post-stimulus: 54.3% versus 66.5%), more bradygastria (fasting: 37.7% versus 13%) (post-stimulus: 31.9% versus 16.3%), and more tachygastria (fasting: 16.1% versus 4.6%) (post-stimulus: 18.3% versus 5.2%). Gastroparetics had less change in post-stimulus dominant power (1.45 dB versus 5.03 dB) and less power ratio (1.4 versus 5.26).

## Conclusions
Gastroparetic patients present abnormalities in the frequency and changes in the post-stimulus power of slow waves, possibly secondary to a reduced number of interstitial cells of Cajal, as described in these patients.

## Introduction
Gastroparesis is defined as a delay in gastric emptying without evidence of mechanical obstruction. It reduces the quality of life and represents a significant economic burden for the health system [ , ]. Multiple histological abnormalities have been described in gastroparesis, including myenteric inflammation, decreased innervation, enteric changes, and loss of interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC) [ – ]. These changes alter the gastric myoelectrical activity, affecting the onset and conduction of slow waves [ ].
Electrogastrography (EGG) evaluates gastric myoelectrical activity by recording the generation and propagation of slow waves through electrodes that can be placed directly on the stomach serosa or abdominal wall. This test could be an aid in the diagnosis of diseases in which alterations in gastric myoelectric activity are described; however, it has not achieved widespread clinical adoption [ ]. The ICCs facilitate both the generation and regeneration of gastric slow waves. In the electrogastrographic registries of patients with a reduced number of ICC, there were significantly fewer slow waves and more tachygastria than in patients with an adequate ICC number [ ].
Gastroparesis is a poorly understood disease, without a clear pathophysiological mechanism. Although altered myoelectrical activity may have a mechanistic role in gastroparesis, the contribution of slow-wave abnormalities to the pathophysiology of these conditions remains unclear [ ]. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate gastric myoelectrical activity, through the assessment of gastric slow waves using electrogastrography. Our aim was to determine the prevalence and range of abnormalities in gastric slow waves in adults with gastroparesis using electrogastrography.

## Methods
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ]. We followed the recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration and Joanna Briggs Institute [ , ]. This study was registered in the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO) as CRD42023435301.

## Search Strategy
We conducted a systematic search in Medline (PubMed), Embase, LILACS, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, collecting publications from inception to September 2023. No restrictions on language, publication format, or publication date were applied to the search. The detailed search strategy can be found in Supplementary Material 1. We screened the reference lists of all the review articles identified through our electronic search for additional studies. We checked the reference lists of all the included primary studies for additional references. We contacted the corresponding authors of the published and ongoing trials to seek recent data or additional data where necessary.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
We included original research articles in which electrogastrography was used to evaluate adults with gastroparesis. We excluded review articles, letters/correspondence, short communications, editorials/opinions, commentaries, pictorial essays, gray literature such as conference proceedings, posters, abstracts, government reports, and online forums for reports from for-profit and non-profit organizations.
We included studies on patients older than 18 years with gastroparesis who underwent electrogastrography. Gastroparesis was defined as a delay in gastric emptying in patients with gastrointestinal symptoms, without a mechanical obstructive cause [ ]. If the word gastroparesis was not mentioned, and when reviewing the inclusion criteria and the description of the subjects in the studies, they met the three criteria for gastroparesis, they were included in our review, even if they were being described with other diseases, such as functional dyspepsia [ ]. Studies that included only healthy subjects or those with poorly defined diseases were excluded. We also included pretreatment electrogastrographic recordings of studies that investigated treatment in patients with gastroparesis. We excluded studies in which invasive electrogastrographic (i.e., serous or mucosal) recordings and high-resolution techniques were performed because they represent different methodologies that are not comparable to surface electrogastrograms. We excluded studies in which there was surgical manipulation or alteration of the gastrointestinal anatomy prior to the electrogastrogram measurement.

## Study Selection and Data Extraction and Management
Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts for inclusion, and subsequently excluded non-relevant ones. Discrepancies were discussed between the authors, with a third reviewer mediating if necessary. The selected results were then screened for full text. A list of articles was generated for full-text review and data were independently extracted by two authors using a form developed by the authors. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with the participation of a third author. Attempts were made to contact the corresponding author by e-mail if data were not available. We extracted information on the author, year of publication, study title, study site (country), study design, diagnostic criteria used, population, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and reference ranges. Data from electrogastrography methodology, such as number and type of electrodes, skin preparation, and method of electrode placement. We also extracted pre-specified primary and secondary outcomes.

## Risk of Bias Assessment
Two authors independently assessed the risk of bias using the risk of bias assessment tool described in the Joanna Briggs Institute Evidence Synthesis Handbook [ ]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and review by a third reviewer. We assessed the risk of bias of the outcomes specified for inclusion in the outcome summary tables based on the following questions: (1) Was the sampling frame appropriate to address the target population? (2) Were the study participants appropriately recruited? (3) Was the sample size adequate? (4) Were the study participants and settings described in detail? (5) Was data analysis performed with sufficient coverage of the identified sample? (6) Were valid methods used to identify the condition? (7) Was the condition measured in a standard and reliable manner for all the participants? (8) Was there an appropriate statistical analysis? 9) Was the response rate adequate? If not, the low response rate was adequately managed.
Each potential source of bias was rated as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not Applicable based on the criteria outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute’s Evidence Synthesis Handbook [ ]. The tool does not allow for a summary assessment of the risk of bias; therefore, we conducted only a narrative review of the results. In cases of uncertainty about the methodology or data, we contacted the study authors for clarification.

## Outcomes
Primary and secondary outcomes were recorded under both fasting and post-stimulus conditions. The primary outcome measured was the percentage of the recording duration where the dominant power was in normogastria, bradygastria, and tachygastria. Secondary outcomes measured were dominant frequency (DF), power ratio (PR), change in post-stimulus dominant power (IDP), dominant frequency instability coefficient (DFIC), relationship between electrogastrogram results and symptoms recorded by study subjects, and relationship between gastric biopsy results and electrogastrogram recording. The dominant frequency instability coefficient was defined as the ratio of the standard deviation (SD) to the mean (M) of the DF and power of the electrogastrogram (CI = SD/M × 100%) [ ].

## Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1; 2023 The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We used the meta and metafor packages and the metoprop, metocont, and metanean functions. If statistical pooling was inappropriate, a narrative summary was performed using the ‘summary of effect estimates’ method, as described in the Cochrane Handbook [ ].
Meta-analyses were performed when there were sufficient similarities between studies with respect to intervention and outcome measures. If data were presented as medians and interquartile ranges or ranges, the means and standard deviations were calculated using the formulas described by Wan et al. [ ]. and Hozo et al. [ ]. Data are presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%) for categorical outcomes and as mean ± standard deviation for continuous outcomes. We report the mode for the number of electrogastrogram electrodes used.
Where controlled studies were available, the measured electrogastrogram results were combined using a random-effects model with the DerSimonian-Laird estimator for interstudy variance with a 95% confidence interval (CI) [ ]. A random-effects model was chosen because of the expected heterogeneity between studies owing to variability in experimental methods. Since different methodologies are used in the electrogastrogram technique for continuous data, we estimated the standardized mean difference (SMD) based on Hedges’ g (adjusted) for comparison between patients and controls due to the small sample sizes in many of the included studies [ ]. When the SMD was zero, there was no difference between the groups. If the SMD result was negative, the mean electrogastrogram measurements in the patient group were lower than those in the control group. For effect size, we considered a small effect for an SMD less than 0.4, a moderate effect between 0.4 and 0.7, and a large effect if it was greater than 0.7 [ , ]. We also reported the SMD 95% CI; if the CI encompassed zero, it was considered not statistically significant [ ].
For the meta-analysis of proportions, to address the asymmetry in the distribution of the observed proportions, we applied transformations, thus improving the validity of subsequent statistical analyses [ ]. During the meta-analysis, transformed proportions and their inverted variances were used for all calculations. The summarized ratio and its CI were then converted back to the original proportion metric for easy interpretation [ ]. We used the Freeman-Tukey double-arc transformation as small sample sizes were expected, and/or extreme proportions needed to be addressed [ ].
Heterogeneity was measured using the I2 test, and considerable heterogeneity was considered if I2 was ≥ 75% [ ]. We also measured the prediction interval (PI) [ ]. In homogeneity, the PI matches the summarized effect CI, and in heterogeneity, the PI covers a wider range than the CI [ ]. We evaluated the bias of small studies using R, meta and metafor packages and funnel and metabias functions. For meta-analyses comparing electrogastrogram recordings between gastroparetics and controls, we performed funnel plots to assess the biases of small studies using a visual aid. If the number of studies in the meta-analysis allowed it (k > 10), the evaluation was complemented with Egger’s test. We assessed publication bias using the Vevea and Hedges weight function model, using the WeightR package and WeightFunct function in R. In this model, we performed a likelihood ratio test, with a p-value < 0.1 indicating publication bias [ ].
Gastroparesis has different etiologies, so we assessed whether these could determine differences in the outcomes evaluated and might explain the expected high heterogeneity. If there was an adequate number of studies, we performed a subgroup analysis. Since the two main etiologies of gastroparesis are diabetic and idiopathic, and most studies were performed with these etiologies, we performed the analysis with these two groups [ ]. Idiopathic gastroparesis was defined as a gastroparesis of unknown cause; that is, not from diabetes, not from prior gastric surgery, not from medications, and not related to other endocrine, oncologic, neurologic, metabolic, or rheumatologic causes. The test for subgroup difference was considered significant if p < 0.05.

## Results
Our database search identified 3730 articles, of which 31 were included. Figure  shows the flowchart of the review process. Table presents the characteristics of the studies included. The 31 studies included 1545 patients and 340 controls with EGG records. There was a higher proportion of women undergoing EGG recordings (71.1% versus 28.9%). Eight studies did not report the sex [ – ]. Electrogastrography recordings in controls were similar between women and men (48.7% versus 51.3%, respectively). Of the studies that reported controls, four studies did not report the sex [ , , , ]. Of the included studies, 21 (67.7%) were conducted in the United States [ , , , , – ], four in Germany [ , , , ], and one each in Taiwan [ ], Turkey [ ], the Netherlands [ ], France [ ], Israel [ ], and Japan [ ]. All studies were prospective. Of the 31 included studies, 14 had a control group [ – , – , , , , , ]. 
Regarding the etiology of gastroparesis 11 studies (35.5%) were diabetic gastroparesis [ , , , – , , , , , ]; two (6.5%) idiopathic gastroparesis [ , ]; eight (25.8%) were diabetic gastroparesis plus idiopathic [ , , , , , , , ]; in three (9.7%) the cause of gastroparesis was not specified [ , , ]; in three (9.7%) the diagnosis was functional dyspepsia with delayed gastric emptying [ , , ]; one study included gastroparetics and systemic sclerosis [ ]; one gastroparesis associated with malignancy [ ]; one post-infectious gastroparesis [ ]; and one included patients with gastroparesis secondary to diabetes, idiopathic, with scleroderma, Parkinson’s disease, postviral, sarcoidosis, or post surgical [ ]. For the diagnosis of gastric emptying, 24 (77.4%) studies used gastric emptying scintigraphy alone [ , , , , – , – , – , ], four studies (12.9%) used a radiopaque marker test [ , , , ], one (3.2%) used the C13 octanoic acid breath test [ ], one study did not specify the test used [ ], and one study used both gastric emptying scintigraphy and the C13 octanoic acid breath test [ ]. Further clinical definitions and study characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

## Subgroup Analysis for Percentage of Dominant Power
Gastroparesis has different etiologies, so we analyzed the percentage of dominant power in the two most frequent etiologies, diabetic and idiopathic. Forest plots of the subgroup analysis are presented in Supplementary Material 2. The percentage of fasting and post-stimulus normogastria was lower in diabetic gastroparesis with 30% (95% CI [23.8 to 40.6%], I2 = 99%) and 36.2% (95% CI [18.9 to 55.6%], I2 = 100%) respectively, compared to idiopathic gastroparesis with 53% (95% CI [23.4 to 81.5%], I2 = 100%) and 68.6% (95% CI [35 to 93.8%], I2 = 97%) respectively. This difference between these groups was not significant both in fasting (p = 0.20) or post-stimulus (p = 0.10) normogastria. The percentage of fasting bradygastria was higher in diabetic gastroparesis (54.1%, 95% CI [34.8 to 72.9%], I2 = 99%), compared to idiopathic gastroparesis (33.7%, 95% CI [9.3 to 64.2%], I2 = 100%), but the difference was not significant between the groups (p = 0.27). We did not analyze the subgroups in post-stimulus bradygastria due to the small number of studies. The percentage of fasting and post-stimulus tachygastria was higher in diabetic gastroparesis with 21.7% (95% CI [17.6 to 26.2%], I2 = 90%) and 21.9% (95% CI [16 to 28.4%], I2 = 98%) respectively, compared to idiopathic gastroparesis with 13.4% (95% CI [8.8 to 18.8%], I2 = 92%) and 10.2% (95% CI [4 to 18.7%], I2 = 95%) respectively. This was a significant difference in fasting (p = 0.02) or post-stimulus (p = 0.03) tachygastria.

The pooled data from ten studies [ – , – , , , , ], showed a mean of 3.28 cpm (95% CI 2.71 to 3.86, I2 = 99%, PI: 1.07 to 5.5). Ten studies reported the fasting DF in controls [ – , , – , , , ]. The pooled data from eight studies [ – , , , , , ], showed a mean of 2.91 cpm cycles (95% CI 2.63 to 3.19, I2 = 96%, PI: 1.92 to 3.91). Nine studies reported the post-stimulus DF in controls [ – , , – , , ]. The pooled data from seven studies [ – , , , , ], showed a mean of 2.9 cpm (95% CI 2.62 to 3.18, I2 = 97%, PI: 1.93 to3.86). We included 10 controlled studies that reported the fasting DF [ – , , , , , , , ]. In the pooled data from seven studies [ – , , , , ], there was no significant difference in the DF between the gastroparetic and control groups (SMD = − 0.171 [95% CI − 0.522 to 0.181], I2 = 62%, PI: − 1.187 to 0.846). We included nine controlled studies that reported the post-stimulus DF [ – , , , , , , ]. ## Dominant Frequency Instability Coefficient
Of the included studies, four reported the fasting DFIC in gastroparetics [ , , , ]. The pooled data from two studies [ , ], showed a mean of 0.49 (95% CI 0.27 to 0.71, I2 = 95%). Four studies reported the post-stimulus DFIC in gastroparetics [ , , , ]. The pooled data from two studies [ , ], showed a mean of 0.49 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.67, I2 = 90%). Al Kafee et al. compared the fasting and post-stimulus DFIC in gastroparetics, reporting no significant difference (0.6 ± 0.19 and 0.58 ± 0.21, respectively, p = 0.464) [ ]. Two studies reported the DFIC fasting and post-stimulus in the controls. Al Kafee et al. reported a DFIC of 0.37 ± 0.24 and 0.44 ± 0.17, respectively (p = 0.401) [ ], and Pfaffenbach B et al. reported a DFIC of 0.275 (0.08–0.535) and 0.22 (0.065–0.475), respectively (median, 5th/95th percentiles) [ ].
We included three controlled studies that reported the fasting DFIC. A meta-analysis could not be performed using the data presented herein. Al Kafee et al. reported a significantly higher fasting DFIC in gastroparetics (0.6 ± 0.19 versus 0.37 ± 0.24, p = 0.043) [ ]. Similar results were reported by Pfaffenbach et al. were gastroparetic patients had a higher DFIC (0.31 [0.23– 0.42] versus 0.275 [0.08–0.535] [median, 5th/95th percentiles]) [ ]. However, a previous study by Pfaffenbach et al. reported a lower fasting DFIC in gastroparetic patients than in controls (0.15 [0.05–0.29] versus 0.275 [0.08–0.535] [median, 5th/95th percentiles]) [ ]. We included three controlled studies that reported information on post-stimulus DFIC. A meta- analysis could not be performed with the data presented. Al Kafee et al. reported a higher post-stimulus DFIC in gastroparetics (0.58 ± 0.21 versus 0.44 ± 0.17, p = 0.061) [ ]. Similar results were presented by Pfaffenbach et al., with a higher post-stimulus DFIC in gastroparetics (0.28 [0.18–0.37] versus 0.22 [0.065–0.475], [median, 5th/95th percentiles]) [ ]. However, a previous study by Pfaffenbach et al. reported a lower post-stimulus DFIC in gastroparetic patients than in controls (0.13 [0.04–0.34] versus 0.22 [0.065–0.475], [median, 5th/95th percentiles]) [ ].

## Changes in Post-stimulus Dominant Power
Of the included studies, seven reported the IDP in gastroparetics [ , , , , , , ]. The pooled data from seven studies [ , , , , , , ], showed a mean of 1.45 decibels (dB) (95% CI 0.25 to 2.65, I2 = 85%, PI: -2.64 to 5.54) (Fig.  ). Of the included studies, three reported the IDP in controls [ , , ]. The pooled data from three studies [ , , ], showed a mean of 5.03 dB (95% CI 4.24 to 5.82, I2 = 50%, PI: − 3.17 to 13.22) (Fig.  ). We included three controlled studies that reported the IDP [ , , ]. In the pooled data from three studies [ , , ], there was no significant difference in IDP between the gastroparetic and control groups (SMD = − 1.530 [95% CI − 3.476 to 0.415], I2 = 97%, PI: − 26.400 to 23.340) (Fig.  ). 

## Power Ratio
Of the included studies, eight reported the PR in gastroparetics [ , – , , , , ]. The pooled data from four studies [ , , , ], showed a mean of 1.4 (95% CI 0.8 to 2.0, I2 = 61%, PI: − 0.98 to 3.78) (Fig.  ). Of the included studies, seven reported the PR in controls [ – , , , , ]. The pooled data from five studies [ – , , ], showed a mean of 5.26 (95% CI 2.58 to 7.95, I2 = 97%, PI: − 4.08 to 14.60) (Fig.  ). We included seven controlled studies that reported the PR [ , – , , , ]. In the pooled data from four studies [ , , , ], there was no significant difference in PR between the gastroparetic and control groups (SMD = − 0.790 [95% CI − 1.964 to 0.384], I2 = 90%, PI: − 6.299 to 4.719) (Fig.  ). 

## Subgroup Analysis for Power Ratio
Subgrouping the data based on the gastroparesia etiology revealed that the mean PR was significantly lower in idiopathic gastroparesia (0.98, 95% CI [0.71 to 1.25], I2 = 0%) compared to diabetic gastroparesia (2.01, 95% CI [1.32 to 2.71], I2 = 0%) (p < 0.01). In the subgroup analysis of controlled studies, although the SMD was smaller in the idiopathic gastroparesia (− 1.290, 95% CI [− 2.947 to 0.366], I2 = 94%) compared to diabetic gastroparesia (− 0.291, 95% CI [− 1.945 to 1.363], I2 = 45%), it was not significant in either group. The difference between the two groups was also not significant (p = 0.40). Forest plots of the subgroup analysis are presented in Supplementary Material 2.

## Relationship of Symptoms with Electrogastrography
The results of the relationship between the symptoms reported by gastroparetics and electrogastrogram measurements were contradictory. Two studies reported no difference in the frequency or severity of symptoms between subjects with normal and abnormal electrogastrograms [ , ]. While Shine et al. reported that the skin frequency-amplitude relationship was positively correlated with vomiting (r = 0.08, p = 0.02), nausea (r = 0.07, p = 0.02), and total symptom scores (r = 0.07, p = 0.04) [ ].
Regarding the relationship of gastric emptying tests with EGG, contradictory results are also reported. In two studies, there was no correlation between the type of arrhythmia in the EGG and delay in gastric emptying [ , ]. But Pfaffenbach et al. reported that patients with delayed gastric emptying revealed a significant increase in fasting and post-stimulus tachygastria compared to patients with normal gastric emptying (p < 0.05) [ ].
The results also varied regarding the relationship between power and gastric emptying. Nohara et al., reported that gastric emptying times were not significantly correlated with a fasting DF of 3 cpm (r = − 0.20, r = − 0.14, NS), post-stimulus DF of 3 cpm (r = − 0.02, r = − 0.13, NS), and post-stimulus PR (r = 0.19, r = 0.12, NS) [ ]. However, Marie et al. reported delayed gastric emptying in 80% of patients with post-stimulus to fasting PR values of < 1 [ ]. When evaluating the relationship between EGG amplitude and gastric emptying tests, Shine et al. reported that EGG amplitude correlated with gastric emptying time at only two hours [ ]. Van Der Voort et al. reported a sensitivity of 0.87 and a specificity of 0.94 for the lack of a post-stimulus increase in the amplitude of the EGG to predict a pathological gastric emptying test in the group of patients, with a positive predictive value is 0.78 and the negative predictive value is 0.97 [ ].

## Relationship of Gastric Biopsies with Electrogastrography
Two studies reported the results of gastric biopsies and EGG. Lin et al. reported fewer ICC in the gastric antrum in 26% of patients with gastroparesis, with abnormal EGG records in 80% of patients with decreased ICC [ ]. Second, Lin et al. evaluated the number of ICC in gastric biopsies, considering a count < 10 cells/high-power field as a severe decrease in ICC (ICC-). They identified a significant association between EGG records and ICC status (P ≤ 0.05), with a 5.62 (95% CI 1.38 to 26.82) times greater likelihood of being ICC- for patients with an abnormal EGG than for patients with a normal EGG. They also had more alterations in the EGG such as: a fewer increases in post-stimulus dominant power (ICC-: − 0.6 ± 0.9 dB versus CCI + : 0.6 ± 0.7 dB), higher DF (ICC-: 3.7 ± 0.4 cpm versus ICC + : 3.0 ± 0.1 cpm, p = 0.03), greater tachygastria both in fasting (ICC-: 32 ± 8% versus ICC + : 11 ± 2%, p = 0.001) and post-stimulus (ICC-: 27 ± 9% versus ICC + : 12 ± 2%, p = 0.02), and lower percentage of normogastria, both in fasting (ICC-: 60 ± 7% versus ICC + : 76 ± 3%, p = 0.02) and post-stimulus (ICC-: 67 ± 8% versus ICC + : 80 ± 2%, p = 0.03) [ ].

## Electrogastrography Methodology
Of the included studies, only one did not report the number of electrodes used [ ]. Thirteen (41.9%) studies used three electrodes [ , , , , , , , , , – ], ranging from 2 to 6 electrodes. Eight (25.8%) studies did not report the type of electrodes used [ , , , – , , ]. Silver-silver chloride electrodes were used in the rest of the studies to record myoelectric activity. Four (12.9%) studies did not report the type of signal recorded [ , – ], 18 (58.1%) took bipolar recordings, eight (25.8%) took unipolar recordings, and one performed unipolar and bipolar recordings [ ]. Thirteen (41.9%) studies did not report the method of skin preparation prior to the placement of the electrodes [ , – , , , , , , , , , ]. Two (6.5%) reported that the skin was cleaned without specifying the element used [ , ], four (12.9%) cleaned with alcohol [ , , , ], and 13 (41.9%) used exfoliating paste [ , , , , , , , , – ]. Eight (25.8%) studies reported that the skin was shaved if hair was present, prior to electrode placement, to improve conductance [ , , , , , , , ]. Five (16.1%) studies did not report electrode position [ , , , , ], three (9.7%) used ultrasonography to guide electrode placement [ , , ], and 23 (74.2%) used anatomical landmarks (xiphoid appendix, umbilicus, and left costal border with midclavicular line) to determine electrode position [ , , , , – , – , – ].
Sixteen (51.6%) studies did not specify whether medications were discontinued prior to the electrogastrogram [ , , , – , – , – , , ], one reported that subjects were not taking any medications [ ], in one the medications were continued [ ], in two studies with diabetics medications were continued with this disease [ , ], and 11 (35.5%) discontinued medications that affected gastrointestinal motility between one day and one week before the study [ , , , , , , – , , ]. Ten (32.3%) studies did not report fasting time prior to electrogastrogram [ , , , – , , , , ], three (9.7%) had a 6 h fast prior to the study [ , , ], and 18 (58%) had overnight fasts prior to registration [ – , – , , , – , , ]. Twenty-six (83.9%) studies reported the use of food stimuli, of which two did not specify what type of food they used [ , ] and five used nutritious liquid foods [ , , , , ], while the remaining studies used solid and liquid foods, with variation in food and caloric intake between studies. Of the rest of the stimuli used in the other studies, two studies did not use stimuli (passive recordings) [ , ], one used only a water load test [ ], one used a visual stimulus [ ], and the other used both the water load test and nutrient bar intake [ ].
The total duration of recording was reported in 24 (77.4%) studies, ranging from 30 min to 24 h, with 90 min being the most recorded time (19.4%). In one study, the recording was performed for 24 h [ ]. The duration of fasting recording was reported in 24 (77.4%) studies, ranging from 15 to 60 min, with a recording of 30 min in 45.2% of the studies. The duration of post-stimulus recording was reported in 23 (74.2%) studies, ranging from 15 to 120 min, with a recording of 60 min in 35.5% of the studies. Four studies reported no register duration [ , , , ]. Two studies reported the total time, but not fasting or post-stimulus time [ , ]. One study only reported the duration of fasting, but not the total or post-stimulus duration [ ]. Gastric slow-wave frequencies defined as normogastria in the studies were on average 2.2 cpm and 3.8 cpm for the lower and upper limit, respectively. The range at the lower limit was 2 to 3 cpm, and for the upper limit from 3.3 to 4 cpm. For the lower limit, 15 (48.4%) studies defined a value of 2 cpm [ , , , – , , – ], one (3.2%) study with 2.2 cpm [ ], four (12.9%) with 2.4 cpm [ , , , ], seven (22.6%) with 2.5 cpm [ , , – , ], one (3.2%) study with 3 cpm [ ], and three (9.7%) did not report a lower limit [ , , ]. For the upper limit, 16 (51.6%) studies defined a value of 4 cpm [ , , , – , , , – ], one (3.2%) study with 3.8 cpm [ ], five (16.1%) with 3.7 cpm [ , , , , ], two (6.5%) studies with 3.6 cpm [ , ], three (9.7%) with 3.5 cpm [ , , ], one (3.2%) study with 3.3 cpm [ ], and three (9.7%) did not report a value [ , , ]. A more detailed description of EGG methods is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

## Risk of Bias

## Quality Assessment
The results of the quality assessment are shown in Fig.  . In the evaluation of the methodological quality of the included studies, the sampling frame was adequate to address the target population in 90.3% of the included studies. In terms of how participants were recruited, 83.9% of the studies were not adequately recruited and 12.9% of the studies were unclear because random sampling of a population or how the sampling had been carried out was not reported. The sample size was inadequate in 83.9% of the included studies because of the lack of evidence that the authors estimated the sample size, and the studies were small, with fewer than 88 patients. In 67.7% of the studies, an adequate description of the subjects was made. We assessed the coverage bias between the study subgroups through data analyses, with sufficient coverage of the samples analyzed. There were no subgroups in 45.2% of the included studies; therefore, they did not apply. 87.1% of the studies reported valid methods for the identification of gastroparesis and in only 12.9% the information was unclear. The information provided regarding the measurement of the condition in 54.8% of the studies was unclear, considering that the training of those performing the tests or the clinical or research experience was not clearly described, or whether there was more than one assessor, whether a comparison was made between the results, or whether the measurement was the same for all participants. Appropriate statistical analysis was performed in 80.6% of studies. The response rate was adequate in 93.5% of studies. The results of quality assessment are presented in Supplementary Table 3. 

## Small-Study Effect and Publication Bias
We performed funnel plots on the pooled analyses that compared the results of the electrogastrogram measurements between the gastroparetic and control groups (Supplementary Material 2). Three to eight studies are included in the graphs. Asymmetry was evident in all funnel plots; however, Egger’s test could not be used because the number of studies was too small. Our evaluation of the Vevea and Hedges weight function model is presented in Table  . As all p-values were greater than 0.1, publication bias was not identified. 

## Discussion
We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence of electrogastrographic abnormalities in adults with gastroparesis. Although there is a similar study in adults with nausea and vomiting syndrome [ ], our study is the first to present data on electrogastrographic abnormalities in adults with only gastroparesis. From a systematic search of five databases, we identified 31 articles that met our inclusion criteria.
In healthy humans, a percentage of normogastria of > 70% is considered normal [ , ]. Considering the normal value, our pooled prevalence of normogastria in gastroparetic patients was lower in both the fasting and post-stimulus periods. Our pooled prevalence of bradygastria was higher than 30% under both fasting and post-stimulus conditions. Carson et al. reported similar results for fasting and post-stimulus normogastria (50.45% and 52.8%, respectively) and fasting and post-stimulus bradygastria (34.1% and 24.56%, respectively) [ ]. In our comparative pooled analysis, the percentage of normogastria was significantly and moderately lower in adults with gastroparesis than in the controls, both during fasting and post-stimulus. Patients with diabetic gastroparesis had a lower percentage of normogastria and more bradygastria and tachygastria, both fasting and post-stimulus; compared to idiopathic gastroparesis and the pooled result of all studies. Diabetic patients present more histologic abnormalities including, increased intercellular fibrosis in the muscle layer and loss of ICC, and loss of fibroblast-like cells; these abnormalities may contribute to changes in gastric motor activity in patients with diabetes mellitus [ ].
Our pooled results for IDP and PR were lower in patients with gastroparesis than in the controls. However, when we made a pooled comparison between gastroparetic patients and controls, despite showing a large decrease in the values, these were not significant. Our results were similar to those reported by Carson et al., in which there was a moderate decrease in the IDP in gastroparetics when compared to controls (SMD: -0.62, 95% CI -2.10 to 0.86) [ ]. The dominant power of the electrogastrogram is proportional to the regularity and amplitude of the gastric slow wave. However, the absolute value of the dominant power may not be significant, as it can be affected by many factors, such as electrode position, skin preparation, and abdominal wall thickness [ , ]. The ratio of the dominant powers of the electrogastrogram before and after an intervention is a parameter associated with alterations in gastric contractions. A ratio > 1 reflects an increase in gastric contractility due to the intervention, whereas a ratio < 1 reflects a decrease in gastric contractility [ , ]. In our study, the PR in gastroparetic patients was close to one, while the controls showed an increase of four times greater than in the gastroparetic patients.
We found that changes in EGG could reflect histological abnormalities. Histological abnormalities include decreased nerve fibers and ganglion cells [ – ], fibrosis of the circular and longitudinal smooth muscle [ ], myenteric inflammation [ – ], and ICC loss [ – ]. Lin et al. found that patients with a decrease in ICC number were 5.62 times more likely to have an abnormal electrogastrogram [ ]. Two types of gastric electrical waves have been described, the first being potential pacemakers that precede the second, slow waves. The former is generated by ICC, mainly in the myenteric region (ICC-MY), and the latter by smooth muscle cells [ – ]. ICC-MY can function as pacemaker cells by electrically connecting them to the adjacent circular and longitudinal muscle layers [ ]. In absence of ICCs, there are alterations in electrical rhythmicity, with a failure to show any slow-wave action potential [ ]. Another type of ICC is intramuscular ICC (ICC-IM), which generates the secondary regenerative component of a slow wave, increasing the depolarization arising from ICC-MY. ICC-IM deficiency results in the absence of a secondary component in the depolarization waves of smooth muscle cells [ ].
Heterogeneity was evaluated using I2 and PI values. The calculated I2 values varied according to the variables pooled in the meta-analysis. All proportion meta-analyses had an I2 > 95%, while 70% of meta-analyses summarizing means had an I2 > 90%, one in 85%, and two < 75%. Considering that the different etiologies of gastroparesis could determine the high heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analysis. In this analysis high heterogeneity persisted in all the groups (≥ 97%). Of the meta-analysis comparing electrogastrograms in gastroparetics versus controls, 20% had an I2 ≥ 90%, 40% had an I2 between 75 and 90%, and 40% had an I2 < 75%. When I2 has been calculated in prevalence meta-analyses, the median I2 was 96.9% (interquartile range: 90.5–98.7) [ ]. In a meta-analysis of other types of data, the median I2 was 21.1% (interquartile range: 0.0–49.7), with 5.6% of analyses having I2 ≥ 75% [ ]. Some variables can affect I2 estimation, such as sample size, variance estimator, type of result being pooled, transformation method used in the proportion meta-analysis, and even the result itself [ – ]. On the other hand, five meta-analyses (post-stimulus bradygastria in gastroparetic patients, fasting tachygastria in gastroparetic patients, fasting DF in controls, post-stimulus DF in controls, and fasting tachygastria in adults) had a PI similar to the CI of the summary estimator. In most of our meta-analyses, a wide PI was observed. One possible explanation for this wide PI is that it occurred more frequently in meta-analyses with continuous results [ , ], such as those presented in our meta-analyses. Our meta-analysis included a few studies with asymmetry in visual inspection of funnel plots. Prediction intervals are strongly based on the assumption of a normal distribution of effects across studies and can be very problematic when the number of studies is small, in which case they may appear falsely broad or narrow. Therefore, their use is recommended when the number of studies is reasonable and there is no clear asymmetry in the funnel plot [ ]. Our results show a high degree of heterogeneity. Meta-analyses that address wide-ranging issues or the incidence or prevalence of a phenomenon in various settings can bring together very heterogeneous studies [ ].
To date, low-resolution electrogastrography has not been clinically adopted. It is a study with technical limitations, which may limit the clinical potential of electrogastrography [ ]. The first limitation is anatomical variability, making it difficult to place electrodes without superposition on the stomach and affecting the recording [ ]. The second limitation is the low reliability of only measuring the slow-wave frequency, defining arrhythmias using this measure. Beyond frequency, EGG data on amplitude can provide valuable information on whether a normal contractile response has occurred after a stimulus [ ]. The third limitation is the weak amplitude of the gastric signal, which can be easily contaminated by intrinsic and extrinsic sources of noise as well as by motion artifacts [ ]. On the other hand, high-resolution techniques apply dense arrays of electrodes such that bioelectrical propagation sequences can be resolved in accurate spatiotemporal detail [ ]. Despite these limitations, our data obtained from low-resolution electrogastrography can provide a basis for the interpretation and analysis of the results of new high-resolution methods of gastric electrophysiology. This can be combined with high-resolution methods to overcome the limitations of traditional electrogastrography. The resulting high-resolution electrogastrography adds a measurement of slow-wave propagation, a measure that demonstrates an association with gastric symptoms [ ].
This study had some limitations. First, it was not possible to review all the studies found because of limitations in full-text access. Second, considerable heterogeneity was observed in the meta-analysis, mainly in the meta-analysis of proportions when all studies were pooled and in the subgroup analyses. Third, few studies have been conducted on adults. Fourth, because we used low-resolution electrogastrography studies, we were unable to assess the slow-wave propagation. We recommend that future analyses include more participants in the studies; standardize a protocol for performing electrogastrography defining the number and type of electrodes, skin preparation, method for placing the electrodes on the abdominal wall; fasting time; stimulus to be used and recording time. High-resolution recordings offer the advantage of overcoming many limitations of low-resolution electrography, in addition to measuring the propagation of slow waves. These high-resolution recordings should be the first choice for studying patients with gastroparesis, but if these are not available, low-resolution electrogastrography is an alternative.
In this study, we concluded that adults with gastroparesis had a significantly lower percentage of normogastria than the controls. They also had a higher percentage of bradygastria and tachygastria, although this difference was not statistically significant. There were no significant differences in DF between the adults with gastroparesis and controls. Patients with gastroparesis had less IDP and PR than the controls. Considering our results in patients with gastroparesis, measurements in low-resolution electrogastrography that could be considered are the percentage of frequency of dominant power and the changes in power after stimuli, the changes in dominant power post-stimulus, or the power ratio. The dominant frequency or coefficient of instability of the dominant frequency is not useful in low-resolution electrogastrography.
