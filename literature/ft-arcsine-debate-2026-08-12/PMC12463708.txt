## ABSTRACT

## Introduction
Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI) is seen in primary pancreatic disease but has also been seen in extrapancreatic conditions including celiac disease (CeD). The symptoms of PEI and CeD often overlap, which makes diagnostics challenging. In this systematic review and meta‐analysis, we aimed to investigate the prevalence of PEI in CeD.

## Methods
With the assistance of a professional librarian, we searched five databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science Core Collection, and Google Scholar, up until October 21, 2024. The pooled prevalence of PEI in biopsy‐confirmed CeD was estimated, and the quality of studies appraised.

## Results
We identified and screened the titles and abstracts of 1432 publications, of which 60 were reviewed in full text and 12 were included in the analyses. The overall pooled weighted prevalence of PEI in CeD was 13.5% (95% CI 7.2–21.0). The prevalence was similar among children (14.2%; 95% CI 2.0–32.8) and adults (12.8%; 95% CI 7.8–18.7) with CeD. The prevalence in studies that used secretory testing to define PEI was 13.1% (95% CI 6.4–21.3) and in those requiring digestive tests 17.1% (95% CI 5.8–32.1). The weighted prevalence of PEI was significantly higher among untreated CeD patients (18.2%) than patients on a gluten‐free diet (6.9%) (p = 0.03), in both adults and children.

## Conclusion
One in eight individuals with CeD may suffer from PEI. The prevalence was particularly high in untreated CeD. PEI should be considered in patients who do not respond to a gluten‐free diet.

Keywords: celiac disease, enzyme replacement therapy, gluten‐free diet, malabsorption, meta‐analysis, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, prevalence

## Introduction
Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI) is defined as a reduction of the exocrine pancreatic secretion and/or intraluminal activity of pancreatic enzymes below what is needed for a normal digestion of nutrients [ ]. PEI has been traditionally understood as impaired secretion of digestive enzymes and bicarbonate by the pancreas, most commonly associated with chronic pancreatitis (CP), cystic fibrosis, pancreatic cancer, and pancreatic resection [ ]. Consequently, clinical guidelines and therapeutic strategies have mainly focused on managing PEI in these specific conditions [ , ]. Emerging evidence suggests that the traditional view of PEI as an isolated pancreatic defect is overly narrow. Instead, it should be considered a maldigestion syndrome that disrupts the entire digestive process, leading to nutritional deficiencies, gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, and systemic complications [ ].
Like PEI, celiac disease (CeD) is also associated with nutrient malabsorption and GI symptoms [ , ]. CeD is an immune‐mediated disorder triggered by gluten and related prolamins in genetically susceptible individuals, characterized by gluten‐dependent clinical manifestations, CeD‐specific antibodies, HLA‐DQ2 or HLA‐DQ8 haplotypes, and enteropathy [ ].
The exocrine pancreatic function can be evaluated through direct invasive tests that measure the stimulated pancreatic secretion in duodenal fluid or non‐invasive tests that quantify fecal pancreatic enzymes (secretory tests) [ ]. Indirect non‐invasive tests can be used to evaluate the effect of pancreatic enzyme deficiency on digestion [ ]. The fecal elastase (FE‐1) test is widely used in clinical studies and practice, but has limited diagnostic accuracy due to its low specificity and positive predictive value [ ]. The FE‐1 concentration should be measured in a solid stool sample to reduce the rate of false positive results caused by samples taken from liquid or loose stool. However, this is challenging in patients with CeD, who often experience chronic or intermittent diarrhea [ , ]. PEI can also be assessed by the evaluation of the digestive function of the pancreas [ ]. Available non‐invasive digestion tests include quantification of the coefficient of fat absorption (CFA) after a 72h stool collection in patients on standardized 100g fat diet and 13C‐mixed triglyceride breath test (13C‐MTG) [ ]. A key challenge in PEI research is the variability in diagnostic methods, particularly the distinction between secretory and digestive tests. Additionally, symptom overlap between CeD and PEI complicates differential diagnosis [ , , ].
Recent European guidelines on PEI have specifically addressed the prevalence and clinical relevance of PEI in other diseases, including CeD [ ]. According to the guidelines, PEI testing should be considered if significant malnutrition is present at CeD diagnosis or if there are persisting symptoms that do not respond to a gluten‐free diet (GFD) [ ]. Although the consensus agreement rate for this statement was 97.1%, the Oxford level of evidence was low (Level 4). This indicates an existing knowledge gap [ ].
This study aims to investigate the prevalence of PEI in patients with CeD through a systematic review and meta‐analysis, and to evaluate the outcomes of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) in this patient population.

2.

## Methods
This systematic review and meta‐analysis were reported according to the PRISMA guidelines [ ]. The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database with the registration number CRD42024589065.
2.1.

## Search Strategy
With the assistance of a professional librarian, we searched five databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science Core Collection, and Google Scholar) from inception until October 21, 2024. Complete search strategies are presented in Supporting Information  : Table 1 and Figure  . All full‐text English‐language articles with human data that reported the prevalence of PEI in patients with biopsy‐confirmed CeD were included. Articles without original data, conference abstracts, studies that included a highly selected population, studies using tests with low accuracy for PEI, and studies where any patient had any non‐celiac condition that might impact the pancreas were excluded (Supporting Information  : Table 2).
 Two reviewers independently screened the search results. Disagreement on inclusion of studies and interpretation of data was resolved by discussion between the reviewers, and in unresolved cases through discussion with the senior author. The reference lists of all publications eligible for full‐text screening were screened for additional publications not captured by the search strategies (“snowball strategy”).

2.2.

## Identification of Patients and Outcome
2.2.1.

## Celiac Disease
For the diagnosis of CeD, we required a reported small intestinal biopsy with Marsh stage II or III, according to the modified Marsh classification system, which ranges from stage 0 to stage IIIc and is widely used as a histopathological classification system for CeD [ ].

2.2.2.

## Pancreatic Exocrine Insufficiency
For the diagnosis of PEI, we required PEI to be assessed by any of the following: group (1) gold standard digestive tests: CFA or 72h fecal fat; group (2) other digestive tests: para amino benzoid acid (PABA) test, pancreolauryl test, 13C‐MTG; group (3) secretory direct tests: direct intubation tests (secretin‐cholecystokinin (CCK) and modifications, and Lundh's test); group (4) secretory non‐invasive test: FE‐1.
Participants of all ages were included. The study characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table  .
 

2.3.

## Data Items and Risk of Bias
Data on the following items were retrieved: (1) first author, (2) year of publication, (3) country and region, (4) study population size, (5) age group (children and adults) and mean/median age, (6) proportion of males and females, (7) diagnostic criteria for CeD, (8) duration of CeD, (9) diagnostic test for PEI, (10) timing of PEI diagnosis, (11) PEI prevalence, and (12) dosage and duration of PERT. The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tool [ ] was applied to evaluate the quality of the included publications (Supporting Information  : Table 3).

3.

## Statistics
A random‐effects model with Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to estimate pooled prevalence. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Begg's test (Supporting Information  : Figure 1), and meta‐regression was performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

4.

## Results
We screened the titles and abstracts of 1432 publications (Figure  ). Of these, 60 publications were deemed relevant and eligible for a full‐text review. A total of 48 publications were excluded and reasons for exclusion are presented in Figure  . The remaining 12 publications comprised a total of 659 patients with CeD, but 675 patients were included in the quantitative analysis (Table  ) [ , , , , , , , , , , , ]. The difference between these two numbers is because the study by Walowiak et al. [ ] performed PEI testing on the same group of patients (n = 16) twice—without and on a GFD—so the patients were counted twice.
4.1.

## Description of Included and Excluded Studies
Of the 12 included studies, eight were prospective [ , , , , , , , ], three were cross‐sectional studies [ , , ], and one was a retrospective study [ ]. Small bowel biopsy was performed in all studies, serology tests were used in eight studies [ , , , , , , , ], and genetic testing was carried out in two studies [ , ]. 12 studies were included in the final analysis, 10 from Europe [ , , , , , , , , , ] and two from Asia [ , ]. Six studies included adults [ , , , , , ] [ , , , , , ] and six children [ , , , , , ]. FE‐1 was used in nine studies [ , , , , , , , , ], and PABA [ ], secretin‐cerulein test [ ] and 13C‐MTG test [ ] in one study. CP was an exclusion criterion in four studies [ , , , ]. Three studies [ , , ] reported prevalence of PEI in both untreated and treated patients, and one study [ ] reported prevalence of PEI in the same group of patients before and after treatment with a GFD.

4.2.

## Prevalence of PEI in CeD
Crude prevalence of PEI in CeD was 14.8% (100/675). The overall pooled weighted prevalence of PEI in CeD was 13.5% (95% CI 7.2–21.0) (Figure  , Table  ). The pooled weighted prevalence was similar among studies in children (14.2%; 95% CI 2.0–32.8) and in adults (12.8%; 95% CI 7.8–18.7), and in studies reporting PEI as measured by secretory tests (13.1%; 95% CI 6.4–21.3) or based on digestive tests (17.1%; 95% CI 5.8–32.1) (Table  , Supporting Information  : Figures 2–4). The weighted prevalence of PEI was however significantly higher among untreated CeD patients (18.2%; 95% CI 10.7–34.9) than patients on a GFD (6.9%; 95% CI 1.5–15.0) (p = 0.03) (Table  ). The prevalence of PEI was higher among untreated children (21.2%) compared with children on a GFD (1.5%). A similar difference was observed in adults (untreated: 15.0% vs. GFD: 11.7%) (Figure  ).
 Two studies [ , ] reported remarkably high prevalence estimates (50.0% and 56.3%). When excluding these studies, the weighted pooled prevalence was 10.8% (95% CI 5.7–16.9). Similar observations were obtained in the sub‐group analysis according to the study quality group, where no difference in main results was found.

4.3.

## Gluten‐Free Diet and CeD Duration
Patients with CeD were on a GFD at the time of the study (e.g. when PEI testing was performed) in seven studies [ , , , , , , ], but patients were only followed up in terms of evaluating the response to a GFD in four studies [ , , , ]. The duration of follow‐up on a GFD was reported in three studies and was between three and 12 months [ , , ]. Two studies reported patients with and without response to a GFD (e.g. change in symptoms and results of a follow‐up small bowel biopsy) [ , ], and two studies did not report the response to a GFD during the follow‐up [ , ]. In three studies, a GFD was introduced after the PEI testing, and the duration of follow‐up on a GFD was also between three and 12 months [ , , ].
Newly diagnosed CeD patients were included in seven studies [ , , , , , , ], patients on a GFD were included in seven studies [ , , , , , , ], and one study included patients on gluten challenge (this refers to patients who underwent gluten challenge at a later age, including those initially diagnosed at age ≤ 2 years with delayed challenge, adolescents seeking diagnostic reconfirmation, and individuals previously diagnosed with CeD who had not adhered to a GFD) [ ]. The duration of CeD was reported in three studies [ , , ], and varied from 1 year to 9.2 years. Only one study reported the type of stool according to the Bristol classification [ ]. The serum nutritional panel (fat‐soluble vitamins and trace elements) was reported in three studies [ , , ].

5.

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta‐analysis investigated the prevalence of PEI in patients with biopsy‐verified CeD along with the association between PEI prevalence and demographic data, and treatment‐related factors. In a total of 675 patients with CeD, we found a pooled weighted prevalence of PEI of 13.5%. Even after omitting two studies with a PEI prevalence of > 50% [ , ], more than one in 10 patients with CeD suffered from PEI. We found a strikingly higher PEI prevalence in untreated CeD as opposed to treated CeD, and in contrast to earlier studies, we also included children with CeD in our meta‐analysis.
The pathophysiology of PEI in CeD is complex (Figure  ). Proposed mechanisms include mucosal damage and a reduction in the absorbing surface due to duodenal intestinal villi atrophy in CeD, which reduce the secretion of the gastrointestinal hormone CCK‐pancreozymin (CCK‐PZ) and thus prejudice intraluminal fat digestion because the secretory and motor functions of the target organs, the pancreas and the gallbladder, are impaired [ , ]. Impairment of enterocyte function, increased mucosal permeability, and impaired intestinal enteropeptidase activity have also been reported in patients with PEI and CeD [ , ]. Peptide YY (PYY) has a strong inhibitory effect on pancreatic enzyme output, and was studied in untreated CeD patients; authors concluded that basal PYY levels were elevated, that predigestion of fat enhances PYY release in these patients, and that PYY secretion is correlated with CCK release [ ].
 A previous systematic review and meta‐analysis by Jiang et al. on the same topic published in 2023 [ ] reported a pooled weighted prevalence of PEI in patients with untreated CeD of 26.2% (95% CI 8.43%–43.9%). In contrast to their study (covering the years 1986–2016), we applied no restrictions regarding the year of publication, which enabled us to capture changes in the methodology of PEI diagnostics during a longer time period. Accurately defining the diagnosis of PEI is the most crucial part of every review in the field of PEI. However, in previous reviews, numerous definitions of PEI have been used, some of which are considered unreliable (such as fecal chymotrypsin, duodenal amylase, trypsin activities, pancreatic isoamylase, plasma secretin, serum CCK, and fecal quantification test shorter than 72h). In our study, we applied strict criteria for the diagnosis of PEI and CeD. We only included studies reporting PEI confirmed by digestive tests [PABA [ ] and 13C‐MTG [ ]] and secretory direct [ ] and indirect tests [ , , , , , , , , ], and studies reporting biopsy‐confirmed CeD. For this reason, unlike Jiang et al. [ ], we did not include the study by Fine et al. that showed PEI in two out of eleven (18.2%) CeD patients on a GFD, based on the method of fat analysis in stool samples collected during 48h [ ], which is shorter than the gold standard (72h stool sample collection). Another difference from the previous meta‐analysis is our inclusion of children with CeD. The potential association between PEI and refractory CeD is clinically important; however, current data on this topic are highly limited [ ]. Patients with refractory CeD often have persistent symptoms (e.g. diarrhea) that increase the chance of undergoing PEI investigation, but also that PEI is misinterpreted as refractory CeD ‐ both circumstances will increase the prevalence of PEI in CeD.
There is a critical gap in current knowledge about PEI and refractory CeD, highlighting the necessity for targeted research to address this gap.
Unfortunately, there is a lack of a high‐quality studies on the use of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT), which is crucial for assessing the clinical significance of PEI. Studies on PERT are heterogeneous and characterized by small sample sizes and varying diagnostic methods for PEI and PERT efficacy, but none have met the criteria necessary to draw definitive conclusions regarding the clinical significance of PEI and the success of therapy [ , , ]. This primarily refers to the optimal dose of PERT and the diagnostic method for PEI. FE‐1 is frequently used in clinical practice, but its accuracy for PEI is low mainly because of low specificity and low positive predictive value, from which follows that low FE‐1 values and PEI are not synonymous [ ]. Administration of a minimum dose of 40,000–50,000 units of lipase with main meals and half of that dose (20,000–25,000 units) with snacks has been shown to be effective in adult patients, and is recommended by the guidelines [ ]. A nationwide study in Sweden determined the risk of pancreatitis among patients with biopsy‐verified CeD, showing an almost three‐fold increase in the risk of developing pancreatitis in CeD patients, compared with the general population [ ]. These findings pave the way for future prospective studies on this topic that may fill the knowledge gap on PEI in CeD.
This meta‐analysis has several strengths. First, we searched five major databases to identify relevant studies, enabling a comprehensive screening of existing literature. Second, our main analysis was restricted to histologically confirmed cases of CeD to increase validity, since diagnosis of CeD based exclusively on serological markers could overestimate the true prevalence of CeD. Third, we applied strict criteria for defining PEI and performed several subgroup analyses to explore potential differences between study characteristics, age groups, and PEI test methods that could shed light on our findings. Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis, excluding studies with extreme prevalence estimates of PEI.
We acknowledge some limitations to our study. First, we lacked data on PEI in patients with CeD from large parts of the world, including East Asia, Oceania, USA, South America, and Africa. Second, we did not have data on the ethnicity of the included patients on an individual level. Our results should therefore be transferred to different ethnic populations with caution. Third, determining the prevalence of PEI in diseases not primarily related to the pancreas, such as CeD, requires the exclusion of other primary pancreatic disorders, particularly CP. This exclusion was only addressed in four of the included studies, which represents a significant limitation of this review. Fourth, data on fat‐soluble vitamins and trace elements, which are characteristic of PEI, were reported in only three studies. Given that diarrhea is a common symptom of CeD, and that FE‐1 is not a reliable method when analyzed in liquid stool, it can be concluded that diagnostic methods constitute a major limiting factor in most of the studies reviewed in our study. This, as well as other differential diagnoses in these patients, such as microscopic colitis or diarrhea‐predominant type of irritable bowel syndrome, should be considered when interpreting the results. Finally, meta‐analyses of prevalence often yield high degrees of heterogeneity and should hence be interpreted with caution since they do not always accurately reflect the heterogeneity of the included studies.

6.

## Conclusion
In our study, 13.5% of individuals with CeD suffered from PEI. The prevalence was particularly high in patients with untreated CeD. PEI is one of the many causes of persistent symptoms in treated CeD. Given the evolving understanding of PEI as a maldigestion syndrome, clinicians are encouraged to consider PEI as a differential diagnosis, incorporating appropriate enzyme replacement therapy and nutritional support to improve patient outcomes and quality of life. However, there remains an unmet need for more prospective studies to evaluate the effectiveness of PERT and to optimize treatment strategies for this patient population. Further research is essential to refine clinical approaches and ensure better management of PEI in the context of CeD.
