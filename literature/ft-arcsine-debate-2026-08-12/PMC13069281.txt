## Abstract

## Background
Use of 3-dimensional electroanatomic mapping (3D-EAM) has become a standard when ablating ventricular arrhythmias (VA) in patients with structural heart disease (SHD). Integrating cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) with 3D-EAM might improve substrate detection and ablation outcomes. Our study aims to evaluate the outcomes of integrating CMR with 3D-EAM for VA ablation in patients with SHD.

## Methods
A literature search was conducted up to December 2025, focusing on the integration of CMR into ablation procedures in patients with VA and SHD. CMR studies were categorized into CMR-guided (CMR data were merged with 3D-EAM) and CMR-aided (CMR data were used to assist in mapping or procedural approach). The control group was patients who received 3D-EAM alone. Primary endpoint was VA recurrence. Secondary endpoints were procedural success, procedural time, fluoroscopy time, radiofrequency ablation (RF) time, and major procedural-related complications.

## Results
A total of 18 studies were included (1,243 participants, mean age 62.7 ± 12.5 years). Both the CMR-guided and CMR-aided had a VA recurrence of 25% and 26%, compared to 51% in 3D-EAM. The CMR-guided showed significantly lower VA recurrence (OR 0.26, 95% CI: 0.17–0.39, I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0) and higher procedural success (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.16-4.41, I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0) compared to 3D-EAM. Major procedural-related complications were low across all groups with 3%, 4%, and 2% in CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM, respectively. There were no differences in procedural time, fluoroscopy time, and RF time.

## Conclusion
The CMR-guided ablation approach in patients with VA and SHD demonstrated a lower VA recurrence and higher procedural success with comparable safety to 3D-EAM alone.

1.

## Introduction
Ventricular arrhythmias (VA) may occur in patients with structural heart disease (SHD). While implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICD) may prevent sudden cardiac death in these patients, recurrent VAs may result in adverse quality of life outcomes due to ICD shocks. In the SHD population, catheter ablation has been increasingly performed for management of VA [ ].
To date, the outcomes after VA ablation in patients with SHD remain unfavorable. Compared to patients with structurally normal hearts, patients with SHD experienced longer procedural time and a lower procedural success rate [ ]. Furthermore, long-term VA recurrence was high in both ischemic and non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, with the rates of 18.7 and 26.7 per 100 patient-year, respectively [ ].
More precise mapping tools for identifying underlying VA substrate may lead to improved outcomes following VA ablation. Currently, three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping (3D-EAM) has been regarded as the gold standard and a fundamental technique for scar identification during ablation procedures. Unfortunately, bipolar voltage recording in 3D-EAM can miss a considerable amount of substrates because it cancels out the far-field signals [ ]. Moreover, identified scars could be false negatives in scenarios where there is poor catheter contact. These could be corrected with advanced imaging modalities, including cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) [ ].
Therefore, our meta-analysis aimed to investigate the efficacy of integrating CMR with 3D-EAM during VA ablation in the SHD population.

2.

## Methods
Our meta-analysis was conducted according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines. The present study was registered with the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) under CRD No. 42025645887.
2.1.

## Search strategy
A literature search through MEDLINE, EMBASE and Cochrane database was performed independently by two authors (W.C. and P.P.) from inception until December 2025. We used the following search terms: (“magnetic resonance imaging” OR MRI OR “cardiac magnetic resonance imaging” OR “Cardiac MRI” OR CMRI) AND (“ventricular tachy∗" OR “ventricular arrhythmia∗" OR “premature ventricular complex∗" OR “premature ventricular contraction∗" OR PVC OR VA OR VT OR VF OR “ventricular fibrillation”) AND (ablation∗). There were no language or publication date restrictions. All animal studies, review articles, case reports, case series, commentaries and editorial letters were excluded. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with the third author (S.P.). The full search strategies are detailed in Supplementary .

2.2.

## Eligibility criteria
We included adults aged 18 or older with VA and SHD who underwent VA ablation. Studies comparing CMR-integration during ablation procedure with 3D-EAM were included. The ablation procedure using CMR as part of the mapping process was divided into two groups: CMR-guided and CMR-aided. The CMR-guided group included those with real-time integration of CMR-derived scar with 3D-EAM during the ablation procedure, while the CMR-aided group included those procedures in which CMR data were used to identify the location of structural substrates, select mapping or ablation sites, or plan the procedural approach without integrating with 3D-EAM. The control group consisted of patients included as comparators in the CMR studies, where 3D-EAM was performed alone. Patients with structurally normal hearts, for whom imaging registration and ablation were done through post-hoc analysis, were excluded.
The primary endpoint was recurrent sustained VA after ablation, defined as any documented sustained VA or ICD therapy (including antitachycardia pacing or ICD shock). Secondary endpoints included procedural success, procedural time, fluoroscopy time, RF ablation time, RF application points, and major procedural-related complications. Procedural success was defined as non-inducibility of any sustained VA, while procedural-related complications were defined as any complications related to the index ablation procedure that required additional intervention or prolonged hospitalization. The Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) were used to assess the quality of included studies, as shown in and Supplementary .
 
2.3.

## Data abstraction
Baseline characteristics collected included demographic data, procedural characteristics, and imaging parameters such as CMR sequences, voxel size, imaging registration programs, anatomical landmarks, and procedural approaches. The statistical values obtained included both categorical and continuous data. For categorical data, we collected the proportions of patients with VA recurrence, procedural success, and procedure-related complications in the CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM alone groups. Odd ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported when available. For continuous data, the means and standard deviations (SD) of procedural time, fluoroscopy time, RF ablation time, and RF application points were collected in the CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM alone groups. Mean differences (MD) with SD were reported when available. In cases of missing data, attempts were made to contact the original investigators for the necessary information. If the data could not be retrieved, an imputation method was applied in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [ ].

2.4.

## Statistical analysis
All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.3. The Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied in cases of zero event proportions to minimize variance instability. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q test, I2 statistics, and the between-study variance (τ2). Cut-off values of 50% and 75% were used to indicate substantial and considerable heterogeneity. A random-effect model was employed for all of the analyses. Publication bias was assessed by inspecting the symmetry of funnel plots. Meta-regression analysis was performed based on the percentage of ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM) and publication year to explore sources of heterogeneity.

## Results
3.1.

## Search results
The systematic search identified a total of 2,053 studies. After removing duplicate studies, 1,705 studies were passed through abstract screening and the remaining 60 studies underwent full-text review. A total of 18 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] (5 non-randomized controlled trial, 8 quasi-experimental trials, 2 prospective cohorts, and 3 retrospective cohorts) were included in the final analysis. The screening process was summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram ( ).
 
3.2.

## Study population
A total of 1,243 patients with VA and SHD who underwent RF ablation for VA were included in the study, with a mean age of 62.7 ± 12.4 years and a mean follow-up period of 18 ± 13.5 months after VA ablation. The majority of patients had impaired left ventricular function (mean left ventricular ejection fraction 36.4 ± 11.8%), and the etiology of cardiomyopathy was evenly distributed between ischemic and non-ischemic causes (56.6% and 43.0% respectively). An overall 49% of included patients had cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED) implanted. All baseline characteristics are presented in .

## CMR characteristics
Out of the 18 studies included, 17 [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] reported on CMR sequences and CMR systems. Apart from Penala et al. [ ] and Pier et al. [ ], which used turbo field echo (TFE), all other studies used inversion recovery (IR). Among the 15 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] that used IR, 4 studies [ , , , ] used IR alone, while 11 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , ] combined IR with other sequences. Of these, 7 studies [ , , , , , , ] combined IR with gradient echo (GRE), 5 [ , , , , ] combined IR with wideband (WB), 3 [ , , ] combined IR with turbo fast-low angle shot (turbo-FLASH), and 1 [ ] used Short Tau IR (STIR). Five studies [ , , , , ] used scar-modification strategies by targeting abnormal electrograms or low-voltage areas that corresponded to CMR-identified scar.
Regarding the CMR system, 11 studies [ , , , , , , , , ] used 1.5 Teslar (T) scanners, while 5 studies [ , , , , ] used 3 T scanners and 1 study [ ] used both. The mean time from contrast injection to imaging acquisition was 12.72 ± 3.3 min. Position error was reported in only 5 studies [ , , , , ], with a mean of 4.0 ± 1.4 mm. Most of the CMR-guided studies [ , , , , , ] employed commercial programs to integrate imaging data with 3D-EAM. Integration programs used included CARTO-Merge [ , , ] and ADAS-VT [ , , , ]. ## RF ablation time

## RF application points
A total of 5 studies [ , , , , ] reported on RF application points. The CMR-aided group showed the highest number of RF application points (49.2 points), followed by 3D-EAM (32.1 points) and CMR-guided (27.2 points), respectively ( ). Significant differences in RF application points were found between CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM (p = 0.03). Results comparing CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM are presented in .

3.10.

## Major procedural-related complications
A total of 10 studies [ , , , , , , , , , ] reported on procedure-related complications. The CMR-guided and CMR-aided groups had complication rates of 3% and 4%, respectively, comparable to 2% in the 3D-EAM group ( ). There was no significant difference in complication rates among the CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM groups (p = 0.86). The most commonly reported complications were cardiac tamponade and high-grade atrioventricular block (AVB). Cardiac tamponade occurred in 1.2% (4/338) of CMR-guided, 1.6% (2/129) of CMR-aided, and 2.4% (1/42) of 3D-EAM. Meanwhile, high-grade AVB occurred in 3.3% (11/338) of CMR-guided, 0% (0/129) of CMR-aided, and 0% (0/42) of 3D-EAM. Results comparing CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM are presented in .

3.11.

## Publication bias
Funnel plots are shown in . Since all plots were symmetrical, the analyses had no publication bias.

3.12.

## Meta-regression and subgroup analysis
Meta-regression analysis was performed based on the proportion of ICM, as there was a difference in the ICM proportion between the groups (55.6% in CMR-guided, 56.5% in CMR-aided, and 60.5% in 3D-EAM alone). The results showed no effect of the ICM proportion on VA recurrence and procedural success. Additionally, a regression analysis based on publication was conducted, with no effect observed on VA recurrence and procedural success. The results of the meta-regression analysis are presented in , and bubble plots are shown in Supplementary . Finally, a subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of the CMR system (1.5 or 3 T scanners) on VA recurrence. We found no significant effect of the CMR system on VA recurrence in both the CMR-guided and CMR-aided groups (p = 0.453 and 0.065, respectively). The forest and funnel plots are presented in Supplementary .

4.

## Discussion
Our meta-analysis is the first to compare the efficacy and safety among CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM for VA ablation in SHD population. The main finding was that the CMR-guided group demonstrated a lower risk of VA recurrence compared to 3D-EAM alone (OR 0.26, 95% CI: 0.17–0.39, I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0). Procedural success rates were higher in CMR-guided compared to 3D-EAM alone (OR 2.27, 95%CI: 1.16-4.41, I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0). Both CMR-guided and CMR-aided showed similar procedural time, fluoroscopy time, RF ablation time, number of VA inductions, and procedural-related complication rates (p = 0.69, 0.67, 0.95, 0.82, and 0.86, respectively). Consistent findings demonstrated a high VA recurrence rate when 3D-EAM was used alone. A large cohort study reported a high VA recurrence rate of 30% in 3D-EAM over 18 months after ablation [ ]. Similarly, a previous meta-analysis showed a VA recurrence rate of 39% in 3D-EAM during a median follow-up of 22 months [ ]. The VA recurrence rate in 3D-EAM is even higher in our study (51%). However, this could be affected by the lower proportions of anti-arrhythmic drugs used, especially amiodarone, after the ablation procedure in our 3D-EAM group (22 %), compared to the others (28% [ , ]). This is supported by a 22% reduction of VA recurrence in patients receiving post-ablation amiodarone which was significantly different compared to those not receiving amiodarone (p = 0.01) [ ]. Meanwhile, other potential contributing factors to differences in VA recurrence in 3D-EAM, including the proportion of non-ischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM) and left ventricular ejection fraction, did not differ between our study and the others [ , ].
A significant advantage of CMR over 3D-EAM is its superior ability to identify scar location, as the primary mechanism of VAs in SHD is scar-related reentry circuits [ , , ]. When CMR is merged into 3D-EAM, additional scar information can be collected, including the location, extension, characteristics, and functional correlation of scars. While identification of regions with abnormal unipolar voltage and visualization of scar on intracardiac echocardiography can help to identify intramural and epicardial scars, CMR may further assist with scar localization. Wijnmaalen et al. [ ] identified additional subendocardial and intramural septal scars with CMR-guided imaging that were not observed as low-voltage areas in 3D-EAM. CMR guidance also demonstrated good scar registration accuracy of 3.8 ± 0.6 mm [ ]. Furthermore, the additional identification of intramural scars can provide information about transmurality. High scar transmurality (>75%) has been shown to be associated with critical VA isthmus sites[ ].
Procedural success was higher in CMR-guided (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.16-4.41, I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0) compared to 3D-EAM alone. This is supported by a better identification of high-risk of arrhythmogenicity scar. Recent CMR technologies can identify heterogeneous tissue channel (HTC). CMR can provide HTC characteristics, including mass, length, and protected areas of the VA isthmus, which are correlated with arrhythmogenicity [ , ]. Sanchez-Somonte et al. [ ] defined protected areas as regions without any healthy tissue. The percentage of protected areas around the core tissue could be classified into fully unprotected (<15%), partially protected (15-40%), and fully protected (>40%) using CMR. High-risk HTC, which had mass >2.5 g, length >6.4 cm, and protected areas >26.2%, were associated with an increased risk of arrhythmogenicity [ ].
Additionally, CMR could shorten the total procedural duration. Our study showed total procedural times of 274 and 288 min for CMR-guided and CMR-aided, respectively, versus 319 min for 3D-EAM alone. This finding aligns with a previous study showing shorter procedural times with CMR-guided compared to 3D-EAM (151 vs. 180 min, respectively) [ ].
Interestingly, the number of RF application points was significantly higher in the CMR-aided group compared to the others (p = 0.03). We postulate that CMR-aided approach may have shown more false-positive scars since it is not integrated with 3D-EAM in real-time, which could lead to excess ablation points. However, all CMR-aided studies reporting on RF application [ , , ] used the 3D-GRE sequence. Although 3D-GRE was effective in identifying HTCs, a considerable number of HTCs were non-specific and not associated with arrhythmogenicity. This could contribute to the high number of false-positive ablation points. A previous study showed that 64% (56/87) of HTCs identified by 3D-GRE were non-specific [ ].
Both CMR-guided and CMR-aided approaches demonstrated favorable safety profiles. Our study showed no significant difference in complication rates between CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM (p = 0.86). These complications may be the result of advancements in ablation techniques and catheter materials. The most common major procedural complications were cardiac tamponade followed by high-grade AVB. However, our study only included major complications that required medical attention or intervention. No previous studies have reported on complications associated with the combination of CMR and 3D-EAM in VA ablation. The overall complication rate for 3D-EAM alone could be approximately 10% [ ].
4.1.

## Clinical implications
General guidelines for management of VAs recommend the use of pre-procedural imaging prior to VA ablation in patients with SHD for procedure planning and localizing structural substrates [ , ]. The guideline on catheter ablation of VAs also supports pre-procedural CMR for both ICM and NICM patients undergoing VA ablation to improve arrhythmia-free survival [ ]. Additionally, incorporating pre-procedural imaging (CMR and/or MDCT) with real-time 3D-EAM integration has been shown to decrease total procedural time and fluoroscopy exposure, according to the recent expert consensus on pre- and post-procedural imaging in electrophysiology [ ]. Hence, our study supported these recommendations by showing the advantages of CMR-guided VA ablation over 3D-EAM alone when performing VA mapping in patients with SHD due to a lower VA recurrence rate and improved procedural success with a favorable safety profile. Nevertheless, the CMR-guided approach requires specific techniques and compatible software, which may limit accessibility in some regions.

4.2.

## Limitations
Our study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the distribution between ICM and NICM could affect outcomes. To address this, we performed a meta-regression analysis on the proportion of ICM, and the results showed no effect ( 
and
 ). However, remaining heterogeneity could result within the NICM population due to various etiologies, leading to varying prognosis. According to prior studies, results of VA recurrence rates could vary from 29% to 58%, and procedural success rates from 38 to 72% [ ]. Second, technological advancement over time may have also affected outcomes post-ablation. Therefore, we performed a meta-regression analysis on the publication year, and the results showed no effect ( 
and
 ).
Third, residual heterogeneity could occur from insufficient data to perform a meta-regression or subgroup analysis. Specifically, the time window between CMR acquisition and ablation varied among the included studies. This variation may lead to disease progression or structural remodeling, potentially affecting outcomes. Therefore, the optimal time window before the ablation procedure remains to be determined. Moreover, the spatial resolution of CMR varied from a voxel size of 2.74 mm3 to 24.64 mm3. Although there is no current recommendation on MRI resolution for VA ablation, poorer resolution might obscure scar visualization.
For heterogeneity within the study population, 49% of the included patients had CIEDs, which could introduce imaging artifacts; however, wide-band sequences were used in patients with CIEDs to mitigate these effects. In addition, none of the included studies enrolled patients with congenital heart disease (CHD). For this specific subgroup, most of the available evidence consists of case reports or case series; therefore, we did not include them in our analysis, and the real-world applicability of our findings to the CHD population should be interpreted with caution.
Finally, all included studies were non-randomized and could introduce selection bias. Of these, some were retrospective cohorts, leading to potential variations in procedure timing among the CMR-guided, CMR-aided, and 3D-EAM groups (e.g., some ablations may have been performed earlier than others, and ablation technology could be different). Nonetheless, given that the majority of studies in our meta-analysis were prospectively designed, the impact of this limitation is expected to be minimal. Still, a well-designed randomized controlled trial is recommended.

5.

## Conclusion
Combining CMR with 3D-EAM reduces the risk of VA recurrence and improves procedural success compared to 3D-EAM alone. This approach also offered a favorable safety profile and should be preferred over 3D-EAM alone for VA ablation in the SHD population.
