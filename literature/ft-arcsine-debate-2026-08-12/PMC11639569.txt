As part of this inequity, persons of color continue to be underrepresented in images depicting illness, a phenomenon well described in the medical literature [ , ]. Lack of representation contributes to misdiagnosis in populations with darker skin [ ]. Infectious disease is no exception [ ], where patients of color experiencing septic shock, central line–associated bloodstream infections, and HIV infection, among other conditions, have worse outcomes [ , ].
To address this inequity, we must increase the diversity of skin color presented in medical literature and teaching materials. While we expect a review of infectious disease teaching materials to affirm underrepresentation of dark skin, we are not aware of a study that examines whether this underrepresentation persists in images of conditions associated with social stigma. Understanding this relationship can inform publication and teaching practices to mitigate implicit biases.
We aim to determine whether there is unequal representation of skin color in infectious disease textbooks and, if so, advocate for more equitable representation. Novel to this work is a focused analysis of skin color depiction in conditions associated with social stigma, which may perpetuate implicit biases among healthcare professionals.

## METHODS

## Image Selection and Condition Categorization
Four infectious disease textbooks in wide circulation were chosen for analysis: Mandell, Douglas, and Bennett's Principles and Practice of Infectious Disease (2020), Manson's Tropical Diseases (2023), Comprehensive Review of Infectious Disease (2019), and Netter's Infectious Diseases (2022). Conditions were categorized as socially stigmatized if linked to sexually transmitted infections, intravenous drug use, poverty, or incarceration ( ), with face validity of the list informed by review of several infectious disease faculty at our institution.

## Positionality
Our raters approached the image classification process through the lens of cisgender, White, and Asian American women. Because we are interested primarily in racial and cultural biases in America, conditions were only included if they were endemic to the United States. All photographic images depicting the prespecified list of conditions ( ) were included, except for those with poor resolution, insufficient skin unaffected by disease to allow for rating, or images in black and white or grayscale.

## Quantification of Skin Color
Skin color of depicted patients was assessed by 2 independent raters (J.Th. and A.G.) using the Fitzpatrick skin type (FST) and Massey-Martin (MM) scales. Images were rated on the individual number scale as well as in groups (FST grouped scale: 1–3 [light/White], 4–6 [dark/non-White]; MM grouped scale: 1–2 [light/White], 3–5 [medium/brown], 6–10 [dark/Black]), classifications used in existing medical literature [ , ]. After individual ratings, raters designated a consensus grouped scale rating for each image. Discrepancies were adjudicated by a third party (J.Ti.). Given lower agreement on individual scale numbers between primary coders compared to group rating, only group designation was adjudicated and used for primary analysis ( ).

## Statistical Analysis
We summarized all categorical and binary variables using raw counts, frequencies, and percentages. We compared all variables between stigmatized and non-stigmatized groups using chi-squared tests. When encountering group comparisons with cells less than 5, we used a Fisher exact test. To find predictors of stigmatized disease images, we used a logistic regression model and reported odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and P values. We conducted univariate odds analysis using consensus data and data from both rater 1 and rater 2. Furthermore, we conducted a reliability analysis to determine agreement between the 2 raters using percent agreement and Cohen kappa for precision. We created graphical illustrations of stigmatized scales using subgroup forest plots with I2 tests and pooled estimates. We used the Freeman-Tukey test to compute proportions in each group of skin color and rater group. All P values less than or equal to .05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 18 (StataCorp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, Texas: StataCorp LLC).

## RESULTS

## Image Characteristics
A total of 285 images were included for analysis. Most images portray conditions associated with social stigma (58.6%). Images were predominantly of lighter skin with 60.4% of photos classified as FST 1–3 compared to 39.6% FST 4–6; 50.9% were classified as MM 1–2, 24.2% as MM3–5, and 24.9% as MM 6–10 ( ).
 

## Interrater Reliability of the Skin Tone Scales
Individual ratings demonstrated fair reliability (FST Cohen kappa 0.34, percent agreement 46%; MM Cohen kappa 0.31, percent agreement 43%). Grouped ratings demonstrated much better reliability; FST grouped scale had near perfect agreement (Cohen kappa 0.82, percent agreement 92%) and MM grouped scale substantial agreement (Cohen kappa 0.71, percent agreement 82%) ( ).

## Skin Type and Association With Stigmatized Conditions
Images depicting conditions associated with social stigma were more likely to be represented on dark skin tones (FST 4–6 and MM 6–10, P < .001). Among images depicting darker skin tones (FST 4–6, MM 3–5, MM 6–10), more stigmatized conditions were depicted than nonstigmatized conditions (P < .001*) ( ).
Images rated FST 4–6 (dark/non-White) have significantly higher odds of depicting a stigmatized condition compared to images displaying FST 1–3 (light/White), with an OR of 4.83 (P < .001*). Likewise for MM groupings, both non-White groups (MM 3–5 [medium/brown] and MM 6–10 [dark/Black]) have significantly higher odds of portraying a stigmatized condition compared to images showing MM 1–2 (light/white), with ORs of 2.51 (P = .002*) and 7.30 (P < .001*) respectively ( ).
 For both raters, there is a significant positive association between higher FST ratings and stigmatized conditions. This association becomes stronger as the FST rating increases from 1 to 6. Similarly, there is a significant positive association between higher MM skin scale ratings and stigmatized conditions ( ).

## DISCUSSION
Skin of color is underrepresented in several major infectious disease textbooks in wide circulation.
Our findings affirm recent work that documented underrepresentation of dark skin in the New England Journal of Medicine “Images in Medicine” series [ ]. Advancing on this work, we observed a strong association between dark skin tones and depiction of stigmatized conditions in textbooks. Indeed, the darker the skin tone depicted, the more likely the image was representing a disease associated with social stigma.
This overrepresentation of darker skin tones in diseases associated with social stigma may contribute to biases that can influence care of patients. A meta-analysis on implicit bias in healthcare professionals showed a significant correlation between implicit bias test scores and negative ratings of clinicians by Black patients, emphasizing that these subconscious biases do influence care of patients [ ]. Inequitable photographic representation can bias health professionals to associate certain conditions with a specific skin tone, which may contribute to misdiagnosis and other negative interactions with the health care system.
We did not correlate skin color representation directly to disease epidemiology, given that persons who may identify as a certain race may not fit into our artificial groups of skin of color. Despite this, a brief comparison of syphilis data supports our conclusion that stigmatized diseases are likely overrepresented on dark skin. In 2022, 66% of syphilis cases from 2022 were in non-White populations, but 76% of our images of syphilis findings were depicted on FST dark/non-White skin tones, supporting likely overrepresentation [ ].
Our study has several limitations. Unfortunately, there has not been a skin tone quantification system devised for the specific analysis of pictures. We chose to use both the FST and MM systems to give a more comprehensive quantification of skin color in our image population as each scale has different limitations [ ]. Another limitation is the implicit biases of the researchers when constructing categories of stigmatized versus nonstigmatized disease; we attempted to mitigate individual biases by seeking input from several infectious disease physicians and trainees of varied identities. We also acknowledge textbooks are a small proportion of images referenced by infectious disease learners and are beholden to updates only when new editions are released. A more comprehensive review of online materials could lend a broader perspective. Despite these limitations, we believe our work affirms existing literature demonstrating underrepresentation of darker skin tones in general while demonstrating a concerning trend regarding the depiction of conditions associated with social stigma.
There have been attempts to reduce implicit bias and close the representation gap in medical literature. Professional societies, such as the Skin of Color Society, were created. The American Academy of Family Physicians, the Journal of American Academy of Dermatology, VisualDx, and others have all generated databases of images with clinical findings on skin of color for health professionals to access quickly and easily online [ ]. Homegrown online databases have proliferated through social media, including the Instagram account Brown Skin Matters (@brownskinmatters) [ ].
These results should serve as a call to action for the publishers of these textbooks to focus on greater equity in skin color representation in future editions. Increasing the number of images of dark skin may improve diagnostic skills. Attention to the distribution of skin color in images depicting conditions associated with social stigma may lessen the impact of bias in the care of patients with infectious diseases.
