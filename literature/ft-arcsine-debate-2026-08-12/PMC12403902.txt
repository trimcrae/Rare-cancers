## Background
Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide. Several studies have examined HPV prevalence in West Africa, but comprehensive regional estimates remain unavailable. This study estimates the overall prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV in West African populations.

## Methods
We conducted a systematic search in Medline, Embase, Global Health, Web of Science, and Africa Index Medicus for articles published up to March 5, 2024. We estimated pooled HPV prevalence using a random-effects model. The Hoy et al. tool assessed the risk of bias, while the I² test measured between-study heterogeneity. Egger’s regression test evaluated publication bias. The study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023463042).

## Results
The analysis included 145 studies from 12 West African countries, comprising 71,324 participants. The pooled prevalence of HPV was 42.3% (95% CI: 38.5-46.1%), with significant heterogeneity (I² = 99.0%; P < 0.001). HPV prevalence varied across population subgroups, with a significantly higher pooled prevalence of 81.4% (95% CI: 68.2-91.8%) among women with cervical cancer. Egger’s test did not indicate significant publication bias (P > 0.05). Among high-risk HPV genotypes, HPV-16 (19.9%, 95% CI: 15.9-24.1%) and HPV-82 (18.9%, 95% CI: 7.5-33.7%) were the most prevalent. Among low-risk genotypes, HPV-81 was the most common, with a prevalence of 12.9% (95% CI: 7.1-20.0%).

## Conclusion
Human papillomavirus prevalence is high in West Africa, with diverse genotypes, including several with oncogenic potential. Targeted health interventions, including vaccination programs and enhanced screening efforts, are recommended to reduce HPV burden in this region.

## Introduction
Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a sexually transmitted infection that is widespread throughout the world [ ]. It is mainly transmitted by skin or mucous membrane contact during sexual relations, but also from mother to child during childbirth [ ]. HPV is highly diverse, with over 450 genotypes identified to date [ ]. Of these, 54 are associated with genital infections [ ], some of which can cause cancer, notably of the cervix, anus and oropharynx [ ]. HPVs are classified into two main groups: low-risk HPV (LR-HPV), generally associated with genital warts and benign lesions (HPV-6,-11,-40,-42,-43,-44,-54, -61, -70,-72,-81 and CP 6108), and high-risk HPV (HR-HPV), responsible for the majority of HPV-related cancers (HPV16,-18,-31,-33,-35,-39,-45,-51,-52,-56,-58,-59,-68,-73, et-82) [ ].
Worldwide, around 5.2% of all cancers are attributable to HPV [ ], primarily affecting the cervix, vagina, penis, vulva, anus and oropharynx. Although many HR-HPV types are involved, types 16 and 18 are responsible for around 70% of HPV-related cancers [ ]. The geographic distribution of HPV genotypes is heterogeneous. For example, HPV 51 is more common in Caucasian populations, while HPV 58 is more prevalent in East Asia and Latin America [ ]. In Africa, types 16 and 18 are dominant, accounting for over 65% of cervical cancer cases in sub-Saharan Africa [ ]. Over 95% of cervical cancers are linked to high-risk HPV infection, as are a significant proportion of cancers of the anus, vagina, penis, vulva and oropharynx [ ]. HPV prevalence is particularly high in sub-Saharan Africa, averaging 24% [ ]. Genotypic diversity is also notable, with HPV 66 prevalent in North Africa and types 35 and 52 in sub-Saharan Africa [ ]. These geographical disparities and the high prevalence of HPV in sub-Saharan Africa are partly linked to HIV co-infection, which favors the development of HPV-related cancers, including cervical cancer, now classified as an AIDS-defining disease [ , ].
Three recombinant prophylactic vaccines have been developed to prevent HPV infection. These vaccines target different HPV genotypes: a bivalent vaccine (types 16 and 18), a quadrivalent vaccine (types 16, 18, 6 and 11) and a nonavalent vaccine (types 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58, 6 and 11) [ ]. To reduce the burden of HPV-related cervical cancer, many countries have implemented national vaccination programs. In sub-Saharan Africa, these programs began to roll out from 2011 [ ]. However, only 27 of the region’s 46 countries have integrated HPV vaccination into their immunization schedules [ ]. In addition, a large proportion of West African countries have yet to introduce this essential vaccine [ ]. The absence of effective preventive measures could lead to a 50% increase in cancer-related deaths by 2040 in these regions, exacerbating health inequalities [ ].
Although several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have investigated HPV prevalence in West Africa, methodological inconsistencies and limited focus on specific populations have hindered accurate generalization of their findings [ – ]. A comprehensive, up-to-date understanding of the epidemiological landscape of HPV in West Africa is lacking. This gap is concerning, as effective HPV prevention and control rely on detailed knowledge of the geographic distribution and predominant genotypes of HPV. Despite the availability of preventive strategies such as vaccination and screening, our understanding of the burden of HPV-related disease in West Africa remains incomplete. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to address these gaps by providing a comprehensive synthesis of available data on the prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes across diverse West African populations.

## Methods

## Registration
The protocol was registered with PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews, under registration number CRD42023463042. This systematic review and meta-analysis were reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. (Appendix ) [ ].

## Search strategy
A comprehensive database search was conducted through Medline, Embase, Global Health, Web of Science, and Africa Index Medicus using the following terms and their variants for HPV infection research: terms such as “Papillomavirus Infections” or “Human Papillomavirus” and “West Africa.” Additional search terms were used to expand the search for abstracts in subject titles and abstracts of all eligible primary research articles up to March 5, 2024 (Appendix ). The search included articles published in English and French.

## Study selection
We included articles reporting on HPV prevalence and genotype distribution, covering both high-risk oncogenic and low-risk HPV genotypes within West African countries. Studies used PCR or ELISA on samples from cervical, vaginal, blood, biopsy, oral, nasopharyngeal, penile, or anal sites. The review considered data from patients who consulted for gynecological issues or participated in screening campaigns, with or without cancer, both hospitalized and non-hospitalized, across all populations, including those who did not seek medical consultation for acute infections. Baseline HPV prevalence data from cohort studies and control groups of case-control studies were also included. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors (LHM and ABN) for relevant publications. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and in cases of uncertainty, publications were included for full-text screening. Studies were excluded if they did not present HPV data from West Africa, were duplicates, or were reviews, letters, or editorials. Identified studies were exported, and duplicates were removed using EndNote X9. Full texts were screened, with discrepancies documented and resolved through discussion.

## Data extraction and risk of bias evaluation
Data extraction was independently performed by LHM and ABN, capturing information including study design, publication date, research area, sampling method, inclusion criteria, laboratory protocols, participant enrollment regions, HPV genotypes, and clinical manifestations. Key characteristics, including sample size, number positive, and outcomes related to HPV infection (e.g., prevalence), were also recorded. The risk of bias in included studies was assessed using the Hoy et al. [ ] checklist for prevalence studies, evaluating aspects such as the representativeness of the study population, adequacy of inclusion criteria, and reliability of HPV detection methods (Appendix ). Each criterion was scored, with a total risk of bias score calculated per study. The final risk of bias was categorized as low (7–10), moderate (4–6), or high (0–3) based on the total score, with discrepancies resolved through consensus.

## Data analysis
We performed data analysis using the R software and"meta” package (version 3.6.2.) [ , ]. We estimated HPV prevalence in each population category using a random-effects meta-analysis with a double arcsine Freeman-Tukey transformation [ , ]. We chose the random-effects model because we assumed substantial heterogeneity across studies due to diverse populations, settings, and HPV detection assays. We used the I² statistic (> 50%) to indicate significant heterogeneity [ ]. We performed subgroup analyses of HPV prevalence by country, study setting, HPV detection assay, and sample type. We analysed the distribution of HR-HPV and LR-HPV genotypes by population categories and countries. For population categories with at least three data points, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that included leave-one-out approach and an analysis restricted to studies with low risk of bias to assess robustness of our findings. We used Egger’s test (P-value < 0.1) to detect publication bias [ , ]. We selected the Egger model to identify potential missing studies with smaller or unfavourable results.

## Results

## Study selection
After conducting a comprehensive database search, we identified a total of 2439 articles. After removing 1206 duplicates, 1233 articles remained for screening. We reviewed their titles and abstracts, excluding 984 articles that were not relevant to the study objectives. Based on the inclusion criteria, we retrieved and assessed the full texts of 249 articles. During this process, we excluded 104 articles for a variety of reasons. Ultimately, 145 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in our meta-analysis to evaluate HPV prevalence in West African countries (Fig.  ).

 

## Characteristics of included studies
This review included 145 studies from 12 West African countries, encompassing 71,324 male and female participants. PCR was the predominant technique for HPV DNA testing, with sample types including cervical, vaginal, nasopharyngeal swabs, tissue blocks, anal, oral, blood, and urine. Most studies (83.45%) were cross-sectional and prospective, primarily hospital-based (83.45%), with a low risk of bias (84.21%). Age, sexual history, pregnancy data, and other demographic variables were inconsistently reported, with gaps in vaccination status (96.55% unreported) and HIV co-infection data (86.21% unreported). Female participants represented 96.55% of the sample, with only 2.76% of studies involving men. Classical PCR was the main diagnostic method (67.59%), and cervical samples were the most common (52.41%) (Appendix ). Most of the prevalence data from the articles included in this review were free of bias (n = 145 prevalence data) (Appendix ).

## Prevalence of HPV in West Africa
Overall, we conducted analyses on 145 data points encompassing 30,170 cases (Table  ). The pooled HPV prevalence was 42.3% (95% CI: 38.5–46.1%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 99.0%; P < 0.001) and no significant evidence of publication bias (P-value = 0).

 

## Prevalence of HPV according to population categories and countries
The combined prevalence of HPV varied significantly by study population (p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis according to population groups shows that the reported prevalence tends to be higher for women with cervical cancer: 81.4% (95% CI: 68.2–91.8%), followed by men with non-cancer conditions: 66.1% (95% CI: 36.3–90.4%) and women with non-cancer conditions: 52.5% (95% CI: 42.3–62.6%). The lowest prevalence was obtained in other cancer patients: 47.6% (95% CI: 2.1–96.1%), apparently healthy men: 30.6% (95% CI: 17.9–45%) and apparently healthy women: 29.3% (95% CI: 25.5–33.2%). Globally, heterogeneity in reported outcomes remained high (I2 = 99.0%, p < 0,06) (Appendix ). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and the analysis restricted to studies with a low risk of bias demonstrated overall robust findings (Table  , Appendices 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11). The highest cases were reported in Ghana (22%), with Nigeria showing 18% and Senegal 13%, while the lowest prevalence was found in Benin, Burkina Faso, Ivory Coast, and Niger (12%) (Fig.  ).

 

## Prevalence of HPV according to study setting, detection assay, and sample type
Subgroup analyses reveal higher HPV prevalence in hospital-based studies, particularly among cervical cancer patients 85.5% (95% CI: 78.4–91.5%), men with non-cancer conditions 66.1% (95% CI: 36.3–90.4%), and women with non-cancer conditions 52.8% (95% CI: 42.1–63.3%) (Appendix ). Multiplex PCR and Real-time PCR reported higher prevalence, especially for cervical cancer patients and men with non-cancer conditions (Appendix ). For sample types, biopsy specimens and cervical samples reported high prevalence in cervical cancer patients [88% (95% CI: 81.4–93.4%) and 78.5% (95% CI: 58.6–93.2%), respectively] (Appendix ). Lower prevalence was observed in community-based studies and apparently healthy individuals across various detection methods and sample types.

## Prevalence and distribution of human papillomavirus (HPV) genotype
Our meta-analysis of 145 studies identified 53 HPV genotypes in West Africa. Pooled analysis reveals a high degree of genotypic diversity, varying according to country and population studied (Appendix ).
Among HR-HPV genotypes, HPV16 is the most frequently detected, with a prevalence of 19.9% (95% CI: 15.9–24.1), followed by HPV 82, 52, 18, 35, 45, 58, 56, 31, 51,73, 39, 59 and 33 (Appendix ). HPV16 is particularly prevalent in Cape Verde (72% 95% CI: 52.6–88.1) (Appendix ) and is common among women with cervical cancer, with a rate of 39.8% (95% CI: 26.3–54.2) (Appendix ).
Among LR-HPV genotypes, HPV 81 is the most widespread with a prevalence of 12.9% (95% CI: 7.1–20.0), notably in Benin with 24.3% (95% CI: 20.6–28.3) and in women with cervical cancer with 37.1% (95% CI: 25.4–49.5). The other LR-HPVs identified were HPV 6, 61, 54, 70, 72, 44, 42, 43, 11 and 40.

## Discussion
Our meta-analysis of 145 studies and 71,324 participants from West Africa revealed a pooled prevalence of HPV infection of 42.3%. This figure is higher than previous estimates of 28.6% reported in 2023 [ ], but lower than the pooled prevalence of 50.5% previously reported in 2015 [ ] and other similar prevalences in Asia [ ]. The observed differences in HPV prevalence rates can be attributed to several factors, such as geographical disparities, the quantity and quality of biological samples, the DNA extraction methods employed and the sensitivity of HPV detection tests [ ] and by the difference in sample sizes of the participants included in each study, which complicates the true estimation of pooled prevalence. These results emphasize the importance of implementing HPV prevention and control strategies, particularly targeting high-prevalence countries such as Ghana and Nigeria. HPV vaccination, regular screening and treatment of precancerous lesions are key interventions to reduce the burden of HPV-related cervical cancer in West Africa. Further research is needed to understand the specific risk factors contributing to high HPV prevalence, such HIV co-infection, sexual behavior, and limited access to healthcare. Additionally, assessing the effectiveness of public health interventions remains essential.
The results of this study show that the prevalence of HPV is significantly higher in patients with cervical cancer (81.4%). This result can be justified by the fact that recent data show a trend towards increasing incidence of cervical cancer in sub-Saharan Africa [ ], and more particularly in West Africa, where incidence was 97.1 per 100,000 women, with a mortality rate of 16.6 per 100,000 women in 2022 [ ]. Furthermore, this high prevalence of HPV in cervical cancer patients in our study is not surprising, given that infection with HR-HPV genotypes has been recognized for several years as a major risk factor for the development of this cancer [ ]. These findings are in line with those of previous studies, notably that of Emeribe et al. [ ], who also highlighted a high prevalence of HPV in West African populations, particularly among cervical cancer patients. These data confirm the central role of HPV in cervical carcinogenesis, and underline the importance of implementing effective prevention strategies, such as HPV vaccination and regular screening, to reduce the incidence and mortality of this cancer.
Our results indicate a high prevalence of HPV infection in Ghana and Nigeria, reaching 22% and 18%. Although no statistically significant differences were detected between countries, the high prevalence reported in Ghana and Nigeria suggest that these countries may face a particularly severe HPV burden. This highlights the need for targeted public health interventions, including vaccinations programs, awareness campaigns, and improved screening strategies, especially in high-prevalence countries like Ghana and Nigeria [ , ]. Expanding research efforts in countries with lower reported prevalence, such as Benin, Burkina Faso, Ivory Coast, and Niger, is essential to determine if these findings reflect genuine or underestimation due to limited data. Overall, strengthening surveillance systems and promoting HPV vaccination programs adapted to the specific epidemiological context of each country will be crucial to reduce HPV-related morbidity and mortality in the region.
Laboratory methodologies and anatomical locations employed for HPV detection vary across studies, potentially explaining the observed disparities in HPV prevalence between populations in different countries. Our investigation demonstrated elevated prevalence rates when utilizing PCR-based methods, specifically multiplex PCR and real-time PCR techniques. These findings align with prior research, which has documented superior sensitivity for these techniques compared to alternatives [ – ]. Furthermore, HPV prevalence was notably high in biopsy specimens (88%) and cervical lesion specimens (78.5%) in cervical cancer patients. Indeed, the cervical epithelium serves as the primary site of viral colonization, potentially contributing to the elevated HPV prevalence observed. Consequently, these findings underscore the necessity of prioritizing biopsy and cervical lesion specimen collection to optimize HPV detection.
The genotypes identified in our study were classified into two categories: high-risk HPV and low-risk HPV. Among HR-HPVs, genotypes 16, 82, 52, 18, 35, 45, 58, 56, 31, 51, 73, 39, 59 and 33 were the most frequently detected. This distribution is relatively consistent with findings from Ouedraogo et al. (2023) in West Africa [ ] but differs from those reported in Southern and East Africa [ ], highlighting genotypic heterogeneity across the African continent. In addition to genetic factors, several non-genetic factors could contribute to these differences. Variations in sexual behavior, cultural practices, contraceptive use, HIV coinfection, and access to healthcare services may influence the distribution of HPV genotypes [ ]. Environmental conditions, including hygiene practices and immunological status influenced by endemic infections, could also play a role. Addressing these factors in future studies would be valuable for implementing more effective prevention and control measures. Our findings confirm the predominance of the HPV 16 genotype among HR-HPVs, particularly in women with cervical cancer (38.9%). These observation aligns with global data [ , , ], showing that HPV 16, often combined with HPV 18, accounts for 60–80% of HPV infections associated with cervical cancer in sub-Saharan Africa and worldwide [ , ].
For LR-HPVs, our study revealed the presence of genotypes 81, 6, 61, 54, 70, 72, 44, 42, 43, 11 and 40, with HPV 81 (12.9%) being the most frequently detected genotype. Notably, HPV 81 was highly prevalent among women with cervical cancer, which contrasts with previous reports from sub-Saharan Africa where HPV 16 and 11 were commonly associated with benign lesions [ , ]. This unexpected finding suggests a possible shift in genotype distribution, with the emergence of HPV 81 potentially replacing the traditionally dominant HPV 6 and 11. Overall, our findings underscore the importance of considering both genotypic and non-genotypic factors in understanding HPV epidemiology. Tailored cervical cancer prevention and control programs should integrate these diverse influences to effectively address the HPV burden in West Africa.
Our study offers several strengths. Firstly, it provides an overview of the epidemiology of HPV and the distribution of its genotypes in West Africa. In addition, the rigorous methodology, including an exhaustive search of four databases, precise inclusion and exclusion criteria, and independent evaluation, guarantees the reliability of the results. By following the PRISMA recommendations, we have ensured the transparency of our analysis and the reproducibility of our results. The diversity of the populations studied, covering 12 of the 16 countries in the region, reinforces the representativeness of our results for the whole of West Africa. This study thus provides a solid basis for estimating the burden of HPV-related disease in the region, and for guiding future prevention, screening and treatment strategies. Although our analysis is robust, it should be noted that generalization of the results should be made with caution, given the diversity of the contexts and populations studied.
Our study has certain limitations that should be highlighted to guide future research. Despite our exhaustive efforts to collect data for the whole of West Africa, certain gaps persist, notably in the availability of data for certain countries. These limitations, including data gaps for certain countries, potential selection bias, and limited stratification by age and gender, may result in an underestimation or overestimation of the true prevalence of HPV in the region. Future studies should aim for more comprehensive data collection and improved standardization of diagnostic methods to enhance accuracy. Finally, the meta-analysis did not allow us to thoroughly assess the association between risk factors (HIV, sexual behavior, smoking, etc.) and HPV prevalence. It is therefore essential that future studies on HPV in West Africa take these limitations into account. More systematic and standardized data collection, as well as a more detailed analysis of risk factors, are needed to refine our knowledge of the epidemiology of HPV infection and to implement effective prevention strategies.

## Conclusion
Our results reveal a high prevalence of HPV in West Africa, particularly among women with cervical cancer. These findings highlight the need for improved prevention and screening strategies tailored to the region, particularly in high-prevalence areas. HPV 16 and 82 genotypes are the most frequently identified HR-HPV, while HPV 81 is the predominant LR-HPV. These results may guide the development of vaccines and screening tests targeting the most prevalent genotypes in the region.
