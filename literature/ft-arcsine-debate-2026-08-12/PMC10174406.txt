## Background:
Despite its central role in drug metabolism, the exact prevalence estimates and factors affecting global trends of polypharmacy in patients with chronic liver disease (CLD) have remained unexamined. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to estimate the prevalence of polypharmacy in patients with CLD and to comprehensively synthesize the socio-demographic factors that drive this.

## Methods:
We conducted a comprehensive search of relevant databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Science citation index, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and database of abstracts of reviews of effectiveness) for studies published from inception to May 30, 2022 that reported on prevalence estimates of polypharmacy in patients with CLD. The risk of bias was conducted utilizing Loney criteria. The primary outcome was the pooled prevalence of polypharmacy in patients with CLD. We subsequently performed a systematic review and weighted meta-analysis to ascertain the exact pooled prevalence of polypharmacy among patients with CLD.

## Results:
We identified approximately 50 studies from the initial literature search, of which 7 (enrolling N = 521,435 patients) with CLD met the inclusion criteria; of these, 58.7% were male, with a mean age of 53.9 (SD ± 12.2) years. The overall pooled prevalence of polypharmacy among patients with CLD was 31% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 4%–66%, I2 = 100%, τ2 ≤ 0.001, P ≤ .0001). We found higher pooled prevalence estimates among patients aged 50 years and older compared to their younger cohorts (42%, [CI 10–77]; I2 = 100%, P = <.001 vs 21%, [CI 0–70]; I2 = 100%, P = <.001).

## Conclusion:
In an examination of multiple community- and hospital-based databases of patients with CLD, we found a pooled prevalence estimate of polypharmacy of approximately 31%. This represents a case burden within the range reported in the general population and will likely respond to mitigation strategies employed thus far for patients in that population.

## 1. Introduction
Patients with chronic liver disease (CLD) have a disproportionately high burden of drug-related adverse effects.[ – ] This is due to the central role of the liver in drug metabolism (including other aspects of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics). Increasing survival in various morbidities (such as patients with adverse cardiovascular events) driven largely by novel therapies means that this liver-related burden has exponentially increased over the past few years. Despite recent attempts to characterize the pattern and clinical phenotypes of the increasing medication burden (especially polypharmacy) in the general population,[ ] the volume of work on liver-related therapeutic morbidities has been minimal. Polypharmacy incurs huge costs to global health through a combination of adverse drug reactions as well as established and emerging bidirectional interactions (drug-drug and drug-food interactions).[ ] Until recently, the numerical threshold for polypharmacy in patients with CLD remained a matter of debate. A recent systematic review by Danjuma et al proposed a threshold of >5 medications as a cutoff for polypharmacy in these cohorts of patients. The authors of this review have highlighted the uncertainty that remains regarding whether the determination of more specific “liver-related polypharmacy” is more appropriate and likely to better stratify these patients. Indeed, in a recent randomized assessment of medication-related problems in patients with decompensated cirrhosis and its association with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs, Hayward et al added a novel perspective to the rising burden of non-liver-specific medications.[ ] In this report, the authors found a sizeable proportion of all medications taken by both groups of patients to be non-liver-related medications. Additionally, CLD patients who are exposed to inappropriate polypharmacy have exponentially higher risks of developing adverse reactions and other bidirectional interactions.[ ] The latter sometimes was associated with increased risk of drug-induced liver injury.[ ]
Consequently, having an exact understanding of the prevalence of polypharmacy in patients with CLD as well as the sociodemographic factors that influence its global trends will be critical in channeling targeted interventions aimed at addressing it. Unlike cohorts in the general population, to date, no study has explored the true prevalence of polypharmacy in this vulnerable population. Additionally, it remains uncertain if the primary liver disease phenotype (such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD], “hepatitic” cirrhosis, etc) does have any significant impact on these estimates. Few studies that have explored polypharmacy in patients with CLD have exclusively included patients with NAFLD.[ , ] In this report, we have carried out a systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of polypharmacy in patients with CLD, as well as a comprehensive synthesis of the socio-demographic factors that drive this.

## 2. Methods
This study was conducted in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses procedure for study selection, as shown in Figure .[ ] The review primary outcome was the pooled prevalence of polypharmacy among studies reporting on this key therapeutic morbidity in patients with CLD. We explored other secondary outcomes, including the influence of sociodemographic factors (age, sex, etc) on the final pooled prevalence estimates.
 

## 2.1. Data sources and searches
We searched for all relevant publications that met the review inclusion criteria from inception to May 30th, 2022 from the following databases: PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Science Citation Index, and the database of abstracts of reviews of effectiveness. We used the following medical subject headings (MESH) terms and key words: ((polypharmacy) OR (“inappropriate medication”)) AND (“liver disease”) OR “chronic liver disease” OR cirrhosis). Supplementary material 1 (see Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/MD/I279) shows the comprehensive result of literature search strategy.

## 2.2. Study selection
Following an initial literature search (with duplicates removed), an exhaustive study eligibility assessment was performed on the resulting abstracts. Where this was not possible, a review of a full-text version of the study was conducted before inclusion in the review. Determination of study eligibility and inclusion in the review was carried out by 2 independent reviewers (MID and LN), and in case of disagreement between the 2 reviewers, this was resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer (SA). A final list of all studies that satisfied the prespecified review eligibility criteria was then drawn. This includes studies published in English language from inception to May 30th, 2022, study participants aged ≥18 years, patients with CLD (regardless of the underlying cause) on medication for various indications, and studies exploring different themes of polypharmacy in patients with CLD. Additionally, we included all studies regardless of what the authors considered to be the threshold for diagnosis of polypharmacy (i.e., ≥5, ≥10 medications, etc).[ ] Studies that explored other morbidities but for which data on polypharmacy among CLD patient cohorts were available were also included.[ ]
Studies that failed to meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Furthermore, we excluded studies with case reports or case series designs and studies in which numerical or descriptive data on polypharmacy prevalence were lacking.

## 2.3. Data extraction and study quality assessment
A data collection sheet was developed to extract variables from the studies included in the review. Variables extracted from each study included age, sex, last name of the first author, year of publication, study center location, number of CLD patients, number of CLD patients with polypharmacy, numerical and descriptive definitions of polypharmacy (where available), median number of medications, and number of long-term conditions.
We utilized the validated Loney criteria to carry out a comprehensive quality and risk of bias assessment of all the included studies. This was done to determine any methodological flaws in their design and how this may potentially impact our final point estimates.[ ] These criteria have been exhaustively described elsewhere; however,[ ] in brief, they comprise 8 appraisal domain checklists. These include sampling adequacy, prevalence with confidence intervals and relevant subgroups, measures of outcomes, unbiased sampling frame, unbiased assessors, sample size, response rate with refusals described, and an appropriate description of the study subjects for the research question. For each criterion, 2 independent reviewers (LN and MID) allocated 1 point. The overall scores ranged from zero to 8. Higher scores indicate studies with good methodological quality. Methodological scoring systems were used to rate the quality of each study on the prevalence of polypharmacy. Where disagreement arose between reviewers on point allocation, this was resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer (SA).

## 2.4. Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were presented as means (±SD) or medians (interquartile range), as appropriate, while categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). We quantified the pooled prevalence estimates of polypharmacy (utilizing the random-effects model) among patients with CLD using the default double arcsine transformation. We did not apply any continuity correction because this transformation does not require 1 correction. To ascertain the source of significant heterogeneity among the included studies (where this exists), we subsequently carried out subgroup analyses to examine the effect of age, sex, source of primary data, and the risk of bias scores of the reviewed studies on the final point prevalence estimates. We assessed heterogeneity between studies using I2 and τ2 statistics.[ ] We considered the I2 thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to represent low, moderate, and high heterogeneity between-study variances, respectively. where τ2 is reported to be zero, which is indicative of no heterogeneity.[ ] We used funnel and Doi plots to visualize the small-study effect as well as publication bias.[ ] Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding each study to ascertain its effect on the final point prevalence estimate. All statistical analyses were performed using Meta XL, version 5.3 (EpiGear International, Queensland, Australia).

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Characteristics of included studies
We retrieved 50 studies from the initial literature search, of which (n = 23) studies were excluded following the screening of titles and abstracts. The Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses chart (Fig. ) outlines the reasons for exclusion of various studies. Thereafter, potentially relevant studies were selected for full-text review, of which 7 studies[ , , , , – ] were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. The composition of the data sources of the reviewed studies ranged from community primary care-based electronic longitudinal patient records,[ , , ] multicenter linked NAFLD registries,[ ] and university/tertiary hospital records[ ] (Table ). The reviewed studies were distributed across 4 continents, with 2 studies conducted in Iran[ , ] and 1 each in Australia,[ ] UK[ ], Qatar,[ ] Spain[ ], and the United States[ ] (Map).
 Overall, a total of N = 521 435 participants were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, of which 58.7% were males (Table ). The mean age of reviewed study cohorts was 53.9 (SD ± 12.2) years. All constituent studies had a patient population with a median of >3 long-term conditions. The underlying primary liver disease phenotypes among the reviewed studies were variable. Patel et al[ ] and Alrasheed et al,[ ] for example, exclusively explored NAFLD patients or used different diagnostic criteria for inclusion in their respective studies. Soly-Bori et al reported a wide variety of patient populations with different clinical risk profiles.[ ] Those with “Liver” morbidities were reported as having “chronic liver disease” or “viral hepatitis.”

The overall pooled prevalence of polypharmacy among patients with CLD was approximately 31% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 4%–66%). The overall heterogeneity among the included studies was significant (I2 = 100%, P < .0001), and subgroup analyses (I2 = 100%, P < .001). See Table .
 

## 3.3. Impact of age on the prevalence of polypharmacy in patients with CLD
Studies reporting on patient population cohorts with a mean age <50 years have a lower pooled prevalence of polypharmacy (21%, [CI 0–70]; I2 = 100%, P = <.001), compared to those with older patient cohorts (42%, [CI 10–77]; I2 = 100%, P = <.001) (See supplemental Fig. S3, http://links.lww.com/MD/I281, Supplemental Content, which is a Forest plot showing the distribution of pooled polypharmacy prevalence point estimates stratified according to age range of the reviewed studies).

## 3.4. Gender disposition of polypharmacy amongst the study population
When we evaluated the prevalence of polypharmacy dichotomized between predominant (>50%) male and female study populations, we found a disproportionately higher pooled period prevalence estimate of 58% (I2 = 100%, CI: 51–63; P = .63) in male patient cohort than in their female counterparts 21% (CI: 0–63, I2 = 100%, P = <.001). See supplemental Figure S4, http://links.lww.com/MD/I282, Supplemental Content, which shows a Forest plot depicting pooled polypharmacy prevalence point estimates stratified according to the gender proportion of the reviewed studies.

## 3.5. Effect of data source on the prevalence of polypharmacy amongst patients with CLD
There was a higher prevalence of polypharmacy among studies examining the burden of polypharmacy within inpatient and multicenter registries (45%) than among those exploring community-based longitudinal population registries (21%). see supplemental Figure S5, http://links.lww.com/MD/I283, Supplemental Content, which shows a Forest plot of the pooled prevalence estimates stratified according to the countries and regional locations where the studies were carried out.

## 3.6. Effect of study methodological quality and risk of bias on polypharmacy prevalence estimates
When studies were based on their methodological quality and risk of bias, we found the following prevalence estimates; “unlikely biased” 30%[ , , , ]; “some suggestion of bias” 21%[ , ]; “substantial suggestion of bias” 31%.[ ] See supplemental Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/MD/I280, Supplemental Content, which illustrates the differential effect of study methodological quality on the risk of bias.

## 4. Discussion
This systematic review pooled examination of longitudinal data of over 521,435 patients from 6 countries represents the first comprehensive synthesis of the pooled prevalence estimate of polypharmacy as well as the global trend of factors impacting it in patients with CLD. Investigating polypharmacy in patients with CLD is of extreme importance due to the central role of the liver in drug metabolism, making it particularly vulnerable to the risks of adverse drug reactions and their sequalae.
We found an overall pooled prevalence of polypharmacy amongst patients with CLD of 31%. Despite the apparent disparity in the underlying liver pathology amongst the included studies, our period prevalence estimate provides the first attempt exploring the burden of this growing therapeutic morbidity in these cohorts of patients and falls within the “ballpark” reported in the general population.[ , – ] Data from pharmaco-epidemiological studies in patients with alternative morbidities, such as people living with HIV/AIDS, have shown that among the factors that drive adverse consequences of polypharmacy include the inherent propensity of parent antiretroviral drugs to bidirectional interactions, as well as the metabolizing pathways of some of the antiretroviral drugs which are highly polymorphic and therefore prone to pharmacogenetic interactions.[ – ] In patients with CLD it is unlikely that the nature of underlying primary liver pathology will significantly alter the prevalence estimates of polypharmacy; as previous studies have shown that very often what principally drives the medication count in these cohorts of patients has been the rising number of drugs prescribed to manage, sometimes, inevitable complications (such as portal hypertension and its sequelae, sub-acute bacterial peritonitis, and portal hypertensive gastropathy, etc) that accompany decompensation in these patients.[ , ] However, devising intervention strategies aimed at both the identification and reduction of the risks and consequences of polypharmacy in this vulnerable population will require an unambiguous risk-stratification tool.
Until recently (Danjuma et al; in press), uncertainty still subsists regarding the numerical threshold for what exactly constitutes polypharmacy among patients with CLD. A threshold of more than 5 medications used in the definition of polypharmacy in the general population has since been adopted by some reports in defining polypharmacy among patients with CLD.[ , ] Other studies have raised concerns regarding the discriminant value of this numerical threshold that includes both liver-specific and other general medications. It is our opinion that restricting the numerical count to non-liver-related therapeutics will more robustly identify inappropriate medical burden, which has consistently been associated with worse clinical outcomes, including death, across clinical risks and patient populations.[ ]
Our finding of doubling the prevalence estimates of polypharmacy in patient cohorts aged 50 years and above compared to those with lower age thresholds is consistent with what has been reported from polypharmacy studies both in the general population as well as those examining it in specific organ systems.[ , , , ] The mean age of our review cohort was 53.9 (±12.2) years, with Troncoso-Mariño et al’s report representing the oldest patient cohort.[ ] The therapeutic underpinning behind the rising medication count per patient with aging has been extensively discussed elsewhere[ ]; however, a common denominator driving this entire process has been increasing multimorbidity and the inevitable need for a rising medication census to manage them. Very often, the overall therapeutic objective of this (i.e., reducing mortality in these patients) is achieved; however, this is at the expense of novel polypharmacy-related morbidities, such as drug-drug, drug-food, and pharmacogenetic interactions which are inevitable. All of these factors could lead to various downstream adverse drug reaction phenotypes.
Until recently, the exact link between polypharmacy and mortality remained a subject of intense therapeutic debate.[ ] However, a recent study reporting on the Newcastle 85+ community-based longitudinal patient cohort found that in the very old, each additional medication prescribed conferred a 3% increased risk of mortality (hazard ratio: 1.03, 95% confidence interval: 1.00–1.06).[ ] The findings of this study will serve as a watershed in an ever-increasing attempt to address the determinants and consequences of polypharmacy in this vulnerable population. Whilst the mean age of our review patient population was less than those reported in the Newcastle 85+ cohort, often the “biological age” of patients with CLD are well ahead of their “chronological age” and may not be inconceivable for these patients to have similar or worst outcomes as those reported by the Newcastle 85+ study. This requires further examination in future prospective studies.
Our finding of a higher prevalence estimate of polypharmacy (58%) among studies with a predominantly male population is inconsistent with that reported in the general population.[ – ] We were unable to discern any regional pattern to this gender dichotomy, as studies with predominant female populations were carried out in various parts of the world.[ ] Despite the lack of consensus regarding the exact socio-demographic and biological characteristics that explain this higher polypharmacy prevalence among female patient cohorts, factors such as the propensity of the female sex to seek medication help early[ ] as well as the higher prevalence of some specific multi-morbidities among female patients (e.g., depression, endocrine disorders) may all account for a higher medication census among female patient cohorts overall. Sex differences in the epidemiology of cirrhosis and CLD have been extensively reported. In general, according to the National Centre for Health Statistics report, men are twice as likely to die from CLD and cirrhosis compared to the female cohort,[ ] with the latter accounting for about 30% of the liver transplant population.[ ] In general, studies have shown a higher burden of adverse drug reactions among female patient cohorts; our finding of a lower burden of polypharmacy among female population cohorts (if replicated by future studies) will mean a lower burden of adverse drug reactions associated with liver-related polypharmacy in patients with CLD.
The increasing survival seen across organ-specific morbidities (such as heart failure, stroke, ischemic heart disease, etc) is often associated with a cumulative increase in polypharmacy over the succeeding years. A recent report by Oktora et al,[ ] from a comparative series between patient cohorts in the Netherlands and the United States, showed a doubling in the prevalence of polypharmacy in the general population between 1999 and 2014. A sizeable proportion of the reviewed studies in our synthesis that explored polypharmacy outcomes were published within the past 2 to 3 years; representing a remarkable gap in our understanding of both burdens and the exact factors that perpetuate this in patients with CLD. It was therefore impossible to robustly examine the cumulative effects of succeeding years on the overall burden of polypharmacy in these cohorts of patients. However, it is unlikely that this trend will differ from that reported in the general population.
Sub-categorization of the included studies based on their methodological quality and risk of bias showed that studies with probable risk of bias”[ ] had the same polypharmacy prevalence estimates consistent with those reported for the whole population of patients with CLD. The increasing risk of bias appears to underestimate the point prevalence estimates, which is worth considering in the design of future prospective studies.

## 5. Strengths and limitations
We identify the novelty of this review as its principal strength; it represents the first comprehensive attempt at a systematic estimation of the burden of this rising morbidity in patients with CLD. Despite its limitations, it provides a therapeutic numerical scaffold upon which further work in this area could be undertaken; in addition, of course to the immediate need to investigate how known polypharmacy-ameliorating measures (such as drug reconciliation) will impact this case burden. This scaffold includes the first report of the pooled prevalence of polypharmacy amongst CLD patient cohorts from published studies which will undoubtedly enable a more targeted intervention of therapeutic morbidity amongst these cohorts of patients.
The lack of certainty regarding the exact numerical cutoff for the definition of polypharmacy across the limited studies that have explored this morbidity in these cohorts of patients may have contributed to the imprecision of our final prevalence estimates. Additionally, the high percentage of heterogeneity between the studies in this review may have accounted for some of the imprecise prevalence estimates. However, this is unlikely to significantly impact our reported estimate of approximately 31%, which lies within the range reported for most organ/disease-specific polypharmacy. The determination of the prevalence of polypharmacy in patients with CLD is limited to the estimation of risk and case burden. Therefore, further studies are needed to explore how such rising case burdens and prevalence estimate the impact on hard clinical endpoints (such as length of hospital stay, quality-adjusted life years, drug-drug interactions, drug-food interactions, and mortality, among others).

## 6. Conclusion
In an examination of multiple community- and hospital-based databases of patients with CLD, we found a pooled prevalence estimate of polypharmacy of approximately 31%. This represents a case burden within the range reported in the general population, and will respond to mitigation strategies thus far employed for patients in that population.
