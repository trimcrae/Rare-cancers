## Summary

## Background
Guidelines recommend that adults with peripheral artery disease (PAD) take antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives. However, it is unclear how frequently clinicians do not prescribe these medications (ie, underprescription), how often patients fail to fill/refill their prescriptions (ie, nonadherence), which factors increase underprescription/nonadherence risk, and whether underprescription/nonadherence are associated with outcomes.

## Methods
We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, and Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews (January 1, 2006-to-February 18th, 2025) for studies reporting cumulative incidences/point prevalences of clinician underprescription and/or patient nonadherence to antiplatelets, statins, and/or antihypertensives; adjusted-risk factors for underprescription/nonadherence; and adjusted-outcomes associated with underprescription/nonadherence among adults with PAD. Two investigators independently screened citations, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias. Data were pooled using random-effects models. Estimate certainty was communicated using GRADE. The study was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42022362801).

## Findings
Among 4206 citations identified, 125 studies (n = 14,681,801 participants; 37% female) were included. The pooled cumulative incidence of antiplatelet, statin, and antihypertensive (among those with PAD and hypertension) underprescription was 28% (95% confidence interval [CI] = 21–36%; moderate-certainty), 34% (95% CI = 31–38%; high-certainty), and 43% (95% CI = 33–53%; moderate-certainty), respectively. The cumulative incidence of antiplatelet, statin, and antihypertensive nonadherence was 27% (95% CI = 20–35%; moderate-certainty), 28% (95% CI = 24–33%; high-certainty), and 23% (95% CI = 22–24%; low-certainty), respectively. Underprescription was more common in population-based studies and those enrolling more females and past/current smokers while nonadherence was more common in studies enrolling more patients with diabetes. Underprescription risk factors included female sex, advanced age, malignancy history, and chronic limb-threatening ischemia (all moderate-certainty). Nonadherence risk factors included advanced age, comorbidity burden, and receiving specialist mental health care (all moderate-certainty). Underprescription was associated with increased major adverse cardiac events, all-cause mortality, and decreased amputation-free time (all moderate-certainty).

## Interpretation
One-quarter-to-one-half of adults with PAD are not prescribed antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives. Further, approximately one-quarter of these patients do not adhere to these medications after prescription.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
Clinical practice guidelines strongly recommend that people with peripheral artery disease (PAD) take antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives (when they also have hypertension) because systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) report that these medications reduce their risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE); stroke; worsening claudication or progression to chronic limb threatening ischemia (CLTI: defined as ischemic rest pain, non-healing wounds, or lower limb gangrene); need for coronary, carotid, and lower limb revascularisation; above-ankle amputation; and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. However, evidence suggests that individuals with PAD may often not be prescribed these medications and may not adhere to them after prescription. We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, and Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews from January 1st, 2006, to February 18th, 2025, and found no systematic review on this topic.

## Added value of this study
We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies examining adults with PAD that reported one or more of: 1) the cumulative incidence (ie, new cases during study follow-up) or point prevalence (ie, existing cases at the time of study conduct) of underprescription of and/or nonadherence to one or more clinical practice guideline-recommended medications; 2) adjusted-risk factors for medication underprescription/nonadherence; 3) adjusted-outcomes associated with medication underprescription and/or nonadherence.

## Implications of all the available evidence
Our findings suggest that one-quarter to one-half of adults with PAD are not prescribed antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives. Further, approximately one-quarter of these patients do not adhere to these medications following prescription. Future studies should examine whether PAD patients underrepresented in existing secondary prevention RCTs (ie, females, those with advanced age, malignancy history, and CLTI) achieve similar benefits from guideline-recommended medications. Future trials investigating strategies for improving clinician prescription of and PAD patient adherence to these medications also appear warranted. Interventions for improving clinician prescription deserving of further study include improving healthcare access for PAD patients (especially those from socioeconomically-disadvantaged backgrounds); specialized secondary PAD prevention clinics; improved education for patients and families, medical trainees, and physicians regarding PAD medical management; clinician reminders; prescriber audit and feedback; and shared decision-making. Potentially promising interventions for improving patient adherence deserving of further study include patient education; more routine follow-up of PAD patients; reminder systems (eg, text message prompts), and other tailored adherence-support interventions; involving pharmacists in the PAD patient care pathway (to assess adherence barriers, coordinate medication refills, etc); and face-to-face motivational interviewing (a non-coercive form of counselling that empowers patient behavior change through understanding and engaging with intrinsic motivation sources).

## Introduction
Approximately 200-million people worldwide have peripheral artery disease (PAD), and its incidence is rising. People with PAD, including intermittent claudication and chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI: advanced PAD manifested by ischemic rest pain, tissue loss, and/or gangrene), are typically 60 years-of-age or older and possess high-risk cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Those with PAD have an increased risk/hazard of living in lower socioeconomic situations and having received limited education; major adverse cardiac (MACE) and limb events (MALE); hospitalization; need for coronary, carotid, and lower limb revascularization; and death compared to individuals in the general population. , Estimated PAD-associated healthcare costs range between $84 and $381 billion per-year in the United States, while the average inpatient stay for PAD patients is approximately six-times more costly than the general inpatient population. 
Improving the medical management of people with PAD is a leading priority for The Society for Vascular Surgery, The Vascular Society of Great Britain and Ireland, and all medical and surgical societies involved in the care of PAD patients. , , Clinical practice guidelines strongly recommend that adults with PAD take antiplatelets and statins. , , , They also strongly recommend that adults with PAD and hypertension take antihypertensives (with some , preferring angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors [ACEIs] or angiotensin receptor blockers [ARBs]). , , , These recommendations are based on meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reporting that PAD patients allocated to these medications have a reduced risk of MACE; stroke; worsening claudication; progression to CLTI; need for coronary, carotid, and lower limb revascularisation; above-ankle amputation; and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. , , 
However, some studies report that antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives may be underprescribed to PAD patients. , Others suggest that PAD patients may often be nonadherent to these medications after prescription. This may explain why PAD patients have a high risk of hospitalization; MACE; MALE; and mortality. , This systematic review sought to estimate the incidence and prevalence of guideline-recommended medication underprescription and nonadherence among adults with PAD, identify adjusted-risk factors for underprescription/nonadherence, and examine whether underprescription/nonadherence are associated with clinical or healthcare resource use outcomes.

## Methods

## Registration and protocol
We pre-specified our methods in a protocol that was published and registered on PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD42022362801). Results are reported following the PRISMA and Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines.

## Eligibility criteria and definitions
We included observational studies of adults (≥18-years) with PAD reporting one or more of: 1) cumulative incidences, incidence rates, or prevalences of underprescription and/or nonadherence to one or more clinical practice guideline-recommended medications; 2) adjusted risk factors for underprescription/nonadherence; 3) adjusted associations between underprescription/nonadherence and clinical or healthcare resource use outcomes (as reported by study authors). We defined underprescription as clinicians failing to prescribe one or more guideline-recommended medications to adults with PAD and nonadherence as failing to fill the first prescription (ie, primary nonadherence) of a guideline-recommended medication within an appropriate time-period or failing to refill a prescription (ie, secondary nonadherence). Cumulative incidence was defined as the number of PAD patients underprescribed or nonadherent to medications during study follow-up divided by the total number of PAD patients in whom medications were recommended by clinical practice guidelines. Point prevalence was defined as the number of PAD patients underprescribed or nonadherent to medications at the time the study was conducted divided by the total number of PAD patients in whom medications were recommended by clinical practice guidelines. We excluded studies that were: 1) grey literature; 2) published only as abstracts; 3) only enrolled participants before 2006 (one-year after publication of the first PAD practice guideline ); 4) only reported unadjusted risk factor or outcome associations; or 5) did not distinguish between underprescription and nonadherence (eg, reported “underuse”).

## Information sources and search strategy
We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews from January 1, 2006-to-February 18, 2025, without restrictions. Search strategies were designed with an information-scientist (RS) and then piloted and peer-reviewed by another information-scientist (see for full search strategy). We also used PubMed to search for the first 5 “similar articles” to each included study and manually searched bibliographies of included studies and relevant reviews identified during searches.

## Selection process
The titles/abstracts of all citations were independently reviewed in duplicate by two of three investigators (AMK, MP, DL). Abstracts deemed potentially-relevant were reviewed in full by two independent investigators (MP, DL) to determine final eligibility. Inter-investigator agreement on full-text inclusion was quantified using the kappa (κ)-statistic. Disagreements were resolved through consensus.

## Data items and collection process
Two of four investigators (AMK, MP, DL, SAA) independently extracted data in duplicate using a pre-designed spreadsheet. We extracted data on: 1) study design, data source, and setting; 2) whether studies were conducted in lower-to-middle-income, upper middle-income, or high-income countries (according to the World Bank Method ); 3) patient recruitment period; 4) sample size; 5) patient/clinician characteristics; 6) incidence/prevalence of underprescription/nonadherence; 7) adjusted potential risk factors for underprescription/nonadherence; 8) adjusted associations between underprescription/nonadherence and clinical or healthcare resource use outcomes (as reported by study authors); and 9) covariates used in multivariable adjustment. Medications of interest included antiplatelets, statins, antihypertensives (in those with PAD and hypertension), and all guideline-recommended medications (ie, antiplatelets and statins in those with PAD without concomitant hypertension and antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives in those with PAD and hypertension). , , , We excluded antihypertensive data from studies not reporting data on whether individuals had hypertension. Disagreements were resolved through consensus.

## Risk of bias assessment
Risk of bias was assessed independently by two investigators (AMK, MP) using the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tool for incidence/prevalence studies and the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) Tool for risk factors/outcomes studies. The JBI tool assessed whether the sampling frame was appropriate to address the target population, study participants were sampled appropriately, sample size was adequate, study participants and setting were described in sufficient detail, data analysis was conducted with sufficient coverage of the sample, validated methods or techniques were used to identify the condition of interest, the condition of interest was measured in a standardized and reliable way, and the statistical analyses were appropriate. The QUIPS tool assessed risk of bias related to study participation and attrition, risk factor and outcome description and measurement, confounder measurement and adjustment, and methods and reporting of statistical analyses. Operationalized definitions of the varying risks of bias across the above domains may be found in . Disagreements were resolved through consensus.

## Effect measures and synthesis methods

## Pre-planned analyses
We tabulated each study's design, data source, setting, recruitment period, included patients, and reported outcomes to identify potentially-duplicate data and where meta-analyses were appropriate. Descriptive data were summarized using weighted means and standard deviations (SDs), medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and pooled percentages and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We used adjusted-odds (aORs), -risk (aRRs), or -hazard (aHRs) ratios as association measures for the risk factors and outcomes meta-analyses.
Reported or investigator-calculated cumulative incidences or point prevalences were pooled separately using random-effects models. All estimates of medication nonadherence (ie, primary and secondary) were pooled in meta-analyses. Estimates were double-arcsine transformed and then back-transformed after meta-analyses. For risk factors and outcomes meta-analyses, effect estimates relating adjusted-risk factors or -outcomes with similar definitions to underprescription or nonadherence were pooled using random-effects models. When potentially-duplicate estimates were identified (ie, derived from the same data source with overlapping time periods across multiple studies), estimates from the largest study were included in meta-analyses.
Heterogeneity was quantified using I2-statistics. In the presence of at least low-heterogeneity (I2 >25%, a threshold representing more than low-heterogeneity ), we conducted stratified random-effects meta-analyses and meta-regressions using the following predictors: 1) clinical practice guideline used; 2) World Health Organization region of study origin ; 3) whether studies were conducted in primary or tertiary care settings or had a population- or non-population-based design; 4) proportion of females, non-white participants, low socioeconomic status (SES) participants (as defined by authors), past/current smokers, and participants with cerebrovascular disease (CVD) or coronary artery disease (CAD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, CKD, and malignancy; and 5) proportion of prescribers who were cardiovascular specialists (defined as cardiologists and/or vascular surgeons) or vascular surgeons, cardiologists, internal medicine physicians, or primary care practitioners.

## Post-hoc analyses
We performed several post-hoc analyses after peer-review, including: 1) sensitivity meta-analyses removing studies that recruited asymptomatic PAD patients; 2) stratified meta-analyses examining whether the incidence/prevalence of underprescription/nonadherence varied between lower-middle-income, upper-middle-income, or high-income countries ; and 3) stratified meta-analyses and meta-regressions examining whether the incidence/prevalence of underprescription/nonadherence varied by study recruitment period (1985–1990, 1991–1996, 1997–2002, 2003–2007, 2008–2013, 2014–2019, and 2020–2025).

## Reporting bias assessment and statistical software
Evidence of small study effects potentially due to publication bias was assessed by inspecting funnel plots and using Egger's test. Analyses were performed using Stata SE version 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA). We considered p-values <0.05 significant.

## Certainty assessment
Estimate certainty was communicated using Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE). This involved examining the risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias associated with estimates. Estimate certainty was subsequently communicated as high (“further research is very unlikely to change our certainty in the estimate”), moderate (“further research is likely to have an important impact on our certainty in the estimate and may change the estimate”), or low (“further research is very likely to have an important impact on our certainty in the estimate and is likely to change the estimate”). GRADE certainty assessments were reported alongside pooled and unpooled estimates.

## Stratified meta-analyses and meta-regressions
All stratified meta-analyses and meta-regressions can be seen in . The prevalence of statin underprescription was higher among studies enrolling more females (p = 0.035) and those that did not report which guidelines they used to define underprescription (p = 0.044). ## Risk factors
Female sex was associated with an increased pooled adjusted-odds of antiplatelet (aOR = 1.17; 95% CI = 1.05–1.32; three-studies; low-certainty) and statin (aOR = 1.28; 95% CI = 1.16–1.40; eight-studies; moderate-certainty) underprescription. Age ≥80-years was associated with an increased pooled adjusted-odds of statin underprescription (aOR = 1.16; 95% CI = 1.02–1.31; four-studies; moderate-certainty). Significant unpooled risk factors associated with an increased adjusted-odds of statin underprescription included age <50 and ≥90 (moderate-certainty for both); Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) ≥4 (moderate-certainty); increasing diagnostic cost group relative risk score (defined as the ratio of a patient's predicted care cost to the mean cost of care for the other study patients) (moderate-certainty); history of previous atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease without statin use in the past 12-months (moderate-certainty); critical condition necessitating urgent medical treatment (low-certainty); malignancy history (moderate-certainty); concurrent antiplatelet (moderate-certainty) or ACEI/ARB (low-certainty) use; ≥3 hospitalizations in the past year (low-certainty); length of hospital stay ≥10-days (low-certainty); receiving care at a teaching facility (moderate-certainty); and discharge from departments other than cardiovascular surgery or cardiology, neurology, or endocrinology (moderate-certainty for both). Significant risk factors associated with an increased pooled adjusted-odds of underprescription of all medications included CLTI (moderate-certainty) and COPD (moderate-certainty). Significant unpooled risk factors associated with an increased adjusted-odds of underprescription of all medications included increasing age (moderate-certainty), non-white race (low-certainty), and current smoking (low-certainty).
Significant unpooled risk factors associated with an increased adjusted-hazard of antiplatelet nonadherence included female sex (moderate-certainty); atrial fibrillation (low-certainty); anxiety disorders (low-certainty); COPD (low-certainty); taking concurrent anticoagulants or antiarrhythmic agents (low-certainty for both); and being a new antiplatelet user (low-certainty). Significant unpooled risk factors associated with an increased adjusted-hazard of statin nonadherence included age <50, ≥65, ≥80, and ≥90-years (moderate-certainty for all); CCI ≥3 and ≥4 (moderate-certainty for both); Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation quintile of 3 (low-certainty); history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease without statin use in the past 12-months (low-certainty); rosuvastatin or atorvastatin vs simvastatin as the initially prescribed statin (low-certainty for both); being a new statin user (low-certainty); decreasing patient medication copayment (low-certainty); and receiving specialist mental health care (moderate-certainty). Significant unpooled risk factors associated with an increased adjusted-hazard of antihypertensive/ACEI/ARB underprescription included imidapril, fosinopril, or valsartan vs perindopril as the initially prescribed antihypertensive/ACEI/ARB (low-certainty for all); concurrent statins prescription (low-certainty); increasing number of cardiovascular medications (low-certainty); and being a new antihypertensive/ACEI/ARB user (low-certainty). Significant unpooled risk factors associated with nonadherence to all medications included female sex (moderate-certainty); atrial fibrillation (low-certainty); and being a new user of guideline-recommended medications (low-certainty).

## Outcomes
Statin underprescription was associated with an increased pooled adjusted-hazard of late (>30-days after follow-up start) major amputation (aHR = 1.19; 95% CI = 1.01–1.39; low-certainty) and all-cause mortality (aHR = 1.22; 95% CI = 1.19–1.25; moderate-certainty). Underprescription of all medications was associated with an increased pooled-adjusted hazard of late all-cause mortality (aHR = 1.31; 95% CI = 1.08–1.58; low-certainty). Antiplatelet underprescription was associated with an increased unpooled adjusted-odds and/or–hazard of late revascularization (low-certainty) ; MACE (moderate-certainty) ; MALE (low-certainty) ; and decreased overall-survival-time (moderate-certainty). Statin underprescription was associated with an increased unpooled adjusted-odds, -risk and/or–hazard of early (≤30-days after follow-up start) MACE (moderate-certainty); late MACE , (moderate-certainty); late stent patency loss (low-certainty); late ipsilateral reintervention (low-certainty); and late all-cause mortality (low-certainty). It was also associated with an increased unpooled adjusted-hazard of decreased amputation-free-time (moderate-certainty) and amputation-free-survival-time (moderate-certainty). Underprescription of any medication was associated with an increased unpooled adjusted-odds of late MALE (moderate-certainty). Finally, underprescription of all medications was associated with an increased unpooled adjusted-odds or–hazard of early MI (moderate-certainty); early all-cause mortality (moderate-certainty); late hospital readmission (low-certainty); late MACE (low-certainty) and MALE (low-certainty); and late MI (moderate-certainty).

## Post-hoc analyses
Post-hoc analyses suggested that removing studies including asymptomatic PAD patients did not significantly change the observed cumulative incidence and prevalence of antiplatelet underprescription ( ). They also suggested that there were no differences in the pooled incidence/prevalence of underprescription or nonadherence between lower-middle-income, upper-middle-income, or high-income countries or based on study recruitment period ( ).

## Small study effects
Egger's test suggested evidence of small study effects potentially due to publication bias for meta-analyses examining the incidence of antiplatelet (p = 0.001), antihypertensive (p = 0.018), and ACEI (p = 0.026) underprescription and all medication nonadherence (p < 0.0001) ( ). There was also evidence of small study effects in meta-analyses examining adjusted-associations between age ≥80 years (p = 0.008), non-white race (p = 0.016), prior vascular surgery (p < 0.0001), and statin underprescription; and between prior vascular surgery (p = 0.017), and all medication underprescription ( and ). ## Discussion
This systematic review suggests that one-quarter to one-half of adults with PAD are not prescribed antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives. One-quarter also appear to not fill/refill their medication prescriptions. Although underprescription and nonadherence did not vary by region of study origin, prescriber specialty, clinical practice guidelines followed, or clinical practice setting, most studies (96%) were conducted in high-income countries. Stratified meta-analyses and meta-regression suggested underprescription incidence was higher in population-based studies and those enrolling more females and past/current smokers while nonadherence incidence was higher in studies enrolling more patients with diabetes. Underprescription risk factors with at least moderate certainty included female sex, advanced age, malignancy history, and CLTI. Nonadherence risk factors with at least moderate certainty included advanced age, increasing comorbidity burden, and receiving specialist mental health care. Underprescription was associated with myocardial infarction, MALE, above-ankle amputation, and all-cause mortality. Nonadherence-outcome associations were not reported by studies.
There are several potential explanations for the high underprescription and nonadherence incidences/prevalences observed in this systematic review. Potential underprescription explanations include a lack of clinician awareness of guideline recommendations , , , surrounding PAD medical management, failure to use shared decision-making with patients when determining medication appropriateness, and/or beliefs that evidence supporting the use of these medications is not valid, medications are not cost-effective, or that adverse event risks associated with medication use outweighs their potential benefits for some patients (eg, those of advanced age or with malignancy). , Underprescription may also result from prescriber confusion because of conflicting guideline recommendations. , , , For example, the 2016 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines recommend antiplatelets for all PAD patients, while the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and 2022 Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) guidelines only recommend antiplatelets for those with symptomatic PAD. Clinicians may also not prescribe antiplatelets to patients at elevated bleeding risk or statins over concerns regarding statin-associated adverse events. Potential explanations for the high incidence/prevalence of patient nonadherence include advanced age, poor patient health literacy and/or insufficient knowledge regarding the benefits of PAD medical management, perceptions that medications are ineffective or may produce adverse side effects, forgetfulness, multimorbidity, lack of motivation, lifestyle-related factors, and/or medication costs. , , 
Underprescription risk factors included female sex, non-white race, advanced age, malignancy history, and CLTI. Nonadherence risk factors were similar to those for underprescription. Higher underprescription risk among females may result from erroneous assumptions that females have lower cardiovascular disease risk while that for non-white patients may result from patient-provider distrust, language barriers, or inexperience caring for certain ethnicities. Underprescription risk may be higher among older individuals and those with a malignancy history because providers may believe that medications are not cost-effective in these patients and/or that their adverse event risks outweigh any potential benefits. , These patients were also underrepresented in the RCTs used to develop PAD medical management recommendations. , , Higher underprescription risk among those with CLTI may result from an underappreciation of the benefits these medications have in those with a limb-threatening problem. , , , Greater underprescription frequencies were also observed in population-based studies compared to non-population-based studies (ie those conducted in tertiary academic centers). This may be because the patients included in these studies had less severe disease, were more socioeconomically diverse, or had less frequent contact with cardiovascular specialists such as vascular surgeons. 
Our systematic review has limitations. First, many studies ascertained PAD diagnoses using diagnostic and procedural codes, introducing misclassification bias risk. To estimate the effects of misclassification bias on our estimates, stratified meta-analyses were preformed based on whether studies used population-level data (where misclassification bias would be more likely). These analyses suggested that underprescription incidences were significantly higher for several medications among population-based studies potentially because of differences in the patient populations captured in population-based studies as outlined above. Second, although including patients with asymptomatic PAD (who are not strongly recommended to be prescribed antiplatelets in certain guidelines , ) in our meta-analyses of the incidence/prevalence of antiplatelet underprescription/nonadherence could have biased our estimates, these patients accounted for only 1% of our total study population. Further, post-hoc analyses removing these patients from our analyses did not significantly alter our observed antiplatelet underprescription/nonadherence estimates. Third, we pooled all estimates of medication nonadherence (both primary and secondary) together in our meta-analyses. Although we feel this approach was justified given the small number of published nonadherence studies, future evidence synthesis should considering reporting separate estimates of primary and secondary nonadherence. Fourth, although we only included adjusted risk factor and outcome associations, our estimates may be limited by residual confounding. Finally, many of our stratified meta-analyses and meta-regressions are likely underpowered.
Our findings have important implications for future research and practice. Our findings support those of meta-analyses of RCTs , , by suggesting that improving PAD medical management may improve patient outcomes and reduce healthcare costs by reducing the risk of MACE, MALE, lower limb revascularization procedures, and major amputation. Costs for arterial bypass procedures have been reported to range between $15,000 and $40,000 USD , (while long-term costs can exceed $120,000 USD) while those for major amputation have been reported to range from $55,000 to $83,000 USD (while lifetime costs may exceed $500,000 USD). Further, 30–57% of people undergoing a major lower limb amputation will not return to work and 33–78% will have to change their occupation and/or switch to part-time work. Future studies should examine whether PAD patients underrepresented in secondary prevention RCTs , , (ie, females, those with advanced age, malignancy history, and CLTI) achieve similar benefits from guideline-recommended medications. More studies examining nonadherence (particularly primary nonadherence) are also needed as improvements in prescription will not increase medication use unless adherence also improves. Studies should also examine outcomes associated with nonadherence. Trials examining strategies for improving prescription and adherence among people with PAD are also warranted. Interventions for improving clinician prescription deserving of further study include improving healthcare access for PAD patients (especially those from socioeconomically-disadvantaged backgrounds); specialized secondary PAD prevention clinics; improved education for patients and families, medical trainees, and physicians regarding PAD medical management; clinician reminders; prescriber audit and feedback; and shared decision-making. , Potentially promising interventions for improving patient adherence deserving of further study include patient education, more routine follow-up; reminder systems (eg, text message prompts), and other tailored adherence-support interventions; involving pharmacists in the PAD patient care pathway (to assess adherence barriers, coordinate medication refills, etc); and face-to-face motivational interviewing (a non-coercive form of counselling that empowers patient behavior change through understanding and engaging with intrinsic motivation sources). , , 
In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that one-quarter to one-half of adults with PAD are not prescribed antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives and one-quarter do not adhere to these medications after prescription. Underprescription and/or nonadherence are more common in females and those of advanced age, non-white race, and with a history of malignancy and CLTI and associated with worse outcomes. Understanding the reasons underlying the above and identifying interventions for improving guideline-recommended medication prescription and adherence among patients with PAD are urgently required.
