To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/.Complex upper urinary-tract disorders (CUUTD) pose challenges owing to multifactorial etiology. Evidence on hybrid endoscopic-robotic approaches (HERA) remains limited. We evaluated the efficacy, safety, and outcomes of HERA for CUUTD across adult and pediatric populations. We performed a PRISMA-compliant systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis of pooled proportions (Freeman–Tukey transformation) registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251022229). PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science databases were searched for English full-texts only (last searched: June 22, 2025). Inclusion criteria were: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomized studies using HERA for CUUTD. Exclusion criteria were: isolated pathologies, single-modality, non-English, and insufficient data. Populations were grouped as adult, pediatric, or mixed. The independent dual review and risk of bias (RoB) assessment used Joanna Briggs Institute checklists. Heterogeneity (I2) and publication bias (Egger’s test) were evaluated. Fourteen retrospective studies (2005–2023) from international centers (n = 95: 26 adults, 44 pediatric, and 25 mixed populations; low bias) were included. In the absence of comparator arms, our findings represent descriptive pooled estimates of efficacy and safety. Pooled stone-free rate was 90.3% (95% CI 82.9–95.8%; I2 = 75.8%%) and was higher in adults (100%) than in pediatrics (81.8%; p = 0.0082). Obstruction resolution was 100%; although no intraoperative complications were observed, postoperative complications were noted in 11.6% cases, mostly Clavien–Dindo II–IIIa. The reoperation rate was 9.6%, mean operative time was 185.2 min, and the mean hospital stay was 3.5 days. Stone recurrence and obstruction recurrence were noted in 4.2% and 0% cases, respectively. In selected patients and experienced centers, HERA may achieve high SFR and obstruction resolution with acceptable morbidity; however, the overall strength of evidence is low, heterogeneity is substantial, and publication bias was detected. Findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Rigorous prospective comparative studies with standardized outcome definitions and follow-up are needed before drawing firm practice-changing conclusions.

## Introduction
Complex upper urinary tract disorders (CUUTD) such as urolithiasis with large stone burden, ureteral strictures, and ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO) pose significant challenges due to their multifactorial etiology and complex anatomy. Traditionally, open surgery was the mainstay, offering high efficacy but with notable morbidity, prolonged recovery, and postoperative discomfort [ ]. Minimally invasive techniques like percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and ureterorenoscopic lithotripsy (URSL) have improved outcomes by reducing hospital stay and complications [ ]. However, these are often insufficient for complex conditions [ ].
Hybrid endoscopic-robotic approaches (HERA) integrate robot-assisted surgery (RAS) with endoscopy, allowing simultaneous reconstruction (e.g., pyeloplasty) and stone clearance within a single procedure. In principle, HERA consolidates reconstruction and stone management in a single session for carefully selected patients; however, its comparative effectiveness versus standard single-modality options has not been established. It may reduce the need for staged operations and facilitate access in anatomically complex systems where single-modality methods may be limited. The published evidence is sparse and fragmented, consisting predominantly of small retrospective series with heterogeneous outcome definitions, imaging schedules, and follow-up durations, which limits certainty, generalizability, and may introduce reporting and publication biases. Prior systematic reviews on upper urinary tract (UUT) interventions have focused on single-modality approaches, excluding complex conditions such as UPJO with stones. Thus, a focused synthesis of the sparse evidence on hybrid approaches is needed to clarify their potential role in multifactorial disease. To address this gap, we systematically reviewed published literature on HERA for CUUTD in adults and children. The aim of this study was to evaluate efficacy, safety, and procedural outcomes, and to explore potential differences between adult and pediatric populations, with the intent to generate descriptive, hypothesis-generating signals rather than definitive comparative claims.
Given the absence of randomized or comparative studies, this work was designed as a single-arm meta-analysis, pooling outcomes across available case series and reports.

## Methods
This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [ ]. The protocol was registered prospectively in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=1022229). During the review process, the protocol was amended to apply the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for Case Reports and Case Series, as neither RoB 2 nor ROBINS-I were suitable given the predominance of retrospective reports and case series and the absence of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [ ].

## Search strategy
A systematic search was conducted on 22 June 2025 in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. A tailored combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms was applied using Boolean operators. No date restrictions were set, but only English-language full-text studies were included. Screening and selection were done manually; no automation tools were used. Two reviewers (RBD, FNP) independently performed the search using the following string:
(“simultaneous” OR “concurrent” OR “concomitant” OR “hybrid” OR “combined”) AND (“robotic” OR “robot-assisted” OR “robotic laparoscopic” OR “robot-assisted laparoscopic”) AND (“pyeloplasty” OR “segmental resection” OR “nephrolithotomy” OR “ureterolithotomy” OR “pyelolithotomy” OR “ureteral strictures” OR “ureteropelvic junction obstruction” OR “UPJO” OR “ureteral stricture” OR “ureterocalycostomy” OR “ureterolithotomy” OR “lithotomy”) AND (“endoscopic” OR “RIRS” OR “URSL” OR “URS” OR “cystoscopy” OR “flexible cystoscopy” OR “ureteroscopy” OR “ureterorenoscopy” OR “nephroscopy”).
The strategy was peer-reviewed using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist [ ]. Databases were accessed via native platforms (PubMed: NCBI; Scopus: Elsevier; Web of Science: Clarivate). Reference lists of included studies were screened for additional citations. No other data sources were consulted.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Study eligibility was assessed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Study design (PICOS) framework. Studies were included if they met the following criteria:Population: Adults or children with complex UUT conditions involving at least two coexisting disorders (e.g., UPJO or ureteral stricture with renal stones)
Intervention: Hybrid techniques combining RAS with endoscopic procedures (e.g., ureteroscopy, cystoscopy)
Comparison: Although the original protocol anticipated inclusion of studies with direct comparators (e.g., PCNL, URSL, extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy [ESWL], or open/laparoscopic surgery), no randomized or controlled studies were identified. Conventional treatments were therefore considered only as narrative benchmarks from the literature, and no head-to-head analyses were performed.
Outcomes: Collected data included study characteristics (1–5) and clinical outcomes (6–16): (1) author, year of publication, (2) study type, (3) study design, (4) number of patients, (5) population (pediatric/adult/mixed), (6) UUT conditions, (7) stone-free rate (SFR; %), (8) SFR assessment method, (9) obstruction resolution (%), (10) intraoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo), (11) postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo), (12) reoperation rate (%), (13) operative time (minutes), (14) hospital stay (days), (15) 30-day readmission rate (%), (16) stone recurrence (%) with follow-up period, and (17) obstruction recurrence (%) with follow-up period.
Study design: RCTs, non-randomized comparative studies, and single-arm clinical studies (retrospective cohorts, case series, case reports) with detailed surgical and outcome data. However, no RCTs or comparative studies were identified during the review.

Exclusion criteria were studies on isolated UUT conditions, those with single-modality treatments (e.g., standalone PCNL), preclinical or non-clinical studies, those lacking hybrid-specific outcome data, or those not written in the English language.
Only single-arm clinical studies (retrospective cohorts, case series, case reports) were identified. No randomized or comparative studies were available; thus, the pooled analysis was restricted to a single-arm design.

## Objectives
The primary objectives were to (1) assess the efficacy of these approaches by measuring SFR (no residual stones on imaging) and resolution of ureteral obstruction (normal urinary flow on imaging or functional testing) and (2) evaluate their safety by reporting intraoperative (e.g., bleeding, organ injury) and postoperative (e.g., infection, urinoma) complications using the Clavien–Dindo classification and reoperation rates. The secondary objectives were to (1) examine the procedural efficiency and safety indicators of these approaches, including operative time (incision to closure), hospital stay (days), and 30-day readmission rates; (2) analyze recurrence outcomes, including new stones or recurrent obstruction (symptoms or imaging findings) at standard follow-up intervals (e.g., 1, 3, 6, 12 months); and (3) compare outcomes by age group (adult versus pediatric), where available.

## Screening process and data extraction
A structured, multi-phase manual screening process was used to identify eligible studies. Two independent reviewers (RBD, FNP) screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially relevant articles. Reviewers were blinded to each other’s decisions during initial screening. Inter-reviewer agreement was very high (Cohen’s κ = 0.87), indicating excellent concordance, with only minimal discrepancies resolved through discussion, with unresolved cases adjudicated by a senior investigator (AAA). No automation tools were employed.
The same reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized template. Data extraction also demonstrated strong agreement, with rare differences in interpretation, which were reconciled collaboratively or resolved by AAA if consensus was not reached. In cases of unclear reporting, corresponding authors were contacted; if no response was received, missing data were noted, and its effect was considered in the analysis.

## Quality assessment and risk of bias (RoB)
RoB was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists for Case Reports and Case Series. Domains included eligibility criteria, diagnostic accuracy, completeness of case capture, demographic and clinical consistency, clarity of interventions and outcomes, adverse event reporting, and clinical conclusions. Two reviewers assessed each study independently; disagreements were resolved via discussion or AAA’s arbitration. Studies with critical RoB were excluded. This ensured the inclusion of methodologically transparent studies with clinical relevance.

## Certainty assessment
Certainty of evidence for primary outcomes (SFR, complications, reoperations) was assessed using GRADE (version 20) [ ]. Factors included: RoB (JBI), inconsistency (I2 >50%), indirectness (retrospective design), imprecision (CI width >20%), and publication bias (Egger’s test). Certainty levels (high to very low) were downgraded by –1 or –2 for serious or very serious issues. Two reviewers (RBD, FNP) performed independent assessments; AAA resolved disagreements. No automation tools were used.

## Heterogeneity tests
Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q statistic and I2. SFR: Q = 53.72 (df = 13), I2 = 75.8%. Postoperative complications: Q = 28.18, I2 = 53.9%. Reoperations: Q = 52.55, I2 = 75.3%. Obstruction resolution and intraoperative complications were uniform (I2 = 0%).

## Statistical analysis and data synthesis
All estimates were derived using a single-arm random-effects pooled proportion approach. No comparative meta-analysis was feasible. Binary outcomes (e.g., SFR, complications) were reported as pooled proportions to improve interpretability in non-comparative studies. Interpretation thresholds: SFR ≥90% indicated high efficacy (aligned with PCNL/RIRS benchmarks); complication rates <15% reflected low risk (Clavien–Dindo II+); reoperation rates <10% were acceptable for complex cases. Binary outcomes were pooled using the Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine transformation to stabilize extreme proportions. Between-study variance (τ2) was estimated with the DerSimonian–Laird method. Estimates were back-transformed to proportions with 95% CIs. Analyses were performed in Python (version 3.12.3) using scipy and statsmodels. Heterogeneity was assessed with Q and I2: 0% (none), <25% (low), 25–74% (moderate), ≥75% (high). Continuous outcomes (operative time, hospital stay) were summarized using weighted means by study sample size; CIs were not computed due to missing variances. Meta-regression used weighted least squares on transformed outcomes, with population type as the covariate (adult = 0, mixed = 0.5, pediatric = 1), and inverse variance weighting. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test and funnel plot inspection. Data for pooling were derived from Online Resource 1, using reported percentages applied to patient numbers. When event counts were unreported, zero events were conservatively assumed. Fourteen studies (n = 95: 26 adult, 25 mixed, 44 pediatric) were included. Study characteristics were tabulated for demographic and procedural comparison. Forest plots were generated for pooled binary outcomes using Python’s matplotlib and scipy. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) excluding high-risk studies (≥2 JBI failures), and (2) fixed-effects model alternative.

## Reporting bias assessment
Reporting bias was evaluated using Egger’s test and funnel plots. Asymmetry (P<0.10) indicated potential bias; no adjustments were applied. High risk was assigned if P<0.10. Two reviewers (RBD, FNP) conducted independent assessments; AAA resolved disagreements. No author contact or automation tools were used.

## Ethical considerations
As this was a systematic review of published data, no new studies involving humans or animals were conducted. Thus, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. The review followed PRISMA guidelines.

## Results

## Search results
The search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science yielded 192 records. After deduplication (n = 75) and exclusion of studies not written in the English language (n = 16), 101 titles/abstracts were screened. Fourteen records were excluded for ineligible formats (three book chapters, one editorial, one letter, nine conference abstracts). Of the remaining 87 full texts, 66 were excluded for irrelevance. From 21 eligible reports, 7 were excluded: isolated UUT conditions (n = 5), inaccessible full text (n = 1), or overlapping cohorts (n = 1).
Excluded studies (initially eligible): Mershon et al. (2023) [ ], Osbun et al. (2016) [ ], Anakievski et al. (2021) [ ], Yang et al. (2023) [ ], and Chen et al. (2023) [ ] were excluded due to isolated pathologies (e.g., retroperitoneal fibrosis, strictures without calculi), not meeting the PICOS criteria requiring coexisting UUT disorders.
A total of fourteen retrospective single-arm studies (n = 95) were included in the final synthesis (Online Resource 2). No comparator arms were available.

## Risk of bias assessment and quality evaluation
All included studies were retrospective; however, low RoB and high quality across most domains were observed. No study met criteria for critical risk (failure in ≥3 core domains, e.g., unclear inclusion or inconsistent outcome reporting). Online Resource 3 [ – ] and Online Resource 4 [ – ] show consensus ratings and bias assessments.

## Overview
Key characteristics, procedural outcomes, and study-level details of hybrid endoscopic-robotic interventions are summarized in Online Resource 1, allowing comparison of efficacy, safety, and efficiency metrics.
Binary outcomes (study-specific, 95% CI): SFR ranged from 70.4% (CI 47.1–88.7%; Roth et al. [ ]) to 100% (CI 59.0–100%); Obstruction resolution: 100% across all studies (CI 96.2–100%); Post-op complications: 0% (CI 0–30.8%) to 18.2% (CI 2.3–51.8%; Esposito et al. [ ]); Reoperations: 0% (CI 0–30.8%) to 29.6% (CI 11.9–53.5%; Roth et al. [ ]).
Continuous outcomes (means with ranges): operative time and hospital stay. Variances were not uniformly reported.

## Primary outcomes

## Efficacy
The pooled initial SFR after the hybrid procedure was 90.3% (95% CI: 82.9–95.8%, I2 = 75.8%; 14 studies, 95 patients; Fig. ). Obstruction resolution was 100% across all studies (95% CI: 96.2–100.0%, I2 = 0%), with no heterogeneity observed. 

## Safety
Intraoperative complications were 0% (95% CI 0.0–3.8%, I2 = 0%). The pooled postoperative complication rate was 11.6% (95% CI 7.0–17.2%, I2 = 53.9%;14 studies, 95 patients; Fig. ), including urinary leakage (IIIa), UTI/fever (II), stent migration (IIIa), urosepsis (IVa), hematuria (II), and grade I events (unspecified; treated as 0). The reoperation rate was 9.6% (95% CI 4.2–16.9%, I2 = 75.3%;14 studies, 95 patients; Fig. ), primarily for residual stones (URS, ESWL, PCNL). 

## Secondary outcomes

## Procedural efficiency and safety indicators
The weighted mean operative time was 185.2 minutes (11 studies, 92 patients; excluded: Locke et al. [ ], Gabrielson et al. [ ], Nishio et al. [ ]). The weighted mean hospital stay was 3.5 days (same cohort). A 30-day readmission rate was reported in one study (Roth et al. [ ]: 4.8%, 1/21 for urosepsis); the crude rate was 1.1% (95% CI 0.0–5.6%).

## Recurrence
Stone recurrence was 18.5% in one study (Roth et al. [ ]: ~4/21 at 12 months) and 0% or unreported in others (mean follow-up ~13 months); crude recurrence was 4.2% (4/95). Obstruction recurrence was 0% (95% CI 0.0–3.8%).

## Differences between adult and pediatric populations
Meta-regression showed lower SFR in pediatrics (coefficient = –0.5801, p = 0.0082; Fig. and Online Resource 5). Adults had higher crude SFR (100% vs. 81.8%), fewer complications (3.8% vs. 11.4%), and no reoperations (vs. 18.2%). Operative time was shorter in adults (162.5 min, n = 24) than in pediatrics (190.3 min, n = 43); hospital stay was similar (2.7 vs. 2.2 days). 

## Sensitivity analyses
Primary findings remained robust on sensitivity testing. Removing moderate-risk studies (n = 2; ≥2 JBI domain issues) yielded similar pooled SFR (91.2%; 95% CI 83.5–96.7%; I2 = 72.4%) and postoperative complications (10.8%; 95% CI 6.1–16.5%; I2 = 51.2%). Fixed-effects analysis showed minimal changes: SFR 92.1% (95% CI 85.6–96.3%), complications 10.9% (95% CI 5.8–17.4%), reoperation 8.7% (95% CI 4.1–15.2%). Forest plots (Online Resource 6) showed consistent results.

## Reporting bias assessment
Egger’s test for SFR revealed possible bias (effect = 2.14, p = 0.04); funnel plot asymmetry suggested underreporting of smaller studies with lower SFRs (Online Resource 7). No bias detected for complications (p = 0.12) or reoperations (p = 0.21). Trim-and-fill adjusted SFR to 88.7% (95% CI 80.2–94.8%), confirming result stability. A study-synthesis matrix showed no missing protocol outcomes.

## Certainty of evidence
GRADE rated SFR and reoperation certainty as low (downgraded for retrospective design, inconsistency (I2 = 75.8%), and publication bias); obstruction resolution was moderate (downgraded for indirectness); complication certainty was low due to inconsistency (I2 = 53.9–75.3%) (Table ). 

## Discussion

## Comparison to conventional surgical modalities
As a single-arm meta-analysis, our pooled estimates should be interpreted as descriptive benchmarks rather than direct comparative evidence. Given the low certainty for several outcomes (GRADE) and the detection of publication bias for SFR, these estimates are hypothesis‑generating and not suitable for claims of superiority over standard care. Our pooled initial SFR was 90.3%, with complete obstruction resolution. Outcomes varied by population: 100% in adults (n = 26), 81.8% in children (n = 44), and 88% in mixed groups (n = 25).
These figures appear broadly within the range reported for single-modality techniques in other contexts; however, these are indirect narrative contrasts and the cohorts, definitions, and imaging schedules differ substantively. In adults, Ying et al. [ ] (n = 1259) found 87% SFR for PCNL, and ~91.2% final SFR for negative pressure aspiration-assisted retrograde intrarenal surgery (NPAA-RIRS). Qiu et al. [ ] showed Mini-PCNL outperformed RIRS in obese patients (86.2% vs. 70.1%). Liu et al. [ ] reported higher Mini-PCNL efficacy than RIRS (87.5% vs. 81%). Jung et al. [ ] confirmed ultra-mini PCNL superiority (OR  =  2.01). In pediatrics, Yuan et al. [ ] found better outcomes with micro-PCNL over RIRS (OR = 0.50), whereas Wicaksono et al. [ ] reported no significant difference (85.7% vs. 84.6%). Taken together, these literature benchmarks provide useful clinical context but do not establish the equivalence or superiority of HERA.
Mean HERA operative time was 185.2 min (range 92–302 min). This is longer than times reported for single-modality methods: Higazy et al. [ ] found 84.26 ± 14.5 min for F-mPCNL and 74.43 ± 14.5 min for RIRS (p < 0.001); Qiu et al. [ ] reported no significant difference in pooled operative time between Mini-PCNL and RIRS (MD = 7.97 min, 95% CI −6.91 to 22.86, p = 0.29); Ying et al. [ ] showed shorter operative time for PCNL over NPAA-RIRS (weighted mean difference = 0.68 min, 95% CI 0.22–1.13, P = 0.003); and Liu et al. [ ] demonstrated a weighted mean difference of −10.85 min favoring Mini-PCNL over RIRS. Although prolonged, HERA’s time reflects dual reconstructive–litholytic goals that may obviate staged procedures; whether this translates to fewer total anesthetics or better patient-centred recovery requires prospective evaluation.
No intraoperative complications were reported in HERA (0%, 95% CI 0.0–3.8%). Postoperative complications occurred in 11.6% (Clavien–Dindo I–IV), with 3.8% in adults and 11.4% in pediatrics. These pooled rates fall within ranges reported for standalone techniques in selected series, but indirectness and case-mix differences limit any inference. Söderberg et al. [ ] (n = 4088) reported major complications with little to no difference between PCNL and RIRS (RR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.59–1.25), equating to ~27 per 1000 in PCNL versus 31 per 1000 in RIRS. Ying et al. [ ] found lower overall complications for NPAA-RIRS over PCNL (RR = 0.52, 95% CI 0.40–0.68, P<0.001), with lower Clavien–Dindo I–II (RR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.38–0.67, P<0.001) but no difference in Clavien–Dindo III–IV (RR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.28–1.51, P = 0.316). Liu et al. [ ] found significantly higher complication rates for Mini-PCNL over RIRS (RR  =  1.33), including major complications (RR  =  1.89). Liu et al. [ ] identified endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery (ECIRS) as the safest option (major complications OR  =  0.29), noting that these data largely derive from patients without fixed anatomic obstruction, which may limit generalizability to CUUTD. Moreover, with small samples, the “0%” intraoperative estimate carries wide uncertainty and may be vulnerable to under-reporting.
Combined modalities like ECIRS are increasingly preferred for complex nephrolithiasis due to superior access to calyceal groups, improved clearance, and less tract repositioning. Kallidonis et al. [ ] reported higher SFR for PCNL and RIRS compared to ESWL in lower pole stones <2 cm, despite longer operative times and higher complication rates for PCNL. These observations are conceptually consistent with dual-access strategies; however, HERA cohorts frequently include concomitant obstruction and reconstruction, so direct extrapolation remains uncertain.
The HERA reoperation rate was 9.6%, with notable variation by age: 0% in adults vs. 18.2% in pediatric patients. Most reinterventions involved secondary URS or ESWL. In comparison, Liu et al. [ ] reported 6.2% (Mini-PCNL) and 8.7% (RIRS); Yuan et al. [ ] found 10.2% (Micro-PCNL) and 13.6% (RIRS); and ECIRS cases reviewed by Liu et al. [ ] had a 3.8% rate. Ying et al. [ ] demonstrated fewer reoperations in PCNL versus NPAA-RIRS (RR = 2.51) [ , ]. Differences likely reflect underlying complexity, selection factors, and follow-up heterogeneity; pediatric subgroup estimates, in particular, should be interpreted cautiously.
The mean hospital stay following hybrid procedures was 3.5 days. In comparison, Mini-PCNL and RIRS had shorter stays: Liu et al. [ ] reported 3.1 and 2.1 days; Jung et al. [ ], 2.9 and 1.4 days; and Ying et al. [ ], ~2.3 days for NPAA-RIRS versus 3.7 days for PCNL. A longer stay in HERA may reflect its broader surgical scope, including suturing and stenting, but differences in discharge practices and health-system factors also affect LOS and limit cross-study comparability.
Overall, our synthesis supports HERA as a feasible single-session option for carefully selected CUUTD cases when reconstruction and stone management are both indicated; however, in the absence of controlled data we cannot infer superiority over PCNL, RIRS, or ECIRS. Conclusions are preliminary and exploratory, and clinical decisions should prioritize individual anatomy, stone burden, surgeon expertise, and resource availability.

## Study limitations
This review has some limitations linked to study design and evidence quality. Most included studies were retrospective case reports or series (n = 95), lacking RCTs or prospective cohorts, which raises risks of selection bias, incomplete reporting, and confounding. Although JBI appraisals suggested generally low risk in reporting domains, the study designs themselves introduce serious limitations that dominate certainty judgments. Variability in outcome measures (e.g., differing SFR imaging methods—CT, ultrasound, kidney-ureter-bladder) and short follow-up durations (~13 months) may inflate efficacy estimates and miss long-term recurrence. High heterogeneity in key outcomes (I2>75% for SFR, reoperation) likely reflects differences in population (adult versus pediatric), stone burden, and procedural variation (flexible vs. rigid nephroscopy), limiting pooled estimate reliability even with random-effects modeling. Publication bias is possible, as Egger’s test showed asymmetry (p = 0.04 for SFR), potentially skewing results toward favorable outcomes. Absence of direct comparators precluded pooled analysis against standard techniques (e.g., PCNL, open pyeloplasty), limiting conclusions to descriptive inference. Continuous outcomes lacked variance reporting, precluding CI calculations. Operator experience, learning curves, and center volume were not systematically reported and could bias outcomes toward high-volume settings. Overall, certainty for most primary outcomes was low (GRADE), and we therefore refrain from practice-changing interpretations. Lastly, English-only searches may have excluded global evidence; automation tools were not used, but dual manual screening minimized selection errors.

## Future directions
To advance HERA for CUUTD, prospective comparative studies—ideally randomized where feasible, or rigorous multicenter cohorts with prespecified adjustment—are required to establish long‑term efficacy, safety, and cost‑effectiveness versus PCNL, RIRS, and ECIRS [ ]. Standardization of core outcomes (e.g., the tetrafecta: stone clearance, absence of complications, no auxiliary procedures, preserved renal function), uniform SFR definitions and imaging time points, and routine reporting of resource use (operative time, number of anesthetics, LOS), Clavien–Dindo complications, and patient‑reported outcomes are priorities [ ]. Emerging robotic flexible ureteroscopy technologies (e.g., force‑feedback systems and continuum designs), and early clinical experiences with dedicated platforms (e.g., ILY®, Zamenix RTM) or combined mini‑PCNL–fURS workflows demonstrate technical feasibility and promising early SFR, but current data are preliminary and largely non‑comparative; head‑to‑head evaluations are needed before broad adoption [ , – ]. Preclinical work (e.g., EasyUreteroTM) provides a rationale for carefully staged human studies [ ]. Implementation should be supported by structured training pathways and competency frameworks, building on state‑of‑the‑art reviews and historical insights into robotic RIRS evolution [ , ]. Pediatric‑specific registries and trials are particularly needed to clarify indications, optimize safety, and define age‑appropriate endpoints. None of these platforms or strategies should be considered standard of care without higher‑quality comparative evidence.
