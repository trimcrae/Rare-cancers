## Summary

## Background
Aggressive care near patients’ end-of-life (EOL) entails limited therapeutic values, high costs, and compromised quality of life (QoL). In this study, we aimed to estimate the global prevalence of aggressive care in patients with cancer and explore potential subgroup differences.

## Methods
We searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to Feb 16, 2024. Eligible studies reported the prevalence of aggressive EOL care using at least one quantifiable measure. Random-effects models were used to derive the pooled prevalence and subgroup analyses were performed to investigate differences in the prevalence of aggressive care across regions, the country's level of economic development, tumor types, ages, and sample sizes. This review is registered with PROSPERO, number CRD42023467839.

## Findings
A total of 129 studies were included in this systematic review, of which 118 (91.5%) were from high-income countries. Studies were mostly conducted in the Americas (60, 46.5%), Europe (34, 26.4%), and Western Pacific (31, 24.0%). Measures of aggressive care were inconsistent across studies, with the most commonly used measure being the use of chemotherapy in the last 14 days of life (DOLs) (n = 87, 67.4%) and intensive care unit (ICU) stay in the last 30 DOLs (n = 87, 67.4%). The prevalence of the five claims-based measures of aggressive care, i.e., chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs, ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs, repeated hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs, repeated emergency room (ER) visit in the last 30 DOLs, and hospice care <3 days before death were 11.6% (95% CI, 9.8%–13.4%), 14.4% (95% CI, 11.8%–17.0%), 17.9% (95% CI, 14.4%–21.4%), 14.8% (95% CI, 12.0%–17.6%), and 14.4% (95% CI, 11.2%–17.6%), respectively. Regional differences were statistically significant in the prevalence of ICU stay and repeated hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs (p < 0.01; p = 0.03). Patients with hematologic malignancies were more likely to receive aggressive care than those with solid tumors, as seen in their higher rates of chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs (21.7% versus 11.6%; p = 0.03), ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs (25.5% versus 10.8%; p < 0.01), and hospice care <3 days before death (26.7% versus 14.2%; p < 0.01). In addition, the prevalence of chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs (26.2%; p < 0.01) and repeated hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs (31.4%; p < 0.01) were highest among pediatric patients with cancer.

## Interpretation
This meta-analysis found that aggressive EOL care was common in patients with cancer, regardless of the definition used, and varied by regions and populations. It is necessary to be aware of the global burden of aggressive care for patients with cancer near their EOL and take prompt action to address it.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
We searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from inception to February 16, 2024, using combinations of terms encompassing, end-of-life care, aggressive or intense care, and cancer. While the regional prevalence of aggressive end-of-life (EOL) care in patients with cancer have been reported by many studies, the global burden remains uncertain. Only one published systematic review summarized 42 studies investigated EOL care in patients with lung cancer and indicated that aggressive EOL care was frequent. However, this review was only a qualitative synthesis of data collected from a subgroup of patients with cancer. Another systematic review evaluated interventions to reduce aggressive care in patients with cancer at EOL and found limited evidence of improvements as there lacked standardized measurements and robust study design. We did not find any study that attempted to synthesis the global prevalence of aggressive cancer care near the EOL.

## Added value of this study
This systematic review and meta-analysis included a total of 2,734,741 patients from 129 studies evaluating EOL cancer care intensity. We reported the pooled prevalence of aggressive EOL care as defined by the five claims-based measures as well as other commonly used measures. Additionally, we investigated subgroup differences in prevalence estimates based on geographical region, the country's level of economic development, age, tumor type, and the study's sample size. We found that the aggressive EOL cancer care occurred commonly, regardless of the complex definitions. Another salient finding was that pediatric patients and patients with hematologic malignancies showed increased prevalence of chemotherapy in the last 14 days of life (DOLs). Data from less economically developed regions were limited and variation in the prevalence among these areas was large, pending further research.

## Implications of all the available evidence
Patients with cancer, especially pediatric patients and patients with hematologic tumors, commonly experience aggressive EOL care. Data on the prevalence of aggressive care were primarily reported by studies in high-income countries. Our results, together with previous studies, highlight the necessity to raise awareness of the burden of aggressive EOL care in patients with cancer, and implement targeted policies and interventions to reduce its prevalence.

## Introduction
Cancer is a leading cause of death across all countries and entails tremendous economic burdens on patients and their families. , Since the prognosis of patients with cancer is at times limited and uncertain, many patients will eventually proceed to a stage when discussing and planning for end-of-life (EOL) care is necessary. Though anticancer treatments have been making considerable advances over the past two decades, aggressive cancer care, especially near the EOL, also arises as a public health concern. , The intensive use of anticancer treatments near patients' EOL usually entails decreased therapeutic benefits while decreasing the quality of life (QoL) of patients and families' satisfaction with care and increasing medical costs and incidence of adverse events. , , , , , Advancing care for patients with cancer and promoting patients’ right to death with dignity would imply constraining such low-value care.
During the 2000s, Earle et al. proposed, validated, and established benchmarks for a series of quality indicators that measure the aggressiveness of cancer care. , The National Quality Forum (NQF) and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) adopted several validated claims-based indicators for aggressive EOL care to measure quality in cancer care, , which include receiving chemotherapy during the last 14 days of life (DOLs), any intensive care unit (ICU) admission or repeated hospitalizations or emergency room (ER) visit during the last 30 DOLs, and late referral to hospice care. However, indicators used to measure the aggressiveness of EOL care varied widely across studies. Moreover, as many concerned studies lacked a standardized measure of aggressive care and robust study design, limited evidence of consistent improvements was found in interventions that attempted to reduce the prevalence of aggressive EOL cancer care. 
An accurate estimate of the global prevalence of aggressive EOL care among patients with cancer would be the cornerstone for future advocacy programs to raise awareness and development of targeted healthcare policy and interventions to reduce its prevalence. Although many studies reported regional prevalence of aggressive EOL care in patients with cancer, they were conducted in one geographical area and reported varying prevalence rates due to the inconsistent use of different measures of aggressiveness, which limits a comprehensive assessment of the global prevalence and thus hinders the understanding of its significance. Furthermore, most studies were not able to illustrate differences across regions and populations based on available data. Hence, we conducted a systematic review of studies that reported the prevalence of aggressive care in patients with cancer near their EOL. This study aimed to synthesize the global prevalence of aggressive EOL care in patients with cancer, as measured by various indicators using all available data in published studies. We also compared prevalence estimates between different subgroups, categorized by geographical regions, countries’ level of economic development, patient age, tumor type, and study sample size.

## Methods
The protocol for this systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO (CRD42023467839) and the reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement. 

## Search strategy
Two researchers (ZM and HL) independently conducted literature search in PubMed, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Embase, for studies published between database inception and February 16, 2024, using combinations of relevant search terms such as “end-of-life care”, “aggressive or intense care”, and “cancer”. The full search strategy is available in the .

## Selection criteria
We included cohort studies or cross-sectional studies that were conducted among patients with cancer and used at least one quantifiable outcome measure of aggressive EOL care. Studies that were not written in English were excluded, as were case reports, qualitative studies, non-human studies, case control studies, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), or reviews. Studies that were confined to patients with specific comorbidities or did not report both the number of cases and sample size were excluded. For cohorts generated from the same source, the one with the most comprehensive results or the largest sample size was retained. However, when different indicators or different subgroups of the same indicator were separately reported in different articles, all those articles were kept, and specific populations in these publications were only included in the subgroup analysis.
Retrieved results were imported into the citation manager EndNote X9, and duplicates were removed. Two researchers (ZM and HL) independently screened the titles and abstracts of these articles and further read the full text that initially met the inclusion criteria to determine final eligibility. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher (XG).

## Data extraction
From each retrieved article, two researchers (ZM and YZ) independently extracted relevant data according to a pre-specified spreadsheet, which required researchers to fill in the following data: title of the research article, year of publication, name of the first author, country/region(s), sample size, study design, data source, age range, tumor type, death period, indicator(s) of aggressive care, and the numerator and denominator for each measure.

## Definitions and outcomes
The regions of study location were designated as Africa, the Americas, South-East Asia, Europe, the Eastern Mediterranean, and the Western Pacific according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria and as high-income countries and low- and middle-income countries according to the World Bank (WB) criteria. , 
We defined quantifiable outcome measures of aggressive EOL care in accordance with a previous systematic review. Measures included claims-based measures and other commonly used validated measures formulated by Earle and colleagues. , Identified claims-based measures of aggressive care were: 1) chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs; 2) >1 ER visit in the last 30 DOLs; 3) any ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs; 4) >1 hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs; and 5) hospice care <3 days before death. Other commonly used validated measures include: 1) hospice admission; 2) hospital death; 3) extended hospital stay (>14 days) in the last 30 DOLs; 4) any exposure to chemotherapy or targeted agent in the last 30 DOLs; 5) initiation of a new chemotherapy regimen in the last 30 DOLs; and 6) a composite score for total aggressive EOL care. All other measures were considered unvalidated.
The pooled and stratified prevalence of aggressive EOL care as defined by the five claims-based measures were assessed. In addition, we synthesized the overall prevalence of aggressive EOL care as defined by other measures that were reported in at least five studies.

## Quality assessment
We assessed the risk of study bias using the methods of Hoy et al. When a checklist item was not reported or unclear, it was identified as high-risk. Each study was independently assessed for bias by two reviewers (GH and LZ) and given a score out of nine. Discrepancies were discussed in consultation with a third reviewer (ZM).

## Statistical analysis
Before pooling prevalence estimates, the variance of the raw prevalence from each included study was stabilized by using the Freeman-Tukey double arc-sine transformation. , All estimates were presented after back transformation. A random-effects meta-analysis model was used owing to the anticipated heterogeneity of the included studies. The prevalence would be presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was assessed using the inconsistency index (I2) test, showing the proportion of the total variance in pooled estimates that is explained by variation between studies. Subgroup analyses were conducted by study location, level of economic development of the country (categorized based on the country's national income; referred to as income level hereafter), tumor type, age, and sample size to explore sources of heterogeneity. As a rule, at least 1000 samples should be available per subgroup. As studies have shown that traditional methods, such as funnel plots and asymmetry tests, are not suitable for assessing publication bias in prevalence studies, this study did not evaluate publication bias. In addition, we computed a sensitivity analysis by re-calculating the pooled prevalence estimates after omitting one study at a time. p-values of less than 0.05 were considered to be significant. All statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.3.2).

## Ethical approval
Ethics approval was not required because all data used in this study were collected from previous published studies and were anonymous.

## Role of the funding source
The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

## Results
Our database search yielded 12,116 publications (3962 from CENTRAL, 3982 from Embase, and 4172 from PubMed). After removing duplicates, we identified 10,649 unique articles that could potentially be included in our review. After screening the titles and abstracts and applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we retained 129 articles ( ). 
All except for two were conducted in a single country or region (n = 127, 98.4%) and 60 studies (46.5%) were conducted in the Americas, followed by 34 studies (26.4%) in Europe, 31 studies (24.0%) in the Western Pacific, three studies (2.3%) in the Eastern Mediterranean, and one study (0.8%) in Southeast Asia. Of the 129 studies, 118 (91.5%) were conducted in high-income nations, nine (7.0%) were in upper-middle income nations, and only one (0.8%) was in lower-middle income nations. No study was conducted on aggressive care in patients with cancer in low-income countries or regions. All studies were published between 2004 and 2024, with the majority (n = 120, 93.0%) published after the year 2012. Sample size ranged widely from 40 to 248,978 patients, with 33 studies (25.6%) involving more than 10,000 patients. Nearly half of studies (n = 62, 48.1%) reported exclusively on solid tumors, ten studies (7.8%) exclusively on hematologic malignancies, and four studies (3.1%) on both solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. Many studies (n = 53, 41.1%) did not distinguish between tumor types. Six studies (4.7%) focused on aggressive care in pediatric patients with cancer.
Studies defined aggressive care variably across studies and some used several indicators simultaneously to reflect the intensity of aggressive care. All studies used at least one validated measure of aggressive end-of-life care while 76 studies (58.9%) also used unvalidated measures conjunctively. Overall, eight studies (6.2%) used all five validated claims-based measures while ten studies (7.8%) did not use any validated claims-based measures. The most commonly reported indicators were receipt of chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs (n = 87, 67.4%), having any ICU admission in the last 30 DOLs (n = 87, 67.4%), any use of chemotherapy in the last 30 DOLs (n = 59, 45.7%), >1 ER visit in the last 30 DOLs (n = 58, 45.0%), having >1 hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs (n = 53, 41.1%), and death in acute care hospital (n = 51, 39.5%).

The quality assessment of included studies showed that 96 studies (74.4%) scored 8–9, 32 studies (24.8%) scored 6–7, and one study (0.8%) scored 5. The summarizes all findings of the literature quality assessment.
 gives the results of the overall and subgroup meta-analyses of the claims-based measures of aggressive care. Eighty-seven studies reported chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs, of which 81 (93.1%) were included in the pooled meta-analysis, showing a prevalence of 11.6% (95% CI, 9.8%–13.4%). The prevalence of chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs was highest in pediatric patients (26.2%, 95% CI 6.7%–45.7%) and lowest in patients aged 65 years or older (6.6%, 95% CI 4.6%–8.5%), with significant variations across age groups (p < 0.01). Additionally, chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs was more common in patients with hematologic malignancies (21.7%, 95% CI 14.1%–29.2%) than in patients with solid tumors (11.6%, 95% CI 9.4%–13.8%; p = 0.03). There was also a difference in the prevalence across studies by sample size, with the highest prevalence (13.4%, 95% CI 11.0%–15.8%) observed in studies with a sample size <1,000, followed by studies with a sample size of 1000–9999 (10.9%, 95% CI 6.1%–15.6%), and lowest in studies with a sample size >10,000 (6.9%, 95% CI 5.0%–8.9%) (p < 0.01). However, we found no differences in prevalence across income levels or geographic regions ( A and ). 
Eighty-seven studies reported the prevalence of ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs and 77 (88.5%) were included in the pooled meta-analysis. The global prevalence of the indicator was 14.4% (95% CI, 11.8%–17.0%), which differed significantly across the various study regions (p < 0.01). The pooled prevalence was 20.6% (95% CI, 0.0%–43.0%) in the Eastern Mediterranean, the highest of all regions, 17.5% (95% CI, 14.2%–20.8%) in the Americas, 13.3% (95% CI, 7.3%–19.4%) in the Western Pacific, and lowest in Europe (6.4%; 95% CI, 3.8%–8.9%). As with chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs, patients with hematologic malignancies had a significantly higher prevalence of ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs (25.5%, 95% CI 17.0%–34.0%) than patients with solid tumors (10.8%, 95% CI 8.5%–13.0%; p < 0.01). The prevalence of ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs did not differ significantly when stratified by income level, age, or sample size ( B and ).
Fifty-three studies reported data on the prevalence of >1 hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs and 49 of them (92.5%) were included in the pooled meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence was 17.9% (95% CI, 14.4%–21.4%), which differed significantly by age (p < 0.01). Prevalence was highest in pediatric patients (31.4%, 95% CI 16.3%–46.4%), followed by adolescents and young adults (22.5%, 95% CI 21.7%–23.4%), adults (18.1%, 95% CI 13.3%–22.9%), and was lowest in the elderly (13.7%, 95% CI 6.8%–20.5%) (p < 0.01). As for regional differences, the prevalence was highest in Europe (26.0%, 95% CI 14.4%–37.5%) and lowest in the Americas (13.1%, 95% CI 10.6%–15.6%) (p = 0.03). Results of the subgroup analysis showed no significant differences in the prevalence of >1 hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs according to income level, type of cancer, or sample size ( C and ).
Fifty-two out of 58 studies (89.7%) on the prevalence of >1 ER visit in the last 30 DOLs were eligible for inclusion in the pooled meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence from the random-effects meta-analysis was 14.8% (95% CI, 12.0%–17.6%). Subgroup analyses did not show significant differences regarding income level, region, age, type of cancer, or sample size ( D and ).
Twenty-four studies reported on the prevalence of hospice <3 days before death. Of these, five studies were excluded from meta-analyses as the denominator was the general population, rather than the population admitted into hospice care. All included 19 studies (79.2%) were conducted in high-income countries. The pooled prevalence was 14.4% (95% CI, 11.2%–17.6%) and patients with hematologic malignancies (26.7%, 95% CI 26.0%–27.3%) were more likely to have late hospice care enrolment than patients with solid tumors (14.2%, 95% CI 9.8%–18.7%) (p < 0.01). There were no significant differences in the prevalence according to region, age range, or sample size ( E and ).
Additionally, we investigated the prevalence of other commonly used aggressive care measures reported in at least five studies. A composite score implying the intensity of aggressive EOL care was reported in 36 studies, among which 31 (86.1%) were included in the pooled meta-analysis. More than half of enrolled patients in these studies (51.7%; 95% CI 43.9%–59.5%) used at least one of the aggressive care measures, which were defined differently across studies. Any use of chemotherapy in the last 30 DOLs was the most frequently reported validated indicator, with a high prevalence of 24.4% (95% CI 20.8%–28.0%). In addition, 12.5% (95% CI 7.8%–17.2%) of patients started a new chemotherapy regimen in the last 30DOLs. Half of the enrolled patients (50.3%; 95% CI 44.3%–56.4%) had at least one hospitalization in the last 30 DOLs and 37.6% (95% CI 28.0%–47.2%) experienced >14 inpatient hospital days in the last 30 DOLs. The prevalence of death in acute care hospital was as high as 40.5% (95% CI 34.6%–46.4%) ( and ). 
The sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs, >1 ER visit, ICU stay, or >1 hospital admission in the last 30 DOLs, and hospice care <3 days before death remained stable after removing one single study at a time ( and ), indicating that our findings were robust and not dependent on any single study. 

## Discussion
In this systematic review, we found that aggressive EOL care was common in patients with cancer. Additionally, prevalence estimates of aggressive EOL cancer varied by populations and regions. Data on the prevalence of aggressive care from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are severely lacking. There is also a substantial variation in measures of aggressive EOL care. Given the negative effect of aggressive EOL cancer care, our findings have important clinical and policy implications.
A previous systematic review indicated very frequent aggressive EOL care for patients with lung cancer, though there was no qualitative analysis. We quantitatively synthesized the prevalence of aggressive EOL care for all types of tumors worldwide and confirmed that aggressive care was a common practice, regardless of indicators used and regional variations. “Anticipating the EOL for patients with cancer is challenging due to the continuing potential and hope for cure and the often rapid pace of decline near death. Protecting patients from both overtreatment and undertreatment is equally important but challenging in clinical practices due to prognostic uncertainty, which makes it difficult for health care providers to determine when it is appropriate to transition patient from curative to EOL care. , Patients with a shorter survival were more likely to receive aggressive EOL care, compared with patients who experienced a better prognosis. However, the time between patients' diagnosis and death was rarely reported in individual studies of aggressive EOL care. In recent years, the importance of reducing aggressive care has been increasingly recognized. Since 2012, the NQF and CMS have used indicators such as chemotherapy use in the last 14 DOLs as key quality measures for clinical processes. , In addition, the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) stated in 2012 that palliative care shall be provided concurrently with standard oncology care. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) held a similar position and suggested that palliative care should be considered from the diagnosis of any advanced cancer onwards. Palliative care can alleviate some aspects of aggressive EOL care, however, if patients are to fully benefit from it, they need to be referred early in the disease trajectory and ideally upon the diagnosis of any incurable cancer. , , , , , , Despite being identified as a facilitator for the basic human right to death with dignity, access to palliative care for millions of individuals in need, especially in LMICs, continues to be limited or even absent. , Lack of available and accessible interventions that reduce aggressive care such as palliative care services may in turn exaggerates aggressive care near patients’ EOL, making it a vicious cycle.
As noted in our findings, the prevalence of aggressive care varied. We observed that patients with hematologic malignancies were more likely to receive chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs, have ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs, and have late referral to hospice care, consistent with previous studies, which directly compared the EOL care in patients with hematological malignancies and solid tumors. , , A previous study reported that adult patients with hematologic malignancies were more likely to have multiple ER visits, ICU admissions, and cancer treatments in the last weeks of life compared with patients with solid tumors, and that hematologic malignancy was a significant factor associated with aggressive EOL care (p < 0.0001). Pediatric patients with hematologic malignancies were more likely than those with solid tumors to have intubation and/or mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, hemodialysis, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in their last 30 days, or intravenous chemotherapy within their last 14 DOLs (all p < 0.01). This is likely due to that patients with hematologic malignancies are often treated with intensive antineoplastic regimens that may continue until the last days of life. In addition, most hematologic malignancies typically follow unpredictable remitting-relapsing pathways, making their curability difficult to predict. , Differences in attitudes and beliefs toward EOL care between oncology specialists in hematologic and solid tumors may also contribute to the higher prevalence of aggressive care among hematologic patients toward their EOL. 
Our results revealed that a much higher proportion of pediatric patients experienced chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs. A study conducted in Ontario, Canada showed that 40.6% of children experienced at least one measure of high intensity-EOL care, compared with 22.4% of adults in this region. This observed difference in EOL cancer care intensity may reflect the unique challenges associated with pediatric palliative care. Discussing death and dying with the patient's family can be difficult for pediatric oncologists. Predicting death in children also can be challenging. Moreover, treatment decisions for pediatric patients with cancer are often made by parents, who tend to pursue therapy even when they know that there is no realistic chance of a cure. 
We did not find subgroup differences by income status, which may be due to the small sample sizes and large variations in prevalence among studies in less economically developed regions. More than 90% of the included studies were conducted in high-income countries. We did not identify any study from low-income countries and studies from middle-income countries were also rare with limited sample size. This could be because countries with lower economic levels might have less awareness about the negative impact of aggressive EOL cancer care or have limited academic resources and research capacities. The inconsistent regional differences in the prevalence of aggressive care as defined by different measures need to be further confirmed and possible causes explored.
This synthesis shows the complexity and variability of quality indicators for EOL care, consistent with a previous systematic review. Among validated measures, , chemotherapy in the last 14 DOLs and ICU stay in the last 30 DOLs, the most commonly reported indicators, were analyzed in 87/129 studies and hospice care <3 days before death, the least commonly reported indicator, was analyzed in 19/129 studies. More than half of the studies used unvalidated indicators and studies that used a composite measure of aggressiveness had different definitions. Establishing and promoting standardized indicators for the aggressiveness of EOL care are essential to compare the quality of EOL care across different regions and to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions to reduce aggressive care. In addition, to improve quality of care, such measures must not only be feasible but also acceptable to relevant stakeholders. It is worth noting that the various definitions of aggressive care in our study come from a provider perspective, which may not be equally recognized by patients. Patients' perspectives on their care at the EOL need to be further studied.
Our study has the following strengths. First, our systematic review included 129 studies, which to our knowledge is the most comprehensive evaluation of the worldwide prevalence of aggressive care to date. Second, we evaluated the prevalence of all validated and commonly reported unvalidated indicators to provide a thorough understanding of the current practices of aggressive care. Third, we explored differences in claims-based rates of aggressive care across regions and demographics to investigate the possible sources of heterogeneity.
Our systematic review also has some limitations. First, heterogeneity between studies was high and, though we performed subgroup analyses to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, heterogeneity could only be explored in a limited set of groups due to restrictions by the design of the original studies. Meta-analyses of prevalence frequently yield high I2 values and larger I2 was observed in meta-analyses with a larger number of studies. Hence, I2 can be biased and it is not an absolute index for heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses showed that regions, age ranges, cancer types, and sample sizes may partially explain high levels of heterogeneity. In addition, results of the sensitivity analysis showed that our results were stable and not affected by any single study. Second, the included studies were from resource-rich countries, with well-developed palliative care systems, while studies from low-income countries were under-represented. Therefore, global prevalence should be interpreted with caution. Third, as aggressive care has been variably defined and measured, we could only derive the prevalence of each indicator and cannot report the prevalence of aggressive care as a whole, as can be done for other specific diseases. Fourth, cancer care has changed drastically over the last 20 years, possibly influencing the prevalence of aggressive care. However, we were unable to analyze temporal trends in the aggressive EOL care due to data limitation. Fifth, although we defined the minimum participants required for each subgroup in the subgroup analysis, the number of studies and sample sizes were unevenly distributed across each sub-group, which may affect the reliability of the results of the subgroup analysis. Therefore, results of subgroup analyses should be interpreted with sufficient caution. Sixth, our study only included indicators of aggressive care while the quality of EOL care encompasses more aspects, such as the availability of multidisciplinary care, adverse events, and continuity of care, which could not be assessed in this study and thus need further evaluation. Finally, we only included English-language reports, although it has been suggested that pooled estimates do not appear to be affected by language restrictions in meta-analyses. 
In conclusion, aggressive EOL care in patients with cancer has become a considerable global public health challenge, especially for pediatric patients and patients with hematologic tumors. The high prevalence of aggressive EOL care in patients with cancer also highlights the urgent need for targeted policies and interventions to reduce its prevalence and improve overall cancer care quality.
