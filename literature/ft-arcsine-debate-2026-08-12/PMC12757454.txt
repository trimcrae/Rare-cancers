## Abstract

## Background
Periprosthetic joint infection after total elbow arthroplasty (TEA) is associated with substantial morbidity and the need for revision surgery. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to summarize studies on 2-stage exchange and débridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) to determine differences in rates of recurrent infection, risk factors for recurrent infection, noninfectious complications, and functional outcomes.

## Methods
A systematic search was conducted of the MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane, Scopus, and Web of Science databases in March 2024 to identify studies on infected TEAs treated with either 2-stage revision surgery or DAIR. Exclusion criteria included duplicate publications, review articles, and studies with a mean follow-up of less than 1 year. Fourteen studies were included in the final analysis. Data abstracted included demographics, treatment modalities, bacterial speciation, rates of recurrent infection, and functional outcomes (range of motion data and Mayo Elbow Performance Score.

## Results
Two hundred ninety infected TEAs were included across all 14 studies, 131 treated with DAIR and 159 treated with 2-stage exchange. The overall pooled random-effects rate of recurrent infection was 34% (95% confidence interval: 21%-47%). The rate of recurrent infection was significantly lower following 2-stage exchange compared to DAIR (18% vs. 51%, P = .004). The overall pooled rate of noninfectious complications was 34% (95% confidence interval: 22%-46%). With the numbers available, the rate of noninfectious complications between 2-stage exchange and DAIR was smaller for 2-stage exchange, although the difference did not achieve statistical significance (27% vs. 43%, P = .244). Patients infected with coagulase-negative Staphylococci experienced a higher rate of recurrent infection than those infected with other species (44% vs. 14%), although again this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .060). In patients undergoing 2-stage exchange, incomplete cement removal was associated with a significantly higher rate of recurrent infection than complete removal (56% vs. 19%, P = .001), but intentional retention of 1 component was not significantly associated with a higher rate of recurrent infection (37% vs. 20%, P = .315).

## Discussion
The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis of the outcomes associated with the treatment of TEA periprosthetic joint infection with either DAIR or 2-stage reimplantation, 2-stage exchange was associated with a lower rate of recurrent infection. Incomplete cement removal was a risk factor for recurrent infection. Although it did not reach significance, it appeared that infection with coagulase-negative Staphylococci could also be a risk factor for failure of infection eradication.

The rate of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) following total elbow arthroplasty (TEA) remains persistently high relative to arthroplasty of the shoulder, hip, and knee. Some studies have estimated that PJI complicates 3%-12% of all elbow arthroplasties and account for approximately 25% of all revision surgeries. , , , The treatment of elbow PJI is further complicated by the difficulty surgical management of this complication: the risk of nerve injury is higher than in other joints due to the proximity of the ulnar and radial nerves, complete removal of cement is difficult to achieve, and strategies to manage bone loss and triceps insufficiency are commonly required. In addition, sometimes the diagnosis is difficult to establish preoperatively, since there is very limited information on the diagnostic yield of usual preoperative infection markers such as the results of aspiration or inflammatory markers. , 
Current recommendations for treatment strategies for elbow PJI are based largely on surgeon experience and data from studies on hip, knee, or shoulder arthroplasty, in addition to a few studies specifically devoted to the management of PJI after TEA. Many consider 2-stage reimplantation the present gold standard for treatment of elbow PJI. , However, many reports in the literature on the outcomes of 2-stage revision are small retrospective case series. , , , , , , , , , , Additionally, given the morbidity associated with removing well-fixed components, débridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) has been utilized in certain clinical situations. , , However, little is known about how outcomes compare between 2-stage revision and DAIR or what risk factors might be predictive of failure.
The aim of this study was to rigorously summarize data available in the current literature on 2-stage exchange and DAIR and to determine: (1) the rate of recurrent infection following treatment with either procedure, (2) risk factors for recurrent infection, (3) the rate of noninfectious postoperative complications, and (4) the functional outcomes of each procedure. We hypothesized a higher rate of recurrent infection following DAIR as compared to 2-stage exchange for elbow PJI.

## Materials and methods

## Search strategy
This review was performed using a protocol in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. , A systematic search of several databases on March 13, 2024, without language restrictions. The databases included Ovid MEDLINE(R) and Epub Ahead of Print, In-Process & Other Nonindexed Citations, and Daily, Ovid EMBASE, Ovid Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Ovid Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search strategy was designed and conducted by an experienced librarian with input from the study's principal investigator. Controlled vocabulary supplemented with keywords was used to search for studies of treatment strategies for infected TEA in humans. The actual strategy listing all search terms used and how they were combined is available in the appendix ( ).
Inclusion criteria included longitudinal observational studies or randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the use of 2-stage revision surgery or DAIR for infected TEA. Exclusion criteria were duplicate articles, review articles, and mean follow-up of less than 1 year. If studies had overlapping cohorts, the most recent study or the one with the most relevant information to this review was chosen. The initial search yielded 137 studies, 26 of which were excluded as duplicate or review articles ( ). The titles and abstracts of the remaining 111 studies were screened by 2 of the authors (A.K. and C.M.H.) using the systematic review tool, Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). A third reviewer (T.B.W.) resolved any disagreements and made final decisions regarding inclusion.
 Application of additional criteria resulted in the elimination of an additional 91 studies. Full text review was conducted of the remaining 20 studies in a manner as described above with application of the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 14 studies were selected for inclusion in the final analysis ( ). This included 6 studies which assessed 2-stage revision, , , , , , 4 studies which assessed DAIR, , , , and 4 studies which assessed both. , , , 
 

## Quality assessment
No RCTs were found in the systematic search, and as such, the quality of each included study was assessed using the Methodological Index for Nonrandomized Studies (MINORS) checklist. The MINORS checklist is a previously validated metric for evaluating the quality of nonrandomized studies which grades each study on a scale from 0-16 based on 8 factors for noncomparative studies and a scale of 0-24 based on 12 factors for comparative studies. The 13 noncomparative studies included in this review had an average MINORS score of 12, , , , , , , , , , , , , and the 1 comparative study had a MINORS score of 16. 

## Risk of bias assessment
Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger's tests. Neither 2-stage revision nor DAIR showed a significant result in Egger's test (P = .704 and P = .330, respectively). Funnel plots regarding recurrent infection rates are shown in for 2-stage revision and for DAIR. This same method was used to assess publication bias for complication rates. Again, no significant result was seen in Egger's test for 2-stage revision (P = .524) or DAIR (P = .696). Funnel plots for each are shown in and .

## Data extraction and statistical analysis
Data from each included study were extracted using an Excel spreadsheet template developed specifically for this study. Data collected included author, year of publication, number of patients, sex, mean age, mean follow-up, type of arthroplasty, reason for arthroplasty, treatment modality, laboratory and infecting organism details, noninfectious complications, and functional outcomes (range of motion and Mayo Elbow Performance Score [MEP]).
Data for this meta-analysis were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation due to the small proportions being assessed in the included studies. Studies which assessed both 2-stage revision and DAIR were split and analyzed as if they were 2 separate studies. Subgroup analysis of pooled prevalences was conducted to determine whether treatment modality affected rates of recurrent infection and complication rates. Additional subgroup analyses were conducted to determine the effect of bacterial species and prosthesis/cement removal on the rate of recurrent infection. Random-effects models were used in all pooled analyses to limit the effect of between-study heterogeneity. Postoperative functional outcomes were compared using t tests. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

## Results

## Patients
A total of 290 elbows were included across all studies in this review: 131 (45%) had undergone DAIR and 159 (55%) had undergone 2-stage revision. PJI had complicated 202 primary elbow arthroplasties and 66 revision elbow arthroplasties; the type of surgery (primary or revision) was not reported for 22 elbows. Patients in the DAIR cohort had a mean age of 59 years (range, 22-89 years), overall female predominance (62%, n = 81), and a mean reported follow-up of 51 months (range, 12-192 months). Patients in the 2-stage revision cohort had a mean age of 64 years (range, 35-89 years), a predominance of female patients (58%, n = 92), and a mean reported follow-up of 56 months (range, 6-189 months). The most common indication for the index elbow arthroplasty across all studies was inflammatory arthritis, followed by post-traumatic arthritis, osteoarthritis, acute fracture, and failed internal fixation.

## Baseline infection and laboratory characteristics
Infections were defined as acute (within 3 months), subacute (between 3 months and 1 year), and chronic (greater than 1 year). In the 9 studies which reported time to infection, there were 32 acute infections (18%), 34 subacute infections (19%), and 113 chronic infections (63%). Inflammatory markers were reported in 6 studies, revealing a mean white blood cell count of 9,800 cells/μL (range, cells/μL), mean erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 53 mm/hour (range, mm/hour), and mean C-reactive protein level of 54 mg/L (range, mg/L).
Staphylococcus aureus was responsible for most infections (39%) followed closely by coagulase-negative Staphylococci (36%). Cultures were polymicrobial in 6% and negative in 5% of the elbows ( )
 

## Overall rates of recurrent infection
There was a recurrence of infection in 103 of the 290 elbows included, yielding an overall pooled random-effects rate of recurrent infection of 34% (95% confidence interval (CI): 21%-47%). Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 73%, τ2 = 0.0458, P < .001) ( ).
 Of the 159 elbows which were treated with 2-stage revision, there was a recurrence of infection in 39 elbows. The pooled rate of recurrent infection was 18% (95% CI: 6%-32%) and heterogeneity was moderate to high (I2 = 59%, τ2 = 0.0263, P = .009). Of the 131 elbows treated with DAIR, there was a recurrence of infection in 64 elbows. The pooled rate of recurrent infection was 51% (95% CI: 35-66%) and heterogeneity was high (I2 = 67%, τ2 = 0.0303, P = .004). There was a significantly lower rate of recurrent infection for patients treated with 2-stage revision compared to those treated with DAIR (P = .004).

## Factors affecting rates of recurrent infection
The speciation of bacteria associated with infection were reported in 12 of 15 studies. Coagulase negative Staphylococci was most common (54%) followed by S. aureus (31%). Despite not reaching statistical significance (P = .060), the pooled rate of recurrent infection for patients infected with coagulase-negative Staphylococci was substantially higher, 44% (95% CI: 18%-72%) versus 14% (95% CI: 2%-30%) for patients infected with other bacterial species. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 50%, τ2 = 0.0448, P = .043) and moderate (I2 = 49%, τ2 = 0.0496, P = .003), respectively. The rate of recurrent infection for coagulase-negative Staphylococci did not differ significantly between 2-stage revision and DAIR (P = .359). Rates of recurrent infection for the other bacterial species were as follows: 16% (95% CI: 2%-37%) for Staphylococcal aureus, 10% (95% CI: 0%-36%) for polymicrobial infections, 18% (95% CI: 0%-83%) for culture negative infections, and 23% (95% CI: 0%-68%) for all others.
Data on complete versus incomplete prosthesis and cement removal during 2-stage revision were available in 8 of the 10 studies assessing 2-stage revision. The pooled rate of recurrent infection for elbows with removal of all components was 20% (95% CI: 5%-40%) versus 37% (95% CI: 14%-64%) for elbows with intentional retention of at least 1 component and exchange of at least 1 component. This difference was substantial but again it did not reach statistical significance (P = .315). There was no heterogeneity in either case (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = .620) and (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = .796), respectively. The pooled rate of recurrent infection for patients with complete cement removal during their 2-stage revision was 19% (95% CI: 10-30%) whereas it was 56% (95% CI: 38-74%) for patients with incomplete cement removal. This difference reached statistical significance (P = .001). Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 72%, τ2 = 0.0432, P < .001) and none (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = .556), respectively.
There were other risk factors investigated by individual studies, including patient smoking status, comorbid conditions (diabetes, osteoporosis), and number of elbow surgeries prior to infection. However, there was insufficient data for us to conduct a pooled analysis of these variables.

## Noninfectious complications
There were several noninfectious complications reported, including triceps weakness/insufficiency, ulnar/radial nerve complications, aseptic loosening, nonunion, and skin breakdown. Complications were reported by 11 of 15 studies. In total, 64 of 202 elbows had a complication after treatment. The overall pooled incidence was 34% (95% CI: 22-46%) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 64%, τ2 = 0.0293, P = .001) ( ).
 There were 37 complications reported in patients undergoing 2-stage revision, yielding a pooled complication rate of 27% (95% CI: 16%-39%) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 44%, τ2 = 0.0125, P = .097). There were 27 complications reported in patients undergoing DAIR, yielding a pooled complication rate of 43% (95% CI: 19%-69%) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 74%, τ2 = 0.0567, P = .004). There was no significant difference between the groups (P = .244).

## Functional outcomes
Preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes data are presented in . Preoperative range of motion data was available in 4 studies evaluating 2-stage revision and 2 studies evaluating DAIR. Preoperatively, patients in the 2-stage revision cohort had a significantly lower mean flexion–extension arc compared to patients in the DAIR group (65 ± 14° vs. 75 ± 8°, P < .001). Postoperative range of motion data were available in 8 studies evaluating 2-stage revision and 5 studies evaluating DAIR. Postoperatively, patients in the 2-stage revision cohort had a significantly greater mean flexion–extension arc compared to patients in the DAIR group (106 ± 13° vs. 94 ± 17°, P < .001). When only considering studies which provided both preoperative and postoperative range of motion data, patients in the 2-stage revision cohort had significantly greater improvement in their flexion–extension arc compared to patients in the DAIR group (44 ± 28° vs. 22 ± 16°, P < .001).
 The MEPS was not reported frequently in the studies included. Postoperative MEPS was available in 5 studies evaluating 2-stage revision and 2 studies evaluating DAIR. Patients undergoing 2-stage revision had significantly lower postoperative MEPS than patients undergoing DAIR (77 ± 4 points vs. 83 ± 6 points, P < .001).

## Discussion
The purpose of this study was to provide a contemporary systematic review and meta-analysis of the efficacy and outcomes of 2 of the most common treatment strategies for elbow PJI, namely DAIR and 2-stage exchange arthroplasty. We found a lower rate of recurrent infection following 2-stage exchange as compared to DAIR (18% vs. 51%, P = .004). In patients undergoing 2-stage exchange, incomplete cement removal was associated with a significantly higher rate of recurrent infection than complete removal (56% vs. 19%, P = .001), but intentional retention of one component was not found to be significantly associated with a higher rate of recurrent infection (37% vs. 20%, P = .315). Finally, infection with coagulase-negative Staphylococci was associated with a higher rate of recurrent infection than those infected with other species (44% vs. 14%) though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .060).
In 2018, Gutman et al conducted a systematic review of the clinical outcomes of DAIR, 1-stage exchange, 2-stage exchange, and resection arthroplasty for elbow PJI. Since that time, 4 additional studies on the outcomes of 2-stage exchange have been published, including the largest series in the literature by Martinez-Catalan et al. , , , This represents a 165% increase in the number of patients that underwent 2-stage exchange included in the present study. Gutman et al reported a recurrent infection rate for 2-stage exchange and DAIR of 18.8% and 44.2%, respectively, as compared to 18% and 51% in the present study. The similar rates of recurrent infection in the present study despite a large increase in the number of patients included suggests these numbers are broadly representative of the outcomes of these procedures across multiple institutions. Gutman et al did not comment on any specific risk factors for the failure of infection eradication following 2-stage exchange. 
Overall, S. aureus was responsible for most initial infections (39%) followed closely by coagulase-negative staphylococci (36%). However, patients infected with coagulase-negative Staphylococci had a higher rate of recurrent infection than those infected with other species (44% vs. 14%). Although this finding did not reach statistical significance (P = .060) with the numbers available for analysis, it may represent an important clinical relationship. Two previous studies have shown a higher rate of failure following 2-stage exchange in patients infected with coagulase-negative Staphylococci. , Given that certain pathogens may increase the risk of treatment failure, it is particularly important to make every effort to establish a responsible pathogen preoperatively to guide treatment. One study reported that aspiration had a low negative predictive value in the setting of elbow PJI. If the clinical picture is highly suggestive of infection, consideration may be given to obtaining arthroscopic or open biopsies to establish a diagnosis. 
At the time of revision surgery for PJI, infection eradication must be balanced with the morbidity of removing well fixed components and cement. In the present study, incomplete cement removal during 2-stage exchange was associated with a significantly higher rate of recurrent infection than complete removal (56% vs. 19%, P = .001), but intentional retention of a well-fixed component was not found to be significantly associated with a higher rate of recurrent infection (37% vs. 20%, P = .315). It has been well documented in the orthopedic literature that retained hardware in the setting of infection may provide a nidus for bacterial biofilm and is associated with an increased risk of treatment failure. , , This is the theoretical explanation for improved infection eradication with 2-stage revision as compared to DAIR. , , The finding of a higher rate of recurrent infection in the setting of incomplete cement removal may be explained along similar lines. Intentional retention of well-fixed components, on the other hand, was not found to be a significant risk factor for recurrent infection in the present study. This may be a type II error secondary to low numbers analyzed or may be explained by selection bias, whereby less severe infections were more likely to be considered for partial component retention. Further prospective studies are required to determine whether 2-stage revision with partial component retention is a risk factor for failure of infection eradication.
The MEPS was not reported frequently in the studies included (5 studies evaluating 2-stage revision and 2 studies evaluating DAIR), but there was a significantly lower postoperative MEPS in patients who underwent 2-stage revision compared to patients undergoing DAIR (77 ± 4 points vs. 83 ± 6 points, P < .001). It is unclear why the MEPS scores were significantly lower in the 2-stage exchange group despite a larger improvement in range of motion compared to the DAIR group. The larger improvement in range of motion may be explained by a more extensive débridement and a higher rate of infection eradication in the 2-stage exchange group. The lower MEPS score despite this greater improvement in range of motion may reflect the complex and varied experience of undergoing a complex 2-stage revision that is not fully captured by the MEPS score, including postoperative pain, time required for recovery, and the psychological trauma of undergoing an extended treatment for elbow PJI. The lower MEPS score following 2-stage exchange, despite superior infection eradication, underscores the significant clinical challenge presented by elbow PJI. In the present study, the overall pooled incidence for noninfectious complications was 34% (95% CI: 22%-46%). Unsurprisingly, many elbows with surviving implants but multiple complex surgeries fail to achieve satisfactory functional and patient-reported outcomes even when infection eradication is achieved.
This systematic review is limited by the overall small sample size and the retrospective nature of the studies included for analysis. Additionally, there was no standard definition of elbow PJI across the studies, and no uniform protocol even within treatment categories. There was also variable follow-up time across the studies, which could influence the reported recurrence rates. Because not all studies presented uniform data, there was a limited ability to make comparisons across studies. An example of this is that the criteria used to define infection recurrence and noninfectious complications across studies was not uniform or well-defined in all cases. Additionally, the overall level of evidence remains low (level IV) and as such, it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions from this data. However, this does represent the largest and most up-to-date systematic review and meta-analysis in the literature on the outcomes of the 2 most common treatment strategies for elbow PJI. In the future, comparative and prospective studies with standardized definitions of elbow PJI and treatment algorithms would be ideal to more accurately assess outcomes, risk factors for failure, and optimal treatment strategy.

## Conclusion
In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we found a lower rate of recurrent infection following 2-stage exchange as compared to DAIR (18% vs. 51%, P = .004). In patients undergoing 2-stage exchange, incomplete cement removal was associated with a significantly higher rate of recurrent infection than complete removal (56% vs. 19%, P = .001). Finally, infection with coagulase-negative Staphylococci was associated with a higher rate of recurrent infection than those infected with other species (44% vs. 14%) though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .060).
