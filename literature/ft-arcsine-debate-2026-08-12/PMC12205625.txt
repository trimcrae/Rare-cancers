Among these pathogens, the West Nile virus (WNV) became endemic in the region. This systematic review and meta-analysis study summarizes WNV epidemiology, molecular characteristics, and surveillance in various MB countries, relying on data and scientific articles on WNV published during 2010–2023. The number of published articles varied across the three MB subregions: Southern Europe (n = 153), North Africa (n = 33), and Western Asia (n = 14). During this period, 5765 WNV cases in humans were documented across 19 MB countries with peaks during 2018–2022, mostly in Italy and Greece. The latter had neurological disease in 70.3% of 1868 cases, and a high case fatality rate (13.7%). Fewer WNV cases were reported in North Africa despite having the highest pooled human seroprevalence (15.4%), suggesting potential underdiagnosis and cryptic transmission. Overall, 6.5% out of 160,775 individuals had WNV-IgG antibodies in their sera, and WNV-neutralizing antibodies (NA) were detected in ∼50% of 731 IgG-seropositive individuals, indicating co-circulation of other flaviviruses in the MB. In horses, 698 confirmed cases were reported across 14 MB countries. Compared to humans, horses had a higher pooled IgG (26.0%) and NA (71.8%) prevalence, but a lower case fatality rate (6.1%). Wild birds, the key amplifying hosts, displayed a pooled IgG seroprevalence of 11.9%. WNV-RNA was detected in 4.5% of 3040 dead or neurologically diseased wild birds. Entomological surveillance revealed WNV-RNA in 2.5% of 52,159 mosquito pools tested, mostly Culex pipiens. WNV lineages 1 and 2 co-circulate, with Lineage 2 being dominant in Southern Europe. Surveillance capabilities vary across subregions, with robust integrated strategies in Southern Europe and limited efforts in North Africa and Western Asia. Since no vaccines are available for humans, integrated One Health approaches are crucial for effective surveillance and early warning.

## Introduction
West Nile virus (WNV) belongs to the family Flaviviridae and genus Flavivirus. The virus was first isolated from blood sample of a febrile woman in the West Nile district of Uganda in 1937, and has spread worldwide since then ( ; ). WNV typically circulates in a mosquito-bird enzootic cycle, and the migratory birds have a major role in the introduction of this virus into new regions ( ). Humans and equines are infected through the bite of an infected female mosquito, particularly of the genus Culex (e.g. Culex pipiens) ( ). However, humans and equines cannot transmit the virus due to insufficient viremia, and therefore, they are considered dead-end hosts ( ). In humans, the virus can also be transmitted via blood transfusion as well as tissue and organ transplantation ( ; ).
Around 80% of WNV-infected humans remain asymptomatic. Conversely, 20% develop WNV febrile syndrome, and less than 1% develop severe to fatal neurological manifestations (West Nile neuroinvasive disease, WNND), including acute flaccid paralysis, encephalitis, meningitis, meningoencephalitis, or some combination of these ( ). Currently, there is no WNV vaccine for humans; therefore, preventing WNV transmission requires sustained vector control and the use of personal protective measures. In animals, the virus can cause severe illness and death in horses ( ).
West Nile virus was first detected in the Mediterranean Basin (MB) 13 years after its discovery in Uganda. In 1950, the virus was isolated from blood of 23 febrile children in Egypt. Sporadic WNV cases were also detected in birds, mosquitoes, and equines from Egypt in the 1950s ( ; ; ). In the same period, the virus was isolated in 1951 from a febrile child near Haifa, Israel ( ). On the other side of the Mediterranean, France was the first Southern European country to report WNV cases in 1962 ( ). The first cases of WNV that occurred in different hosts from various countries in the MB are summarized in : .
In the 1990s, regular WNV outbreaks occurred in the MB, and the virus has expanded its area of circulation in many MB countries. The outbreaks were attributed to WNV lineages 1 and 2, and the occurrence of both lineages varied across different countries. In the last 20 years, several MB countries have witnessed large WNV outbreaks in humans and animals, particularly in 2018 and 2022 ( , ) and more recently in 2024 ( ; Israeli Ministry of Health, https://www.gov.il/en/pages/disease-west-nile-fever?chapterIndex=8, accessed 14-Sep-2024). This suggests strong spatio-temporal heterogeneities in the epidemiology of WNV over the past two decades, attributed to transport and climatic changes that significantly affect the life cycle and dispersal of avian and mosquito species ( ).
Many reviews have been published to describe WNV infections in the MB; however, these reviews have focused on limited geographical subregions in the MB, for example, Southern Europe ( ; ; ; ; ). The present study aimed to provide a holistic overview of WNV for the MB in terms of epidemiology, molecular epidemiology, and surveillance of WNV during the last 15 years. The study would contribute to a better understanding of various WNV transmission patterns, especially during its emergence and establishment, and update the strategies to control this serious viral zoonosis throughout the Mediterranean Basin.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Search strategy
The Mediterranean is a semi-enclosed sea that covers around 2.5 million km2 and is surrounded by Africa, Asia, and Europe. Geographically, the MB is divided into three main subregions comprising 26 countries; North Africa (Egypt, Algeria, Libya, Morocco, and Tunisia), Western Asia (Iraq, Israel, Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, and Palestine), and Southern Europe (Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Cyprus, France, Greece, Italy, Malta, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Portugal, San Marino, Slovenia, Spain, and Turkey) ( : ). The Mediterranean climate is generally characterized by warm to hot dry summers and mild wet winters, although marked differences exist between the three subregions ( ).
This systematic review was conducted in line with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) ( ) (see ). Various international databases including PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science were systematically searched to collect peer-reviewed research articles published on West Nile virus infections in the MB. To support our search for peer-reviewed articles and identify potentially relevant grey literature on WNV, Google Scholar was also consulted. An initial search was conducted during January-April 2024 and repeated during May-August 2024 to ensure the collection of all relevant articles. Various keywords were used during the search, and all are listed in : . These keywords included: (i) those describing the virus and pathologies it causes (West Nile virus; West Nile fever, West Nile neuroinvasive disease; West Nile encephalitis); (ii) those related to the hosts (animals, humans, equines, birds, vectors, and mosquitoes); (iii) those related the geographical region/subregion (Mediterranean Basin, North Africa, Western Asia, Southern Europe, and each of the previously mentioned countries); and (iv) those related to the scope of this review (epidemiology, molecular epidemiology, seroprevalence, confirmed cases, and surveillance). These highly diversified keywords were used in various combinations using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” ( : ).
The following databases of arboviruses surveillance networks and those of the national (when publicly available) and international health organizations were included: European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC, https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en), World Health Organization (WHO, https://www.who.int/), World Health Organization Eastern Mediterranean Region (WHO-EMR, https://www.emro.who.int/index.html), World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH, https://www.woah.org/en/home/), Network for communicable disease control in Southern Europe and Mediterranean countries (EpiSouth, https://www.episouth.org/home.html), Greek National Public Health Organization (NPHO, https://eody.gov.gr/en/npho/), Italian National Institute of Health (EpiCentro-Epidemiology for Public Health, https://www.epicentro.iss.it/en/), and Israeli Ministry of Health (https://www.gov.il/en/departments/ministry_of_health/govil-landing-page). From these, the number of WNV-confirmed cases in humans, equines, and birds was extracted and compared against those reported in the published articles.

2.2

## Eligibility criteria and data extraction
The collected peer-reviewed articles were subject to two screening rounds: title/abstract and full text screening. Both rounds were conducted independently by two of the authors (IA and FA), and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus and consulting a third author (LS). Eligible studies for inclusion in this review were: (i) studies presenting quantitative data on confirmed WNV cases or detection of WNV antibodies/RNA in humans, birds and other animals, and various vectors; (ii) studies describing various ecological factors associated with WNV occurrence in any MB country; (iii) studies evaluating WNV surveillance programmes in any MB country; (iv) studies describing the molecular characters of WNV isolates from humans, animals, birds, or vectors; (v) articles published in English; (vi) articles published between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2023; and (vii) research articles. 2010 is considered a key year for WNV in MB, due to the sustainability of the virus circulation and the establishment of endemic status in various areas of the MB in the subsequent years. The following articles were considered ineligible and thus excluded: (i) articles of non-original contributions (e.g. review articles, book chapters, and seminars); (ii) articles published before 2010; (iii) articles published in languages other than English; (iv) experimental studies; and (v) articles with inadequate methodologies. Data from the eligible studies were independently extracted and organized in Microsoft Excel® spreadsheets. The following information was extracted from the eligible studies whenever possible: authors, publication year, country, sampling year, type of samples, host, host population, number of tested individuals, number of positive individuals, test conducted, and lineages detected.

2.3

## Data analysis
Data describing WNV antibodies/RNA prevalence in different hosts ( ) were subjected to various meta-analyses using the software Open Meta [Analyst] ( ). All analyses were conducted based on a 95% confidence interval. The seroprevalence for WNV was calculated as “a pooled estimate” using the random-effects model coupled with the DerSimonian-Laird method ( ). The heterogeneity between studies was estimated based on the I2 statistic. The heterogeneity values were considered high when I2 exceeded 50% ( ). Subgroup analyses were conducted to detect the source of heterogeneity and included prevalence variation based on the test used, host population, and the surveyed subregion/country. The publication bias across the included studies was assessed via visual inspection of funnel plots generated using the online statistical tool “METAANALYSISONLINE” based on Freeman-Turkey double arcsine-transformed proportion (https://metaanalysisonline.com). Testing the significance of variations throughout the collected data was conducted using the software GraphPad Prism (https://www.graphpad.com/) employing Fisher’s exact test for variables 2 × 2 or the chi-square test for variables 2 × k. Results were considered statistically significant when the P-value was < 0.05. The scientific image and illustration software BioRender (https://www.biorender.com/) was used to produce the figures included in the present review.

## Results and discussion
3.1

## Eligible studies
A total of 201 studies were found eligible ( ). The majority (n = 154) of these studies came from the Southern European subregion, particularly from Italy (n = 45), Greece (n = 31), Spain (n = 30), and Turkey (n = 26). A few reports came from other countries in this subregion, including Cyprus (n = 6), Croatia (n = 6), France (n = 4), Portugal (n = 3), Slovenia (n = 1), and Bosnia (n = 1). North Africa (n = 33) ranked second in the number of eligible studies, and most reports from this subregion were from Tunisia (n = 14), followed by Morocco (n = 6), Egypt (n = 6), Algeria (n = 5), and Libya (n = 2). The lowest number (n = 14) of eligible studies came from Western Asia: Israel (n = 8), Jordan (n = 2), Iraq (n = 1), Lebanon (n = 1), and Palestine (n = 2). Out of the 201 studies, 45 described WNV infections in humans, 96 in animals/birds, and 49 in vectors. : summarize characteristics of the eligible studies included in the present review. 

3.2

## WNV epidemiology in the Mediterranean Basin
3.2.1

## WNV confirmed cases
A total of 62 published articles documented WNV-confirmed cases in humans, animals, and birds across various MB countries, with 55 originating from Southern Europe ( : ). This geographical bias in reporting is likely to reflect the greater inequalities in surveillance infrastructure and research activities, rather than the actual spread of the virus.
3.2.1.1

## Humans
According to various regional health organizations (e.g. ECDC, EpiSouth, and WHO-EMR), 5765 WNV cases were reported from 19 MB countries between 2010 and 2023 ( : ). This number substantially exceeds the 3060 cases described in peer-reviewed publications relative to 10 MB countries ( : ).
The majority of MB human cases were reported from Southern Europe (n = 4546), equating to 1.54 WNV cases per 100,000 residents. Italy (n = 2280 cases) and Greece (n = 1868 cases) were the most affected countries in the subregion, pointing to persistent local foci and recurrent transmission cycles. From Western Asia, 861 cases (0.85 cases per 100,000 residents) were reported, with Israel alone accounting for 854 cases. Although most of the cases were detected in Israel, this figure confirms the challenges in the surveillance of WNV in endemic countries ( : ), especially with limited resources available ( ). Similarly, only 192 cases (0.09 cases per 100,000 residents) were reported from the North African countries, with 190 in Tunisia ( : ).
In Egypt, only one WNV case was reported in humans in the last 15 years; other sero-surveys indicated high WNV exposure among humans (24.0%) and equines (32.0%) ( ; ). In 2014, WNV was also diagnosed in a UK traveler returning from Egypt (https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/staging/thp/fact-single.php?new=47&title=west-nile-virus, accessed 10-Aug-2024). This suggests WNV cryptic transmission in countries with limited reports ( ).
The spatial distribution of WNV cases reveals the presence of established hotspots associated with episodic expansion. In Italy, WNV infections occurred regularly in the valley of Po River, a well-documented area for bird reservoirs and mosquito vectors suitability ( ). Since the first outbreak in 2010 in Central Macedonia in Greece, outbreaks have emerged southward and eastward, followed by transmission establishment ( ) due to a suitable ecological environment, including the presence of competent vectors and avian hosts ( ).
Over time WNV human cases peaked during 2018 (1277 cases), 2022 (1054 cases), and 2023 (624 cases), supported by favorable climatic conditions and the extension of WNV transmission season ( ; ; ; ) ( : ). In Italy, WNV was detected in 2018 at least two weeks before the observed circulation in 2013–2017 ( ). In 2023, WNV detection started on May 30 and extended to November 5 ( ).
As is typical of emerging diseases and in the presence of strong immunity cycles ( ), WNV alternated periods of quiescence (e.g. Greece, where no cases were reported during 2015 and 2016) to others with large outbreaks ( ). In Europe, it is possible that WNV has circulated silently for months, or even years, before spillover events occured ( ). The spillover is usually associated with one or more bird species involved in the WNV amplification chain. Following the events of spillover and outbreak, a subsidence phase may occur as a consequence of the rise of herd immunity in the reservoir birds ( ). Countries with relatively stable transmission can be considered as sentinel areas for early detection and regional forecasting of WNV activity ( : ).
Most of the WNV-associated deaths occur in elderly patients. Between 2010 and 2023, 70.3% (1314/1868) of confirmed cases in Greece had WNND, including 157 that died, giving rise to a case fatality rate of 13.7% ( : ). These rates are notably higher than those averaged across all published articles in the region: 60.5% (1838/3036) of confirmed cases and 8.2% (254/3036) fatality rate. Globally, WNV case fatality rate ranges between 3 and 15% ( ), depending on age, comorbidities, and healthcare accessibility ( ). These disparities are associated with demographic and structural factors, although their full understanding is hampered by the absence of population-based case-control studies ( ).

3.2.1.2

## Equines
Between 2010 and 2023, 367 confirmed WNV cases in horses were reported in the literature across seven MB countries, including 41 deaths, corresponding to a case fatality rate of 11.2% ( : ). However, data from the WOAH reveal a considerably higher number (698 cases) reported across 14 MB countries during the same period ( : ). These differences underline the underreporting of horse WNV cases in peer-reviewed publications, especially in countries with less robust veterinary public health systems ( ). This is also confirmed for the case fatality rate: out of the 698 cases, 43 deaths occurred, equating to a case fatality rate of 6.1%, a low estimated value compared to the global average (29.0–34.0%) ( ).
Similar to the epidemiology in humans, Italy (314 cases) and Greece (162 cases) had the highest numbers of WNV-reported equine cases during 2019–2023 ( : ). The geographical overlap between equine and human cases in these countries confirms the significance of horses as sentinels for WNV circulation ( ). It is worth mentioning that some countries with no confirmed WNV cases in humans had WNV cases in equines during the study period, e.g. Libya and San Marino. Conversely, certain countries with human cases had no equine WNV cases, e.g. Palestine, Syria, and Slovenia ( ). These mismatches suggest considerable variability in WNV surveillance strategies and reporting systems across the MB, stressing the need for enhanced and integrated surveillance across species. 

3.2.1.3

## Birds
Only a few reports described WNV cases that occurred in wild birds from MB countries, reflecting that WNV surveillance in wild birds across the MB remains weak. Raptors, particularly the northern goshawk, frequently exhibit neurological symptoms accompanied by death when infected. In total, 177 infected birds were documented across five countries (Israel, Spain, Italy, Croatia, and Turkey). Strikingly, the majority (n = 151) of these cases were detected in Italy ( : ).
The limited detection of WNV in wild birds from other MB countries indicates a lack of regular ornithological surveillance rather than the true absence of viral dissemination. Wild birds are both amplifying hosts and indicators of WNV enzootic transmission; therefore, improving avian surveillance, especially in migratory corridors and wetland areas, would improve early warning systems and contribute to a better understanding of WNV eco-epidemiology in the region ( ).

3.2.2

## WNV seroprevalence in humans and animals from the Mediterranean Basin countries
During 2010–2023, a total of 198 datasets published in 116 peer-reviewed studies tested the presence of WNV antibodies or RNA in samples from humans (73 datasets), equines (76 datasets), and wild birds (49 datasets) across various MB countries. The funnel plot analysis generated based on these studies suggested a potential publication bias ( : ) due to the lack of publications about negative or low WNV seroprevalence in different hosts from various MB regions.
3.2.2.1

## Humans
Specific antibody (IgG/IgM) detection is the most widely used approach to diagnose human WNV infections ( ). WNV IgM antibodies appear 3–8 days after illness onset and commonly persist for 30–90 days. WNV IgG antibodies appear shortly after IgM antibodies (4–16 days post-infection) but can persist for a longer time (up to 500 days) ( ). These patterns of antibody dynamics are source of diagnostic uncertainty since the presence of WNV-specific IgM does not necessarily indicate a recent infection, and a negative WNV IgM result in samples taken before the 8th day post-infection does not rule out a WNV infection. In addition, the appearance of IgM antibodies can also be delayed in WNV patients with previous exposure to the Usutu virus (USUV) ( ).
In MB countries, 44 eligible serosurveys were conducted to evaluate WNV occurrence in humans, and these surveys frequently employed the capture enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA), targeting antibodies directed against the virus envelope protein. A total of 35 datasets screened WNV IgG antibodies in sera of individuals from various groups (e.g. general population, blood donors, and hospitalized patients) in MB countries ( ). In total, 160,775 individuals were tested between 2010 and 2023, and 3068 were found positive, corresponding to an estimated pooled prevalence of 6.5% (95% CI: 5.6–7.4%) ( ; : ). Variations among the three Mediterranean subregions were statistically significant (χ2 = 21126, df = 2, P < 0.0001). The North African countries had the highest pooled prevalence (15.4%, 95% CI: 6.7–24.2%), despite having the fewest confirmed WNV cases among humans ( and : ). This discrepancy confirms underdiagnosing or underreporting in this subregion. Results of a recent report by support this assumption. Out of 316 hospitalized WNV-suspected patients from various parts in Tunisia, 91 had WNV IgM antibodies, 225 had IgG antibodies, and 29 had WNV RNA. This underlines the importance of establishing integrated systems and central facilities for WNV surveillance in the North African countries. Southern Europe displayed the lowest pooled IgG-seroprevalence (2.7%, 95% CI: 2.1–3.2%), although accounting for the highest number of reported WNV cases in MB ( and : ). This disparity is partly explained by IgM testing results; 5761 persons from this subregion had an estimated IgM pooled prevalence of 5.8% (95% CI: 3.7–7.8%), assuming that surveillance in Southern Europe focuses on symptomatic WNV cases ( ). In Western Asia, 7426 were tested, and 783 had WNV-IgG antibodies corresponding to a pooled prevalence of 10.6% (95% CI: 8.3–13.0%), indicating moderate transmission intensity ( ). Western Asia may act as a transitory zone between Africa and Europe ( ). The genus Flavivirus includes various species (e.g. WNV and USUV), which may cross-react and affect the specificities of the serological testing. Various antibody neutralization assays, e.g. plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) and virus neutralization test (VNT), were developed and have shown high specificity and accuracy in the serological identification of flaviviruses ( ). Twelve datasets from various MB countries tested sera from 753 IgG-seropositive individuals; 331 had WNV neutralizing antibodies in high titers (> 1:20), with an estimated pooled prevalence of 49.4% (95% CI: 36.2–62.7%), see . This indicates that almost half of the IgG-seropositive individuals had a prior WNV infection, while the remaining half might be subjected to the other co-circulating flaviviruses. 
Routine testing for WNV of blood donors during the transmission season is an important surveillance and control strategy to prevent human-to-human transmission. Testing sera from 137,135 blood donors from three Southern European countries (Italy, Cyprus, and Turkey) revealed 1.8% (95% CI: 1.2–2.4%) IgG pooled prevalence. Sera of blood donors are regularly tested during the transmission season within integrated WNV surveillance systems in several European countries. In Italy, RT-PCR testing of 194,270 blood samples revealed occurrence of WNV RNA in 18 cases ( ). In Tunisia, a single study found IgG antibodies in 148 out of 844 (17.5%) blood donors, of them 46 (31.1%) also tested positive for WNV neutralization assays. This highlights the endemic situation of flaviviruses in Tunisia and strongly suggests circulation of several flaviviruses in the country. In fact, a recent survey documented circulation of WNV, USUV, and Sindbis virus in mosquitoes and horses ( ). 

3.2.2.2

## Equines
The present meta-analysis incorporated 43 studies comprising 76 datasets on occurrence of WNV antibodies in equines across MB countries. Between 2010 and 2023, a total of 24,645 horses from various MB countries were WNV IgG-tested, and the estimated pooled prevalence was 26.0% (95% CI: 21.6–30.4%) ( ). Prevalence differences between the three Mediterranean subregions were statistically significant (χ2 = 1143, df = 2, P < 0.0001). Horses from Western Asia had the highest pooled prevalence (46.9%, 95% CI: 16.0–77.7%), followed by North Africa (30.0%, 95% CI: 19.9–40.0%) ( , : ). This constitutes further proof for endemic circulation of the virus in these subregions supported by the favorable ecological conditions for the avian reservoir hosts and the vectors ( ). Notably, horses from Southern Europe had the lowest estimated pooled prevalence (17.9%, 95% CI: 14.1–21.6%) ( , : ); although doubling that estimated in the period 2001–2018 ( ), suggesting the increasing endemicity or intensified transmission of WNV in Southern Europe ( ). The consistency of neutralizing antibody detection conducted on horses with high titers in 1467 out of 1851 tested horses confirm our interpretation (71.8%, 95% CI: 63.0–80.6%) ( ). Data from recent outbreak emphasize the importance of serological surveillance. For example, compared WNV-IgG occurrence in vaccinated against unvaccinated horses during the 2020 outbreak in Spain. Vaccinated horses (207/239) had a higher statistically significant (P < 0.0001, Fisher’s exact test, two-sided) prevalence than unvaccinated ones (300/485). In addition, WNV-IgM antibodies were detected in 8 unvaccinated animals. It is worth mentioning that horses can be vaccinated against WNV, and the vaccines are effective and can generate high levels of protective immunity ( ). 
In North Africa and West Asia, 1723 donkeys across five countries (Egypt, Algeria, Tunisia, Palestine, and Turkey) were screened: 327 had WNV IgG antibodies with an estimated pooled prevalence of 19.0% (95% CI: 10.2–27.8%) ( ), a slightly lower prevalence (P = 0.0012, Fisher’s exact test, two-sided) than that detected for horses (26.6%, 95% CI: 18.8–34.4%; 1142/5022) in the same five countries ( : ). Despite this difference, donkeys may have a potential role as sentinels for the WNV ( ). This is particularly important in countries such as Egypt, where the donkey population is remarkably larger than that of horses. A few clinical WNV cases were reported from donkeys worldwide, and the only WNND case detected in MB countries was in France ( ).

3.2.2.3

## Birds
By combining the published data from the MB, it was found that IgG sero-testing on 6123 wild birds of different species between 2010 and 2023 returned 732 IgG positives, corresponding to a pooled prevalence of 11.9% (95% CI: 8.4–15.5%) ( , : ). Similar prevalence (11.4%) was detected in 1304 ELISA-tested wild birds caught between 2018 and 2019 in Romania, a highly WNV-endemic country ( ). This Romanian study detected specific WNV neutralizing antibodies in 182 (81.3%) out of 224 IgG-positive or doubtful sera. However, in the MB, the present analysis found WNV neutralizing antibodies in 213 (49.2%, 95% CI: 30.0–68.4%) out of 535 IgG-positive wild birds ( ), a rate comparable to that estimated for humans, but lower than that estimated for horses (71.8%). This difference between the 3 host species was statistically significant (χ2 = 1650, df = 2, P < 0.0001), due to differences in host susceptibility and immunological response. Data from the MB also revealed occurrence of WNV neutralizing antibodies in 325 (6.1%, 95% CI: 3.3–9.0%) out of 5972 randomly-collected sera from wild birds in Southern Europe ( ). Detection of WNV antibodies in apparently healthy birds suggests either asymptomatic infection or recovery from a non-fatal infection ( ). 
On the other hand, WNV is usually detected in tissues of wild birds found dead or moribund through wildlife surveillance programmes. In the MB, WNV RNA was detected in tissues of 137 out of 3040 dead or neurologically-diseased wild birds, with a pooled prevalence of 3.3% (95% CI: 0.5–6.0%). As expected, this prevalence was significantly higher (P < 0.0001, Fisher’s exact test, two-sided) than that estimated for randomly sampled wild birds (1.9%, 95% CI: 1.1–2.7%) ( , : ), emphasizing the importance of passive surveillance in identifying acutely infected birds. Comparative data from northern Europe reflect great geographic and temporal disparity in both live and dead wild birds ( ; ).

3.2.3

## WNV testing in the vectors
Mosquitoes of the genus Culex, particularly Culex pipiens, are the principal WNV vectors ( ). Between 2010 and 2023, 50 reports were published detecting WNV RNA in mosquito pools in various MB countries. The majority of the reports are from Southern Europe (n = 42), fewer from North Africa (n = 7), and only one from Western Asia (n = 1). Six reports from Southern Europe were not used to calculate WNV RNA pooled prevalence because they did not mention the total number of vectors tested. In total, 52,159 mosquito pools were tested from various countries in the MB in 2010–2023, and 1282 had WNV RNA with a pooled prevalence of 1.9% (95% CI: 1.5–2.2%) ( ). 
There were statistically significant variations in mosquito infections between the three regions: Southern Europe (1.7%), North Africa (3.1%), and Western Asia (4.7%) ( ) (χ2 = 170.0, df = 2, P < 0.001). Vector infections do not often correlate with the occurrence of WNV cases in humans and animals. Italy, while being the country with the largest number of WNV cases reported in humans and equines during the last 15 years, occurrence of WNV RNA in mosquito pools from this country was limited (0.9%, 95% CI: 0.5–1.2%), probably because of a dilution effect due to large passive surveillance (26,192 of mosquito pools tested). In addition, entomological and epidemiological data from Greece revealed that although WNV infections in mosquitoes greatly varied among different years, there was no relationship between the number of human cases detected in a particular year and the WNV infection rates in mosquitoes. For example, no human cases were detected in 2015 and 2016; nonetheless, high WNV infection rates (7.0 and 9.4%, respectively) were detected in mosquitoes. In 2022, 262 human cases were detected, while the infection in mosquitoes was 5.9%, whereas in the previous year only 59 human cases were detected, but a higher infection rate (9.5%) was found in mosquitoes ( ).
In North Africa and Western Asia, WNV transmission was through Cx. pipiens and Culex perexiguus, while in Southern Europe through a number of mosquito species (Cx. pipiens, Cx. perexiguus, Culex modestus, Ochlerotatus caspius, Aedes caspius, and Aedes albopictus) with Cx. pipiens as the principal WNV vector ( ). Interestingly, Italy and Greece had the highest WNV vector diversity, a potential factor explaining why WNV is highly endemic in humans and animals from both countries. 

3.3

## WNV molecular epidemiology in the Mediterranean Basin
Molecular characterization of WNV isolates is instrumental to identifying various WNV transmission patterns, the possible sources of human infections, and therefore designing optimal surveillance and control. In the MB, molecular data are unevenly distributed, with only 11 out of 26 countries reporting genetic data on WNV isolates. The countries include three in North Africa (Algeria, Egypt, and Tunisia), one in Western Asia (Israel), and seven in Southern Europe (Croatia, Cyprus, Greece, Italy, Slovenia, Spain, and Turkey). Data from the remaining 15 Mediterranean countries are missing, which represents a significant surveillance gap.
The tested isolates from the eleven countries belonged mostly to WNV-infected vectors and humans (particularly clinical WNND cases), and a few isolates came from infected horses and wild birds. Notably, there were great inter-country variations considering the numbers of the tested isolates, where a few isolates were tested from Algeria, Egypt, Cyprus, Croatia, and Slovenia, which limits lineage tracking and phylogeographic comparisons throughout the MB.
WNV lineages 1 (L1) and 2 (L2) were found co-circulating in the MB over the past 14 years (2010–2023). Lineage distribution and persistence differ significantly between the subregions. According to studies from North Africa, Lineage 1, particularly Clade 1a, was the only one circulating in this subregion, which suggests either limited WNV diversity or failure of detection of other lineages due to constrained surveillance capability. This was also the case in Western Asia, although reports from this subregion were only available from Israel, where the Mediterranean cluster and Eastern European clusters of L1a were detected during 2010–2018. Lineage 2 was detected in mosquito pools once in 2010 ( ), with no evidence of persistent transmission ( ). 
The situation is different in Southern Europe, where L1 and L2 were circulating with different spatiotemporal scenarios. For example, in Italy, L1a (Western Mediterranean cluster) was responsible for the first WNV outbreak in 1998, which occurred in horses, and also for the large 2008 outbreak in humans. L2 emerged in 2011, rapidly became the dominant lineage with strains (e.g. Nea Santa Greece, 2010 strain and Hungarian strain) belonging to various clades (e.g. Central European and Eastern European clades) detected in the subsequent years. L1 re-emerged in 2022 in Italy; however, L2 continued to be dominant in this country. In Spain, L1 was also associated with the reported WNV cases from 2004 till 2017. L2 had a delayed introduction (in 2017) compared to Italy. L2 (Central European Clade) was first isolated from a neurologically diseased goshawk in Catalonia, Spain ( ), and subsequently isolated from humans and birds. Currently, in Spain, both L1 and L2 co-occur, with the former being the dominant one.
Although Greece and Turkey are neighboring countries, they had different patterns of lineage distribution during the same year (2010) outbreaks. In Greece, only L2 (Nea Santa Greece, 2010 strain) circulated and was regularly reported in the period 2010–2022. Contrastingly, L1 (Clade 1a) was the only one circulating in Turkey, and remained endemic during 2011–2016. This geographical segregation of lineages, although within shared geographical borders and bird migration pathways, suggests the effect of host, vector, and local ecological factors on WNV molecular dynamics. Additionally, the permanent circulation of a certain lineage in a country suggests the ability of this lineage to overwinter, and the virus is likely to be reactivated each year via previously infected mosquitoes ( ), challenging the fact that WNV is an absolutely seasonal virus ( ). A report from Spain supports this assumption ( ). The authors documented WNV L2 infection in a neurologically diseased female goshawk in January. In addition, L1a was isolated from WNND cases in horses and humans from Anatolia, Turkey, during February and March. The reason for WNV overwintering may be related to the rise of the median winter temperatures to levels permissive for vector survival and viral persistence ( ).
Other countries showed similarities during the same seasons. In 2018, for example, L2 was isolated from wild birds, horses, and humans in three neighboring countries, i.e. Italy, Slovenia, and Croatia, indicating regional spread. WNV L1a was also detected in mosquitoes and humans in Turkey and Cyprus in 2016. The only L2 case reported in Turkey was phylogenetically related to L2 Greek isolates identified in horses from a province bordering Greece ( ).
The global circulation of WNV lineages has been associated with bird migration ( ). The MB lies along major bird migratory flyways worldwide (Black Sea-Mediterranean and West Asia-East Africa flyways), which may be responsible for introduction of new WNV strains to various MB countries. The importance of flyways for the diffusion of WNV and its lineages is not well understood, primarily because the limited number of studies conducted where the flyways pass (see, for example, in Egypt).
Overall, the eco-epidemiology of WNV in the MB is influenced by a complex interaction of viral genetics, host and vector ecology, climate fluctuation, and migratory bird movements. However, the complete picture is hidden by inconsistent molecular surveillance and underreporting in numerous high-risk countries. To increase our understanding on WNV transmission and guide successful regional control efforts, coordinated, cross-border genomic surveillance is critical, particularly along significant migratory corridors and in countries with insufficient molecular data.

3.4

## WNV surveillance in the Mediterranean Basin
Surveillance of WNV includes two complementary activities, i.e. epidemiological and environmental surveillance. Epidemiological surveillance focuses on human disease (e.g. disease burden, clinical presentation, and disease outcome), and the associated risk factors. Human surveillance is a passive case-based system and is insufficient alone for preparedness and prevention of WNV outbreaks. Outbreaks in humans occur over a few weeks during WNV transmission peak, and the time from human infection to appearance of disease symptoms can extend to several weeks. As a result, reporting human cases usually comes much later than when the virus is circulating in vectors. West Nile virus is a notifiable disease, therefore, infected cases must be reported to the local/regional health authorities. It follows that passive surveillance in humans depends largely on clinicians considering WNV diagnosis, obtaining the appropriate diagnostic test, and reporting the cases to public health authorities. Therefore, underreporting, particularly in mild cases with non-neuroinvasive disease, is a common limitation of the passive surveillance in humans. The degree of underreporting varies by disease awareness and healthcare-seeking behavior in any area ( ).
This underlines the importance of the environmental surveillance that monitors WNV activity in vectors and non-human vertebrate hosts (e.g. birds and equines), and gathering information about the local conditions and habitats that impact vector abundance and infection can provide a predictive index of human infection risk. However, passive surveillance in horses, like in humans, has limited value for early detecting WNV circulation. Passive surveillance on dead birds may also not be very useful for WNV surveillance in some regions worldwide because the majority of the circulating WNV strains do not cause significant mortality in birds, and there is a lack of systems of carcass reporting ( ). On the other hand, active WNV surveillance in mosquito and bird populations could provide information on where to expect the next outbreak; however, this requires capacity and economic resources not always available everywhere.
After WNV re-emergence in the Mediterranean in the 1990s, various countries developed surveillance programmes to monitor WNV infections in humans, horses, birds, and mosquitoes. However, integration of WNV surveillance systems varies at the subregional level. WNV surveillance is well established in Southern European countries. These countries have strong reporting systems. Southern European countries are requested to report human cases to the ECDC on a weekly basis during the transmission season from the beginning of June until one month after the onset of the clinical signs of the last reported case. In animals (birds and equids), WNV outbreaks are notified to the Animal Disease Information System (ADIS) of the European Commission, and to Terrestrial Code of the WOAH. It is mandatory to report WNV equine encephalomyelitis cases. Active surveillance in equids and/or poultry occurs in some Southern European countries (e.g. Croatia, Cyprus, Spain, Greece, and Italy).
The situation in Israel resembles that of Southern Europe. Tunisia, on the other hand, has a relatively effective surveillance system, except for data collection and analysis ( ). The other Mediterranean countries in North Africa and Western Asia have no or weak WNV surveillance systems. Isolated surveys for WNV surveillance are usually conducted in these countries in response to an outbreak. The majority of these countries lack the infrastructure, funding, and trained personnel necessary for WNV surveillance. In addition, inadequate knowledge of physicians about the nature of the disease and misdiagnosis of other common infectious diseases due to similarity in clinical presentations are limitations of WNV surveillance in these countries. The unstable political situation in some countries (e.g. Palestine and Iraq), also hampers efforts of the national and international agencies for WNV surveillance ( ).

3.5

## Challenges
An ‘emerging infection’, as WNV in Southern Europe, is defined as “has newly appeared in the population, or has existed previously but is presently rapidly increasing in incidence or geographic range” ( ). There are several factors that support the emergence or recurring outbreaks of WNV in the MB, including spread due to international trade, travel, and bird migration, and medical unpreparedness. As highlighted by the Institute of Medicine of the National Academies, USA ( ), emergence is a combination of microbial adaptation and change; human susceptibility to infection; climate and weather; changing ecosystems; human demographics and behaviour; economic development and land use; international travel and commerce; technology and industry; breakdown of public health measures; poverty and social inequality; war and famine; and lack of political will to institute monitoring surveillance and control systems before a disease becomes a problem. For WNV, climate change has been considered the major cause of the occurrence in new areas ( ). Apart from climate change, while the presence of competent vectors and transmission hosts is necessary, it is not a sufficient condition if environmental factors (in the broad sense) limit the transmission potential to below the threshold that is required for persistence ( ).
The complexity described in the present review, the lack of information around WNV in likely hotspots areas, such as North Africa, the large scale of morbidity of WNV in Southern Europe and Asia, the large proportion (a major component of all infections) of likely unreported and asymptomatic infections, and finally the adaptive plasticity of pathogens and arthropods make WNV especially difficult to control or eradicate. Since vaccines for humans are unavailable for WNV, WNV control requires the existence of effective surveillance and early warning systems ( ). Policy decision makers need to be able to prioritise areas at high risk of introduction or spread, focusing on active surveillance of vectors, animals, and human populations ( ). However, this can be successful only if surveillance and control can be coordinated at multi-country and continental levels, an approach that already exists between international organizations such as OIE, WHO, and FAO. To support prioritisation of surveillance areas, statistical or mathematical modelling need to consider the impacts of biotic interactions involving the vector (e.g. adult mosquito lifespan) and host, and the effects of environmental and climate change ( ).
Clearly, WNV cannot be controlled by single efforts of clinicians or veterinarians or biologists or environmental modellers or climatologists or policy makers or epidemiologists. Instead, an integration of their knowledge is required.

3.6

## Limitations
The present study has some limitations, likely due to the great gap in the published data on the confirmed clinical cases, host surveillance, and molecular data between the three MB subregions. These limitations also include (i) the potential publication bias since the apparent disease burden is likely distorted by underreporting, particularly in under-resourced countries, (ii) differences in diagnostic capabilities and veterinary infrastructure between various MB countries, (iii) lack of data integration across humans, horses, birds, and vectors in various MB countries, and (iv) missing modelling or spatial analysis, which could have improved risk mapping and predictive insights.

4

## Conclusions
In conclusion, the eco-epidemiology of WNV in the MB is markedly influenced by a complex interaction of genetics, host and vector ecology, climate change, and movements of migratory birds. However, the complete picture is hidden by inconsistent molecular surveillance and underreporting in various high-risk countries. To deepen our understanding of WNV transmission and guide successful regional control efforts, coordinated, cross-border genomic surveillance is crucial, particularly along the migratory corridors and in countries with inadequate molecular data.
