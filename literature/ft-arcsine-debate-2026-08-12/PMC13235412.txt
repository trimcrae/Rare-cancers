## Background
The purpose of this review was to estimate the prevalence of substance use during TB treatment and to synthesize the relationship between substance use and TB care engagement in the African region. Our secondary aim was to identify methods of substance use evaluation.

## Methods
Adhering to the PRISMA method, search strategies for PubMed, Embase and CINAHL were developed to maximize yield. Substance use was defined as use of illicit substances including cannabis and khat/chat. TB care engagement was defined loosely through sub-optimal adherence, missed appointments, treatment interruption and loss to follow up. Peer reviewed research, published in English that evaluated a relationship between substance use and TB care engagement were included. We estimated the pooled prevalence of substance use among people with TB using the meta command in Stata 18.

## Results
The initial search generated 1311 titles; 18 studies were included spanning six countries. Substance use ranged from 3 to 48% with an estimated pooled prevalence 11.3% across studies. There was conflicting evidence linking substance use and care engagement. While three studies used a validated tool to comprehensively evaluate substance use, most studies used a single indicator (yes/no) to evaluate substance use and did not quantify the frequency of use.

## Conclusions
We estimated a pooled prevalence of substance use during TB treatment at 11.3%; however, as self-report was used to collect substance use data, desirability bias likely led to an underestimation of the true prevalence. Additional research is warranted to evaluate the true effect of substance use on TB care engagement.

## Introduction
In 2023, tuberculosis (TB) returned to its position as the world's leading infectious killer with 1.25 million deaths . Twenty-two of the 30 countries most burdened by TB are in Africa comprising 24% of the world's TB cases . The past ten years are notable for remarkable advances in TB treatment tolerability, efficacy, and reductions in treatment duration. This has contributed to a commendable 42% decline in TB related deaths in the African region ; however, TB treatment adherence and strong care engagement remain a challenge to TB elimination efforts. Further, these efforts and current support systems may be hampered by cuts to global funding for infectious disease care to many TB endemic countries , , . The World Health Organization outlines five TB treatment outcomes, two positive – cure and treatment completion, and three negative outcomes, death, treatment failure and loss to follow up (LTFU) . All five treatment outcomes are variably influenced by patterns of care engagement and treatment adherence. In addition to the increased risk of individual morbidity and mortality, suboptimal treatment adherence also poses a threat to public health through continued bacterial transmission and development of antimicrobial resistance. To facilitate successful treatment, it is essential to understand social and behavioral factors that affect TB treatment adherence and overall care engagement.
Many clinicians and researchers speculate that substance use (SU) negatively impacts TB treatment adherence and reduces care engagement. Underlying this relationship is the theory that during uncontrolled use, substances can have a destabilizing effect on an individual's medical, social, and financial behaviors . Operational definitions for SU may include tobacco products, alcohol, marijuana and other drugs such as stimulants and opiate agonists. Researchers have long considered the role of alcohol in TB-care engagement and medication adherence, particularly in South Africa where Apartheid policies cultivated high levels of harmful alcohol consumption and dependence among communities of color . A variety of studies indicate that moderate to heavy alcohol use is associated with TB disease . A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that alcohol use was associated with higher risk of LTFU, treatment failure, and death among people with drug-sensitive, and drug-resistant TB , . Despite the myriad of reviews evaluating alcohol use and TB, similar reviews that evaluate the impact of other types of SU and TB are lacking.
1.1

## Study purpose
The purpose of this analysis was to systematically review the literature to summarize the prevalence of illicit SU during TB treatment and to synthesize the impact of illicit SU on TB care engagement in the African region. Our secondary aim was to identify methods of SU evaluation during TB treatment.

2

## Methods
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses method was used to perform the search and synthesis of literature . The review was registered on PROSPERO on 11/18/2024 (Prospero ID: CRD42024611641).
2.1

## Search strategy
Search strategies for PubMed, Embase and CINAHL were developed with a library informaticist to maximize yield (see supplemental file 1). Keywords and truncations for the search included variations of the term “tuberculosis or TB” and “substance use or drug use”. The search strategy listed all countries within the World Health Organization African Region to ensure completeness. Studies from outside of the African region were excluded due to differences in substance use patterns that vary by geography and culture.

2.2

## Inclusion criteria
Our search included published peer-reviewed research conducted in Africa that evaluated a) prevalence of SU among a sample of people with TB and b) the impact of SU on a TB related outcome. SU was inclusive of any illicit substance, as well as substances with some legal tolerance including cannabis/marijuana and khat/qat. We defined TB care engagement including missed appointments, treatment interruptions, sub-optimal treatment adherence, and WHO defined outcomes which are directly related to sub-optimal care engagement .

2.3

## Exclusion criteria
We excluded non-English language publications, tobacco only and/or alcohol only studies, and studies that did not evaluate correlations between SU and a TB-related outcome. We also excluded studies that sampled exclusively people with SU disorder as this would not allow us to estimate SU prevalence during TB treatment. Primary sources from systematic reviews and meta-analyses were checked for eligibility. (See PRISMA diagram). 

2.4

## Procedure
Manuscripts were imported into the Covidence web-platform to facilitate screening and extraction. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two members of the study team. To mitigate publication bias, during our initial review, we also looked at lists of study variables because insignificant associations with SU may not have been reported in the abstracts. Studies that moved beyond the initial screen were reviewed in full to evaluate their inclusion. Disagreements were resolved by a separate author. Quality appraisals were done using Joanna Briggs Institute criteria (See ); despite variation in quality, no articles were excluded. Data was extracted and verified by two team members using an extraction template. 

2.5

## Analysis
A meta-analysis of prevalence was conducted using the meta command in Stata version 18. We used funnel plots to evaluate publication bias. To determine the pooled prevalence of SU across studies, we used the Tukey-Freeman double arcsine transformation (FTT). Illicit SU is uncommon and FTT is a preferred method to stabilize variance of proportion when data is primarily clustered near 0. The FTT creates a transformed prevalence that may be outside of expected proportions (0–1) and is not clinically interpretable without back transformation. We used a restricted maximum likelihood random effects model which assumes variation both within and between studies. Significance level was set at 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals for all analyses.
Using Hedge's I2 statistic we identified substantial heterogeneity in the data (>75% using 95% CI), indicating differences were due to study design rather than random error . We additionally evaluated heterogeneity using a Galbraith plot which visually reaffirmed the heterogeneity within studies and highlighted outliers (see supplemental file 2). We were thus unable to calculate pooled effect sizes of SU on LTFU or treatment adherence.

## Results
The search generated 1311 initial title and abstracts for screening; 588 studies from PubMed, 444 from CINAHL, 275 from Embase and 4 studies were identified through citation searching of references in identified papers. Please see supplemental file 1 for the specific search strings used for each database. 200 duplicate titles were identified and removed. 119 studies moved forward for full text assessment resulting in 18 studies (See ) spanning six countries. Most studies were conducted in South Africa (n = 9) , , , , , , , , and Ethiopia (n = 6) , , , , , while the remaining studies were conducted in Burkina Faso , Ghana , Morocco and Uganda . Included studies were published between 2006 and 2024 (See ). Notably, two studies conducted by Anley and colleagues used the same sample population; however, their aims and analyses differed , . Most studies did not specify whether their analysis included people with resistance to TB drugs (n = 10) , , , , , , , , , . Eight studies reported evaluating drug-resistant TB , , , and others specifically enrolling those with multi-drug resistant TB , , , , . One study reported inclusion of only those with drug-sensitive TB . (See .) 

4

## Substance use measurement/evaluation
All of the included studies used self-report to obtain SU data either through primary data collection or from data abstracted from medical records, none used biomarkers of substance use for verification. Of the included studies, three used a validated measure to evaluate SU. Two studies used the Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) , , and one used the Drug Use Disorders Identification Test (DUDIT) . More often, SU was evaluated as a dichotomous variable considering ever vs never SU, or yes/no history of SU during TB treatment. In five studies, authors did not define SU or inquire about individual substances , , , , . The most commonly evaluated substances were khat/chat , , , , , marijuana , , , , , and methaqualone/mandrax, a sedative hypnotic , , . All studies evaluating khat/chat use were conducted in Ethiopia while all studies evaluating methaqualone/mandrax were conducted in South Africa. Previously mentioned studies that used the ASSIST or DUDIT collected data on any/all substances used and these data were queried systematically as detailed in the instrument instructions. Notably, none of the included studies specifically measured or discussed opiate use. Full details about SU measurement are available in .
4.1

## Prevalence
Overall SU prevalence ranged from 3%–48% across studies. One study with high SU prevalence reported data that did not disaggregate illicit drug use from tobacco/alcohol use and was excluded from the analysis of prevalence . Studies that used validated instruments to measure SU were able to stratify use by risk category. Reuter and colleagues identified that of the 23% of participants who reported marijuana, methamphetamine, cocaine, or mandrax use, nearly half scored in the high-risk category . Rynhoud and Kagee found that the mean substance-use score in their sample was above the threshold for harmful drug use and 14.3% of all participants scored in a range indicating dependence .
Sixteen studies inclusive of 5112 individuals were included in the pooled prevalence estimate. We excluded one study by Ansley and colleagues to avoid double counting of observations, we also excluded one study by Lelisho and colleagues because they did not disaggregate illicit SU from tobacco and alcohol use, all substance use was presented as a composite which would overestimate the prevalence of illicit SU. Using the FTT, SU prevalence was calculated to be 0.695 with a confidence interval of 0.53, 0.86. Transformed pooled effect sizes are represented as a forest plot (See Fig. 3). Using the back transformation, the estimated overall prevalence of SU among people with TB from this sample is 11.3% (95% CI 0.069, 0.167, I2 96.92%).

4.2

## TB care engagement data
Among the included studies, most analyzed the relationship between SU and treatment adherence. Of these, six were focused on a treatment outcome of LTFU using the World Health Organization definition – a person with TB whose “treatment was interrupted for 2 consecutive months or more” , , , , , . Other studies evaluated and defined treatment adherence in other ways including self-reported medication non-adherence , using a validated adherence scale , , remaining in care through intensive phase , and via directly observed therapy, pill counts, or electronic pill monitors , . Three studies evaluated correlations between SU and TB treatment outcomes more generally comparing positive (cure and treatment complete) to negative outcomes (death, treatment failure and LTFU) , , . One study specifically modeled the relationship between SU and time to death .
SU was variably correlated with LTFU. Articles written by Dooley , Holtz , and Kendall found that SU was statistically significantly associated with LTFU. Conversely, Finlay , and Ndwandew were unable to establish a significant correlation. Across treatment adherence more broadly there was similar variation. Overall, ten studies were unable to establish a link between SU and TB treatment adherence , , , , , , , , , , while seven (39%) did find a significant correlation , , , , , , . We were unable to identify patterns of SU affecting care engagement related to TB drug resistance or year of data collection.

5

## Discussion
Overall we found relatively few articles (n = 18) in a large geographic area that specifically evaluated the impact of illicit SU on TB treatment adherence and TB care engagement despite the theorized relationship between the two. In this review, we found variable rates of self-reported SU among people with TB, with an emphasis upon SU during drug-resistant TB though the motivation for this was unclear. While most studies were from the Republic of South Africa, the regions of East, West and North Africa were represented totaling 5475 people that have not previously been analyzed systematically. Overall, studies were split on whether SU significantly affected TB care engagement such as treatment adherence and LTFU. Some of the variability in these findings is likely attributable to variation in the definition of SU and in SU data collection. Among studies that used a validated instrument to measure SU, we saw higher rates of SU and were able to differentiate recreational use from hazardous use or dependence in this population. In studies that collected SU as a self-reported dichotomous variable, desirability bias may have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence of the results. Due to heterogeneity we were unable to conduct a meta-analysis evaluating the pooled impact of SU on TB care engagement.
5.1

## Global impact of SU on TB care engagement
This review demonstrates the need for further studies evaluating the impact of substance use on TB care engagement and outcomes in the African region. While this review was restricted to one geographic area, studies on this topic are also limited in other global regions. A few studies conducted in the United States do tackle this relationship but were conducted during the 1990s when the HIV epidemic contributed to a spike in TB disease and deaths . A more recent analysis of more than 10,000 people with TB in the Netherlands identified that people misusing substances had 6-fold greater odds of a poor TB outcome . However, the behavior and treatment patterns reflected in high-income countries may not be generalizable to historically resource-deprived areas in the global south.
Researchers in Ukraine demonstrated improved TB treatment adherence and treatment completion among people using drugs when methadone maintenance therapy was provided during TB treatment . This middle-income country with a high burden of drug-resistant TB may provide insight for national TB programs in similar economic brackets. In other contexts such as HIV, integrated treatment models where infectious disease and SU disorders are treated concurrently demonstrate improved treatment adherence and better rates of viral suppression , . These studies highlight opportunities for streamlined TB care for people using substances.

5.2

## Frequency/timing of use
SU occurs on a continuum varying from nonuse, to recreational use, through SU disorder . In this review, 13 of 18 studies evaluated SU as a dichotomous variable and were unable to characterize SU frequency or harm. As historically illicit substances such as marijuana and psychoactive drugs become available for medicinal and recreational use, understanding the continuum of SU may become more important. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 5th edition uses criteria such as social or interpersonal problems, neglecting major roles or duties, physical or psychological problems, and giving up activities in order to use as important diagnostic criteria for SU disorder . Details about SU habits would help to inform TB treatment planning as clinicians schedule appointments, screen for adverse effects, interpret lab results and consider the role of harm reduction in the TB treatment plan.

5.3

## Type of use
The most frequently identified substances in this review were khat and marijuana, however, these findings reflect a bias in the survey design where many studies specifically queried khat or marijuana use but did not inquire about other illicit substances. However, SU encompasses a broad variety of substances ranging from socially acceptable and innocuous substances such as caffeine, to legal but harmful substances such as tobacco, to substances which can be immediately fatal such as synthetic opiates. Given the current global opioid epidemic and changing patterns of opiate use production and trafficking, we were surprised that no studies specifically addressed opiate use. Recent reports in Southern and West Africa describe a worrying increase in opiate consumption in countries with high TB prevalence . This rise in non-medical opiate use and in SU generally, warrants attention and systematic evaluation to estimate true prevalence. As researchers attempt to tease out the true impact of SU on TB care engagement, assessing for all emergent substances and differentiating between substances is essential.

5.4

## Methods of evaluation
Most studies in this review used a self-reported dichotomous variable, asking participants to disclose past or current SU. More nuanced approaches offer more nuanced information. The ASSIST scale (Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test) used by two studies in this review, was developed and validated by the World Health Organization, to enable SU screening in community-based primary-care settings . The ASSIST scale specifically queries respondents about nine different substance classes with opportunities to discuss additional substances. The tool asks about lifetime use, frequency of social, legal, financial and health problems, impact on daily responsibilities, and if and when injection use has occurred . Adding to its utility, ASSIST has been validated in more than 42 countries . Another study in this review used the DUDIT (Drug use disorders identification test) . DUDIT is an 11-question validated screener that complements the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, and characterizes frequency, cravings, compulsive consumption, morning use, and feelings of guilt . Some have argued that more important than the instrument used, are the contextual factors that influence accuracy of self-reported SU . Cultural norms about SU, assurances of confidentiality, and mode of collection (self-completed surveys vs information gathered during clinical interview) all impact an individual's willingness to self-report. To determine the true impact of SU on TB care engagement, findings from this systematic review indicate that African clinicians and researchers should choose validated instruments and thoughtfully plan data collection to minimize desirability bias and improve the accuracy of self-reported SU.

5.5

## Limitations
In this review we excluded studies conducted outside of the African region which limits the generalizability of the findings and our ability to synthesize the true impact of SU on TB treatment. Our geographic selection criteria prioritized regional and cultural patterns in SU that occur in a geographic area significantly burdened by TB disease. Additionally, there are considerable differences in substance use patterns comparing low-to-middle vs high income countries, where over the past two decades we have seen an explosion of opiate use disorder in the United States and Canada, similar epidemics have not been documented in the African Region. We used the FTT to stabilize the variance of each study's reported prevalence of SU. However, there are limitations to this method, primarily difficulty in interpreting results for clinical relevance . There are several statistical strategies to conduct meta-analysis of proportion including logit transformation, generalized linear mixed models and arcsine transformation . While logit transformations report results between 0 and 1 as expected with proportions, this method does not stabilize variance to the same degree as FTT . Similarly, generalized linear mixed models perform transformations in one step, with less bias, but are prone to computational issues . Given the lack of consensus on a gold standard for synthesizing binary proportions, we believe that the FTT is a reasonable choice.

6

## Conclusions
In this analysis we estimated SU at 11% among people undergoing TB treatment in the African region though the true prevalence is likely higher as these responses were based on self-report and likely reflect a desirability bias. We surprisingly found that the current published literature offers no clear answer to whether SU negatively impacts TB care engagement or treatment adherence. However, this review underscores substance use as a potential barrier to TB care engagement, a relationship that requires critical further research and programmatic data to clarify. To answer this key question, future research must consider nuances in SU patterns including type and frequency of use and should use a validated measure. Substance use is increasing globally. In response to this trend, manufacturing and trafficking patterns are increasing across Africa to meet global demands . This also has an impact on SU across the continent with growing markets for cannabis, methamphetamine and opiates. Choosing strategies that actively improve screening for SU during TB treatment may be the first step in a comprehensive plan to reducing additional morbidity and mortality during TB treatment.
