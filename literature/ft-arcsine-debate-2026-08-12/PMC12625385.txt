## Background
Hepatitis B is a silently devastating disease that presents a public health concern and affects millions of people. Recent estimates indicate that 254 million individuals globally are afflicted with chronic HBV infection, with around 1.2 million new cases emerging annually and roughly 1.1 million fatalities primarily resulting from long-term consequences. Like other developing countries, Morocco still lacks consolidated epidemiological data on the real burden of hepatitis B. In this context, our work aims to fill this gap through a systematic review and a meta-analysis of studies conducted over the last 25 years.

## Methods
We conducted a comprehensive search in databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, focusing on studies related to Hepatitis B in Morocco. Our inclusion criteria encompass all observational studies conducted and published between 2000 and 2024 reporting HBsAg prevalence among individuals residing in Morocco regardless of age, sex, or subpopulations. The prevalence studies included were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data. Where appropriate, a pooled prevalence was calculated using a DerSimonian-Laird random effects model.

## Results
30 studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing 34 cohorts and 750,784 individuals with prevalence ranging between 0.07% and 7.98%. HBsAg prevalence was assessed across four endemicity levels. About 74% of cohorts showed low prevalence (< 2%), seven studies lay in the 2–4.99% band. Only two studies reach the 5–7.99% band, while no study showed high endemicity (≥ 8%). The pooled prevalence of HBsAg in Morocco was 1.33% (95% CI: 1.07–1.61). Subgroup analysis revealed the lowest prevalence among military populations (0.36%) and the highest in vulnerable high-risk groups (5.43%), underscoring subpopulation-specific disparities in HBV burden.

## Conclusions
The pooled prevalence observed across included studies reinforces the hypothesis that Morocco is transitioning towards a low-endemic HBV status, which reflects the comprehensive national measures, along with the positive impact of systematic vaccination of newborn introduced in 1999 and certain groups as well. However, additional efforts remain necessary to sustain this progress and adequately address the weaknesses observed at the national level in managing HBV infection, particularly among certain at-risk groups.

## Introduction
Hepatitis B is a viral infection that affects mainly the liver and spread through contact with infected body fluids notably blood, semen, vaginal secretions, or from mother to child during childbirth. This silently devastating and life-threatening disease presents a significant global public health concern and affects millions of people with an uneven distribution around the world: nearly 88% of chronic cases are concentrated in the Western Pacific (38%), African (25%) and South-East Asia (24%) Regions, while the Eastern Mediterranean (6%), European (4%) and American (2%) Regions account for the remaining 12% [ ]. A systematic review published in 2015 indicates that 3,61% of the world population were carrying Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) in 2010, representing about 248 million people living with the virus, with the highest rates recorded in Africa (8.83%) and the Western Pacific (5.26%) [ ]. In 2019, Sheena and al. estimated that it reached 4,1%, representing roughly 316 million people, and confirmed the peak of infection witnessed in the Western Pacific and Africa, with 10.8% and 10.2% respectively [ ]. The World Health Organization (WHO) latest figures indicate that 254 million individuals globally are afflicted with chronic HBV infection, with around 1.2 million new cases emerging annually and roughly 1.1 million fatalities primarily resulting from long-term consequences, as reported in 2022 [ ].
Patterns of HBV transmission are closely linked to the endemicity level of the affected region. In high-endemic regions, vertical transmission (from mother to child) and horizontal transmission (mainly through exposure to infected family members) are the most frequent, while in countries with intermediate endemicity, parenteral and sexual transmission are the most commonly reported routes. The same applies to low-endemic regions, with higher prevalence of HBsAg among people who inject drugs, incarcerated individuals, men who have sex with men, sex workers, and homeless populations. Moreover, several individual factors, such as male sex, older age, socioeconomic status, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, obesity, maternal HBV status during pregnancy, certain genetic predispositions, and various comorbidities, appear to be independently involved in the progression and complications of chronic HBV infection [ – ].
The main complications of hepatitis B are cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, which represent the major outcomes of chronic infection. Fulminant hepatitis B, a more severe form of the disease, occurs sporadically in less than 1% of acute hepatitis B cases. Less frequently, a wide range of extrahepatic manifestations may occur during both acute and chronic infection, including systemic, hematological, neurological, ophthalmological, and rheumatological complications [ – ].
The unexpected discovery of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg, formerly called the Australia antigen) by Baruch Samuel Blumberg in 1967 marked the start of a series of breakthroughs that greatly deepened our grasp of this infection, which had already wrought considerable harm throughout history. This advance opened the door for the development of reliable diagnostic tests and other laboratory investigations, among which are liver function tests, HBV DNA quantification, genotyping assays, and a panel of serological markers such as HBsAg, surface antibody (anti HBs), core antibody (IgM and IgG anti HBc), e antigen (HBeAg), and e antibody (anti HBe) which are used to diagnose and monitor HBV infection, determine disease activity, and guide patient management [ , ]. Other measures such as safer blood transfusions, conception of effective vaccines against the hepatitis B virus (HBV), the development of antiviral therapies, a considerable raise in public awareness, and large-scale epidemiological surveys to track the virus prevalence worldwide were also determinant [ , ]. These efforts have contributed to an overall 31.3% reduction in the prevalence of chronic hepatitis B between 1990 and 2019 [ ].
Despite this overall decline in HBsAg seroprevalence, recent data rank HBV infection as the third leading cause associated with age-adjusted mortality from liver cirrhosis and other chronic diseases of the liver, particularly in endemic regions [ ]. Worldwide, this virus is responsible for around 47% of deaths attributable to viral hepatitis complications [ ]. This finding highlights the persistence of the long-term consequences of HBV, particularly in people infected before the widespread vaccination of newborns. Moreover, only a minority (10%) of chronic carriers of the virus know their status, and fewer than 23% of patients eligible for treatment receive it [ ].
In Morocco, the fight against HBV began indirectly in the early 1980s, in parallel with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) prevention efforts, due to shared transmission routes. Systematic HBsAg screening of blood donors was introduced in 1987. Other measures have followed such as improving the safety of injection practices (since 2002), setting up sexual and reproductive health programs, services for drug users, generalizing screening tests, reinforcing care for infected people (notably through access to antiviral treatment), extending medical coverage to reduce the cost of treatment, and more importantly, introducing the national vaccination program in 1999 [ ].
Morocco, like other developing countries, still lacks consolidated epidemiological data on the real burden of hepatitis B, thus limiting the possibilities of targeted intervention at the public policy level. While several isolated studies have examined the prevalence of HBsAg in different Moroccan sub-populations, the literature lacks a recent systematic review that could offer a recent aggregated national estimate of HBsAg prevalence among the Moroccan population. In this context, our work aims to fill this gap through a systematic review and a meta-analysis of studies conducted over the last 25 years (2000–2024). The main objective was to provide an up-to-date overview of HBsAg seroprevalence among the Moroccan population and the various population categories in Morocco, to identify priority groups to be targeted in national strategies, and to provide solid evidence to guide future public health policies as well as priority areas for research. By consolidating these fragmented data, we hope not only to improve the epidemiological understanding of hepatitis B in Morocco, but also to raise awareness among decision-makers of the specific needs in the fight against this disease, in terms of prevention, early detection, and appropriate care.

## Materials and methods

## Study area
Bordered by the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea, Morocco is a country located in the northwestern part of Africa and covers approximately 710,850 km² with a population of nearly 36.8 million [ ]. Economically, Morocco’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) reached about $165.56 billion this year, roughly $4,400 per capita, which classified the country as a lower-middle-income economy [ ]. Health spending totals about 5% of GDP, which is approximately $199 per capita in 2022, with an estimated 43% of health expenses paid out-of-pocket [ ]. In 2021, the national health insurance agency reported that health insurance coverage exceeded 74% [ ]. In terms of access to health care services, data from the WHO report a ratio of 7.4 physicians per 10,000 individuals [ ]. While access to healthcare varies, in urban regions, all residents live within 5 km of a healthcare institution, whereas in rural areas, just 30% live within 5 km, 59% live within 5–10 km, and 11% live more than 8 km away [ ].

## Search strategy
We conducted a comprehensive search in databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, focusing on studies related to Hepatitis B in Morocco. Our search strategy included terms like “Hepatitis B,” “Hepatitis B virus,” “HBV,” “Hepatitis B surface antigen”, “HBsAg “, “viral hepatitis,” and “prevalence” (see Appendix for details), ensuring a thorough retrieval of relevant literature. This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ].

## Inclusion/exclusion criteria
Our inclusion criteria encompass all observational studies (case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, and prospective or retrospective cohort studies), published in peer-reviewed journals, and reporting HBsAg prevalence among individuals residing in Morocco. Screened using serological tests such as ELISA, CMIA, ECLIA, or rapid diagnostic kits, regardless of age, sex, or subpopulations. The search period was restricted to studies conducted and published between 2000 and 2024. Studies published in English and French were included. Additionally, we also searched for relevant papers in Spanish and Arabic, given Morocco’s multilingual context [ ]. Our exclusion criteria included: Studies outside the predefined period, studies including Moroccan individuals living outside Morocco, studies not reporting HBsAg prevalence, reviews of all types, case studies, case series, government surveillance reports, conference abstracts, and studies including fewer than 100 individuals.

## Data screening
Two investigators worked independently. F.M. organized the citations in Mendeley Desktop v2.132.2 (Elsevier, Amsterdam, the Netherlands), while S.M. used Zotero v6.0.36 (Roy Rosenzweig Center for History and New Media, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA, USA). Citations were exported in BibTeX format from each database and merged into a single file. The dataset included, if available, author(s), document title, year, source, volume, issue, and pages, source and document type, and the abstract. Any discrepancies between their libraries were reviewed jointly and resolved in consultation with a third author until consensus was reached. The screening process involved merging all records, removing duplicates, screening titles, source and abstracts against the inclusion criteria, assessing the remaining papers in full-text format for eligibility, and retaining only studies that met all criteria.

## Data extraction
Data extraction was independently conducted by F.M. and K.H., and involved collecting all available information on the first author, publication year, study period, study design, population characteristics, prevalence of HBsAg, sample size, diagnostic methods, and risk of bias. This process was carried out in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA; version 16). Any discrepancies between F.M. and K.H. were discussed with a third author until consensus was reached.

## Quality assessment
The prevalence studies were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data, which evaluates nine key methodological questions (see Appendix for details). A score of 1 was assigned if the response was “Yes” and 0 if it was “No” or “Unclear”. Studies were then categorized as having low risk (≥ 70% which correspond to scores between 7 and 9), “moderate risk” (50–69% corresponding to a score of 5 or 6), or high risk of bias (< 50% which range from 0 to 4) as reported previously [ , ]. The percentages (%) represent the proportion of checklist items satisfied out of the maximum score (9/9). (See Appendix for more details). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with additional reviewer input sought as needed. F.M performed the initial evaluation and was subsequently reviewed and verified by S.M.

## Data analysis
All analyses and graphs were performed in R version 4.4.2, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated using the Wilson method. Pooled HBsAg seroprevalence was estimated with a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model after Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine transformation. Heterogeneity was assessed by Cochran Q and I² (25%, 50%, 75% denoting low, moderate, high). When I² >25%, random effects were retained. Publication bias was screened visually (funnel plot) and analytically (Egger and Begg tests, p < 0.05). We performed a leave-one-out analysis by study to test the robustness of the estimates and a meta-regression was conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. To compare disparities in HBsAg carriage among different Moroccan groups, studies were classified into seven categories:

## General public
Individuals sampled through national surveys, designed to reflect the resident population irrespective of occupation, risk, clinical status, socio-economic statut, etc.

## Civilian blood donors
Volunteer individuals donating blood at transfusion centers, routinely screened for HBsAg.

## Military recruits/donors
Conscripts and active-duty personnel donating blood within military hospitals or transfusion services.

## Healthcare workers
Medical and allied health professionals whose professional activities place them at increased risk of HBV infection.

## Clinical population
Hospital-based groups, including mixed inpatients and outpatients. This category includes individuals seeking medical care for various conditions not necessarily related to HBV infection (e.g., pregnant women attending antenatal clinics, immunosuppressed or dialysis patients, psychiatric patients, those undergoing routine checkups or preoperative screening, etc.).

## Traditional-practice group
Barbers and clients engaged in traditional hair-cutting or blood-exposure practices using non-sterile instruments.

## Vulnerable/high-risk group
Patients with chronic diseases characterized by increased exposure risk, impaired immune response, and reduced responsiveness to vaccination.

## Results

## Selection process of studies
Figure illustrates the overall study selection process, carried out in accordance with the PRISMA flow diagram. Records were identified through four databases: PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus. 1,198 records were initially retrieved. After removing 293 duplicates, 905 unique records underwent title, source, and abstract screening, resulting in 858 exclusions (irrelevant studies, case studies, systematic reviews, government reports, thesis, studies reporting HBsAg among individuals living in outside Morocco, etc.). The remaining 47 articles were then assessed in full-text format for eligibility. Of these, 17 were excluded for reasons such as unavailability in full-text format (n = 6), sample size fewer than 100 participants (n = 3), conference proceedings (n = 3). In addition, five other studies were excluded because they reported HBsAg prevalence only among patients already diagnosed with chronic hepatitis B (n = 3), did not report HBsAg prevalence (n = 1), or were government surveillance report (n = 1), as reported in Fig.  . Ultimately, 30 studies met all inclusion requirements and were included in the final review. These papers were published in English or French, and no pertinent or related studies in Spanish or Arabic were identified.

 

 

## Quality assessment for the included studies
Of the 30 studies included and assessed for quality using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist, 19 (63%) were categorized as having a low risk of bias, 10 (33%) exhibited a moderate risk, and just 1 (3%) was deemed to have a high risk. The data suggest that most of the research included was methodologically robust. Nevertheless, a notable proportion still exhibited moderate limitations requiring cautious interpretation.
Figure displays the distribution of risk of bias judgements (Yes = low, Unclear = moderate, No = high) across the nine JBI items/questions for the 30 prevalence studies included in this work. Sampling frame appropriateness (Q1), sample size (Q3), subject/context description (Q4), coverage of the sample in analysis (Q5), case definition (Q6), and measurement standardisation (Q7) all scored well, with low-risk ratings ranging between 83% and 100% of studies. Recruitment strategy (Q2) showed some weaknesses with only 14/30 studies (47%) classified as low-risk, 11/30 (37%) moderate, and 5/30 (16%) high for this item, chiefly because some studies omitted or vaguely described recruitment procedures. Statistical analysis (Q8) recorded the poorest performance, with appropriate design-adjusted methods in just 12/30 studies (40%). Finally, response rates (Q9) were unreported explicitly in 13/30 studies (43%), posing potential selection bias.

 

## Analysis of the included studies
In the 30 studies included in this work, 34 cohorts were studied, comprising 750,784 individuals [ – ]. The mean sample size was approximately 22,082 (SD = 48,359), with a median of 1,037, highlighting substantial variability across studies (I² = 99.36%). Three studies contributed more than one cohort: for instance, one study [ ] included both traditional barbers and their clients; another [ ] examined the general population and blood donors; while a third [ ] analyzed three cohorts of civilian blood donors, including one at the national level in 2011 and two in the Rabat region in 2000 and 2011.
As shown in Table  , these cohorts fell into 16 distinct study groups. Civilian blood donors were the most frequently represented group (n = 7; 20.6%), followed by healthcare workers (n = 5; 14.7%) and military blood donors (n = 4; 11.8%). Pregnant women were included in three groups (8.8%), while hospitalized patients, traditional barbers, patients undergoing haemodialysis, and HIV‑positive patients each appeared in two groups (5.9%). Each of the remaining seven categories accounted for a single groups (2.9%) which included clients of traditional barbers, the general population, the working/active population, patients with chronic inflammatory bowel disease, patients with rheumatoid arthritis, psychiatric patients with schizophrenia, and military recruits.
Most included studies (64.7%, n = 22) focused on individual cities (e.g., Rabat, Casablanca, Fes, or Marrakesh). A smaller share (23.5%, n = 8) covered broader regions comprising at least two cities or provinces, while only a few (11.8%, n = 4) were national in scope. As shown in Fig.  , this distribution reflects a strong concentration of research at the local level and underscores the relative scarcity of nationwide data on HBV prevalence.

 

To evaluate disparities in HBsAg prevalence within the Moroccan population, we classified our cohorts into four endemicity levels, low (< 2%), lower intermediate (2–4.99%), higher-intermediate (5–7.99%), and high (≥ 8%) according to the classification of Ott et al. [ ]. The box-and-whisker plot indicates that HBsAg prevalence in Moroccan studies is generally low (Fig. ). Twenty-five of the 34 investigations (≈ 74%) all in the < 2% band, ranging from 0.07% to 1.96% (median = 1.24%) with limited dispersion. Nine studies (≈ 26%) exceed this threshold, highlighting specific cohorts where HBV remains a public-health concern. Indeed, seven studies lay in the 2–4.99% bnd, with estimates from 2.00% t 4.90% and a median of 2.48%. nearly two studies reach the 5–7.99% band, one among haemodialysis patients (7.98%) and one among HIV-positive patients (5.17%). No study reported a prevalence ≥ 8%; thus, high endemicity was not observed. Overall, intermediate endemicity appears confined to a few context-specific settings, whereas most evidence points to low HBsAg prevalence across the populations examined.

 

Our study period was divided into three distinct phases. Figure  illustrates a steady increase in both the number of studies included and the overall scholarly output over time. The years 2000–2008 correspond to the immediate post-vaccination phase (started in 1999), during which only three studies on HBsAg prevalence were identified. These accounted for approximately 0.3 studies per year, involving a total of 1,488 participants. The period 2009–2016 was characterized by a marked expansion in research activity, with 13 studies published (around 1.6 per year) and a peak of five publications in 2013. This phase included the first national surveys conducted among blood donors, the general population, and the active workforce, encompassing approximately 520,000 individuals.
Finally, during 2017–2024, research continued to diversify with 14 studies (about 1.8 per year) including 229,299 participants. This period saw the emergence of new studied populations, such as patients with chronic inflammatory bowel disease, psychiatric patients with schizophrenia, rheumatoid arthritis, but also a noticeable decline in sample sizes, a trend that warrants closer attention and may reflect the potential impact of contextual factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted HBV screening programs both nationally and globally [ , ].

 

## Overall pooled prevalence in Morocco
Among the cohorts included, reported HBsAg prevalence rates ranged from 0.07% to 7.97%. The pooled prevalence of HBsAg in Morocco was estimated at 1.33% (95% CI: 1.07–1.61%), as illustrated in Fig.  , with a high degree of heterogeneity (I² = 99.36%). Applying this rate to the national population of roughly 36.83 million people yields an estimated 490,000 chronic HBV carriers (95% CI ≈ 394 000–593 000).

 

## Subgroups analysis
Cohorts included in this study were classified into seven study groups as seen in Fig.  . Vulnerable /high-risk group1 including HIV-positive patients and those undergoing dialysis (4 study groups), Healthcare workers2 (5 study groups), the general public3 (2 study groups), Groups involved in traditional practices4 (3 study groups), civilian blood donors5 (7 study groups), the military population6 (5 study groups) and clinical population7 (8 study groups, including pregnant women, chronic disease patients on immunosuppressive therapy, particularly those with inflammatory bowel disease and rheumatoid arthritis, psychiatric patients such as those with schizophrenia, as well as patients of the Mohammed V Military Hospital and the ten facilities of the Ibn Sina University Hospital of Rabat. The military population showed the lowest HBsAg prevalence at 0.36% (95% CI: 0.17–0.62), while the vulnerable/high-risk group had the highest at 5.43% (4.06–6.97). General-public surveys revealed 1.74% (1.62–1.88), and civilian blood donors 1.14% (0.84–1.48). Health-care workers registered 1.77% (1.17–2.47) and the traditional-practice group 1.75% (0.97–2.73), while clinical population averaged 1.56% (1.01–2.21).

 

## Meta-regression
We conducted univariable meta-regression analyses on four moderators: study year, sample size (log-transformed), population subgroup, and risk of bias to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in HBsAg prevalence across the included studies as shown in Table  . Study year was not significantly associated with prevalence (β = − 0.002, p = 0.23) and explained only about 3% of the between-study variability. Sample size showed a significant negative association (β = − 0.027, p = 0.0003), accounting for roughly 28% of heterogeneity, indicating that smaller studies tended to report higher prevalence estimates. Population subgroup was the strongest moderator (QM p < 0.0001), explaining about 76% of the total heterogeneity; prevalence was markedly higher in high-risk populations (β = +0.135, p < 0.0001) and significantly lower in military populations (β = − 0.047, p = 0.0004) consistent with the results of the subgroup analysis, while other groups showed smaller, non-significant differences. Finally, the risk-of-bias category had no significant effect on prevalence (QM p = 0.62; R² ≈ 0%).
This finding indicates that population subgroup was the main determinant of heterogeneity, whereas sample size contributed less to the additional variance explained. The large between-study variability in HBsAg prevalence therefore appears to be primarily driven by differences in the populations investigated rather than by methodological or temporal characteristics.

 

## Assessment of publication bias
The funnel plot (Fig.  ) illustrates the logit-transformed proportions of HBsAg prevalence from all included cohorts (n = 34), plotted against their standard errors. It enables a visual appraisal of potential publication bias and heterogeneity among studies. Considerable dispersion is readily apparent, underscoring the extreme statistical heterogeneity identified (Cochran’s Q = 1645.40, I² = 99.36%, Tau² = 0.76078). Formal assessments for publication bias, including Egger’s test (p = 0.498) and Begg’s test (p = 0.377), revealed no evidence of significant publication bias.

 

## Sensitivity analysis
The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated that omitting any single study resulted in only minor changes to the pooled HBsAg prevalence (1.26–1.40%), with confidence intervals consistently overlapping and heterogeneity remaining essentially unchanged (I² ≈ 98%). This suggests that no individual study had a dominant influence on the overall estimates.

## Discussion
Although significant advances have been made in the knowledge, screening, and management of hepatitis B, the benefits of this progress remain unevenly distributed worldwide. High-income countries have largely benefited from significant improvements, while resource-limited countries still concentrate the majority of chronic HBV [ , ]. This situation illustrates the ongoing impact of socio-economic inequalities on the effectiveness of efforts deployed to mitigate hepatitis B infection, especially in endemic regions. It also highlights the urgent need for a coordinated and eventually adapted global response. Essential to respond to the 26th goal of the UN Sustainable Development Goals for 2030, aiming to eradicate disease epidemics, including hepatitis B, as well as the WHO targets fixed for the period 2022–2030 by reducing the incidence of viral hepatitis B and C infections by 90% and mortality by 65% in 2030 [ , – ].
From this perspective, our study aimed to estimate the seroprevalence of HBsAg over the past 25 years in Morocco, following including hepatitis B virus vaccination in the expanded national program in 1999 [ ]. The pooled HBsAg prevalence obtained is 1.33% (5% C: 1.07–1.61%).Applying this rate to the national population of roughly 36.83 million people yields an estimated 490 000 chronic HBV carriers (95% C ≈ 394 000–593 000), this CI encompasses the 2017 WHO estimate that nearly 523,000 Moroccan individuals are chronically hepatitis B [ ].
Our result was lower than that reported in 2012 and 2013, with 1.66% in the working/active population and 1.81% in the general population [ , ], but higher than what has been observed in developed countries with low endemicity, such as the USA and most European countries, where HBsAg rates are generally below 1% [ , ]. In contrast, more important seroprevalences have been recorded in countries with intermediate endemicity, such as Tunisia, Egypt or Bangladesh, posting a prevalence of 1.7%, 3.67%, and 4.0% respectively [ – ]. Even greater HBsAg carriage rates were recorded in endemic sub-Saharan African countries, such as the Democratic Republic of Congo, where prevalence reached 4.9%, or Cameroon with 11.2% [ , ]. The results of our meta-analysis appear consistent with international data unanimously attesting that Morocco has one of the lowest prevalences of HBsAg on the African continent and the Middle East as well, reinforcing the hypothesis of Morocco’s transition to low-endemicity countries, characterized by prevalences below 2% [ , ].
In order to compare disparities in HBsAg carriage between different Moroccan subpopulations, we grouped the cohorts included in this study into seven distinct groups. The group of civilian blood donors was the most represented in this work (with over 500,000 individuals), the overall seroprevalence of HBsAg observed in this group (1.14% (95% CI 0.84–1.48)) was lower than blood donors in Africa (6.93%) [ ] but higher than the overall 0.22% prevalence among first-time blood donors in Europe [ ]. Other studies reported closer results, notably in Morocco among blood donors at the national level, including 16 regional centers for blood transfusion (RCBT) in 2011 (1.34%) [ ]). Higher estimates were recorded regionally in North Africa (1.73%) [ ], or among blood donors of both EMRO and Middle Eastern countries (2.03%) [ ].
The low HBsAg carriage rate in this group results from improved transfusion safety in Morocco, where compulsory HBsAg screening of blood donors was introduced in 1987 and reinforced in 2004 by compulsory prior medical consultation. This has significantly reduced the prevalence of HBsAg in all age categories [ ]. Indeed, studies assessing the prevalence of viral infections among civilian blood donors at the national level unanimously attest to the regression of HBsAg positivity rates within this sub-population. This is the case of RCBT of Rabat, where the seroprevalence fell from 2.47% to 0.8% between 2000 and 2015 [ , ]. In the same way, the RCBT of Agadir, which includes six southern provinces (see Table ) with seroprevalences of (1.40%-1.70%), 1.35% and 0.8% during 2011, 2020, and 2022, respectively, suggesting an improvement in the epidemiological situation within this sub-population [ , , ].
The second most-represented group was military personnel (comprising 123,394 individuals). This group, predominantly represented by men (88% to 98%), included regular and voluntary military blood donors and young conscripted recruits most of whom were born after the systematic introduction of vaccination in 1999. This group had the lowest pooled prevalence, estimated at 0.36% (95% CI: 0.17–0.62). Lower seroprevalences have been reported in other armies from low-endemicity countries, particularly 0.22% in Brazil and 0.002% in the USA [ , ]. Higher prevalence has been noted in group from intermediate endemicity countries such as Lithuanian Army Soldiers (1.97%) [ ] and military personnel in china with seroprevalences ranging from 0,44% to 1,72%. In endemic countries such as Ethiopia (4.2%) and Senegal (10.8%), HBsAg seroprevalence in the army population and the general population are generally of the same order [ , ]. The low HBsAg seroprevalence among the Moroccan military population probably attests to the cumulative effectiveness of transfusion safety programs and vaccination of the military population as demonstrated by surveys carried out at the Blood Transfusion Center of the Mohammed V Military Teaching Hospital, where HBsAg seroprevalence fell from 2.17% in 1991 to 0.20% in 2020 [ ].
The clinical population studied in this work comprised over 81,000 individuals. The pooled HBsAg seroprevalence observed in this subpopulation was 1.56% (95% CI: 1.01–2.21), which represents a rather encouraging result in a population at moderate risk for HBV, given that the hospital remains a crossroads of potential transmission and at which HBsAg prevalence can exceed 2% even in low-endemic countries, in the image of pregnant women in Slovakia (2.1–2.3%) [ ], patients suffering from rheumatic diseases in Italy (2.0%) [ ], as well as patients with psychiatric disorders in North America (2.2%) and Europe (2.7%) [ ].
The prevalence of hepatitis B among the clinical population in Morocco appears to be following the same downward trend as observed in the previous two groups. For example, at the CHU Ibn Sina in Rabat, the kingdom’s largest hospital center, HBsAg prevalence fell from 15.8% (1990–2004) to 2.47% (2015–2018) [ , ]. At Ibn Rochd Hospital in Casablanca, it reached 1.9% between 2020 and 2022 [ ], and was even lower at Mohammed V Military Hospital, with 1.32% between 2017 and 2022 [ ], which suggest a gradual and steady reduction in HBsAg prevalence in the country’s main hospital facilities despite existing gaps that need to be addressed to sustain this momentum. Indeed, two recent studies carried out in Moroccan University Hospitals in 2022 and 2023 revealed a prevalence of anti-HBc antibodies (a marker of past or present HBV infection) around twice as high as that measured in a national survey conducted in 2019 among the general population (17.91% to 21.2% versus 10%). Furthermore, only a third of hospitalized patients had anti-HBs antibody levels considered protective, highlighting a persistent immune vulnerability to HBV and the need for targeted screening and increased vaccination coverage in this population [ , , ].
The healthcare professionals included in our review constitute more than 4,000 workers. The overall HBsAg seroprevalence was 1.77% (1.17–2.47% 95% CI). This rate represents a low prevalence compared to aggregated African estimates with a prevalence of 6.81% on the continent as a whole, with the highest rate recorded in the West African region at 11.67% and the lowest rate in North Africa at 3.50% [ ]. Our estimate falls within the range observed in the European Union (0.6–2.2%) [ ] and is slightly lower than the 2% reported in Tunisia among 1,497 healthcare workers [ ], while remaining slightly higher than the recent Egyptian meta-analysis, which recorded 1.1% [ ].
Occupational exposure to HBV remains a major threat for Moroccan healthcare workers, mainly through accidental exposure to blood (AEB), with an average annual incidence of 1.5 ± 4.3 AEB per healthcare worker, and an accumulation of around 14.3 ± 28.1 accidents throughout a career [ ], often unreported due to ignorance of procedures, administrative difficulties, or negligence [ ]. In 2008, Djeriri et al. indicated that more than a quarter of Moroccan healthcare workers (28%) carred anti-HBc antibodies. Yet full vaccination, defined as the complete three-dose schedule, covered only 28.8% to 55% of stff [ , , , , ]. This shortfall likely reflects the absence of a legal vaccination requirement in Morocco, unlike in many EU countries where immunisation of healthcare workers is mandatory or strongly recommended, leading to higher coverage [ ]. Similar policies explain high uptake in Pakistan (mandatory for employment) and China (free, accessible vaccination) [ ].
Moreover, studies carried out among Moroccan healthcare staff also highlight significant gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding HBV [ , , ]. Those persistent gaps could be also responsible for the higher rates of infection seen in countries of moderate-to-high endemicity, characterized by poor vaccine uptake, limited knowledge, and several misconceptions [ ].
The group of traditional practices comprises some 946 individuals corresponding to traditional barbers and their clients, based on two studies carried out in 2001 and 2007. Traditional risk practices are common in developing and underdeveloped countries, especially in rural areas, and include hijama, traditional uvulectomy, traditional dental care, ritual scarification and tattooing, circumcision, and other blood rituals [ ]. In Morocco, the only group studied was that of traditional barbers and their clients, as this is a common practice in certain regions of the country (mostly rural and subrural), exposing both hairdressers and clients to various pathogens [ , ]. The overall prevalence obtained for this group was 1.75% (95% IC: 0.97–2.73). This is a lower prevalence than that reported among barbers and hairdressers, even in non-traditional settings in countries such as Pakistan, Egypt, Nigeria, and Ghana, of 2.1%, 2.2%, 4.2%, 13%, and 14.5%, respectively [ – ]. Despite this lower prevalence compared to other countries, the only two nationwide studies included in this work highlight several concerning factors that could sustain a viral reservoir within this largely unvaccinated population, which shows nearly 30% HBV positivity. Among these risk factors are unfavourable socio-economic conditions, including precarious hygiene practices, and low educational levels ranging from illiteracy to secondary schooling, as well as a general lack of awareness regarding the risks associated with the use of unsterilized instruments. Furthermore, traditional barbers may participate in high-risk procedures (e.g., dental extractions, circumcisions, metal /oral suction, etc.) which underscore the urgent need for targeted strategies integrating preventive measures, health education, and improved access to vaccination to reduce the burden of hepatitis B infection among this group [ , ].
The final group studied included vulnerable high-risk groups. This group, totalling 975 individuals, included patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) or end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing dialysis (peritoneal or haemodialysis), as well as HIV-positive patients. They had the highest overall prevalence, reaching 5.43% (95% CI: 4.06–6.97). This group presents the highest risk of exposure to HBV, with a national rate of cumulative infection (defined by anti-HBc positivity) ranging from 20% to 53% [ , – ]. In subjects with CKD or ESRD, this high risk is explained by the high frequency of hospitalizations, repeated exposure to blood products and invasive procedures such as vascular punctures and multiple transfusions, as well as the reuse or inadequate disinfection of hemodialysis devices, thus favouring nosocomial transmissions [ ]. As for HIV-positive patients, their high risk of exposure to HBV stems mainly from the convergence of the two viruses transmission modes (sexual, blood and perinatal), as well as behavioural factors such as sharing injection equipment, risky sexual practices, etc [ ].
This group is also characterized by an altered immune system [ , ], which is likely to result in an inadequate response to HBV vaccination [ , , ] and potentially more rapid and aggressive disease progression [ , ]. The relatively high prevalence of occult hepatitis B in this group constitutes an additional challenge nationally but also worldwide [ , ], which can increase the risk of escaping from standard serological screening and favour continued transmission of the virus within this particularly vulnerable population as a result. The above factors contribute to higher HBsAg seroprevalences observed worldwide in this category of patients, irrespective of the three endemic zones considered [ , ].
On a national level, the two recent studies included in our review, which assess HBsAg seroprevalence in HIV positive patients, represent the first available estimates for this specific subpopulation in Morocco. The absence of previous data nevertheless limits the possibility of accurately tracking the epidemiological evolution of HBV infection in this group and prevents comparisons essential for epidemiological monitoring. In the case of hemodialysis patients, two regional studies not included in this work, one carried out at the Hassan II University Hospital in Fes and the other involving eight hemodialysis centers in the Fes-Boulemane region, both in 2008, show prevalences ranging between 10.4% and 12.3%. These values exceed those reported in the two more recent studies included in our analysis [ ], but highlight nevertheless the need to improve medical management of this high-risk and particularly vulnerable sub-population, characterized not only by increased exposure to HBV but also by a specific immune susceptibility that could worsen their clinical prognosis and diminish the effectiveness of vaccine strategies.

## Limitations
At the end, it is also important to point out that this study presents several limitations that must be acknowledged to provide guidance and potential improvements for future research focused on hepatitis B at the national level. A key limitation lies in certain methodological constraints: many of the included studies describe their recruitment procedures only briefly and do not always report response-rate information. They also tend to offer limited detail on contextual factors, such as HBsAg prevalence by sex or age group, relevant risk factors, distinctions between first-time and replacement blood donors, clinical profiles of hospitalised patients, and the level of hepatitis B knowledge among healthcare personnel, which could have enriched the analyses.
The second major issue involves the over-representation of men in almost all studies including both sexes, despite the male/female ratio within the Moroccan population being close to equilibrium (around 1) in both urban and rural areas [ ]. In this study, 518,594 individuals with available gender data reveal that only 1/5 of our sample consists of women, indicating a significant over-representation of men in national studies of hepatitis B, particularly since the data suggest a higher seroprevalence of HBsAg among men [ ]. This distortion, along with the lack of results presented by gender, hindered an accurate estimate of prevalence differentiated by this demographic criterion.
Our study also reveals a significant data gap concerning several specific high-risk groups, including prisoners, sex workers, men who have sex with men, people with multiple sexual partners or who have unprotected sex, injecting drug users, infants born to HBV-infected mothers, household members of HBV-infected people, and so on. To make up for these shortcomings, it would be appropriate to encourage studies specifically targeting these populations, with appropriate recruitment strategies, to obtain more representative and reliable data as reported by the third objective of the Moroccan Program, aiming to reduce the new cases of hepatitis B by 50% in 2026 [ ].
The urban or rural origin of participants was not systematically reported in the majority of included studies, and data collection predominantly occurred in urban-based health facilities. Consequently, it remains challenging to rigorously compare HBV prevalence between urban and rural populations. This limitation is particularly significant given that rural regions harbour approximately 37% of Morocco’s population [ ] and continental data suggesting a higher HBsAg prevalence in rural areas [ ], which prevents us to present a proper comparison between these two groups.
Finally, although around a third of our sample is national in scope, the remaining two-thirds mainly concern the central regions (Casablanca, Rabat, Fes, Meknès) as well as certain areas in the south-west (Agadir, Marrakesh). As a result, the northern, eastern, and far southern regions remain insufficiently covered. This limitation hindered our ability to provide a reliable representation of HBsAg prevalence by geographical location, which could have led us to better understand the epidemiological dynamics specific to these regions.

## Conclusion
The pooled prevalence observed across included studies reinforces the hypothesis that Morocco is transitioning towards a low-endemic HBV status, with results more closely aligned with low-endemic countries than with moderate or high-endemic regions. These findings reflect the effective technical, financial, political, educational, human resource, and organizational measures implemented nationally, along with the positive impact of systematic newborn vaccination introduced in 1999. However, as highlighted in the discussion and limitations sections, additional efforts remain necessary to sustain this progress and adequately address the weaknesses observed at the national level in managing HBV infection.
