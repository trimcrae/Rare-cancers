Globally, the median HIV prevalence among MSM was 7.7%, significantly surpassing that of general adult population aged 14–49, which was 0.7% [ ]. The overall prevalence of people living with HIV in China remains relatively low, with the majority of newly reported HIV infections attributed to sexual contact in recent years. The proportion of HIV transmission through homosexual sex increased from 9.1% in 2009 to 23.3% in 2020 [ ]. According to the national AIDS sentinel surveillance data from 2022, the prevalence of MSM living with HIV in China reached 7.0%, with certain regions exceeding 20.0% [ ]. This prevalence exceeded the global median level, signifying a concerning HIV epidemic among MSM in China.
Syphilis is a sexually transmitted disease caused by the Treponema pallidum, which can lead to damage in multiple organs and severe health complications. WHO estimated that approximately 7.1 million adults aged 15 to 49 acquired syphilis in 2020, with a particularly severe impact on key populations like MSM [ ]. A global meta-analysis reported a syphilis infection prevalence of approximately 7.5% among MSM [ ]. Several countries, including New Zealand [ ] and the United States [ ], have witnessed an increase in syphilis infections among MSM. In response, WHO has implemented an array of measures targeting key populations, such as syphilis screening [ ]. However, the implementation of these measures remains suboptimal in many countries, posing a challenge to achieving the goal of reducing new syphilis cases by 90% by 2030 compared to 2020 [ ]. In China, the reported incidence of syphilis has shown an upward trend, rising from 30.93 per 100,000 in 2014 to 38.37 per 100,000 in 2019, with an average annual increase of 4.4% [ ]. Recently, syphilis prevalence among MSM in key regions of China has surpassed 10.0% [ , ], with Shanghai exceeding 20.0% [ ]. The rising prevalence of syphilis infection among MSM in China calls for greater attention.
A mutually reinforcing relationship exists between HIV and syphilis infections. Firstly, HIV infection can weaken the immune system, increasing the risk of acquiring syphilis and other diseases [ ]. Conversely, syphilis infection can lead to mucosal damage and other effects, roughly doubling the risk of HIV infection [ ]. Consequently, HIV/syphilis co-infection represents a significant public health concern attention. In multiple countries, MSM are at particularly high risk of HIV/syphilis co-infection [ , ]. In Asia, the HIV/syphilis co-infection is prevalent among MSM and demonstrates an upward trend [ ]. But in China, more attention has been paid to HIV or syphilis mono-infection among MSM in China, with limited data on co-infection. Thus, we need more updated data on the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM in China to inform and implement targeted measures.
To our knowledge, several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have estimated the prevalence of HIV or syphilis among MSM in China [ , ]. However, there is a notable gap in recent studies specifically focusing on HIV/syphilis co-infection. In response to this critical gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of published literature to ascertain the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM, with the aim of developing targeted public health interventions.

## Methods

## Search strategy and selection criteria
We systematically searched for relevant studies published up to December 31, 2022, in databases including PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang. We used the following keywords in the title and abstract: (MSM OR men who have sex with men OR gay OR bisexual) AND (HIV OR human immunodeficiency virus OR AIDS) AND (syphilis OR sexually transmitted disease OR STD OR STI) AND (China OR Chinese). These keywords were determined by researchers through discussion and reviewing relevant literature. Additionally, we checked the references of included literature to identify any potentially missed studies. All identified studies were managed using EndNote software (Version X9.3.3) to remove duplicates.
Studies meeting the following criteria were included: (1) studies related to HIV or syphilis infection among MSM in China; (2) studies based on a cross-sectional survey methodology. The following studies were excluded: (1) a case report, review, meta-analysis, conference presentation, or PHD/master dissertation; (2) studies involving HIV-positive MSM as subjects; (3) studies with subjects younger than 18 years old; (4) studies with unavailable full texts; (5) studies lacking descriptions of HIV/syphilis testing methods or studies where HIV/syphilis infection was not confirmed through standard laboratory serologic testing; (6) studies where the study period, location, number of co-infected cases, and basic demographic information could not be extracted; (7) duplicate results from the same study. All literature was independently screened by two researchers based on the above criteria, and any disagreements were resolved by a third researcher.

## Quality assessment and data extraction
We assessed quality of the literature using the STROBE checklist [ ], which includes 5 sections: title and abstract, introduction, methods, results, and discussion, with a total of 22 items. Each met item earned 1 point, with possible scores ranging from 0 (lowest quality) to 22 (highest quality). We categorized the quality of studies as either low (score ≤ 11) or high (score > 11). Two researchers independently conducted the quality assessment, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher.
The following information from high-quality studies was independently extracted by two researchers: (1) basic information of the study: first author, publication year, study period, study location, and sampling method; (2) study subject details: recruitment site, sample size, age, marital status, educational level, and condom usage during anal sex in the past 6 months; (3) information on HIV/syphilis co-infection: the number of co-infected individuals.

## Statistical analysis
The pooled prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was estimated using a random-effects model fitting Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation. Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using the I2 statistics and Cochran’s Q test, with the I2 statistics > 50% or Cochran’s Q < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity. Subsequently, we conducted subgroup and meta-regression analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Various variables were considered, including study period (2005–2010, 2011–2016, 2017–2021), region (Northeast, Eastern, Central, Western), sampling method (multiple methods, RDS, snowball sampling, convenience sampling), recruitment site (STD clinics, other sites), age groups (18-, 25-, 30-), proportion of married or cohabiting individuals (< 20%, ≥ 20%), proportion of individuals with high school or less education(< 35%, ≥ 35%), and proportion of individuals reporting consistent condom use during anal sex in the past 6 months (< 50%, ≥ 50%). Each subgroup contained a minimum of 2 studies. To evaluate publication bias, we utilized funnel plots and Egger’s test. Additionally, we conducted sensitivity analysis using a leave-on-out method. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.2.3).

## Results
In the initial research, a total of 1,569 documents were obtained, comprising 362 from PubMed, 271 from Web of Science, 406 from CNKI, 558 from Wanfang, and 8 from other sources. After removing 472 duplicates, we excluded 824 records based on title and abstract screening. Subsequently, we assessed the eligibility of 273 full-text articles, of which 227 were excluded. Finally, 46 records were included in the meta-analysis, with a total sample size of 50,187 MSM and 1,459 co-infected individuals. The flow chart of the study selection is presented in Fig.  .

 

## Characteristics of the included studies
Table  describes the basic characteristics of the included studies. The 46 included studies spanned from 2005 to 2021 and covered four regions of China, encompassing 15 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions. Specifically, the Eastern region included Beijing, Guangdong, Jiangsu, Shanghai, Hebei, and Zhejiang. The Central region comprised Anhui and Henan. The Western region encompassed Chongqing, Sichuan, Gansu, Guangxi, Inner Mongolia, and Xinjiang. And the Northeast region included Liaoning. Study participants were primarily recruited from other sites (such as bars, public baths, parks, online platforms, and VCT clinics) than STD clinic sites, totaling 33 studies. Convenience sampling was the predominant sampling method, utilized in 21 studies. All incorporated studies scored high on quality assessment, with scores ranging from 12 to 20 points (Additional file: Table ).

 

## Pooled prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM
The prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM in China ranged from 0.4 to 13.1%. The pooled prevalence of co-infection was 2.7% (95% CI: 2.2-3.4%), based on the random-effects model. The heterogeneity among the 46 included studies was significant (I2 = 90.5%, Q = 474.65, P < 0.001) (Fig.  ).

 

## Subgroup analysis
Considering that the study period might contribute to heterogeneity, we performed a subgroup analysis based on it. We divided the included studies into 3 groups (2005–2010, 2011–2016, 2017–2021), with 17, 16, and 6 articles, respectively. The pooled co-infection prevalence was 2.7% (95% CI: 1.8-3.8%), 2.6% (95% CI: 2.4-2.9%), and 1.7% (95% CI: 1.4-2.1%) for each group. The differences between subgroups were not significant (P = 0.424). (Table  ).
In accordance with the socioeconomic disparities across different regions in China, a classification was established including 4 regions: Northeast, Eastern, Central, and Western regions. And the meta-analysis included studies with counts of 3, 29, 2, and 12 for the respective regions. The prevalence of co-infection varied across these regions, with the highest prevalence observed in the Central region (4.5%, 95% CI: 0.0-17.7%). Following this, the Eastern region exhibited a prevalence of 3.1% (95% CI: 2.4-3.9%), while the Western region had a prevalence of 2.4% (95% CI: 1.8-3.1%). And the lowest prevalence was found in the Northeast region (0.6%, 95% CI: 0.3-1.0%). (Table  , Fig ).
Sampling methods for the included studies varied from multiple sampling methods, RDS, snowball sampling, and convenience sampling. We estimated the pooled prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection separately. The results demonstrated that 7 studies using multiple sampling methods had the highest co-infection prevalence of 4.7% (95% CI: 3.6-6.0%). There were 6 studies using RDS with a co-infection prevalence of 2.9% (95% CI: 1.8-4.3%) and 9 studies using snowball sampling with a co-infection prevalence of 1.4% (95% CI: 0.9-2.0%). The most prevalent sampling method was convenience sampling, with a total of 21 studies. Their pooled prevalence of co-infection was 2.6% (95% CI:2.4-2.8%). (Table  , Fig ).
We conducted a subgroup analysis based on whether the study participants were recruited from STD clinics. The results showed that there were 2 studies recruiting participants from STD clinics with an extremely high co-infection prevalence of 9.1% (95% CI: 3.2-17.6%). In contrast, a total of 33 studies recruited participants from other sites with a lower co-infection prevalence of 2.8% (95% CI: 2.2-3.4%). (Table  , Fig ).
We divided the studies into three age groups based on the median age of the study participants and estimated the pooled prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection. The results revealed a gradual increase in co-infection prevalence with age. Comprising 16 studies, the 30- age group exhibited the highest co-infection prevalence 4.0% (95% CI: 2.7-5.5%). Consisting of 13 studies, the 25- age group showed a prevalence of 2.6% (95% CI: 1.9-3.3%). Consisting of 13 studies, the 25- age group showed a prevalence of 0.8% (95% CI: 0.4-1.2%). (Table  , Fig ).
We categorized the included studies into two groups based on the proportion of individuals with a high school education or less. The < 35% group was consisted of 6 studies, and it reported a lower co-infection prevalence of 1.6% (95% CI: 1.0-2.5%). While the ≥ 35% group included 34 studies, and it had a higher co-infection prevalence of 2.8% (95% CI: 2.2-3.4%). (Table  , Fig ).
We also performed a subgroup analysis based on the proportion of participants who used condoms consistently during anal sex in the past 6 months, dividing the included studies into two groups: <50% and ≥ 50% groups. These two groups contained 15 and 7 studies, respectively. In the < 50% group, the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection was 2.8% (95% CI: 1.7-4.1%), which was higher than the prevalence in the ≥ 50% group (1.5%, 95% CI: 1.0-2.1%). (Table  , Fig ).

 

## Meta-regression analysis
We included all factors analyzed in subgroup analysis in multivariate meta-regression analysis. The results revealed that the heterogeneity for HIV/syphilis co-infection prevalence can be significantly attributed to the region, sampling method, recruitment site, and age (Table  ). Compared to the Northeast region, the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection increased by 0.100 units (95% CI: 0.033, 0.168) in the Eastern region, 0.138 units (95% CI: 0.035, 0.242) in the Central region, and 0.080 units (95% CI: 0.008, 0.152) in the Western region. On the contrary, compared to studies using multiple methods, the co-infection prevalence decreased by 0.098 units (95% CI: -0.154, -0.432) in those using snowball sampling method and 0.052 units (95% CI: -0.101, -0.004) in those using convenience sampling, respectively. And the co-infection prevalence decreased by 0.140 units (95% CI: -0.218, -0.062) among MSM recruited from other sites compared to MSM recruited from STD clinics. Moreover, compared to the age group of 18-, the co-infection prevalence increased by 0.064 units (95% CI: 0.005, 0.124) for the age group of 25-, and 0.104 units (95% CI: 0.047, 0.162) for the age group of 30-, respectively.

 

## Publication bias and sensitivity analysis
We created a funnel plot and conducted the Egger’s test to assess publication bias. Both results indicated no significant publication bias (t = 0.40, P = 0.693) for HIV/syphilis co-infection (Fig.  ). Then, we performed sensitivity analysis using a leave-on-out method. After excluding each study one by one, the pooled prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection showed no significant changes, indicating that the results of meta-analysis were stable (Additional file: Table ).

 

## Discussion
To our knowledge, HIV/syphilis co-infection poses significant health risks to individuals. For one thing, both diseases could impair individuals’ immune system, and the disease progression among co-infected individuals may be more rapid and aggressive [ ]. For another, HIV/syphilis co-infection can accelerate HIV replication, resulting in higher viral loads and increasing risk of HIV transmission [ , ]. Additionally, co-infection may cause neurological and cardiovascular complications, further contributing to the overall disease burden [ ]. Moreover, the treatment of co-infected cases is more complex compared to mono-infected individuals. There is a higher likelihood of syphilis treatment failure and re-infection among those with HIV/syphilis co-infection compared to HIV-negative individuals [ – ].
In this meta-analysis, we systematically reviewed 46 eligible studies involving a total of 50,187 individuals. Considering the heterogeneity across studies, a random-effects model was applied to estimate the pooled prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection in our study. Our analysis revealed that the HIV/syphilis co-infection prevalence among MSM in China was 2.7%, exceeding that reported in other Asia countries, such as Thailand (2.1%) [ ] and India (2.4%) [ ]. This finding uncovered the relatively severe burden of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM in China, emphasizing the pressing need for comprehensive preventive and control measures in this population.
In the subgroup and meta-regression analyses, we found notable regional disparities in the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM. The lowest prevalence was found in the Northeast region (0.6%), while the Central region had the highest(4.5%). It’s essential to acknowledge that these disparities may be influenced by the limited number of included studies in the above regions, which may not be fully representative. For instance, the Northeast region was represented by only 3 studies, all conducted in Liaoning province. Similarly, despite there are 6 provinces in the Central region, our meta-analysis extracted data from just 2 studies conducted in Anhui and Henan. Moreover, the studies of the Eastern region predominantly concentrated on the southeastern coastal area, which was known for higher HIV and syphilis infection rates [ ], likely contributing to the higher prevalence of co-infection within this subgroup. Additionally, previous studies have shown that STD clinics visits were characterized by insufficient knowledge regarding HIV and STDs [ , ]. As a result, the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM recruited from STD clinics was much higher (9.1%) than among those recruited from other sites (2.8%).
The HIV/syphilis co-infection prevalence among MSM in China increased with age, peaking at 4.0% within the 30- age group. This trend can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, older MSM often exhibit lower risk awareness [ ] and a higher prevalence of risky behaviors [ ], which collectively elevated the risk of co-infection in this demographic. Secondly, younger individuals tend to be more open to adopting novel strategies and are more inclined to utilize unconventional HIV prevention and control measures such as pre-exposure and post-exposure prophylaxis [ – ]. These measures effectively mitigate the risk of HIV infection, subsequently reducing the co-infection. Additionally, several investigations have reported a higher prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM with less education [ , ]. Our subgroup analysis confirmed this, showing that the co-infection prevalence was higher in the group with a higher proportion of individuals with a high school education or less (2.8%) compared to the group of individuals with higher levels of literacy (1.6%). This suggested that lower literacy might be a facilitator of HIV/syphilis co-infection in MSM.
Both HIV and syphilis can be transmitted through sexual contact, and the consistent use of condoms during sex is an effective measure to reduce the risk of infection [ , ]. In our subgroup analysis, it was evident that the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection was lower among individuals who reported a higher proportion of consistent condom use during anal sex in the past 6 months, consistent with previous research findings. The Chinese CDC issued the Guidelines for HIV Prevention and Interventions for MSM in 2016, explicitly advocating for the promotion of condom usage among MSM [ ]. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that MSM’s pursuit of sexual pleasure, coupled with inadequate awareness of self-protection has resulted in a low proportion of consistent condom use during anal sex [ , ]. A study conducted in the United States demonstrated the effectiveness of social media-based interventions in reducing the occurrence of unprotected anal sex [ ]. Therefore, moving forward, it is imperative to leverage the full potential of social media platforms for promoting condom use among MSM.
Testing serves as a crucial means of identifying co-infected individuals. However, HIV and syphilis testing rates among MSM in China was low, influenced by various factors [ ]. As a result, many co-infected individuals may go undetected. Several studies have proposed that the implementation of HIV/syphilis dual self-testing could enhance testing rates among MSM, thereby reducing the risk of HIV and syphilis infection in this population [ , ]. Hence, the promotion of HIV/syphilis dual self-testing could prove instrumental in mitigating co-infections among MSM. Moreover, certain biomedical prevention measures have demonstrated effectiveness in preventing HIV and syphilis infection among MSM. For instance, HIV pre-exposure and post-exposure prophylaxis could reduce the risk of HIV infection by more than 80.0% [ , ], and doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis could reduce the risk of syphilis infection by 73.0% in MSM [ ]. Consequently, advocating for unconventional HIV and syphilis prevention measures should be considered as a crucial step in the prevention of co-infection.
There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, the included studies were conducted across 15 provinces, municipalities and autonomous regions, with over half of provinces in China not included. Thus, the findings of this meta-analysis may not fully represent the overall status of China. Secondly, the primary sampling methods employed in the included studies were convenience sampling, potentially introducing selection bias. Thirdly, the included spanned a wide period, with the majority predating 2017, which may not reflect the current situation. The result of subgroup analysis, which suggested that the study period may not be the source of heterogeneity, might also be influenced by the factors mentioned above. Fourthly, in the subgroup and meta-regression analyses, we included a limited number of variables and primarily centered around factors that might be directly associated with co-infection, including demographic factors and condom usage during anal intercourse. This could lead to the potential omission of variables leading to heterogeneity. Additionally, variability in serological testing methods and their sensitivities could contribute to heterogeneity. However, due to the diversity in testing methods used across the included studies, a detailed analysis of these was not feasible.

## Conclusions
In our study, it came to light that the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM in China was at a relatively high level. The co-infection was notably pronounced in the Eastern region, among STD clinics attendees, and in older individuals. In the foreseeable future, prioritized interventions should focus on these groups, intensifying efforts in preventing and controlling HIV/syphilis co-infection. This entails the widespread promotion of condom utilization and implementation of unconventional preventive measures, all with the overarching goal of mitigating the prevalence of HIV/syphilis co-infection among MSM and enhancing the overall health outcomes of this population.
