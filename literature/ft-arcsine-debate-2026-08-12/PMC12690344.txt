## Objective
To synthesise data evaluating the diagnostic utility of prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) in intermediate‐risk prostate cancer (PCa), as its role in intermediate‐risk PCa remains uncertain.

## Methods
A systematic search was conducted for studies evaluating PSMA PET/CT in primary staging of newly diagnosed European Association of Urology (EAU)/National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) intermediate‐risk, or Gleason Score 7 (International Society of Urological Pathology [ISUP] Grade Group [GG] 2–3) PCa. The primary outcome was overall positive scans (lymph nodes or distant metastases). We also secondarily analysed diagnostic accuracy measuring sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value.

## Results
In total, 16 studies reported positivity rates ranging from 2.2% to 50.0% in intermediate‐risk PCa. A pooled analysis of 13 studies found a PSMA PET/CT positivity rate of 9% (95% confidence interval [CI] 6–11%). In ISUP GG 2 PCa, positivity ranged from 2.2% to 21.4%, compared to 13.6–33.3% in ISUP GG 3 PCa (across two studies). The overall nodal and metastatic sensitivity of PSMA PET/CT was low in intermediate‐risk men, 33% (95% CI 14–60%). Limitations included small sample sizes, retrospective designs, and limited histopathological confirmation.

## Conclusion
For primary staging in intermediate‐risk PCa PSMA PET/CT showed a low yield. Limited data exist to guide its use in favourable vs unfavourable subgroups. Robust, prospective studies are needed to define its role in clinical decision‐making, further prognostic benefits, and to inform future guidelines.

## Diagnostic Accuracy
There were seven studies included in the analysis that reported on sensitivity (Fig.  ) and specificity of PSMA PET/CT in intermediate‐risk PCa (Fig.  ). The overall sensitivity of PSMA PET/CT was low in intermediate‐risk men, 33% (95% CI 14–60%); however, specificity was high at 95% (95% CI 92–97%; Fig.  ).
 

## Subgroup Analyses
Two studies reported PSMA PET/CT positivity stratified by ISUP GG. Evangelista et al. [ ] found higher positivity rates in ISUP GG 3 compared to GG 2 for both nodal (13.6% vs 2.2%) and metastatic (13.6% vs 5.4%) staging. Similarly, Moradi et al. [ ] reported higher positivity in ISUP GG 3 (33.3%) than GG 2 (21.4%). This suggests increased PSMA PET/CT detection in higher‐grade disease; however, histopathological confirmation is lacking.
Studies using lymph node histopathology as the reference standard included Özman et al. [ ] (11.7%), Yaxley et al. [ ] (10.5%), Kopp et al. [ ] (7.7%), Dekalo et al. [ ] (4.5%), Incesu et al. [ ] (8.9%), Cytawa et al. [ ] (9.4%), and Amiel et al. [ ] (6.3%). Studies with partial or no histopathological confirmation reported more variable rates, such as Ergül et al. [ ] (38.9%) and Simsek et al. [ ] (3.7%), highlighting the importance of consistent reference standards for accurate interpretation.

## Discussion
The key findings of this review were: (i) PSMA PET/CT has a low yield for primary staging in intermediate‐risk PCa with low positivity rates, (ii) insufficient evidence exists to support the routine use of PSMA PET/CT for primary staging in both favourable and unfavourable intermediate‐risk PCa, and (iii) limited data suggest that even when PSMA PET/CT is positive in intermediate‐risk PCa, its low sensitivity means it may miss small‐volume nodal metastases, potentially under‐staging disease.
Our findings underscore the limited diagnostic yield of PSMA PET/CT in this patient population, particularly when compared to higher‐risk cohorts. The proPSMA trial was instrumental in demonstrating the benefits of PSMA PET/CT over CVI for staging intermediate‐ to high‐risk PCa (ISUP GG ≥3) [ ]. In this study, PSMA PET‐CT in 148 men detected pelvic nodal disease in 30 (20%), abdominal nodal metastases in 13 (9%), bone metastases in 15 (10%), and visceral metastases in one (1%) [ ]. The relatively low rate of detectable metastatic disease in our intermediate‐risk cohort suggests that PSMA PET/CT in this group may lead to overuse of resources without significant clinical benefit. This reinforces the need for more selective imaging criteria and clearer guidance on which intermediate‐risk patients are most likely to benefit from routine PSMA PET/CT for primary staging.
Sensitivity was found to be low across the studies included in this analysis. This is similar to other studies in the literature, where sensitivity may fall short [ ], and this could be attributed to the large distribution of intermediate‐risk cases in the current literature, as well as the challenge of PSMA PET/CT detecting micro‐metastatic disease (lymph nodes <5 mm) [ ]. This constraint is especially relevant in intermediate‐risk PCa, where the volume of metastatic spread is typically lower than in high‐risk disease.
Intermediate‐risk disease encompasses a large spectrum of biological aggressiveness and therefore there may still be a subset where staging using PSMA PET/CT is warranted. These findings underscore the importance of stratifying results by ISUP GG, as PSMA PET/CT appears more likely to detect disease outside the prostate in GG 3 cancers than in GG 2. Yaxley et al. [ ] reported a statistically significant correlation between identifiable metastases on PSMA PET/CT and an increasing ISUP GG (specifically ≥4). However, information regarding the role of PSMA PET/CT in staging ISUP GG 3 is minimal. Current guidelines only weakly differentiate between the recommended use of PSMA PET/CT in primary staging of favourable and unfavourable intermediate‐risk PCa [ ]. This was also demonstrated by the Esen et al. [ ] study, which concluded that PSMA PET/CT is accurate for lymph node staging but did not distinguish between its use in ISUP GG 2 and GG 3 PCa in their discussions. Despite this, PSMA PET/CT is publicly funded in Australia for primary staging of all intermediate‐risk PCa where available [ ], despite the limited evidence [ ]. Unique to Evangelista et al. [ ], including this scan in the diagnostic evaluation resulted in the management plan of 13% of cases to be altered (based on multidisciplinary input). Major changes (e.g., radiotherapy to systemic therapy, or surgery to systemic therapy) were more frequently seen in the unfavourable intermediate‐risk group, compared to the favourable group. These findings are not consistent throughout the literature as other studies such as Simsek et al. [ ] have suggested a low utility for staging intermediate‐risk disease. Although there is a lack of consensus among current guidelines, PSMA PET/CT may still have utility in intermediate‐risk PCa. Given the high cost and morbidity of pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) (complications including lymphocele, venous thromboembolism, and infection), PSMA PET/CT presents an alternative non‐invasive method for staging patients with intermediate‐risk PCa. The NPV of PSMA PET/CT is higher in patients who have a low risk of lymph node spread [ ]; suggesting these patients may be excluded from unnecessary PLND based on their PSMA PET/CT scan. However, the use of PSMA PET/CT to select men for PLND still remains controversial due to its low sensitivity, inevitably resulting in some men with lymph nodes involvement being excluded from PLND [ ]. However, given that there is no proven oncological benefit to overall survival or progression‐free survival, the benefits of performing PLND as a staging procedure remains dubious [ ].
Furthermore, PSMA PET/CT may also have a role in prognosticating and detecting PCa that was under‐graded at time of initial biopsy. There is growing evidence that a higher maximum standardised uptake value (SUVmax) on PSMA PET/CT is correlated with more aggressive disease, including a higher Gleason Grade Group and extra‐prostatic disease [ ]. Specifically, an SUVmax >11 in a Gleason 3 + 4 biopsy has been associated with a 90% chance of being upgraded to Gleason 4 + 3 or a higher grade on final histopathology following radical prostatectomy [ ]. Given up to 27% of men may be under‐graded on initial MRI‐targeted biopsy, this together suggests that PSMA PET/CT may have a role to play in detecting men who were under‐graded at time of initial biopsy [ , ]. Additionally, MRI may miss up to 20% of clinically significant PCa, and recent evidence from the PRIMARY trial (ANZCTR identifier: ACTRN12618001640291) has demonstrated that PSMA PET/CT combined with MRI can improve the detection of clinically significant PCa [ , ]. Given the move towards using active surveillance as a treatment modality for favourable intermediate‐risk PCa, this suggests PSMA PET may also be a useful tool in detecting and risk‐stratifying men that may have been under‐graded on initial biopsy. The PASPorT trial (ANZCTR identifier: ACTRN12622000188730) [ ] has been the first to assess the utility of PSMA PET/CT in active surveillance. Currently there are two ongoing prospective trials assessing its utility in this setting [ , ]. However until the evidence base is firmly established, the use of PSMA PET/CT in this setting remains experimental. [ , , ].
This review highlights the lack of clear guidance and consistent data on the role of PSMA PET/CT in stratifying favourable vs unfavourable intermediate‐risk PCa, despite growing recognition of their distinct clinical trajectories. Current guidelines, such as those from the EAU and the AUA, broadly recommend consideration of PSMA PET/CT for staging intermediate‐ and high‐risk PCa, but do not differentiate between these subgroups in imaging recommendations [ , , ]. Only one study in our review, Maestroni et al. [ ] provided direct comparative data for PSMA PET/CT in both favourable and unfavourable intermediate‐risk groups, using histopathological confirmation as the reference standard. Unexpectedly, they reported a positivity rate of 22% in favourable intermediate‐risk PCa vs 10% in the unfavourable group. This finding is not consistent with what would be expected biologically given the greater tumour burden and biological aggressiveness typically associated with unfavourable disease. However, the small sample size (n = 19), with only two positive cases in the favourable group, limits the utility of these data. This outlier result underscores a broader issue: heterogeneity within intermediate‐risk PCa. Favourable intermediate‐risk PCa includes men with ISUP GG 1 but higher PSA (10–20 ng/mL) or higher clinical stage (T2b–c), while unfavourable risk includes ISUP GG 3 or multiple adverse features [ ].
There are limitations in this review that need to be considered when evaluating the results. The majority of studies are of low quality and retrospective design, which introduces a number of biases. Not all of the included studies had histopathological confirmation of which makes assessing the diagnostic accuracy challenging. High‐risk morphological features of PCa on histology, such as cribriform or intraductal carcinoma, are associated with increasing disease aggressiveness [ ] and the role of PSMA PET/CT in staging patients with intermediate‐risk PCa was not assessed in these studies. Cribriform pattern has been correlated with higher Gleason 4 ratios and increased SUVmax on PSMA PET/CT [ ] and may have a future role in identifying PCa characteristics. Future studies should prioritise prospective, multicentre designs with adequate sample sizes that include histopathological correlation as the ‘gold standard’ for nodal involvement and metastatic disease. Without such validation, the diagnostic performance of PSMA PET/CT in this specific risk group remains uncertain. Uniform imaging protocols and positivity criteria are also needed. Differences in radiotracer selection, PET acquisition protocols, reader experience, and interpretation thresholds likely contribute to heterogeneity in the reported performance of PSMA PET/CT (Table  ). Lastly, future research should investigate how PSMA PET/CT findings alter management decisions and long‐term outcomes in this population. Very few studies have evaluated whether the use of PSMA PET/CT in intermediate‐risk patients meaningfully changes treatment intent, surgical planning, or improves survival outcomes. Assessing the clinical utility, not just diagnostic accuracy, will be critical in determining whether PSMA PET/CT should be incorporated into guidelines for this group.

## Conclusion
The use of PSMA PET/CT in primary staging of intermediate‐risk PCa is hindered by its low sensitivity (33%) and the low rates of nodal and metastatic disease (9%) within the patient cohort. This is especially the case in favourable‐intermediate risk disease. However, intermediate‐risk disease represents a large biological spectrum of PCa and the yield appears to be higher with unfavourable intermediate‐risk disease. Despite the low positivity rates, PSMA PET/CT may have additional prognostic benefits in this patient subgroup that require further exploration including, detecting under‐graded favourable intermediate‐risk PCa, providing an alternative to invasive PLND staging, and in risk stratification when selecting eligible patients for active surveillance. The overall level of evidence is low, and future studies should focus on this subset of men to more accurately define the diagnostic accuracy of PSMA PET/CT in primary staging of unfavourable‐intermediate risk disease.
