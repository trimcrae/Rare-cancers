## Abstract

## Background
The burden of acute kidney injury (AKI) in low- and middle-income countries is frequently underestimated, often due to limited diagnostic resources and underreporting. Information about the burden of AKI would help inform the implementation of health policies and improve the allocation of health resources aimed at enhancing patient outcomes. This study determined the pooled incidence of AKI and associated mortality in Nigeria.

## Method
A systematic search of PubMed, African Journals Online (AJOL), and Google Scholar was conducted to identify relevant studies on AKI in both adult and paediatric populations in Nigeria between 1980 and 2024. Case reports, case series, and abstracts without full text on AKI were excluded. The pooled incidence of AKI and the associated all-cause mortality rate were estimated using meta-analytic methods. The Joanna-Briggs scoring was used for risk bias assessment. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024620822).

## Results
The initial systematic literature search identified 384 articles from which 44 eligible hospital-based studies involving 123,324 participants were included. The median age of the study participants was 31.1 years, and the median proportion of females was 42.9%. The overall pooled incidence of AKI in this study was 15.0% [CI: 12.0–18.0%], based on 44 studies, with I2 = 99.3% and p < 0.001. The pooled incidence of AKI in the adult and paediatric populations was 18.0% [CI: 12.0%-25.0%, n = 22 studies, I2 = 99.0%, p < 0.001], and 12.0% [CI: 9.0%-15.0%, n = 20 studies, I2 = 99.2%, p < 0.001], respectively. The leading aetiologies of AKI were hypovolaemia (32.3%), sepsis (28.8%), malaria (12.8%), and acute glomerulonephritis (11.5%). The most common criteria used in diagnosing AKI were those outlined in the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines in 27 studies (58.7%). There was a significant difference in the pooled incidence of AKI with the different diagnostic criteria (p < 0.001). About 46.0% of the AKI population presented in stage 3, and haemodialysis was the kidney replacement therapy option in 76.2% of those dialysed. The overall pooled all-cause mortality rate in patients with AKI was 26.0% [CI: 19.0%-33.0%], n = 34 studies, I2 = 92.6%, p < 0.001, 34 studies.

## Limitations of the study
The review included only hospital-based studies, and high heterogeneity was observed in the studies. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution.

## Conclusion
The incidence and mortality rates of AKI in Nigeria are high. Prompt and adequate treatment of the underlying and preventable causes may reduce the disease burden.

## Introduction
Acute kidney injury (AKI) is the sudden decline in kidney function characterised by a reduction in urine output or a steady rise in serum creatinine or both [ ]. It is associated with an increased rate of mortality, morbidity, and hospitalisation [ ]. Globally, AKI affects about 13.3 million people, with about 75–80% of cases recorded in developing countries [ , ]. In low and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Nigeria, AKI is mostly community-acquired, and young adults are mostly affected [ , ]. In addition, sepsis, obstetric and gynaecological causes are the leading aetiologies [ , ]. This is different from what is obtainable in developed countries, where AKI is mostly hospital-acquired, commonly found in the older age groups in the intensive care unit or post-surgery [ , ].
In Nigeria, the mortality rate associated with AKI is alarmingly high [ , , ]. Despite the considerable burden of AKI, related deaths are largely preventable and treatable, especially in populations where access to healthcare is poor. Hence, in 2013, the International Society of Nephrology (ISN) AKI initiative of zero by 2025 (“0 by 25”) was launched with the overall aim of reducing the burden associated with AKI and improving overall outcomes of AKI, especially in low-resource settings [ ].
The outcomes of AKI are dependent on the age of the individual, the aetiology of AKI, the presence of underlying medical conditions, the setting in which AKI occurred, the time of presentation, the promptness of diagnosis and the institution of appropriate management, as well as the availability, accessibility, and affordability of kidney replacement therapy (KRT) [ , ]. The long-term consequences of AKI include increased risk of recurrent AKI, development or worsening of chronic kidney disease, systemic hypertension, myocardial infarction, strokes, and death [ , ].
Despite the disproportionately higher burden of AKI in LMICs like Nigeria, comprehensive nationwide estimates of AKI incidence and mortality are unavailable [ ]. Therefore, this study determined the pooled incidence of AKI and associated mortality in Nigeria. The information from this systematic review will be highly valuable in advocating for health policies and better resource allocation that will reduce the overall burden of AKI in Nigeria.

## Methods
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guideline was used as a template for reporting this systematic review [ ]. A systematic search using PubMed, African Journals Online (AJOL), and Google Scholar was used to identify related studies. Searches were done and selected independently by two researchers using predetermined criteria. The inclusion criteria for this study were cross-sectional, prospective, and retrospective studies on AKI conducted in Nigeria, encompassing both the adult and pediatric populations between 1980 and 2024. Case reports, case series, abstracts without full text on AKI, and studies on AKI that did not report AKI prevalence were excluded. There were no language restrictions. For this review, studies involving individuals who were18 years and above were categorised as studies in the adult population. In contrast, those that involved those below were categorised as studies in the paediatric population. The search terms are detailed in Supplementary Table .
The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna-Briggs Institute (JBI) 2017 Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies [ ]. The JBI scoring system uses a checklist of yes/no for nine questions. A score of 1 is given for a yes response and 0 for a no response. The total score obtainable is 9. A score of 7–9 indicates high quality, 4–6 indicates medium quality, and 0–3 indicates poor quality [ ]. A random-effects (DerSimonian-Laird) model was used to pool study-specific estimates and obtain an overall estimate of AKI incidence, after stabilising the variance of the study-specific estimates using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation [ , ]. Additionally, we undertook subgroup analyses comparing summary estimates according to AKI definitions, clinical settings where AKI occurred, adult versus pediatric AKI populations, and all-cause mortality associated with in-hospital AKI. To assess and adjust for potential publication bias, we visually inspected the funnel plot for asymmetry, statistically tested with both Egger and Begg tests, and applied the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method to estimate the number of missing studies and obtain an adjusted pooled effect estimate [ ]. Subgroup analysis was performed using the Q-test based on ANOVA. The I2 statistic was used to determine the between-study heterogeneity.
Random-effects meta-regression was performed on Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine–transformed AKI incidence estimates using binomial within-study variance. Geographic zone, age category, female proportion, study setting, and AKI diagnostic criteria were evaluated as study-level moderators. The study sample sizealuated separately as a marker of small-study effects, rather than being included in the primary meta-regression, as sample size is not a substantive determinant of AKI incidence but may reflect reporting and selection biases.
Statistical analysis was performed with Stata 19.5 (Stata Corp., 2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release 19.5, College Station, Texas, USA).

## Results

## Study selection and characteristics
The systematic literature search identified 384 articles between 1980 and 2024. A total of 44 articles [ – ] were eligible and included in this systematic review (Fig. showing the flow chart of literature screening). The included studies reported on 123,324 study participants across Nigeria.
 The sample size of the component studies ranged from 40 [ ] to 46,354 participants [ ], with the proportion of females ranging from 16.0% [ ] to 100.0% [ , , ]. The mean age of the participants ranged from 1 day [ ] to 56.9 years [ ]. The median age of the study participants was 31.1 years, while the median proportion of females was 42.9%.
Twenty (45.5%) of the studies were in the adult population [ , , , , – , , , , – , , , , , , , ], 22(50%) were in the paediatric population [ , – , , , – , , , , , , , , , ]. In comparison, the remaining two studies (4.5%) were a combination of both paediatric and adult populations [ , ] There were 17,365 adults, 105,302 children, and 657 persons in studies comprising both adult and paediatric populations. The leading causes of AKI were hypovolaemia (32.3%), sepsis (28.8%), malaria (12.8%), and acute glomerulonephritis (11.5%). The standard criteria used in the diagnosis of AKI were KDIGO in 27 studies (58.7%), oliguria or a rise in serum creatinine in 8 studies (17.4%), pRIFLE in 5 studies (10.9%), and RIFLE in 4 studies (8.7%). Among the patients with AKI, 33.4% were in stage 1, 20.4% in stage 2, and 46.2% in stage 3. Approximately 32% of those who required KRT could not be dialysed, mainly due to financial constraints (Table , showing the basic characteristics of the included studies). The majority of the studies were of high quality (39 studies, 88.6%). Supplementary Table shows information about the methodological quality of the studies.
 

## Pooled AKI incidence by age group
The overall pooled incidence of AKI in this study was 15.0% (95% CI: 12.0%-18.0%), n = 44 studies, I2 = 99.3%, p < 0.001). The pooled incidence of AKI in the adult population was 18.0% (95% CI: 12.0%-25.0%, n = 20 studies, I2 = 99.0%, p < 0.001), and the pediatric population was 12.0% (95% CI: 9.0%-15.0%, n = 22 studies, I2 = 99.2%, p < 0.001). The pooled incidence of AKI in studies that combined both adult and pediatric populations was 15.0% (95% CI: 11.0%-19.0%, n = 2 studies, I2 = 39.5%, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in the pooled incidence of AKI between the adult and paediatric populations, p = 0.13. (Figure showing the pooled incidence of AKI by age group)
 

## Pooled AKI incidence by aetiology
The pooled incidence of medical-related AKI was 23.0% (95% CI:15.0%-32.0%, n = 11 studies I2 = 95.1%, p < 0.001); obstetric AKI was 17.0% (95% CI:4.0%-35.0%, n = 3 studies, I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001) and surgery-associated AKI was 23.0% (95% CI: (17.0%-29.0%, n = 3 studies, I2 = 73.5%, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in the incidence of AKI across the specialities in the adult population (p = 0.79). (Figure showing the pooled incidence of AKI based on speciality)
 The incidence of AKI in those with Lassa fever was 20.0% (95% CI: (14.0%-27.0%, n = 7 studies, I2 = 89.7%, p < 0.001). (Supplementary Figure : The pooled incidence of AKI based on aetiology).

## Pooled AKI incidence by diagnostic criteria
The incidence of AKI in the constituent studies varied widely, ranging from 1.0% to 54.0%, according to the criteria used. The pooled incidence of AKI in studies that used KDIGO criteria was 18.0% [95% CI:13.0%-24.0%], n = 26 studies; I2 = 99.2%, p < 0.001; oliguria and serum creatinine was 6.0% [95% CI: 4.0%-10.0%], n = 6 studies, I2 = 98.3%, p < 0.001; paediatric RIFLE was 10.0% [95% CI: 3.0%-21.0%], n = 4 studies, I2 = 96.9%, p < 0.001; RIFLE was 13.0% [95% CI: 3.0%-28.0%], n = 3 studies I2 = 99.7% p < 0.001 and serum creatinine was 20.0% [95% CI: 7.0%-37.0%], n = 2 studies, I2 = 93.3% p < 0.001, (Figure showing the pooled incidence of AKI based on diagnostic criteria). There was a significant difference in the pooled incidence of AKI with the different diagnostic criteria (p < 0.001).
 Further sensitivity analyses performed on studies with a sample size of 100 patients or more, regardless of AKI diagnostic criteria (Supplementary Figure ), or with KDIGO criteria alone (Supplementary Figure ), yielded a pooled AKI incidence of 14.0% (11.0–16.0%) and 15.0% (10.0–20.0%), respectively. Neither figure deviated significantly from the overall pooled incidence of AKI.

## Pooled AKI incidence across geographical regions
There was no significant difference in the pooled AKI incidence across the six geopolitical zones of Nigeria (p = 0.16), as shown in Supplementary Figure .

## Publication bias
Both Egger and Begg tests suggested the presence of potential publication bias (p < 0.001 for both tests). After applying the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method to adjust for possible publication bias, seven additional studies were imputed, resulting in a revised overall pooled incidence of 10.7% (95% CI: 8.6%–13.0%). The corresponding trim-and-fill funnel plot is presented in Supplementary Figure showing the trim and fill funnel plot.

## Meta-regression
In random-effects meta-regression of 44 studies using Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine–transformed AKI incidence, study design emerged as the dominant source of between-study heterogeneity. Studies with extended follow-up periods (spanning multiple years) reported significantly lower AKI incidence compared with time-limited studies (coefficient −0.60, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.46; p < 0.001) (Table ). After accounting for study design, no independent associations were observed for age category (pediatric vs. adult/mixed), clinical speciality (medical, surgical, or obstetric), or AKI diagnostic criteria (KDIGO vs. non-KDIGO). Geographic zone showed a modest, non-significant trend toward lower AKI incidence in northern regions compared with southern regions.
 Overall, this model explained approximately 43% of between-study heterogeneity (τ2 reduced from 0.21 to 0.035), although substantial residual heterogeneity remained (I2 ≈ 99%), consistent with the multifactorial nature of AKI incidence in observational studies.
In a sensitivity meta-regression that included study sample size (per 1,000 participants), larger studies reported significantly lower AKI incidence, consistent with a small-study effect. This pattern suggests systematic differences in reported incidence by study size (possibly due to selection or reporting bias) and was therefore presented as a supplementary analysis (Supplementary Table ), rather than as a substantive determinant of AKI incidence.

## Pooled AKI-Associated all-cause mortality
The overall pooled mortality rate in this study was 26.0% [CI: 19.0%-33.0%], I2 = 92.6%, n = 34 studies, I2 = 92.6%, p < 0.001. The mortality rate in patients with AKI in the studies ranged from 0% to 76%. The pooled mortality rates in the adult and paediatric patients with AKI in this study were 34% and 16%, respectively. ## Discussion
This study determined the aetiologies, the pooled incidence, and associated mortality of AKI in Nigeria and found that the overall pooled incidence and all-cause mortality rate of AKI in Nigeria were 15% and 26%, respectively. The identified common aetiologies were hypovolaemia, sepsis, malaria, and acute glomerulonephritis.
The incidence of AKI across the various geopolitical regions was not significantly different. This may be a reflection of the similarity in risk factors for AKI, socioeconomic characteristics and challenges facing the health system in other regions of Nigeria. The overall pooled incidence of AKI in this study is lower than the 19.3–25.2% reported in some high-income countries, possibly due to better diagnostic facilities and reporting systems and availability of sophisticated health services to care for critically ill patients who are more likely to have AKI [ ]. However, it is similar to the 12.4% and 13.4% reported in Côte d’Ivoire and in East Africa, respectively [ , ]. It is lower than the 23.5% reported in central Africa [ ]. The difference in incidence rates observed across different African countries or regions may be due to variations in the criteria used for diagnosing AKI, the promptness of presentation, and the method of diagnosis in the hospital.
The incidence of medical-related AKI, surgery-related AKI, and obstetric AKI in the adult populations was 23.0%, 23.0% and 17.0%, respectively. The incidence of obstetric AKI is similar to the 15.6% reported by Jalo et al. [ ] in a systematic review conducted in sub-Saharan Africa. It is higher than the 2% reported by Trakarnvanich et al. [ ] This and vast difference may be partly explained by the fact that only two out of thirty-one studies included in the systematic review by Trakarnvanich et al. [ ] were from Africa, where the burden of obstetric complications that can lead to AKI remains very high. Some factors contributing to the high incidence of obstetric AKI in Nigeria and other African countries are the high prevalence of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, obstetric haemorrhage, and inadequate antenatal care among pregnant women [ – ].
The incidence of surgery-associated AKI in this study is higher than the 8.7% and 4.2% reported in those who had urological and general abdominal surgeries, respectively [ ]. It is lower than the 34.6% reported in those who had cardiac surgeries [ ]. The incidence of AKI in surgical patients may vary with the type of surgery, severity of the clinical condition of the patients, and presence of other risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and pre-existing CKD.
Studies that used KDIGO criteria for the definition of AKI reported a higher incidence rate than those that used AKIN and RIFLE criteria. This may be because the KDIGO criteria have better diagnostic sensitivity, especially in diagnosing those with an early stage of AKI. Notably, most studies conducted after 2012 employed the KDIGO AKI criteria. This suggests a good level of awareness and application of the criteria among clinicians practising in Nigeria.
The common causes of AKI in Nigeria were hypovolaemia, sepsis, malaria, and acute glomerulonephritis. This is in keeping with the report of a systematic review on AKI in sub-Saharan Africa by Olowu et al. [ ]. Another important and peculiar cause of AKI in Nigeria is Lassa fever, which is endemic in some West African countries with seasonal epidemic outbreaks. Most of the causes of AKI in Nigeria are preventable and treatable if stakeholders, particularly the government and healthcare providers, take early and adequate preventive measures. The government should improve the healthcare system so that prompt and quality health services are available. They should implement policies to ensure environmental, food and water safety. Healthcare workers should be trained on prompt fluid repletion in those with acute fluid loss, timely treatment of infections, strict adherence to universal precautions and maintaining a high index of suspicion in Lassa fever-prone communities [ ]. All these measures will significantly reduce the high incidence of AKI and associated burden in the country.
Approximately half of the patients with AKI had a late diagnosis. This is similar to the findings of a previous study on AKI in sub-Saharan Africa [ ]. This may be explained by the poor health-seeking habits of Nigerians, ignorance and delays in recognising AKI among healthcare workers [ , ], which can lead to delayed nephrology consultations. Health education of the public using social media may help improve the health-seeking behaviour of Nigerians. Continuous medical education on early diagnosis and management of AKI should be regularly conducted among health workers. Early nephrology consultation or referral should be encouraged among healthcare practitioners to improve overall outcomes in patients with AKI [ ].
Haemodialysis was the mode of KRT in the majority of the patients with AKI, similar to what has been reported in most African countries [ ]. Furthermore, it was noted that approximately one-third of those who required KRT could not undergo dialysis, mainly due to financial constraints. This further underscores the need to enhance access to KRT by offering PD treatment, a more affordable form of KRT, particularly in underserved areas of the country. The identified factors that have limited the expansion of the PD program in Nigeria and other African countries include the unavailability of PD consumables, especially PD fluids, financial constraints, limited expertise, and inadequate training centres [ , ]. The National and African associations of nephrology can address some of these challenges by continuous engagement of the government to create an enabling environment for indigenous manufacturing of PD consumables and facilitating exchange training programs on PD within Africa. The importance of training in improving AKI outcomes was highlighted by Esezobor et al. [ ], who reported the successful management of paediatric AKI patients using improvised peritoneal dialysis (PD) techniques. This demonstrates the impact of capacity-building in achieving effective treatment even in resource-limited settings.
The pooled mortality rate of AKI in Nigeria was 26%. Among adults, the mortality rate was significantly higher at 34%, compared to 16% in the pediatric population. The higher mortality rate in adults may be because some medical conditions, which are more common in them, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart failure increase their susceptibility to a severe form of AKI and higher mortality. The overall mortality rate in this study align with the broader African context, where mortality rates among patients with AKI range from 11.5% to 43.5%, as reported by Adu et al. [ ]. The adult mortality rate observed in this study is comparable to the 32% reported by Olowu et al. [ ], while the pediatric mortality rate closely mirrors the 12.3% reported by Zarei et al. [ ] in a meta-analysis of AKI outcomes in African children. The mortality rate associated with AKI in Nigeria is still high and far from the ISN goal of achieving zero mortality from AKI by 2025. Late presentation, limited access to critical care nephrology services, and the high cost of dialysis are factors that contribute to the high AKI-associated mortality rate in Nigeria and Africa. All stakeholders, including the government, healthcare professionals, and the Nephrology Association, must focus their efforts on preventing, recognizing, and treating AKI, as highlighted earlier to reduce the magnitude of the AKI burden.
The strong association between extended follow-up study design and lower reported AKI incidence likely reflects temporal misclassification arising from evolving diagnostic criteria, variable diagnostic intensity over time, and dilution of high-risk periods in long-term cohorts. The attenuation of associations with AKI definition after adjustment for study design supports this interpretation. These findings suggest that study design and calendar-time effects are more influential determinants of reported AKI incidence than clinical setting or population age structure in this literature.

## Limitations of the Study
A limitation of this study is the high heterogeneity observed in most of the studies included in the meta-analysis, therefore the results should be interpreted with caution. We used multiple subgroup analyses and meta-regression to identify the sources of heterogeneity. In addition, the AKI incidence and mortality reported in this systematic review may be overestimated because it involved only hospital-based studies, mainly from tertiary centres where severely ill patients are usually referred. Most AKI data from Nigeria come from tertiary hospitals because that is where creatinine is measured, nephrologists practice, and research infrastructure exists, even though the majority of AKI in the region actually begins in the community. Community-acquired AKI is common, but largely invisible to the health system until patients reach referral centers, which skews the literature toward “hospital-based” cohorts. Nigeria, like most African countries, desperately needs an AKI registry that will capture both community-acquired and hospital-based AKI.
Despite these limitations, this study is the first in Nigeria to determine the pooled incidence of AKI, its causes, and the associated mortality in Nigeria. It brought to the fore the high magnitude of AKI and associated mortality in Nigeria.

## Conclusion
The incidence and mortality rates of AKI in Nigeria are high. Prompt and adequate treatment of the underlying and preventable causes will reduce the disease burden.
Recommendations
Clinicians should be regularly trained on prompt management of hypovolemia and sepsis, early recognition of AKI using KDIGO criteria and AKI management.
The Nigerian nephrology association should drive the renal registry initiative that will provide evidence-based information on the burden of AKI. This will be useful in driving advocacy for better health resource allocation and health reforms aimed at reducing the AKI burden.
There is a need for researchers to conduct studies that focus on community-AKI where implementation of cost-saving strategies, such as oral rehydration in those with diarrhoeal diseases, could significantly reduce the risk of AKI.
