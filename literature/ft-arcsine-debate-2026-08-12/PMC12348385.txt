## Background:
Gender-affirming breast augmentation (GABA) with implants is most commonly compared with cosmetic breast augmentation. A better comparator is tuberous breast augmentation (TBA). Anatomically, both feature relative medial pole deficiency, constricted breast footprints, and parenchymal herniation through the areola. Technically, both often require radial scoring and inframammary fold lowering. This review summarized techniques and outcomes of GABA and TBA.

## Methods:
A systematic review was performed in 3 databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Database of Controlled Trials from 2000 to 2024. In total, 1319 articles were identified, of which 29 met inclusion criteria: outcomes-based primary literature detailing GABA and/or TBA. Meta-analysis was performed, but due to the noncomparative nature of the included studies, no comparative statistics were performed.

## Results:
In total, the GABA cohort included 3234 breasts in 1617 patients from 13 studies, and the TBA cohort featured 1093 breasts in 583 patients from 16 studies. Cohort characteristics were similar between groups. Surgical characteristics included primarily prepectoral augmentation via inframammary fold incision in the GABA cohort and dual-plane augmentation via periareolar incision in the TBA cohort. In both the GABA and TBA cohorts, rates of common complications, including explantation, infection, hematoma, and seroma, were all below 1%. The rate of reoperation was 4.5% in the GABA cohort and 11.9% in the TBA.

## Conclusions:
This systematic review found low rates of complications in GABA and confirmed that GABA uses standard surgical techniques, which are routinely used in other breast procedures. Our findings demonstrate a similar complication profile between GABA and TBA.

## Takeaways
Question: What are the outcomes and complications of gender-affirming breast augmentation (GABA) in the context of its closest technical analogue: tuberous breast augmentation (TBA)?
Findings: Our review demonstrated low complication rates in both GABA and TBA. We demonstrated that similar techniques are used between the 2 cohorts.
Meaning: The comparison of GABA to TBA highlights the safety of both procedures and reinforces that GABA uses techniques that are well established and generally accepted in another indication: tuberous breast.

## INTRODUCTION
In appropriately identified individuals, gender-affirming breast augmentation (GABA) is a medically necessary surgical intervention that helps to alleviate gender dysphoria and has high rates of patient satisfaction. – GABA is most commonly compared with cosmetic breast augmentation. Recent systematic reviews and database analyses have demonstrated similar rates of complications between the 2 procedures. – However, comparison of GABA to cosmetic breast augmentation in cisgender women has 2 main limitations: (1) GABA is not purely cosmetic in nature (ie, it treats gender dysphoria), and (2) GABA has additional anatomical and technical considerations that may not be used in cosmetic breast augmentation.
We argue that GABA is more similar to tuberous breast augmentation (TBA). Anatomical similarities between the breasts of transfeminine people and cisgender people with tuberous breasts may include a constricted base; a short inframammary fold (IMF) to nipple distance; an elevated IMF; and, occasionally, herniation of breast parenchyma through the nipple–areolar complex (NAC) (Figs. – ). – (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the transfeminine patient with tuberous breasts and NAC herniation. Smooth, low-profile 440-mL implants were placed in the prepectoral plane [C and D]. Radial parenchymal scoring was performed. The IMF was lowered and reinforced. Tattoos have been obscured to preserve patient anonymity, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E222.) In TBA, varying degrees of medial pole deficiency have been described, ranging from types 1 to 4. Type 1 features lower medial pole hypoplasia, and type 4 features severe breast constriction with minimal breast base. A similar anatomy often presents in transgender women after estrogen exposure. In transgender women and cisgender men, the NAC is lateralized compared with that of cisgender women. Because estrogen induces breast parenchyma to grow primarily beneath the NAC, the breast may appear laterally displaced with less medial pole fullness. In transgender women with limited breast development, there is a small breast bud directly beneath the NAC, similar to a type 4 tuberous breast. In some GABA patients, the constricted breast bud herniates through the NAC, as is often seen in TBA. 
 Breast augmentation in transgender women may use techniques that are well described for the treatment of cisgender women with tuberous breasts. In both, radial scoring of the breast parenchyma expands the breast footprint to reduce the likelihood of a “double bubble” appearance. In our experience, the use of wider-based implants centered beneath the NAC fills the breast footprint and aids with widening the constricted breast mound. In cases with a high IMF, the IMF may require lowering and reinforcement.
Although the indications for GABA and TBA are different, the patient populations share similarities. Both patient populations are often young and relatively healthy. , Both GABA and TBA are typically offered on an elective basis. Patients undergoing GABA have gender dysphoria and/or gender incongruence, whereas the psychiatric profile of TBA patients has not been well described.
The purpose of this study was to characterize outcomes and complications of GABA in the context of the closest analogous nongender-affirming procedure, TBA. A systematic review was performed, with the hypothesis that both cohorts will feature similarities in patient and surgical characteristics and will report low rates of complications.

## METHODS

## Systematic Review
A comprehensive literature search was developed by the authors and conducted by an experienced medical librarian (Jennifer Westrick) in August 2024 across the following databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Database of Controlled Trials. Both controlled vocabularies (eg, MeSH terms), and keywords in the title or abstract fields were searched. Two separate searches were developed, 1 for each cohort in question: GABA and TBA. Results were limited to the past 24 years (2000 to present). There were no restrictions on geography. Additionally, a hand search was conducted of the reference lists of selected articles. Institutional review board approval was not required for this search given the lack of human subjects.
Articles were screened by 2 reviewers independently at each stage. Inclusion criteria were separate for each cohort. For the GABA cohort, inclusion criteria were breast augmentation with silicone implants and transfeminine gender identity. For the TBA cohort, inclusion criteria were breast augmentation with silicone implants and diagnosis of tuberous breast. Overall exclusion criteria included age younger than 18 years and publication language other than English. Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) was used to organize the screening process. Level of evidence was defined according to the American Society of Plastic Surgeons guidelines. Study quality was assessed by 2 blinded independent reviewers using the methodological index for nonrandomized studies criteria. 

## Data Analysis
Data were extracted into an Excel workbook (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). In cases where standardly reported complications were omitted, inferences were made. Specifically, rates of several complications (including infection, hematoma, seroma, implant rupture, extrusion, and explantation) were considered “standardly reported” by author consensus and were expected to be reported by all studies. If these complications were not explicitly reported, they were recorded with an incidence of “0” if the following criteria were met: the study (1) included rates of at least 3 other standardly reported complications, or (2) explicitly stated that there were no major complications. No inferences were made regarding less standardly reported outcomes, such as implant malposition, excessive scarring, animation deformity, rippling, or capsular contracture.
Severity of complications as determined by the Clavien-Dindo score or “major versus minor” designation was queried but was not consistently reported in the literature and could not be factored into the analysis. Similarly, the grade of capsular contracture was not consistently reported and could not be stratified. The outcome of “infection” included both superficial infections and abscesses because these were not consistently differentiated in the literature.
Data analysis was performed using Excel. Due to the noncomparative nature of most included studies (ie, single studies generally did not report outcomes of more than 1 cohort in question), no statistical comparisons were made between cohorts. Descriptive within-cohort meta-analyses were run using the R version 4.4.0 package “meta.” Proportions were pooled using Freeman–Tukey double arcsine values and then back-transformed to the original scale. In studies that provided medians, interquartile ranges, and ranges, the methods described by Luo et al and Wan et al were used to provide estimated means and SDs, respectively. All studies assumed a random-effects model, except where denoted. When only a single study reported an outcome in a cohort, no confidence interval could be calculated.

## RESULTS

## Characteristics of Studies
In total, 1319 unique articles were identified, of which 29 met inclusion criteria for noncomparative (ie, descriptive) analysis. A PRISMA (preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta analyses) diagram is shown in Figure . Of the 29 included articles, 13 pertained to GABA. , , , , – Sixteen studies pertained to TBA. – Table shows summary information of included studies. Level of evidence was IV in all cases. Methodological index for nonrandomized studies criteria scores ranged from 10 to 14. GRADE criteria demonstrated low quality of evidence, primarily due to the classification of included studies as nonrandomized observational studies. 
 A total of 4327 breasts in 2200 patients were represented in the included studies. Of these, the GABA cohort included 3234 breasts (1617 patients), and the TBA cohort included an estimated 1093 breasts (583 patients). Two studies in the TBA cohort did not report the number of breasts operated on but did report the number of patients. , In these instances, the ratio of 1.87 breasts per patient identified in the other 14 studies was used to estimate the number of breasts operated on.

## Patient Characteristics and Comorbidities
Patient characteristics are summarized in Table . Age was 35.9 years in the GABA cohort and 23.5 years in the TBA cohort. The older age of the GABA cohort is notable as it is consistent with the slightly older transgender cohorts identified in recent publications comparing GABA to cosmetic breast augmentation. , Body mass index was 25.0 in the GABA cohort and 23.5 in the TBA cohort.
 The smoking rate was 16.9% in the GABA cohort (Table ). However, the actual rate of tobacco use in the GABA cohort is somewhat unclear, as the high rate in our analysis was dominated by the largest study in this cohort, Sijben et al, which simply reported a 51% rate of “history of intoxication with tobacco,” not specifying current versus past tobacco use. However, a high rate of tobacco use has been previously documented in transgender populations, with 1 large-scale national survey of 27,715 transgender and gender minority people reporting a rate of 23.6%. Tobacco use was only reported by 2 TBA studies, with no patients having a tobacco history. Tobacco use increases the risk of complications such as infection and wound healing, making this an important consideration. The rate of diabetes was low in both cohorts (3.8% in GABA and 0% in TBA).
Follow-up was 41.6 months in the GABA cohort and 43.6 months in the TBA cohort. The follow-up period for both cohorts was consistently reported. Long-term evaluation of outcomes is important for both cohorts, as outcomes such as capsular contracture, malposition, extrusion, rupture, and rippling may not present for several months or even years. 

## Surgical Characteristics and Outcomes
Incision patterns differed between cohorts (Table ). Incision patterns for the GABA cohort were most commonly along the IMF (94.2%, n = 1324 of 1406 patients reporting), whereas the incision was almost always periareolar in the TBA cohort (98.5%, n = 540 of 548 patients reporting). The prevalence of periareolar incisions in the TBA cohort likely reflects the need to reduce an enlarged and/or herniated NAC, which is only sometimes a feature of GABA cases. 
 It is possible that the use of the periareolar incision contributed to the higher rate of “excessive or poor scarring” in the TBA cohort, which was 17.6% versus 2.2% in the GABA cohort (Table ). Periareolar incisions carry a risk of widening over time due to thin periareolar tissue and pressure from the implant. The specific location and nature of the “excessive or poor scarring” was not further elaborated, thereby limiting conclusions. Although rates of infection have been theorized to be higher with periareolar incisions, we did not identify any difference between GABA and TBA cohorts regarding this outcome.
 Plane of augmentation also varied between cohorts (Table ). In the GABA cohort, prepectoral augmentations were most common (64.3%, n = 1039 of 1615 patients reporting), whereas dual-plane augmentation was more common in the TBA cohort (73.6%, n = 381 of 518 patients reporting). A predominantly prepectoral placement of implants in the GABA cohort likely reflects another anatomical difference: a wider and more developed pectoralis muscle in patients exposed to testosterone during puberty. Complications most likely to be affected by submuscular versus prepectoral implant placement, that is, animation deformity, were not consistently reported across cohorts and could not be assessed.
Capsular contracture rates were less than 4% in both cohorts (Table ). Factors likely to affect capsular contracture that differed between cohorts include the plane of implant placement and a periareolar incision pattern. However, the specific causes of capsular contracture are yet unknown, and the importance of certain factors, such as incision pattern, is debated. 
Implant size was 377.0 mL in the GABA cohort and 247.3 mL in the TBA cohort. Although implant size was consistently reported in the GABA cohort (reported in 1407 of 1617 total patients), it was inconsistently reported in the TBA cohort (only 128 of 583 total patients). The use of larger implants in the GABA cohort is expected, as patients often have a wider chest, necessitating a wider implant to fill the base diameter of the breast. Implant size is likely to affect outcomes such as extrusion and malposition. Extrusion rates, however, were low in both cohorts (0% in both cohorts). Malposition was not commonly reported. However, differences in body habitus and breast/chest anatomy between transfeminine people and cisgender women likely impact implant position. As breast implants are generally centered beneath the NAC, and the NAC is more laterally positioned in transfeminine people, less medial pole fullness is achieved. Some studies may refer to the perceived lateral position of the implants as “malposition.”
The complications most frequently reported in the literature were explantation, infection, hematoma, seroma, and capsular contracture (Table ). In both the GABA and TBA cohorts, rates of common complications, including explantation, infection, hematoma, and seroma, were all less than 1%. Rates were not compared statistically between cohorts due to the noncomparative nature of all included studies.
Rates of reoperation were 4.5% in the GABA cohort and 11.9% in the TBA cohort; however, this outcome was not commonly reported in either cohort. Indications for revision in the GABA cohort included aesthetic concerns (n = 24), hematoma (n = 3), extrusion (n = 2), rupture (n = 2), capsular contracture (n = 1), or otherwise unspecified (n = 11). Indications for revision in the TBA cohort included aesthetic concerns (n = 9), capsular contracture (n = 3), rupture (n = 2), or otherwise unspecified (n = 20).
Satisfaction (via BREAST-Q or otherwise) was reported by 6 of 13 studies in the GABA cohort and 11 of 16 studies in the TBA cohort. Satisfaction was most often reported by 5-point Likert scales, ratings of “aesthetic satisfaction,” or simple “yes/no” questionnaires without further description. In all cases, satisfaction by these scales was reported as “high” or “very high” or similar. This pattern was consistent between both cohorts. BREAST-Q outcomes specifically were reported by 3 studies in the TBA cohort, with each subscale scoring between 73.8 and 80.4 (Table ). In the GABA cohort, subscale scores ranged from 70.3 to 92.5.
 Decisional regret was reported by few studies. In each reporting study, rates of regret were low. However, methodologies reporting regret differed between GABA and TBA, making a comparison difficult. Regret was discussed by 2 GABA studies. Schoffer et al reported that 95% (n = 95) of their 99 patients did not regret their surgery, whereas 3% (n = 3) would not undergo the surgery again. No further reasons were given for the regret. Sijben et al reported regret in 1% (n = 5) of their 527 patients. Of these 5 patients, 4 had their implants removed, and of these 4, 1 had her implants removed due to rupture before expressing regret. Regret was also discussed in 1 study within the TBA cohort. Brault et al reported a mean score of 2.73 among their 22 patients for the BREAST-Q statement “I have no regrets about having the surgery.” For this statement, scores are interpreted as follows: 1, “disagree”; 2, “somewhat agree”; and 3, “definitely agree.” 

## DISCUSSION
In this systematic review, we report low rates of complications from GABA (level III evidence). We demonstrate that rates of most complications in GABA were similar to those in TBA: less than 1%.
Low complication rates in GABA have been previously reported. However, previous reviews compare GABA to nontuberous cosmetic breast augmentation procedures in cisgender women. In an analysis of the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database, Gabrick et al reported no increased rates of perioperative complications in GABA when compared with cosmetic breast augmentation for cisgender women. In 2019, Cuccolo et al demonstrated similar findings using the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program. Most recently in 2023, Liu et al performed a systematic review of complication rates of GABA and compared these to historic rates in cisgender cosmetic breast augmentation patients. Their study demonstrated higher rates of hematoma/seroma (pooled outcome) and implant malposition in the transgender cohort.
In contrast to previous studies that compare GABA to cosmetic breast augmentation, we report pooled complication rates of GABA in the context of complication rates for an analogous procedure: TBA. Our finding of low complication rates in TBA is also congruent with other recent reviews examining this topic, although our review focused on implant-based augmentation and excluded cases of augmentation with fat transfer alone. , 
Comparisons of gender-affirming procedures to similar procedures in cisgender patients should be made to the most technically similar procedure, which in the case of GABA is TBA. GABA is best compared with TBA due to anatomical similarities between transfeminine people exposed to estrogen and cisgender women with tuberous breasts: a constricted breast with lower pole deficiency; an elevated IMF; and, occasionally, herniation of breast parenchyma through the NAC. , These anatomical similarities may require the use of the same established techniques used in TBA: radial scoring of the breast parenchyma and lowering of the IMF. Addition of these techniques adds considerable complexity to GABA and TBA cases that is not accounted for in cosmetic breast augmentation cases.
Even though TBA is the closest analogue to GABA, comparison between the 2 has limitations. Technical differences did exist between cohorts, namely the plane of reconstruction and the incision pattern used. Anatomical differences, such as a wider chest plate in transgender females, add further complexity to GABA cases. However, these differences did not seem to meaningfully affect complication rates, as rates of all complications were low.
Conclusions regarding cosmetic outcomes are limited by the lack of consistent reporting of specific cosmetic outcomes. In future studies, implant malposition and IMF position should be better characterized because several of the techniques used in both GABA and TBA aim to address medial pole deficiency and IMF position.
Another limitation is that unlike GABA, TBA does not treat gender dysphoria. Therefore, satisfaction and regret with GABA are likely to reflect factors different from those that influence satisfaction with TBA. Regret was uncommonly reported in both cohorts, limiting our conclusions. However, regret in gender-affirming surgery is rare and multifactorial. Given the relatively complex nature of GABA (eg, a wider chest plate demanding a large implant and widely spaced NACs), cosmetic goals may be difficult to achieve, which may contribute to regret unrelated to a patient’s gender identity or the treatment of their gender dysphoria. We caution against interpreting “explantation” as synonymous with “regret” in either cohort. After both GABA and TBA, breast implants may be removed for a number of reasons, including evolving patient priorities, change in health status, complications, and cosmetic dissatisfaction, among others. Removal should not automatically signify regret, or in the case of GABA, detransition. We anticipate that the development of patient-reported outcome measures in GABA, such as the CHEST-Q and upcoming GENDER-Q, will provide higher quality data.
Limitations of the search process include the imperfect nature of any single search strategy. Limitations of the available literature include the lack of comparative studies. Due to the noncomparative nature of the available studies, no direct statistical comparisons could be made.

## CONCLUSIONS
In this systematic review, we found low rates of complications in GABA and similar rates of complications in TBA. When comparing GABA to breast augmentation, we suggest using TBA as a comparison group due to anatomical and technical similarities between these clinical scenarios.
