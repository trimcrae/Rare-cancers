## Abstract

## Objectives
Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy of infantile onset. While epidemiological data from Western countries and global estimates are available, consolidated evidence on the burden of IESS in India remains limited. Given India's large and diverse population, regional variations in healthcare access, diagnostic capabilities, and risk factors may significantly influence the prevalence of IESS. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to estimate the incidence and prevalence of IESS in India.

## Methods
We conducted a comprehensive search across four electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science—in May 2025 to identify studies focusing on the prevalence and incidence of IESS in Indian settings. Data extraction was carried out using a standardized, predesigned extraction form. The methodological rigor of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist for prevalence and incidence studies. A meta‐analysis was conducted using the Freeman‐Tukey double arcsine transformation, with pooled estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals reported.

## Results
A total of five eligible studies from different regions of India were included. The overall pooled prevalence of active cases of IESS was 3.33 per 100 000 population (95% CI, 1.46–5.82). A subgroup analysis was also conducted for the age group 0–18 years, yielding a pooled prevalence of 9.71 per 100 000 children surveyed (95% CI, 4.16–17.06). No studies reported the incidence of IESS in India.

## Significance
The study is pivotal in estimating the burden of IESS in a low‐middle‐income country like India, where structural etiologies, treatment delays, and limited access to therapy pose major challenges. Although IESS accounts for only 0.5% of all epilepsies and 1.2% of epilepsy in children, its considerable clinical impact emphasizes the need for early recognition, standardized diagnostic protocols, and increased awareness among healthcare professionals and caregivers.

## Plain Language Summary
This systematic review summarizes the current evidence on the prevalence of active cases of IESS in India. Based on five eligible studies, the overall pooled prevalence of active cases of IESS was 3.33 per 100 000 population and 9.71 per 100 000 children (<18 years). This study is the first to provide a consolidated estimate and a clearer understanding of the burden of IESS in India and may support clinicians and researchers in improving awareness and early recognition of this condition.

## INTRODUCTION
Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS), including West syndrome, is one of the most common developmental and epileptic encephalopathies of infantile onset and is characterized by the occurrence of epileptic spasms and often hypsarrhythmia in the electroencephalogram (EEG). , This is a disorder necessitating urgent treatment with hormonal or vigabatrin therapy to achieve complete cessation of epileptic spasms, a surrogate of long‐term neurodevelopmental outcome. , The pooled global prevalence of IESS has been reported to be around 1.5 cases/100 000 population, with a pooled incidence of 24.9 cases per 100 000 live births in a meta‐analysis. Besides a particularly elevated Scandinavian incidence, the study reported a continental effect (Europe with a higher incidence than Asia and North America) and a latitudinal effect for incidence. 

Studies from Western countries demonstrate a considerable burden of IESS, with notable variations across different study settings and regions, as well as among different age groups in the denominator and the time frame of the study (whether recent or historical). The reported prevalence varies from 2.2 per 100 000 population to 37.25 per 100 000 children, while the cumulative incidence varies from 3 to 22 per lakh live births. , , , , , , From the Asian Oceanian region, few studies have tried to capture the burden of IESS, but mostly from developed countries like Australia, New Zealand, Japan, Singapore, and Taiwan. , , , These reports indicate incidences ranging from 6 to 58.2 per lakh children or live births. , Only a few studies reported prevalence from India. There are no studies estimating the incidence of IESS from India.
India, with over 1.4 billion people, ranks as the most populous country in the world. The pooled prevalence of lifetime epilepsy in India is 5.7 (95% CI, 4.3–7.2) per 1000, with a pooled prevalence of epilepsy of around 0.8% in Indian children and adolescents. , However, there are no precise pooled estimates available on IESS from India. Previous studies from India have highlighted that neonatal hypoxic and hypoglycemic brain injuries are dominant causes of IESS in this region, and there is a distinctive epidemiological profile of IESS in this region. , , Therefore, it can be argued that the prevalence estimates may differ from those reported in Western countries. In the present era of the WHO's Intersectoral Global Action Plan (IGAP), it is essential to understand the precise burden estimates of various epilepsies and epilepsy syndromes, as these not only affect the child but also impact the whole family and society. , , The first‐line treatment options of IESS include oral steroids, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and vigabatrin. Of these, ACTH (natural form) and vigabatrin are costly. However, in South Asia, the synthetic form (carboxymethyl cellulose‐based formulation) of ACTH is used, which is relatively cheaper and affordable. Nevertheless, the availability and cost still pose challenges for patients in resource‐limited settings. , Considering the importance of this condition, this study was undertaken to estimate the pooled prevalence and incidence of IESS in India.

## METHODOLOGY
This study was conducted and reported in line with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and the protocol was registered prospectively with PROSPERO (CRD420251071759).
2.1.

## Literature searches and selection criteria
An extensive search across four electronic databases—MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus, from inception to May 2025, was performed. The initial search strategy was formulated in the MEDLINE database. Relevant keywords, along with Medical Subject Headings terms such as “Infantile spasms,” “IESS,” “Epilepsy,” and “India” (including all the states and union territories) were employed to construct the search queries. The search strategy has been detailed in Table  . This search framework served as a template and was subsequently tailored to align with the indexing structure and format of different databases, such as Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus. A snowballing approach was also used by manually reviewing the reference lists of included articles and related articles to uncover additional relevant studies. Besides, the studies included in previous prevalence‐based meta‐analyses on epilepsy syndrome were also reviewed. There were no time or language restrictions imposed.
 We included population‐based observational studies, including cohort (retrospective, or prospective), longitudinal, and cross‐sectional, reporting sufficient data to estimate the prevalence or incidence of IESS, specifically providing the number of identified cases of IESS and the corresponding denominator (total population as well as pediatric population for prevalence and live births for incidence). Given the long period of included studies, we applied the ILAE 2022 classification and definition of epilepsy syndromes in the neonate and infant to standardize case definitions and ensure comparability of prevalence estimates. Randomized controlled trials, case reports, abstracts from conferences, editorials, and reviews were excluded. Additionally, studies that investigated epilepsy syndromes other than IESS or provoked/ triggered seizures or febrile convulsions were excluded.

2.2.

## Selection of studies and data extraction
Two independent reviewers (AS and DB) screened the titles and abstracts of all the records retrieved after de‐duplication using EndNote. Potentially eligible articles underwent full‐text screening based on predefined inclusion criteria. Any disagreements regarding study selection were settled through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (JKS) to reach consensus. Data extraction was carried out using a standardized extraction form tailored for this review, which captured variables including authors, publication year, study design, state/region, data collection method, diagnostic method, population characteristics, and prevalence data. Potential overlap in included studies was assessed by examining the data sources, geographic regions, settings, and period of data collection.

2.3.

## Quality assessment
Two reviewers (AS and DB) independently assessed the methodological quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies. The checklist included nine items, each rated as “yes”(1 point), “no” (0 points), and unclear/ not applicable (0 points). The percentage score was calculated for each study based on the number of “yes” responses. Based on this score, studies were classified according to their risk of bias: low (80%–100% of items scored “yes”), moderate risk (50%–80% of items scored “yes”), and high risk (20%–50% of items scored “yes”). 

2.4.

## Outcome measure and data analysis
Prevalence was classified in two categories: first, to estimate the overall prevalence of IESS, the number of confirmed cases was divided by the total population encompassing all age groups (infants, children, and adults), with results expressed per 100 000 individuals. Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted for the 0–18 year age group and for individual states to assess the burden of this disease within this population.
The statistical analyses were performed in RStudio (version 4.5.1) utilizing the meta package. To ensure variance stabilization of proportions, the Freeman‐Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied. A fixed‐effect model was employed to derive pooled estimates reported with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using I
2 statistics and τ
2 (tau‐squared) measures with I
2 thresholds categorized as follows: low (25%), moderate (50%), and high (75%). To assess the robustness of findings, a sensitivity analysis using the Hartung‐Knapp adjustments was also performed, which provides stable CIs with a small number of studies.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## Search results
Extensive searches across four databases, MEDLINE (n = 1002), Embase (n = 1907), Scopus (n = 460), and Web of Science (n = 1827), yielded a total of 5196 articles. Based on pre‐defined eligibility criteria, 852 duplicates were removed, and 4344 titles and abstracts were screened. Of this curated selection, 36 hits were thoroughly reviewed for full text. Five studies were included in the systematic review and meta‐analysis. The stepwise process of article selection is visually mapped in Figure  .

3.2.

## Characteristics of included studies
A total of five studies, encompassing 327 603 individuals, reported the prevalence of IESS in India and were published between 1988 and 2024. Geographically, two studies (2/5) were conducted in Punjab, while the remaining three studies were from Kashmir (1/5), Maharashtra (1/5), and West Bengal (1/5), respectively. The study characteristics are detailed in Table  . Of the included studies, two studies (2/5) were conducted in urban settings, and three studies (3/5) in rural settings. In terms of study design, two were cross‐sectional studies, one study each was population‐based, longitudinal observational, and a community‐based survey. The data collection method included a door‐to‐door survey conducted by trained personnel.
 In all studies, neurologists confirmed the cases based on clinical description, medical records, and patient history, with seizures being classified according to the ILAE classification. Additionally, diagnosis was confirmed using EEG video monitoring in conjunction with clinical assessment in two studies. , The study by Srivastava et al. reported the etiology of four patients with IESS: hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, tuberous sclerosis, neonatal insult, and cryptogenic. All the studies were conducted in different time frames and geographical regions with distinct populations. Hence, no study overlap was identified across the studies.

3.3.

## Quality assessment
The JBI for prevalence studies was used to assess the methodological quality of all included studies. The percentage scores ranged from 80% to 100% across the five studies, indicating a low risk of bias. All studies met the predefined threshold; none were excluded based on the assessment. Detailed quality assessment scores are provided in Table  .

3.4.

## Prevalence of IESS

The overall pooled prevalence of IESS was 3.33 per 100 000 population (95% CI: 1.46–5.82; number of studies [N]: 5, I
2: 0.0%, τ
2:0, p = 0.6; Figure  ) in India. Study‐specific prevalence estimates range from 1.91 per 100 000 population to 6.28 per 100 000 population. The sensitivity analysis was also performed using Hartung–Knapp adjustments, which yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the findings.

3.5.

## Sub‐group analysis
All five studies provided data for the children, covering a total population of 110 186. The pooled prevalence of IESS in this cohort was estimated at 9.71 per 100 000 individuals (95% CI: 4.16, 17.06; Figure  ), derived using a fixed‐effect model. The heterogeneity among studies was minimal, with I
2 = 0%, τ
2 < 0.0001, and p = 0.51. To verify the robustness of these findings, a sensitivity analysis using the Hartung–Knapp adjustment was conducted, which yielded comparable results, indicating that the overall estimate was stable and not driven by model assumptions. Srivastava et al. reported the highest (20.85; 95% CI, 8.11; 53.61) prevalence among all the included studies. The state‐wise pooled prevalence of IESS is represented in Figure  .

## DISCUSSION
This meta‐analysis represents the first comprehensive synthesis of available literature on the prevalence of IESS in India. This pool of data is derived from five distinct epidemiological studies across India, encompassing a total population of 249 175 individuals. , , , , The included studies were conducted over several decades and included Kashmir, Punjab, Kolkata, rural Pune, and another district in Northwest India, reflecting a broad geographic and temporal scope. These studies employed varied designs, including door‐to‐door surveys, longitudinal observations, and community‐based screening programs.
The analysis yields a overall pooled prevalence of 3.33 per 100 000 population and an age‐specific pooled prevalence of 9.71 per 100 000 children. These summary statistics provide a crucial benchmark for IESS prevalence in India. Based on the recent estimates of epilepsy prevalence, IESS constituted 0.5% of all epilepsies in India and 1.2% of epilepsy in Indian children (0–18 years). , This pooled prevalence is numerically higher but not statistically different from the global pooled prevalence, as the denominators vary across studies, with some based on the pediatric population and others on the general population. The age‐specific prevalence is numerically higher than estimates from countries with high prevalence, like Denmark (reported age‐specific prevalence of 9.7 per 100 000) and Finland (age‐specific prevalence of 5.7 per 100 000). , , However, given the wide CI (4.16–17.06), these figures are overlapping and fall within the range of previously reported child‐specific prevalence estimates. The higher pooled estimates from India might reflect the relatively higher frequency of acquired brain injuries, like hypoxic and hypoglycemic brain injuries. , , Unlike most other countries, there was a striking difference between age‐specific prevalence and pooled prevalence in this study due to the huge population of India.
No studies reported the incidence of IESS from India. This might be due to the lack of surveillance systems, including birth and epilepsy registries. This might also be attributed to healthcare infrastructure limitations, including underdiagnosis, diagnostic lag, and limited access to diagnostic tools, and sociocultural factors, including stigma and traditional belief systems. , Furthermore, the dispersed model of healthcare in India, with a significant impact of the private sector, might also be contributory. Also, the relevance of methodological challenges cannot be underscored. These include resource constraints (especially for longitudinal studies) and limited public health funding, high attrition rates, inadequate collaboration between the public and private sectors, and relatively low prioritization of epilepsy research. 

Negligible statistical heterogeneity was observed among the included studies, indicating consistency in quantitative findings. However, clinical heterogeneity persists due to inherent variations in study design, methodological approaches, case ascertainment, diagnostic rigor, and population characteristics. The studies by Srivastava et al. and Sulena et al. were conducted exclusively in pediatric populations (though they also reported total population figures), explaining their higher age‐specific point estimates. In contrast, the other studies surveying all age groups reported lower overall rates, likely attributable to the absence of age‐specific or syndromic screening protocols in their methodologies. , Furthermore, methodological differences, such as the use of EEG for diagnosis, may have allowed for more precise case identification in the study by Srivastava et al. Koul et al., the oldest included study, reported four cases of IESS with diagnoses based solely on clinical descriptions, yielding the highest prevalence estimate (6.28/100 000). Taken together, the forest plot and study‐level analysis reemphasize that accurate estimation of IESS prevalence hinges on pediatric‐specific, community‐based methodologies employing syndromic diagnostic standards.
This meta‐analysis possesses notable strengths, namely, the first synthesis of IESS prevalence data from multiple Indian epidemiological studies. This provides a robust summary estimate by combining data from diverse geographical regions over several decades. The limited number of studies and limited geographical representation may have an impact on the generalizability of findings. Moreover, a formal trend analysis could not be done due to a lack of longitudinal estimates on the same population. It is important to note that some studies were limited to pediatric populations and varied in diagnostic rigor—some relying solely on clinical evaluation, while others incorporated EEG. Also, there is a concern of survival bias, as the population does not account for potential mortality among IESS cases and reflects only those children who survived long enough to be diagnosed and captured in the studies. Children who may have died early or been lost to follow‐up are not represented. This could result in an underestimation of the true disease burden. Thus, while the pooled analysis provides a useful benchmark, the true prevalence of IESS in India may still vary across regions and clinical settings due to differences in access to diagnostic tools and healthcare infrastructure. Another inadvertent limitation of our systematic review is that all included studies did not uniformly apply EEG for electroclinical syndrome diagnosis, and therefore, external applicability based on the recent 2022 ILAE Nosology is limited.
From a public health perspective, these findings underscore the need for early detection, standardized diagnostic protocols, and increased awareness among healthcare providers and caregivers. Establishing national surveillance systems and pediatric epilepsy registries would enhance understanding of incidence, regional trends, and inform resource allocation. Aligning with IGAP priorities, such measures can support evidence‐based interventions and policy planning to improve outcomes for children with epilepsy in India.
To conclude, this meta‐analysis provides a comprehensive synthesis of existing literature on the prevalence of IESS in India. Future research should focus on large‐scale, multicentric, population‐based studies to account for regional variability and to improve methodological consistency, and strengthen national epilepsy data systems, thereby informing targeted interventions and policy initiatives.
