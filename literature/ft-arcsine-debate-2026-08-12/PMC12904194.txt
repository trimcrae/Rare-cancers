## ABSTRACT

## Background and Aims
Disentangling whether disordered eating is a cause, consequence or manifestation of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) symptoms remains a challenge. We conducted an updated systematic review and the first meta‐analysis to estimate the prevalence of eating disorders in individuals with IBD.

## Methods
We systematically searched MEDLINE, Embase and PsycINFO from inception to 28 October 2025, for original observational studies reporting the prevalence of at least one eating disorder in an IBD population. Pooled prevalence estimates were calculated using random‐effects models and stratified by IBD type, sex, age and assessment method. Between‐study heterogeneity was assessed using Q and I
2 statistics.

## Results
Twenty‐three studies were included. Prevalence estimates varied substantially depending on how eating disorders were assessed. Studies using self‐report questionnaires yielded a pooled prevalence of 13.60% (95% CI = 9.86%–17.81%; I
2 = 90.7%; n = 18), whereas studies employing physician‐assigned diagnoses yielded a lower pooled prevalence of 2.84% (95% CI = 0.00%–9.03%; I
2 = 99.9%; n = 5). The highest prevalence was observed in studies using the Nine‐Item Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder Screen, with a pooled estimate of 17.10% (95% CI = 12.81%–21.88%; I
2 = 87.4%; n = 9). No significant differences in prevalence were found by sex, IBD subtype, age at time of study or disease activity.

## Conclusions
Eating disorders are prevalent among individuals with IBD, particularly avoidant/restrictive types. These findings highlight the need for improved screening and greater clinical awareness to better detect and manage disordered eating in the IBD population.

## Introduction
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic, inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract with rising prevalence throughout the world [ , , , ]. Being diagnosed with a gastrointestinal disease that is influenced by diet during childhood or adolescence may increase the risk of developing an eating disorder [ ], likely because such illnesses fundamentally alter an individual's relationship with food [ , ]. While disordered eating does not always progress into an eating disorder—clinically diagnosed disorders that include anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder and avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID)—it remains a significant risk factor [ ].
Case studies highlight the complex relationship between IBD and eating disorders. While some individuals with IBD use a restrictive diet to quell IBD‐related symptoms, restrictive eating may also influence IBD symptoms and even potentially contribute to disease development [ , ]. Furthermore, IBD symptoms can also mimic eating disorders in those with the disease [ ]. Consequently, distinguishing whether disordered eating is a cause, consequence or manifestation of IBD symptoms is challenging. Regardless, eating disorders represent a significant burden in the IBD population.
Prior systematic reviews have estimated the prevalence of eating disorders in the IBD population to be between 13% and 29% [ , , ]. However, these systematic reviews did not include meta‐analyses and often relied on case‐series or limited cohort data. Further, some also omitted newer DSM‐5 diagnoses, such as Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED), which may underestimate the overall prevalence of eating disorders.
With increased recognition of the importance of eating disorders in IBD and increased research investigating this relationship in recent years, an updated systematic review is warranted. We present a comprehensive systematic review and the first meta‐analysis to estimate the prevalence of eating disorders in individuals with IBD and explore how prevalence varies by patient factors (e.g., sex, IBD type, age, active disease) and methodological approach (e.g., questionnaire vs. administrative data).

## Methods
This review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 reporting guidelines [ ] and follows a pre‐specified review protocol registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024606264). We developed search queries for observational studies assessing the prevalence of eating disorders in IBD. This search was done using search software Ovid and queried databases MEDLINE, Embase and PsycINFO, the latter of which was included to capture psychiatric studies that might not be indexed in traditional medical databases. The search spanned database inception to 28 October 2025, with no restrictions on language. The search strategy, reviewed by medical librarians and specialists in systematic review methodologies, is provided in Table  .
We included all available full‐text articles regardless of publication year, while restricting conference proceedings and abstracts to those published in the last 3 years of the study period (i.e., 2022 or later). Titles and abstracts were independently screened in duplicate (A.M. and Q.G.). Both authors hand‐searched reference lists for further inclusions. Studies in any language meeting the inclusion criteria proceeded to full‐text review if they: (1) investigated at least one DSM‐5 eating disorder; (2) investigated the point prevalence of one or more eating disorders within an IBD population; (3) had a clear IBD population (n > 10); (4) were a primary/original study; (5) were an observational study; and (5) recruited based on IBD status alone if not utilising administrative data (Table  ). Inter‐rater agreement was calculated by Cohen's Kappa Coefficient, and conflicts were resolved through consensus.
Data extraction was completed in duplicate (Q.G. and A.M.) and included publication year, publication type (abstract or full‐text article), study period, geographic region and study design (i.e., cross‐sectional, retrospective cohort or prospective cohort), as well as information used to determine prevalence including total number of individuals with IBD, the number of participants with eating disorders, as well as stratified prevalence by age at the time of study (paediatric [< 18 years] or adult [≥ 18 years]), sex (male or female), IBD type (Crohn's disease vs. ulcerative colitis), data source (self‐report questionnaire vs. clinician assessment). The proportion of participants with active disease (as described in each study) and the mean age of study participants were also recorded. For questionnaires, we documented the type administered to patients (e.g., NIAS, EAT‐26, EDE‐Q, SCOFF and EDI‐3), the cutoff points considered indicative of disordered eating, and the number of people who met the cutoff for each subscale. A high‐level overview of each questionnaire, as well as the studies which employed them, is provided in Table  .
 For any study with missing relevant information, the corresponding author was contacted to request additional data. Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) inventory tool [ ], which has been previously adapted for other incidence/prevalence studies involving IBD populations [ ], and was used to determine sample representativeness, sample adequacy and methodological validity (Table  ). Disagreements were resolved by consensus.
The primary analysis compared the pooled prevalence of eating disorders identified by questionnaires versus clinician assessments (i.e., ICD codes or formal psychiatric assessments). Secondary analyses restricted to questionnaire‐based studies explored subgroups, including sex, IBD type, and type of questionnaire used. Since most questionnaires do not assess for a specific type of eating disorder (e.g., anorexia) and studies of administrative data have found that individuals often have ICD codes for multiple eating disorders, stratified analysis by type of eating disorder (e.g., anorexia) was not performed [ ]. One of the retrieved studies, Werlang (2024) [ ], investigated only a single type of eating disorder in administrative data. We conduct a sensitivity analysis removing this study to account for the fact that it may skew the prevalence of eating disorders more generally. Further, for studies employing multiple instruments (e.g., NIAS and EAT‐26 within the same study population), only the estimate yielding the lowest prevalence was included in the primary analysis to produce a more conservative estimate and avoid double‐counting. Sensitivity analyses using the higher of the two estimates are provided for comparison.
Statistical analysis was conducted in R (4.3.1) using the ‘meta’ package for meta‐analysis, as well as the ‘dplyr’ and ‘tidyr’ packages for data wrangling. Pooled prevalence of disordered eating and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were obtained using the ‘metaprop’ command in the ‘meta’ package. Random effects models with inverse variance weighting and restricted maximum likelihood (REML) methods were used. Given the rarity of eating disorders, we applied the Freeman‐Tukey double arcsine transformation to address the skewed distribution of prevalence estimates to stabilise the calculation of variance estimates [ ]. The Wilson's score interval was also used to compute 95% confidence intervals. Pooled estimates were visualised using forest plots and between‐study heterogeneity was assessed using Q‐ and I
2‐statistics. Meta‐regression was conducted to determine if the proportion of participants with active disease and the mean age of study participants were significant predictors of heterogeneity using the ‘metareg’ command in the ‘meta’ package.

## Results
The search strategy identified 2964 abstracts (MEDLINE = 971; Embase = 1797; PsycINFO = 196). After removing duplicates, 2288 abstracts were screened with moderate inter‐rater agreement (percent agreement = 98.0%, Cohen's kappa = 0.652). We contacted authors from 20 studies for additional data, receiving three responses. After full‐text review, 23 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] were included in this systematic review (Figure  ).
 A summary of study characteristics is presented in Table  . The 23 included studies were primarily published in 2022 or later (n = 17) and were primarily peer‐reviewed full texts (n = 17). The majority were cross‐sectional (n = 20) in design. Five focused exclusively on paediatric populations (with three being questionnaire‐based), 10 exclusively on adults, with the remaining having either no clear restrictions on age or studying narrower age ranges (e.g., ages 8–25). Sample size ranged from 18 to 331,950. While seven countries were represented, studies primarily took place in the United States (n = 13). Quality assessment is provided in Figure  . Many studies lacked a validated outcome measure, primarily because most relied on questionnaires not validated for use in IBD populations to assess disordered eating. Additionally, most studies did not clearly report recruitment numbers or response rates. Several studies scored poorly on sample representativeness, as some restricted inclusion to patients with quiescent disease, hospitalised individuals or excluded newly diagnosed cases of IBD.

## Meta‐Analysis
The included studies fall into two primary methodological camps: (1) Those which used physician‐assigned diagnoses (e.g., ICD codes for eating disorders in administrative data or formal assessments from a psychiatrist) and (2) those which administered a self‐report questionnaire in a clinic‐run study. We observe a significant difference in prevalence (p < 0.001) between studies employing these two approaches. Among the 18 studies using questionnaires, we used the lower of the two prevalence estimates reported by the three studies [ , , ] that employed multiple instruments to avoid double counting. The pooled prevalence of eating disorders was 13.60% (95% CI = 9.86%–17.81%; I
2 = 90.7%; Q = 183.06, p < 0.001) (Figure  ). In a sensitivity analysis using the higher prevalence estimates of the three studies [ , , ], the pooled prevalence increased to 15.15% (95% CI = 11.04%–19.74%; I
2 = 90.6%; Q = 180.86; p < 0.001). In contrast, among the five studies employing physician‐assigned diagnoses (i.e., ICD codes or physician assessments), the pooled prevalence of eating disorders was only 2.84% (95% CI = 0.00%–9.03%; I
2 = 99.9%; Q = 5179.53, p < 0.001). If we remove the one study, Werlang (2024) [ ], which investigated only a single eating disorder (ARFID), the prevalence rises to 3.61% (95% CI = 1.03%–7.34%; I
2 = 0.74.8%) (Figure  ).
 Among the studies which used questionnaires (n = 18), a wide variety of questionnaires were employed (Table  ). The type of questionnaire contributed to heterogeneity in prevalence estimates. A significant difference in prevalence was observed across instruments (p = 0.007): Studies using EAT‐26 had a pooled prevalence of 6.74% (95% CI = 2.89%–11.86%; I
2 = 74.6%), those using the Nine Item Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder Screen (NIAS) reported 17.10% (95% CI = 12.81%–21.88%, I
2 = 87.4%) and studies using other questionnaires (EDE‐Q, EDI‐3, SCOFF) reported a prevalence of 18.43% (95% CI = 10.85%–27.40%; I
2 = 87.2%) (Figure  ). The estimate from Grossberg (2025) [ ], which employed the PARDI‐AR‐Q, was excluded from this stratified analysis, as both NIAS and PARDI‐AR‐Q are designed specifically to assess risk of ARFID.
 While most questionnaires assess general eating disorder risk rather than risk for a specific condition like anorexia nervosa or bulimia, the NIAS exclusively evaluates risk for Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID). Based on NIAS estimates, the pooled prevalence of ARFID was 17.10% (95% CI: 12.81%–21.88%; I
2 = 87.4%).
Further subgroup analyses revealed no significant differences in prevalence by IBD type or sex among questionnaire‐based studies (Table  ). A meta‐regression on the proportion of participants with active disease in the nine studies that reported disease activity (all questionnaire‐based) showed no significant association with prevalence estimates (p = 0.778). Similarly, the meta‐regression on the mean age of study participants among the 10 studies that reported it was also not significant (p = 0.161).

## Discussion
We found the overall prevalence for eating disorders varied dramatically based on the type of methodology used: While a pooled prevalence of only 2.8% was observed for studies using clinician assessments, questionnaire‐based studies revealed a 10‐fold higher prevalence of eating disorder risk at 13.6%. Further, stratifying by the type of questionnaire yielded further differences in prevalence: Studies employing NIAS yielded a pooled prevalence of 17.1%, while studies using EAT‐26 had a pooled prevalence of 6.7%. These findings highlight the need for an eating disorder screening tool validated specifically for use in individuals with IBD and support a push for improved screening practices in this population (Box  ).

## Key takeaways and recommendations.
We observed a high burden of eating disorders in the IBD population, but the true prevalence is obscured by the lack of consistency in the types of screening tools employed and the threshold used to determine clinically significant risk.
In agreement with the review by Soliman et al. [ ], we see a prominent need for an eating disorder screening tool validated for use in an IBD population which acknowledges the complex relationship with food that comes with the disease.
Individuals living with IBD should receive improved screening for eating disorders to combat the potential underdiagnosis we observe. Further, the fact that we do not see significant differences in prevalence by sex, IBD type, age or active disease status means that clinicians should be aware of eating disorder risk regardless of the demographics of their patient.
Accurately ascertaining the true burden of eating disorders in IBD is difficult, since dietary restrictions often blur the line between disordered and non‐disordered eating. Further, the tool used to measure prevalence significantly clouds the ability to determine a true estimate. This review therefore emphasises the importance of future studies using more consistent methodology, as well as highlights the need for screening tools validated in the IBD population to be developed. Regardless of how it is measured, this systematic review and meta‐analysis still present a significant burden of eating disorders in the IBD population, underscoring the importance of routine screening to enable timely intervention for this vulnerable group, such as psychotherapy [ ].
We identified five studies that used clinician assessments (i.e., ICD codes in administrative data or formal psychiatric assessments) to estimate the prevalence of eating disorders in IBD patient populations. The relatively small number of studies using administrative data highlights an opportunity for future high‐quality population‐based studies to more accurately investigate the prevalence of diagnosed eating disorders in IBD. While we observed a pooled prevalence of roughly 3% among these studies, this may be an underestimate of the true prevalence due to underdiagnosis, misclassification error or capturing only severe cases (such as those hospitalised for their eating disorder) within these data sources. In contrast, studies based on self‐report questionnaires may overestimate prevalence due to potential selection bias (e.g., recruiting on disease severity), assessing risk of an eating disorder rather than providing a formal diagnosis or not being validated for use in an IBD population [ ]. A recent systematic review [ ] found that existing psychometric tools are not well‐equipped to assess eating disorder risk in individuals with gastrointestinal conditions and that the resulting scores should be interpreted cautiously. As a result, the pooled estimates in the meta‐analysis may not reflect true prevalence and could represent either under‐ or overestimates. This highlights the need for validation studies of ICD coding within administrative databases and the use of validated questionnaires for diagnosing the spectrum of eating disorders in IBD.
Prior systematic reviews, which included both disordered and non‐disordered restrictive eating behaviours, reported higher prevalence estimates in the IBD population [ , , , ], although this study presents the first pooled estimates. Further, estimates of eating disorder prevalence in the general population vary widely. For instance, a systematic review [ ] reported a point prevalence of 7.8% from 2013 to 2018, while lifetime prevalence estimates range from 1.69% to 9% [ ]. This variability makes determining whether eating disorder prevalence in the IBD population differs from the general population challenging. Similarly, a paucity of case–control studies meant the current meta‐analysis was unable to provide comparable risk estimates for IBD vs. non‐IBD populations. Consequently, prospective cohort studies comparing the risk of eating disorders in the IBD population to age‐ and sex‐matched general population controls are needed to determine if the risk is elevated in individuals with IBD.
We observed significant heterogeneity in our pooled estimates, acknowledging that I
2 should be interpreted cautiously when pooling prevalence data [ ]. The type of tool used to determine prevalence was a key source of heterogeneity. Interestingly, we did not find any statistically significant differences in prevalence by sex, IBD type, age or disease activity, contrary to Day et al. [ ] who identified female sex, having Crohn's disease and perceived active disease as relevant predictors of eating disorders in those with IBD. It is possible that these factors may be more relevant to non‐disordered restrictive eating behaviours than eating disorders proper or additional covariates confounded our analyses. For instance, the fact that different studies used different questionnaire thresholds as the cutoff for clinical significance (Table  ) is likely a source of heterogeneity; for instance, some of the lowest heterogeneity (I
2 = 74.6%) was observed in the pooled EAT‐26 estimate, which happened to have a more homogenous threshold (≥ 20 global score; Table  ).
This study has several key strengths. This is the first meta‐analysis exploring the prevalence of eating disorders in those with IBD. The recent surge in research (most included studies were published during or after 2022) on eating disorders in IBD enabled us to identify many new studies not included in previous systematic reviews, while our stringent search criteria excluded low‐quality case reports and case series included in earlier reviews. Finally, we investigated heterogeneity through sub‐group analyses looking at study design (clinician assessment vs. questionnaire studies) and clinically relevant variables (e.g., disease activity, IBD type), allowing this systematic review to more rigorously explore methodological and clinical factors relevant to eating disorders in IBD.
Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. While I
2 can inherently be high in prevalence meta‐analyses [ ], the observed heterogeneity between studies may also be driven by underlying limitations of the original studies, including potential misclassification bias in administrative studies (e.g., due to unvalidated ICD coding) or potential selection bias in questionnaire‐based studies [ ]. Additionally, stratified analyses may have been underpowered, yielding no significant differences between groups. Likewise, studies typically did not include a control population, meaning that the current meta‐analysis was unable to provide comparable prevalence estimates between IBD and non‐IBD populations. Furthermore, questionnaires employed were not validated for IBD populations, raising concerns of construct validity given the unique relationship individuals with IBD have with food. Studies also differed in the thresholds used to determine clinical significance of questionnaire scores.
While this review found that nearly 1 in 10 individuals living with IBD are likely at risk of disordered eating, the findings do not clarify whether eating disorders are a cause or consequence of IBD. To explore this potential causal relationship, high‐quality incidence studies are needed. Six studies [ , , , , , ] investigated new‐onset eating disorders in IBD populations, all reporting an increased risk compared to the comparator populations. However, heterogeneity in the definition of IBD populations, selection of matched controls and identification of eating disorders in administrative data limited the ability to meaningfully pool these estimates. Prospective cohort studies assessing the incidence of eating disorders in those with IBD remain an important area for future research.
This systematic review and meta‐analysis highlights the significant burden of eating disorders in individuals with IBD, while additionally finding a significant divergence in prevalence estimates measured through clinician assessments (3%) versus self‐report questionnaires (14%), muddying our ability to quantify the true burden of eating disorders in IBD. We found that studies employing the NIAS questionnaire specifically had the highest pooled prevalence (17%), indicating ARFID may pose the largest eating disorder burden in the IBD population, aligning with previous findings of highly restrictive eating behaviours in IBD populations [ ]. We found no significant difference in prevalence by sex, IBD type, age or disease activity. However, the lack of eating disorder diagnostic tools validated for use in an IBD population hinders accurate prevalence estimation. Future research should therefore focus on refining prevalence estimates and developing validated tools to accurately diagnose eating disorders in those with IBD.
