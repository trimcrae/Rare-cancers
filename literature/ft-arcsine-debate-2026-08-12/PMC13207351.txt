## Summary

## Background
Implemented in Spain in 2019, pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective HIV prevention strategy that reached an estimated 28,800 users by 2024. We aimed to address HIV incidence and characteristics in PrEP users.

## Methods
Ambispective observational cohort study conducted across 12 centers in Spain. Data from adults diagnosed with HIV who had used oral PrEP within six months prior to seroconversion were collected from August 2024 to March 2025. Main outcomes were HIV incidence among PrEP users, and clinical, virological, and drug resistance characteristics at diagnosis for all cases, as well as 12-month follow-up data for retrospective cases.

## Findings
Until March 2025, 53 seroconversions occurred among 19,884 PrEP users, yielding an incidence of 0.06 cases per 100 person-years (95% CI: 0.01–0.15). Most individuals were cisgender men who have sex with men (91%), median age of 32 years [IQR:27–37], and from Latin America (31/52; 60%) or Spain (17/52; 33%). Chemsex was reported by 24 (45%), and 49% had a concomitant sexually-transmitted infection. Daily emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (FTC/TDF) was the most frequent regimen (36/50; 72%). PrEP interruptions occurred in 71% (32/45) and 42% (15/36) had suboptimal adherence. Time on PrEP at diagnosis was 8 months [IQR:3–19] and time from last negative to first positive HIV test 3.6 months [IQR:2.7–9.3]. Baseline median viral load (VL) was 14,917 copies/mL [IQR:825–221,000]. Resistance testing was successful in 90% of individuals (44/49), with nucleos(t)ide reverse transcriptase inhibitor-related substitutions detected in 41% (18/44), mainly M184V/I. Thirty-five retrospective cases completed 1-year follow-up, with VL suppression of 87% (20/23) at 12 months.

## Interpretation
PrEP interruptions and/or poor adherence were very prevalent. Most cases were migrants and chemsex use was frequent. Resistance substitutions were frequently found. Despite overall low HIV incidence, some subgroups of PrEP users require additional interventions to prevent HIV.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
Before undertaking this study, we conducted a literature review using PubMed to identify reports of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection occurring in the context of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use in real-world settings. We included studies published up to February 28, 2025, without language restrictions, focusing on European cohorts, particularly from countries with early PrEP implementation. Search terms included combinations of: ‘‘HIV infection’’, ‘‘PrEP’’, ‘‘Pre-exposure prophylaxis’’, ‘‘HIV prevention’’, and ‘‘HIV resistance’’. We screened titles and abstracts. Available evidence indicated that HIV infections among PrEP users were uncommon and mostly linked to poor adherence, with reported incidence rates across European cohorts ranging from 0.078 to 0.210 per 100 person-years. These reports also highlighted the potential for resistance mutations to PrEP components in such cases, compromising antiretroviral treatment options. However, most studies had methodological limitations, such as absence of longitudinal follow-up, and were developed during the COVID-19 pandemic period, when many PrEP programs were compromised. Data from other countries with later PrEP implementation remained scarce.

## Added value of this study
While a centralized data warehouse for PrEP users at either the national or autonomic level does not currently exist in Spain, the individually collected data compiled in our research provides the largest and most comprehensive analysis of HIV infections among PrEP users in Spain to date, covering a recent period beyond the COVID-19 pandemic and including data from major PrEP centers nationwide. Of the 28,800 PrEP users reported in Spain in 2024, this study covers nearly 20,000 (69%), demonstrating its strong representativeness. We characterized incidence, behavioral risk factors, and resistance profiles, but also provided longitudinal data (an aspect rarely addressed in previous studies) on antiretroviral therapy (ART) outcomes and identified programmatic gaps relevant for public health interventions.

## Implications of all the available evidence
Taken together, current evidence confirms that PrEP is highly effective at the population level, yet HIV infections can still occur in the context of inconsistent use or undiagnosed acute HIV infection. From a public health perspective, this underscores the need for targeted strategies to address adherence gaps among high-risk populations and for rigorous screening to exclude HIV infection at PrEP initiation. In addition, both PrEP-related and non-PrEP-related resistance substitutions were commonly observed in our cohort, reinforcing the importance of baseline resistance genotyping in all newly diagnosed HIV infections, especially those occurring during PrEP use. ART initiation and observed suppression rates demonstrate the appropriate selection of antiretroviral regimens in this setting, but also emphasize the importance of maintaining uniform clinical guidelines. Suppression rates on ART, however, were slightly lower than those observed in naïve people with HIV with similar regimens. Finally, the characterization of individuals with breakthrough HIV infection despite oral PrEP supports evaluating alternative preventive strategies still poorly implemented in Europe, such as long-acting injective PrEP formulations, to enhance program effectiveness. Indeed, the United Kingdom has recently adopted this strategy by recommending long-acting injectable cabotegravir for individuals unable to use daily oral PrEP and Spain became the first country of the European Union to approve injectable cabotegravir for PrEP in February 2026. However, the appropriate use of long-acting injectable PrEP requires a thorough, individualized approach to reach individuals at the highest risk of oral PrEP failure.

## Introduction
Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) has emerged as a key biomedical strategy for preventing human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection among individuals at substantial risk. The oral combination of generic emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (FTC/TDF) was first approved in the United States in 2012 and in several European countries, such as France and the United Kingdom, in 2016 (https://www.niaid.nih.gov/diseases-conditions/pre-exposure-prophylaxis-prep and https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/truvada; last accessed February 4, 2026). In 2019, the Spanish Ministry of Health authorized public funding for FTC/TDF as daily PrEP, marking a major step towards equitable access within the national health system. PrEP implementation in Spain was heterogeneous, influenced by differing regional health structures and the involvement of multiple providers, among which hospital-based services, community organizations, and sexually transmitted infection (STI) clinics. Since then, PrEP has expanded rapidly, increasing from 13,652 users in 2022 to 28,798 in 2024. , In parallel, new HIV diagnoses –previously stable or slightly declining between 2013 and 2019– dropped by 19% from 2019 to 2023. This tendency likely reflects the combined impact of multiple HIV prevention strategies, including PrEP programs –particularly among gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (GBMSM), who account for more than half of these new HIV diagnoses– as well as treatment-as-prevention (TasP) and Test&Treat approaches. At present, no centralized national data warehouse of PrEP users is available, which makes it impossible to characterize the overall PrEP population and requires individual data collection for those cases of interest.
Despite robust evidence demonstrating PrEP's effectiveness across diverse high-risk populations –including those engaging in chemsex, substance use, and multiple sexual partners–, , , , HIV seroconversions can still occur. Most cases are related to suboptimal adherence or interruptions in use. , In addition, initiating PrEP in individuals with undiagnosed acute HIV infection, not detected by rapid tests, may be associated with a higher risk of developing drug-resistance mutations, potentially limiting future treatment options. , , , 
While overall HIV incidence in Spain shows a downward trend, the sustained expansion of PrEP uptake suggests that the absolute number of infections among users might rise, which highlights the need for systematic surveillance in this setting. A better understanding of these infections is essential to inform public health strategies, optimize PrEP programs (including accurate exclusion of acute HIV infection before initiation), and guide clinical decision-making regarding monitoring protocols, adherence interventions, and antiretroviral therapy (ART) selection.
Experiences of HIV infection in the context of PrEP use have been reported in other regions, , but data from Europe remain scarce, with reports available from only a limited number of those countries with very early national PrEP implementation. , , This study provides a comprehensive characterization of HIV infection in PrEP users in Spain, including incidence, epidemiological, clinical, and virological features, as well as antiretroviral resistance profiles, to inform prevention policy and clinical practice.

## Methods
The study was reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (https://www.strobe-statement.org, last accessed February 2, 2026).

## Study design and participants
We conducted an ambispective, observational cohort study to evaluate HIV infections among PrEP users across 12 centers located in different Spanish regions (Catalonia, Andalucia, Galicia, and Madrid autonomic communities). The centers were selected to reflect the real distribution of PrEP provision in Spain, ensuring representativeness in terms of data availability and geographical coverage. Data were collected until March 2025.
Eligible participants were adults diagnosed with HIV who had received oral PrEP (either daily, on-demand, or mixed regimen) within the six months prior to seroconversion. There were no exclusion criteria. Long-acting injectable PrEP (LA-PrEP) was not available in Spain during the study period.
The study was started in August 2024. Participants were classified as retrospective, ambispective, or prospective cases, according to the timing of HIV diagnosis and follow-up in relation to study inclusion. Retrospective cases were those in which both the first HIV positive test and the full 12-month follow-up occurred before study inclusion. Ambispective cases had their first HIV positive test more than 30 days before study inclusion, with follow-up spanning both before and after consent. Prospective cases were diagnosed at or within 30 days before study inclusion, and their follow-up took place entirely after enrollment. Inclusion of prospective cases took place from study start to March 2025. Participants diagnosed outside Spain prior to 2019 were allowed to be included as retrospective cases for the clinical and epidemiological analyses, but were excluded from the incidence calculations.

## Study procedure
No data warehouse for PrEP users exists at the national or regional level in Spain, thereby requiring individual data collection for people meeting the inclusion criterion. In addition, there was no standardized national procedure for confirming HIV infection in PrEP users, and centers applied their own diagnostic pathways.
Baseline (at HIV diagnosis) socio-demographic –age, gender, sexual orientation, geographic origin, level of education, chemsex or recreational drug use, sexual practices–, clinical –signs or symptoms of primary infection, concomitant STIs at HIV diagnosis, ART–, and laboratory data –CD4 cell count, CD4 percentage, viral load (VL) count, type of last negative HIV test and type of available positive HIV tests at diagnosis, Fiebig Stage, documented resistance substitutions– were collected and analyzed for all participants. Chemsex and recreational drug use were distinguished based on the reported purpose of substance use (sexual versus non-sexual) according to individuals’ medical records. In retrospective cases, follow-up laboratory data up to 12 months after HIV diagnosis were also analyzed. Substitutions were evaluated based on the Major HIV-1 Drug Resistance Mutations list from the Stanford University HIV Drug Resistance Database, most recently updated on 28 March 2024 (https://hivdb.stanford.edu/; last accessed August 14, 2025). Self-reported suboptimal adherence could only be reliably determined for individuals on a daily regimen, and was defined as missing four or more doses per week during the three months before the first positive HIV test. For individuals using the on-demand regimen, we recorded the number of incomplete 2-1-1 cycles, and for those on mixed regimens, who alternated between daily and on-demand dosing, both types of adherence data were documented. PrEP-to-PEP conversion was defined as the switch from pre-exposure prophylaxis to post-exposure prophylaxis, consisting of escalation to a 3-drug ART regimen following a potential HIV exposure in the context of inconsistent PrEP use (https://eacs.sanfordguide.com/; last accessed February 2, 2026). Serious adverse events (SAEs) were collected at scheduled visits for prospective and ambispective participants, and obtained from medical records for retrospective ones.
Data were collected using a specifically designed electronic case report form (eCRF) implemented in the REDCap system (Research Electronic Data Capture) hosted at Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (HCB).

## Statistical analyses
Data management and statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19 (StataCorp, 2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release 19. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). Categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared between groups with the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Each variable was analyzed on the subset of observations with valid values, and no specific methods were used to handle missing data. The incidence rate per 100 person-years was calculated in centers able to report the total person-time in their cohort. For incidence measures, confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method. To obtain the pooled estimates, we performed a meta-analysis. A logistic-normal random-effects model was fitted, applying the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation of proportions to stabilize the variances. The random effects model used the restricted maximum likelihood method, with inverse variance weighting. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, which describes the percentage of total variation in effect estimates that is due to between-center heterogeneity rather than chance. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a significance level of 0.05 was applied.

## Ethics approval
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the HCB (HCB/2024/0227). Written informed consent was obtained for all prospective and ambispective cases, as well as for retrospective cases who could still be contacted. For retrospective cases who were no longer followed at the participating centers and could not be reached, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee in accordance with the approved protocol.

## Role of the funding source
The funding source had no role in study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, writing of the report, or the decision to submit the article for publication.

## Results

## HIV incidence
By March 2025, a total of 53 HIV seroconversions were identified within a cohort of 19,884 PrEP users –equivalent to 69% of the Spanish users documented in the 2024 SiPrEP report–, including 40 retrospective, 2 ambispective, and 11 prospective cases. Overall incidence rate (IR) was estimated at 0.06 cases per 100 person-years (95% CI: 0.01−0.15) in 8 centers able to report total person-time of follow-up (42 out of the 53 total cases, ).
 

## Baseline characteristics
 summarizes the socio-demographic and epidemiological characteristics at the time of HIV diagnosis. The study population was predominantly composed of young cisgender GBMSM (48/53; 91%) with a significant proportion originating from Latin America (60%). University education was reported among 50% (23/46) of participants. Notably, a substantial number reported recent engagement in chemsex (24/53; 45%) as well as recreational substance use (29/53; 55%), often characterized by polydrug use. Slamming (the injection of drugs before or during planned sexual activity) was reported in four cases. Sexual risk practices were common, including group sex (25/53; 47%) and participation in chills or sexualized parties (20/52; 38%), alongside a high level of sexual activity and partner turnover, with participants reporting an average of approximately four different partners per month. Nearly half of the individuals (26/53; 49%) had a concurrent STI, most frequently gonorrhea (13/26; 50%), syphilis (11/26; 42%), and chlamydia (8/26; 31%); the proportion of symptomatic infections was not recorded.
 

## PrEP use and characteristics
All cases included received oral PrEP. The median time between the first and last PrEP dose was 8 months [N = 51; IQR: 3–19] among all participants, compared with 3.6 months [N = 16; IQR: 2.1–15.2] among those with a documented NRTI-associated M184V/I substitution (p = 0.058). At the time of seroconversion, 68% (36/53) were using daily oral PrEP, 26% (14/53) were using on-demand PrEP, and 6% (3/53) had mixed use. Out of the 52 cases with available information, 49 (94%) were taking FTC/TDF, while 3 (6%) were on FTC/TAF (emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide fumarate). No PrEP-to-PEP conversion was documented in the three months prior to diagnosis. The interval between the last PrEP dose to first HIV positive test had a median of 12 days [N = 52; IQR: 0–93], and the median time from last negative to first positive HIV test was 3.6 months [N = 52; IQR: 2.7–9.3] ( a). A significant proportion of cases reported interruptions in PrEP only once (8/45; 18%) or more than once (24/45; 53%). Among those on a daily regimen, 15 (N = 36; 42%) showed suboptimal adherence. In the group using the on-demand, data were available for four cases, with only one reporting incomplete dosing cycles.
 As shown in , most last negative HIV tests had been performed using laboratory-based serological assays (43/52; 83%), with a smaller proportion relying on rapid tests (9/52; 17%). Signs and symptoms consistent with primarily HIV infection were reported in 15 cases (N = 53; 28%). The most frequently used diagnostic tests for the first positive HIV result were the fourth-generation HIV antigen/antibody assay and Western blot for confirmation (50/53; 94%), followed by HIV RNA detection (49/53; 92%). Two participants reported having used PrEP outside Spain prior to its national implementation in 2019 and they had tested HIV-positive before this reference date. At diagnosis, 5 (10%) individuals had undetectable VL, and 38% (19/50) showed a positive p31 band on Western blot, indicative of complete seroconversion (Fiebig Stage VI). A total of 47 (N = 52; 90%) participants were classified as being in Fiebig Stages IV−VI. Median baseline CD4 cell count and VL were 620 cells/mm3 [IQR: 474–760] and 14,917 copies/mL [IQR: 825–221,000], respectively. The highest baseline VL was >10 million copies/mL, and there were 20 cases with VL above 100,000 copies/mL, six of which exceeded 1 million copies/mL.
 

## Genotyping resistance testing
Baseline RNA genotypic resistance testing (GRT) was available in 92% (49/53) of cases and successfully amplified in 90% (44/49) of those samples. Resistance-associated substitutions were identified across all major antiretroviral drug classes except protease inhibitors ( ). Substitutions affecting nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) were the most common (41%), driven predominantly by substitutions compromising emtricitabine (M184V/I, detected in 17 individuals), while K65R, affecting tenofovir, was observed in only one case. Substitutions related to non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs, 18%), and integrase inhibitors (INSTIs, 2%) were also observed, including the E138A (NNRTI) and G163K (INSTI) polymorphic substitutions. Nine individuals had more than one substitution detected, and no virological failure requiring repeated genotyping were identified among retrospective cases during follow-up.
 

## Antiretroviral therapy
ART was started a median of 10 days [N = 51; IQR: 4–21] after the first HIV positive test ( b) and 34 days [N = 50; IQR: 10–107] after the last PrEP dose. PrEP was continued until ART initiation in 13 out of 50 individuals (26%).
The most frequently prescribed regimen was bictegravir (BIC)/FTC/TAF, used in 30 cases (57%). Other combinations included BIC or dolutegravir (DTG) plus darunavir/cobicistat (DRV/c) and FTC/TAF in 8 cases (15%), DTG/lamivudine (3TC) in 4 (8%), DTG/rilpivirine (RPV) in 2 (4%), DRV/c + FTC/TAF in 2 (4%), and various other regimens in 7 cases (13%), including BIC/FTC/TAF + doravirine (DOR), DRV/c + FTC/TAF, DTG/abacavir (ABC)/3TC, DTG/3TC + BIC/FTC/TAF, DTG + RPV/FTC/TAF, efavirenz (EFV)/FTC/TDF, FTC/TDF + elvitegravir (EVG/c), and FTC/TDF + atazanavir (ATV) + ritonavir (RTV). Among the four individuals who initiated DTG/3TC, two had GRT results showing no resistance, while amplification failed in the remaining two. Both individuals starting DTG/RPV had the M184V/I substitution detected prior to initiating this NRTI-sparing regimen.

## Follow-up (retrospective cases)
Among 40 retrospective participants, 35 (88%) completed a 51.5-week follow-up [IQR: 48−55.5], and 5 (12%) were lost to follow-up or transferred. ART simplifications were documented in 5 cases (13%), including one individual who underwent treatment switches on two separate occasions. Moreover, ART discontinuation occurred in 4 participants (10%).
The evolution of CD4 cell count, CD4 percentage, and VL from diagnosis to month 12 is detailed in . Four individuals (N = 36; 11%) were undetectable at diagnosis. Viral load dropped sharply after initiation of ART, with 44% of individuals achieving undetectable levels (<50 copies/mL) by month 1, increasing to 79% at month 3, and reaching 87% at both months 6 and 12. No SAEs related to either PrEP or ART were reported.
 

## Discussion
In this multicenter ambispective cohort study of PrEP users in Spain, we observed a very low incidence of HIV infection, primarily associated with intermittent or poor PrEP adherence, consistent with reports across European countries with earlier PrEP implementation. , , In comparison with these previous studies, our analysis contributes novel insights by covering a more recent period that extends beyond the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2025), incorporating follow-up of individuals who seroconverted, and drawing on data from multiple centers (including the major PrEP centers nationwide). In addition, the size of our cohort is significantly larger than that reported in previous studies, thereby enhancing the broad representativeness of our findings in Spain and providing actionable evidence for national surveillance and program optimization.
Among GBMSM, our cohort is in line with the patterns reported in Spanish settings. , , , Across these studies, chemsex prevalence ranged from 26.5% to 33.8%, reaching 55% in the PrEP-specific cohort from the coordinating center of the present study, compared with 45% in our sample. Group sex varied between 11% and 63%, with our cohort falling in the mid-range (47%). Reported STI diagnoses ranged from 17.9% and 52.4%, with our estimate (49%) being closest to the highest value reported. University education ranged from 50% to 71%, similar to the 50% documented in our cases.
Resistance substitutions affecting FTC/3TC (17 cases) and, to a much lesser extent, TDF/TAF (one case) were identified in our sample of new HIV infections, as previously described. , , The continuation of PrEP until ART initiation in some cases may have contributed to the emergence of M184V. Additional substitutions across other antiretroviral classes were also detected. Among these, we observed two polymorphic substitutions, which confer low-level resistance but do not indicate exposure to resistant strains. The resistance rates observed in our cohort were higher than those reported among treatment-naïve individuals in Spain in 2024, where the prevalence was 5% of transmitted drug resistance for NRTIs (M184V, 1.3%), 11.1% for NNRTIs (K103N, 7.1%), and 0% for INSTIs. The detection of PrEP-related resistance substitutions suggests ongoing selective pressure due to intermittent FTC/TDF exposure, while the presence of non-PrEP-related substitutions at baseline in 7 participants points to transmission of resistant circulating strains –an important message for prevention policy and surveillance systems.
Interestingly, participants with a documented M184V/I substitution had a shorter PrEP exposure compared with the overall cohort (3.6 versus 8 months). These findings suggest that a subset of individuals with detected M184V/I may actually represent cases of unrecognized acute HIV infection at the time of PrEP initiation –populations inherently at greater risk for the emergence of resistance– rather than genuine instances of PrEP failure. Unfortunately, it is not possible to differentiate an infection acquired just a few days before starting PrEP from one that occurred shortly after. Moreover, at diagnosis, there were 20 cases (41%) with VL values above 100,000 copies/mL, which is consistent with poor or no PrEP intake around the time of infection.
The median interval between the last negative and first positive HIV test was 3.6 months; however, only 38% had a positive p31 band in the confirmatory test, which might suggest a slight delay in achieving full seroconversion, yet without compromising early diagnosis in PrEP users. ART was initiated a median of 10 days after diagnosis, with overall favorable virological and immunological outcomes during follow-up. Despite existing guidelines (https://gesida-seimc.org/guias-clinicas/ and https://eacs.sanfordguide.com/; last accessed February 2, 2026), diagnostic and management practices were not standardized and no unified algorithm was applied across centers, although all HIV infections were ultimately confirmed through RNA/DNA testing, and eventually by confirmatory serological tests. The median time between the first positive test and ART initiation likely reflects the time required for confirmatory procedures. However, continuing PrEP while awaiting diagnostic confirmation increases the risk of developing resistance to PrEP components and should be discouraged. A safer approach would be to transition immediately from PrEP to ART during the confirmation process and, if HIV infection is ultimately excluded, to resume PrEP thereafter. While 87% of participants achieved viral suppression by month 6 and month 12, and no SAEs were reported, these suppression rates appear slightly lower than those observed in real-world studies with similar ART regimens. Whether individuals experiencing PrEP adherence issues continue to face ART adherence challenges after infection must be explored in future studies. The ART regimens documented in our study were heterogeneous, ranging from dual regimens to 4-drug combinations, reflecting the lack of a uniform recommendation in clinical guidelines until recently (https://gesida-seimc.org/guias-clinicas/; last accessed February 2, 2026). Currently, triple ART regimens, with the addition of a high genetic barrier drug (such as DTG, BIC, or DRV) to PrEP components, are recommended, and these have been the most frequently used combinations in recent cases (BIC/FTC/TAF). This research underscores that although PrEP is highly effective at the population level, HIV infections can still occur in the context of inconsistent use or undiagnosed acute HIV infection at initiation. From a policy perspective, national surveillance of HIV infections on PrEP should be reinforced to ensure systematic screening to exclude acute HIV infection before starting PrEP, monitor resistance patterns, and inform clinical guidelines. Despite potential cost challenges, our findings support evaluating innovative approaches such as cabotegravir , or lenacapavir , for individuals with adherence challenges, a subgroup that has been clearly characterized in this study. Very recently, England implemented such an approach (https://www.nice.org.uk/news/articles/first-hiv-prevention-injection-for-people-unable-to-have-daily-prep-recommended, last accessed February 4, 2026), approving injectable cabotegravir for approximately 1000 people unable to use oral daily PrEP. SCOPE study may help to implement similar approaches in Spain or other countries in the region with similar epidemiological characteristics. Indeed, in February 2026, Spain became the first country of the European Union to approve cabotegravir for PrEP.
Our study had some limitations. As ethical approval and available data were restricted to individuals who seroconverted while using PrEP, we were unable to provide a full description for the 19,884 users in Spain, limiting our capacity to contextualize incidence and associated factors, beyond the seroconverter cohort. The absence of a totally precise denominator data limited the accurate estimation of incidence rates; additionally, only some centers were able to report institutional person-years of follow-up. Additional limitations include reliance on self-reported adherence and behavioral data, which may be affected by recall bias, as well as variability in diagnostic and monitoring practices across centers. Due to the ambispective nature of the study, we were unable to define and report ‘‘PrEP interruptions’’ with precision. Adherence to on-demand and mixed regimens could not always be reliably assessed because cycle-level information was inconsistently recorded.
Further prospective studies are needed to better characterize pharmacokinetics, resistance emergence, psychological consequences, and outcomes of breakthrough infections under various PrEP regimens, including LA formulations. Research should focus on the contribution of specific behavioral factors to PrEP failure risk, and evaluate targeted interventions to improve adherence in high-risk populations. Molecular and immunological studies may clarify transmission cluster dynamics, resistance patterns, and the viral reservoir in this context, which could differ from those not exposed to PrEP components, and will be explored in subsequent sub-studies of the present project.
In summary, this study showed a very low incidence of HIV infection in the context of PrEP use, alongside with a high prevalence of poor adherence among seroconverters. Chemsex use and associated practices (group sex and chills) were also frequent, but within the large range of rates reported in other GBMSM cohorts in Spain. PrEP-related and PrEP-unrelated HIV resistance substitutions were frequently found, and ART suppression rates at month 12 were slightly lower than those observed in people with HIV treated with similar recommended first line regimens in real-word settings.
