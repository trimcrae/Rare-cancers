## ABSTRACT
Hyponatremia has been found to be associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD). However, there is a lack of systematic evidence on the prevalence and impact of hyponatremia in COPD and AECOPD patients. We performed a systematic search on three major databases for articles published on or before 31 July 2025. We included all the studies reporting the prevalence or number of hyponatremia cases in COPD patients (stable and exacerbation) aged 18 years and above. Pooled prevalence was calculated by a random effects model using RStudio. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool was used for quality evaluation of the included studies. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we included 11 studies out of 284 studies retrieved in the initial search. Of these 11 studies, three were COPD studies, and eight were AECOPD studies. The pooled prevalence of hyponatremia among COPD patients was 14% (95% CI: 4%–28%), with significant heterogeneity (I² = 98.3%, p < 0.0001). The pooled prevalence of hyponatremia in AECOPD patients was 19% (95% CI: 9%–32%), with significant heterogeneity (I² = 98.8%, p < 0.0001). Most of the studies showed that hyponatremia was not associated with mortality as an outcome of AECOPD. This study found a high prevalence of hyponatremia in both COPD and AECOPD patients; however, substantial heterogeneity (I² > 98%) necessitates cautious interpretation of pooled estimates. Future studies with standardized diagnostic criteria and larger sample sizes are needed to establish more precise prevalence rates.

1.

## Introduction
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is one of the leading causes of morbidity, mortality, and a rise in healthcare costs around the world. This condition is characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation, primarily due to significant exposure to noxious particles or gases [ ]. Further, it may worsen respiratory symptoms and lead to hospitalization of patients, usually due to acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) [ , ]. The global prevalence of COPD among individuals aged 30–79 years was estimated at 10.3% in 2019, representing approximately 391.9 million people worldwide. Projections indicate an increase to nearly 600 million cases globally by 2050 [ ].
Hyponatremia is a body electrolyte disturbance characterized by a decrease in the serum sodium concentration below 135 mmol/L, where there is excessive total body water compared to sodium [ , ]. This condition occurs in up to 10% of hospitalized patients and has a prevalence of 7.7% among community-dwelling adults aged 55 years or older. Symptoms are often nonspecific, including falls, weakness, vertigo, nausea, and headache, but can progress to severe, life-threatening brain edema [ ].
Hyponatremia is a relatively common complication in patients with COPD. Studies examining acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) report prevalence rates ranging from 10.8% to 51.4% in hospitalized patients and up to 22% in nonhospitalized patients presenting to emergency departments [ ]. However, in clinically stable COPD patients, the prevalence of hyponatremia was found in the range of 3.87%–24.6% in those with COPD [ , ].
Hyponatremia in COPD and AECOPD patients is consistently associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including prolonged hospital stays, increased need for mechanical ventilation, higher rates of hospital readmission within 30 days or 1 year, and elevated in-hospital and overall mortality [ , ]. However, some studies have not found a significant association between hyponatremia and adverse outcomes [ , ]. This shows the inconsistent findings regarding hyponatremia and its impact on COPD and AECOPD.
Identification of the pooled prevalence through meta-analysis could provide a better understanding of the exact prevalence of hyponatremia among COPD and AECOPD patients globally. Similarly, a systematic approach for the main outcome associated with the impact of hyponatremia in COPD and AECOPD patients will help clinicians and policymakers to focus on specific outcomes and early interventions for better treatment approaches. Some studies show that early management of hyponatremia has been associated with reduced mortality in various disease conditions [ ].
This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to estimate the prevalence of hyponatremia in both stable and exacerbated forms of COPD, along with its impact. This study may help to fill this gap and provide insight into the relationship between hyponatremia and COPD.

2.

## Materials and methods
We followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to write this systematic review and meta-analysis, which was prepared based on the PRISMA 2020 checklist [ ]. This protocol was registered in PROSPERO with registration number (CRD42023476479). The checklist is available as a Supplementary file 1.
2.1.

## Eligibility criteria
We follow the framework condition, context, and population (CoCoPop) for the establishment of inclusion and exclusion criteria [ ].

2.2.

## Condition
We have included those studies that have clearly defined a hyponatremia measure in the serum of patients with COPD (stable and exacerbation).

2.3.

## Context
All the observational studies reporting the prevalence or number of people with hyponatremia and its impact on COPD (stable and exacerbation), irrespective of geographical location and study location, have been included in this study. We have excluded studies published in languages other than English, reviews, unpublished studies, editorials, randomized controlled trials, case studies, and abstracts.

2.4.

## Population
Studies conducted among COPD patients (stable and exacerbation) aged ≥18 years in both genders. We have excluded a study conducted among children.

2.5.

## Database search
Two authors (PA and RR) conducted an independent systematic search on the major databases PubMed, Scopus, and EMBASE from inception until 31 July 2025. The keywords used to identify articles are as follows: ‘prevalence’ AND ‘hyponatremia’ AND ‘COPD.’ For all these keywords, entry terms were used during the advanced search in all the databases in combination with the Boolean operators ‘OR’ and ‘AND.’ No filters were used while searching the data. Further, an additional hand search was done for references of the included articles for any additional papers. The details of the data search strategy are provided in Supplementary file 2.

2.6.

## Study selection
The data obtained after a thorough search in the major databases were imported into Excel, and duplicates were removed. After the removal of duplicate data, PA and RR independently screened the titles and abstracts of all the articles to check the eligibility of the articles. Those articles that met the inclusion criteria during title and abstract screening were further screened for full texts and were later excluded if they did not meet our inclusion criteria. The disagreements during all the phases of data screening were further discussed with the third author, CG, and the final decision was made.

2.7.

## Data extraction
The included studies were first classified as follows: 1. prevalence of hyponatremia in COPD patients (stable), 2. prevalence of hyponatremia in COPD patients (exacerbation) 3. impact of hyponatremia on COPD (stable), and 4. impact of hyponatremia on COPD (exacerbation). An Excel spreadsheet was prepared to enter all the relevant information required for the analysis of the data. For all the included studies, we made two Excel entries, one for prevalence and the other for the impact of hyponatremia. The following data were extracted: first author, date of publication, diagnosis of hyponatremia, study country, age, prevalence, and outcomes (hospitalization, mortality, length of hospital stay, and other outcomes). PA and RR performed independent data extraction. Any disagreement during the data extraction phase was finalized by the third author, CG.

2.8.

## Quality assessment
Owing to the variability in the included studies, we included three JBI critical appraisal checklists (prevalence studies, analytical cross-sectional studies, and cohort studies) for the assessment of the quality of the included studies [ ]. PA and RR independently assess the quality of all the included studies using these three checklists, depending on the study design. To assist the quality of prevalence studies, there are a total of nine questions; for analytical cross-sectional studies, there are a total of eight questions; and for cohort studies, there are a total of eleven questions. All these appraisal tools have four common possible answers (yes, no, unclear, and not applicable) for all the questions.
This study did not incorporate quality assessment as a criterion for study inclusion. Studies were classified as having a high risk of bias when any assessment item received a ‘no’ or ‘unclear’ response, while low risk of bias was assigned when all items were answered affirmatively.

2.9.

## Statistical analysis
The pooled prevalence of hyponatremia and its 95% confidence interval were calculated using a random-effects model with the inverse variance method and Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. Heterogeneity was examined by I² tests. An I² more than 75% was considered as substantial heterogeneity [ ]. Owing to studies, publication bias was not assessed (<10 studies) [ ]. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing the study with different cutoffs for hyponatremia. Owing to the variability in the reporting of outcome data, the impact of hyponatremia on COPD and AECOPD patients was summarized narratively. The significance level was set at a p value of less than 0.05. All the statistical analyzes were done using R (version 4.5.1) through RStudio (version 2025.5.1.513) using (meta package 8.2-0) [ , ].

## Results
3.1.

## Study selection
A total of 284 articles were retrieved in the initial search. After duplicate removal, 260 articles underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 18 articles were subjected to full-text screening, and ultimately, 11 articles met the inclusion criteria for analysis. Among the 11 included studies, three investigated stable COPD patients [ ], while eight examined patients with AECOPD [ ]. The study selection process is detailed in .
 
3.2.

## Study characteristics
Among the 11 included studies, eight employed cohort designs (three prospective [ , , ] and five retrospective [ , , , , ]), while the remaining three utilized cross-sectional designs [ , , ]. Most studies were hospital-based, with one exception that utilized an online database [ ]. The majority of studies originated from developed countries, and nearly all defined hyponatremia as serum sodium levels were less than 135 mmol/L. One study used 137 mmol/L as the cutoff [ ], and another employed two thresholds: 133 mmol/L and 136 mmol/L [ ]. The detailed study characteristics are presented in .
 
3.3.

## Risk of bias
In this study, we employed three Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality assessment tools tailored to the respective study designs: cohort, cross-sectional, and prevalence studies. The results are presented in Tables 1–3 and Figure 1 (Supplementary file 3). Among the eight included cohort studies, quality assessment revealed variable adherence to methodological standards. Only one study satisfied all the quality criteria [ ], while two studies failed to meet one criterion each [ , ], two studies did not satisfy two criteria each [ , ], and three studies fell short on three criteria each out of 11 criteria [ , , ]. One cross-sectional study met all the quality criteria [ ], whereas the other failed to satisfy two out of eight criteria [ ]. The single prevalence study included in our analysis did not meet one quality criterion out of nine criteria [ ].

3.4.

## Hyponatremia in COPD
The overall pooled prevalence of hyponatremia among patients with COPD (N = 1585) was 14% (95% CI, 4%–28%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98.3%, τ2 = 0.0253, p < 0.0001). The forest plot is shown in .
 
3.5.

## Hyponatremia in AECOPD
We have included 7 studies (N = 5052) that report the number of cases of hyponatremia in their studies for meta-analysis. The overall pooled prevalence of hyponatremia in AECOPD patients was 19% (95% CI 9%–32%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98.8%, τ2 = 0.0386, p < 0.0001). The forest plot is shown in .
 
3.6.

## Sensitivity analysis
We have done a sensitivity analysis by excluding the study that used a different definition of hyponatremia in both COPD [ ] and AECOPD [ ]. In the sensitivity analysis of COPD, the pooled prevalence of hyponatremia was found to be 20% (95% CI, 13%–29%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 84.9%, τ2 = 0.0046, p = 0.0100). Further in AECOPD, the pooled prevalence was 18% (95% CI 7%–33%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 99%, τ2 = 0.0451, p < 0.0001). The forest plot is shown in Figures 2 and 3 (Supplementary file 4).

3.7.

## Impact of hyponatremia in COPD
Among the three studies involving COPD patients, only two studies reported outcomes associated with hyponatremia. Kwok et al. [ ] demonstrated significant mortality in patients with hyponatremia, whereas Eckart et al. [ ] found no significant association with mortality. Kwok et al. [ ] demonstrated that patients with high baseline sodium levels experienced significantly lower frequencies of AECOPD occurrence and hospital visits due to AECOPD. This is shown in .
 
3.8.

## Impact of hyponatremia in AECOPD
Mortality as an outcome of hyponatremia was reported in five studies involving AECOPD patients. This is shown in . Only postdischarge mortality was significantly associated with hyponatremia [ ], while studies examining overall and in-hospital mortality showed no significant association [ , , , ]. Tokgöz et al. [ ] found significantly higher rates of 30-day hospital readmission in hyponatremia patients compared to non-hyponatremia patients, whereas two other studies showed no significant differences between groups [ , ]. Chalela et al. and García Sanz MT et al. reported significantly longer hospital stays in AECOPD patients with hyponatremia compared to those without hyponatremia [ , ]. In contrast, Aryal et al. found no significant difference in the length of stay between groups [ ]. Additionally, García Sanz MT et al. demonstrated that hyponatremia is associated with poor disease progression in AECOPD patients [ ].

4.

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis, being the first of its kind, aimed to comprehensively synthesize the existing literature on the prevalence and impact of hyponatremia in both COPD and AECOPD patients. Our pooled prevalence analysis indicates that hyponatremia affects approximately 19% of AECOPD patients and 14% of patients with stable COPD. A sensitivity analysis, which considered variations in the definition of hyponatremia, yielded slightly different but comparable figures of 18% for AECOPD and 20% for COPD patients. These findings underscore that hyponatremia is a relatively common electrolyte disturbance in both stable and exacerbated COPD patient populations.
These prevalence rates align with findings from individual studies, which have reported rates ranging from 10.8% to 23% in AECOPD patients and up to 24.6% in stable COPD populations [ , , ]. However, a study by Aryal SB et al. reported a very high prevalence of 51.4% in patients with AECOPD [ ]. The variation in reported prevalence reflects differences in hyponatremia definitions, study populations, clinical settings, and methodological approaches, such as correction for hyperglycemia. The slightly higher prevalence observed in our sensitivity analysis for stable COPD (20%) likely reflects the exclusion of studies with low prevalence rates and larger sample sizes [ ]. Furthermore, a similar prevalence was found among hospitalized geriatric patients [ ] and lung cancer patients [ ].
Hyponatremia in patients with COPD and AECOPD is associated with a range of adverse clinical outcomes, although some studies present conflicting findings. Multiple studies demonstrate that hyponatremia, particularly in AECOPD, is linked to prolonged hospital stays, a 30-day readmission rate, an increased need for mechanical ventilation, and increased in-hospital and overall mortality [ , , ]. Despite this substantial evidence, some studies, including Lindner G et al. and Winther et al., did not find statistically significant associations between hyponatremia and adverse outcomes, such as intensive care unit admission, mechanical ventilation, 30-day readmission, or in-hospital mortality, in AECOPD patients [ , ].
Lindner et al. acknowledged that their study's case number might have been low, and the study was underpowered to detect such associations [ ]. Similarly, Winther et al. noted that their study might not have been powered to detect small-scale effects of more severe hyponatremia [ ]. The underlying mechanisms of hyponatremia in COPD patients are multifactorial, including syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), increased anion gap, and comorbidities, such as heart failure and diuretic use [ , , ].
The evidence regarding postdischarge mortality in COPD patients with hyponatremia by Eckart et al. presents conflicting results found in AECOPD patients that highlight the complexity of this electrolyte disorder's prognostic significance [ ]. Kwok et al. demonstrated a significant relationship when examining overall mortality outcomes [ ]. This discrepancy may reflect important methodological differences, hyponatremia severity classifications, patient selection criteria, or adjustments for confounding variables across studies.
The conflicting findings suggest that hyponatremia's prognostic impact in COPD and AECOPD may be context-dependent, potentially influenced by factors, such as the underlying etiology of electrolyte disturbance, the presence of comorbidities, or the adequacy of sodium correction during hospitalization. These inconsistent results underscore the need for standardized outcome definitions, longer follow-up periods, and comprehensive adjustments for potential confounders to clarify whether hyponatremia represents a modifiable risk factor or merely a marker of disease severity in these patients.
The strength of this study lies in being the first comprehensive meta-analysis examining both the prevalence of hyponatremia and its clinical impact across stable COPD and acute exacerbations. Additionally, we conducted a systematic search across three major databases to ensure comprehensive literature coverage. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Most of the included studies utilized cohort designs rather than being designed primarily for prevalence estimation, which may affect the accuracy of our prevalence estimates. The limited number of studies focusing on stable COPD patients may not reflect the true prevalence in this population and could impact overall prevalence calculations.
Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity between studies necessitates cautious interpretation of pooled results. We were unable to assess publication bias due to the insufficient number of included studies. The predominance of studies from developed countries may limit the generalizability of our global prevalence estimates, as healthcare systems, patient populations, and disease management practices vary significantly across different economic settings.
Future research should prioritize specifically designed prevalence studies in COPD populations, with particular emphasis on stable disease states and the inclusion of diverse geographic regions, to establish precise burden estimates and inform evidence-based management strategies for optimizing outcomes in COPD patients with hyponatremia.

5.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates a high prevalence of hyponatremia in COPD patients across both stable and exacerbated disease states. Healthcare providers should implement routine sodium monitoring in COPD patients, with particular attention given to those presenting with identified risk factors. Early recognition and appropriate management of hyponatremia may improve clinical outcomes, reduce healthcare utilization, and decrease mortality in this vulnerable population.
