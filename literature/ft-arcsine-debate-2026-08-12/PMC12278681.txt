## Abstract

## Background
Numerous epidemiological studies on hepatitis B virus among pregnant women in Ethiopia have been conducted, showing significant variation over time and between different geographical regions. The aim of this systematic review and Meta-analysis is to estimate the overall prevalence of hepatitis B virus infection and its associated factors among pregnant women in Ethiopia.

## Methods
We use the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for articles. All observational published studies were retrieved using relevant search terms PubMed, Embase, web of science and google scholar databases. Joanna Briggs Institute Meta-Analysis of Statistics Assessment and Review Instruments for observational studies was used for critical appraisal of the included articles. The meta-analysis was done with STATA version 18 software. The I2 statistics were used to test heterogeneity whereas Begg’s and Egger’s tests were used to assess publication bias. The pooled prevalence was presented using the forest plot.

## Results
Forty –three studies with a total of 17,056 participants were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of the HBV infection among pregnant women was 6%(95% CI: 5.0, 7.0). a high pooled prevalence HBV infection among pregnant women was found in Tigray 7%(95% CI: 4.0, 8.0) and Somali region 7%(95% CI: 4.0, 11.0). history of surgical procedure, having multiple sexual partners, history of body tattooing, history of abortion, history of sharing sharp material, blood transfusion and family history of HBV were significant risk factors associated with HBV among pregnant women in Ethiopia.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provided evidence regarding the intermediate prevalence of HBV among pregnant women in Ethiopia and also showed history of surgical procedures, multiple sexual partners, body tattooing, sharing sharp objects, abortion, blood transfusions, and family history of HBV infection were associated factors. The findings highlight the need of health education to prevent the spread of HBV infection among pregnant women.

## Background
Hepatitis B is a liver infection resulting from the hepatitis B virus (HBV). The infection may either be acute (short and severe) or chronic (long-term). Chronic hepatitis B increases the likelihood of serious conditions like cirrhosis and liver cancer, which can be lead to death [ ].
In 2022, around 254 million individuals were living with hepatitis B and approximately 1.3 million people died from viral hepatitis, making it the second leading causes of death from communicable diseases after COVID-19. Updated data from 187 countries revealed an increase in hepatitis-related deaths from 1.1 million in 2019 to 1.3 million in 2022, with hepatitis B accounting for 83% of the death. the increase in estimated mortality since 2019 suggests that the number of hepatitis-related cancer cases and deaths are increasing. Access to effective interventions must be urgently expanded to save lives and prevent a future generation of new infections, cancer cases and deaths [ ].
In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced a global health sector strategy to control viral hepatitis, aiming to eliminate it as a major public health threat by 2030 [ ]. One key objective is to reduce HBV prevalence in children under 5 to 0.1% [ ]. Since mother-to-child transmission is the primary way HBV spreads, preventing this transmission is crucial for eliminating the virus [ ]. An effective approach is to implement standardized health management for pregnant women with chronic HBV infection.
So many studies have been conducted on the prevalence of the HBV among pregnant women in Ethiopia. For each separate study, different key risk factors were identified for pregnant women, like abortion history, History of multiple sexual partners, surgical procedure, History of hospitalization, traditional tonsillectomy, history of sexually transmitted infection, history of blood transfusion, tattooing [ – ].
In Ethiopia, the prevalence of all type of viral hepatitis infection is increasing [ – ]. Several studies have been conducted about hepatitis B Prevalence among pregnant women in Ethiopia, However, with the results ranging from 2.3% to 7.9% [ , , ]. Further studies are needed to support the health policy development and guide future research to reduce HBV infection.
Findings from other parts of Africa support similar concerns. A recent meta-analysis showed that the prevalence of hepatitis B among pregnant women in both Africa and East African was around 6% to 6.77%. respectively [ , ].
A high prevalence of HBV among pregnant women can have significant public health implications, impacting both maternal and neonatal outcomes. HBV increases the risk of liver-related complications in pregnant women, such as liver cirrhosis and liver failure, which can affect overall maternal health [ ]. The transmission of HBV from mother to child during childbirth is a major concern, leading to a higher risk of chronic HBV infection in newborns. This can result in lifelong health issues for the child, including liver disease, cirrhosis, and an increased risk of liver cancer [ ]. Additionally, the burden of managing HBV in a population with high transmission rates can strain healthcare systems, requiring more resources for screening, vaccination, and long-term care [ ].
The high prevalence of HBV among pregnant women underlines the need for comprehensive public health strategies, including routine prenatal screening, vaccination programs, and education to reduce the burden of HBV and prevent perinatal transmission [ ]. Effective management of HBV in pregnant women is crucial for protecting the health of both the mother and the child, as chronic HBV infection can lead to severe liver disease later in life [ ]. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to give a quantitative estimate of the prevalence of HBV infection and its associated factors among pregnant women in Ethiopia.

## Objective of the review
To assess the prevalence of hepatitis B virus infection and its associated factors among pregnant women in Ethiopia.

## Research question

What is the prevalence of hepatitis B virus infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia?
What are the associated factors of hepatitis B virus infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia?

## Methods

## Design and registration
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA)-P guidelines have been adhered throughout the process. The results were presented following the PRISMA-2020 standard [ ]. A preliminary database search is being conducted, and a data extraction sheet was prepared. This review was registered in The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration number CRD42024583487.
The reporting flowchart is presented in Fig. .
 

## Inclusion criteria
This systematic review and meta-analysis only included cross-sectional study designs. It was included studies published in peer-reviewed journals that report the prevalence of hepatitis B virus and associated factors among pregnant women in Ethiopia. The review covered studies published from 2003 to 2024 and only consider articles written in English.

## Exclusion criteria
Studies that did not report on the prevalence of hepatitis B virus and its associated factors were excluded from this investigation. Additionally, articles published in languages other than English, conference proceedings, qualitative studies, commentaries, editorial letters, case reports, and case series were also excluded.

## Search strategy
Studies published from 2003 to 2024 were systematically searched and identified in the following electronic databases: PubMed, EMBASE, web of science and Google Scholar. The search was used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), keywords, and free-text terms in PubMed's advanced search. Different Ethiopian university repository was searched to address gray literatures. Terms such as Hepatitis B virus, pregnant mother, associated factors and Ethiopia were combined using Boolean operators. the search results were imported into reference management software (EndnoteTM, Thomson Reuters, Toronto, ON, Canada) to remove duplicates.

## Selection of studies
To decide which papers to include, two authors (STS and YG) evaluated the studies according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Initially, they reviewed the titles of the articles retrieved from the search. Next, they screened the abstracts based on the predefined criteria to determine eligibility. Finally, the abstracts of these selected titles underwent a full-text review. The data charting and screening process was conducted using Microsoft ExcelTM. Only studies approved by both authors were proceed to the full article review. Any disagreements between the authors were resolved through discussion. A final list of articles for data extraction was compiled, including reasons for excluding certain studies.

## Data extraction and management
Once all eligible articles are identified, two independent reviewers (STS and HB) extracted the relevant data using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The data extraction format is based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) data extraction form for systematic reviews and research syntheses [ ] For each included article, the tool collected information on the authors'names, sample size, region, prevalence, type of study design, year of publication, sampling method, test method, risk factors/associated factors and the studies’ quality score. Any disagreements between data extractors were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

## Quality assessment
Two reviewers (STS and HB) independently evaluated the listed studies. For studies on prevalence or proportion, the JBI criteria was used to assess the quality of the articles [ ].
The assessment tool includes nine parameters: (1) an appropriate sampling frame, (2) an appropriate sampling technique, (3) an adequate sample size, (4) a description of the study subjects and setting, (5) adequate data analysis, (6) the use of valid methods for the identified conditions, (7) valid measurement for all participants, (8) the use of appropriate statistical analysis, and (9) an adequate response rate. The JBI tool uses a scoring system based on the questions listed above to assess the overall quality of each study. Articles assessed with a score of ≥ 50% considered as achieving desired quality. Finally, articles of medium and high quality were included for analysis. Any disagreements between the two authors were resolved through consensus during the quality appraisal Table .

 

## Statistical analysis
Data entry and analysis was performed using Stata Version 18 (STATA Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA) software. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using visual and statistical techniques. a forest plot was used to visually assess the presence of heterogeneity. The Q test and I-squared (I2) test statistics were considered to examine the variations. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicate low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [ ]. This review demonstrated that there was significant heterogeneity among studies (P-value < 0.001). thus, we used the random effect model with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to compute the HBV prevalence among pregnant women [ , ] however, the analysis demonstrated that there was a non-significant heterogeneity in estimating the associated factors, and the fixed effect model with inverse variance was used to analyze the association between HBV and factors. Subgroup analys and meta-regression were conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine the impact of individual studies on pooled estimates [ ].

## Publication bias
Publication bias was checked using a funnel plot and Egger’s statistical test. Based on the shape of the graph, a symmetrical graph was interpreted to suggest absence of publication bias, whereas an asymmetrical graph was interpreted to indicate presence of publication bias. Statistically significant Egger’s test (P-value < 0.05) indicates that the presence of a small study effect.

## Ethical considerations
Since this study is a systematic review and meta-analysis that relies solely on already published data and does not involve any patient participation, ethical approval and informed consent are not required.

## Result

## Characteristics of the studies
The included studies were conducted between 2003 and 2024 across different regions of Ethiopia among pregnant women. All the included studies were cross-sectional in design. Of the 3,624 identified studies, 712 were excluded due to duplication. Of the remaining 2,912 studies, 2,780 were excluded after reviewing their abstracts and titles. A total of 132 studies were sought for retrieval, and 89 were excluded based on the inclusion criteria. Finally, 43 studies, with a combined sample size of 17,056, were included in the quantitative analysis Fig. .
The median sample size was 328. Regarding the publication year, out of the 43 studies, 14 were published between 2020 and 2021. Six studies were published in 2016, five in 2022, four in 2018, and three in 2019. Two studies were published in each of the years 2017, 2023, and 2024, while one study was published in each of the years 2003, 2008, 2011, 2014, and 2015. Of the included studies, 13 (30%) were conducted in the Southern region, 9 (21%) in Oromia, 8 (19%) in the Amhara region, and the remaining 13 (30%) were conducted in other regions Table .

 

## Pooled prevalence of HBV infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia
This meta-analysis included 43 studies [ – , , , , , , , – ], and the overall pooled prevalence of HBV infection among pregnant women was 6.0% (95% CI: 5.0%–7.0%).
Prevalence across regions were as follows: Oromia, South, and Addis Ababa each had an overall prevalence of 6% (95% CI: 5%–7%, 4%–9%, and 4%–8%, respectively); the Amhara region had a prevalence of 5% (95% CI: 4%–6%); Tigray, 7% (95% CI: 4%–11%); and the Somali region, 7% (95% CI: 4%–8%) Figs.  and .
 

## Heterogeneity
The I2 statistic for HBV infection among pregnant women was 52.01 (p < 0.001), where a p-value of < 0.001 indicates the presence of significant heterogeneity among the included studies (Fig. ).
A subgroup meta-analysis was conducted to identify the source of this heterogeneity using the following grouping variables: year of publication (2003–2019 and 2020–2024), region, and mean/median sample size. None of these grouping variables were found to be sources of heterogeneity (p-difference > 0.05) Fig. .
In addition, we conducted a meta-regression analysis using year of publication and sample size, and the results revealed that neither variable was statistically significant Table .

 

## Sensitivity analysis
A sensitivity analysis was conducted, and the results showed that no single study had a value lying outside the 95% confidence interval of the overall estimate Fig. .
 

## Publication bias
Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test. The resulting funnel plot and regression test indicated evidence of symmetry and were not statistically significant (p-value = 0.53), suggesting that the included studies were free from publication bias Fig. Table .
 

## Associated factors of HBV infection
A history of multiple sexual partners, blood transfusion, abortion, body tattooing, surgery, sharing sharp materials, history of hospital admission, cesarean section, and a family history of HBV infection were found to be significant risk factors for HBV infection.
The pooled meta-analysis showed a strong association between a history of multiple sexual partners and HBV infection, with an odds ratio, OR = 12.69 (95% CI: 4.38, 36.77), I.2 = 8.07% and p -value = 0.36) [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , – , ].
Analysis also indicated higher odds of HBV infection among pregnant women who had a history of blood transfusion compared to those who did not, OR = 1.99(95%CI = 1.32, 3.00), I2 = 32.98%, p -value = 0.09) [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , – ]. Additionally, pregnant women with a history of abortion were three times more likely to be infected with HBV, OR = 3.52 (95% CI: 2.25, 5.5), I.2 = 54.1% and p-value < 0.001) [ , , , , , – , , , , , , – ].
Pregnant women with a history of body tattooing were 1.7 times more likely to be infected than those without (OR = 1.70 (95% CI: 1.45, 2.01), I2 = 19.67% p-value = 0.21) [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , – ]. Similarly, pregnant women who had; history of Cesarean section (OR = 1.65 (95% CI: 1.47, 1.86), I2 = 45.50% p-value = 0.14) [ , , , ], history of surgery (OR = 9.87 (95% CI: 2.59, 37.61), I2 = 0.00% p-value = 0.97) [ , , , , , , , , – , , ], history of sharing sharp materials (OR = 9.24 (95% CI: 2.34, 36.50), I2 = 0.00% p-value = 0.97) [ , , , , , , ], history of hospital admission (OR = 3.33 (95% CI: 1.71, 6.48), I2 = 0.00% p-value = 0.72) [ , , , , , , , , , , ], family history of HBV infection (OR = 11.20 (95% CI: 2.79, 44.96), I2 = 0.00% p-value = 0.69) [ , , , , , ] had higher risk of HBV infection than their counterpart Table  .

 

## Discussion
Prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection among pregnant women is a significant public health concern, given its potential for mother-to-child transmission, which can lead to chronic infection in newborns. Understanding the prevalence of HBV in this population through systematic reviews and meta-analyses is crucial for informing public health interventions, improving screening programs, and promoting effective management strategies.
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of HBV infection and its associated factors among pregnant women in Ethiopia. The findings from this systematic review and meta-analysis could enhance awareness of the prevalence of HBV infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia.
In the current study, all the included studies were cross-sectional and thus reflect the point prevalence of the disease. In this review, the pooled prevalence of HBV infection in Ethiopia among pregnant women was 6.0%(95% CI: 5.0–7.0%), this finding aligns with the WHO intermediate endemic definition of HBV infection (2–7%) [ ]. This indicates HBV infection remains a public health problem in Ethiopia. Factors significantly associated with HBV infection among pregnant women were, history surgical procedure, having multiple sexual partners, history of body tattooing, history of abortion, history of sharing sharp materials, blood transfusion and family history of HBV infection.
Compared to other systematic review and meta-analysis conducted elsewhere, the result is more or less comparable to a finding in Africa 5.89% based on 91 studies across 28 countries, and from East African countries, which reported a prevalence of 6% [ , ]. The magnitude of HBV infection among pregnant women in the present study was higher than that reported in a previous study among blood donors in Ethiopia (4.9%) [ ] in Iran (1.2%) [ ], and at the global level based on 222 studies (3%) [ ]. In contrast, it was lower than the prevalence reported in Nigeria (6.49%), [ ], among HIV infected individuals in Ethiopia (6.5%), [ ], and in China (6.6%) [ ]. The discrepancies may be attributed to variations in socioeconomic status, age, sexual behavior, maternal attitudes toward HBV risk, and medical exposure across study areas. Differences in sample size may also contribute to these inconsistencies.
A meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia 2016, reported that the prevalence of hepatitis B infection was 7.4% in the general population and 4.7% among pregnant women [ , ]. An updated meta-analysis in 2019 showed a decline in general population prevalence to 6.0% [ ]. In contrast, findings from the current study indicate that the prevalence among pregnant women has increase to 6.0%, making it comparable to the updated estimate for the general population. This convergence in prevalence may be attributed to several factors. The decline in the general population likely reflects the impact of national hepatitis B infection prevention efforts, including widespread vaccination, better infection control, and public health education. Meanwhile, the rise in prevalence among pregnant women could be linked to ongoing exposure to risk factors such as unsafe medical practices and unprotected sex, as well as gaps in pre-pregnancy immunization. Additionally, the expansion of antenatal screening and wider testing coverage may have improved case detection, leading to a higher reported prevalence. These findings indicted the importance of integrating prevention strategies with early screening and targeted interventions for women of reproductive age.
Our study found that pregnant women were at a higher risk of hepatitis B virus infection due to several factors. These included having a history of surgery, multiple sexual partners, body tattoos, sharp objects like needles, abortions, blood transfusions, and a family history of HBV infection. Having multiple sexual partners is known risk factors for sexually transmitted infections, including HBV. Our findings showed that pregnant women with multiple sexual partners had a higher risk of HBV infection compared to those with only one partner. This increased risk may be due to a greater likelihood of exposure to the virus through unprotected sexual contact. This finding matches what other studies have shown [ , ], although it’s different from results reported in a Nigerian study [ ].
We also found that women with tattoos were more likely to be infected with HBV. This could be because tattoos are sometimes done with non-sterile equipment, which can easily spread the virus. The same risk applies to traditional practices that involve sharing sharp tools. In fact, HBV can survive on surfaces like dried blood for up to seven days, making it easy to spread through contaminated objects [ , ].
Pregnant women who had undergone surgical procedures were at a greater risk of contracting HBV infection compared to their counterpart, this is a result of inadequate sterilization of medical equipment’s. These results are consistent with findings from prior studies [ , ]. In contrast, this result is inconsistent with a study done in Nigeria among pregnant women [ ]. this disagreement may be because of sample size and other socioeconomic factors.
Additionally, pregnant women with a history of abortion were found to have a higher likelihood of HBV infection. This may be due to women who have undergone abortions being exposed to healthcare facilities with insufficient sterilization practices, which increase their risk of contracting infectious diseases. These findings are consistent with previously conducted studies [ , ].
Pregnant women with a history of sharing sharp objects were at greater risk compared to those who did not. This may be due to the increased chances of getting contact with infected blood or body fluids, leading to direct exposure. This finding aligns with a meta-analysis studies from Africa and east African countries among pregnant women [ , ].
Pregnant women with a history of blood transfusion had higher risk of HBV infection compared to those without such a history. This can be linked to the risk of HBV transmission through transfusions, which remains considerable due to factors like the pre-seroconversion window period, infection by immune-variant viruses, and the presence of occult HBV carriers.
These findings are consistent with studies from Somaliiland [ ] and east Africa [ ]. However, the results are inconsistent with a study done in Nigeria and Alexandrea [ , ]. This is likely due to difference in sample size and the way procedures are performed in different countries. Furthermore, pregnant women with a family history of HBV infection were more likely to be at risk compared to those without a family history. This could be because they may have been exposed to the virus at a young age through close contact with infected relatives, and genetic factors might contribute to susceptibility. This result is in agreement with a previously conducted study among pregnant women [ , ].
Despite the valuable insights provided by this systematic reviews and meta-analyses, there are limitations that must be admitted. First, the quality and heterogeneity of the included studies can affect the reliability of the findings. Some studies may have small sample sizes, varying methodologies, or inconsistent diagnostic criteria, which can introduce bias into the pooled prevalence estimates.
Furthermore, almost all of the included studies are cross sectional study designs, which reduce our ability to make strong causal inferences in health research. Although cross-sectional studies have these weakness, they are more commonly used in this context and allowed us to increase the strength of our meta-analysis through the inclusion of a greater number of studies with more homogenous study design. Addressing these limitations in future research through the use of standardized methodologies and robust study designs are essential for obtaining more accurate estimates.
The findings from systematic reviews and meta-analyses of HBV infection prevalence among pregnant women in Ethiopia have significant implications for future research.
Ethiopian ministry of health should prioritize efforts to improve screening. This may include scaling up HBV testing during antenatal care, ensuring access to antiviral therapy for HBV-positive mothers, and strengthening vaccination programs to reduce future transmission.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provided evidence regarding the intermediate prevalence of HBV among pregnant women in Ethiopia and also showed history of surgical procedures, multiple sexual partners, body tattooing, sharing sharp objects, abortion, blood transfusions, and family history of HBV were associated factors. The findings highlight the need of health education to prevent the spread of HBV infection among pregnant women.
