## Introduction
Hemiplegic migraine (HM) is a rare subtype of migraine with aura, and its pathophysiology is similar to that of migraine with aura, primarily involving cortical spreading depression. Electroencephalography (EEG) may serve as a valuable tool for investigating brain function in patients with hemiplegic migraine. Therefore, this study aims to synthesize available evidence through a systematic review and pooled prevalence meta-analysis to assess the types of EEG abnormalities observed in each migraine phase in patients with hemiplegic migraine and to estimate the prevalence of EEG abnormalities during the interictal period.

## Method
We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane up to January 2025 and included articles, including case reports, case series, and retrospective studies that reported EEG abnormalities in hemiplegic migraine. The prevalence of abnormal EEG during the interictal phase was analyzed through pooled data analysis.

## Result
A total of 40 articles involving 65 patients were included in the analysis. The most common EEG abnormality During the aura phase was low-amplitude activity affecting the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis, observed in 71.4% (5/7) of cases. Similarly, During the headache and post-headache phases, the most frequently observed EEG abnormality was medium- to high-amplitude slowing, particularly in the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis, occurring in 54.9% (28/51) and 37.5% (9/24) of cases, respectively. Meanwhile, 78.8% (26/33) of patients’ EEG recordings during the interictal phase were normal.

## Conclusion
This study found that the most common EEG abnormality during the aura phase was low-amplitude activity in the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis, while during the headache and post-headache phases, medium- to high-amplitude focal slowing was frequently observed in the same hemisphere. EEGs during the interictal phase were mostly normal. These findings provide an overview of characteristic EEG patterns in each phase of hemiplegic migraine and may support the hypothesis that widespread cortical spreading depression precedes subcortical propagation.

## Introduction
Hemiplegic migraine (HM) is a rare subtype of migraine with aura, characterized by transient motor weakness or hemiparesis (motor aura), accompanied by unilateral throbbing headache, nausea, vomiting, or photophobia and phonophobia. The diagnosis is based on criteria established by The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-III) [ ]. HM can be either sporadic hemiplegic migraine (SHM) or familial hemiplegic migraine (FHM), with the latter typically exhibiting autosomal dominant inheritance. FHM is associated with genetic mutations in the CACNA1A, ATP1A2, and SCN1A genes, which encode proteins involved in ion transport [ ].
The estimated overall prevalence of HM is approximately 0.01%, with the condition most commonly occurring in females During adolescence, typically between 12 and 17 years of age [ ]. The pathophysiology of HM shares similarities with that of migraine with aura, particularly involving cortical spreading depression (CSD)—a wave of neuronal depolarization followed by a temporary suppression of brain activity. CSD can be measured through electrophysiological techniques such as electroencephalography (EEG) or neuroimaging modalities such as SPECT or MR perfusion [ – ]. However, the primary challenge in studying HM lies in its rarity, which limits the ability to conduct large-scale investigations. Understanding the electroencephalographic correlates in HM is essential for elucidating its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and improving diagnostic precision.
EEG has emerged as a valuable tool for investigating the dynamic electrical activity of the brain during migraine episodes, offering insights into both the temporal and spatial patterns of cortical function. While previous studies on migraine with aura have revealed abnormal cortical excitability and disrupted neurovascular interactions [ ], similar findings in HM remain underexplored. Applying EEG in patients with HM presents an opportunity to characterize distinct neurophysiological patterns associated with this rare condition, thereby advancing our understanding and potentially informing future diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Therefore, it is essential to synthesize the available evidence on EEG findings in HM to identify EEG pattern changes across different phases of the condition. In addition, it is important to determine the prevalence of abnormal EEG during the interictal phase through a systematic review and meta-analysis.

## Method

## Approval and registration of standard protocols
We conducted this study in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration Guidelines for systematic review of interventions and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines (PRISMA) 2020 [ ]. Our study protocol was registered in PROSPERO database (CRD42025548975). Deduplication of records, title and abstract screening, full-text review, and data extraction were conducted using the Covidence software.

## Data source and search strategy
We searched the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Clinical Trials (CENTRAL). The database search was conducted from inception to January 5, 2025. Additionally, we searched grey literature to capture relevant unpublished or non-peer-reviewed studies. Our search strategy included both free-text terms and controlled vocabulary (thesaurus) to encompass potential synonyms of key concepts. In accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement, we also employed additional search techniques beyond database searches. These included snowballing and citation tracking in Scopus. The complete search strategy is provided in Appendix 1.

## Selection criteria
We included articles (case reports, case series, and retrospective studies) that met the following criteria:
Population (P): Patients diagnosed with HM, including both familial and sporadic forms.
Exposure (E): EEG performed during or between episodes of HM.
Outcome (O): EEG patterns observed during or between episodes of HM attacks, such as diffuse slowing, focal changes, or epileptiform discharges.
Conference abstracts, review articles without primary data, or non-English article were excluded.
Two reviewers (PK and PA) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the search results to determine whether the studies met the inclusion criteria. Full-text articles that passed the title and abstract screening were subsequently assessed independently by the same two reviewers (PK and PA). Any disagreements or uncertainties were discussed and resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (TS) if a consensus could not be reached.

## Data extraction
Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers (PK and TS) to ensure accuracy, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (PA). The extracted information included: study details (authors, year of publication, country, and study design); patient demographics (age and sex); clinical features of HM (age at onset, frequency of migraine attacks, duration of HM episodes, family history, presence of underlying epilepsy, and identified gene mutations); and EEG findings (during aura, during headache, after recent headache, during the interictal period, duration of EEG monitoring, use of photic stimulation, and hyperventilation). EEG waveform characteristics included prevalence (persistence), location (distribution), morphology, amplitude, frequency, and symmetry/asymmetry.
When amplitude or other waveform parameters were not reported in the source text but EEG images with calibration were available, categorization was performed by board-certified epileptologists (ABCN or CSCN) (TS, CL). The evaluation was based on standardized references to ensure consistency and accuracy. Waveform characteristics—including prevalence and persistence, location and distribution, morphology, amplitude, frequency, and symmetry or asymmetry—were defined according to Fisch and Spehlmann’s EEG Primer [ ]. Taxonomic classification followed the guidelines outlined in Electroencephalography: Textbook and Atlas by Hans Lüders et al. [ ]. Additionally, rhythmic or periodic patterns were interpreted using the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society’s Standardized Critical Care EEG Terminology: 2021 Version [ ].

## Quality assessment
The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using appropriate critical appraisal tools. For case reports and case series, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist was used to evaluate factors such as clarity of diagnostic criteria and adequacy of patient follow-up [ ]. Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers (PA and TS), with any disagreements resolved through consensus. The instrument scores risk of bias from 0 to 100% based on nine domains, where higher scores indicate lower risk of bias. The risk of bias is then graded as high risk (≤ 49%), moderate risk (50%−69%), or low risk (≥ 70%) [ ].

## Data synthesis
A narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize the findings of the included studies. Key EEG findings were described, with an emphasis on identifying patterns such as diffuse slowing, focal abnormalities, and epileptiform activity in patients with HM. Additionally, we performed subgroup analyses of EEG results based on genetic mutations and separately analyzed patients without a history of epilepsy. The prevalence of EEG patterns was calculated through pooled data analysis. To pool the prevalence of abnormal EEG findings, in order to avoid inappropriate weight of the findings Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation were used. Fixed-effect model with inverse-variance method was employed. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed, and potential sources of variability—such as differences in diagnostic criteria or EEG recording protocols—were explored. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

## Results

## Search results
A total of 407 records were initially identified through the literature search (Fig.  ). After screening titles and abstracts, 56 papers were selected for full-text review. Of these, 16 studies were excluded for the following reasons: 7 were not in English, 1 included a patient population not of interest, 3 had an inappropriate study design, and 5 did not report relevant outcomes (Appendix 2) Consequently, 40 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis, comprising 34 case reports and 6 case series.

## Quality assessment
The risk of bias in each study was assessed individually (Appendix 3). All studies were determined to have a low risk of bias across all domains, as well as overall.

## Patient demographics, co-morbidities and clinical characteristic of hemiplegic migraine
Across these studies, 65 patients with HM were identified and analyzed (Table  ). Of these, 41 were female (63.1%). The median age of the patients was 19.5 years (interquartile range [IQR], 13.0–39.8). The median age of onset for the first HM attack was 12.0 years (IQR, 7.0–15.5). The Duration of attacks varied widely, ranging from 3 h to 40 days. A total of 44 patients (67.7%) had a family history of HM, and 11 patients (16.9%) had a history of epilepsy. 
A total of 43 patients (66.2%) had a positive genetic test. Among them, 22 had a genetic mutation only in the ATP1A2 gene, and 19 had a mutation only in the CACNA1A gene. One patient had mutations in both ATP1A2 and CACNA1A, and one patient had a mutation in the SCN1A gene.

 

## EEG pattern and prevalence of EEG pattern in each migraine phrase
Electroencephalography (EEG) was performed in 65 patients across the 40 included studies, with varying temporal relationships to migraine episodes. Importantly, 24 studies involving 38 patients reported EEG recordings across more than one migraine phase, allowing intra-individual and phase-specific comparisons of EEG patterns over the course of the migraine episode (Tables and , and Fig.  ). 
EEG recordings during the aura phase were reported in 12.5% (5/40) of the studies, documenting a total of 7 patients. The most common EEG finding was low-amplitude background activity in both awake and sleep states, affecting the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis in 71.4% (5/7) of cases. Other EEG abnormality observed was lateralized spike-and-wave (LSW) activity in the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis in 14.3% (1/7) of cases (Table ).
EEG recordings during the headache phase were reported in 75.9% (30/40) of the studies, documenting a total of 51 patients. EEG findings reported during the headache phase showed that the most common finding was medium- to high-amplitude slow waves in the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis (54.9%, 28/51). Other EEG abnormalities included diffuse slowing in 11.8% (6/51), focal slow activity in 7.8% (4/51), low-amplitude awake and sleep background activity over the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis in 7.8% (4/51), and sporadic focal sharp or spike waves in 3.9% (2/51), as summarized in Table . Meanwhile, normal EEG findings were reported in 5.9% (3/51) of cases.
During the post–recent headache phase, EEG recordings were reported in 47.5% (17/40) of the studies, including a total of 24 patients. The most common finding was medium- to high-amplitude slow waves in the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis, observed in 37.5% (9/24) of cases. Other reported EEG findings included low-amplitude activity, lateralized periodic discharges (LPDs), and temporal slow activity, each observed over the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis in 12.5% (3/24) of patients. Among those with LPDs, one patient exhibited triphasic morphology. Additionally, diffuse slow waves were present in 12.5% (3/24) of patients, with two of these showing superimposed hemispheric slowing. Normal EEG activity was noted in 25.0% (6/24) of patients (Table ). Among patients in the post–recent headache phase, follow-up EEGs were available for eight cases. One patient demonstrated normalization within one day, while the remaining seven underwent follow-up during the interictal period at varying intervals. Of these, five patients showed normalized EEGs at 5 days (n = 1), 1 month (n = 2), and 3 months (n = 2), indicating that although EEG abnormalities persisted in some cases, they tended to resolve gradually over time.
From the pooled analysis of the interictal phase (Fig.  ), a total of 33 patients from 22 studies underwent EEG assessment during this period. The meta-analysis yielded an overall pooled proportion of 22.0% (95% CI: 17.0–28.0%) for abnormal EEG findings during the interictal phase, with no statistical heterogeneity (I² = 0%), indicating highly consistent results across studies. This supports the observation that most interictal EEGs were normal (78.8%, 26/33). The most commonly observed EEG abnormality was hemispheric slowing contralateral to the hemiparesis, found in 71.4% (5/7) of patients with EEG abnormalities. Other EEG abnormalities included focal spikes or sharp waves over the bilateral centrotemporal regions and temporal slowing contralateral to the hemiparesis, each observed in 14.3% (1/7) of patients with EEG abnormalities (Table ). 
Apart from the above EEG findings, we also extracted data on hyperventilation and photic stimulation. One case demonstrated a photic driving response during the post-recent headache phase and another one case demonstrated photoparoxysmal response during headache phase. Hyperventilation was not mentioned in any of the included studies.

## Subgroup analysis of EEG patterns in each migraine phase among HM patients without a history of epilepsy
After excluding 11 patients with a history of epilepsy and 10 patients with unknown epilepsy history, we enrolled 44 patients for this subgroup analysis. Seven patients had EEG recordings during the aura phase. The most frequent EEG finding was asymmetric low-amplitude alpha activity contralateral to hemiparesis (5/7, 71.4%). EEG recordings during the headache phase were available for 35 patients, with hemispheric slow waves (delta/theta) contralateral to hemiparesis being the most common abnormality (51.4%, 18/35). Fourteen patients had EEGs during the post–recent headache phase, where the most common finding was a normal EEG (6/14, 42.9%). Finally, EEGs during the interictal period were available for 24 patients, with normal EEGs found in 75.0% (18/24). ## Subgroup analysis of EEG patterns in each migraine phase among HM patients by genetic mutation
A total of 43 patients with HM had confirmed genetic mutations. Among them, 22 had a mutation only in the ATP1A2 gene, 19 had a mutation only in the CACNA1A gene, one patient had mutations in both ATP1A2 and CACNA1A, and one patient had a mutation in the SCN1A gene.

## ATP1A2 mutation only
Twenty-two patients with confirmed exclusive ATP1A2 mutations were recruited. EEG during the aura phase was reported in 4 patients, all of whom (100%, 4/4) showed asymmetric, lower amplitude activity in the affected hemisphere. During the headache phase, EEG was reported in 18 patients. Hemispheric slow activity was the most frequent abnormality (77.8%, 14/19). In the post-headache phase, EEG was available for 6 patients. Hemispheric slow activity over the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis was reported in 33.3% (3/6), with one case showing concurrent diffuse slow activity, and asymmetric, lower-amplitude activity in the affected hemisphere was reported in 33.3% (3/6). In the interictal phase, EEG was available for 13 patients, with normal findings observed in 92.3% (12/13). A summary of EEG findings in HM patients with ATP1A2 mutations is presented in Table  .

 

## CACNA1A mutation only
Nineteen patients with confirmed mutations exclusively in CACNA1A were enrolled. EEG during the aura phase was reported in 1 patient, who showed asymmetric, lower amplitude activity in the affected hemisphere. During the headache phase, EEG was reported in 14 patients, with diffuse slow activity and hemispheric slow activity over the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis being the most common findings, observed in 28.6% (4/14) and 21.4% (3/14) of cases, respectively. In the post-headache phase, EEG was reported in 5 patients, with hemispheric slow activity present in 40.0% (2/5) of them. EEG During the interictal phase was reported in 9 patients, of whom 77.8% (7/9) had normal EEG findings. A summary of EEG findings in HM patients with CANA1A mutations is presented in Table  . 

## Discussion
In this review, we assessed the EEG findings in each phase of HM attack and found that low-amplitude background activity affecting the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis was the most common during the aura phase. In the headache and post-headache phases, the most frequently observed EEG abnormality was hemispheric slowing over the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis. Meanwhile, during the interictal phase, the majority of EEG recordings in HM patients were normal. Subgroup analysis according to genetic mutation and in patients without a history of epilepsy revealed similar EEG patterns, except that normal EEGs were more frequently observed during the post-headache phase in HM patients without epilepsy. Additionally, during the headache phase of HM patients with CACNA1A mutations, there was a lower prevalence of hemispheric slowing over the hemisphere contralateral to the hemiparesis, but a higher occurrence of diffuse slow activity was observed. Therefore, the EEG characteristics of HM patients appear consistent, which will be further discussed in relation to the underlying pathophysiology.
In scalp EEG, amplitude reflects the summed postsynaptic potentials of cortical neurons transmitted to the recording electrodes, modulated by the distance and conductivity of intervening tissues. Medium background amplitude is typically 20–50 μV, with values below this range defined as low amplitude and those above as high amplitude[ ]. Low-amplitude recordings are most often encountered when pathology involves only the cortex[ , ] or the anatomical space[ ] between the cortical surface and the recording electrodes, where electrical potentials are attenuated before reaching the electrodes. Examples[ ] include CSD in migraine and subdural hemorrhage. By contrast, the dominant background frequency is determined primarily by oscillatory activity generated in the thalamus[ , ] and, to a lesser degree, brainstem structures[ , ], which project to and are modulated by the cortex. In the awake state, the posterior head regions predominantly exhibit an alpha rhythm (8–13 Hz)[ ] with medium amplitude, which slows to theta or delta frequencies during drowsiness and deeper stages of sleep. Pathological slowing occurs when lesions or dysfunction disrupt the thalamocortical or brainstem–cortical pathways[ , ], such as in white matter disease, thalamic infarction, or upper brainstem lesions producing slowing on EEG over the hemisphere ipsilateral to the lesion. This slowing is often accompanied by increased amplitude, reflecting desynchronized cortical activity, unless there is concomitant cortical pathology, in which case the slow activity may be of low amplitude. Importantly, the presence of low amplitude or slow wave activity in a given hemisphere serves as a localizing rather than etiologic marker, indicating the site of dysfunction rather than its precise cause. Determining etiology requires temporal correlation[ ]: abnormalities caused by transient functional disturbances, such as migraine-related CSD, may resolve completely, whereas those due to structural lesions—such as infarcts or malformations—often persist.
Based on our results, EEG findings in HM appear to vary depending on the clinical phase. During the aura phase or when the hemiplegic attack is present, EEG commonly shows low-amplitude activity in the hemisphere contralateral to the weakness. In some cases, focal slow waves may also accompany the low-amplitude pattern in the same hemisphere. These changes are usually transient and resolve spontaneously. This phenomenon may reflect a transient cortical dysfunction and corresponds pathophysiologically to CSD, a known mechanism in migraine aura characterized by a propagating wave of altered activities in cortical neuron and glia cells [ – ].
Occasionally, LPDs are reported[ , ] and may indicate acute cortical involvement[ – ]. Other rare findings include generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave discharges[ , ], temporal rhythmic delta activity [ ], LSW[ ], lateralized rhythmic delta activity (LRDA)[ ], electrographic status epilepticus[ ], sporadic interictal epileptiform discharges such as sharp or spike-and-wave complexes[ , , , ], and photoparoxysmal response[ ]. All of these patterns suggest increased epileptogenicity[ – ], supporting a possible overlap between migraine and epilepsy[ – ]. This association aligns with the concept of migralepsy or migraine aura-triggered seizure, where a migraine attack triggers a seizure [ – ]. However, such patterns are rare in HM; in our review, only one case from Zangaladze et al. [ ] experienced a HM attack followed by a seizure, presenting as electrographic status epilepticus arising from the temporal region. This presentation may fall within the definition of migralepsy, highlighting its exceptionality. In our review, one patient from Nardello et al.[ ] demonstrated a generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave pattern during the headache phase; the source study did not report whether there was a comorbid diagnosis of epilepsy. In that patient, the discharges were electrographic only, without clinical absence events during EEG recording, and disappeared after a trial of valproic acid, which the patient had not previously received — a response that might support an epileptic mechanism rather than HM alone. The absence of comorbidity data limits the interpretation of the specificity of such EEG findings for HM.
During the headache phase, EEG may reveal medium to high-amplitude slow waves over the contralateral hemisphere, which is thought to represent involvement of deeper structures such as the thalamus, midbrain, or extensive subcortical white matter[ , ]. This supports the notion that migraine activity can propagate from the cortex (as in CSD) into subcortical structures, contributing to the sustained hemiparesis. Notably, the disappearance of previously seen low-amplitude cortical signals during this phase further supports the resolution of CSD.
Among the studied patients[ , ], three cases demonstrated persistent low-amplitude EEG activity during the post-headache phase. Notably, two patients from Iizuka et al. [ ] experienced prolonged aura lasting several days, which correlates well with the sustained suppression of cortical activity and provides further evidence supporting the role of CSD in the pathophysiology of prolonged aura[ ]. In contrast, the majority of patients exhibited medium to high-amplitude hemispheric slow waves, particularly contralateral to the side of hemiparesis. These EEG findings likely reflect residual subcortical dysfunction, possibly involving deep midline structures such as the thalamus or brainstem[ , ], and suggest resolution of CSD, as evidenced by the absence of low-amplitude activity during this phase.
Additionally, our study revealed EEG findings during the interictal period, showing that 33 patients from 22 studies underwent EEG During this phase, with 78.8% demonstrating normal results. Further supporting this observation, multimodal imaging studies have demonstrated that neuronal dysfunction during a HM attack is predominantly transient and reversible. Bosemani et al. reported that perfusion-weighted MRI (PWI) and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) during an HM attack showed significant hypoperfusion and prominent hypointense sulcal veins, which completely normalized within 24 hours, corresponding with full clinical recovery[ ]. In contrast, Toldo et al. documented a case of prolonged sporadic HM where brain MRI and 99mTc-ECD SPECT imaging revealed hypoperfusion and decreased neuronal metabolic markers, which gradually returned to normal at six months after onset[ ]. The discrepancy in the time course of neuronal recovery across these studies suggests that while neuronal dysfunction in HM is typically reversible, the duration required for full recovery can vary significantly. This variability in the timeline of cortical restoration may partly explain why normal interictal EEG is observed in the majority but not all HM patients during the interictal phase. It highlights that, although a normal EEG between attacks is highly supportive of HM, incomplete recovery at the time of EEG could account for occasional residual abnormalities. This finding may be an important clue in differentiating HM from other conditions that present with transient weakness, such as transient ischemic attack (TIA) or Todd’s paralysis following a seizure. One notable study investigated the diagnostic utility of EEG in patients presenting with transient hemiparesis, a clinical syndrome most attributed to TIA or Todd’s paralysis. In the study by Bentes et al.[ ], patients with transient hemiparesis that resolved completely within 24 hours underwent a full diagnostic work-up followed by EEG assessments both in the early phase and at one month after symptom onset (late EEG). In the late EEG, focal slow wave activity (FSWA) was observed in 91.7% of patients with seizures and in 45.5% of patients with TIA. While normal EEGs were thus estimated in approximately 8.0% of seizure patients and 54.0% of TIA patients by subtracting FSWA, it is important to note that additional interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) were also reported in the seizure group, suggesting that the true percentage of normal EEGs in this group may be even lower. By comparison, our pooled analysis of interictal EEG findings in patients with HM demonstrated normal EEGs in 78.8% of cases during the interictal phase. These findings highlight that, in cases of transient hemiparesis with unclear etiology, a normal interictal EEG may support a diagnosis of HM over TIA or Todd’s paralysis from seizure.
In fact, the location of slowing observed in most cases was hemispheric, reflecting a broad spread of CSD across the cortex. Only one patient exhibited slowing localized to the central region during the headache phase, suggesting that although focal motor area dysfunction may underlie hemiplegia in HM, the overall pattern of hemispheric involvement is similar to that seen in other types of migraine. A remarkable case report by Chastan et al.[ ] documented continuous video-EEG throughout a full HM attack. The EEG showed a temporospatial progression of slow wave activity, beginning in posterior regions after the onset of visual aura, spreading anteriorly in correlation with evolving clinical symptoms such as hemiplegia and pain. This supports the notion that CSD propagates along the symptomatogenic cortex. However, most EEG recordings in the literature were performed during the headache or after the end of the aura, which explains why the majority of reported EEG abnormalities appear hemispheric rather than focal, as the CSD would have already propagated to various cortical regions causing widespread dysfunction by the time of recording.
In comparing subgroups with and without epilepsy, our data suggest that CSD–related changes in the aura and headache phases—most notably hemispheric slowing contralateral to hemiparesis—occur with similar frequency in both groups, supporting the notion that the underlying pathophysiology during these early phases is comparable regardless of epilepsy comorbidity. However, notable differences emerged in the post–recent headache phase. All HM patients, including those with a history of epilepsy, showed a lower proportion of normalized EEGs (25.0%) compared to those without a history of epilepsy (42.9%). By the interictal phase, the proportion of normal EEGs converged between groups, indicating eventual recovery of brain activity after HM attack in both cohorts. This pattern suggests that patients with comorbid epilepsy may experience a slower resolution of HM-related cortical and subcortical dysfunction, plausibly due to pre-existing network instability or reduced functional reserve. In contrast, patients without epilepsy may demonstrate a more rapid return to baseline activity following an HM attack. As expected, interictal epileptiform discharges were more frequent in the epilepsy group, a finding consistent with their underlying diagnosis and not specific to HM pathophysiology.
In contrast to these differences in recovery speed, analysis of genetic subgroups revealed distinctions in the spatial distribution of dysfunction, particularly during the headache phase. At first glance, hemispheric slowing contralateral to hemiparesis was markedly more prevalent in ATP1A2 (78.9%) compared to CACNA1A (21.4%), consistent with a predominantly unilateral cortical–subcortical disturbance in ATP1A2-related HM. However, a more nuanced review showed that CACNA1A uniquely exhibited diffuse slow activity in 28.6% of cases—a pattern entirely absent in ATP1A2. The occurrence of diffuse slowing suggests bilateral involvement of neural structures beyond the initial cortical site of CSD onset. In migraine pathophysiology, CSD is expected to propagate along ipsilateral cortical–subcortical tracts, potentially involving the same-side white matter and thalamic nuclei. Cross-hemispheric spread via the corpus callosum is a possible mechanism[ ]. A more plausible mechanism for diffuse slowing in CACNA1A is bilateral upper brainstem involvement, given that this region provides the shortest and most efficient interhemispheric connections. Experimental data in animal models[ ] indicate that unilateral upper brainstem involvement may not produce diffuse slowing, whereas bilateral involvement reliably induces it. This raises the possibility that CACNA1A-related HM with diffuse slow activity represents a phenotype with more extensive activation of bilateral pain-processing networks, potentially translating to broader or more severe headache symptoms. Unfortunately, headache severity and distribution were not systematically documented in the present cohort, limiting clinical correlation. Another consideration is the timing of EEG recording within the headache phase. Diffuse slow activity in CACNA1A may represent a later stage in the attack, evolving from an initial hemispheric slowing pattern as CSD-related dysfunction descends to bilateral upper brainstem structures. Such temporal dynamics could not be fully evaluated in this study due to single-time-point EEG recordings. Future longitudinal EEG monitoring within individual attacks could clarify whether diffuse slowing reflects a distinct anatomical signature or a temporal progression of HM-related brain dysfunction.

## Limitations and future directions
Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although the meta-analysis component focused on pooled interictal EEG data, the majority of the included studies consisted of case reports and small case series, which may introduce reporting bias and limit generalizability. Second, considerable heterogeneity was noted in EEG recording techniques and the timing of EEG recordings during each phase of the HM attack. The timing of EEG recordings in the headache phase varied considerably among studies, reducing consistency in data interpretation. Most studies did not report key parameters such as montage type (e.g., bipolar vs. referential), which can affect the amplitude and localization of observed abnormalities. Activation procedures like photic stimulation and hyperventilation were rarely performed, limiting the ability to evaluate activation-related EEG responses. Furthermore, although sampling rates were infrequently reported, their influence may be minimal given the low-frequency focus of most findings. Overall, the lack of standardization in EEG acquisition methods across studies may have introduced variability in the reported findings. Additionally, for studies describing “low amplitude” EEG, none specified the quantitative voltage threshold or method used to define this term. This absence of standardized criteria may affect both the interpretability and reproducibility of such findings. Moreover, in each phase—especially in the post-headache and interictal phases—the exact timing of EEG acquisition following symptom resolution was often not clearly reported, which may affect conclusions regarding the temporal evolution of EEG changes. Third, the diagnostic criteria for HM used in the included studies evolved over time, from ICHD-1 to ICHD-3 [ , , ]. Moreover, genetic testing was not widely available in the past, which may have contributed to bias in the diagnosis of HM. Finally, in some cases—such as those with generalized 3 Hz spike-and-wave discharges—the source studies did not report whether the patients had a comorbid diagnosis of epilepsy. This absence of information limits the interpretation of the specificity of such EEG patterns for HM, particularly when the patterns responded to antiseizure medications such as valproic acid.
Future prospective studies using standardized EEG acquisition protocols across clearly defined clinical phases are warranted. Furthermore, given recent insights into the role of slow-frequency activity in migraine—particularly in relation to CSD—future research should explore advanced EEG techniques, such as the use of wide-band EEG with long time constant settings, to detect direct current (DC) shifts and subdelta components. These slow potentials, which may reflect astrocyte-mediated pathophysiological mechanisms, could serve as novel biomarkers for capturing CSD-related activity during migraine phases, especially when recorded during the aura or symptomatic periods. This approach may enhance our mechanistic understanding and improve diagnostic precision in migraine subtypes, including HM. In parallel, the integration of EEG with functional MRI (EEG-fMRI) represents a promising avenue for future investigation. This multimodal approach could elucidate spatial-temporal correlations between electrophysiological disturbances and hemodynamic changes during various migraine phases, providing insights into network-level dysfunctions and subcortical involvement that are not easily captured by EEG alone. Moreover, wearable EEG systems may offer the possibility of continuous, real-world monitoring, allowing for early detection of subtle EEG alterations preceding neurological deficits such as hemiparesis. This could open new avenues for preventive interventions and improve patient safety, especially in familial or recurrent cases. Lastly, machine learning-based spectral analysis has the potential to detect complex and subtle patterns across frequency bands, enabling more refined differentiation of migraine subtypes. Such data-driven approaches may also help characterize the electrophysiological signature of HM at the level of both neuronal and glial dynamics, advancing our understanding of its underlying pathophysiology. Together, these emerging tools may significantly augment the diagnostic and prognostic capabilities of EEG in HM and related disorders.

## Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis highlight the dynamic evolution of EEG findings across different clinical phases of HM. Low-amplitude activity during the aura phase, progression to medium to high-amplitude focal slow waves during the headache and post-headache phases, and predominantly normal EEG patterns during the interictal phase reflect the underlying pathophysiological propagation of migraine activity from the cortex to subcortical structures and brainstem. These EEG patterns are consistent with the established pathophysiology of migraine, where widespread cortical involvement precedes subcortical propagation, rather than being restricted to paracentral regions adjacent to the motor cortex. This suggests that hemiparesis in HM may not arise from focal cortical dysfunction alone, but from a more extensive pattern of cortical spreading, similar to that seen in other types of migraine, followed by involvement of deeper subcortical and brainstem structures during the headache phase.
