## Background:
Systematic reviews on low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) in nonunion fractures report a positive effect on healing. The quality of evidence is, however, limited as it is mostly based on observational studies. The literature on LIPUS in nonunion fractures has grown considerably over the past years. We aimed to conduct an up-to-date systematic review and meta-analysis of the evidence on the effect of LIPUS on healing rate and time in established nonunion fractures.

## Methods:
A literature search was conducted in Medline, CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science, from their inception to May 6, 2025, using keywords and MeSH terms of LIPUS and nonunions. Case series, cohort studies, or randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the effect of LIPUS on healing rate or healing time of nonunion fractures were included. Studies in a language other than English or French, on animal populations, or fresh fractures were excluded. Two independent reviewers performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment with the risk of bias tool for randomized controlled trial or the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tools. Distinct meta-analyses were performed for observational studies and RCTs to estimate the pooled effects on healing rate and time. The certainty of evidence was assessed with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation.

## Results:
Three RCTs (n = 264 participants) and 30 observational studies (n = 3437 participants) presenting low to serious risk of bias were included. The pooled healing rate following LIPUS in observational studies (n = 3439 nonunions) was 0.79 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.73–0.85; P < .01) with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 92%) and a very low certainty of evidence. The meta-analysis of RCTs on healing rate (3 studies, n = 264 nonunions) reached a nonsignificant risk ratio of 1.13 (95% CI: 0.93–1.37, P = .23) in favor of LIPUS compared to sham, with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 60%) and low certainty. The meta-analysis of RCTs on healing time (2 studies, n = 135 nonunions) reached a nonsignificant mean difference of 14.99 days (95% CI: −120.37 to 150.35; P = .83) between LIPUS and sham with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 74%) and low certainty of evidence.

## Conclusion:
Current evidence does not support the use of LIPUS as a first-line intervention in established nonunion fracture management, although uncertainty remains. High-quality placebo-controlled RCTs are warranted.

## 1. Introduction
Bone fractures present a major burden globally.[ , ] They are usually expected to heal within 3 to 4 months,[ ] but 5 to 10 percent progress to delayed healing or nonunion.[ , ] Personal factors such as age, sex, smoking status, the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or the presence of comorbidities like diabetes or obesity may affect fracture healing.[ , ] Factors related to the injury and its management, such as the location of the fracture, its severity, and its stability,[ ] or the type of surgical or conservative management chosen may also affect healing.[ , ] In addition to extended disability, nonunion represents important incremental costs for health care payers that may exceed $1,00,000 per patient in the United States.[ ] Most experts agree that a delayed union occurs 3 months after the initial fracture and established nonunion between 3 and 6 months.[ ] An established nonunion is not expected to heal without an intervention: management usually includes surgery with debridement and application of external or internal fixation, with a success rate varying from 68% to 96%.[ ] As surgeries are highly invasive, costly, and involve risks such as infection,[ ] other noninvasive interventions could be better suited for nonunion fractures.
Treatment options for acute fractures include cast immobilization, surgical intervention, and non-weight bearing.[ ] After these interventions, physiotherapy is needed to restore the range of motion, muscle strength, and function,[ ] with modalities such as home exercise programs, active and passive mobilizations, and adjuvant treatment for bone healing (e.g., ultrasound).[ ] Rehabilitation guidance for nonunion fractures is less commonly provided. A recent review of the evidence on the management of malleolar nonunion fractures, however, suggested that rehabilitation should occur after surgical treatment and immobilization for pain management, swelling control, and range of motion exercises.[ ] Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS), a modality that has been used as an alternative approach to surgery for nonunion fractures, may also be included in the rehabilitation plan.[ , - ] Indeed, LIPUS is thought to stimulate bone healing through nonthermal mechanisms, that is, acoustic streaming and cavitation[ ] and mechanical stimulation, that is, the piezoelectric effect.[ ] A meta-analysis conducted in 2017 reported that LIPUS could induce successful healing in 82% of people with nonunion fractures.[ ] A 2021 meta-analysis of LIPUS in instrumented, infected, and fragility nonunions reported similar healing rates ranging from 82% to 91%.[ ] These studies estimated the healing rate induced by LIPUS treatment in nonunion fractures by pooling the results from observational studies and from the experimental LIPUS group of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), providing low quality of evidence. This limited the ability to draw firm conclusions on the effect of LIPUS.[ , ]
A 2017 clinical practice guideline strongly advised against the use of LIPUS for patients with nonunions.[ ] However, this recommendation was mostly based on high-quality evidence coming from RCTs reporting that LIPUS was not more effective than placebo or no treatment in improving patient outcomes such as function and pain in fresh fractures.[ ] They also supported their opposition considering the costly[ ] nature of LIPUS. In contrast, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (United Kingdom) recommended the use of LIPUS in the treatment of long bone nonunion fractures in a 2013 guidance document.[ ] The authors judged that the efficacy of LIPUS was supported by the evidence while recognizing their limited certainty. They also justified this recommendation as adverse events related to LIPUS use are minor, whereas those reported in surgical treatments of nonunions include serious harms such as postoperative wound infection and osteomyelitis.[ ] Other guidance produced by National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in 2018 on the use of LIPUS in nonunion and delayed union fractures stated that the evidence regarding its efficacy lacked quality and encouraged further research.[ ] In the last 8 years, the literature on the effectiveness of LIPUS in nonunion fractures continued to grow considerably.[ , - ] Notably, a recent RCT by White et al showed that LIPUS was not effective compared to a sham LIPUS in the treatment of nonunions[ ] which contrasts with the interpretation of prior meta-analyses of observational studies suggesting high healing rates.[ , ] Importantly, the last systematic reviews could not perform meta-analyses of RCTs due to their scarcity, thus limiting the interpretability of the results.
An up-to-date systematic review of the literature with meta-analysis is needed to clarify and strengthen recommendations and certainty of evidence on the LIPUS effect in nonunion fractures. The aim of this study was to systematically review the available literature and perform meta-analyses to determine the effectiveness of LIPUS in enhancing the healing rate of nonunion fractures and improving clinical outcomes (e.g., pain and function).

## 2. Materials and methods
This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement (PRISMA).[ ] It was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024550084). We added 1 exclusion criterion after registration (stress fractures) as these fractures have potential for healing; that is, they usually resolve within 6 to 12 weeks with conservative management.[ ] We, however, included nonunion stress fractures. We also broadened the inclusion criteria for peripheral bones. We initially wanted to solely include bones of the foot, ankle, hand, and wrist, but we realized there was a lot of literature regarding long bones, especially the tibia, and we did not want to exclude this evidence from our review. This modification increases the generalizability of the results to all peripheral bones, but we acknowledge that it increases the heterogeneity of included studies. Given the nature of the study design, no informed consent or ethical approval was required.

## 2.1. Search strategy
The search was conducted in Medline, CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science. Articles were included since the databases’ inception to May 6, 2025. The search strategy targeted 2 main concepts: LIPUS and nonunion fracture, using keywords and MeSH terms ( , Supplemental Digital Content 1). Additionally, a manual screening of previous systematic reviews[ , , , - was conducted to identify any other eligible studies.

## 2.2. Selection criteria
Studies were included if they assessed the effect of LIPUS alone or in combination with another treatment for any peripheral bones’ delayed union or nonunion, had at least one of our primary outcomes (healing rate or time to healing), were case series, cohort studies, or RCTs, and were written in English or French. The definition proposed by Bhandari et al for nonunion or delayed union fractures was used for inclusion criteria, which is no bone consolidation at least 3 months after the initial injury.[ ] Studies had to have an ethics statement (approval or exemption by an ethics committee) to be included. If no ethics statement was written, the study had to be published in a journal requiring ethics committee approval or official exemption for publication. Studies were excluded if they concerned spine or skull bone fractures, were single case studies, textbooks, expert comments and opinions, or conference abstracts, were conducted in individuals with fresh fractures or nonunion due to osteotomy, or in animal populations. Studies including mixed populations (i.e., nonunion following fracture and nonunion due to osteotomy) were included if most participants corresponded to the inclusion criteria.

## 2.3. Screening and data extraction
References of potential studies were exported from databases and imported to the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Title and abstract screening, as well as full-text screening, were performed on Covidence by 2 independent reviewers. Any disagreements were discussed and resolved by a third reviewer when no consensus was reached. Two independent reviewers extracted data from all included studies using a predefined extraction grid including primary outcomes (healing rate and healing time), secondary outcomes (measures of pain intensity, function, or disability), participants’ and fracture characteristics, intervention provided and data on harms or adverse effects caused by LIPUS. Any disagreements in data extraction sheets were discussed with a third reviewer if no consensus was reached.

## 2.4. Risk of bias and quality of evidence
Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias for each study and the certainty of the evidence of the meta-analyses, and disagreements were discussed with a third reviewer if no consensus was reached. The risk of bias was assessed using either the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool[ ] or the Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized controlled trial.[ ] Certainty of the evidence was assessed with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.[ ] It recommends that evidence provided by the pooling of RCTs begin with a high-quality level and that those of observational studies begin with low quality.[ ] However, in cases where the risks of bias of observational studies are assessed with the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool, they also start with a high-quality level.[ ] The quality of evidence was downgraded by one level when serious problems arose from each of the 5 domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias ( , Supplemental Digital Content 10).[ - ] We also performed a visual assessment of funnel plots of meta-analyses that included more than 10 studies, as recommended.[ ]

## 2.5. Data synthesis and analysis
We used a PRISMA flow diagram to illustrate the number of included studies in each database, the number of removed references, and the reasons for exclusion.[ ] Descriptive synthesis of the results was performed for all included studies regarding demographic, baseline, and clinical characteristics and presented as tables. Primary outcomes were healing rate and time to healing. Healing rate is the proportion of the number of participants who completely healed divided by the total number of participants treated with LIPUS. Healing time is defined as the time interval between the start of LIPUS treatment and healing (i.e., presence of clinical and radiologic signs[ , ]), reported in days. Meta-analyses were performed in 3 steps: overarching analysis of LIPUS regardless of co-interventions, LIPUS alone, and LIPUS combined with surgery. When surgery took place at least 3 months before the start of LIPUS, the surgical intervention was considered as failed, and LIPUS was considered as used alone. This definition is widely used in the nonunion and LIPUS literature.[ , , , ] Exploratory meta-analyses were also performed for each bone separately. Meta-analyses were computed if ≥2 studies were available for a given outcome. Also, only the studies that had a sample size of 5 nonunions or more were included in the meta-analyses, as smaller studies present a risk of overestimating intervention effects.[ ]

## 2.5.1. Healing rate
Meta-analyses of observational studies were performed in Stata/BE 18.0 with the meta macro. This function uses a random effects model with the restricted maximum likelihood method and estimates the effect size of LIPUS across studies with the Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine transformation. This method stabilizes the variance, which is preferable when proportions are closer to 0 or 1, as was the case in many included studies.[ ] The estimate of the healing rate and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. The α threshold was set at 0.05. If the P-value was below .05, the healing rate was considered different from 0%. Forest plots were used to present the results of the meta-analyses. Meta-analyses of RCTs were performed using the Mantel–Haenszel method in Review Manager 5.4.1. Risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals were obtained using a random effects model.[ ] An overarching analysis of the healing rate measured at the last follow-up of each study was performed with the 3 included RCTs. Heterogeneity was calculated using I2, interpreted as not important (I2 < 40%), moderate (30% < I2 < 60%), substantial (50% < I2 < 90%), or considerable (I2 > 75%).[ ]

## 2.5.2. Healing time
A weighted mean of healing time was measured by combining all observational studies. A meta-analysis pooling healing times of RCTs was also performed using the inverse variance method in Review Manager 5.4.1. A mean difference and 95% confidence intervals were obtained using a random effects model.[ ]

## 2.5.3. Sensitivity analysis
Sensitivity analyses were performed using observational studies to explore the impact of different factors, including smoking status, age, sex, fracture age, and time interval from the last surgery to LIPUS on healing rate, as these factors were expected to influence healing,[ , , , ] using meta-regressions in Stata/BE 18.0 with the meta regress macro.

## 3. Results
From 4 databases, 2546 articles were identified. A manual search of systematic reviews’ reference list added 117 articles, but none remained after duplicate removal. After removing duplicates, 1411 were screened for eligibility by title and abstract. Sixty-three articles were screened for full text, and 30 observational studies (3437 participants) and 3 RCTs (264 participants) were included (PRISMA flow diagram).[ , , - , - , - 

## 3.1. Study characteristics
Among the included studies, the definition of nonunion varied as the absence of healing for 3 to more than 12 months after initial injury or after the last surgery.[ , ] The mean fracture age varied from 3.3[ ] to 44.5 months,[ ] and fractured bones included the hand, foot, and long bones of the lower and upper extremities (i.e., tibia, radius). LIPUS daily dosage was consistent across studies (i.e., 20 minutes), while the application duration varied (i.e., between 2 months to complete healing). The follow-up period varied between 3 months and 4.6 years (i.e., until healing). However, almost half of the included studies did not report follow-up time. The definition of fracture healings across studies was mostly determined by radiological criteria (e.g., bridging of trabeculae of at least 75%). ## 3.2. Harms related to LIPUS
Only 11 out of 30 included observational studies presented results on adverse events. Out of those, 7 reported no adverse events.[ , , , , , , ] One study reported swelling and pain around the LIPUS treatment site in 1 patient that resolved after treatment was stopped for 2 weeks.[ ] Another study reported the development of an abscess in the fourth week of LIPUS that needed to be treated surgically in 1 patient with a history of osteitis.[ ] It was, however, impossible to confirm if this event was due to LIPUS or if it was a coincidence, as there was no control group without LIPUS treatment in this study. One study reported the development of exuberant heterotopic bone at the site of treatment that was likely the result of the surgery, according to the authors, as the patient had open reduction and internal fixation and autogenous bone grafting.[ ] Haller et al reported an initial increase in pain during the first week of LIPUS treatment that quickly settled in 3 patients.[ ] Two out of 3 included RCTs[ , ] presented results on adverse events, but none of them were due to LIPUS.

## 3.3. Risk of bias assessment of included studies
Fifteen (50%) observational studies were at moderate risk of bias, and fifteen (50%) were at serious risk (Table ). Two RCTs (66%) presented some concerns,[ , ] and 1 (33%) presented low risk of bias.[ ] The lack of control for confounding factors, as well as the lack of details on participant selection and provided intervention, were common flaws ( , Supplemental Digital Content 11 and , Supplemental Digital Content 2).

## 3.4. Systematic review

## 3.4.1. Effect of LIPUS on pain and function
Four observational studies[ , , , ] with serious or moderate risks of bias, and 1 RCT[ ] with some concerns reported measures of pain and/or function. A significant reduction in pain intensity and disability (P < .001) from baseline to post-LIPUS was observed in Majeed et al.[ ] Bawale et al and Bhan et al reported measures of pain intensity pre- and post- LIPUS but did not perform any statistical analysis on this outcome.[ , ] Biglari et al reported that 60.7% of participants found no change in their pain, and 21.3% observed a minimal improvement.[ ]
For RCTs, only White et al reported pain and disability and found no statistically significant difference in the improvement of these outcomes between LIPUS and sham LIPUS groups.[ ]

## 3.5. Meta-analyses
It is important to consider that meta-analyses of observational studies present within-group averages (pre-post) for both time to healing and healing rate due to the lack of a control group. Meta-analyses of RCTs present between-group differences (risk ratio [healing rate] or mean differences [time to healing]).

## 3.5.1. Effect of LIPUS (regardless of any co-intervention) on healing rate – observational studies
All 30 observational studies were included in the meta-analysis (n = 3439 nonunions), regardless of the fracture’s location and the presence of any co-intervention (i.e., surgery). The pooled estimate of healing rate following LIPUS was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.85; P < .001; Fig. , , Supplemental Digital Content 12), with considerable heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 92%). The funnel plot is asymmetrical, suggesting a risk of publication bias ( , Supplemental Digital Content 3).[ ] The certainty of evidence is very low (Table ).
 When pooling healing rates according to the fracture’s location, the lowest healing rate was obtained for fractures located at the humerus, (0.75, 95% CI: 0.68–0.81; P < .0001, n = 224 nonunions, 7 studies[ , , , , , , ]), without heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). The highest healing rate was obtained for fractures located at the foot (0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.97; P < .0001, n = 508 nonunions, 8 studies[ , , , , , , , ]), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 54%). Results of the meta-analysis of each bone are presented in , Supplemental Digital Content 12 and , Supplemental Digital Content 4. Some of these meta-analyses included very few studies and nonunions (e.g., clavicle: 2 studies; 56 nonunions) and should be interpreted with caution.

## 3.5.2. Effect of LIPUS alone on healing rate – observational studies
Twenty-six observational studies[ , , - , , , - , , , - , - , , ] were included in the meta-analysis assessing the effect of LIPUS alone (i.e., without any co-intervention). The estimate of the healing rate was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.72–0.85; P < .0001, n = 1314 nonunions, 26 studies), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86%; Fig. ). The certainty of evidence is very low (Table ). Radius-ulna and humerus reached the lowest healing rate estimates, and the foot still reached the highest. Detailed results of these meta-analyses are presented in , Supplemental Digital Content 13. An asymmetrical distribution of the effect sizes is present in funnel plots ( , Supplemental Digital Content 5).
 

## 3.5.3. Effect of LIPUS + surgery on healing rate – observational studies
Only 2 studies (n = 40 nonunions) were included in the LIPUS + surgery meta-analysis.[ , ] The estimated healing rate was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.32–1.00; P < .001), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86%; Fig. ). The certainty of evidence is very low (Table ).
 

## 3.5.4. Effect of LIPUS on healing rate – RCTs
The meta-analysis of RCTs (3 studies, n = 264 nonunions) that compared the effect of LIPUS to sham LIPUS on the healing rate reached a nonsignificant risk ratio (RR) of 1.13 (95% CI: 0.93–1.37, P = .23 – Fig. ) in favor of LIPUS.[ , , ] The heterogeneity across studies was substantial, with an I2 of 60%. The certainty of evidence is low (Table ). The funnel plot is presented in , Supplemental Digital Content 6. The meta-analysis comparing the effect of LIPUS to sham LIPUS on the healing rate of the scaphoid included 2 RCTs[ , ] (n = 163) and reached a nonsignificant RR of 1.07 (95% CI: 0.92–1.24, P = .37) in favor of LIPUS and non-important heterogeneity between studies with an I2 of 32% ( , Supplemental Digital Content 7).
 

## 3.5.5. Effect of LIPUS on healing time – observational studies
Nineteen observational studies were included in this meta-analysis.[ , , - , - , - , , , , , ] The overall time to healing (all bones; n = 2313 nonunions) was 146 days ( , Supplemental Digital Content 14 and , Supplemental Digital Content 8). The certainty of evidence is low (Table ). The longest healing time was reached for the tibia-fibula, with 151 days (8 studies,[ , , , , , , , ] n = 593 nonunions), and the shortest was reached for the hand, with 96 days (4 studies,[ , , , ] n = 141 nonunions).
The mean healing time when treating with LIPUS alone was 155 days ( , Supplemental Digital Content 15; 16 studies,[ , , - , , , - , , , , ] n = 463 nonunions). The certainty of evidence is low (Table ). No meta-analysis could be performed on healing time for LIPUS combined with surgery, as only one of the two studies using LIPUS and surgery reported healing time.[ ]

## 3.5.6. Effect of LIPUS on healing time – RCTs
The meta-analysis included 2 RCTs (n = 135 nonunions) and compared the effect of LIPUS to sham LIPUS on healing time. It reached a nonsignificant mean difference of 14.99 days (95% CI: −120.37–150.35, P = .83; , Supplemental Digital Content 9) in favor of sham.[ , ] The heterogeneity was substantial, with an I2 of 74%. The certainty of evidence is low (Table ).

## 3.5.7. Sensitivity analysis
Exploratory sensitivity analyses were solely performed with observational studies, as meta-regressions are recommended to be used only when more than ten studies are included in the meta-analysis.[ ] Indeed, only 2 or 3 studies were included in the meta-analyses of RCTs. Meta-regressions were performed on the healing rate of LIPUS, regardless of co-interventions. The only covariable that had an influence on healing rate was the female sex (regression coefficient 0.69, 95% CI: 0.01–1.38, P = .05, I2 = 85.7, Table ), suggesting females had higher healing rates.
 

## 4. Discussion
This was an updated systematic review of the literature with meta-analyses on the effect of LIPUS in the treatment of established nonunions. In this study, the meta-analysis of observational studies estimated a 79% healing rate in participants with established nonunions treated with LIPUS, with a very low certainty of evidence, similar to a 2017 meta-analysis of observational studies that estimated an 82% healing rate.[ , ] However, our meta-analysis of RCTs showed that LIPUS was not more effective than sham LIPUS in inducing the healing rate, with a low certainty of evidence. The striking differences between observational studies and RCTs’ meta-analyses suggest the impact of nonspecific effects on nonunion healing (rate and time) in meta-analyses of observational studies. Factors such as misunderstanding of healing in nonunion (e.g., the effect of time), definition of healing, and maximal treatment duration are not controlled in observational studies and might contribute to the overestimation of the LIPUS effect reported in observational studies. The potential placebo effect could also be further studied. The results of this study showed, with a low certainty of evidence, that LIPUS may induce healing in an average of 146 days regardless of co-interventions, and in an average of 155 days when used alone. Again, it is impossible to confirm that LIPUS accelerated healing compared to the natural evolution with this meta-analysis due to the absence of a control group. In fact, in the meta-analysis of RCTs that assessed the time to healing induced by LIPUS compared with sham, no significant difference was obtained, with a low certainty of evidence. This contrasts with a 2016 meta-analysis of RCTs by Rutten et al, which showed that LIPUS decreased healing time by 37 days compared to a control group.[ ] This difference could be due to the very low number of included studies (n = 2) in both our meta-analysis and theirs, and the difference in the number of participants included (n = 29 in Rutten et al[ ] and n = 135 in ours). Also, our inclusion criteria differed from theirs, as we excluded nonunions due to surgery (e.g., osteotomy), whereas they included them.
Due to the paucity of data regarding the effect of LIPUS on pain and function, a meta-analysis could not be conducted for these outcomes. Four observational studies reported measures of pain and/or function pre- and post-LIPUS and reported some improvement.[ , , , ] Only 1 RCT reported measures of pain and function and showed no difference between LIPUS and sham.[ ] These conflicting results impede the ability to draw conclusions on the effect of LIPUS on these outcomes. Only 11 out of 30 included observational studies presented results on adverse events: 2 reported severe harms (development of an abscess in a patient with a history of osteitis and development of exuberant heterotopic bone),[ , ] and 2 reported mild adverse events (pain and swelling that resolved rapidly).[ , ] However, it was not possible to confirm if these adverse events were caused by LIPUS or other treatment (e.g., surgery), as no placebo group was included in those studies. None of the RCTs reported adverse events.[ , ] Likewise, in their systematic review of RCTs, Rutten et al reported no adverse events due to LIPUS across 24 included studies.[ ] Their population, however, included many types of fractures or osteotomies, and only 5 included studies were about delayed unions or nonunions. Although LIPUS seems to be a safe modality for the treatment of nonunions, the lack of standardized assessment of adverse events in both LIPUS and placebo groups might underestimate LIPUS-induced adverse effects. Thus, clinicians should be aware that LIPUS might induce, in rare cases, severe harms. This should be considered in the decision to use LIPUS for the treatment of established nonunions.

## 4.1. Limitations of included studies
Half of the included observational studies presented high risks of bias, and half presented moderate risks. Two included RCTs presented some concerns regarding risks of bias, and 1 presented a low risk of bias. The identified risks of bias were mostly due to the lack of control for confounding factors and the lack of clarity on participant selection and interventions’ characteristics, such as treatment adherence. Indeed, many observational studies did not provide a measure of adherence to LIPUS, and this could highly influence its effect. The risk of bias in the included studies is reflected in the very low and low certainty of evidence.
An important limitation of the included studies is in the methodological choices related to the duration of LIPUS application and the lack of control groups. Some studies predetermined the duration of the intervention (e.g., 6 months), whereas others used it until healing. A predetermined LIPUS duration might have underestimated the healing rate, as it is unclear what healing time should be expected in the case of a successful LIPUS intervention. On the other hand, when LIPUS was used until healing, an overestimation of the effect might have occurred, as it is impossible to determine if healing was due to LIPUS or time in the absence of a proper control group.

## 4.2. Strengths and limitations of this review
A strength of this updated systematic review and meta-analysis on the effect of LIPUS in the treatment of nonunions is that we used the GRADE approach to assess the quality of the evidence. Also, we pooled data from observational studies as well as from RCTs, including the most recent by White et al.[ ] This provided an increase in the certainty of evidence compared to those obtained with meta-analyses of observational studies that are at higher risk of bias (low instead of very low). The certainty of the evidence remains, however, limited, mainly because of the heterogeneity between included studies and the low sample size.
A limitation of this study is the fact that we only included studies in French or English, which might increase the risk of publication bias. However, as we conducted the literature search in 4 distinct databases, we judge our search strategy to be sufficient to ensure external validity. The funnel plots of the meta-analyses, including observational studies on the effect of LIPUS alone or regardless of co-interventions, were considered asymmetrical upon visual inspection. This could reflect the presence of publication bias,[ ] but it was accounted for when assessing the certainty of evidence with the GRADE approach. Another limitation is that the effect of LIPUS alone was defined as LIPUS used at least 3 months after the last surgery. This 3-month period reduces the potential confounding effect of the surgery on healing. However, it is possible that the presence of surgery has biased the results, as it is impossible to confirm that surgery had no residual influence on healing at this stage. The heterogeneity measures obtained in the meta-analyses were, for the most part, substantial. This could be explained by clinical and methodological variability across studies,[ ] which might impact LIPUS treatment’s effectiveness. However, the meta-regression sensitivity analysis showed that most clinical variability does not impact healing rates, and only the female sex was associated with greater healing rates, although this was an exploratory analysis and should be interpreted with caution. This contrasts with a previous meta-analysis by Leighton et al that revealed no impact of patient sex on outcomes in patients treated with LIPUS for nonunion fractures.[ ]

## 4.3. Implications for research and clinical practice
There remains high uncertainty regarding the use of LIPUS in the treatment of nonunion fractures. Most studies, including the latest ones, are observational studies (mostly retrospective) with serious risks of bias.[ , , , , , ] There is a clear lack of high-quality RCTs to conclude on the effectiveness and safety of LIPUS for nonunion healing. Further well-powered, high-quality RCTs should be conducted to strengthen recommendations on the use of LIPUS in nonunion fracture management and include pain and function as outcomes. In addition, a consensus between researchers, clinicians, and patient-partners is needed to determine a definition of healing for nonunion fractures, including the maximal duration of a treatment to better estimate treatment success or failure. This is necessary to decrease heterogeneity due to various methodological choices. In this meta-analysis, LIPUS was shown, with a low certainty of evidence, to be ineffective compared to a sham on healing rate and healing time in patients with an established nonunion. This evidence, alongside the weak but existing risk of severe harms, does not support the use of LIPUS as a first-line treatment for established nonunions. Rehabilitation clinicians, such as physiotherapists, might consider including LIPUS in the rehabilitation plan, as well as usual interventions (pain management, swelling control, and range of motion exercises),[ ] in patients who cannot undergo surgery. This decision should also be guided by economic considerations, as LIPUS was not proven cost-effective against sham LIPUS in patients with a surgically treated tibial fracture.[ ]

## 5. Conclusions
In this study, the meta-analysis of observational studies estimated a 79% healing rate in participants with established nonunions treated with LIPUS, with a very low certainty of evidence. However, the meta-analysis of RCTs showed, with a low certainty of evidence, that LIPUS was not more effective than a sham in healing nonunion fractures. Current evidence does not support the routine use of LIPUS in nonunion treatment and rehabilitation, and further high-quality RCTs are needed to determine its clinical utility.
