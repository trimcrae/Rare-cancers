## Abstract

## Background
Chronic symptomatic internal carotid artery occlusion is an important cause of ischemic strokes. Medical management alone remains suboptimal for secondary prevention, and randomized controlled trials failed to demonstrate the efficacy and safety of extracranial‐intracranial vascular bypass. Carotid occlusion endovascular surgery (COES) is a promising technique, yet its efficacy and safety remain unclear.

## Methods
This systematic review and meta‐analysis included studies in which patients with chronic symptomatic internal carotid artery occlusion underwent treatment with COES and medical management. Primary outcomes included successful reperfusion rates and periprocedural ischemic and hemorrhagic events rates. Secondary outcomes included rates of ischemic events recurrence, other periprocedural events, and mortality. Studies contained at least one of the treatment groups and outcomes of interest.

## Results
Twenty‐two studies were selected for systematic review, with 18 of them for meta‐analysis. From 14 studies (N=561) the rate of successful recanalization was achieved in 74% of all patients undergoing COES. Thirteen studies (N=534) showed that the rate of COES was 2% both for periprocedural ischemic and hemorrhagic events. At long‐term follow‐up, the COES cohort included a total of 10 studies (N=311) and had a 12% rate of ischemic events, while the medical management group, which included 5 studies (N=313), showed a rate of 19%, with nonsignificant subgroup differences (P=0.09, I
2, 12%). Rates of other periprocedural mortality were 4% and 1%, respectively.

## Conclusion
This meta‐analysis supports the use of COES as a promising and innovative technique for the secondary prevention of symptomatic internal carotid artery occlusion. Our findings suggest that COES may be superior to medical management alone, although further research is needed to fully evaluate its efficacy and safety.

Keywords: chronic carotid occlusion, endovascular treatment, ischemic stroke

## Clinical Perspective

In this meta‐analysis, we have demonstrated that carotid occlusion endovascular surgery is a highly effective and safe procedure for the treatment of chronic symptomatic internal carotid artery occlusion.
Patients who received medical management alone presented a higher rate of ischemic events at long‐term follow‐up, compared with patients treated with carotid occlusion endovascular surgery.
Further research is necessary to identify imaging biomarkers and clinical features associated with the technique's success before its efficacy can be formally tested. These critical steps are necessary to optimize patient outcomes and refine clinical practice.

Chronic symptomatic internal carotid artery occlusion (SICO) remains one of the most devastating causes of stroke. It is estimated that carotid disease my cause 15 000 to 20 000 ischemic events annually in the United States, although the prevalence of chronic lesions remains uncertain. , After initial presentation, SICO carries an overall risk of subsequent ipsilateral stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) of 7% per year. , This risk can increase up to 24% in 2 years in a subgroup of patients with poor collateral flow, despite receiving medical management (MM). , In actuality, there is no proven intervention to treat patients affected by SICO.
An initial attempt to treat this vulnerable population included surgical bypass techniques, until the extracranial‐intracranial arterial bypass randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed no benefit for surgery. Subsequent work focused on identifying a high‐risk subgroup owing to poor collateral flow and impaired cerebral hemodynamics using positron emission tomography imaging. This led to the COSS study (Carotid Occlusion Study) of surgical bypass in this high‐risk subgroup. This RCT was stopped for futility after randomizing 200 patients. Despite the surgical group showing adequate prevention of recurrent events after surgery was successfully performed, the interim analysis demonstrated that surgery was not going to do better due to the unexpected high incidence of periprocedural stroke or death. A third RCT study, (The Extracranial‐Intracranial (EC‐IC) Bypass Study) the JET (Japanese EC‐IC Bypass Trial), in a second interim analysis showed a lower incidence of stroke in the surgical group (5% versus 14%; P=0.046), however, final results have not been yet published. , 

Over the past decade, we have developed considerable experience with endovascular treatment of acute cervical carotid occlusions. In ≈10% to 15% of acute stroke endovascular treatments, we face acute cervical tandem occlusions of the proximal internal carotid artery and a distal intracranial thrombus. , This translated in the publication of a few case series using endovascular recanalization of subacute/chronic carotid occlusion, which reported success rates up to 60% to 70% with low complication rates (≈5%). The popularity of this technique continues to increase, resulting in a growing number of patients being treated with little evidence for efficacy and safety. Herein, we sought to study recanalization rates and the safety of SICO treated with carotid occlusion endovascular surgery (COES) during the periprocedural period and at follow‐up through systematic review and aggregated data meta‐analysis of all the available literature. Additionally, we compared recurrent rates of stroke between MM and COES at follow‐up when both treatment arms were included in the same study.

## Methods
This systematic review and meta‐analysis is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analysis guidelines. The study is registered with PROSPERO (ID CRD42022315110). The data that support our findings are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

## Studies
We included RCTs, cohort studies, cross‐sectional studies, case series (≥10 cases), and case controls of endovascular treatment and MM for people with a diagnosis of SICO. Studies were included if they contained 1 of the interventions of interest or head‐to‐head comparisons, and at least 1 of the outcomes of interest described below.
Participants were adults (≥18 years old) with a diagnosis of SICO, defined as neuroimaging evidence of the lesions plus neurological symptoms correlated to the affected territory and/or ipsilateral hypoperfusion demonstrated in single‐photon emission computed tomography, positron emission tomography, perfusion magnetic resonance imaging, or computed tomography perfusion. We defined chronic lesions as those treated ≥2 weeks before COES when a SICO diagnosis was available. When the timing of the SICO diagnosis was unclear, we included studies that used the term chronic in their inclusion criteria. If studies reported participants treated with 1 of the interventions of interest plus any other intervention, we included data of only the first group. All studies containing patients presenting with acute tandem lesions and acute‐subacute isolated carotid occlusion <2 weeks from symptom onset were excluded.

## Interventions
Due to a lack of specific guidelines, MM was defined as best medical treatment according to contemporary medical practice. It consisted of single or dual antiplatelet therapy and pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions for coronary risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and smoking. COES was defined as revascularization and stenting of the carotid lesions, with or without embolic protection devices, and without restrictions for device type or techniques.

## Primary and Secondary Outcomes
Our primary efficacy outcome was recanalization success after COES, defined as deployment of stent and restoration of anterograde flow from the cervical segment into the intracranial vasculature without acute reocclusion during the procedure. Primary safety outcomes were symptomatic ischemic and hemorrhagic periprocedural events at 30 days after COES, including TIA, ischemic stroke, and any intracranial hemorrhage. Ischemic lesions evidenced on neuroimaging were considered only if they were reported to be associated with neurological symptoms.
Secondary outcomes included recurrence of symptomatic ischemic events including strokes and TIA at long‐term follow‐up for both groups, and rates of other periprocedural events and mortality at 30 days after COES. Long‐term follow‐up was established at >30 days. Periprocedural events included hemodynamic instability, carotid‐cavernous fistula, hyperperfusion syndrome without hemorrhage, and artery dissection without hemorrhage. Mortality included all causes of death.

## Search Strategy and Study Selection
We performed a systematic electronic literature search by entries to PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science through July 2022. No languages restriction was used, and translation was arranged when necessary. To identify further studies, we screened the reference list of relevant records. Our complete strategy is detailed in the .
Four independent reviewers (M.G.C., C.B.Z., A.R.C., J.W.) performed a preliminary screening of all identified records using an online application for systematic reviews (https://rayyan.qcri.org/). Potentially relevant studies were assessed as full text by 2 review authors (M.G.C., A.R.C.) independently, who identified studies for inclusion. Then, reviewers carried out data extraction using a data collection tool designed as a table and cross‐checked the extracted data. Disagreements were solved after discussion with a senior reviewer (S.O.G.).

## Baseline Data and Outcome Variables
We extracted the following data, if available: authors; year of publication; affiliated institutions; type of study; and patient demographics and characteristics, including number of participants; age; sex; comorbidities; smoking status; ischemic events; presence of cognitive impairment; contralateral stenosis or occlusion; hemodynamic impairment; premedications, medications received during intervention, and postmedications; type of intervention; technical success; complications; time of follow‐up; recurrence of stroke and TIA; intracerebral hemorrhage; mortality; and occlusion characteristics.

## Risk‐of‐Bias Assessment
Two independent reviewers (M.G.C., J.V.S.) evaluated the quality of the studies using tools according to the study's design. For cohorts with and without control groups, we used the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions, with the overall risk of bias rated as low, moderate, serious, and critical. For randomized clinical trials, we used a revised Cochrane risk of bias in randomized trials. 

## Statistical Analysis

## Measures of Intervention Effect
For each intervention, we pooled rates from included studies and obtained a weighted overall proportion with 95% CIs. We used generalized linear mixed models or Freeman–Tukey transformation, accordingly. , For studies with pairwise comparisons, we calculated the weighted estimate of intervention effect and reported odds ratios and 95% CI.
For all the analyses, we used a random‐effects model and calculated prediction intervals. For the main efficacy outcome, we performed a subgroup meta‐analysis to explore the difference between groups when measuring heterogeneity. We reported the test for subgroup difference, with significance level at P<0.05.

## Heterogeneity ad Publication Bias
We used the I
2 statistics to measure the percentage of variability due to between‐study heterogeneity, and values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered low, moderate, and high risk of heterogeneity, respectively. Additionally, because of its better performance for meta‐analysis of proportions, we calculated tau,2
 with significance level at P<1.0.
Publication bias was assessed with Doi plots and Luis Furuya‐Kanamori indexes. Egger's and Begg's tests were not performed due to their questionable reliability for meta‐analysis of proportions. 

## Sensitivity Analyses
Due to the variability of time length used to define “chronic occlusion” among the literature reviewed and the potential effect of lesions’ age on the primary and secondary outcomes of interest, we performed the following sensitive analyses: (1) excluding studies/patients where the occlusion time was not clearly defined, and (2) excluding studies/patients with an occlusion time <1 month (30 days).
To account for statistical heterogeneity, we conducted tests for outliers and generated Baujat plots, as well as sensitivity analyses to observe the influence of each study and the effect of its exclusion on effect size and I
2. All statistical analyses and graphs included in this study were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.1.3) and R Studio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). , 

## Certainty of the Evidence
Following Cochrane recommendations, 2 reviewers (M.G.C., A.R.C.) assessed the quality of the body of evidence using the grading of recommendation, assessment, development, and evaluation approach. For each outcome, we downgraded the evidence for serious study limitations in risk of bias, indirectness of evidence, inconsistency, imprecision of effect estimates, or publication bias, and analyses in which only pooled proportions were calculated. It is reported in the Supplemental Material section as a summary of findings table, performed in the grading of recommendation, assessment, development, and evaluation online tool (http://gradepro.org).

## Results

## Search Results and Included Studies
Our systematic search identified 7173 records, and 3 additional records were added from hand searching and reference list checking. After removal of duplicates, we screened 5511 records and retrieved 55 articles for full‐text assessment. Of those, 22 studies were selected for systematic review, with 18 of them for meta‐analysis. Figure  summarizes the study selection process according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analysis guidelines. From the studies not included for meta‐analyses, 2 had >50% population overlap and 2 included only patients with high risk for COES failure. Of the meta‐analyses, the studies by Kao et al. and Chen et al. had a population overlap of >90%. We included the study by Chen et al. only for the safety and technical success analyses, and the study by Kao et al. for efficacy; thus, these studies were not analyzed simultaneously. Two studies , provided individual patient data, and we collected information only for participants who met our inclusion criteria.
 

## Risk of Bias Within Studies and Certainty of the Evidence
All the observational studies were evaluated with the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions tool. Description of the assessment is displayed in Figure . Most outcomes had a high risk of publication bias, except for ischemic events recurrence in the MM group and hemorrhagic periprocedural events (Figure ). The summary of the certainty of the evidence findings is shown in Table . Additionally, after generating Baujat plots and performing influence analyses, there was not a significant difference when excluding from analyses the identified studies for all the outcomes (Figures and ).

## Studies’ Characteristics
Baseline characteristics of the included studies are described in Table  . From the 22 studies, 750 participants were treated with COES and 278 with MM. From the COES group, 561 were considered for the meta‐analysis. Mean age ranged from 55.4 to 67 years, and female population rates from 0% to 51.8%. Mean time of long‐term follow‐up ranged from 9 to 26 months for COES, and 10.2 to 56.4 months in the MM group. In the COES group, 6 studies included participants with ≥1 month of known SICO, 6 with ≥ 2 weeks, and 3 studies did not provide clear times (Table ).
 

## Meta‐Analysis

## Primary Efficacy Outcome: COES and Recanalization Success
Fourteen studies and 561 participants provided data demonstrating that COES had an overall recanalization success pooled rate of 74% (95% CI, 0.66–0.80; I2 41%) (Figure  ). Sensitive meta‐analyses segregating studies by time length of chronic lesions showed lower rates of recanalization success when excluding studies/patients with <1 month of known occlusion (65% [95% CI, 0.60–0.70]; I
2, 0%), and with unspecific time (72% [95% CI, 0.63–0.79]; I
2, 43%) (Table  ).
 

## Primary Safety Outcomes: COES and Periprocedural Events
Thirteen studies and 534 participants were included for the analysis of periprocedural events. We found similar pooled rates of periprocedural ischemia: 2% (95% CI, 0.01–0.04; I
2, 0%), and hemorrhagic events: 2% (95% CI, 0.01–0.04; I2, 0%) (Figure  ). In sensitive analyses, when excluding studies/patients with ≤1 month of known disease, rates of ischemic events were 2%, and hemorrhagic event rates were 3%. When excluding studies with unspecific times, we obtained a 2% rate for both outcomes of interest (Table  ).

## Secondary Efficacy Outcome: Recurrent Ischemic Events after COES or MM
Ten studies with 311 participants who underwent COES were included. The COES cohorts had a pooled rate of 12% (95% CI, 0.07–0.20; I
2, 6%) recurrent ischemic events at long‐term follow‐up (mean, 9–52 months). Pooled rates of recurrent ischemic events at follow‐up were 14% (95% CI, 0.10–0.19; I
2, 18%) when excluding <1‐month lesions, and 11% (95% CI, 0.06–0.21; I
2, 15%) when excluding unclear‐time lesions (Table  ).
In the MM cohorts, 5 studies and 313 participants were included, showing a pooled rate of recurrent ischemic events of 19% (95% CI, 0.15–0.25; I
2, 25%) at long‐term follow‐up (mean, 10.2–56.4 months). In subgroup analysis to investigate the difference of recurrence rates achieved when using COES versus MM, P value for test of subgroup differences was 0.09 (I
2, 12%) (Figure ). Only 2 studies were included for pairwise analysis, obtaining a pooled odds ratio of 0.52 (95% CI, 0.17–1.59; I
2, 0%) (Figure ).

## Secondary Efficacy Outcomes: Other Periprocedural Events and Mortality After COES
From 13 studies (N=534), the pooled rate of other periprocedural events was 4% (95% CI, 0.02–0.10; I
2, 20%), and from 11 studies (N=409) it was 1% for periprocedural mortality (95% CI, 0.00–0.02; I
2, 0%) (Figure ).

## Discussion
In this systematic review and meta‐analysis, we reviewed the published evidence on COES to treat SICO, in patients with ischemic symptoms±brain hemodynamic impairment, compared with outcomes on medical therapy. We showed that COES attained a recanalization success rate of 74% in a total of 574 cases treated. The COES safety profile was high, with a total rate of both ischemic and hemorrhagic events of 4% at 30 days after the procedure. Finally, once COES was successfully performed, it was associated with lower rates of recurrent symptomatic events at long‐term follow‐up with a higher efficacy than MM alone (12% versus 19%; P value for test of subgroup differences=0.09).
The development of new devices and techniques to treat acute ischemic stroke have been instrumental in the development of COES techniques to treat chronic SICO. We found that an average of 74% of patients achieve recanalization of the cervical segment and cerebral reperfusion. However, prediction intervals indicate that revascularization can be as low as 50% or as high as 88%, with a moderate statistical heterogeneity of 41%. This degree of variability could be explained by the differential amount of fibrotic clot organization and gradual extension after the occlusion, which needs to be blindly navigated without a reference road map. In fact, we found reduced recanalization rates when exclusively analyzed studies incorporating participants with ≥1 month of known SICOs. Although the degree of lesions’ hardness varies between individuals and is affected by several innate and acquired factors, time plays a crucial role in its maturation. In addition, the underlying cause of occlusion, most often atherosclerotic, may also influence the rate of revascularization as well. The higher the atherosclerotic and calcified plaques, the more challenging to cross the lesion. In fact, hybrid surgery using a combination of endarterectomy followed COES has been proposed by our group as an option for highly calcified occlusions in the proximal cervical segment. Nevertheless, the most important factor accounting for this variability is probably the large heterogeneity in endovascular techniques and devices used and lack of uniform selection criteria to identify imaging as well as anatomic factors that are associated with technical endovascular success. 

It is now well established that evaluation of cerebral hemodynamics in patients presenting with chronic SICO represent a critical factor to select patients who might benefit cervical carotid revascularization. The presence of hemodynamic impairment must be present before revascularization, since it identifies the highest‐risk population to experience a subsequent stroke within the next 2 years despite medical therapy. , However, there is a paucity of evidence to define the optimal predictors for patients who might better respond to COES. The chronically occluded internal carotid artery classification revealed that shorter lengths of occlusion found on digital subtraction angiography and noninvasive neuroimaging (computed tomography angiography or magnetic resonance angiography), and presence of a proximal and tampered internal carotid artery stump at the bifurcation were associated with higher COES revascularization success rates. Recent studies have used a 3‐Tesla perfusion magnetic resonance imaging scanner and 3‐dimensional T1‐weighted sampling with application‐optimized contrasts using different flip angle evolutions sequence to analyze vessel wall and lumen features in patients with SICO undergoing COES. Exhibition of high intraluminal signals in unenhanced T1‐weighted sampling with application‐optimized contrasts using different flip angle evolutions sequence was associated with successful recanalization, suggesting that such finding might be predictive of carotid artery stenting success due to a younger and softer plaque. In contrast, a collapsed vessel was identified in 90.5% in the failed recanalization group. Nevertheless, there is an urgent need to further improve imaging techniques, and to standardize imaging biomarkers to optimize the selection of patients who ultimately benefit from COES interventions.
An accurate periprocedural safety profile is a key feature for any revascularization before being widely used. COSS failed to show a benefit of the extracranial‐intracranial bypass compared with medical therapy alone in preventing subsequent strokes or death in patients with SICO who are hemodynamically compromised, despite excellent graft patency and rapid improvement of hemodynamic impairment, owing to an ≈15% periprocedural rate of stroke and death. However, it showed that when successful revascularization was achieved, it reduced this 2‐year risk to ≈6% versus 24%. More importantly, it suggested that any successful revascularization technique must have a 30‐day stroke and death rate <10% before being tested in a randomized clinical trial. , In our study, 30‐day periprocedural pooled rates of all stroke and death were 2% and 1%, which were considerably lower, and did not seem to be affected by the chronicity of the occlusion. While these results might be promising, they need to be interpreted with caution and without losing sight of the limitations of this study.
The main reason to recanalize a chronically occluded internal carotid artery in a symptomatic patient is to reduce the risk of recurrent ischemic stroke. In our analysis, the risk of recurrent stroke in the COES group is 12%, higher than the reported 6% rates of extracranial‐intracranial bypass, but lower than in the MM group (19%). Although our meta‐analysis of proportions does not allow us to accurately compare the pooled results of COES against those of MM, the patients who received MM had a nonsignificant trend toward a higher rate of recurrent strokes or TIAs at long‐term follow‐up than patients in the stenting group (19% versus 12%; P value of test for subgroup differences=0.09). Importantly, the mean follow‐up time for the COES group was 9 to 26 months (comparable to the COSS trial), while the follow‐up time for the medical treatment group was 10.2 to 56.4 months. However, as observed in the Kaplan–Meier curve of the COSS trial, patients with SICO are at a highest risk of ischemic events recurrence in their first year after enrollment and therefore potential lead‐time bias. On the other hand, similarly to endovascular techniques, medical therapy of SICO has improved in the past years, with a better control of cardiovascular risk factors (dyslipidemia, hypertension, smoking, and others) and use of dual antiplatelets, as supported by the CARESS (Clopidogrel and Aspirin for Reduction of Emboli in Symptomatic Carotid Stenosis) and CLAIR (Clopidogrel Plus Aspirin Versus Aspirin Alone for Reducing Embolization in Patients With Acute Symptomatic Cerebral or Carotid Artery Stenosis) studies. , As such, it is important to consider that the MM effect size in preventing stroke recurrence and death in 2 years might be lower than the 24% rate reported in COSS.

## Implications for Practice and Research
Our study summarizes the most complete objective evidence reporting the safety and efficacy of COES to treat SICO. While innovative endovascular interventions are very promising, the evidence to support it remains weak. First, complication rates in a prospective, independently adjudicated study are likely to be higher. In the future, strict inclusion criteria including the timing from diagnosis or SICO to COES should be used to avoid the limitations encountered in this meta‐analysis. Second, most of the reported patients treated with this procedure were selected on the basis of clinical or anatomic factors, without consistent hemodynamic assessment. Finally, standardizing well‐described technical approaches is critical for reproducibility of the results. There is an urgent need for a prospective safety study of endovascular recanalization to treat patients with SICO. Results will be used to optimize patient selection, the surgical technique, and technical success rate as well as 30‐day outcomes in this medically high‐risk population. Additionally, imaging techniques other than positron emission tomography are needed to measure cerebral hemodynamics in patients with symptomatic chronic carotid occlusions.

## Limitations
This meta‐analysis holds several limitations. First, the periprocedural events are self‐adjudicated and in 15 studies were retrospectively collected, which introduces important risks of selection and reporting bias.
Second, studies populations are hardly comparable. Most of the reported patients treated with this procedure were selected on the basis of clinical or anatomic factors, and only 9 studies formally evaluated hemodynamic impairment before intervention. Patients with severe hemodynamic impairment may have a much higher risk of periprocedural stroke (hemorrhagic or ischemic). Importantly, no RCT for comparison of both interventions was found. Consequently, it was necessary to perform an analysis of proportions obtained from single arms of different nonrandomized studies and 1 RCT. Additionally, the current nature of the study selection criteria likely contained selection bias, where patients managed conservatively probably exhibited more technically challenging lesions based on neurointerventionalists’ criteria and only the lesions with a more favorable profile were stented.
Since the majority of pooled analyses were obtained from observational, retrospective studies, there was high heterogeneity in definitions and populations. When it was feasible and data were provided, researchers manually extracted subgroups of patients who met our inclusion criteria. However, in some included studies, there was some uncertainty regarding the intervened segments and detailed description of the endovascular technique.
Regarding medical therapy, due to the lack of specification of the regimen used, many studies did not meet our inclusion criteria and could not be included in our analysis, limiting our sample size. Moreover, different times of follow‐up is another important limitation. As described before, the rate of stroke recurrence varies through time, and the lack of proper and consistent follow‐up time might have led to under‐ or overestimating the number of events.
Finally, the term chronic occlusion lacks a uniform time‐length definition. COES is fundamentally different in a patient who has symptoms of 3 days’ duration (especially when ongoing with blood pressure dependency or clinical/infarct mismatch indicating severe, near penumbral‐level hemodynamic impairment) versus a patient with a stable clinical deficit following an ischemic event (even if recurrent) that occurred 3 months prior. As shown in the sensitivity analysis, older occlusions have lower revascularization rates but not higher periprocedural complication rates. Furthermore, this category of patients seems to have a more ominous natural history, as noted in the number of recurrent events’ rates. Therefore, there is a clear need to define the term chronic occlusion for future research. The definition must consider the timeline of most recent symptoms relative to the timeline of carotid occlusion diagnosis and revascularization.

## Conclusion
COES represents a promising, innovative therapeutic approach for the secondary prevention of patients with SICO. Our findings indicate that this technique may be superior to optimal MM alone. While proceeding with further randomized trials remains our ultimate goal, it is necessary to identify imaging biomarkers and clinical features associated with technical endovascular success and chronic hemodynamic impairment. This will enable us to better assess the safety of the procedure, improve selection criteria, and characterize it more accurately. The natural next step would be to conduct a single‐arm phase multicenter trial, incorporating independent safety outcomes adjudication, a standard surgical approach, and appropriate inclusion criteria.

## Perspectives
Chronic SICO is an important cause of ischemic stroke; however, the optimal treatment has not been established. Literature concerning the efficacy and safety of the endovascular approach is scarce. Hence, this meta‐analysis pools all available studies and concludes that endovascular treatment of patients affected by chronic SICO might provide a high rate of recanalization success, with a remarkable safety profile. Furthermore, the limitations encountered by this study strongly suggest that criteria for patient selection should be improved before conducting a phase‐3 RCT.
