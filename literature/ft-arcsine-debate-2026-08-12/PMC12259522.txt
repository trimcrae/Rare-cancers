## Abstract

## Background
Cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL) is a tropical disease of public health concern, resulting from infection with Leishmania parasites and transmitted through the bite of infected female sandflies. Community awareness is an essential component of disease control and prevention. This study aimed to synthesize evidence on knowledge, attitudes, and practices about CL among the general population.

## Methods
A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, EMBASE, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, Springer, and ProQuest for studies published in English up to January 2025. Search terms included both MeSH and free-text keywords related to KAP toward CL. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Data were extracted, and a random-effects meta-analysis of proportions was applied for pooling of studies. Heterogeneity was explored through subgroup and sensitivity analyses. The certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach.

## Results
Knowledge was assessed by 47 studies (n = 21,930), while attitude was assessed by 32 studies (n = 13,171), and practice was evaluated by 30 studies (n = 13,729) for CL. Overall, the general population had an estimated 54.5%(95% CI: 47.8 to 61.1) good knowledge of CL, with positive attitudes at 56.2% (95% CI: 48.5 to 63.8), and practically 49.5% (95% CI: 40.3–53.8). A substantial heterogeneity was observed between studies (I2 > 98% in all cases). The European region had the highest level of knowledge (75.3%), while the Southeast Asia region had a positive attitude (65.6%) and good practice in the Eastern Mediterranean region (45.9%). Meta-regression analyses indicated that the year of publication was significantly associated with heterogeneity in knowledge and attitude outcomes. In contrast, the WHO region was significantly associated with heterogeneity in practice outcomes (p < 0.05). According to the GRADE approach, the overall certainty of evidence was moderate for both knowledge and attitude outcomes, and high for practice outcomes.

## Conclusion
The level of knowledge, attitude, and practice of the general population regarding CL is not at the desired level in many regions, especially in endemic countries. These results emphasize the need to design and implement educational interventions and targeted awareness programs to promote public KAP about CL, especially in areas with high prevalence and low practice.

## Introduction
Leishmaniasis is listed by the World Health Organization(WHO) as a neglected tropical disease, reflecting its substantial public health burden and socioeconomic impact [ ].
This is a parasitic disease caused by flagellated protozoa of the Leishmania species. It is an endemic disease transmitted via the bite of infected female phlebotomine sandflies, affecting humans and some animal reservoirs [ ].
The global incidence is estimated at approximately 2,000,000 new cases annually, and the overall prevalence is estimated at 12,000,000 cases worldwide. However, these incidents are probably underestimated in many countries due to underreporting and a lack of mandatory notification [ ].
Leishmaniasis presents clinically as cutaneous (CL), mucocutaneous (MCL), and visceral Leishmaniasis (VL) [ ]. CL is the most common form of Leishmaniasis [ ], with 205,990 cases reported to the WHO in 2022. Only six countries, Afghanistan, Brazil, Colombia, Morocco, Peru, and the Syrian Arab Republic, contributed to 99.98% of all global CL cases. The burden of CL is most significant in the Eastern Mediterranean Region, with approximately 75% of all reported cases originating from that area, with more than half of all cases reported globally from Afghanistan and the Syrian Arab Republic alone. Expanding access to physical therapy (e.g., cryotherapy and thermotherapy), integrating CL control into NTD programs, and increasing the availability of medicines, especially in crisis-affected countries in the region, have supported improvements in the systems for detecting and managing cases.
Recent CL events from endemic areas have been attributed to environmental change, treatment failures, host immune status, and emerging drug resistance [ ].
In addition, CL outbreaks have been worsened in conflict zones where access to health services is compromised, and human-to-human transmission in overcrowded situations is more probable. The disease has also been disseminated among refugees in neighboring countries of Turkey, Jordan, and Lebanon [ , ].
CL not only causes physical disfigurement but is also implicated in various psychological and social problems like anxiety, stress, and depression, leading to a decrease in the affected individuals'quality of life [ ]. These elements affect economic productivity in their own right [ ].
The burden of CL remains largely unquantified in sub-Saharan African countries, where implementing needed control measures [ ].
No vaccine for human use has been approved, and treatment is complex, especially in resource-limited settings [ , ]. It means that prevention strategies are the top priority. Effective risk reduction for CL involves integrated interventions such as vector control, health education, community engagement, and strengthening disease surveillance systems [ , ].
Despite the increasing burden of CL, global knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward the disease are not well characterized. Several studies have assessed the KAP related to CL in different regions, revealing considerable variations across countries. In Ghana, Doe et al. reported that 88.3% of respondents had a reasonable knowledge of CL, and only 17% recognized its vector [ ]. In Pakistan, Irum et al. observed that only 48.2% of participants were aware of CL, with moderate knowledge of its transmission and vector [ ]. In northeastern Ethiopia, Dires et al. reported that 27.6% of respondents had good knowledge, while 34.5% exhibited a favorable attitude towards CL [ ].
With CL's significant public health burden and the indispensable role of community knowledge in its prevention and control, comprehensive global data on KAP levels is fundamental. Knowledge about these areas can be even a root for targeted health education programs, policy development, and strengthened surveillance. No systematic global assessment of KAP regarding CL has, however, yet been performed. To fill this evidence gap, this study aimed to synthesize evidence on knowledge, attitudes, and practices about CL among the general population.

## Materials and Methods

## Study Design and Setting
This review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020 [ ]) checklist, which contains 27 items to standardize the accurate reporting of systematic reviews/meta-analyses. The study was also registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration): CRD42025645971.
Available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42025645971.

## Search Strategy
A comprehensive, systematic search of electronic databases was performed to identify relevant publications. The search was conducted from 01 January 2025 to 06 March 2025, covering studies published up to January 2025. The databases consulted included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, EMBASE, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, Springer, and ProQuest.
A formal search strategy was applied using a combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords aligned with the study objectives (see Supplementary Table  ). Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT), truncation symbols, and phrase searching techniques were used to enhance the sensitivity and specificity of the search. Other databases such as HINARI, LIVIVO, and CINAHL were not included due to substantial content overlap with the selected sources and their lower expected yield for the topic of interest.
In addition to database searching, we explored grey literature sources, including preprint servers and conference abstracts. A manual search of the reference lists from all eligible full-text articles was also performed to identify additional relevant studies. Two independent reviewers screened all retrieved records for eligibility based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Studies were included according to prespecified inclusion criteria. We included all observational studies that reported data on KAP regarding CL among the general population. Eligible studies were published in English with full text available; study subjects were selected through census and/or random sampling.
We excluded studies with inconsistent data, ambiguous sampling, non-random sampling, duplicates, review articles, meta-analyses, short reports, and case reports. Moreover, studies that did not report data for key variables (level of awareness, positive attitude, or appropriate preventive Practice for CL) were also excluded. Studies that focused on populations other than the general population were excluded from the analysis.

## Risk of Bias (Quality) Assessment
To evaluate the quality of the included non-randomized studies, we employed the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), adapted from the University of Newcastle and the University of Ottawa. This tool assesses methodological quality across three domains: selection of the study population, comparability of study groups, and ascertainment of the outcome or exposure. Each study received a score from 0 to 10. Based on the total score, studies were categorized as Good (7–10 points), Fair (5–6 points), and Poor (0–4 points). Two independent reviewers conducted the assessments, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion.

## Data Extraction
Data extraction in this study was performed systematically and with care. EndNote X20 was used to remove duplicate entries after importing all selected articles. They then reviewed the remaining publications and titles/abstracts to eliminate inappropriate research (Fig.  ). The articles were then selected and finalized by group discussion.
 The characteristics that will be extracted from studies included author(s), year of study and type of study, demographic factors (such as age groups, sex), geographical area of study (country), number of subjects studied, the level of appropriate knowledge or positive attitude or good practice toward CL.
In this study, the knowledge, attitude, and practice about CL were as follows:

## Knowledge
Knowledge of CL encompasses awareness of the causative agent (the Leishmania parasite), the mode of transmission (sandfly bites), the clinical signs and symptoms (e.g., skin ulcers), the risk factors, and the preventive strategies (e.g., personal protection and environmental control). A good level of knowledge was defined as achieving an above-average score or meeting the cut-off point specified in each original study.

## Attitude
Attitude referred to an individual's beliefs and perceptions relating to CL, as well as their willingness to engage in preventive and control behaviours, including their perceived severity of the disease, perceived susceptibility, and trust in public health measures. A positive attitude was defined as scoring above the mean or surpassing a predefined threshold, as reported in the original studies.

## Practice
Practice referred to the actual behaviors individuals adopted regarding the prevention, early detection, and management of CL. This included the use of personal protective measures (such as insecticide-treated bed nets, repellents, and wearing protective clothing), timely health-seeking behaviors upon noticing suspicious skin lesions, adherence to medical treatment, and participation in community-based vector control initiatives (e.g., environmental sanitation and cooperation with spraying programs). A good level of practice was defined as achieving a score higher than the mean or meeting a predefined threshold established in the respective original studies.

## Strategy for Data Synthesis
The statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14 and ArcGIS 10.3. This meta-analysis focused on the synthesis of proportions (knowledge, attitude, and practice—KAP—levels regarding CL in the general population). To stabilize the variance of raw proportions and address skewness near 0 or 1, we applied the double-arcsine transformation before pooling.
A random-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird method was used to allow for between-study heterogeneity and to estimate pooled prevalence/proportion values and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A random-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird method was used to allow for between-study heterogeneity and to estimate pooled prevalence/proportion values and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Given its wide application, computational simplicity, and interpretability in meta-analyses of proportions, the DL method was deemed suitable for our context. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the Cochran Q statistic and quantified with the I2 index. The degree of heterogeneity was classified as low (< 50%), moderate (50%–79%), or high (≥ 80%) [ ].
To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted univariate meta-regressions using covariates such as study country, publication year, sample size, and methodological quality.
Potential publication bias was assessed visually by funnel plots and statistically using Egger's regression test. When asymmetry was detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to estimate the impact of potentially missing studies on pooled estimates.
A sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method was conducted to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall summary effect.
Additionally, we used ArcGIS 10.3 to spatially map the distribution of KAP levels across countries and regions, providing a geographic visualization of the burden and awareness of CL in different populations.

## Certainty Assessment
The certainty of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach, following the guidance provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.5.1 (updated May 2025). This method evaluates the quality of evidence across five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Each outcome was rated according to one of four standardized levels of certainty: high, moderate, low, or very low. A high certainty rating indicates strong confidence that the actual effect is close to the estimated effect. In contrast, a moderate certainty rating reflects moderate confidence, acknowledging that the actual effect might differ. Low certainty indicates limited confidence, with a substantial likelihood that the actual effect is different, and very low certainty reflects very little confidence in the estimate.

## Results

## Search Results and Eligibility Studies
Forty-seven studies were included in the final meta-analysis of KAP of the general population towards CL. They were underpinned by a wide geographic range representing diverse continents in Asia [ , – ], Africa [ , – ], Europe [ ], and South America [ , ]. However, they were mostly conducted in endemic areas, including Iran [ , – , , ], Ethiopia [ , – ], Pakistan [ , , , , ], and other regions. All studies included were cross-sectional designs with sample sizes ranging from 18 to 1824 participants.
In addition to the database and grey literature search, manual screening of reference lists of included studies identified five additional eligible articles, which were also included in the final analysis. The study selection process, including the number of records screened, excluded, and included based on the predefined KAP criteria for CL, is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig.  ).
Studies included in this review were of moderate quality, with scores ranging from 5 to 8 (Supplementary Table  ). There was a wide variation in the reported level of good knowledge (14.6% to 92.2%), positive attitudes (7.2% to 91%), and good practices (7.05% to 96.5%) towards CL (Table  and Fig.  ).

 

## Pooled Good Knowledge of CL
Knowledge about CL was assessed in 47 studies comprising 21,930 participants (Fig.  ). The overall pooled estimate of good knowledge among the general population was 54.5% (95% CI: 47.8–61.1). However, a high level of heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 99.1%, Q = 5164.77, df = 46, p < 0.0001, Tau2 = 0.0537). A random-effects model was therefore applied to account for this heterogeneity.
 A sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out approach confirmed the stability of the findings, indicating that no single study significantly affected the overall estimate (Supplementary Fig. ). Meta-regression analysis suggested that the year of publication was a statistically significant contributor to the observed heterogeneity (p < 0.05), as shown in Table  .

 Subgroup analyses based on WHO region and study period revealed considerable variation in knowledge levels (Table  ). The highest knowledge estimate was reported in the European Region (75.3%, 95% CI: 71.5–79.5), although this was derived from a single study. The Region of the Americas followed with an estimate of 70.1% (95% CI: 66.2–74.0), based on two studies with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). The Eastern Mediterranean Region and the African Region had similar pooled knowledge levels of 56.8% (95% CI: 48.4–65.2) and 56.1% (95% CI: 44.1–68.2), respectively. The lowest knowledge estimate was found in the Southeast Asia Region at 39.4% (95% CI: 28.4–54.0).

 In terms of publication period, studies conducted from 2010 to 2014 reported the highest level of knowledge (67.5%, 95% CI: 59.5–75.4), followed by those published between 2020 and 2024 (63.4%, 95% CI: 50.0–76.7), whereas the lowest estimate was observed for studies from 2015 to 2019 (47.2%, 95% CI: 39.6–84.8). At the country level (Fig.  ), high knowledge levels exceeding 60% were reported in Portugal (75.3%) and Brazil (70.1%). Moderate levels, ranging from 40 to 60%, were found in Iran (59.9%), Saudi Arabia (59.9%), and Ethiopia (55.8%). In contrast, India (43%) and Bangladesh (26%) had the lowest reported knowledge levels.
 

## Pooled Positive Attitudes Toward CL
Positive attitudes toward CL were assessed in 32 studies, including a total of 13,171 participants (Fig.  ). The overall pooled estimate of a positive attitude was 56.2% (95% CI: 48.5–63.8). There was substantial heterogeneity among the included studies, with I2 = 98.7%, Q = 2953.53, df = 31, p < 0.0001, and Tau2 = 0.0457. A random-effects model was therefore applied.
 Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method showed that no single study had a significant impact on the pooled estimate, confirming the robustness of the results (Supplementary Fig. ). Meta-regression analyses suggested that the year of publication was an important source of heterogeneity (p < 0.05), as indicated in Table  .
Subgroup analyses based on WHO region and study period revealed notable variation in attitudes across geographical and temporal contexts (Table  ). The highest pooled proportion of positive attitudes was found in the Southeast Asia Region (65.6%, 95% CI: 48.6–82.5), followed by the African Region (59.8%, 95% CI: 46.4–73.1) and the Eastern Mediterranean Region (54.0%, 95% CI: 46.4–61.7). The lowest estimate was reported from the Region of the Americas (38.1%, 95% CI: 32.5–43.7), although this was based on a single study, limiting the generalizability of findings for that subgroup.
Concerning publication year, studies conducted from 2010 to 2014 showed the highest pooled estimate of positive attitude (70.0%, 95% CI: 57.7–82.3), followed by those from 2015 to 2019 (55.7%, 95% CI: 34.5–76.5), and those published between 2020 and 2024 (52.5%, 95% CI: 45.8–59.1).
At the country level (Fig.  ), the highest proportions of positive attitude were observed in India (65.6%), Ethiopia (64.6%), Iran (57.9%), Yemen (52.5%), Bangladesh (54.1%), Egypt (54.1%), Nigeria (53.4%), Pakistan (45.3%), Kenya (44.0%), Saudi Arabia (43.3%), and Paraguay (38.1%). The lowest estimate was reported from Ghana, at 30.2%.
 

## Pooled Good Practice Toward CL
Good practice toward CL was assessed in 30 studies involving a total of 13,729 participants (Fig.  ). The pooled estimate of good practice in the general population was 49.5% (95% CI: 40.3–58.8). A very high degree of heterogeneity was observed among the studies (I2 = 99.6%, Q = 3951.75, df = 29, p < 0.0001, Tau2 = 0.0680), necessitating the use of a random-effects model.
 Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method demonstrated that the exclusion of individual studies did not materially alter the overall estimate, indicating that no single study had a disproportionate effect on the results (Supplementary Fig. ). Meta-regression analysis identified WHO region as a statistically significant contributor to heterogeneity in practice outcomes (p < 0.05), as presented in Table  .
Subgroup analyses by WHO region and study period (Table  ) revealed substantial geographic and temporal variability in practice levels. The highest proportion of good practice was observed in the European Region (75.0%, 95% CI: 71.2–78.8), although this was based on a single study. The Southeast Asia Region followed with 74.0% (95% CI: 45.6–99.2), based on three studies. The African Region reported a pooled estimate of 51.6% (95% CI: 38.0–65.2), and the Eastern Mediterranean Region showed the lowest estimate at 45.9% (95% CI: 34.9–56.9).
Based on the year of study, the highest proportion of good practice was found in studies conducted between 2010 and 2014 (57.5%, 95% CI: 41.5–73.4), followed by those published between 2015 and 2019 (52.5%, 95% CI: 31.9–73.1), and the lowest in studies from 2020 to 2024 (48.7%, 95% CI: 34.1–63.3).
Country-level estimates of good practice (Fig.  ) further highlighted this variation. Portugal recorded the highest practice level at 75.0%, followed by India (74.0%), Pakistan (63.5%), Nigeria (61.3%), and Kenya (58.0%). Morocco (51.3%), Saudi Arabia (52.6%), and Ethiopia (50.6%) fell within the moderate range. Lower estimates were seen in Ghana and Iran (both 40.0%), while Yemen showed the lowest level of good practice at 16.3%.
 

## Publication Bias
As for knowledge, no significant publication bias was shown according to the funnel plot; thus, the distribution of studies in the funnel plot was symmetrical. Moreover, Egger's regression test showed no evidence of bias (bias = 0.313, 95% CI: − 7.44 to 8.07, p = 0.936) (Fig.  A).
 Similarly, for attitudes in the corresponding funnel plot, a relatively symmetrical pattern was a prominent feature, and Egger's regression test showed no evidence of publication bias (bias =  − 2.312, 95% CI: − 11.69 to 7.06, p = 0.620) (Fig.  B).
Regarding practices, the analysis once again demonstrated no evidence of publication bias, as indicated by the symmetrical funnel plot and the results of Egger's regression test (bias = 9.51, 95% CI: −0.902 to 19.92, p = 0.072) (Fig.  C).

## Certainty of Evidence
According to the GRADE approach, the overall certainty of evidence was moderate for both knowledge and attitude outcomes, and high for practice outcomes. Most included studies were of moderate to good methodological quality, with low risk of bias. However, all three domains demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 97%), indicating serious inconsistency. In addition, the wide confidence intervals observed in the pooled estimates for knowledge and attitude suggest imprecision. No evidence of publication bias was found, as demonstrated by symmetrical funnel plots and non-significant Egger’s tests (p > 0.05; Supplementary Table  ).

## Discussion
This Meta-analysis of 47 studies with over 21,000 participants demonstrated that the overall good knowledge of CL was moderate at 54.5%. The positive attitude was observed in approximately 56.2% of the population, and good practice was observed in 49.5%. Significant heterogeneity was noted among the studies, reflecting considerable geographic and temporal disparities. The European and Southeast Asia areas reflected the highest level of knowledge and practice, and the lowest level of knowledge was found in Southeast Asia. Presumably, these disparities reflect disparities in cultural, social, and health education variables and control program implementation. Sensitivity analyses and tests for publication bias confirmed the stability and reliability of the aggregated results. GRADE evaluation indicated moderate or high certainty of evidence despite the observed heterogeneity and imprecision of some outcomes. In total, these findings point to the urgent need for specific interventions to promote awareness, foster positive attitudes, and induce efficient preventive practices to control CL.
The meta-analysis findings demonstrate that while over half of the population possesses a general knowledge of CL, substantial deficiencies remain regarding specific and essential epidemiological understanding. The findings of the meta-analysis show that there are significant knowledge gaps concerning specific and basic epidemiological information. This partial knowledge is concerning, as awareness of the disease being infectious but unknown in terms of transmission vector—i.e., the sand fly—can prevent the accomplishment of preventive interventions [ ]. All this heterogeneity in knowledge, e.g., as low as 19% in some situations with zero knowledge of vector biology, reflects ongoing education gaps in vector [ ]. These discrepancies are owed to heterogeneity in public health infrastructure, access to targeted health education, and socio-cultural determinants of information dissemination. These gaps must be addressed since, in the absence of correct knowledge regarding modes of transmission, communities do not apply the desired protection measures, and cycles of disease transmission prevail. Therefore, special education programs focusing on vector identification and transmission behaviors form an essential component of integrated CL control programs.
Subgroup analyses indicated higher knowledge levels in certain regions, possibly reflecting stronger health education systems or better access to public health information. In contrast, lower knowledge levels were reported in the other areas, where a large proportion of participants were unaware of prevention methods. These disparities highlight the need for tailored health education programs, particularly in areas with limited knowledge, to address misconceptions about transmission and vectors.
Our analysis revealed significant variations in knowledge levels across different publication periods. Specifically, higher knowledge estimates were observed in the earliest and most recent periods, while a notable decline was evident in the middle period. These fluctuations may reflect changes in public awareness influenced by varying intensities of public health campaigns, research priorities, and possibly the epidemiological burden of the studied condition during these times. The increased knowledge observed in the early and recent periods aligns with intensified health promotion efforts and renewed research focus reported in the literature. Conversely, the dip in the middle period might suggest a temporary decline in public engagement or a shift in research emphasis. This pattern highlights the importance of sustained and adaptive educational strategies to maintain and improve public knowledge over time [ , ].
This aligns with global efforts to improve community-based interventions for CL, which rely heavily on the knowledge of affected populations [ ]. Building a baseline understanding of local perceptions, as emphasized in several studies, is crucial to designing effective health communication strategies.
Other results of this study revealed a moderate overall level of positive attitudes toward CL, with about 56% of participants recognizing it as a significant health concern. Nevertheless, the considerable variability observed across studies underscores the complex interplay of sociocultural and contextual influences shaping public perceptions.
For instance, in Saudi Arabia, despite a high proportion (71.6%) acknowledging CL as a health issue, a notable 62.8% believed it to be untreatable, indicating the coexistence of awareness and fatalistic beliefs that may hinder effective health-seeking behaviors [ ]. Similarly, findings from Ethiopia illustrate this duality: while 71.6% perceived CL as a health problem, nearly a third (29.1%) expressed stigmatizing attitudes, highlighting cultural stigma as a significant barrier to fostering supportive community responses [ ]. These contrasting attitudes suggest that knowledge alone is insufficient to ensure positive public perceptions; addressing deep-rooted cultural beliefs and misinformation is critical for designing effective educational and behavioral interventions aimed at improving CL management and reducing stigma.
The considerable heterogeneity observed between studies, combined with meta-regression findings of publication year as a substantial covariate, may be a sign of temporal trends in public attitudes, possibly resulting from an increasing over time in health education activities. Subgroup analyses illustrated that the Southeast Asia Region had the highest percentage of positive attitudes (65.6%), specifically in India, and this may possibly be explained by universal community-based campaign outreach. Conversely, the lowest prevalence of positive attitude was seen from the Region of the Americas (38.1%), although this finding is based on one study conducted in Paraguay and should therefore be interpreted with reservation. These variations by regions are consistent with earlier studies, which suggest that the failure of prevention practices often accompanies negative public feelings towards control measures [ ]. Intensive education intervention to reduce stigma and debunk myths—a concern emphasized in some studies included—remains essential to improve public opinion, especially in those with lower estimates [ ]. Temporal patterns indicate a gradual decline in positive attitudes toward CL over the past decade. This downward trend may reflect diminished intensity of public health outreach, shifting health priorities, or reduced community engagement. The observed pattern aligns with calls in the literature for revitalized awareness campaigns and sustained educational interventions [ ]. Some studies have also demonstrated the potential for improvement when supported by consistent community-level engagement and structured interventions [ , ].
The results also point to a suboptimal level of engagement in preventive practices toward CL, with less than half of the participants demonstrating appropriate behaviors. This highlights potential gaps in awareness, accessibility, or effectiveness of health communication strategies across diverse populations. These findings are consistent with prior evidence, such as a study in which only 27% of participants recognized chemoprophylaxis and 22% identified insect repellents as protective measures, while merely 13.4% reported using bed nets [ , ]. Similarly, in Ethiopia, only 5.5% used bed nets and 3.3% applied indoor sprays, reflecting neglect of CL prevention [ ]. The marked heterogeneity observed in preventive practices appears to be shaped by regional differences, as highlighted in meta-regression analyses. Higher engagement in some areas may be linked to stronger health systems and greater public awareness, whereas weaker performance in others seems to reflect socioeconomic and informational barriers. Notably, certain regions with robust infrastructure showed more proactive behaviors, while others—particularly those affected by instability or limited resources—exhibited consistently low levels of practice. These disparities underline the importance of tailoring vector control and health education strategies to specific regional contexts.
Preventive practices appeared to peak in the early 2010 s, with a subsequent decline in more recent years. These trends may reflect changes in CL prevalence, fluctuations in public health campaign intensity, or evolving public perceptions of disease risk, as a study noted increased cases with urban expansion [ ]. Countries like Nigeria (61.3%) and Kenya (58%) showed moderate practices, akin to Ethiopia’s limited community-level control, hindered by reliance on traditional healers (77%) [ , ].
The extremely low prevalence of preventive practices in Yemen (16.3%) is also reflective of the general neglect of CL as a public health issue and testifies to a universal lack of knowledge about existing preventive practices. The absence of large-scale sensitivity studies adds weight to these findings. However, the high heterogeneity reported suggests that context-dependent solutions are called for. As identified in previous literature, strengthening preventive interventions calls for focused education in sand fly control, consistent application of bed nets, and environmental control, particularly in rural areas where literacy is low, as reported in Ethiopia and Pakistan [ , , , , ]. Future interventions should focus on underperforming regions such as the Eastern Mediterranean and countries such as Yemen, employing culturally and economically appropriate methods that will best translate knowledge into practice for the long-term control of CL.

## Limitations
There are several limitations to our systematic review and meta-analysis that should be addressed. The included studies were highly heterogeneous in the measuring instruments for the KAP, which could have resulted in pooled estimates. Second, the selected studies were primarily performed in low- and middle-income countries, and therefore, the generalizability of findings to high-income environments may be limited. Thirdly, although we initially attempted to identify grey literature and unpublished reports, these sources were ultimately not included in the final analysis, and therefore, we cannot completely rule out the potential impact of publication bias. Fourth, the language limit to searches that were conducted to English-only studies would have resulted in the exclusion of studies published in other languages. Fifth, differences in cultural and educational contexts between study sites may have affected how respondents interpreted survey questions, thereby influencing the reliability of the reported KAP levels.

## Conclusion
This review provides a regionally synthesized estimate of population KAP on CL prevention. Overall, knowledge and attitudes were moderate, but preventive practices were insufficient, especially in the Eastern Mediterranean and rural low-literacy settings. Between-study heterogeneity was high, reflecting underlying variations in educational and health system contexts.
The findings reaffirm the need for culturally appropriate, community-based health education to improve adoption of CL preventive behavior. Standardized KAP tools should be employed in future research, and underserved populations should be taken into account to provide enhanced national and global policy. The behavioral dimensions of CL control need to be prioritized by policy-makers and health planners.

Significance
While cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL) is an important public health problem in most tropical and some sub-tropical countries, public knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) about its prevention and control are lacking and usually not encouraged. This study is the first systematic review and meta-analysis, to our knowledge, that has tackled worldwide KAP towards CL among the general population. The most important results of this study are that the overall level of knowledge and preventive practice against CL is not sufficient to support sustainable disease control. Gathering together evidence from various geographic settings, the review demonstrates the fundamental gaps in public knowledge and practice that hamper an effective response to disease prevention.
