## Abstract

## Rationale
Trachoma remains a leading cause of preventable blindness that has long
persisted in Egypt. Global estimates tend to undervalue the actual national
prevalence, and no previous analysis has comprehensively mapped the temporal
and regional trends within the country. The present study addresses these
gaps to guide elimination efforts and policy planning.

## Objective
To estimate the prevalence of active and chronic trachoma and associated
visual impairment and blindness in Egypt through a comprehensive systematic
review and meta analysis.

## Methods
We prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251070078) and adhered to
PRISMA 2020 and Cochrane standards. Three independent reviewers searched 10
databases without language restrictions for population-based,
cross-sectional surveys that reported WHO-graded trachoma outcomes. Two
reviewers independently screened records, extracted data, and assessed bias
using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist; Grading of
Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) appraised
evidence certainty. We transformed proportions using the Freeman–Tukey
double arcsine transformation, pooled estimates using random-effects
meta-analysis and directly age-standardized them to Egypt’s 2000-2020
population structure. We performed sensitivity, subgroup, and
meta-regression analyses to explore heterogeneity.

## Results
From 16 cross sectional surveys (n = 45 934), the pooled age standardized
prevalence estimates were as follows: active trachoma in children (1-9
years) 10.8% (95% CI; 7.0-14.6), visual impairment in those cases 5.3% (95%
CI; 3.2-7.5), chronic trachoma in adults (≥ 15 years) 7.2% (95% CI;
4.0-10.4), associated visual impairment 1.6% (95% CI; 1.0-2.2), and
blindness 0.7% (95% CI; 0.1-1.9). Sensitivity, subgroup, and meta regression
analyses demonstrated consistent estimates and no evidence of small study
effects.

## Conclusion
Despite substantial declines, active and chronic trachoma remain well above
elimination thresholds in Egypt. These findings underscore the need for
sustained implementation of the SAFE strategy—particularly enhanced water,
sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, ongoing surveillance, and
targeted surgical outreach—and call for harmonized survey methods to guide
and evaluate future control efforts.

Keywords: Trachoma, prevalence, meta analysis, Egypt, visual impairment, blindness, neglected tropical diseases

## Introduction
Trachoma persists as the world’s foremost infectious cause of preventable blindness,
with around 115 million active infections and 1.5 million individuals suffering from
chronic disease. It disproportionately afflicts impoverished rural populations
across Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean, and certain regions of Asia [ , ].
Repeated ocular infection with Chlamydia trachomatis drives a
clinical course from active inflammatory stages—trachomatous inflammation–follicular
(TF) and –intense (TI)—to scarring sequelae including trachomatous trichiasis (TT)
and corneal opacity (CO), which can culminate in irreversible vision loss [ ]. The World Health Organization’s (WHO)
simplified grading system has facilitated surveillance and guided the SAFE strategy
(Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, Environmental improvement) toward global
elimination [ , ].
Although Egypt is historically known as a trachoma-endemic nation—with accounts
dating back to antiquity and persistent reports into the late twentieth century—its
national burden remains poorly delineated [ , ]. In Egypt’s healthcare
system, the traditional designation for an ophthalmologist—Tabīb
Ramad, literally “physician of trachoma”—underscores the profound
cultural and public-health significance of trachoma in the country. Unlike other
endemic conditions, such as schistosomiasis or hepatitis C, for which no analogous
clinical titles exist, this nomenclature reflects both the historical disease burden
and ongoing societal awareness of trachoma’s impact on ocular health.
Nonetheless, existing data on trachoma prevalence derived from geographically limited
surveys, and Egypt was not prioritized under the WHO’s Global Elimination of
trachoma by 2020 initiative, leaving a critical void in comprehensive, countrywide
prevalence mapping [ ]. Small-scale studies
report active TF prevalence ranging from 8% to over 50% in selected rural districts;
however, methodological heterogeneity, variable diagnostic protocols, and uneven
geographic coverage have precluded the aggregation of findings or assessment of
temporal trends [ , ].
In light of newly published survey data and the urgent need to monitor Egypt’s
progress toward WHO elimination thresholds (TF < 5% among 1–9-year-olds; TT <
0.2% in adults ≥ 15 years), a quantitative synthesis is timely. Pooling data across
regions and decades can reconcile inconsistencies, adjust for demographic shifts
through age-standardization, and explore sources of inter-study variability through
subgroup and meta-regression analyses. Such an approach promises to deliver robust,
nationally representative prevalence estimates to inform policy, allocate resources,
and reinvigorate SAFE implementation where trachoma persists.

## Objective
This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to estimate the pooled
age-standardized prevalence of active trachoma (TF/TI) in children (1–9 years
old) and chronic trachoma (TT/CO) in adults (≥15 years old) across Egypt.
Secondary objectives include (1) evaluating geographic (governorate,
urban/rural), demographic (age, sex), and temporal variations; (2) quantifying
associated visual impairment and blindness; and (3) identifying trends that can
guide elimination strategies and public health policy.

## Methods
The present systematic review and meta-analysis strictly followed the PRISMA 2020
reporting guidelines (see Appendix A: Reporting Checklist) and the recommendations
of the Cochrane Handbook in formulating research questions, conducting electronic
searches, selecting studies, extracting data, assessing quality, performing
statistical analyses, and reporting results [ , ]. To ensure transparency
and minimize bias, we prospectively registered our protocol with PROSPERO
(CRD420251070078). This work synthesizes anonymized, previously published data and
does not involve new human subject research. Under local regulations and the
Declaration of Helsinki, the analysis was exempt from institutional review board
oversight. No individual informed consent was required.

## Information Sources and Literature Search Strategy
Three independent reviewers (AMM, EM, and HM) conducted a systematic search
across PubMed, Scopus, Ovid Embase, EBSCOhost, ScienceDirect, Web of Science,
Google Scholar, the WHO African Index Medicus (AIM), the WHO Index Medicus for
Eastern Mediterranean Regional Index (IMEMR), and the Egyptian Knowledge Bank
(EKB)–indexed journals. Searches combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH) and
free-text terms—such as “trachoma,” “trichiasis,” “trachomatous inflammation,”
“visual impairment,” “blindness,” “prevalence,” “epidemiology,” and “Egypt”—with
Boolean operators. The search strategy considered all published manuscripts,
with no restrictions on language or publication period. Detailed
database-specific search strings appear in Appendix A: Table S1. Finally, we
manually reviewed the bibliographies of included studies to identify any
additional relevant reports.

## Eligibility Criteria and Study Selection Process
Population-based, cross-sectional surveys conducted in Egypt among children and
adults, which reported the prevalence of active trachoma (TF/TI), chronic
trachoma (TT/CO), and associated visual impairment or blindness, were eligible
for inclusion. Studies published from 1996 onwards informed pooled prevalence
estimates, while earlier surveys provided historical context, ie, before the WHO
initiative for the Global Elimination of Trachoma (GET2020) [ ]. The screening process did not adopt
any language restrictions but only considered peer-reviewed publications.
Excluded were non-Egyptian studies, animal or clinic-based case series, reviews,
editorials, and reports lacking original prevalence data, studies without
extractable numerators and denominators, studies with extremely low samples (eg,
<100 participants), and duplicate populations (by author, setting, or time
overlap). In cases of duplication, we retained the most recent or comprehensive
dataset. EndNote X7 imported all search results for deduplication and exported
them to Excel sheets for review and revision. Two independent reviewers (EM and
HM) screened the titles and abstracts of each record against the eligibility
criteria, followed by a full-text assessment of potentially relevant articles. A
third senior reviewer (AMM) validated the data and held meetings to resolve
discrepancies at any stage after discussion and consensus.

## Operational Definitions and Outcome Measures
Active and Chronic Trachoma: Defined Active as TF (Trachomatous
Inflammation – Follicular) or TI (Trachomatous Inflammation – Intense), and
Chronic as TT (Trachomatous Trichiasis) or CO (Corneal Opacity). Calculated
primary outcomes as the prevalence proportions (%) of active trachoma in
children and chronic trachoma in adults, using the WHO simplified grading
criteria [ ]:
TF: ≥ 5 follicles (≥ 0.5 mm) on the upper tarsal conjunctiva
TI: Inflammatory thickening obscuring >50% of the deep tarsal
vessels
Trachomatous Scarring (TS): Conjunctival scarring indicating healed
trachoma (not necessarily a chronic trachoma)
TT: Presence of at least one eyelash rubbing the eyeball or evidence
of recent epilation
CO: Corneal opacity visible over the pupil
Trachoma-Associated Visual Impairment and Blindness: Captured visual impairment
and blindness as secondary outcomes, categorizing vision loss by best-corrected
acuity in the better eye:
Mild–Moderate Impairment: Worse than 6/12 but at least 6/18
Severe Impairment: Worse than 6/18 but at least 3/60
Blindness: Worse than 3/60 or visual field constriction to
<10°
Computed pooled prevalence proportions (%) for any vision impairment (worse than
6/12) and separately for blindness.
Risk-Factor Associations: Expressed associations between trachoma and potential
risk factors as odds ratios (ORs), calculated from 2×2 contingency tables (OR =
ad/bc).

## Data Extraction and Quality Assessment
Data Collection Process and Items: Using Cochrane’s data extraction form and
guidelines, the extracted data involved the following: author(s), year of
publication, study period, and geographic setting (governorate and urban/rural
classification); design features, inclusion criteria, sample size, and response
rate; population demographics (age distribution, sex ratio, education, and
socioeconomic status, when reported); diagnostic criteria and grading system for
trachoma subtypes (TF, TI, TS, TT, CO); numbers of cases and total examined for
each outcome; visual acuity measurements; and any reported confounders or
quality indicators. All fields were verified by a second reviewer, with
discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer.
Risk of Bias Assessment and Evidence Appraisal: Two reviewers applied the Joanna
Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence studies to evaluate each study’s
internal validity across nine domains: appropriate sampling frame, sampling
method, sample size justification, detailed description of participants and
setting, adequate sample coverage, valid diagnostic criteria, standardized
measurement, proper statistical analysis, and management of low response rates
[ ]. Three reviewers independently
scored each item (1 = yes, 0 = no) and resolved discrepancies by consensus;
studies scoring 5/9 or higher (indicating a low risk of bias) qualified for
meta-analysis.
In addition, overall confidence in the pooled estimates followed the Grading of
Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) framework,
rating the evidence from high to very low quality according to the degree of
risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias [ ]. Within the GRADE framework, we
operationalized imprecision as the presence of wide confidence intervals (CI)
around the pooled prevalence estimate (typically spanning more than 15
percentage points) or a small number of contributing studies.

## Data Synthesis
Data Transformation and Pooled Estimates: To estimate the pooled prevalence of
active (TF/TI) and chronic (TT/CO) trachoma in Egypt, we first computed each
study’s standard error (SE) from the original binomial distribution, using the
formula of untransformed proportion (p) and sample size (n) for each study:
SE(p)= √’p(1−p)’/√n. We then transformed individual
proportions using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine method, pooled them with a
random-effects meta-analysis (to accommodate anticipated between-study
variability) [ ], and derived 95% CI in
both OpenMeta[Analyst] (AHRQ/CEBM, Brown University) and Stata 16.0 (StataCorp).
To assess robustness, we repeated the analysis using logit and single arcsine
transformations if any instability or bias arose in the Freeman–Tukey results.
The overall pooled effect (θ) was tested for statistical significance using
Z-tests, and studentized residuals or outlier detection methods (often referred
to as θi) identified individual studies whose effect estimates significantly
deviated from the overall estimate, thereby highlighting potential outliers and
driving heterogeneity.
Age Standardization and Sensitivity Analyses: Recognizing that Egypt’s population
has grown and aged over the study period, we directly age-standardized all
prevalence estimates to the 2020 national population structure, utilizing
demographic data obtained from the United Nations World Population Prospects
2022 report [ ]. This approach ensures
that our primary estimate reflects recent demographic realities. To quantify the
impact of this choice, we conducted sensitivity analyses by re-standardizing all
estimates to the 2000 and 2010 age structures derived from the same UN source
and comparing significant shifts in the overall pooled prevalence.
Assessment of Heterogeneity: Ultimately, Cochran’s Q(χ²) and the derived I²
statistic evaluated statistical heterogeneity: a Cochran’s Q(χ²) p-value <0.1
indicated possible heterogeneity overall, while I² values below 25%, around 50%,
and above 75% corresponded to a low, moderate, and substantial amount of
heterogeneity, respectively. Between-study variance (τ²) quantified the variance
of True effects as an absolute measure, where a value of 0 implies no
variability beyond sampling error. The H² Statistic (Q/df) relatively measures
the ratio of total observed variability to that expected by sampling error
alone, where values of 1 suggest no heterogeneity beyond the sampling error
expectation; values greater than 1 indicate increasing heterogeneity due to
other causes (eg, differences in populations, methods, or environments).
Given the range of settings, periods, and populations surveyed, we prespecified a
random-effects model; a fixed-effects approach was deemed inappropriate for
proportional data drawn from diverse prevalence studies.
Publication-Bias Evaluation: When data from at least 10 studies were available,
we visually evaluated small-study effects using funnel plots (prevalence versus
SE) and analytically with Egger’s regression and Begg’s rank correlation tests
(p <0.05 indicating potential bias) [ , ]. Because
normality-based tests lose reliability when proportions approach the boundaries
of 0% or 100%, even after transformation, we analyzed all bias-test results
cautiously. Considering the reflection of real-world differences, we interpreted
any asymmetry in light of clinical and methodological heterogeneity and Orwin’s
fail‑safe N method to quantify the number of hypothetical “null” studies
required to reduce the overall prevalence to a trivial or clinically unimportant
level—while acknowledging its debated use in prevalence meta-analyses [ ]. However, because it works directly on
the pooled estimate, it remains valid regardless of how extreme the proportions
are, making it a more conservative and transparent check in a context where
transformation cannot fully normalize the data.
Subgroup and Meta-Regression Analyses: To investigate the sources of residual
heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses based on patients’ age, sex, and
geographic region, as well as meta-regressions modeling study-level prevalence
as the outcome (Y-axis) and publication year or/and sample size as predictors
(X-axis). These analyses aimed to determine whether demographic differences,
temporal trends, study scale, or geographic location contributed to the
variability in reported trachoma prevalence.

## Results

## Study Selection
Our search across ten databases identified 1081 records, of which 662 were
duplicates. After screening 419 unique titles and abstracts, excluding 382 that
did not meet the inclusion criteria, we retrieved 37 full-text articles. Of
these, we ruled out 17 (11 conducted outside Egypt, four reviews or editorials,
and two without primary data). Upon full-text review of the remaining 20
articles, we excluded four more—two reporting duplicate populations, one using
outdated diagnostic criteria, and one addressing irrelevant outcomes (see
Appendix A: Table S1 Excluded list). Sixteen studies fulfilled all inclusion
requirements for quantitative synthesis [ , - ] ( a).
 

## Characteristics and Quality of Included Studies
The 16 cross-sectional surveys, conducted between 1984 and 2020, evaluated a
total of 45 934 individuals (range per study: 525–9264) across 13+ Egyptian
governorates—Alexandria, Beheira, Beni-Suef, Fayoum, Gharbia, Ismailia, Menofya,
Minya, New Valley, Qalyubia, Sharqia, Sohag, and Multiple Rural Delta Regions
( ). Study settings encompassed
rural villages, urban communities, primary schools, and outreach campaigns.
Sampling frames varied from population-based household clusters (for example,
the RAAB survey in Sohag, n = 4,033) to school-based screenings
(for example, Zagazig primary schools, n = 808).
 All studies applied the WHO’s simplified trachoma grading system or adopted
similar clinical domains (classifying follicles, inflammation, scarring,
trichiasis, and corneal opacity). Several studies supplemented clinical grading
with laboratory confirmation using monoclonal fluorescent antibody or direct
fluorescent antibody testing. Reported active-trachoma prevalence ranged from
7.9 % among Zagazig schoolchildren to 59% in a rural Northern Delta hamlet.
Chronic sequelae (TS, TT, CO) affected between 1.1% and 8.8% of adult
participants. Where measured, visual impairment (VA < 6/60) and blindness
increased with age, ranging from 5.9% to 9.3% in older cohorts aged 50 years or
older.
Quality assessment with the JBI checklist yielded scores of ≥ 7 out of 9 for all
but one study (which scored 6), reflecting robust sampling strategies,
standardized diagnostic methods, high response rates (> 80% in all but one
survey), and appropriate statistical analyses. Common limitations included
sporadic socioeconomic reporting and infrequent assessment of interobserver
reliability.

## Quantitative Synthesis of Outcomes
Active Trachoma Prevalence in Children (1-9 years): A total of nine
cross-sectional studies (n = 12 200) contributed to estimates of active trachoma
prevalence. Pooling unadjusted proportions via a Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine
random-effects model yielded a prevalence of 24.4% (95% CI 15.6% – 33.1%; I² =
99%, p < 0.01). After direct age-standardization to Egypt’s 2020 population,
the pooled estimate decreased to 10.8% (95% CI 7% – 14.6%; I² = 98%, p <
0.01), demonstrating a substantial demographic influence (See a,b).
 In the rural subgroup (seven studies; n = 10 622), age-standardized active
trachoma prevalence remained at 10.8% (95% CI 6.4% – 15%; I² = 99%, p <
0.01). Geographic stratification within Monufia (two studies; n = 2661) yielded
a pooled prevalence of 17% (95% CI , 14 %–20%; I² = 77%, p = 0.04). A single
study from Fayoum (n = 878) reported 21% (95% CI 19%–24%) (See b,c).
Active Trachoma-Associated Visual Impairment (<15 years): Nine studies (n = 20
194) estimated any visual impairment among active trachoma cases at 12.2%
unadjusted (95% CI 7.2% – 16.8%; I² = 100%, p < 0.01) and 5.3%
age-standardized (95% CI 3.2% – 7.5%; I² = 99%, p < 0.01). In rural settings
(six studies; n = 12 627), the age-standardized prevalence was 5.6% (95% CI 3.2%
– 8.1%; I² = 98 %, p < 0.01). Monufia (two reports; n = 3,222) exhibited 13%
visual impairment (95% CI 12% –15%; I² = 13.27%, p < 0.01), whereas a single
Ismailia survey (n = 5765) found 0.2% (95% CI 0.1% – 0.4 %) (See a-c).
 Chronic Trachoma Prevalence in Adults (≥15 years): Eight studies (n = 15 497)
showed an unadjusted chronic-trachoma prevalence of 11% (95% CI 6.2% – 15.8%; I²
= 99%, p < 0.01), decreasing to 7.2% (95% CI 4% – 10.4%; I² = 99%, p <
0.01) after age-standardization. In rural areas (seven studies; n = 14 849), the
standardized prevalence was 7.9% (95% CI 4% – 10.4%; I² = 99%, p < 0.01).
Single-study estimates included New Valley at 17.4% (95% CI 16.2 % – 18.6%; n =
3780) and Fayoum at 25.3% (95% CI 21.4% – 29.2%; n = 475) (See a-c).
 Chronic Trachoma-Associated Visual Impairment (≥40 years): Eight studies (n = 17
071) reported unadjusted prevalence of visual impairment at 2.6% (95% CI 1.6% –
3.5%; I² = 97%, p < 0.01) and 1.6% age-standardized (95% CI 1%– 2.2%; I² =
96%, p < 0.01). Among rural populations (six studies; n = 9816), the estimate
remained at 2.2% (95% CI 1.3% – 3.1%; I² = 90%, p < 0.01). In Minya (three
studies; n = 7274), pooled impairment was 3.3% (95% CI 1.7% – 5%; I² = 93%, p
< 0.01). Monufia and Beni Suef single-site surveys reported 0.4% (95% CI 0.2
% – 0.6%; n = 3222) and 0.8% (95% CI 0.2 %–1.4%; n = 1223), respectively (See
 a-c).
 Chronic Trachoma Associated Blindness (≥40 years): Five studies (n = 11 050)
indicated an unadjusted blindness prevalence of 1.3% (95% CI 0.4% – 2%; I² =
96%, p < 0.01), which age-standardization reduced to 0.7% (95% CI 0.1 %–1.9%;
I² = 94 %, p < 0.01). Two rural studies (n = 1369) reported 0.7 % (95% CI 0.3
%–1.6 %; I² = 11%, p < 0.01). No significant differences emerged across
governorates (See a,b).
 Historical Prevalence Trends: Six pre-1996 studies (n = 11 037) yielded a pooled
active-trachoma prevalence of 23% (95% CI 18% – 27.4%; I² = 94%, p < 0.01),
underscoring substantial reductions in more recent surveys (See c).
However, the drawn line graph ( d)
reveals a volatile rather than steadily declining pattern in active trachoma
prevalence. Between 2000 and 2005, prevalence peaked before falling sharply by
2010. A subsequent rise followed by another decline by 2020. Urban data after
2001 are sparse, limiting analysis in non-rural areas.
Rural surveys consistently reported higher prevalence and provided more data
points, reflecting trachoma’s strong association with rural poverty, inadequate
sanitation, and limited access to clean water. In contrast, urban estimates show
an early downward trend followed by a single significant spike, suggesting that
intermittent, survey-driven investigations rather than systematic surveillance
drive observed fluctuations in urban settings (See d).

## Sensitivity Analyses
Re-standardizing prevalence estimates to Egypt’s 2000, 2010, and 2020 age
structures produced nearly identical results, indicating minimal demographic
bias:
• Active trachoma prevalence: 10% (95% CI 6%–15%)
across all three standard populations.
• Active trachoma–associated visual impairment: 4.3%
(95% CI 1.6%–8.3%).
• Chronic trachoma prevalence: 5.8% (95% CI
2.0%–11.4%) using 2020 data; 5.7% (95% CI 2.0%–11.1%) for 2010; and 5.4% (95% CI
1.9%–10.6%) for 2000.
• Chronic trachoma–associated visual impairment: 1.6%
(95% CI 0.8%–2.7%) for 2010 and 2020; 1.6% (95% CI 0.8%–2.6%) for 2000.
• Chronic trachoma–associated blindness: 0.5% (95% CI
0.1%–1.3%) for all three standard populations.
These sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent findings: re-standardizing to
the 2000, 2010, or 2020 age structures produced only minor shifts in pooled
estimates of chronic trachoma, yet age-standardized prevalence remained
substantially lower than the crude values. This pattern suggests that
demographic aging, rather than actual changes in trachoma risk, accounts for a
significant portion of the historically observed burden of trachoma.
The influence diagnostics (Zeta tests) confirm that our pooled age‑standardized
prevalence estimate is robust, remaining unchanged when any single study is
omitted (see Appendix A: Figure S1). Nonetheless, we observed consistently high
heterogeneity (I² and τ²), indicating that variability is not attributable to
any one outlier but rather reflects genuine differences in trachoma prevalence
across study populations, geographical settings, and methodological approaches.
Subsequent subgroup analyses and meta‑regression will explore these sources of
heterogeneity in greater depth.

## Subgroup Analyses
Gender did not significantly influence trachoma outcomes, with pooled odds ratios
(male vs female) close to unity and wide CI:
• Active trachoma: OR 1.26 (95% CI 0.80–1.98)
• Active trachoma–associated visual impairment: OR
1.00 (95% CI 0.86–1.18)
• Chronic trachoma: OR 1.00 (95% CI 0.41–2.46)
• Chronic trachoma–associated visual impairment: OR
0.90 (95% CI 0.37–2.19)
• Chronic trachoma–associated blindness: OR 1.00 (95%
CI 0.61–1.66)

## Meta Regression Analyses
Meta-regressions examined whether publication year or study sample size explained
between-study variability in prevalence estimates, using Freeman–Tukey
double–arcsine proportions (except for blindness, which used raw proportions).
None of the moderators reached statistical significance, and omnibus tests
confirmed a lack of model fit for all outcomes (See Appendix A: Table S2).
These findings indicate that neither temporal trends nor study size significantly
influenced reported prevalence or visual impairment rates, suggesting that other
factors (eg, geographic or demographic differences) likely drive the
heterogeneity observed in our pooled analyses.

## Publication Bias
For active trachoma prevalence, the visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed
a symmetrical distribution of effect estimates against their standard errors
( b). Formal testing
corroborated this observation: Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of 1.75
(p = 0.08), and Begg’s rank-correlation (Kendall’s τ = 0.199; p = 0.324) showed
no evidence of small-study effects. Orwin’s fail-safe N indicated that 14
hypothetical studies with a null prevalence would be required to lower the
pooled estimate to a clinically negligible level, further suggesting that
missing null reports would not significantly alter the pooled estimate.
However, for chronic trachoma, fewer than 10 studies were available in each
meta-analysis, precluding a reliable assessment of small-study effects via
funnel–plot–based methods or formal statistical tests.

## Certainty of Evidence
Using GRADE for descriptive prevalence outcomes, we rated certainty as Moderate
for age‑standardized active trachoma prevalence (1–9 yrs), Low for active
trachoma–associated visual impairment (<15 yrs), chronic trachoma prevalence
(≥ 15 yrs), and chronic trachoma–associated visual impairment (≥ 40 yrs). We
downgraded to Very Low certainty for blindness (≥ 40 yrs) due to the small
number of contributing studies (see ).
 

## Discussion

## Summary of Principal Findings
In this comprehensive meta-analysis of 16 population-based cross-sectional
surveys in Egypt (n = 45 934), we estimated that the age-standardized prevalence
of active trachoma (TF/TI) in children aged 1–9 years was 10.8% (95% CI
7.0–14.6%; I² = 98%), and chronic trachoma (TT/CO) in adults ≥ 15 years was 7.2%
(95% CI 4.0–10.4%; I² = 99%). Trachoma-associated visual impairment affected
5.3% of active cases (95% CI 3.2–7.5%; I² = 99%) and 1.6% of adults with chronic
disease (95% CI 1.0–2.2%; I² = 96%), while blindness in those ≥ 40 years was
relatively low at 0.7% (95% CI 0.1–1.9%; I² = 94%). Subgroup analyses revealed
similar prevalence rates across male and female participants, and
meta-regression found that neither the year of publication nor study sample size
explained the remaining between-study variability. Sensitivity checks further
showed that shifts in population demographics, rather than actual changes in
disease risk, primarily drove the historical prevalence patterns.

## Strengths of the Review
Key strengths of our review include adherence to a prespecified
PROSPERO-registered protocol and exhaustive searches across ten databases
without language restrictions, ensuring comprehensive study identification.
Multiple independent screenings and data extractions, together with rigorous JBI
risk-of-bias assessment and GRADE certainty appraisal, minimized subjectivity.
We applied advanced statistical techniques—including the Freeman–Tukey
double-arcsine transformation, random-effects meta-analysis, direct age
standardization to three census years, and comprehensive sensitivity, subgroup,
and meta-regression analyses—to ensure robust and generalizable prevalence
estimates. To our knowledge, this represents the first systematic review and
meta-analysis to generate a pooled, nationally representative prevalence
estimate of trachoma in Egypt.

## Limitations
First, all included reports were descriptive cross-sectional surveys, which
deprived the ability to make causal inferences. Second, there was high
statistical heterogeneity (I² > 90%) across outcomes, reflecting variations
in epidemiological settings, sampling frames, and diagnostic verification,
despite sensitivity and age-standardization analyses substantially reducing
demographic bias. Third, many studies provided limited data on socioeconomic
status, water and sanitation variables, and inter-observer reliability, which
may have introduced residual confounding. Finally, a statistical assessment of
publication bias was appropriate only for active trachoma.

## Comparison with Other Evidence
Our pooled active trachoma prevalence of 10.8% aligns closely with the 2019 WHO
report for the Eastern Mediterranean region (11.3%) [ ] and compares with estimates from large community
surveys in neighboring countries: a multi-center national survey from Sudan with
a total of 12 570 individuals from 3286 households across 116 survey clusters
reported an active prevalence of trachoma among children ages 1–9 years ranging
from 5.6% to 18.1% [ ], while a rural
household survey in Ethiopia found 11.8% prevalence in the same age group [ ]. The decline from historical peaks
mirrors the positive impact of the WHO’s SAFE strategy (surgery, antibiotics,
facial cleanliness, and environmental improvements), as documented in
multi-country evaluations [ , ]. However, our analyses also reveal
volatile temporal patterns—rather than steady declines—likely reflecting
episodic antibiotic campaigns and intermittent surveillance efforts, consistent
with observations in Mali and Niger [ , ].
Meanwhile, Wang et al. estimate the global prevalence of blindness due to
trachomatous corneal opacity in adults aged ≥50 years at 0.0094% (95% CI
0–0.0693%) and moderate-to-severe vision impairment at 0.012% (95% CI 0–0.0761%)
[ ]. Despite its valuable global
perspective, the study estimated trachomatous corneal blindness in Egypt based
only on a single survey from Sohag Governorate—substantially underestimating the
actual national burden, which our age-standardized synthesis shows remains far
higher across multiple governorates. Particularly where environmental and
socioeconomic risk factors—such as overcrowding, limited access to water and
sanitation, and poor hygiene—remain pronounced [ ]. Moreover, global corneal opacity projections derive their
trachomatous component from these limited data, thereby under-representing
Egypt’s burden of trachoma-related vision loss in the Eastern Mediterranean
region, which contradicts the regional WHO estimates [ ]. To accurately inform elimination strategies, national
and regional planning must incorporate comprehensive, age-standardized
prevalence data from diverse settings rather than relying on a
single-governorate survey.

## Historical Trends and Interpretation
Contrary to expectations of a steady decline typical of many infectious disease
elimination programs, the trend lines for active trachoma from 1980 to 2020
exhibit pronounced volatility, marked by sharp peaks and troughs. This pattern
likely reflects both the actual underlying disease burden and the evolving
landscape of public health priorities, fluctuations in geographic survey
coverage, and the intermittent nature of trachoma data collection efforts across
Egypt. The most prominent feature in the historical data is the dramatic surge
in recorded prevalence during the early 2000s, with rural estimates peaking at
over 40% and a notable urban spike above 30%. Rather than indicating a sudden
epidemic, this rise coincides temporally with the global scale-up of trachoma
mapping initiatives under the WHO GET2020 [ ]. During this time, Egypt conducted more extensive epidemiological
surveys—particularly in remote or underserved districts where the trachoma
burden was previously undocumented [ ].
Consequently, the observed peak likely represents broader survey coverage and
enhanced case detection rather than an actual surge in disease incidence.
Following this apex, we observed a steep decline in rural active trachoma
prevalence—falling to below 10% within less than a decade. This trend strongly
suggests the successful deployment of control strategies during that period,
particularly components of the WHO’s SAFE strategy. Evidence from global
trachoma programs has demonstrated that the mass distribution of azithromycin,
combined with targeted hygiene and sanitation campaigns, can yield significant
reductions in transmission within a few years of implementation [ - ]. Egypt’s participation in such efforts likely played a key role
in driving the post-2004 decline.
However, several factors can explain the recent increase in trachoma prevalence
among rural communities: the disease could be re-emerging in investigated areas
where interventions have waned due to resource limitations or declining
community participation. Second, implemented surveys may include districts that
had not yet benefited from mass treatment or hygiene campaigns, thereby
elevating the pooled estimate. Finally, differences in study methodologies—such
as sampling protocols, grader training, and practice settings—could contribute
to apparent variability in prevalence rather than true epidemiological shifts
[ ].
The urban data were relatively limited, showing a decline in reporting after
2003, which suggests that surveillance may have been less of a priority in urban
areas due to a lower disease burden. However, the available data consistently
display a lower prevalence of active trachoma. This difference is likely due to
improved access to water supply, sanitation, and healthcare infrastructure in
urban environments. Still, the spike recorded around 2003 in one urban survey
reminds us that assumptions about urban immunity to neglected tropical diseases
(NTDs) should be approached cautiously, especially in peri-urban slums or areas
with high levels of informal settlement [ ].
These trends underscore the importance of continuous surveillance and periodic
reassessment to address trachoma re-emergence, as well as targeted interventions
in areas of persistent transmission. These historical patterns serve as a public
health lesson that initial progress in disease control, no matter how promising,
can be reversed by later complacency, emerging health system constraints, or a
lack of community engagement. Future success will depend not only on maintaining
the gains achieved through SAFE but also on addressing upstream social
determinants—such as poverty, overcrowding, and inadequate water supply and
sanitation—that perpetuate trachoma transmission.

## Clinical and Policy Implications
Trachoma persists as one of the WHO’s 20 NTDs, striking the poorest and most
isolated populations. Chlamydia trachomatis repeatedly infects
the conjunctiva and spreads directly between individuals via contaminated
fingers, fomites (eg, towels), and eye-seeking flies (Musca
sorbens) that breed in human feces and transfer infected ocular or
nasal secretions [ ]. Environmental and
socioeconomic conditions—such as overcrowded housing and inadequate water,
sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure—facilitate ongoing transmission
and reinfection cycles [ ].
WHO defines elimination as a sustained reduction in TF to < 5% among 1–9
year-olds children and TT “unknown to the health system” to < 0.2% among
adults ≥ 15 years—each maintained for at least two years without mass antibiotic
treatment—combined with an established case-finding and management system with
secured financing [ ].
To achieve these targets, the SAFE strategy provides a holistic framework for
interrupting transmission, curing infection, and addressing sequelae [ , ]. Despite considerable progress, our pooled age-standardized TF
prevalence of 10.8% in children and TT prevalence of 7.2% in adults indicate
that Egypt remains well above elimination thresholds. This ongoing burden
underscores the critical importance of bolstering the SAFE strategy—particularly
by ensuring long-term improvements in WASH infrastructure, engaging communities
to prevent reinfection, and expanding surgical capacity to address existing
trachomatous trichiasis. Only through such comprehensive, multi-sectoral action
can Egypt align with the WHO’s 2021–2030 roadmap for NTDs and make decisive
progress toward the elimination of trachoma as a public health problem.
Although Egypt has made impressive strides toward trachoma elimination, the
persistence of significant active disease in specific governorates highlights
the need for targeted programmatic action. Age-standardized prevalence estimates
provide policymakers with precise metrics to allocate resources efficiently and
prioritize high-burden districts for enhanced antibiotic distribution and
surgical outreach [ , ]. The low but non-negligible rates of
trachomatous trichiasis and corneal opacity underscore the importance of
maintaining surgical capacity and integrating trachoma screening into broader
primary eye health services. Furthermore, our findings support ongoing
interventions for facial cleanliness and environmental improvement, which remain
pivotal to sustained disease control [ ].

## Unanswered Questions and Future Research
Important gaps remain in understanding the particular socio-environmental drivers
of trachoma in Egypt. Future work should systematically collect data on
water–sanitation–hygiene indicators, socioeconomic status, and interobserver
grading reliability to clarify residual heterogeneity. Longitudinal cohort
studies are necessary to evaluate the incidence, dynamics of reinfection, and
long-term effects of SAFE interventions over multiple decades. Moreover,
geospatial analyses combining high-resolution prevalence data with environmental
covariates would help pinpoint transmission hotspots and optimize intervention
coverage. Finally, randomized controlled trials assessing intensified antibiotic
distribution methods and community-driven sanitation initiatives would generate
robust evidence to inform the subsequent phase of trachoma elimination
efforts.

## Conclusions
Our meta-analysis demonstrates that, after age standardization, both active and
chronic trachoma remain endemic in parts of Egypt, with appreciable burdens of
visual impairment among affected individuals. While the SAFE strategy has driven
significant declines, our findings underscore the need for sustained, targeted
interventions—particularly in rural and high-prevalence districts—to achieve the
WHO’s elimination thresholds. Harmonized survey designs, standardized diagnostic
protocols, and comprehensive socio-environmental data collection will be essential
for tracking progress and ultimately eliminating trachoma as a public health problem
in Egypt and beyond.
