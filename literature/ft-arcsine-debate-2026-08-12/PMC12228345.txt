## Objectives
This study aimed to synthesize existing evidence to compare the immunogenicity and safety of domestic and imported live-attenuated varicella vaccine (VarV) in healthy Chinese populations.

## Methods
We searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, China National Knowledge Internet (CNKI), Wan Fang Database, and Chinese Biomedical Literature Service System (SinoMed) using predefined search terms to identify relevant studies. Retrieve all language articles up to March 15, 2024. Articles reported varicella vaccination in healthy Chinese populations were included. We calculated the pooled rates of seroconversions and adverse events using the random effects model and assessed the quality of each study using the modified Jadad Scale and Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS). Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test.

## Results
In our immunogenicity analysis, which included 16,655 Chinese individuals from 21 studies, the pooled seroconversion rate was 89% (95%CI: 86-91%) for domestic VarV and 93% (95%CI: 88-98%) for imported Varv, with no statistically significant difference. In our safety analysis, which included 29,696 Chinese individuals from 25 studies, the pooled rate of systemic reactions was higher for domestic Varv (11%, 95%CI: 10-13%) than for imported Varv (8%, 95%CI: 6-10%; P < 0.001), while the results for local reactions were the opposite (domestic Varv: 3%, 95%CI: 2-3%; imported Varv: 7%, 95%CI: 3-10%; P = 0.020). The results are based on pooled proportions rather than direct comparison. Egger’s test suggested that publication bias was not negligible.

## Conclusions
Both domestic and imported varicella vaccines appear to be generally immunogenic and safe in healthy Chinese populations. However, due to limited and heterogeneous data on imported vaccines, further high-quality studies are needed to validate these comparative findings.

## Background
Varicella (chickenpox) is an infectious disease caused by the varicella zoster virus (VZV), which affects children, adolescents, and adults [ ]. Varicella is highly contagious and is transmitted through direct contact with vesicular skin lesions that contain high titers of infectious virus and respiratory droplets [ ]. It is prevalent globally and carries a substantial disease burden. The annual global burden of varicella is estimated to be around 140 million cases, with 4.2 million severe complications requiring hospitalisation, and 4,200 deaths [ ]. Primary healthcare visits for varicella accounted for 30-85% of total direct costs; hospitalization costs ranged from $1,308 to $38,268 per episode, contributing between 2% and 60% to total direct costs [ ]. Recently, the incidence of varicella has increased significantly in China. The reported incidence of varicella increased from 3.17/100,000 (41,211 cases) to 70.14/100,000 (979,482 cases) during the period from 2005 to 2019 [ , ]. A total of 10,446 varicella public health emergency events were reported in China from 2007 to 2021, with annual reports ranging from 261 (in 2012) to 1,327 (in 2018) [ ].
Vaccination is the most effective approach to prevent varicella. In the United States and Japan, since the implementation of the varicella vaccination programs, the incidence of varicella has markedly decreased [ ], as have the hospitalization and mortality rates [ , ]. Currently, the varicella vaccine has not been included in China’s national immunization program [ ]. The average vaccination rate for the varicella vaccine in China is 61.1% (95%CI: 55.7-66.5%) [ ], which is significantly lower than that in countries such as Spain, Italy, and Germany, which have incorporated the varicella vaccine into their national or regional immunization schedules [ , ]. In addition, the positive rate of VZV IgG antibody in the healthy population in China was relatively low (64%, 95%CI: 60-67%) [ ].
Globally, several formulations of varicella vaccines are available; all contain live attenuated VZV. Except for Korean-licensed vaccine, all are based on the Oka VZV strain from Japan [ ]. Imported live-attenuated varicella vaccine (VarV) began to be introduced into China in 1997. After 2001, some domestic VarV were developed. In VarV use, there are concerns about the safety and immunogenicity of domestic VarV among managers, doctors, vaccine recipients, and parents. Also, the higher cost of imported varicella vaccine than domestic ones limits VarV’s widespread adoption and promotion in China.
Therefore, this study used systematic review and meta-analysis to analyze published data on domestic Varv and imported VarV in Chinese populations in recent years. It aimed to compare their immunogenicity and safety, providing a reference for VarV’s further application in China.

## Methods
We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis using PRISMA guidelines [ ]. The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/; identifier CRD42024546352).

## Search strategy
We searched articles in all languages up to March 15, 2024, from PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, China National Knowledge Internet (CNKI), Wan Fang Database, and Chinese Biomedical Literature Service System (SinoMed). PubMed and Web of Science are different platforms for accessing the same MEDLINE database. Depending on the database used, the following terms were used in different combinations and structures. The search terms we used included “varicella zoster virus infection”, “varicella”, “chickenpox”, “immunization”, “vaccination”, “ chickenpox vaccine”, “vaccines”, “vaccine”, “safety”, “security”, “adverse effect”, “adverse reaction”, “side effect”, “side-effect”, “side reaction”, “immunogenicity”, “efficacy”, “potency”, “effectiveness”, and “effect”. When searching for English articles, we used “China” or “Chinese” to identify articles providing immunogenicity and safety data for the Chinese populations. Details information on the complete search strategy can be found in Supplementary file 1.

## Study selection
Two reviewers independently applied the inclusion criteria to assess eligibility, with discrepancies between reviewers resolved by other authors. Articles meeting the following criteria were included: (1) studies involving healthy Chinese populations; (2) randomized controlled trials and observational studies on the safety or immunogenicity of varicella vaccine; (3) studies providing key information, such as sample size, number of vaccines administered, definition of seroconversion after vaccination, vaccine manufacturer, or the number of systemic or local reactions; (4) publication types of journal articles or conference papers. We excluded: (1) critical or review articles; (2) articles without full text availability, (3) cost-effectiveness analyses and non-human studies; (4) studies with a sample size of less than 10 participants; (5) clinical trials and studies on post-exposure prophylaxis; (6) studies involving participants with known autoimmune diseases or a history of immunodeficiency and other conditions that do not represent healthy status.

## Data extraction
Two reviewers independently extracted the data from each other, and all disagreements were resolved by consensus. The extracted information included: first author, year of publication, study design, study time period, inclusion and exclusion criteria, age, vaccine dose, sample size, mean or median age at vaccination, proportion of female, vaccine manufacturer, interval between vaccination and serum test, definition of seroconversion, number of serum samples, number of seroconversion cases in all populations, number of susceptible populations (with a preimmunization antibody titer of less than 1:4 or 1:8), number of seroconversion cases in susceptible populations, number of non-susceptible populations (with a preimmunization antibody titer of ≥ 1:4 or ≥ 1:8), number of seroconversion cases in non-susceptible populations, duration of safety observation, number of systemic reactions and local reactions. The number of cases with systemic reactions such as fever and headache, as well as local reactions such as redness and swelling, were extracted in addition to safety data.The definitions of seroconversion, serological assay methods and classifications of adverse reactions of each study are detailed in Supplementary file 2.

## Quality assessment
Two reviewers independently evaluated the quality of randomized controlled trials using the modified Jadad Scale [ ], which adds randomization hiding to the original scale [ ]. This scale assesses random sequence generation, randomization hiding, blind method and quitting and shedding, with scores from 0 to 7. For cohort studies, as unvaccinated control groups are not required was not necessary, we excluded the non-exposed cohort selection and comparability from the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), focusing on selection criteria and outcome assessment. The quality score ranges from 0 to 6 based on quality criteria achievement [ ]. See Supplementary file 3 for the rating criteria of both scales. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus or by consulting a third member of the team.

## Statistical analysis
Meta-analysis was used to estimate the pooled rates of seroconversion and adverse reactions. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for binomial proportions were calculated by inverting the equaltailed test based on the binomial distribution, and combined with the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation of the rates [ ]. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistics, with values greater than 50% indicating substantial inconsistency. When heterogeneity exceeded 50%, a random effects model was employed for pooled estimates; otherwise a fixed effects model was used. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the sources of heterogeneity in the studies. Analyses were stratified by age, publication year, vaccine dose, manufacturer, and quality score. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially removing each study to compare whether there were significantly changes in the overall effect size before and after removal. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test [ ]. We also used the trim-and-fill method to assess potentially missing studies. This meta-analysis procedures were completed using Stata 15.1 software. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Evidence quality rating
We evaluated the findings using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria [ ]. Two reviewers rated the quality of the evidence independently, and any disagreements were resolved by consulting a third member of the team. The quality evidence is categorized into four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low, indicating the degree of confidence in the estimated. Evidence from randomized trials is considered high-quality, while that from observational studies is regarded as low-quality. Five factors can decrease the quality of evidence: limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Three factors can increase it: large effect, plausible residual confounding, and dose-response gradi.

## Results

## Included studies
Our search strategy retrieved 1,553 articles (Fig.  ). After removing duplicates, we screened 952 titles and abstracts, and reviewed the full texts of 142 studies. Finally, 34 articles met the inclusion and exclusion criteria: 21 on immunogenicity and 25 on safety. Table  summarizes the characteristics of the selected studies. Among the 21 immunogenicity studies, 11 were randomized controlled trials and 10 were cohort studies. Eighteen studies assessed the immunogenicity of 1 dose of Varv, and 7 studies assessed the immunogenicity of 2 doses of Varv. Five articles evaluated the immunogenicity of imported Varv. Among the 25 safety studies, 11 were randomized controlled trials and 14 were cohort studies. Twenty-three studies assessed the safety of 1 dose of Varv; 5 studies assessed the safety of 2 doses of Varv. Three articles evaluated the safety of imported Varv.

 

 

## Immunogenicity of domestic Varv and imported Varv
According to the random effects model, the pooled seroconversion rate after Varv vaccination in all populations was 89% (95%CI: 87-92%). High heterogeneity among studies was observed (I2 = 97.03%, P < 0.001), likely due to differences in study populations, serologic methods, vaccine types, and study designs. The pooled seroconversion rate was 89% (95%CI: 86-91%) of domestic Varv and 93% (95%CI: 88-98%) for imported Varv (Fig.  ). No significant difference was found in seroconversion rates between domestic and imported Varv (P = 0.160).

 

In susceptible populations, the pooled seroconversion rate for domestic Varv was 96% (95%CI: 95-97%). For imported VarV, the pooled seroconversion rate was 93% (95%CI: 87-100%) (Fig.  ). In non-susceptible populations, the pooled seroconversion ratewas 80% (93%CI: 75-86%) for domestic Varv and 81% (93%CI: 67-96%) for imported Varv (Fig.  ). No significant differences in seroconversion rates were observed between domestic and imported Varv in both susceptible (P = 0.440) and non-susceptible populations (P = 0.900).

 

## Safety of domestic Varv and imported Varv
According to the random effects model, the pooled rate of systemic reactions was 11% (95%CI: 10-13%), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 98.49%, P < 0.001). The heterogeneity likely stemmed from differences in study populations, serologic methods, vaccine types, and study designs. The pooled rate of systemic reactions was 11% (95%CI: 10-13%) for domestic Varv and 8% (95%CI: 6-10%) for imported Varv (Fig.  ). Domestic Varv had a higher rate of systemic reactions than imported Varv (P < 0.001). For domestic VarV, the pooled rates of fever and headache reactions were 9% (95%CI: 8-10%) and 1% (95%CI: 1-1%), respectively (Supplementary file 4: Fig. S1; Fig. S2). For imported VarV, the pooled rates of fever and headache reactions were 6% (95%CI: 5-8%) and 0% (95%CI: 0-1%), respectively (Supplementary file 4: Fig. S1; Fig. S2).

 

The pooled rate of local reactions was 3% (95%CI: 2-3%), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 94.32%, P < 0.001). The heterogeneity likely stemmed from differences in study populations, serologic methods, vaccine types, and study designs. The pooled rate of local reactions was 3% (95%CI: 2-3%) for domestic Varv and 7% (95%CI: 3-10%) for imported Varv (Fig.  ). Domestic Varv had a lower rate of local reactions than imported Varv (P = 0.020). The pooled rate of redness and swelling reactions for domestic Varv was 1% (95%CI: 1-1%) and 1% (95%CI: 0-1%), respectively. The pooled rate of redness and swelling reactions for imported Varv was 1% (95%CI: 0-2%) and 1%, respectively (95%CI: 0-1%) (Supplementary file 4: Fig. S3; Fig. S4).

## Quality assessment
Among the 34 included studies, the quality scores of the 15 randomized controlled trials ranged from 2 to 7, with 5 studies rated low (< 4 points) and 10 studies rated high (≥ 4 points). The quality scores of the 19 cohort studies ranged from 2 to 6, among which 4 studies were rated low (< 4 points) and 15 studies were rated high (≥ 4 points). See Supplementary file 3 for details.

## Subgroup analyses
The results revealed significant heterogeneity among the selected studies. To identify potential factors contributing to this heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses. The detail information is shown in Table  . The immunogenicity subgroup analyses showed the seroconversion rate was lower in studies conducted after 2015 (86%, 95%CI: 82-89%) compared with studies conducted before 2015 (95%, 95%CI: 93-97%). When stratified by vaccine manufacturer, the pooled seroconversion rate in studies on vaccines from Changchun BCHT Biotechnology Co., Ltd. was higher (95%, 95%CI: 93-98%); the pooled seroconversion rate in studies on vaccines from Changchun Qijian Biological Products Co., Ltd. was lower (75%, 95%CI: 70-80%). When stratified by study quality, the seroconversion rate was lower in high-quality studies (87%, 95%CI: 84-90%) than in low-quality studies (95%, 95%CI: 92-97%).

 

For the subgroup analyses of systemic reactions, the rate of systemic reaction in studies conducted after 2015 (14%, 95%CI: 11-17%) was higher than in studies conducted before 2015 (9%, 95%CI: 7-11%). The pooled rate of systemic reactions was higher in studies on vaccines from Shanghai Institute of Biological Products (21%, 95%CI: 10-32%); the pooled rate of systemic reactions was lower studies on vaccines from Changchun BCHT Biotechnology (8%, 95%CI: 5-10%). High-quality studies had a lower rate of systemic reactions (9%, 95%CI: 8-11%) compared with low-quality studies (17%, 95%CI: 11-23%). For the subgroup analyses of local reactions, the pooled rate of local reactions was higher in studies on two-dose vaccines. High-quality studies had a lower rate of local reactions.

## Sensitivity analyses and publication bias
Sensitivity analyses indicated that omitting any single studies did not significantly alter the overall estimates, suggesting our analysis was stable (Supplementary file 5). Egger’s test suggested that publication bias was not negligible based on immunogenicity (P < 0.001) and safety (P = 0.011; P = 0.030). The trim-and-fill method showed that the pooled seroconversion rate after Varv vaccination in all populations was 91% (95%CI: 89-93%). The pooled seroconversion rates were 97% (95% CI: 96-98%) in susceptible populations and 85% (95% CI: 82-88%) in non-susceptible populations. The pooled rates of systemic and local reactions were 8% (95%CI: 6-11%) and 3% (95%CI: 2-4%), respectively.

## Quality of evidence assessment in GRADE method
Immunogenicity: the number of included randomized controlled trial exceeded that of cohort studies, with an initial evidence quality rating of “high”. Limitations were not rated down due to the absence of selection bias in the study populations. Inconsistency was rated down because of substantial statistical heterogeneity. Indirectness was not rated down, as the populations, interventions, comparisons and outcomes of the studies were basically consistent. Imprecision was not rated down because our studies had relatively adequate sample sizes, moderate CIs, and did not cross the invalid line. Publication bias was rated down due to evidence of publication bias indicated by Egger’s regression test. The evidence for immunogenicity was rated down on two criteria, and reviewers unanimously agreed not to rate it up further. Overall, the evidence quality for immunogenicity was rated as “low”.
Safety: the number of included cohort studies was greater than that of randomized controlled trials, with an initial evidence quality rating of “low”. Inconsistency was rated down due to substantial statistical heterogeneity. Publication bias was rated down by one category as the Egger’s regression test indicated an evidence of publication bias. Overall, the evidence quality for safety was rated as “very low”.

## Discussion
In this study, we analyzed the immunogenicity of domestic and imported Varv in healthy Chinese populations. Our analysis included 16,655 Chinese individuals from 21 studies. Both vaccines showed highly immunogenic. The seroconversion rate for domestic Varv (89%) was slightly lower than that for imported Varv (93%), with no statistically significant difference. Our seroconversion rates were slightly lower than those in previous studies of healthy individuals receiving Varv (≥ 95%) [ , ] but higher than the 88% rate in pediatric solid-organ transplant recipients [ ]. This discrepancy might be due to differences in serological assays. Most of the studies included in our analysis used fluorescent antibody to membrane antigen test for detecting antibodies in serum samples. In contrast, enzyme immunoassay and enzyme linked immunosorbent assay used in previous studies may lack sufficient sensitivity for detecting post-vaccination antibodies (generally ranging between 74% and 99%, depending on the assay used), which can lead to misclassification bias [ ]. When interpreting seroconversion results, variations in serological test sensitivity and specificity, as well as differences in study populations should be considered.
When we further classified domestic vaccine manufacturers, we found immunogenicity differences among them. There was significant heterogeneity in studies for each manufacturer, so results need cautious interpretation. Additionally, all imported vaccines in this study were produced by GlaxoSmithKline Biologics in Belgium. There is a lack of research on other imported vaccines, like those from Merck in the United States. Future studies can further explore the immunogenicity differences between domestic and imported Varv.
In our safety analysis involving 29,696 Chinese individuals from 25 studies, domestic Varv showed higher systemic reaction rates but lower local reaction rates than imported Varv. Subgroup analyses by manufacturers showed consistent results. Our findings on local reactions are also consistent with previous studies [ , ]. These findings suggest potential safety differences between domestic and imported Varv in Chinese populations. Further studies are needed to identify the root causes of these differences and to ensure the safety of Varv vaccines for all individuals.
In short, both domestic and imported varicella vaccines are immunogenic and safe in healthy Chinese populations. However, given the considerable heterogeneity observed across studies, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive. Domestic vaccines, being less expensive, may have limitations in performance. Imported vaccines, being more costly and requiring stricter cold-chain transportation, might face challenges in large-scale implementation. Overall, each type of vaccine has its own advantages and limitations, and public health departments should consider these factors when promoting varicella vaccination in China.
Our study showed that individuals aged 13 years old or younger had lower systemic reaction rates compared to those over 13. This finding suggests that younger individuals are at a lower risk of experiencing systemic reactions following varicella vaccination than older individuals. This is consistent with previous findings in people living with HIV [ ]. Fewer studies have focused on the use of varicella vaccines in adults compared to children, highlighting an important gap in current research. While the varicella vaccine is commonly administered to children as part of routine immunization programs, there is relatively limited data on the vaccine’s safety and immunogenicity in adults.
Future research is indeed needed to evaluate the safety and immunogenicity of varicella vaccination in adults. This can inform public health policies on vaccine use in adults, especially those at high risk of varicella complications. Additionally, further studies can offer valuable insights into the potential benefits of of adult varicella vaccinating adults, such as reducing the overall burden of varicella-related diseases and complications in the population [ ].
We also found that the local reaction rate was higher after two doses of Varv compared to one dose. Similar results were observed in previous meta-analysis, where a stronge local reaction was seen after the second dose of varicella vaccine [ ]. Local reactions are indeed the most common type of reactions after vaccination, usually involving redness and swelling at the injection site, which are typically mild and self-limited [ ]. The higher local reaction rate observed after the second dose of varicella vaccine could be due to several factors, including a increased immune response during the second administration or individual variability in reactions. Although these reactions are generally not a cause for concern, healthcare providers should be aware of this finding and conduct appropriate monitoring of patients after vaccination. Previous studies have also found the rate of systemic reactions, such as fever or headache, is lower after the second dose compared to the first [ , ]. This study also found similar findings, but without statistical significant.
These findings may support the ongoing discussions regarding the inclusion of varicella vaccines into China’s national immunization program. However, further evaluation of cost-effectiveness and real-world impact of both domestic and imported VarV is warranted. Additional real-world studies — particularly evaluating imported vaccine brands beyond Glaxosmithkline — are needed to inform large-scale policy decisions.
Several limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, the number of studies assessing imported VarV was limited and restricted to one manufacturer. The absence of data on other internationally available varicella vaccines (e.g., Merck) limits our ability to generalize these findings. Second, there was substantial heterogeneity in the results of immunogenicity and safety, indicating significant differences among the included studies. Heterogeneity remained high even after our subgroup analyses. This imbalance in evidence volume should be considered when interpreting safety and immunogenicity comparisons. However, sensitivity analyses results showed that omitting any single study did not significantly alter the overall estimate, indicating that the values for immunogenicity and safety were reliable. Finally, we conducted a subgroup analysis of the outcomes of single-dose and two-dose vaccinations, but our main results did not distinguish between incomplete and complete vaccination.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, both domestic and imported varicella vaccines had high immunogenicity and safe in healthy Chinese populations. However, due to limited and heterogeneous data on imported vaccines, further high-quality studies are needed to validate these comparative findings. Domestic Varv had a slightly higher rate of systemic reactions, while imported Varv had a higher rate of local reactions. These differences should be interpreted with caution given variability in study design and reporting.
