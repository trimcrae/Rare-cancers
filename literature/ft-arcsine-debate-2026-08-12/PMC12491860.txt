Sport and Health Research Center (IMUDs), Parque Tecnológico de la Salud, Granada 18007, Spain HNSCC can occur in the oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx or hypopharynx; furthermore, HNSCC can be influenced by various risk factors depending on the location of the cancer. , The most important risk factors identified to date are alcohol consumption, tobacco usage, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, male sex, and older age. HNC is the seventh most common type of cancer, and its incidence rate varies across regions. More than 600 000 new cancer cases are estimated to occur worldwide, and the 5-years survival rate of HNSCC is only 40%-50%. Cancer survivorship is described as a focus on the health and life of a person with cancer between the end of treatment and the end of life. Latest advances in HNC diagnosis and treatment have increased patient survival rates. Consequently, this drew attention in measuring patient-related outcomes in the survival period due to the sequelae caused by the cancer treatment. , 
Treatment approaches for HNC mainly include surgery, radiotherapy (RT), chemotherapy, immunotherapy or combination approaches. However, despite the benefits that can be obtained with medical treatment, multiple side effects tend to occur after treatment, including difficulties with swallowing, breathing, eating and speaking. , Furthermore, oral mucositis, fatigue, facial disfiguration and oral tissue damage can be caused by RT combined with chemotherapy and/or surgery, leading to a disruption in activities of daily living. Such disruptions in activities of daily living are associated with psychological alterations and a decrease in quality of life. On the other hand, patients with HNC reported a high prevalence of post-treatment pain (up to 70%) ; this prevalence remains at 20%-30% at 2 to 5 years postdiagnosis. Among this group of patients, one-third reported worse pain after treatment than before treatment. On top of that, HNC patients often experience greater rates of pain compared to other cancers. , The complex neural innervation in the head and neck area may explain the high level of sensitivity that characterizes this region. 
HNC-related pain may appear throughout different clinical features, or phenotypes, not only involving the craniofacial but also the cervicogenic regions. Besides, jaw bone pain, myofascial orofacial pain or trigeminal neuralgia are common symptoms among this population. , Cancer-related pain can be categorized as nociceptive pain, which is pain that arises from actual or threatened damage to nonneural tissue and occurs due to the activation of nociceptors, neuropathic pain, which is caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system or nociplastic pain, which arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage causing the activation of peripheral nociceptors or evidence for disease or lesion of the somatosensory system causing the pain. The diverse and complex clinical profile may result in HNC patients presenting with more than one pain phenotype. 
HNC patients might be more susceptible to persistent pain, which is defined as pain that persists or recurs for longer than 3 months, persisting beyond the healing process and in the absence of other criteria. Furthermore, persistent pain is associated with biological factors as well as an increased risk of worsening psychological symptoms and pain-related disability. , Recent findings have suggested that chronic mucositis is a risk factor for persistent pain, as it is accompanied by mucosal swelling, erythema, and atrophy. The multifactorial etiology of cancer-related pain is distinctly apparent in HNC patients , ; this pain may occur due to the cancer treatment, or it may occur due to non-cancer-related aspects. Additionally, widespread pain hypersensitivity and hyperalgesia symptoms have been detected in HNC survivors with persistent pain, which may suggest an altered pain processing mechanism, leading to a decrease in pain pressure thresholds. , 
Although persistent pain is present in HNC patients, they do not often receive therapeutic approaches tailored to their needs. This could be attributed to the fact that each pain mechanism has its own underlying pathophysiology and evidence-based treatment. Hence, there is currently a growing need for data on the prevalence and characteristics of persistent pain across various locations among HNC patients who have completed curative treatment. The HNC cancer locations that are most susceptible to persistent pain remain unclear. To our knowledge, there are no updated systematic reviews examining the prevalence of persistent pain among HNC survivors. Thus, the objective of this systematic review was to identify the prevalence rates of persistent pain, the characteristics of the pain, the pain measurement methods, the timing of pain assessments and the mechanisms underlying persistent pain among HNC survivors.

## Methods
This systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. It was also registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews on 21 December 2023 (PROSPERO reference CRD42024494926).

## Search strategy
The MEDLINE via PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, Ovid and Cochrane Library databases were searched on December 14, 2023. There were no restrictions regarding the publication date. The search strategy was tailored to each database. displays all keywords used in the search strategy. The detailed search strategies are provided in .
 

## Study selection
The exclusion criteria were as follows: studies with heterogeneous or mixed samples (eg, cancer and noncancer participants; HNC along with another type of cancer) that included participants who differed from our target population without subgroup differentiation. If participants with and without persistent pain were mixed in the same group or persistent pain prevalence data could not be obtained or calculated, the studies were excluded. outlines the inclusion and exclusion criteria.
 Two reviewers (M.L.L. and M.A.F.G.) performed the database searches. One reviewer (M.A.F.G.) eliminated duplicates using a digital data extraction platform (Rayyan). Two reviewers (C.F.L. and M.A.F.G.) independently screened the titles, abstracts and full texts of the potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved by consulting one reviewer (M.L.L.) for a discussion and to reach a consensus.

## Data extraction
The following data were extracted from the included studies: first author, year of publication, study design, continent, sex, mean age, sample size, cancer location, cancer stage, type of cancer treatment, pain measurement method, timing of pain assessment, persistent pain prevalence, definition of type of pain, and pain severity. The main outcome was the prevalence of persistent pain in HNC survivors who completed curative cancer treatment at least 3 months ago.

## Methodological quality
The quality of the included studies was assessed by three authors (M.A.F.G., C.F.L., and M.L.L.) using the critical appraisal tool for prevalence studies developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). The reviewers were previously trained to assess any possible risk of bias. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with the third reviewer (M.L.L.). The JBI tool contains 9 evaluation items, and the response options include “yes,” “no,” or “unclear.” Scores were summed across all items to obtain an overall score of quality. Total scores of 0 to 3, 4 to 6, and 7 to 9 indicated a low, moderate, and high risk of bias, respectively. 

## Data synthesis
Statistical heterogeneity was assessed before performing the meta-analysis using the τ2, I2, and Cochran’s Q statistics to evaluate between-study variance. Additionally, a visual inspection of forest plots was conducted to identify potential patterns or sources of heterogeneity. Given the consistently high levels of heterogeneity observed across the included studies (I2 > 88% for most outcomes), we used a random-effects model for data synthesis, following the DerSimonian and Laird method. , 
To address the bounded nature of prevalence proportions (values between 0 and 1) and stabilize variances, we applied the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation prior to pooling the data. The back-transformed pooled prevalence rates and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for interpretability.

## Handling multiple prevalence rates within studies
For studies that reported multiple prevalence rates at different follow-up times, we selected the highest rate of persistent pain prevalence for analysis. The decision to use the highest prevalence rate reported in each study is consistent with previous meta-analyses in similar fields. , This approach provides a conservative estimate, highlighting the maximum burden of persistent pain in the population studied.

## Assessment of small-study effects and publication bias
Small-study effects and potential publication bias were evaluated using Egger’s test and by visually inspecting funnel plots. In cases where asymmetry was detected, we performed a trim-and-fill analysis to estimate adjusted pooled prevalence rates.

## Sources of heterogeneity
To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, univariate meta-regression analyses were performed. Variables considered included: cancer location (eg, oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx, or other sites), cancer treatment (eg, surgery, RT, chemotherapy and immunotherapy), pain measurement method (eg, interviews, Numeric Rating Scales [NRS], or other validated questionnaires), timing of pain assessment (eg, 1, 2, or 5 years post-diagnosis).
In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted to compare persistent pain prevalence across these factors. The significance of the meta-regression results was evaluated using P values and R2 statistics, providing insight into the proportion of heterogeneity explained by each factor.

## Sensitivity analyses
We performed sensitivity analyses by sequentially excluding studies with small sample sizes to assess the stability of the pooled prevalence estimates. The overall estimate remained stable, indicating the robustness of the results, although heterogeneity levels (I2 and τ2) showed no substantial reduction.

## Software and reproducibility
All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 for MAC. Scripts and output files are available upon request to ensure reproducibility.

## Study characteristics
A total of 23 014 participants were included in this systematic review. The prevalence rates of persistent pain in the included studies ranged from 3.65% to 78.8%, with an average prevalence of 31%. Only 1 study reported more than 1 persistent pain prevalence data at different follow-up times. The majority of the included records (88.23%) were cohort studies, , , , and the remaining articles , were cross-sectional studies. The median age was 59.49 years (range 49.6 to 65.2 years), and the median sample size was 1353.76 (range 43 to 7803). The most commonly affected cancer locations were the oral cavity and oropharynx (64.70%). One study included only survivors of advanced stage III/IV disease. The remaining studies that provided cancer stage data included patients with disease stages ranging from 0 to IV. , , , The NRS was the most frequently used tool to measure persistent pain (17.65%). , , The follow-up periods ranged from 3 months to 6 years after the last cancer treatment. An overview of the included studies is presented in .
Diverse locations of persistent pain were reported when pain symptoms were assessed. The most common types of persistent pain were oral pain (n = 4; 23.53%), , , , neck pain (n = 4; 23.53%), , , , facial pain (n = 3; 17.65%), , , head pain (n = 2; 11.76%), , mandibular pain (n = 2; 11.76%), , shoulder pain (n = 2; 11.76%), , and throat pain (n = 2; 11.76%). , 
Although the NRS was the most commonly used pain assessment tool (n = 3; 17.65%), , , multiple other tools were reported, including the University of Washington Quality of Life Questionnaire (UW-QOL; n = 1, 5.88%), a seven-point Likert-type scale to measure pain intensity (n = 1, 5.88%), the University of California San Francisco (UCSF) Oral Cancer Pain Questionnaire (n = 1, 5.88%), the Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (n = 1, 5.88%), the Head and Neck Lymphedema and Fibrosis Symptom Inventory (n = 1, 5.88%), the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) and head and neck module (QLQ-H&N35; n = 1, 5.88%), the Functional Living Index-Cancer (FLIC) (n = 1, 5.88%), the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS; n = 1, 5.88%), and the visual analog scale (VAS; n = 1, 5.88%). Furthermore, many studies assessed persistent pain via interviews (n = 6; 35.29%). , , , 
Concerning the evaluation of pain phenotypes (nociceptive, neuropathic, nociplastic, or a mixture of pain types), 3 studies assessed neuropathic pain, , , and only 2 studies assessed nociceptive pain. , Lou et al measured mucosal neuropathic pain with the Vanderbilt Head and Neck Symptom Survey (VHNSS); Terkawi et al assessed neuropathic pain with the Neuropathic Pain Questionnaire‑Short Form (NPQ‑SF); and one study did not report a tool for assessing neuropathic pain. In contrast, Srivastava et al assessed nociceptive pain with the NRS; and Terkawi et al assessed nociceptive pain with the Brief Pain Inventory, Short Form (BPI.SF). Therefore, the 82.35% of included studies did not use the different pain phenotypes to classify persistent pain.
Six studies (35.29%) did not report pain severity or solely mentioned the existence of persistent pain without measuring its severity. , , , The remaining 11 articles reported pain severity with different questionnaires, categorized pain severity as mild/low to severe/high pain or categorized pain severity as moderate/intermediate to severe/high pain. , , , , A score of 1 to 4 on the NRS indicates mild pain, a score of 5 to 6 indicates moderate pain, and a score of 7 to 10 indicates severe pain. 

## Risk of bias
Of the seventeen included studies, only three exhibited a moderate risk of bias according to the JBI tool. , , The other fourteen articles showed a low risk of bias ( ). , , , , Twelve studies did not report how random sampling was performed. , , , , , , , Only five studies provided sufficient information about sample size calculations. , , , , Fifteen studies described the study subjects (ie, cancer treatment, cancer stage) and setting in detail. , , , Thirteen studies conducted data analysis with sufficient coverage of the identified sample. , , , , The authors identified only one study in which the method for the measurement of persistent pain was unclear. Furthermore, one study did not measure persistent pain reliably. ## Global persistent pain prevalence
The pooled estimate for the average prevalence of persistent pain in HNC patients was 31% (95% CI, 20% to 42%), as shown in the overall meta-analysis (I2 = 99.63%, τ2 = 0.24) ( ). Despite the large variation, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of this findings, as the pooled prevalence remained stable even after excluding studies with small sample sizes. The Egger’s test showed no evidence of small-study effects or publication bias (Egger’s test, β = −3.55, SE = 2.87, P = 0.215).
 Meta-regression analyses were conducted to investigate sources of heterogeneity. Cancer location, timing of pain assessment, and measurement methods were assessed. The results indicated that timing of pain assessment significantly contributed to the heterogeneity (P = 0.014), with studies reporting persistent pain measured at different times showing a notable influence on the pooled prevalence. The coefficient for timing was −0.130 (95% CI, −0.233 to −0.026). Overall, the meta-regression explained approximately 40% of the observed heterogeneity (R2 = 40.57).

## Persistent pain prevalence by cancer location
Persistent pain prevalence varied depending on the cancer location. Patients with tumors located in the floor of the mouth exhibited the highest prevalence, at 65% (95% CI, 57% to 73%). By contrast, patients with tumors in the oral cavity and oropharynx had a much lower pooled prevalence of 19% (95% CI, 0% to 63%), (I2 = 95.98%) ( ).

## Persistent pain prevalence by cancer treatment
The type of cancer treatment was also a determinant of persistent pain prevalence. Patients who underwent combined surgery, RT, and chemotherapy reported a pooled prevalence of 38% (95% CI, 18% to 60%), indicating a higher burden of persistent pain compared to those who only underwent surgery and RT, whose prevalence was 25% (95% CI, 0% to 81%) ( ).

## Persistent pain prevalence by measurement method and timing of pain assessment
The prevalence of persistent pain varied depending on the pain measurement instrument and the timing of pain assessment. Studies using NRS to assess persistent pain reported the highest pooled prevalence of 35% (95% CI, 19% to 53%), (I2 = 99.72%) ( ).
The timing of pain assessment also played a role in the reported prevalence. One year after diagnosis, the pooled prevalence was 38% (95% CI, 27% to 50%), (I2 = 88.96%). Two years after diagnosis, the prevalence remained similar at 38% (95% CI, 19% to 59%), but with much higher heterogeneity (I2 = 99.79%). At five years or more post-diagnosis, the prevalence increased to 43% (95% CI, 23% to 65%) (I2 = 93.37%) ( ).

## Discussion
The aim of this systematic review was to pool the prevalence rates of persistent pain among HNC survivors. In addition to prevalence rates, we examined the characteristics of the pain, the methods for measuring persistent pain, the timing of pain assessments and the mechanisms underlying persistent pain.
Our in-depth analysis revealed that persistent pain was present in 31% of HNC survivors.
Moreover, this systematic review revealed that there is considerable variability in the methods and timing of pain assessments, and there are heterogeneous definitions of persistent pain. This heterogeneity is attributable to a deficit in the number of articles focused on persistent HNC pain.
Due to the lack of studies, we are not able to reach a conclusion on the presence of persistent pain for each specific HNC cancer location. We assumed that different HNC cancer locations would present distinct persistent pain prevalence rates. Apparently, there are no articles that indicate which HNC cancer locations often present a higher percentage of persistent pain in HNC survivors. Therefore, it is not possible to compare our results obtained by means of the meta-regression. However, as shown in the analysis, the floor of the mouth exhibited the highest persistent pain prevalence. Pain in the floor of the mouth can be either confined to the site of the lesion or present as a shooting, sporadic, neuropathic pain deep inside the ear, which can persist after surgery and RT and usually may intensify by associated trismus, dysphagia and speaking disorders. 
Furthermore, we aimed to determine the prevalence rates of the different pain phenotypes. Unfortunately, only thre studies specifically assessed neuropathic pain, , , and only two studies specifically assessed nociceptive pain , ; in contrast, the other included studies did not allude to any of the different persistent pain mechanisms defined by the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP). Thus, there is insufficient scientific evidence regarding the different persistent pain phenotypes among HNC survivors.

## Risk of bias
The majority of included studies in this systematic review exhibited low risk of bias. Nevertheless, most of the studies did not cover sample size calculations and sample randomisation method in sufficient detail. Forthcoming studies should provide this information to address such frequent biases.
Nonetheless, we highlight the extensive information that most of the included studies provide on patient characteristics (socio-demographic and clinical data), the statistical analyses used and the different methods to measure persistent pain safely. Consequently, providing safer and more reliable results despite the lack of studies focusing on HNC survivors with persistent pain.

## Meta-analysis
On the other hand, our analysis revealed that patients who underwent combined surgery, RT, and chemotherapy reported higher persistent pain prevalence. This could be attributed to the fact that chemo-radiotherapy appears to increase the frequency of persistent pain, whereas the prevalence of persistent pain is similar for patients treated by surgery and/or RT. 
Regarding the persistent pain measurement method, those studies that assessed NRS reported the highest pooled prevalence. We hypothesized that persistent pain measured with NRS could overestimate the presence of persistent pain. This may be due to NRS does not have clear descriptor for the anchors except for the two extreme ends, which could influence the interpretation for each score as it can be considerably subjective. However, social and psychological aspects could be influencing the values obtained by the NRS. , 
Concerning the timing of pain assessment, those patients assessed at five years or more post-diagnosis, exhibited higher rates of persistent pain prevalence than those assessed one or two years after diagnosis. This may be explained due to neural damage from RT treatment and deterioration of mental health can appear in a later stage ; consequently, impacting social, work, or leisure activities, struggling to return to daily activities and assuming meaningful roles. 
This systematic review provides comprehensive insight into the emerging knowledge about persistent pain after curative cancer treatment. However, to draw solid conclusions on the different persistent pain manifestations, healthcare professionals should include more pain assessment tools in their patients’ follow-up appointments. Classifying patients into subgroups based on pain phenotypes (ie, nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain) would improve precision pain medicine practices and facilitate tailored treatments based on individual patient characteristics. Specifically, in terms of nociceptive pain, pain at the surgical site, shoulder pain after radical neck surgery, pain due to sternocleidomastoid spasm and fibrosis and radiotherapy induced oral mucositis could be effectively treated with physical therapy interventions, including exercise, manual therapy, low-level laser therapy, heat therapy and cryotherapy. Moreover, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, opioids, and local anesthetics as part of a comprehensive analgesic strategy could yield benefits in relieving nociceptive pain in HNC survivors. Conversely, anticonvulsants (eg, gabapentin, pregabalin), tricyclic antidepressants, ketamine, methadone, and serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, , , as well as peripheral nerve field stimulation, spinal cord stimulation, transauricular vagus nerve stimulation and cognitive behavioral therapy have been suggested to improve neuropathic pain symptoms. On the other hand, non-pharmacological approaches such as exercise, pain neuroscience education and cognitive behavioral therapy, as well as multimodal treatments that target the biopsychosocial model and promote lifestyle habits, should be used as first-line treatment for HNC survivors with nociplastic pain. Therefore, despite centrally acting drugs such as antidepressants and anticonvulsants may present moderate effectiveness in patients with nociplastic pain, side effects are highly likely to occur. 
In view of the above, future studies should incorporate reliable and valid questionnaires to measure persistent pain among HNC survivors. Future research should also broaden current knowledge on the prevalence of different pain phenotypes among HNC survivors to make precision pain medicine a reality.

## Strengths and limitations
The first strength of this systematic review is its scope, as it included HNC survivors at all stages of the disease. This enabled us to have a more comprehensive overview of the differences in the prevalence of persistent pain and pain severity among the included studies. Another major strength of this study was the use of the JBI critical appraisal checklist to evaluate the risk of bias among the studies reporting prevalence data. Finally, a meta-analysis was performed to calculate the pooled prevalence of persistent pain in HNC survivors.
Nevertheless, this systematic review has several limitations. First, several studies did not provide pain severity data or did not describe the persistent pain measurement method in sufficient detail. Thus, we cannot draw conclusions about whether HNC survivors had clinically significant persistent pain (indicated by a VAS score of 30/100). Moreover, pain measurement approaches, timing of pain assessments and pain characteristics were especially heterogeneous, which precludes drawing firm conclusions. Besides, we acknowledge that selecting the highest prevalence rate could introduce a selection bias by potentially overestimating the pooled prevalence. However, this approach was chosen to address the heterogeneity in follow-up time points across studies, as aggregating prevalence rates from different time points would have compromised methodological consistency. In addition, the majority of included studies were carried out in high-income countries, which could affect the generalizability of our results. This is important because persistent pain is more prevalent among people with low socioeconomic status, , as such patients tend to receive less specialized treatments. , Additionally, most of the included studies did not mention pain phenotypes (nociceptive, neuropathic, nociplastic, mixed). Furthermore, most of the included studies did not specify whether the persistent pain was caused by curative cancer treatment or whether the persistent pain was already present before receiving the treatments. One of the included studies did not clarify whether all participants with delayed complications experienced persistent pain. Moreover, certain HNC cancer locations were overrepresented (with greater sample sizes) with low or high persistent pain prevalence, which could affect the overall prevalence rates of persistent pain in our target population. For the abovementioned reasons, future studies could reduce heterogeneity in pain data and support more sophisticated and precise evaluation across different HNC subsites and time points through the use of standardized pain assessment tools and international guidelines for pain phenotyping, clear definition of assessment timelines, and stratification of analyses based on tumor location, treatment modality, and disease trajectory.

## Conclusion
Our systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that persistent pain is present in 31% of HNC patients who finished cancer treatment at least 3 months ago. No final conclusions can be drawn as to which extent cancer location, cancer treatment, persistent pain measurement method and timing of pain assessments could modify this persistent pain prevalence rate. Furthermore, we could not reach a conclusion on the mechanisms underlying persistent pain in HNC survivors. These results should be interpreted with caution since there is considerable variability in the characteristics of persistent pain, the methods and timing of pain assessments. This heterogeneity is attributable to a deficit in the number of articles focused on persistent pain in HNC patients in the survivorship period. Future studies are needed to further explore the association between persistent pain prevalence rate and the various characteristics of persistent pain among HNC survivors and the different types of cancers.
