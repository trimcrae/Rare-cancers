## Background
Early childhood caries (ECC) is a disease of global public health significance and a source of concern, especially in resource-poor countries like Nigeria. This study aims to determine the prevalence and associated risk indicators for ECC in Nigeria.

## Methods
This systematic review protocol for this systematic review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420250654057). A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, African Journals Online, Scopus, ProQuest, and Directory of Open Access Journals was conducted from inception to April 1, 2025, for studies reporting the prevalence of ECC and its risk indicators in children in Nigeria aged 0–71 months. Grey literature was sought from the Nigeria and West Africa Postgraduate Medical Colleges. Eligible studies included peer-reviewed articles, conference proceedings, government and international organization reports, and unpublished studies. Quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. A random effect model was used to calculate the pooled prevalence, and sensitivity and subgroup analysis were performed to explore heterogeneity.

## Results
Thirty-one studies published between 2006 and 2025 were included in the systematic review, and 27 in the meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of ECC was 22% (95% CI: 17–26%; I²=98%), decreasing to 19% (95% CI: 15–23) after the sensitivity analysis. Subgroup analysis revealed no significant difference in the prevalence over time (p = 0.060) or by sex (females slightly higher, p = 0.720). Regional prevalence was highest in the Northeast and Southwest (p < 0.001), children from high-income groups had the highest ECC prevalence (p = 0.030), and prevalence increased significantly with age (p < 0.001).

## Conclusion
Approximately one in every five children in Nigeria has ECC, indicating a substantial burden. These findings underscore the need for sustained preventive strategies and have significant implications for oral health policy and practice in Nigeria.

## Introduction
Early childhood caries (ECC) is a significant public health concern characterized by the presence of one or more decayed (cavitated or non-cavitated), missing (due to caries), or filled tooth surfaces in any primary tooth in preschool children between 6 and 71 months of age [ ]. Globally, ECC affects nearly half of preschool-aged children [ ] with a pooled prevalence of 48% [ ], and is associated with detrimental impacts on child growth [ ], academic performance [ ], and school attendance [ ], alongside substantial financial burdens for households [ ] and the health systems [ – ].
In Africa, the increasing prevalence of dental caries presents a pressing public health challenge [ ], and reliable data are needed for assessing the disease burden, advocating for policy and resource allocation [ , ], and guiding effective public health interventions [ ], especially in settings where oral health is often a low priority and services are limited [ – ].
In Nigeria, there is no national ECC survey data [ ]. A previous systematic review estimated the pooled ECC prevalence at 17% [ ], which is lower than the African average of 30% [ ] and rates reported in other African countries such as Namibia (31.7%) [ ], South Africa (44.94%-51.72%) [ ], Kenya (59.5%) [ ], Uganda (17.6%) [ ] and Tanzania (30.1%) [ ]. Known risk indicators in the Nigerian context include infant feeding practices [ ], frequent consumption of sugary snacks [ ], and oral hygiene practices [ ], while the use of fluoridated toothpaste and twice daily toothbrushing are protective [ – ]. Community water fluoridation is not implemented, though fluoride levels in groundwater vary [ ].
However, the prior estimate of ECC in Nigeria was based solely on published literature, excluding data from grey sources such as unpublished postgraduate theses, and did not report on the risk indicators for ECC in the country [ ]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the estimated prevalence of ECC and the risk indicators associated with ECC in Nigeria by systematically reviewing both published and grey literature.

## Methods
This systematic review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the identifier CRD420250654057. The review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and checklist [ ]. At each stage of the review, two authors independently carried out the process in a blinded manner, with any disagreements resolved through consultation with a third author.

## Research questions
The primary research question guiding this systematic review was: What is the estimated prevalence of ECC in Nigeria? The secondary question was: What are the risk indicators for ECC in Nigeria?

## Eligibility criteria
The study’s PECO criteria [ ] included population (young children aged 0–71 months resident in Nigeria with ECC experience), Exposure at the individual and household levels, and comparators who are children aged 0–71 months, resident in Nigeria without ECC experience. The outcome of the study was the ECC experience.
Studies included were peer-reviewed articles, conference proceedings, reports on websites of governmental agencies and international organizations, and unpublished studies providing information on the prevalence of ECC and its associated risk factors. Clinical trials were also included if they provided baseline prevalence data. Studies were excluded if they were case reports and case series, purely qualitative studies, animal studies, or in vitro studies. Primary studies that reported data exclusively on children with congenital abnormalities, systemic diseases, syndromes, special needs, or comorbid conditions were also excluded. Literature reviews, like scoping reviews and systematic reviews, and meta-analyses, were also excluded.

## Data sources
A comprehensive search strategy was employed, utilising Medical Subject Headings, truncation, Boolean operators, limits, and search filters tailored to each database’s specific needs and based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The databases searched were PubMed, Web of Science, African Journals Online, SCOPUS, ProQuest, and Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ). The search was from the date of inception of the database till the 1st of April 2025. A grey literature search was also conducted in the library of the National Postgraduate Medical College of Nigeria and the library of the West African College of Surgeons. The reference list of all published articles, as well as the reference list of systematic reviews and meta-analyses, was also searched for possibly missed publications. To enhance the comprehensiveness of the grey literature search, direct contact was made with study investigators to request copies of publications not accessible through the library, relevant theses, and unpublished manuscripts. A targeted call for information on any studies conducted in Nigeria on ECC was also disseminated via the WhatsApp platform of the Paediatric Dentists Association of Nigeria.

## Search strategy
The search terms are combinations of keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH terms) such as caries, dental caries, toddler, under 6 children, preschool children, and Nigeria. The search combined terms and synonyms for (1) prevalence, (2) risk indicators, (3) preschool children, (4) dental caries, and (5) Nigeria using Boolean operators. These terms were combined using conjunctions like “AND” and “OR.” The various combinations of free-text words and controlled terms were used while searching bibliographic databases. The full search strategies for all databases are available in Supplemental Table 1. As an example, the search string for Scopus was:
( TITLE-ABS-KEY (“prevalence” OR “epidemiology”)) AND (TITLE-ABS-KEY (“risk factor*” OR “determinants”)) AND (TITLE-ABS-KEY (“child, preschool” OR “infant” OR “under 5”)) AND (TITLE-ABS-KEY (“dental caries” OR “caries” OR “dmft”)) AND (TITLE-ABS-KEY(“Nigeria”)).
Search results were consolidated using EndNote software (version 20), which removed duplicate articles. The consolidated data was uploaded into the Rayyan software, where additional duplicates were eliminated. Details of the search strategies are provided in Appendix I.

## Study selection
Two authors (EEO and NMC) independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Articles meeting the criteria proceeded to full-text review, with any disagreements resolved through consensus. The reviewers were not blinded to the identity of the journal names, article authors, or the results of the search.

## Data extraction
All full articles of potentially eligible studies were obtained and assessed independently by two reviewers. Studies were retained when the two investigators (EEO and NMC) reached consensus. Authors of full articles that could not be retrieved were contacted for a soft copy of their manuscript.
A detailed data extraction sheet was used to extract the data of interest, such as study characteristics (first author, year of publication), study design, location within Nigeria (state, geopolitical zone, urban/rural), sample size, participant demographics, and the prevalence of ECC. Age was categorised into 0–11 months, 12–23 months, 24–35 months, 36–47 months, 48–59 months, and 60–71 months. Sex was categorised into male and female. The geopolitical zones were South-west, South-east, South-south, North-west, North-central, and North-east. Details on the identified risk indicators for ECC were also extracted.
The extracted data were systematically recorded in Microsoft Excel by the two authors (EEO and NMC) and checked by a third author (MOF). Discrepancies in recorded data between authors were resolved by the third author (MOF). For studies with incomplete data, the corresponding authors were contacted to request additional information.

## Risk of bias assessment
The quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal tool for prevalence and cross-sectional studies [ ]. Two trained authors (EEO and NMC) independently assessed the risk of bias, with inter-rater reliability provided (Supplemental file 2), and disagreements or ambiguities were resolved through discussion. The inter-rater reliability score was 93.8% based on simple percentage agreement.
The assessment tool checked (1) if the sample was sample clearly defined (2) if the study participants and the setting was described in detail (3) if the exposure was measured validly and reliably (4) if the criteria used for measurement of the condition were objective and standard (5) if the cofounding factors were identified (6) if the strategies to deal with the cofounding factors were stated (7) if the outcomes were measured validly and reliably (8) if appropriate statistical analysis was used and (9) if the response rate was adequate and where it was not if the low response rate was managed appropriately. A score of 1 was given where each criterion was met, and 0 where it was not met. A score of 0–3 was considered low, 4–6 moderate, and 7–9 high quality.

## Sensitivity analysis
A sensitivity analysis was performed with leave-one-out analysis to investigate the impact of risk of bias on the prevalence of ECC in Nigeria. This was carried out to uncover potential sources of heterogeneity.

## Assessment of publication bias
Symmetry of the funnel plot was used to assess the publication bias of the articles included in the meta-analysis.

## Data synthesis
The grey literature was excluded from the meta-analysis to ensure the pooled estimate was based on data that had undergone this additional layer of external validation, thereby prioritizing maximal reliability for the quantitative synthesis. Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4.1. (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, Copenhagen, Denmark). The pooled prevalence of ECC was calculated using the inverse variance method, after applying a Freeman-Turkey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variances. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was used as the effect size and presented in forest plots. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q and I² tests. Given the substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2 >75% in most analyses), a random-effect model was applied using the DerSimonian and Laird estimator, while fixed-effect models were reserved only for analyses with low heterogeneity.
A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (except for heterogeneity testing). Subgroup analysis was conducted to determine the prevalence of ECC by sex, geopolitical zones, year using a 5-year window (2006–2010, 2011–2015, 2016–2020, 2021–2025), age (less than 12 months, 12–23 months, 24–35 months, 36–47 months, 48–59 months, and 60–71 months) and socioeconomic status (high, middle and low). The pooled prevalence of ECC per group was determined, and the subgroup differences were analysed using the Review Manager. Results were presented in tables, charts, and a PRISMA flow diagram.

## Risk indicators associated with early childhood caries
Supplemental Table 4 outlines 64 investigated risk indicators for ECC. The most extensively studied categories are demographic factors, particularly age (25 studies), sex (21 studies), and socioeconomic status (15 studies), followed by oral hygiene practices, such as overall status (11 studies), toothbrushing frequency (4 studies), and fluoridated toothpaste use (5 studies). Dietary research concentrates on specific sugar-related behaviors, including daily frequency of sugar intake and between-meal sugar consumption, rather than general snacking. Early-life feeding practices, notably bottle feeding (4 studies), breastfeeding duration (4 studies), and method (2 studies), also receive attention.
Factors like dental characteristics (e.g., affected tooth, prior visits, and gingival health), maternal factors (age, education, oral health knowledge), and school-related variables (school type) were also studied. Nutritional status and systemic health indicators, such as medication use, illness history, and genotype, were occasionally explored, suggesting broader health connections to dental caries risk.
However, a substantial number of factors appeared only once or twice across studies. These include detailed oral hygiene practices (cleaning tools and methods, dentifrice type, initiation timing), nuanced feeding behaviors (exclusive bottle feeding, mixed feeding, night feeding, sleeping while feeding), specific dental features (tooth surface, affected jaw, eruption count, tooth wear), dietary habits (between-meal and school-time snacks), maternal-specific variables (parity, caries experience, food premastication), systemic health measures (retroviral status, CD4 count, ART exposure, membrane rupture), and other niche factors like access to care, anthropometrics, birth order, and non-nutritive habits.
Table synthesizes risk indicators associated with ECC [ ]. Biological risks included older age [ , , , , , , , , ] and younger age [ ]; female sex [ , , , , ] and male sex [ , ]; and specific tooth anatomy, such as the second mandibular primary molar [ ] and primary molars [ ]. Additional biological indicators were HIV infection or low CD4 count [ ], sickle cell anaemia [ ], nutritional extremes (underweight, overweight) [ ], low BMI-for-age z-score [ ], birth rank (first, only, or last child) [ , ], and maternal age [ ].
Social risk indicators comprised high [ , ], middle [ ], and low [ , ] socioeconomic status; poor maternal attitude and knowledge [ , ]; and attendance at private [ ] or public [ ] schools.
Behavioral risk indicators included breastfeeding beyond 18 months [ , ], exclusive breastfeeding and nursing at the breast [ ], night feeding [ ], school-time snacking [ ], consumption of sugary foods [ , , , ], poor [ ] and fair [ ] oral hygiene, maternal premastication of food [ ], prior dental visits [ , ], and use of glycerine for cleaning [ ].
Conversely, protective indicators for ECC reported in Table were young age (6–16 months [ ] or under one year [ ]), breastfeeding for 3–12 months [ ], female sex [ ], stunted or underweight status [ ], and consumption of fruits and vegetables [ ].

## Pooled prevalence of early childhood caries in Nigeria
Twenty-seven studies [ – , , ] were subjected to meta-analysis to compute the pooled prevalence of ECC in Nigeria. Figure shows that the pooled prevalence of ECC during the study period was 22% (95% CI: 17 to 26%) with a heterogeneity index of 98% (p < 0.001).

 

## Subgroup analysis

## Prevalence of early childhood caries by year
Figure shows the variability in the prevalence of ECC in Nigeria over the years. Between 2006 and 2010, the prevalence was 19% (95% CI: 10–27%), with high variability across studies (heterogeneity index I² = 97%, p < 0.001). This rose to 29% (95% CI: 18–39%) in 2011–2015 (I² = 96%, p < 0.001) and 27% (95% CI: 14–40%) in 2016–2020 (I² = 99%, p < 0.001). However, in the 2021–2025 period, the prevalence was 14% (95% CI: 7–20%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 95%, p < 0.001). The differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.060).

 

## Prevalence of early childhood caries by geopolitical zone
Figure highlights the variation in ECC prevalence across Nigeria’s geopolitical zones. The highest prevalence was observed in the Southwest with a pooled prevalence of 25% (95% CI: 19–32%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 98%, p < 0.001). and Northeast also at 25% (95% CI: 15–34%, I² = 83%, p = 0.01). The South-South and South-East regions both showed a prevalence of 10% with 95% CIs of 6–13% and 7–13%, respectively. Heterogeneity was moderate in the South-South (I² = 61%, p = 0.05) and high in the South-East (p < 0.001). In contrast, the Northwest recorded the lowest prevalence at 3% (95% CI: -15–22%). The ECC prevalence differed significantly across regions (p < 0.001).

 

## Prevalence of early childhood caries by sex
Figure presents the prevalence of ECC in Nigeria by sex. The pooled prevalence among males was 14% (95% CI: 7–22%), and among females, it was slightly higher at 17% (95% CI: 6–28%), However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.720). Both estimates showed high variability across studies, with a heterogeneity index of 98% (p < 0.001) for both groups.

 

## Prevalence of early childhood caries by age
Figure shows that no cases of ECC were found in children under 12 months. Among those aged 12–23 months, ECC prevalence was 4% (95% CI: 3–5%), rising to 8% (95% CI: 4–12%) in the 24–35-month group, and 18% (95%CI: 12–23%) in children aged 36–47 months. The highest prevalence was observed in the 48–59-month group at 32% (95% CI: 22–41%), followed by 22% in the 60–71-month group (95% CI: 14–29%). The differences in ECC prevalence across age groups were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

 

## Prevalence of early childhood caries by socioeconomic status
Figure presents data from five studies [ , , , , ] on the association between ECC and socioeconomic status. In the five studies, socioeconomic status was assessed using the same validated composite index that considered mothers’ level of education and fathers’ occupation [ ] The highest pooled prevalence of ECC was among children from high socioeconomic backgrounds at 16% (95% CI: 12–20%), with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%, p < 0.001). In contrast, children from low socioeconomic groups had the lowest prevalence at 8% (95% CI: 3–12%), though this group showed high variability across studies (I² = 70%, p = 0.010). The middle socioeconomic group had a prevalence of 10% (95% CI: 3–17%), with similarly high heterogeneity (I²=89%, p < 0.001). The difference in ECC prevalence across socioeconomic levels was statistically significant (p = 0.030).

 

## Sensitivity analysis
The random-effects sensitivity analysis indicated that several studies, including those by Poopola et al. [ ], Adeniyi et al. [ ], Iyun et al. [ ], Ligali and Olatosi [ ], Adeniyi et al. [ ], Nzomiwu et al. [ ], and Ashiwaju et al. [ ], influenced the pooled prevalence estimate of ECC in Nigeria. The leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that excluding Poopola et al. [ ], Iyun et al. [ ], or Ligali and Olatosi [ ] each reduced the prevalence estimate from 22% to 21% (95% CI: 16–26). The pooled prevalence dropped further to 20% with the exclusion of Nzomiwu et al. [ ] or Ashiwaju et al. [ ] (95% CI: 16–25). When Adeniyi et al. [ ] was omitted, the prevalence decreased to 19% (95% CI: 15–25), and a similar reduction to 19% (95% CI: 15–23) was observed when Adeniyi et al. [ ] was excluded.

## Publication bias
Figure shows that the funnel plot has no asymmetry. This suggests a lack of significant publication bias. However, due to the high heterogeneity observed (I2 =98%), the interpretation of the funnel plot should be cautious, as asymmetry may be due to other factors other than publication bias.

 

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis confirm that ECC remains a significant public health issue in Nigeria, affecting approximately one in five preschool children. Although this prevalence is lower than global [ ] and continental [ ] averages, the absolute disease burden is substantial due to the country’s large population under five years old. Notable regional, socioeconomic, and age-related disparities underscore the context-specific epidemiology of ECC within Nigeria.
Geographic variation was pronounced, with prevalence ranging from very low in the Northwest to significantly higher levels in the Northeast and Southwest. These differences likely stem from a combination of true epidemiological variation, unequal healthcare access, socioeconomic inequalities, and uneven research coverage. ECC prevalence also increased with age, peaking among children aged 48–59 months, where approximately one in three to four was affected. This trend reflects the cumulative nature of dental caries, driven by prolonged dietary and microbial exposures [ , ]. These findings emphasize the need for early and sustained preventive interventions beginning before or shortly after tooth eruption.
This review provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of ECC prevalence and associated risk indicators in Nigeria. Methodological rigor was ensured through transparent reporting, reproducible procedures, and a broad search strategy that included both electronic databases and grey literature. The inclusion of grey literature helped minimize publication bias and capture locally generated evidence, while the synthesis of studies spanning nearly two decades enabled analysis of temporal trends and changes in ECC prevalence. Subgroup analyses by age, sex, socioeconomic status, and geopolitical zone enhanced interpretation, and the predominance of peer-reviewed studies supports confidence in the findings.
Nevertheless, substantial heterogeneity across studies limits straightforward interpretation of pooled prevalence estimates. This variability largely stemmed from methodological differences rather than random variation. Clinic-based studies, for instance, reported higher prevalence than household or community-based surveys, skewing aggregate estimates. Inconsistent diagnostic indices and varying thresholds for defining ECC further compromised comparability. As a result, the estimated prevalence warrants cautious interpretation. These findings underscore the urgent need for harmonized ECC assessment protocols in Nigerian research to improve data comparability and strengthen future evidence synthesis.
Despite methodological variation across studies, this review offers the most robust evidence base currently available to inform national oral health planning for preschool children in Nigeria. The pooled prevalence of ECC exceeded the 17% reported in earlier research [ ] yet remained below continental and global averages. Historically, Nigeria has been characterised by relatively low dental caries prevalence [ ], a trend attributed partly to regulatory policies mandating fluoride in toothpaste, which has ensured widespread access to fluoridated products [ ]. Nonetheless, the absolute burden is substantial, given Nigeria’s estimated 30 million children under five [ ]. Discrepancies with prior estimates may also reflect the inclusion of studies capturing early non-cavitated lesions, underscoring the need for caution in cross-national comparisons where methodological differences can influence reported prevalence.
Policy gaps likely contribute to the persistent burden of ECC. Although early childhood oral health is acknowledged in national policy frameworks, Nigeria lacks a fully implemented oral health programme specifically designed for preschool children [ ]. Several socioeconomic trends threaten to exacerbate future risk, including rising sugar consumption, economic decline [ ], and growing household reliance on low-cost, non-fluoridated toothpaste [ , ]. Workforce attrition due to the emigration of dental professionals [ ], limited access to oral health services [ ], and chronic underinvestment in oral health, despite evidence that integrating oral health into universal health coverage reduces ECC risk [ ], collectively point to a potential epidemic of childhood caries in Nigeria.
However, the findings also reveal an intriguing paradox. While global and regional trends indicate rising ECC rates in low- and middle-income countries [ ], temporal analysis suggests a decline in prevalence over the last decade, with peak rates observed between 2011 and 2015. This decrease may reflect the cumulative effect of diverse oral health initiatives, though these efforts have not been systematically documented. At the primary prevention level, national campaigns, often led by dental associations and aligned with events such as World Oral Health Day and Children’s Day, have promoted key messages on twice-daily brushing with fluoridated toothpaste, reduced sugar intake, and regular oral hygiene. These events have also facilitated access to preventive procedures, including oral prophylaxis, topical fluoride application, and fissure sealants. Dental schools have further embedded community outreach into academic programmes, disseminating evidence-based strategies to reduce ECC risk [ ]. In addition, grant-funded research has targeted vulnerable populations with preventive interventions [ , ]. Nevertheless, the absence of systematic monitoring and evaluation constrains efforts to assess the true impact of these activities and limits the potential for evidence-informed policy development.
Regional disparities in the reviewed studies highlight how oral diseases reflect broader social inequities. The Northeast region recorded some of the highest prevalences of ECC, likely driven by prolonged insecurity, population displacement, and fragile health systems [ , ]. Internally displaced populations often face limited access to clean water, nutrition, hygiene resources, and healthcare, increasing caries vulnerability [ – ]. Weak infrastructure and shortages of trained oral health professionals further constrain preventive services [ ]. In contrast, higher estimates in the Southwest may reflect greater research activity rather than true epidemiological differences. These findings underscore the need to integrate oral health into humanitarian responses and development agendas to ensure equitable prevention and care.
An unexpected pattern emerged regarding socioeconomic status: children from higher-income households showed greater ECC prevalence, contrary to global patterns where caries typically affects disadvantaged populations more severely [ ]. This likely reflects changing dietary behaviors linked to purchasing power, including greater access to processed foods, sugary snacks, and prolonged bottle-feeding [ , ]. Prevention strategies should therefore address behavioural risks across all socioeconomic groups, not only the disadvantaged.
Sex-based differences in ECC were minimal and not statistically significant, diverging from studies in other settings that report higher prevalence among boys [ , ]. This suggests gender-related influences may vary by cultural context; similar caregiving and dietary practices for boys and girls in Nigeria may account for the lack of disparity. Accordingly, prevention efforts should prioritize age-related and behavioural factors over gender.
The review also revealed important gaps in risk indicator research. Nigerian studies have largely focused on variables such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, oral hygiene practices, and sugar consumption frequency, while many biological, behavioural, and social determinants remain underexplored. ECC is a multifactorial disease influenced by interactions among host susceptibility, microbial factors, dietary exposures, and environmental conditions. However, biological mechanisms such as enamel defects, saliva composition, microbiological profiles, and genetic susceptibility have received limited attention. Maternal influences, including transmission of cariogenic bacteria and parental health behaviors, also appear insufficiently investigated despite their biological plausibility. Similarly, healthcare access and utilization patterns, which influence disease progression and outcomes, remain underrepresented.
The diversity of risk indicators examined across studies reflects fragmentation within the research landscape and limits quantitative synthesis of determinants. The lack of standardized definitions and measurement approaches limits the identification of key predictors necessary for developing a Nigeria dental caries risk assessment tool. Such a tool could strengthen early detection within primary healthcare settings and promote collaboration between dental and medical professionals [ ]. Future research should prioritize standardized methodologies, longitudinal study designs, and nationally representative sampling to improve evidence quality and clarify causal pathways.
From a public health perspective, preventive strategies should prioritize regions with heightened vulnerability, particularly the Northeast, while also addressing emerging risks among higher socioeconomic groups. Integration of oral health services into maternal and child primary healthcare platforms offers a practical and sustainable pathway for early intervention. Preventive measures such as fluoride varnish application, caregiver education, dietary counselling, and early dental visits should be incorporated into routine child health services to reduce disease burden.
Overall, this review highlights ECC as a persistent yet preventable childhood disease in Nigeria. Although prevalence remains lower than global averages, the large number of affected children represents a significant public health challenge. Regional inequalities, evolving socioeconomic risk patterns, methodological inconsistencies, and substantial research gaps underscore the need for coordinated national action. Strengthening surveillance systems, standardizing research methods, expanding preventive programs, and integrating oral health into broader child health policies are essential steps toward reducing ECC burden and improving early childhood oral health outcomes in Nigeria.

## Conclusion
Nigeria faces a substantial ECC burden shaped by regional disparities and evolving socioeconomic and dietary patterns. Although national prevalence remains below global averages, the concentration of cases in specific populations calls for a coordinated response that prioritizes high-burden regions and high-risk groups. Policymakers and healthcare professionals must implement evidence-based strategies focused on prevention, education, and equitable access to care.
