## ABSTRACT

Campylobacter is responsible for campylobacteriosis, the most reported foodborne gastrointestinal infection in humans in the European Union, and broiler meat was the food primarily associated with outbreaks with stronger evidence. This study aimed to revise the literature and estimate the pooled prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in three different chicken matrices in Italy from 2003 to 2023, as well as potential subgroup moderators to the observed value. The total number of eligible studies was 48. On the basis of the meta‐analysis, skin and faeces matrices exhibited a high pooled proportion of Campylobacter spp., 0.62 and 0.67, respectively, whereas meat had the lowest proportion of 0.20%, with high heterogeneity detected in the three matrices I
2 = 97.5%–98.8%. As for the moderator subgroup analysis, Campylobacter spp. prevalence in meat was higher in the published literature (0.38) than in the national zoonoses country reports (0.10), whereas no significant difference was observed for skin matrix (p‐value = 0.6). Moreover, Campylobacter spp. prevalence in the meat matrix was two times more in samples collected at the slaughterhouse step than those collected at the retail step (pvalue < 0.05). In the case of faeces, no significant differences (p‐value > 0.5) were detected comparing sampling point (farm or slaughterhouse) and sample types (cloacal or caecal content). This result highlights that Campylobacter spp. is frequently inhabits the intestines of chickens, and it is detectable with a comparable frequency on the skin following the slaughtering process. Moreover, not only Campylobacter spp. persist along the food chain but also cross‐contamination occurring after the primary production steps can determine a relevant prevalence of the microorganism in meat sold at retail in Italy. The outcome of this meta‐analysis could be beneficial as input for future quantitative risk assessment.

## Summary

This systematic review summarizes the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in different chicken matrices in Italy over the last 20 years, which may be useful for conducting quantitative microbial risk assessment (QMRA) in the future.
Higher Campylobacter spp. prevalence rate was observed in faeces (67%) and skin (62%) compared to the meat matrix (20%), suggesting frequent cross‐contamination events during slaughtering, processing and handling.
The result showed that Campylobacter spp. are isolated from meat at retail (10%) and most of them belong to pathogenic species for the consumers.

2.3

## Data Extraction
The following data were extracted from each study using a standardized Excel data extraction form: (a) details of the publication (title, authors, year and Italian region); (b) total number of samples collected and their demographic characteristics (sampling period, matrix and sampling point); (c) prevalence estimates; and (d) Campylobacter species (total number of isolates and their species identification where available).
The subgroups considered for the study included different matrices (faeces, skin and meat). In detail, the category ‘faeces’ includes samples collected from cloacal swabs at farm level and caecal content at slaughterhouse; the category ‘skin’ includes skin swab, a piece of skin and carcass rinse; lastly, the meat category includes meat (i.e., breast, thigh and other parts). In case the same record reported data regarding different matrices or different sampling points, we consider each set as a separate study. Information on laboratory diagnostic techniques was extracted from each included study. All the included studies employed culture‐based methods for the isolation of Campylobacter spp., typically using selective media under microaerophilic conditions according to the method described in ISO 10272: . Some studies further applied PCR assays (Multiplex or Convential) or s‐AFLP typing for species‐level identification.

2.4

## Quality Assessment and Risk of Publication Bias
Quality assessment of the included articles was assessed by using JBI's critical appraisal tools (Munn et al. ). This assessment was performed through the analysis of seven possible domains of bias:
Was the sample frame appropriate to address the target population?
Were study participants sampled in an appropriate way?
Was the sample size adequate?
Were the study subjects and the setting described in detail?
Were objective, standard and reliable methodology used for the detection of the pathogen?
Was there appropriate statistical analysis?
Are all the positive samples investigated for the identification of Campylobacter species (i.e., C. jejuni and C. coli)?

All of the aforementioned domains were assessed individually using the ratings ‘Yes’, ‘Unclear’ and ‘No’, with ratings of two, one and zero, respectively. The total scores ranged from 0 to 14, with scores ≥11 considered high quality, scores ≥7 considered moderate quality and scores less than 7 considered low‐quality studies. Ratings were provided by two reviewers; discrepancies were resolved either through consensus or with the assistance of a third investigator. Moreover, funnel plots and Egger's regression test were used to investigate the presence of publication bias.

2.5

## Data Analysis
2.5.1

## Summary Measures
Meta‐analysis was performed to evaluate the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in chicken in Italy according to the different matrices. Prevalence was defined as the proportion of animals that tested positive out of the total number of animals tested. The analysis was done using the ’meta’ and ‘metaprop’ packages in R software version 4.4.0 (R Core Team ).

2.5.2

## Synthesis of Results
Estimated pooled prevalence was calculated from pooled data for each study. In the case of several prevalence data reported in a single record (e.g., different matrix and sampling point) that information was considered a separate study. The Freeman–Tukey double arcsine method was used to transform the data to approximate normality, along with their 95% confidence intervals, and then were back‐transformed to proportions for the interpretation (Barendregt et al. ; Negeri et al. ; Lin et al. ; Zarea et al. ). The restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method with a random effects model was used to pool the individual prevalence. Heterogeneity was quantified with the inverse variance index (I
2), with the values of 25%, 50% and 75% indicating low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively (Higgins and Thompson ). The Baujat plot and leave‐one‐out diagnostics tests were built to investigate the presence of outliers.

2.5.3

## Moderator Analysis
We investigated potential sources of heterogeneity through subgroup analysis. For faeces matrix, sample type (caecal content or cloacal swabs) and sampling point (slaughterhouse or farm) were analysed. Although for skin matrix, considering the limited information reported, we investigated only the sources of records (Bibliographic Databases, NZCR submitted to EFSA) as a moderator. Moreover, source of records (Bibliographic Databases, NZCR submitted to EFSA) and sampling point (slaughterhouse, retail) were investigated for the meat matrix. Moreover, due to the lack of information on the study regions, it was not possible to assess the effect of geographical location on the prevalence of Campylobacter spp.
For each moderator, a random‐effects model was built to combine studies within each subgroup, and a fixed‐effect model was used to combine subgroups and compute the estimate for the summary effect size.

## Results
3.1

## Literature Investigation
A total of 302 records were retrieved from the bibliographic electronic databases. After removing duplicates (n = 95), 207 records were submitted for title and abstract screening. The reasons for exclusion are listed in the selection process flowchart (Figure  ). A total of 39 records were included as follows: 21 from databases and 18 from the NZCR submitted to EFSA. At the end, 48 studies (30 bibliographic electronic databases, 18 NZCR) were included in this review (Table  ).
 
3.2

## Quality Assessment
The included records received a quality assessment score ranging from 10 to 13 based on JBI's critical evaluation, with more than half of them (57.1%) having a score more than 12 (Table ). No study was excluded based on its quality. Publication bias was not detected between the included studies using Egger's test (p value = 0.8) (Figure  ).

3.3

## Overtime Distribution of Studies
This study was limited to the last 20 years (2003–2024), with a peak of records obtained in 2007 (n = 7). In terms of publication years, the records cover almost the full 20‐year timeframe, apart from 2 years (2012–2015). Over these years, the annual frequency fluctuated between one and three studies. Taking into account the inclusion criteria specific to the NZCR submitted to EFSA, it is important to note that the submitted data for 7 years (from 2012 to 2018) did not fit our inclusion criteria and were thus excluded from this meta‐analysis (Figure  ).
 
3.4

## Eligible Study Characteristics
3.4.1

## Studies Profile
Concerning the type of matrices tested, among the three matrices identified, ‘meat’ was the matrix sampled by the highest number of studies, with a total of 25 (10 from bibliographic electronic databases and 15 from NZCR). Additionally, eleven studies (eight from bibliographic databases and three from NZCR) based on skin samples, while another twelve studies (all from bibliographic databases) reported data based on faecal samples.
Regarding the sampling point, the slaughterhouse was the most frequent location for sampling (n = 21, 55.6%), followed by the retail establishments (n = 14, 29.2%). However, only five studies (10.1%) were sampled on farms, whereas there was no information for six studies (Figure  ). For the regional data, the included studies were unevenly distributed across Italy. Six studies were conducted in Northern Italy (Lombardia, Veneto, Emilia‐Romagna, and Friuli Venezia Giulia), seven in Central Italy (Tuscany, Abruzzo, Molise, and Bojano), and eight in Southern Italy (Campania, Sardinia, Campobasso, and Catanzaro), while 27 studies did not specify a regional location.
 
3.4.2

## Prevalence Data and Time Trend Prevalence
The number of samples in the included studies ranged from 14 to 1611, with a total number of samples screened of 11,498. Considering all matrices, the proportion of Campylobacter spp. varied greatly (from 0.00 to 1.00) with an overall prevalence of 0.41 (n = 4682). Skin and faeces had similar prevalence rates of 0.63 and 0.64, respectively, whereas meat had a remarkably lower positivity rate (0.24). On the basis of the publication year, the Campylobacter spp. prevalence appears variable, with a decreasing trend over the years in the three matrices; however, these trends were not statistically significant (p‐value > 0.05) (Figure  ).
 
3.4.3

## 
Campylobacter spp. Identification
Out of the 4682 positive samples, a total of 2820 (60.3%) isolates were identified at species level across 33 studies; a higher prevalence rate for C. jejuni 47.8% (n = 1349) was observed than C. coli 42.9% (n = 1209). Moreover, co‐infection with both species was detected in 3.5% (n = 99). In addition, C. lari, Campylobacter upsaliensis, Campylobacter sputorum faecalis and other Campylobacter spp. were detected in 0.49% (n = 14), 0.25% (n = 5), 3.01% (n = 85) and 2.06% (n = 58) of isolates, respectively.

3.5

## Meta‐Analysis of Campylobacter spp
All of the selected studies (n = 48) were included in the meta‐analysis of Campylobacter spp. prevalence. The studies of Manfreda et al. ( ) and De Cesare et al. ( ) were identified as outliers both by the Baujat plot and leave‐one‐out diagnostics (Figures  and ). Although the removal of these studies could have reduced the amount of heterogeneity and increased the precision of the estimated average outcome, both studies were kept in the quantitative synthesis, as they are characterized by a large sample size and a high‐quality study design.
3.5.1

## Estimated Pooled Prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in Chicken
A high estimated pooled prevalence rate was detected in both skin (0.65, 95% CI: 0.46–0.81) and faeces (0.67, 95% CI: 0.47–0.84) matrix, whereas the pooled prevalence in meat matrix was (0.20, 95% CI: 0.11–0.30). Within all matrices, there was a significant heterogeneity (p value < 0.01) among the studies, with I
2 = 97.1%–98.9% in the meta‐analysis of them (Figure  ).
 
3.5.2

## Subgroup Analysis
3.5.2.1

## Sources of Data
For the prevalence rate of Campylobacter spp. in chicken meat, the subgroup analysis revealed a difference (p value = 0.0017) between bibliographic electronic databases (0.38, 95% CI: 0.20–0.59) and NZCR reports (0.10, 95% CI: 0.5–0.17). The amount of the heterogeneity explained (R
2) by the source of data was 27.1% for the meat matrix. On the contrary, there was not any relevant differences in the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in skin by type of source (p value = 0.6) (Table  ).
 
3.5.2.2

## Sampling Point and Type
Regarding the sampling point, differences in Campylobacter occurrence in poultry meat (p value = 0.02, R
2 = 20.7%) were observed between the sampling points, whereas Campylobacter spp. was more prevalent in slaughterhouse (0.51, 95% CI: 0.15–0.86) than retail stage (0.22, 95% CI: 0.11–0.37). However, no differences were observed regarding the faeces matrix between studies performed at farm and slaughterhouse (p value = 0.5). Furthermore, there was no remarkable difference in Campylobacter spp. prevalence between caecal content samples and cloacal samples (p value = 0.2) (Table  ).

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta‐analysis provide a comprehensive synthesis regarding the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in chicken in Italy based on data from published literature and the NZCR submitted to EFSA. The output of our study revealed that Campylobacter spp. was prevalent in faeces from a remarkable proportion of chickens raised/slaughtered in Italy (0.65), and a similar high contamination rate was found for skin matrix (0.67). Despite the lower prevalence in meat (0.20), the microorganism was frequently isolated from chicken meat, and most of the isolates belonged to pathogenic species for humans.
Numerous studies and data reports have been published during the timeframe of our review regarding the Campylobacter spp. prevalence in chicken along the food chain with, at least one study or report published almost every year. This confirms the high interest from the scientific community in such microorganism that indeed is responsible for an important impact on public health and whose human cases of infection are often attributed to the consumption of chicken (Igwaran and Okoh ).

Campylobacter spp. can easily colonize GIT of poultry, which is considered a natural reservoir. In most commercial farms, Campylobacter colonization is noticed post the third week of age in chicken (El‐Saadony et al. ), and, once introduced, Campylobacter spreads rapidly throughout the flock. Up to 80%–100% of the birds of one flock may become infected within 3–7 days after initial exposure (Guerin et al. ; Newell ). Faeces represent a direct source of contamination and are responsible for Campylobacter spread in the external environment (Humphrey et al. ; Silva et al. ). The results of our meta‐analysis showed that chickens raised or slaughtered in Italy are frequently carriers of Campylobacter spp. Several studies performed in other countries investigated Campylobacter prevalence in faeces from chicken, reporting values that mostly varied between approximately 30% and 75% (Berndtson et al. ; Bouwknegt et al. ; Ansari‐Lari et al. ; Natsos et al. ; Wang et al. ). This difference could be due to dissimilarities of the study designs between the surveys. However, it could also depend on the different levels of biosecurity and hygiene at farm or more suitable ecological conditions for Campylobacter in some countries. The occurrence rate obtained through our investigation in faeces (0.65) was compatible with the above‐mentioned range, whereas it is close to the upper limit. We do not have a definitive response for such a high value, but the mild climate of Italy might favour the spread of Campylobacter spp. within and between farms in comparison to other colder countries, as the survival period of this pathogen in the environment is briefer at low temperatures (Patrick et al. ; Urdaneta et al. ). It is important to highlight that we were not able to collect information regarding the country of origin of the chickens tested at slaughterhouses, but they were likely raised in Italy, as the import of live chickens is negligible in this country (Carni Sostenibili 2016). Caecal content sample is considered the standard method for estimating the Campylobacter occurrence in GIT (European Commission ), whereas cloacal swabs are also collected for the same purpose at farms (Hansson et al. ; Sandberg et al. ). Regarding the different sample types, a previous study reported that there are no significant differences in the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. between different sampling methods (Ingresa‐Capaccioni et al. ). The same result was obtained in our study, showing no variance (pvalue = 0.52) in the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. between faeces collected on the farm (cloacal swabs) and at the slaughterhouse (caecal content).
Contamination of carcasses with faecal matter during the slaughtering phase is the main cause of the presence of Campylobacter on the skin of chicken carcasses or derived parts. In particular, the occurrence of the pathogen on the skin is mainly given by intestine ruptures that occur during the carcass evisceration and possibly followed by cross‐contamination due to contacts between carcasses along the slaughtering chain. Skin can also be contaminated indirectly by contaminated equipment and surfaces during slaughtering and processing (D. G. Newell and C. Fearnley ; Newell et al. ). It constitutes a favourable environment for bacterial adherence and survival due to the presence of organic material and protective niches that could allow Campylobacter survival (Doyle et al. ). In this study, the pooled prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in skin (0.67) was very similar to that we reported for faeces (0.65). Such a high rate suggests that skin contamination of carcasses seems to be a frequent event during slaughtering in Italy. However, other studies found even higher occurrence rate (Jeffrey et al. ) or lower values that were within our confidential interval (Kottawatta et al. ; Lynch et al. ; Popa et al., ). Furthermore, the level of concentration on the skin is a criterion to assess the hygiene of the slaughterhouse process as requested by the European legislation (Reg. EC. 2073/2005).
The transfer of Campylobacter cells to meat can occur through the de‐skinning and cutting of chicken carcasses/parts at the processing plant or during the product manipulation at home. It is expected that the pathogen concentration on meat is around one log10 CFU/g lower than those on skin, but this factor can significantly change based on the food preparation practices that are performed before the consumption (EFSA BIOHAZ Panel ; Nauta et al. ). In our study, Campylobacter spp. prevalence in the meat matrix did not show a one log reduction value; however, it was definitely lower than those of faeces and skin. The reduction of the occurrence rate in meat can be consequence of the cold chain that is applied after the slaughtering and retained until retail (Maziero et al. ; Gunther et al. ; Stella et al. ). Moreover, only a limited area of meat with a non‐homogeneous distribution may be affected by cross‐contamination from skin, which could involve the passage of a limited number of cells. In addition to that, the sampling procedures and subsequent laboratory tests might not be able to detect the presence of Campylobacter spp. in certain cases, either due to their investigation of a non‐contaminated portion of the meat product or because the contamination falls below the detection limit.
Similar to the previous matrix (skin and faeces), the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in the meat matrix can vary greatly. In this study, the Campylobacter spp. rate was 0.20, which seems lower than the EU average according to data reported by the national zoonoses report of the European Union, where Campylobacter spp. was found in 38.6% and 29.6% of broiler meat samples tested in 2018 and 2019, respectively (EFSA and ECDC , , ). Higher rates were reported also in surveys performed in South Korea (36.2%) (Je et al. ), the United Kingdom (73.3%) (Jorgensen et al. ), Romania (42.6%) (Popa et al., ), Latvia (36.8%) and Lithuania (66.9%) (Tedersoo et al. ), whereas the same occurrence rate was found in the United States (20%) (Golden et al. ). This may suggest that there is a certain attention to the respect of the good hygiene practices and procedures in cutting and processing plants in Italy. However, the occurrence rate in meat matrix dramatically increases if we exclude data derived from official control sampling (NZCR) (p value = 0.002). Indeed, our analysis revealed a significant difference in the detection rate of the pathogen from meat when samples are not collected for research but for institutional purposes. This observation may be referred to the study design, as the published bibliographic databases focused on specific populations, conditions, locations, seasons and different methods, whereas the national reports by EFSA represent the national population without stressing on these criteria.
Moreover, the present study revealed that the contamination rate of meat samples collected at retail was more than double (0.51) when compared to those found at slaughterhouses (0.22). The same result was reported by Wang et al. ( ), where the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. was higher in the steps following the primary production, such as processing plant and retail. It could be explained by the fact that cross‐contamination events can occur during the following food chain steps given by contacts between carcass, handling by different workers, contaminated equipment and surfaces, which can spread the microorganism and lead to high prevalence at retail (Samapundo et al. ; Golden et al. ). However, only few studies with a limited number of samples have been conducted at slaughterhouses in Italy, so additional investigations might be useful to reduce the uncertainty. In addition, the prevalence of Campylobacter in chicken meat was higher at each individual sampling point than the overall pooled estimate, because it was derived from a smaller subset of studies reporting this information, whereas the lower pooled prevalence represents the weighted average across all included studies.
Moreover, It is worth noting that the prevalence of Campylobacter spp. seemed to decrease over the years in all three matrices, based on the publication year of the included studies. However, although this downward trend was visually evident, it was not statistically significant. This may be due to the limited number of studies available for certain years, differences in study design and sampling strategies and the fact that publication year does not always correspond to the actual sampling period. Seasonal variation could also have influenced the observed pattern. Therefore, further studies covering different regions of Italy and multiple sampling seasons, with larger sample sizes, are recommended to confirm this tendency and better understand geographical trends in Campylobacter occurrence in Italian chicken. The apparent decrease might also reflect the progressive implementation of stricter biosecurity measures, improved hygienic practices, and better control systems at different stages of the production chain (farm, slaughterhouse, and retail) aimed at reducing contamination with foodborne pathogens (Newell et al. ; Chowdhury et al. ).
Among Campylobacter spp., C. jejuni is the species responsible for most of the human infections, followed by C. coli, with an increasing resistance to clinically important antibiotics (Sheppard et al. ; Igwaran and Okoh ; Poudel et al. ; Tedersoo et al. ; Marotta et al. ). According to Zerbato et al. (2024), 83.7% of human cases in Italy were caused by C. jejuni followed by C. coli (13.5%) and C. fetus (0.6%). The same finding was observed in the Netherlands, whereas most of human infection cases were accounted for by consuming poultry and undercooked meat more for C. jejuni infections than C. coli infections (Doorduyn et al. ). We observed that these two species were remarkably more prevalent than the other ones after the identification of isolates obtained from contaminated samples. Indeed, almost the totality of the isolates obtained from chicken samples in Italy could be ascribed to C. jejuni or coli. This finding is consistent with previous investigations (Suzuki et al. ; Je et al. ) and confirms the frequent circulation of Campylobacter strains with pathogenic potential for human infection in the chicken production sector.

5

## Conclusion
This comprehensive review summarized the occurrence of Campylobacter spp. along the chicken food chain in Italy, considering different matrices. The detection rate of Campylobacter species that are pathogenic for humans was remarkable in all the analysed matrices. This confirms the potential exposure of Italian consumers to these zoonotic microorganisms through the consumption of chicken products and their relevance in terms of public health perspectives. Furthermore, this systematic review might be an important source of information for eventual QMRAs. Indeed, future QMRAs could provide us with an estimate of the risk associated with the consumption of the different chicken products and explore potential intervention measures at the various steps to assure adequate food safety standards for the consumers.
