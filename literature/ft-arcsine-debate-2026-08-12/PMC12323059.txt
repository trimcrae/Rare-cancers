## Background
Breast cancer is the most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer mortality among women in Ethiopia, accounting for 32% of new cancer cases and 17.6% of cancer deaths. Despite its growing burden, comprehensive data on survival rates and contributing factors remain limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize existing data on breast cancer survival in Ethiopia and identify key determinants influencing outcomes.

## Methods
A comprehensive systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and CINAHL to identify studies on breast cancer survival in Ethiopia published between January 2014 and August 2024. Eligible studies were screened and assessed for quality. A random-effects model was used to estimate pooled 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates and examine factors associated with survival.

## Results
This review included 15 cohort studies with 6,232 breast cancer patients. The reported median age of patients ranged from 39.0 to 61.0 years, with median survival times varying between 10.0 and 58.7 months. Pooled 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 92.0% (95% CI: 85.0, 97.0), 66.0% (95% CI: 52.0, 78.0), and 22.0% (95% CI: 8.0, 40.0), respectively. Rural residence (AHR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.15, 2.17), advanced-stage diagnosis (AHR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.86, 3.52), and presence of comorbidity (AHR = 1.54, 95%CI: 1.20, 1.98) were associated with higher mortality, while initiation of hormonal therapy (AHR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.30, 0.61) was associated with improved survival.

## Conclusion
While short-term survival among breast cancer patients in Ethiopia is favorable, the 5-year survival rate remains significantly lower than regional and global estimates. Strengthening health system capacity and enhancing public awareness are critical to promoting early detection, ensuring timely treatment, and improving long-term outcomes.

## Background
Breast cancer has emerged as the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide, overtaking lung cancer in 2020, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths reported. It is the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality among women worldwide, accounting for one in four cancer cases and one in six cancer deaths globally [ , ]. Since 1990, global breast cancer incidence has risen by 1.44% annually across all age groups, with the highest increase in women under 50. Global breast cancer mortality has similarly risen by 0.23% annually, with the Middle East and North Africa showing the highest increase at 2.38%, even after adjusting for life expectancy and fertility rates. While mortality rates have declined in Europe and North America, they continue to rise in all other regions [ ]. 
Breast cancer incidence is nearly double in high-income countries compared to lower-income regions, yet mortality is 17% higher in the latter [ ]. In Europe and North America, breast cancer mortality began to decline between 1988 and 1996, achieving a 39% reduction by 2015 due to advances in early detection and treatment. In contrast, breast cancer mortality in developing countries resembles that of the pre-screening era in the United States (before 1976), accounting for 60% of global deaths. This disparity is largely due to limited resources, advanced-stage diagnosis, and poor access to screening and treatment [ , ]. In 2020, Africa saw an estimated 186,598 breast cancer cases and 85,787 deaths, with significant regional variation. Breast cancer was the leading cause of cancer deaths among women in Northern and Western Africa, and the second after cervical cancer in Middle and Eastern Africa. Late-stage diagnoses, with over 70% of patients identified at advanced stages, significantly contribute to high mortality rates. These rates are further exacerbated by limited access to treatment and the prevalence of aggressive tumor subtypes [ , ]. 
Although Sub-Saharan Africa has a relatively lower incidence of breast cancer, it accounts for 15% of global breast cancer deaths, with alarmingly low survival rates [ ]. Ethiopia exemplifies these regional challenges. According to the Global Cancer Observatory, Ethiopia had an estimated 16,904 new breast cancer cases in 2022, which constituted 21.0% of all cancer diagnoses and 32% of cancers among women, with 9,626 deaths representing 17.6% of the country’s cancer fatalities [ ]. However, with only one population-based cancer registry that lacks comprehensive coverage, particularly in rural areas, and with limited mortality data, the actual cancer incidence and mortality rates are likely still underestimated, a trend observed across many African countries [ – ]. 
Despite the growing burden of breast cancer, comprehensive data on the survival rate and its determinants in Ethiopia remain limited. Addressing this gap is essential for developing targeted interventions and improving breast cancer outcomes. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesize evidence on survival rates and key prognostic factors among breast cancer patients in Ethiopia.

## Method
This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ] (see Additional file 1) and the protocol was registered with PROSPERO (Registration No. CRD42024574529).

## Information source and search strategy
A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and CINAHL to identify studies on breast cancer survival in Ethiopia. The search strategy combined MeSH terms and free-text terms related to breast cancer, survival outcomes, and Ethiopia, using appropriate Boolean operators (AND/OR). Full search strings for each database are provided in Additional file 2. We also screened the reference lists of included studies and used Google Scholar to retrieve relevant articles not identified through the initial database searches.

## Eligibility criteria
This review included studies conducted in Ethiopia or involving Ethiopian breast cancer patients that reported survival outcomes as median overall survival (in months), survival rates (1-year, 3-year, or 5-year proportions), or prognostic factors with hazard ratios as effect sizes. Eligible studies were longitudinal, with either retrospective or prospective follow-up, and published between January 1, 2014, and August 19, 2024. We limited the review to the past 10 years to ensure the findings reflect recent survival patterns and current healthcare practices in Ethiopia. No language restrictions were applied during the search. Studies reporting only metastasis-free survival (MFS) or disease-free survival (DFS), which capture only recurrence or progression, were excluded as they do not reflect mortality outcomes. Reviews, abstracts, case reports, and editorials were also excluded.

## Selection process
All records from the initial search were imported into EndNote 21, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (A.T. and M.F.) independently screened titles and abstracts based on predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were retrieved and then assessed for final inclusion. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer (B.D.) was invited when consensus could not be reached.

## Data extraction
Two reviewers (A.T. and M.F.) independently extracted data using a standardized Excel sheet. After extraction, accuracy and consistency between reviewers were evaluated, with discrepancies resolved through cross-referencing the original study and discussion. A third reviewer (B.D.) was invited when consensus could not be reached. Extracted data included the author’s name, publication year, study location, study design, sample size, median participant age, median survival (months), survival rates (proportions), and prognostic factors.

## Quality assessment
The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cohort Studies. The checklist evaluates key methodological domains, including the selection of participants, comparability of groups, identification and control of confounding factors, measurement of outcomes, and adequacy of follow-up. Each domain is scored with a maximum of one point, resulting in a total possible score of 11 [ ]. Two Authors (A.T. and B.D.) assessed each study, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (M.F.) when necessary. No studies were excluded based on quality.

## Outcomes
This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated two primary outcomes. The first outcome was the overall survival (OS) rate of breast cancer patients, reported as the proportion of patients surviving at 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years. These survival rates were used to derive pooled survival estimates across the included studies. The second outcome was the identification of predictors consistently associated with breast cancer survival, as reported in three or more studies. We estimated the pooled Hazard ratios for survival predictors using adjusted hazard ratios (AHRs) derived from the multivariate Cox proportional hazard model. We examined four predictors of survival, which were consistently defined across included studies: place of residence, disease stage, comorbidity, and the use of hormonal therapy. The place of residence was categorized as urban or rural based on patient reports or chart records. The breast cancer stage was classified into early stages (I and II) and advanced stages (III and IV). Comorbidity was defined as the presence of one or more chronic medical conditions, such as HIV, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or asthma. Lastly, the use of hormonal therapy refers to patients receiving any treatment with aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole or letrozole) or tamoxifen.

## Data synthesis
The cleaned dataset was imported and analyzed using STATA version 17.0. All meta-analyses were conducted using a random-effects model with the DerSimonian–Laird (DL) estimator to account for between-study variability. Survival proportions were pooled using the metaprop command, applying the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variance. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled using the metan command. Hazard ratios were log-transformed prior to pooling, and pooled estimates were back-transformed for interpretation. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values above 50% considered indicative of moderate to substantial heterogeneity. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates, with deviations greater than 10% from the overall estimate considered indicative of sensitivity.

## Assessment of publication bias
Due to the limited number of included studies, visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test were not performed, as these methods are underpowered and unreliable when fewer than 10 studies are analyzed [ ]. Instead, small-study effects were evaluated using the Doi plot and Luis Furuya–Kanamori (LFK) index, which demonstrate greater sensitivity and diagnostic accuracy than traditional funnel plots in meta-analyses with a small number of studies. An LFK index <|1| indicates no asymmetry; values between|1| and|2| suggest minor asymmetry, while values >|2| indicate major asymmetry [ ]. These analyses were conducted using MetaXL version 5.3.

## Ethical considerations
Ethical approval and informed consent were not required, as this study involved the analysis of previously published data.

## Results

## Study selection
A total of 701 studies were identified through database searches: Web of Science (n = 173), PubMed (n = 128), Embase (n = 232), Scopus (n = 135), CINAHL (n = 28), and Google Scholar (n = 5). After removing 393 duplicates, 308 studies remained for screening, of which 277 were excluded. Full texts were sought for 31 studies, but one could not be retrieved. Of the 30 assessed for eligibility, 15 were excluded (study population, n = 2; outcome of interest, n = 11; study design, n = 2). Ultimately, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review (Fig.  ).

 

## Characteristics of included studies
This systematic review included 6,232 patients with breast cancer from 15 cohort studies (Table  ), of which two were prospective and 13 were retrospective. These studies were conducted across different regions of Ethiopia, including Amhara (n = 5), Addis Ababa (n = 4), Oromia (n = 3), Tigray (n = 1), and Sidama (n = 1), ensuring broad regional coverage. Quality assessment using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist demonstrated good methodological quality, with scores ranging from 8 to 9. No studies were excluded based on quality.
In most of the included studies, participants were predominantly urban residents, with the proportion of urban women ranging from 57.3 to 85.5%. Three studies did not report urban-rural residence, one study included only rural women [ ], and one had a predominantly rural sample [ ]. All studies included patients across all stages of breast cancer. The median age of women with breast cancer, reported in 12 studies, ranged from 39.0 to 61.0 years, with 10 studies reporting a median age below 50 years. The overall median survival ranged from 10.0 to 58.7 months. Of the 15 studies, 10 were included in the meta-analysis to assess survival rates and prognostic factors.

 

## Breast cancer survival
A total of seven studies were included to estimate the pooled 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival outcomes among breast cancer patients in Ethiopia. A random-effects model was used to account for between-study heterogeneity. The pooled 1-year survival rate, based on four studies, was 92.0% (95% CI: 85.0, 97.0%; I² = 93.4%, p < 0.001). The 3-year survival rate, based on five studies, was 66.0% (95% CI: 52.0,78.0; I² = 97.1%, p < 0.001). The 5-year survival rate, based on four studies, was 22.0% (95% CI: 8.0, 40.0%; I² = 98.8%, p < 0.001). Details of the individual studies and corresponding survival estimates are presented in Table  .

 

## Predictors of breast cancer survival
This meta-analysis identified place of residence, stage at diagnosis, receipt of hormonal therapy, and presence of comorbidities as key predictors of breast cancer survival in Ethiopia. Pooled analysis from five studies showed that women residing in rural areas had a significantly higher risk of mortality compared to those in urban settings (AHR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.15, 2.17; I² = 44.8%, p = 0.123) (Fig.  ). Stage at diagnosis was also strongly associated with survival outcomes. Based on four studies, women diagnosed at advanced stages (III or IV) had more than twice the risk of mortality compared to those diagnosed at early stages (I or II) (AHR = 2.56; 95% CI: 1.86, 3.52; I² = 0.0%, p = 0.443) (Fig.  ).

 

 

Hormonal therapy was significantly associated with improved survival. Based on four studies, women who received hormonal therapy had a 57% lower risk of death compared to those who did not receive hormonal treatment (AHR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.30, 0.61; I² = 65.3%, p = 0.034) (Fig.  ). The presence of comorbidities was also a significant predictor of high mortality. Based on three studies, women with one or more comorbid conditions had a 54% higher risk of mortality compared to those without comorbidities (AHR = 1.54; 95% CI: 1.20, 1.98; I² = 0.0%, p = 0.916) (Fig.  ).

 

 

## Sensitivity analysis
Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses showed that the 1-year and 3-year survival estimates were stable, with all deviations from the pooled estimates remaining below 10%, suggesting a low influence of individual studies. In contrast, the 5-year survival estimate demonstrated sensitivity to specific studies. Excluding Areri et al. (2018) reduced the pooled estimate by 32%, while the omission of Marine and Mengistie (2020) or Misganaw et al. (2023) increased it by approximately 19%, highlighting their substantial impact on the overall result. However, heterogeneity (I²) remained high across all leave-one-out analyses, indicating that no single study was the primary contributor to between-study variability. (see Additional file 3).

## Publication bias and small-study effects
Doi plots and the Luis Furuya–Kanamori (LFK) index, which offer greater sensitivity than traditional funnel plots in meta-analyses with a small number of studies, were utilized to assess small-study effects. The LFK index indicated major asymmetry for 1-year survival (–2.79), but no evidence of asymmetry for 3-year (–0.13) and 5-year (–0.96) survival (see Additional file 4). However, due to the limited number of studies per outcome (n = 4), these results should be interpreted with caution.

## Discussion
Ethiopia, like many developing countries, is undergoing a significant epidemiological transition, with a growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cancer [ , ]. In 2022, an estimated 80,334 new cancer cases and 54,698 cancer-related deaths were reported in Ethiopia [ ]. Breast cancer accounted for approximately one-third of all female cancer cases and one-fifth of cancer-related deaths among women, making it the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in the country [ , , ]. However, the absence of a national cancer registry limits the availability of reliable data on breast cancer incidence and survival, thereby constraining the development of evidence-based cancer control strategies. Given this gap, we conducted a meta-analysis to synthesize available evidence on breast cancer survival in Ethiopia and to inform national efforts aimed at improving cancer outcomes.
The pooled 1-year and 3-year survival rates were 92% and 66%, respectively, indicating relatively favorable short-term outcomes but a steep decline in survival over time. These rates are significantly higher than those reported by McCormack et al. (2020), who found 1-year and 3-year overall survival rates of 79% and 50% in a prospective cohort study conducted across eight hospitals in five sub-Saharan African countries [ ]. Similarly, our findings surpass those of Limenih et al. (2024), who reported 1-year and 3-year survival rates of 79% and 56% in their meta-analysis of survival in sub-Saharan Africa [ ]. These comparisons suggest that breast cancer patients in Ethiopia may have slightly better short-term survival outcomes than those observed in other sub-Saharan African regions. However, compared to the SURVCAN-3 study, which included 32 countries and reported 3-year survival rates ranging from 80.9 to 90.5% in Central and South America, 61.7–87.9% in Africa, and 56.0–93.9% in Asia, our findings fell at the lower end of the survival spectrum observed in Africa and Asia [ ]. 
However, long-term survival remains critically low. The pooled 5-year survival rate was 22%, which is substantially lower than estimates reported by other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including 58% in Sudan, 65% in Nigeria, and 66.4% in India [ – ]. It is also below the regional average of 40% for sub-Saharan Africa and the global average of 73% [ , ]. The disparity is even more pronounced when compared to high-income countries. According to the CONCORD-3 study, 5-year survival rates were approximately 90% in the United States, 88% in Canada, 89.5% in Australia, and 87% in New Zealand, while European countries ranged from 79% in Croatia to 93% in Cyprus [ , ]. This stark contrast with both regional and global survival likely reflects multifactorial barriers to care, including late-stage diagnosis, limited access to essential cancer therapies, fragmented referral systems, and inadequate follow-up, all of which compromise long-term outcomes. To address these gaps, Ethiopia has implemented strategies to expand cancer care, including nationwide efforts to raise public awareness and decentralize oncology services to regional hospitals [ , ]. Radiotherapy, previously available only at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, has expanded to three public facilities, with four additional centers under development. Chemotherapy is now provided in 24 public hospitals across the country, marking a major step toward improving equitable access to treatment [ ]. 
The sharp decline in 3- and 5-year survival rates in our study could be attributed to a combination of patient-related and health system-related barriers. While many patients survive the first year because of short-term disease control achieved through surgery and first-line chemotherapy, long-term outcomes are affected by several factors. Some patients discontinue follow-up after completing initial treatment, believing they are cured. Access to radiotherapy, which is essential for preventing recurrence and treating advanced disease, has historically been very limited, with only one treatment center serving the entire country until recent years [ ]. The absence of organized palliative and supportive care services also contributes to poor outcomes among patients with recurrent or metastatic disease [ , ]. These challenges together may explain the steep drop in survival beyond the first year after diagnosis.
Our meta-analysis also identified several key predictors of breast cancer survival in Ethiopia, including rural residence, cancer stage, comorbidities, and endocrine therapy. Rural residence was associated with a 58% increase in breast cancer mortality, reflecting the structural, socioeconomic, and cultural barriers that hinder timely diagnosis and treatment. Women in rural areas often face significant geographic and logistical challenges, including long travel distances, lack of nearby oncology services, high diagnostic costs, and fragmented referral systems [ – ]. Limited awareness about breast cancer further exacerbates delays in care-seeking [ – ]. Cultural beliefs and stigma also play a vital role. Breast cancer is often viewed as a spiritual affliction or punishment, and mastectomy is feared due to beliefs that it may lead to death, infertility, or spousal abandonment. As a result, many women hide symptoms and seek care from traditional healers, often postponing medical treatment until the disease has advanced [ ]. This finding aligns with studies from sub-Saharan Africa by McCormack et al. (2020) and Kim et al. (2024), which reported consistently higher breast cancer mortality among rural women [ , ]. Similar disparities have been observed in high-income countries, where a systematic review by Afshar et al. (2019) found that rural residence was frequently associated with poorer cancer survival outcomes [ ]. 
Stage at diagnosis emerged as a critical predictor of breast cancer survival, with advanced-stage disease associated with more than a twofold increase in mortality. This is consistent with findings from McCormack et al. (2020), who identified breast cancer stage as the most significant prognostic factor in a prospective cohort study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, where 3-year survival rates ranged from 80% for stage IIA to just 11% for stage IV disease [ ]. Similarly, Jhokru et al.(2021) reported a significant difference in 5-year survival rates, with early-stage patients showing a 62.5% survival probability compared to 35.8% for advanced-stage patients in a sub-Saharan African population-based study [ ]. These findings demonstrate the urgent need for public health interventions to address delayed presentations, particularly as two-thirds of breast cancer patients in Ethiopia are diagnosed at an advanced stage, where survival outcomes are significantly diminished [ ]. 
Comorbidity was also a significant predictor of poor breast cancer survival, with patients who had comorbidity experiencing a 54% increased risk of mortality. This finding is in line with previous studies that have demonstrated the detrimental impact of comorbidities on cancer survival. A study by Parés-Badell et al. (2017) in Spain, which included 8,273 cancer patients, 3,193 of whom had breast cancer, found that while comorbidity significantly predicted overall survival across various cancer types, its impact was more pronounced in breast cancer compared to colorectal and lung cancers [ ]. Similarly, our findings align with a meta-analysis on colorectal cancer survival conducted in Ethiopia, suggesting a consistent pattern of comorbidity’s impact on survival across different cancer types [ ]. A prospective multi-center cohort study by Ayeni et al. (2023), which included 3261 breast cancer patients participating in South African Breast Cancer and HIV Outcomes study, found that women with multimorbidity (≥2 other chronic conditions) had poorer overall survival at 3 years than women without multimorbidity (60.8% vs. 64.3%) [ ].
Conversely, we observed a 57% reduction in mortality risk among breast cancer patients treated with endocrine therapy, affirming the well-established efficacy of hormonal therapy in improving survival for hormone receptor-positive (ER/PR+) breast cancer patients. Joko-Fru et al. (2021) found that not receiving endocrine therapy led to a three-fold increase in mortality risk for breast cancer patients in Sub-Saharan Africa [ ]. Endocrine therapy remains the mainstay of treatment across all stages of hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, reducing recurrence in early stages, serving as adjuvant or neoadjuvant therapy in advanced stages, and controlling disease progression in metastatic breast cancer patients [ ]. Studies have shown that adjuvant tamoxifen reduces the annual odds of recurrence by 41% and the annual odds of death by 31% in patients with ER-positive breast cancer, regardless of chemotherapy use, age, menopausal status, or axillary lymph node (ALN) involvement [ ]. 
Taken together, these findings underscore the urgent need for an integrated national response to improve breast cancer survival in Ethiopia. One important strategic framework that can guide this response is the WHO Global Breast Cancer Initiative (GBCI), launched in 2021 to reduce global disparities in breast cancer outcomes. The initiative builds on the experience of high-income countries, where mortality rates have declined by 40% since the 1980 s, largely due to improvements in early detection, timely diagnosis, and access to comprehensive treatment. The GBCI is structured around three core pillars: health promotion and early detection, timely diagnosis, and access to multimodal treatment. It aims to reduce global breast cancer mortality by 2.5% annually by 2040, potentially averting an estimated 2.5 million deaths. To achieve this, the initiative has set specific targets: diagnosing 60% of cases at an early stage, completing diagnostic evaluation within 60 days, and ensuring that at least 80% of patients complete a full course of multimodal treatment [ ]. 
In line with the global WHO priorities, Ethiopia recently launched its first National Guideline for Early Diagnosis and Timely Breast Cancer Management (2024–2028), which outlines strategies to expand screening coverage, decentralize cancer services, and strengthen referral systems. It also aims to reduce stigma and promote breast health by educating school-age children on common cancers and providing women with information on risk factors, symptoms, and curability [ ]. These policies represent a crucial step toward reducing breast cancer mortality, but their success relies on sustained political commitment, adequate resource allocation, and robust monitoring to ensure equitable implementation nationwide.

## Strengths and limitations
This systematic review and meta-analysis provide the most up-to-date synthesis of breast cancer survival and its predictors in Ethiopia, based on studies published in the past decade to ensure clinical and policy relevance. A comprehensive search strategy, meticulous data extraction by a physician and public health professional with clinical experience in breast cancer care in Ethiopia, and robust statistical analysis enhance the methodological rigor and contextual relevance of this review. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. Substantial heterogeneity in survival estimates was observed, likely reflecting differences in study populations (such as age, place of residence, and socioeconomic status), clinical characteristics (including stage at diagnosis and molecular subtype), and access to oncology services. Although subgroup analyses were not feasible due to the limited number of studies, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses helped partially mitigate this limitation. Traditional methods for assessing publication bias, such as funnel plots and Egger’s regression test, were not suitable given the small number of included studies; instead, small-study effects were assessed using the Doi plot and LFK index. Lastly, the role of molecular subtypes, treatment modalities, and sociodemographic factors could not be examined due to insufficient and inconsistent data. Despite these limitations, this review provides valuable evidence to guide future population-based cohort studies, inform clinical decision-making, and support cancer control efforts in Ethiopia.

## Conclusion
Our analysis revealed that long-term breast cancer survival in Ethiopia remains critically low, with a 5-year survival rate of just 22%, which is substantially below regional and global estimates. We also observed marked disparities based on stage at diagnosis, place of residence, comorbidities, and treatment status. These findings highlight the urgent need for public health strategies that enhance awareness, promote early detection, and ensure timely access to treatment by decentralizing oncology services to reach underserved populations. Establishing a national cancer registry is essential for monitoring the cancer burden, tracking survival trends, and identifying geographic disparities. Moreover, future population-based cohort studies with standardized data collection are necessary to generate robust evidence that informs cancer control efforts. The recent launch of Ethiopia’s National Breast Cancer Guideline represents a timely strategy to advance these priorities and to stay on track with the goals of the Global Breast Cancer Initiative. Its effective implementation will require sustained political commitment, adequate resource allocation, and rigorous monitoring and evaluation.
