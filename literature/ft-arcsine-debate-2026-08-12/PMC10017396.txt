## Background:
Endoscopically assisted craniofacial surgery (EACS) has numerous advantages over traditional, open approaches, such as fronto-orbital advancement in treating nonsyndromic craniosynostosis. However, several articles report high reoperation rates in syndromic patients treated with EACS. This meta-analysis and review examines undesirable outcome rates (UORs), defined as reoperation or Whitaker category III/IV, in syndromic patients undergoing primary EACS compared with procedures that actively expand the cranial vault.

## Methods:
PubMed and Embase were searched in June 2022 to identify all articles reporting primary reoperation or Whitaker outcomes for syndromic patients undergoing cranial vault expanding surgery or suturectomy. A meta-analysis of proportions was performed comparing UORs, and a trim-and-fill adjustment method was used to validate sensitivity and assess publication bias.

## Results:
A total of 721 articles were screened. Five EACS articles (83 patients) and 22 active approach articles (478 patients) met inclusion criteria. Average UORs for EACS and active approaches were 26% (14%–38%) and 20% (13%–28%), respectively (P = 0.18). Reoperation occurred earlier in EACS patients (13.7 months postprimary surgery versus 37.1 months for active approaches, P = 0.003). Relapse presentations and reason for reoperation were also reviewed. Subjectively, EACS UORs were higher in all syndromes except Apert, and Saethre-Chotzen patients had the highest UOR for both approaches.

## Conclusions:
There was no statistically significant increase in UORs among syndromic patients treated with EACS compared with traditional approaches, although EACS patients required revision significantly sooner. Uncertainties regarding the long-term efficacy of EACS in children with syndromic craniosynostosis should be revisited as more data become available.

## Takeaways
Question: Are undesirable outcome rates (UORs), defined as reoperation or Whitaker category III/IV, comparable in syndromic patients undergoing endoscopically assisted craniofacial surgery compared with procedures that actively expand the cranial vault?
Findings: Average UORs for endoscopically assisted craniofacial surgery and active approaches were 26% (14%–38%) and 20% (13%–28%), respectively (P = 0.18). Meta-regression showed no effect of operative age or follow-up length (after 2 years) on UOR.
Meaning: There was no statistically significant increase in UORs among syndromic patients treated with endoscopically assisted craniofacial surgery compared with traditional approaches.

## INTRODUCTION
Craniosynostosis, defined as the premature closure of a cranial suture, is one of the most common craniofacial malformations in children, occurring in 1:300 to 1:2300 births. – Surgical release of the implicated suture(s) allows for unrestricted brain growth, improves the craniofacial appearance, and minimizes cranial injury secondary to elevated intracranial pressure (ICP). Surgical approaches to craniosynostosis may be categorized into active or passive interventions. In active approaches, the skull is actively enlarged either immediately via cranial vault remodeling (CVR) or gradually with spring-mediated or traditional distraction osteogenesis (DO). More recently, EACS with postoperative helmet therapy has allowed surgeons to endoscopically release affected sutures, which permits passive reshaping of the skull via brain expansion.
While craniosynostosis most often presents as isolated single suture synostosis, approximately 9%–27% of cases are associated with genetic syndromes, most commonly Apert, Crouzon, and Saethre-Chotzen, although there are over 100 syndromes associated with craniosynostosis. , – Syndromic craniosynostosis often involves multiple sutures, resulting in a more severe deformity and challenging reconstruction. As a result, syndromic patients are reported to have greater intraoperative transfusion needs, greater reoperation requirements, poorer cosmesis, and worse intellectual outcomes. – 
The application of passive approaches to syndromic craniosynostosis has not been reviewed to date. A 2018 meta-analysis from Goyal and colleagues demonstrated decreased blood loss, operative time, length of stay, and rates of perioperative complications, reoperation, and transfusion in endoscopic approaches, but did not stratify by syndromic status. The purpose of the present study was to inform clinical practice by performing a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing reoperation rates and Whitaker outcomes of syndromic craniosynostosis patients treated with passive and active surgical approaches. We hypothesized that passive approaches would display higher reoperation rates and worse Whitaker outcomes than active approaches due to lack of overcorrection.

## METHODS

## Literature Search
Our systematic review was conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Two electronic searches were performed on the PubMed (available from http://www.pubmed.gov) and Embase (available from https://www.embase.com) databases using the terms described in Table . Studies published from January 1990 to June 2022 were included. Bibliographies of articles from the first search (“PubMed/Embase 1” in Table ) were reviewed, and missing search terms were added for the second search (“PubMed/Embase 2” in Table ). The two searches were compiled, and duplicates were removed in Mendeley Desktop V1.19.8. Institutional review board approval was not necessary for this systematic review and meta-analysis.
 

## Study Selection
Articles that did not mention calvarial reconstruction, or which only focused on midface correction, on patients with craniosynostosis in the abstract were removed. To be included, an article must have had (1) Whitaker outcomes or reoperation rates presented such that the primary UOR could be calculated for syndromic patients specifically (rates that included nonsyndromic patients or nonprimary procedures were excluded), (2) consecutive inclusion of patients (small case series and case reports were excluded), (3) a unique study population, (4) a mean or median follow-up of over 24 months, and (5) an article or abstract written in English.

## Data Collection
One author (JG) independently screened abstracts for relevance, and two authors (JG and JP) independently reviewed selected articles for article type, surgery type, age of surgery, syndrome type, UOR, and follow-up length (one reviewer assessed each study). Surgery type was categorized as either active (examples include FOA, CVR, DO, spring-assisted craniectomy, and variants) or passive (EACS without distraction or springs). Primary UOR was defined as an additional, unplanned surgery or undesirable cosmetic outcome after a patient’s first surgical treatment for syndromic craniosynostosis. Whitaker outcomes III/IV were considered undesirable, even when no additional surgery was undergone or planned. Articles without reported follow-up data were excluded.

## Data Analysis
All analyses were performed in R statistical software (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Summary statistics were calculated using individual study UORs. A meta-analysis of proportions was performed, in which the UORs were weighted by the inverse of their sampling variance using random-effects models. Proportions were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation proportions. Graph 1. http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/C461.) (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation proportions. Graph 2. http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/C462.)
Confidence intervals for individual studies were calculated using a normal approximation interval based on the summary measure (ie, reoperation events and number of study observations). Confidence intervals estimating the between-study variance (ie, τ² and τ) and statistical significance were determined using Cochran Q test. The between-study variance, τ², was calculated using restricted-maximum likelihood estimator methods. Subgroup analysis was performed to compare effects between active and passive surgical techniques and test for a significant difference between the two approaches. Pooled statistics were also calculated. A forest plot of the results was created to demonstrate 95% confidence intervals and relative weights of each study. For the overall summary statistic, mean proportion and 95% confidence interval are represented by the middle and width of the diamond, respectively.
Heterogeneity was assessed with the I2 statistic, and random-effects models were utilized to account for methodological variation across studies. Although funnel plots are often used to assess publication bias, they are not performed in meta-analyses comparing subgroups because asymmetry is most likely a result of multiple subgroup inclusion. A trim-and-fill adjustment method was instead used to validate sensitivity and assess publication bias.
A weighted average age of each subgroup was calculated using means or medians. All studies using passive surgical techniques reported mean or median age. However, six studies utilizing active surgical techniques included in the meta-analysis of proportions did not report the mean or median age and were excluded from age-related analyses. A meta-regression of the remaining studies was performed to examine the relationship between age, follow-up duration (excluding studies with mean/median follow-up under 2 years), study nationality (US versus international), and surgery type (DO/spring versus CVR in the active group) as covariates and effect sizes.

## RESULTS

## Results of Data Search
The initial literature search did not yield all eligible studies (which was determined after reviewing the bibliographies of included articles), and so the search terms were expanded and the search repeated (Table ). A third literature search was performed in June 2022 (using the more-comprehensive search terms), so that more recent articles were included. The combined literature searches yielded 721 unique articles and abstracts. After screening by authors JG and JP, 338 were selected for full-text evaluation, of which 30 met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final cohort (Fig. ). One presentation, which was given within the search date range, was added later because the abstract was not available in PubMed or Embase at the time of search.
 

## Sensitivity Analysis
For sensitivity analysis, we calculated trim-and-fill-adjusted estimates and compared these with the observed estimates. The difference between the two estimates was negligible, indicating the observed estimates are valid and not meaningfully impacted by significant publication bias. Additionally, the trim-and-fill method did not indicate a need to remove any of the included studies for concerns of individual study bias and only added one with an incidence of reoperations around zero. Egger testing could not be performed due to the small study effects in the presence of subgroups.

## Meta-analysis of Undesirable Outcomes
The average age (± SD) of patients undergoing surgery was 19.0 ± 0.5 months for active procedures (reported for 245 of 478 patients) and 2.8 ± 0.7 months (reported in 83 of 83 patients) for EACS. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 3, which provides a summary of study characteristics. http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/C463.) Twenty-five studies were retrospective, one was prospective, and one was mixed retrospective/prospective. All articles included patients from a single institution. Four studies – included in Supplemental Digital Content 3 were not included in the meta-analysis due to potential patient overlap. These studies are included in Supplemental Digital Content 3 because they are referenced in the syndromic-specific UOR review (the article disqualifying them from UOR meta-analysis is not included in syndrome-specific review).
A minimum follow-up of 2 years (mean or median) was required for inclusion in UOR meta-analysis (n = 27). Five studies and 83 patients were included in the passive subgroup (EACS), while 22 studies and 478 patients were included in the active subgroup. The values reported in the forest plot were obtained using an adaptation of an analysis of variance to first obtain subgroup effects, which were then compared using Cochran’s Q-statistic (Fig. ).
 Passive surgical techniques (EACS) displayed a pooled UOR of 26% (95% CI, 14%–38%), which was not significantly greater than the pooled 20% UOR for active techniques (95% CI, 13%–28%; P = 0.18). Interestingly, the two-year cutoff requirement resulted in a decreased UOR for passive procedures. Thirty-one publications (two passive , and 29 active) were excluded because the follow-up inclusion criterion of 2+ years was not met. When these articles were re-added, the passive surgery UOR increased to 38% (95% CI, 19%–57%), whereas the active surgery UOR decreased to 12% (95% CI, 7%–17%; P = 0.0046).
Reason for reoperation requirement was also gathered from included articles. Of the passive patients, reason for reoperation was described as necessary without elaboration in 21 of 25 and aesthetic in four of 25. Of the active patients, reason for reoperation was functional (to relieve intracranial hypertension) in 34 of 106, aesthetic in 45 of 106, and necessary without elaboration in the remaining 27 of 106.

## Meta-regression of Covariates
Subgroup analysis revealed that the heterogeneity primarily existed in the active approach group (67% versus 21%). A mixed effects model was used to gauge the impact of several covariates, including surgery type (DO/spring versus FOA/CVR), nationality (USA versus international), age, and follow-up length (after 2 years) on heterogeneity. This analysis yielded significant (<0.05) estimates of 0.38 and -0.59 for surgery type and nationality, indicating that inclusion of DO/spring data increased the reoperation rate, and inclusion of international articles decreased it. Age and follow-up length (after 2 years) had little effect (estimates of -0.0039 and 0.0016, respectively). The calculated R² was 100%, indicating that 100% of the heterogeneity in our meta-analysis resulted from the covariates above.

## Undesirable Outcome Rate by Syndrome Type
Of the 27 articles meeting inclusion criteria for undesirable outcome analysis, 16 articles (three passive, 13 active) included outcome data on individual syndromes, the results of which are included in Table . The syndrome breakdown in Riordan 2020 was not available, so articles previously excluded due to overlapping patient populations – were included in the subgroup table. Patients in the Riesel 2022 study undergoing FOA were still excluded due to potential overlap with an included study. 
 Meta-analysis of UOR by syndrome was not conducted due to small patient samples in the passive category. Interestingly, the UOR for Apert patients was lower in the passive category, although these patients were from a single study. UORs for the remaining syndromes were higher among patients undergoing EACS, although sample sizes are small.

## Review of Relapse Presentation
Thirteen articles commented on age and/or presentation of relapse after primary surgery (Table ). Three – of the 13 did not meet the follow-up inclusion criterion and are included separately at the bottom of Table . Two , of the 13 were disqualified from UOR analysis by Riordan 2020, but because Riordan 2020 is not included here, these articles were added as well.
 Fundoscopy was the most common method of gauging relapse. Of the 128 patients who underwent reoperation in this study, fundoscopic data were available for 30, of which 22 exhibited papilledema. Twenty-one patients receiving reoperation had CT data reported, of which 17 displayed signs of resynostosis or increased ICP such as copper-beaten appearance, ventriculomegaly, or premature ossification. Eleven of the 17 reoperation patients with head circumference data showed stagnant head growth, and subjective signs such as headache or irritability were present in seven of 13 reoperation patients with such data reported. Secondary craniosynostosis of previously opened sutures was present in three of the seven reoperation patients where this information was available.
In the four EACS articles , , , which reported precise reoperation times, reoperation occurred on average 13.7 (95% CI, 7.4–20) months postsurgery (n = 10 patients), whereas active surgery required reoperation 37.1 (95% CI, 24.5–49.7) months postsurgery on average (20 patients, five articles , – ) (P = 0.003) (Table ).

## DISCUSSION
This is the first meta-analysis to compare reoperation rates in syndromic patients undergoing passive (EACS) and active procedures for craniosynostosis. Although EACS patients experienced a pooled UOR of 26% (6% higher than the pooled UOR for active procedures), the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.18). It should be considered that patients selected for EACS often have less severe craniosynostosis compared with patients undergoing CVR/FOA. However, EACS is comparatively safer in terms of blood loss and operative length, and patients can undergo EACS at a younger age. EACS may therefore be viewed as an earlier opportunity for cranial decompression, allowing for both brain and skull development, and hopefully avoiding the need for additional craniofacial surgery until future Le Fort III or monobloc frontofacial advancement. In the analysis presented here, passive approaches were performed over a year earlier on average than active ones.
Active procedure UORs decreased without the follow-up restriction, as was expected, likely because relapse of surgically released sutures can take years to present, especially after open procedures which overcorrect. In our analysis, reoperation for open procedures occurred on average over 3 years after primary surgery (37.1 months). This differed significantly from the average EACS reoperation timing of 13.7 months postprimary surgery. Without follow-up restrictions, the open surgery UOR decreased substantially, and the difference between passive and active UORs became significant, highlighting the importance of adequate follow-up in studies reporting active surgery outcomes. The data presented here support the need for extended follow-up periods of at least 2 years in future studies on active approaches to syndromic craniosynostosis.
The average age of operation in the active group was 19 months, which is later than typical in the United States. Reviews and meta-analyses similar to our own report later and more variable age ranges. Richardson et al compared anterior versus posterior CVR, and although a pooled age of operation was not calculated, five of the seven included articles had an average age greater than 19 months. Goyal et al similarly did not calculate a pooled average but reported a range of 5–29.5 months. Mundinger et al reported median ages of 16.8–26 months for over 800 patients across approximately 50 studies. Several articles describe syndromic patients undergoing operation at a later age than nonsyndromic patients, which likely explains the discrepancy. In Seruya et al, for example, nonsyndromic patients underwent reconstruction at 10.6 months, whereas the average for syndromic patients was 19.3 months. Similarly, in Han et al, nonsyndromic patients underwent open repair at 15.5 months on average compared with 41.4 months for syndromic patients. Importantly, meta-regression in the present study showed that age of operation did not influence results.
Overlapping patient populations in studies reporting EACS outcomes is a shortcoming in the literature. Five of the 10 eligible EACS articles are from the same group at Boston Children’s Hospital (a short summary of the potential overlap is available in Supplemental Digital Content 4). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 4, which displays articles from Boston Children’s Hospital, potential patient population overlap review. http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/C464.) As pioneers in EACS, UORs reported in recent articles , are among the lowest in the literature. In our review, Riordan 2020 was chosen over the other options because the patient population was largest and not limited to a single syndrome or suture. The relatively low UORs from this group suggest that surgeons’ skill and experience may contribute to the need for reoperation among syndromic EACS patients, especially in the context of frequent 50%+ revision rates in other studies. , – , 
Subgroup analysis revealed the heterogeneity in our model primarily existed in the active approach group (67% versus 21%). The inclusion of DO/spring procedures with CVR/FOA and the combination of US and international articles were significant sources of heterogeneity, and these two factors accounted for 100% of the heterogeneity in the meta-analysis (R² = 100%). Although use of random effect models takes heterogeneity into consideration in the calculation of effect estimates, we acknowledge this amount of heterogeneity is a limitation. While it would be interesting to investigate CVR versus DO/springs versus strip suturectomy with helmet as separate categories, limitations of the current literature would yield an even lower powered study than our current effort. Rather, we chose to combine CVR with spring/DO groups as “active” because of the rapid expansion of the skull. The endoscopic/helmet group was assigned the “passive” moniker, as it relies on slower brain growth to expand the skull under the guidance of a molding helmet. The spring/DO and suturectomy/helmet groups share some similarities; both are procedures requiring less operative/recovery times, less blood loss, and less morbidity relative to CVR. Some may argue therefore that these groups are a better fit. However, the underlying question we are interested in answering involves the longevity of improved cephalocranial dynamics for patients with syndromic forms of craniosynostosis. Specifically, we hypothesized that the “passive” suturectomy/helmet approaches may lead to higher rates of secondary surgery given the lack of overcorrection.
There are several additional limitations to this study. The data were derived almost entirely from retrospective reports, which carry biases inherent to their designs. Using a trim-and-fill adjustment method, we found no significant evidence of publication bias in our study. The absence of internal controls within studies allows for inter-institutional factors to confound UORs and is another limitation. An alternate analysis might pool odds or risk ratios of UOR from procedures undergone at the same institution. This approach presents with separate confounders, however, since the decision to undergo EACS versus open procedures is usually nonrandom. Furthermore, only four articles , , , (three of which were excluded due to potential patient overlap or inadequate follow-up) reported outcomes from both approaches, and so such an analysis is not possible with the literature currently available. Finally, Whitaker outcome and need for reoperation is often subjective, and in most of the EACS patients in this study, the reason for reoperation was not explicitly stated beyond that the attending surgeon deemed it necessary.
Two interesting but ultimately unnecessary paragraphs were removed here to meet the word limit. The first paragraph described the caveat that average follow-ups often included nonsyndromic patients. This is still noted in the legend of Table (averages including nonsyndromic patients are marked with asterisks) and does not influence results/conclusions. The second paragraph described future directions, which were somewhat self-evident (more research from other hospitals, prospective data, longer-follow up, etc).

## CONCLUSIONS
At present, the literature does not show a statistically significant difference in the rate of UORs among patients undergoing passive (EACS) procedures for syndromic craniosynostosis compared with active procedures (26% versus 20%, P = 0.18), although passive approaches required reoperation significantly sooner (13.7 versus 37.1 months postprimary surgery, P = 0.003). Most of the EACS data come from just two hospitals, which limits the generalizability of these results. Ultimately, the relative risk of needing additional surgery after EACS in syndromic patients is a topic that should be revisited when additional research becomes available. Whether this potential drawback offsets the proven advantages of EACS, such as lower blood loss, hospital stay, cost, and operation time, has yet to be determined.
