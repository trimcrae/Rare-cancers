KushwahaAnshul Kumar DeborahArpitha Anbu AsokanAbirami TripathiGunjan ] Infertility is a global health issue affecting millions of people of reproductive age worldwide, and it is widely recognised as both a social and public health concern, though it remains under-prioritised in many countries.[ ] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), infertility affects approximately one in six adults globally, with a lifetime prevalence of 17.8% in high-income countries and 16.5% in low- and middle-income countries.[ ] Infertility is associated with significant psychological distress and economic challenges for affected individuals.[ ]
South Asia, home to more than one-fifth of the world’s population, presents unique challenges in the epidemiology of infertility.[ ] Infertility represents a major public health concern across the region, although prevalence estimates differ widely between countries. Socio-cultural norms, persistent pressure on women to bear children, limited access to reproductive health services and a high burden of untreated reproductive tract infections lead to increased risk of infertility in South Asia.[ ] In many South Asian communities, infertility often triggers social stigma, marital disruption and substantial emotional distress, which disproportionately affects women.[ ]
The hospital and community-based survey from India has reported a prevalence of infertility between 7%[ ] and 15%.[ ] A community-based study in Pakistan has reported infertility prevalence estimates of approximately 22%,[ ] while a clinic-based study from Nepal indicates an infertility prevalence of around 9.95% among women attending infertility services.[ ] The recently published WHO report on global infertility highlights a significant lack of reliable data from Southeast Asian countries.[ ] This evidence gap highlights the need for region-specific analyses to understand the burden of infertility and inform effective public health interventions. From a health policy perspective, identifying the magnitude of infertility is crucial for promoting policies and programs that support couples and individuals in achieving their reproductive potential. Accordingly, this review was planned to provide a pooled prevalence of infertility in South Asia and critically appraise the quality of existing literature, thereby guiding future research and informing health policies in the region.

## METHODS
We reported this systematic review in accordance with the standards of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Statement[ ] and prospectively registered this systematic review protocol on PROSPERO CRD42024623937 (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews).[ ]

## Types of studies
We included cross-sectional studies that reported the prevalence of infertility in South Asia and were published in English. We did not include studies that did not report the total population from which the infertility prevalence was calculated. In addition, case–control studies, case reports, case series and interventional studies, research articles, such as reviews, editorials, letters, as well as theses, book chapters and conference abstracts, were also excluded.

## Electronic searches
We constructed a search strategy using free-text keywords related to infertility, study design and country context. We searched in the Title/Abstract fields, combining them with Boolean operators (AND/OR). [ ] and searched in three databases, PubMed, Scopus and Embase, from their inception to 12 April 2025, to retrieve studies published in English. In addition, we manually searched other relevant sources, such as the bibliographies of published studies, to identify any additional records not captured in the database search.
 

## Selection of studies
We uploaded all articles retrieved to EndNote and removed duplicate references. Two reviewers independently conducted screening in three stages: First by title review, second by abstract, and then by full-text screening (AKK and GT). We resolved any disagreements through discussion among reviewers or through adjudication by a third reviewer (MSK).

## Data extraction and management
Three reviewers (AKK, SJ and AAD) independently extracted study characteristics using a standardised data-collection template, and all extracted information was cross-verified for accuracy. The extracted information included first author, year of publication, study design, sample size, country in which the research was conducted, study setting (community or hospital), prevalence of infertility, type of infertility assessed and participant characteristics such as age and gender. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, in consultation with an independent reviewer (MSK).

## Assessment of risk of bias
Three reviewers (AKK, SJ and AAD) independently assessed the quality of the studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence studies.[ ] This tool consists of nine items that evaluate key aspects of study quality, including the adequacy of the sampling framework and method, sample size, reliability of the measurement, statistical analysis and response rate.

## Data synthesis
We calculated study-level prevalence as the number of infertility cases divided by the total sample size. Pooled prevalence was estimated through a meta-analysis of proportions. To stabilise the variance of proportions, the Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine transformation[ ] was applied, and transformed estimates were combined using inverse-variance weighting under a random-effects model with Hartung–Knapp adjustment for confidence intervals (CIs). Pooled estimates were then back-transformed to the original proportion scale for interpretation. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I² statistics. Where substantial heterogeneity was identified, we investigated its possible sources. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

## RESULTS

## Search results
We identified a total of 4326 records through electronic searches of Scopus (n = 1784), Embase (n = 1477) and PubMed (n = 1065), and 50 articles from other sources. After removing 2743 duplicates, a total of 1633 records were screened. We excluded 1567 records after the abstract and title screening review and identified 66 records for further screening. After a full-text review, we excluded 50 records, and finally, studies (n = 16)[ ] met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review, of which five studies were eligible for inclusion in the primary analysis [ ].
 

## Study characteristics
Included studies were published between 1993[ ] and 2025.[ ] The majority of the studies were from India, except for two studies, each from Pakistan[ ] and Nepal.[ ] All the included 16 studies were cross-sectional study designs' Most of the Studies were conducted in community settings[ ] while five studies were conducted in the hospital[ ] and one study reported data from both the community and the hospital.[ ] The sample size of these studies ranges from 232[ ] to 49,9627.[ ]
The included studies used different sampling approaches to recruit participants, as described in . Data collection methods predominantly involved structured, pretested or semi-structured interviewer-administered questionnaires, ensuring standardised information capture across participants. However, a few studies[ ] did not specify the instrument used, limiting assessment of methodological comparability across all included studies.
 Most included studies used standard clinical definitions of infertility. Typically, primary infertility was defined as failure to conceive after 1 year (≥12 months) of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse,[ ] consistent with the WHO definition, which describes infertility as a disease of the reproductive system characterised by the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after 12 months of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse.[ ] For the primary analysis, we only included and pooled studies that defined failure to conceive after 1 year (≥12 months).
Some studies used a longer exposure criterion, defining infertility as ≥2 years of marriage or cohabiting, being sexually active, not using contraception, and remaining childless, which was used in the definition.[18,21-24,27,29] One study[ ] did not report an explicit operational definition of infertility.
In contrast, three[ ] survey-based studies derived infertility status indirectly, as the dataset did not include a direct question on infertility. In these studies, infertility was operationalised using multiple criteria, including marital and union status, duration of marriage, interview date, absence of terminated pregnancies, history of not using contraception and zero children ever born. In the National Family Health Survey (NFHS)-4,[ ] and NFHS-5,[ ] women who had been married for ≥5 years, were currently in union, had no history of terminated pregnancies, had never used contraception and had no children ever born were classified as having probable primary infertility. In NFHS-3,[ ] infertility was identified among married men with zero children ever born, a marital duration of ≥5 years, and whose wives were not pregnant at the time of the survey. These varied definitional approaches contribute to heterogeneity across studies.

## Risk of bias assessment
A detailed risk of bias assessment for each included study is presented in . Most studies met the majority of the checklist criteria, particularly those related to sampling, measurement and statistical analysis. A few studies showed unclear reporting in certain domains, such as sample adequacy or response rate. Overall, the studies demonstrated acceptable methodological quality and were included in the quantitative synthesis.
 

## Pooled prevalence of infertility
The pooled prevalence of infertility from a random-effects meta-analysis of five studies was 8% (95% CI: 5–13%; I² = 98.6%, P < 0.0001) [ ]. The pooled prevalence of infertility varied based on the operational definitions: 8% (95% CI: 5%–13%) for 12 months, 10% (95% CI: 6%–14%) for 24 months and 2% (95% CI: 1%–2%) for 60 months [ ]. Among the five studies included, pooled results from three community-based studies showed a prevalence of 10% (95% CI: 2%–23%), while one study from hospital demonstrated a prevalence of 5% (95% CI: 4%–6%) and one study from both community and hospital-based study showed a prevalence of 8% (95% CI: 6–10%) [ ]. Primary infertility, reported in five studies, had a prevalence of 7% (95% CI: 2–14%), whereas secondary infertility, reported in two studies, had a prevalence of 3% (95% CI: 0–12%) [ ].
 A country-based subgroup meta-analysis of four studies from India showed a pooled infertility prevalence of 9% (95% CI: 4%–16%), while one study from Nepal reported a prevalence of 5% (95% CI: 4%–6%) [Supplementary Figure 1]. A post
hoc analysis was performed to investigate the heterogeneity, which resulted in a reduction in heterogeneity from I² = 98.6% to I² = 73.2%, indicating moderate residual heterogeneity after exclusion of the outlier study [Supplementary Figure 2].

## DISCUSSION
The current review found a pooled infertility prevalence of 8% across the South Asian region. The prevalence of infertility in India was 9%, closely aligning with the regional prevalence. These findings highlight a substantial burden of infertility across South Asia, underscoring its relevance as a key public health concern.
According to the WHO 2023 report,[ ] the global lifetime prevalence of infertility was estimated at 17.5%, while the global period prevalence was 12.6%. Similarly, a systematic review by Cox et
al.,[ ] reported that the pooled lifetime and period prevalence estimates were 17.5% (95% CI: 15.0–20.3) and 12.6% (95% CI: 10.7–14.6). In the present review, the pooled period prevalence of infertility in South Asia was 8%, based on studies applying the standard 12-month definition of infertility, consistent with the WHO criteria. The lower prevalence observed in the review compared with the global WHO estimate may reflect true regional variation, differences in population characteristics, healthcare-seeking behaviour, sociocultural factors, access to reproductive health services and methodological heterogeneity across studies. These findings underscore the importance of generating region-specific estimates to address existing data gaps.
In our review, the pooled prevalence of primary and secondary infertility was 7% and 3%, respectively. In comparison, Cox et
al.,[ ] reported a pooled period prevalence of 9.0% (95% CI: 6.6–12.2) for primary infertility and 4.9% (95% CI: 2.7–8.8) for secondary infertility. However, their review also highlighted data gaps for many regions, including South Asia.
Mascarenhas et
al.,[ ] using data from 277 demographic and reproductive health surveys, estimated the prevalence of primary infertility in South Asia at 2.4% and secondary infertility at 11.3% in 2010, restricted their analyses to women in union for at least 5 years, who were not using contraception, and desired a child. They defined primary infertility as the absence of a live birth and secondary infertility as the absence of a live birth since the last live birth. Demographic definition in the NFHS study[ ] included in the current review also applied the same ≥60-month infertility duration criterion. Estimates derived from pooling these studies in the current review also showed a similar lower prevalence, consistent with the study by Mascarenhas et
al.[ ] Such a more stringent definition tends to yield lower estimates of primary infertility but higher estimates of secondary infertility. The stratified analysis by study setting (community vs. hospital) showed that the pooled prevalence among community-based studies was 10%, slightly higher than the overall pooled estimate, indicating that the hospital-based study (8%) did not inflate the overall prevalence estimate.
Moreover, the inclusion of studies covering several decades – from the early 1990s to more recent years-likely contributes to between-study variability and reflects both temporal changes in fertility patterns over time as well as evolving methodological approaches in the measurement of infertility across South Asia.
Despite these global syntheses, no previous systematic review has produced a pooled prevalence estimate specifically for the South Asian region. Existing reviews have largely focused on global or multi-regional patterns rather than consolidating evidence across the eight South Asian countries.
Among the studies included in this systematic review, Sharma et
al.,[ ] explicitly stated that infertility is a disease of the male and/or female reproductive system; however, the study focuses mainly on the female perspective, which is consistent with trends observed across South Asian research. Except for two studies that included couples, most studies exclusively assessed women. Future population-based studies should adopt couple-centred approaches to more comprehensively capture the burden of infertility in South Asia.
The current review has several strengths. It is the first systematic review to synthesise infertility prevalence specifically for the South Asian region. It is based on a comprehensive, multi-database search strategy that includes both community-based and clinic-based evidence. In addition, study quality was systematically assessed using the JBI critical appraisal checklist.
However, the review is limited to English-language studies, which may have excluded relevant local evidence. One of the included studies was clinic-based, and another was conducted in both community and hospital settings, which are not ideal for estimating population prevalence. According to the WHO methodological guidance, the ideal design for measuring infertility prevalence is a large, population-based cross-sectional survey with probability sampling and standardised WHO definitions, which was lacking in parts of the available literature. Additionally, many demographic and reproductive health surveys rely on simplified demographic definitions of infertility based on marital status, duration of union and absence of live births. Such definitions may underestimate the true prevalence of infertility because they often exclude women who have used contraception or experienced a shorter duration of infertility, highlighting the need for refinement of survey questions and improved measurement approaches in large-scale surveys. The current review did not identify any eligible studies from many countries in the South Asian countries, highlighting a significant evidence gap in the region. More country-specific research is needed to generate a comprehensive and representative understanding of infertility patterns across South Asia. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Despite these limitations, the findings provide an important regional estimate to guide future research and policy efforts and highlight the need to prioritise infertility within public health agendas, improve accessibility and affordability of fertility services and advance progress toward equitable reproductive healthcare coverage across the region.

## CONCLUSION
The current review shows that infertility remains a significant public health concern in South Asia, with a pooled prevalence of approximately 8%, which is lower than recent global estimates, highlighting the need for robust region-specific data to inform evidence-based policies and strengthen reproductive health services aimed at addressing the needs of couples experiencing infertility.
