## Abstract

## Objective
Dental professionals experience stress daily due to the nature of the excessively demanding working environment in addition to the trust laid upon them by the public. This review aims to evaluate the available evidence on the prevalence and incidence of stress in UK dental students. The paper critically assesses current evidence, shedding light on mental health issues faced by dental healthcare workers.

## Methods
The paper followed PRISMA guidelines and was preregistered on PROSPERO, CRD42024482310. Several databases were searched including PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Scopus. Studies included involved cohort and cross-sectional studies. The primary outcomes were the prevalence and incidence of stress in UK dental students. Additionally, sources of stress and coping strategies were considered as secondary outcomes.

## Results
Nine studies out of 2986 screened were included with a sample size of 1447. The pooled prevalence of stress was in 596/1198 (pooled prevalence = 0.498 95% confidence intervals = 0.466-0.530). The incidence rate is estimated to be 5% per year. Sources of stress were found to be examinations, grades, and fear of failing. Coping techniques were hobbies, and emotional support, in addition to maladaptive behaviours.

## Conclusion
The outcomes of the papers suggest that dental professionals experience extreme levels of stress. While acknowledging the results, this review also underscores the need for further comprehensive research to fully evaluate the burden of stress on dentists. It also shows the importance of incorporating stress management in dental education.

Key words: Dental students, Stress, United Kingdom, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

## Introduction
Healthcare providers are frequently under significant stress due to the demanding nature of their work. This pressure is an intrinsic aspect of their roles, mainly because they are expected to meet high standards of care and performance. The anticipations placed on them by patients, colleagues, and society can be extreme. They must make rapid, critical decisions that considerably influence patients’ lives, manage emotionally charged conditions, and sometimes do so in understaffed environments. These factors contribute to the higher stress levels experienced by healthcare workers, which can affect both their mental and physical health, and the quality of care they deliver.
Dentistry is no different where professionals experience stress on a day-to-day basis. Common sources of stress for dentists include long working hours, managing anxious patients, running behind schedule, and persistent drive to perfection. , Symptoms they might face because of stress include headaches, fear, fatigue, sleeplessness, and abdominal or intestinal problems. , Interestingly, both musculoskeletal disorders and stress-related diseases were the main reasons causing dentists’ early retirement. 
Work-related anxiety and stress, appear to begin early on when one starts their dental education. Several studies have stated that dental programmes are more stressful than other medical education studies. , This is because dental students undergo extensive training theoretically but more notably practically including learning meticulous surgical skills. What raises the burden of stress even further is the fact that in their clinical training, students are responsible for sourcing their patients, managing their care, and performing irreversible dental treatments, alongside fulfilling other clinical requirements and studying for exams. , These challenges can exert significant psychological stress on dental students, potentially impacting their mental health, academic performance, and future professional competence. In the United Kingdom, dental students operate in a particularly intense environment, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of the mental health difficulties they face, particularly stress.
The prevalence and incidence of stress in academic settings, especially among healthcare profession students, have been studied significantly due to their implications on students’ well-being and academic outcomes. Despite the acknowledged importance of these issues, systematic data specifically concerning UK dental students remain sparse. Therefore, the authors considered a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively examine the existing evidence on the prevalence and incidence of stress in dental undergraduate students in the United Kingdom. By synthesising data across different studies, this paper aims to offer a robust estimate of stress prevalence and incidence. Also, where present, any data on sources of stress along with coping techniques used to manage this will be collected.
In sum, this review could support, and guide future research directions and policy decisions aimed at mitigating stress in dental schools. By addressing this gap, the study contributes to a better understanding of mental health issues in dental education environments, eventually fostering a more supportive learning environment for upcoming dental professionals.

## Methods

## Registration
This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, which offer a standardised approach for carrying out systematic reviews. Moreover, the review protocol was registered in advance with the National Institute of Health Research Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), which enhances transparency and lowers any risk of bias occurring in the review process. The registration number is CRD42024482310.

## Search strategy
The review was carried out using multiple databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Scopus. With the consultation of senior authors, a well-developed search strategy was used, using a combination of free-text keywords, Boolean logic operators, and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms where appropriate. Furthermore, the reference lists of papers included were screened for any relevant articles. An example of a search strategy used for the databases mentioned above is as follows: (Stress OR anxiety OR depression OR mental issues OR fear) AND (Dental OR dentistry OR oral) AND (student OR undergraduate OR academic) AND (UK OR United Kingdom OR England OR Wales OR Scotland OR Northern Ireland). The following MeSH terms were used where appropriate to ensure a comprehensive search: ‘Stress, Psychological’[Mesh], ‘Anxiety’[Mesh], ‘Students, Dental’[Mesh], and ‘United Kingdom’[Mesh].

## Eligibility criteria
A robust methodology was followed with predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure a comprehensive approach.
Inclusion Criteria:
1.Studies included without time frame limitations in date of publication.
2.Studies published in the English language.
3.Studies assessed dental undergraduate students in the United Kingdom.
4.The included studies reported the following outcomes: stress prevalence or incidence, sources of stress, or coping mechanisms.
5.The following study types were included in the review: cross-sectional and cohort studies.
Exclusion Criteria:
1.Studies not published in the English language.
2.Studies not involving UK dental students.
3.Studies that did not report outcomes of interest for the clinical question.
4.Unpublished papers uploaded online.
5.Articles with no full text, just title and abstract
6.Studies were screened for duplication, and duplicates will be excluded.
7.Experimental studies and case-control studies were excluded.
8.Review articles, editorials, conference abstracts, and commentaries were excluded.
By applying these criteria, we ensure that the systematic review is thorough, coherent, and transparent. This structured approach helps minimise bias and enhances the reliability and validity of the findings.

## Outcomes
The primary outcome was to determine the prevalence and incidence of stress among UK dental undergraduate students. Additionally, the main sources of stress and techniques used to cope with it in UK dental students were investigated as a secondary objective.

## Process of screening and data extraction
All studies were retrieved from a database search and organised into an Excel spreadsheet. Initially, two reviewers, SA and ED, independently screened titles and abstracts of all results of the search engine. Both followed the predesigned review form (see ). If there were any doubts about an article, it was proceeded to a full-text review, where alongside potentially eligible papers, they were retrieved and independently screened, by SA and ED, by reading the full-text and assessing the eligibility with our inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved by consulting the senior author, AG, who offered an unbiased expert opinion for the final decision on whether to include or exclude the article. Data extraction from the included articles was carried out using a standardised extraction form (see ) by SA and ED, with any discrepancies resolved by the senior author, PR, as well. The following information was extracted from each paper: first author, publication year, study design, sample size, student demographics (age and gender), type of questionnaire administered (self-made, Perceived Stress Scale [PSS], General Health Questionnaire [GHQ], or Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment [GAD-7]) and its results.

## Risk of bias and quality assessment
The risk of bias was calculated using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal List for Cross-sectional and Cohort Studies, by SA and ED independently. It is one of the recommended critical appraisal checklists for such studies. For cross-sectional studies, it comprises of 8 questions which can be answered by either a yes, no, unclear, or not applicable. These questions focus on study design, protocol, and reporting, and use specific questions to collect information related to the risk of bias. SA and ED followed a predefined algorithm to determine if the study had a high or low risk of bias. If all 8 answers to the questions were ‘Yes’, the study is concluded as having a low risk of bias. If the opposite, where all questions had ‘No’ as an answer, the study has a high risk of bias. In the case of a mix of ‘Yes’, ‘No’, or ‘Unclear’ across the domains, the study is considered to have some concerns. High risk of bias studies in this method are excluded. After critical appraisal, SA and ED decide on whether to include, or exclude the paper, and if there is any disagreement the advice of the senior author, AG, is taken. Similarly, the method was applied for the cohort study using its appropriate checklist, which comprises of 11 questions. Both checklists have been added as a supplementary file in the manuscript (see and ). This appraisal offered further understandings of the robustness of the data provided in the included studies. By applying these evaluation approaches and guidelines, this systematic review intended to provide a thorough assessment of the strength of the data in the included articles.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis of this systematic review was conducted through Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4, which is a software made by the Cochrane Collaboration. A meta-analysis of proportions was applied to all outcomes. For binary data, a random effects model was utilized. The studies were then stratified into subgroups made based on the type of questionnaire administered in the study (self-made, PSS, GHQ, or GAD-7). Heterogeneity was examined using Higgins's I2. The heterogeneity was interpreted as follows: low heterogeneity (0%-25%), moderate heterogeneity (25%-50%), and high heterogeneity (50%-100%). The reported outcomes were presented at 95% confidence intervals in the forest plot. A value of P < .05 was deemed statistically significant. The results are presented and discussed in the discussion.

## Results

## Search results
A total of 2986 articles were found via all the electronic databases after the conduction of the systematic search. After removing all of the duplicates (238), 2748 articles were screened for eligibility. Out of these, full texts of 51 articles were retrieved and checked if they matched the inclusion criteria. Nine studies were found to match the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. This is shown in which was carried out following PRISMA guidelines for article screening. 
 

## Demographic characteristics
Out of all the studies, only 9 were included in the review. , , , , , , , , They were published from 2002 to 2021. All of the studies were cross-sectional study except one. The sample size ranged from 102 to 412. The pooled sample size was 1447 participants. The number of male participants ranged from 25 to 103. The total population of males was 417 (32.1%). The mean ages ranged from 21 to 24. Only three studies showed age as a categorical variable. , , Of these, only two were poolable and divided into below and above 20 years. The total number of participants aged less than 20 was 150 (42.6%) out of a total of 352 cumulative sample sizes of these two studies. This data has been summarized in .
 

## Primary outcome

## Stress prevalence and incidence
A meta-analysis of proportions was performed for each outcome. To handle binary data, a random effects model was applied. To stabilise the variances of the raw proportions, the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was employed. The adjusted proportions were then combined using a random effects model with the DerSimonian-Laird approach. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistics, with substantial heterogeneity being defined as I2 greater than 50%. The pooled prevalence of stress in dental students studying in the United Kingdom was in 596/1198 (Pooled prevalence = 0.498 95% confidence intervals = 0.466-0.530). Significant heterogeneity was observed with I2 = 86.3%. The studies were then stratified into subgroups made based on the type of questionnaire administered (GHQ, self-made, PSS, or GAD-7). The subgroup analysis was done to explore heterogeneity. Knipe used the GAD-7 questionnaire and reported a score of ≥10 for moderate to severe stress. The forest plot is shown in .
 Regarding the incidence of stress among dental students in the UK, none of the studies, including Newbury-Birch, provided well-defined data on the subject. However, Gorter is a follow-up study to Humphris, which revisited dental students in their final year who were originally surveyed in their first year, offers some insight. Gorter reassessed students from Manchester and Belfast in the UK. Notably, while Liverpool students were involved in the Humphris study, they were not part of the Gorter study. Gorter identified an increase in GHQ scores suggestive of stress from 36% to 47% in Manchester and from 24% to 49% in Belfast. It is important to note that the sample size in the Gorter study was reduced due to a lower response rate of 55%. To estimate the incidence of stress, calculations were made using the data from Manchester and Belfast, comparing the primary (Year 1) stress levels to those found four years later. The calculation revealed that new cases of stress amounted to approximately 20% over the 4 years, interpreting an incidence rate of about 5% per year. This approximation assumes that students who were initially stressed in their first year of studies, were not always stressed throughout their academic life, or that their stress levels did not fluctuate significantly, which is a simplification of the actual dynamics of stress. More accurate incidence rates would ideally involve precise tracking of individual students’ stress levels over time.

## Secondary outcomes

## Sources of stress
Collin used the DES (Dental Environment Stress) questionnaire and reported that the most common source of stress was examination and grades, with a causative rate of 72.3%. On the DES subscale, out of the top 10 most common stressors reported, five were related to the academic nature of the course for example examinations and fear of failing. In this study, they also used the Ohio Financial Wellness Survey to check for financial stresses and reported 261/412, 63.34% of students feeling stressed about their finances in general. Narwal also reported examinations to be the most common source of stress, which is present in 74% of the students. Jenkins reported that 63% of the students were worried about their studies. They also showed that there was a moderately strong and significant correlation between stress and financial problems (r = 0.350, P < .001). Moreover, they also reported that financial stress and other factors influenced academic grades (r = 0.250, P < .01). Humphris reported that students who lived in accommodations of their own or student halls had a higher stress level (41%) than those who lived with their families (20%). Turner used regression analysis and reported journey difficulty (P = .02) and family responsibilities (P = .03) to be significantly associated with stress in dental students. They also reported that females had significantly higher PSS scores (P = .001) than males (20.23 and 16.92, respectively).

## Coping techniques
Out of all the studies, only three , , reported coping strategies used by dental students to combat stress. Jenkins reported a moderately strong negative correlation between the amount of stress perceived and the ability to pursue hobbies (r = –0.518, P < .001). Collin reported emotional support, active coping, planning, acceptance, and positive reframing as the most popular coping strategies. The use of planning, religion, and positive reframing were significantly more common amongst the female population (P < .05). Moreover, they also reported that maladaptive coping strategies (substance abuse, binge eating, procrastination) were more common in students facing higher stress (P = .001). Narwal reported exercise to be the most practised coping technique (48% of the students), which was followed closely by hobbies (45%) and emotional support (44%). Professional help was the least commonly practised technique, in just 7% of the students.

## Risk of bias assessment
The assessment of bias risk was carried out for all studies by two different reviewers independently, SA and ED. The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cross-sectional and Cohort studies were used accordingly. It is interesting to note, that all the studies included in this review presented with some level of bias risk. This was mainly recognised as utilising convenience sampling methods and lack of blinding. In the first case, the concern was that the sample may have not represented the broader dental student population and thus limit the generalisability of the outcomes; in the second case, we recognised the possibility of measurement bias, where the knowledge of the hypothesis could impact the results. and show the results of the assessment of bias of the studies and the answers to each question of the checklist, respectively.
 

## Discussion
Mental health and stress management have become critical aspects of our modern society, with rising attention focused on these matters. Dental schools, known for their intense and stressful academic environments, are exceptionally competitive, both in terms of admission and academic success. Upon fulfilling their undergraduate training, dentists are usually expected to excel in their practice and be ongoing learners. This high expectation can lead to greater stress, especially for professionals early in their careers. It has been reported that dental students who experience higher stress levels frequently employ poor coping strategies. Thus, it is advised that stress management, besides personal and professional types of training, should ideally be part of the dental undergraduate programme curricula. 
This systematic review sought to primarily assess the prevalence and incidence of stress among UK dental students by synthesising data across nine included studies. , , , , , , , , The analysis revealed a noteworthy, pooled prevalence of stress at 49.8% with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86.3%), implying that nearly half of the dental students experienced stress, but with substantial variability across studies. Newbury-Birch presented the highest prevalence of stress possibly as it is the only cohort study among the included articles reflecting variances in methodology or measurement biases compared to the cross-sectional studies that may not capture similar temporal fluctuations in stress levels. The high heterogeneity indicates that factors contributing to stress may vary greatly among different student groups or settings. The subgroup analysis, made based on the type of questionnaire used, did not meaningly reduce heterogeneity, emphasising that the variation in stress levels might be due to more than just methodological differences in stress assessment. This points to the complex nature of stressors affecting dental undergraduates. A more detailed examination of the stress experienced by dental students reveals that responses can significantly vary across various demographic factors. , With regards to gender, females were found to report 3.8 times higher levels of stress compared to their male counterparts, indicating a substantial gender disparity in stress experience. Furthermore, stress rates were significantly higher in older age groups, and students in their final years. Also, living arrangements played a crucial role in stress levels, where it was found that students living away from their family home reported higher levels of stress compared to those living with family (41% and 20%, respectively). 
Notably, the incidence of stress could not be directly analysed due to a lack of precise longitudinal data. However, using Gorter follow-up study as a proxy, an estimated incidence rate of about 5% per year was calculated by matching stress levels in the first and fifth years among students from Manchester and Belfast. This rate indicates a remarkable increase in stress as students advance through their dental education, likely exaggerated by the increased clinical responsibilities and academic requirements.
Moreover, the sources of stress discovered in the studies, primarily examinations and financial concerns, emphasise areas where interventions could be beneficial. These results align with the broader literature suggesting that academic burdens and financial insecurity are major stressors for students in rigorous professional programs. By identifying academic and examination burdens as major stressors, academic administrators might explore the feasibility of curriculum modifications that would help control the increased stress rates. Besides the characteristic stress of their academic and professional demands, dentists also face many occupational hazards specific to their profession. These include biological risks like potential infections, chemical injuries from exposure to hazardous substances, and physical risks such as radiation. 
The conclusions of this systematic review clarify various critical sources of stress affecting dental students in the UK, as well as the coping strategies they employ. These insights offer a valuable framework for reevaluating and boosting the support systems within dental education. Recognising the exact stressors mentioned above allows educational institutions and policymakers to tailor interventions that directly address the exceptional challenges faced by dental students. The most common coping strategies reported were exercise and emotional support. , Importantly, some students adopt inappropriate approaches to alleviate their stress such as alcohol and substance abuse. Universities should think about adding electives focused on mind-body techniques, offering counselling, implementing stress management programs led by students, promoting student social activities, switching to pass-fail grading systems rather than traditional grading methods, and teaching effective coping methods. Additionally, policies encouraging routine monitoring of student stress levels could help schools identify at-risk individuals earlier and provide timely interventions. Such programs could be designed to help students develop resilience and efficient coping mechanisms in their educational journey. The recommendations for stress management are for it to extend beyond individual efforts, advocating for organisational responsibility in providing stress-free environments for everyone. Chapman reported the efficiency of a specifically designed CPD package in the reduction of anxiety, stress and burnout levels which might affect the clinical decision-making of a dentist. These findings propose that interventions for dental professionals should allow them to initiate and maintain changes freely and that offering feedback on stress levels might be enough to encourage change in motivated individuals.
Similar research has been conducted in different geographical regions which highlighted high stress levels among dental students. Smolana studied dental students in a broad, nonspecific geographical area, while Khanagar focused on students in Saudi Arabia. Despite the variations in study locations, both studies consistently reported elevated stress levels among dental student populations, , mirroring our findings.
There are several limitations in this review. First, many of the study results rely on small, cross-sectional studies, which usually provide weaker evidence. Second, there is a clear absence of data regarding students’ stress levels before getting into dental school, as none of the papers have measured stress before admission. Third, most papers have relied on subjective assessments of stress, which might explain females’ higher stress levels as they tend to express their emotions more than males. Furthermore, the diversity in cultural backgrounds between the different studies across the UK can impact the perception and reporting of stress by students. Society can affect how students perceive stress and their willingness to report it. Differences in the educational systems, and assessment methods within the schools also pose major challenges. For instance, some schools may place importance on continuous assessment, which can lead to altered stress patterns compared to systems that depend on final exams. One further limitation of our review is the exclusion of unpublished data. This may have led to publication bias, as studies with significant or positive results are more likely to be published and including grey literature or unpublished papers could offer a broader understanding of the stress rates. Lastly, human involvement in the risk of bias assessment can potentially introduce bias. Despite implementing rigorous protocols, the subjective nature of their judgments cannot be eliminated. They may interpret criteria differently based on their backgrounds or experiences, possibly leading to inconsistent evaluations.
It is advisable for future research to include more objective monitoring of students’ stress levels, both at the start of their studies and at regular intervals throughout their careers. Moreover, longitudinal studies could be beneficial for tracking changes in students’ stress levels throughout their academic careers, providing valuable insights for dental program administrators. Also, this paper emphasises the need for future researchers and organisations to undertake a comprehensive national longitudinal study across all UK dental programs. This would precisely measure the burden of stress faced by students and facilitate more effective strategies to address this issue.

## Conclusion
This review to the best of the authors’ knowledge is the first systematic review of stress among UK dental students. In this paper, we provide data on the prevalence and incidence of stress, sources of stress, and coping techniques in dental undergraduates studying in different universities in the United Kingdom. This study is of the utmost importance since maintaining good mental health is crucial for dentists, not only for their well-being but also for the optimal care of their patients. Nevertheless, we are not aiming to diminish stress levels to zero but targeting for lower manageable levels that boost not hinder performance.

## Ethical approval statement
This systematic review was carried out in accordance with all relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval was not required for this paper since it involved reviewing and analysing existing publicly available data. The information and data used in this study were obtained from previously published academic literature, which is freely accessible and does not involve direct interaction with human or animal subjects.
Moreover, this study did not include any experiments, clinical trials, or interventions involving human participants. Instead, it focused on synthesizing and analysing existing information to assess the current evidence on the prevalence and incidence of stress among dental students in the UK. Therefore, ethical approval was not considered necessary.
