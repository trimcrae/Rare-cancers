## Abstract

## Background
Self-medication is widespread in many developing countries and may lead to serious consequences such as antimicrobial resistance and delays in receiving appropriate healthcare. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of self-medication in Pakistan and explore possible sources of variation across different populations and regions. Although the search covered studies published between 2000 and 2025, all eligible studies included in the analysis were published between 2008 and 2025.

## Methods
A systematic search was conducted across Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, and PakMediNet to identify studies published between 2000 and August 2025. The final database search was performed in August 2025. The review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251117353). Observational studies reporting the prevalence of self-medication were included. A random-effects meta-analysis, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analysis, and meta-regression were performed.

## Results
A total of 65 studies involving 35,960 participants were included. The overall pooled prevalence of self-medication was estimated at 64% (95% CI: 57–71). However, a very high level of heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 99.7%), indicating substantial variation between populations and study settings. Therefore, the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution. The highest prevalence was observed in the Sindh province (69.8%, 95% CI: 60.3% to 79.3%) and among university students (71.3%, 95% CI: 64.7–77.8), while lower rates were reported in the general population and the Punjab province. Meta-regression showed no significant association between prevalence and publication year, sample size, or study quality. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the findings, showing that no single study substantially influenced the overall estimate.

## Conclusion
Self-medication is highly prevalent in Pakistan. These findings highlight the urgent need for regulatory policies to control over-the-counter drug use and public health interventions to raise awareness about the risks associated with self-medication.

## Background
Self-medication is a common health-related behavior in developing countries and may pose significant risks such as antimicrobial resistance, adverse drug reactions, misdiagnosis, and delayed access to appropriate medical care, thereby burdening both individuals and health systems [ ]. In this review, we adopted the World Health Organization (WHO) definition of self-medication as the use of medicines to treat self-diagnosed disorders or symptoms, or the continued use of previously prescribed drugs without consulting a healthcare professional [ ]. However, the included studies used varying operational definitions of self-medication—some focusing narrowly on antibiotics or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs, while others included traditional or herbal remedies—which may have contributed to inconsistencies in reported prevalence [ ].
Self-medication refers to using pharmaceutical or traditional remedies without professional guidance. It often involves taking leftover medications, following non-professional advice, or buying drugs without a prescription [ ]. Although it may be acceptable for minor ailments, self-medication can lead to serious risks, including misdiagnosis, delayed treatment, antimicrobial resistance, and adverse drug reactions [ ]. In Pakistan, self-medication is largely driven by limited access to healthcare, high consultation costs, and cultural norms that encourage treating illnesses at home. Misleading pharmaceutical advertising and misconceptions about the safety of certain drugs further worsen the situation [ , ]. Several interconnected factors contribute to the widespread practice of self-medication in Pakistan. Economic challenges such as the high cost of consultations, poverty, and unemployment lead many people to choose cheaper, informal treatment options [ , ]. In rural areas, poor access to healthcare services pushes individuals toward pharmacies and non-prescribed drugs. Cultural beliefs and traditional healing practices also promote self-treatment, while misunderstandings about the safety of common medications, especially antibiotics, contribute to their misuse [ ]. Weak regulatory enforcement allows pharmacies to dispense prescription-only drugs, including antibiotics and analgesics, without authorization [ ].
Low health literacy and misinformation spread through social media further shape the perception that self-medication is harmless [ ]. In addition, shortages of healthcare providers, long waiting times, and dissatisfaction with service quality discourage people from seeking medical advice [ , , ]. These behaviors have serious consequences, including antimicrobial resistance, delayed diagnoses, drug toxicity, and increasing healthcare costs [ , – ]. Unsupervised medication use may also hide the symptoms of serious illnesses, leading to avoidable complications and higher treatment costs over time [ , ].
This study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the pooled prevalence of self-medication in Pakistan and to identify sources of heterogeneity across different populations and regions. By synthesizing available evidence, the review seeks to provide a clearer understanding of the magnitude of self-medication and the factors contributing to its variation. Specifically, this study addresses the following question: What is the pooled prevalence of self-medication in Pakistan, and what sources explain the heterogeneity observed across studies?

## Method
This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [ ] (Supplementary Table 1). The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251117353) on 1 August 2025.

## Search strategy
This systematic review included observational studies published between January 2000 and August 2025. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across five major databases—Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL with Full Text, Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection—between August 1 and August 15, 2025. Both controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH and Emtree) and free-text terms related to “self-medication” and “Pakistan” were used. Boolean operators were applied to combine search terms appropriately. To ensure reproducibility, full search strings are provided in Supplementary Table 2. In addition to database searches, a manual search was conducted in Google Scholar and PakMediNet to identify locally published or non-indexed studies. Only studies available online or in print by August 15, 2025, were eligible for inclusion.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The inclusion criteria were observational studies that reported the prevalence or frequency of self-medication, were conducted in Pakistan, published in English, and had full-text availability. Interventional studies, qualitative studies, review articles, and letters to the editor were excluded from the analysis. Studies assessed as low methodological quality based on the JBI checklist were excluded before data synthesis. To ensure comprehensive coverage, the reference lists of all included articles were screened to identify any additional eligible studies.

## Data extraction
Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers using a predesigned standardized form. Extracted information included the first author’s name, year of publication, sample size, study location, reported prevalence of self-medication, and quality assessment score. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, and, if disagreement persisted, a third reviewer was consulted. Inter-rater reliability (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) was not formally calculated; however, the independent double-extraction process and the consensus approach helped ensure consistency and accuracy. Additionally, all extracted data were carefully cross-checked by one reviewer to confirm completeness and accuracy.

## Certainty of evidence
The certainty of the evidence was not assessed in this review, as the primary focus was on estimating the pooled prevalence rather than grading the strength of recommendations. The GRADE approach was not used in this study, because this review evaluates prevalence data rather than intervention effects or causal relationships. The GRADE framework is primarily designed to assess the quality of evidence in intervention studies and is not suitable for prevalence meta-analyses. For this reason, the JBI checklist was considered the most appropriate tool for evaluating the methodological quality of the observational studies included in this review.

## Quality assessment
The methodological rigor of the included studies was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist. This checklist comprises nine criteria assessing various aspects of study design and reporting, including the suitability of the sampling frame, participant selection, sample size adequacy, clarity of study settings, data analysis coverage, validity of measurement tools, consistency of measurement, appropriateness of statistical analysis, and adequacy of response rate. Each item was rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.” A value of 1 was assigned for each “Yes” response, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating superior methodological quality [ ]. Based on the total JBI score, studies were categorized as high quality (7–9 points), moderate quality (4–6 points), or low quality (< 4 points). This simplified classification was used to enhance consistency between reviewers and make comparisons across studies clearer. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed independently by two reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. Studies scoring below 4 on the JBI checklist were considered low quality and excluded from the meta-analysis.

## Statistical analysis
The collected data were analyzed using STATA version 17. Study-specific standard errors for prevalence estimates were calculated using the binomial formula (gen q = 1 − p, gen pq = p*q, gen se = pq/sample, gen SEp = sqrt(se)), after which the pooled prevalence was obtained using the updated STATA meta-analysis commands (meta set, meta summarize). Meta-regression analyses were performed using the metareg command to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.
To assess heterogeneity among studies, the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test were used. I² values were categorized as low (25%), moderate (50%), and high (75%) heterogeneity [ ]. In cases of high heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. The standard errors of the proportions were calculated using the binomial formula without applying any data transformation, as all prevalence values were within a moderate range (i.e., not close to 0 or 1). The results were presented as pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals and displayed in a forest plot. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine whether any single study had a disproportionate influence on the overall results, ensuring that the pooled estimate remained stable even after sequentially removing individual studies [ ]. Publication bias was assessed visually using a funnel plot and statistically using Begg’s test. When publication bias was detected, the Trim-and-Fill method was used to estimate the effect of potentially missing studies and provide an adjusted pooled prevalence [ ]. Meta-regression analysis was performed to examine whether publication year and sample size influenced the prevalence of self-medication [ ]. Subgroup analyses were also performed based on study quality (moderate vs. high), study location (Sindh, Punjab, and other provinces), and target population (general population vs. students).
Because heterogeneity was extremely high (I² > 99%), we performed an additional variance-stabilizing analysis using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation in STATA. This analysis was used to check the robustness of the pooled estimate. The transformed pooled prevalence was then compared with the untransformed estimate, and both were found to be highly consistent, supporting the stability of the overall findings.

## Results
A total of 560 records were initially retrieved from five major databases: Ovid MEDLINE (n = 106), Embase (n = 130), CINAHL with Full Text (n = 33), Scopus (n = 140), and Web of Science Core Collection (n = 151). An additional 12 records were identified through manual searches in Google Scholar and PakMediNet, resulting in a total of 572 records. After removing 277 duplicates, 295 unique records remained for screening. During title and abstract screening, 65 records were excluded because they were irrelevant to the research question (e.g., qualitative studies, reviews, interventional studies, or letters to the editor). The full texts of 230 articles were then assessed for eligibility. Of these, six studies were excluded for not reporting prevalence data, and 159 full-text articles were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. The main reasons for exclusion included: (a) studies focusing only on knowledge, attitudes, or perceptions toward self-medication without reporting prevalence (n = 78); (b) qualitative studies or review articles (n = 81). Ultimately, 65 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig.  ). Details are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.
 In this meta-analysis, 65 cross-sectional studies comprising a total of 35,960 participants were included [ , – , , – ]. The studies were published between 2008 and 2025. Sample sizes ranged from 30 to 3,388 participants, with two studies including fewer than 100 participants [ , ]. Regarding geographical distribution, 23 studies were conducted in Punjab, 23 in Sindh, and 17 in other regions of Pakistan. With respect to the target population, 32 studies focused on students, 27 on the general population, and 8 on patients (Supplementary Table 3). Of the initially screened studies, six were rated as low quality based on the JBI checklist and were excluded prior to meta-analysis. Among the 65 included studies, 36 were of high quality and 29 of moderate quality (Supplementary Table 4).
The overall pooled prevalence of self-medication in Pakistan was 64% (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 57% to 71%), with a very high level of heterogeneity across studies (I² = 99.71%) (Fig.  ). The 95% prediction interval ranged from 19% to 100%, indicating that the true prevalence may vary widely across different populations and settings. The high heterogeneity likely reflects differences in study populations, definitions of self-medication, and regional contexts. The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that excluding each study individually resulted in only minimal changes in the pooled prevalence (ranging from approximately 0.63 to 0.65), and the confidence intervals remained almost unchanged. No single study produced a major shift in the overall estimate or altered the direction of the effect, and all p-values remained at 0.001. This consistency indicates that no individual study had a disproportionate influence on the overall meta-analysis results. In addition, when we repeated the analysis using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation, the pooled prevalence was 65% (95% CI: 58% to 72%), closely matching the original estimate. This demonstrates that the findings were robust regardless of the transformation applied.
 In the subgroup analysis by region, the prevalence of self-medication was 69.8% (95% CI: 60.3% to 79.3%) in Sindh, 63.1% (95% CI: 52.2% to 73.9%) in Punjab, and 64.5% (95% CI: 53.2% to 75.8%) in other provinces of Pakistan. These differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.617). Furthermore, the subgroup analysis by population type showed that the highest prevalence of self-medication was found among university students (71.3%, 95% CI: 64.7–77.8), followed by patients (57.7%, 95% CI: 38.2–77.1), and the general population (57.2%, 95% CI: 44.6–69.7) (p = 0.089). Although the differences across population groups were not statistically significant, students consistently showed the highest prevalence.
A subgroup meta-analysis was also conducted based on the quality of the included studies (high vs. moderate) using the JBI checklist. The pooled prevalence of self-medication among high-quality studies was 62.2% (95% CI: 53–71.4), while among moderate-quality studies it was 65.8% (95% CI: 54.3–77.2). The test of subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.639), indicating that study quality did not substantially influence the overall prevalence estimate. Although substantial heterogeneity was present within both subgroups (I² > 99%), the similarity in pooled estimates suggests that the overall findings were stable and not driven by study quality (Table  ).
 It is also important to note that Pa refers to the p-value comparing differences between subgroups, whereas Pb represents the p-value assessing heterogeneity within each subgroup based on Cochran’s Q test. As shown in Table  , Pa values were non-significant, while Pb values were significant, indicating that most of the variability came from within subgroups rather than between them.
Meta-regression analysis did not detect any statistically significant association between the prevalence of self-medication and study-level variables, including year of publication (p = 0.614) (Fig.  A), quality score (p = 0.424), and sample size (p = 0.321). This lack of significant findings may reflect limited statistical power or the considerable between-study heterogeneity observed in this review. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Begg’s test. Begg’s test was not statistically significant (p = 0.091), and the funnel plot appeared symmetrical, suggesting that publication bias was unlikely (Fig.  B).
 

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis, including 65 studies with a combined sample of 35,960 participants, estimated the overall prevalence of self-medication in Pakistan at 64% (95% CI: 57–71.0). Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as such extreme heterogeneity limits the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimate. The 95% prediction interval (19% to 100%) further highlights the extent of between-study variability, indicating that the true prevalence of self-medication may differ markedly across regions and population groups in Pakistan.
Subgroup analysis indicated the highest prevalence of self-medication in Sindh, followed by Punjab and other provinces, though the differences were not statistically significant. These regional patterns may reflect differences in healthcare access and health literacy, with urban populations more likely to self-medicate due to easier access to pharmacies [ ]. By population type, university students reported the highest prevalence, which is consistent with previous studies showing that stress, easier access to medicines, and misconceptions about safety drive this behavior [ , ]. Several factors may explain why university students, particularly those in health-related disciplines, exhibit the highest prevalence of self-medication. At the individual level, students often face academic stress, sleep deprivation, and tight schedules that discourage them from seeking professional medical care. Many consider minor ailments unworthy of a clinic visit and instead rely on past personal experiences or advice from peers. Moreover, medical and pharmacy students tend to possess partial pharmacological knowledge, which can create overconfidence in self-diagnosis and medication choices. At the systemic level, limited access to affordable student healthcare services, combined with the widespread availability of over-the-counter drugs, further enables self-medication [ , ].
Alarmingly, this trend is not limited to Pakistan; very high rates of self-medication have also been reported among medical students in neighboring countries [ ]. Rashid et al. (2020) found that many students routinely self-treated mild symptoms such as colds and coughs [ ]. These findings indicate that self-medication has become a normalized and widely accepted practice among university students, particularly in health-related fields [ , ].
Meta-regression showed no significant association between the prevalence of self-medication and year of publication, sample size, or study quality. However, this does not mean that no real relationship exists. The absence of statistically significant findings is likely due to the very high heterogeneity among the included studies and the small number of studies within some subgroups, which reduces the statistical power to detect meaningful trends. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the substantial changes in economic conditions, access to healthcare, and enforcement of drug regulations in Pakistan between 2008 and 2025, real differences in self-medication prevalence may indeed exist; however, the available evidence may not be sufficient to clearly capture these trends. Previous studies support this possibility. For example, Chaudhary et al. (2025) found that smaller studies reported higher prevalence estimates (71.6%) compared with larger studies (55.8%), suggesting that study characteristics may influence reported prevalence [ ].
This estimate suggests a potentially serious public health concern and is comparable to neighboring countries such as India (64.4%) [ ] and Bangladesh (49.9%) [ ], suggesting that self-medication is a region-wide challenge in South Asia. A review by Shahid et al. (2024), which synthesized 30 studies from multiple South Asian countries, also confirmed the widespread nature of this behavior, largely driven by poor engagement with healthcare providers and the easy availability of non-prescription drugs [ ]. Similarly, a meta-analysis from Africa reported an average prevalence of antibiotic self-medication of 55.7% (95% CI: 41%–75%), highlighting that this issue is also widespread across many low- and middle-income countries [ ].
The prevalence of self-medication in Pakistan (63.9%) is markedly higher than that reported in most developed countries. The very high heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis (I² = 99.7%) indicates substantial variability across the included studies. This high level of heterogeneity likely stems from multiple methodological and contextual differences, including variation in operational definitions of self-medication, differences in sampling strategies (such as community-based versus hospital-based samples), inconsistent recall periods, and significant demographic and regional variations in access to healthcare services. These inconsistencies reflect differences in study populations, settings (urban vs. rural), and how self-medication was defined and measured, all of which may have influenced the pooled estimate. For these reasons, the combined prevalence should be interpreted as a general indication rather than a precise national figure.
Rates in the United States and Western Europe typically range from 20 to 50%, while earlier reports documented even lower figures (8–14%) across regions such as South Asia, the United States, Australia, Germany, Spain, the United Kingdom, Sweden, Switzerland, Mexico, and Italy [ ]. A recent meta-analysis by Gueorguiev et al. (2023) further confirmed these disparities, showing very low prevalence in Northern Europe (e.g., 4.5% in Denmark, 0.43% in Sweden, and 5.1% in Israel), but considerably higher rates in Southern and Eastern Europe (18.9% in Portugal, 43% in Bulgaria, and 44.6% in Greece, with 22–31% in Lithuania [ ]. Overall, self-medication remains far less common in developed settings—particularly in Northern Europe compared with Pakistan.
The stark contrasts in self-medication patterns between Pakistan and developed countries can be explained by both structural and cultural factors. Easy and largely unregulated access to over-the-counter drugs, combined with shortages of physicians and healthcare facilities—especially in rural areas—pushes individuals toward self-medication. In addition, cultural beliefs in traditional remedies and a reliance on advice from family and peers further reinforce this practice, creating a cycle that normalizes self-medication [ , ]. These findings highlight self-medication as a pressing public health challenge that requires immediate policy attention. Strengthening pharmacy regulations and improving oversight could help reduce the sale of non-prescribed medications. In Pakistan, effective enforcement of existing pharmacy regulations is crucial to prevent prescription-only medicines from being dispensed without authorization. Enhancing the role of pharmacists within primary healthcare may also improve medication counseling and encourage rational drug use.
In terms of specific risk factors, a meta-analysis by Ahmed et al. (2023) found that male gender (95% CI: 1.19–1.75) and dissatisfaction with healthcare services or providers (95% CI: 2.26–4.75) were significantly associated with antibiotic self-medication. Subgroup analysis also showed that younger age was directly associated with self-medication in high-income countries (95% CI: 1.10–2.36), whereas in low- and middle-income countries, individuals with better knowledge about antibiotics were less likely to self-medicate (95% CI: 0.08–0.47) [ ].
Public awareness campaigns through electronic and social media can also play an important role in informing communities about the potential harms of self-medication. Together, these measures could promote more responsible medicine use and help reduce self-medication nationwide. At the same time, public education initiatives and improved access to affordable healthcare—particularly in underserved regions—could further reduce reliance on self-medication. Future studies should also explore cultural and economic drivers of self-medication and evaluate interventions designed to modify these behaviors. These findings are consistent with regional policy recommendations. For example, the Self-Care Federation’s South Asia summary highlights widespread self-medication in the region and calls for greater public awareness, stronger pharmacy oversight, and integration of self-care into national health strategies [ ]. We acknowledge that restricting the search to English-language publications may introduce language bias; however, this limitation is expected to have only a minor impact, as most scientific research in Pakistan is published in English.

## Limitations and strengths
This review has several limitations that should be considered. First, there was extremely high heterogeneity across studies (I² = 99.71%), likely resulting from differences in the definition of self-medication, sampling methods, participant populations, and data collection tools. Such variability may have led to inconsistencies in how outcomes were classified and reduced the comparability of results. Second, the studies covered a long period (2008–2025), during which Pakistan experienced major changes in economic conditions, healthcare access, and regulatory enforcement. These changes may have influenced self-medication practices over time, making the pooled estimate less stable. Third, most included studies were cross-sectional and relied on self-reported information, introducing recall and reporting bias and limiting the ability to draw causal conclusions. Fourth, the search was restricted to English-language publications, which may have caused language bias; however, this limitation is likely minor because most scientific research in Pakistan is published in English. Additionally, grey literature and conference abstracts were not included, which may have contributed to publication bias, although Begg’s test did not indicate significant asymmetry. Finally, nearly half of the included studies focused on university students, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader Pakistani population. The results should therefore be interpreted with caution, particularly when considering differences across regions, populations, and study designs. Despite these limitations, this study has important strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first comprehensive meta-analysis to estimate the pooled prevalence of self-medication in Pakistan, drawing on data from 65 studies and over 35,000 participants across diverse populations and provinces. The findings provide a valuable evidence base to guide public health policies, educational campaigns, and future research on medication practices in low- and middle-income settings.

## Conclusion
This meta-analysis suggests that self-medication is widespread in Pakistan, although the precise magnitude should be interpreted cautiously due to the substantial heterogeneity across studies. This behavior poses significant risks to individual health and is associated with broader public health challenges such as antimicrobial resistance and increased pressure on the healthcare system. Although the evidence synthesized in this review is observational and heterogeneous, the findings clearly indicate that self-medication represents a major public health concern in Pakistan and warrants careful attention from policymakers.
Rather than calling for immediate or prescriptive government action, the results of this study can help guide evidence-based strategies aimed at improving healthcare accessibility, promoting rational drug use, and strengthening regulatory oversight. Addressing this issue will require a coordinated effort involving government authorities, healthcare professionals, educators, and the general public. Without sustained attention, self-medication may continue to threaten individual well-being and undermine the long-term effectiveness of Pakistan’s healthcare system. By providing evidence on the prevalence and potential drivers of self-medication, this study can support policymakers and stakeholders in designing more targeted and effective public health interventions.
