## Abstract

## Background and Objectives
There is a paucity of data directly comparing the outcome of surgical techniques available for the treatment of hypothalamic hamartomas (HHs). This study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of commonly used surgical approaches in the treatment of HH-related epilepsy.

## Methods
A systematic review and individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis was conducted. The PubMed, Embase, and Scopus online databases were searched without any date restrictions for original studies with more than 1 participant reporting on patients with HH-related epilepsy who underwent surgical treatment. Random-effects modeling was used to calculate the pooled proportions of seizure freedom (Engel I) at the last follow-up. IPD were used to perform mixed-effects logistic regression to identify predictors of seizure freedom and major postoperative complications.

## Results
Sixty-four studies were included, and IPD on 517 patients were obtained. The overall quality of evidence was low. After the index procedure, the pooled proportion of overall seizure freedom was 50.0% (95% CI 42.7%–57.4%), which increased to 64.5% (95% CI 57.2%–71.5%) after multiple treatments. Magnetic resonance–guided laser interstitial thermal therapy (MRgLITT) and radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC) demonstrated the highest efficacy at the last follow-up, with seizure freedom rates of 74.5% (95% CI 66.8%–81.7%) and 78.5% (95% CI 71.6%–84.8%), respectively. Factors independently associated with a lower likelihood of seizure freedom included multiple seizure types (odds ratio [OR] 0.296, 95% CI 0.140–0.624, p = 0.001) and previous surgery (OR 0.418, 95% CI 0.198–0.884, p = 0.023). Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) was the safest approach, with a pooled proportion of major complications of 0.0% (95% CI 0.0%–1.4%). Only surgical technique emerged as an independent predictor of major complications, with SRS (OR 0.024, 95% CI 0.002–0.292, p = 0.004), RFTC (OR 0.133, 95% CI 0.026–0.692, p = 0.017), and MRgLITT (OR 0.234, 95% CI 0.056–0.968, p = 0.045) being associated with a lower likelihood of major complications.

## Discussion
MRgLITT and RFTC offer superior efficacy and safety compared with open microsurgery and should be considered as first-line options. Despite its lower efficacy, SRS is associated with few reported long-term complications, making it a viable alternative for select cases, such as small HHs with good baseline functioning. Direct comparisons between techniques are limited by short follow-up durations in RFTC and MRgLITT cohorts. Further large-scale, multicenter studies directly comparing these modalities are warranted.

## Introduction
Hypothalamic hamartoma (HH) is a rare ciliopathy characterized by an epileptogenic neuroglial lesion in the hypothalamus typically presenting with childhood-onset gelastic seizures. HH-related epilepsy is characteristically unresponsive to medical therapy, with more than 80% of individuals progressing to drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). Up to half of these patients develop a progressive encephalopathy characterized by catastrophic epilepsy, endocrine dysfunction, cognitive impairment, psychiatric comorbidities, and a major decline in quality of life. The development and progression of encephalopathy in HH is believed to be a result of chronic synchronized and independent interictal and ictal epileptiform discharges that spread from the hamartoma to distributed frontal, parietal, and temporal nodes of important cognitive networks. , Seizures are believed to spread to these extrahypothalamic nodes through the mammillo-tegmental fasciculus, mammillo-thalamo-cingulate pathway, and the fornices of the Papez circuit. , In the presence of long-standing epilepsy, some authors have described a broadening of the epileptic network and formation of secondary extrahypothalamic epileptic foci associated with additional focal or generalized seizure types—although the exact pathophysiology of this phenomenon is unknown. , , 
Given the intrinsic epileptogenicity of HH, identification and surgical resection/disconnection of the lesion are crucial for optimal outcomes. Hamartomas can be challenging to detect on imaging because of their location and small size. However, high-resolution 3T MRI with specific epilepsy protocols can effectively identify HHs. In addition, despite the proven benefit of surgery for seizure control, surgical resection of HHs is not without risks. The significant morbidity associated with open surgery has prompted the adoption of less invasive techniques in recent years. Transventricular endoscopic disconnection emerged as a less invasive option with reduced postoperative morbidity for a subset of patients with smaller intraventricular HHs and a unilateral, vertical plane of attachment. Despite shorter length of stay and improved safety compared with open microsurgery, approximately 10% of patients experience postoperative deficits. , Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) represents a noninvasive alternative with low rates of complications. However, SRS is relatively less effective compared with other approaches, and its delayed efficacy makes it less ideal for patients with rapidly progressive disease. , In recent years, thermoablation techniques, including radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC) and magnetic resonance–guided laser interstitial thermal therapy (MRgLITT), have been adopted as first-line treatments in many centers worldwide because of their improved safety profile and high efficacy. High-volume Japanese and American centers have reported overall seizure freedom rates of more than 70% in patients after multiple procedures. , Despite the availability of various options, there is little evidence directly comparing these techniques. In this study, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the safety and efficacy of various surgical techniques used in the treatment of HH-related epilepsy and identify predictors of seizure freedom and postoperative complications. 

## Methods
A systematic review and individual participant data meta-analysis (IPDMA) was performed. Findings were reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Individual Participant Data guideline. This study was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42022378876), and our study protocol was previously published. 

## Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents
This study did not involve direct experimentation on human participants or animals. Therefore, no ethical standards committee approval or written informed consent was required. The authors received no funding for this study.

## Search Strategy
The PubMed, Scopus, and Embase databases were searched from inception to June 18, 2022, without restrictions on language or study design. The search strategy consisted of the following keywords and their variations: “hypothalamic hamartoma,” “laser ablation,” “radiofrequency ablation,” “radiosurgery,” “endoscopic,” and “neurosurgical procedures.” The full search strategy is available in eTable 1. Our search was updated in January 2024 and June 2024, and all relevant articles were added manually.

## Screening and Eligibility Criteria
Two authors (F.N., K.G.) independently screened articles during title and abstract screening and full-text screening for eligibility based on predetermined criteria. All conflicts were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Studies had to meet the following criteria: (1) case-control, cohort, or randomized controlled design, (2) at least 30% of the reported patients undergoing surgical treatment for HH-related DRE, and (3) available postoperative seizure outcomes. Exclusion criteria were the following: (1) case reports, (2) studies reporting on HH-related endocrinopathy without epilepsy, (3) nonoriginal studies (e.g., reviews and book chapters), and (4) studies for which full text was not available in English. If multiple studies with overlapping data were available from one center, only the most recent study with the largest cohort that fit our eligibility criteria was included.

## Data Extraction
Authors of all studies without IPD were contacted to request IPD. Once included, relevant data from all articles were extracted independently by 2 authors (F.N., K.G.). Variables of interest for IPD included basic demographic data, seizure types and frequency, Delalande classification, HH size, preoperative comorbidities (neuropsychological, neuropsychiatric, and endocrine), previous surgery, type of surgery and the technique used, overall seizure outcome, duration of follow-up, occurrence of major and minor postoperative complications, outcome of preoperative comorbidities, type of repeat procedures, and outcome after repeat procedures.

## Outcomes
The primary outcome was the likelihood of seizure freedom, defined as Engel I or International League Against Epilepsy 1 or 2. The secondary outcome of interest was the risk of major complications after surgical intervention. Complications were defined as new-onset postoperative deficit or worsening of existing preoperative comorbidities. In accordance with previous literature, complications were considered major if they persisted more than 3 months and affected activities of daily living. All complications were divided into the following subcategories: hypothalamic (e.g., hyperphagia, polydipsia, and poikilothermia), endocrine (e.g., hormone deficiencies and diabetes insipidus), neurologic (e.g., motor and visual deficits), surgical (e.g., procedure-related hemorrhage or infection), behavioral (behavioral disturbance), and cognitive (deficits in any cognitive domain such as language or memory). A category combining injuries to the hypothalamic-pituitary axis into one subtype was also created for statistical analyses.

## Quality Assessment
Two authors (F.N., K.G.) independently assessed the quality and risk of bias of included articles. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework was used for quality appraisal, and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies—of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used for assessment of bias. 

## Statistical Analysis
A 2-stage meta-analysis of proportions with random-effects modeling was performed. Pooled proportions of seizure freedom after the index procedure and after multiple procedures were calculated. For the meta-analysis of seizure outcomes after multiple procedures, studies including patients undergoing multiple different treatment modalities were excluded. The incidence of major complications was calculated using a similar methodology. Individual proportions and effect strengths of each study were calculated using the inverse-variance model, from which pooled proportions of all studies were derived. A Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used for seizure freedom analyses, and an arcsine transformation was used for complication analysis. Results were visualized using forest plots. Interstudy heterogeneity was studied with the I2 statistic. Subgroup analyses based on intervention type and meta-regressions were performed to study sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots.
For our IPDMA, missing data were imputed using multiple imputations by chained equations. Variables with <45% missing data were imputed, while other variables were excluded. To account for presurgical differences in Delalande classification between various surgical techniques, propensity scores were calculated using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Ten datasets were imputed for each analysis. The parameters and standard errors were then pooled using the Rubin rule. 
Mixed-effects logistic regression using the study as the random variable was performed to identify predictors of seizure freedom at follow-up durations >12 months. We then repeated this process for patients with >36 months of follow-up to account for the delayed efficacy of SRS. We first performed univariable analysis to identify putative predictors of outcomes. We set our threshold at a p value of <0.200 for selection of variables for the multivariable analysis. During multivariable analysis, backward stepwise selection of variables using the Akaike information criterion was performed. During regression analysis, the reference category was open microsurgery for surgical technique and Delalande type II for Delalande classification. A similar methodology was used to identify predictors of major complications. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.2; RStudio, Inc., Boston, MA).

## Data Availability
Data used in this article are available upon request to the corresponding author.

## Results

## Study Selection
Our search yielded 1,835 articles, of which 55 met the inclusion criteria. An additional 9 articles were added manually after the search was updated in January 2024 and June 2024 for a total of 64 articles ( ). IPD were obtained for 54 studies including a total of 517 patients. No issues regarding IPD integrity were identified. In total, 58 studies had a nonrandomized retrospective design while 6 were prospective observational studies. The overall quality of evidence of included studies was low according to the GRADE framework. ## Study-Level Meta-Analysis
The main findings of our study-level analyses are summarized in . The overall likelihood of seizure freedom at the last follow-up after the index procedure was 50.0% (95% CI 42.7%–57.4%). Pooled proportions of overall seizure freedom of all surgical techniques are displayed in . On subgroup analysis, MRgLITT achieved the highest rate of seizure freedom after index procedures (69.5%, 95% CI 59.6%–78.8%), which was superior to open microsurgery (χ2 = 5.44, p = 0.020) but not RFTC (χ2 = 1.39, p = 0.238). SRS achieved the lowest rate of seizure freedom (31.0%, 95% CI 17.1%–46.4%). Interstudy heterogeneity persisted within the RFTC (Q statistic: p = 0.002, I2 = 66%) and open microsurgery (Q statistic: p < 0.001, I2 = 76%) subgroups. Subgroup analysis of open microsurgery and RFTC based on technique is available in eFigures 1 and 2. The test for subgroup difference demonstrated the superiority of the transcallosal approach (59.8%, 95% CI 43.6%–75.3%) compared with lateral techniques (26.3%, 95% CI 12.0%–42.8%) through pterional and orbitozygomatic craniotomies (χ2 = 7.84, p = 0.005). Within the open microsurgery subgroup, studies from Asia/Oceania were more likely to report better outcomes (odds ratio [OR] 1.334, 95% CI 1.017–1.749, p = 0.037) (eTable 3). Subgroup analysis based on the RFTC technique (eFigure 2) revealed no difference between SEEG-guided (55.4%, 95% CI 26.6%–82.5%) and volumetric (59.3%, 95% CI 34.6%–82.2%) RFTC (χ2 = 0.02, p = 0.890). On meta-regression, a more recent year of publication was associated with a better outcome within the RFTC subgroup (OR 1.033, 95% CI 1.013–1.053, p = 0.001, eTable 4).
 When considering both index and repeat procedures, the overall likelihood of seizure freedom improved to 64.5% (95% CI 57.2%–71.5%). The pooled proportion of seizure freedom stratified by surgical technique is displayed in . On subgroup analysis, RFTC and MRgLITT demonstrated superior efficacy over endoscopic surgery and SRS with seizure freedom rates of 78.5% (95% CI 71.6%–84.8%) and 74.5% (95% CI 66.8%–81.7%), respectively. On the test for subgroup differences between these approaches and open microsurgery, both RFTC and MRgLITT achieved better outcomes but this did not reach significance for MRgLITT (RFTC: χ2 = 4.52, p = 0.034; MRgLITT: χ2 = 2.97, p = 0.085). Open microsurgery was the only subgroup with substantial heterogeneity (Q statistic: p < 0.001, I2 = 66%). Subgroup analysis based on surgical approaches demonstrated superior efficacy (χ2 = 7.94, p = 0.005) of the vertical approach (67.2%, 95% CI 50.0%–82.9%), compared with lateral approaches (29.3%, 95% CI 12.0%–49.3%, eFigure 3). On meta-regression, we did not identify significant predictors of outcome (eTable 5).
The pooled proportion of major complications after all procedures was 7.6% (95% CI 3.4%–13.2%) ( ). Open microsurgery carried the highest risk of major complications at 29.1% (95% CI 9.5%–54.2%). SRS was the safest technique with a pooled proportion of major complications of 0.0% (95% CI 0.0%–1.4%). The test for subgroup differences between the minimally invasive approaches and open microsurgery revealed that both MRgLITT (χ2 = 5.15, p = 0.023) and SRS (χ2 = 15.31, p < 0.001) were associated with a lower likelihood of major complications. RFTC's pooled proportion of major complications was lower than that of open microsurgery, but this did not reach significance on the test for subgroup differences (9.4% vs 29.1%, χ2 = 3.35, p = 0.067). No difference was observed between RFTC and MRgLITT (χ2 = 0.91, p = 0.341).
 Visual inspection of funnel plots revealed no publication bias in our analysis of seizure freedom and overall major complications (eFigure 4). During sensitivity analysis, removing cohorts with less than 5 patients did not significantly change the outcomes (eFigures 5 and 6).

## IPD Meta-Analysis
IPD were obtained for a total of 517 patients. Clinical characteristics of included patients are presented in . Most patients harbored a Delalande type II or III hamartoma (215/316, 68.0%). The most common seizure type was gelastic seizure (456/496, 91.9%). However, most patients (302/455, 66.4%) also had additional focal and/or generalized seizures. Seizure freedom at the last follow-up was achieved in 243 (47.0%) of 517 patients after the index procedure. Complications occurred in 118 (36.1%) of 327 patients and were major in 53 (15.1%) of 352. The most common type of major complication was hypothalamic injury occurring in 23 (7.0%) of 327 patients. Overall, major hypothalamic/endocrine complications occurred in 34 (10.4%) of 327 patients. A breakdown of outcomes based on the Delalande type is available in eFigure 7. Overall, giant hamartomas (type IV) did worse with an overall seizure freedom rate of 31.6% while other hamartomas tended to achieve similar outcomes ranging from 45.2% to 51.6%. Surprisingly, despite having a high rate of overall complications, giant hamartomas had the lowest rate of major complications (10.9%), compared with other types. Clinical presentations, surgical techniques, and outcomes of patients stratified by Delalande type are available in eTable 6. Among all 517 patients, 87 (16.8%) underwent reoperations. Nine patients underwent 3 procedures, and 1 underwent 4 procedures. After all reoperations, Engel I was achieved in 39 (51.3%) of patients for whom outcome was reported.
 Comorbidities were common among patients. A summary of comorbidities and their outcome can be found in eTable 7. The most common type of comorbidity was cognitive impairment (e.g., memory deficits and language impairment), present in 188 (63.3%) of 297 patients, followed by neuropsychiatric impairment (e.g., rage attacks and generalized anxiety disorder) in 133 (52.4%) of 254 and precocious puberty in 92 (29.4%) of 313. The status of comorbidities did not change in most patients at the last follow-up, and a small portion of patients experienced an improvement in their comorbidities. Although a paired t test between the presurgical and postsurgical full-scale IQ (FSIQ) revealed an increase in FSIQ (mean difference = 6.76, 95% CI 4.97–8.57, p < 0.001), the difference was less than half of the 1 standard deviation threshold (an increase in more than 15 points) of significant clinical improvement.
On multivariable logistic regression, the presence of both gelastic and nongelastic seizures and previous epilepsy surgery before inclusion in the study were independently associated with a lower likelihood of seizure freedom (OR 0.296, 95% CI 0.140–0.624, p = 0.001 and OR 0.418, 95% CI 0.198–0.884, p = 0.023, respectively) among patients who were followed for more than a year after their initial procedure ( ). This association was not present when only patients with follow-up periods longer than 3 years were included (eTable 8). No independent predictors of seizure freedom were identified among patients with follow-up time longer than 3 years.
 Multivariable logistic regression for major complications revealed that only surgical technique was an independent predictor of outcomes ( ). SRS (OR 0.024, 95% CI 0.002–0.292, p = 0.004), RFTC (OR 0.133, 95% CI 0.026–0.692, p = 0.017), and MRgLITT (OR 0.234, 95% CI 0.056–0.968, p = 0.045) were all independently associated with a lower likelihood of major complications compared with open microsurgery. Among complication subtypes, only major hypothalamic/endocrine complications contained sufficient data to perform a multivariable logistic regression analysis. Our regression model showed that SRS (OR 0.038, 95% CI 0.003–0.482, p = 0.012) was independently associated with a lower risk of major hypothalamic/endocrine complications compared with other interventions (eTable 9).

## Discussion
Although a multitude of surgical techniques with inherently different risks and benefits are now available for the treatment of HH-related epilepsy, very few studies directly compare these techniques. This knowledge gap precludes evidence-based, data-driven recommendations to guide surgical management. We performed an IPDMA to compare the safety and efficacy of all commonly used surgical techniques in the treatment of HH-related DRE. We found an overall seizure freedom rate of 50.0% after index procedures, which improved to 64.5% after all interventions. RFTC and MRgLITT offered the highest rate of overall seizure freedom while SRS was the safest approach on study-level and IPD meta-analysis. Presence of multiple seizure types and previous intervention were independently associated with worse seizure outcomes. Surgical technique was the only predictor of postoperative complications, with minimally invasive approaches (i.e., SRS, MRgLITT, and RFTC) being independently associated with a lower likelihood of major complications.
On study-level analysis, we observed a 14.5% increase in the overall seizure freedom rate after all procedures compared with index procedures alone. While the inclusion of different studies in these analyses precludes a direct comparison, this difference highlights the clear therapeutic benefit of repeat or staged operations for appropriately selected patients. It also highlights the importance of a complete resection and/or disconnection of the hamartoma and the potential benefit of staged operations, particularly with less invasive procedures, because they are likely to maximize the likelihood of resection/disconnection and minimize seizure recurrence. Regarding safety, the overall rate of major complications was 7.6% after all interventions, which was lower than that obtained in the IPD analysis (15.1%). We attribute this difference to the absence of IPD from larger cohorts of SRS, MRgLITT, and RFTC with more expertise and better safety profiles that were solely included in our study-level analysis.
Our findings demonstrated that open microsurgery offered markedly lower efficacy compared with thermoablative techniques and had the highest rate of major complications. Subgroup analysis revealed that the vertical transcallosal interforniceal approach offered better efficacy compared with the lateral transsylvian approaches. Because most of the patients with HH-related epilepsy harbor intrahypothalamic lesions (types II and III), the transcallosal interforniceal window provides a better view of the hamartoma and its lateral connections with the hypothalamus and mammillary bodies, leading to a more complete and safer resection. However, the lateral approaches may prove beneficial in pedunculated lesions with associated endocrinopathies. Overall, given the higher risk of major complications and the lower efficacy of open microsurgery compared with RFTC and MRgLITT, it should solely be considered in situations where these less invasive alternatives are not available.
After all procedures were considered, SRS achieved an overall seizure freedom rate of 43.4%, which was comparable with endoscopic surgery. The efficacy of SRS may increase even further over longer follow-up durations, given its well-known delayed efficacy of up to 3 years. , Because long-term data are unavailable after MRgLITT and RFTC in most studies with IPD, a direct comparison of these techniques is challenging. While several studies have suggested that secondary malignancies do not occur at a higher rate after SRS than in the general population, there are several reported cases of post-SRS malignancy, which should be considered, particularly for pediatric patients. Both study-level and IPD analyses suggest that SRS has the lowest rate of permanent morbidity. Although transient hypothalamic complications occurred in some patients, only 2 patients in the cohort experienced major, permanent complications (i.e., hypothalamic injury and behavioral disturbances) after SRS. , Overall, SRS offers great safety at the cost of lower efficacy compared with MRgLITT, RFTC, and open microsurgery, making it a plausible alternative for patients with small HHs and lower tolerance to postoperative deficits or older patients who are not surgical candidates.
Endoscopic surgery achieved similar outcomes to open microsurgery after the index procedure. A slight decrease in the seizure freedom rate after multiple procedures was observed, which is likely related to the fact that studies with multiple treatment modalities were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, a large number of patients were excluded from our final analysis, limiting the total number of patients to 53. The largest experience with endoscopic surgery reported a 57.1% seizure freedom (Engel 1); however, many patients underwent open microsurgery, leading to the exclusion of this study from our analysis. Although several studies have reported slightly improved safety of endoscopic surgery compared with open microsurgery, , our multivariable regression did not reveal a significant difference between endoscopic surgery and open microsurgery, highlighting the non-negligible risk of morbidity associated with this technique. It is, however, important to highlight that the abovementioned largest endoscopic series of 112 patients had a 8.3% rate of morbidity, which was similar to the overall rate of major complications in our study-level analysis (6.9%). Thus, despite its limitations, endoscopic surgery can be considered for small type II hamartomas, if other minimally invasive options are not available. It may also have a role in the resection/disconnection of residual hamartoma causing seizure recurrence in previously treated patients.
Considering all procedures, MRgLITT and RFTC had the highest rate of overall seizure freedom at 74.5% and 78.5%, respectively. Regarding surgical technique, we identified no significant difference between the SEEG-guided and volumetric RFTC. Both RFTC and MRgLITT have been increasingly adopted as first-line treatment of HH, given their improved safety and efficacy over open microsurgery. Although MRgLITT and RFTC were initially recommended for smaller lesions, indications have expanded to include larger hamartomas with irregular shapes. Most Delalande type IV hamartomas (59.6%) in our study were treated using either RFTC or MRgLITT, which explains the surprisingly low rate of major complications among patients with giant hamartomas (10.9%). Both techniques offer an advantage over open microsurgery because of their superior safety, possibility of reoperations, and the use of multiple trajectories in one intervention, allowing a larger ablation of the hamartoma in a safe manner. , , However, MRgLITT offers the advantage of greater ablation per probe and real-time feedback with MR thermometry. Large case series have shown that greater ablation volumes may be associated with greater likelihood of seizure freedom. Furthermore, many authors have postulated that certain areas within hamartomas such as its connections with the mammillary bodies are more involved in seizure propagation. For example, targeting of specific regions within the hamartoma using resting-state fMRI may improve seizure outcomes while minimizing postoperative morbidity. Using this method, a previous study achieved a marked improvement in both seizure reduction rate and overall seizure freedom compared with their control group not guided by resting-state fMRI, as well as other reports in the literature, although these results remain to be replicated by other centers. Regarding safety, transient complications were common in both MRgLITT and RFTC cohorts, but both tended to have a lower likelihood of major complications compared with open microsurgery on study-level and IPD analyses. Thus, considering their superior safety compared with traditional open microsurgery and their higher efficacy compared with endoscopic surgery and SRS, MRgLITT and RFTC should be considered as first-line treatments for patients with HH-related DRE, provided they are available at the physician's center.
The IPD analysis revealed that in patients with a minimum of 1-year follow-up, only the presence of multiple seizure types and previous epilepsy surgery were associated with a lower likelihood of seizure freedom. In appropriately selected patients, notably those treated with minimally invasive procedures, staged operations may be beneficial to target residual HH or hamartomas with irregular shapes to reduce the seizure burden and improve the neuropsychological function. , However, it must be noted that most patients with previous surgery in our study underwent microsurgical or endoscopic resection, suggesting that reoperation after previous resective surgery may be associated with risks of seizure recurrence, as demonstrated previously in the literature. , This may also reflect a selection bias because many patients with previous open microsurgery, who have more recently undergone contemporary less invasive interventions, have a protracted clinical course characterized by long-standing seizures and possibly a more widespread, complex epileptic network contributing to their worse outcomes. Presence of other seizure types in addition to gelastic seizures is also often considered a sign of long-standing disease and complex seizure networks with potential secondary epileptogenesis in other cortical areas, reducing the likelihood of seizure freedom even after total resection/disconnection of the hamartoma. , , These findings suggest that earlier diagnosis and surgical treatment of HH are crucial in improving outcomes in patients. , , 
Our study has some limitations. First, most included studies had a retrospective design with high risk of bias. In addition, although we sought to provide a comprehensive overview of all surgical techniques used in the treatment of HH, alternative approaches such as stereotactic interstitial radiosurgery and focused ultrasound were not included because of the lack of sufficient long-term data in the literature. An important component of meta-analyses is the assumption of transitivity, which posits the comparability of treatment effects across different studies. However, there are inherent biases in patient selection for various surgical techniques, and not all hamartomas are amenable to the approaches described in our study. To address these potential biases, our IPD analyses included mixed-effects modeling and propensity score weighting to account for interstudy variability and differences in baseline characteristics between patients, respectively. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed for individual surgical techniques that demonstrated high interstudy heterogeneity on study-level meta-analysis. However, ideal surgical strategies for various clinical presentations can only be addressed in future prospective trials. Furthermore, the lack of data on the exact time of seizure recurrence precluded survival analyses to account for variability in follow-up durations. This may have introduced a potential bias favoring surgical techniques with shorter follow-up durations such as MRgLITT and RFTC, compared with open microsurgery. Finally, our analysis on comorbidities was limited by the lack of standardized testing and reliance on descriptive assessments based on patient/family reports. These comorbidities pose a significant burden to patients and caregivers. Thus, long-term prospective trials collecting data on various comorbidities in patients with HH are warranted to address current knowledge gaps and optimize treatment for these patients.
We performed a study-level and IPDMA assessing the safety and efficacy of the most commonly used surgical techniques in the treatment of HH-related epilepsy. Our findings suggest that MRgLITT and RFTC offer the highest rate of overall seizure freedom and should be used as first-line treatments, given their immediate efficacy. SRS has the lowest rate of reported major complications, making it a viable alternative in a subset of patients with small HHs, an indolent disease course, and a high level of baseline functioning. Open microsurgery is rarely indicated and should only be used as a last resort when other techniques are unavailable, considering its lower efficacy in comparison with thermoablative techniques and the associated postoperative morbidity. Our conclusions remain limited by the quality of data available in the literature. Further multicenter retrospective and prospective trials directly comparing the safety and efficacy of all surgical techniques are thus warranted to confirm the findings of our review.
