Sex Disparities in Chlamydia and Gonorrhea Treatment in US Adult Emergency Departments: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

20820242024.08.20.24312317medRxiv10.1101/2024.08.20.24312317PMC1137051339228735

## Abstract

## Background
Empiric antibiotic treatment for gonorrhea (GC) and chlamydia (CT) is common in U.S. emergency departments (EDs) due to the delayed availability of test results. Local studies have identified sex‐based disparities in sexually transmitted infection (STI) care, with females potentially receiving less empiric treatment than males. This study evaluates how STI treatment aligns with laboratory‐confirmed results to quantify potential overtreatment and undertreatment rates and investigates sex differences for these outcomes.

## Methods
We conducted a preregistered (PROSPERO #241429) systematic review and meta‐analysis of studies published between January 2010 and January 2025 in U.S. EDs, excluding studies conducted exclusively in pediatric settings. Searches were performed in Medline, Cochrane, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, and PsycINFO. Data from 19 studies encompassing 32,593 tested patients were analyzed using a random‐effects model to calculate pooled estimates. Primary outcomes included GC/CT positivity, empiric treatment rates, and discordance between treatment and test results stratified by sex.

## Results
Overall GC/CT positivity was 14% (95% confidence interval [CI] 12%–16%): 11% (95% CI 8%–14%) in females and 25% (95% CI 23%–26%) in males. Antibiotics were given to 46% (95% CI 38%–54%) of patients: 31% (95% CI 24%–37%) of females and 73% (95% CI 65%–80%) of males. Among patients with negative test results, 38% (95% CI 30%–47%) received antibiotics (i.e., potential overtreatment): 27% (95% CI 20%–34%) of females and 64% (95% CI 55%–73%) of males. Conversely, 39% (95% CI 31%–46%) of laboratory‐positive patients did not receive antibiotics (i.e., potential undertreatment): 52% (95% CI 46%–57%) of females and 15% (95% CI 12%–17%) of males.

## Conclusions
Significant sex‐based disparities exist in ED empiric antibiotic treatment for GC/CT. Females were 3.5 times more likely than males to be potentially undertreated. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to reduce disparities and improve treatment accuracy. Interpretation is limited by study heterogeneity and incomplete sex‐specific data.

## DISCUSSION
This GC/CT treatment meta‐analysis in U.S. EDs reveals substantial discordance between empiric treatment practices and final laboratory results, resulting in high levels of potential undertreatment and overtreatment of STIs. Results also demonstrate large sex differences in empiric treatment, with females more frequently undertreated. Females tested for GC/CT were approximately half as likely to receive empiric antibiotics and 3.5 times more likely to be undertreated than males. These findings underscore critical sex disparities in empiric antibiotic treatment in EDs, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to address the undertreatment of females for STIs.
A key contributor to these discrepancies is the inherent delay in laboratory test results, which are often not available during the ED visit. This time lag reduces the applicability of laboratory‐based criterion standard diagnostics for real‐time ED decision making, necessitating reliance on clinical judgment. Consequently, clinicians must navigate uncertainty which may inadvertently contribute to overtreatment and undertreatment. Patients’ health outcomes are negatively impacted by this reduced diagnostic accuracy.
While reducing undertreatment is critical to preventing complications from untreated infections and reducing the spread of STIs, overtreatment also carries consequences. Overuse of empiric antibiotics increases the risk of adverse reactions, contributes to antimicrobial resistance, exposes patients to unnecessary medication side effects, and when considering the nursing tasks involved in intramuscular ceftriaxone, diverts resources from other patients. Balancing these risks in the absence of STI laboratory results requires consideration of the patient's risk factors and, in certain cases, shared decision making (SDM) on patients’ ability to follow‐up. These considerations may be challenging to accomplish in time‐constrained ED settings and highlight the pressing clinical and public health need for implementing rapid STI tests for STIs to the ED.
This meta‐analysis builds on previous findings of discordance between empiric treatment and laboratory results in EDs. A 2013 database review of 1877 visits by females aged 14–21 at an academic ED of a Children's Hospital found that over half were potentially overtreated based on clinical judgment. Our findings show a lower rate of potential overtreatment (38%), likely due to the inclusion of older age groups with lower STI incidence. The most recent comprehensive review of ED STI treatment before this meta‐analysis, published in 2013, found wide variation in potential undertreatment at 38%–84% of general patients. We found potential undertreatment at the lower end of the range at 39%, which may reflect a change in practice, such as an increase in patients receiving treatment for STIs versus a more narrow screening selection.
Sex differences in empiric antibiotic treatment for GC/CT may stem from epidemiologic trends, disease etiology, and clinician biases. Males, particularly those who are gay and bisexual, have higher rates of GC and syphilis. They are more likely to present with symptomatic GC, potentially leading to higher empiric treatment rates due to higher clinician pretest probabilities, as demonstrated in our results. Conversely, females may be more frequently tested for GC/CT in the ED due to a broader differential diagnosis in patients with lower abdominal or pelvic complaints, increasing the denominator of those tested. But due to lower test positivity rates and because females are often asymptomatic, , they may receive lower empiric treatment rates. Implicit and explicit sex biases may also drive some of these disparities. These biases may result in less medical treatment for females overall; for example, one ED prospective cohort study found that females were up to 25% less likely than males to receive opioids for acute abdominal pain. Addressing biases through clinician awareness is one approach that may facilitate improved treatment equity. 

Despite the rationale for these sex‐based treatment differences, sex disparities in sexual and reproductive health care have serious negative consequences for females. Untreated females face an increased risk of HIV and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, and infant death. Considering the grave reproductive health implications for females, addressing undertreatment in females must be a clinical and public health priority.

## Practice and policy implications and solutions
Given these gaps in empiric treatment practices, this study highlights the need for interventions to align STI treatment with laboratory‐confirmed outcomes. Implementing rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) in EDs could reduce discordance between empiric treatment and laboratory results. RDTs yield test results during the ED visit and improve the clinical accuracy of treatment, decrease antimicrobial usage, and reduce ED return rates. , , While RDTs can cost an ED more than a standard laboratory test, they may reduce costs by saving clinicians time and length of stay for patients in the ED. While RDTs for GC/CT are not yet widely used in EDs, new technologies now available for use in EDs , , , , , , offer expansion opportunities. RDTs may not only improve treatment accuracy overall, but also reduce undertreatment in females , and mitigate sex‐based disparities by reducing subjective decision making, a particularly challenging approach to empiric treatment when considering STIs are often asymptomatic. Future studies should evaluate how RDT implementation affects disparities in treatment between males and females and their relationship to empiric antibiotic treatment.
Developing a SDM approach might mitigate the potential overtreatment of STIs in EDs. SDM is already employed in the ED for situations such as CT scans for renal colic. In patients with reliable contact mechanisms and at EDs with clear follow‐up protocols, SDM could be used with clinical gestalt to decide whether to empirically treat in the ED or defer until laboratory confirmation is available after the ED visit. Depending on local ED workflows and system capacity, this aftercare option may be provided by routine telephone follow‐up “callbacks.” The viability of delaying treatment until laboratory‐confirmation is supported by studies reporting ED callback success ranging between 53% and 93%, , , , , and a reduction in STI undertreatment from an average of 67% to 16%. Future research could investigate SDM's role in ED antibiotic stewardship for STIs.
The high proportion of potentially undertreated ED patients presents an opportunity to improve STI care during telephone follow‐up callbacks. During a callback, an ED clinician typically reports the positive STI finding and either prescribes medication or advises the patient to return to the ED for treatment. Another opportunity during this call is STI partner management. Expedited partner therapy (EPT), the evidence‐based practice of treating unseen partners of patients with STIs, is little used in EDs. Although the CDC has endorsed EPT for nearly two decades, variation in state laws meant it only recently became permissible or potentially allowable in all U.S. states. , , Thus, offering EPT in an ED callback for STIs is a readily attainable intervention to increase treatment and reduce STI reinfections. Additionally, HIV prevention medication, PrEP, has recently been recommended by the CDC for an expanded number of populations and indications, with STIs being a recognized risk factor for HIV seroconversion. This STI callback setting also presents a natural opportunity for EDs to consider PrEP education and initiation in settings with good linkage to community partners, especially considering the underrecognized role EDs can play in HIV prevention and ED patients’ interests in starting PrEP. , , , 

## STRENGTH AND LIMITATIONS
The strength of this meta‐analysis lies in its extensive, high‐quality clinical data set, which encompasses a large cohort of individuals tested and treated across various EDs in the United States. Additionally, the study addresses sex‐specific differences in empiric treatment, a critical gap in the existing literature on STI management in ED settings that can inform policy and clinical changes regarding how EDs treat empiric STIs.
This study has limitations in cross‐study comparisons common to meta‐analyses of prevalence studies, including limited geographic diversity, the heterogeneity of study populations, and variation in the treatment time frame. First, there was high heterogeneity in the pooled prevalence estimates, as indicated by the I
2 value of over 95%. However, this is in keeping with similar literature, as prevalence meta‐analyses frequently have higher I
2 values—a recent review of meta‐analyses in prevalence studies reported a median I
2 of 97%. Due to inconsistent sex‐specific data reporting across studies, some subgroups (e.g., male‐only) a few studies, which limited the ability to calculate heterogeneity statistics such as I
2. This limited data constrained our ability to fully explore sources of heterogeneity via meta‐regression or more granular subgroup analyses. Next, none of the included studies were from the West, a region that, together with the South, reports the highest STI rates. None of the studies specifically focused on patients from rural regions, and it is unclear how many patients included in the studies might have originated from rural areas before seeking care. Rural populations often face unique barriers to STI care, such as limited access to EDs and diagnostic facilities, which could lead to higher undertreatment rates.
Second, there were comparatively limited data on males. Only one study included data on male patients, compared to eight for female patients. Many articles did not provide sex‐specific results on GC/CT prevalence or treatment, limiting our ability to analyze sex differences across all studies. The overrepresentation of females in included studies may amplify observed undertreatment rates and limit insights into male‐specific treatment practices.
Third, this analysis focused primarily on treatment administered during the ED visit and had limited data on treatment during ED follow‐up (e.g., via telephone callbacks), the inclusion of which would have reduced the apparent undertreatment rates. Moreover, data on follow‐up treatment outside of ED visits were not reported and could have also reduced the undertreatment rates. In patients without laboratory‐confirmed infection, recent exposure may have occurred within the window before laboratory detection, making empiric treatment appropriate even if not confirmed by laboratory results. Data did not differentiate between genital and extragenital GC/CT diagnoses, which is crucial for managing STIs, especially among men who have sex men. However, national administrative claims data demonstrate that extragenital diagnoses are infrequently made in EDs, so it is unlikely that these data included any substantial proportion. Lastly, the studies had limited specification on whether the included populations were symptomatic. Asymptomatic patients likely receive less empiric treatment; thus, pooling the symptomatic and asymptomatic patients may show higher than expected undertreatment rates.

## CONCLUSIONS
In this meta‐analysis of adult patients tested for gonorrhea/chlamydia in U.S. EDs, we found significant sex differences in empiric antibiotic treatment and large discordances between empiric antibiotic treatment and laboratory test results. These findings highlight an urgent need to develop strategies to address sex disparities and improve the accuracy of empiric antibiotic treatment for gonorrhea/chlamydia in ED settings.
