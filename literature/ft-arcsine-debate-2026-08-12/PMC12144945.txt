## Background
Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) for proximal humerus bone loss is linked with increased risks of complications and reoperation. This study aimed to compare the complication and reoperation rates between patients with unaddressed humeral defect (control group [CG]) and those receiving proximal humerus allograft prosthesis composite (APC) or mega-prosthesis (MP) (study group [SG]).

## Methods
Systematic searches were conducted in MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase to find studies on rTSA outcomes in proximal humerus bone loss cases. Search terms included boolean combinations of “proximal humerus bone loss,” “revision shoulder replacement,” “arthroplasty,” “prosthesis,” and “reconstruction.” Studies with <24 months follow-up, humeral defects over 7 cm or under 3 cm, case reports, and oncologic resections were excluded. Study design, demographic data, surgical methods, and outcomes were extracted and analyzed. A proportional meta-analysis compared complication and reoperation rates between CG and SG, with subgroup analyses for APC and MP. Multivariate logistic regression identified predictive prognostic factors, using the modified Coleman Methodology Score for bias risk assessment.

## Results
Thirteen studies were included; seven (167 cases) in CG, four (113 cases) in SG treated with APC, and two (51 cases) with MP. The two groups were comparable in modified Coleman Methodology Score, age, sex, medium follow-up, preoperative visual analog scale, elevation, number of previous surgeries, medium bone loss, and preoperative diagnosis. No significant differences were found in complication and reoperation rates between CG and SG or between APC and MP groups (P = .3, .6, .19, and .36, respectively). Only preoperative infection was associated with higher complications and reoperation rates (P = .04 and P = .015, respectively).

## Conclusion
Using APC or MP during rTSA in case of Proximal Humeral Arthroplasty Revision Osseous inSufficiency (PHAROS) 2 bone loss is neither associated with a lower complication and reoperation rate nor with better postoperative pain or elevation. Patients affected by preoperative infection are at higher risk of developing symptomatic complications and suffer reoperation.

It could have different causes, such as humeral stress shielding, tuberosities resorption, , proximal humerus fracture sequelae, , humeral stem removal, or multiple previous procedures, including the placement of an antibiotic spacer. Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that unaddressed bone loss results in increased bending and torsional forces on the humeral component, potentially leading to early stem loosening. Clinical studies further associate humeral bone defects with prosthesis instability, loss of active elevation and external rotation (ER), humeral stem loosening, and prosthesis breakage or disassembly. , 
The literature has explored the following three different options for addressing PHBL: (1) using a proud humeral stem, with eventually larger glenospheres, higher and/or constraint polyethylene liners, and cement tuberoplasty ( ); (2) employing an allograft prosthesis composite (APC) ( A); and (3) using a tumoral mega-prosthesis (MP) ( B). 
In 2019, Chalmers et al introduced the Proximal Humeral Arthroplasty Revision Osseous inSufficiency (PHAROS) classification system to guide the treatment of PHBL during shoulder revision arthroplasty. This system took in consideration the area of bone loss (greater tuberosity, calcar, humeral diaphysis above or below the deltoid tuberosity), rather than a specific size of the defect. However, even if this classification demonstrated a good reliability, it leaves unsolved the following two important questions: which is the best treatment in case of moderate PHBL (PHAROS 2) ( ) and, which treatment should be preferred between APC and MP. There are no comparative studies between these different options of treatment, so the clinical usefulness of APC and MP in the setting of revision shoulder arthroplasty cannot be definitively confirmed or excluded either. 
This study aimed to evaluate the complication and reoperation rates in patients treated with APC or MP (study group [SG]), compared to a control group (CG) with unaddressed bone loss, and to identify any predictive prognostic factor for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) in case of PHAROS 2 PHBL. Furthermore, a subgroup analysis was run to compare APC and MP for the same outcomes.

## Materials and methods

## Registration
The study protocol was registered online on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (reg. prot. CRD42023429388). This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses guidelines. 

## Search strategy
To identify clinical studies investigating rTSA in cases of PHBL, a combination of the following keywords was used for article search: “proximal humerus bone loss” AND “replacement” OR “arthroplasty” OR “prosthesis” OR “reconstruction”, “revision shoulder replacement”. The search covered articles published before February 2024.

## Study selection
Two independent reviewers (E.T.G. and L.M.) conducted a literature search on three electronic databases: MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Embase. Titles and abstracts of all search results were reviewed, and potentially eligible studies were selected. Results were then cross-checked. In case of disagreement, a third author (A.C.) was involved until consensus was reached. Included articles underwent a full-text review. Finally, the reference lists of included articles and of review studies were also screened for any additional articles that could have not been identified in the database search.

## Eligibility criteria
The inclusion criteria comprised longitudinal studies (retrospective and prospective) evaluating the treatment outcomes of reverse shoulder prosthesis in cases of PHBL, published in peer-reviewed journals in English, with no limitation concerning the publication date. Exclusion criteria included: bone loss greater than 7 cm and inferior to 3 cm, PHAROS types 1 and 3 bone defect, case reports, expert opinions, prior systematic reviews, letters to the editor, cadaveric studies, biomechanical studies, and description of surgical techniques without clinical results. Studies with less than a 24-month minimum follow-up, patients with humeral bone loss secondary to tumor resection, studies including different treatment procedures in which data for CG, APC, or MP alone could not be extrapolated, and studies with missing data regarding the complication and reoperation rates were excluded. Finally, in cases of multiple studies from the same author, the study with the lowest follow-up was excluded to avoid any risk of patient overlapping.

## Study assessment and data extraction
Two reviewers (R.d.G. and P.M.) independently extracted relevant data from included articles and recorded them onto Microsoft Excel spreadsheets (version 2019, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). The two excel files were then matched, with the senior author (A.C.) arbitrating in case of disagreement. Data extracted included the following: study design, level of evidence, demographic data, preoperative diagnosis, amount of humeral bone loss, type and number of previous surgery, any eventual additional surgical procedure associated, preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes, humeral stem and the graft fixation technique, complication and reoperation rates, and types. Regarding complications, we focused on the following: infection, instability, glenoid and humeral loosening, periprosthetic intraoperative or postoperative fractures, prosthesis breakage or disassembly, cement extravasation, persistent pain, nerve palsy, hardware, and, in case of APC, graft-related complications (resorption and nonunion).

## Quality assessment
To evaluate the quality of studies included, we utilized the modified Coleman Methodology Score (mCMS), which evaluates the risk of bias within studies based on 10 factors and yields a total score between 0 and 100. A score of 100 implies that the study avoids chance, biases, and confounding factors to a great extent. Each study was individually and independently assessed by two reviewers (R.d.G. and P.M.) for each of the criteria used to calculate the mCMS. Each author carried through this process twice. The senior author (A.C.) arbitrated any disagreement between the two reviewers.

## Outcome measures
The main outcome assessed in this study was the rate of symptomatic complication and reoperation. Symptomatic complications included humeral and glenoid loosening, prosthesis instability, infection, postoperative periprosthetic fractures and acromion fractures, nerve palsy, prosthesis breakage or dissociation, and stiffness or chronic pain. On the contrary, asymptomatic events included scapular notching, APC graft resorption or nonunion, intraoperative humerus fracture, or heterotopic ossification not associated with worse clinical outcome. Secondary outcomes included the most frequent types of specific complications, ie, infection and instability, postoperative shoulder elevation, and visual analog scale (VAS).

## Statistical analysis
Data were reported as mean ± standard deviation or as numbers (percentages). Distribution of variables was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups (SG vs. CG) and subgroups, were performed using Student t-test (continuous normally distributed variables), Mann–Whitney test (continuous non-normally distributed variables), and chi-squared test (categorical variables). Forward stepwise multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine whether age, sex, follow-up, bone loss, type of preoperative diagnosis, number of previous surgeries preoperative VAS, and active elevation were significantly associated with the symptomatic complication rate and the reoperation rate.
A proportional meta-analysis was run to pool data regarding the symptomatic complication rate, the instability and infection rate, and the reoperation rate. The ‘metaprop’ command was used to compute 95% confidence intervals using the score statistic and the exact binomial method and incorporate the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation of proportions. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed through the Higgins’ I2 statistic and a random-effects model was applied in all cases. A metaregression (metareg module) was used to compare pooled proportions between the two groups.
Multiple subgroup analyses were conducted as follows: the symptomatic complication, reoperation, instability, and infection rates of APC group were compared to MP group. Moreover, the same outcomes were evaluated in APC vs. CG and MP vs. CG. The level of significance was set at P .05. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

## Results

## Research results

Out of 13,816 studies identified through a computer search and manual screening of reference lists across databases, 13 papers were included in the final analysis. These comprised 7 series of patients with untreated PHBL (CG), , , , , , , 4 with APC, , , , and 2 with MP. , 

## Study characteristics
Overall, the CG included 167 cases, while the SG consisted of 164 cases, further divided into 113 cases of APC subgroup and 51 cases of MP subgroup. Demographics, preoperative VAS, elevation, bone loss, and diagnosis of the two groups and subgroups, and comparisons have been reported in . At baseline, both groups and subgroups were comparable in mCMS (P = .7 and .18), level of evidence, mean age at surgery (P = .54 and .2), sex (P = .36 and .31), medium follow-up (P = .58 and .4), preoperative VAS (P = .39 and .48) and elevation (P = .19 and .38), number of previous surgery (P = .91 and .62), amount of medium PHBL (P = .55 and .71), and preoperative diagnosis ( ). 
Clinical outcomes were reported heterogeneously among studies, with various scoring system used. The American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, Constant score, Simple Shoulder Test, Subjective Shoulder Value, and Disability of Arm, Shoulder and Hand score were employed in 9, 6, 7, 1, and 2 studies, respectively.
Because of missing data, no statistical analysis of these patient-reported outcomes measures was performed. Nevertheless, preoperative and postoperative data on VAS and active elevation were consistently reported.

## Surgical techniques
Some technical differences could be observed within each group in this study. While in the CG, the humeral stem was fixed both with and without cement (89% and 11%, respectively), all patient of the SG were treated with a cemented stem. A medialized stem design with 155° shaft–neck angle was used in 67% of the cases of the CG against a 4% of the SG. In the APC group, different methods for graft fixation were used such as cement and cable fixation alone in 6%, associated with a step cut in 90% or with plate and screws in 4%.

## Complication and reoperation rate
No significant difference was observed in symptomatic complication and reoperation rates (P = .45 and .88, respectively) between the CG and the SG. Pooled proportions also showed no significant difference (P = .3 for complications and .6 for reoperations) ( , ). The symptomatic complication rate and reoperation rate in SG and CG were 32.5% and 25.5% and 19.3% and 20.6%, respectively. Similarly, no specific type of complication was associated with the type of treatment ( ). Multivariate regression analysis identified infection as a preoperative diagnosis, as the only factor associated with higher rates of symptomatic complication (P = .4) and reoperation (P = .15). 

## Clinical outcomes
No difference between postoperative active elevation and VAS was observed between the SG and the CG (P = .6 and P = .23, respectively). Mean active ER improved from 4.5°-16.4° after surgery. The ER improvement was more consistent in the SG (mean 19.4°) than in the CG (mean 12.8°). The mean American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score raised from 25 points preoperatively to 59.4 points postoperatively, without any noticeable difference between the SG and the CG (59 and 61 points, respectively). However, since these data were not reported in every series of patients, no statistical analysis could be performed.

## Subgroup analyses
Symptomatic complication and reoperation rates did not differ when comparing APC with MP ( ) or even the CG with APC. However, MP showed a higher rate of prosthesis instability (P = .028) and a not statistically significant higher rate of reoperation (P = .15) compared to the CG. Meta-regression confirmed this finding (P = .3 for both) ( ). 

## Discussion
The primary finding of the study challenges the justification for addressing the bone defect in case of rTSA associated with PHAROS 2 PHBL, expecting less complications or a lower rate of reoperations. Indeed, the lack of significant difference in VAS, forward elevation, symptomatic complication rate, and reoperation rate between addressing and not addressing a 3–7 cm PHBL, at a mean follow-up of four years, questions the necessity of these interventions.
Historically, moderate PHBL in revision arthroplasty has been managed using a proud cemented long stem, whereas APC and MP were reserved for cases of massive humeral bone loss due to malignancy. , In 2007, Levy et al started to treat even moderate PHBL in the setting of revision shoulder arthroplasty with APC ; and recently, new design of proximal humerus mega prosthesis have been developed to improve deltoid wrapping and prosthetic stability, also in case of 5 cm of bone loss. 
The theoretical advantages of using APC include restoration of humeral bone stock and length, increased deltoid wrapping, and support for tendon repair or transfer to restore the muscle balance and at the same time to improve active ER. Similarly, the use of MP offers advantages such as an easier reconstruction of the proximal humerus, especially in case of large humeral bone loss, cementless implantation for versatile handling of humeral length, and increased deltoid wrapping trough the large metaphyseal component. However, on the other side, graft-related complications, such as graft resorption, fracture or nonunion; theoretical higher infection rates, system availability, and higher costs and operative times are considerations that should be taken into account when choosing between these solutions.
In the recent literature, a cutoff value of 5 cm of PHBL has been proposed as the limit to consider the use of an APC or MP. , However, this value was based on surgeons’ personal experiences, and no comparative analysis has been conducted to confirm this postulate. In 2019, the PHAROS classification system overtook the previous centimetric classification, by erasing the patient size effect and the problem linked to the indirect radiographic measure of bone loss. However, while it provides a reliable classification system for PHBL, it does not offer a clinical evaluation of outcomes associated with specific grades or types of treatment. The study results support previous findings about the maximum amount of bone loss that can be tolerated without addressing the bone loss. Specifically, no advantage of using APC or MP in cases of 5 cm of PHBL was identified. The only possible reason to consider the use of APC in case of moderate bone loss could be a complete loss of ER, with a complete positive lag sign in ER and positive Hornblower’s sign. Boileau et al, in 2020, suggested that in this situation, an associated L’Episcopo transfer attached to the allograft could restore active ER and improve forward flexion. However, this data could not be confirmed in the current review due to variations in reporting and lack of specific evaluation of associated tendon transfers in case of APC.
Looking specifically at the different types of complications, patients in which the bone loss was treated with APC or MP showed a lower rate of humeral loosening and a higher rate of infection, although these differences were not statistically significant (P .39 and .48, respectively). The only significant difference observed was a higher rate of instability in case of MP compared to the CG treatment (P = .28). Two possible reasons could explain this result: first, the MP group has a significantly higher bone loss mean respect to the CG (P = .48). Second, the MP do not allow the use of the allograft tendon stump for rotator cuff repair or tendon transfer as for the APC. In addition, although not statistically significant, a tendency toward a higher reoperation rate was observed in the MP group (P = .15).
The definition of symptomatic and asymptomatic complications following APC procedures was borrowed from Levy et al as compelling to the objective of the study, which identified the preoperative diagnosis infection as the only factor associated with a higher risk of developing symptomatic complications or requiring reoperation. This suggests that factors such as the amount of bone loss, the preoperative range of movement, and the type of treatment are not the primary concerns when treating shoulder arthroplasty associated with PHBL. The impact of infection is indirectly associated with a greater number of previous surgical procedures, more scar tissue, and muscle imbalance, which could affect outcomes. On the contrary, the low impact of the amount of PHBL is quite surprising, even if the same result has already been reported by previous authors, which did not observe any differences even comparing PHAROS bone loss type 2 with 3.
Several limitations are acknowledged in the study. First, it is important to underline that our results could be valid only in case of PHAROS 2 bone loss. Second, the heterogenicity among included studies in terms of the evaluation of clinical outcomes and the surgical techniques, limited the ability to draw final conclusions about patient satisfaction, functionality, and the evaluation of different types of prosthesis, graft fixation techniques, and tendon transfers.
Moreover, we were only able to pool nonrandomized studies, which reduces the strength of our results. Of the 12 included studies, no one was prospective and comparative, and the mean sample size in both treatments groups is around 25 patients, which reflects the lack of high-quality studies dealing with PHBL during shoulder arthroplasty.

## Conclusion
Patients affected by preoperative infection should be informed about their higher risk of developing symptomatic complications and reoperation before reimplantation. The use of APC or MP during rTSA in case of PHAROS 2 bone loss is neither associated with a lower complication and reoperation rate nor with better postoperative pain or elevation.
