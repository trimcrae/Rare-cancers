Among these, the envelope proteins include at least 15 proteins, 11 of which are glycosylated. gB, gD and gE are the key glycosylated proteins, which play an important role in the process of receptor binding, antibody induction, and determining the pathogenicity to the host [ ]. In China, gE-deleted vaccine strains are predominantly used for immunization. Consequently, the gE protein serves as a key marker for distinguishing between vaccinated animals and those infected with wild-type PRV, making it a valuable tool for detecting PRV infections in swine populations [ ]. In addition, gC gene is highly variable in PRV genome, making it a commonly used marker for genotyping [ ]. PRV is usually classified into two genotypes based on gC protein, genotype I and genotype II. Genotype II comprises strains originating from China, including classical strains and variants strains, while genotype I mainly includes strains from regions such as Europe and the United States [ – ].
The first case of pseudorabies in China was reported in a cat in 1947 [ ]. Subsequently, PRV was detected in various animals [ ]. PRV had become widespread in numerous regions of China in the early 1980s and characterized by itching, vomiting, diarrhea, and nervous disorders, which led to serious economic losses to the pig industry worldwide [ , ]. It was not until the Bartha-K61 vaccine was purchased from Hungary that the disease was effectively controlled [ ].
With the widespread emergence of PRV variant strains in China, the efficacy of traditional vaccine has significantly declined. Previous studies have compared the gE protein sequences of early PRV isolates (including the SC strain) with the PRV TJ variant. These analyses revealed two key modifications: a 1-amino acid insertion (D) at position 494, and a single amino acid substitution (G→D) at position 54. These structural changes might explain why the Bartha-K61 vaccine failed to provide complete protection against the newly emerged PRV strains [ ]. As a result, the prevalence of PRV in pigs has risen markedly compared to the levels observed before 2011 [ – ]. The latency infection established in the nervous system was a main character of PRV infection [ ]. Pigs infected with PRV can become lifelong carriers of the virus [ , ]. These carrier pigs will not show any symptoms until the virus is reactivated due to external stimuli or re-infection [ , ]. Furthermore, current scientific evidence demonstrates that PRV can evade host immunity by targeting key immune pathways: interfering TLR, RLR, and cGAS-STING signaling, degrading IRF3, inhibiting TNF-α-mediated NF-κB activation, blocking JAK-STAT, and suppressing ISGs. It also manipulates apoptosis, autophagy, and ER stress to promote viral survival [ ]. These bring great difficulties in identifying infected pigs in time and pose significant challenges in controlling the spread of the virus. Given these challenges, it is important to understand the prevalence trend and spatial distribution of PRV for preventing and controlling PRV infection.
We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the prevalence of PRV in China after 2010, and evaluated the risk factors associated with PRV infection, including geographic region, province, detection method, sampling time (year/season), sampling strategy, and pig demographics (age/gender). Our findings aim to deepen the understanding of PRV prevalence in pigs and facilitate the development of control measures.

## Materials and methods

## Search strategy
We totally searched the relative studies from five literature databases, including PubMed, Web of Science (WOS), ScienceDirect, the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and the Wanfang Database. The following key words or terms were used to retrieve related papers in PubMed: ((Herpesvirus type I) OR (Herpes virus) OR (Pseudorabies) OR (Pseudorabies virus) OR (Infectious bulbar paralysis virus) OR (Itch virus) OR (Mad itch) OR (Aujeszky’s disease) OR (Aujeszky disease) OR (Aujeszkys disease) OR (PRV) OR (PR)) AND ((Seroprevalence) OR (Prevalence) OR (Epidemiological survey) OR (Epidemiologic methods) OR (Surveillance) OR (Morbidity) OR (Diagnose)) AND ((Porcine) OR (Swine) OR (Pig) OR (Hog)) AND (China), and the years are limited to 2010–2025. The same terms were used to search in WOS. The terms of “Pseudorabies” and ”Prevalence” and ”Pig” and ”China” were used to searched in ScienceDirect in advanced. We used the following keywords, “Pseudorabies”, “pigs”, “prevalence”, and “China”, as well as their synonyms, to search in two Chinese literature databases. All those articles were yielded from inception to 23 February 2025.

## Data extraction
The included studies were managed using Microsoft® Excel®2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and EndNote version 21 (1500 Spring Garden Street, Fourth Floor. Philadelphia, PA 19,130, USA). Data from the included studies were extracted and summarized independently by two of the authors. Any disagreement was resolved by the adjudicating senior authors. The following information was recorded from the included studies: first author, year of publication, total sample size, number of positive samples, geographical region, province, year of sampling, testing method, the age of pigs. Additionally, incidence rates were extracted and calculated. However, we did not obtain raw data or unpublished information from the authors of the included studies.

## Selection criteria
Two researchers independently conducted the review process, resolving any disagreement by introducing a third author’s opinion. The selection criteria of the literature included the following five points: (1) The research subjects were limited to porcine species within China. (2) The literature was published later than 2010. (3) Provided comprehensive epidemiological raw data (total number of samples and the number of positive samples). (4) The sample size was greater than 30. (5) Natural infections were included in the study. We removed the studies that didn’t meet the criteria above.

## Quality assessment
We selected the STROBE-Vet guidelines to assess the quality of the included literature. STROBE-Vet is an extension of the STROBE statement specifically developed for observational studies in veterinary medicine. It emphasizes standardized quality assessment, ensures methodological rigor, and accounts for the unique characteristics of veterinary observational data as well as the distinct sources of bias inherent in veterinary research [ , ]. These features make it particularly suitable for conducting meta-analyses in the veterinary field. Two authors independently assessed studies. Any disagreement was resolved by the third reviewer. As all included studies were cross-sectional, we selected core indicators from the guideline to adapt to our research needs, including clarity of the title and abstract (1(a)(b)), justification of background and rationale (2), explicit research objectives (3(a)), appropriateness of study design (4), transparent reporting of timing and location (5(a)), accurate presentation of findings (14(a)), concise summary of main results (15, 16(a), 18), discussion of limitations (19), interpretation of results (20), and assessment of generalizability (21), to ensure the accuracy and applicability of quality assessment [ ]. Based on these criteria, we classified the included studies into three quality tiers: high quality (all key criteria rated as “fully met”), moderate quality (≤ 2 criteria “partially met” and ≤ 1 criterion “not met”), and low quality (> 2 criteria “partially met” or > 1 criterion “not met”). We excluded the low-quality studies to enhance the robustness of our findings.

## Statistics analysis
Before conducting the meta-analysis, we performed normality tests. These tests were applied to both the original rates and the converted rates. We used four different conversion methods for comparison. The methods included logarithmic conversion (PLN), logit conversion (PLOGIT), arcsine transformation (PAS), and Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation (PFT). The goal was to select the model that best approximated a normal distribution [ ]. Based on the test results, Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was chosen to convert the rate. Subsequently, heterogeneity was anticipated and evaluated via I2 statistics and Cochran’s Q test. A statistically significant level of heterogeneity was defined by a p-value < 0.05 and an I2 value exceeding 50%. Given high heterogeneity observed among the included studies, a random effects model was employed for meta-analysis to generate forest maps. The analysis was conducted using R 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2024) and the ‘meta’ package was utilized to calculate the pooled rate and 95% confidence interval with the following code: ‘metaprop (Events, Total, data = mydata, studylab $ Study, sm = ‘PFT’, Commom = F)’ [ , ]. In addition, we used funnel plots to qualitatively observe whether publication bias existed. Egger’s method and Begg’s method were also used to quantitatively test whether there was publication bias. The results of Egger’s and Begg’s tests showed that there was no significant publication bias. Furthermore, we performed sensitivity analysis to test the stability of the results by excluding individual studies one at a time.
After that, we performed a subgroup analysis and meta-regression analysis to further investigate the source of heterogeneity. The subgroup analysis was based on sampling year (2016 and before, vs. 2017 or later), detection method (comparing ELISA with other methods), sampling areas (comparing Eastern China and other regions), sampling strategies (comparing random sampling and non-random sampling or unreported sampling methods), the gender of animals (comparing sows and boars), seasons (comparing spring and other seasons), and the age of animals (comparing suckling piglets and pigs of other ages) and then we performed a forest plot to visualize the results. The meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ].

## Results

## Search results and eligible studies
A total of 2434 articles were yielding from the database search. Following the removal of 148 duplicate records, we conducted preliminary eligibility assessments that excluded an additional 2162 studies based on title and abstract. Subsequently, from the full-text evaluation of the remaining 124 studies, 66 were excluded for various reasons. Mainly because the themes deviated and the absence of complete epidemiological data and information. Finally, only 58 articles were included for data extraction and analysis. 15 of the 58 articles reported two prevalence estimates (using two different detection methods to detecting different samples), and each estimate was considered as an independent study (Fig.  ). As a result, our meta-analysis incorporated a total of 73 studies. Based on the quality assessment, there were 30 high-quality articles, 40 medium-quality articles and 3 low-quality articles. Low-quality studies were excluded. Ultimately, we included 70 studies in the meta-analysis. Relevant information was extracted from the literature which met the criteria (Additional file ).

 

## Heterogeneity and publication bias tests
Due to the high heterogeneity of the literature (I2 = 99.9%, P = 0), a random effects model was used for meta-analysis (Fig.  ). We then assessed whether there is a publication bias using visual funnel plot symmetry, and complementary Egger’s and Begg’s methods. The result demonstrated funnel plot had a good symmetry, meanwhile, and the p-values of Egger’s and Begg’s methods were 0.9308 and 0.2105, respectively, which indicated that there was no significant publication bias in our meta-analysis (Fig.  ). Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding individual study. The results remained stable throughout this process, as the exclusion of any single study did not substantially alter the overall outcomes, suggesting the reliability of our meta-analysis (Fig.  ). According to univariate meta-regression results, the main source of heterogeneity in our study might be detection methods (P < 0.05) (Table  ).

 

 

 

## Pooled prevalence of pseudorabies in swine in China
The meta-analysis, encompassing 70 studies with a total of 693,371 samples, revealed that a national pooled prevalence of PRV in pigs in China was 21.5% (95% CI 17.3–26.0) since 2010 (Fig.  ). The prevalence of PRV from 2010 to 2016 was 26.1% (95% CI 19.7–33.0), while the prevalence of PRV after 2017 declined to 18.2% (95% CI 13.5–23.4). The PRV prevalence rate in randomly sampled specimens [19.6% (95% CI 9.5–32.4)] was slightly lower than that in specimens with non-random sampling or unreported sampling methods [95% CI 21.8% (17.2–26.7)] (Table  ).

 

## Pooled prevalence of porcine pseudorabies in administrative districts or provinces of China
To evaluate the geographical distribution of PRV prevalence, samples were detected from Central China, Eastern China, Northern China, Southern China, Northeast China, Northwest China, and Southwest China. Central China contributed the largest number of samples, followed by Eastern China also had more literature support. We found that Northern China had the highest infection rate at 27.6% (95% CI 16.3–40.5), followed by Eastern China at 27.5% (95% CI 20.4–35.2). In contrast, Southwest China showed the lowest prevalence rate of 9.7% (95% CI 4.8–16.2) (Table  ). Furthermore, given that the two largest northern-region studies contributed > 50% of the region’s sample size (potentially introducing disproportionate weight in meta-analysis), we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding these studies to assess their influence on the overall findings. The results showed that even after excluding the two large-sample studies, the PRV prevalence in northern China remained higher at 27.6% (95% CI 15.2–42.2) compared to other regions, demonstrating the robustness of our findings.
At the provincial level, the prevalence of PRV in Inner Mongolia was higher than that in other provinces by 47.4% (95% CI 17.5–78.4), followed by Beijing 47.2% (95% CI 24.6–70.3) and Zhejiang 44.8% (95% CI 30.6–59.5). Ningxia showed the lowest incidence rate at 2.0% (95% CI 0.9–3.9). Notably, the majority of provinces exhibited sample prevalence below 30% (Table  ).

 

## Prevalence of porcine pseudorabies in different detection methods
Forty-three of the 70 studies performed serological analysis using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), with a PRV detection rate of 29.1% (95% CI 24.5–34.0). The remaining 27 studies using molecular biological assays, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), fluorescent quantitative PCR (qPCR), and real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), yielded a significantly lower detection rate of 11.1% (95% CI 6.2–17.3) (P < 0.05) (Table  ).

## Prevalence of pseudorabies in various ages of swine
In our systematic review and meta-analysis, the prevalence of PRV in growing-finishing pigs was 25.0% (95% CI 17.5–33.3), while reaching 24.6% (95% CI 16.4–33.9) in suckling piglets and 24.8% (95% CI 18.2–32.1) in nursery pigs, revealing no significant differences in detection rates between age groups (P > 0.05) (Table  ). However, it had to be noted that the sample size of suckling piglets was much smaller than those for nursery pigs and growing-finishing pigs, which might affect the results of the study, and more studies were needed to confirm these conclusions.

## Prevalence of pseudorabies in different genders of swine
We extracted PRV epidemiological data from 21 studies on boars, with a PRV prevalence of 26.2% (95% CI 19.9–32.9). Data from 23 studies on sows showed a higher PRV prevalence of 34.9% (95% CI 27.9–42.1) (P = 0.0827) (Table  ).

## Prevalence of pseudorabies in different seasons
Subgroup analysis based on the specimens of different seasons revealed four PRV prevalence rates: 29.5% (95% CI 22.0–37.7) in spring, 29.0% (95% CI 23.8–34.5) in summer, 24.3% (95% CI 16.8–32.6) in autumn, and 31.0% (95% CI 21.7–41.2) in winter. Meta-regression analysis revealed no statistically significant seasonal variation (P = 0.3565), yet the limited number of available studies might reduce the statistical power (Table  ).

## Discussion
Pseudorabies, also called Aujeszky’s disease (AD), causes neurological and respiratory diseases in animals [ ]. PRV can be transmitted not only via close contact, but also through air, water and other polluted fomites [ ]. Moreover, Previous studies reported that PRV can inhibit apoptosis, necrosis, autophagy, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, contributing to evade immune responses [ ]. Furthermore, Hui-Hua Zheng et al. found that PRV can invade the host’s peripheral nervous system and establish lifelong latent infection within sensory ganglia [ ]. These biological characteristics have rendered PRV eradication exceptionally challenging and the global swine industry continues to endure substantial economic losses [ ].
While complete eradication of PRV remains challenging in swine industry, there persists a critical need for implementing PRV eradication programs on pig farms to minimize economic losses. Therefore, it has great necessity of carrying out a nationwide epidemiological survey. Prior to our investigation, Wenyao Lin et al. conducted a seroepidemiological study across 23 Chinese provinces between 2011 and 2015, revealing a PRV seroprevalence rate of 31.6% [ ]. In addition, Lei Tan et al. summarized the literature on PRV serological detection, reporting a pooled PRV seropositivity rate of 29.87% in China [ ]. Compared with them, we applied meta-analysis to investigate the epidemic status of PRV from 2010 to 2024, which included as many as 693,371 samples and covered nearly all provinces in China. The analysis revealed a pooled prevalent rate of 21.5% (95% CI 17.3–26.0). The observed discrepancies in PRV infection rate across studies may stem from methodological differences, including variations in sampling strategies, diagnostic assays, temporal-spatial distribution of samples, and sample size disparities. During the period, Y Liu et al. reported a 46.0% prevalence of Porcine Circovirus Type 2 (2015–2019), while Y Chen et al. found a 34.1% prevalence of Hepatitis E Virus in swine (2010–2019) [ , ]. Both rates were higher than the 21.5% prevalence of PRV. However, the newly emerged PRV variants—which exhibited vaccine escape mutations—posed a more significant biosecurity threat due to their potential for immune evasion [ – ].
The meta-analysis revealed high heterogeneity (I²=99.9%), which might originate from multiple factors. Regarding sampling methodology, most included studies did not mention the use of random sampling designs, potentially compromising sample representativeness. Although meta-regression suggested sampling method might not be a major source of heterogeneity (P > 0.05), this conclusion was limited since most studies failed to explicitly describe their sampling approach. More studies with standardized reporting of sampling methods were needed for verification. In terms of detection methods, the prolonged antibody persistence characteristic of ELISA assays might lead to seroprevalence overestimation, while molecular testing variations using clinical specimens or tissues from deceased pigs might inflate detection rates due to selection bias. Meta-regression confirmed detection method as the primary heterogeneity source (P < 0.05), yet substantial heterogeneity persisted even after excluding low-quality studies and categorizing data by detection methods. High heterogeneity remains a common and important challenge in burden of disease research (such as prevalence meta-analyses), potentially affecting pooled prevalence estimate stability and result interpretation reliability. However, some authorities questioned the applicability of heterogeneity indices like I² in observational study meta-analyses, particularly given their characteristic large sample sizes and numerous included studies [ , ]. The core objective of the disease burden studies was to derive optimal prevalence estimates through systematic data integration [ ]. Therefore, on the basis of full consideration of methodological limitations, we maintained that the reported pooled prevalence estimates could still serve as valid reference indicators for assessing PRV prevalence status.
Lei Tan et al.‘s study showed that there were significant differences in PRV prevalence among pigs in different regions of China [ ]. Our results showed that the highest prevalence rates of PRV were consistently observed in Northern China, Eastern China and Northeast China. This might be attributed to several key factors related to different farming practices and environmental conditions. Large-scale and intensive farming often has more advantages in disease prevention and control compared to traditional free-range and small-scale farming [ ]. Environmental factors also play a crucial role in viral transmission dynamics, as the PRV exhibits reduced viability under conditions of high temperature and low humidity [ ]. Furthermore, the duration and intensity of ultraviolet radiation also affect viral survival [ ]. The high prevalence of PRV in pigs in northern and northeastern China may be partially explained by the region’s climatic conditions. In order to maintain the appropriate temperature, swine housing facilities are often maintained in enclosed conditions with air circulation reduction, which creates favorable conditions for viral transmission.
At the provincial level, the PRV prevalence of seventeen provinces was higher than 20% while only three provinces had a PRV prevalence lower than 10%. It demonstrated that the popularity of PRV was quite widespread between provinces in China. Long-distance transportation, introduction of breeding pigs, contamination of semen products and movement of people might be the main factors for the widespread and interprovincial transmission of PRV [ , ].
In subgroup analyses by detection method, serological assays demonstrated higher detection rates compared to molecular diagnostic techniques. The observed divergence in detection rates between ELISA and PCR-based methods arose from fundamental differences in their biological targets, sensitivity, and temporal dynamics. Serological assays (primarily ELISA) detect antibodies, which emerge after infection and persist for extended periods, leading to high cumulative prevalence that may reflect past exposure rather than active infection. While ELISA demonstrates high specificity for antibody detection, its sensitivity is limited during early infection due to delayed seroconversion [ , ]. In contrast, molecular methods (PCR, RT-PCR, qPCR) directly amplify viral nucleic acids, providing superior sensitivity for early-stage infections and enabling detection even at low viral loads. However, PCR’s window of detection is relatively narrow, as it depends on the presence of viable virus or residual nucleic acids [ ]. This trade-off explains why ELISA often yields higher detection rates in convalescent or vaccinated animals (as in the context of this study), whereas PCR is more reliable for diagnosing acute infections. For comprehensive surveillance, these methods serve complementary roles: ELISA is optimal for assessing population immunity and historical exposure, while PCR is critical for early outbreak detection, active case confirmation, and molecular epidemiology. A combined approach—leveraging ELISA for broad screening and PCR for targeted verification—enhances diagnostic accuracy and supports more informed public health decisions.
Our meta-regression analysis found no significant link between age and PRV prevalence heterogeneity (P > 0.05). However, this result should be interpreted cautiously due to potential sample size limitations in the included studies. The results of subgroup analyses showed that PRV prevalence rates were similar among piglets, weaned piglets, and fattening pigs. This pattern might be due to the virus’s lifelong latent infection—once infected, pigs carry the virus indefinitely, regardless of age [ ]. Moreover, clinical presentation of PRV infection varies by age group: neonatal piglets often face higher mortality, while adult pigs typically exhibit respiratory symptoms [ ]. Future studies with age-stratified data are needed to clarify age-related epidemiological dynamics.
In this study, differences in PRV prevalence by sex and season were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This might be due to small sample sizes and the limited number of available relevant studies too. In the subgroup analyses by genders, sows showed higher PRV prevalence than boars. This difference could be linked to pregnancy, which might weaken sows’ immune systems and increase their susceptibility to viral infection [ ]. Seasonal trends suggested a potential peak in PRV prevalence during winter. One possible explanation was reduced barn ventilation caused by cold-weather insulation measures, which could facilitate viral transmission.
The primary strategies for PRV prevention and control are vaccination and biosecurity measures. The post-2017 reduction in PRV prevalence (from 26.1 to 18.2%) reflected the success of China’s targeted interventions, most likely driven by novel vaccines developed for prevalent variant strains. Notably, C Wang et al. found that the rPRVTJ-delgE vaccine, developed based on the currently dominant PRV TJ strain, demonstrated outstanding immunoprotective efficacy. The vaccine provided complete protection within just one week of immunization—a marked improvement over the partial protection offered by the traditional Bartha-K61 vaccine [ ]. Moreover, in the challenge trial with PRV variant HN1201 conducted by S. Yan et al., while Bartha-K61-vaccinated pigs exhibited transient fever and unvaccinated controls died within 7 days, rHN1201TK−/gE−/gI−/11k−/28k− vaccinated pigs showed no clinical signs or mortality, demonstrating superior protective efficacy against PRV [ ]. These breakthroughs underscored the critical importance of genomic matching between vaccine strains and circulating strains. While China has made remarkable progress in PRV control, its prevalence remained relatively high compared to developed countries in Europe and North America [Germany 12.09% (2010–2015), America 18% (2009–2012), Switzerland 0.57% (2008–2013)] [ ]. Germany’s success in maintaining low prevalence could be traced to its systematic eradication program initiated in the last century, which integrated not only vaccination but also rigorous breeding stock purification, reproduction management, region-specific differentiated control strategies, and a comprehensive precision monitoring system. These practical experiences conclusively demonstrate that effective PRV control requires both genomically matched vaccines and systematic biosecurity measures implemented in tandem [ , ].
However, there was still some limitations in our analysis. First, although statistical tests showed no significant publication bias (Egger’s p = 0.9308; Begg’s p = 0.2105), our comprehensive search of five major Chinese and English databases revealed no PRV prevalence studies for Qinghai, Hong Kong, Macao, or Taiwan, leaving data gaps for these regions. Second, most included studies did not clearly report random sampling methods, which might affect the representativeness of findings. Future studies should improve design for more reliable results. Third, to ensure methodological rigor, we excluded unpublished data, review articles, validation studies, case reports, and low-quality literature. While this maintained quality, it may have omitted useful information and introduced potential bias. Finally, high model heterogeneity suggested unaccounted factors, requiring further validation in future research.

## Conclusions
We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the prevalence of PRV in China from 2010 to 2024. Our findings revealed a decreasing trend in PRV prevalence, yet the virus remained endemic at various degrees across regions in China. The higher prevalence of PRV in Northern China highlighted a discrepancy of regional distribution. At the provincial level, Inner Mongolia has the highest prevalence of PRV. Moreover, pigs across all age groups showed susceptibility to PRV infection. In conclusion, PRV prevention and control should focus on two key aspects: firstly, select appropriate vaccine. Secondly, implement strict biosecurity measures, including rigorous breeding stock purification, reproduction management, region-specific differentiated control strategies, and a comprehensive molecular surveillance network.
