## Abstract

## Background
Amphetamine-type stimulant use disorder (ATSUD) contributes to the global burden of disease, notably due to its social, physical, and psychological consequences. Psychiatric disorders are frequently observed among people with ATSUD, while we still do not know their exact global prevalence because of multiple sources of heterogeneity. Here, we propose a protocol for a systematic review and a series of meta-analyses to describe the global prevalence of psychiatric disorders observed in individuals living with ATSUD.

## Methods
A pilot systematic search was conducted to develop a protocol for a systematic review and a series of meta-analyses. A final systematic search will be conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, and CINAHL to retrieve, among articles indexed since 1999, prevalence estimates of psychiatric disorders within individuals living with ATSUD. The final systematic review will support multiple separate meta-analyses, each investigating one or more concomitant psychiatric disorders. Diagnosis proportions of psychiatric disorders will be obtained using DSM-IV, DSM-5, ICD-10, and ICD-11 criteria or validated psychometric methods. Risk of bias will be assessed using the prevalence studies checklist from the Joanna Briggs Institute’s critical appraisal tool. Proportions of diagnoses will be pooled using random-effect meta-analysis for each psychiatric disorder. Heterogeneity linked to methodological, sociodemographic, and spatiotemporal characteristics will be assessed using subgroup analyses and meta-regressions.

## Discussion
The pilot search allowed the identification of 10 psychiatric disorders associated with ATSUD and refinement of the final search and meta-analysis protocol. This project will provide one of the first global and comprehensive syntheses of psychiatric disorders’ prevalence in people with ATSUD to date, a set of evidence that will contribute to a more adapted and equitable public health response to this epidemic.

## Background
Amphetamine-type stimulant (ATS) use is highly prevalent worldwide. For instance, reports from the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime highlighted that globally, 30 million adults reported having used ATS in 2022, the most commonly consumed group of substances in East Asia, South-East Asia, and Oceania [ ]. Available and distributed through multiple legal and illegal supply chains [ – ], ATS substances like amphetamines, methamphetamines, methcathinones, and prescription stimulants can lead to the development of ATS use disorder (ATSUD), defined as chronic patterns of ATS use that cause clinically significant impairment or distress [ – ]. ATS use was reported to be the primary drug-related cause of death and reason for seeking drug treatment in 9% and 14% of countries, respectively, during 2022 [ ]. Between 1990 and 2016, collaborators from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2016 project observed an increase in crude prevalence and a decrease in age-standardized prevalence for ATSUD [ ]. However, based on the latest GBD results (2021), the ATSUD mortality rate increased by 30% from 1999 to 2021 [ ], a trend that could reflect the increased potency and heterogeneous purity of new synthetic ATS recently found in unregulated markets [ , ].
Substance use disorders, including ATSUD, have been observed to co-occur frequently with other sets of psychiatric disorders and mental health behaviors [ , ]. Concurrent presentation of ATSUD and psychiatric disorders is a challenge for practitioners and their care recipients. For instance, diagnosed and undiagnosed psychiatric disorder symptoms can interact with ATS use, motivate ATS use, worsen symptoms, and result in lower quality of life [ , ]. Moreover, it tends to complicate clinical evaluation and management of concomitant disorders, which can result in poorer adherence to treatment and prognoses [ , ]. Altogether, the seemingly increased complexity and morbidity of concurrent psychiatric disorders in ATSUD is believed to add a burden on concerned users, public health systems, and communities worldwide [ – ]. This burden warrants more comprehensive and harmonized evidence-based research about the global epidemiology of psychiatric disorders in people with ATSUD. Such research could inform public health authorities and healthcare providers in terms of needs, trends, and potential divergences linked to the identification and management of these concomitant disorders.
Important sources of heterogeneity contribute to the observed prevalence estimates of psychiatric disorders in people living with ATSUD. Some subgroups, like women, seem more afflicted by certain types of psychiatric mental health disorders and present on average more current and lifetime psychiatric comorbidities [ – , ]. Additionally, observed heterogeneity in prevalence estimates could be explained by methods used to identify ATSUD’s comorbidities, particularly revisions of the classification systems and heterogeneous psychometric methods, although comprehensive evidence syntheses to support these claims are scarce and disorder-specific [ ]. Since 1994, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) classification system underwent four revisions, where major changes were applied to the criteria within and across psychiatric disorder categories [ – ]. For example, in the DSM-5, post-traumatic stress disorders (PTSD) moved from the anxiety disorders category toward a new category, trauma- and stressor-related disorders, and the minimal required age for symptom manifestation of attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) was raised from seven to twelve years old [ – ]. Similarly, the threshold to be diagnosed with ATSUD was reduced to a minimum of 2 observed symptoms within criterion A, and the concepts of dependence and abuse were merged into the concept of substance use disorder [ , ]. These few examples may impact the observed prevalence of these disorders. Therefore, accurately estimating the distribution of ATSUD psychiatric comorbidities as well as identifying sources of heterogeneity in the prevalence estimates is paramount for the development of global and efficient public health strategies adapted for this population.
Innovative attempts were previously made to explore different comorbid disorders, mental health outcomes, and risk factors associated with substance use disorders. For example, McKetin and collaborators attempted, through a meta-analysis, to capture the risk of observing five mental health outcomes associated with ATS consumption: psychosis, violence, depression, anxiety, and suicidality [ ]. They found from 59 pooled studies that ATS consumption was associated with higher odds of psychosis, violence, and suicidality. Since their goal was to assess the strength of relationships between different patterns of amphetamine use and targeted symptoms, the design did not allow the authors to estimate the global distribution of concomitant disorders. On the other end, Santo and colleagues estimated the prevalence of different psychopathologies appearing concurrently with opioid use disorder (OUD) in a meta-analysis [ ]. With their methodology, the group successfully included a large volume of participants (n = 77 212) and pooled a diverse set of studies across five geographical regions. From 345 included studies, they found a significantly higher prevalence of mental health comorbidities in OUD compared to their prevalence in the global general population. The group also evaluated the heterogeneity of psychiatric disorder prevalence based on sample characteristics and evaluation methods, for which they found significant differences in the pooled results between proxy measures and direct diagnosis classification systems used for disorders assessment [ ]. To our knowledge, no initiatives have attempted to capture the global picture of ATSUD’s concurrent psychiatric disorders prevalence with a similar approach [ ].
This project aims to bridge the gaps described above. This paper will present a protocol for the COmprehensive Meta-analysis and meta-regression of Psychiatric disorders in people with Amphetamine-type Stimulant use disorder Study (COMPASS), a project that comprehensively assesses the global prevalence of psychiatric disorders in people living with ATSUD by using a systematic review and a series of meta-analyses. The systematic review and meta-analyses will address two main objectives. First, we will describe the prevalence of psychiatric disorders and mental health outcomes among people living with ATSUD worldwide (objective 1), and second, we will evaluate the effect of key modifying parameters on the prevalence of psychiatric disorders among people with ATSUD (objective 2).

## Methods
We will conduct a systematic review and a series of meta-analyses operationalized with the Condition-Context-Population (CoCoPop) framework as described by Munn and collaborators [ ]. In contrast with the PICO framework, which is designed to conceptualize key aspects of studies evaluating the effects of an intervention on outcome measures [ ], the CoCoPop framework is best suited for epidemiological studies aiming to capture the frequency of disorders within a specific population, a framework that allows us the operationalization of outcomes that capture the frequencies of psychiatric disorders among the ATSUD population. The systematic reviews and meta-analyses will be reported based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [ ]. The protocol is constructed following the PRISMA-P checklist available in supplementary materials [ ]. We developed a three-step systematic search and meta-analysis strategy to classify and document findings efficiently. The implementation of these steps was designed to guide subsequent decisions for the selection of concurrent disorders and covariates of interest for each meta-analysis. The final systematic review and meta-analyses design is supported by feasibility evidence found during our pilot study (step 1). The final global search feeds separate meta-analyses, each studying one or a set of psychiatric disorders (Fig.  ).
 

## Step 1—pilot study
The primary goals of the pilot study were to train reviewers, select a priori psychiatric disorder outcomes for the main systematic search based on outcome relevance and availability, and to adjust the final search protocol based on the experiences, feasibility, and conflicts observed during consensus procedures.

## Step 1—pilot search and selection strategy
In August 2024, we performed a preliminary pilot search in PubMed. This search aimed to retrieve articles, regardless of language, that were indexed to the database in 1999, 2007, 2015, and 2023 and that assessed, at baseline, the diagnostic prevalence of psychiatric disorders, symptom severity, or related mental health outcomes (i.e., suicidality) among people living with ATSUD. Articles’ titles, abstracts, and full-text selection procedures were conducted by two reviewers independently in duplicate on Covidence [ ]. The selection process was conducted in 2 phases: simultaneous title and abstract screening, followed by full-text selection. First authors of unavailable publications were not contacted during the pilot study. Reviewers independently extracted the availability of outcome measures in duplicate into an Excel spreadsheet, and conflicts were resolved through consensus or by the principal investigator’s decision. Complete details about outcome definitions, eligibility, search, selection, and extraction strategies are described in the pilot study instructions available on OSF (https://osf.io/qdf9j).

## Step 1—pilot statistical analysis and results
A descriptive analysis was conducted on Anaconda Distribution Python version 3.12.7 using open-access libraries pandas, numpy, seaborn, and matplotlib [ – ]. The pilot selection results are presented in a PRISMA flow diagram in Fig.  [ ]. We retrieved 4007 articles for eligibility assessment, among which 72 studies were selected for data extraction. After data extraction, authors (AMB, HS, and DJA) reached a consensus to decide which identified psychiatric results will be kept for the final systematic review outcomes. From these articles, we identified 65 articles with relevant psychiatric outcomes for the series. From these relevant studies, we extracted 216 single data entries of psychiatric outcomes. Furthermore, we identified 7 studies with additional psychiatric outcomes (61 single data entries), deemed to be irrelevant to this study. Upon consensus, we decided to exclude these outcomes from the final systematic search and meta-analyses, as well as the descriptive analysis presented below. The rationale for outcome selection is provided in the post-pilot adjustments section.
 Psychiatric outcome frequencies per measure type and data availability are presented in Figs.  and , respectively. The most frequently measured psychiatric outcomes were depressive disorders, followed by anxiety disorders, schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders, suicide and self-injury behaviors, bipolar disorders, ADHD, obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), PTSD, eating disorders, unspecified mood disorders, and personality disorders. We also identified studies reporting psychiatric symptoms and proportion measures of any psychiatric disorders. Most diagnosis observations were based on DSM-IV and ICD-10 classification systems. Schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders category adds the most frequent number of available results (93.8%), followed by obsessive–compulsive and related disorders (87.5%) and bipolar disorders (82.4%). Overall, 75.5% of results were available across outcomes categories. Among the 65 included studies, the median number of participants with ATSUD was 152 (IQR = 184). Furthermore, we retrieved studies from 21 countries distributed across all continents, with the majority coming from the USA (n = 32, 49.2%), China (n = 9, 13.8%), and Iran (n = 5, 7.7%).
 

## Step 1—post-pilot protocol adjustments
The experience from conducting the pilot study, as well as conflicts raised during the consensus phases, primed the need to adjust the final search protocol. First, we defined a list of exclusion keywords to be filtered in the titles and abstract during the final systematic search. This list was mostly based on non-human terms such as animal names. Additionally, we decided to exclude studies including only participants in remission for more than 3 months. We observed that remission information is rarely disclosed in the methods section. For the overarching search and selection strategy (step 2), we decided to use average abstinence duration as a proxy measure of remission. In the instance where neither remission nor abstinence duration specification is available, we will assume eligibility. The minimal ATSUD sampling size has been decreased from 50 to 40 to include more relevant studies while controlling for study power [ ]. Furthermore, we established an order of priority for eligibility assessment to avoid unnecessary conflicts: (1) verification of the study design, (2) verification of the study population, (3) verification of the number of ATSUD participants, (4) verification of the presence of a priori psychiatric disorder of interest, and (5) verification of other eligibility criteria.
For the a priori psychiatric outcomes of interest, we selected depressive disorders, bipolar disorders, anxiety disorders, schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders, PTSD, ADHD, OCD, eating disorders, or personality disorders [ , ]. Furthermore, we will include suicidal and non-suicidal self-injury behaviors and proportion measures labelled as “any psychiatric disorders” as ancillary meta-analysis outcomes [ ].
We decided to exclude conduct disorder and risk behaviors from the final systematic review because there were no results available among the retrieved study measurements (n = 4) and because it is mostly identified among minors [ – ]. During the initial extraction, we found measures for intellectual disability (n = 1), motor neurodegenerative disorders (n = 1), gambling disorder (n = 1), hostility and aggression (n = 7), paranoia (n = 5), impulsivity (n = 7), dysphoria (n = 1), somatization (n = 6), interpersonal sensitivity (n = 5), and cognition measures (n = 13). However, we decided not to include these outcomes in the final systematic review because they either present themselves in multiple disorder categories, or they reach outside of our project’s psychiatric scope, or they rather reflect proxy covariates. Although believed to be an impactful co-variate, exposure to childhood trauma (n = 8) was not included for the final list of psychiatric outcomes. Lastly, for the final systematic review and meta-analyses series, we decided we would not extract continuous symptom data reported as mean values. Instead, reviewers will only extract the proportion of participants screening positive for the outcomes of interest.

## Step 2—final systematic review

## Step 2—search and selection strategy
The final search will be conducted with the expertise of a CHUM librarian on MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, and CINAHL on Ovid and EBSCO interfaces [ ]. Publications indexed since 1999 studying human samples will be retrieved and filtered for duplicates and animal studies. Articles will be screened independently in duplicate on Covidence [ ] based on titles and abstracts and selected based on a two-phase full-text eligibility assessment to confirm the presence of the population (ATSUD) and proportional measurements of our a priori psychiatric disorders of interest. First authors will be contacted to retrieve unavailable full text with a 2-week response time window. Retrieved articles written in languages other than English or French will be translated using large language model algorithms that do not retain article content. The search and selection process will be reported with a PRISMA-2020 flow diagram [ ]. To be eligible, published works or dissertations about a primary study with observational, interventional, or qualitative designs that include a sample of at least 40 human participants identified with a current ATSUD at the time of assessment must be included. Studies must also include the evaluation of at least one outcome of interest at baseline using DSM- or ICD-based standardized methods or proxy screening questionnaires with clear symptom cut-offs. Complete details about the final search outcome definitions, eligibility, selection, and extraction strategies are described in the final search instructions available on OSF.

## Step 2—extraction procedure
The extraction procedure will be conducted independently in duplicate on Microsoft Excel [ ]. This main dataset will be the reference for all concomitant disorders and mental health-related events, where the availability of relevant outcomes of interest will be flagged for subsequent meta-analyses (step 3). For this main dataset, we will collect the following variables: study identification, data collection year, study location, study design, recruitment methods, study settings, ATSUD population type (i.e., ATSUD only or contains an ATSUD subgroup), ATSUD sample size, psychiatry disorder category, specific psychiatry disorder name, result availability (i.e., yes or no), classification criteria for ATSUD and psychiatry disorder (i.e., DSM or ICD or other), measurement name (e.g., MINI, SCID, or Beck Depression Inventory), and relevant comments about measures (e.g., symptoms cut-off). Dataset entries will be made in duplicate, and conflicts will be resolved through consensus. The full extraction procedure is described in the final search instruction available on OSF.

## Step 3—meta-analyses of psychiatric disorders
As each comorbid psychiatric disorder or mental health-related outcome is different in the way they are expressed, measured, and conceptualized, each meta-analysis on one or a set of psychiatric disorder(s) would have its own final eligibility criteria, data extraction, quality assessment, and statistical analysis. Each team member will directly access the reference table and start a third-level eligibility assessment and extraction procedure adapted to their condition of interest, constraints, and specific objectives. The step-3 selection procedures will integrate previous inclusion and exclusion criteria with additional disorder-specific selection criteria. The main search will be updated for each meta-analysis upon designing a new study covering the previous search period if more than a year has passed since the previous search. For each meta-analysis, a protocol will be registered on PROSPERO, and the study selection and extraction procedures will be managed through Covidence in duplicate by independent reviewers [ ].

## Step 3—extraction procedure
Two reviewers will independently select and extract information and data of interest for all eligible studies in a study-specific dataset on Microsoft Excel [ ]. In addition to the variables extracted in the main COMPASS dataset, we will extract the number of participants with a psychiatric diagnosis (n), additional information about the comorbidity identification methods and classification system. Based on psychopathologies, study’s aims and feasibility, additional sociodemographic information could be extracted to describe the pooled samples with descriptive analysis (e.g., age, biological sex, ethnicity, years of education, housing status, employment, past incarceration, and relationship status). Extraction procedures can be customized based on specificities for each psychiatric disorder of interest. Each data entry will be revised through a consensus, and remaining conflicts will be escalated to the principal investigator.

## Step 3—quality assessment
We will assess study-level quality using the peer-reviewed prevalence studies checklist from the critical appraisal tool for use in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Systematic Reviews [ ]. This tool is best suited for cross-sectional baseline prevalence and incidence measurements that we aim to evaluate and will be applied to all study designs. Quality assessment templates will be made available on OSF. Publication bias will be assessed using the funnel plots and Egger’s regression test when there are 10 or more studies available for each outcome measurement. An overt asymmetry in the dispersion of the study-level estimates on the funnel plot will be regarded as a sign of the risk of reporting bias. The risk of reporting bias will be additionally investigated through the evaluation of the small-study effects in the meta-analysis using the regression-based Egger’s test.

## Step 3—quantitative synthesis
We will perform proportional meta-analyses of diagnosis proportions and mental health-related events and assess the heterogeneity by sociodemographic and methodological covariates. Future authors of this series of meta-analyses will choose between the latest versions of Python, STATA, or R Studio to conduct their analyses. For example, we could use the metaprop or PythonMeta packages of STATA or Anaconda Distribution Python, respectively [ – ].
Each meta-analysis will select one or a set of outcomes of interest (psychiatric disorders or mental health-related events). The proportion of ATSUD participants diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder will be the primary outcome in each meta-analysis. The outcomes can be stratified by psychiatric disorders’ timeline (i.e., lifetime or current).
Data pooling will involve a Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation followed by an inverse-variance random-effects meta-analysis. To ensure epidemiological relevance, the pooled estimates and their 95% confidence intervals will be back-transformed into proportions for final reporting. The random-effects approach was chosen a priori to address potential heterogeneity stemming from spatial, temporal, diagnostic, and setting differences across studies. Results will be presented by forest plots, and general inter-study heterogeneity will be represented by the Higgins test (I2).
To account for geographical heterogeneity, the ATSUD population size, and unavailability of the prevalence estimates by countries, we will implement a strategy for the adjustment of results by the ATSUD population size based on the country of origin and imputation for the countries with unavailable estimates. This strategy will determine the number of countries with available estimates to provide meaningful regional and global estimates, as certain countries may publish fewer articles and have a de facto larger ATSUD population compared to other countries, which could disproportionally create imbalances in prevalence pooling. Following arbitrary thresholds based on the number of countries with available estimates for each outcome, one of the following adjustments will be integrated into the data analysis:
Data pooling without population size adjustment: when the number of countries with the estimate is < 10, the data will be pooled without adjustment for the ATSUD population size of the country of origin.
Data pooling with population size adjustment:Global-only adjusted estimate without imputation for missing country estimates: when the number of countries with an available estimate ranges between 10 and 20, the data will be pooled with adjustment for the country-specific ATSUD population size based on the latest version of the GBD database from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) [ ].
Regional and global adjusted estimates with consideration of imputation for missing country estimates: when the number of countries with the estimate is ≥ 20, the regional and global estimates will be synthesized by adjustment for the ATSUD population size based on the country of origin. Additionally, we will substitute the regional estimates for the countries with missing estimates if we have at least two countries with available results in the same region, or if we only have one country with an available estimate in the same region with an ATSUD population size accounting for more than 50% of the total regional ATSUD population size. Furthermore, we will substitute the global estimate in regions with a missing estimate if the region has no countries with an available estimate or if we have one country with an estimate, but the total ATSUD population size accounts for less than 50% of the total regional ATSUD population size. This imputation framework aligns with established methodologies used in previous global prevalence meta-analyses to derive robust international estimates [ , ].

The above adjustment and imputation method could finally be fit based on the geographical distribution of studies and population size. The adjustment of regional and global estimates for ATSUD population size will be executed using a Monte Carlo simulation with 100,000 iterations. The regional and global population size-adjusted estimates and their uncertainties (Monte Carlo simulation results) will include the country-level estimates and their 95% confidence intervals, either single study or pooled estimates and country-level ATSUD population sizes and their uncertainty [ ]. We arbitrarily developed eight regions based on the United Nations map presented in Fig. S1. A STATA code framework and its forest plot (Fig. S2) for a random-effect meta-analysis using the pilot data is presented in additional file 2.
Sensitivity analyses could be incorporated based on representativity and feasibility parameters for each psychiatric disorder, including a meta-analysis of untransformed data using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM), a meta-analysis of the data from the past 5 years, and a meta-analysis of adjusted results without imputation for countries without an estimate. Each meta-analysis can have more specific sensitivity analyses based on their psychiatry disorder of interest in terms of study and population characteristics (e.g., DSM and ICD versions).
To further evaluate the sources of heterogeneity, we will perform meta-regression and subgroup analyses to evaluate the relationship of sociodemographic and methodological covariates on the study-level estimates. These results will come from crude pooled estimates (neither adjusted by population size nor imputed). The main covariates will be dependent on each meta-analysis context, but we are mainly interested in age, biological sex, study year, study location, study design, ATSUD and psychiatry disorder diagnostic methods, study setting, and study recruitment strategy. Nevertheless, additional sociodemographic covariates such as education level, judicialization, and housing status will be added and tailored for each meta-analysis based on the selected psychiatric disorder. These additional variables will be disclosed in each PROSPERO protocol. Sub-group analyses will include the group-specific estimates and their 95% confidence intervals, and the significance testing for the inter-group difference of the estimates. When there are 10 or more study results for each outcome measure, the effect of each covariate can be investigated using a bivariable meta-regression. The covariates with a P < 0.1 will be included in a multivariable meta-regression model to calculate their adjusted effect estimates (i.e., regression coefficient) and P values.

## Discussion
In the present article, we provided a protocol for a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis project about the global distribution of concomitant psychiatric disorders in people living with ATSUD. We developed a three-phase systematic review and meta-analysis protocol to gather the past 25 years of scientific literature on concurrent disorders. The development of the final protocol was supported by a pilot study assessing overall concurrent disorder results in the scientific literature, as well as the observations made during the selection and extraction phases.
The COMPASS protocol has been thoroughly designed based on evidence acquired through a pilot and shows innovation by the inclusion of all types of studies based on measurements, regardless of the studies’ primary objectives. Furthermore, the current design accounts for multiple sources of heterogeneity that could affect findings using meta-regression and subgroup analyses while also incorporating tailored adjustment methods for population size. The systematic nature of this comprehensive protocol selection and extraction process will allow the creation of a large database for future knowledge synthesis initiatives and has the potential to highlight prevalence changes associated with the evolution of ATSUD and comorbid disorders since the beginning of the XXI century.
We believe this systematic review and a series of meta-analyses will have an important impact on public health research and global policies by addressing challenges related to ATSUD surveillance worldwide. Notably, it will provide a comprehensive and rigorous update of the prevalence of multiple ATSUD’s comorbidities, as most prevalences reported by authoritative sources look at each psychiatric disorder frequency separately from substance use disorder within the general population, an approach that tends to underestimate the prevalence of psychiatric disorders among populations with ATSUD.
Despite its robustness, the heterogeneity resulting from diverse diagnostic methods, study design, recruitment, and sampling settings, as well as the uneven distribution of studies over space and time, could also be viewed as one important limitation of this design. The unavailability of results and relevant covariates from included studies may add a layer of challenge, as extreme values in prevalence estimates could add instability of variance [ , ]. Furthermore, while this project aims to be as holistic and culturally broad, the generalization and interpretation of results for concurrent disorders’ prevalence are inherently restricted by the heterogeneous diagnosis classification systems used when assessing mental health worldwide in the past decades [ ]. One strength and challenge of this series is to capture and interpret the impact of DSM and ICD criteria changes over time in relation to the observed prevalences worldwide. We will attempt to investigate these relationships by conducting a series of subgroup analyses for revisions of the classification system, study dates, countries, and designs.
Studying relationships between the observed national prevalence and hypothesized sources of heterogeneity will also be challenging regarding external validity. In most cases, we might not have access to complete information related to sociodemographic covariates. For instance, stratification of comorbidities’ prevalence by biological sex and socioeconomic markers will not be reported in most studies, or bias toward specific subgroups (e.g. sample composed only of biological males). This lack of information can be mitigated by contacting the authors, but in most cases, the missingness of covariates will limit their inclusion in meta-regression analysis and limit the representativity of our interpretations.
Both methodological (e.g., changes to the classification systems of DSM and ICD) and sociodemographic factors (location and time) are believed to influence how diagnoses are identified and how their prevalence is reported. For instance, we hypothesize that changes to the DSM and ICD might have inflated some comorbidities’ prevalence over time (e.g. higher age threshold for the diagnosis of ADHD), but we also know that psychiatric disorder tends to be more frequently diagnosed in high-income countries [ ]. This could be influenced by many factors, such as access to the healthcare system, social protection and stigma, national drug policies and legal framework, and resource allocation for research in the mental health and substance use fields [ – ]. Nevertheless, this robust project framework allows for adaptability and follows the best scientific practices for systematic reviews and meta-analyses. We do believe that creating a clear, simple, and systematic method could allow the reproduction of the project over time, not only within our research group but also with collaborators worldwide.

## Conclusion
The purpose of this project is to form the most accurate global picture of psychiatric disorders and mental health-related events in people living with ATSUD to inform the best public health practices internationally. Notably, the project’s methodology has the potential to produce valid evidence-based epidemiological estimates for concurrent disorders to inform mental health agencies as well as clinical and regulatory practices, such as screening practices, resource provision strategies, and psychiatric semiology.
