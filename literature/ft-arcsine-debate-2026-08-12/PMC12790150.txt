## Summary

## Background
Childhood diarrhoea is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality among children under five years of age. This study provides an analysis of global childhood diarrhoea prevalence, drawing on standardised, nationally representative survey data collected from 1985 to 2024.

## Methods
A systematic review of Demographic and Health Surveys and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys conducted up to October 30, 2024, was undertaken, with findings reported in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Random-effects meta-analyses and meta-regression analyses were conducted. Factor analysis was employed to construct a Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index, integrating socioeconomic, water, sanitation, hygiene, and nutrition indicators.

## Findings
The analysis identified 593 relevant studies, estimating a global pooled mean childhood diarrhoea prevalence of 14.4% (95% CI: 13.8–15.0%) across all regions and time periods. Prevalence declined at a rate of 1% per year, falling from 22.3% in 1985–1989 to 10.9% in 2020–2024, with consistent declines observed in all regions except the Eastern Mediterranean Region. Prevalence was highest in the African Region and lowest in the European Region. Higher prevalence was observed in countries with larger household sizes, longer water collection times, and higher rates of underweight, stunting, and wasting. In contrast, lower prevalence was associated with higher levels of urbanisation, maternal education, population density, Human Development Index, income per capita, and access to improved water sources and sanitation facilities. A higher Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index was strongly and consistently associated with lower diarrhoea prevalence, demonstrating a dose–response relationship.

## Interpretation
Childhood diarrhoea prevalence has declined over recent decades, apparently reflecting the synergistic effects of targeted public health interventions and broader socioeconomic progress in addressing upstream determinants of child health.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
Diarrhoea remains a global health challenge and the third leading cause of death among children under five, responsible for an estimated 445,000 deaths annually. This burden is disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, where 90% of these deaths occur. Although numerous studies have reported childhood diarrhoea prevalence, variations in recall periods, case definitions, and survey methodologies have limited data comparability across countries and over time, impeding a clear understanding of global patterns and associations. A PubMed search using broad terms ((Diarrhoea [MeSH] OR Diarrhoea [MeSH]) AND Review [All Fields]), with an added filter for systematic reviews or meta-analyses and no restrictions on language or date, identified no systematic reviews or meta-analyses focused on global or regional childhood diarrhoea prevalence based exclusively on high-quality, nationally representative data collected through standardised methods, with consistent variable definitions and uniform questionnaires—underscoring a critical gap in the literature.

## Added value of this study
Using rigorous methodologies and a range of analyses, this study synthesised high-quality global and regional data on childhood diarrhoea from 129 countries across all world regions. The study documented a consistent global decline in childhood diarrhoea prevalence over the past four decades, observed in all but one region, with an estimated reduction of 1% per year. Despite this progress, substantial disparities persist, with the African Region continuing to bear the highest burden. A key contribution of the study was the development of a composite indicator—the Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index—which was strongly and significantly associated with diarrhoea prevalence and accounted for more than one-quarter of the variation observed across studies. By integrating socioeconomic, water, sanitation, and nutritional indicators, the index captures the complex, multidimensional factors linked to diarrhoeal disease. Its strong association with prevalence underscores that childhood diarrhoea is not solely a health issue, but also a manifestation of broader systemic challenges, including poverty and underdevelopment.

## Implications of all the available evidence
The substantial global decline in childhood diarrhoea prevalence represents a major public health achievement for a disease that has historically caused widespread morbidity and mortality and remains a leading cause of death among children under five years of age. This decline may reflect the cumulative impact of multiple public health interventions over recent decades, including the introduction of rotavirus vaccination programmes and the promotion of exclusive breastfeeding, alongside broader improvements in socioeconomic conditions and nutritional status. These advances are associated with reduced exposure to enteric pathogens and greater resilience to infection. The findings underscore the importance of a comprehensive approach that combines targeted health interventions with sustained investments in socioeconomic development—addressing the broader contextual factors linked to diarrhoeal disease and supporting long-term improvements in child health.

## Introduction
Diarrhoea, defined as at least three loose or watery bowel movements per day, remains a major global health burden, primarily resulting from pathogen exposure through contaminated food and water. , , Diarrhoea is the third leading cause of mortality among children under five, accounting for an estimated 445,000 deaths annually. This burden is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, where 90% of these deaths occur. While high-income regions report fewer than one death per 100,000 children under five, mortality exceeds 130 per 100,000 in the African Region. , 
Global initiatives, including oral rehydration therapy, rotavirus vaccination, and improved water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions, have reduced the incidence and severity of childhood diarrhoea. , , , However, progress towards Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6, which aims to “ensure universal access to water and sanitation”, remains insufficient. As of 2022, an estimated 2.2 billion people lacked access to safe drinking water and basic handwashing facilities, while 3.5 billion lacked safely managed sanitation. These gaps hinder efforts to achieve the SDG 3.2 target of “ending preventable deaths of newborns and children under five by 2030”. 
The socioeconomic burden of diarrhoea is most severe in disadvantaged communities with limited healthcare access. , , Beyond medical costs, it can impair child growth and cognitive development, straining societies, economies, and healthcare systems. , , The bidirectional link between diarrhoea and malnutrition further exacerbates morbidity and mortality, as malnourished children are more vulnerable to severe diarrhoea, while recurrent diarrhoeal episodes worsen malnutrition, stunting, and immune dysfunction. , 
In this context, this study provides a global analysis of childhood diarrhoea prevalence from 1985 to 2024. It estimates pooled prevalence levels and trends at both global and World Health Organisation (WHO) regional levels, compares these levels and trends across regions, and investigates associations with prevalence and sources of heterogeneity in the measures. A key strength of this study is its exclusive use of high-quality, nationally representative data collected through standardised methods and consistent questionnaires, and subjected to rigorous quality control, ensuring methodological robustness and comparability across countries and over time.

## Methods

## Data sources, study approach, and search strategy
The protocol for this study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251106807). To enable the comparison of childhood diarrhoea prevalence levels and trends across regions and over time using high-quality, nationally representative data, this study exclusively utilised Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS). The advantage of these surveys is their use of standardised data collection procedures, uniform variable and outcome definitions, and consistent questionnaires, ensuring methodological comparability across countries and time periods. , , , These data also undergo rigorous quality control, enhancing their reliability and validity for global analysis. , , , Additional background information on both surveys is provided in .
DHS and MICS have been conducted in over 120 countries through more than 800 independent surveys over four decades, providing a comprehensive dataset for this study. , The survey results are publicly available in detailed reports, which were systematically searched, downloaded, and screened from the DHS and MICS databases. , The data search included all available records up to October 30, 2024.
In both surveys, childhood diarrhoea was assessed through structured interviews with mothers or primary caregivers, conducted by rigorously trained field staff in accordance with standardised DHS and MICS protocols. , These interviews collected detailed demographic information, including the child's age and sex, enabling the reporting of diarrhoea prevalence by these characteristics. In addition to diarrhoea data, the surveys gathered a comprehensive set of child health and household indicators, incorporating direct anthropometric measurements (e.g., height and weight) and household self-reports on environmental, demographic, and socioeconomic factors. This provided rich contextual data for analysing child health outcomes. Race and ethnicity data were not collected, as these variables are generally excluded from DHS and MICS surveys.
Although this study could be perceived as a data integration project, its implementation adhered to established systematic review methodology in handling the literature. The DHS and MICS reports were systematically reviewed in accordance with Cochrane Collaboration methods, and the findings were reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines ( ). , The process included a clearly defined research question; pre-specified study selection process and inclusion/exclusion criteria; systematic literature screening; structured data extraction; assessment of evidence quality; assessment of publication bias; and the conduct of meta-analyses and meta-regressions.
The only substantive deviation from a traditional systematic review was in the choice of databases for report identification. The search was restricted to the DHS and MICS databases because the study design required exclusive use of high-quality, nationally representative data collected through standardised methods, with consistent questionnaires, and subjected to rigorous quality control. This approach was supported by the DHS and MICS programmes' respective platforms, , which serve as comprehensive repositories of survey documentation, ensuring methodological robustness and comparability across countries and over time.
This systematic review covered all 194 WHO member states, as well as additional entities recognised or reported within WHO frameworks. For the purposes of this study, the term “country” is used inclusively to refer to both WHO member states and these non-member entities.
All included countries were categorised according to the six official WHO regions: African Region, Region of the Americas, Eastern Mediterranean Region, European Region, South-East Asia Region, and Western Pacific Region ( and ). 

## Study selection process and inclusion and exclusion criteria
Survey reports were screened for childhood diarrhoea prevalence using predefined eligibility criteria by ZMT. Reports were included if they provided representative estimates of diarrhoea prevalence in children under five years of age (or under three years in some older DHS surveys). While the vast majority of included surveys were nationally representative, a limited number of subnational surveys—conducted at the level of administrative regions—were also included if they covered at least 50% of the national population, under the assumption that they provided adequate population coverage to approximate national representativeness.
Reports were excluded if they were conducted nationally but only provided data for region- or age-specific subsamples, were unpublished or from ongoing surveys at the time of this study, or employed non-standard recall periods deviating from the established two-week window, in which mothers or caregivers reported diarrhoea episodes occurring within the 14 days prior to the survey. 

## Data extraction, additional data sources, and composite index development
Prevalence measures and associated variables were extracted and double-extracted from the relevant DHS and MICS reports by ZMT and HA. The extracted variables, described in , included sociodemographic, WASH, and nutrition indicators, all selected a priori based on their relevance to childhood diarrhoea epidemiology in the existing literature. , , , , , , , , , , , , Discrepancies between extractors were resolved through consensus, with input from LJA when necessary.
Data were obtained from publicly available reports rather than through analysis of raw datasets, for several reasons. First, many countries do not make underlying datasets publicly accessible, which would have constrained the geographical coverage of the analysis. Second, published reports provide nationally representative prevalence estimates that account for the complex survey design—including sampling weights, stratification, and clustering—calculated by the original investigators using standardised, validated methodologies, thereby minimising the risk of error or bias from independent data processing. Third, consistent with systematic review principles for publicly available literature, , , the use of standardised reports ensured methodological consistency and transparency in data sourcing.
When a DHS report did not provide a variable in the required definition, the DHS STATcompiler was used to generate standardised estimates, ensuring consistency across survey rounds.
If a DHS or MICS variable—other than prevalence—was not reported in the survey report, its value was obtained from alternative sources, specifically the World Bank, the United Nations Population Division, the WHO/United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP), and the WHO ( ). , , 
Additional variables, including population density, the Human Development Index, and gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, were extracted from the databases of the United Nations Population Division, United Nations Development Programme, and International Monetary Fund, respectively. To ensure temporal alignment between outcomes and covariates, each country-level covariate was extracted to match the year in which the corresponding survey was conducted, maintaining temporal consistency between covariate data and outcome measures.
For any remaining missing values, imputation was applied. In countries with multiple survey rounds, missing values were imputed using the last observation carried forward or the next observation carried backward, depending on the position of the missing data within the temporal sequence. When missing values were located between two survey rounds within the same country, the next observation carried backward was prioritised to ensure recency in the imputed estimates. In countries with only a single survey, missing values were imputed using the median value of the corresponding variable within the same WHO region, calculated over a 10-year interval to preserve temporal and regional relevance.
Two composite indices were generated from the extracted data for use in subsequent analyses: the Maternal Education Level Index and the Water Remoteness Index. The Maternal Education Level Index quantified maternal educational attainment on a continuous scale from 0 to 1. Weights were assigned to each education category: 0 for mothers with no formal education, 0.5 for those with primary education, and 1 for those with secondary education or higher. The index was calculated by summing the products of each category's weight and its corresponding population proportion, providing a population-level measure of maternal education.
The Water Remoteness Index measured household access to water based on collection time, also on a continuous scale from 0 to 1. Weights were assigned as follows: 0 for households with water on premises, 0.5 for those requiring less than 30 min to collect water, and 1 for those requiring 30 min or more. The index was similarly calculated as the sum of the products of each category's weight and its corresponding population proportion, providing a population-level measure of water inaccessibility.
For the WASH variables, inconsistencies in survey definitions for classifying improved water sources and sanitation facilities were occasionally encountered. To address these variations and fill data gaps, standardised data were obtained, when needed, from the WHO/UNICEF JMP, the official United Nations source for global WASH monitoring. The WHO/UNICEF JMP reconciles differing survey definitions using statistical methods to produce harmonised, comparable estimates across countries and over time. Specifically, it employs simple linear regression with the least squares method to model trends and estimate the proportion of the population using each facility type at rural, urban, and national levels. 

## Assessment of methodological quality: precision and risk of bias
Evaluations of the precision of the relevant surveys were conducted by ZMT with input from LJA. Surveys were classified as having high precision (as opposed to low precision) if they included a sample of at least 2495 children under five years of age. This threshold was determined to provide acceptable precision for prevalence estimates, accounting for a design effect of 1.5, a 10% non-response rate, and a 12% relative error, in line with established survey methodology guidelines. , Specifically, assuming an expected childhood diarrhoea prevalence of 15%, a sample size of 2495 yields a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 13.6%–16.5%, representing an acceptable level of precision for prevalence estimation.
Risk of bias (ROB) evaluations for the relevant surveys were conducted by ZMT with input from LJA. These evaluations followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines for prevalence studies. , , The nine assessment domains included the appropriateness of the sampling frame, appropriateness of the sampling method, adequacy of sample size, clarity in the description of study subjects and settings, robustness of data analysis (i.e., analysis conducted with sufficient coverage of the identified sample), validity of methods for condition identification, reliability of condition measurement, appropriateness of statistical analysis, and proper handling of response rates ( ).
Each domain was rated as “yes” for low ROB, “no” for high ROB, or “unclear” for unclear ROB. A survey was classified as having low overall ROB if it received a “yes” rating for all critical domains—sampling frame, sampling method, methods for condition identification, and reliability of condition measurement—had no “no” ratings in any domain, and no more than two “unclear” ratings overall.
A survey was classified as having high overall ROB if it received a “no” rating in any critical domain or in the domain assessing appropriateness of statistical analysis, more than one “no” rating across any domains, or more than three “unclear” ratings. Surveys that did not meet the criteria for either low or high overall ROB were classified as having unclear overall ROB.

## Assessment of publication bias
Publication bias was assessed using Doi plots in conjunction with the Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index. Asymmetry in the Doi plot was interpreted as evidence of potential publication bias, suggesting that the observed variation in prevalence estimates may not be attributable to chance alone. An LFK index value exceeding ±1 was considered indicative of potential publication bias. 

## Meta-analyses
The extracted childhood diarrhoea prevalence estimates were pooled using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood model, selected for its methodological advantages over the DerSimonian-Laird approach. To stabilise variance, the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied , after considering its suitability for the data. Pooled mean prevalence estimates and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated at 5-year intervals, both globally and for each WHO region.
Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q statistic, with a p-value <0.1 indicating the presence of heterogeneity across surveys. , The I2 statistic was used to quantify the proportion of between-survey variation attributable to true differences in prevalence rather than chance. , The prediction interval was calculated to estimate the distribution of true prevalence values around the pooled mean. , Meta-analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3, utilising the “meta” package. 

## Modelling framework for examining childhood diarrhoea prevalence: meta-regressions and factor analysis
Linear univariable random-effects meta-regression analyses were conducted using log-transformed proportions to investigate sources of between-survey heterogeneity and identify factors associated with childhood diarrhoea prevalence. Log transformation was selected over logit transformation to yield prevalence ratios, offering more intuitive and interpretable epidemiological insights compared to prevalence odds ratios. Both the random error and random-effects terms were assumed to follow a normal distribution. A p-value ≤0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant association with prevalence.
To account for potential confounding and better characterise the observed variability in prevalence, a multivariable random-effects meta-regression model was constructed. This approach allowed for the simultaneous estimation of associations between several covariates and childhood diarrhoea prevalence, while adjusting for temporal trends by including survey year as a continuous covariate. This enabled the assessment of covariate relationships independent of time-related changes.
An initial multivariable model including multiple individual covariates was considered but ultimately deemed inappropriate due to substantial collinearity among the extracted variables, particularly those representing social and economic development, WASH conditions, and child nutritional status. To address this issue, a composite Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index was developed using factor analysis, which reduced data dimensionality by capturing the shared variance among the socioeconomic, WASH, and nutrition variables.
Specifically, the variables included in this index were the proportion of urban households, Maternal Education Level Index, average household size, population density, Human Development Index, GDP per capita, household access to improved water sources and sanitation facilities, Water Remoteness Index, and the prevalences of underweight, stunting, and wasting among children under five years of age. The number of factors retained was determined using Kaiser's criterion, which selects factors with eigenvalues greater than one, ensuring that each retained factor explains more variance than any single observed variable. 
Factor scores were extracted and categorised into quintiles—very low, low, intermediate, high, and very high. The inclusion of these categorised factors in a multivariable random-effects meta-regression model was systematically assessed using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating improved model fit. 
Based on these criteria, the multivariable random-effects meta-regression model included only the first extracted factor, which accounted for the largest proportion of variance, alongside population type (children under five versus children under three), WHO region, and survey year as covariates. Additional models were tested to evaluate whether including more factors improved model fit. Variables with a p-value ≤0.2 in univariable analyses were considered eligible for inclusion in the multivariable model, while associations with a p-value ≤0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant. Model fit was assessed using the adjusted R2.
In this study, an association denotes a statistical relationship between two variables, without necessarily implying directionality or causality. Meta-regressions were conducted in Stata/SE version 18 using the “metareg” package, and in R using the “meta” and “psych” packages.

## Sensitivity analyses
Missing data were minimal (<5%) for most variables but exceeded 10% for a few, including the Water Remoteness Index (20.7%), wasting (14.3%), stunting (14.0%), and the Human Development Index (10.7%). To evaluate the robustness of the imputation strategy used in the main analysis, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using both mean and median imputation. Country-specific values were used for countries with multiple survey rounds, while WHO regional values—calculated over a 10-year interval—were applied to countries with only one round.
In addition, a second sensitivity analysis assessed the impact of study quality by restricting the dataset to surveys rated as low ROB. Both sensitivity analyses were conducted using the same multivariable meta-regression framework as the primary model.

## Ethics
This study was conducted in accordance with established ethical guidelines and principles. As all data were obtained from publicly available sources, and no primary data collection involving human participants was conducted, formal ethics approval was not required.

## Results

## Search results and scope of evidence
 outlines the study selection process in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Searches of the DHS and MICS databases identified a total of 865 reports—467 from DHS and 398 from MICS. After removing 9 duplicate reports, 856 reports remained for full-text screening. Of these, 199 were excluded due to the absence of childhood diarrhoea prevalence measures. 
The remaining 657 reports were assessed for eligibility, with 64 excluded for the following reasons: lack of national representativeness (n = 49), inconsistent age criteria (n = 4), reporting only prevalence estimates based on subsamples (n = 4), data from former countries (e.g., Yugoslavia; n = 3), non-standard recall periods (n = 3), and data collection issues (n = 1). In total, 593 reports met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study, yielding 593 childhood diarrhoea prevalence measures.
The African Region contributed the largest share of data, with 280 prevalence measures derived from surveys that included 2,143,360 children under five years of age. This was followed by the Region of the Americas (91 measures; 483,875 children), the Eastern Mediterranean Region (68 measures; 753,544 children), the European Region (57 measures; 165,895 children), the South-East Asia Region (56 measures; 1,055,612 children), and the Western Pacific Region (41 measures; 207,585 children). Surveys were conducted in a total of 129 countries. The prevalence measures spanned a broad timeframe beginning in 1985, with the majority (52.6%) of surveys conducted between 2000 and 2014.

 presents a summary of the precision assessment for the included prevalence surveys. Among the extracted prevalence measures, 499 (84.1%) were derived from surveys classified as having high precision, defined by a sample size of ≥2495 children under five years of age.
The ROB assessment for the included prevalence surveys is shown in , with results summarised in and visually depicted in . The vast majority of surveys (563, 94.9%) were rated as having low ROB, while a small proportion were classified as having high ROB (14, 2.4%) or unclear ROB (16, 2.7%). All surveys demonstrated low ROB across four of the assessment domains, and over 88% showed low ROB in the remaining domains, indicating consistent methodological rigour.

## Assessment of publication bias
The publication bias assessment is summarised in . No evidence of publication bias was detected in the global meta-analysis or in most regional meta-analyses. Minor indications of potential publication bias were observed in the meta-analyses for the Region of the Americas and the European Region; however, the evidence in both cases was minimal. Doi plots for all meta-analyses appeared virtually symmetrical ( ), and the corresponding LFK index values were within ±1 or only slightly above this threshold, supporting the conclusion that publication bias is unlikely ( ).

Pooled mean prevalence estimates are also reported in , with corresponding visualisations over calendar time shown in for global prevalence and in for prevalence by WHO region. 
The overall global pooled mean prevalence across all regions and time periods was estimated at 14.4% (95% CI: 13.8–15.0%) ( ). A consistent decline in global prevalence over time was observed, decreasing from 22.3% (95% CI: 18.4–26.4%) in 1985–1989 to 10.9% (95% CI: 8.4–13.8%) in 2020–2024 ( ). This downward trend was observed across all WHO regions, with the exception of the Eastern Mediterranean Region ( ).
Key results of the meta-analyses are illustrated in forest plots in . All meta-analyses demonstrated significant heterogeneity (p-value <0.1) ( ). The observed heterogeneity was largely attributable to true differences in prevalence across surveys, as indicated by I2 values exceeding 75%. This interpretation was further supported by the presence of relatively wide prediction intervals. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored through meta-regression analyses described below.

## Associations with childhood diarrhoea prevalence and sources of heterogeneity: the global univariable analysis
 presents the results of the univariable meta-regression analyses of childhood diarrhoea prevalence, incorporating all available prevalence measures across WHO regions. Notably, diarrhoea prevalence was found to decline at a relative rate of 2% per year. 
Prevalence was highest in the African Region, followed by the Eastern Mediterranean Region, the Region of the Americas, the South-East Asia Region, the Western Pacific Region, and was lowest in the European Region ( ). It was also higher among children under three years of age compared to those under five. Additionally, higher prevalence was observed in countries with larger average household sizes, longer water collection times (i.e., increased water remoteness), and higher rates of underweight, stunting, and wasting among children.
Conversely, lower diarrhoea prevalence was observed in countries with higher proportions of urban households, greater maternal education levels, higher population density, higher Human Development Index, greater GDP per capita, and a higher proportion of households with access to improved water sources and sanitation facilities ( ).

## Associations with childhood diarrhoea prevalence and sources of heterogeneity: regional univariable analyses
 present the results of the univariable meta-regression analyses of childhood diarrhoea prevalence conducted separately for each WHO region. A declining trend in diarrhoea prevalence was observed in all regions except the Eastern Mediterranean Region, where no evidence of a decline was detected ( ). The most rapid decline was observed in the European Region (4% per year), followed by the Region of the Americas (3% per year). A more moderate decline of 2% per year was observed in the African Region, South-East Asia Region, and Western Pacific Region. 
Regional analyses revealed patterns largely consistent with those observed in the global analysis ( ), with similar associations between diarrhoea prevalence and key covariates ( ). However, the strength of these associations varied across regions, and several did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the smaller number of prevalence measures available in the regional analyses compared to the global analysis.

## Latent structure of socioeconomic, WASH, and nutrition indicators and optimal model selection
The results of the factor analysis are presented in . Based on Kaiser's criterion, three factors with eigenvalues greater than one were retained. The first factor accounted for 55% of the total variance and demonstrated strong loadings (≥±0.5) on 11 of the 12 variables, with the exception of population density. This factor captured dimensions related to urbanisation, the Maternal Education Level Index, average household size, Human Development Index, GDP per capita, WASH indicators (access to improved water and sanitation facilities, and the Water Remoteness Index), as well as nutritional and health indicators, including underweight, stunting, and wasting.
The second factor was characterised by a strong loading for population density (0.85), along with moderate loadings for underweight (0.42) and wasting (0.46). The third factor was defined primarily by a strong loading on average household size (0.77).
Multivariable meta-regression model comparisons ( ) showed that including the second factor alongside the first did not improve model fit, as both AIC and BIC values increased. Although adding the third factor or including all three factors slightly reduced the AIC, BIC values increased, reflecting BIC's stricter penalty for model complexity. Given BIC's robustness in identifying parsimonious models, only the first factor was retained for interpretation.

## Associations with childhood diarrhoea prevalence and sources of heterogeneity: the global multivariable meta-regression analysis
 presents results of the multivariable meta-regression analysis of childhood diarrhoea prevalence, incorporating all available prevalence measures across WHO regions and including the primary factor from the factor analysis—hereafter referred to as the Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index. This index accounted for 27.1% of the variation in prevalence measures, while the full model explained 45.1% of the variation. 
A higher Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index was associated with lower diarrhoea prevalence, showing a dose–response relationship ( ). Regional differences in prevalence—consistent with patterns observed in the univariable analyses ( )—were confirmed. In addition, the analysis reaffirmed a declining trend in diarrhoea prevalence, at a relative rate of 1% per year.

## Sensitivity analyses
Sensitivity analyses using mean and median imputation ( ) produced results consistent with the primary analysis, showing minimal differences in the associations between childhood diarrhoea prevalence and key covariates.
Similarly, restricting the analysis to surveys rated as low ROB ( ) yielded comparable findings, reinforcing the robustness of the primary results.

## Discussion
The first key finding of this study is that childhood diarrhoea prevalence has declined globally over the past four decades, at an estimated rate of 1% per year. Prevalence decreased from 22.3% in 1985–1989 to 10.9% in 2020–2024, with an overall average of 14.4% across the surveyed countries during this period.
This downward trend was observed across all WHO regions, with the notable exception of the Eastern Mediterranean Region. The substantial global decline, consistent with historical trends in diarrhoea incidence and mortality rates, , represents a major global health achievement for a disease that has historically caused widespread morbidity and mortality and remains the third leading cause of death among children under five years of age. 
This decline appears associated with the cumulative impact of multiple public health interventions implemented over recent decades, including the introduction and scale-up of rotavirus vaccination programmes, , the widespread adoption of oral rehydration therapy, the promotion of exclusive breastfeeding, and vitamin A supplementation. These interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing the burden of diarrhoeal diseases. , , , , 
In contrast, the persistent burden of diarrhoea in the Eastern Mediterranean Region—with no observable decline in prevalence over time—may reflect the impact of ongoing political instability, conflict, population displacement, and chronic underinvestment in public health infrastructure. Countries such as Afghanistan and Yemen, which have been heavily impacted by these challenges, contributed substantially to the most recent pooled estimate, potentially explaining the elevated prevalence of 27.5% during 2020–2024. These conditions may have constrained the delivery and effectiveness of disease prevention and child health programmes in the region. , 
A second key finding of this study is that it provides insights into the broader factors associated with childhood diarrhoea prevalence and its global decline. The analysis identified a composite indicator—the Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index–as a significant factor strongly associated with diarrhoea prevalence, accounting for over one-quarter of the variation observed across surveys.
This index integrates socioeconomic, WASH, and nutritional indicators, capturing the complex, multidimensional context in which diarrhoeal disease occurs. Its association with prevalence suggests that childhood diarrhoea is not merely a health issue but also a reflection of broader systemic conditions, including poverty, inequity, and underdevelopment. For example, prolonged water collection times make water a scarce resource in daily life, limiting essential hygiene practices such as handwashing, safe disposal of child faeces, and proper cleaning of food utensils—all of which are vital for preventing diarrhoeal infections. , 
The observed association between higher index values and lower diarrhoea prevalence is consistent with the interpretation that improvements in socioeconomic status and nutrition may contribute to reduced risk, potentially by enabling structural changes that lower exposure to enteric pathogens and enhance resilience to infection. Such changes include improved access to safe water and sanitation, better living conditions, enhanced gut function and immune response through improved nutrition, increased health literacy, and strengthened caregiving capacity. This finding aligns with the view that addressing childhood diarrhoea is closely linked to advancing a country's broader development agenda. , , 
This interpretation is further supported by recurrent associations identified in the analysis: countries with a higher Human Development Index, greater GDP per capita, higher levels of urbanisation, broader access to improved water and sanitation, better nutritional outcomes, higher maternal education, and smaller household sizes had lower diarrhoea prevalence. Collectively, these factors reflect systemic development that may influence the upstream determinants of diarrhoeal disease. Progress in these areas has been shown to reduce caregiving burdens, alleviate overcrowding, mitigate unhygienic behaviours and living conditions, and strengthen both biological immunity and health system capacity. , , , , , , , , , 
Marked disparities in childhood diarrhoea prevalence across WHO regions persisted even after adjusting for explanatory factors, including the Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index, with the African Region continuing to bear the highest burden. This regional variation suggests the presence of additional, context-specific factors not captured by the current set of indicators and underscores the need for further investigation. Possible contributors include climatic and seasonal variability, which may affect water availability and the transmission dynamics of enteric pathogens, as well as regional differences in the coverage, timeliness, and effectiveness of rotavirus vaccination programmes. , , These factors may compound existing structural vulnerabilities and help explain why certain regions continue to experience disproportionately high levels of childhood diarrhoea.
The study also yielded additional findings, including one that remains unexplained. Specifically, higher population density at the national level was associated with lower childhood diarrhoea prevalence—an unexpected association. Factor analysis confirmed that population density operates independently of other socioeconomic, WASH, and nutritional indicators, suggesting that its influence may involve distinct mechanisms. Another consistent finding was that children under three years of age exhibited higher diarrhoea prevalence compared to those under five. This age-specific pattern is likely attributable to the gradual development of natural immunity. As children grow older, repeated exposure to enteric pathogens strengthens their immune response, thereby reducing susceptibility to diarrhoeal illness over time. , , 
This study has limitations. First, although the analysis included DHS and MICS data from 129 countries, it did not cover all low- and middle-income countries. The distribution of surveys was also uneven across regions and countries, with some contributing disproportionately more data points. For example, the African Region accounted for nearly half of all surveys, which may influence regional comparisons and global estimates. Additionally, some early regional estimates may have been skewed by limited country representation—for instance, the elevated 24.8% prevalence recorded in the European Region during 1990–1994 was based solely on data from Turkey during a period of political and economic instability, which may not have been representative of the broader region at that time.
Second, data on childhood diarrhoea were based on caregiver self-reports, which may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. However, this bias is likely minimal given the short two-week recall period and the generally non-stigmatising nature of diarrhoeal illness. Third, the cross-sectional design of the surveys limits causal inference, allowing for the identification of associations but not definitive conclusions regarding causality.
Fourth, considerable heterogeneity in diarrhoea prevalence was observed across the included surveys. Nonetheless, much of this variation was accounted for through meta-regression analyses that incorporated key explanatory variables, including child age, WHO region, survey year, and the Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index.
Fifth, the study could not assess the potential impact of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic as a confounder, as most surveys were conducted prior to the pandemic and did not capture data on pandemic-related disruptions to sanitation practices, healthcare access, or disease transmission. In addition, key intervention-level factors—such as rotavirus vaccination coverage and breastfeeding practices—were excluded due to inconsistent data availability across countries and survey years, limiting the ability to directly evaluate their influence on observed trends despite evidence of their effectiveness. , , , Future research should address these gaps by analysing data collected before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and by incorporating intervention-specific data where available.
This study has strengths. First, it draws on an extensive dataset comprising nationally representative prevalence data from the majority of countries and all WHO regions, providing a comprehensive global perspective. This broad geographic and temporal coverage enabled detailed analyses of childhood diarrhoea prevalence and its key associations over a four-decade period.
Second, the use of meta-regression analyses enabled a nuanced examination of epidemiological associations, providing in-depth insights into the sources of variability in prevalence across countries, regions, and time. Both the univariable and multivariable models incorporated survey-level random effects to account for unobserved heterogeneity, including differences in seasonal timing, survey duration, and implementation, and to allow each survey from each country to have its own unique intercept. The multivariable model also included survey year and WHO region to explicitly capture temporal and regional trends. This approach helped clarify the contributions of socioeconomic, WASH, and nutritional factors in shaping diarrhoea outcomes. Moreover, the robustness of the study findings was reinforced by sensitivity analyses, which showed consistent results across different imputation strategies and when analyses were restricted to low ROB surveys.
Third, all data were sourced from DHS and MICS surveys, which apply standardised, validated methods and harmonised questionnaires consistently across countries and over time. Both programmes have made deliberate efforts to align indicator definitions and survey instruments, thereby strengthening cross-programme comparability in measuring childhood diarrhoea, water, sanitation, and other child health indicators. This methodological consistency ensures high data quality and minimises measurement error across programmes and survey rounds, with any residual differences likely to be minor and unlikely to meaningfully influence the pooled analyses.
Finally, the study incorporated rigorous assessments of study precision, ROB, and publication bias, all of which confirmed the robustness of the data. Given the structure and governance of the DHS and MICS processes, the likelihood of conventional publication bias is low. This was validated in the present analysis, as the publication bias assessment identified no evidence of selective reporting or withheld reports—representing a unique analytical evaluation of publication bias in DHS and MICS data. This finding reinforces the high quality and reliability of these surveys and reports. Combined with precise prevalence estimates, the absence of other sources of bias, and the application of robust analytical methods, these results strengthen the reliability and generalisability of the study's findings, positioning it as a resource for informing global health policy and guiding evidence-based interventions.
In conclusion, this study provides evidence of a substantial global decline in childhood diarrhoea prevalence over the past four decades, with an estimated annual reduction of 1%—a milestone in global child health for a condition that remains a leading cause of mortality among children under five. This decline may reflect the cumulative impact of different public health interventions. Importantly, the study also demonstrates a strong and consistent association between broader structural determinants—captured by the Socioeconomic and Child Nutrition Index—and a lower burden of diarrhoea in countries with higher levels of development. These findings underscore the importance of a comprehensive strategy that combines targeted health interventions with sustained investments in socioeconomic development, addressing the broader contextual drivers of diarrhoeal disease and fostering long-term gains in child health.
