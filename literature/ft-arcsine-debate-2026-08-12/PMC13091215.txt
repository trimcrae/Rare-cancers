## Summary

## Background
Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) is a re-emerging Aedes-borne virus with documented vertical transmission in pregnancy. Neurological and neurodevelopmental outcomes in neonates and children have important clinical and societal implications, yet evidence remains limited and fragmented. We therefore undertook a quantitative meta-analysis and narrative synthesis on neurological and neurodevelopmental outcomes in neonates and children after maternal chikungunya infection in pregnancy.

## Methods
In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to January 3, 2026, with no language restrictions. We included observational studies reporting neurological outcomes in neonates and/or children after maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy. Prespecified exclusion criteria were duplicate records/publications; non-original reports; non-human studies; and reports without useable outcome data for synthesis. Quantitative outcomes were: (1) the pooled proportion of acute neonatal neurological outcomes among neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection (≤28 days), and (2) the relative risk (RR) of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes (>28 days) in children born to mothers with CHIKV infection compared with children of uninfected mothers. Quantitative outcomes were pooled using random-effects meta-analysis (Freeman–Tukey for proportions; inverse-variance pooling for RRs), with heterogeneity assessed by I2. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist (case series/cross-sectional) and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) (cohort and case–control studies), and certainty of evidence was appraised with Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE). Qualitative synthesis summarized acute neonatal neurologic manifestations, long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes, and neuroimaging/cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings. The protocol was registered in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251120058).

## Findings
The search identified 1080 records; 26 studies met inclusion criteria. All 26 contributed to the qualitative synthesis and 9 entered the quantitative meta-analysis. Among neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection, the pooled proportion with neurological outcomes was 48.0% (95% CI 24.0–73.0; 7 studies; n = 157 neonates; I2 = 88.14%; risk of bias: JBI: 3 high, 4 moderate; GRADE: low) with substantial heterogeneity. In cohort studies, maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy was associated with a higher risk of adverse post-neonatal neurodevelopmental outcomes (RR 1.87, 95% CI 1.30–2.70; 2 studies, n = 264 mother–child pairs/children; I2 = 36.03%; risk of bias: low (NOS 8–9); GRADE: low; predominantly late-pregnancy/peripartum exposure). Qualitative evidence indicated that affected neonates are often asymptomatic at birth but typically develop signs on days 3–7 of life. Severe cases feature encephalopathy, encephalitis/meningoencephalitis, or seizures, and neuroimaging is dominated by white-matter injury, often with splenial diffusion restriction.

## Interpretation
Our findings suggest that vertical CHIKV infection often causes early neonatal neurological manifestations, and maternal infection in late pregnancy or the perinatal period may be associated with a nearly twofold increase in adverse long-term neurodevelopment in offspring. However, these inferences are based on a small, predominantly observational evidence base with heterogeneous outcome definitions and ascertainment, which limits the precision and comparability of pooled estimates. Despite a limited and heterogeneous evidence base, these findings support proactive identification of high-risk peripartum exposure, targeted diagnostic testing and close observation during the first postnatal week, and longitudinal developmental follow-up. From a public-health perspective, they emphasize the importance of mosquito-bite prevention for pregnant women and robust vector control, as well as strengthened newborn monitoring and timely access to early-intervention and family support services in affected settings.

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study
We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from database inception to Jan 3, 2026, without language restrictions, to identify existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses on maternal chikungunya virus infection during pregnancy, vertical transmission, and neurological or neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring. Our search terms included “chikungunya OR CHIKV” AND “pregnancy OR maternal OR gestation OR congenital OR vertical” AND “neurolog OR neurodevelopment OR neurocognit∗ OR developmental delay OR encephalitis OR encephalopathy OR seizure∗ OR convulsion∗” AND “systematic review OR meta-analysis OR review”. We identified a small number of relevant reviews on maternal chikungunya virus infection in pregnancy and perinatal outcomes, including one systematic review with meta-analysis focusing mainly on mother-to-child transmission and perinatal outcomes. Overall, the evidence base was limited and largely descriptive: existing reviews often combined maternal, fetal, and neonatal outcomes and summarized neonatal manifestations. We did not identify a contemporary systematic review or meta-analysis specifically focused on offspring neurological and longer-term neurodevelopmental outcomes after maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy.

## Added value of this study
To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify both the pooled proportion of acute neonatal neurological outcomes among neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection and the relative risk of adverse post-neonatal neurodevelopmental outcomes following maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy. Unlike prior narrative summaries, we prespecified two outcomes domains with distinct time windows, separated disease burden (proportion) from comparative risk (RR), and integrated qualitative evidence on early postnatal presentation and commonly reported neuroimaging/CSF abnormalities in symptomatic infants. We found that neurological outcomes occur in a substantial proportion of neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection, although the evidence is heterogeneous with wide uncertainty, and that maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy may be associated with a higher risk of adverse neurodevelopment, with stronger associations when comparing exposed–infected infants directly with uninfected infants. Qualitative evidence suggests that severe neurodevelopmental sequelae are concentrated in infants with neonatal encephalitis/encephalopathy, whereas first- and second-trimester exposure without confirmed infant infection shows heterogeneous findings.

## Implications of all the available evidence
Taken together, the available evidence indicates that confirmed vertical CHIKV infection frequently entails early neonatal neurological outcomes, and that maternal infection in late pregnancy or around delivery is associated with a higher risk of adverse longer-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. Although the evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous, these findings support proactive identification of high-risk peripartum exposure with targeted confirmatory testing when feasible, monitored under protocol during the first postnatal week (when symptoms commonly emerge), and planned longitudinal developmental follow-up with access to early-intervention and family support services in affected settings. At the public-health level, they also reinforce the importance of pregnancy-focused mosquito-bite prevention and vector control to reduce perinatal infections.

## Introduction
Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) is a re-emerging, arthropod-borne alphavirus (family Togaviridae) transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. In the past two decades, it has spread from focal outbreaks to sustained transmission across the Americas, Africa, and Southeast Asia, with autochthonous clusters also reported in Europe. , , In 2025, outbreaks and autochthonous transmission across multiple regions underscored continued transmission. , , Since the mid-2000s, spread has been facilitated partly by adaptation to A. albopictus (eg, E1-A226V) and the vector's establishment in temperate settings, with climate and mobility factors further increasing suitability. , , , 
Clinically, while CHIKV typically presents with fever, rash, and severe polyarthralgia, increasing evidence indicates that the virus also exhibits significant neurovirulence. Severe neurological complications, predominantly affecting the central nervous system, including encephalopathy and meningoencephalitis, have been reported in a proportion of patients, distinguishing these neuroinvasive forms from the classic rheumatologic presentation. , , This neurotropism is of particular concern in vulnerable populations, setting the stage for the specific risks observed in the perinatal context.
CHIKV is also a concern for maternal and child health. , In addition to maternal health risks, maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy can result in vertical transmission. This occurs primarily during the intrapartum period (reaching a transmission rate of about 50% in viremic mothers), placing neonates at risk of severe acute illness and long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae. , , Among the potential effects on offspring, neurological outcomes are a central, clinically consequential concern. Distinct from the teratogenic mechanisms of Zika virus (ZIKV) which target neural progenitors, CHIKV typically causes acute encephalitis and white matter injury driven by neuroinflammation and infection of glial cells. , , , , Although this acute encephalitic presentation is well-described in outbreak reports, the precise prevalence of neurological outcomes varies across studies. , , The persistence beyond infancy is also uncertain, partly because existing evidence is limited, historically overshadowed by ZIKV, and fragmented across regions and study designs. , , 
Among pregnant women with CHIKV infection, past studies have reported neurological complications spanning from acute neonatal outcome to adverse long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes, affecting both neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection and children following maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy. , , , , , , However, findings are inconsistent. Variations in timing of maternal infection, diagnostic criteria for both CHIKV infection and neurological outcomes, outcome ascertainment, and variable follow-up duration may contribute to substantial heterogeneity. Some studies show increased risks, whereas others report little or no association. , , , Overall, the evidence remains fragmented, and no contemporary synthesis has quantified the burden of offspring neurological outcomes linked to maternal CHIKV infection.
Based on these research gaps, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence to evaluate the neurological sequelae of maternal CHIKV infection. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) estimate the prevalence of acute neonatal neurological outcome among neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection using data from observational studies; (2) quantify the risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in children born to mothers with CHIKV infection compared with children of uninfected mothers based on cohort and case–control studies; and (3) characterize the clinical spectrum of neurological manifestations through a narrative synthesis of all eligible reports, including case series.

## Methods
We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251120058). The protocol is accessible via the PROSPERO record, where one post-registration amendment is also documented.

## Search strategy and selection criteria
Two independent reviewers (LZ and CQ) systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to January 3, 2026, with no language restrictions. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text terms covering three domains: chikungunya virus, pregnancy or maternal infection, and offspring neurological outcomes. Duplicates were removed in EndNote and by manual checking; reference lists of included studies were hand-searched. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in . Study selection was conducted in two steps: First, two reviewers (LZ and CQ) independently screened titles and abstracts of all records retrieved from the searches. Second, the same two reviewers independently assessed the full texts of potentially eligible articles against the prespecified eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (JL) adjudicated. No automation tools were used at any stage of study selection.
We prespecified two outcomes with distinct time windows: the primary outcome was acute neonatal neurological outcomes among neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection (≤28 days; pooled-proportion meta-analysis), and the secondary outcome was neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed beyond the neonatal period (>28 days; relative-effect meta-analysis) among children born to mothers with CHIKV infection during pregnancy compared with children of uninfected mothers.
For quantitative synthesis, we included observational studies or case series (≥5 participants) that (1) documented maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy or around delivery, (2) reported outcomes relevant to at least one prespecified outcome, and (3) provided extractable or derivable data for meta-analysis. For narrative synthesis, we included observational reports (including case reports) describing neonatal/child neurological manifestations following confirmed maternal CHIKV infection.
We excluded (1) duplicate records/publications; (2) reviews, editorials, commentaries, protocols, or letters without original data; (3) non-human studies; and (4) reports without useable outcome data for synthesis (e.g., missing denominators, exposure groups not separable, or insufficient statistics to derive effect estimates).

## Data analysis
We conducted separate syntheses for primary and secondary outcomes. Two reviewers (LZ and CQ) independently extracted data, with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (JL). Extracted data were organized into three domains: (1) study characteristics: first author, year, country/region and study design; (2) participant characteristics: maternal infection status and timing during pregnancy, sample size, offspring age at outcome assessment/follow-up and sex (when reported); and (3) outcomes, including neurological manifestations and the methods used for their assessment. For primary outcome, we extracted the number of neonates with neurological outcomes (events) and the total number of neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection (denominator). For secondary outcome, we extracted adjusted effect estimates (RR/IRR/HR) with 95% CIs for post-neonatal neurodevelopmental outcomes, or derived comparable measures when necessary. When both crude and adjusted estimates were reported, we preferentially used the most fully adjusted estimate; otherwise, crude estimates were used. If the original study did not directly report an overall exposure effect estimate, we derived an unadjusted RR from the published 2 × 2 counts. For the narrative synthesis, we summarized neonatal clinical features and maternal/perinatal factors. Data were extracted using a piloted standardized form; when key data were unclear, we contacted study authors when feasible.
Vertical transmission was defined as mother-to-child transmission of CHIKV, supported by laboratory-confirmed maternal infection and early neonatal laboratory confirmation (typically neonatal RT-PCR/viral RNA detection at birth or within the first week of life and/or CHIKV-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) in neonatal serum/CSF), with onset timing considered when reported. , , Maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy was defined as either laboratory-confirmed (PCR or CHIKV-specific IgM/IgG) or clinically diagnosed according to original study criteria, with both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases included. Perinatal infection was defined in included studies as maternal infection occurring shortly before or during delivery, not including postpartum.
Primary outcome (≤28 days): neonatal neurological outcomes were defined as any acute central or peripheral nervous system manifestation among neonates with confirmed vertical CHIKV infection, including encephalopathy, encephalitis/meningoencephalitis, seizures, focal neurologic deficits, intracranial hemorrhage, or acute neuroimaging abnormalities temporally related to the neonatal episode. , , , , , , , , Non-neurological outcomes (e.g., fever, rash) were not considered. Secondary outcome (>28 days): neurodevelopmental outcomes were defined as adverse developmental/cognitive outcomes assessed beyond the neonatal period using validated developmental instruments (global composite and/or domain-specific scores) and/or clinically diagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders, as defined in the original studies. , , , 
The methodological quality and risk of bias of included studies were assessed using standardized tools. Cohort and case–control studies were evaluated with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which has a maximum of nine points across three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome/exposure. For cross-sectional studies and case series, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Checklist was applied, which considers factors such as clear inclusion criteria, valid measurement of exposure and outcomes, and adequacy of follow-up. Two reviewers (LZ and CQ) independently evaluated study quality, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (JL). In addition, we assessed the certainty of evidence for each outcome using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.
For the meta-analysis of proportions, neurological outcome rates were calculated for each study based on our predefined operational definition. When multiple neonatal neurological outcomes were reported, we extracted the broadest composite as explicitly reported by the study authors. If unavailable, we used a hierarchy of encephalitis/meningoencephalitis > encephalopathy (as labeled by authors) > seizures > acute neuroimaging abnormalities to avoid double counting. To maximize comparability across studies, we did not reclassify isolated non-specific symptoms (e.g., lethargy or feeding refusal) as encephalopathy unless the original report defined/labeled them as such; study-level extraction decisions and justifications are transparently documented in . The choice was based on comprehensiveness and clinical relevance, and alternative outcomes were considered in sensitivity analyses. Proportions were pooled using a random-effects Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine (PFT) transformation (REML τ2; Hartung–Knapp), 0%/100% studies were kept without correction and estimates were inverse-transformed. , Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 and Cochran's Q (χ2) (I2 > 50% or p < 0.05 indicating substantial heterogeneity). Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies (leave-one-out), and subgroup analyses were conducted according to World Health Organization (WHO) region, publication year and symptom onset. Publication bias was evaluated by funnel plots and Egger's test. 
For the relative-effect meta-analysis, RRs with 95% CIs were pooled. Since the baseline risk was low, HRs and IRRs were treated as approximating RRs. , When multiple outcomes were reported within the same study, only one representative outcome was included in the primary analysis. We extracted the global neurodevelopmental composite; if unavailable, the most comprehensive validated measure, using the latest validated follow-up per cohort to avoid double counting. A fixed-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was low, whereas a random-effects model was used in the presence of substantial heterogeneity. We additionally conducted two sensitivity analyses: (1) stratification by infant infection status (exposed–infected [E–I] vs exposed–uninfected [E–U] plus unexposed–uninfected [U–U] pooled) using the same analytic approach as the primary model; and (2) an exploratory analysis alternatively including potentially overlapping publications to assess the impact of duplicate reporting.
Results were tabulated in evidence tables and displayed in forest plots. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.4.2 (packages meta, metafor, and ggplot 2), with two-sided p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results
We identified 1080 records and removed 486 duplicates, leaving 594 for screening; 536 were excluded at title/abstract stage and 57 full texts were assessed. A total of 26 studies were included. All 26 contributed to the qualitative synthesis; 9 of these also entered the quantitative meta-analysis. Reasons for full-text exclusion are shown in and detailed in .

## Proportion of neurological outcomes in neonates with vertically transmitted CHIKV infection
 shows the pooled proportion of neurological outcomes among neonates with vertically transmitted CHIKV infection (GRADE:low; ). The pooled proportion was 48.0% (95% CI 24.0–73.0) under a random-effects model, with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 88.14%, τ2 = 0.35 on the transformed scale; Cochran's Q test, p < 0.001); the 95% prediction interval was 0%–100% on the proportion scale.
 The results of subgroup analyses are shown in . No statistically significant differences were observed between subgroups stratified by WHO region (p = 0.08), publication year (p = 0.11), or symptom onset (p = 0.17). The pooled proportion of neurological outcomes among neonates with vertically transmitted CHIKV infection was 77% in SEAR, 26% in AFR, and 31% in AMR. By publication year, pooled estimates were 70% for studies published ≥2020 and 41% for those <2020. Subgroup analyses showed heterogeneity (I2 = 87.9%–91%). Publication bias was assessed by a funnel plot ( ). The funnel plot did not suggest marked asymmetry, and Egger's test was non-significant (p = 0.34). With k = 7, formal tests are underpowered and these results do not exclude small-study effects. Sensitivity analyses using leave-one-out meta-analysis are shown in . Excluding any single study yielded pooled proportions within the same range as the primary estimate.
 

## Relative risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in children following maternal CHIKV infection
 shows the pooled relative risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes associated with maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy (GRADE:low; ). Compared with children born to uninfected mothers, those exposed to maternal CHIKV infection had a higher risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes (fixed-effects inverse-variance pooled RR = 1.87, 95% CI 1.30–2.70). Heterogeneity was moderate but not statistically significant (I2 = 36.03%; Q (1) = 1.56, p = 0.21). The overall effect test was statistically significant (z = 3.37, p < 0.001). With only two studies, subgroup analyses and publication-bias assessments were not performed.
 In a sensitivity analysis stratified by infant infection status, we compared exposed-infected infants (E-I) with uninfected infants pooled from exposed-uninfected and unexposed controls (E-U + U–U pooled) ( ; ). The pooled estimate was 2.56 (95% CI 1.81–3.63), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0.00%; Q (1) = 0.09, p = 0.76). The overall effect test was statistically significant (z = 5.31, p < 0.001). We further performed an exploratory sensitivity analysis by alternatively including publications with potentially overlapping cohorts to assess the impact of duplicate reporting ( ), and the pooled estimate remained essentially unchanged.

## Qualitative data synthesis
We included 26 qualitative reports covering (i) confirmed neonatal infection after vertical/perinatal transmission and (ii) maternal infection exposure without confirmed neonatal infection ( ). This pathway-based classification and the rationale for each study are provided in . For clarity, we organized the qualitative findings into three domains: (1) acute neonatal neurologic manifestations, (2) long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes ( ), and (3) other relevant studies.
Among neonates with confirmed vertically acquired CHIKV infection, acute neurologic outcome was early and prominent, typically emerging between day 3–7 of life (day of life [DOL] 3–7) (broader DOL 3–10), often around DOL 4 in outbreak cohorts ( ). , , , , The core acute presentations were encephalopathy and seizures, often accompanied by irritability/persistent crying, poor feeding, and fever ( ). Reported seizure frequencies spanned 15.8% (6/38) in Réunion, 46.2% (6/13) in a New Delhi cohort recruited in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) setting, 50% (8/16) in North India, and 66% (4/6) in Mysore. Encephalopathy ranged from 19% (3/16) to 100% (13/13) in a tertiary NICU cohort, and accounted for most severe presentations in outbreak cohorts. A multicountry symptomatic series documented meningoencephalitis in 12/169 (7.1%) neonates. Respiratory instability (including apnea and convulsive apnea) was described in several cohorts and at times required respiratory support. , , In studies obtaining CSF/MRI, abnormalities were frequently documented in infants with acute neurologic signs. , , Cranial ultrasound abnormalities (e.g., diffuse cerebral edema) were also occasionally reported. Several case series with post-neonatal follow-up also reported early tone/milestone abnormalities by 3 months and persistence of neurologic morbidity to 1 year in a subset, including visual impairment/strabismus and ongoing seizures. , 
As shown in , among children with vertically transmitted CHIKV infection, long-term neurodevelopmental morbidity was frequent and persistent: at 21 months, 51% (17/33) had global developmental delay compared with 15% (20/135) of controls (adjusted IRR 2.79). At school age (10 years), 57.9% (11/19) of perinatally infected children had subnormal/abnormal EDA screening across verbal, non-verbal and learning domains, and 61.9% had language disorders. Children who had neonatal encephalopathy were more likely to show ≥1 cognitive/learning difficulty than those without encephalopathy (RR 2.13, 95% CI 1.05–4.33). Notably, neurocognitive developmental delay was documented during follow-up even in infected children who had unremarkable neuroimaging findings. 
Prenatal exposures without confirmed neonatal infection showed heterogeneity. A Grenada cohort (N = 683) followed to 2 years reported no association between exposure and neurodevelopmental delay, and no CNS malformations were reported among CHIKV-exposed infants born to HIV-positive mothers. Placental pathology in high-risk pregnancies included intervillous/perivillous fibrin, cord edema, and chronic villitis/chorioamnionitis, which were observed even in cases with negative placental RT-PCR. Conversely, other case reports confirmed viral RNA in placental tissue associated with fetal distress. In Brazil (infections mostly in the third trimester), domain-specific analyses showed associations across language, motor, and cognitive outcomes, with cognition showing the strongest effect (RR 7.7). A Rio cohort reported largely normal development to 24 months, with an isolated case of postnatal-onset macrocrania (normal head circumference at birth) and two children with nonspecific subependymal cysts on ultrasound, without apparent sequelae. But another Rio cohort identified 19% (7/36) abnormal general movements, 20% (7/35) Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III) delay (vs 3% (2/78) in controls), 23% (8/35) positive autism spectrum disorder (ASD) screening, and white-matter/corpus callosum abnormalities with one cerebral palsy case—signals observed in both infected and exposed-uninfected children. 
Neuroimaging across cohorts indicated predominantly white-matter injury among perinatally infected neonates ( ). In Réunion outbreak cohorts, acute MRI frequently showed cytotoxic/vasogenic edema, with subsequent persistent white-matter injury, gliosis, hypometabolism, and corpus-callosum thinning; postnatal microcephaly (n = 5) and cerebral palsy (n = 4) were reported ( ). A Brazilian perinatal encephalitis series described characteristic corpus callosum/white-matter injury evolving to cavitation and ventriculomegaly, with later corpus callosum thinning on follow-up MRI. In CHIMERE follow-ups, neonatal MRI documented white-matter loss, swelling, and hemorrhages, with later corpus-callosum thinning and centrum semiovale changes at school age. In Rio de Janeiro, MRI abnormalities were present in 5/13 symptomatic infants (e.g., restricted diffusion in corpus callosum/white matter, cystic cavitations) with partial resolution on serial imaging ; in a separate exposed but PCR-negative cohort, two infants had nonspecific subependymal cysts without functional sequelae and head growth was otherwise normal through 24 months. Cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities were reported in acute encephalitic cases, including lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose/protein and occasional CHIKV RT-PCR positivity in CSF. Epilepsy was uncommon on follow-up, and postnatal microcephaly was mainly reported within encephalitic subsets. , , 

## Discussion
Our results show that vertical CHIKV infection is frequently accompanied by early neonatal neurologic outcomes and is associated with higher long-term neurodevelopmental risk in offspring. Among neonates with confirmed vertical infection, the pooled proportion of neurological outcomes was 48%. This estimate is broadly consistent with neurological outcomes reported in prior outbreak-based neonatal series, despite substantial between-study variability. In parallel, maternal CHIKV infection during pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in children (pooled RR = 1.87). This estimate is largely informed by cohorts focusing on third-trimester/peripartum infection. Notably, the risk was most pronounced when comparing exposed-infected infants directly to uninfected infants (RR = 2.56). Affected infants are usually asymptomatic at birth but develop signs on days 3–7. Severe cases feature encephalopathy, encephalitis, or seizures, and neuroimaging often shows white-matter injury with splenial restricted diffusion. Clinical care should prioritize timely identification in this window, close observation during the first postnatal week, and longitudinal developmental follow-up. At the public-health level, the findings support targeted perinatal screening in affected areas, standardized neonatal observation protocols, and investment in accessible early-intervention and family support services to mitigate downstream educational and social burdens. Importantly, these downstream services should be paired with primary prevention during pregnancy (mosquito-bite avoidance and vector control) to reduce the incidence of perinatal infection in the first place.
Our findings support a biologically plausible, time-linked model for peripartum maternal infection leading to vertical transmission and early neonatal neurologic outcomes. For exposure-only cohorts, we interpret the evidence more cautiously, as infant infection is not required and long-term outcomes are heterogeneous. Near-delivery maternal viremia coinciding with the third-trimester acceleration of transplacental IgG transfer creates a brief “mismatch window” in which passive immunity is still insufficient, increasing the likelihood of vertical transmission and clinical onset in the first postnatal week. , , , In late pregnancy placental inflammation, microvascular lesions, and barrier disruption provide routes for transplacental or perinatal exposure. , Newborns are particularly vulnerable because their developing white matter and late oligodendrocyte progenitors are highly sensitive to inflammatory and hypoxic–ischemic injury. This susceptibility is reflected in neuroimaging of perinatal CHIKV cases, which frequently shows white matter injury, such as restricted diffusion in the splenium of the corpus callosum, correlating with clinical presentations of encephalopathy and seizures. , , , However, splenial restricted diffusion is a non-specific neuroimaging pattern reported across multiple infectious and inflammatory conditions and should be interpreted in the clinical context. Notably, CHIKV-associated neonatal encephalitis would be more plausibly linked to secondary (postnatal) microcephaly due to impaired postnatal head growth following brain injury, rather than primary (congenital) microcephaly. , Birth serology may be negative with later seroconversion, supporting serial testing after late-gestation exposure. , Compared with earlier-trimester infection, when maternal viremia has usually resolved by delivery and passive IgG has accumulated, perinatal exposure aligns more consistently with the risk window for early neonatal neuroinflammatory injury and white-matter vulnerability. This alignment may help explain the concentration of neurologic presentations in the first postnatal week, whereas “exposure-only” cohorts show heterogeneous long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. , , , 
Among neonates with vertically acquired CHIKV infection or documented in-utero exposure, CHIKV-associated encephalitis in the neonatal period is linked to poor prognosis, with multi-domain neurodevelopmental impairment (language, motor, cognitive) that may persist into school age. , , Regarding mortality, data availability across the reviewed studies was variable and often incomplete. , However, severe cases can be fatal. At present, there is no CHIKV-specific antiviral for neonates, management remains primarily supportive. , Given the potential for severe neurodevelopmental outcomes, neonates presenting with signs of encephalopathy or seizures should be considered for NICU-level monitoring/admission. NICU admission is described here as a care-level consideration rather than a neurological outcome. In the NICU, care should include frequent neurologic assessment, with consideration of continuous video-EEG (cEEG) when seizures are suspected or encephalopathy is present, given the possibility of electrographic-only/subclinical seizures. , Care should focus on standard neonatal protocols for seizure management, maintenance of electrolyte and fluid balance, and respiratory support as indicated. , For analgesia and fever control, acetaminophen (paracetamol) is preferred. Aspirin and other NSAIDs should be avoided in neonates, particularly until dengue is excluded, due to bleeding risk , 
Regarding prevention, the landscape of prophylaxis is evolving. In the United States, as of Aug 22, 2025 the FDA has suspended the biologics license for the live-attenuated vaccine IXCHIQ (≥18 y). The currently available product is the recombinant virus-like particle (VLP) vaccine, VIMKUNYA, authorized under accelerated approval for individuals ≥12 years. ACIP recommends it for travelers aged ≥12 years at risk. , Prevention should focus on mosquito-bite avoidance and individualized exposure-risk assessment. , Public health implications extend beyond postnatal developmental screening to primary prevention of maternal infection during pregnancy, particularly in outbreak-prone or endemic settings. Prevention should emphasize mosquito-bite avoidance (e.g., repellents, protective clothing, bed nets as appropriate) and community-level Aedes control (e.g., reducing breeding sites through safe water storage and elimination of standing water, environmental sanitation, and outbreak surveillance/rapid response). , , , Given the potential life-course consequences of perinatal infection, vector control and pregnancy-focused risk communication should be prioritized alongside newborn follow-up and early-intervention services. , 
To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis focused on offspring neurological outcomes after maternal chikungunya infection in pregnancy. However, our findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations regarding both the available evidence and the review process. First, the evidence base was small and largely observational. While meta-analyzing such a limited number warrants caution regarding precision and generalizability, we considered this synthesis valuable to provide a preliminary quantitative estimate of the risk magnitude (suggesting a near-doubling of risk), which serves as a critical baseline that narrative synthesis alone might obscure. Second, “neurological outcome” lacked a harmonized definition and outcome ascertainment varied (assessment tools and access to MRI), so our operational definition and the selection of a single representative outcome per study may have introduced classification/selection bias. Third, exposure and outcome misclassification are possible: maternal infection timing was often inferred, neonatal infection may be under-ascertained without serial testing, and some analyses pooled laboratory-confirmed with clinically diagnosed cases. Fourth, regarding the review process, although we searched multiple databases, relevant studies may have been missed due to inconsistent indexing or heterogeneous terminology across settings. Furthermore, individual participant data were unavailable, limiting uniform covariate adjustment, harmonized reclassification of exposure timing, and exploration of effect modifiers. High-quality long-term follow-up evidence remains limited.
Future work should undertake multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up and adopt harmonized outcome definitions. Serial testing and neuroimaging in the early postnatal period should be strengthened by standardizing repeat nucleic-acid testing across the first week of life (especially around DOL 3–7), with follow-up testing beyond day 7 when clinically indicated, and by adopting harmonized imaging triggers/protocols for neonates with neurologic signs. In addition, research priorities should include developing and evaluating safe antivirals for pregnant women and neonates. Individual participant data meta-analyses are also needed to refine risk stratification and identify modifiable targets.
In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis found that among neonates with vertically acquired chikungunya, neurological outcomes are common, and that maternal infection in the late pregnancy or perinatal period is associated with a higher risk of adverse long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring. Qualitative evidence indicates symptom onset on days 3–7, severe illness with encephalopathy, encephalitis, or seizures, and neuroimaging often characterized by white-matter injury, sometimes involving splenial diffusion restriction. These findings support proactive identification of perinatal exposure with serial testing, intensified monitoring during the first postnatal week, and longitudinal developmental follow-up. At the public-health level, the results support perinatal screening in affected areas, standardized neonatal observation, and accessible early-intervention and family support services.
