## ABSTRACT

## Background
Selection of recipient vessels in phalloplasty is challenging due to the absence of adequate vessels in the immediate proximity. There is no consensus on the appropriate vessel selection. We hypothesized that recipient vessel selection in phalloplasty is associated with differences in complications and flap survival.

## Methods
A literature search following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines across Ovid MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, and CENTRAL databases identified 46 articles. Collected data included demographics, surgical details, donor and recipient vessels, use of interpositional grafts, number of venous anastomoses, and outcomes. Statistical analysis followed the random‐effects model.

## Results
One thousand nine hundred and seventy six phalloplasty cases were identified. The deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA) was used in 1413 cases (71.5%) and the femoral artery (FA) was used in 563 cases (28.5%). Pooled proportions of arterial and venous thrombosis were 2.0% and 2.2%, respectively. The use of an interpositional vein graft or arteriovenous loop with the FA was associated with a significantly higher arterial thrombosis rate (16.0%) compared to direct anastomosis to the FA (0.1%, p < 0.001) and to the DIEA (1.2%, p < 0.001). In the absence of vascular thrombosis, partial flap necrosis occurred significantly less often with direct anastomosis to the FA (0.6%) than with the DIEA (6.2%, p = 0.016).

## Conclusions
The choice of recipient vessels is an important consideration in phalloplasty flap survival. The DIEA is associated with a low thrombosis rate. However, the significantly higher proportion of thrombosis observed with the FA may be attributable to the use of an interpositional vein graft or arteriovenous loop. Despite lower thrombosis rates, the use of the DIEA was associated with a higher proportion of partial flap necrosis when vessels were patent.

## Introduction
Phalloplasty is a pivotal gender‐affirming surgery addressing both the functional and esthetic aspects of penile reconstruction, with proven quality of life improvements (Papadopulos et al.  ). There are an estimated 25 million (Winter et al.  ) people worldwide identifying as transgender, including about 1.4 million in the United States, of whom 480,000 (35.9%) (Herman et al.  ) are transgender men. Following the Department of Health and Human Services' 2014 guidance and the 2016 Affordable Care Act policy clarification mandating insurance coverage (Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act  ), the number of gender‐affirming surgeries in the USA increased by over 400% between 2016 and 2021, escalating from 421 to 2224 procedures (Ha et al.  ).
Despite technical refinements in microsurgical penile reconstruction, flap‐related complications, often due to vascular complications, remain high at 25.9% (Falcone et al.  ). Ensuring proper recipient vessel selection is essential for the success of any free flap procedure, yet literature on recipient vessel selection for phalloplasty remains limited. This systematic review aims to consolidate existing research on the recipient vessels utilized during phalloplasty and identify possible correlations between vessel choice and outcomes. We hypothesized that recipient vessel selection in phalloplasty is associated with differences in surgical outcomes, including complications and flap survival.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Literature Search
A systematic review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. On January 10, 2025, Ovid MEDLINE (1946–2025), Embase (1974–2025), and CENTRAL were searched using free text and MeSH terms. Keywords included “phalloplasty” or “penile/penis/phallic reconstruction” combined with “flap,” “vessel,” “artery,” “vein,” “gender affirming,” or “amputation.” Cross‐referencing identified additional studies. Details of the full strategy are provided in the Table  . Records were imported into the web‐based tool Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation). Titles and abstracts were screened by two independent researchers. Reviewer disagreements were resolved by discussion or a third researcher. After the initial screening of titles and abstracts, full‐text review was conducted using predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

2.2.

## Eligibility Criteria
Studies included reported on free flap phalloplasty, described the recipient vessels used during anastomosis, and documented surgical outcomes and complications. Case reports, review articles, abstracts, nonhuman or cadaver studies, studies reporting on partial penile reconstruction, and studies that reported complications in aggregate, without specifying them for each recipient vessel, were excluded. When multiple studies originated from the same institution, study periods, surgical indications, and phalloplasty techniques were compared to assess for potential overlap in patient populations. Both studies were included only when overlap could be reasonably excluded. Otherwise, the study with the most detailed vessel data was used. If inconclusive, preference went to the larger sample size, followed by the most recent publication. Non‐English studies were translated before eligibility assessment.

2.3.

## Quality Assessment
Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series. Evidence levels were assigned per the American Society of Plastic Surgeons criteria: Level III for retrospective studies and Level IV for case series.

2.4.

## Data Extraction and Variables of Interest
The qualitative and quantitative data, included author, year, location, study design, evidence level, sample size, demographics, BMI, surgical indications, comorbidities, concurrent surgeries, staged procedures, flap type and size, donor/recipient vessels, arterial anastomosis technique, number of venous anastomoses (total count of donor‐to‐recipient venous connections; no interpositional grafts were used between donor and recipient veins), complications, and follow‐up. Primary outcomes included anastomosis‐related thrombosis, partial flap necrosis, and total flap necrosis, with further categorization by association with arterial or venous compromise. Partial flap necrosis with patent anastomoses included cases where necrosis occurred without evidence of thrombosis or reported re‐anastomosis. Secondary variables included hematoma, infection, delayed healing, scar contracture, wound dehiscence, and hernia.

2.5.

## Data Analysis
Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (Stata Statistical Software: Release 17). A random‐effects model accounted for inter‐study variability. Complication proportions and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Freeman–Tukey transformation and displayed in forest plots. Heterogeneity was assessed using τ
2, I
2 and H
2, and Cochran's Q (p < 0.05 significant). Subgroup analysis was conducted for recipient artery, anastomosis technique, recipient vein(s), number of venous anastomoses, flap type, and surgical indications. Comparisons involving direct DIEA anastomosis, direct femoral artery (FA) anastomosis, and cases requiring an arteriovenous loop or interpositional vein graft were treated as the clinically prioritized analyses. Comparisons involving individual recipient vessels, number of venous anastomoses, flap type, and surgical indication were considered exploratory, and p‐values for these analyses were interpreted with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess whether significance was driven by any single study. Only findings that remained robust on sensitivity analysis were emphasized in the main Results text, whereas non‐robust findings were retained in the forest plots for descriptive completeness. Cochran's Q test (Qb) assessed between‐group heterogeneity, with pairwise tests for significant findings. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger's test.

3.

## Results
The literature database search yielded 958 records after duplicates were removed. After initial screening of titles and abstracts, 746 articles were excluded, leaving 212 for full‐text review, of which 46 met the inclusion criteria (Figure  ). Using the JBI Case Series critical appraisal tool, five articles achieved a full score. Of the remainder, 16 were deficient in one category, 10 in two, and 15 in three or more (Figure  ; Table  ).
 The studies accounted for 1976 phalloplasty procedures (1585 gender‐affirming; 391 non‐gender‐affirming) with a mean age of 30.2 years (range: 9–74). The most common free flap was the radial forearm (51.0%, 1008 cases), followed by latissimus dorsi (38.7%, 765), fibula (4.2%, 83), scapular (3.7%, 73), anterolateral thigh (1.9%, 38), lateral arm (0.2%, 4), groin (0.2%, 3), and deltoid (0.1%, 2). The tube‐within‐a‐tube technique was the most frequent design (50.8%), followed by urethroplasty inlay (23.2%), shaft only (17.9%), neourethral prefabrication (4.3%), and skin graft neourethra (1.2%). The most common recipient artery was the deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA) (71.5%, 1413 cases), followed by the superficial FA (9.2%, 181), common FA (7.8%, 154), circumflex FA (1.4%, 28), deep FA (0.8%, 15), and an unspecified branch of the FA (9.4%, 185) (Figure  ).
 Direct donor–recipient arterial anastomosis was performed in all 1413 DIEA cases (71.5%) and 410 of the FA cases (20.7%). FA refers to the main vessel or its branches when a branch is not specified. In 153 cases (7.7%), either an arteriovenous loop (2.8%, 55 cases) or an interpositional vein graft (5.0%, 98) was used. All arteriovenous loop cases were used with RFFFs. Interpositional vein grafts were used with the RFFFs (3.1%, 62), fibula flaps (1.7%, 33), and groin flaps (0.2%, 3). Vein grafts were sourced from the distal GSV (2.3%, 45), an unspecified GSV segment (1.6%, 31), or an unspecified vein (1.1%, 22). The interpositional vein graft connected the donor artery to either the common FA (3.0%, 60), superficial FA (1.7%, 33), or deep FA (0.3%, 5) (Table  ).
 The most common transposed recipient vein was the deep inferior epigastric vein (DIEV) and GSV combination (35.6%, 704 cases), followed by the DIEV alone (34.6%, 683), the GSV alone (26.6%, 526), the circumflex femoral vein (1.3%, 26), and unspecified vein(s) or other combination (1.9%, 37). The median number of venous anastomoses per flap was 2.0 (IQR: 1.0–2.0). There were no instances where an interpositional vein graft was used between recipient and donor veins (Table  ).
 Egger's test identified small‐study effects in arterial thrombosis (p = 0.032), partial flap necrosis associated with arterial compromise (p < 0.001), partial flap necrosis associated with venous compromise (p < 0.001), total flap necrosis associated with arterial compromise (p < 0.001), and total flap necrosis associated with venous compromise (p = 0.006). No significant small‐study effects were observed in other subgroups. Subsequent analysis of funnel plots showed asymmetry in the significant subgroups and milder asymmetry in the nonsignificant subgroups (Figure  ).
3.1.

## Arterial Thrombosis
Arterial thrombosis was observed in 45 cases, with a pooled proportion of 2.0% (CI: [0.5%–4.0%]; I
2 = 42.4%; p < 0.001). Of these, 33 (73.3%) were salvaged by anastomotic revision without necrosis, 5 (11.1%) were salvaged by anastomotic revision with partial necrosis, 4 (8.9%) experienced complete flap necrosis, and 3 (6.7%) lacked re‐anastomosis details. After sensitivity analysis, heterogeneity remained significant for arterial anastomosis technique (p < 0.001). The use of an interpositional vein graft or arteriovenous loop with the FA was associated with a significantly higher occurrence of thrombosis at 16.0% compared to 0.1% for the FA with direct anastomosis (p < 0.001) and 1.2% for the DIEA with direct anastomosis (p < 0.001) (Figure  ).
 
3.2.

## Venous Thrombosis
Venous thrombosis occurred in 38 cases, with a pooled proportion of 2.2% (CI: [1.1%–3.6%]; I
2 = 7.2%; p = 1.00). Of these, 24 (63.2%) were salvaged by anastomotic revision without necrosis, 3 (7.9%) were salvaged by anastomotic revision with partial necrosis, 10 (26.3%) experienced complete flap necrosis, and 1 (2.6%) recovered with heparin, antiplatelets, and leech therapy (Figure  ).

3.3.

## Partial Flap Necrosis
Partial flap necrosis occurred in 110 cases, with a pooled proportion of 6.7% (CI: [4.3%–9.5%]; I
2 = 58.9%; p < 0.001). Of these, 5 (4.5%) were associated with arterial compromise, 9 (8.2%) were associated with venous compromise, 50 (45.5%) occurred with patent vessels, and 46 (41.8%) were unspecified.

Arterial: Partial flap necrosis associated with arterial thrombosis occurred in 5 cases, with a pooled proportion of < 0.1% (CI: [0.0%–0.3%]; I
2 = 0.26%; p = 1.00). Of the 5 cases, two used an interpositional vein graft with the FA, one used an arteriovenous loop, and two used either method (unspecified). No cases with direct arterial anastomosis developed partial flap necrosis associated with thrombosis. Subgroup analysis showed the use of an interpositional vein graft with the FA was associated with a significantly higher occurrence of partial flap necrosis associated with arterial compromise at 3.9% compared to 0.0% for the DIEA with direct anastomosis (p = 0.008) (Figure  ).
 
Venous: Partial flap necrosis occurred in 9 cases, with a pooled proportion of 0.2% (CI: [0.0%–1.1%]; I
2 = 16.5%; p = 0.95). Three were associated with thrombosis, 3 with hematoma‐induced congestion, and 3 with ischemia/congestion of unknown origin. Six cases required debridement and/or skin grafting, while the three cases of hematoma‐induced venous congestion lacked follow‐up care details, leaving it unknown whether further treatment was required. No significant subgroup heterogeneity was observed (Figure  ).

Patent vessels: Partial flap necrosis occurred in 50 cases with patent anastomoses, with a pooled proportion of 4.2% (CI: [1.9%–6.9%]; I2=43.5%; p = 0.010). Of these, 20 (40.0%) involved the distal neophallus, 13 (26.0%) the proximal and distal third of the ventral aspect, and 17 (34.0%) did not provide details. Operative revision was necessary in 29 cases (58.0%): 16 skin grafts, 1 shortening of the neophallus, and 12 unspecified operative revisions were performed. Healing by secondary intention occurred in 17 cases (34.0%), and 4 cases (8.0%) did not report follow‐up care. After sensitivity analysis, heterogeneity remained significant for arterial anastomosis technique (p = 0.023) and number of venous anastomoses (p = 0.001). A patent FA with direct anastomosis demonstrated significantly lower partial flap necrosis at 0.6% compared to 6.2% for a patent DIEA (p = 0.016). The use of more than two venous anastomoses was associated with significantly higher proportions of partial flap necrosis at 13.6% compared to the use of a single venous anastomosis at 0.5% (p = 0.002) (Figure  ; Table  ).

3.4.

## Total Flap Necrosis
Total flap necrosis was observed in 39 cases, with a pooled proportion of 1.5% (CI: [0.7%–2.4%]; I
2 = 11.4%; p = 0.96). Reported associations were arterial thrombosis in 3 cases (7.7%), venous thrombosis in 9 (23.1%), combined arterial/venous thrombosis in 1 (2.6%), venous congestion from prolonged sitting in 1 (2.6%), and unspecified in 25 (64.1%) (Figure  ).

3.5.

## Hematoma, Infection, and Other Complications
Hematoma occurred in 88 cases, representing a pooled proportion of 5.5% (CI: [2.8%–8.9%]; I
2 = 68.3%; p < 0.001). Infection occurred in 72 cases, representing a pooled prevalence of 4.5% (CI: [2.5%–6.9%]; I
2 = 51.7%; p < 0.001). Delayed wound healing occurred in 63 cases (4.7%), scar contracture in 35 (2.7%), and wound dehiscence in 13 (0.9%). In three cases using the deep inferior epigastric vessels (0.2%), damage to the rectus abdominis sheath during surgery resulted in an abdominal hernia (Figure  ; Table  ).

## Discussion
Phalloplasty aims to reconstruct a functional and esthetic penis. The selection of the appropriate recipient vessels in phalloplasty is an important consideration in minimizing complications. Yet, there is a scarcity of detailed literature explaining the rationale for selection of a specific recipient vessel (Danker et al.  ; Smart et al.  ). This study presents a detailed systematic review to examine recipient vessels documented in the literature and analyze whether vessel selection is associated with differences in outcomes.
The primary choices for the recipient artery in phalloplasty are the DIEA and FA. In our review, 1413 cases (71.5%) utilized the DIEA, while 563 (28.5%) used the FA. One challenging aspect of the deep inferior epigastric vessels is anatomical variability (Al‐Talalwah  ; Rozen et al.  ), though Smart et al. reported this usually occurs distal to the anastomosis site (Smart et al.  ). Another concern is the potential for abdominal wall or inguinal hernia formation, as the procedure often requires an abdominal wall myo‐fasciotomy or exteriorization of the vessels through the external inguinal ring (Danker et al.  ; Yuan et al.  ) (Figure  ). Our systematic review identified three reported cases of abdominal herniation due to damage to the rectus abdominis sheath during surgery, representing 0.2% of DIEA cases. Despite these challenges, the DIEA is most frequently used due to adequate caliber match for end to end anastomosis, ease of access, and adequate inflow from the external iliac artery (Danker et al.  ; Li et al.  ). Literature reports a mean DIEA diameter of 2.4 ± 0.3 mm, which closely approximates the caliber of the radial artery of the radial forearm flap (2.3 ± 0.5 mm) and the arterial pedicle of the ALT flap (2.51 ± 0.52 mm) (Seong and Woo  ; Shima et al.  ; Hung et al.  ).
 Our review found no DIEA cases requiring an interpositional vein graft. Cases using the DIEA reported a significantly lower proportion of arterial thrombosis at 1.2% compared to the FA with an interpositional vein graft or arteriovenous loop at 16.0% (p < 0.001). While there was no significant difference in total flap necrosis between the FA and DIEA, partial flap necrosis with a patent vessel was significantly higher when using the DIEA (6.2%) compared to direct anastomosis with the FA (0.6%) (p = 0.016). Although the exact cause of partial flap necrosis cannot always be determined, prior research (Danker et al.  ) suggests flap size exceeding the vascular capacity of the donor artery could be a contributing factor. This suggests that the stronger inflow of the FA may be associated with lower observed rates of partial flap necrosis (Zhang et al.  ).
The FA was the second most commonly used artery. Its advantages include consistent anatomy, robust blood inflow (Doornaert et al.  ), and the ability to expose the superficial inferior epigastric vein, superficial circumflex iliac vein, and GSV through a single incision (Li et al.  ). A technically challenging end‐to‐side anastomosis is needed with the superficial or common FA, whereas an end to end anastomosis can be performed with smaller femoral branches. Anatomic studies report a mean common FA diameter of 6.2 ± 1.2 mm (Ahn et al.  ). When direct connection is not feasible, an arteriovenous loop with the proximal GSV or an interposition vein graft is necessary, increasing technical difficulty and complication risk (Jain et al.  ; Khouri et al.  ). In our review, 410 cases (20.7%) used direct anastomosis to the FA, 98 cases (5.0%) used an interpositional vein graft, and 55 cases (2.8%) used an arteriovenous loop. Interpositional vein grafts were predominantly used with the RFFF (3.1%, 62 cases) and fibula free flap (1.7%, 33). Among the 325 RFFF cases revascularized through the FA, 62 (19.1%) incorporated a vein graft and 55 (16.9%) an arteriovenous loop. Although vein graft use is dependent on patient‐specific factors, Song et al. ( ) noted that the pedicle length of the radial vessels is often insufficient to reach the femoral vessels. When vein grafts were necessary, Monstrey et al. ( ) reported that the use of an arteriovenous loop between the greater saphenous vein and common FA frequently resulted in size mismatch, leading to micro‐turbulence. They recommended using the distal great saphenous vein graft instead. In fibula free flaps, vein grafts were used in 39.8% of cases. Among cases requiring an interpositional vein graft, arterial thrombosis was reported only in RFFF cases, whereas no thrombosis was reported in fibula flap cases. (Sadove et al.  ) after initially using a graft, later found fibula pedicle length generally sufficient for direct end‐to‐side FA anastomosis. Although high central arterial pressure and interposed grafts can raise the risk of blowouts and pseudoaneurysms (Koza and Kaya  ), none were reported, nor were FA thrombosis, inguinal lymphatic injury, or femoral nerve injury.
Use of an interpositional vein graft or arteriovenous loop was associated with a significantly higher proportion of arterial thrombosis at 16.0% versus 0.1% for the FA with direct anastomosis (p < 0.001). This suggests that differences in thrombosis rates may reflect vein graft or arteriovenous loop use more than the recipient artery itself. Matti et al. ( ) observed high thrombosis rates (60%, 3 out of 5 patients) linked to interpositional vein grafts between the femoral and donor artery. Similarly, Gilbert et al. ( ) noted thrombosis in all three FA cases using a vein graft, but none with the DIEA (without graft). Other branches of the femoral (lateral and medial femoral circumflex) have been used as recipient vessels, but their deep location complicates dissection (Cheng et al.  ). Our analysis found the circumflex FA had the highest hematoma rate at 16.7%, though not significantly different from other arteries. Table  summarizes the reported associations of using the DIEA versus the FA.
 Selection of transposed recipient veins in phalloplasty varies widely. In our review, the median number of venous anastomoses was 2.0 (range: 1–5). The DIEV and GSV combination was used in 704 cases (35.6%), DIEV alone in 683 cases (34.6%), and GSV alone in 526 cases (26.6%). DIEV is the only locoregional vein not crossing the groin crease, reducing compression risk while sitting, though prolonged sitting is discouraged with any recipient vein (Danker et al.  ). Li et al. reported flap necrosis when DIEV was compressed during prolonged postsurgical sitting (Li et al.  ). Anatomic studies report a mean DIEV diameter of 2.9 ± 0.3 mm, while the great saphenous vein has been reported to range from 2.3 to 4.4 mm in diameter (Seong and Woo  ; Spivack et al.  ). The DIEV commonly consists of one large‐caliber vessel and one smaller vessel, allowing for dual venous outflow. Furthermore, flap supercharging, with additional venous anastomoses, has been suggested to reduce complications (Chang et al.  ). Interestingly, we report a greater proportion of partial flap necrosis with patent vessels associated with more than two venous anastomoses (13.6%) compared to one (0.5%, p = 0.002), which may reflect reduced blood velocity (Hanasono et al.  ). Phalloplasty free flaps are particularly sensitive to venous outflow issues due to their tubular structure, further complicated by postoperative swelling (Danker et al.  ; Li et al.  ; Yuan et al.  ; Smart et al.  ; De Wolf et al.  ).
The limitations of this study include heterogeneity among the included studies attributable to differences in preoperative factors, surgical design, and intraoperative technique. In addition, definitions and assessment criteria for outcomes such as partial flap necrosis and thrombosis, while part of common surgical knowledge and practice, were not standardized across studies. Interpretation is further influenced by the learning curve, as complication rates decrease with surgical experience, suggesting that early reports may overstate risk (Danker et al.  ; Wirthmann et al.  ). The ability to account for learning‐curve effects across the decades spanned by the included studies was limited. Additionally, several studies had small sample sizes, which reduce statistical power and generalizability. Some subgroup comparisons were based on limited numbers of events and should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly where confidence intervals were wide or findings were not robust to sensitivity analysis. Important operative details such as the rationale behind recipient vessel selection, vessel caliber match, and perforator characteristics in perforator‐based flaps were inconsistently reported or omitted entirely, which limited the extent to which these variables could be compared. Microsurgical factors known to influence anastomotic success, such as use of vein grafts and overall flap size, were also rarely documented in a standardized manner. These gaps in primary reporting may contribute to variation in outcomes across studies. Accordingly, pooled proportions and subgroup comparisons should be interpreted as associative rather than causal (Karamitros et al.  ). Observed differences by recipient vessel or technique do not establish that vessel selection itself caused differences in thrombosis or flap necrosis and may instead reflect confounding by surgeon experience, flap size and design, patient selection, case complexity, institutional practice patterns, and selective outcome reporting. In addition, because outcomes were synthesized from aggregate study‐level data rather than patient‐level data, this review is subject to ecological fallacy (Schwartz  ). Observed associations between recipient vessel choice, vein graft use, and complications may not reflect individual‐level relationships, particularly given nonrandom surgeon decision‐making.

5.

## Conclusions
The choice of recipient vessels is an important consideration in phalloplasty success. Current literature indicates the deep inferior epigastric vessels are associated with a low proportion of vascular complications and thrombosis. However, the higher proportion of arterial thrombosis seen with the FA appears more strongly associated with interpositional vein graft or arteriovenous loop use than with the recipient artery itself. Despite lower thrombosis rates, deep inferior epigastric vessels were associated with a higher proportion of partial flap necrosis when vessels remained patent.
