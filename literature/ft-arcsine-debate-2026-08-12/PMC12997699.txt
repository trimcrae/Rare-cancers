## Abstract

## Background
Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain a significant public health challenge in Ghana, with varying prevalence across regions and populations. A nationwide synthesis of STI burden and risk factors is lacking, hindering targeted interventions. This systematic review and meta-analysis examined STI prevalence, risk factors, and distribution patterns across the country, providing data to support SDG 3 and inform effective public health interventions.

## Methods
We conducted literature searches using the PubMed repository and three databases: Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. Eligible studies were observational studies that reported STI prevalence based on clinical diagnosis across Ghana. The quality of the included studies was assessed using standardized JBI checklists for prevalence studies. Random-effects meta-analysis with a 95% confidence interval was performed to estimate pooled prevalence and conduct subgroup analyses.

## Results
The pooled STI prevalence was 7.5%. Urban areas exhibited the highest prevalence (9.06%) compared to rural areas (6.67%), likely reflecting differences in healthcare access, awareness, and risk behavior. Risk factors included multiple sexual partners, homosexuality, and needle sharing. Pathogen-specific analysis revealed higher prevalence rates for viral infections (8.03%) than bacterial infections (6.01%). Co-infections of both bacterial and viral infections had a prevalence of 10.88%. 

## Conclusions
STIs pose a substantial burden in Ghana, particularly among urban populations and high-risk groups. Key public health strategies – including outreach to high-risk groups, free preventive commodities, health systems strengthening, risk behavior education, and improved diagnostic approaches – are critical to addressing Ghana’s diverse STI burden and reducing prevalence.

## Introduction
Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain a critical public health concern, even amid ongoing pandemics and outbreaks of other life-threatening diseases. STIs are caused by more than 30 different microorganisms including, bacteria, viruses, and parasites, and are primarily transmitted through unprotected sexual contact [ ]. The global burden of STIs is mainly due to eight pathogens: four curable bacterial and parasitic infections (syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, trichomoniasis) and four incurable viral pathogens- hepatitis B, herpes simplex virus (HSV), Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and Human papillomavirus (HPV) [ ]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), over a million people globally are infected daily with curable STIs, reflecting the immense scale of this public health issue [ ]. These infections disproportionately affect sexually active young people, who are often asymptomatic, facilitating rapid transmission [ ]. Biological vulnerability, high-risk behaviors, and barriers to healthcare access increase their susceptibility [ , ], while social factors such as peer influence, misinformation, and power imbalances further amplify the risk [ ]. Infertility, chronic liver disease, ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease, and cervical cancer are a few of the myriad health problems associated with STIs [ ]. In addition to health implications, STIs impose a substantial economic burden. In 2018, the United States faced an estimated $15.9 billion (USD) in medical expenses related to STI cases [ ]. However, a more recent study suggests that the actual cost may be even higher, as the initial estimate does not account for additional factors such as prevention efforts [ ]. Meanwhile, the WHO, in its Global Health Sector Strategy (GHSS), projects that the total cost of HIV will rise from approximately $23.7 billion (USD) in 2022 to just over $27.9 billion (USD) in 2025, before gradually declining to $26.5 billion (USD) in 2030 [ ]. The financial strain is even more severe in low-income countries, where limited resources amplify the impact on households and health systems [ ]. Regions such as South and Southeast Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and the Caribbean report the highest proportion of STIs. In Africa alone, an estimated 109.7 million individuals are currently affected by STIs, contributing to a significant 17% economic loss for the continent [ ].
In Ghana, STIs pose a significant public health burden. A recent report indicates that as of 2022, the prevalence rates among individuals aged 15–19 were 7.1% for males and 12.5% for females [ ]. These rates are shaped by high-risk behaviors, including multiple sexual partnerships, with 2% of women and 7% of men aged 15–49 reporting having multiple sexual partners [ ]. Additionally, socio-cultural norms, low contraception use, and unsafe practices among high-risk groups further contribute to the overall disease burden [ ]. Limited access to healthcare services further exacerbates the situation, as many individuals face barriers such as financial constraints, inadequate facilities, and a lack of trained healthcare providers. These factors create a complex web of challenges that not only increase the spread of STIs but also hinder efforts to prevent and effectively treat them.
Although Ghana has established surveillance centers and sentinel sites to monitor STIs, comprehensive data on the prevalence of these infections remains scarce. Most available information comes from individual research studies, which are often limited in scope, relying on small sample sizes and region-specific assessments [ – ]. These studies primarily examine the social determinants of STIs, alongside knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to these infections [ – ]. Additionally, there is a lack of comprehensive data on biological testing and the prevalence of specific STI pathogens. This is due to challenges such as difficulties in sample collection and the reluctance of individuals to participate, often influenced by stigma and social repercussions [ ].
Given these gaps, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to provide a comprehensive overview of STI prevalence in Ghana, focusing on individual etiologies and the distribution across various settings. By synthesizing existing evidence, this study contributes to broader global health efforts aligned with Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3), which seeks to ensure good health and well-being for all [ ]. Addressing STI prevalence is critical to achieving SDG 3, as these infections pose significant reproductive health risks and exacerbate vulnerabilities to other diseases [ ]. Strengthening surveillance systems, improving healthcare access, and implementing targeted prevention strategies will not only fill existing data gaps but also support public health interventions that advance the goal of universal health coverage and disease prevention in Ghana.

## Method
The systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [ ]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO with registration ID (CRD42024608995). The PRISMA checklist is included in the Supplementary Materials.

## Search strategy
The literature search was performed from 1 to 15 November 2024. The search included studies published in English and conducted within Ghana. The search strategy was designed to include studies published from the inception of the databases to 15 November 2024. Original research articles including observational, cohort, or case-control studies, describing the proportion of cases of STI and their risk factors were identified from PubMed, and three other databases (Scopus, Web of Science, and Science Direct). The search was conducted using specific keywords: terms within the same concept were connected with the Boolean operator “OR” and combined with other components of the search terms using “AND”. The final search terms for the databases were: 
PubMed:(“sexually transmitted infections” OR “sexually transmitted diseases” OR “STI” OR “STD”) AND “Ghana”.
Scopus: (“sexually transmitted infections” OR “sexually transmitted diseases” OR “STI” OR “STD”) AND “Ghana”.
Web of Science: (“sexually transmitted infections” OR “sexually transmitted diseases” OR “STI” OR “STD”) AND “Ghana”.
ScienceDirect: (“sexually transmitted infections” OR “sexually transmitted diseases” OR “STI” OR “STD”) AND “Ghana”.
The reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also manually searched to identify additional publications not indexed in the databases. All publications from the databases were downloaded and imported into the Zotero reference manager (version 6.0.36), where they were merged into a single collection. The merged file yielded 1839 articles that were later imported into the Rayyan online platform (Rayyan-Intelligent Systematic Review, available at www.rayyan.ai, accessed on 16 November 2024) and deduplicated before the study selection process.

## Study selection process
Prior to the screening, two reviewers (F.K. and A.A.D.) designed a screening protocol with instructions that clearly defined characteristics that qualified a study for inclusion or exclusion. The reviewers independently screened all deduplicated records in three stages - title, abstract, and full text, using the Rayyan online platform to assess eligibility based on pre-defined criteria. References were coded as “Included”, “Maybe” or “Excluded”. Records whose titles and abstracts did not align with the study’s objectives were excluded during the first two stages. Full texts of studies labeled as “Included” or “Maybe” progressed to the next screening stage. A calibration exercise was performed among reviewers to ensure consistency in applying inclusion/exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were exported to the Zotero reference manager (version 6.0.36) and assessed independently against eligibility criteria by the two reviewers. Reasons for final exclusion at this stage were recorded. Any discrepancies in study selection were discussed with a third reviewer until a consensus was reached. All included studies proceeded to the data extraction stage.

## Eligibility criteria

## Inclusion criteria

Study design: Cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control studies reporting original data.
Participants: Studies conducted among the general population or specific subgroups (e.g., children, communities, etc.) in Ghana.
Outcome: Studies reporting prevalence and specific etiology of sexually transmitted infections.
Diagnosis: Studies that diagnose sexually transmitted infections (STIs) using appropriate microbiological, serological, or molecular techniques specific to the pathogen of interest (e.g., bacterial culture, nucleic acid amplification tests, or serological assays).
Language: Studies published in English.
Date: Studies published from the inception of each database to November 15, 2024.

## Exclusion criteria

Study design: Reviews, case reports, editorials, abstracts without full text.
Participants: Studies conducted outside Ghana, studies not involving human participants, and studies that include Ghana but do not provide Ghana-exclusive data.
Outcome: Studies reporting outcomes unrelated to STI prevalence (e.g., contraception use pattern, outbreak investigations, or people’s knowledge and behavior towards STIs).
Diagnosis: Studies that report STI symptoms without confirmatory diagnostic tests or do not distinguish specific sexually transmitted infections from other conditions.
Language: Non-English full-text publications.

## Data extraction
A standardized data extraction form was developed in Microsoft Excel 2019 (Table  ) and piloted on three randomly selected studies before full-scale data extraction. The following key information was extracted from each eligible study:
Bibliographic details: Author(s), publication year.
Study characteristics: Location, setting (urban/rural), period, design.
Population characteristics: Sample size, age groups, sex.
Outcomes of interest: Reported STI prevalence, specific etiologies, and risk factors for STI transmission.
 Two reviewers extracted the data, which was checked by a third reviewer. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. The extracted information was synthesized qualitatively due to heterogeneity in reported outcomes. This process ensured all relevant information was captured uniformly across studies for subsequent analysis.

## Statistical analysis
Meta-analysis was performed using R Studio version 4.3.3. The software was used to estimate the proportion of STI etiologies reported. The funnel plot and Egger’s regression test assessed publication bias visually and statistically (Supplemental Fig. 1). The Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to stabilize variances among studies, and the pooled prevalence estimates were computed using the DerSimonian-Laird method. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated using I2 statistic with values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and ≥ 75% representing no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. The proportion of STIs was measured by random-effects meta-analysis with a confidence interval of 95%. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and meta-regression were performed to assess the robustness and sources of heterogeneity in our study, respectively. Two covariates, type of infection and study setting, were used in the meta-regression model. An alpha value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Quality assessment
Two reviewers independently used the standardized Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence studies to methodologically assess the quality of the included studies [ ]. The checklist comprised tools that included: the appropriateness of the sample frame, appropriate recruitment of study participants, adequacy of sample size, detailed description of study subjects and setting, comprehensiveness of data analysis to cover the entire sample, validity of diagnostic methods, reliability of standards to measure condition, the availability of appropriate statistical analysis, and the adequacy of response rate. Each checklist item was either scored “Yes”, “No”, “Unclear” or “Not Applicable”. A “Yes” response to each question was scored 1, while “Unclear”, “No” and “Not Applicable” were scored 0. All included studies were subjected to each of these tools and the quality of the study (risk of bias) was determined as either “Good” (low-risk; score = 7–9), “Fair” (moderate risk; score = 4–6), or “Poor” (high-risk; score = 0–3). Disagreements were discussed until a consensus was met, ensuring that the final quality analysis reflected a comprehensive understanding of the included studies’ methodologies, findings, and potential biases. The general quality of the included papers was high with the majority (20) of studies scoring “good” quality (achieving 7–9 points), and the rest (10) rated “fair” quality with scores of 5–6 points (Supplemental Table 1).

## Results

## Search results
The search of the databases yielded a total of 1839 articles, with 241 of them being duplicates. After resolving the duplicates, 1693 non-duplicate articles were subjected to further evaluation. Of the 1693 articles, 1637 were excluded based on title and abstract screening. The remaining 56 included articles were then subjected to a detailed full-text review. After full-text evaluation, 26/56 articles were excluded for various reasons (study inaccessible (6), STI not tested/specified (13), data not segregated (2), population used by included research (3), population/sample size not stated (2), leaving 30 eligible articles (Fig.  ).
 

## Characteristics of the included studies
The included studies [ – , – ] published between 1985 and 2024 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The studies had sample sizes ranging from 96 to 91,386. Geographically, the studies were distributed across the country, with some covering all regions, and others spanning selected regions. The areas most studied were the Ashanti, Greater Accra, and Volta regions with (9,11, and 8) studies, respectively. Only one study did not specify the region where it was conducted. Of the 30 included studies, 13 were conducted in urban areas, 8 in rural areas, and 7 in both urban and rural areas; the remaining 2 studies did not specify the type of area where they were conducted (Table  ). Hospitalized patients were the most studied population (7 studies), followed by pregnant women (5 studies), and high-risk populations (4 studies). The data from each study were separated by infection type to account for each STI individually and grouped according to the type of microbial pathogen. A total of 190,173 participants were tested for various STIs across the studies, and 8,857 were confirmed positive. The most frequently reported STI were HIV infection, Syphilis, and Chlamydia, with 10 studies each, while HPV, M. genitalium, M. hominis, and U. parvum infections were the least reported with one study each. Additionally, coinfection was reported in 6 studies (Table  ). Risk factors associated with STIs were reported in 13 of the 30 studies (43%), with multiple sexual partners identified as the most common risk factor in 6 of these studies.
 

## Pooled prevalence of STIs
The pooled prevalence of STIs based on the included studies was 7.50% (95% CI: 6.27–8.82) (Fig.  ). Due to the significant heterogeneity observed across the included studies (I2 = 98.9%), a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis.
 

## Prevalence of STIs based on the microbial pathogen type
The subgroup analysis of the prevalence of STIs based on the microbial pathogen type is shown in Fig.  . Parasitic infections recorded the highest prevalence at 14.19% (95% CI: 3.82–29.47). Viral infections were more prevalent at 8.03% (95% CI: 5.30–11.24) than bacterial infections at 6.01% (95% CI: 4.69–7.47). Co-infections of both bacterial and viral infections recorded a prevalence of 10.88% (95% CI: 0.00–52.95)
 

## Prevalence of STIs based on geographical setting
 A subgroup analysis based on the type of setting of the included studies is presented in Fig.  . The prevalence of STIs in urban settings was relatively high, at 9.06% (95% CI: 6.68–11.75), compared to that in rural settings, at 6.67% (95% CI: 3.11–11.37). Studies conducted in both rural and urban settings recorded a prevalence of 7.17% (95% CI: 4.43–10.49). The lowest prevalence, 3.67% (95% CI: 1.37–7.01), was recorded in two studies that did not specify the type of setting in which they were conducted.
 

## Meta-regression
 Our meta-regression identified HPV, Mycoplasma hominis, and Trichomonas vaginalis as significant positive predictors of STI prevalence (p-values < 0.05). Studies conducted in urban settings were also significantly associated with a higher pooled prevalence (p = 0.0371). Hepatitis B exhibited a positive effect; however, this was not statistically significant (p = 0.0706). Gonorrhea, Chlamydia, and HIV infection had no significant effect on STI prevalence (p-values > 0.05). These findings highlight the contributions of specific pathogens and urban settings to the heterogeneity in STI prevalence (Table  ).
 

## Sensitivity analysis
A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of each included study on the pooled prevalence of STIs in the meta-analysis. The point estimates ranged from 7.11 to 7.66%, and the confidence intervals showed an overlap. These overlapping confidence intervals indicate there is an insignificant difference in the point estimates. Despite the observed variability in point estimates, our study selection can be considered robust since individual prevalence rates were consistently clustered around the reported pooled prevalence of 7.50% (Supplemental Table 22).

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis provide an overview of the prevalence and distribution of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in Ghana. Thirty studies conducted in Ghana with 190,1733 participants were analyzed to determine the pooled prevalence of STI, and its associated risk factors. The studies covered the entire country, with some focusing on individual regions while others covered multiple regions. The Ashanti, Greater Accra, and Volta regions were the most studied, contributing 11, 9, and 8 studies, respectively, indicating a potential focus on areas with larger populations or better research infrastructure. Most of the studies were conducted in urban areas (13 studies), followed by rural areas (8 studies), and those conducted in both urban and rural areas (7 studies). The relatively even distribution of studies across different settings provides a comprehensive view of STI prevalence nationwide. However, this distribution may reflect differences in STI prevalence between the urban and rural settings due to varying access to healthcare, awareness levels, and exposure to risk factors.
The populations studied varied, with hospitalized patients being the most represented group (7 studies), followed by pregnant women (5 studies). This high representation may stem from the increased likelihood of these individuals undergoing testing or screening. In the case of pregnant women, routine screening for various STIs is conducted during pregnancy as part of efforts to prevent mother-child disease transmission [ ]. Additionally, the studies included high-risk populations such as sex workers and men who have sex with men (MSM), who significantly shape STI epidemiology in the country [ ]. Positive STI results among blood donors require significant attention, as these individuals could serve as major sources of STI transmission [ ]. This variability underscores the importance of tailoring public health interventions to the unique needs of different population groups.
Risk factors associated with STIs were reported in 13 of the 30 studies (43%). Among these, having multiple sexual partners was the most identified risk factor, reported in 6 of the 13 studies. This finding aligns with established evidence that high-risk sexual behaviors contribute significantly to STI transmission and prevalence [ ]. Other reported risk factors associated with disease transmission were homosexuality and the sharing of needles and tattoo tools, which were prevalent among prisoners and drug users [ ]. Addressing these behaviors through targeted educational campaigns and interventions could play a critical role in reducing STI rates. Coinfection was reported in six studies, highlighting the complexity of managing STIs when multiple pathogens are present. This underscores the need for comprehensive diagnostic approaches and integrated treatment strategies to address coexisting infections effectively.
The pooled prevalence of STI among the included studies was 7.50%. Regionally, our reported prevalence is lower than those reported in other West African countries, including 9% in Nigeria [ ], 35.1% in Mali [ ], and 53.6% in The Gambia [ ]. However, the high prevalences reported in The Gambia and Mali were from studies with low sample sizes. In constrast, a systematic review in Ethiopia reported a relatively higher pooled prevalence of 13.6% [ ]. Although the pooled STI prevalence of 7.50% in this study is lower than some reports from other West African countries and parts of East Africa [ , – ], it still indicates a high prevalence at the population level and suggests ongoing transmission. Our meta-analysis highlights the prevalence of individual STIs, with a particular focus on commonly reported infections like HIV infection, syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea. The pooled prevalence of these infections was significant, around 5% for each. This level of persistence highlights the importance of integrated STI and HIV prevention programs, as well as enhanced surveillance systems [ , ]. 
The subgroup analysis of STI based on microbial pathogen type reveals notable variations in infection rates. The highest prevalence was in parasitic infection with a rate of 14.19% (95% CI: 3.82–29.4). However, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously, as it is based solely on T. vaginalis, which was reported in only 20% (6/30) of the included studies, Table . Viral infections were more prevalent (8.03%; 95% CI: 5.30–11.24) than bacterial infections (6.01%; 95% CI: 4.69–7.47), highlighting the continued public health challenge posed by viral infections. This could be due to the availability of antibiotics which foster the effective treatment of bacterial infections, whereas many viral infections lack curative treatments [ ]. Also, viral infections are often asymptomatic and tend to be chronic, persisting for longer periods. This highlights the need for greater focus on viral infections and increased public education about their impacts. Co-infections involving both bacterial and viral pathogens exhibited a prevalence of 10.88% (95% CI: 0.00–52.95), though the wide confidence intervals suggest variability among studies. These findings, however, emphasize the need for detailed diagnostic approaches to address the diverse microbial causes of STIs.
The subgroup analysis based on the study setting highlights the differences in the prevalence of STIs between urban and rural settings in Ghana. The highest prevalence (9.06%; 95% CI: 6.68–11.75) was recorded in urban areas, suggesting that urban populations may be at a greater risk for these infections [ ]. This elevated prevalence could be attributed to several factors, including the higher population density, higher rates of sexual activity, and exposure to high-risk behaviors in urban areas. In contrast, the lower prevalence of STIs in rural settings at 6.67% (95% CI: 3.11–11.37%), may reflect the impact of population size, access to healthcare services, and cultural-moral beliefs which are associated with sexual behavior in these areas. Additionally, the barriers often faced by rural populations in accessing health care services can result in underdiagnosing and underreporting of STIs. The prevalence recorded for studies conducted in both rural and urban settings (7.17; 95% CI: 4.43–10.49) reflects a blended disease burden and the complexity of STI transmission dynamics across different environments. The lowest prevalence of 3.67% (95% CI: 1.37–7.01) was recorded in studies that did not specify their settings, highlighting the difficulty of interpreting and comparing STI prevalence estimates in the absence of clear contextual information.

## Strengths and Limitations
This study offers valuable insight into the prevalence and distribution of STIs in Ghana by synthesizing data from multiple sources using a robust systematic review and meta-analysis approach. The use of standardized JBI checklists enhances the reliability of the included studies, while subgroup analyses provide a detailed understanding of STI trends across different regions and populations. Additionally, the pathogen-specific estimates contribute to a more comprehensive epidemiological picture, facilitating targeted public health interventions. Despite these strengths, several challenges hinder the accurate determination of STI prevalence in the population. These include the asymptomatic nature of many infections and limited access to diagnostic facilities, particularly in rural areas. Diagnostic challenges, such as false positives or negatives, may further compromise the accuracy of STI test results [ ]. Furthermore, the stigma and social barriers associated with STIs also exacerbate underdiagnosis and underreporting [ ]. This also impacts sample collection for studies on STIs, as participants are often reluctant to participate, fearing judgment, breach of confidentiality, or social repercussions. As a result, many studies conducted on STIs in Ghana primarily focus on the social and risk factors associated with these infections [ , , ], rather than collecting biological samples.
Studies also neglect other modes of STI transmission, such as vertical transmission (from mother to child), transmission through blood donations, or contaminated medical equipment, and sharing of needles and tattoo tools among drug users. This can significantly lead to skewed or incomplete prevalence data, which may lead to underestimation of the true disease burden of STIs in the population. Our study could not assess the difference in prevalence rates based on age groups and sex of the participants since they were not specified for the STI positive participants.

## Conclusion
This systematic review highlights significant variability in the geographic distribution, population focus, and STI etiologies studied across Ghana. While certain regions and infections have been well-researched, others remain underrepresented, pointing to the need for more inclusive and comprehensive STI surveillance. The findings underscore the importance of strengthened diagnostic capabilities, targeted public health interventions, and expanded research coverage to effectively reduce Ghana’s STI burden.

## Recommendations
To mitigate the spread of STIs, priority should be given to addressing key risk factors, particularly the prevalence of multiple sexual partners, through education and behavior modification programs. Additionally, more attention should be directed toward viral pathogens, which are largely asymptomatic, incurable, and contribute significantly to healthcare costs. Implementing cost-effective prevention strategies, including free preventive commodities and outreach programs targeting high-risk populations, is essential for reducing the economic and health burden of STIs in Ghana.

## Implications for policy, practice, and research
This study aligns with the UN Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) by emphasizing the need for improved STI surveillance, prevention, and treatment strategies [ ]. Strengthening national STI control programs and integrating them into broader health policies will contribute to Ghana’s progress toward achieving SDG 3 and related national health strategies. For practice, enhancing healthcare accessibility, particularly in rural areas where STI underdiagnosis is prevalent, will ensure better disease management. Expanding STI testing and treatment facilities and promoting community-based intervention programs can help bridge existing gaps. Future studies should focus on underrepresented regions and populations to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of STI distribution in Ghana. Additionally, more research should be directed toward emerging STI trends, coinfections, and the long-term economic burden of viral pathogens to inform effective healthcare planning and policy development.
