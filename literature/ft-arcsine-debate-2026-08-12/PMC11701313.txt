## Abstract

## Background
As populations age, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are rapidly adapting hospital services to meet the needs of older populations. This systematic review aimed to establish the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty amongst older people admitted to hospital in LMICs, and to compare levels with existing estimates for high-income settings.

## Methods
Databases Medline, Embase, CINAHL and PsychInfo were searched, and results were manually screened by two researchers. Included studies were cross-sectional or cohort design and reported data from hospital inpatients, aged ≥60 years, in LMICs, using validated methods for identifying frailty. Risk of bias was assessed by two researchers using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. Where possible, results were synthesised using meta-analysis.

## Results
Twenty-nine studies were included, all reporting data from middle-income countries. Physical frailty tools were the most common, with Fried’s phenotype and the FRAIL Scale being the most often used methods of assessment. The pooled estimate of the prevalence of frailty was 39.1% [95% confidence interval (CI) 31.9–46.6%] comprising data from 23 311 older inpatients. For pre-frailty, prevalence was 40.0% (95% CI 25.1–51.4%) from 6954 individuals.

## Discussion
Due to the paucity of eligible studies and their geographical distribution, these pooled estimates are only representative of the burden of frailty in select middle-income settings (particularly China). Future research should seek to establish the prevalence of frailty in hospitals in low-income countries, and to assess clinical outcomes by frailty status, in order to develop bespoke clinical tools and to aid the planning of future geriatric services.

Keywords: frailty, prefrailty, prevalence, low- and middle-income, low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), systematic review, older people

## Key Points
Amongst older hospital inpatients in middle-income countries, the pooled prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty was 39.1% and 40.0%, respectively.
There are major gaps in the existing literature and these estimates reflect the experiences of just 29 studies from middle-income settings, 14 of which were from China.
Future research should also seek to establish the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty in older hospital inpatients in low-income countries, and to assess clinical outcomes by frailty status.

## Introduction
The global population is ageing rapidly. It is projected that by 2050, there will be 2.1 billion people aged 60 years and over, and that more than two-thirds of these will be living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [ ]. Though life-expectancy is rising globally, quality of life in old age is too often negatively impacted by multimorbidity, disability and geriatric syndromes [ ]. These complex, multisystem health problems now pose an unprecedented challenge to healthcare systems in LMICs, which have had little time to adapt services for older populations [ , ]. To improve ageing trajectories the World Health Organisation has advocated for coordinated government efforts to design integrated, person-centred health and social care services, which are better aligned to the needs of older people [ ].
Frailty is one of the key constructs of geriatric medicine. Though it has several operational definitions, frailty is broadly understood as a state of poor health and reduced resilience resulting from multiple problems that are related to age, but that are not inevitable consequences of ageing [ ]. The identification of older people with frailty on admission to hospital is important for several reasons. In studies from high-income countries (HICs), frailty and its prodromal state pre-frailty are significant predictors of longer stay, risk of functional decline and mortality [ ]. Moreover, timely intervention for older adults with frailty who are admitted to hospital can improve these outcomes, extending life, prolonging independence and reducing the need for professional care [ ].
As a result, frailty is increasingly being applied and adapted for use outside of the Western sociocultural context in which it evolved. Existing reviews have estimated the global prevalence of frailty amongst community-dwelling adults to be between 10% and 24% [ ]. Looking specifically at studies from LMICs, Siriwardhana et al. estimated the pooled prevalence of frailty in these settings to be 17.4%, which is at least as high as in HICs [ ], though there is significant heterogeneity and a large range between studies (5%–44%) [ ]. Emerging data has also suggested that frailty is a useful construct for identifying those at greatest risk from dependency and mortality in community-dwelling older adults in LMICs [ ]. Meanwhile, systematic reviews, which have examined the prevalence of frailty in hospital settings, have been overwhelmingly based on data from HICs [ , , , ].
Given that frailty appears to be prevalent in the community in LMICs, and associated with adverse outcomes, understanding its burden amongst older hospitalised adults in these settings may prove to be an important factor for services striving to adapt to the needs of older populations. With this in mind, this systematic review aimed to establish the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty amongst older people admitted to hospital in LMICs, with the hypothesis that the burden would be similar to existing estimates for high-income settings.

## Methods
This systematic review was prospectively registered (PROSPERO CRD42022340466) and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ]. Ethical approval was not required as it provided a secondary summary of existing published data.

## Search strategy
Medline (OVID), Embase (OVID), CINAHL (EBSCO) and PsychInfo (ProQuest) were searched from their inception to 11 October 2024. Titles and abstracts were searched for the terms ‘frail’, ‘frailty’ or ‘frail elderly’ as a keyword and/or subject heading, or the truncated keyword ‘frail*’. These were combined with every country listed as an LMIC by the World Bank as of 2022–23, also as a keyword and/or subject heading [ ]. No additional filters or limits were used. In addition to this primary database search, references of included studies were searched manually and considered for inclusion.

## Eligibility criteria
To be considered eligible for inclusion, studies had to meet the following criteria: (i) cross-sectional or cohort design, (ii) report data from LMICs, (iii) report data from adults aged ≥60 years, (iv) report data from hospital inpatients, (v) report frailty prevalence using clearly defined and validated methods (note this criterion excludes studies which reported frailty as a continuous variable). Hospital inpatients were defined as those admitted to medical and surgical ward environments and expected to stay in hospital overnight. Studies which assessed frailty amongst a subpopulation based on an index disease (e.g. acute coronary syndrome or COVID-19) were excluded on the basis that the prevalence of frailty in these groups might not necessarily reflect the prevalence in the general hospital inpatient population. To minimise the risk of selection bias, included studies were required to at least attempt to assess all members (e.g. whole hospital, ward or department), or a random sample, of the target clinical population. Studies relating to community, primary care, outpatient, emergency department or short-stay settings were excluded. Given the international scope of this review, it was deemed important to include studies which were not available in the English language. Google Translate® was used to translate non-English-language studies and is increasingly recognised as a viable and accurate tool for use in systematic reviews [ ].

## Study selection
Database search results were imported to Rayyan open access software for systematic reviews [ ]. After deduplication, titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility. All records were screened by two people; SD screened all records with LE, EB, GR and ED providing the second screen. Disagreements were resolved by consensus and where there were conflicting views CD and RW adjudicated. Full-text articles were all sought for retrieval. For conference abstracts, corresponding authors were contacted and given 30-days to respond to establish whether their data were available in a full-text article.

## Data extraction and quality assessment
All eligible full-text articles were examined by SD and JL who both independently extracted the data and conducted a methodological quality assessment, blinded to one another. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for studies reporting prevalence data was used both to assess the scientific rigour of each study (its methodological quality) and to consider the potential impact of methodological decisions on the frailty prevalence estimates produced (risk of bias) [ ]. The JBI checklist is structured around nine items which consider important factors relating to methodological quality in prevalence studies. The final item is an overall appraisal based on the rater’s judgement in which the study may be included, excluded, or further information sought. Where there was disagreement over whether to include a study, RW and CD were given the final say.

## Data synthesis
Studies of sufficient quality and methodological homogeneity based on JBI checklist assessment were included in meta-analyses. It is well recognised that different tools produce different estimates of frailty prevalence, with those based on physical factors (such as the frailty phenotype) tending to give lower figures than those based on a multi-dimensional assessment of deficits (such as the frailty index), or a global impression (such as the Clinical Frailty Scale) [ ]. To examine the impact of different assessment tools, subgroup analyses were conducted to produce pooled estimates for frailty using studies with the same method, provided there were a minimum of five to ensure adequate power [ ]. The prevalence of frailty also usually increases with age and tends to be higher in females [ ]. To examine these factors, Pearson’s R was used to examine the correlation between the mean age of participants and frailty prevalence, and subgroup analyses were conducted to calculate the pooled prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty by sex where this was reported.
The prevalence estimates for individual studies were displayed in tabular form. Many studies used the binary categorisations of ‘frail’ and ‘non-frail’ (the latter being an amalgamation of ‘prefrail’ and ‘robust’) which is reflected in the table headings. Meta-analyses were performed using the MetaXL plugin for Excel version 16 with a random-effects model and the double arcsine transformation employed to stabilise variance between studies. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Higgins I2 [ ]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by repetition of primary analyses with the removal of one study at a time to assess if any were exerting an outsized influence on the pooled estimates and statistical heterogeneity. Pooled estimates of frailty and pre-frailty are presented with the 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Results of meta-analyses are displayed in forest plots with studies ordered by prevalence.

## Results

## Study selection
Searches identified 7797 records and 3354 remained after deduplication. After title and abstract screening, 65 were identified as potentially eligible for inclusion, with a further 13 identified from references. After retrieval, full-text review and methodological quality assessment, 29 studies were included in the review. demonstrates the selection process for included studies and reasons for exclusion. Studies which could not be retrieved, or that were excluded after full text review, are cited in Supplementary Data along with the reason for their exclusion. The characteristics and prevalence data from the 29 included studies are displayed in .
 

## Frailty prevalence
A total of 27 studies, comprising data from 23 311 older adults admitted to hospital inpatient environments, demonstrated a pooled estimate of the prevalence of frailty of 39.1% (95% CI 31.9–46.6%). Two studies included in the review were excluded from the meta-analyses due to methodological heterogeneity: (i) Kizilarslanoglu et al. were excluded due to the use of an unusually high cut-off (>0.4 rather than the more usual >0.25) to define frailty in their index [ ]; (ii) Yin et al. were excluded as they only recruited older people with a hospital stay of greater than seven days, potentially representing a group with a higher frailty burden [ ]. The summary estimate for each study and a visual depiction of the pooled estimate for frailty prevalence can be found in the forest plot in . Estimates of frailty ranged widely between studies from 8.7% to 66.6%. This was reflected in the I2 test which at 99.1% indicated high levels of statistical heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis (through repetition of the primary analysis with the removal of each study individually) did not indicate that any one study was exerting an outsized influence on the pooled estimate or statistical heterogeneity (see Supplementary Data for the full sensitivity analysis).
 Enough studies were available for subgroup analyses of prevalence based on Fried’s phenotype (n = 14), the FRAIL Scale (n = 7) and the Clinical Frailty Scale (n = 5). Studies using the Clinical Frailty Scale produced the highest pooled prevalence for frailty at 52.2% (95% CI 40.7–63.5%, I2 = 96.5). Meanwhile, those using versions of Fried’s frailty phenotype gave a lower figure of 36.2% (95% CI 27.1–45.8%, I2 = 98.7). Studies using the FRAIL Scale (a screening questionnaire based on the same physical domains as Fried’s phenotype) gave a pooled prevalence of 31.8% (95% CI 20.1%—44.8%, I2 = 99.4).

## Pre-frailty prevalence
A total of 14 studies reported pre-frailty prevalence comprising 6954 older adults admitted to hospital inpatient environments (see ). These gave rise to a pooled estimate of the prevalence of pre-frailty of 40.0% (95% CI 25.1–51.4%). Again, statistical heterogeneity was high, indicated by an I2 of 99.2%. On sensitivity analysis, removal of each study one-by-one resulted in <5% variation in the overall pooled estimate and no major change in the I2 statistic (see Supplementary Data for the full sensitivity analysis).
 Only Fried’s phenotype was utilised by enough studies (n = 10) for subgroup analysis by frailty tool. These produced a pooled estimate for the prevalence of pre-frailty of 41.1 (95% CI 22.0–56.4%, I2 = 99.3). Unsurprisingly given that this accounted for most of the studies, this is similar to the overall pooled estimate for pre-frailty prevalence.

## Geographical distribution of included studies
As is displayed in , reports of the prevalence of frailty amongst hospital inpatients in LMICs were generally sparse, the notable exception being in China which accounted for nearly half of the included studies (n = 14) [ , , , ]. Only two studies from the African continent met the criteria for inclusion [ , ]. All included studies were from middle-income countries, and only a minority of those were from lower-middle-income settings (n = 8) [ , , , , , , , ].
 

## Participant demographics
There were notable disparities in the demographic characteristics of participants between studies. The proportion of females ranged from just 20.3% in Hao et al.’s study from China [ ] to 74.0% in Chehrehgosha et al.’s study from Iran [ ]. With regards to age, 12 studies used 65 years and over, rather than 60 years and over, as the age criterion for inclusion [ , , , , , , , , , ]. The study with the youngest population was Khandelwal et al.’s from Delhi, India, where the mean age of participants was 66.4 (±6.3) years [ ]. By contrast, Xang et al. found a mean age of 83.3 (±8.3) amongst inpatients in a hospital in Hangzhou on the east coast of China, a 16.9 year difference [ ].
There was no correlation between the average age of participants and frailty prevalence in the 22 studies included in the meta-analyses which reported both of these variables (R = 0.095, P = .675). Amongst the 17 included studies which gave a breakdown of frailty status by sex, the pooled prevalence in females was 42.0% (95% CI 32.4–52.0%, I2 = 98.2) and in males 42.2% (95% CI 31.1–53.8%, I2 = 98.4). Several studies did not report the sex breakdown and thus could not be included in this subgroup analysis [ , , , , , , ]. For pre-frailty, only eight studies reported prevalence by sex category [ , , , , , ]. Amongst these, the pooled prevalence of pre-frailty was 36.3% in females (95% CI 26.6–46.7%, I2 = 94.9) and 39.0% in males (95% CI 30.5%—47.7%, I2 = 89.1).

## Clinical settings
Study populations were drawn from a wide range of clinical environments. Eight studies assessed the prevalence of frailty across all inpatient wards [ , , , , , , , ]. The remaining studies focussed on one or more specific environments, with internal medical wards being the most commonly surveyed (n = 13) [ , , , , , , , , , , , , ], followed by geriatrics (n = 8) [ , , , , , , , ], cardiology (n = 2) [ , ], general surgery (n = 4) [ , , , ] and intensive care (n = 1) [ ].

## Frailty measures
A range of tools were used in the assessment of frailty. The Fried frailty phenotype was the most commonly used instrument (n = 14) [ , , , , , , , ], followed by the FRAIL Scale which is based upon the same domains (n = 8) [ , , , , , ]. A number of studies utilised tools with origins in a deficit accumulation model of frailty including the Frailty Index (n = 4) [ , , , ], Clinical Frailty Scale (n = 5) [ , , , , ] and its older version the CHSA Clinical Frailty Scale (n = 1) [ ]. Other measures used included the Edmonton FRAIL Scale [ , , ] and the Tilburg Frailty Indicator [ ].

## Discussion
This systematic review included 29 eligible studies and is to the authors’ knowledge the first to examine the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty amongst older hospital inpatients in LMICs. Pooled estimates of the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty in inpatient ward settings were 39.1% and 40.0%, respectively. This review has highlighted the relative paucity of data on this subject as only eight of the world’s 136 LMICs were represented in this review. All included studies were conducted in middle-income countries and more than half were from China. Meanwhile, there are enormous gaps in the literature from low-income countries and geographical areas such as the African continent.
There are several existing reviews that have sought to estimate frailty prevalence amongst hospital populations. Perhaps the most comprehensive was performed by Doody et al., whose meta-analysis included over 460 000 older inpatients [ ]. This produced pooled estimates for the prevalence of frailty of 47.4% (95% CI 43.7–51.1%) and pre-frailty of 25.8% (95% CI 22.0–29.6%) [ ]. As in the present investigation, over 70% of the population were somewhere on the spectrum of frailty. However, Doody et al.’s meta-analysis demonstrated a greater ratio of frailty to pre-frailty [ ], whilst the present review found an almost one-to-one ratio of frailty (39.1%) to pre-frailty (40.0%). The majority of studies in this review which reported pre-frailty used the phenotype (n = 10/14) which tends to produce lower prevalence estimates. If more studies had utilised multidimensional or global assessment tools, it is quite likely the estimates of pre-frailty prevalence would have been even higher. One possible explanation for the difference in the ratio seen in Doody et al.’s investigation is that 91 of the 96 studies included in their review were from HICs and the populations in these studies were considerably older, thus skewing more towards the frail end of the spectrum.
Pre-frailty and frailty have previously demonstrated higher proportions in females, both in the community in LMICs and in existing data from hospital inpatients in HICs [ , ]. Sex differences in frailty prevalence have a complex aetiology that is likely due to fundamental biological mechanisms and variation in disease susceptibility, as well as differences in life expectancy [ ]. In contrast to existing literature, this review found no real difference in the prevalence of frailty between the sexes, and only a very small female predominance for pre-frailty (39.0% versus 36.3% in males). The reasons behind this may be complex and include social and cultural, in addition to biological factors. However, it is also possible the lack of a difference was due to the small numbers of studies that could be included in these subgroup analyses.
High heterogeneity was present in this investigation and is a theme amongst other meta-analyses on this topic [ , ]. Given the array of tools for frailty assessment, and the vastly different contexts in which they have been applied, this is hardly surprising. Fried’s frailty phenotype was the most commonly utilised tool for the measurement of frailty in LMICs and produced lower estimates for frailty prevalence compared with the Clinical Frailty Scale which is in keeping with existing literature [ , , ]. However, variation in frailty prevalence can also occur between studies in which the same tool is used, depending on how it is operationalised. For example, the frailty phenotype is often modified, affecting both prevalence estimates and its ability to predict adverse outcomes [ ]. The lack of a consensus definition for frailty certainly poses a major challenge when synthesising evidence and is sometimes decried as a weakness of the construct. However, this diversity in the understanding of frailty may also prove to be a major advantage when applying it to new settings, allowing for its adaptation for different sociocultural contexts.
Only seven of the studies included in this review reported clinical outcomes such as mortality, readmission and length of stay [ , , , , , , ], whilst all the remaining studies were cross-sectional by design. Following cohorts over time is much more resource intensive, but the data gained is considerably more valuable. Ultimately, the clinical strength of the frailty construct has been its ability to predict which older adults are at greatest risk of adverse outcomes. Future work in hospital inpatients in LMICs should seek to establish whether frailty remains a good predictor of adverse outcomes when applied to new sociocultural settings.
Several studies included in this review excluded groups of older people for practical reasons around consent, or inability to complete assessments. However, some of these exclusions may have led to selection bias and an underestimation of the prevalence of frailty. They were terminal illnesses [ , , , , , ], cancer [ , , ], cognitive impairment [ , , , , , , , , , , ], mobility problems [ , , , , , , , , ] and impairments of hearing or vision [ , , , , , , ]. For further information regarding the risk of bias amongst included studies, see .
This systematic review had a number of limitations. Eligibility criteria were restricted to whole ward populations, thus excluding many articles where participants were recruited on the basis of a specific disease. Though this was done out of concern that the prevalence of frailty differs in different conditions, cumulatively these data may have been informative. The focus on observational studies may also have led to missing experimental research in which frailty prevalence was assessed pre-intervention. Furthermore, Google Translate software was used to convert three reports into English to assess eligibility, risk of bias and to extract data [ , , ]. This was done in the interests of inclusivity but introduces a greater risk of error than there would be with the gold standard of professional translation which was not performed due to resource limitations. As has been discussed, the small number of included studies were highly heterogeneous, and reflective of experiences in middle-income settings and a single country, China, accounted for just under half of all included studies.
In conclusion, the few studies that have examined the prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty amongst older inpatients in middle-income countries, China in particular, indicate that the burden is considerable. However, there are major gaps in the existing literature which need to be urgently addressed in light of rapidly ageing global populations and current data are insufficient to determine whether levels of frailty are as high as in HICs. Future research must also seek to establish the prevalence of frailty, and pre-frailty, in hospitals in low-income countries, and to assess clinical outcomes by frailty status in order to adapt clinical tools for these settings, and help in the planning and provision of future healthcare services.
