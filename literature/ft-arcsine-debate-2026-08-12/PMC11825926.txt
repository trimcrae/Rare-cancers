## ABSTRACT

## Background
Exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN) is an established dietary therapy for Crohn's disease but its role in ulcerative colitis remains unclear.

## Aims
To investigate the efficacy of EEN in adults with active ulcerative colitis and compare variations in treatment protocols, safety, tolerability and adherence.

## Methods
We conducted a systematic search of MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Emcare, CINAHL, Web of Science and trial registries for articles published from inception until July 21, 2024. We included all experimental and observational studies that described the use of EEN in adults with active ulcerative colitis. This review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42022319584).

## Results
Of 3273 articles screened, we included 10 studies (334 adults). Overall, there was no difference between EEN and comparator for ulcerative colitis remission induction (median follow‐up 14 days, risk ratio (RR) 1.15, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.71–1.85; 2 studies). In acute severe ulcerative colitis, there was no difference between EEN and comparator for corticosteroid failure (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.48–1.20; 2 studies) or risk of colectomy (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.51–1.51, n = 2 studies) during index admission. The pooled discontinuation rate was 3% (95% CI 0–10; 9 studies). There was heterogeneity in trial design, methodology and assessment of outcomes.

## Conclusion
EEN was well tolerated with low therapy discontinuation in adults with active ulcerative colitis. However, there is insufficient evidence to support the use of EEN as an adjunctive therapy to standard of care. Further, well‐designed studies with reproducible methodology and endpoints are necessary to evaluate its effectiveness.

## Introduction
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is an inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterised by chronic colonic inflammation extending from the rectum [ ]. Although the aetiology is uncertain, UC is thought to arise from a complex interplay of aberrant immune response, gut microbial dysbiosis and environmental factors in genetically susceptible individuals [ ]. In developed countries, IBD is on the rise with prevalence as high as 1 in 200 persons in western countries [ ]. Current therapies predominantly affect the immune system and harbour risks associated with immunosuppression. Importantly, they do not target the luminal microbial factors despite the purported impact on disease susceptibility and activity [ , ]. There is a clear need for new therapies in UC that are effective and safe.
Exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN) is a liquid diet therapy used to treat inflammation in Crohn's disease (CD). EEN provides 100% of nutritional requirements by oral ingestion or nasogastric tube typically for a defined period of time [ ]. The mechanism of action of EEN remains uncertain, however, it is postulated to act through immune modulation of intestinal inflammation, alteration of gut microbiota, as well as its influence on intestinal barrier function [ , , , ]. In CD, EEN is increasingly used for remission induction of active disease and is first‐line therapy in paediatric patients with active CD [ , ]. EEN has demonstrated the capacity to induce both clinical remission and endoscopic mucosal healing, as well as ameliorate complicated disease and need for surgery in CD, all without the immunosuppressive effects of current therapies [ , , , , ]. In UC, the current role of EEN is less clear.
Diet as therapy for UC is in the grips of a revolution, with multiple current trials exploring the role of defined whole food strategies for both induction and maintenance of remission [ , , , , , ]. Despite enthusiasm for diet as a key therapeutic strategy in UC, EEN is not commonly used as an anti‐inflammatory therapy. Instead, nutritional formulas in UC are largely prescribed as part of partial enteral nutrition for nutritional supplementation where oral feeding is insufficient to meet requirements [ ]. Early reports in the 1970s described the benefits of using an adjunctive elemental diet alongside corticosteroid and mesalamine therapy to induce remission in medically refractory UC [ , ]. When studied as a primary therapy in two retrospective cohort studies in children and adults with UC [ , , ], the rate of remission with EEN was similar to total parental nutrition and ‘bowel rest’, the contemporary standard of care (SoC), causing physicians to cast doubt on its efficacy [ , ]. Recently, there has been renewed interest in EEN in both adult and paediatric UC, including two recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) in adult acute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC), which demonstrated efficacy when comparing EEN with SoC steroid therapy versus SoC alone on a per‐protocol analysis [ , , , ]. This has sparked a controversy among clinicians as to whether EEN plays a role in the therapeutic armamentarium in UC. Specifically, the potential efficacy of EEN in ASUC is appealing, where therapeutic failure rates remain high despite advanced therapies, with a 30%–40% short‐term risk of colectomy in some studies and an overall 10‐year colectomy rate of 10% [ , , , ]. A dietary strategy that may potentially augment the response of current SoC is worthy of further investigation.
A systematic review has never been performed to evaluate the existing literature on the efficacy and safety of EEN in adult patients with UC. Accordingly, this systematic review aimed to collate and evaluate the current evidence for EEN in adults with UC, capturing protocol, efficacy, safety and adherence to therapy. Specifically, the objectives of this systematic review were to:

Establish the efficacy of EEN in the treatment of active UC in adults, specifically its effect on clinical response, remission and rate of colectomy
Evaluate and compare treatment protocols used to provide EEN in adults with active UC
Examine the safety, tolerance and adherence of EEN in adults with active UC.

## Methods
2.1.

## Study Identification and Selection
A systematic search of MEDLINE (Ovid 1946), Embase (Ovid 1974), Cochrane CENTRAL (Ovid 1991), Emcare (Ovid 1995), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) (EBSCO 1937), Web of Science, WHO International Trial Registry, Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, ClinicalTrials.gov and the EU Clinical Trials Register from database inception until July 21, 2024, was performed. A manual search was performed on references of included studies. The first 200 relevant results on Google Scholar were reviewed as recommended by previously published guidance on search strategies for grey literature [ ]. The initial search strategy and study protocol were developed to examine efficacy and treatment protocols for EEN administered for all phenotypes of IBD in adults, within which all studies reporting on UC were captured. A detailed search strategy is outlined in Appendix  . This systematic review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (Registration no. CRD42022319584) and was carried out according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses Protocols guidelines for systematic reviews [ ].

2.2.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
As the initial search was conducted for all IBD, only IBD studies that provided separated data for UC are eligible for inclusion in this review. All interventional studies, observational studies including cohort studies or case–controls and case series with > 10 patients, both in full manuscript and conference abstract form, were included in the review. Excluded were other forms of literature. Participants were adults with a formal diagnosis of UC aged 18 years or older. Eligible studies required reporting of UC disease activity using either clinical assessment or a clinical disease activity score. EEN was defined as a liquid diet that provides complete nutrition with the exclusion of intake of all other table foods or fluids [ ]. EEN was required to be used as an intervention in one or more arms of the study, either as sole therapy or in combination with other therapies, with the intent of ameliorating active UC. Studies that used EEN for nutritional support, perioperative optimisation or recurrence prevention were excluded. Studies were excluded if they included paediatric patients (< 18 years of age), and results were not separable for analysis of adult patients only. There were no language or publication date restrictions for the scope of the search but only studies with full‐text available in English were included. For full inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Table  ).

2.3.

## Data Extraction and Analysis
Two reviewers (KWMC, LB) independently screened and extracted data from the included studies. Screening and data extraction were performed using Covidence (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Where differences in screening and data extraction occurred, arbitration by a third reviewer (ASD) was sought. Included articles were categorised based on disease phenotype (CD, UC, IBD‐related pouchitis), indication, concurrent use of medical therapy and use in pregnancy. For this review, the data pertaining to UC studies are described.
Patient and disease characteristics, prescribing practices and EEN therapy protocol were extracted. Data pertaining to prespecified outcomes were recorded. This included remission and response rates; biochemical, radiological or endoscopic outcomes; rates of adverse effects and discontinuation rates; as well as instruments for assessing tolerability and adherence rates. Articles that presented data in the graphical format were independently interpreted by the reviewers (MC, LB) as a dichotomous result (e.g., remission or otherwise based on individual study definition). If this was not possible, graphical data were not extracted.
Data analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software (Release 17.0 College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). For dichotomous outcomes, individual and pooled trial statistics were calculated as risk ratios (RR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Where proportions for pooled trial statistics were at the extremities, continuity correction by Freeman‐Tukey transformation was applied [ ]. For continuous variables, mean difference and 95% CI were calculated between intervention and control groups. A meta‐analysis was performed if at least two articles sufficiently reported on the outcome. A random‐effects model was used to pool data and mitigate study heterogeneity. The I
2 statistic > 50% or Cochran's Q p value < 0.10 indicates significant heterogeneity [ ]. A p‐value ≤ 0.05 denoted statistical significance. To evaluate for potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed and grouped based on indication of therapy, criteria for remission or response, use of concomitant medications and EEN formulation. Sensitivity analyses were conducted based on methodological quality and design. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to evaluate the quality of the evidence [ ].

2.4.

## Quality Assessment of Studies
Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers (MKWC, LB). Randomised controlled trials were assessed using the Cochrane risk‐of‐bias (RoB 1.0) tool [ ]. For observational studies (e.g., case–control, cohort studies), the relevant Newcastle‐Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale was used [ ]. For nonrandomised controlled trials of intervention, the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomised Studies—of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool was used [ ].

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Study Characteristics
The systematic search was conducted on July 21, 2024, and identified 17,287 records. After duplicates were removed, 3273 articles were screened, 389 articles were selected for full‐text review, 319 studies were excluded and 70 studies met criteria for inclusion. Ten studies included patients with ulcerative colitis. Figure  shows the PRISMA flow chart for the study retrieval and selection.
 Among 10 studies reporting on EEN in UC, seven studies included only UC patients [ , , , , , , ] and three studies included both CD and UC patients [ , , ]. Six were full‐text articles including four RCT [ , , , ], one prospective cohort study [ ] and one retrospective cohort study [ ]. Due to inclusion of paediatric patients in the cohort of the retrospective cohort study [ ], only the data relating to the adult cohort was included in this systematic review. There were also four conference abstracts: two were retrospective cohort studies [ , ] and two were prospective cohort studies [ , ].
Across all studies, there were 334 adult patients with UC, of which 315 (94.3%) had moderate to severe disease according to the study‐specified criteria. Eighty‐nine patients (26.6%) were medically refractory to corticosteroid therapy. A total of 253 (75.7%) patients met the Truelove and Witt's Criteria for ASUC criteria. The key characteristics of the included trials are summarised in Tables  and .
 3.1.1.

## Indications for EEN
All 10 studies examined the efficacy of EEN as an adjunctive therapy to SoC for the treatment of active UC across various disease severity [ , , , , , , , , ]. ASUC was the most studied severity (6 studies, 60.0%) [ , , , , ]. One study (10.0%) included patients of all severity of disease according to the Binder activity score [ ], although most were predominantly mild–moderate severity. Three studies (30.0%) used EEN in patients with moderate to severe UC [ , , ], of which one trial also included patients that met Truelove and Witt's ASUC criteria [ ]. Two studies (20%) only recruited adults who were medically refractory despite 48 h of corticosteroid therapy [ , ]. One study (10.0%) did not report on disease severity or whether the disease was medically refractory [ ].

3.1.2.

## Comparator Arms
The therapy or diet used as a comparator varied significantly between studies. This included the use of nutritionally matched blended hospital diet as placebo (1 study, 10.0%) [ ] or a nonexclusive liquid diet that included energy drinks used in historical controls (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. In studies including patients with ASUC, comparator arms that were used adjunctive to SoC included TPN (1 study, 10.0%) [ ] and ward diet with dietary counselling (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. In another ASUC study (10.0%), EEN was used in both arms of the RCT and the intervention arm consisted of daily albumin infusions [ ]. In this study, only the arm that consisted only of EEN was included in the meta‐analysis. Lastly, in a retrospective cohort study (1 study, 10.0%), the comparator arm was propensity‐matched against historical ASUC controls that did not use EEN from the same institution in the preceding 5 years [ ].

3.1.3.

## Concurrent Medical Therapy
In one study (10.0%) examining predominantly mild–moderate UC, therapy consisted of continued doses of sulfasalazine [ ]. For moderate–severe UC, concurrent therapies included infliximab induction therapy (1 study, 10.0%) [ ] and prednisone (2 studies, 20.0%) [ , ]. For studies investigating EEN in ASUC, corticosteroid was used in all studies as SoC (6 studies, 60.0%) [ , , , , , ] with intravenous route specified in four studies (40.0%) [ , , , ] and two studies (20.0%) also prescribing per rectal corticosteroids [ , ]. Medical rescue therapy in the event of failure was used in four studies (40.0%) before colectomy was considered for refractory disease [ , , , ]. One study did not report on concurrent therapy [ ].

3.2.

## Efficacy of EEN in UC

3.2.1.

## Clinical Remission and Therapy Response in UC

3.2.1.1.

## Clinical Remission
Clinical remission was an endpoint reported in two RCTs (20%) [ , ] and one retrospective cohort study (10%) [ ]. There was heterogeneity with regard to the clinical end points for each trial and its definition. In one study examining predominantly mild and moderate UC [ ], EEN and a placebo liquid diet were compared. Patients with active disease despite already receiving sulfasalazine were maintained on their dose of medication. In this study, clinical remission criteria utilised a CD activity score, Harvey Bradshaw Index (HBI), and was met when the score was 0. Remission was no different between EEN and placebo group (EEN: 7/11, 63.6% vs. placebo: 4/8, 50.0%, p = 0.658) [ ]. In another study comparing EEN versus TPN in moderate to severe steroid‐refractory UC [ ], clinical remission at day 14 was defined as both a study‐specific quantitative Truelove and Witts Criteria of < 11 points as well as prednisone requirement of < 30 mg/day. In this study, EEN was commenced after nonresponse to 48 h of IV prednisone and steroids were tapered after diet commencement. This study reported no difference between the groups (EEN: 12/23 patients, 52.2% vs. TPN: 10/21, 47.6%, p = 0.763) [ ]. In a retrospective cohort study with steroid‐refractory UC, 9/14 patients (64.3%) achieved remission when EEN was used in addition to steroids [ ]. A meta‐analysis performed on the two RCT demonstrated no difference between EEN and control with a risk ratio of 1.15 (n = 122, 95% CI: 0.71–1.85, I
2 = 0%, Figure  ).
 
3.2.1.2.

## Clinical Response
One study (10%) in moderate–severe UC reported specifically on clinical response as defined as a SCCAI ≤ 4. In this study, there was no difference in clinical response with infliximab induction when given with EEN adjunct versus without EEN (EEN: 13/16 (81.3%) vs. control: 7/12 (58.3%), p = 0.231) [ ].

3.2.2.

## Corticosteroid Failure in ASUC

Corticosteroid failure as a clinical outcome was reported in six studies in ASUC (60.0%) [ , , , , , ]. The definition of corticosteroid failure varied including the need for surgery or medical rescue as predicted by the Oxford Criteria in four recent studies (40.0%) [ , , , ] and a study‐defined criteria (1 study, 10.0%) [ , ]. Meta‐analysis was performed on two RCTs comparing EEN versus control with this outcome. Overall, EEN did not significantly reduce the risk of corticosteroid failure in the treatment of UC with a risk ratio of 0.76 (95% CI 0.48–1.20, I
2 = 0.0%, see Figure  ). The overall effect did not significantly differ after sensitivity analysis when including studies with a high risk of bias. The pooled corticosteroid failure rate when EEN was used adjunct to SoC for ASUC was 39% (95% CI 20–57, I
2 = 84.30%, Figure  ).

3.2.3.

## Efficacy of Medical Rescue Therapy in ASUC

Two studies (20.0%) on ASUC reported on the efficacy of medical rescue therapy when coupled with EEN [ , ]. In one study [ ], EEN adjunct to SoC was compared to SoC control group and there was no difference in efficacy of medical rescue therapy in ASUC (EEN: 4/5 patients, 80% vs. control: 10/12 patients, 83%, p = 0.72). The pooled efficacy of medical rescue therapy after EEN and SoC was 78% (95% CI 59–97, I
2 = 0.00, Figure  ).

3.2.4.

## Colectomy Rate
The effect of adjunct EEN on colectomy rate was reported in most studies (8 studies, 80%) [ , , , , , , , ]. The overall pooled colectomy rate after EEN was 11% (95% CI 0.01–0.25, I
2 = 81.91%, Figure  ). Significant heterogeneity is likely driven by included studies that use advanced therapies in the treatment of ASUC (see Figure  ). Two studies (20.0%) [ , ] reported colectomy rates for moderate to severe UC patients with a rate ranging from 0% to 13%. For the indication of ASUC, six studies (60%) reported colectomy rates [ , , , , ] for adjunctive EEN. The pooled colectomy rate was 13% (95% CI 1–32, I
2 86.0%, Figure  ). Three studies (30.0%) compared adjunct EEN to SoC alone in ASUC, two of which were RCT. The overall risk ratio of colectomy in ASUC after the use of EEN was 0.88 (95% CI 0.51–1.51, I
2 = 0%, Figure  ). The outcome did not vary after sensitivity analysis with the inclusion of studies of other methodological quality.

3.2.5.

## Hospitalisation
Three studies (30.0%) reported on duration of hospitalisation [ , , ]. Two studies (20.0%) examined hospitalisation in ASUC and one study reported a significantly reduced median duration of hospitalisation in the EEN arm (EEN: 10 (8–17) days vs. control: 13 (8–24) days, p = 0.04) [ ]. The second study did not find a difference when comparing SoC with EEN and albumin infusions versus SoC with EEN alone (median duration of hospitalisation in Albumin + EEN + SoC 10.5 (7–16) days vs. EEN + SoC: 10 (7–20) days, p = 0.43) [ ]. Meta‐analysis was not possible due to the significantly skewed distribution of results prohibiting estimation of mean and standard deviation [ ].
A study from Japan evaluated the duration of hospitalisation in patients with moderate–severe UC admitted for infliximab induction [ ]. In this study, patients were placed on IV fluids and liquid diet (either EEN or sports drinks) for 1‐week postinduction. Patients were considered for discharge when they were able to tolerate a low residue diet. This study reported significantly reduced length of hospitalisation in the EEN group (EEN: 0 (3.5–9.3) days vs. control: 8 (15–21) days, p = 0.0012), as they were able to transition to low residue diet earlier than historical controls [ ].

3.2.6.

## Maintenance of Remission and Rehospitalisation
Rehospitalisation was also examined in two studies (20.0%) for ASUC patients at 6 months [ , ]. At 6 months, a composite outcome of colectomy or re‐admission was statistically lower in those who received EEN versus controls (EEN: 4/25 patients, 16% vs. control: 9/23 patients, 39%, p = 0.045) [ ]. However, in another RCT on ASUC patients, when comparing albumin with EEN versus EEN alone, there was no difference (Albumin + EEN: 10/27 (37.0%) vs. 5/28 (17.9%), p = 0.09). One study (10.0%) also reported on maintenance of remission in moderate UC but was not included in analysis due to the inability to separate paediatric and adult cases for this outcome [ ].

3.2.7.

## Change in Biochemical Markers
3.2.7.1.

## C‐Reactive Protein
Four studies (40.0%) reported on CRP changes after therapy [ , , , ]. Among these, three studies were conducted in ASUC patients and median CRP was elevated at baseline [ , , ], whereas in one study on moderate–severe UC, CRP was normal in some patients. In each of the four studies that measured CRP levels at follow‐up, the reduction of CRP was not statistically significant when compared to control [ , , , ]. Two studies with similar methodology in ASUC reported a day 3 CRP reduction of 16 mg/L (IQR 5.8–39.4) [ ] and 23.4 mg/L (IQR 6.8–38.9) [ ]. Due to significantly skewed data, meta‐analysis was not possible [ ].

3.2.7.2.

## Faecal calprotectin
Faecal calprotectin (FCP) levels were only monitored in one study (10.0%) [ ]. In this study, median FCP was elevated at baseline (1356.1, IQR 880.1–1794.3 μg/g) and significantly lower FCP levels at day 3 were reported when compared to controls (EEN: 720 (415–1126) μg/g vs. control: 1103 (676–1552) μg/g, p = 0.03). Furthermore, a statistically greater fall in FCP levels was observed between day 1 and 3 of treatment (EEN: 315 (129–797) μg/g vs. control: 140 (−386–459) μg/g, p = 0.04).

3.2.7.3.

## Albumin
Albumin levels at the end of therapy were reported in six studies (60.0%) [ , , , , , ]. When pooling both RCT and cohort study results, mean end of therapy albumin levels was statistically higher in the EEN group versus controls (Mean difference 3.89 g/L, 95% CI 1.50–6.29, I
2 = 70.3%, Figure  ). Sensitivity analysis excluding studies with a high risk of bias based on poor comparability scores on the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale for cohort studies as well as exclusion of cohort studies yielded similarly higher levels of albumin in the EEN group but without heterogeneity [ ].

3.2.8.

## Change in Endoscopic Disease Activity
Endoscopic end points were examined in two studies (20.0%) with a follow‐up flexible sigmoidoscopy [ , ]. In one RCT with predominantly mild to moderate UC, there was no difference in change in mucosal disease activity (2/11 patients, 18.1% vs. 1/8 patients, 12.5%, p = 1.000) [ ]. In a prospective cohort study of 28 patients, end of therapy endoscopic Mayo Score was better in the infliximab with EEN group versus infliximab alone (infliximab with EEN: 0 (0–1) vs. infliximab alone: 1 (0.75–2), p = 0.042) [ ].

3.2.9.

## Outcomes That Were Not Reported
The following predefined outcomes were not reported in any of the included studies: histological response and remission at end of therapy, transmural response and healing at end of therapy and health‐related quality of life.

3.3.

## Variations in EEN Protocol for UC

3.3.1.

## 
EEN Formulation
Of the included studies, eight studies (80.0%) reported on the type of EEN formula used. Four studies used polymeric EEN (40%) [ , , , ], one study used semielemental EEN (10%) [ ] and three studies used elemental EEN (30%) [ , , ]. The formulation and nutritional information are summarised in Table  .

3.3.2.

## 
EEN Prescription
3.3.2.1.

## EEN Therapy Duration
For predominantly mild–moderate disease, EEN was continued for 14 days (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. In moderate to severe disease, EEN therapy was continued for 7–14 days (2 studies, 20.0%) [ , ] and one study (10.0%) continued EEN for 7 days and based on a clinical response would determine the duration of therapy [ ]. For the indication of ASUC, EEN was continued for 5 days (1 study, 10%) [ ], 7 days (3 studies, 30.0%) [ , , ], 7–14 days (1 study, 10.0%) [ ] and another study (1 study, 10.0%) [ ] which continued EEN for a mean duration of 11.2 days without stating the prescribed duration. One study (10.0%) reported that EEN had been used for up to 4 weeks, however, due to the limited reporting it remains unclear if this applied to both CD and UC patients included in the study [ ].

3.3.2.2.

## Caloric and Other Requirements
The caloric requirements were calculated using various methods and were reported in four studies (40.0%), these were as follows: Long's equation modified for ideal body weight (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]; caloric requirement calculated from their BMI (15–19 kg/m2: 31–35 kcal/kg/day; 20–29 kg/m2: 26–30 kcal/kg/day; > 30 kg/m2: 15–25 kcal/kg/day) (2 study, 20.0%) [ , ]; as well as a study‐specific standard dose of EEN for all patients (2 studies, 20.0%) [ , ]. Two studies (20.0%) calculated protein requirements but did not report the method of calculation [ , ]. No studies explicitly reported on the total volume of fluid required for each patient to consume.

3.3.2.3.

## Dietetic Involvement
Dietitian involvement in prescribing and monitoring of EEN was explicitly described in two studies (20.0%) [ , ]. Their role in this study included prescribing EEN and monitoring adherence and tolerability of therapy.

3.3.3.

## Inclusion of Other Table Foods and Fluids
One study (10.0%) permitted coffee, tea and mineral water as well as sugar‐free chewing gum to be consumed during EEN [ ]. Three studies (30.0%) explicitly forbid the consumption of any other table food and fluids aside from EEN and water [ , , ].

3.3.4.

## Delivery and Adjustment Protocol
3.3.4.1.

## Route of Administration
In two studies (25.0%), EEN was provided via a nasogastric feeding tube and feeds were uptitrated to the target caloric requirement over 3–5 days [ , ]. EEN was given orally in six studies (60.0%) [ , , , , , ] and two studies did not report the route of administration.

3.3.4.2.

## Therapy Adjustments During Treatment
In one study (10.0%), rate of nasogastric feeds infusion was reduced if patients developed symptoms or poor tolerability to feeds [ ]. In two studies (20.0%), if patients were not able to tolerate oral EEN, nasogastric tube would be inserted for nasogastric feeding [ , ].

3.3.4.3.

## Diet After EEN Therapy
Three studies reported on what diet patients would be transitioned to after completing EEN therapy, these included normal ward diet (2 study, 20.0%) [ , ] and a low residue diet (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. Pace of transitioning to diet or timeframe for the use of partial enteral nutrition post‐EEN was not reported.

3.3.5.

## Monitoring and Assessment of Disease Activity
There were significant variations in the disease parameters and timing of assessments. Clinical, biochemical, radiological and endoscopic disease activity assessments are summarised in Table  .

3.3.6.

## Monitoring of Nutritional Indices
Nutritional parameters were only reported in one study (10.0%) [ ] which included body weight, percent of ideal body weight, triceps skinfold thickness, mid‐arm muscle circumference. These were measured at day 0 and 14. Other studies did not report on measuring nutritional indices.

3.4.

## Safety, Adherence and Tolerability of EEN

There was variation in the definition and assessment methodology for adherence, compliance and tolerability. These are summarised in Table  .
 3.4.1.

## Assessment of Adherence and Tolerability
Adherence to therapy was explicitly reported in three studies (30.0%) [ , , ]. Its assessment varied between studies, parameters assessed included whether patients met ≥ 80% of their target caloric requirements (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]; and whether patients excluded all other foods or drinks while on EEN (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]; or whether patients average daily consumption of calorie and protein over the course of treatment met predefined adherence categories (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. One study also used the terms adherence and tolerability interchangeably to describe the same parameter [ ].
In most studies, tolerability was reported with relation to the discontinuation of therapy and inability to complete the target duration for therapy (9 studies, 90.0%) [ , , , , , , , ]. One study did not comment on discontinuation but specified the number of patients who reported poor tolerability without specifying further [ ]. Furthermore, in three studies (30.0%), tolerability was also assessed by the volume of EEN consumed relative to their target caloric requirement [ , , ]. In two studies (20%), patients were considered to tolerate treatment if they consumed ≥ 80% of their target requirement [ , ]. Methods to measure tolerability included the use of self‐reported consumption diary (1 study, 10.0%) [ ] and dietitian‐assessed daily caloric and protein intake (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. In another study, self‐reported tolerability was assessed via online survey at the end of therapy (1 study, 10.0%) [ ].
Overall, the pooled discontinuation rate was 3% (95% CI 0–9, I
2 = 59.71%) across all included studies (see Figure  ). When grouped by type of formulation, the overall discontinuation rate for polymeric EEN was 2% (3 studies, 95% CI 0–16), semi‐elemental EEN was 16% (1 study, 95% CI 7–32) and elemental EEN was 1% (3 studies, 95% CI 0–8) (see Figure  ). Two studies did not specify their formulation [ , ]. Sensitivity analysis did not significantly alter the estimated rate after removal of cohort studies or cohort studies with a higher risk of bias in the outcome assessment criteria of the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale.

3.4.2.

## Adverse Effects of EEN

Several studies (70.0%) reported on adverse effects experienced while on EEN therapy [ , , , , , , ]. The data from one study could not be separated from the CD cohort and were therefore not included in analysis [ ]. Severity of adverse effects was graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) (2 study, 20.0%) [ , ] as well as predefined categories of severity (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. Table  outlines the methods of assessing adherence and tolerability reported by the included studies.
Reported adverse effects include: worsening diarrhoea (3 studies, 30.0%) [ , , ], nausea and vomiting (3 studies, 30.0%) [ , , ], bloating (1 study, 10.0%) [ ], abdominal pain (1 study, 10.0%) [ ] and hyperglycaemia (1 study, 10.0%) [ ]. Unpalatability of EEN diet was reported in three studies (30.0%) [ , , ]. Overall, the pooled rate of adverse effects attributable to EEN was 12% (95% CI 6–18, I
2 = 0.00%) (see Figure  ). Pooled rates did not differ significantly when compared between formulations of EEN (Cochran's Q p = 0.886 for heterogeneity between groups).

## Summary of Findings
In this systematic review, the use of EEN was evaluated as a therapeutic modality in the treatment of adults with ulcerative colitis. Two RCTs examined the effect of EEN on inducing clinical remission in ulcerative colitis and there was no difference in rate of remission at median follow‐up of 14 days (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.71 to 1.85; GRADE very low certainty). Corticosteroid failure in ASUC was examined in two RCTs and no difference in rate of failure was found (follow‐up range 5–14 days; RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.48–1.20, GRADE very low certainty). In the treatment of ASUC, there was also no difference detected in the need for colectomy in these patients during their admission (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.51–1.51, GRADE very low certainty). The pooled rate of successful medical rescue in ASUC after EEN and SoC was 78% during their admission (95% CI 59–97, GRADE low certainty). The pooled discontinuation rate of EEN in UC was 3% (95% CI 0.0–9.0, GRADE very low certainty) and the rate of adverse effects while on EEN was 12% (95% CI 6–18, GRADE very low certainty). The most common reasons that culminated in lowering of the certainty of evidence included the lack of blinding in the assessment of outcomes as well as significant heterogeneity in study design, outcome definition and controls. ## Discussion
This was the first systematic review to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of EEN in adults with active UC. EEN as an adjunctive therapeutic modality in active UC was no different from comparator interventions in terms of achieving clinical response or remission rates. Furthermore, when utilised as an adjunct to corticosteroid therapy in the treatment of ASUC, EEN did not reduce rates of corticosteroid failure or colectomy. However, EEN was well tolerated in this population and a high completion rate and tolerability were demonstrated across studies. Meta‐analysis identified significant heterogeneity in the outcomes and is likely related to variations in trial design, definition of primary and secondary outcomes, treatment protocols and available therapies.
In comparison to CD where the efficacy of EEN for clinical outcomes is clearly established [ , , , , , , , ], this review did not demonstrate significant improvement in clinical remission, response or biomarkers in UC, nor benefit in corticosteroid failure or colectomy rates when used in ASUC. However, several factors need to be considered when interpreting these efficacy data. The included studies span a period of almost five decades, during which time access to advanced therapies as well as the understanding of disease has significantly progressed. These advances are reflected in the colectomy rates where older studies quote a colectomy rate of 47.8%–52.3% [ ] compared to 0%–7.4% in contemporary studies [ , , ]. Furthermore, the studies are broadly heterogeneous, in terms of EEN regimen, comparator, concurrent therapy and outcomes assessed. Despite these limitations, signals of potential benefit in using adjunctive EEN must be acknowledged. When analysed per protocol, the recent RCT by Sahu et al. (2021) in ASUC demonstrated reduced rates of corticosteroid failure, shorter duration of hospitalisation, reduced colectomy rate during index admission [ ]. This effect was observed despite the early cessation of the trial due to the COVID‐19 pandemic. While no similar contemporary study has been conducted to affirm these findings, the benefit demonstrated in these important endpoints highlights the crucial need for further research in this area.
The capacity for EEN to improve serum albumin levels in patients with active UC was a noteworthy finding of this review. The end of therapy albumin was found to be significantly higher than control across all studies where EEN was used. Albumin is a prognostic marker for clinical outcomes in UC, including the need for medication escalation, mucosal healing as well as risk of needing colectomy [ , , , ]. However, contrary to the proposed advantages of albumin, the most recent trial in ASUC by Mundhra et al. (2024), reported no improvement in outcomes when targeting an albumin level of 35 g/L using both EEN and albumin infusion compared to EEN alone. These data may signal that albumin is not directly involved in the improvement of disease but merely a surrogate marker of reduction in inflammatory burden as well as reduced intestinal permeability and protein loss [ ].
This reviewed highlighted significant variability in EEN protocols across included studies. Duration of therapy varied with indication and ASUC patients were prescribed courses < 7 days, whereas those with moderate–severe UC were prescribed a course of at least 7–14 days. Overall, the duration of EEN was substantially shorter than the 4–8 weeks of therapy often used in CD, likely accounted for by the preponderance of patient with ASUC receiving EEN only while in hospital [ ]. Among the included studies, the most common type of EEN formulation was elemental and polymeric enteral nutrition. Nutritional assessment, a necessary component of diet therapy provision [ , ], was not adequately reported in trials, nor were caloric and protein requirement calculations or BMI. Further studies in this area can elicit key nutritional markers that may guide the prescription and adjustment of EEN in UC.
Contrary to findings from studies on EEN use in CD, tolerability and completion of therapy was high in UC patients. A major concern and obstacle to the implementation of EEN in clinical practice, particularly in adults with CD, had been the perceived lack of adherence to the therapy and this has been demonstrated to be a key predictor of therapeutic efficacy in CD [ , ]. Adherence to EEN has been reported at 50%–80% in adult CD due to poor tolerability [ ]. Encouragingly, this review suggests that the tolerability of EEN in active UC is relatively high, with low rates of reported adverse effects and therapy discontinuation. Discrepancy in reported historical adherence may be explained by differences in disease pathophysiology between UC and CD, such as the presence of stricturing disease, or may relate to differences in the medical and dietetic management received by patients. However, at present, data are insufficient to conclude whether adherence or completion of therapy translates to meaningful improvement in outcomes in UC. Reassuringly, no signal for harm has been detected by this systematic review with low rates of adverse effects or serious effects reported.
Future studies on EEN in UC should employ consistent protocols for its administration and monitoring. Key considerations include standardised methods for the assessment of adherence and tolerability, as well as clear guidelines for modifying EEN in cases of adverse effects or intolerance. If further studies were conducted in ASUC where rapid clinical deterioration is a concern, a duration of 1–2 weeks of EEN as an adjunctive therapy could be trialled. Dietetic involvement is essential, mirroring the use of EEN in CD, where dietetic supervision is advocated by international guidelines [ ]. Clinical assessment and endpoints should align with current standards of care, including day 3 assessment of corticosteroid response in ASUC, success rates of medical rescue and colectomy‐free survival.
The strength of this systematic review lies in its novelty, comprehensive search strategy, inclusion criteria and data extraction process. Outcomes and methodology for analysis were determined a priori and prospectively registered to minimise bias and ensure the reliability of the findings. Nevertheless, there are several limitations to this review. First, only a very small number of studies have investigated EEN in UC and importantly, most of the included studies were conducted for ASUC with few conducted in mild–moderate UC. As such, conclusions cannot be drawn about whether EEN has any effect in less severe forms of UC. Furthermore, with significant variations in the methodology, outcomes and access to therapy, meta‐analyses on prespecified outcomes were limited. Nonetheless, meta‐analysis was performed on both observational and RCT data to yield clinical insight [ , , , ]. Another limitation in this review is the overall concern for bias in the included studies. Treatment blinding is a challenge in diet interventional studies and use of a placebo diet or sham diet requires rigorous development [ ]. Quality assessment of the studies identified all but one study to have an unclear risk of bias in at least one assessment domain.

5.

## Conclusion
This review has identified significant knowledge gaps in the use of EEN for management of active UC. Based on the current studies included in this review, there is a lack of evidence to support the use of EEN as an adjunctive therapy in active UC. However, the evidence base was too heterogenous to draw any conclusions with confidence. Further high‐quality trials utilising harmonised protocols for prescribing and monitoring EEN are needed, capturing contemporary clinical, biomarker and endoscopic trial endpoints at appropriate timepoints, as well as standardised measurement of adherence, tolerability and duration of therapy.
