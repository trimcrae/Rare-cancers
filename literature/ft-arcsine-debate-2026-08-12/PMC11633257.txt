## Abstract

## Background:
Drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) significantly increases the risk of depression, yet research on mental health in this population remains limited.

## Aim:
This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the prevalence of depression among multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) patients, emphasizing the necessity for integrated mental health care.

## Methods:
A systematic search across PubMed, Cochrane, EBSCOhost, and Google Scholar identified 536 articles, which were screened for eligibility after removing duplicates. Prevalence data were analyzed using R programming, with back-transformation applied to enhance accuracy. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed with the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist, and the study protocol is registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023449156).

## Results:
Ultimately, twelve studies involving 3,258 participants were included in the analysis. The pooled prevalence of depression among MDR-TB patients was found to be 54% (95% CI: 42%-65%). Although substantial heterogeneity was noted (I² = 72%; P < 0.01), excluding one study (Walker et al.) due to high Cook’s distance revised the prevalence to 55% (95% CI: 43%-68%), with reduced heterogeneity (I² = 68%; P < 0.01). Subgroup analyses indicated minimal variability among studies using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (52%, 95% CI: 40%-64%) and substantial variability among those employing the Patient Health Questionnaire (54%, 95% CI: 30%-79%). Funnel plot analysis and Egger’s test revealed no significant publication bias (P = 0.2105).

## Conclusion:
This study highlights a high prevalence of depression among MDR-TB patients, underscoring the urgent need for integrated mental health care strategies tailored for this vulnerable population. Further research is essential to refine these strategies effectively.

Keywords: Depression, MDR-TB, mental health, meta-analysis, prevalence, South Asia, tuberculosis

## INTRODUCTION
In 2021, tuberculosis (TB) claimed the lives of 1.6 million individuals globally and currently ranks as one of the top 13 causes of death worldwide and is the next most prominent infectious killer, trailing only behind COVID-19. More than 10 million people contracted TB in the year, out of which approximately 56% were men, 32% were women, and 11.3% were children.[ ]
The burden of TB cases in South Asia, encompassing nations such as India, Pakistan, Myanmar, Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives, is a matter of concern and is often underestimated. This region bears nearly 40% of the global TB burden, with 4,290,500 new cases reported in 2021. Additionally, South Asia also accounts for a disproportionate share of multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) cases, with 157,073 incidents, making up 35% of the global burden.[ ] Among the South Asian countries, India holds the highest percentage of the total MDR-TB burden, with approximately 76% of cases, followed by Pakistan (10%) and Myanmar at (6%).[ ]
The rise of drug resistance in TB can be linked to improper utilization of TB medications, including incorrect prescriptions from doctors, the availability of substandard drugs, or patients prematurely stopping their treatment. Although MDR-TB is treatable and curable through the utilization of second-line drugs, only about one in three individuals with drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) were able to access treatment in 2021.[ ]
Treating DR-TB poses numerous challenges due to the demanding nature of the regimens involved and the psychological stressors faced by individuals with this condition. The treatment for MDR-TB typically spans at least 9 months and often leads to various adverse events. The usage of core drugs within the treatment regimen, such as isoniazid, fluoroquinolones, and cycloserine, has been linked to adverse psychiatric effects.[ , , ] Cycloserine, in particular, has been identified as a common contributor to psychiatric complications.[ ] Furthermore, individuals with MDR-TB encounter various psychosocial stressors, including stigma, discrimination, and financial constraints, which may lead to the onset of depression and anxiety. The long-lasting nature of the disease and uncertainty about treatment outcomes make people with MDR-TB feel hopeless and stressed, increasing the risk of depression. Having depression and MDR-TB together makes each condition worse, leading to poorer health and lower quality of life for patients. Therefore, it is imperative to address both the physical and mental aspects of MDR-TB to ensure comprehensive care and successful treatment for affected individuals.
A systematic review and meta-analysis by Alene et al.[ ] include studies from across the globe from the year 2000 to 2017; they looked at mental health issues and quality of life in MDR-TB patients including depression and found out that the overall pooled prevalence of depression was 25%. Another study in 2020 examined depression rates among all TB patients, regardless of drug resistance.[ ] Our current study focuses on depression rates among MDR-TB patients in South Asian countries by combining the latest research on the topic from the year 2017 onward. Since South Asia has many TB patients, it is important to understand depression’s prevalence and impact in this group. Our research aims to improve the overall care of MDR-TB patients in South Asia and improve treatment results.

## Study objective
To determine the pooled prevalence of depression among MDR-TB patients in South Asia.

## MATERIAL AND METHODS
Research question: What is the pooled prevalence of depression among patients diagnosed with DR-TB in the South Asian region?

## Databases included and search strategy
We incorporated four databases: PubMed, Cochrane, EBSCOhost, and Google Scholar. Additionally, we explored preprint servers and individual journals, thoroughly examining the references of included studies for potential findings. The registration of this study in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) is under the reference number CRD42023449156. Our search utilized keywords such as ‘Drug resistant tuberculosis’, ‘depression’, and ‘depressive disorder’. Details of the adjusted search terms as per searched electronic databases are mentioned in [ ]. Articles were organized using Mendeley Desktop V2.85.0 software, facilitating citation management, duplicate removal, and coordination of the review process.

## Data extraction and management

## Screening of studies
Title abstract screening
Two authors independently assessed the title abstracts of the studies obtained through the aforementioned systematic search, applying the eligibility criteria to identify articles for full-text screening. In cases of disagreement regarding the inclusion of a study for full-text review, the authors engaged in discussion to reach a consensus and determine eligibility [ - ].
Full-text screening and data extraction
Two authors independently reviewed the potentially eligible full-text articles for suitability and then extracted data from these. In case of disagreement at any step, the authors conversed among themselves to build consensus. The third author decided on any unresolved contradictions. All articles published from 2017 till May 2023 were considered for this study, aiming to provide updated information. A brief summary of the included studies is mentioned in and geographic distribution of the studies included in the analysis mentioned in .[ , , , , , , , , , , , ]
 Two authors independently conducted data extraction. A final data extraction table was created using Microsoft Excel for subsequent analysis. The extracted information from the eligible studies included the author’s name, study location, publication year, study design, number of cases, and positive cases. The entire process of literature search, screening, data extraction, systematic review, and meta-analysis was documented following the Preferred Reporting Standard of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA-2020) guidelines to ensure scientific rigor. The process was visually depicted using a PRISMA flowchart [ ] and is detailed in [ ].
 Study outcome: The prevalence of depression among DR-TB patients in South Asia was estimated.

## Quality assessment
Two authors separately assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data. This assessment was also independently conducted by other two investigators. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion [ 
 ]. The purpose of this checklist is to evaluate the quality of a study and assess how well it has addressed potential bias in its design, conduct, and analysis. Following this assessment, sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding studies deemed to be of poor quality.[ ]

## Statistical analysis
The meta-analysis was conducted using R software.[ ] A forest plot was constructed to visualize the overall prevalence alongside the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance was determined at a level of P < 0.05. The I2 statistic was employed to assess between-study heterogeneity. A random effects model was utilized when the I2 value exceeded 50%, indicating substantial heterogeneity among the studies.[ ] In order to stabilize variance and address the impact of extreme prevalence values, we conducted a back-transformation after performing the double arcsine transformation in this meta-analysis. This strategy guaranteed the stability and lucidity of the final estimates while facilitating pooling across studies and reducing heterogeneity. To improve the robustness and interpretability of our results, we present both the overall and subgroup results following back-transformation.[ ]
Depression prevalence was calculated by dividing the count of patients screening positive on standardized scales by the total study participants. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and doi plots. To detect outliers in our meta-analysis, we performed an analysis using Cook’s distance plot. This method allowed us to identify influential studies that may disproportionately affect the overall results. The Cook’s distance plot [ ] provides a visual representation of the impact of each study, aiding in the assessment of potential outliers. The R programming language (v4.0) was utilized for all statistical analyses and graphical representations.
 

## RESULTS
The systematic search across multiple databases initially identified 536 articles, which were reduced to 176 after eliminating duplicates. Title and abstract screening further refined the selection to 64 articles. Following a thorough full-text review, 52 articles were excluded due to irrelevant outcomes, inappropriate study design, or insufficient data.
Finally, 12 studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, each documenting the prevalence of depression among DR-TB patients in South Asia. Various validated tools were used to assess depression, with the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) being the most commonly used in five studies, followed by the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) in four studies. One study each used the SCID DSM-IV, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D). Prevalence rates varied widely, ranging from as low as 10% in Myanmar to as high as 63% in Nepal, with the highest rates found in Nepal (63%) and Bangladesh (34%) [ ].[ , , , , , , , , , , , ]
Our meta-analysis focused on 3258 individuals with DR-TB across these 12 studies. Of these individuals, 661 screened positive for depression. The pooled prevalence of depression, after back-transforming from the double arcsine transformation, was found to be 54% (95% CI: 42%–65%). Due to substantial heterogeneity among the studies (I² =72%; P < 0.01), a random-effects model was applied to account for this variability [ ].
 Sensitivity Analysis A sensitivity analysis, including the assessment of Cook’s distance, indicated that the study by Walker et al. had a notably high Cook’s distance, suggesting it exerted a disproportionate influence on the meta-analytic results.[ ] Other studies showed Cook’s distances within acceptable ranges, indicating consistent influence on the pooled estimates [ ]. The funnel plot was symmetrical, and Egger’s test showed no significant publication bias with a P value of 0.2105, and the bias estimate of -2.3631 indicated some asymmetry that was not statistically significant [ - Supplementary File, ].

Subgroup Meta-Analyses

Excluding Outliers: Excluding the study by Walker et al., which was identified as an outlier through Cook’s distance analysis, the meta-analysis of 11 studies demonstrated a reduction in heterogeneity.[ ] The revised pooled prevalence estimate was 55% (95% CI: 43%–68%) with an I² of 68% (P < 0.01), reflecting decreased variability and improved consistency in prevalence estimates across the remaining studies [ a].
PHQ-9 Assessment Tool: For studies utilizing the PHQ-9 assessment tool, the meta-analysis of five studies yielded a pooled back-transformed prevalence estimate of 54% (95% CI: 30%–79%). Heterogeneity analysis revealed substantial variability (I² =88%, P < 0.0655), indicating diverse outcomes among populations and settings [ b].
HAM-D Assessment Tool: Among studies using the HAM-D assessment tool, which included four studies, the pooled estimate of back-transformed prevalence was 52% (95% CI: 40%–64%). This analysis showed minimal variability (I² =0.0%, P = 0.54), suggesting consistent findings across different populations and settings [ c].

 

## DISCUSSION

## Prevalence landscape
Our meta-analysis reveals a significant prevalence of depression among individuals with DR-TB in South Asia, with a pooled prevalence of 54% (95% CI: 42%–65%). This figure starkly contrasts with the global depression rate of approximately 5% reported by the World Health Organization (WHO).[ ] A review by Alene et al. (2018)[ ] on mental health disorders, including depression among multidrug-resistant patients, included studies from around the world conducted between the year 2000 and 2017. They found that the pooled prevalence of depression was around 25%. The prevalence found in our study is notably higher than the prevalence reported by them. This discrepancy highlights a severe mental health burden within this population, which may be influenced by regional and contextual factors unique to South Asia.

## Exploring the elevated rates
The markedly higher depression rates observed in our study may be attributed to several factors related to the chronic nature of DR-TB. The prolonged and demanding treatment regimens associated with MDR-TB can impose substantial psychological strain, potentially exceeding the challenges faced by individuals with nonresistant tuberculosis.[ , ] The chronicity of DR-TB may contribute to heightened stress, anxiety, and ultimately higher rates of depression among this specific population.[ ]
This observation underscores the urgency of addressing not only the physical aspects of DR-TB but also the associated mental health challenges. Comprehensive care strategies should be devised to mitigate the psychological impact of chronic morbidity, thereby fostering improved overall well-being and treatment outcomes for individuals in this vulnerable population.

## Regional dynamics and unique study influences
Our meta-analysis reveals notable regional dynamics and unique study influences that impact the overall prevalence estimates of depression among individuals with DR-TB. Within the 12 studies included, a distinct cluster emerges from Pakistan, where prevalence estimates range from 12% to 33%. Particularly influential is the study conducted by Walker et al. (2018)[ ] in Punjab, Pakistan, which reported a 16% prevalence among 1279 participants. This study notably influences our overall estimate due to its extensive sample size, emphasizing the need to recognize and address challenges specific to subgroups within our studied population.
The variability in prevalence rates across studies from different regions underscores the importance of considering regional and contextual factors when interpreting the results. Furthermore, recognizing and understanding these regional variations is crucial for developing targeted interventions and support strategies that address the unique needs of different subgroups affected by DR-TB.

## Effect of study tools
Studies such as Laxmeshwar et al. (2022), Theingi et al. (2021), Retnakumar et al. (2021), Huque et al. (2020), Shrestha et al. (2020), and Walker et al. (2018) employ the same study tool (PHQ-9) to identify depression, and the transformed pooled prevalence for these studies was also found to be 54%.[ , , , , , ] Similarly, the pooled prevalence of studies done by Ayub et al. (2023), Sharma et al. (2022), Singh et al. (2019), and Javaid et al. (2017) utilizing HAM-D for depression assessment was found to be 52%.[ , , , ] [ ] The consistency of these findings mirrors the impactful methodologies identified in the current study. There were differences in individual proportion that can be attributed to differences in measurement tools, coupled with potentially distinct diagnostic criteria, cultural influences, or regional nuances, which contribute to the observed heterogeneity in our meta-analysis.

## Contextual interpretation of results
Our results underscore the significance of interpreting prevalence rates within the context of study-specific factors. For instance, lower prevalence rates reported by Walker et al. (2018) and Javaid et al. (2017) may stem from variations in cultural nuances influencing symptom expression.[ , ] Conversely, higher rates observed in studies like Shrestha et al. (2020) from Nepal and Huque et al. (2020) from Bangladesh highlight the substantial impact of regional contexts on mental health assessments.[ , ]

## Context-specific mental health strategies
This highlights the necessity for context-specific mental health strategies, recognizing the diverse sociocultural, economic, and healthcare landscapes in the region.[ , , ] Developing targeted interventions that consider these unique contextual factors is imperative for improving mental health outcomes among individuals dealing with DR-TB in South Asia.

## Methodological considerations
Our meta-analysis revealed that subgroup analyses based on assessment tools, such as PHQ-9 and HAM-D, illustrated distinct prevalence patterns, which were influenced by the choice of these tools. Specifically, the pooled prevalence for studies using the HAM-D tool was 52% (95% CI: 40%–64%) with minimal heterogeneity (I² =0.0%, P = 0.54), while studies using the PHQ-9 tool reported a higher pooled prevalence of 54% (95% CI: 30%–79%) but with substantial heterogeneity (I² =88%, P = 0.0655). These variations highlight the impact that different assessment tools can have on prevalence estimates.
Furthermore, after the exclusion of outliers, particularly the study by Walker et al., sensitivity analyses showed a reduction in heterogeneity, with the overall pooled prevalence estimate reaching 55% (95% CI: 43%–68%) and an I² of 68% (P < 0.01). These methodological refinements underscore the importance of conducting sensitivity analyses and applying appropriate transformations, such as the double arcsine transformation, to stabilize variance and improve the reliability and applicability of meta-analytic findings.

## Publication bias and sensitivity analysis
Egger’s test, used to assess the presence of publication bias, yielded a P value of 0.2105, which suggests no significant publication bias in this meta-analysis. Although this result indicates some asymmetry in the funnel plot, it is not statistically significant. This finding suggests that while there may be slight publication-related influences, they are not substantial enough to undermine the overall robustness of our results. However, it is essential to consider potential publication bias cautiously when interpreting the findings as even minimal bias can influence the synthesized prevalence estimates. Sensitivity analyses, including Cook’s distance evaluation, further reinforced the robustness of the results by identifying and addressing outlier studies that could disproportionately impact the overall meta-analytic outcomes.

## Implications for clinical practice and policy
Our findings, with a pooled prevalence of depression at 55% and heterogeneity at 68%, underscore the need for tailored mental health interventions for individuals with DR-TB in South Asia. The remaining variability highlights the importance of considering regional differences in healthcare practices and cultural factors.

## CONCLUSION
Our meta-analysis offers a comprehensive estimate of the prevalence of depression among MDR-TB patients in South Asia, revealing a substantial burden of this comorbidity. The final pooled prevalence, after back-transformation from the double arcsine method, was 55%, with a reduced heterogeneity of 68%. This highlights the critical need for integrating mental health services within TB treatment programs to address the high prevalence of depression in this population. Future research should focus on large multicentric studies using standardized assessment tools to further refine these estimates and explore factors contributing to the remaining variability.

## Strengths and limitations
A key strength of this study is its comprehensive analysis, offering detailed estimates of depression prevalence among MDR-TB patients in South Asia, and the use of advanced statistical methods, such as the back-transformation and subgroup analysis, to manage variability. Although significant heterogeneity and potential publication bias are limitations, these were carefully addressed through rigorous statistical approaches, enhancing the reliability of our findings. Furthermore, it is difficult to determine the temporal association between MDR-TB and depression because of the cross-sectional nature of included studies. Although our study concentrated on quantitative prevalence estimates, qualitative research may be used in future research to explore psychological components that can potentially explain and impact the outcome.
