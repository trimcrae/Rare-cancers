## Abstract

## Background.
Potential kidney donors with active hepatitis B virus (HBV: positive hepatitis B surface antigen [HBsAg] and/or nucleic acid test [NAT]) are usually declined for HBsAg-negative recipients. Safety may be improved by recipient vaccination and/or antivirals, thereby increasing transplantation opportunities. We quantified HBV transmission risk in this setting to inform donation decisions.

## Methods.
Systematic review and meta-analysis (MEDLINE, to November 2024) of cohorts comprised of kidney donors with active HBV intentionally used for HBsAg-negative recipients. Transmission was defined as new HBsAg or NAT positivity posttransplant. Transmission proportions and exact 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled using generalized linear mixed models with logistic transformation and random effects.

## Results.
We included 20 cohorts involving 600 HBsAg-negative recipients from donors with active HBV. Most donors were living (52%), with negative NAT (60%). Most recipients had positive surface antibody (>10 IU/L, 86%); many were core antibody positive (47%). Antiviral prophylaxis was given to 49% recipients, varying in type, duration, and strategy. There were 29 of 600 HBV transmissions, mostly transient low-level viremia only (62%). The pooled transmission rate was 4.0% (95% CI, 1.8%-8.3%) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) but some between-study variance (Tau2 = 1.21). Transmission rates were higher where all donors had positive NAT (16.0%; 95% CI, 10.2%-24.3%), and lower where all recipients were living (0.8%; 95% CI, 0.1%-6.4%) or had positive surface antibody (1.4%; 95% CI, 0.2%-8.8%). Three deaths because of HBV transmissions occurred, all among recipients not taking posttransplant antiviral prophylaxis.

## Conclusions.
Given low transmission rates and mitigating strategies, kidney transplantation may be considered from donors with active HBV, with donor/recipient risk stratification, consent, and monitoring/prophylaxis.

## INTRODUCTION
Demand for kidney transplantation exceeds the supply of organs. Many potentially appropriate donors are declined because of perceived biovigilance risks. The intentional use of organs from donors with some infections is accepted for seronegative recipients, including cytomegalovirus and, more recently, hepatitis C, with appropriate prophylaxis, monitoring, and/or treatment. , 
Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a blood-borne DNA virus, which can be transmitted via organ transplantation. HBV infection can lead to fulminant hepatic failure or chronic infection, with complications including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Given increased risks of HBV infection with immunosuppression, pretransplant testing of both potential organ donors and recipients is recommended. , Tests include hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb), which indicates HBV exposure, and hepatitis B surface antibody (HBsAb), which provides immunity, arising from vaccination or cleared infection. A positive HBsAb titer is usually defined at 10 IU/L4. Active HBV infection is usually characterized by positive hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and/or nucleic acid testing (NAT). The viral load in chronic infection varies with the immune response, defined by the presence or absence of hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg, typically associated with higher viral load), and antiviral treatments, which suppress viral replication ; hence, NAT can be undetectable in active infection. Conversely, active HBV may rarely have detectable NAT in the absence of HBsAg, including acute HBV infections in the serological window period, or occult infection (associated with HBcAb positivity, HBsAg mutation, and low viral load). 
Kidney transplantation can be safely performed where donors have past HBV exposure (without active infection), or for recipients with past or active HBV infection, with appropriate prophylaxis/monitoring. However, recommendations around transplantation from donors with active HBV to recipients without active infection are variable globally and generally remain cautious. , , This is despite effective strategies for transplant recipients to mitigate transmission risk including pretransplant vaccination and posttransplant antiviral therapies. Several recent case series suggest low transmission rates and good outcomes. - However, rare cases of adverse recipient outcomes can lead to a heightened perception of risk and risk aversion among clinicians. 
We aimed to understand the absolute risk of infection transmission by synthesizing data from cohorts of intentional kidney transplantation from donors with active HBV to recipients without active HBV.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis, registered prospectively on PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/; CRD42023495003). Inclusion criteria were studies describing HBV transmission rates among recipients without active hepatitis B (including those with past HBV exposure, vaccination, and those naive to HBV) receiving a kidney transplant from donors with active hepatitis B; all included recipients were considered at risk of infection. Active hepatitis B was defined by HBsAg positivity and/or hepatitis B NAT positivity. Both deceased and living donors were included. Recipients of multiple organ transplants were excluded, as organ type can influence the probability of infection transmission. Where the outcomes of the specific recipients from donors with active hepatitis B could not be identified (eg, cohorts with mixed donor HBV status without stratified results), cohorts were excluded. Studies were also required to report a cohort of >1 donor for inclusion in synthesis, to ensure an appropriate denominator and reduce reporting bias. However, identified case reports were included separately. Where the donor’s active HBV status was not known before transplant (unintentional transplantation to recipients at risk), records were excluded from main analysis, as the lack of mitigating strategies would influence transmission rate and not reflect contemporary practice; these records are summarized separately. Duplicate or overlapping cohort reports were noted and excluded to avoid double-counting, although where relevant, additional information was extracted to inform analysis.
We searched MEDLINE via OVID on November 5, 2024 (Table S1A, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327). In brief, we combined synonyms for “kidney transplantation” and “hepatitis B.” To identify unpublished trials, we performed a supplemental search of the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (Table S1B, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327) on November 11, 2024. Additional relevant articles were identified from searching bibliographies of articles from the initial search. No language or time restriction was applied.
After de-duplication in sysrev, titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility independently by 2 authors (R.D., E.G.), with disagreements resolved through consensus. Full-text screening was then performed by 3 authors (R.D., E.G., K.M.J.W.).

## Outcomes
The primary outcome was the proportion of HBV transmission events, defined by the new presence posttransplant of hepatitis B DNA or HBsAg in the recipient on at least 1 occasion. Secondary outcomes were death because of transmitted infection, adverse liver outcomes related to transmitted infection (eg, hepatitis, liver failure, liver transplantation, liver cancer), death-censored kidney transplant failure, and overall survival.

## Data Extraction/Abstraction
Data were extracted by a single author (K.M.J.W.), and checked for accuracy by a coauthor (J.A.H. or T.S.); disagreements were resolved by consensus. Extracted data included study details (author, country, year of transplants), characteristics of donors and recipients (deceased or living donation, serology, and virology details), prophylaxis strategies (stratification, type, timing, duration), and outcome data.

## Bias Assessment
Quality and risk of bias assessment was performed using a tailored version of the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Instrument for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data, assessing bias across 8 of 9 standardized domains (1 domain deemed not applicable), by a single author (K.M.J.W.). A random subset (25%) was checked by a second author, with plan to expand to dual extraction for all records in the event of any discordance. The overall risk of bias of each study was assessed by considering the number of domains a low, medium/unclear, or high risk of bias.

## Analysis
First, HBV transmissions and deaths because of transmissions were described. The overall transmission proportion was then synthesized with a meta-analysis of proportions, using the metaprop command from meta package. 
While the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation is the most common transformation applied to meta-analyses of proportions because of its ability to stabilize variance where estimates are close to zero, concerns have been raised regarding its performance on back transformation, for instance, where there is substantial variability in sample sizes. Hence, we used generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with logit transformation and random effects to produce the pooled proportion with 95% exact confidence intervals (CIs) and 90% prediction intervals. GLMMs have shown more robustness and accuracy in these scenarios but do not provide individual study weights. We also recalculated using meta-analysis with logit transformation and continuity correction for zero values to assess the consistency of pooled transmission proportion estimates. 
We also stratified meta-analyses to determine the relative contribution of different donor and recipient factors to heterogeneity. Further sensitivity analyses were performed excluding studies identified to be at high risk of bias and small studies, examining for changes in synthesis results. We also performed a leave-one-out analysis, where we would exclude each study, 1 at a time, from the meta-analysis and report the new estimated pooled prevalence. Publication bias was assessed visually using a funnel plot, and quantitatively using Egger’s and Begg’s tests for bias.
Excluded studies describing HBV transmissions in case reports, or where donors with active hepatitis B donated to recipients without active hepatitis B unintentionally, were also summarized.
For secondary outcomes, incidence was calculated per 100 person-years using 95% Poisson exact CIs.
Data were analyzed using R Studio (Version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024) and Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, LLC, College Station, TX, 2023).

## RESULTS
After identifying 3211 records from MEDLINE, 9 from reference mining, and 26 records from the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, 2723 were excluded on title and abstract screening, and 503 at full-text screening (Figure ). In total, 20 studies were included with 600 kidney recipients. An additional 8 case reports described 9 HBsAg-negative recipients of kidneys from donors with active hepatitis B, and 3 records (including 2 reports with 1 donor each) described 8 HBsAg-negative recipients unintentionally transplanted with kidneys from donors with active hepatitis B.
 

## Characteristics of Included Studies
The included studies were published between 1988 and 2024 (Table ), with median recipient cohort size being 14 (interquartile interval, 6–50). Publications were from all continents, the most common countries being Turkey (5 studies), the United States (4 studies), and China (3 studies).
 Cohorts were a mix of deceased kidney donors only (7/20), living donors only (5/20), or mixed donor types (6/20), while donor type was not stated for 2 of 20 studies. The specific number of deceased donors was not always described in articles. Considering that living donors can only donate to 1 recipient but deceased donors can donate to either 1 or 2 recipients, the total number of donors was between 454 and 595. For simplicity, we assumed the maximum possible number of deceased donors throughout our analysis, that is, we assumed that each donor donated 1 kidney only (unless otherwise stated). There were similar numbers of living (52%, 303/579) and deceased donors (48%, 276/579; Table ). Donor HBsAg was reported for 18 of 20 studies with 518 of 523 (99%) donors HBsAg positive. Of the 5 HBsAg-negative donors, 1 was HBsAg positive during workup but resolved to HBsAg negative at transplant, and 4 donors were NAT positive but HBsAg negative. Two cohorts of 72 donors were defined by HBV DNA positivity only and did not report HBsAg status. Donor HBV DNA was reported in 13 of 20 studies and was available for 89% donors (533/595); among these, most donors (319/533, 60%) did not have detectable HBV DNA. E-antigen status (HBeAg) was reported in 10 of 20 studies and was available for 50% donors (299/595): the vast majority were HBeAg negative (284/299, 95%).
 Recipient surface antibody (HBsAb) status was reported in 18 of 20 studies for 94% recipients (562/600): most had positive (>10 IU/mL) titers (485/562, 86%). Recipient core antibody (HBcAb) status was reported in 13 of 20 studies for 69% recipients (412/600); of these, almost half had previous hepatitis B exposure (HBcAb positive, 195/412, 47%).
Posttransplant prophylaxis strategies varied in type, timing, and strategy. Hepatitis B immunoglobulin was used for at least some recipients in 10 of 20 studies, given to 214 of 501 (43%) recipients (not specified for 99 recipients). Antiviral prescribing was documented in 17 of 20 studies, and not described in 3 studies (20 recipients). Among studies documenting antiviral administration, 283 of 580 (49%) recipients across 13 of 17 studies received them (4/17 studies with no antivirals prescribed, 144 recipients). Lamivudine was the most prescribed antiviral (10/13 studies), followed by entecavir (6/13 studies), including some studies which used different antivirals for different recipients (Table ). Antiviral prescribing strategies were sometimes universal (7/13 studies, 125 recipients), sometimes stratified based on defined criteria (1 study for recipients with donor DNA positivity: 7/65 recipients; 1 study for recipients of deceased donors: 3/7 recipients), and sometimes given to only some recipients without a clear reported strategy (4/12 studies, comprising 239 recipients). The planned duration of recipient antivirals was not stated for 3 of the 12 studies where they were used; among the 9 remaining studies, the most common duration was 1 y or longer (5 studies), but was 3–6 mo in 3 studies, and 1–3 mo in 2 studies.
Case ascertainment also varied by the timing and type of recipient posttransplant monitoring testing performed. Most adopted some form of active case ascertainment (13/20 studies), 1 study assessed for transmissions only reactively in the setting of “suspected viral hepatitis”; the remaining 6 of 20 studies did not describe their testing strategy in detail. Among the 13 studies with active surveillance, most (8/13) studies tested at least every 3 mo for the first 6 mo (most then testing less frequently subsequently), and 2/13 studies tested every 6 mo; testing timing was unclear for 3 of 13 studies. Type of HBV testing in 3 of 20 studies was HBsAg measurement only, 6 of 20 studies used HBsAg with or without HBV DNA, and 8 of 20 studies used both HBsAg and HBV DNA testing; testing type was unclear for 3 of 20 studies.

## Risk of Bias Assessment
Domains indicating a high risk of bias across many studies were those with low sample size (9 studies <10 recipients) and unclear surveillance strategies for transmissions (7 studies; Table S2 [SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327]). Other domains were frequently unclear including where the sampling criteria for included cohorts was not stated, and where detailed description of donors/recipients were lacking. One study only had detailed follow-up for 12 of 59 possible recipients (high risk of bias) while many had short or unclear follow-up durations. Most studies used valid methods for identification of transmissions and appropriate statistics. Overall, no studies were felt to be unacceptably high risk of bias and excluded from the main analysis, but additional sensitivity analyses were undertaken after risk of bias assessment (1) excluding those with <10 recipients and (2) excluding studies with >1 domain at high risk of bias.

## Hepatitis B Transmissions
Among the 600 kidney recipients at risk, and during >1447 person-years of follow-up (median follow-up time 37.4 mo; follow-up period not recorded for 4/20 studies), there were 29 HBV transmissions. Transmissions were largely from donors with detectable DNA (22/29; Table ) without reported HBeAg (20/29). Most recipients had positive HBsAb (13/29) without reported HBcAb (18/29). Among 22/29 transmissions with timing recorded, the median time to transmission diagnosis was 173 d (interquartile interval, 56–274 d).
The virological nature of transmissions varied: there were 4 of 29 with both HBV DNA positivity and HBsAg positivity, 4 of 29 defined by HBsAg positivity only (1 NAT negative, 3 NAT not reported), and 21 of 29 with HBV DNA positivity only (18 without HBsAg detection; 3 HBsAg not reported). The 18 cases without HBsAg were transient detections of low-level HBV DNA (all without clinical sequelae). Among the 8 of 29 with new HBsAg detection, at least 3 later reverted (1 after antiviral treatment).
Similarly, the clinical implications of transmissions were varied. Clinical hepatitis was reported in 10 of 29 cases. Death because of complications of HBV transmission occurred in 3 of 29 kidney recipients (Table ); all 3 cases involved new HBsAg detection. Donor and pretransplant recipient virology/serology were poorly recorded in these 3 cases. Donor DNA was positive for 1/3 and not documented for the other 2 donors. Only 1/3 recipients who died because of HBV transmission was documented to have a positive HBsAb at transplantation (1 negative, 1 not recorded), while HBcAb was negative for 1/3 recipients and not reported for the other 2. However, deaths occurred exclusively in recipients who were not taking antiviral prophylaxis at the time of transmission diagnosis: 1 had finished a short course (2-mo lamivudine), 1 was not prescribed any antiviral, and 1 was prescribed lamivudine but was nonadherent. Additionally, all deaths occurred in studies without clearly defined time points of surveillance for transmission posttransplant.
 Although of interest, other adverse liver-related outcomes were not specifically reported.

## Overall Kidney Transplant and Recipient Survival
Total number of deaths and death-censored kidney transplant failures were recorded among 14 of 20 studies, comprising 474 recipients and >1139 person-years of follow-up (3 studies follow-up not reported). Among these, there were 20 deaths, and 24 additional kidney transplant failures. Including only the 11 studies with follow-up time reported, over a total 1074 person-years), the incidence of death was 1.2 of 100 person-years (95% CI, 0.6-2.1), and incidence of death-censored kidney transplant loss was 1.5 of 100 transplant years (95% CI, 0.8-2.4). Multiple studies did not report any difference in overall survival or kidney transplant survival for susceptible recipients of kidney transplants from donors with active HBV to a comparator cohort of recipients from donors without active hepatitis B - , , ; however, comparator cohorts varied significantly, precluding further analysis.

## Meta-Analysis of HBV Transmission Risk
The pooled risk of HBV transmission was estimated at 4.0% (95% CI, 1.8%-8.3%), with a 90% prediction interval of 0.0%-23.8% (Figure ). The was little evidence for substantial heterogeneity among studies, where I2 indicated low percentage of variability in effect sizes because of heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), although there was some between-study variance (Tau2 = 1.21). Stratified meta-analyses were performed considering the proportion of recipients with positive surface antibody titers, the proportion of donors with detectable HBV DNA and proportions of donors who were living versus deceased (Figure S1A–C, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327), albeit not all studies had reported these measures (ie, 2/20 studies did not report vaccination, 7/20 studies did not report donor HBV DNA levels, and 2/20 studies did not report donor type). These estimated lower transmission proportions where all donors had undetectable HBV DNA (0.5%, 95% CI, 0.0%-16.1%, versus 16.0%, 95% CI, 10.2%-24.3%, where all donors had detectable HBV DNA), all recipients were vaccinated (1.4%, 95% CI, 0.2%-8.8%, versus 5.9%, 95% CI, 2.5%-13.0% where not all recipients were vaccinated), and all donors were living (0.8%, 95% CI, 0.1%-6.4%, versus 5.8%, 95% CI, 1.9%-16.4%, where all donors were deceased). There was insufficient similarity in antiviral treatment protocols or monitoring protocols to stratify results by these factors. Meta-regression was not undertaken given the limited number of studies and the likelihood of over-fitting data.
 The overall pooled transmission estimated using a logit transformation was higher than using the GLMM, at 8.9% (95% CI, 5.7%-13.7% and 90% prediction interval, 2.9%-24.1%) with some study heterogeneity (I2 = 35%; Tau2 = 0.400; and Figure S2 [SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327]). Although the point estimate is numerically higher, the 90% prediction interval remains similar.

## Publication Bias
There was no publication bias evident on a funnel plot (Figure S3, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327), or through Begg’s test (P = 0.974) or Egger’s test (P = 0.159).

## Sensitivity Analyses
Exclusion of 8 studies with samples sizes of <10 led to a slight increase in the estimated pooled transmission risk (4.3%, 95% CI, 1.9%-9.2%, 90% prediction interval 0.5%-27.4%) with some study heterogeneity (I2 = 33.4%; Tau2 = 1.115). Exclusion of 7 studies with high risk of bias in at least 2 domains led to a slight decrease in the estimated pooled transmission risk (3.8%, 95% CI, 0.2%-9.1%, 90% prediction interval 0.0%-29.7%), with some study heterogeneity (I2 = 32.4%; Tau2 = 1.243). Leave-one-out analysis (Figure S4, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327) showed similar estimated pooled transmission risk regardless of excluding any 1 study. The highest estimated pooled transmission risk when excluding Asuman Yavuz et al was 5.2% (95% CI, 2.8%-9.6%) and the lowest at 3.4% (95% CI, 1.5%-7.4%) when excluding Delman et al. 

## Case Reports and Unintentional Donor-positive Transplants
We identified a further 9 case reports of intentional kidney transplants from 8 donors with active HBV to recipients without active HBV, which reported 2 of 9 hepatitis B transmissions, and 1 death because of HBV. Meanwhile, among 8 recipients of unintentional transplants from donors with active HBV to recipients without, there were 3 of 8 transmissions with no clinical sequelae. These additional reports are described in more detail in the Supplementary Results (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327) and Tables S3 and S4 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TP/D327), for completion.

## DISCUSSION
This study finds the risk of HBV transmission is low (<5%–10%) when kidney donors with active hepatitis B are intentionally used for recipients without active hepatitis B. Most transmissions had no reported clinical impacts, although 3 deaths did occur. This is in the setting of overall high rates of recipients with positive HBsAb titers (because of a mix of previous infection and vaccination), varied donor characteristics, and mixed prophylaxis use. Importantly, we identified donor (presence of viremia) and recipient (vaccination, antiviral use) factors mitigating the risk and impact of transmission. These data could be used to support change in practice to increase the use of kidney donors with active HBV.
The implications of our findings are different when considering programs involving living versus deceased kidney donation. Although we found a higher risk of transmission among studies with all deceased versus all living donors, this may be related to differences in the proportions of donors with detectable HBV DNA, which led to starkly different transmission rates. For living donation, transmission risk could hence be modified with the use of pretransplantation antivirals in the potential donor, to reach an undetectable viral load. In contrast, for deceased donation, the transmission risk can be stratified by donor characteristics but not modified because of the short donation timeframe and the expected timeframe (months) for response to oral antivirals for HBV. The risk of transmission is hence expected to be higher.
Regardless of donor type, all potential kidney recipients should aim for protective surface antibody titers, per existing guidelines, which we found was associated with lower HBV transmission rates. Ideally, vaccination programs in patients with chronic kidney disease should begin before the development of kidney failure because of a high frequency of suboptimal vaccine responses among dialysis patients. Additionally, the optimal target for vaccination may need to be higher in the kidney transplant recipient because of waning titers posttransplant, with proposed HBsAb thresholds of 100 IU/L or even 250 IU/L for recipients of transplants from donors with active hepatitis B. , 
The importance of HBV transmission events varied, and most (19/29) of the detected transmissions had no clinical impact on recipients. However, the 3 deaths because of HBV transmission, although rare events, are described in detail, as the most serious potential outcome of this donation strategy. The key similarities are that all 3 recipients were not taking prophylaxis when transmission occurred, and only 1 recipient had a positive HBsAb pretransplantation. Review of case reports identified an additional death because of transmissions, in a recipient with a low positive HBsAb titer at transplant (11.6 IU/L), who also was not receiving nucleo(s)tide analogue prophylaxis. There is insufficient similarity between studies to advise on the optimal duration of prophylaxis, although the absence of deaths among recipients continuing antiviral prophylaxis suggests this improves the safety of donation considerably. Given that oral antivirals for HBV are now widely available and highly effective at suppressing HBV, their prescription to facilitate increased donation from HBV-positive donors is likely to be beneficial overall; for example, entecavir is now off-patent, has minimal toxicity, and can be safely renally adjusted. Tailored approaches considering individual circumstances, combined with close monitoring where antivirals are not used or stopped, are likely to be beneficial; future randomized data may help provide more detailed guidance. 
The novelty of our work is to comprehensively quantitatively synthesize the existing evidence about the transmission risk from kidney donors with active hepatitis B. We provide actionable evidence with stratified transmission rates by important donor and recipient factors, which allow meaningful interpretation of varied studies. This work is timely and includes 5 recent publications (2020 onward), not included in published international guidelines.
Although we have used the best evidence available, we note the variable quality of evidence, with most cohorts retrospective in nature; nonetheless, our results were robust to exclusion of studies at highest risk of bias and alternative statistical analysis, with no additional concerns identified on review of case reports or unintentional kidney transplants from donors with active hepatitis B to recipients without. Unpublished experiences with hepatitis B mismatched kidney transplants could alter results; where adverse events from transmission, or negative results (nontransmissions) are not published, this may increase or decrease transmission risk, respectively. However, we did not detect systematic publication bias on statistical testing. Although not studied here, the risk of transmission may be expected to be similar among most other nonliver solid organ transplants, which is a strategy under consideration. , However, these data should not be generalized to liver recipients, as although some centers use donors with active HBV safely, - the transmission risk is expected to be much higher because of the volume of transmitted virus in the transplanted organ. As our primary outcome was the rate of HBV transmissions, and we did not gather comparative data on kidney transplant and overall survival, data from elsewhere suggest these are similar. We did not access individual patient level data to investigate the effects of donor and recipient characteristics in more detail, which could be helpful given the significant heterogeneity between studies. Specifically, we are unable to comment on the need for higher recipient surface antibody titers (eg, >100 IU/L), suggested by some studies. We found limited data on the safety of eAg positive donors, who may have significantly higher DNA viral loads. The high proportion of recipients with past HBV exposure (HBcAb positive) may mean we have misclassified reactivation as transmission in some cases, and overstated the risk of transmission. NAT assays may have varied in sensitivity over time or between locations, resulting in misclassification of detectable viremia by the standard of current assays. The relatively short median follow-up of recipients also means we cannot comment on the emergence of late complications such as liver-related adverse events (eg, hepatocellular carcinoma), which is a hypothetical risk. 
Future use of this strategy to increase donation requires informed recipient consent and should be ideally within prospective or even randomized cohorts to compare recipient outcomes. Long-term follow-up of these identified cohorts would also be of interest to examine for late adverse events. There also may be benefit from cost-effectiveness studies to examine this approach from a systems level, or studies that consider ethical considerations and patient preferences regarding risk tolerance.

## CONCLUSIONS
Given low transmission rates, mostly comprising transient viremia, and clear mitigating factors, increased kidney transplantation may be considered from donors with active HBV with specific recipient consent. This would optimally be undertaken where there is prior recipient vaccination and with the use of antivirals posttransplantation. Risk stratification should include donor NAT status, which modifies transmission risk substantially. Given the limited included patients, the safety of this practice either for surface antibody-negative recipients, or from eAg-positive donors, remains unclear.
