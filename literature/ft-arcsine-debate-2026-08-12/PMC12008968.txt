Annually, an estimated 2 million stillbirths occur worldwide, making it a common occurrence [ – ]. However, health problems are overlooked in the global agenda and policy, which makes them invisible and underfunded. It is a preventable tragedy that has serious maternal health consequences, including mental health issues [ – ]. The trend of stillbirth has declined globally (2.3% annually) over time but is exceptionally high (> 20 per 1000 births) in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia [ ].
Ethiopia ranks fourth among the six countries and accounts for half of the global stillbirth burden [ ]. Individual studies estimate that stillbirth rates differ substantially across regions of Ethiopia [ – ].
Although the causes of stillbirth vary across contexts, they are mainly due to birth defects, neural tube defects, perinatal asphyxia or hypoxia, infections, and maternal malnutrition [ – ], and they are due mainly to modifiable risk factors such as overweight and age in developed countries [ ]. Similarly, in Ethiopia, there are many contributing factors, ranging from poor sociodemographic and economic factors of the mother and access to health services during pregnancy and childbirth [ , – ]. Poor health-seeking behavior as well as maternal morbidity and mortality increase the risk of stillbirth in Ethiopia [ ].
Despite the efforts made, including the establishment of maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response (MPDSR), stillbirth is a problem in Ethiopia [ ]. However, individual studies show different estimates with conflicting results across Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aims to estimate the pooled stillbirth rate to better understand stillbirth in Ethiopia and inform policy makers and donors in the health sector to aid in decision making.

## Methods
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines were used to conduct this systematic review and meta-analysis [ ].

## Eligibility criteria (inclusion and exclusion criteria)
We included all published observational quantitative studies (cross-sectional, cohort and spatial) conducted between 1/1/2013 and 31/3/2024 in Ethiopia and available in the English language. The studies must report the following primary outcome to be included: prevalence of stillbirth with numerators and denominators in community and public health facilities as well as private facilities. In contrast, commentaries, concept papers, newsletters, and papers with incomplete variables of interest and animal stillbirths were excluded.

## Search strategy
Finally, an exhaustive search was conducted on 31/3/2024 to identify all relevant studies available in the English language, free, and published in the following electronic databases: PubMed/Medline, Science Direct, Hinari and Google scholar. This was complemented with a manual search of reference lists of eligible studies through a citation search. No contact with the study authors to identify additional studies was made. The following search strategy and MeSH terms were applied to find published articles on still births in Ethiopia in PubMed:
((((((Stillbirth[MeSH Terms]) OR (Stillbirth[Title/Abstract])) OR (Stillborn[MeSH Terms])) OR (Stillborn[Title/Abstract])) OR (Fetal death[MeSH Terms])) OR (Fetal death[Title/Abstract])) AND (Ethiopia). However, the key terms stillbirth, stillborn, fetal death/mortality and perinatal mortality were used in other search engines.

## Study selection
First, all the studies that were located from databases and those searched from the reference lists of eligible studies were brought into Zotero Citation Manager. Duplicate studies were identified and eliminated. Studies were identified by screening the titles and abstracts of all studies against the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (AT. A and MA. A.). The two reviewers assessed the full texts of the identified studies against the eligibility criteria. Discrepancies such as ambiguity of the outcome of interest in some studies titled adverse birth outcomes or perinatal mortality were resolved through discussion and consensus by all reviewers (AT. A., MA. A., AH. A, and HB.A.). Any paper that met the exclusion criteria was excluded if a decision could not be reached (Fig.  ).

 

## Data extraction
The two authors (AT. A., MA. A) The relevant data for each included study were independently extracted via an Excel sheet. For each identified study, survey year, region, study setting, number of stillborn births and total population were extracted for analysis (Table  ).

 

## Data items
For this review, Stillbirth is a baby that is born with no signs of life at or after 28 weeks of gestation or weighing more than 1000 g.

## Risk of bias in individual studies (quality assessment)
A tool with nine criteria adapted from Hoy 2012 [ ] was used to evaluate the quality of the included studies. The following items were considered when evaluating quality: (1) representation of population, (2) sampling frame, (3) participant selection technique, (4) nonresponse bias, (5) direct data collection from subjects, (6) acceptability of case definitions, (7) study tool reliability and validity, (8) data collection mode, and (9) appropriateness of the numerator and denominator.
The total score was used as the summary assessment for the risk of bias: low risk bias (0–3), moderate risk bias (4–6) and high risk bias (7–9). Two reviewers (AT. A., MA. A) were evaluated for each study. In the case of any discrepancy in the assessment of a study between the authors, a final decision was made by consensus with all reviewers. In summary, all the studies (both high- and moderate-quality) were considered for this review to prevent publication bias (supplementary file ).

## Data analysis and synthesis
Zotero citation manager and Microsoft Excel sheet were used for study selection and data cleaning, respectively. Freeman–Tukey transformation (arcsine square root transformation; Freeman and Tukey, 1950) and MEDCALC statistical software version 23.2.1 were used to estimate the pooled stillbirth rate and perform subgroup analysis. A high degree of heterogeneity was detected and reported as significant since I2 was well above 50% (I2 = 99.08% and P value < 0.0001). To address the high degree of heterogeneity, a random effects model was chosen to estimate the pooled stillbirth rate with a 95% confidence interval. A funnel plot and Egger’s test were used to check for publication bias. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were also performed to help explain study variability, estimate the pooled stillbirth rate according to different variables and understand the robustness of the results.

## Results

## Study selection
A total of 1007 publications were found by initially searching for the prevalence of stillbirths, perinatal mortality and adverse birth outcomes in Ethiopia in different databases (PubMed, Hinari, Google Scholar, and Science Direct) and manual searching. We did not consider accessible repositories for unpublished studies, as they are not in line with the inclusion criteria. We set a lower time limit of 2013 to exclude older publications, but we did not limit the upper time bound to increase the comprehensiveness of the search. Zotero citation manager was used to manage study selection. A total of 102 publications were removed as duplicates. A total of 848 additional publications, including case‒control studies and systematic reviews on perinatal mortality and adverse birth outcomes, were also excluded from this analysis after screening the titles and abstracts of the studies. After the remaining 57 articles were compared with the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 22 of them were also removed from the exclusion criteria, such as studies on perinatal mortality and birth outcomes that did not report stillbirth (6), relatively old studies that were published before 2013 (4), east African or African studies that were conducted outside of the country but could be identified only when the full text was assessed (2), studies that were not fully accessible (4), overlapping studies that used EDHS, 2016 (2), unpublished studies (2), perinatal mortality studies that reported only the IUFD (1) and studies that reported stillbirth rates but were incorrectly calculated (1). Finally, 35 studies that met the inclusion criteria were considered for this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig.  ).

## Characteristics of the included studies
A total of 35 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, which was performed in six regions of Ethiopia: Amhara, Oromia, Tigray, SNNP, Harari and Benishangul-Gumuz. The rest of the pastoralist regions (Somali, Afar and Gambela) were not captured in this review, as there were no studies available. Most (27 out of 35) of the included studies were conducted in health facility settings, whereas the remaining 6 and 2 were conducted in community and mixed (both HF and community) settings, respectively. However, only 5 out of 35 included studies had a moderate risk of bias.
All included studies were observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort and spatial analyses); therefore, heterogeneity due to study selection was addressed at this stage. To estimate the pooled stillbirth rate of stillborn neonates in Ethiopia, studies conducted between 2013 and 2024 with a total of 120,249 births as the denominator were considered. Most included studies were performed in the Amhara region [ , – , , , ] and Oromia [ – , , , , , , , , ], followed by SNNP [ , – ], Harari [ , – ], Tigray [ , ] and Benishangul-Gumuz [ ]. Additionally, one spatial study conducted in Ethiopia based on the 2016 EDHS was also included [ ]. Furthermore, most of the research was carried out in 2019 and beyond (Table  ).

## Risk of bias within studies
The quality of each included study was assessed by adhering to the risk of bias tool of prevalent studies (20). The two reviewers (AT. Ahmed & MA. Abdulahi) were evaluated in depth for each included study one by one utilizing the criteria and procedures covered in the above subtopic quality assessment. Our summary assessment findings (Table  ) revealed that of the 35 included studies, the majority (85.7%) had a low risk of bias, whereas the remaining five original studies had a moderate risk of bias. All 35 studies were included in the estimation of pooled estimates to reduce publication bias.

## Publication bias
All the reviewers analyzed the funnel plot visually and statistically to check for possible publication bias. The publication bias is likely due to the Egger test results (P value = < 0.001) and the fact that the original studies are asymmetrically distributed on a funnel plot (Fig.  ). However, the asymmetric distribution may be due to the presence of high heterogeneity among the studies and the exclusion of unpublished studies.

 

## Meta-analysis

## Pooled stillbirth rate
The results of the current meta-analysis revealed that the pooled stillbirth rate was 68 (95% CI 52–85) per 1000 births in Ethiopia based on 35 studies and random effect model estimates. The forest plot revealed statistically significant differences among the included studies (I2 = 99.08% and P value < 0.0001). Therefore, a random effects model was chosen for the pooled estimate of the study (Fig.  ; Table  ).

 

 

## Subgroup analysis
Subgroup analysis was conducted to determine whether the considered categories help explain the observed high heterogeneity between the studies and estimate the pooled stillbirth rate for different variables. As shown in Table  , subgroup analyses were performed by year of publication, study setting and region where the studies were conducted. However, heterogeneity remained high in all subgroup analyses except for Tigray, which declined (I2 = 68.29%). The significant heterogeneity observed may be due to other variables that were not considered in this study, such as differences in healthcare access and utilization across regions and even within regions of Ethiopia [ – ]. In terms of publication year, the pooled stillbirth rate was higher among studies published recently (2021–2024), with a rate of 72 (95% CI 48–100) per 1000 births, than among those published earlier (2013–2020), with a rate of 63 (95% CI, 38–93) (Figs.  and ). Moreover, in the study setting, the highest pooled stillbirth rate was registered for health facilities, with 82 (95% CI 65–101) of the 27 studies, and the lowest was registered in the community, with 21.7 (95% CI 13.3–32.3) per 1000 births, from 6 studies (Figs.  , and ). By region, the highest rate was observed in Harar, with a rate of 143.6 (95% CI 80.8–220.7) per 1000 births from 5 studies, followed by Oromia, with a rate of 68.5 (95% CI 42–100) from 10 studies. The lowest rate was observed in the Tigray region, with a pooled rate of 49 (26–78) per 1000 births from 2 studies (Figs.  , , , and ). However, the Benishangul-Gumuz region and Ethiopia were not included in the subgroup analysis, as both had only one eligible study.

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

## Sensitivity analysis
Sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the robustness of the pooled stillbirth rate in line with the influence of each individual study. This is done by eliminating all studies, including studies with a higher risk of bias and outlier studies, one at a time. However, the overall stillbirth rate remained similar and fell within the overall confidence interval, revealing the robustness of the study (Table  ).

 

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled stillbirth rate in Ethiopia based on 35 included studies. Subgroup analysis was conducted by region, survey year, and study setting. Despite the use of subgroup analysis, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies. Persistent heterogeneity might be due to variations in health care access and practices across regions and within regions in Ethiopia, as evidenced by evidence from the 2019 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey Mini Report (EDHS, 2019) [ – ].
The pooled estimate of the stillbirth rate was 68 (95% CI 52–85) per 1000 births in Ethiopia. This implies that stillbirth is high in the country, compared with the global target of 12 per 1000 births or lower set for 2030 [ ]. This might be due to multilevel risk factors for stillbirth, which include poor health access and health-seeking behavior, as well as socioeconomic factors [ , – ]. Although this pooled estimate considers both community, mixed and health facility studies, it needs to be interpreted carefully, as home delivery is practiced in Ethiopia [ ], and pastoral regions of the country (Somali, Afar and Gambela) were not captured for this estimate (no study on stillbirths is available).
The stillbirth rate in our study was higher than the pooled rate (8.6/1000) of 12 East African countries, with the highest rate in Burundi (22.8/1000) [ ]. It is also higher than the pooled rate in the African region of great lakes: Burundi (23/1000), Kenya (13/1000), Rwanda (15/1000), Tanzania (18/1000) and Uganda (16/1000). A relatively high rate of stillbirths is common in Ethiopia. Despite the fact that many stillbirths are unclassified in low- and middle-income countries [ ], the higher stillbirth rate in Ethiopia might be due to the significant rate of iodine insufficiency among pregnant women [ ], poor socioeconomic conditions (such as poverty, low education and rural residency) and inadequate maternal health care quality, including the absence of companionate and respectful child birth care [ , , – ], as well as poor access to maternal health services such as antenatal care (ANC) and skilled delivery [ , , – , – ]. This might also be due to variations in the study settings since most of the current study estimates come from studies conducted at health institutions (Table  ).
Subgroup analysis revealed discrepancies in stillbirth rates between regions. The highest rate was observed in Harar, with a rate of 143.6 (95% CI 80.8–220.7) per 1000 births from 5 studies, followed by Oromia, with a rate of 68.5 (95% CI 42–100) from 10 studies. The lowest rate was observed in the Tigray region, with a pooled rate of 49 (26–78) per 1000 births from 2 studies. This finding is in line with a study performed in Ethiopia [ ], where significant variation was observed across the country. This may be due to lower access to basic services, including health services, in the eastern part of the country than in the northern regions [ – ].
Subgroup analysis revealed different rates of stillbirth over time. The pooled estimate of the stillbirth rate of recently published studies (2021–2024) was 72 (95% CI, 48–100), which is higher than the rate reported in earlier studies (2013–2020), which was 63 (95% CI, 38–93) per 1000 births. This means that the stillbirth rate has increased over time. This finding is consistent with a study performed earlier in Ethiopia [ ], which demonstrated increasing trends in stillbirth and declining trends in early neonatal mortality. The increasing rates of stillbirth over time might be due to less attention given to stillbirth than to neonatal and maternal mortality [ ] and the challenging implementation of MPDSR in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia [ – ].
Additionally, subgroup analysis revealed different stillbirth rates by study setting. The highest pooled stillbirth rate was registered for health facilities, with 82 (95% CI 65–101) of the 27 studies, and the lowest was registered in the community, with 21.7 (95% CI 13.3–32.3) per 1000 births from 6 studies. This discrepancy in stillbirth rate by setting must be due to MPDSR implementation at health facilities rather than at the community level (underreported).
This review revealed that stillbirth is a problem in the country. This might reflect the existing situation of the health system and the quality of care for pregnant women. It is useful for policy makers and health actors to revitalize clinical policy implementation, including MPDSR. The strength of the study is the pooled estimate, which at least considered studies that involved both community and health facilities. However, there might be potential bias, such as language bias (including only articles published in English and potentially having missed studies in other languages), and unpublished data were excluded. Certain pastoral regions (Somali, Afar, and Gambela) were not considered due to a lack of studies available. Additionally, limitations related to the high heterogeneity of the study results were observed. The pooled stillbirth data should be interpreted carefully, as most of the included studies were health facility-based studies. In summary, possible bias is addressed during selection and analysis.

## Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the pooled estimate of stillbirth is high in Ethiopia compared with the global target rate of 12 per 1000 births, which is set for 2030. These rates are higher than those reported in African countries. The rate has also increased over time in Ethiopia. These findings can inform policy makers and program implementers about the burden of stillbirth in Ethiopia. Health sector actors should adjust and improve the quality of maternal health service implementation, including MPDSR, with a special focus on stillbirth to reach the global target.
