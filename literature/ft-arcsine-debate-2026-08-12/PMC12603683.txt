## Abstract

## Introduction
Despite Nigeria's high prostate cancer burden, data remain fragmented due to systemic challenges. This scoping review aims to comprehensively synthesize existing evidence on epidemiological and clinical process indicators during prostate cancer detection and management, and to provide insights on the status of prostate cancer interventions in order to guide informed practice and support policy decisions.

## Methods
The Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science databases were searched for journal articles published in English from 2020 to 2024. Studies were included if they captured thematic areas on process indicators and intervention mapping concepts. Covidence® and Mendeley software were used for data screening, a Microsoft Excel Spreadsheet template for data charting and summarization, and Stata software for meta-analysis.

## Results
A total of 448 articles were screened, and 48 were included. The majority of the retrieved studies, 83.3 % (n = 40), had an observational study design. Studies on screening and diagnosis (11 studies, 22.9 %) dominated thematic area on the concepts of interest related to process indicators. Studies on intervention tools and markers (14 studies, 29.2 %) dominated thematic areas on the concepts of interest related to intervention mapping, and none of the studies reported implementation outcomes.

## Conclusions
This research has provided baseline values of multiple process indicators that can be used as reference points to evaluate progress during prostate cancer detection and management interventions. It has also highlighted a limited number of studies on intervention implementation, therefore, underscoring the critical need for system-level and translational health services research to support health systems strengthening.

Keywords: Implementation outcome, Intervention mapping, Process indicator, Prostate cancer, Validation

## Highlight

•It highlighted baseline values of multiple epidemiological and clinical indicators, and limited application of interventions.
•Global oncology stakeholders can use this data to inform real-world implementation of quality-assured interventions.

## Introduction
Prostate cancer is a global public health problem, ranking as the most commonly diagnosed cancer among men in around two-thirds of countries worldwide, and the leading cause of mortality in 52 countries [ ]. In resource-limited settings like Nigeria, the most populous country in sub-Saharan Africa, prostate cancer is the most common cancer among men. Its age-standardized incidence rate (41.2 per 100,000 population) far exceeds the global average (29.4 per 100,000 population), highlighting the critical burden [ , ]. A significant proportion of prostate cancer in Nigeria is diagnosed during opportunistic testing of symptomatic disease [ ]. Diagnostic evaluation and definitive treatment are typically limited, with the majority of patients presenting with advanced prostate cancer and often undergoing only androgen deprivation therapy-based management [ ]. In contrast, the patient care pathway in high-income countries includes PSA screening of asymptomatic men [ ], prompt evaluation of symptomatic men, advanced imaging for disease staging, and the rising use of curative treatment for predominantly early-stage disease [ ]. This contrast highlights the disparity in prostate cancer detection and management strategies across these two settings of varying system capacity and socioeconomic context.
Over the years, various studies have provided substantial evidence on different aspects of prostate cancer detection and management in Nigeria. There is growing attention to integrating implementation science concepts into cancer care to improve the quality and outcomes of cancer detection and management, especially in low-resource settings. This requires analysing and understanding current status of implementation to develop optimal strategies aligned with present priorities. Epidemiological and clinical process indicators are essential for assessing the efficacy and efficiency of healthcare delivery. These metrics provide helpful information about systemic bottlenecks, provider practices, and patient-level barriers that affect timely and high-quality care, and are crucial for benchmarking progress, spotting inequalities, and directing evidence-based service delivery [ ]. Despite Nigeria's high prostate cancer burden, synthesized evidence on epidemiological and clinical process indicators of prostate cancer detection and management are limited due to several systemic challenges, including weak cancer surveillance systems and underdeveloped cancer registries, which hinder data-driven decision-making. Furthermore, the scope of recently published evidence relating to process indicators of prostate cancer detection and management, and the breadth of available evidence on the current needs, utility of validated tools and markers, and the implementation of tailored interventions in Nigeria is unclear.
To inform the development, implementation, or evaluation of quality-assured evidence-based interventions for prostate cancer control and recognize gaps for future research efforts in the Nigerian context, this scoping review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of recent evidence on epidemiological and clinical process indicators and intervention strategies, tools and markers of prostate cancer detection and management in Nigeria. To the best of our knowledge, no prior review has comprehensively examined these concepts through the lens of implementation in Nigeria. Our review will provide a methodically organized summary of evidence on the entire spectrum of the patient care pathway for clinicians, health policy-makers, and advocates to guide healthcare practice and policy decision-making.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Framework and protocol
The study design was guided by Arksey and O'Malley's methodological framework [ ]. Its six sequential stages include: (1) identification of research questions, (2) identification of relevant studies, (3) selection of studies, (4) data charting, (5) data collation, summary, and reporting, and (6) relevant stakeholder consultation (optional). Literature searching and reporting were conducted in line with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist and explanation [ ].
2.1.1.

## Stage 1: identification of research questions
The overarching aim of this scoping review was to map available information and identify knowledge-to-practice gaps on the detection and management of prostate cancer in Nigeria. The research questions for this review were:
1.What are the epidemiological and clinical process indicators associated with prostate cancer screening and diagnosis in Nigeria?
2.What are the observed complications and survival outcomes following the treatment of prostate cancer in Nigeria?
3.What are the current barriers and unmet needs in prostate cancer detection and management in Nigeria, and what intervention strategies, tools, and markers presently exist to address these challenges?

2.1.2.

## Stage 2: identification of relevant studies
The study used the Population, Concept, and Content (PCC) framework to define the eligibility criteria for this study ( ) [ ]. Relevant articles were identified by a team comprising a prostate cancer expert (MA) and two cancer epidemiologists (PB, SP) with guidance from a specialized cancer librarian. Keywords in the full search strategy (“Prostate cancer” and “Nigeria”, which were pre-tested before the final search) were informed by the research questions and the population, concept, and content (PCC) criteria. The keywords were linked using the Boolean operators ‘AND’, and a filter limiting the search to human studies published between 2020 and 2024 in English. The literature search for potentially eligible studies was carried out in the indexed databases of Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science.
 
2.1.3.

## Stage 3: selection of studies
The citations of potentially eligible articles were imported into Covidence, an online software platform to seamlessly manage the process of conducting reviews. Duplicate references were automatically removed during citation import, and other duplicates were recognized and excluded manually during the screening process. During primary screening, the titles and abstracts of all the articles retrieved from the databases were screened for relevance by one of the authors (MA) using Covidence, to exclude clearly ineligible studies. All studies passing the primary screening were exported into the Mendeley Reference Management Software version 2.131.0. During the secondary screening stage, all full-text articles were read by two authors (MA and SP) independently to assess for inclusion. At each stage, the number of screened titles/abstracts, full-texts evaluated, any reason for excluding full-texts, and full-texts retained for data extraction were recorded according to PRISMA-ScR standards. Any discrepancies occurring during the secondary screening between the authors were resolved by mutual consensus.

2.1.4.

## Stage 4: data charting
The authors developed a data abstraction template on a Microsoft Excel Spreadsheet that aligned with the purpose of this review to organize data from the retained full-text articles. Extracted data included general study characteristics (first author name, year of publication, study population, study design and data type, study site, state and geopolitical zone of study, and primary study objective) and the mapped thematic areas on the concepts of interest (refer to footnote). One author (MA) extracted data from the retained full-texts using this spreadsheet. Two authors (MA and SP) independently verified the completeness of the data and checked for errors. Disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussions, and updates were made accordingly on the spreadsheet.

2.1.5.

## Stage 5: data collation, summary, and reporting
The charted data on the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was read multiple times by two authors (MA and RS) for familiarization. It was then sorted based on the thematic areas (refer to footnote). Qualitative data were reported narratively, structured by thematic domains and subdomains of the concept of interest. Quantitative data were presented in tables and maps, and summarized using descriptive statistics where applicable. The meta-analysis of pooled data on screening and diagnosis, biopsy practice and patterns, and disease staging was completed through STATA version 14.2 Statistical software (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) using the metaprop command. For each of the studies, standard error was calculated using the reported prevalence of MS and total sample size. To minimize the effect of extremely small or large prevalence on the overall estimate, Freeman Tukey double arc-sine transformation was done to stabilise the variance [ ]. We used a random effects model because of the variability in study designs and sampling in this scoping review. The pooled estimates of prevalence were then graphically represented using forest plots. 95 % confidence interval and I2 statistics were computed to estimate between-study heterogeneity. I2 statistic of more than 75 % was considered as substantial heterogeneity. Publication bias was not assessed since the number of studies reporting any particular outcome were less than ten.

2.1.6.

## Stage 6: relevant stakeholder consultation
The authors were subject matter experts and health policy-makers in the research area, and experience sharing between them facilitated the understanding of the study results and placed it in the context of prostate cancer control in Nigeria. There was no formal involvement of patient stakeholders in this review.

2.2.

## Quality of evidence assessment
Quality assessment was done by SP. The risk of bias of the included observational studies was assessed using eight criteria of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale [ ]. The criteria include sample representativeness, sample size justification, non-respondents, ascertainment of the exposure, subject comparability in different outcome groups, outcome assessment, statistical test description and appropriateness, and control for confounding. We used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for assessing the quality of included interventional studies. The tool had 13 criteria to evaluate the studies, including randomization, allocation concealment, baseline similarity, participant blinding, interventionist blinding, identical treatment, outcome assessor blinding, outcome measurement uniformity, outcome measurement reliability, participant retention, intent-to-treat analysis, statistical analysis appropriateness, and trial design appropriateness [ ]. Finally, the only mixed methods study was assessed using Quality assessment with diverse studies (QuADS) tool [ ].

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Selection of sources of evidence
The automated search of three electronic databases (Embase, PubMed, Web of Science) identified 448 studies. After the removal of 154 duplicates, titles and abstracts of 294 unique studies were screened, and 212 ineligible studies were excluded. A total of 77 potentially relevant studies with available full texts were retrieved, and 48 studies that met the predefined eligibility criteria were included in this review. The PRISMA flow diagram summarizes the record screening process and the main reasons for the exclusion of full texts ( ).
 
3.2.

## Quality of studies
For the cross-sectional studies (n = 31), 20 publications were rated with moderate risk of bias, poor ratings were assigned to seven publications, and good ratings to four publications. All the cohort studies (n = 4) had a moderate risk of bias, and all the case-control studies (n = 6) had a low risk of bias. For the interventional studies (n = 4), three publications had a moderate risk of bias, and one publication had a low risk of bias. There was one qualitative study and two other studies with an undefined study design that were not included in the risk of bias assessment.

3.3.

## Characteristics of included studies
3.3.1.

## Year of publication
The highest number of publications was in 2022 (25.0 %, n = 12), while 2023 recorded the fewest (14.6 %, n = 7).

3.3.2.

## Study spatial distribution
Most publications were single-country research (n = 40), and others were multi-country research (n = 8). All six geopolitical zones of Nigeria were represented, except the Northeast. A total of 14 states and the Federal Capital Territory accounted for 42 of the 48 publications with location data ( ). There were 35 single-state publications and 7 multi-state publications. For the single state publications, four-fifths were in southern Nigeria [South West = 3 states hosting 11 studies; South East = 4 states hosting 10 studies; and South South = 2 states hosting 7 studies]. The remaining one-fifth were in the North Central (2 states hosting 3 studies), North West (2 states hosting 2 studies), and the Federal Capital Territory (hosting 2 studies). For the multi-state publications, there were six multi-zone publications (n = 2 single-country and 4 multi-country publications) and one single-zone publication.

3.3.3.

## Study design and data source
Forty-four studies used quantitative designs, including observational studies in 83.3 % (n = 40) and interventional studies in 8.3 % (n = 4). A minority of the studies employed qualitative (2.1 %, n = 1) and mixed methods study designs (2.1 %, n = 1). The design of the two other studies was not clear, as detailed in . Primary data collection was done in 60.4 % (n = 29), research data was retrieved from a secondary source in 31.3 % (n = 15), and the data source was unclear in 8.3 % (n = 4).
 
3.3.4.

## Study context and sample population
Four studies [sample size 6–104] explored research questions at the system level, and 44 studies targeted individual-level data. At the individual level, 77.3 % of the studies (n = 34) assessed patients [sample size 10–331], 15.9 % (n = 7) assessed community participants [sample size 191–1080], 4.5 % (n = 2) assessed providers [sample size 14–102], and 2.3 % (n = 1) assessed a mixed population of patients, caregivers, and providers [sample size 52].). Twenty-seven studies were conducted at a single site. Fifteen were multi-site studies, including nine studies in the hospital setting (range of 2–4 sites), four studies in the community setting (range of 3–108 sites), and two studies at the system level (range of 6–104 sites). In six studies, the number of sites was unspecified or not applicable.

3.4.

## Primary study focus
Process indicators measuring screening and diagnosis (n = 11; 22.9 %) and treatment and outcomes (n = 9; 18.8 %) were the most frequently addressed thematic areas. Four studies (8.3 %) highlighted biopsy practice and patterns. Across the five-year time horizon, only one study (2.1 %) focused on disease staging.
Studies focused on intervention tools and markers (n = 14; 29.2 %) were the most common under the intervention mapping category, followed by barriers and needs (n = 8; 16.7 %). Only one study (2.1 %) addressed intervention strategies for educational intervention on illness perception in prostate cancer patients.

3.5.

## Process indicators of disease detection and management
3.5.1.

## Screening and diagnosis
Four studies highlighted the importance of considering prostate cancer family history during risk assessment. Three studies reported on diagnosed patients stratified by population-offered PSA testing and symptom presence, and three studies reported clinical and biochemical marker utilization and/or their outcome. Community-based screening uptake was reported in three studies, and an age-stratified screening pattern was described in two studies ( ).
The proportion of diagnosed patients reporting prior screening participation was 39.7 %. In community samples, 17.8 % had undergone PSA screening, and 7.4 % had abnormal PSA levels. Among patients with suspected prostate cancer, 85.6 %–100 % had abnormal PSA results. During population-offered PSA-testing, 62.1 % of the PSA screen-positive patients were asymptomatic at presentation ( ).
 On meta-analysis of pooled results, the frequency for community-based screening uptake was 12 % (95 % CI, 7 to 17; I2 = 85.6 %). The pooled frequency for a positive family history of prostate cancer was 15 % (95 % CI: 3–27; I2 = 91.0 %), and suspicious patients with abnormal digital rectal examination had a pooled frequency of 70 % (95 % CI, 63 to 77; I2 = 0.0 %) ( ).
 
3.5.2.

## Biopsy practice and patterns
A total of seven studies described the use of the transrectal biopsy approach, and four studies described the use of at least a ten-core biopsy. One study noted the use of transrectal ultrasound guidance, while none reported the use of the transperineal approach or MRI-guided biopsy ( ).
The proportion recommending biopsy with prostate specific antigen threshold >10 ng/ml was 100 %, with prostate specific antigen density was 63.7 %, with trans-rectal ultrasound findings was 52.9 %, and with a prostate specific antigen threshold of PSA 4–10 ng/ml was 42.2 % (n = 43) ( ).
On meta-analysis, the pooled proportion of patients with positive biopsy in those performed with a diagnostic intent was 58 % (95 % CI, 53 to 63; I2 = 29.0 %). When positive biopsy was stratified by age, prostate specific antigen, and Gleason's score; the pooled proportion of patients aged 50–59 years across all ages was 13 % (95 % CI, 10 to 16; I2 = 0.0 %), pooled proportion with prostate specific antigen <10 ng/ml was 8 % (95 % CI, 2 to 14; I2 = 76.8 %), and pooled proportion with Gleason score of 6 was 31 % (95 % CI, 22 to 39; I2 = 88.2 %) ( ).

3.5.3.

## Disease staging
One study reported the pattern of advanced imaging techniques for staging, and two other studies reported a process indicator, organ-confined disease, as a secondary objective ( ). On meta-analysis, the pooled frequency of patients with organ-confined disease was 5 % (95 % CI, 3 to 7; I2 = 0.0 %) ( ).

3.5.4.

## Treatment and outcomes
Studies included data on the pattern of treatment for advanced disease (n = 3), the use of radiotherapy to the prostate (n = 3), and the use of radical prostatectomy (n = 2). The scope of other process indicators in this thematic area is outlined in ( ). The 2-year cancer-specific survival for radical prostatectomy was 94.3 % ( ).

3.6.

## Prostate cancer intervention mapping
3.6.1.

## Barriers and needs assessment
The studies included data under the domains of quality of care (n = 3), cost of care (n = 3), and general multi-level barriers (n = 1). Under quality of care, all three studies reported care delays. Two focused on general time intervals; one assessed treatment-specific intervals (time to palliative radiotherapy and radical prostatectomy, benchmarked at 10 and 30 days, respectively). The terminologies used to define the endpoints of the time intervals from hospital presentation to treatment, hospital presentation to diagnosis, and diagnosis to treatment varied considerably between these studies. For cost of care, one study each reported on willingness to pay for screening, indirect treatment costs for hypofractionation, and financial toxicity. One study used and validated the COST-FACIT instrument locally. A single study addressed general multi-level barriers. Details of the specific results of domains in this thematic area are presented in .
 
3.6.2.

## Intervention strategies
One study highlighted multiple stakeholder recommendations to address barriers to detection and management. Another study reported the implementation of a single intervention focused on one-on-one education to newly diagnosed patients and those on follow-up. None of these studies reported implementation outcomes ( ).

3.6.3.

## Intervention tools and markers
Five studies examined quality-of-life screening tools, though only three of these studies documented the local validity of the tool used. In the domain of quality improvement tools, studies on guidelines (n = 1) and quality indicators (n = 1) were identified.
Regarding precision prevention markers, a total of five studies reported on biochemical markers with a focus on the prediction value of different assays (n = 3) and reference ranges for prostate specific antigen (n = 2). Two studies highlighted clinical markers with a focus on the detection rate of digital rectal examination and ultrasound examination. Two studies reported genomic markers and identified increased odds of germline HOXB13 and higher predicted polygenic risk for prostate cancer. One study documented the absence of a local nomogram for prostate cancer.

4.

## Discussion
As the burden of prostate cancer continues to escalate, addressing disparities in detection and management has become an urgent public health priority to better understand the knowledge-to-practice gaps across the prostate cancer detection and management pathway in Nigeria and other African nations. This scoping review, including 48 studies, provides a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on process indicators and intervention themes across the prostate cancer care continuum reported in the country. We observed that the included studies were predominantly targeting individual patient level, process indicators focusing on screening and diagnosis, and interventions were frequently centered around tools and markers rather than actual data on implementation outcomes.
4.1.

## Research evidence mapping
Our results highlighting the prominent use of patient-level studies could suggest strong access to patient research participants in the Nigerian context. This finding reflects the principal study investigators’ core clinical background and research focus within their primary domains of influence in the hospital setting. On one hand, this reflects the rare engagement of the investigators in collaborative projects, as 35 of the studies were single-state publications, and around 80 % of these were single-site research. On the other hand, it could also mean that there is a limitation in expertise for system-level studies and non-observational study designs among prostate cancer researchers in Nigeria, as projected by the paucity of studies in these areas.
Surprisingly, the majority of the studies were based on a primary data source despite the herculean tasks of data collection in resource-limited settings. While this enhances the reliability of specific findings, it also reveals systemic gaps—the seeming lack of prospectively developed clinical and epidemiological databases from longitudinal studies. Therefore, leveraging the availability of investigators, addressing data storage challenges, and supporting a team science approach are important considerations for the strengthening of the prostate cancer research landscape in Nigeria and reflect broader needs for collaboration in the global oncology landscape.

4.2.

## Process indicators of detection and management
Our results describe the present status of various process indicators, including those related to prostate cancer screening and diagnosis in Nigeria. The integration of community-based screening in Nigeria appears to be limited, as pooled analysis showed that the frequency of community-based screening uptake was 12 %- a concerningly low figure in a country where most prostate cancers are detected at an advanced stage. This is not surprising because this review did not identify any implemented interventions to optimize prostate cancer risk assessment in the community setting. In consonance with the study, which identified that 7 % of community-based participants had abnormal prostate specific antigen, Chidebe et al. in an earlier study of 1661 men showed that 8 % of screened men across three cities in Nigeria had elevated prostate specific antigen [ ]. Moreover, a state-wide prostate specific antigen plus digital rectal examination (DRE) screening intervention of 5223 participants across multiple local government areas in Lagos state of Nigeria, recognized that 8 % of all screened patients had abnormal results and required biopsy, but the available data did not disaggregate this finding by the outcome of prostate specific antigen and digital rectal examination ( ) , . In contrast to this review and Chidebe et al.’s study, indicators such as the proportion of screened patients presenting for biopsy (2–5 %) or with a definitive diagnosis of prostate cancer (1 %) were comprehensively described in only the Lagos screening study [ ]. This might be due to the incomplete linkage of the screening evaluation to a urological evaluation for diagnostic confirmation. This present review, unlike other available evidence, however, documents that around two-thirds (62 %) of screened patients were asymptomatic at presentation. Asymptomatic patients are more likely to have early-stage disease. This could mean that these populations are the potential actionable targets to reverse the trend from the diagnosis of advanced disease to the identification of patients with a high potential for cure.
Looking at biopsy outcomes, the pooled frequency of patients with positive biopsies in those performed with a diagnostic intent was 58 %. Expectedly, this was almost five-fold greater than the 12 % diagnosis rate of prostate cancer among those requiring biopsy in the screening population in Nigeria - highlighting the risk of overdiagnosis and potentially unnecessary biopsies [ ]. Moreover, it appears that biopsy decisions in Nigeria are predominantly hinged on a PSA test, as the majority of providers identified that they recommend prostate biopsy with a PSA above 10 ng/ml or using PSA density values. With the identification of one in three biopsy-positive patients on diagnostic intent having indolent disease (Gleason Score of 6), there is an overall need for multivariable risk assessment to address the problem of unnecessary biopsies in the diagnostic and screening population while attaining the benefits of early detection. Despite the result that 75 % of patients within the age group 50–59 have positive prostate biopsies, the age of evaluation of prostate cancer appears to be skewed to older age groups because only 13 % of patients aged 50–59 years had positive biopsies across all ages evaluated for prostate cancer. Also, transrectal ultrasound findings contributed to biopsy decisions in more than half of the providers, and one in seven prostate cancer patients has a positive family history. The extent of utilization of these variables in combination with abnormal prostate specific antigen and prostate specific antigen density values for screening decisions was limited, as none of the retrieved studies suggested the availability of a nomogram to integrate the variables.
Regarding current biopsy practice, this review identified that despite the availability of transrectal ultrasound for biopsy decisions in 53 %, only 26 % of the providers integrated transrectal ultrasound guidance during prostate biopsy. This suggests that there might be widespread limitations on the availability of biopsy guides to facilitate targeted biopsy. The practice of prostate biopsy is rapidly developing, and so is that of prostate cancer staging. The recent European Association of Urology guideline strongly recommends prostate biopsy via the transperineal route due to low infectious complication risk and the use of Magnetic Resonance Imaging prior to biopsy in men with suspicion of organ-confined disease. Nigeria lags in the integration of these methods into cancer care services as none of the current studies employed these approaches during prostate biopsy, and the only study documenting Magnetic Resonance Imaging was limited to the context of disease staging [ ].
Regarding prostate cancer treatment, studies describing patients receiving radical prostatectomy, high-dose brachytherapy, external beam radiotherapy, and androgen-deprivation therapy were identified. Only the study on the radical prostatectomy cohort described cancer-specific survival with a 2-year survival rate of 94 %. Similar to this study, Cassell et al.’s review of prostate cancer across sub-Saharan Africa also had a limited documentation of survival rate, with only one study reporting a 6-year overall survival of 90 % in Senegal [ ]. In another study, the 5-year overall survival rate for advanced prostate cancer was 0–7.4 % [ ]. Several factors contribute to the poor reporting of survival outcomes, including difficulty in data retrieval from hospital-based records, the poor capture of cancer mortality figures in hospital-based registries, and the non-deliberate development of treatment cohorts to capture this data. Cassell et al. also noted that the majority of patients in sub-Saharan Africa received androgen deprivation therapy rather than radical treatment for early-stage disease [ ]. Given the low 5 % rate of diagnosis of organ-confined disease, the high cancer-specific survival rate for radical treatment of 94 % is a green light on the availability of effective treatment for early-stage disease, as efforts continue to optimize access to early prostate cancer detection and appropriate curative treatment in Nigeria. While doing this, there is a simultaneous need for more robust monitoring and reporting of treatment response and treatment complications, which is deficient in the treatment cohort of current publications.

4.3.

## Prostate cancer intervention mapping
This review identified eight studies on barriers and needs within the domains of general barriers, quality of care, including timing of and retention in care, and cost of care. While these represent building blocks for intervention development to improve various aspects of cancer control, only one study further explored stakeholder-recommended actions that will address the identified challenges. However, it appears that there is incremental progress in intervention development as various clinical, biochemical, and genomic markers and quality improvement tools were identified. These tools and markers can be implemented to strengthen the patient care pathway of prostate cancer in Nigeria. Nevertheless, the question of the best markers to be translated into real-world practice remains unanswered, as most of the tools were not rigorously validated for the Nigerian prostate cancer population. The availability of validated tools might have strong implications for how they can be appropriately adapted to the Nigerian setting and further shape the development and implementation of setting-tailored policies on prostate cancer control.
In sharp contrast to the identification of various clinical process indicators in this study, none of the publications assessed implementation outcomes of interventions in the Nigerian prostate cancer setting. This might be because investigators are either relatively new to implementation science concepts, as around 4 in 5 surveyed participants in Africa have less than 2 years of experience in the field [ ]. As a result, evidence-based interventions are not routinely developed for patients at risk, with suspicion, or confirmed prostate cancers or even other cancers.
To sustainably support the strengthening of the fragmented healthcare system in this resource-limited setting, there is a need to understand how interventions can be effectively integrated into healthcare services by applying appropriate implementation strategies such as training, supportive supervision, and audit and feedback. Incorporating the principles of implementation science from the beginning of the needs assessment may improve the documentation of proximal outcomes of implementation and aid in identifying implementation determinants relevant to the scale-up of evidence-based interventions [ ].

4.4.

## Strengths and limitations
To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review on prostate cancer that maps and pools process indicators, explores the validity of available tools and markers, and broadly looks at the utilization of implementation outcomes for available interventions across the pathway of prostate cancer detection and management in Nigeria. This will be of interest to stakeholders, including clinicians, health policy-makers, and advocates who either conduct research or support policy development. This scoping review, however, has some limitations. We included only studies written in English; therefore, there is a potential for the omission of relevant studies conducted by investigators from non-English-speaking countries. Although our search strategy encompassed prostate cancer and Nigeria to provide the breadth required to answer the research questions, there is the possibility of missing out studies that are neither available online nor captured in the explored databases.

5.

## Conclusion
The results of this scoping review provide baseline values of multiple process indicators, especially in the clinical setting, that can be used as a benchmark to assess progress across prostate cancer detection and management. It also highlighted a limited number of studies focusing on the context of health systems. Community-based screening uptake was low despite optimal survival outcomes following radical surgery for early-stage prostate cancer. Rigorously validating available markers of precision diagnosis and quality improvement and integrating implementation outcome concepts during the design of interventions will assist in strengthening the health system. As a next step, we hope to triangulate the study findings with results of capacity assessment so as to develop a quality-assured capacity response plan and support the sustainable implementation of evidence-based interventions that directly address the identified barriers and needs.
