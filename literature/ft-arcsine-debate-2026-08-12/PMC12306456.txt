## Abstract

## Introduction
Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health challenge in Sub-Saharan Africa, exacerbated by the high prevalence of drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) and its strong association with HIV. Bedaquiline (BDQ), approved by the WHO in 2013, offers a promising treatment for DR-TB, including multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) and extensively DR-TB (XDR-TB). However, BDQ has been associated with QT interval prolongation, a condition that can lead to serious cardiac arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to quantify the incidence of QT interval prolongation in patients receiving BDQ for DR-TB in Sub-Saharan Africa and identify predictors of this adverse effect.

## Methods and analysis
We will conduct a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and African Journals Online using medical subject headings and keywords related to ‘BDQ’, ‘DR-TB’, ‘QT interval prolongation’ and ‘Sub-Saharan Africa’. Eligible studies will include randomised controlled trials, cohort studies, case-control studies and observational studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa. Study titles and abstracts will be initially screened, and full texts will be retrieved and reviewed against eligibility criteria. Relevant data will be extracted from the selected articles and assessed for risk of bias. The primary outcome will be the pooled incidence of QT interval prolongation. Data will be synthesised using a random-effects model meta-analysis if significant heterogeneity is present; otherwise, a fixed-effects model will be applied.

## Ethics and dissemination
This study will use published data, requiring no ethical approval. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations to inform clinical guidelines and DR-TB treatment policies in Sub-Saharan Africa.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This systematic review employs a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases, including African Journals Online, to capture relevant studies in Sub-Saharan Africa.
The methodology follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols checklist, ensuring a rigorous, transparent and reproducible research process.
By including data from both observational studies and randomised controlled trials, the review provides a comprehensive assessment of real-world and controlled clinical settings.
Significant heterogeneity in QT interval monitoring protocols and follow-up durations among included studies may limit the reliability of pooled estimates.
The exclusion of non-English and non-French studies introduces potential language bias, which could result in the omission of relevant data.

## Introduction
 Tuberculosis (TB) is the world’s leading cause of death from a single infectious agent, surpassing COVID-19 after a 3-year period. TB now claims nearly twice as many lives as HIV/AIDS. Each year, over 10 million people continue to contract TB, with incidence rates increasing since 2021. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for a significant proportion of global TB cases, with high prevalence particularly noted among people living with HIV. The TB epidemic in Sub-Saharan Africa has worsened due to the synergy between TB and HIV, complicating treatment and increasing mortality rates. Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) further compound the problem, making effective treatment regimens critical. 
Bedaquiline (BDQ), a diarylquinoline antimicrobial agent, was approved by the WHO in 2013 as part of the global effort to combat drug-resistant TB (DR-TB). BDQ targeted mycobacterial ATP synthase, disrupting the energy production of Mycobacterium TB and offering a new mechanism of action against resistant strains. Uganda and several other TB high-burden countries adopted BDQ shortly after its WHO endorsement, incorporating it into national TB treatment programmes in 2016 for patients 6 years and above. At the end of May 2019, 133 patients had received BDQ under programme conditions. 
The introduction of BDQ marked a significant shift from injectable drugs to oral treatments for DR-TB. BDQ replaced drugs such as kanamycin and capreomycin, which were associated with severe side effects, including hearing loss and nephrotoxicity. A multicountry, randomised controlled trial (RCT) of 24-week, all-oral BDQ-containing regimens for the treatment of rifampicin-resistant TB showed that treatment with BDQ-containing regimens was more effective and had a better safety profile than standard care, which included the injectable. The transition to BDQ not only simplified the treatment regimen but also improved patient compliance and overall treatment outcomes. 
However, despite its therapeutic advantages, BDQ has been associated with QT interval prolongation, a condition where the heart’s electrical cycle is delayed. QT prolongation can lead to torsade de pointes, a potentially fatal arrhythmia, raising significant concerns about the cardiac safety of BDQ. Several studies have reported varying incidences of QT interval prolongation with BDQ use. For instance, a phase 2 RCT in patients with rifampicin-resistant TB found that 11.0% of patients receiving BDQ experienced QT prolongation (>450 ms), with some cases requiring treatment discontinuation. A retrospective cohort study in South Africa reported a lower incidence of 6.7%, potentially due to differences in monitoring protocols and patient comorbidities. A scoping review by Putra et al found that DR-TB patients receiving BDQ and delamanid had a higher incidence of QT prolongation (up to 44.1% in some cohorts), particularly in the elderly, those with HIV or with hypokalaemia, though no severe clinical outcomes like torsades de pointes were reported. Potential predictors include HIV co-infection, concomitant use of QT-prolonging drugs (eg, fluoroquinolones or clofazimine), and electrolyte imbalances, though findings are inconsistent across studies. , These variations highlight the need for a systematic synthesis to clarify the true incidence and risk factors in Sub-Saharan Africa, where HIV prevalence and resource constraints may amplify risks. Given the high prevalence of comorbidities, including HIV, among DR-TB patients, the risk of QT interval prolongation necessitates careful monitoring and management.
While individual studies have reported varying incidences of QT interval prolongation in patients treated with BDQ, a comprehensive synthesis of these findings is limited. Prior reviews have examined BDQ’s safety, including cardiac toxicity globally, but a Sub-Saharan Africa-specific synthesis is limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to address this gap by providing a pooled estimate of QT prolongation incidence in Sub-Saharan Africa and exploring predictors, such as HIV status, concomitant medications and electrolyte imbalances, to guide safer treatment protocols in high-burden settings.

## Methods and analysis

## Study design
This protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis was developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols statement to ensure a transparent and standardised approach. The protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration Number: CRD42024560368).

## Study selection criteria
Duplicate studies will be identified and merged using Covidence, an advanced web-based software for systematic reviews. Two independent reviewers will primarily screen titles and abstracts to exclude those unrelated to the study using the Covidence interface. After the initial screening, full texts of potentially eligible studies will be retrieved and examined for eligibility. Disagreements will be resolved by discussion or by a third reviewer. Studies with overlapping patient populations will be cross-checked using study details like study site to ensure each patient is included only once, prioritising the most recent or comprehensive dataset.

## Search strategy
With the help of a qualified medical librarian, all studies published from January 2013 to May 2024 will be searched from EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science and the African Journals Online (AJOL).
The following search terms will be used: (‘BDQ’(Mesh) OR ‘BDQ’(tiab) OR ‘BDQ’(Mesh) OR ‘BDQ’(tiab)) AND (‘TB, Multidrug-Resistant’(Mesh) OR ‘MDR-TB’(tiab) OR ‘XDR-TB’(tiab) OR ‘DR-TB’(tiab)) AND (‘QT interval prolongation’(Mesh) OR ‘QT prolongation’(tiab) OR ‘QTc prolongation’(tiab) OR ‘QT interval’(tiab)) AND (‘Africa’(Mesh) OR ‘Africa’(tiab) OR ‘Sub-Saharan Africa’(tiab)).
Search terms will also incorporate wildcards (eg, ‘drugresistant*’ for drug-resistant, ‘QT*prolongation’ for QTc prolongation). To ensure comprehensive retrieval of eligible studies, we will include a broad range of terms for cardiac side effects and list all Sub-Saharan African countries individually in the search string to capture studies that may not explicitly mention ‘Africa’ or ‘Sub-Saharan Africa.’ We will also perform a manual literature search for all the references to the included articles to find applicable studies. Additionally, we will screen the full texts of studies reporting BDQ use in DR-TB patients to identify any mention of QT prolongation, even if not highlighted in the abstract. The search will be limited to human studies only. Studies written in English and French, the most common official languages, and published in Sub-Saharan Africa will be selected. Search terms were piloted against a set of known relevant studies, for example, Dooley et al and Pai et al to ensure retrieval accuracy.

## Inclusion criteria
We will use the PEO framework (Patient, Exposure, Outcome) to select the eligible studies to be included;
Patient (P): studies involving patients diagnosed with DR-TB in Sub-Saharan Africa; defined as resistance to one or more first-line anti-TB drugs, including isoniazid-resistant TB, MDR-TB and XDR-TB, as BDQ is primarily indicated for DR-TB in WHO guidelines.
Exposure (E): treatment with BDQ.
Outcome (O): studies reporting the incidence of QT interval prolongation.
Language: studies published in the English language and French. These are the most commonly used official languages in Sub-Saharan Africa.
Publication period: studies published from January 2013 to May 2024, during the time BDQ was recommended by the 2013 WHO interim guidelines for use in the treatment of DR-TB. Earlier studies are excluded as BDQ was not widely used pre-2013 for treatment of DR-TB.
Study design: studies designed as a retrospective or prospective observational study or randomised clinical trial (RCT), or case series with 10 or more participants will be included in the review. Inclusion of both RCTs and observational studies is mainly to capture a comprehensive range of evidence, as RCTs provide controlled data on BDQ’s cardiac safety, while observational studies reflect real-world clinical practice in resource-constrained Sub-Saharan African settings. Studies must report at least one ECG measurement to assess QT prolongation.

## Exclusion criteria
Single case reports, case series reporting <10 cases, study protocols and mathematical modelling. Case series with fewer than 10 cases are excluded to ensure sufficient sample size for reliable incidence estimates, because smaller series may lack statistical power and generalisability.
Studies without primary data (eg, reviews, editorials).
Animal studies.

## Information sources
Studies will be searched from PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and AJOL. All studies published from January 2013 to May 2024 will be imported. Individual-level data from the WHO Collaborating Centre for TB Research and Innovation will be explored, with care to avoid duplication of patients already included in other studies.

## Patient and public involvement
This study will analyse published studies; there will not be any patient and public involvement.

## Data extraction
A pre-designed macro form in Microsoft Excel 2016 will be used to extract data from each article on the following information:
Study characteristics: author, year of publication, study country, study design, period of data collection.
Patient characteristics: sample size, age (mean or median), sex, comorbidities, HIV status, electrolyte levels (eg, potassium, magnesium).
Treatment details: BDQ dosage, duration, concomitant medications, use of other QT-prolonging drugs (eg, fluoroquinolones, clofazimine).
Outcomes: incidence of QT interval prolongation and severity, and time to onset of QT prolongation (assessed within 4 weeks of BDQ initiation to capture peak M2 metabolite effects, with longer-term data extracted if available), distribution of QTc values, and change in QTc from baseline.
Two independent reviewers will extract data and enter it in duplicate to maintain consistency. Conflicts will be resolved by discussion and consensus among the authors. In case of any uncertainties about the study methods or results, an attempt to contact the primary authors will be made to seek clarity.

## Operational definition
QT interval prolongation is defined as a condition where the QT interval on an ECG is longer than normal (>450 ms for men and >460 ms for women).
DR-TB is resistant to one or more of the first-line anti-TB drugs. This includes MDR-TB (resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin) and XDR-TB (TB caused by Mycobacterium TB strains that fulfil the definition of multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB)) and are also resistant to any fluoroquinolone and at least one additional Group A drug. Group A drugs include levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, BDQ and linezolid, which are the most potent second-line medicines for treating DR-TB using longer treatment regimens.

## Study outcomes

## Primary outcome
The study outcome is the incidence of QT interval prolongation in patients receiving BDQ for DR-TB in Sub-Saharan Africa. This includes all patients on treatment, with QTc distribution reported for those with and without baseline prolongation.

## Additional outcomes
Severity of QT interval prolongation.
Time to QT interval prolongation.
Change in QTc from baseline before BDQ initiation.

## Risk of bias assessment
The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool will be used for RCTs and the Newcastle-Ottawa scale for cohort and case-control studies. Two reviewers will independently assess the risk of bias, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer. Study-specific biases, such as selection bias from excluding patients with prior QT prolongation, will be assessed using COSMOS-E guidelines, with impacts evaluated in sensitivity analyses. The risk of bias in individual studies will be graded as low, moderate and high. All eligible studies will be included in the meta-analysis, with sensitivity analyses conducted to assess the impact of studies with a high risk of bias, defined as those with critical flaws such as inadequate QT monitoring or high loss to follow-up (>20%).

## Analysis
All analyses will be performed using Microsoft Office 2016 (Microsoft, Washington, USA), GraphPad Prism V.8.0 and STATA V.18.0 statistical software (Stata, College Station, Texas, USA). QTc values corrected using Bazett’s formula will be prioritised for pooling; sensitivity analyses will assess the impact of other corrections (eg, Fridericia). Study authors will be contacted for raw heart rate data to standardise corrections where feasible. The primary outcome will be the pooled incidence of QT interval prolongation. A Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation will be applied to incidence proportions to stabilise variance prior to meta-analysis. For studies with repeated QT measurements, the maximum QTc per patient will be used for incidence estimates to avoid correlation. Longitudinal data, if available, will be analysed using generalised estimating equations to model changes in QTc over time, accounting for within-individual correlation. Heterogeneity assessment: heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic. I² will measure the percentage of total variability due to between-study heterogeneity, with I² >50% indicating significant heterogeneity. The Cochran’s Q statistic will also be used to complement I². Suhail Doi’s IVhet model will be used for meta-analysis to minimise small-study bias, with fixed effects applied if no heterogeneity is present. Subgroup analysis: subgroup analyses will explore potential sources of heterogeneity, such as study design, population characteristics and concomitant medications. Subgroup analyses will also include trial versus routine clinical practice to assess differences in QT prolongation incidence under controlled versus real-world conditions. The subgroup analyses will also examine measurement strategies (single ECG vs repeated ECGs or Holter monitoring) to assess the impact of measurement variability on QT prolongation estimates, and will include stratification by pretreatment QTc (normal vs prolonged) to assess its impact on incidence and by time since treatment initiation (eg, ≤4 weeks vs >4 weeks) to account for M2 metabolite kinetics. Sensitivity analysis: sensitivity analyses will be conducted by excluding studies with moderate or high risk of bias or outliers to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. A funnel plot will be generated to assess for publication bias. For studies with comparator groups (eg, non-BDQ regimens), relative risks of QT prolongation will be estimated and pooled where sufficient data are available, using the IVhet model if heterogeneity is present. p<0.05 will be considered statistically significant at the 95% CI. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise data from individual studies. A narrative synthesis will be employed to present the results and discussion of the different studies included.

## Ethics and dissemination
This systematic review will analyse published studies without identifiable patient data, requiring no ethical approval. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and stakeholder engagement with TB control programmes in Sub-Saharan Africa to inform clinical guidelines and optimise DR-TB treatment protocols. Data extracted from included studies will be made publicly available on publication.

## Ethical considerations
This study will analyse published studies without identifiable patient data; as such, ethical approvals will not be required.
