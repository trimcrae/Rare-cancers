## Abstract

## Objective
Endoscopic sinus and skull base surgery has led to significant improvements in patient outcomes, yet may have come at a cost to surgeons' musculoskeletal (MSK) health. We aimed to determine the prevalence and characteristics of work‐related MSK disorders (WRMDs) in endoscopic sinus and skull base surgeons; to investigate contributing factors for WRMD in this population; and to evaluate the effectiveness of ergonomic interventions on the severity or prevalence of WRMD in this population.

## Data Sources
Medline, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to April 2, 2024. A bibliographic examination was performed for further papers.

## Review Methods
Inclusion criteria included original peer‐reviewed papers with work‐related MSK outcomes (prevalence, contributing factors, and interventions) relating to endoscopic sinus and/or skull base surgeons in any language.

## Results
Of 25,772 unique citations, 37 studies met the inclusion criteria. The pooled lifetime, point, and 12‐month prevalences of WRMD were 75.9% (95% confidence interval; I
2, 67.2%‐83.6%, I
2 95.6%), 80.8% (77.0%‐84.3%, I
2 98.0%), and 82.0% (71.8%‐90.3%, I
2 60.96%) respectively. The neck, lumbar spine, and thoracic spine were the most commonly involved areas. One of 9 studies on contributing factors investigated discomfort as an outcome. The remainder focussed on surrogate outcomes (eg, posture, hand dysfunction). Two of the 13 intervention studies investigated pain or fatigue as an outcome. The remainder targeted posture, muscle activity, or workload.

## Conclusion
WRMDs are highly prevalent in endoscopic sinus and skull base surgeons. Further studies focusing on the direct outcomes of WRMD such as pain are needed.

Health care has some of the highest prevalence of musculoskeletal (MSK) injuries in any workplace and this rate is increasing, particularly among in surgeons. , , , MSK disorders are the most common work‐related injury, the most common cause of severe long‐term pain and physical disability, the greatest contributor to the demand for rehabilitation worldwide, and the most common cause of long‐term absenteeism. , , , Occupational demands are a major contributor to MSK disorders and surgeons are at particularly high risk of work‐related musculoskeletal disorders (WRMD). Ergonomic interventions can reduce or prevent WRMD and also improve productivity, efficiency, work quality, psychosocial factors, economic benefits, and reduce absenteeism. , , , , , , Despite this evidence surgical ergonomics lags behind that of most other occupations or industries. , 

Otorhinolaryngologists (ORLs) have some of the highest rates of occupational MSK pain, with up to 97% reporting discomfort attributed to work tasks. , Endoscopic sinus and skull base surgeons are at particularly high risk, due to the ergonomics of nasal endoscopy and the duration of some of these procedures as expanded minimally invasive endoscopic approaches to the sinuses and anterior skull base become more complex. , , , , , The advent of minimally invasive surgery has provided immense benefits to patients with reduced morbidity, complications and lengths of hospitalization, and improved cosmesis. , , , However, little attention has been focused on the ergonomics of these technologies and their impact on surgeons' well‐being.
To date, no systematic review has been published on MSK injuries in endoscopic sinus and skull base surgeons (ESSBSs). There is an urgent need to establish the prevalence of WRMD in this population, and to better understand risk factors and intervention and prevention strategies for MSK injuries.
The aims of this systematic review were to determine the prevalence and characteristics of WRMD in ESSBSs, to investigate contributing factors for WRMD in this population, and to evaluate the effectiveness of ergonomic interventions on the severity or prevalence of WRMD in this population.

## Methods
As a preliminary step, PROSPERO (the National Institute for Health and Research International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) and the National Library of Medicine Clinical Trials Registry were searched to ensure no similar reviews had been registered. A preliminary literature search was also performed and no similar review was identified, supporting the novelty of this review. This systematic review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/logout.php CRD42021227270). The search strategy followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐analyses 2020 Checklist. A Population, Exposure/Intervention, Comparison, Outcome model was utilized to evaluate studies for inclusion as follows: Population: otolaryngologists and neurosurgeons performing endoscopic sinus and skull base surgery including trainees; Exposure: endoscopic sinus and skull base surgery; Intervention: any ergonomic intervention aimed to address WRMD; Comparison: nil; Outcomes: MSK disorders, risk/contributing factors.
The search was not limited to specific language sources. Duplicates were removed and EndNote (X9.3.3; Clarivate Analytics) was used as the bibliography manager. Covidence review software (Veritas Health Innovation) was used for study screening and selection. Studies in languages other than English were translated by certified translators.

## Study Selection
Five reviewers (R.G.C., J.R.Z., A.R.G., L.H.P., G.O.) assessed all retrieved records (in teams of 2 with R.G.C. reviewing all records) screening titles and abstracts. Two reviewers then undertook full‐text screening and independently selected the final studies (R.G.C, A.R.G.). Disagreements were discussed between the reviewers, and, when necessary, resolved in discussions with a reviewer not involved in screening (C.L.C.).

## Search Strategy
A search of Medline, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, and Scopus was conducted without time restriction on September 11, 2023 and was updated on April 2, 2024 (see supplemental File  , available online, for full search strategy). Search terms included keywords for MSK pain and injury, ergonomics, and endoscopic sinus and skull base surgery.

## Eligibility Criteria
Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (i) contained original peer‐reviewed data (eg, retrospective, cross‐sectional and prospective observational studies, and randomized controlled trials); (ii) participants/population were ORL or neurosurgeons who perform endoscopic sinus and skull base surgery (ESSBS) (trainees and/or consultants/attendings); and (iii) reported at least 1 outcome on WRMD prevalence, risk factors or ergonomic interventions to address WRMD.

## Critical Appraisal of the Studies
Two independent reviewers critically assessed each study using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) system for the Unified Management of the Assessment and Review of Information (SUMARI) application. The JBI SUMARI checklist tool is designed for health, social sciences, and humanities research fields. Two reviewers (R.G.C and A.R.G.) then independently rated the studies as “high quality,” “moderate quality,” or “low quality.” Each item was scored as 0 for not reported, 1 for uncertain or inadequate, and 2 for adequate. Disagreements between individual judgements were resolved by a third independent reviewer (C.L.C.).

## Certainty of the Evidence
Two reviewers (R.G.C. and M.G.M.) independently rated the certainty of the evidence derived following the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach. , This tool evaluates the evidence using 5 main factors: risk of bias, indirectness, imprecision, inconsistency, and publication bias. This results in 4 possible levels of certainty: high, moderate, low, and very low. GRADE has been endorsed and adopted by multiple international professional medical societies and organizations, governments, and regulatory bodies. 

## Data Extraction and Meta‐analysis
Data extraction included study design and methodology, participant demographics, sample size, subspecialty, response rate, prevalence, descriptive characteristics, and type of WRMD. Tools used to evaluate diagnoses and pain were also recorded. Any contributing factors or ergonomic interventions to mitigate WRMD were also recorded such as the type of intervention, ergonomic data, evaluation tools utilized, and outcome measures.
Data were extracted from studies of ergonomic assessments or interventions. These data were grouped into 3 categories: baseline ergonomics assessment, ergonomic products and technologies, and ergonomic education/behavior modification strategies.
A custom data spreadsheet was developed independently by 2 reviewers and adapted from 2 studies prior to commencement of data extraction. One reviewer (R.G.C.) extracted and another (J.R.Z.) checked the data. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by a third independent reviewer (C.L.C.).
Study investigators were contacted for any unreported or missing data, or additional details. Data were recorded in an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel for Mac 16.4; Microsoft). Random effects meta‐analysis was used to estimate pooled prevalence data/overall summary proportions using Meta‐XL (version 5.3; Epigear International). Fixed effects meta‐analysis was used when there were only 2 studies providing data. Analysis used a double arcsine transformation and the DerSimonian and Laird method to estimate between‐study variance and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The I
2 statistic was used to evaluate the proportion of variance in the observed effects reflecting the heterogeneity between studies. Meta‐analysis was performed for any WRMD, symptom type, specific body parts affected, and timing of onset of symptoms. Other results were summarized using a narrative synthesis.

## Results

## Study Characteristics
The initial search produced 41,559 papers. After duplicates were removed, 25,772 papers remained. Title and abstract screening led to 257 papers being selected for full‐text review. Thirty‐seven papers met the inclusion criteria and were included ( ). Two of these studies were published in languages other than English (German, Farsi) and were professionally translated. Selected studies were published between 2003 and 2024.
 Of the 37 papers, 16 were prevalence studies using surveys. Two observational studies and 1 crossover study also reported prevalence data. , , Nine studies were of contributing factors for WRMD and 13 were studies of interventions aimed to reduce WRMD. One study investigated both risk factors, and the effectiveness of an intervention, for WRMD. 

## Critical Appraisal
Using JBI, the studies were categorized as analytical cross‐sectional (n = 3), prevalence (n = 16), quasi‐experimental (n = 15), or randomized controlled trials (n = 3) (Supplemental File  , available online).
One analytical cross‐sectional study was rated as having high methodological quality and 2 were rated as having low methodological quality (due to limited detail on the subjects and setting and on adjustment strategies for confounding). , 

Of the 16 prevalence studies, 8 studies were rated as high quality, , , , , , , , 2 were rated as medium quality (due to poorly described statistical analyses and insufficient participant detail description), , and 6 studies were graded as low quality (due to low/unknown response rates, insufficient participant information, and/or not specifying that participants answered questions specifically regarding WRMD). , , , , , 

All 15 quasi‐experimental studies were graded as low quality. , , , , , , , , , , , , , , Many studies did not adequately describe the participants, have a control group, use reliable outcome measures, or provide adequate/complete data sets.
All 3 randomized controlled trials were appraised as low quality. , , All lacked allocation concealment or participant, therapist, and assessor blinding.

## Overall Quality of the Evidence
Using the GRADE criteria, the quality of the evidence for studies investigating prevalence was rated as low, and low to very low for studies investigating contributing factors and interventions for WRMD (Supplemental File  , available online). Studies of contributing factors and interventions for WRMD were mostly downgraded due to serious risk of indirectness (as many studies only evaluated 1 population or intervention and outcomes were mostly surrogate measures of risk), serious risk of imprecision (small sample size), serious risk of bias (nonstandard trial designs, lack of randomization, concealment or blinding), and publication bias (small studies).

## Study Demographics
The mean age of participants was 41.8 years (±SD; range; number of studies, ±6.4; 32‐53 years; n = 15), mean weight was 73.2 kg/161.4 lbs (±6.1; 65.3‐81.7 kg; n = 6). Most of the study population was right‐handed (mean; ±SD; range; number of studies, 94.0% ±8.2; 66.7%‐ 100.0%; n = 19) and male (79.1% ±15.62; 45.5%‐100.0%; n = 25). Most surgeons stand during surgery (83.8% ±14.2, 58.0%‐100.0%; n = 10) and, of those who stand, most stand on the right‐hand side of the bed when operating alone (83.7% ±8.0; 78.0%‐95%; n = 3) , , and when operating with another surgeon (65.0%; n = 1). The majority use a remote monitor screen for visualization rather than a beam‐splitter (83.1% ±8.4; 69.0%‐93.0%; n = 5).

## Prevalence of WRMD
Nineteen studies reported prevalence data ( ) from a total of 3340 participants (range 6‐417). This included 16 surveys, 2 observational studies, and 1 within‐subject crossover study. Approximately 31,530 surveys were distributed (range 102‐12,000), with response rates ranging from 7.0% to 65.0%.
 The overall lifetime prevalence of WRMD was 75.9% (95% CI; the number of studies; 67.2%‐ 83.6%; n = 13), point prevalence was 80.8% (77.0%‐84.3%; n = 2) and 12‐month prevalence was 82.0% (71.8%‐90.3%; n = 4) ( ;  , , ). Pain was the most common symptom (63.9%; 58.3%‐69.3%; n = 6) followed by stiffness (48.4%; 43.3%‐53.5%; n = 7), fatigue (40.6%; 34.5%‐46.8%; n = 4), and paresthesia (19.4%; 12.0%‐28.0%; n = 7) ( ). In 1 observational study, surgeons reported a significantly increased prevalence of symptoms in both wrists and thenar eminences, right metacarpophalangeal joints, and right palmar eminence after surgery. 

 The most common body areas affected were the neck (54.0% of all respondents; 44.7%‐63.3%; n = 17), lower back (44.9%; 37.1%‐52.8%; n = 15), thoracic spine (39.8%; 23.3%‐57.6%; n = 7), shoulders (32.2%; 22.9%‐42.3%; n = 14), hands (18.5%; 13.7%‐23.9%; n = 6), wrists (16.0%; 3.2%‐34.9%; n = 5), knees (13.1%; 9.5%‐17.2%; n = 8), legs (10.2%; 4.8%‐17.2%; n = 8), and elbows (9.5%; 6.5%‐13.0%; n = 5) ( ;  ). It was noted in 1 pre‐post observational study that mean grip strength reduced in both hands (by 6.2% right hand, 7.4% left hand; P < .01) and right‐hand dexterity reduced by 2.3% (P = .04) after endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS). 

 Most respondents who developed WRMD developed it during specialty surgical training (75.4%; 62.2‐86.6; n = 3). One within subject crossover study reported that onset of WRMD symptoms most commonly occurs 1 to 3 hours into surgery and also after surgery. 

Of those with WRMD, 5.4% restricted their practice (±2.2; 2.9%‐8.9%; n = 6), 8.2% retired early or planned to do so (±7.6; 0.3%‐23.0%; n = 7), 25.0% felt that WRMD negatively impacted their hobbies or sport (±1.6; 23.0%‐27.0%; n = 3), and 8.0% underwent surgery for their WRMD (±8.4; 0.62%‐28.6%; n = 8). , , , , , , , There was very limited information on the types of surgery or the formal diagnoses for the WRMD.
Less than half of those with WRMD sought treatment (48.1% ±20.8; 19.2%‐84.3%; n = 13). Ten studies provided details on the type of treatment received for WRMD. The most common form of treatment was medication, then physiotherapy, massage, and various forms of exercise.
Over 70.0% of respondents lacked awareness of ergonomic principles (72.6% ±8.1; 63.0%‐83.8%; n = 6) yet nearly 85.0% were interested in ergonomic training (84.6% ±8.0; 74.0%‐95.6%; n = 6).

## Contributing Factors
Of the 9 studies evaluating contributing factors, 8 evaluated posture , , , , , , , and 1 evaluated hand dysfunction ( ). Outcome measures represented different constructs or were collected in a nonhomogeneous manner, and therefore meta‐analysis was not possible.
 

## Results Regarding Posture/Postural Analysis

## Spinal Range and Angle
While performing ESS, 1 analytical cross‐sectional study found that surgeons were noted to adopt high‐risk cervical angles of flexion and extension combined with lateral flexion and rotation according to the RULA tool. In this study, surgeons also adopted a thoracolumbar slouch position with coexistent lateral flexion and rotation and they performed greater/higher than recommended repetitive movements at the neck and right shoulder. The right shoulder was constantly abducted during surgery requiring continuous or prolonged muscle activity (all participants held the endoscope in the left hand.). These data on joints were based on mean excursions for surgeons measured throughout an entire surgical procedure.
In another analytical cross‐sectional study, trainees adopted greater/more angles of flexion in the back and neck compared to attendings, particularly during the last 15 minutes of ESS. Further, all participants adopted increasing angles of neck and back flexion as the surgery progressed. It is unknown if these postures were sustained. In a within‐subject crossover study, male surgeons, taller surgeons (≥5′10″/177.8 cm), and those performing head and neck ablative surgery spent the least amount of time in an upright posture (72.0%; P = .002, 71.3%; P = .006, and 54.9%; P < .0001, respectively). This same study also found surgeons performing endoscopic surgeries spent the greatest proportion of time in upright postures (90%; P < .0001). 

## Sitting Versus Standing
A within‐subject crossover case study (n = 1) comparing sitting and standing during ESS found that operating in the standing position had a higher risk of discomfort in the lower limbs, lower back, and postural muscles while operating in the sitting position had a higher risk of discomfort in the shoulders and upper limbs. A separate observational study found nasal examination in the outpatient/clinic setting carried moderate risk of MSK injury using the Loading on Upper Body Assessment tool (Supplemental File  , available online), particularly for the shoulders and arms. However, using the Quick Exposure Check tool (see Supplemental File  , available online), the risk was considered low.

## Patient, Surgeon, and Monitor Position
According to 2 observational studies by the same author, the following positioning was found to be the most ergonomically favorable for 2‐person, 4‐handed ESSBS: operating surgeon standing to the patient's right, assisting surgeon standing to the left with the patient's head centrally positioned. The team utilizes 2 monitor screens, 1 to the left and the other to the right of the patient. , The least ergonomically favorable positions involved only 1 monitor screen or both surgeons standing on the patient's right. , 

## Ergonomic Interventions for WRMD
Thirteen studies evaluated a variety of ergonomic interventions and meta‐analysis was not possible ( ). , , , , , , , , , , , , One study evaluated a robotic endoscope holder, 2 studies evaluated robotic instrument handles, , and 4 evaluated augmented image guidance technology. , , , The remaining studies evaluated microbreaks, an exoskeleton, a body support device, a postural training device, an ergonomic operating room configuration, and a pistol‐grip endoscope. Only 2 studies measured the impact of an intervention on pain and/or fatigue. , The other 11 studies evaluated the impact of an intervention on workload, surface electromyography (sEMG) values, posture, or comfort. , , , , , , , , , , 

 

## Interventions that Reduce Pain and Fatigue
The use of an external body support device or an exoskeleton worn while operating was shown to reduce overall fatigue and pain in 2 within‐subject crossover studies. , These devices also stabilized the body axis, and reduced MSK loading in the dominant hand and in the foot that did not operate foot pedals. , In a separate within‐subject crossover study, intraoperative stretching microbreaks during ORL procedures (including ESS) were noted to significantly reduce pain scores in the neck, shoulders, hands, and lower back after surgery. However, the intervention had no effect on pain scores in the upper back, feet, or knees. 

## Interventions that Reduce Workload and Ergonomic Risk

Auditory alerts and image guidance: The addition of an auditory alert (warning a surgeon when their instruments were within a critical distance of vital structures) and real‐time image guidance in ESSBS, was shown to reduce overall subjective task workload, mental demand, perceived effort, and frustration in 1 nonrandomized and 3 randomized within‐subject crossover studies. , , , However, these strategies did not reduce perceived physical demand. , , 

Instrumentation and operating theaters: Forceps with 3 degrees of freedom (DoF) attached by a forearm‐mounted handle (FMH) and controlled by a joystick with 4 DoF were compared to a conventional pair of endoscopic forceps using an ESSBS simulator in a within‐subject crossover study. Using the FMH resulted in a lower RULA/ergonomic risk score and lower scores for mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, task complexity and situational stress. As the study had only 1 subject, its results are not generalizable. It is also difficult to ascertain which component of the prototype handle contributed to the lower ergonomic scores: the joystick control, forearm mounting or the end‐effector instrument with 3 DoF.
This same FMH was then compared by 9 surgeons to standard forceps and to a hand‐held rotatable joystick‐operated tool (RJH) with a finger trigger controlling the forceps in a partially randomized within‐subject crossover study. The RJH tool utilized a palmar grip and had a modifiable, rotating body to fit 7 different configurations accommodating small, medium, and large hand sizes and left‐ and right‐handed surgeons. Surgeons using the RJH had lower RULA scores, particularly for upper arm, wrist, and trunk postures compared to the other 2 tools. When using both prototype handles, surgeons had lower SURG‐TLX/workload scores compared to the standard instrument. However, the mean SURG‐TLX/workload score was lowest when using the FMH. The authors attributed this difference to the limited amount of time that participants had to familiarize themselves with the prototypes. With more extensive use, it was felt that the RJH would be more comfortable. 

Utilizing a pistol grip flexible endoscope recorded lower upper limb surface sEMG values in the musculature of the hand gripping the endoscope compared to a conventional endoscope in a within‐subject crossover study. No differences were noted in the musculature of the insertion hand. A robotic endoscope holder with 7 DoF was compared to a handheld endoscope in a simulated ESSBS trainer in a randomized within‐subject crossover study. Surgeons felt less successful performing a grasping task, and more successful performing a septal flap task, with the robotic endoscope holder. There was no difference in mental workload or stress between tasks. 

A within‐subject crossover study of a prototype operating room with ergonomic adaptations compared to a traditional operating room ( ) noted shorter times for total patient treatment, pre‐ and postsurgical activities, documentation, and total cumulative work hours. The prototype operating room had better average ergonomic questionnaire scores and perceived personal comfort scores. A within‐subject crossover trial noted surgeons spent more time in an upright/neutral posture with biofeedback intervention than without. However, the subsequent improvement was modest (mean 5% improvement). 

## Discussion
This is the first systematic review and meta‐analysis to evaluate WRMD in ESSBS including papers in any language. We found 12‐month prevalence of WRMD to be 82.0%, which is consistent with findings in ORL in general and in other surgical subspecialties (up to 79% and 96% reported, respectively). , , The varied findings may be due to the type of prevalence reported (single timepoint, over 12 months or lifetime), whether the population was asked specifically to report only WRMD, and the reliance on self‐reporting. We also found a point prevalence of 80.8% and a lifetime prevalence of 75.9%. While this is seemingly contradictory, the data comes from different studies, thus it may be explained by the different populations and methodologies. Additionally, only 2 studies measured point prevalence, therefore, the confidence in this measure may be low. Due to the age of 1 study, we ran a sensitivity analysis excluding this study to investigate if excluding the older study would impact the results. We found virtually identical results (prevalence of back pain changed from 44.9% to 44.6% and prevalence of neck pain changed from 54% to 54.3%) and therefore, we have presented the results including all studies. Overall, it is evident that WRMD in ESSBS is concerningly high, especially if we compare this to WRMD prevalence in other industries. For example, from 2015 to 2016, the Australian health care and social assistance industry had the highest number of serious WRMD of all industries (11,370). In the same period, the manufacturing industry had 7095, and the construction industry had 6980, serious WRMD claims. The point prevalence of low back and neck pain in the general global population is 7.3% and 4.8%, respectively. However, this review found an overall prevalence of low back and neck pain in ESSBS of 45% and 54.0%, respectively. Two studies in this review reported point prevalence data on low back and neck pain which was calculated as 50.1% and 63.7%, respectively, on a subgroup analysis. , These rates of low back pain are similar to those of professional football and floorball athletes and are higher than in many other elite athletes. , , Therefore, the prevalence of WRMD in ESSBS is indeed comparable to, or higher than, that in athletes or those who work in industries that require manual labor. However, while it is widely known that the risk is high in these industries, WRMD among skull base surgeons has received a lot less attention.
The neck, lumbar and thoracic spine, and shoulders are the most affected body parts and pain is the most common symptom. This is consistent with findings in studies of health care professionals in general and specifically surgeons. 

WRMDs not only impact the surgeon but also their patients and the health care system. This review found that 5% of surgeons restricted their practice and 8.2% retired early due to their WRMD. Surgeons' performance is negatively impacted by poorly designed ergonomic tasks and MSK pain contributes to burnout and impaired quality of life in surgeons. , , Despite this, we found that few ESSBSs had received any ergonomic training, yet most were interested.
Posture is a known risk factor for WRMD in surgery. It has been traditionally held that achieving a neutral body posture will reduce WRMD. This has been described as standing with the head over the shoulders, chin parallel to the floor with the ears, shoulders, hips, and ankles aligned. Maintaining this neutral posture while operating is not practical for a surgeon. Further, current evidence refutes the existence of a universal neutral body posture as described, but rather neutral postures vary between individuals. , Sustained, awkward, and repetitive postures have been identified as the main contributing factor to WRMD in health care professionals. , Prolonged postures, especially while performing a complex cognitive task such as surgery, result in greater muscular activity and energy expenditure and less postural control, reducing endurance and efficiency, ultimately leading to MSK injuries. , , , , Therefore, surgeons should aim to achieve their unique neutral posture during surgery which should also be active and incorporate stretching microbreaks. This review found low‐quality evidence that an auditory alert system utilized with real‐time image guidance may reduce the cognitive workload in ESSBSs, but did not reduce physical demand. , , 

We noted that ESSBSs, particularly trainees, adopt flexed postures considered to be high risk as assessed using validated postural assessment tools. However, there is no evidence that upright (or extended) postures reduce WRMD. , In fact, slouching for periods of up to 20 minutes has been shown to have no adverse effects on spinal biomechanics or low back pain. Therefore, it is uncertain if optimizing time spent in upright postures is a useful focus for reducing WRMD in ESSBSs. It is also not certain if these flexed postures are prolonged or static based on the literature.
When it comes to ergonomic interventions, there is low‐quality evidence to support the effectiveness of stretching microbreaks and exoskeletons in reducing WRMD symptoms. , , Endoscopic instruments designed to be forearm mounted with greater DoF and utilizing a pistol grip reduce workload and improve posture scores. Further, developing modifiable instruments to fit different hand sizes and handedness and modifiable/modular operating theaters that accommodate the particular nuances of ESSBS and of the people utilizing them may also reduce WRMD. , We have little research on robotics in ESSBS mainly due to the size of the robotic instruments. However, robotic endoscope holders do show promise, yet higher quality studies are needed in this area. 

## Limitations
Interpretation of this study's findings is limited by the poor overall quality of the evidence and high heterogeneity among the studies. This is common in prevalence studies but may also be due to the lack of standardized ergonomic outcome measures and reporting methods. Prevalence studies are also prone to response bias. Further, it can be difficult for surgeons to ascertain whether their MSK symptoms are purely occupational, potentially introducing recall bias. Unfortunately, very few studies assessed the direct relationship between risk factors or interventions on pain or other WRMD symptoms. Of the 2 studies reporting pain outcomes, neither controlled for other factors that could contribute to pain such as pre‐existing sporting injuries. , Therefore, we cannot be certain that these “high‐risk” postures are responsible for WRMD in ESSBS. Finally, WRMD takes years to develop, limiting the outcomes of short‐term intervention studies.

## Unanswered Questions and Future Research
Larger international prevalence studies are required to evaluate WRMD in this population more accurately. Research defining, and evaluating the impact of, “high‐risk” postures (including joint angles and movement frequencies) and interventions to reduce or prevent WRMD is required. There is no universal definition of acceptable movement frequencies for each joint at force levels typically adopted during ESS/ESSBS. Acceptable movement frequencies have been estimated in an industrial setting for the shoulder (2.5/minute) and wrist (10/minute) only. This study actually states that “There is no basis for conclusions concerning minimum rates of repetitiveness…that will abolish the risk.” The RULA and REBA tools consider >4 repeated small‐range actions per minute at any joint as repetitive based on a general definition of the risk level. , However, the authors acknowledge that further assessment in this area is required. , Given the findings of different frequencies for levels of risk for the shoulder and elbow, it is likely that a universal frequency for each joint is inaccurate for calculating risk and an area requiring more research. It is also uncertain whether performing ESSBS in the sitting or standing position is likely to lead to less WRMD Finally, there are no studies evaluating the impact of an ergonomic curriculum in ESSBS which is a glaring oversight.

## Conclusion
This review has highlighted the high prevalence of WRMD in ESSBSs and the potential for this population to adopt postures that are traditionally considered high‐risk. Strategies are available to mitigate this risk, however, more research with standardized assessment tools and outcomes to define which postures pose risk and which strategies are most effective is required. Further, awareness and early identification of the warning signs of WRMD could further assist in the prevention and highlight areas of focus for hazard reduction.
