## ABSTRACT
The prevalence of depression among visually impaired or blind children and adolescents has not been systematically reviewed. This study aims to provide the prevalence of depression among visually impaired or blind children and adolescents. This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines (PRISMA) (2020) and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines. A systematic search of various online databases was done to identify and include studies reporting the prevalence of depression among visually impaired or blind children and adolescents (aged up to 20 years). A random-effects meta-analysis was used to estimate the pooled prevalence of depression. Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 to explain heterogeneity meta-regressive analysis and subgroup analyses were done. With the finally selected 13 studies consisting of 822 participants, the overall pooled prevalence of depression or dysthymia among visually impaired children or adolescents was 14% (137/822 individuals, 95% CI = 9% to 20%), with high heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 80.11%; P < 0.001). Five studies that expressed gender distribution showed a cumulative prevalence of diagnosed depressive disorders was 6.85% and 18.96%, respectively, for male (n = 219, I2 = 47.52) and female (n = 116, I2 = 60.6%) participants. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we selected and analyzed 13 studies and estimated pooled prevalence of depression was 14% (95% CI = 9% to 20%), among visually impaired or blind children and adolescents.

## INTRODUCTION
The estimated childhood disability due to various causes is about 5.1% according to WHO and World Bank,[ ] out of this the estimated prevalence of visual impairment is about 0.1 to 1.1/1000 children[ ] and a recent meta-analysis reported a prevalence of 3.82%, based on presenting visual acuity, and 1.67% based on best corrected visual acuity.[ ] In a systematic review and meta-analysis (n = 5934 participants), a pooled prevalence estimate of depression was found to be 14.3% (95% CI, 10.5%–18.6%) among children, adolescents, and young adults population with life-limiting conditions (like HIV, cancer, cystic fibrosis, thalassemia, or chronic kidney disease),[ ] suggesting that life adversity or disability and illness has an implicating role on occurrence of depression. A meta-analytic study revealed the prevalence of depression at 24.2% [95% CI 20.6-28.0] among children and adolescents exposed to a traumatic event.[ ]
Visual impairments impose various medical, interpersonal, psychological, academic, and social challenges, thus affecting overall “health related quality of life” for the children and their parents.[ , ] Higher burden and depression are found among caregivers of the visually impaired,[ ] which further negatively affects the impaired child. These burdens are attributable to higher multi-morbidity,[ ] economic burden costs of medical and social care,[ , ] and restricted educational, occupational, and social opportunities.[ ] Many visually impaired due to their disability may experience bullying, a lifetime prevalence may be as high as 41.7%,[ ] maltreatment,[ ] violence,[ , ] and sexual exploitation or abuse.[ , ] In addition to a few preventable ophthalmic causes, many avoidable inequalities and abuse lead to the misery of visual impairment.
Community-based studies indicated that adult people with low vision are 1.6–2.8 times more likely to develop depression compared to those without vision impairment.[ , ] Visual impairment itself has increased the risk of depression (hazard ratio = 1.22),[ ] and it is even higher for the younger population (0–29 years old, n = 3,485; adjusted HR = 1.30, 95% CI = 0.88–1.92).[ ] Studies also suggested that the cause and onset of visual impairment are also significant determinants of depression. Late-onset and deteriorating vision loss show higher depression, whereas congenitally blind adolescents do not differ much from peers without visual impairment.[ ]
Many studies suggest that mood disorders, particularly depression, are associated with eye disease.[ – ] A recent meta-analysis indicated the prevalence of depression among “eye disease patients” was 25% (1502/6589 individuals, 95% CI, 0.20–0.30), ranging from 5.4% to 57%.[ ] However, this meta-analysis included the adult population with eye disease, excluding the child and adolescent populations. One systematic review[ ] was published in 2017 on the mental health of visually impaired children and young adults. This review was inclusive of various mental health problems, including emotional problems, distress, depression, anxiety, self-esteem, etc., without any measure of prevalence for specific diagnoses. Hence, we planned to carry out this systematic review of epidemiological studies and proportional meta-analysis, to assess the global prevalence of diagnosed depressive disorders like depression or dysthymia among visually impaired or blind children or adolescents.

## METHODS

## Planning and registration
This systematic review and meta-analysis were planned and conducted in accordance with the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) with the checklist (Stroup et al., 2000) and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines 2020 (PRISMA).[ ] The protocol was applied for registration on PROSPERO and we received registration number: CRD42021239562, on March 28, 2021.

## Search strategy and selection criteria
A comprehensive search of five electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Database, Web of Science, and Google Scholar was done by two researchers (AKB and NG) independently. The language of publication included in the study was English; we conducted the research based on the data selected from the databases according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. To ensure the comprehensiveness of published data, this research was expanded and retrieved from the reference list of literature. An advanced Google Scholar search was carried out with all keywords (“depression AND prevalence AND visual impairments OR eye AND child OR adolescents”) “with all the words”, anywhere in the article retrieved approx. 1,55,000 results. We completed about 100 pages of search but found relevant articles within the initial 20–25 pages only. The search was conducted from March 25, 2021 to April 30, 2021, the search strategy for all electronic databases is shown in .
Pre-defined inclusion criteria were used to include studies for the prevalence of depression and/or dysthymia among visually impaired or blind children and adolescents (aged up to 20 years). Included were studies that showed a total number of sample size (denominator) and showed the percentage or number of diagnosed depressive disorders (numerator). Regarding criteria for the diagnosis of depression not being pre-defined, it was because various diagnostic tools, rating scales, and clinical assessments were used for the diagnosis of depression. We accepted the same definition used in the published studies for the diagnosis of depression in our current meta-analysis. Certain studies were excluded due to the reason of reporting only group mean scores of rating scales and not establishing the diagnosis of depression. These were observational studies, including cross-sectional studies (such as epidemiological surveys, questionnaire surveys, online surveys, etc.), irrespective of sex, race, or setting (institution [special school for visually impaired] or hospital and community).

## Data selection, extraction
It was done in duplicate independently by two teams of reviewers (AKB with NG and PP with RRK). Selected literature and extracted data were cross-checked for repetition. The titles and abstracts of the literature were read and then the full texts were read to determine whether they could be included in the research. If important information involved in the research was missing or unclear, we contacted the author of the original study through emails and telephone. The data extraction table included the following: 1) Name of the first author with the year of publication, 2) Country, 3) Setting of study - whether special or integrated school or hospital-based study, 4) Age, 5) Gender ratio, 6) Diagnostic methods or rating scales used for the diagnosis of depression, 7) Definition/establishment of visual impairment, and 8) Total sample size, and the diagnosed case of depression. Any disagreement would be settled through adjudication based on discussion or consultation with all members of the research group.

## Methodological quality assessment
The two authors AKB and PP assessed the quality of the methodology and independently cross-checked the results. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated by the “JBI Quality Assessment Tool for prevalence Studies.”[ ] The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality assessment tool for prevalence studies has nine items with response options “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” and “not applicable.” The result of each paper was graded, and any discrepancies with scoring were resolved with discussion among all reviewers.

## Statistical analysis
We computed the pooled proportion of the presence of diagnosed depressive disorders like depression or dysthymia among visually impaired or blind children or adolescents across the studies using the DerSimonian and Laired random-effects model. We used Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize the variance. The exact method was used for computing a 95% confidence interval. Heterogeneity between the studies was examined by Higgins I2 statistics in which heterogeneity of more than 50% was considered as high heterogeneity. We used the metareg command in STATA software version 13 to (Stata Corp. 2013. Stata Statistical Software: Release 13. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP) to conduct the meta-regression analysis, to examine whether the source of heterogeneity could be found. We assessed the publication bias using the funnel plot and Egger test.

## RESULTS

## Study selection and characteristics
Initially, the search strategies retrieved 610 records from a database, and from these, 156 duplicate records were removed and 454 records were screened further. We identified 415 studies that were non-relevant to study questions based on screening of title and abstract, and the remaining 39 articles were retrieved for detailed full-text assessment; out of these, 10 studies met the inclusion criteria and the rest 29 were excluded. Also, 6 articles from other methods like Research gate, individual contact, etc., were identified and retrieved, and 3 articles were included for review, thus a final total of 13 studies were included for review [ Flowchart of study selection process].
 The summary of the main characteristics of studies included (n = 13) in the systematic review is shown in . Five studies were conducted in India,[ – ] two studies each in Finland[ , ] and Turkey,[ , ] and the remaining four studies one each in Saudi Arabia, China, Istanbul, and the USA.[ , – ] The sample size of the studies ranged from 20 to 146. Thirteen cross-sectional studies involving a total of 822 participants were included in the study. There were 548 male participants, 236 female participants, and the gender of 38 participants was not available in one study.[ ] A total of 564 (68.61%) participants were congenitally blind and the remaining others were visually impaired with various known and unknown acquired causes. However, funnel plots indicated publication bias.
 

## Settings and measuring instruments
Seven studies had been carried out at special schools for the visually impaired,[ , – , ] two in integrated schools,[ , ] three at a hospital OPD setting,[ , , ] and one was an online survey.[ ]
Depression was assessed using a number of different tools. Depression was diagnosed by diagnostic schedules for four studies[ , , , ] and others by rating scales like Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI),[ , ] Beck Depression Inventory (BDI),[ , ] the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale - 21 Items (DASS-21),[ , , ] and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).[ , ]
There was no standard definition of visual impairment adopted in studies [ ]. Studies conducted in special schools assumed every student as visually impaired.[ , – , ] Two studies carried out in the hospital setting use visual equity 0.1–0.5 for the definition of visual impairment through the Snellen chart.[ , ] One of the studies used a disease-specific vision-related quality-of-life diagnosis based on National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ-25). Diagnosed cases of Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) participated in the online study in which a specific scale of diagnosis was not mentioned.[ ]
The risk of bias assessment for all included studies was done with JBI critical appraisal checklist for studies reporting prevalence data[ ]; two of the 13 studies reached a score of 3 points.[ , ] Rest of the 11 studies had 4 points in major domains of quality scale used.[ ]

## Overall pooled prevalence of depression and gender distribution
As shown in , the prevalence of depression or dysthymia was reported in 13 articles involving 822 visually impaired children or adolescents. The range of the estimated prevalence across individual studies was found to be 3.26% to 35.61% and pooled prevalence by random-effects meta-analysis was 14% (95% CI = 9% to 20%), with high heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 80.11%; P < 0.001) [ ].
 Gender distribution: For a total of 822 samples, the gender distribution was 548 males, 236 females, and for 38, data could not be retrieved from one study. We could extract and retrieve gender-wise data of diagnosed depression for only 335 (219 male and 116 female) participants from five studies.[ , – , ] We observed a trend for the higher pooled prevalence of depression or dysthymia among visually impaired children and adolescents among female participants (18%, 95% CI: 7% to 31%) compared to male participants (6%, 95% CI: 2% to 12%), which did not reach the appropriate level of statistical significance (P = 0.07) [ ].
 Our meta-regression analysis did not observe the significant subgroup effects for differences in prevalence between studies conducted from school or hospital. Considering that the number of studies was small, we did subgroup analysis based on clinical judgments, like the Indian population vs. other populations as moderator variable and diagnosis established by diagnostic schedule compared to rating scales, but did not find any significant difference across these subgroups.
We attempted meta-regression with age as a covariate, however only nine studies reported the mean age of participants, and other studies only reported the age range [ ]. With data from nine studies, meta-regression shows higher depression with lower age and lower depression with increasing age, but it was statistically non-significant (P = 0.72). This may be due to the very small number of studies.

## DISCUSSION

## Main findings
This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a summary measure of the prevalence of depression or dysthymia among visually impaired children and adolescents. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no systematic review and meta-analysis that has been reported on this subject. The results of this meta-analysis will highlight the psychological well-being of this special population. The relevant studies were few, highlighting the need for more studies on the psychological health of visually impaired children and adolescents. Visual impairment is known to be associated with higher mortality (hazard ratios 1·29) (95% CI 1·20 to 1·39)[ ] and we found 14% (95% CI = 9% to 20%) to be the pooled prevalence of depression. We would like to compare this finding with two meta-analytic studies; one representing 28 studies of adult eye disease patients (1502/6589 individuals),[ ] and another consisted of 27 studies of older patients (mean aged 76 ± 13.9 years) with visual impairment visiting eye clinics and low vision rehabilitation services.[ ] Both of these two meta-analyses show a prevalence of 25% for depression, suggesting a higher prevalence of depression among aged or older persons with visual impairments.
We found a relatively low prevalence of 14% may suggest better resilience against depression among children and adolescents in response to early-life visual impairments. Alternatively, it may be a reflection of a higher level of difficulty in identifying or measuring depression among visually impaired child and adolescent populations for various reasons, like psychopathology itself, atypical symptoms, and problems with the sensitivity of measuring instruments or scales.
To compare with the sighted child and adolescent population, global data of depression among the child population is 6.16% ranging from 0.10% to 48.95 across different global regions.[ ] A meta-analysis from China reported the pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms among children and adolescents was 22.2% (95% CI: 19.9–24.6%, I2 = 99.6%, P < 0.001).[ ] In a meta-analysis from Iran, the prevalence of depression was estimated as 42.3% (95%, CI: 34.9–50.0).[ ] Comparatively, the prevalence of depression found by us is only 14% but almost similar to 14.3% was found among children with life-limiting conditions.[ ] It seems the perceived miseries of impairment by sighted people are not the same as those perceived by the people themselves with impairment. The relatively lower prevalence of depression in this meta-analysis is in concurrence with the notion that depression is not all about life adversity but individual resiliency may be crucial.[ ] It may also be attributable to methodological issues like non-random, non-community, school-based approaches by included studies and additionally publication bias found in this review.
Seven studies were conducted on students of special schools, representing a uniquely supportive environment for the visually impaired; however, school-based studies may be biased due to non-randomized convenience sampling.[ ] Many of these schools were residential and children and adolescents spent years with similar peers, without much interference from society. Visually impaired children who are unable to attend any school lack this support and face many psychosocial adversities. However, on subgroup analysis, we did not find a significant difference in the prevalence of depression, with settings of study across special schools (n = 7) vs hospital or community (n = 4) based studies.
Various studies indicate gender differences in prevalence of depression (1.95 odds ratio 95% CI) [1.88, 2.03],[ ] but our review (male = 6%, 95% CI 2% to 12%, n = 219, I2 = 47.52) (female = 18%, 95% CI: 7% to 31%, n = 116, I2 = 60.6%) did not find significant gender difference, possibly due to the small number of studies.

## Diagnostic measures and rating scales
There is a lack of validated scales for depression applicable to the visually impaired adult or child population. In this systematic review, four studies used diagnostic schedules, whereas the remaining nine studies used four different rating scales (DASS-21, BDI, CDI, and HADS). On subgroup analysis, the trend indicated that studies using diagnostic schedules detected a lower prevalence of depression, than studies using rating scales.
There was significant variation in the diagnosis of depression when it came to using structured interviews and/or rating scales. There is a lack of evidence regarding data on screening tools or rating scale sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value or negative predictive value, and issues of appropriate “cut off scores.”[ ] Additionally, scales or schedules might not be very specific or sensitive for this “population-specific” phenomenological presentation like “atypical symptoms of depression” and “trouble verbalizing feelings/depression.”[ ] Poor diagnostic performance results from limitations inherent to the scales, or to the diagnostic systems itself, particularly the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) and International Classification of Diseases (ICD), since they are not specifically designed to assess depression in children and adolescents.[ ] A systematic review of the accuracy of screening instruments for depression found sensitivity ranged from 9% to 55% only in children and adolescents,[ ] suggesting the need for increased psychometric studies in this regard.

## Limitations and strength
Limitation includes an overall lack of adequate data, we estimated a very wide range of prevalence, which reflects wide variation, lack of uniform definition of depression, lack of a specific tool for the visually impaired, and methodological heterogeneity, less number of studies, studies with small sample sizes and use of different diagnostic measures of depression across studies. Further, sample distribution of congenital or acquired blindness, definition, and severity of visual impairments were not provided by most of the studies. Most studies used non-probability sampling and did not provide information on the representativeness of their samples. The overall quality of most of the studies is not very satisfactory, for the reasons of small sample size, convenience sampling or setting, and lack of population-specific and condition-specific measuring tools.
We followed the MOOSE and as per the review protocol in the PROSPERO, International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, which adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses protocol (PRISMA guideline). This review includes studies without the restriction of the publication year and we performed tests for heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis, and subgroup analysis, helping to get the results. We applied the MOOSE checklist for reporting this meta-analysis.[ ]
Future studies should plan community-based randomized surveys with larger sample sizes using validated specific diagnostic tools and rating scales.

## CONCLUSION
In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we finally selected 13 articles to analyze and found 14% (95% CI = 9% to 20%) as the pooled prevalence of depression among visually impaired children and adolescents. For most countries, there are no prevalence studies; with very few available data, we estimated this broad range of prevalence, which reflects wide variation and methodological heterogeneity. The female gender may be associated with higher prevalence. However, there are lack of studies and appropriate diagnostic tools or rating scales, needed for diagnostic identification and to estimate the prevalence of depression among the population of visually impaired children and adolescents. In comparison to previous literature, the prevalence of depression among visually impaired children and adolescents is almost similar to children with life-limiting conditions or sighted children but lower than visually impaired older adults.
