## Abstract
The best frontline treatment for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) in adolescents and young adults (AYAs) is unclear. To support a clinical practice guideline, we systematically reviewed and meta-analyzed evidence on (1) asparaginase-based (pediatric) vs non–asparaginase-based (adult) regimens and (2) allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) vs no HSCT for AYAs (aged 15-39 years) with ALL in first complete remission. Data sources were PubMed, CINAHL, and PsycINFO from inception to 29 November 2023. Eligible studies compared regimens or the use of HSCT and reported survival, remission, toxicity, or quality of life in AYAs. We included 19 studies (14 comparative, 5 single-group; N = 3607) comparing regimens and 7 studies (N = 7492) comparing HSCT and no HSCT. Studies were mostly of poor quality, with sparse randomized controlled trials (RCTs), yielding low-certainty findings. Pediatric regimens were associated with higher 5-year overall survival (OS; relative risk [RR], 1.40; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.18-1.65), event-free survival (RR, 1.80; 95% CI, 1.13-2.85), disease-free survival (DFS; RR, 1.55; 95% CI, 1.32-1.82), and lower treatment-related mortality (TRM; RR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.09-0.90). HSCT was associated with lower 5-year OS (RR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.61-0.84) and DFS (RR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.68-0.90) and higher nonrelapse mortality (RR, 2.70; 95% CI, 1.18-6.18) and TRM (hazard ratio, 6.88; 95% CI, 3.02-15.70). Relapse risk varied by time point. In AYAs with Philadelphia chromosome–negative ALL, pediatric regimens may improve survival; toxicity-related evidence remains limited. HSCT may lead to inferior OS and DFS. With a dearth of RCTs, low-certainty evidence highlights the need for high-quality studies and subanalyses of AYAs.

## Introduction
Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) remains one of the most common hematologic malignancies among children, adolescents, and young adults, with ∼6000 new cases diagnosed annually in the United States. , Although pediatric ALL has seen remarkable improvements in survival over the past few decades, exceeding 80% to 90% cure rates, adolescents and young adults (AYAs) have historically experienced a lack of improvement in survival over time and inferior outcomes, with 5-year overall survival (OS) of only 30% to 45% using traditional adult treatment approaches. , , In light of this and other challenges, national cancer organizations have recognized AYAs to be a unique population, , and the National Cancer Institute (NCI) has leveraged population-level data to codify the AYA age range as 15 to 39 years. , 
Treatment strategies for AYA patients are influenced by physician preferences and treatment settings, with those aged ≤18 years typically treated in pediatric settings using pediatric protocols and those older than 18 years treated in adult settings receiving adult regimens. Pediatric-inspired regimens are characterized by the inclusion of asparaginase, more intensive chemotherapy with higher cumulative doses of steroids, frequent central nervous system prophylaxis, and prolonged maintenance therapy, making them relatively complex with possibly increased toxicity. , 
In 2012, Ram et al conducted a systematic review of 11 studies, demonstrating superior survival outcomes among AYA patients treated with pediatric-inspired regimens compared with conventional adult regimens, with no observed increase in nonrelapse mortality (NRM). More recently, Zeckanovic et al reviewed 25 studies, reporting improved outcomes and acceptable toxicity profiles among AYA patients treated with pediatric protocols compared with conventional adult-type regimens. However, the review included studies not focused on the AYA population, with up to a quarter of participants in some studies who were non-AYA children or adults.
Another debated question is the optimal postremission therapy for AYA patients with ALL. Historically, myeloablative allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) was considered standard consolidation therapy for adult patients with ALL in first clinical remission, based on benefits seen in adults compared with conventional chemotherapy. , , , However, these studies compared HSCT with traditional adult chemotherapy regimens rather than pediatric-inspired chemotherapy protocols.
To support a clinical practice guideline on the treatment of AYAs with ALL, we aimed to provide an updated and more focused analysis of 2 research questions to systematically evaluate the comparative benefits and harms of the following:
•Pediatric-inspired, asparaginase-based regimens vs conventional adult, non–asparaginase-based regimens for AYA patients with ALL receiving frontline therapy; and
•HSCT vs no HSCT for AYA patients with ALL in first complete remission (CR; CR1).

## Methods
We followed the current highest standards for conducting and reporting the systematic review. , The 2 research questions described herein were addressed as part of a broader set of 32 research questions to support a guideline on managing ALL in AYAs convened by the American Society of Hematology. The study protocol covering all research questions was prepared before the review but not registered or published owing to continual revisions and refocusing of the research questions by the guideline panels.

## Data sources and searches
The literature searches addressed the 32 research questions for the entire guideline. We searched for peer-reviewed articles in PubMed, PsycINFO, and CINAHL from inception to 29 November 2023, using relevant free-text terms and Medical Subject Headings (see ). Additional citations were sought from hand-searching reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and consultation with experts from the multidisciplinary panels.

## Study selection
One set of eligible studies compared pediatric-inspired, asparaginase-based regimens (eg, Cancer and Leukemia Group B, Children’s Cancer Group, Gruppo Italiano Malattie Ematologiche dell’Adulto) with adult-inspired regimens without asparaginase (eg, hyper-CVAD [hyperfractionated cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, and dexamethasone]) or limited use of asparaginase (eg, single dose at induction) among AYAs diagnosed as having Philadelphia chromosome–negative (Ph−) B- or T-cell ALL or lymphoma undergoing frontline therapy. Hereafter, we refer to these as pediatric and adult regimens, respectively. We determined whether regimens were pediatric or adult inspired based on an adjudication template developed for an NCI Community Oncology Research Program study that evaluated National Comprehensive Cancer Network ALL guideline–concordant care for AYAs. , We used the NCI definition of AYA (15-39 years at diagnosis), allowing for some flexibility if several participants were close to this age. , The second set of eligible studies compared HSCT with no HSCT as a consolidation strategy among AYAs treated for ALL in CR1. For this topic, based on guidance from the guideline panel, we expanded the criteria to include both studies specific to the AYA population and studies with mean or median ages between 15 and 39 years. For both topics, we included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective or retrospective nonrandomized comparative studies (NRCSs). Prospective single-group studies of chemotherapy regimens were also included for the analyses of toxicity and minimal residual disease (MRD) outcomes. We excluded comparative studies with <10 patients per treatment group and single-group studies with <50 patients. Based on guidance from the guideline panel, we restricted studies to the current era for ALL management, excluding publications before 2000 for the regimen topic and before 2014 for the HSCT topic. We did not apply language or setting restrictions.

## Outcomes
A multistakeholder guideline panel prioritized outcomes. Primary outcomes for both topics included OS, event-free survival (EFS), disease-free survival (DFS), relapse-free survival (RFS), CR, MRD-negative status, grade ≥3 toxicities, fertility, and quality of life. For the HSCT topic, we also included treatment-related mortality (TRM) and NRM. We confirmed that reported outcomes were categorized correctly within each study (eg, EFS vs DFS).

## Data extraction and quality assessment
The research team independently double screened all citations using the Systematic Review Data Repository Plus (data available upon request). The platform used machine learning to expedite citation screening. We extracted data and assessed studies for methodological quality using a customized data extraction form in Systematic Review Data Repository Plus. Data extraction and risk of bias assessment were conducted by 1 reviewer and confirmed by at least a second reviewer. Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool for RCTs. For NRCSs, we applied elements from the ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions) tool, including items related to confounding and selection bias. 

## Data synthesis and analysis
We conducted meta-analyses using random-effects models with restricted maximum-likelihood estimation to calculate pooled effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For comparative studies, we selected relative risk (RR) as a standardized measure of effect size because studies typically report cumulative risk over a defined follow-up period. We approximated RRs from hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios using standard methods. , For the meta-analysis of single-group studies, we pooled proportions using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation. Reported adjusted effect sizes were prioritized over unadjusted comparisons. We separately evaluated outcomes at specific time points. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. When feasible, we examined differences in the outcomes stratified by age group, country income group, design, data collection period, and asparaginase dose. All analyses were performed using the meta package in R version 4.4.1. 

## Results
For all 32 research questions addressed by the guideline, we identified 15 722 records, of which 1464 potentially relevant articles were retrieved for full-text screening. Eight additional studies were identified by searching reference lists and from experts ( ).
 

## Pediatric (asparaginase-based) vs adult (non–asparaginase-based) regimens
Nineteen studies published between 2003 and 2022 reporting on 3607 patients in 14 countries met eligibility criteria addressing pediatric and adult regimens in AYAs. , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , We found 1 RCT, 13 NRCSs, , , , , , , , , , , , , and 5 single-group prospective studies of pediatric regimens. , , , , We included 4 studies that had a maximum age for AYAs of up to 43 years, with few patients aged > 39 years in the sample (ie, <3% assuming symmetrical distributions). , , , Study characteristics are presented in .

## Study quality
The included comparative studies were at high risk of bias. The RCT was not blinded and reported insufficient details regarding methods ( ). Of the 13 NRCSs, 7 were retrospective, , , , , , , 2 compared prospective trials with historical controls, , and 4 were prospective ( ). , , , Across NRCSs, the choice between pediatric and adult protocols was influenced by referral patterns (eg, AYAs referred by pediatric oncologists were more likely to receive pediatric protocols than those referred by general practitioners) , , or at the discretion of the treating physician. , , , , , , , , , Ten studies reported differences in demographic characteristics between comparison groups, primarily pertaining to age. , , , , , , , , , Two studies reported a higher proportion of patients with unfavorable cytogenetics in pediatric arms. , Eleven studies did not account for cohort differences in their analyses and were, thus, at high risk of confounding bias. , , , , , , , , , , None of the NRCSs applied causal inference methods (eg, propensity score analysis).

## OS
Thirteen studies reported OS among 2228 patients. Study follow-up ranged from 2 to 7 years. Meta-analyses favored pediatric regimens (pooled RR at 2 years, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.09-2.37; 2 studies]; pooled RR at 5 years, 1.40 [95% CI, 1.18-1.65; 6 studies]; ). Two studies had large effect sizes (RR >2), , including 1 (Almanza-Huante et al ) that compared a very high cumulative asparaginase dose (252 000 U/m2) with an asparaginase-free regimen and 1 (de Bont et al ) that, in contrast, had a relatively small difference in asparaginase dosage between the arms (101 000 vs 70 000 U/m2). As a sensitivity analysis, we restricted meta-analyses to studies without approximated RR measures, which yielded consistent results ( ). Two studies compared OS in subgroups of younger and older AYAs, with conflicting findings. , Almanza-Huante et al found no significant interaction in effect by age, regardless of pediatric regimen (Cancer and Leukemia Group B 10403, P interaction = .65; ALL-Berlin-Frankfurt-Münster 90, P interaction = .40). In contrast, Kim et al found a larger effect size among younger AYAs (P interaction = .02). Subgroup meta-analyses found that the studies that used relatively low total asparaginase doses in pediatric regimens (<100 000 IU/m2) found no significant difference between regimens (summary RR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.87-1.27), in contrast to higher-dose studies ( ).
 

## EFS, DFS, and RFS
Eight studies reported on EFS among 890 patients. There was evidence of superior EFS with pediatric compared with adult regimens. The pooled RR at 5 years in 5 studies was 1.80 (95% CI, 1.13-2.85; ). A post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding the study with an atypically large effect size (Boissel et al) remained statistically significant ( ). Restricting the meta-analyses to studies with RR estimates also yielded a similar summary estimate. Three studies showed superior DFS at 5 years for pediatric over adult regimens (pooled RR, 1.55; 95% CI, 1.32-1.82; 511 patients). , , One study reported no significant difference between groups at 2 years (RR, 1.48; 95% CI, 0.63-2.48; 75 patients; ). Three studies reported no statistically significant difference in RFS at 5 years between pediatric and adult regimens (pooled RR, 1.25; 95% CI, 0.94-1.67; 392 patients; ). , , 
 

## CR
Ten studies evaluated CR after induction. Meta-analysis showed no significant difference (pooled RR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.996-1.11; 1440 patients), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 63%; ). , , , , , , , , , Subgroup meta-analyses found the studies that used relatively high total asparaginase doses in pediatric regimens (≥100 000 IU/m2) had a significant difference between regimens, favoring pediatric regimens (summary RR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.01-1.14), in contrast to lower-dose studies ( ). Of note, 1 study found a significant benefit of a pediatric regimen in patients with B-cell but not T-cell ALL. Other studies either combined B- and T-cell ALL or did not specify the type.
 

## Safety outcomes
Most included studies did not compare toxicities between pediatric and adult regimens. Three comparative studies reported TRM (pooled RR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.09-0.90; ). , , One older study (2005) reported significantly lower TRM among patients with pediatric regimens, but the 2 more recent studies (2017 and 2022) reported no differences between the arms. , One study reported an imprecise estimate of risk of severe infection (44% vs 41%; RR, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.56-2.09) and reported moderate/severe pancreatitis among 8% with pediatric regimen, but none with adult regimen. Another study reported a significantly higher risk of grade ≥3 toxicities in the pediatric arm during induction, including sepsis (15% vs 4%; RR, 3.93; 95% CI, 1.39-11.10), hepatic toxicity (28% vs 11%; RR, 2.43; 95% CI, 1.34-4.41), and neuropathy (8% vs 0%). 
Overall, the pooled incidence rates of grade ≥3 toxicities in single-group analysis of pediatric regimens were infection (25.4%; 95% CI, 16.0-36.1; 4 studies; 625 patients), , , , elevated liver enzymes (29.8%; 95% CI, 6.3-61.1; 3 studies; 977 patients), , , hyperbilirubinemia (21.9%; 95% CI, 6.1-43.8; 3 studies; 977 patients), , , thrombosis (10.3%; 95% CI, 2.2-23.0; 5 studies; 1012 patients), , , , , asparaginase allergic reaction (9.1%; 95% CI, 1.1-23.0; 3 studies; 609 patients), , , neuropathy (3.9%; 95% CI, 2.2-6.0; 4 studies; 748 patients), , , and pancreatitis (3.5%; 95% CI, 1.0-7.2; 6 studies; 1307 patients; ). , , , , , 

## Additional outcomes
Four studies reported MRD-negative status for pediatric-inspired regimens (pooled incidence, 44.8%; 95% CI, 20.0-71.0; 263 patients; ). MRD status was not evaluated in arms with adult-inspired regimens. No eligible studies reported on quality of life or fertility outcomes.

## Subgroup analyses
There were no statistically significant differences in OS, EFS, or CR by study design (retrospective vs prospective), data collection period (<2006 vs >2006), country income group (high vs middle/low), or (in OS) age group (15-20 vs 20-43 years; ). As noted earlier, pediatric regimens with a high cumulative asparaginase dose (≥100 000 IU/m2) were associated with superior OS (P < .001) and CR (P = .03), whereas no significant findings were observed for lower-dose regimens ( ).

## HSCT vs no HSCT

## Study characteristics
Seven retrospective comparative studies (total N = 7492) spanning treatment periods from 2002 to 2020 examined the comparative effectiveness of HSCT vs no HSCT for AYAs with ALL in CR1. Five studies focused on Ph− patients comprising 82% to 100% of the study population. , , , , One study examined Ph+ ALL, and 1 study did not report on Ph status (which we combined with Ph− studies). Two studies included a patient population in which up to 30% of the sample were older than 39 years. , The study characteristics are presented in . In all but 1 study, analyzed patients were in CR1, without obvious differences in eligibility criteria based on receipt of HSCT.
HSCT groups typically involved allogeneic transplants from matched sibling/related/unrelated donors or haploidentical donors, often with total body irradiation–based conditioning regimens. In the no HSCT groups, pediatric-inspired chemotherapy protocols were generally used as postremission therapy. , , , One study incorporated asparaginase-containing ALL induction regimens and hyper-CVAD. One study of Ph+ ALL examined imatinib-containing regimens (400 mg/d) plus CALLG2008 protocol. 

## Study quality
The 7 included studies were at high risk of bias ( ). All studies were retrospective NRCSs. , , , , , , Patient characteristics were significantly different between intervention groups across studies or in 1 case not reported. Most studies performed simple regression adjustment of confounders, typically including all characteristics that were statistically significantly different at baseline. These most commonly included age, sex, white blood cell count at diagnosis, cytogenetic risk groups, immunophenotype, and time to CR1. One study of Ph+ ALL was at high risk of confounding bias owing to a lack of adjustment in the subanalysis of AYAs. 

## OS
All 7 NRCSs reported on OS across a range of 1 to 10 years. , , , , , , Among patients with Ph− ALL, the pooled analysis of 2 studies at 1-year follow-up found no significant difference (RR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.70-1.17), but the 2 studies had conflicting findings ( ). Muffly et al found no difference in OS between HSCT and either pediatric or hyper-CAVD regimens. Wieduwilt et al found significantly lower OS with HSCT than a pediatric regimen without HSCT. In 1 study, patients without HSCT had better survival at 2 years, whereas, at 3 years, 1 study favored non-HSCT (RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.60-0.79) and another reported no significant difference (HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.53-1.04). A pooled analysis of 2 studies at 5-year follow-up showed superior OS among patients receiving pediatric-inspired chemotherapy with no HSCT (pooled RR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.61-0.84). , At 10-year follow-up, 1 study, which did not report on Ph status, reported no statistically significant differences in OS between groups (RR, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.96-1.20). In contrast, the 1 study that evaluated OS in Ph+ patients reported superior OS with HSCT compared with no HSCT at 2 years (RR, 2.50; 95% CI, 1.45-4.31). 
 

## DFS, EFS, and RFS
Three studies found lower DFS with HSCT than no HSCT across 1 to 6 years of follow-up ( ), , , with RRs mostly ∼0.75 to 0.80. Among patients with Ph− ALL, there was no significant difference in EFS between groups in 1 study at 5 years (HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.55-1.58). In contrast, for Ph+ ALL, HSCT led to superior EFS at 2 years in 1 study (RR, 3.45; 95% CI, 1.74-6.83). At 1 and 2 years of follow-up, 1 study reported that RFS was superior with HSCT ( ). However, another study reported no significant difference in EFS at 3 years (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.55-1.06). 
 

## Relapse
Five studies reported on the incidence of relapse for HSCT ( ). , , , , The findings were highly heterogeneous and inconsistent across studies and time points. Three studies found significantly lower relapse with HSCT at 1, 2, 3, and 5 years, , , 1 study found significantly higher relapse with HSCT at 4 years, and 2 studies found no significant differences at 1, 3, and 5 years. , Meta-analyses at 1 and 5 years were also not statistically significant (with 2 studies in each meta-analysis).

## NRM and TRM
In 2 studies, NRM was higher among patients who underwent HSCT at 1 (RR, 4.41; 95% CI, 2.24-8.65) and 6 years (RR, 4.54; 95% CI, 3.32-6.21; ). , One study reported that TRM was significantly higher among patients who underwent HSCT than those who did not (HR, 6.88; 95% CI, 3.02-15.70; P < .01). 

## Discussion
Across 14 comparative studies, pediatric regimens demonstrated superior survival outcomes over adult regimens in AYAs, including OS, EFS, and DFS. These results align with previous research, suggesting that the intensive and prolonged nature of pediatric protocols provides a survival advantage for AYA populations. , In subgroup analyses, survival benefits were consistent across younger and older AYA cohorts, highlighting the broad applicability of pediatric regimens among AYAs.
Our findings suggest that asparaginase cumulative dose may act as an effect modifier, given that pediatric regimens at higher doses (≥100 000 IU/m2) demonstrated greater effect sizes for OS and CR than those at lower doses. This should be interpreted with caution. RCTs comparing low-dose and high-dose asparaginase reported no statistically significant differences in outcomes among younger children, , and a systematic review of asparaginase dose intensity in pediatric regimens used among children concluded that the level of exposure does not matter as long as a therapeutic activity level of ≥100 IU/L is achieved. Notably, the pediatric regimens included in our systematic review differed in asparaginase formulation (eg, native Escherichia coli asparaginase or polyethylene glycol–asparaginase), asparaginase doses and timing, immunophenotype, and risk group stratification, all of which may have influenced results across studies.
Concerns about the safety of pediatric regimens, given their higher intensity than adult protocols, have been raised in previous research. This systematic review identified a lack of robust evidence on toxicity, particularly regarding steroid-associated complications, infections, and unplanned hospitalizations for AYAs. Safety outcomes were rarely reported in comparative studies, with inconclusive findings owing to the lack of power.
Studies of consolidation strategies for AYA patients suggest that treatment outcomes may vary based on Ph status. Among patients with Ph− ALL, the evidence suggests no significant benefit of adding HSCT after chemotherapy, and transplant-free protocols may actually be preferable for OS. However, substantial statistical heterogeneity across studies might reflect differences in patient selection criteria, transplant timing, conditioning regimens, and/or center-specific practices across studies, which may suggest that there are as-yet-undefined patients who benefit from HSCT. In addition, important selection bias in these retrospective studies may be at play, despite some adjustment for potential confounders, such that patients selected to undergo transplantation may have other explanations for their poorer survival, including, among others, higher underlying risk, number of cycles of chemotherapy required to achieve CR1, and lower likelihood of receiving asparaginase-based regimens. Several studies discussed such caveats, , , although most noted that the increased NRM and transplant-related mortality risks associated with transplantation may outweigh its potential benefits in the general Ph− AYAs. , , , , In addition, a study that evaluated both AYA and non-AYA patients noted a lack of benefit of HSCT in the AYA studies, in contrast with studies of non-AYA adults, that was potentially ascribed to the improved survival achieved with pediatric regimens used in the AYA populations. 
The overall finding of no survival benefit with HSCT challenges the historical paradigm of universal transplantation for high-risk features and suggests that modern HSCT-free treatments, particularly with pediatric regimens, may provide adequate disease control for most Ph− patients. Notably, however, relapse rates may be higher without HSCT, but with inconsistent findings across studies and timing of assessment. In contrast, a single study reported a large survival benefit with HSCT in Ph+ AYAs, aligning with traditional clinical practice and the biological understanding of Ph+ disease’s higher risk profile. 
The strength of the evidence of eligible studies is severely limited by the poor methodological quality, with a dearth of RCTs, and a high risk of bias in most included studies. In particular, we found only 1 small RCT in AYAs comparing adult and pediatric regimens and no RCTs comparing HSCT and no HSCT. The studies of AYAs were largely retrospective comparisons that included both retrospective comparisons of prospectively collected clinical trial data (including from RCTs) and more arbitrarily selected cohorts of patients (some at different centers across different time periods); many did not adjust for important differences between cohorts or confounders, which diminished their methodological quality. The lack of adjustment for age in comparisons in which patients treated with pediatric regimens were younger than those treated with adult regimens is likely to have strongly biased the studies in favor of pediatric regimens. Despite these methodological limitations, the survival findings were consistent across studies, with low to moderate heterogeneity and coherent results across subgroup meta-analyses, supporting the conclusion that the survival advantage of pediatric regimens likely reflects a true effect despite the magnitude of the effect being uncertain.
Our systematic review has several strengths, including the inclusion of contemporary studies reflecting modern treatment approaches, consistent age ranges across studies, long-term follow-up data, and a comprehensive assessment of multiple outcomes. However, important limitations must be considered. A notable gap in the evidence is that most studies did not examine whether treatment effects varied by patient demographics (eg, sex, race/ethnicity, income, payer status), facility type, adult vs pediatric centers, or provider specialty, all of which may influence outcomes. The relatively small number of studies, particularly for Ph+ ALL and adverse events, limits the robustness of some conclusions. Most included studies were conducted in high-income countries, which limits the generalization of findings to low- and middle-income countries. Future studies should focus on better defining specific subgroups of Ph− patients who might benefit from HSCT, potentially through improved risk stratification using MRD status and molecular markers. In addition, most of the included comparative studies were conducted in the late 1990s or early 2000s and did not incorporate immunotherapy, a promising newer treatment for ALL that may influence the observed differences between pediatric and adult regimens. Investigation of novel immunotherapeutic approaches to reducing TRM while maintaining antileukemic efficacy could help improve the risk-to-benefit ratio for patients who do require transplantation. , 
Future research should prioritize prospective studies conducted specifically among AYAs or, at a minimum, subgroup analyses within AYA cohorts. It is critical that studies use the best analytic methods to compare groups. We suggest that future studies use propensity score or equivalent methods to adjust for factors that predict the choice of regimen, with the aim of accounting for differences between groups compared within studies. To allow users of the evidence, including future systematic reviewers, to better interpret studies, researchers should use standard effect size measures (eg, HRs for time-to-event outcomes) and should fully report outcome data items (eg, person-time, events, censoring). The heterogeneity among studies of HSCT suggests that further research is needed to elucidate whether any AYA subgroups may, in fact, benefit from HSCT. A significant gap in the literature is the lack of data on quality of life and fertility outcomes, long-term outcomes of high priority to patients and their caregivers, and important aspects of patient decision-making.

## Conclusion
This systematic review provides evidence supporting pediatric-inspired, asparaginase-containing chemotherapy and HSCT-free approaches for consolidation among most AYA patients with Ph− ALL. However, studies were mostly of poor quality. Future research should focus on better identifying specific subgroups who might benefit from HSCT.
