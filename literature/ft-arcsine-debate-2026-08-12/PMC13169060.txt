## Abstract

## Background
Lung biopsy remains an important consideration in the management of interstitial lung disease (ILD). Transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC) offers an alternative to traditional surgical lung biopsy (SLB) and transbronchial forceps biopsy (TBB); however, up-to-date comparisons between these methods in ILD, particularly fibrotic ILD, remain limited.

## Methods
A comprehensive literature search of Medline, Embase and Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews was performed for studies published between January 2000 and September 2025 to identify all citations reporting yield and complications of the three main lung biopsy methods for ILD.

## Results
A total of 143 studies were included. Pooled multidisciplinary yield (following multidisciplinary discussion) in TBB, TBLC and SLB was 35.3% (95% CI 21.3–52.5), 84.3% (95% CI 81.7–86.5) and 88.6% (95% CI 83.4–92.3), respectively. For fibrotic ILD, the multidisciplinary yield was 12.5% (only one eligible study), 87.6% (95% CI 83.5–90.7) and 87.3% (95%CI 80.5–92.0), respectively. Meta-regression showed an increasing multidisciplinary yield of TBLC with more recent years of patient recruitment and an increasing percentage of fibrotic ILD in each study. The multidisciplinary yield of both TBLC and SLB was 85–90%, suggesting a biopsy “ceiling effect”. All evaluated complications were more frequent in TBLC compared to TBB and some were more frequent in SLB compared to TBLC. Several complications were more frequent in fibrotic ILD compared to all ILD.

## Conclusions
The multidisciplinary yield of TBLC and SLB is equivalent, with both offering an acceptable safety profile. TBB has low yield, particularly in fibrotic ILD. These findings support using either TBLC or SLB as an initial diagnostic tool in fibrotic ILD, mainly based on patient–physician shared decision-making.

## Introduction
Management of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and specifically fibrotic ILD, continues to evolve, driven by improved understanding of disease pathophysiology and clinical behaviour, as well as the introduction of new treatments. Although the presence and progression of substantial fibrosis has become a critical driver of management [ , ], the underlying pattern and aetiologic diagnosis remain important considerations. Lung biopsy is therefore still a significant tool in the physician's armamentarium when facing management uncertainty in fibrotic ILD. Techniques and expertise in lung biopsy for ILD have also evolved substantially in recent years, with transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC) increasingly recognised as an effective and safe alternative to traditional surgical lung biopsy (SLB) and transbronchial forceps biopsy (TBB) [ ]. Given these advances, there is a need for a comprehensive and up-to-date assessment of the utility and safety of the three most commonly used lung biopsy procedures in the general ILD population and particularly in fibrotic ILD [ ].
Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have summarised published data [ – ] with pooled diagnostic yield (whether multidisciplinary or histopathologic yield) of 41%, 77–83% and 93–95% for TBB, TBLC and SLB, respectively, as well as highly variable complication rates. These previous reviews have typically evaluated one biopsy modality at a time and comparison across different biopsy types is complicated by different methodologies for each review. Additionally, these reviews have provided summary estimates for biopsy outcomes that pool all patients with ILD, including those with nonfibrotic ILD and even some with non-ILD diagnoses across variable elective or hospitalised settings. This has resulted in challenges and potential misunderstanding when applying these pooled results to the more clinically relevant population of fibrotic ILD. Lastly, TBLC has gained widespread adoption in recent years, a development not fully reflected in previous reviews, which mostly included studies published prior to 2020. Given these potential limitations, along with the absence of robust head-to-head studies, our objectives were therefore to 1) provide an up-to-date systematic review and meta-analysis on the yield and safety of TBB, TBLC and SLB, 2) evaluate these outcomes both in the general ILD population and specifically in fibrotic ILD, and 3) compare outcomes across the different biopsy modalities using consistent methodology.

## Methods
This systematic review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines [ ]. The protocol was registered prior to study initiation (PROSPERO: CRD42025644412).

## Data source and search strategy
A comprehensive search of Medline, Embase and Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) identified all citations related to TBB, TBLC and SLB for ILD. The search was performed on 29 January 2025 and updated on 23 September 2025. All retrieved citations were uploaded into Covidence (Covidence systematic review software, Melbourne, Australia). The search strategy was modified with permission from a previous American Thoracic Society paper [ ] (supplementary tables E1–E3).

## Study selection
Full-text publications were eligible if reporting yield and/or procedural complications from TBB, TBLC and/or SLB, were original research, enrolled ≥10 adult patients with ILD, were published in English, and had average patient year of enrolment ≥2000. SLB studies were only included if the procedure was performed via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). The publication with the largest sample size was used if multiple eligible studies came from a single cohort.
We excluded studies with ≥10% having critical illness (i.e. acute respiratory failure). Although this threshold lacked precedence, we believe it maintains a cohort composed primarily of clinically stable patients, aiming to reflect real-life decision-making settings. In addition, we excluded studies with ≥10% upfront open lung biopsy (studies included VATS that was converted during the procedure to open lung biopsy were included as an intention-to-treat protocol) or focused on a single diagnosis (e.g. sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)). We included studies targeting populations with specific radiologic/pathologic patterns (e.g. usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP)).
Sequential review of titles, abstracts, and full texts was completed by two independent reviewers (E. Mor and C.J. Ryerson) to determine eligibility of each citation.

## Outcomes evaluated
Primary outcomes included yield and frequency of prespecified procedure-related complications. “Multidisciplinary yield” was defined as the proportion of patients who underwent a procedure and were eventually given a final multidisciplinary diagnosis. We also report “histopathologic yield”, which was defined as the proportion of patients who underwent a procedure with a reported pathologic pattern/diagnosis. Procedure-related complications included bleeding, pneumothorax (only for TBB and TBLC), acute exacerbation of ILD (AE-ILD), pneumonia and 30-day all-cause mortality. Although definitions varied slightly across studies, bleeding was defined as “mild” (grade 1) if it required only intraprocedural suction, “moderate” (grade 2) if it required endoscopic procedures to serve as tamponade and/or instillation of ice-cold saline, and “severe” (grade 3) if causing haemodynamic or respiratory instability or requiring surgical intervention, transfusion or admission to an intensive care unit [ , ]. Specific diagnostic criteria were not mandated for AE-ILD or pneumonia. Additional procedure-related complications for SLB included prolonged air-leak (defined as an air leak lasting ≥5 days), duration of hospital stay and duration of chest tube drainage. Some analyses prespecified in the original protocol were ultimately not performed (e.g. the proportion of elective versus nonelective hospital admissions) due to limitations in data reporting and study eligibility.

## Data extraction and quality assessment
Data were extracted by E. Mor, M. Degrassi and L.A. Belmonte using a standardised data collection sheet. Data related to the study publication included publication year, country of origin, number of centres contributing patients and mean year of patient recruitment (i.e. the mid-point of each study's recruitment window). Study design was categorised as retrospective or prospective, which was further subcategorised as observational or a randomised controlled trial (RCT). Recorded population characteristics included age, sex, forced vital capacity and diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide. The final diagnosis reported in the original publications was subcategorised as fibrotic ILD, nonfibrotic ILD, unknown ILD subtype and non-ILD. Individual diagnoses as established either by histopathology or multidisciplinary discussion (MDD) were assigned to these subcategories, including a prespecified list of ILD diagnoses that were considered “fibrotic ILD” (supplementary table E4). Biopsy characteristics recorded were probe size, type of sedation, use of fluoroscopy, number of sampled areas (1 or ≥2), mean number of samples and mean sample area (mm2). Yield and complication frequency were recorded as described previously. Quality and validity of each article were assessed using the CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme) checklists.

## Data synthesis and analysis
Outcomes were analysed for all studies and for those in which ≥80% of patients had a final diagnosis of fibrotic ILD (based on previously described categorisation method). Additionally, we further analysed relevant outcomes by limiting studies to contemporary cohorts with a mean recruitment year between 2021–2025. Meta-analysis of proportions was performed using a generalised linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) with a logit link to generate pooled estimates of diagnostic yield and complication rates under a random-effects framework. Unlike traditional inverse-variance methods using logit or Freeman–Tukey transformations, the GLMM approach models the binomial likelihood directly, weighing each study according to its underlying binomial precision rather than relying on transformed variances. This results in more stable and accurate estimates, particularly in datasets when event rates approach either 0% or 100% and when studies differ substantially in sample size, avoiding known biases introduced by variance-stabilising transformations [ – ]. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic with I2 values ≥75% considered as substantial heterogeneity. Meta-regression was conducted using a mixed-effects model to assess effects explained by study-level covariates, while accounting for residual between-study heterogeneity via a random-effects structure. The R2 analogue was used to estimate correlation strength with values <25% considered weak, 25–50% moderate and >50% strong. The statistical analysis was performed using RStudio 2024.12.0.

## Results

## Search results and study characteristics
The search yielded 4084 studies, which narrowed to 670 abstracts and 453 full-text publications for review (supplementary figure E1). Eligibility criteria were met by 143 studies ( ), with 18 publications addressing TBB (10 370 patients), 97 addressing TBLC (10 384 patients) and 47 addressing SLB (5878 patients) (supplementary figure E2). Some studies addressed >1 procedure type (without this leading to double counting patients). Most studies originated in Europe, Asia and North America (61, 53 and 23 studies, respectively) (supplementary figure E3 and table E5). There were 134 observational studies [ – ] and nine RCTs [ , – ]. 10 studies evaluated both TBLC and TBB, seven studies evaluated both TBLC and SLB, and two studies evaluated all three biopsy methods. Overall quality and validity of included articles were low to medium. Additional analyses are presented in supplementary figures E4–E72.
 

## Biopsy yield
TBB yield was reported in 16 studies, including six reporting multidisciplinary yield and 13 histopathologic yield (three reported both). Pooled multidisciplinary yield was 35.3% (95% CI 21.3–52.5) and histopathologic yield was 49.8% (95% CI 35.7–63.9). Multidisciplinary yield decreased to 12.5% in the single relevant study when limited to studies comprising patients with ≥80% fibrotic ILD, with histopathologic yield decreasing to 34.4% (95% CI 26.0–43.9) ( ).
 TBLC yield was reported in 85 studies, including 64 reporting multidisciplinary yield and 56 histopathologic yield (35 reported both). Pooled multidisciplinary yield was 84.3% (95% CI 81.7–86.5) and histopathologic yield was 78.7% (95% CI 74.8–82.2). Multidisciplinary yield increased to 88.1% (95% CI 85.0–90.6) when limiting to contemporary cohorts with a mean recruitment year between 2021 and 2025, consistent with meta-regression showing weak yet significant correlation between multidisciplinary yield and mean year of recruitment for each study (k=67; β=0.1061; p<0.001; I2=83.8%; R2=19.7%) ( ). Multidisciplinary yield increased to 87.6% (95% CI 83.5–90.7) and histopathologic yield to 85.7% (95% CI 81.5–89.0) when limiting to studies comprising ≥80% fibrotic ILD, consistent with meta-regression showing a weak correlation between multidisciplinary yield and percentage of patients with fibrotic ILD in each study (k=65; β=0.0109; p=0.023; I2=84.9%; R2=11.5%).
 SLB yield was reported in 41 studies, including 16 reporting multidisciplinary yield and 31 histopathologic yield (six reported both). Pooled multidisciplinary yield was 88.6% (95% CI 83.4–92.3) and histopathologic yield was 95.6% (95% CI 91.7–97.7). Multidisciplinary yield was 87.3% (95% CI 80.5–92.0) and histopathologic yield increased to 98.4% (95% CI 92.9–99.7) when limiting to studies comprising ≥80% fibrotic ILD. Meta-regression showed no association between multidisciplinary yield and mean year of recruitment.

## Complications

## Bleeding
Bleeding associated with TBB was reported in 11 studies, with seven reporting mild bleeding, eight moderate bleeding and seven severe bleeding. Frequency of mild, moderate and severe bleeding was 50.7% (95% CI 29.7–71.4), 6.5% (95% CI 3.1–13.2) and 0%, respectively. Clinically relevant bleeding, defined as the composite of moderate and severe bleeding frequency, was 6.3% (95% CI 2.0–18.2) in those studies reporting this outcome. Bleeding was reported in a single study when limiting the analysis to ≥80% fibrotic ILD, with similar results to a previous study [ ].
Bleeding associated with TBLC was reported in 81 studies, with 45 reporting mild bleeding, 68 moderate bleeding and 63 severe bleeding. Clinically relevant bleeding was reported in 52 studies. The pooled frequency of mild, moderate and severe bleeding was 39.6% (95% CI 29.0–51.3), 13.9% (95% CI 11.1–17.2) and 0.6% (95% CI 0.3–1.1), respectively. The frequency of clinically relevant bleeding was 17.3% (95% CI 13.7–21.6) in studies reporting this outcome. The frequency of severe bleeding was similar when limiting to studies with a mean recruitment year between 2021 and 2025. The frequency of severe bleeding was 0.5% (95 CI 0.1–3.6) when limiting to studies with ≥80% fibrotic ILD ( ), with clinically relevant bleeding slightly increasing to 18.2% (95% CI 10.2–30.3), but without statistical significance on meta-regression.
 

## Pneumothorax
The pooled frequency of pneumothorax following TBB was 2.9% (95% CI 1.7–5.1) (reported in 13 studies) and that for pneumothorax requiring chest drainage was 1.1% (95% CI 0.5–2.2) (nine studies). Pneumothorax frequency increased to 9.2% (95% CI 5.5–15.5) when limiting to the three studies with ≥80% fibrotic ILD and 2.0% (95% CI 0.6–5.9) for pneumothorax requiring chest drainage.
The pooled frequency of pneumothorax following TBLC was 8.1% (95% CI 6.5–9.9) (reported in 86 studies) and that for pneumothorax requiring chest drainage was 4.3% (95% CI 3.2–5.6) (77 studies). The frequency of pneumothorax decreased to 6.1% (95% CI 3.7–10.0) when limiting to studies with a mean recruitment year between 2021 and 2025, although meta-regression did not show a significant correlation between pneumothorax frequency and mean year of recruitment. The frequency of pneumothorax increased to 11.9% (95% CI 8.2–16.9) when limiting to studies comprising patients with ≥80% fibrotic ILD and to 7.5% (95% CI 4.9–11.3) for pneumothorax requiring chest drainage, consistent with meta-regression showing a weak yet significant correlation between pneumothorax requiring chest drainage frequency and the percentage of patients with fibrotic ILD in each study (k=79; β=0.0206; p=0.003; I2=84.6%; R2=14.9%). Meta-regression showed no association between yield and pneumothorax frequency.

## Acute exacerbation of interstitial lung disease
There were insufficient data to reliably estimate the frequency of AE-ILD in TBB, with no events seen in the only study reporting this complication. AE-ILD following TBLC was 0.6% (95% CI 0.4–1.0) (reported in 41 studies), which slightly increased to 1.0% (95% CI 0.5–2.2) when limiting to studies comprising patients with ≥80% fibrotic ILD and with no association in a meta-regression between AE-ILD and mean year of recruitment. The frequency of AE-ILD following SLB was 1.9% (95% CI 1.0–2.3) (reported in 24 studies), with no change when limiting to studies with ≥80% fibrotic ILD and with no association in a meta-regression between AE-ILD and mean year of recruitment.

## Pneumonia
No studies reported pneumonia following TBB. The pooled frequency of post-procedure pneumonia for TBLC was 1.2% (95% CI 0.7–2.1) (reported in 25 studies) and 2.7% (95% CI 1.7–4.4) (21 studies) for SLB. The frequency of pneumonia when limiting the analysis to studies comprising ≥80% fibrotic ILD was 2.1% (95% CI 0.8–5.0) for TBLC and 2.5% (95% CI 1.3–4.5) for SLB.

## 30-day mortality
Two studies reported 30-day mortality following TBB, with one study reporting 0% and another reporting 5%. Pooled 30-day mortality following TBLC was 0.8% (95% CI 0.5–1.2) (reported in 35 studies) and 1.1% (95% CI 0.5–2.4) when limiting to studies with ≥80% fibrotic ILD. Meta-regression showed no association between TBLC 30-day mortality and mean year of recruitment. Pooled 30-day mortality following SLB was 0.8% (95% CI 0.3–1.9) (32 studies) and 0.1% (95% CI 0.0–4.4) when limiting to studies comprising ≥80% fibrotic ILD. Additionally, meta-regression showed a moderate correlation between SLB 30-day mortality and mean year of recruitment (k=30; β=−0.1696; p<0.014; I2=75.7%; R2=35.5%).

## SLB-specific additional complications
The mean hospital stay was 4.1 days (95% CI 3.4–4.9) (reported in 31 studies). The mean duration of chest tube drainage was 2.2 days (95% CI 1.6–2.8 (21 studies). The pooled frequency of prolonged air-leak was 3.3% (95% CI 2.1–5.0) (24 studies).

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated 143 studies to provide estimates of both yield and complication frequencies for TBB, TBLC and SLB in patients with ILD, focusing on multidisciplinary yield for fibrotic ILD. We show that 1) yield varies substantially by definition (i.e. multidisciplinary versus histopathologic yield) and by population (fibrotic ILD versus all ILD), 2) the multidisciplinary yield of TBLC has improved over time and is now similar to that of SLB, particularly in fibrotic ILD, 3) the frequency of complications varies across biopsy types, and 4) the frequency of complications tends to be higher in fibrotic ILD, especially in TBLC. Collectively, these findings provide comprehensive and comparable estimates of the utility and safety of different forms of biopsy in fibrotic ILD, which can assist clinicians managing these patients ( ).
 We report both pooled histopathologic yield and multidisciplinary yield; however, we focus primarily on multidisciplinary yield in most of our analyses to better reflect real-life clinical decision-making that integrates all available data. While TBLC biopsy yield tends to improve when incorporated into an MDD, the multidisciplinary yield from SLB and TBB does not. One possible explanation is that SLB samples are typically more spatially comprehensive and interpretable, thus more readily allowing identification of a histopathologic pattern, which in some cases is misaligned and irreconcilable with clinical and radiologic data, thus leads to a diagnosis of unclassifiable ILD. Conversely, the pattern may be identified with less confidence in TBLC and may therefore be more easily accommodated within an MDD that considers clinical and radiologic findings. There also appears to be substantial heterogeneity in the six SLB studies that reported both histopathologic and multidisciplinary yield, suggesting possible differences in their methods, with three studies showing improved multidisciplinary yield over histopathologic yield [ , , ], one showing the opposite [ ] and two with similar yield [ , ]. We also confirm that TBB is generally not an appropriate choice for fibrotic ILD due to its relatively low yield in this population (12.5–34%). TBB should therefore primarily be reserved for suspected ILDs with a relatively widespread peribronchovascular pattern (e.g. sarcoidosis) [ ].
We evaluated both the change in biopsy yield over time and between all-ILD and mostly fibrotic ILD. There was increased multidisciplinary yield over time, reaching 88% for TBLC studies with a mean year of patient recruitment between 2021 and 2025, consistent with the corresponding meta-regression. Although difficult to confirm, these findings suggest that increased experience and improvements in procedural techniques have likely led to better performance in recent years. This may be supported by previous studies examining TBLC learning curves, showing that diagnostic yield and complications rates improve with increasing proceduralist experience [ , , ]. An alternative possibility is that this observation could be explained by publication bias, where authors of studies with relatively low yield may be more likely to refrain from publishing such reports. In contrast to TBLC, SLB multidisciplinary yield did not show a similar improvement over time. Notably, the multidisciplinary yield for both TBLC and SLB converged around 85–90% when restricting to fibrotic ILD studies, corresponding to previous data showing that 10–15% of patients remain unclassifiable even after comprehensive MDD [ , ]. This finding suggests a ceiling in multidisciplinary yield of 85–90% when using currently available techniques. This “diagnostic ceiling effect” may be surpassed in the future with improved diagnostic tools and the incorporation of novel biomarkers into MDDs.
We similarly evaluated whether complication frequency varied across time or with restriction to mostly fibrotic ILD. We found that despite the increase in yield over time, the risk of serious complications has remained relatively static. We also found higher frequencies of multiple complications when restricting to studies that focused on fibrotic ILD. This includes a non-negligible risk of clinically relevant bleeding with TBLC despite near-universal use of endobronchial blockers or Fogarty balloons in recent studies. Pneumothorax frequency following both TBB and TBLC was higher in fibrotic ILD compared to the overall ILD population, with most pneumothoraces following TBLC requiring chest tube insertion. It remains unclear whether the increased frequency of these complications in fibrotic ILD reflects procedural complexity, anatomical challenges or underlying biological differences. Both AE-ILD and pneumonia were more frequent with SLB compared to TBLC, with no clinically meaningful difference between fibrotic ILD compared to the overall ILD population. 30-day mortality in the all-ILD population following TBLC was similar to SLB, yet with meta-regression showing a decrease in SLB 30-day mortality over time. While the frequency did not change significantly for TBLC in fibrotic ILD, counterintuitively, it dropped to 0.1% with SLB. This lower frequency may be explained by our initial exclusion of studies with critically ill patients, carefully selected patients in this population or by reporting-bias.
The 2022 European Respiratory Society TBLC guideline recommended TBLC as the first choice biopsy method for patients with ILD based on the perception of a favourable risk–benefit ratio compared to SLB. For TBLC, common complications include clinically relevant pneumothorax and bleeding; however, this is considered a safe and minimally invasive outpatient procedure. We showed that SLB via VATS has similar multidisciplinary yield and 30-day mortality compared to TBLC and does not seem to accelerate lung function decline [ , ]. These findings suggest SLB can still be considered a valid alternative to TBLC in appropriate situations, but SLB may be less preferred for many patients given the more frequent requirement for hospitalisation and post-operative chest tube drainage, and its more frequent association with post-operative pain. Overall, our findings support either TBLC or SLB as an initial diagnostic option in centres with appropriate expertise, with the final decision ultimately resting on patient–physician shared decision-making.

## Limitations
Our study has several limitations. Although reflecting real life, studies reporting multidisciplinary yield may also include cases where the final diagnosis was based solely on clinical and radiologic findings following a nondiagnostic biopsy, which may overestimate the utility of lung biopsy. Second, our analysis of fibrotic ILD was based on the final diagnoses reported in each study. Some diagnoses that fall within the spectrum of fibrotic ILD (e.g. connective tissue disease-associated ILD, HP, pneumoconiosis) are not invariably fibrotic and may represent purely inflammatory patterns. However, this potential misclassification is likely low given that diagnostic uncertainty and the impetus to pursue a biopsy is typically greater in fibrotic ILD (with the possible exception of sarcoidosis, which was excluded from our analysis), and that several studies explicitly distinguished between fibrotic and nonfibrotic/cellular patterns. Third, we are limited by a relative absence of head-to-head randomised trials comparing biopsy techniques, instead relying primarily on observational cohorts. Finally, although this systematic review presents a comprehensive summary of a large group of studies, the results should be interpreted with caution, as signs of publication bias were noted (supplementary figures E51–E72), likely reflecting heterogeneity and small-study effects, possibly resulting from our broad eligibility criteria. We chose not to exclude several studies at high-risk of bias from our main analyses, as sensitivity analyses demonstrated that their exclusion did not meaningfully affect the results.

## Conclusion
This systematic review shows similar multidisciplinary yield between TBLC and SLB, particularly in fibrotic ILD, with both techniques offering an acceptable safety profile and potentially lower procedural burden for TBLC. Overall, the findings support either TBLC or SLB as an initial diagnostic option in centres with appropriate expertise, with the final decision ultimately resting on patient–physician shared decision-making.

## Points for clinical practice
Biopsy in ILD evidence is largely based on observational cohorts, with limited randomised clinical trial data.
TBLC and SLB provide similar multidisciplinary yield, especially in fibrotic ILD.
TBLC and SLB multidisciplinary yield converged around 85–90% in fibrotic ILD, suggesting a diagnostic ceiling that may improve with future tools and novel biomarkers.
Several post-biopsy complications are more frequent in fibrotic ILD compared to nonfibrotic ILD, particularly following TBLC.

## Questions for further research
Do novel biomarkers combined with biopsy data improve multidisciplinary yield?
Do novel imaging and navigation systems improve multidisciplinary yield and safety profile in TBLC and/or TBB?
Can new pre- or peri-procedural tools and management strategies be developed to reduce complication rates in TBLC?
What role can robotic bronchoscopy play in fibrotic ILD?
How can post-VATS recovery and patient satisfaction be improved?
