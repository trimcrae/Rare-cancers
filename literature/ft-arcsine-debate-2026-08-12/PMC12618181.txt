## ABSTRACT

## Background
Antifungal resistance in Candida species in HIV/AIDS patients due to the scarcity of antifungal agents is important particularly in Iran with an estimate of 46,000 individuals living with HIV. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to determine the resistance rate of Candida spp. isolates from HIV/AIDS positive individuals in Iran from 1999 to 2024.

## Methods
PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, MagIran, Scientific Information Database, and Google Scholar were searched and the title‐and‐abstract screening was performed independently by two review authors. The risk of bias assessment and the data extraction were performed independently by two review authors. Meta‐analysis was performed using a random‐effects model with Freeman‐Tukey double arcsine transformation. This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses 2020.

## Results
From the retrieved 4925 references, 18 references belonging to 15 studies with 1318 Candida spp. isolates were included. The highest pooled resistance rate of Candida spp. was observed against clotrimazole (21%, 95% CI: 14–30, I
2 = 67.29%) while the lowest pooled resistance rates of Candida spp. were observed against voriconazole, caspofungin, amphotericin B, and nystatin with the pooled estimates of 0%. At the species level, the highest pooled resistance rates were observed for 
Candida krusei
 against fluconazole (72%, 95% CI: 0%–100%) and itraconazole (38%, 95% CI: 17%–62%), and for Candida glabrata against clotrimazole (38%, 95% CI: 27%–50%).

## Conclusions
The use of amphotericin B, caspofungin, and nystatin for the treatment of Candida spp. in HIV‐AIDS patients in Iran is recommended based on clinical indication.

Antimicrobial resistance in Candida spp. is significant in individuals with HIV/AIDS infections, particularly in Iran. In this systematic review, a comprehensive systematic search was conducted to identify all the eligible studies between 1999 and 2024; the pooled resistance rates of Candida spp. against antimicrobial agents were estimated. The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed as high, showing that studies with adequate sample sizes using up‐to‐date guidelines for assessing the antimicrobial resistance of Candida spp. in Iran are necessary.

## Introduction
The HIV/AIDS epidemic remains a critical public health issue [ ], particularly in Iran, which is among the 30 fast‐track countries detected by UNAIDS and is accumulatively responsible for 89% of all new HIV infections in the world. With an estimated 46,000 people living with HIV in 2023 in Iran, the situation of HIV/AIDS infections in Iran persists to be concerning [ ].
In immunocompromised patients, such as HIV‐positive individuals, Candida species are the most prevalent cause of fungal infections [ , ]. Globally, circa 750,000 cases of invasive candidiasis arise each year, causing a mortality of 46%–75% in the total infected population and around 40% in individuals receiving antifungal agents [ ]. The clinical forms of candidiasis include oral candidiasis, onychomycosis [ ], superficial cutaneous candidiasis, invasive candidiasis (candidemia and deep‐seated candidiasis) [ ], Candida infections of the genital tract including vulvovaginal candidiasis [ , ], and chronic forms including chronic disseminated candidiasis and chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis [ , ].
Antifungal resistance is a growing concern worldwide [ ]. Due to the overuse and misuse of antimicrobial agents, including antibiotics and antifungal agents, antimicrobial resistance, including antifungal resistance, is more prevalent in low‐ and middle‐income countries [ , , , ]. The scarcity of antifungal agents in clinical practice worsens the difficulty of managing fungal infections, including infections caused by Candida species [ , ]. Although 
C. albicans
 is known as the most prevalent Candida species, in recent decades, there has been a global shift toward non‐albicans Candida species. Moreover, many non‐albicans species are either intrinsically resistant or are developing resistance to frequently used antifungal agents, in particular against azoles. This fact underscores the urgent need for effective antifungal stewardship for Candida infections and their resistance [ ].
A systematic review and meta‐analysis on the global resistance of Candida spp. causing oral candidiasis among HIV/AIDS patients has been published, showcasing the significance of antifungal resistance in Candida isolates from HIV/AIDS patients [ ]. Moreover, in Iran, the resistance rate of 
Candida albicans
 (
C. albicans
) from clinical samples against conventional antifungal agents has been investigated previously [ ]. However, the antifungal resistance patterns of all Candida species isolated from all the clinical forms of candidiasis among HIV/AIDS patients in Iran have never been investigated. Therefore, this systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to investigate the resistance of Candida spp. isolates from HIV/AIDS patients in Iran between 1999 and 2024.

2.

## Methods
This systematic review and meta‐analysis was designed and conducted according to the instructions of JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [ ] and was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses 2020 (PRISMA 2020) statement [ ].
2.1.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
The inclusion criteria were defined as cross‐sectional studies assessing the antifungal resistance of Candida spp. isolated from HIV/AIDS patients in Iran, written in English or Persian, and published between 1999 and 2024. The exclusion criteria were defined as articles for which the full text was not available or were written in languages except English or Persian.

2.2.

## Search Strategy
PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, MagIran, Scientific Information Database, and Google Scholar were searched using the keywords “Candida” OR “Candidiasis” OR “Oropharyngeal candidiasis” AND “Iran” We did not use HIV/AIDS keywords in order to increase the comprehensiveness of the search. The search strategy algorithms were translated for each database. The date of publication was restricted to January 1999 to January 2024. The database search was performed on 07 January 2024. The search strategy algorithms for the databases are presented in the Data  , Table  . The results were stored in an EndNote file.

2.3.

## Title‐and‐Abstract Screening
A.Y., Y.B, M.S.B., E.Z., A.F., and A.S. independently screened the titles and abstracts of the results from each database. Conflicts arisen on whether or not to include/exclude an article were resolved with the consensus of the reviewers. The full texts of the articles deemed to be relevant were retrieved. A.F.D. screened the titles and abstracts of the references of the included studies in order to maximize the comprehensiveness of the search.

2.4.

## Data Extraction
A.F.D. and F.K. independently extracted the data from the included studies into an Excel file. The extracted data were the last name of the first author, the year of publication, the number of resistant Candida spp. isolates, the total number of Candida isolates, the sampling location, the age of participants, the gender of participants, the history of receiving previous antifungal therapy, the history of receiving antiretroviral therapy (HAART), and the clinical form of candidiasis. For the studies that did not report the prevalence data, the corresponding author of the studies were contacted via e‐mail. A second e‐mail was sent 7 days after the first e‐mail. If the corresponding author did not answer or failed to provide the required data, the study was excluded.

2.5.

## Risk of Bias Assessment
A.F.D. and F.K. independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies with the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence data [ ]. Since F.K. was a co‐author in seven included studies, to avoid confirmation bias, the risk of bias assessment of these studies was performed by A.F.D. and S.B. instead. Conflicts that arose on the risk of bias rating of the studies were resolved with consensus. RevMan 5.4.1 was used for the summarization of the results of the risk of bias assessment.

2.6.

## Statistical Analysis
For pooled estimation, a random‐effects model was applied. If the individual studies reported proportions of 100% or 0%, a Freeman‐Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied before the synthesis in order to stabilize the variance. In order to explore the heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was planned to be carried out for gender, the history of receiving previous antifungal therapy, the history of receiving HAART, and the clinical form of candidiasis. Subgroup analysis was not performed due to the unavailability of data in the included studies. Where feasible, sensitivity analysis was performed for Candida spp. by omitting the studies with high or unclear risk of bias regarding the validity of the methods of antifungal resistance testing. All statistical analyses were performed with STATA.12.1.

3.3.

## Risk of Bias in Included Studies
In the included studies, except for Badiee 2010, all studies were found to have inappropriate sample frames to address the target population since these studies exclusively included HIV/AIDS patients with oral candidiasis. The sample selection methods were inappropriate in Erfaninejad 2023 and appropriate in Farahbakhsh 2012, Khedri 2018, and Salari 2016 while in the rest of the studies, the appropriateness of sample selection was unclear. Except for Badiee 2010, the sample sizes were inadequate. Study subjects and the settings were described in detail in Badiee 2010, Katiraee 2012, Katiraee 2014a, Katiraee 2014b, Khedri 2018, Morovati 2023, and Tamai 2021 while the rest of the studies did not describe their subjects and settings with adequate detail. Except for Farahbakhsh 2012, Yadegari 2011, and Sharifzadeh 2015, the rest of the included studies were considered low risk of bias for conducting analysis with sufficient coverage of the identified samples. For the use of valid methods for the determination of the antifungal resistance of Candida spp. for all participants, Erfaninejad 2023, Katiraee 2014a, Khedri 2018, Morovati 2023, Salari 2016, and Tamai 2021 were considered low risk of bias as they reported adhering to up‐to‐date guidelines for determining the antifungal resistance of Candida species isolates by disc diffusion or microdilution techniques. All studies used appropriate statistical analysis. Except for Farahbakhsh 2012, Katiraee 2015, Sharifzadeh 2015, and Yadegari 2011, which were considered unclear risk of bias for response rate, the rest of the included studies were considered low risk of bias. The risk of bias in the included studies is summarized in Figure  .

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to investigate the resistance rates of Candida spp. isolates from HIV‐AIDS patients in Iran between 1999 and 2024.
Based on our findings, the highest pooled resistance rates for Candida spp. were observed against CLOT (21%) and FCZ (15%), while the lowest pooled estimates were observed against AMB (0%), CAS (0%), and NYT (0%). Meta‐analysis was not feasible for 5‐FC due to the lack of evidence. Among the isolated Candida species, against FCZ, KCZ, CLOT, ICZ, VCZ, and AMB, the highest pooled resistance rates were observed for 
C. krusei
 (72%), 
C. albicans
 (16%), and 
C. glabrata
 (16%), 
C. glabrata
 (38%), 
C. krusei
 (38%), 
C. tropicalis
 (3%), 
C. albicans
 (1%), 
C. krusei
 (1%), and C. dubliniensis (1%), respectively. The pooled resistance rates against CAS and NYT for Candida spp. were zero.
The pooled resistance rates of Candida spp. in our study, with the exception of resistance against CLOT, were lower compared to the findings of Keyvanfar et al. reporting the global pooled resistance rates of Candida spp. isolates from HIV‐AIDS patients with oral candidiasis against KCZ, FCZ, 5‐FC, ICZ, VCZ, miconazole, CLOT, NYT, AMB, and CAS were estimated to be 25.5% (95% CI: 15.1%–35.8%), 24.8% (95% CI: 17.4%–32.1%), 22.9% (95% CI: −13.7% to 59.6%), 20.0% (95% CI: 10.0%–26.0%), 20.0% (95% CI: 1.9%–38.0%), 15.0% (95% CI: 5.1%–26.0%), 13.4% (95% CI: 2.3%–24.5%), 4.9% (95% CI: −0.05% to 10.3%), 2.9% (95% CI: 0.5%–5.3%), and 0.1% (95% CI: −0.3% to 0.6%), respectively [ ]. However, the reported negative confidence intervals of their resistance rates could suggest that, in that systematic review, the meta‐analysis was not performed properly.
Sensitivity analysis did not change the findings for the pooled resistance of Candida spp. against AMB, CAS, and NYT. This finding suggested that the pooled estimates for AMB, CAS, and NYT were robust. However, the pooled resistance against FCZ (9%) and ICZ (4%) was lower after omitting the studies with unclear or high risk of bias. For KCZ, CLOT, 5‐FC, and VCZ, sensitivity analysis was not feasible. The findings of sensitivity analysis for the pooled resistance rates of Candida spp. should be interpreted with caution due to the low number of included studies with low risk of bias and their sample sizes.
The first‐line antifungal agents used for the treatment of Candida infections are azoles and polyenes [ ]. However, echinocandins, including rezafungin, anidulafungin, micafungin, and CAS, possess a favorable safety profile, activity against a broad range of Candida spp. (including C auris), and limited drug–drug interactions. They are suitable as a first‐line therapy for candidemia. Echinocandins are also indicated for use in patients with additional risk factors for candidemia, patients with septic shock due to candidemia, and patients undergoing abdominal surgery with a history of exposure to azoles or in the case of the dominance of azole‐resistant Candida spp. in the local hospital. However, the degree of the strength of recommendation for the use of echinocandins in these conditions varies [ ]. Based on our findings, the use of AMB, CAS, and NYT for the treatment of Candida spp. in HIV‐AIDS patients in Iran is recommended if they are clinically indicated.
At the species level, the highest and lowest pooled resistance rates for 
C. albicans
 isolates were observed against FCZ (26%), CAS (0%), and NYT (0%), respectively. These findings were in line with the findings of Kermani et al. that the highest resistance rates of clinical 
C. albicans
 isolates in Iran were observed against CLOT and econazole (both 26%), KCZ (21%), and FCZ (20%) [ ].
The highest and lowest pooled resistance rates for 
C. glabrata
 isolates were observed against CLOT (38%), VCZ (0%), CAS (0%), AMB (0%), and NYT (0%). Our findings are consistent with the findings of Rodríguez‐Cerdeira et al. that showed C. glabrata isolates in European countries depicted resistance to azoles, including VCZ (14.45%), FCZ (10.47%), ICZ (2.19%) and CLOT (0.58%), increased resistance to echinocandins, in particular to anidulafungin (n = 138, 0.89%), and high sensitivity to AMB [ ]. However, in our search, no studies evaluated the resistance of Candida species to anidulafungin. Therefore, it is recommended to evaluate resistance against anidulafungin in future studies.
The highest and lowest pooled resistance rates for 
C. krusei
 isolates were observed against FCZ (72%), KCZ (0%), and NYT (0%), respectively. Some Candida species such as 
C. krusei
 and 
C. glabrata
 are intrinsically less susceptible to azoles because of their complex resistance mechanisms [ ]. This can explain the high pooled resistance rates observed for FCZ and CLOT for 
C. glabrata
 and 
C. krusei
.
The highest and lowest pooled resistance rates for C. dubliniensis isolates were observed against ICZ (9%), VCZ (0%), and CAS (0%), respectively. These findings were consistent with the findings in European countries that all C. dubliniensis isolates from candidemia cases were susceptible to FCZ and VCZ [ ].
The highest and lowest pooled resistance rates for C. kefyr isolates were observed against KCZ (3%), VCZ (0%), CAS (0%), AMB (0%), and NYT (0%). Although C. kefyr infections are uncommon, they have become more prevalent in recent years [ ].
The pooled resistance rates for 
C. parapsilosis
 isolates against all the tested antifungal agents (FCZ, KCZ, ICZ, CAS, AMB, and NYT) were zero. 
C. parapsilosis
 is ranked as the second to fourth most prevalent cause of candidemia in the world, and recent reports have indicated the emergence of fluconazole resistance in 
C. parapsilosis
 in certain geographical regions [ ]. Our findings did not show this pattern, which could be due to the low number of the isolates of 
C. parapsilosis
 from HIV‐AIDS patients in Iran. However, more detailed investigations of the antifungal resistance of 
C. parapsilosis
 in HIV‐AIDS patients are recommended.
The highest pooled resistance rate for 
C. tropicalis
 isolates was observed against VCZ (3%), while its pooled resistance rates against KCZ, CAS, AMB, and NYT were zero. These findings were in contradiction to the findings of Freitas et al., in which the highest and lowest resistance rates of 
C. tropicalis
 isolates from HIV/AIDS patients were seen against miconazole (77.7%) and AMB (0%), respectively [ ].
For 
C. africana
, 
C. famata
, 
C. lusitaniae
, 
C. intermedia
, meta‐analysis was not feasible due to the lack of evidence about the antifungal resistance among them.
The risk of bias of the included studies varied for the resistance rates of Candida spp. due to the differences in the prevalence rates of Candida spp. among HIV/AIDS individuals in Iran. Therefore, sample sizes should have been calculated for each Candida species separately to account for this issue. The majority of studies lacked adequate sample sizes for their reported resistance rates. Moreover, the majority of the included studies either did not report the guidelines used for conducting antifungal resistance or used out‐of‐date guidelines. Therefore, in future studies, adhering to up‐to‐date guidelines of antifungal resistance testing and reporting the guidelines used is suggested.
Since the majority of included studies among HIV/AIDS patients were conducted on oral candidiasis, it is important to highlight that the other forms of candidiasis, including vulvovaginal candidiasis, are also prevalent among HIV/AIDS patients [ ]. Therefore, it is recommended to investigate the resistance patterns of Candida spp. in the other clinical forms of candidiasis among HIV/AIDS patients in Iran.

5.

## Limitations
It was not possible to perform subgroup analysis due to the incomplete data in the included studies. The risk of bias of the studies investigating the antifungal resistance among Candida spp. was high. The majority of studies were exclusively conducted on oral candidiasis, which limits the applicability of the findings of this systematic review and meta‐analysis to oral candidiasis cases. This limitation showcases the lack of evidence on the antifungal resistance patterns in other forms of candidiasis among HIV/AIDS patients in Iran.

6.

## Conclusions
In summary, the highest pooled resistance rates for Candida spp. were observed against CLOT (21%) and FCZ (15%), while the lowest pooled estimates were observed against AMB (0%), CAS (0%), and NYT (0%). Sensitivity analysis lowered the pooled resistance estimates against FCZ (9%) and ICZ (4%). However, the pooled resistance estimates did not change in sensitivity analysis for AMB (0%), CAS (0%), and NYT (0%). The use of AMB, CAS, and NYT for the treatment of Candida spp. in HIV‐AIDS patients in Iran is recommended if indicated clinically. The risk of bias in the studies investigating the antifungal resistance of Candida spp. isolates among HIV/AIDS patients in Iran was high due to inadequate sample sizes, and the majority of studies were exclusively conducted on oral candidiasis. Moreover, the majority of the included studies either did not report the guidelines utilized in performing the antifungal resistance tests or used out‐of‐date guidelines. Performing more investigations with adequate sample sizes, particularly on the other forms of candidiasis among HIV/AIDS patients in Iran, is recommended. Performing antifungal resistance testing according to up‐to‐date guidelines and reporting the guidelines used are recommended.
