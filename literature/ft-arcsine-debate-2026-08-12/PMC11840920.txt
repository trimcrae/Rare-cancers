## Abstract

## Background
This study systematically analysed global and regional adherence to mask-wearing and its impact on the incidence and deaths of viral respiratory infectious diseases (VRIDs).

## Methods
Relevant studies were sourced from PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Scopus. We included observational studies with available raw data on mask-wearing adherence (rates of acceptability of mask-wearing, mask-wearing in public settings and correct mask-wearing) during VRID pandemics/epidemics. The COVID-19-related incidence and deaths were sourced from Global Burden of Diseases 2021. The quality of each study was assessed using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Scale. Pooled rates and effects of mask-wearing were calculated using random effects models and generalised linear models.

## Results
We included 448 studies from 70 countries/regions. During the VRID pandemics/epidemics, global pooled rates for the acceptability of mask-wearing, mask-wearing in public settings and correct mask-wearing were 65.27% (95% CI 60.34% to 70.05%), 74.67% (95% CI 69.17% to 79.8%) and 63.63% (95% CI 59.28% to 67.87%), respectively. In individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women, the rates of acceptability (42.54%) and correct mask-wearing (60.56%) were both low. Among the general population, the rates of mask-wearing in public settings (68.2%) and correct mask-wearing (57.45%) were the lowest. During the COVID-19 pandemic, countries with a requirement for mask-wearing exhibited a higher rate in public settings (76.10% vs 58.32%), especially in regions with more stringent containment and health measures (β=0.86, p=0.008). The mask-wearing in public settings has been found to significantly reduce the COVID-19 incidence (β=−265.26, p=0.004), COVID-19 deaths (β=−2.04, p<0.001) and other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths (β=−0.83, p<0.001).

## Conclusion
During the VRID pandemics/epidemics, adherence to mask-wearing was relatively low. Implementing policies requiring mask-wearing during the pandemic could enhance the mask-wearing rate, potentially leading to a significant reduction in VRID-related incidence and deaths.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Mask-wearing could effectively prevent and control viral infections during pandemics/epidemics of viral respiratory infectious diseases (VRIDs). Numerous epidemiological studies have focused on adherence to mask-wearing, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, there remains a scarcity of comprehensive studies that quantitatively evaluate and compare adherence to mask-wearing among different populations, regions and time periods from a global perspective.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the most comprehensive synthesised estimates of the global and regional rates of acceptability of mask-wearing, mask-wearing in public settings and correct mask-wearing during the VRID pandemics/epidemics.
Our findings revealed that global adherence to mask-wearing was relatively low, particularly among the general population. While individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women showed a higher rate of mask-wearing in public settings, the rates of acceptability of mask-wearing and correct mask-wearing were similarly low. We also identified potentially modifiable factors that are consistently associated with a lower rate of mask-wearing in public settings, including children aged ≤14 years old, males and individuals with a lower educational level. During the pandemic of VRIDs, countries with a requirement for mask-wearing could enhance the rate of mask-wearing in public settings, especially in regions with more stringent containment and health measures. Meanwhile, our research findings also found that mask-wearing in public settings could reduce COVID-19 incidence, COVID-19 deaths and other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
During the VRID pandemics/epidemics, global adherence to mask-wearing remains relatively low. Understanding the global and regional rates of acceptability of mask-wearing, mask-wearing in public settings and correct mask-wearing, along with their associated factors, would help to understand the mask-wearing behaviour and to identify regions and populations requiring targeted interventions. This insight could provide valuable guidance for formulating mask-wearing policies against future VRID pandemics/epidemics.

## Introduction
 Mask-wearing has been recognised as an important personal protective measure during pandemics or epidemics of viral respiratory infectious diseases (VRIDs). In the prevention and control of influenza, severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) and the recent COVID-19 pandemic, mask-wearing has been recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the WHO as a crucial non-pharmaceutical intervention to reduce the risk of viral infection. The effectiveness of masks (eg, N95 respirators and medical masks) has been reported by many evidence-based studies, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, studies have shown a significant reduction in infection risk of SARS-COV-2 (OR=0.15). 
The public’s adherence to mask-wearing plays a crucial role in determining the effectiveness and promotion of this measure in preventing and controlling VRIDs. This adherence is significantly influenced by sociocultural factors, such as social norms, perceived infection risk and formal institutional requirements. Disparities in adherence to mask-wearing have been observed across various countries, regions, time periods and demographic groups. The 2003 SARS outbreak significantly transformed health perceptions in Asia, leading to the voluntary adoption of mask-wearing in subsequent influenza seasons. This practice not only played a crucial role in combating SARS but also highlighted the high level of self-protection awareness in Asian cultures and the strong self-efficacy in using masks as a means of personal protection. However, the culture of mask-wearing varies across regions. For instance, a study conducted in Australia revealed that mask-wearing as an infection control strategy was highly unpopular among university students in Sydney towards both seasonal and pandemic influenza outbreaks. During the early COVID-19 pandemic, a survey showed that 81.8% of individuals in China wore surgical masks in public, compared with 13.3% in Switzerland and 14% of visitors to the Jimma University Medical Center in Ethiopia. 
Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive research that quantitatively evaluates and compares adherence to mask-wearing during VRID pandemics/epidemics among diverse populations, regions and over various time periods from a global perspective. This gap in knowledge is critical, as it impedes the development of effective mask-wearing strategies to mitigate VRIDs. A detailed and systematic understanding of regional and demographic variations in mask-wearing behaviour, willingness and influencing factors, as well as the impact of mask-wearing on the incidence and mortality of VRIDs, is essential for designing publicly acceptable masks-based prevention strategies. Furthermore, it is crucial to explore measures to enhance adherence to mask-wearing when acceptance is low or to identify alternative Non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) that could serve as substitutes. In this study, our aim is to systematically estimate global, regional and national adherence to mask-wearing and its impact on the incidence and deaths of VRIDs.

## Methods
We developed a study protocol following standard guidelines for systematic reviews. We also completed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses checklist ( ) and registered the review on PROSPERO (International prospective register of systematic reviews) (registration number: CRD42024496464).

## Definition of adherence to mask-wearing and VRIDs
In our study, adherence to mask-wearing was quantified by assessing the rates of acceptability of mask-wearing, mask-wearing in public settings and the correct mask-wearing. The masks were defined as a protective device used to cover the mouth and nose, designed to filter the air and diminish the inhalation of particulates. The primary categories of masks encompassed medical masks, N95 respirators, surgical masks and cloth masks. The rate of acceptability of mask-wearing= the rate of mask-wearing in public settings= ; the rate of correct mask-wearing= The public settings were defined as locations open to the general public, typically including public transportation, stores, restaurants, schools, parks, theatres, medical institutions, meeting places and similar venues. Correct mask-wearing was defined as the mask fully covering the individual’s mouth and nose, with the upper boundary moulded over the nose and the lower boundary below the chin. In our study, the pandemics/epidemics of VRIDs included SARS-CoV-2, influenza, MERS, SARS, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza virus, metapneumovirus, rhinovirus, adenovirus and bocavirus. The data for individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women were analysed collectively. Given that these populations are generally considered high-risk groups for VRIDs, which could lead to severe illness and even death.

## Search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria
We first identified the literature through searches of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Scopus for articles published from database inception to 30 November 2023 and then updated the search on 1 April 2024, using the following search terms: “mask” and (“influenza” or “influenza” or “respiratory infectious disease” or “COVID-19” or “respiratory syncytial virus” or “parainfluenza virus” or metapneumovirus” or “rhinovirus” or “adenovirus” or “bocavirus”) and (“adheren*” or “accept*” or “willing*” or “wear*”) and (“cross-sectional stud*” or “observational stud*”). The full list of search strategies is presented in the . There were no language restrictions on the search results. Studies had to meet all three of the following criteria for inclusion: (i) observational studies without trials and interventions; (ii) availability of raw data necessary for calculating the rate of acceptability of mask-wearing or mask-wearing in public settings or the correct mask-wearing; (iii) The study focused on the aforementioned VRIDs. We excluded several types of studies from our analysis: case reports/studies, modelling and simulation studies, studies that only presented graphical summaries without clear statistical evaluations or outputs, ecological studies offering descriptive summaries without detailed data, non-empirical studies (such as commentaries, editorials and government reports), as well as other reviews. For non-English studies, we use translation tools to translate the content into English and then assess whether the study meets our research objectives and inclusion/exclusion criteria. If it meets the inclusion criteria, we proceed with the data extraction and analysis steps.

## Screening, data extraction and quality assessment
After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts were screened to exclude studies that did not focus on mask-wearing. Subsequently, four independent reviewers (CC, WZ, JQ and MC) scrutinised the retained full-text articles based on the inclusion criteria. The following data were extracted for each study when provided: (i) study information: title, first author, journal name, the year of publication, study design, sample size, the country of study population, WHO region, study period, average age or age range of study population; and (ii) the raw data for calculating the acceptability of mask-wearing or the rate of mask-wearing in public settings or the rate of correct mask-wearing and (iii) the factors associated with adherence to mask-wearing: age (≤14, 15–59 and ≥60), sex, education level, types of public settings, the sociodemographic index (SDI) of country. The articles and citations were managed in the EndNote (V.X21). We evaluated the quality of each study using the scale recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). Since our study is a meta-analysis of rates derived from observational studies, we assessed the representativeness of the samples in the included studies, as greater representativeness in the original studies leads to smaller bias in the rates. We refined the process by adding a scoring criterion for sample representativeness in the AHRQ scale ( ). Uncertainties regarding screening, data extraction and quality assessment were resolved through a consensus reached by discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (Professor Shigui Yang, the principal investigator of this study) for additional insights, helping to resolve discrepancies and ensure high-quality decision-making. For studies that met our inclusion criteria but had missing or incomplete data in subgroup analyses, we sought to supplement the missing or incomplete data by incorporating relevant country-level data from the respective study locations, such as data on mask-wearing requirements and SDI (a comprehensive indicator of a country or region’s development status, assessed by integrating data such as per capita income, average level of education and fertility rates), to ensure as much completeness as possible. The mask-wearing requirements were categorised based on data provided by the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT), which provided official data on mask-wearing requirements during the COVID-19 pandemic. Based on the OxCGRT, we classified the countries in this study as having mask-wearing requirements or not, allowing us to compare mask-wearing adherence. If a study offered raw data on adherence to mask-wearing across multiple time points, each formed a separate dataset in the meta-analysis.

## Data analysis
The rates of acceptability of mask-wearing, mask-wearing in public settings and correct mask-wearing were transformed using a Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation. Due to the expected high heterogeneity, we pooled individual study data with DerSimonian and Laird random effects models. After pooling the estimates, the combined point estimates and their 95% CIs were then back-transformed to proportions and plotting. The I2 statistic was used to assess the heterogeneity which was calculated by the DerSimonian-Laird method. The subgroup analyses were performed to explore the univariate variables associated with pooled rates. The variables included study populations, locations, periods, ages, sex, educational levels, mask-wearing requirements, public settings (eg, medical institution, schools, workplace, public transportation, stores/supermarket, parks and restaurants), SDI levels and types of VRIDs. Sensitivity analysis which omits the studies with low sample sizes was used to evaluate the robustness and reliability of overall pooled rates. Sample sizes were divided into high, medium-high, medium-low and low tertiles, using the 25th, 50th and 75th percentile scores.
We aggregated country-level covariates, including regional health policies and socioeconomic conditions, which were hypothesised to be directly or indirectly associated with the rate of mask-wearing in public settings during the COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, we further investigated the impact of mask-wearing in public settings on the incidence and deaths related to COVID-19. We then examined several types of generalised linear models (GLMs) to fit the rate and each covariate. We determined the best-fit model according to the Akaike information criterion. Country-level covariates encompass sociodemographic factors, index of NPI implementation during the COVID-19 pandemic, obtained from the World Bank and Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker; COVID-19-related incidence and deaths data are sourced from the Global Burden of Diseases 2021. The Pearson correlation was used to explore the univariate correlation between variables. The meta-analyses, Pearson correlation and GLM were conducted by the R V.4.3.2 using the meta, metafor and stats packages.

## Results

## Search results
Our literature search yielded 20 816 records. Following the removal of duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 13 994 published studies were reviewed. A total of 2942 full-text articles were evaluated based on inclusion criteria. Among these, 448 studies, spanning from 2005 to 2024 and conducted in 70 countries/regions, were included, resulting in 654 datasets ( ). The characteristics of the included studies have been summarised in the .
 

## Adherence to mask by study populations, WHO regions and periods
The pooled rate of acceptability of mask-wearing during the VRID pandemics/epidemics was 65.27% (95% CI 60.34% to 70.05%) ( and ). It was lowest among individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women and highest among healthcare workers (42.54% vs 84.71%) ( ). As for WHO regions, the acceptability was lowest in the European Region (49.32%, 95% CI 41.85% to 56.8%), followed by the African Region (57.9%, 95% CI 27.74% to 85.13%) ( ). Compared with the early stage of the COVID-19 pandemic (57.75%), the acceptability significantly increased during the Delta and Omicron variant waves (79.09%) ( ).
 The pooled rate of mask-wearing in public settings during the VRIDs pandemics/epidemics was 74.67% (95% CI 69.17% to 79.8%) ( , and ). It was lowest among the general population and highest among individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women (68.2% vs 90.03%) ( ). As for WHO regions, the rate was lowest in African Region (62.69%, 95% CI 56.3% to 68.86%). ( ). The rate of mask-wearing in public settings was also relatively comparable across different phases of the COVID-19 pandemic (76.7% vs 74.11%) ( ).
 The pooled rate of correct mask-wearing during the VRID pandemics/epidemics was 63.63% (95% CI 59.28% to 67.87%) ( and ). It was lowest in the general population (57.45%, 95% CI 51.7% to 63.11%) and followed by individuals with chronic diseases/elderly pregnant women (60.56%, 95% CI 0.01% to 100%) ( ). As for WHO regions, the rate was lowest in the African Region (23.39%, 95% CI 3.4% to 53.81%). Compared with the early stage of the COVID-19 pandemic (70.04%), the rate decreased during the pandemic of the delta and omicron variants (47.72%) ( ).
In the sensitivity analysis ( ), when all the studies were included in the meta-analysis, the estimated rate of mask-wearing in public settings was 74.67% (95% CI 69.17% to 79.8%). When the studies with low sample sizes were omitted, the estimated rate was 72.74% (95% CI 66.25% to 78.78%). This indicates that the pooled rate was relatively robust and reliable.

## Factors associated with the rate of mask-wearing in public settings
The pooled rate of mask-wearing in public settings was lowest in the ≤14-year group, males and individuals with primary educational level ( ). As for specific public settings, the first three locations for the rate of mask-wearing were medical institutions (80.64%, 95% CI 75.44% to 85.35%), workplaces (79.38%, 95% CI 70.6% to 86.98%) and schools (75.89%, 95% CI 67.34% to 83.51%). The rate was similar across different levels of SDI ( ). During the COVID-19 pandemic, the general population’s rate of mask-wearing in public settings significantly decreased over time ( ). Countries with mask-wearing requirements have shown a higher rate (76.10% vs 58.32%), particularly in regions with more stringent containment and health measures (β=0.8612, p=0.008) ( ).
 The pooled rate of mask-wearing in public settings was similar during the pandemic/epidemic of several VRIDs. It was 75.93% (95% CI 70.07% to 81.35%) for COVID-19, 58.76% (95% CI 38.7% to 77.43%) for 2009 A/H1N1 influenza, 74.95% (95% CI 72.65% to 77.19%) for MERS and 68.84% (95% CI 39.51% to 91.72%) for SARS. However, during the seasonal influenza season, the mask-wearing rate was significantly lower at 37.69% (95% CI 26.56% to 49.51%) ( ).

## The impact of mask-wearing in public settings on the incidence and deaths of COVID-19
In the Pearson correlation analysis, we observed a significant negative correlation between the rate of mask-wearing in public settings and the COVID-19 incidence (r=− 0.38, p=0.0007), COVID-19 deaths (r=− 0.46, p<0.0001) and other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths (r=− 0.44, p<0.0001) ( ). In the GLM, a higher rate of mask-wearing in public settings has been found to significantly reduce the COVID-19 incidence (β=−265.2634, p=0.0039), COVID-19 deaths (β=−2.037, p=0.0002) and other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths (β=−0.8324, p=0.0004) ( ).
 

## Discussion
Our findings indicate that during the VRID pandemics/epidemics, the global rates of acceptability of mask-wearing, mask-wearing in public settings and correct mask-wearing were 65.27%, 74.67% and 63.63%, respectively. The general population exhibited lower levels of these rates. Rates of acceptability and correct mask-wearing were lower in individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women. In the Western Pacific and Southeast Asia regions, these rates are relatively high. However, in the regions of Europe and Africa, the rates of acceptability and correct mask-wearing were significantly lower than in other regions, respectively. In comparison to regular influenza seasons, the rate of mask-wearing in public settings significantly increased during a pandemic, especially in countries where mask-wearing was required. Mask-wearing in public settings could significantly reduce the COVID-19 incidence, COVID-19 deaths and other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths.
Adherence to mask-wearing varied among different populations. Individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women exhibited a relatively high rate of mask-wearing in public settings; however, their actual acceptability and correct wearing rate were relatively low. Individuals with chronic diseases, especially those with respiratory ailments, are less inclined to adopt mask-wearing due to the likelihood of experiencing symptoms associated with wearing masks, such as the need for increased breathing effort and discomfort. For example, studies have shown that wearing N95 masks can exacerbate breathing difficulties in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Asthma patients frequently report heightened discomfort and asthma-like symptoms while wearing masks. This physical discomfort, combined with the fear of being perceived as ‘ill’, may further discourage them from wearing masks. Patients with chronic immune diseases, like lupus and rheumatoid arthritis, may struggle with mask adherence due to skin irritation and worsened lesions from prolonged use. Nevertheless, for safety reasons, they still choose to wear masks in public settings. Among the general population, adherence to mask-wearing was also low. The adoption of masks in the general population has been hindered by a combination of physical, social and informational barriers. Physical discomfort, including skin irritation, breathing difficulties and fogging of glasses, has discouraged widespread usage. Social concerns, such as limited interaction opportunities and perceived threats to personal freedom, further reduce compliance. Besides, there has been relatively little attention paid to the medical value and effectiveness of mask protection in the general population. The extent of mask-wearing also depended on individual risk assessment and management. Requirements for mask-wearing are likely to increase in specific settings, particularly in healthcare facilities, schools and workplaces, where mask-wearing is often more strongly recommended. , Our study also observed a relatively higher mask-wearing rate in these three settings
The adherence to mask-wearing exhibits significant regional variations. We have observed that the acceptability of mask-wearing was lowest in the European Region, while the correct mask-wearing was lowest in the African Region. In the Western Pacific and Southeast Asia Regions, these rates were all high. In the last two decades, Western Pacific and East Asian nations have encountered various epidemics of respiratory infectious diseases such as SARS, avian influenza and A/H1N1. As a result, the widespread use of masks has become a common preventive practice in society. Therefore, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the public is urged to wear masks in public settings, and people frequently adhere to this as a health-conscious behaviour. Nevertheless, in numerous advanced Western economies, individuals wearing masks are frequently regarded with suspicion in public due to mask-wearing occasionally perceived as conflicting with principles of freedom and individualism and can even incite panic in certain circumstances. Discomfort and a sense of social isolation have also been identified as key factors contributing to mask resistance in certain Western countries. Additionally, inconsistency of government directives—initially downplaying the effectiveness of masks and later mandating their use—further fuelled public scepticism towards pandemic measures, thereby impacting adherence. 
The challenges in proper mask usage in African countries during the VRID epidemics and pandemics arise from a combination of economic, educational and environmental factors. In many African countries, insufficient health campaigns have resulted in low public awareness about masks, leaving many residents unaware of their importance or proper usage, including healthcare workers. For low-income groups, masks are often perceived as an unaffordable expense, and limited availability in underserved areas further compounds this issue. Moreover, in many African countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, HIV, malaria and tuberculosis have long been the major health burdens. As a result, health policies and infrastructure have been heavily oriented towards combating these diseases. This sustained high disease burden has led to insufficient capacity, medical resources and personnel to effectively address and raise awareness about VRIDs epidemics and pandemics.
Adherence to mask-wearing also varied across different stages of the VRID pandemic. During the early stages of the pandemic, due to fear and a lack of sufficient understanding of the disease, although public acceptability of mask-wearing was low, the rates of mask-wearing were relatively high. However, in the later stages of the pandemic, there was a significant decrease in the rate of mask-wearing in public settings among the general population and in the rate of correct mask-wearing. This phenomenon, known as pandemic fatigue, has been observed in the implementation of many NPIs, especially among young people. Therefore, if the pandemic persists for an extended period, sustaining rigorous mask measures in the long term will require significant adjustments, and mask-wearing will gradually decline. Therefore, while mask-wearing is effective, it needs to be complemented with other preventive measures like hand washing, and the timely development of vaccines and antiviral drugs is also crucial for controlling pandemic diseases.
Mask-wearing in public settings significantly reduced the incidence and deaths of COVID-19, as well as other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths. Countries with mask-wearing requirements have demonstrated higher rates of mask-wearing compared with those without such regulations. Therefore, we strongly recommend that governments and health organisations encourage or promote mask-wearing in public settings and educate the public on the correct usage of masks during VRID pandemics/epidemics. ‘Adding objects to the environment’, ‘instruction on how to perform the behaviour’ and ‘information about health consequences’ are primary effective methods for promoting health-related behaviours. Widespread dissemination of knowledge related to diseases and masks, as well as health education, is also crucial. A positive correlation was found between mask-wearing behaviour among the general population and knowledge related to VRIDs, perceived norms and positive attitudes (eg, perceived importance, benefits and effectiveness). , In Bangladesh, it was discovered that community-level mask distribution and promotion increased proper mask-wearing from 13.3% in the control group to 42.3% in the intervention group. This indicates that in countries/regions where financially feasible, distributing masks for free could increase mask-wearing. Social media campaigns can also normalise mask-wearing by leveraging trusted influencers, celebrities, healthcare professionals and community leaders who endorse mask-wearing behaviours. For example, using hashtags like # National Mask Campaign in social media has successfully countered misinformation and stigma surrounding masks. Additionally, social media can improve public awareness of correct mask-wearing through educational content such as infographics, instructional videos and live demonstrations. These materials can visually highlight key practices, including securing masks over the nose and mouth, avoiding frequent adjustments and safely removing or cleaning reusable masks.
Our study has several limitations. First, due to the lack of research data on mask-wearing in many countries, especially for low- and middle-income countries, the estimation of mask-wearing in these countries is limited. This limitation may impact the generalisability of pooled estimates to represent each population globally. Second, the wide range of included studies resulted in high heterogeneity, which could not be fully explained by the variables analysed. Additionally, many of the included studies assessed adherence to mask-wearing using self-developed questionnaire surveys, which lack standardised measurement criteria, which may introduce information and recall bias, contributing to the observed heterogeneity in the pooled results. To account for this expected high heterogeneity, a random effects model was employed in this study. Subgroup meta-analyses were also conducted to better understand adherence to mask-wearing within the context of this heterogeneity. Third, since most of the included research does not clearly specify the types of masks used, we were unable to analyse adherence to different mask types. In future research, we will conduct targeted field surveys, building on the findings of this study and implement appropriate measures to control for confounding factors and biases. This will allow us to further understand the behavioural mechanisms of mask-wearing and its variations across different mask types. Fourth, we did not conduct literature searches on platforms dedicated specifically to preprints or grey literature, which may have resulted in some relevant studies being overlooked. However, the databases we searched include some preprints (eg, studies from medRxiv are also often published on PubMed), and our study already included a substantial number of studies, which helps ensure the reliability of the results. Fifth, to minimise information bias, our study excluded research that lacked raw data necessary for calculating the rate of adherence to mask-wearing. This may have resulted in the exclusion of some studies. However, the reported rates in these studies, such as those from Taiwan (China) and the USA, were comparable to those in our research, which suggests that they may have little impact on our pooled estimate.

## Conclusion
Global adherence to mask-wearing during the VRID pandemics/epidemics was relatively low and varied across populations, regions and time periods. Among individuals with chronic diseases/elderly/pregnant women, the rate of mask-wearing in public settings was highest, but the acceptability and correct mask-wearing were low. Both the rates of mask-wearing in public settings and correct mask-wearing were lowest in the general population and in the African Region. Implementing policies that require mask-wearing during pandemics could be an effective strategy to increase compliance in public settings, especially in areas with low adherence. This would support the development of mask-based prevention measures, helping to reduce the transmission of VRIDs during pandemics or epidemics, potentially leading to a significant decrease in both incidence and deaths.
