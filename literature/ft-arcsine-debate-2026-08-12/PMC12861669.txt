## Abstract

## Background
Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is a well-established therapy for patients with heart failure. However, patients with chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy (CCC) were underrepresented in pivotal CRT trials.

## Objective
This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to quantitatively describe the outcomes of CRT in patients with CCC.

## Methods
We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and LILACS registries for studies evaluating the response to CRT in patients with CCC. We applied no language or date restrictions. We applied an inverse-variance random-effects model for a meta-analysis of proportions.

## Results
We included 4 observational cohort studies comprising 250 patients with CCC undergoing CRT. At CRT implantation, the mean age was 59 ± 12 years, 87% of patients (217) were New York Heart Association class III/IV, and the average left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 27% ± 7%. During a mean follow-up of 30 ± 25 months, the all-cause death rate was 38% (95% confidence interval [CI], 21–56), cardiac death rate was 29% (95% CI, 13–47), and the noncardiac death rate was 4% (95% CI, 1–9). After CRT, the proportion of patients rated New York Heart Association class III/IV was improved to 21% (95% CI, 5–44), with a pooled post-CRT LVEF of 36.3% (95% CI, 27.0–48.0).

## Conclusion
In patients with CCC, CRT was associated with improvements in LVEF and symptomatic and functional burden (class III/IV), but overall mortality remained high. Although CRT seems to be beneficial in this population, further research is warranted to better characterize its impact on long-term clinical outcomes.

Keywords: Cardiac resynchronization therapy, Chagas cardiomyopathy, Heart failure, Neglected diseases, Mortality

## Introduction
Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is a well-established treatment for ischemic (ICM) and nonischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM) with significant morbidity and mortality benefits. However, these recommendations are largely informed by trials that excluded patients with chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy (CCC), which has unique features that preclude extrapolation from these findings. , , , , For instance, there is evidence suggesting that CCC is associated with a 2-fold increase in mortality rates compared with other cardiomyopathies. With close to 7 million individuals affected in Latin America and a rising incidence of Chagas disease (CD) in developed countries from surges in migration, this has become an increasing concern. , 
Approximately 40% of individuals with CD experience cardiac involvement, resulting in CCC that is primarily characterized by extensive myocardial fibrosis. , CD may manifest with a wide range of cardiac symptoms, from asymptomatic to severe cases, including sudden cardiac death (SCD) and heart failure (HF). , Among the cardiac manifestations, the occurrence of intrinsic left bundle branch block (LBBB) seems less common than other cardiomyopathies, being reported in approximately 7% of patients in some cohorts. , In contrast, CCC is associated with significantly higher rates of right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, intraventricular block, and conduction abnormalities requiring RV pacing, particularly in the context of advanced disease. 
CRT could be useful for this population, but there is no convincing evidence supporting its routine use. Previous observational studies enrolling these patients have reported improvement in the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class and echocardiographic parameters, but their small sample sizes do not allow for definitive conclusions or recommendations. , , , We herein performed the first systematic review and meta-analysis of proportions to synthesize the clinical outcomes of patients with CCC undergoing CRT.

## Methods
This systematic review, meta-analysis, and reporting were registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under protocol CRD42025641591. This study was designed and conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines. Institutional review board approval was waived owing to the use of retrospective, deidentified data previously published in the literature.

## Study eligibility
This meta-analysis included studies that met all the following eligibility criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials or observational studies, (2) evaluated patients with CCC, (3) patients who underwent CRT, and (5) reported clinical outcomes of interest. Letters, editorials, expert opinions, case reports, reviews, studies without usable data, overlapping study populations, and publications limited to only abstracts were excluded from the analysis.

## Search strategy and data extraction
We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and LILACS from inception to December 2024 with the following search terms: “Chagas disease”, “Chagas cardiomyopathy”, Chagas, “American trypanosomiasis”, “cardiac resynchronization therapy”, “CRT”, “resynchronization”, and “biventricular pacing”. In addition, the references of included studies were evaluated for additional studies. We applied no filters or date or language restrictions to our search. Two authors independently extracted the data (N.N.G.V. and P.M.) following predefined search criteria and quality assessment.

## End points
Evaluated end points of interest included overall mortality, cardiac mortality, noncardiac mortality, changes in left ventricular systolic function, and procedural complications.

## Quality assessment
The methodological quality of all included studies was evaluated independently by 2 authors (I.F.F. and M.K.) using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions, as per Cochrane recommendations. Disagreements were resolved by recruiting a third author (N.F.) to attain consensus.

## Data analysis
This was a single-arm meta-analysis of proportions. The statistical analysis was conducted using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We conducted an inverse-variance random-effects model meta-analysis of proportions for binary and continuous outcome data. We used the “meta” package functions for all endpoints.
Often in single-arm meta-analysis, the proportion is either 0 or 1 (equivalent to 0 or 100%, respectively) in 1 or more studies, which leads to a variance tending toward zero. In this scenario, it is advisable to stabilize the variance by applying the so-called Freeman-Turkey double arcsine transformation, which allows for stabilizing both the variance and the weight of the included studies, leading to a more precise result. The arcsine transformation was applied along with the inverse method for binary endpoints, as recommended by Schwarzer et al. 
Likewise, the inverse method was applied to continuous endpoints without transformation. Whenever available, we used mean and standard deviations to pool continuous data, and when it was missing, we estimated it through the Wan and Luo method, as per Cochrane recommendations. , Skewed data were used in the first moment, and then, through a sensitivity leave-one-out analysis, we assessed their impact on each outcome to ensure proper results. A restricted maximum likelihood random-effects model was used for all analyses, given that we assumed that the data came from varied populations with different distributions. Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistics were used to evaluate heterogeneity; P values inferior to .10 and I2 > 50% were considered significant for heterogeneity. The results were presented as a pooled analysis in forest plots.

## Results

## Study selection and baseline characteristics
The initial search resulted in 210 articles: 40 from PubMed, 143 from Embase, 2 from Cochrane Library, and 25 from LILACS. Eighty-five duplicates were filtered out, and the remaining articles were reviewed through titles and abstracts. After applying the exclusion criteria, 13 studies were subjected to the full-text screening process, and an additional 9 studies were eliminated. A total of 4 articles , , , were identified for further analysis; the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flowchart is shown in . These 4 studies reported 250 patients with CCC undergoing CRT.
 As shown in , all included studies were retrospective cohorts. Among studies that reported the data, at CRT implantation, the overall mean age was 59 ± 12 years, 60% (107) were males, and 87% (217) were NYHA class III or IV. An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was present in 39% of the patients (64) at baseline, mean QRS was 154 ± 32 ms, and mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 27% ± 7%. Importantly, atrial fibrillation was present in 28% of patients (43). Only 26% (62) had intrinsic LBBB, whereas 47% (111) exhibited pacing-induced LBBB. Araújo et al reported implantation of resynchronization through mini thoracotomy in 67% of the patients and via venous sinus in 33%. The other studies did not provide information on the resynchronization technique. Regarding medical management, 82% patients (82%) were on an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor/aldosterone receptor antagonist, 185 (74%) on beta-blockers, 171 (68%) on spironolactone, and 189 (80%) on loop diuretics. The mean overall follow-up was 30 ± 25 months. Outcomes shown followed CRT-pacing implantation, with no studies reporting CRT-defibrillation implantation.
 

## Pooled analysis of included studies

## Mortality
All 4 studies reported the mortality outcomes of interest. The all-cause death rate was 38% (95% confidence interval [CI], 21–56; I2 = 88%) ( A), primarily driven by cardiac causes (29%; 95% CI, 13–47; I2 = 89%) ( B). The noncardiac death rate was 4% (95% CI, 1–9; I2 = 37%) ( C).
 Menezes et al reported arrhythmias (6%), stroke (2%), acute coronary syndrome (2%), and acute HF exacerbation (4%) as the main causes of cardiac death. Araújo et al described worsening HF (60%), stroke (4%), and SCD (8%) as the main causes of cardiac death. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (4%) and pneumonia (4%) were the main causes of noncardiac death. Martinelli Filho et al noted worsening HF (73%) as the main driver for cardiac deaths.

## Clinical and echocardiographic outcomes
After CRT, the pooled NYHA class III or IV rate was 21% (95% CI, 5–44; I2 = 94%) ( ). The pooled LVEF after CRT was 36.0% (95% CI, 27.0–48.0; I2 = 99%) ( ).
 Other echocardiographic data were inconsistently reported across studies. Menezes et al noted a significant increase in LVEF (40% ± 13% vs 29% ± 7%; P < .001) and decrease in left ventricular end-systolic volume (152 ± 59 mL vs 265 ± 59 mL; P < .01). Araújo et al reported a decrease in left ventricular end-systolic diameter (51 vs 58 mm; P < .001) and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (65 mm vs 66 mm; P = .295) after CRT. Martinelli Filho et al described nonsignificant changes in LVEF (27% ± 9% vs 26% ± 6%; P = .1), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (67 ± 9 vs 66 ± 8 mm; P = .052), and left atrium diameter (49 ± 6 vs 48 ± 7 mm; P = .134) after CRT.

## Sensitivity analysis
We performed a sensitivity analysis to explore outcomes with elevated heterogeneity between studies (I2 > 50%). The sensitivity analyses of the overall mortality, cardiac and noncardiac death rates, NYHA class III or IV, and pooled final LVEF are presented in .

## Quality assessment
Quality assessment of the included studies was performed with Cochrane’s Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool. All studies were classified as having a moderate risk of bias in the confounding domain given their retrospective and nonrandomized nature, which leads to a residual risk of confounding in the absence of adjusted analyses. Domain 4 (bias owing to deviations from intended interventions) was assessed as moderate in 2 studies , given their single-arm nature. Domain 6 (bias in measurement of outcomes) was assessed as moderate for all studies given no blindness to outcome assessment. Overall, all studies were classified as having a moderate risk of bias ( ).

## Discussion
Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated the outcomes of ICD implantation for patients with CCC, primarily for secondary prevention. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis evaluating the outcomes of CRT in patients with CCC. We found that a substantial proportion of patients experienced relief from severe HF symptoms after CRT, with improvements observed in functional status and LVEF. However, the overall mortality remained significant, primarily driven by cardiovascular mortality.
CRT plays an evolving role in the management of CCC. Although CRT is a cornerstone treatment for patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction and electrical dyssynchrony, its efficacy in CCC remains less clear. Evidence suggests that patients with CCC may derive fewer benefits than those with NICM or ICM. For instance, studies have shown that patients with CCC experience higher mortality rates, limited functional improvement, and minimal echocardiographic changes after CRT. Current clinical guidelines support CRT based on criteria such as reduced ejection fraction and prolonged QRS duration; however, these recommendations are largely informed by trials that excluded patients with CCC. As a result, the benefits of CRT in this population may be less pronounced, emphasizing the need for careful patient selection.
CRT has been extensively studied in patients with NICM and ICM, with favorable outcomes particularly in NICM. In this population, CRT is associated with significant improvements in LVEF and reductions in left ventricular end-systolic volume and left ventricular end-diastolic volume, reflecting positive ventricular remodeling. , Landmark trials such as REVERSE and MADIT-CRT demonstrated substantial clinical and echocardiographic benefits in this population, including improved survival and reduced HF hospitalizations.
In CCC, the benefits of CRT are often unclear, especially considering the high morbidity, mortality, and symptomatic burden in this population compared with other cardiomyopathies. For instance, a cohort study by Martinelli Filho et al found that patients with CCC who underwent CRT had significantly higher annual mortality rates (25.4%) than those with NICM (10.4%) and ICM (11.3%). The rate of improvement in NYHA functional class 12 months after CRT was also lower in patients with CCC (56.5%) than those with NICM (74.0%) and ICM (73.9%). Furthermore, patients with CCC did not show significant echocardiographic improvements compared with those with the other etiologies. Passos et al also demonstrated that CCC was associated with higher rates of HF hospitalization or death (62% vs 22%) than other causes of HF, with CCC being an independent predictor of the combined outcome. After pooling results from the literature, we found a pooled overall mortality rate of 38%, higher than rates of ICM (18%) and NICM (9.4%) previously described over a similar follow-up time. Moreover, we reported a rate of improvement from NYHA III and IV to I and II of approximately 66% after CRT, which is similar to previous studies reporting 65% and 71% for ICM and NICM, respectively. 
We found an overall remarkably elevated mortality in the included population (38%), despite CRT implantation and reasonable rates of guideline-directed medical therapy for HF. This finding is consistent with previous literature demonstrating that CCC has higher mortality than other etiologies, being an independent predictor of death. The precise reasons for this unfavorable pattern are not fully understood, but it could be ascribed to more extensive myocardial fibrosis with CCC, higher rates of RV dysfunction, and higher ventricular arrhythmia burden. 
Importantly, there was an impressively low rate of intrinsic LBBB in the included studies (26%), which may have contributed to a suboptimal CRT response given that LBBB morphology is a strong predictor of greater clinical and echocardiographic benefits from CRT. Indeed, Menezes et al reported a CRT response rate of 80% in patients with intrinsic LBBB compared with only 60% in those with right bundle branch block or pacing-induced LBBB. Unfortunately, outcome data by intraventricular block type were not reported in the other studies. The precise cause for the low rates of intrinsic LBBB in CCC is unclear given the paucity of literature on CCC. However, the higher presence of RV pacing in many patients (47%) may reflect a more advanced conduction system disease and myocardial fibrosis at baseline for which CRT may not confer the same benefit. 
Furthermore, there was an elevated prevalence of patients with atrial fibrillation in the included studies (28%). These patients typically derive fewer benefits from CRT than patients in sinus rhythm given the suboptimal biventricular pacing owing to irregular ventricular activation, fusion or pseudofusion beats, and loss of atrioventricular synchrony. These factors, when combined with the already complex substrate of CCC, may help explain the high mortality and inconsistent remodeling responses observed in our analysis.
Given elevated heterogeneity, the results from our study should be carefully interpreted. This is most likely derived from the different inclusion criteria for patients across studies. Araújo et al included patients with confirmed serologic diagnosis of CCC, severe HF (LVEF < 35%) despite optimal medical therapy, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter of greater than 55 mm, and QRS of >120 ms. Martinelli Filho et al adopted a NYHA score III or IV for symptoms assessment and QRS cutoff of 150 ms, while also including patients with class II indication such as permanent atrial fibrillation or pacemaker users. In addition, Menezes et al evaluated patients with CRT for at least 6 months at baseline, whereas Passos et al used a QRS cutoff of 150 ms, excluding patients with previous ICD implantation. This emphasizes the need for further studies aiming to better individualize and standardize criteria for CRT placement in this neglected patient population.
We observed significantly lower rates of ICD implantation at baseline (39%) than a previous meta-analysis pooling data from landmark clinical trials evaluating CRT for other cardiomyopathies (78%). This discrepancy is likely attributable to financial constraints associated with concomitant defibrillator placement. In Latin America, where the included studies were conducted and CCC is most prevalent, patients are more likely to receive CRT-only devices owing to the limited accessibility to defibrillators. For instance, all included studies were performed in Brazil, where national insurance rules allow for ICD implantation only for secondary prevention owing to possible low cost-effectiveness. This contrasts with populations in developed countries, where studies on other NICM and ICM predominantly report higher rates of ICD implantation. The recent CAGASICS trial demonstrated a significantly reduced risk of SCD, pacing need, and HF hospitalization for patients with CCC compared with amiodarone for primary prevention, which may lead to future policy changes in developing countries.

## Limitations
This study is not without limitations. First, the limited number of studies meeting inclusion criteria underscores the paucity of data on CCC. Second, the retrospective nature of included studies introduces inherent biases, including selection bias and incomplete data reporting. Third, given elevated heterogeneity, the results from our study should be carefully interpreted. Finally, only 2 studies , directly compared patients with CCC and NICM or ICM. This limits our ability to contextualize the magnitude of CRT benefit in CCC relative to other etiologies and likely contributes to between-study heterogeneity and potential biases in interpreting CRT response.

## Conclusion
The results of this systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis provide valuable insights into the efficacy, safety, and outcomes of CRT in CCC. Although improvements in functional status and LVEF seem promising, the overall mortality rate is high, underscoring the complexities and challenges associated with CRT implantation in this unique patient population. Future research should focus on prospectively addressing comparative approaches to further refine the clinical risks and benefits of CRT in patients with CCC.
