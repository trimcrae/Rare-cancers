## Abstract

## Background
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that increases the risk of systemic complications, particularly metabolic syndrome (MetS). MetS, defined by central obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, not only raises cardiovascular risk but also worsens the prognosis of RA. This meta-analysis aimed to estimate the global prevalence of MetS in RA patients and identify clinical factors contributing to its occurrence.

## Methods
A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Studies included in the analysis diagnosed RA and defined MetS using standardized guidelines. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata. The study is registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251007337).

## Results
The overall pooled prevalence of MetS among RA patients was 30.3% (95% CI: 28.5–32.2). Country-specific analyses showed the highest prevalence in Iraq (57.3%; 95% CI: 49.7–66.4), Croatia (49.6%; 95% CI: 35.8–63.3), and Singapore (47.1%; 95% CI: 42.7–51.6), and the lowest in Congo (12.0%; 95% CI: 5.5–20.5), Algeria (14.0%; 95% CI: 10.0–18.7), and South Korea (17.2%; 95% CI: 7.3–30.1). When stratified by continent, the estimates varied noticeably. In Africa, the proportion was 25.7% (95% CI: 21.6–30.0%); in Asia, the estimate rose to 30.8% (95% CI: 27.1–34.6%); Europe recorded a similar figure at 29.8% (95% CI: 26.9–32.7%); North America had an estimate of 31.1% (95% CI: 25.5–36.9%); and South America demonstrated the highest proportion at 38.8% (95% CI: 34.4–43.3%). Meta-regression analyses identified significant associations between MetS prevalence and key clinical variables, including waist circumference (WC) (β = 0.01; P = 0.01), body mass index (BMI) (β = 0.04; P < 0.01), triglycerides (TG) (β < 0.01; P = 0.04), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) (β < 0.01; P < 0.01), with high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels showing an inverse association (β = -0.01; P < 0.01). Among various diagnostic criteria, the highest prevalence estimates were obtained with the National Cholesterol Education Program and International Diabetes Federation (NCEP/IDF) criteria (39.2%; 95% CI: 30.6–48.1), followed by the Joint Consensus (JC) criteria (37.2%; 95% CI: 28.0–46.9) and the 2004 revision of the National Cholesterol Education Program ATP III (NCEP 2004) criteria (35.4%; 95% CI: 29.0–42.0).

## Conclusion
The substantial prevalence of MetS among RA patients underscores the need for a proactive, integrated approach to cardiovascular risk management. Clinicians should consider routine screening for MetS components—such as central obesity, hypertension, dysglycemia, and dyslipidemia—particularly given the significant associations with WC, BMI, TG, and FBG levels.

## Introduction
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disorder primarily affecting the synovial joints, causing pain, swelling, and progressive joint destruction [ , ]. Beyond these direct articular manifestations, RA is associated with a range of systemic complications, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) and metabolic abnormalities [ , ]. Among these, metabolic syndrome (MetS) has emerged as a prevalent and significant comorbidity in RA patients [ , ]. MetS is defined by a cluster of conditions—central obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia—that substantially increase the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [ ].
The mechanisms linking RA and MetS are thought to involve shared inflammatory pathways. Persistent systemic inflammation, marked by elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, contributes to insulin resistance and altered lipid profiles [ , ]. This chronic inflammatory state can also exacerbate endothelial dysfunction, further elevating cardiovascular risk [ , ]. Additionally, patients with RA may experience reduced physical activity due to joint pain and disability, which can worsen the metabolic abnormalities characteristic of MetS [ , ].
Existing literature reveals considerable variation in the reported prevalence of MetS among individuals with RA, with estimates differing across regions and populations. Factors influencing these discrepancies include genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, lifestyle behaviors (such as diet, exercise, and smoking), and therapeutic interventions (e.g., glucocorticoids used in RA management) [ – ]. Understanding the global burden of MetS in RA is crucial not only for guiding clinical management but also for informing public health strategies aimed at reducing the cumulative cardiovascular risk in these patients. Despite the increasing recognition of MetS as a major contributor to morbidity in RA, a comprehensive global synthesis of its prevalence in this population has yet to be undertaken. The current systematic review and meta-analysis seeks to consolidate data from diverse geographic regions to determine the overall prevalence of MetS in patients with RA.

## Methods
Following the PRISMA guidelines and the standards set forth in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews, this meta-analysis was conducted under a protocol registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251007337) [ , ].

## Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was performed to identify articles reporting the prevalence of MetS in patients with RA. Major electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) were searched from inception to February 1, 2025, using keywords such as “Rheumatoid Arthritis,” “Metabolic Syndrome X,” “Insulin Resistance Syndrome X,” and “Metabolic Cardiovascular Syndrome.” No restrictions were applied; relevant non-English articles were translated whenever feasible. Reference lists of selected full-text articles and pertinent reviews were also hand-searched to identify additional studies. Detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Table .

## Study selection and eligibility criteria
Eligible studies involved individuals with RA diagnosed according to recognized classification systems (e.g., ACR/EULAR criteria). MetS had to be clearly defined using a standard set of guidelines (e.g., NCEP-ATP III, IDF), and the study design needed to be observational (e.g., cross-sectional, case-control, or cohort). We excluded reviews, conference abstracts without primary data, commentaries, and any study population that did not explicitly measure MetS in RA. Disagreements regarding eligibility were resolved through discussion. If a consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer’s opinion was sought.

## Quality assessment
To appraise the methodological quality of each included study, we utilized the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists appropriate to the specific study designs (e.g., cross-sectional, cohort) [ – ]. Any disagreements in the quality assessment were resolved by discussion or through consultation with a third reviewer.

## Data extraction
Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form that had been piloted for consistency. The information collected included: (1) study characteristics such as author name, year of publication, geographical location, and study design; (2) participant demographics, including sample size, mean or median age, sex distribution, disease duration, and relevant RA disease activity measures (e.g., DAS28, CRP, ESR); (3) specific criteria used to diagnose MetS; and (4) reported prevalence of MetS or raw data allowing calculation of prevalence. Discrepancies in data extraction were addressed through reviewer discussion; if unresolved, a third reviewer determined the final decision. Where data were incomplete or not reported, we attempted to contact the authors directly to request clarifications or additional information.

## Statistical analyses
All statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 18). The pooled prevalence of MetS in RA was calculated using a random-effects model to account for between-study heterogeneity. We used the Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic to quantify heterogeneity, with I² values above 50% and P < 0.1 considered indicative of high heterogeneity. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored through subgroup analyses and meta-regression. In addition, 95% prediction intervals were calculated to estimate the range in which the true prevalence would be expected to fall in future similar studies. The dependent variable in the meta-regression was the Freeman-Tukey transformed prevalence of metabolic syndrome, a transformation commonly used in meta-analyses of proportions to stabilize variance [ ].

## Results

## Study selection
A comprehensive search across three major databases—PubMed (n = 371), Web of Science (n = 1,000), and Scopus (n = 1,271)—yielded a total of 2,642 records. Following the removal of 557 duplicate entries, 2,085 unique records were subjected to title and abstract screening. The full texts of 206 potentially relevant articles were sought.
Subsequently, 169 full-text articles underwent detailed eligibility assessment. During this phase, 47 studies were excluded for the following reasons: duplication (n = 7), presentation of inadequate or non-extractable data (n = 14), assessment of ineligible subpopulations (n = 11), publication in non-English languages without available translations (n = 4), and unavailability of full texts (n = 11). Ultimately, 122 studies fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final systematic review (Fig.  ).
 

## Study characteristics
This systematic review and meta-analysis included a comprehensive set of studies assessing the global prevalence of MetS in individuals with RA. The studies, published between 2006 and 2025, spanned multiple continents, covering countries from North America (e.g., United States, Mexico, Canada), South America (e.g., Brazil, Argentina), Europe (e.g., Italy, France, Greece, Romania, Turkey), Asia (e.g., China, India, South Korea, Pakistan), as well as countries from the Middle East and North Africa (e.g., Iran, Egypt, Morocco, Algeria, Iraq).
The majority of the study designs were observational, including cross-sectional and cohort studies. There was substantial variation in sample sizes, ranging from fewer than 50 to over 13,000 participants, and in demographic characteristics. The average age of participants ranged from the early thirties to late sixties, with both sexes represented across studies.
RA diagnosis was confirmed using either the ACR criteria or the ACR/EULAR classification criteria. The definition of MetS varied significantly across studies, with diverse diagnostic frameworks employed, including NCEP/ATP III, IDF, World Health Organization (WHO), and less commonly used criteria such as AACE, AHA/NHLBI, and EGIR.
The reported prevalence of MetS among RA patients demonstrated considerable heterogeneity, with estimates ranging from 5.3% to 58.8%. This variability is likely due to differences in population characteristics, study settings, and the diagnostic criteria used for both RA and MetS. Methodological quality, along with regional and temporal trends, were considered in the synthesis and interpretation of the results. A detailed summary of the included studies and their key characteristics is presented in Table  .
 The risk of bias assessment (Table ) indicated that most studies exhibited a low risk of bias, reflecting overall methodological robustness. A smaller subset showed moderate to high risk, primarily due to insufficient reporting and lack of clarity in handling potential confounders.

## Results of meta-analysis

## Prevalence of Mets in individuals with RA in men and women
The overall prevalence of MetS in individuals with RA was 30.3% (95% CI: 28.5–32.2), with substantial heterogeneity across the included analyses (I² = 95.31%, P < 0.01). Among men with RA, the prevalence of MetS was 32.0% (95% CI: 27.5–36.6) with heterogeneity at I² = 86.93% (P < 0.01). Among women with RA, it was 31.8% (95% CI: 29.2–34.6), with I² = 92.59% (P < 0.01). Despite a small difference in prevalence estimates between the sexes, both indicate that approximately one-third of patients with RA fulfill MetS criteria (Table  ).
 

## Prevalence of Mets in individuals with RA among different continents
When stratified by continent, the prevalence of MetS in RA varied considerably. In Africa, it was 25.7% (95% CI: 21.6–30.0) with I² = 87.16% (P < 0.01). In Asia, it was 30.8% (95% CI: 27.1–34.6) with I² = 97.58% (P < 0.01). Europe showed 29.8% (95% CI: 26.9–32.7) with I² = 90.19% (P < 0.01), while North America had 31.1% (95% CI: 25.5–36.9) with I² = 97.34% (P < 0.01). South America had the highest estimate, at 38.8% (95% CI: 34.4–43.3) with I² = 85.52% (P < 0.01). Notably, MetS was least frequent among RA patients in Africa and most frequent in South America (Table  ).

## Prevalence of Mets in individuals RA among different countries
Further analysis at the country level revealed that Iraq had one of the highest MetS rates at 57.3% (95% CI: 49.7–66.4), followed by Croatia at 49.6% (95% CI: 35.8–63.3, I² = 87.89%), Singapore at 47.1% (95% CI: 42.7–51.6, I² = 63.40%), Serbia at 43.1% (95% CI: 21.3–66.4), and Brazil at 40.4% (95% CI: 35.1–45.9, I² = 82.32%). Conversely, the lowest national estimates came from Congo at 12.0% (95% CI: 5.5–20.5), Algeria at 14.0% (95% CI: 10.0–18.7), South Korea at 17.2% (95% CI: 7.3–30.1, I² = 95.97%), the Netherlands at 17.8% (95% CI: 11.7–24.8, I² = 19.05%), and South Africa at 24.2% (95% CI: 17.0–32.1, I² = 91.44%) (Table  ; Fig.  ).
 

## Prevalence of Mets in individuals with RA based on RA criteria
Regarding RA classification criteria, patients diagnosed using the ACR criteria showed a MetS prevalence of 30.4% (95% CI: 28.1–32.8) with I² = 95.58% (P < 0.01), while those assessed by the ACR/EULAR criteria had a prevalence of 29.3% (95% CI: 26.5–32.2) with I² = 92.70% (P < 0.01) (Table  ).

## Prevalence of Mets in individuals with RA based on Mets criteria
Variations emerged based on the specific diagnostic definitions used for MetS. The four criteria with the highest prevalence estimates were NCEP/IDF at 39.2% (95% CI: 30.6–48.1), JC at 37.2% (95% CI: 28.0–46.9) with I² = 81.36% (P < 0.01), NCEP 2004 at 35.4% (95% CI: 29.0–42.0) with I² = 57.28% (P = 0.09), and IDF at 35.2% (95% CI: 30.5–40.0) with I² = 95.95% (P = 0.01). In contrast, the four criteria with the lowest prevalence estimates were EGIR 1999 at 14.3% (95% CI: 10.8–18.1) with I² = 29.80% (P = 0.24), FRS/ATP III at 16.0% (95% CI: 7.0–27.6), NCEP/ATP III/IDF/WHO at 19.0% (95% CI: 17.2–20.9), and WHO at 20.5% (95% CI: 14.5–27.3) with I² = 87.37% (P = 0.01) (Table  ).

## Results of meta-regression analysis
The meta-regression analysis identified five variables with a statistically significant association to the between-study variability in MetS prevalence among RA patients. Specifically, higher waist circumference (WC) (β = 0.01, P = 0.01), body mass index (BMI) (β = 0.04, P < 0.01), triglycerides (TG) (β < 0.01, P = 0.04), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) (β < 0.01, P < 0.01) were positively correlated with MetS prevalence, whereas high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (β = -0.01, P < 0.01) showed an inverse relationship (Table  ; Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
This meta-analysis demonstrates that MetS affects approximately one-third (30.3%) of patients with RA worldwide, with substantial geographic and demographic variability. Among continents, South America exhibited the highest prevalence (38.8%), while Africa had the lowest (25.7%). At the country level, Iraq (57.3%), Croatia (49.6%), and Singapore (47.1%) showed the highest MetS prevalence, whereas Congo (12.0%) and Algeria (14.0%) reported the lowest rates. Prevalence was similar between sexes, with 32.0% in men and 31.8% in women. Meta-regression analysis identified WC, BMI, TG, and FBG as key determinants of MetS prevalence, while HDL levels exhibited an inverse association. Additionally, prevalence varied according to the diagnostic criteria applied. The NCEP/IDF (39.2%), JC (37.2%), and NCEP 2004 (35.4%) criteria yielded the highest prevalence estimates, while the EGIR 1999 (14.3%) and FRS/ATP III (16.0%) criteria reported the lowest. These findings highlight the significant burden of MetS in RA patients and underscore the need for standardized diagnostic criteria and integrated metabolic and cardiovascular risk management strategies to enhance clinical outcomes in this population (Fig.  ).
 The findings of this study align with recent research indicating a significantly increased prevalence of MetS among patients with RA. A meta-analysis by Cai et al., analyzing 61 studies with 13,644 RA patients, estimated a pooled MetS prevalence of 32% (95% CI: 29.6–34.4%), with the highest rates in Asia and Europe (32.7%) and the lowest in Africa (28%). The study also highlighted variations based on diagnostic criteria, with ATP III yielding the highest prevalence (37.5%) and EGIR the lowest (14.4%). Although the difference between our pooled prevalence estimate (30.3%) and the estimate from Cai et al. (32%) is not significant, it can be attributed to several factors. These include differences in geographical stratification, diagnostic criteria, the inclusion of more recent research in our study, and possibly the use of different statistical methods [ ]. Similarly, Loganathan et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, reporting a pooled MetS prevalence of 31% (95% CI: 27–35%) in RA patients. These findings reinforce the substantial metabolic burden in RA and highlight the need for standardized diagnostic criteria to improve risk assessment and clinical management [ ].
Beyond the overall prevalence, the association between MetS and adverse clinical outcomes in RA is well documented. Santos-Moreno et al. emphasized that MetS significantly exacerbates CVD risk, which remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in RA patients [ ]. Chronic systemic inflammation in RA is thought to accelerate metabolic disturbances, further amplifying CVD risk. This underscores the need for early identification and targeted preventive strategies to mitigate long-term complications.
Supporting these findings, Bagheri-Hosseinabadi et al. reported a notably high MetS prevalence (58.8%) among Iranian RA patients [ ], while Baker et al. identified a 29% prevalence in a US-based cohort, where MetS negatively affected treatment response [ ]. Hee et al. found a MetS prevalence of 49.4% in a multi-ethnic Singaporean RA cohort, noting its strong association with increased risks of stroke and ischemic heart disease, independent of RA disease activity [ ].
Collectively, these studies confirm that MetS is not only highly prevalent in RA but also a critical contributor to cardiovascular complications. The variation in prevalence estimates across populations and diagnostic criteria underscores the urgent need for universal MetS classification and screening protocols. Given the heightened CVD risk in RA patients, integrating routine MetS screening and preventive interventions into RA management is imperative [ – ].
The association between RA and MetS arises from a complex interplay of chronic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, lifestyle factors, and genetic predisposition. As a chronic autoimmune disease, RA induces persistent inflammation that significantly affects insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, and endothelial function, increasing cardiometabolic risk.
Our meta-regression analysis revealed significant associations between MetS prevalence and several key clinical factors, including BMI, WC, TG, FBG, HDL. These factors were found to significantly contribute to the variability in MetS prevalence among RA patients, highlighting their importance as modifiable determinants of MetS in this population [ – ].
BMI and WC were positively associated with MetS prevalence, underscoring the critical role of obesity in driving metabolic abnormalities in RA. Increased adiposity, particularly abdominal obesity, has been linked to higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate insulin resistance and contribute to cardiovascular risk in RA patients [ – ]. Monitoring and managing weight and abdominal fat may, therefore, be essential for reducing MetS-related complications in this population. Studies have shown that central obesity is not only a risk factor for MetS but also a predictor of poor cardiovascular outcomes, further supporting the need for targeted weight management strategies in RA patients [ – ].
Triglyceride levels and FBG were also identified as key determinants, reflecting the relationship between dyslipidemia and dysglycemia with MetS in RA. These findings align with previous research showing that RA patients often experience altered lipid metabolism and insulin resistance, which can lead to an increased risk of CVD [ , , ]. Our results confirm that elevated triglyceride levels and impaired glucose metabolism are important contributors to MetS, suggesting that interventions aimed at managing lipid profiles and blood glucose levels are critical in reducing the risk of CVD in this population.
Interestingly, HDL levels showed an inverse relationship with MetS, reinforcing the protective role of high HDL cholesterol levels in preventing metabolic dysfunction and CVD. The inverse association of HDL with MetS is well-established in the literature, where low levels of HDL have been linked to higher cardiovascular risk [ , , ]. Strategies to increase HDL levels, such as through lifestyle changes or pharmacological interventions, may offer protective benefits in this high-risk group [ , ]. As RA patients are already at an elevated risk for cardiovascular events, targeting HDL cholesterol could be an important part of metabolic and cardiovascular disease management in this population.
Given these significant associations, it is crucial for clinicians to monitor these metabolic parameters—BMI, WC, TG, FBG, and HDL—regularly in RA patients. Targeting these factors through lifestyle modifications, such as dietary changes and increased physical activity, and pharmacologic treatments may offer an integrated approach to reduce the incidence of MetS and associated cardiovascular risks.
The high prevalence of MetS in patients with RA has significant clinical implications, particularly given the strong association between MetS and CVD risk. The presence of MetS in RA patients not only amplifies cardiovascular morbidity but also contributes to higher rates of disability and overall disease burden [ , ]. As such, early detection and timely intervention for MetS are crucial in reducing long-term complications and improving patient outcomes.
Routine metabolic screening should be a fundamental part of clinical care for RA patients, especially given the modifiable nature of key MetS components such as obesity, dyslipidemia, and impaired glucose metabolism. Given the findings of our study, it is recommended that clinicians closely monitor metabolic risk factors such as WC, BMI, TG, FBG, and HDL. This will allow for early identification of patients at risk of MetS, facilitating prompt interventions. There is a need for an integrated approach to managing both inflammation and metabolic health in RA patients. While managing inflammation through disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs is central to RA treatment, addressing metabolic dysfunction is also important. A multidisciplinary strategy involving rheumatologists, cardiologists, and endocrinologists could help optimize clinical care and reduce the high cardiovascular risk faced by RA patients with MetS.

## Limitations and future directions
The present meta-analysis is subject to several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies, stemming from variations in study design, patient populations, and diagnostic criteria for both RA and MetS. Despite performing various subgroup and meta-regression analyses to explore potential sources of this heterogeneity, we were unable to identify clear contributing factors. The I² statistic remained above 50%, indicating considerable variation between studies. This heterogeneity may limit the generalizability of the pooled estimates. Several factors may contribute to the observed heterogeneity, including comorbid diseases, differences in treatment regimens, lifestyle factors (such as diet and physical activity), and genetic and environmental influences. Variations in these factors across different populations may have contributed to the discrepancies in prevalence estimates. Future research should focus on exploring these potential sources of variability in greater detail. In addition, potential publication bias and the variability in the reporting of clinical variables (e.g., WC, BMI, lipid profiles) may have influenced the observed associations. Future research should prioritize large-scale, prospective studies that employ standardized diagnostic criteria and uniform reporting standards. Furthermore, additional investigations into the mechanistic links between chronic inflammation in RA and metabolic dysregulation are warranted. Such studies could help refine targeted interventions and inform strategies to reduce cardiovascular risk in this vulnerable population.

## Conclusion
This meta-analysis demonstrates that approximately one-third of patients with RA globally are affected by metabolic syndrome, with marked differences observed across countries and continents. The significant associations identified through meta-regression—particularly those related to WC, BMI, TG, and FBS—underscore the pivotal role of metabolic factors in exacerbating cardiovascular risk among RA patients. These findings highlight the pressing need for integrated management approaches that address both inflammatory and metabolic components in RA. Moving forward, standardizing diagnostic criteria and enhancing the quality of future studies will be critical for developing effective, targeted strategies to mitigate the burden of MetS in RA.
