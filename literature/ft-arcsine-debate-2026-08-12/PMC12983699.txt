## Purpose
In pediatric emergency departments (PEDs), procedural sedation is frequently used to reduce pain and anxiety to support therapeutic and diagnostic procedures. The purpose of this meta-analysis was to evaluate the safety profiles of common sedatives in the setting of PEDs.

## Methods
An extensive search of four databases was conducted to identify studies reporting adverse effects associated with procedural sedation in pediatric emergency settings. Studies that reported on sedation-related outcomes and involved patients younger than 18 were considered eligible. A proportionate meta-analysis was conducted employing random-effects model.

## Results
Sixty-six trials with a total of 20,043 pediatric patients were included. Propofol had the highest incidence of apnea (39%) and ketamine had the lowest (14%). The combined incidence of apnea was 19% (95% CI: 15–23%). 36% of patients (95% CI: 31–41%) had hypoxia; the highest rates were seen with etomidate (83%) and dexmedetomidine (56%), while the lowest rates were seen with nitrous oxide (9%), and chloral hydrate (11%). 40% of instances had agitation, which was most common with regimens based on midazolam. Among the secondary adverse events were nausea (44%) and vomiting (55%). Higher rates of apnea and hypoxia were noted in children above the age of 3.

## Conclusion
While nitrous oxide and chloral hydrate showed better safety profiles, propofol was linked to the highest risk of respiratory complications. These results highlight the significance of further research to improve sedation protocols and support the necessity of agent-specific, patient-centered sedation techniques in pediatric emergency care.

## Introduction
Procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) is defined as the administration of sedative, anxiolytic, or analgesic agents to reduce a patient’s awareness and discomfort while preserving spontaneous ventilation and protective reflexes [ ]. In pediatric emergency departments (PEDs), PSA plays a crucial role in alleviating pre-procedural anxiety, which affects up to 77% of children [ ], and enables the successful completion of essential interventions such as fracture reductions, abscess drainage, and diagnostic imaging [ , ]. Its use is widespread, with studies reporting sedation rates as high as 49% in some PEDs [ ] and over 30,000 procedures performed annually [ ]. Despite its benefits, PSA is associated with significant risks. Adverse events occur in nearly 12% of cases, including agitation, bradycardia, apnea, and oxygen desaturation (SpO₂ < 90%) [ , ]. The variability in reported complications—ranging from mild, transient effects to life-threatening respiratory depression—reflects differences in sedation protocols, drug selection, and patient factors [ – ]. Midazolam, commonly used for anxiolysis, is linked to paradoxical agitation, respiratory depression, and post-procedural behavioral disturbances [ – ]. On the other hand, ketamine, while effective for painful procedures, carries risks of nausea, vomiting, and emergence dysphoria [ ]. Nitrous oxide, though useful for brief procedures, may cause nausea and headache, limiting its utility [ ]. Combination regimens (e.g., ketamine-propofol) offer balanced sedation but require careful dosing to minimize adverse effects [ ].
The PED environment poses unique challenges, including the need for rapid sedation in high-acuity cases and the management of children with varying ages, weights, and comorbidities [ , , ]. Younger patients often require deeper sedation due to limited cooperation, whereas older children may benefit from anxiolytics combined with psychological support [ , , ]. Current guidelines emphasize individualized approaches, yet institutional variability in protocols contributes to inconsistent safety outcomes [ , ].
Given the lack of consensus on optimal sedation strategies, a systematic comparison of adverse event profiles is essential. This meta-analysis aims to evaluate the safety of individual and combination sedatives used in pediatric procedural sedation, analysing the incidence of different adverse events. This will aid in providing evidence-based guidance to optimize sedation practices and reduce complications.

## Methodology
This meta-analysis follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ] and in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis of Interventions [ ].

## Protocol
Based on a thorough initial literature search and following the PRISMA guidelines, investigators (M.A. and A.S.) created a review protocol and a search strategy following the Patient-Intervention-Control-Outcome (PICO) framework. The protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO (CRD42024539772).

## Data sources and search strategy
A comprehensive search strategy was employed to identify relevant studies comparing various interventions used for procedural sedation anesthesia among pediatric patients admitted to the emergency department. Databases including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and Scopus were systematically searched for all articles published since inception till 6th August, 2025. Keywords such as “procedural sedation,” “conscious sedation,” “anti-anxiety drugs,” and “emergency medicine” were used in conjunction with Boolean operators to refine search results. Detailed search strategy is enclosed in Supplementary Table . References of included studies were also screened for additional relevant articles.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Population: Participants above the age of 28 days and under the age of 18 undergoing PSA, exclusively in the emergency department for multiple medical procedures like fracture reductions, laceration repairs, chest tube insertions, electric cardioversion, abscess incision and drainage, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and feeding tube replacement among others; (2) Intervention: Use of any anaesthetic agent for PSA like esketamine, etomidate, fentanyl, ketamine, midazolam, propofol through any routes like intravenous, intramuscular, intranasal or oral administration; (3) Control: use of placebo, control or blank; Outcome: Primary outcomes include risk of hypoxia or desaturation and apnea. Secondary outcomes include agitation, risk of aspiration, bradycardia, hypotension, bag valve mask ventilation, intubation, laryngospasm and myoclonus among others.; Study design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), trials or cohort studies comparing different sedation anesthesia interventions.
Studies were excluded if they: (1) Included patients above 18 years, i.e., adult population, or neonates, i.e., below 28 days of age; (2) Case reports, case series, snapshot studies like cross-sectional design, case-control studies in order to pool studies of the highest evidence; (3) Secondary analysis and non-original studies like narrative reviews, meta-analyses, systematic reviews; and (4) studies not published in English language, to allow for accurate interpretability of data and conclusions.

## Data selection and screening
Rayyan was used to remove duplicates followed by the primary title and abstract screening. Further, a second screening evaluating full texts of potentially included studies was conducted in an Excel sheet by two reviewers (J.C. & R.S.). Any discrepancies and conflicts were resolved by a senior author (M.A.).

## Data extraction
Two independent reviewers extracted data using a standardized form (X.X., Y.Y.). Key data extracted included study characteristics (author, publication year, place of study, study design, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, time points of follow-up, conclusion), participant characteristics (age, gender, weight, indication for sedation, presence of any lacerations, duration of procedure, baseline vitals like pulse rate, respiratory rate, mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation and pre-procedure sedation score), intervention details (drug used, dose, route of administration, use of concurrent medications), and outcomes (time to onset of sedation, successful airway placement, adverse events like apnea, agitation, risk of aspiration, bradycardia, hypotension, bag valve mask ventilation, hypoxia/desaturation, intubation, laryngospasm and myoclonus among others. Definitions of adverse events varied across studies. Apnea was often described as cessation of breathing requiring stimulation or airway support, but only a few studies specified duration (e.g., > 20 s). Similarly, hypoxia/desaturation thresholds were heterogeneous, most frequently defined as SpO₂ <90%, although some studies used < 92%, < 93%, or a ≥ 5% decrease from baseline. Because uniform definitions were lacking, we extracted adverse events as reported by each study and analyzed them as dichotomous outcomes. Although dosing information was extracted when available, reporting was inconsistent across studies and therefore unsuitable for quantitative pooling. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (M.A.).

## Quality assessment
The quality of the included studies was assessed by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias-2 (RoB-2) tool for RCTs [ ], Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) for non-randomized trials [ ], and Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies [ ]. Under RoB-2, domains such as bias in the randomization process, deviation from intended interventions, missing outcomes, measurement of outcome, and selection of reported results were evaluated. Each study was rated as high, with some concerns, or low risk of bias. Utilizing ROBINS-I, studies were analyzed under seven applicable domains, and graded as “serious”, “moderate” and “low” risk of bias. Utilizing the NOS tool for cohort studies, records were analysed under the broad domains of selection, comparability, and outcome. Studies with a score of < 7 were deemed as “poor” quality, while those with a score of 7 or above were deemed as “high” quality. Discrepancies and disagreements were resolved through consultation with the senior author (M.A.).

## Data synthesis and analysis
All variables were analyzed using a single-arm proportion meta-analysis framework. For each sedative agent, the number of events and the total number of patients were extracted to calculate pooled incidence estimates. We synthesized outcomes as pooled proportions with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).
Analyses were conducted using the meta package in R (version 4.3.0) [ ]. A random-effects model was selected a priori due to expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in patient age, procedural indications, sedation protocols, dosing practices, and outcome definitions. The restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator was used to quantify between-study variance, offering more conservative estimates than fixed-effect models. For outcomes with very high or very low event rates (close to 0% or 100%), or when studies reported rare adverse events, we applied the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variances before pooling. This transformation improves statistical robustness for proportion meta-analyses and preserves interpretability after back-transformation. Funnel plots were constructed using transformed effect sizes and their standard errors to evaluate for small-study effects.
Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, with values > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. When high I² values were observed, sources of heterogeneity were explored using subgroup analyses stratified by sedative agent and by age group (< 3 years vs. ≥3 years). To further assess dispersion and study contribution to heterogeneity, Galbraith plots were generated. To evaluate the stability of pooled estimates, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for each primary outcome. In cases where funnel plot asymmetry was visually evident, Egger’s regression test was conducted to assess small-study effects; when publication bias was suggested, trim-and-fill procedures were applied to generate adjusted estimates. All analyses used two-tailed significance thresholds (α = 0.05). Results are reported as pooled proportions with 95% CIs, stratified by sedative agent and relevant clinical subgroups.

## Results

## Study selection and characteristics
Our systematic search yielded 25,339 records, from which 66 studies involving 20,043 pediatric patients were included after full screening and eligibility assessment (Fig.  ). These studies encompassed 31 randomized controlled trials, 16 cohort studies, two quasi-experimental trials, and 17 observational designs.

 

Participant ages ranged from infancy to adolescence (mean 7.1 ± 5.8 years), with follow-up durations ranging from 60 min to three weeks. Procedures included orthopedic reductions, laceration repairs, and diagnostic imaging. Sedation regimens varied widely in agent, dosage, and route of administration, contributing to the clinical heterogeneity of the dataset. Complete study characteristics are provided in Supplementary Tables and . Table  summarizes major adverse events across all drugs.

 

## Quality assessment of included studies
The methodological quality of the evidence was mixed. Among RCTs, 32% (10/31) were rated low risk of bias using RoB-2, while 19% (6/31) were classified as high risk. The majority of cohort and non-randomized studies were graded moderate to serious risk using ROBINS-I. Observational studies generally met acceptable thresholds on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, with 75% (12/16) scoring ≥ 7. Although quality varied, no studies were excluded based on risk of bias alone. Sensitivity analyses stratified by risk did not materially alter pooled estimates. A detailed breakdown of the ROB-2, ROBINS-II, and NOS assessments is provided in Supplementary Figs.  – , Supplementary Table .

## Primary safety outcomes

## Apnea
The pooled incidence of apnea, across 9,242 patients from 42 studies, was 19% (95% CI: 15–23%; I² = 55.9%, p < 0.001), underscoring its clinical relevance in pediatric procedural sedation. Propofol was associated with the highest apnea rate (0.39 [0.19–0.60]), followed by ketamine/midazolam (0.25) and ketamine/propofol (0.23). The lowest rates were observed with ketamine alone (0.14 [0.09–0.19]) and midazolam alone (0.14 [0.01–0.27]). Although between-group differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.26), the magnitude of variation supports meaningful pharmacologic distinctions (Fig.  A). Subgrouping based on age revealed a significantly higher incidence of apnea in those aged above 3 (0.21 [0.16–0.25]), than those below 3 years of age (0.17 [0.05, 0.29] (Fig.  B))

 

Galbraith plot analysis identified seven studies contributing to between-study heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig.  ). Asymmetry on the funnel plot and a significant Egger’s test (p = 0.0018) suggest potential small-study effects (Supplementary Fig.  ). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed stability, indicating no single study unduly influenced the overall estimate (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Hypoxia
Hypoxia was reported in 54 studies involving 15,216 patients, with a pooled incidence of 36% (95% CI: 31–41%; I² = 85.6%, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis demonstrated statistically significant variation by agent (p < 0.001). Etomidate (0.83 [0.25–1.41]) and esketamine (0.81 [0.31–1.31]) were associated with markedly higher hypoxia rates, while nitrous oxide (0.09 [0.05–0.14]) and chloral hydrate (0.11 [0.04–0.18]) demonstrated the most favorable respiratory safety profiles (Fig.  A). Subgroup based on age groups revealed a significantly higher > 3 years (0.40 [0.34–0.47]) than those below < 3 years (0.26 [0.18–0.33]) (Fig.  B).

 

Heterogeneity was explored through meta-regression and visualized using Galbraith plots (Supplementary Fig.  ). Publication bias was detected via funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression (p = 0.0014) (Supplementary Fig.  ). A leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that no single study substantially impacted the overall hypoxia proportion (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Need for bag-mask ventilation or positive pressure ventilation and intubation
Need for bag-mask ventilation/positive pressure ventilation was assessed by 13 studies involving 2,292 patients, with a pooled incidence of 15% (95%CI: 9–22%; I2 = 43.92%; p = 0.04) (Fig.  ). Need for intubation was assessed by 16 studies with 2,130 patients. A pooled incidence of 8% (95%CI: 4–12%; I2 = 0%; P = 0.94) was noted (Fig.  ).

 

 

## Secondary safety outcomes
Agitation was reported in 29 studies and occurred in 40% of patients (95% CI: 33–47%). The highest rates were observed with midazolam monotherapy (0.53 [0.28–0.78]) and ketamine/midazolam (0.59 [0.28–0.91]), suggesting potential benzodiazepine-related paradoxical reactions. The ketamine/propofol combination had the lowest incidence (0.16 [0.12–0.20]) (Fig.  ).

 

Other adverse events included vomiting (55% [0.50–0.61]; I² = 91.3%), nausea (44% [0.34–0.55]; I² = 84.4%), myoclonus (40% [0.23–0.58]), hypotension (26% [0.16–0.36]), and bradycardia (25% [0.16–0.35]). Serious airway complications were less frequent but clinically significant: intubation (8% [0.04–0.12]), laryngospasm (10% [0.08–0.12]), and aspiration (4% [0.01–0.06]). The need for positive pressure ventilation occurred in 15% of patients, while oral airway placement was required in 19% (Supplementary Figs.  – ). Most outcomes showed high between-study heterogeneity, reflecting underlying differences in patient populations, sedation protocols, and outcome definitions.

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis represents one of the most comprehensive evaluations to date of adverse events associated with PSA in PEDs. The most common adverse events identified were vomiting (55%) and hypoxia (36%), followed by nausea, myoclonus, and agitation. More severe respiratory complications like apnea (19%), laryngospasm (10%), aspiration (4%), and the need for intubation (8%) occurred less frequently but pose substantial clinical concerns. Among the drugs, Propofol carried the highest risk of apnea (39%) and hypoxia, while the risk of agitation was minimal. While Ketamine and Midazolam carried a low risk of severe respiratory complications like apnea (14% each), rates of hypoxia and agitation were higher. Nitrous oxide and chloral hydrate were considered to have the least risk of respiratory complications like apnea or hypoxia.
The risk of vomiting was notably the highest following procedural sedation, occurring in over half (55%) of all recipients. A previous meta-analysis by Bellolio et al. noted similar findings, with the incidence of vomiting at 55.5% [ ]. Multiple key factors may influence the risk of vomiting; one of the most important being the choice of agent. Siripermpool et al. noted the incidence of vomiting following the use of chloral hydrate to be at 20.45% [ ]. On the other hand, Suryaprakash et al. noted a much lower incidence following the use of IM ketamine at 8.4% [ ]. The incidence of vomiting following ketamine may vary; Motamed et al., noted an incidence of 14.54%, which reduced to 13.46% on increasing the dose from 3 to 5 mg/kg [ ]. This variability across studies as well as the heterogeneity in our analysis emphasizes other key factors influencing the risk of vomiting; an age > 8 years was associated with higher rates of emesis [ ]. The addition of ondansetron can significantly reduce the risk of vomiting [ ]. The Royal College of Emergency Medicine guidelines highlight the risk of vomiting following use of ketamine to be at 7–10%, and intractable vomiting necessitates the use of IV ondansetron [ ]. Hence, preprocedural medication, along with variability in drug dosage, duration of procedure, and age of patients, may significantly influence the risk of vomiting and may necessitate prior consideration for choosing a suitable sedative and analgesic agent. Further, apnea was noted in 19% of the patients. Of all drugs, propofol carried the highest risk of apnea. Propofol is known as a deep sedative, and the action may result in apnea [ ]. Kim et al. reported a high risk of respiratory complications following the use of propofol in pediatric population [ ]. A similar conclusion was noted in the study by Li et al., where propofol resulted in a 3.5 times higher risk of apnea [ ]. The sedative dose of propofol is shown to reduce tidal volume and further reduce the inspiratory cycle [ ]. Studies suggest that propofol results in a prolonged activated state of GABA receptors, resulting in hyperpolarization and synaptic transmission inhibition, which further contributed to CNS depression [ ]. On the other hand, Li BD et al. noted propofol to reduce the risk of laryngospasm and agitation, particularly in patients receiving laryngeal mask airway [ ]. These findings suggest the need for a careful evaluation between the protective effect of propofol against vomiting and its respiratory complications. In healthy children below 3 years of age, Hong et al. demonstrated a safe profile of propofol, with no elevated risks noted for respiratory complications [ ]. Thus, key factors influencing the incidence of apnea following propofol use may be the underlying pathology of the child and the age of children. This suggests a careful evaluation of the pre-procedure respiratory status of a patient prior to the use of propofol.
We also noted that ketamine with midazolam increases the risk of apnea. This may be due to the higher overall dosage of benzodiazepines, leading to pronounced respiratory depression. Meta-analysis by Green et al. also noted a higher prevalence of apnea in ketamine combination doses [ ]. Ketamine+propofol regimens, while noted for the better hemodynamic profile offered, also result in longer durations of apnea [ ]. Ketamine-only regimens and midazolam-only regimens were the safest drugs according to our analysis. Ketamine is a respiratory stimulant and has shown to diminish the connection between loss-of-consciousness and apnea [ ]. Lower doses of ketamine may result in apnea [ ], however its efficacy in children remains largely safe. On contrary, midazolam has been linked with a higher risk of apnea in a few studies; Bosson et al. noted a higher risk of apnea among children with seizures who were administered midazolam in an out-of-hospital emergency setting [ ]. Midazolam overdose is known to cause cardiorespiratory depression in children undergoing surgeries, which gets potentiated on its addition with other sedatives [ ]. The incidence of apnea and respiratory depression following midazolam is largely dose-related [ ] and based on the route of administration; midazolam oral solution has preliminarily shown efficacy as a preprocedural anxiolytic without resulting in any adverse events; a dose up to 0.8 mg/kg may be considered safe in children [ ]. Age-wide subgroups revealed a significantly elevated risk of apnea and hypoxia among those above 3 years of age. This way result from increased awareness and anxiety of older children due to cognitive awareness. Further the higher doses used in older children may increase the rates of adverse events. Older children may undergo more lengthy or complex procedures, which may also result in the higher adverse events [ ].
Incidence of hypoxia was noted to be 36% with disproportionately higher incidences on use of etomidate, dexmedetomidine and esketamine. Previous meta-analysis had similar conclusions, with 35 cases of 122 suffering from hypoxia on use of etomidate [ ]. Etomidate has been shown to suppress adrenocortical performance in children acutely, which may impair stress response and result in hypoxia [ ]. Studies have suggested a moderately high risk of hypoxia on the use of dexmedetomidine [ ]. Hypoxia may also stem from the drug’s cardiac effect, leading to bradycardia or hypotension, which may blunt perfusion and oxygenation. For esketamine, a study conducted in children with Rett syndrome noted higher incidences of respiratory depression and oxygen desaturation [ ]. Chloral hydrate and nitrous oxide were noted to be the safest agents. Previous studies have noted in safety of chloral hydrate in pediatric cardiovascular intensive care units [ ]. The low threshold of action and mild sedation offered by the drug results in lower incidences of respiratory depression. Babl et al. also noted the safety of nitrous oxide, with only mild adverse events with no long-term sequelae [ ]. Following use of nitrous oxide, saturation of oxygen remains fairly within normal limits, further suggesting its safety over respiration [ ].
Risk of agitation was highest with ketamine/midazolam and midazolam, whereas the lowest risk was noted with ketamine-only. The higher risk noted in midazolam combinations may be from paradoxical excitement [ ]. The high heterogeneity may have influenced the outcome, and further investigation needs to be done to evaluate the safety of midazolam across all age groups.
In our analysis, the pooled incidence of bag-mask/positive-pressure ventilation (PPV) was 15%, and intubation occurred in 8%, rates that are notably higher than those reported in large multicenter datasets. The Pediatric Sedation Research Consortium (PSRC) found PPV in 0.5% of sedations and intubation in only 0.04% across > 49,000 encounters [ ]. Similarly, Bellolio et al.’s systematic review reported major airway interventions in < 1% of cases [ ]. Several factors likely account for the higher pooled estimates in our study: many included trials used deeper sedative regimens (propofol or ketamine–propofol), involved more painful or prolonged procedures, and applied variable thresholds for defining or reporting PPV and intubation. Differences in monitoring standards (e.g., inconsistent capnography use) and provider experience can also increase or decrease detected event rates across studies [ , ]. Clinically, these findings indicate that while many apnea or desaturation episodes are transient, a non-trivial subset may require active airway support, highlighting the importance of experienced personnel, appropriate monitoring, and careful agent selection in pediatric emergency sedation. Future studies should adopt standardized definitions of ventilatory rescue to enable more reliable comparisons.
Although our pooled analysis showed higher rates of apnea and hypoxia in children ≥ 3 years compared with those < 3 years, this result should be interpreted cautiously. This pattern is contrary to many single-center PED reports where infants and toddlers have higher complication rates. Children ≥ 3 years may be more likely to receive deeper sedatives (e.g., propofol or combination regimens) [ ], to undergo longer or more invasive procedures, or to be treated in settings where thresholds for reporting or intervening (for example: initiating PPV or intubation) differed [ ]. Thus, age in our meta-analysis may be a surrogate for other factors (agent choice, procedure complexity, dosing, or monitoring differences) that increase respiratory risk, rather than a direct protective effect of younger age. In addition, inconsistent reporting of denominators and procedure types limits our ability to fully adjust for these confounders. We encourage future primary studies to report age-specific event counts, agent-specific dosing, and procedure types to enable more definitive age-adjusted risk estimates.
In recent years, remimazolam, a novel ultra–short-acting benzodiazepine, has gained attention for pediatric sedation. Early randomized studies and case reports suggest rapid onset, predictable recovery, and a potentially favorable safety profile compared with midazolam [ – ]. A recent study demonstrated effective sedation with faster recovery and fewer respiratory events [ ]. However, the current use of remimazolam remains largely restricted to pre-operative and controlled perioperative settings rather than acute emergency contexts, which was the focus of our analysis. While promising, its evidence base remains limited, heterogeneous in quality, and not yet generalizable to emergency departments. Future systematic reviews should incorporate remimazolam as more high-quality trials emerge, to clarify its role and safety in pediatric procedural sedation.

## Strengths and limitations
The study represents a comprehensive analysis of all procedural sedatives used in children. However, it is not without its limitations; the study failed to resolve heterogeneity across studies despite subgroup analyses. We were unable to perform age-stratified or procedure-specific subgroup analyses due to insufficient reporting in primary studies. The lack of uniform definitions for key outcomes (e.g., hypoxia thresholds, time frame for vomiting, recovery benchmarks) introduces potential misclassification bias. Furthermore, high between-study heterogeneity limits the precision of pooled estimates, although rigorous sensitivity and leave-one-out analyses confirmed the stability of the core findings. Remimazolam, an emerging procedural sedative, was excluded due to a lack of studies in emergency settings. Finally, the inability to assess time-to-recovery, procedure success, or patient/family satisfaction restricts the scope of our safety-focused analysis but highlights critical domains for future research.
Dose-related heterogeneity represents an important limitation of this review. Although sedative dose is a key determinant of respiratory and hemodynamic adverse events, particularly for agents with wide therapeutic windows such as propofol and midazolam, the included studies reported dosing inconsistently, using varied units, titration protocols, or broad ranges without providing analyzable subgroup data. Several trials did not report exact doses at all. As a result, we were unable to perform dose-response, dose-stratified, or weight-normalized analyses. This lack of standardized dosing information may partly contribute to the high between-study heterogeneity observed in outcomes such as apnea, hypoxia, and agitation. Future studies should use uniform dose reporting standards to enable meta-analytic assessment of dose–effect relationships and to better characterize safe therapeutic thresholds in pediatric procedural sedation.
A key limitation of this meta-analysis is the heterogeneity in definitions of primary adverse events across included studies. Apnea was variably defined, ranging from qualitative descriptions (need for airway repositioning or stimulation) to duration-based criteria (> 20 s), which were used inconsistently. Similarly, hypoxia/desaturation definitions differed, with thresholds ranging from SpO₂ <90% to < 93% or relative drops from baseline. This inconsistency limits direct comparability across trials and likely contributed to the substantial between-study heterogeneity observed in our pooled estimates. Standardized reporting of respiratory adverse events is essential for improving interpretability and enabling dose- or age-specific risk quantification in future work.
A major limitation affecting the validity and generalisability of our pooled estimates is the marked clinical and methodological heterogeneity among primary studies. Different procedural contexts (for example: brief non-invasive imaging versus prolonged, painful procedures such as fracture reduction) carry distinct baseline risks for respiratory depression and hemodynamic instability; pooling across these fundamentally different procedures will therefore yield incidence estimates that are averages of heterogeneous event-risk states rather than measures applicable to any single procedural setting [ ]. For example, deeper sedative strategies (more common for lengthy or painful procedures) are expected to produce higher rates of apnea, hypoxia, and escalation to positive-pressure ventilation than brief, minimally invasive sedation—so a dataset with a larger proportion of fracture-reduction studies will bias pooled incidence upward relative to a dataset dominated by procedural imaging [ ].
Differences in sedation protocols and adjunctive medications (agent selection, bolus vs. titration, co-administration of opioids or benzodiazepines) will change the dose-exposure profile and therefore the observed adverse-event rates [ ]. Similarly, monitoring standards materially affect detection: studies using continuous capnography detect hypoventilation and apnea earlier and more frequently than studies relying on pulse oximetry alone, which can both increase observed event rates (detection bias) and reduce clinically significant progression by enabling earlier intervention [ ]. Randomized and observational data indicate that capnography improves early detection of respiratory depression and can reduce the incidence of oxygen desaturation when used to guide management. Thus, heterogeneous use of capnography across studies will both alter measured incidence and change the proportion of events that progress to require airway support [ ]. Provider factors and institutional practices (anesthesiologist-led vs. emergency-physician-led sedation teams, local airway-management thresholds, and training) further influence both the threshold to report an event and the threshold to escalate care (e.g., start PPV or intubate). Although some consortium data suggest no large difference in adjusted major complication rates by provider specialty, local staffing, training, and escalation policies are plausible sources of systematic variation that will affect pooled rates and between-study heterogeneity [ ].
Despite a number of national and institutional guidelines for PSA in children—including those from the PIER Network (UK) [ ], the Royal College of Emergency Medicine [ ], and the American College of Emergency Physicians [ ]—there remains considerable heterogeneity in practice, agent selection, and monitoring standards. Our findings support the need for more tailored and risk-stratified approaches to pediatric PSA, grounded in both pharmacologic evidence and procedural context. No single agent is universally safe or effective; rather, sedation must be individualized based on procedure type, patient comorbidities, anticipated duration, and available resources. Further studies are needed to characterize the efficacy based on their use, whether diagnostic or therapeutic, or for surgical procedures; this can help develop more targeted and personalized approaches for sedatives. Incorporation of emerging sedatives like remimazolam, either as a single agent or in combination, can help provide a direct comparison to existing regimens. Randomized trials evaluating the comparative efficacy of these agents are essential to generate the highest evidence.

## Conclusion
This meta-analysis highlights meaningful differences in the safety profiles of sedative agents used for pediatric procedural sedation in emergency settings. While propofol-based and combination regimens were associated with higher risks of apnea and hypoxia, agents such as nitrous oxide, chloral hydrate, and ketamine demonstrated more favorable safety margins. These findings reinforce the need for individualized, context-sensitive sedation strategies that balance procedural requirements with patient-specific risk. Future high-quality, comparative trials—stratified by age, procedure type, and comorbidities—are essential to guide evidence-based pediatric sedation practices and improve both safety and efficiency in emergency care.
