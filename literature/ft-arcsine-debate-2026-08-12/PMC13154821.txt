## Abstract
Pneumonia is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in Ghana, especially among children <5 y of age and the elderly. However, comprehensive data on its prevalence and distribution remain limited. This systematic review addresses this gap by analysing the prevalence, regional variations, risk factors, antimicrobial resistance and mortality rates of pneumonia in Ghana. We searched PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science and African Journals Online databases, including 21 studies with a total of 124 582 participants. A random-effects meta-analysis estimated pooled prevalence and subgroup differences based on age, region and participant type. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 test and meta-regression, with sensitivity analysis also conducted. Study quality was evaluated using standardized Joana Briggs Institute checklists for prevalence studies. The pooled prevalence of pneumonia in Ghana was 22.01%, with significant regional variations. The highest prevalence was observed in a study conducted in the Greater Accra and Northern regions (32.31%), while the lowest was in the Upper West (4.83%). The prevalence in children <5 y of age was 17.79%. Hospitalized patients had a higher prevalence (22.14%) than community children (21.49%). Streptococcus pneumoniae was the most common pathogen, with high resistance (>70%) to co-trimoxazole, gentamicin and tetracycline. Key risk factors included indoor air pollution and seasonal changes. The mortality rate for pneumonia was found to be 3.07%. This systematic review highlighted significant regional disparities, pathogen resistance patterns and environmental risk factors that shape the burden of pneumonia in Ghana, providing critical evidence for targeted public health strategies. The findings highlight avenues for future research, including environmental drivers of transmission, focusing on underreported regions and surveillance of antibiotic resistance of pathogens isolated from pneumonia patients.
Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, C. pneumoniae, Ghana, pneumonia, pneumonia epidemiology, prevalence, respiratory infections, S. pneumoniae, systematic review

## Introduction
Pneumonia is a lung infection characterized by inflammation of the alveolar space, whereas the presence of microorganisms without an inflammatory response represents colonization rather than pneumonia. Unlike other infectious diseases, pneumonia is not a single disease but a group of specific infections with distinct epidemiology, pathogenesis and presentation. It is caused by a variety of pathogens, including bacterial, viral, fungal and parasitic agents. The most common pathogens associated with pneumonia include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae and respiratory viruses such as influenza A and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). , These pathogens may act independently or in combination, complicating diagnosis and treatment. Pneumonia remains a leading cause of hospitalization and mortality globally, responsible for 2.2 million deaths annually, including 502 000 children. , It is reported that pneumonia causes approximately 1.58 million deaths in children <5 y of age each year, exceeding the combined fatalities from human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, malaria and measles. By 2021, the burden of respiratory infections grew even more severe, with >6 million deaths linked to pneumonia and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). 
A recent study reported that >800 000 children <5 y of age died from pneumonia globally within a 3-y period, with more than half of these deaths occurring in just five countries. Notably, 26% were concentrated in three sub-Saharan African nations: Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Ethiopia. In West and Central Africa, pneumonia alone accounts for 17% of under-5 mortality rates, highlighting its significant burden on child health in the region. Although child mortality has significantly decreased over the past 2 decades, pneumonia continues to rank among the top five causes of death in children <5 y of age in the sub-Saharan region. 
In Ghana, pneumonia remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly among vulnerable populations such as children <5 y of age and the elderly, contributing to 18% of deaths and 72 000 cases annually. , A critical driver in pneumonia transmission is the high prevalence of pneumococcal carriage—asymptomatic colonization of the nasopharynx by S. pneumoniae, the leading cause of bacterial pneumonia worldwide. In Ghana, Arhin et al. reported prevalence rates of pneumococcal carriage ranging from 11% to 51%. Compounding this threat, multidrug-resistant strains are pervasive, with resistance rates ranging from 7% to 100% depending on geographic and socio-economic factors. Despite this significant burden, the prevalence of pneumonia across Ghana's diverse demographics and regions has not been comprehensively assessed, as is often the case in many low-resource settings. 
Effective pneumonia management relies on accurate knowledge of microbial aetiologies and the ability to differentiate between them. However, Ghana's healthcare infrastructure, like that of most low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), faces critical challenges, including scarcity of diagnostic tools, underresourced laboratories and clinical differentiation of pneumonia cases. Exacerbating these issues is a significant knowledge deficiency regarding pneumonia among Ghanaians, as one study found that two-thirds of participants had never heard of the disease, suggesting low recognition of symptoms and delays in care-seeking.
To address these gaps, this systematic review aimed to examine geographic disparities in pneumonia research, identify literature gaps and provide a clear understanding of the disease's epidemiology by analysing its prevalence, mortality rates, risk factors and antimicrobial resistance rates. The findings will inform targeted interventions, guide evidence-based policies for more effective resource allocation, and contribute to addressing concerns related to antimicrobial resistance, laying the foundation for future research in Ghana.

## Methods
The systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and the study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024627892). The PRISMA checklist is included in the supplementary materials. 

## Search strategy
The literature search was conducted from 1 December to 12 December 2024. The search included studies published in English and conducted in Ghana. The search strategy was designed to include studies published from the inception of each database to 11 December 2024. Original research articles including observational, cohort and case–control studies describing the proportion of pneumonia cases and its driving factors were identified from PubMed and three other databases (Web of Science, ScienceDirect and African Journals Online [AJOL]). The search was conducted using specific keywords; terms within the same concept were connected with the Boolean ‘OR’ and combined with other components of the search terms using ‘AND’. The final search terms for the databases were:
PubMed: (‘Pneumonia’ OR ‘Pneumonitis’ OR ‘Bronchopneumonia’ OR ‘Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP)’ OR ‘Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP)’ OR ‘Aspiration Pneumonia’ OR ‘Fungal Pneumonia’ OR ‘Viral Pneumonia’ OR ‘Mycoplasma Pneumonia’) AND (‘Ghana’ OR ‘Ghanaian’)
Web of Science: (‘Pneumonia’ OR ‘Pneumonitis’ OR ‘Bronchopneumonia’ OR ‘Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP)’ OR ‘Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP)’ OR ‘Aspiration Pneumonia’ OR ‘Fungal Pneumonia’ OR ‘Viral Pneumonia’ OR ‘Mycoplasma Pneumonia’) AND (‘Ghana’ OR ‘Ghanaian’)
ScienceDirect: (‘Pneumonia’ OR ‘Pneumonitis’ OR ‘Aspiration Pneumonia’ OR ‘Fungal Pneumonia’ OR ‘Viral Pneumonia’) AND (‘Ghana’)
AJOL: (‘Pneumonia’ OR ‘Pneumonitis’ OR ‘Bronchopneumonia’ OR ‘Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP)’ OR ‘Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP)’ OR ‘Aspiration Pneumonia’ OR ‘Fungal Pneumonia’ OR ‘Viral Pneumonia’ OR ‘Mycoplasma Pneumonia’) AND (‘Ghana’ OR ‘Ghanaian’)
The reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also manually searched to identify additional publications not indexed in the databases. All publications from the databases were downloaded and imported into the Zotero reference manager (version 6.0.36), where they were merged into a single collection. The merged file yielded 844 articles that were later imported into the Rayyan online platform (www.rayyan.ai) and deduplicated before the study selection process.

## Study selection process
Before screening, two reviewers (F.K and AA-D) designed a protocol that clearly defined characteristics qualifying a study for inclusion or exclusion. The reviewers independently screened all deduplicated records in three stages—title, abstract and full text—using the Rayyan online platform to assess eligibility based on predefined criteria. References were coded as ‘included’, ‘maybe’ or ‘excluded’. Records whose titles and abstracts did not align with the study's objectives were excluded during the first two stages. Full texts of studies labelled as ‘included’ or ‘maybe’ progressed to the next screening stage. A calibration exercise was performed among reviewers to ensure consistency in applying inclusion/exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were exported to the Zotero reference manager (version 6.0.36) and assessed independently against eligibility criteria by two reviewers. Reasons for final exclusion at this stage were recorded. Any discrepancies in study selection were discussed with a third reviewer (ESD) until a consensus was reached. All included studies proceeded to the data extraction stage.

## Eligibility criteria
Inclusion criteria were cross-sectional, cohort or case–control studies reporting original data; studies conducted among the general population or specific subgroups (e.g. children, communities, etc.) in Ghana; studies reporting the prevalence and specific aetiology of pneumonia; studies that diagnosed pneumonia using microbiological, radiological or molecular techniques (e.g. cultures, polymerase chain reaction, chest X-ray, computed tomography scans) and identified the microbial aetiology through appropriate methods, confirmed by clinical symptoms and radiological findings; and studies published from the inception of each database to 12 December 2024.
Studies were excluded if they were reviews, case reports, editorials or abstracts without full text. Additionally, studies conducted outside Ghana, not involving human participants, and those that included Ghana but did not provide Ghana-exclusive studies. Studies reporting outcomes unrelated to pneumonia prevalence or antimicrobial susceptibility were also excluded, including those focused on COVID-19-induced pneumonia, as this is considered a distinct condition of the pandemic, and may skew findings. Furthermore, studies that reported only pneumonia symptoms without conducting diagnostic tests (e.g. microbiological, radiological or molecular methods) to confirm the infection, or those that did not distinguish pneumonia from other respiratory illnesses, were not considered.

## Data extraction
Two reviewers (FK and ESD) developed a standard data extraction form in Excel 2019 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and piloted on three randomly selected studies before full abstracting. From each eligible study, the following key information was extracted: bibliographic details (author[s], publication year), study characteristics (location/setting [region], study design; population characteristics (sample size, age groups); and outcomes of interest, including pneumonia prevalence, aetiology, type of pneumonia, mortality rate and risk factors for its transmission.
The data were extracted independently by two reviewers (FK and AA-D) and were checked by a third reviewer (ESD). All discrepancies were discussed among the three reviewers until a consensus was reached. The extracted information was synthesized qualitatively due to heterogeneity in reported outcomes. This comprehensive data extraction process ensured all relevant information across studies were captured uniformly for subsequent analysis.

## Statistical analysis
Meta-analysis was performed using RStudio version 4.3.3 (Posit, Boston, MA, USA). The software was used to estimate the proportion of pneumonia reported. The funnel plot and Egger's regression test assessed publication bias visually and statistically ( ). The Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to stabilize variances among studies and the pooled prevalences were computed using the DerSimonian–Laird method. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated using I2 statistics with values of 0%, 25%, 50% and ≥75%, representing no, low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively. The proportion of pneumonia was measured by random-effects meta-analysis with a confidence interval (CI) of 95%. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and meta-regression were performed to assess the robustness and sources of heterogeneity in our study, respectively. An α-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Quality assessment
Using the standardized Joana Briggs Institute checklist for prevalence studies, two reviewers methodologically assessed the quality of the included studies. The checklist comprised tools that included the appropriateness of the sample frame, appropriate recruitment of study participants, adequacy of the sample size, detailed description of study subjects and setting, comprehensiveness of data analysis to cover the entire sample, validity of diagnostic methods, reliability of standards to measure condition, the availability of appropriate statistical analysis and the adequacy of response rate. Each checklist item was scored ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’. A ‘yes’ response to each question was scored 1, while ‘unclear’, ‘no’ and ‘not applicable’ were scored 0. All included studies were subjected to each of these tools and the quality of the study (risk of bias) was determined as ‘good’ (low risk; score 7–9), ‘fair’ (moderate risk; score 4–6) or ‘poor’ (high risk; score 0–3). Disagreements were discussed until a consensus was reached, ensuring that the final quality analysis reflected a comprehensive understanding of the included studies’ methodologies, findings and potential biases. The included studies were generally of high quality, with the majority (16 studies) scoring ‘good’ (achieving 7–9 points, indicating low risk) and the rest (7 studies) rated ‘fair’ (achieving 4–6 points, indicating moderate risk) ( ).

## Results

## Search results
The search of the databases yielded a total of 944 articles, with 167 of them being duplicates. After resolving the duplicates, 848 non-duplicate articles were subjected to further evaluation. Of the 848 articles, 778 were excluded based on title and abstract screening. The remaining 70 articles were then subjected to a detailed full-text review. After full-text evaluation, 49 articles were excluded for various reasons (no data on pneumonia epidemiology [n=13], pneumonia not tested or clinically diagnosed [n=9], pneumonia data not segregated from other respiratory tract infections [n=6], population used by included study [n=2], population/sample size not stated [n=2], mortality study [n=2] no data on pneumonia [n=7], worked on animals [n=2] and COVID-19-induced pneumonia [n=6]), leaving 21 eligible articles (Figure  ).
 

## Characteristics of included studies
The included studies had sample sizes ranging from 85 to 70 323. The geographic distribution of the studies restricted the studies to just 6 (Greater Accra, Ashanti, Volta, Upper West, Bono East and Northern) of the 16 regions (38%) in Ghana. The Greater Accra (seven studies) and Ashanti (six studies) regions were the most studied, representing 33% and 29% of the included studies, respectively, while the Volta and Upper West regions were the least studied, with two studies (10%) each. One study, representing 5% of the studies, was conducted in both the Greater Accra and Northern regions. The studies included two types of participants, with hospitalized patients being the most represented, in 71% (15 studies), and the other being community children, in 19% (4 studies). Two studies (10%) included both participant types. The participants belonged to different age groups, including children (0–10 y), adolescents (10–17 y), adults (18–60 y) and the elderly (>60 y). Children between the ages of 0–6 y were the most studied, representing 61% (14 studies), followed by studies involving children and adolescents and those involving people of all ages, with 13% (3 studies) each. Adults only and adults in combination with adolescents and the elderly were reported in three separate studies (4% each). A total of 124 582 participants were tested for pneumonia across the studies, of which 9222 were confirmed positive (Table  ). Of the 21 included studies, 11 (52%) reported the aetiology of pneumonia. Among these, nine studies identified S. pneumoniae as the causative pathogen, one study reported C. pneumoniae and one study identified a combination of both pathogens. Eight studies reported the types of pneumonia: three focused on community-acquired pneumonia, two on hospital-acquired pneumonia, two on severe pneumonia and one study reported cases of bronchopneumonia and lobar pneumonia. Mortality was recorded in four of the included studies, with calculated rates ranging from 1.6% to 36% (Table  ). Antimicrobial resistance was recorded in only two of the studies. However, these reported high resistance of S. pneumoniae isolates to co-trimoxazole (100%), gentamicin (77.8%) and tetracycline (75%). Ciprofloxacin and oxacillin had moderate resistance rates at 47.4% and 46.7%, respectively, while penicillin had the lowest resistance rate at 0.73%. Three studies reported risk factors associated with pneumonia prevalence. Two studies highlighted weather, particularly the wet season, from June to December, as a key factor. One study identified household air pollution as a contributing factor to disease prevalence.
 

## Pooled prevalence of pneumonia and subgroup analysis based on age groups
The pooled prevalence of pneumonia based on the included studies was 22.01% (95% CI 16.52 to 28.03, p=1.64). A random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis, due to the significant heterogeneity observed across the included studies (I2=99.8%). The subgroup analysis based on the age group reported children and adolescents as the group with the highest prevalence of pneumonia (37.21% [95% CI 23.60 to 51.94]), followed by adolescents and adults (32.94% [95% CI 23.30 to 43.34]). The results show similar prevalence rates across studies with a combination of adults and elderly (26.07% [95% CI 21.44 to 30.99]) and those that included all age groups (22.24% [95% CI 5.64 to 45.61]). In comparison, the prevalence of pneumonia was higher in adults only (25.78 [95% CI 22.19 to 29.53]) as compared with children only (17.79% [95% CI 13.13 to 23.00]) (Figure  ).
 

## Subgroup analysis based on participant population
The subgroup analysis based on the type of participants shows a slightly higher prevalence among hospitalized patients (22.14% [95% CI 16.29 to 28.60]) as compared with community children (21.49% [95% CI 11.28 to 33.90] (Figure  ).
 

## Subgroup analysis based on region
Figure  presents the subgroup analysis based on the region in which the studies were conducted. The highest prevalence of pneumonia was recorded at 32.31% (95% CI 29.20 to 35.50) in a multiregional study that involved the Greater Accra and Northern regions. This was followed by the Greater Accra and Bono East regions with prevalence rates of 27.75% (95% CI 12.42 to 46.34) and 26.59% (95% CI 20.49 to 33.17), respectively. Similar prevalence rates were recorded in the Ashanti (21.19% [95% CI 8.29 to 38.02]) and Volta (18.18% [95% CI 0.00 to 63.38]) regions. The lowest prevalence was recorded in the Upper West region, with a rate of 4.83% (95% CI 0.32 to 13.85).
 

## Meta-regression
Our meta-regression indicated that hospitalized patients exhibited a positive effect on pneumonia prevalence, with a notable trend, although not statistically significant (p>0.05). The Upper West region showed a significant negative association with pneumonia rates, suggesting potential regional disparities (p<0.05). Age groups and other regions had no significant effect on pneumonia prevalence (p>0.05). These findings underscore the complexity of pneumonia prevalence, with hospitalized populations and regional differences contributing to its heterogeneity in Ghana (Table  ).
 

## Sensitivity analysis
A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of each included study on the pooled pneumonia prevalence in the meta-analysis. The point estimates ranged from 20.41% to 23.59%, with consistently overlapping CIs. This overlap suggests that the variations in point estimates were not statistically significant. Although some differences were observed, the study selection can be considered reliable, as the individual prevalence estimates remained closely aligned with the pooled prevalence of 22.01% ( ).

## Discussion
Pneumonia remains a significant public health challenge in Ghana, contributing to morbidity and mortality across various age groups. This systematic review examined the prevalence, distribution and characteristics of pneumonia cases reported in Ghana. The review included 21 studies on pneumonia, with a total sample size of 124 582 participants. The studies reveal geographic disparities in research coverage, with studies concentrated in only 6 of the country's 16 regions. The Greater Accra and Ashanti regions accounted for most studies, representing 39% and 26%, respectively, likely due to the urbanization of these regions and the increased probability of access to healthcare facilities. These urban centres are characterized by higher population densities, better healthcare infrastructure and more research institutions, which facilitate the identification and reporting of pneumonia cases. In contrast, regions like Volta and Upper West were underrepresented, highlighting potential inequities in healthcare access and research focus. The studies predominantly involved hospitalized patients (15 studies), with children aged 0–6 y being the most studied demographic group (14 studies). S. pneumoniae emerged as the primary causative pathogen, consistent with findings from a study by Musher et al., which attributed 90% of all pneumonia cases to this pathogen. In contrast, the lowest occurrence of C. pneumoniae may be partly attributed to challenges in its detection and diagnosis, as studies have found that pneumonia associated with this pathogen is clinically indistinguishable from other types of pneumonia. , Additionally, 48% of the studies reported the aetiology of pneumonia, while eight studies provided details on pneumonia types, including community-acquired, hospital-acquired, severe pneumonia, bronchopneumonia and lobar pneumonia. The wet season and polluted household air were risk factors for the epidemiology of pneumonia. Studies highlight that meteorological conditions such as variations in daily temperature, humidity and particulate matter (PM) levels increase pneumonia risk by impairing respiratory defences and prolonging pathogen survival. , For instance, Huh et al. demonstrated that these climatic factors directly correlate with pneumonia incidence, underscoring the need to integrate environmental considerations into public health strategies. Household air pollution (HAP) further exacerbates the burden: in Ghana, Kinney et al. found that prenatal exposure to HAP increased the risk of pneumonia and severe pneumonia in infants. Similarly, exposure to PM that is ≤2.5 μm (PM2.5)—a key pollutant from biomass burning—was linked to a 10% increase in pneumococcal carriage per unit increase in PM2.5 levels, directly increasing pneumonia susceptibility. These findings emphasize the dual role of seasonal environmental shifts and indoor air pollution in driving pneumonia transmission.
The pooled prevalence of pneumonia from the included studies is 22.01%, which is alarming, as it indicates that 22 of 100 Ghanaians are affected by pneumonia. However, these results may be skewed, as most of the studies included were conducted among children (people <18 y of age). Geographically, our reported prevalence is lower than that reported by a study conducted in Uganda, which reported a rate of 25.6%, and other LMICs such as India and Taiwan, with rates of 64% and 31.6%, respectively. , However, a study in Ethiopia reported a lower rate (16.1%) than our reported pooled prevalence. Ghana's intermediate prevalence may reflect its unique demographic and healthcare context. First, most included studies focused on children <18 y of age, a high-risk group for pneumonia in low-resource settings, due to malnutrition, incomplete immunization and exposure to indoor air pollution. Additionally, Ghana's relatively robust healthcare infrastructure, including high pneumococcal vaccine coverage (approximately 96% in 2023), may help moderate the prevalence compared with countries with weaker systems.
Our subgroup analysis based on age groups found variations in prevalence rates across different age groups. In comparison, our study reports a 17.79% prevalence of pneumonia among children, which is half the rates reported by similar studies conducted among children <5 y of age in East Africa (34%) and Ethiopia (30%). Children and adolescents exhibited the highest prevalence, followed by adolescents combined with adults, indicating a substantial burden of pneumonia in younger age groups. Interestingly, studies involving adults and the elderly or the general population showed comparable prevalence rates, suggesting consistent disease distribution when broader populations are considered. Notably, adults alone had a higher prevalence (25.78%) than children alone (17.79%). However, it is important to note that the data for adults only might be skewed, as it is based on a single study with a small sample size. This limitation highlights the need for additional research focusing on adults, to provide a more accurate representation of pneumonia prevalence in this demographic.
The subgroup analysis revealed a slightly higher prevalence of pneumonia among hospitalized patients compared with community children (Figure  ). This difference is typical, as symptomatic children, who are a more vulnerable population to disease mortality, often require medical intervention. Notably, the prevalence of pneumonia among community children, many of whom were asymptomatic controls, is particularly significant. This finding underscores the role of asymptomatic carriers in the community, who may serve as reservoirs for the transmission of respiratory pathogens. Identifying and monitoring asymptomatic cases is critical for understanding the hidden burden of pneumonia and its potential spread within communities. The broader CI for community children also indicates variability in study findings, likely due to differences in study designs, diagnostic methods or population characteristics.
The regional subgroup analysis reveals significant variations in pneumonia prevalence across Ghana. The highest prevalence was found in a multiregional study involving the Greater Accra and Northern regions (32.31%), followed by the Greater Accra and Bono East regions. Similar prevalence rates were observed in the Volta and Ashanti regions, with the Upper West region showing the lowest prevalence (Figure  ). These results highlight regional disparities and suggest the need for targeted public health strategies to address the varying healthcare access and disease burden across regions.
This study reports a lower pneumonia mortality rate of 3.07% compared with higher rates observed in Mali (4.2%) and Malawi (6.6%). The difference in mortality rates may be attributed to variations in healthcare access and disease management, as well as the prevalence of risk factors such as hypoxaemia, severe acute malnutrition and decreased conscious state, which are strongly associated with increased mortality in children with pneumonia. Despite the lower mortality rate in this study, there is still a need for targeted interventions and improvements in healthcare infrastructure to reduce pneumonia-related deaths. Despite most of the studies not assessing antimicrobial resistance in pneumonia cases, two studies that did, found high resistance in S. pneumoniae isolates, with co-trimoxazole (100%), gentamicin (77.8%) and tetracycline (75%) showing the highest resistance. Similar to our findings, a study in Kenya by Irungu reported complete resistance of S. pneumoniae isolates to co-trimoxazole. However, it reported no resistance to gentamicin, which contrasts with our findings. Moderate resistance was observed for ciprofloxacin (47.4%) and oxacillin (46.7%), while penicillin had the lowest resistance rate at 0.73%, aligning with findings by Karcic et al. These findings highlight the need for routine antimicrobial resistance surveillance to guide treatment decisions and ensure effective pneumonia management.
Ghana introduced the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) in 2012 to protect children from pneumococcal diseases like pneumonia and diarrhea. , The program initially included the 10-valent and 13-valent vaccines, achieving an impressive 96% national coverage rate by 2023. While the vaccine has successfully reduced pneumococcal disease rates, particularly pneumonia and meningitis, achieving measurable public health and economic impacts, its impact has not been universal. Projections suggest that the program will reduce overall pneumococcal disease burden by 48% by 2031 compared with no vaccination. However, carriage studies reveal important gaps in protection, with recent data showing 54% of children still carrying pneumococcal bacteria, while showing concerning serotype distributions with only about 33% of circulating serotypes being vaccine covered versus 66% non-vaccine type. This explains why several reports , , found no significant reduction in pneumococcal carriage rates following PCV introduction, with some even reporting a higher prevalence post-vaccination. This suggests that while PCV has been valuable for preventing severe disease burden, its limited coverage of circulating strains allows persistent carriage and potential serotype replacement.

## Strengths and limitations
The strength of our meta-analysis lies in its thorough examination of pneumonia prevalence, considering regional and demographic factors. The consistency of point estimates in the sensitivity analysis further reinforces the robustness of our findings. Our results highlight significant regional disparities, particularly in the Upper West region, and emphasize the impact of hospitalized populations on the overall prevalence. These findings provide valuable insights into the factors contributing to pneumonia variability in Ghana. However, the study highlights several limitations, including significant geographic disparities in research coverage, with studies concentrated in only 6 of Ghana's 16 regions. Most studies focused on children, particularly those <18 y of age, a high-risk group, which may overestimate prevalence among younger populations and underrepresent adults and the elderly. While this reflects clinical priorities, it neglects adults, particularly the elderly, who also have a high risk of pneumonia mortality. Additionally, variability in study design, diagnostic methods and population characteristics could affect the reliability of reported prevalence rates and complicate comparisons. The limited assessment of pneumonia aetiologies and their antimicrobial resistance patterns restricts our understanding of appropriate treatment strategies and likely underestimates the true burden of antibiotic resistance among pneumonia-causing pathogens. The small sample sizes in certain subgroups, especially adults, raise concerns about the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. The review is limited by potential publication bias, as only studies from selected databases were included, possibly excluding relevant grey literature.

## Conclusions
Pneumonia remains a significant public health challenge in Ghana, with a pooled prevalence of 22.01%, indicating that nearly one in five individuals is affected by this disease. The findings highlight substantial regional disparities and variations in pneumonia prevalence across different age groups, with children and hospitalized patients showing notable rates. The higher mortality rate associated with pneumonia underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions to improve healthcare access and disease management. Future studies should cover a wider geographic range in Ghana, include more balanced age demographics, and standardize diagnostic methods for better comparability. Antimicrobial resistance surveillance should be integrated to guide treatment strategies, and longitudinal studies are needed to track trends in pneumonia incidence, risk factors and outcomes for improved public health planning.
