## Background
Transient ischaemic attack (TIA) is a strong predictor of stroke, has a high risk of recurrence, and may lead to a variety of sequelae. The purpose of this study was to understand the epidemiological characteristics and trends of TIA through a systematic review and meta-analysis, to identify high-risk populations, and to provide a basis for prevention and intervention measures.

## Methods
Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, we searched Chinese-language (CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM) and English-language databases (PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, and Web of Science) from inception to October 25, 2024. Cross-sectional or cohort studies reporting the prevalence or incidence of TIA were included. Quality assessment was performed using the AHRQ and NOS scales. Data synthesis was performed with Stata 17.0, employing a random-effects model for high heterogeneity (I² > 50%). Subgroup analyses were conducted on the basis of region, publication year, gender, age and diagnostic criteria.

## Results
From 20,114 screened records, 47 studies were included, contributing a total of 50 datasets for analysis (23 for prevalence and 27 for incidence). The global pooled prevalence of TIA was 1.95% (95% CI: 1.27–2.77%), and the prevalence was elevated in Asia (2.19%) and among older adults (> 65 years, 5.13%). The annual incidence was 54 per 100,000 person-years, with the highest incidence occurring in the Americas (81 per 100,000). No significant gender differences were found in terms of prevalence or incidence. The incidence decreased over time but increased with age. The use of imaging-based criteria is associated with a lower and likely more accurate estimation of TIA prevalence.

## Conclusion
This study provides updated global estimates of TIA epidemiology, revealing significant variations influenced by diagnostic criteria, age, and region. The substantial heterogeneity and potential underreporting highlight the need for standardized, imaging-based diagnostic protocols in future research. The development of precise risk stratification tools and region-specific prevention strategies is imperative for mitigating the global burden of TIA.

## Introduction
Transient ischaemic attack (TIA) is classically and clinically defined as focal neurological symptoms of presumed vascular origin that persist for less than 24 h [ ]. However, this time-based definition has been widely debated with advances in imaging techniques. In 2002, the American Heart Association proposed a modern, tissue-based definition: TIA is a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal brain or retinal ischaemia, typically lasting less than 1 h, with no evidence of acute infarction on neuroimaging [ ]. Notably, the 1-hour duration is descriptive rather than a strict diagnostic threshold—the core of the modern diagnosis is the absence of acute infarction on imaging (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging, MRI) rather than just symptom resolution [ ].
Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, with ischaemic strokes accounting for approximately 70% of all stroke cases and carrying a high risk of long-term recurrence [ , ]. TIA is a strong predictor of subsequent stroke. The 90-day stroke risk after TIA can reach 17.8%, with nearly half of these strokes occurring within 2 days [ ]. The risk of ischaemic stroke after TIA is estimated to be 3–10% within 2 days, 5% at 7 days, and 9–17% at 90 days [ ]. A wide variety of residual problems, including psychological, cognitive, and physical impairments, have been reported after TIA [ ].
Despite the severe consequences of TIA, public awareness and timely seeking of medical attention remain inadequate. In one report, only 64% of people sought medical attention within 24 h of experiencing TIA-related symptoms, and another 3.2% of participants recalled symptoms consistent with TIA but did not seek medical help [ ]. According to a TIA epidemiological survey of 98,000 people in China, the awareness rate of TIA is only 3.08% [ ]. In the United States, only 8.2% of participants correctly identified the definition of TIA, and only 8.6% of them could identify typical symptoms [ ]. Nonetheless, the burden is substantial: a nationally representative survey in China estimated 1.35 million TIA patients and 310,000 new cases annually [ ], while U.S. estimates range from 200,000 to 500,000 cases per year [ ].
Differences in TIA epidemiology across countries highlight the need for updated global estimates. Although prior meta-analyses have been published [ ], they are relatively dated and often compare domestic data with Western estimates. The current study provides an updated synthesis based on population-based door-to-door surveys, which enhances representativeness given low disease awareness and treatment rates [ ]. Through a systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to (1) characterize the prevalence and incidence of TIA across multiple global regions with available data; (2) identify high-risk populations (by age, gender, and region) within the studied cohorts; and (3) provide evidence to inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies, thereby indirectly supporting efforts to improve public awareness and promote healthy lifestyles in the represented regions.

## Methods
This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 and was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (accession number: CRD42024606730). Ethical approval and informed consent were not needed, as all the data used in this study were obtained from previously published articles.

## Search strategy
Two researchers searched Chinese-language databases (CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM) and English-language databases (PubMed, The Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science) from their inception dates to October 25, 2024. Search terms and search strategies were selected according to the characteristics of different databases and included Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, text word searches, and Boolean computational searches; these terms were supplemented with keywords, titles, or abstract terms, including words and phrases such as “Transient ischaemic attack”, “TIA”, “Epidemiology”, “Incidence”, and “Prevalence”. The complete search strategies for all the databases are provided in Supplementary Text S1. The search was limited to cross-sectional studies and cohort studies, and the study language was limited to English or Chinese. In addition, we manually checked the reference lists of the retrieved studies and identified and obtained additional relevant articles.

## Study eligibility criteria
Studies were included in this review if they met the following criteria: (1) Published Chinese- or English-language domestic and international studies on the prevalence or incidence of TIA. We adopted an inclusive approach regarding TIA diagnostic criteria to capture the broadest evidence base. We included all studies that explicitly diagnosed participants as having TIA according to the criteria accepted at the time of the study, acknowledging the evolution from time-based (symptom resolution within 24 h) to tissue-based (no acute infarction on neuroimaging) definitions. (2) Cross-sectional studies or cohort studies that provided sufficient raw data that could be used to calculate the prevalence or incidence.
Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) they were reviews, case reports, conference abstracts or other literature; (2) the full text of the literature was not available; (3) the raw data were unclear or incomplete or could not be used to calculate the prevalence; or (4) they were low-quality studies.
To prevent double counting arising from overlapping populations, we identified studies from the same cohort or region. For any overlaps, we prioritized the publication with the largest sample size or most complete data for inclusion.

## Study selection and data extraction
The two researchers independently extracted valuable data from the screened studies according to the inclusion criteria and entered the data into a predesigned Excel sheet, including the names of the authors, publication date, region, total number of individuals in the study, number of TIA patients, number of men in the survey, number of male patients, and age. Any inconsistencies and disagreements between the two independent reviewers were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer.
The unit of analysis in this meta-analysis was the study dataset. If a single publication reported independent data on both the prevalence and incidence of TIA, it contributed two separate datasets to the respective analyses. Furthermore, if a study reported incidence rates from distinct, non-overlapping time periods, each period was treated as an independent dataset to capture temporal trends accurately.

## Quality assessment

## Agency for healthcare research and quality scale
We used the scale recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) to assess the methodological quality of cross-sectional studies. The scale includes 11 items with the following potential responses: “yes”, “no” and “unclear”. “Yes” is worth 1 point, and “no” or “unclear” is worth 0 points; for quality assessment, 0–3 indicates low quality, 4–7 indicates medium quality, and 8–11 indicates high quality.

## Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale
The NOS scale includes three dimensions (subject selection, comparability, and outcome measurement) with a total of 8 items, the responses for which were evaluated via a scoring system. With the exception of comparability, which has a maximum score of 2, each item has a maximum score of 1, for a total score of 9, with higher scores indicating higher study quality.
Studies scoring ≤ 3 on AHRQ (for cross-sectional studies) or ≤ 5 on the NOS (for cohort studies) were excluded to minimize bias. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by a third investigator.

## Data synthesis and analysis
The meta-analysis of single proportions (prevalence and incidence) was performed using the metaprop command in Stata 17.0. To stabilize the variances and ensure the normality of the sampling distribution, especially for proportions close to 0 or 1, the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transform was applied to the raw proportions before pooling.
The pooled estimate and its 95% confidence interval were then back-transformed to the original proportion scale for interpretation. Study weights were assigned using the inverse variance method with a random-effects model. The DerSimonian-Laird estimator was used to estimate the between-study variance (τ²). The degree of heterogeneity between datasets was quantified using the I² statistic, where I² > 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity and thus justified the use of the random-effects model.
Subgroup analysis included the year of publication, region surveyed, gender, age distribution, and diagnostic criteria. The P values for heterogeneity between subgroups in the forest plot were compared to determine any differences in pooled effect sizes between subgroups. Sensitivity analysis was performed via the literature exclusion method. Egger’s test was used to determine the potential for publication bias. To assess the potential for quality-related bias, we performed a univariable meta-regression to examine the association between study quality scores and the reported prevalence (and incidence) estimates. The significance threshold was α = 0.05.
We included all studies that explicitly diagnosed participants as having TIA according to the criteria accepted at the time of the study. This approach allows for the inclusion of a broader evidence base reflecting real-world diagnostic practices across different eras and health care settings. To account for the substantial heterogeneity introduced by these varying definitions, we performed a prespecified subgroup analysis classifying studies into two categories: (1) “symptom-based”: diagnosis primarily reliant on clinical symptom duration (e.g., resolution within 24 h); (2) “imaging-based”: diagnosis incorporating neuroimaging (e.g., MRI) to exclude cerebral infarction. This stratification allows us to quantitatively assess the impact of diagnostic criteria on the pooled prevalence and incidence estimates.

## Results

## General characteristics
Our systematic search yielded 20,114 records. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, 47 articles met our inclusion criteria. Of these, one article provided independent data for both prevalence and incidence analyses. Two articles each reported incidence data from two independent time periods, thus contributing two datasets each.
Consequently, our meta-analysis included a total of 23 datasets on prevalence and 27 datasets on incidence. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig.  ).

## Description of the included studies
The characteristics and quality ratings of the 23 prevalence datasets are provided in Table . These datasets were derived from 23 individual studies, encompassing 1,272,386 participants. The selected articles were published between 1973 and 2021, with nearly half published within the past decade. Nine articles were in Chinese [ – , , , , ], and the remaining 14 were in English [ , – , , , ]. Sixteen studies reported gender composition. Eleven studies were conducted in China, two in the USA, two in Spain, and eight in Japan, South Korea, Argentina, Italy, the Netherlands, Denmark, Pakistan, and Egypt. Quality assessment using the AHRQ scale indicated high overall quality, with scores of 7 (7 datasets), 8 (11 datasets), and 9 (5 datasets).

 

## Prevalence
Owing to the high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.86%, P < 0.001), random effects meta-analyses and narrative synthesis were performed. The overall prevalence of TIA was 1.95% (95% CI: 1.27–2.77%) (Fig.  ).

 

## Subgroup analysis (the results are summarized in Table  )
Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

 

## Region
The highest prevalence of TIA was observed in the Americas (2.34%, 95% CI: 0.36–5.94%), followed by Asia (2.19%, 95% CI: 1.13–3.58%) and Europe (1.85%, 95% CI: 0.24–4.91%) (Supplementary Figure S1). Notably, heterogeneity remained high within each regional subgroup (P < 0.001).
To assess the impact of Chinese studies on the overall estimate, we conducted stratified analysis by study region (Supplementary Figure S2). Chinese studies (11 datasets, n = 884,459) had a pooled prevalence of 1.67% (95% CI: 0.68–3.08%), whereas non-Chinese studies (12 datasets, n = 387,927) had a prevalence of 2.26% (95% CI: 1.09–3.83%). Meta-regression revealed no statistically significant difference between groups (coefficient = -0.0014, 95% CI: -0.020–0.018%, P = 0.883).

## Publication year
Prevalence estimates varied considerably across decades. Studies published between 2001 and 2010 reported the highest prevalence (4.85%, 95% CI: 2.60–7.75%), which was markedly higher than that in the period 2011–2020 (1.28%, 95% CI: 0.60–2.22%) (Supplementary Figure S3).

## Gender
The prevalence in females (2.61%, 95% CI: 1.56–3.92%) was slightly greater than that in males (2.12%, 95% CI: 1.21–3.28%), but the difference was not statistically significant (Supplementary Figure S4).

## Age group
A strong positive association was observed between age and TIA prevalence. The prevalence increased from 2.22% (95% CI: 0.16–6.32%) in individuals under 45 years to 5.13% (95% CI: 2.94–7.82%) in those over 65 years (Supplementary Figure S5).

## Diagnostic criteria
Studies using symptom-based definitions reported a significantly greater prevalence (3.05%, 95% CI: 1.77–4.66%) than those using imaging-based criteria did (0.74%, 95% CI: 0.37–1.25%), with a statistically significant difference across subgroups (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S6).

## Sensitivity analysis and publication bias
Sensitivity analysis for the prevalence estimates was performed using the leave-one-out method. The results demonstrated that no single study exerted an undue influence on the overall pooled prevalence. The recalculated estimates remained within the confidence interval of the original analysis, indicating the robustness of our finding of a 1.95% global prevalence.
Meta-regression revealed no significant association between study quality (AHRQ) scores and prevalence estimates (coefficient = -0.007, P = 0.216), indicating that the methodological quality of the included studies did not systematically influence the pooled prevalence.
Publication bias was assessed statistically using Egger’s regression test. For the prevalence studies, the test result was statistically significant (p = 0.005), suggesting a potential risk of publication bias. The funnel plot for the prevalence datasets is presented in Supplementary Figure S7, which shows visual asymmetry, with a gap in the lower left region indicating the possible absence of small-scale studies with lower effect sizes.

## Incidence studies

## Description of the included studies
Twenty-five articles (reporting a total of 27 datasets) published from 1987 to 2020 were included in this review. One article was a cross-sectional study [ ], and its literature quality score was assessed with the AHRQ, scoring 8. The remaining articles were cohort studies [ , – ], and their literature quality was assessed using the NOS, with all the studies scoring above 5. The detailed information for each article is shown in Table .

 

## Incidence
All datasets reported data on morbidity (Fig.  ). When a fixed-effects model was used, a meta-analysis of TIA incidence showed remarkable heterogeneity (I2 = 99.7%). The results of the meta-analysis using a random-effects model revealed that the average annual incidence of TIA in the general population was 54 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI: 39–70; P < 0.001).

 

## Subgroup analysis
Subgroup analyses for incidence were conducted to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, with key results summarized in Table  .

 

## Region
Substantial variation in TIA incidence was observed across continents. The highest rate was found in the Americas (81 per 100,000, 95% CI: 32–153), followed by Europe (51 per 100,000, 95% CI: 34–72) and Asia (33 per 100,000, 95% CI: 11–66). Only one study was available from Oceania (Supplementary Figure S8).

## Publication year
A clear temporal trend was evident, with the pooled incidence rate declining from 65 per 100,000 (95% CI: 42–93) in the period 1987–1999 to 55 per 100,000 (95% CI: 24–99) in 2000–2010 and further to 49 per 100,000 (95% CI: 34–67) in 2011–2020 (Supplementary Figure S9).

## Gender
No significant difference in incidence was found between males (52 per 100,000; 95% CI: 39–67) and females (50 per 100,000; 95% CI: 37–64), reinforcing the notion that gender does not substantially influence the occurrence of TIA (Supplementary Figure S10).

## Age group
Incidence rates increased dramatically with age, from 3 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2–4) in those under 45 years to 324 per 100,000 (95% CI: 250–407) in individuals over 85 years (Supplementary Figure S11).

## Diagnostic criteria
In contrast to prevalence, the choice of diagnostic criteria did not significantly affect incidence estimates (symptom-based: 53 per 100,000; imaging-based: 54 per 100,000; P = 0.911) (Supplementary Figure S12).

## Sensitivity analysis and publication bias
Similarly, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted for the incidence estimates. The results confirmed that the pooled annual incidence rate of 54 per 100,000 person-years was robust and not disproportionately influenced by any individual study.
For the incidence analysis, which included studies with design heterogeneity (one cross-sectional study using AHRQ assessment and multiple cohort studies using NOS assessment), sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding studies one by one did not affect the stability of the outcome. Therefore, we excluded cross-sectional studies and conducted regression analysis on the dataset of the remaining 26 longitudinal studies. The results revealed no significant association between the study quality score (NOS) and the incidence estimates (coefficient = 0.000, P = 0.997).
In contrast to that for the prevalence studies, Egger’s test for the incidence studies did not show a statistically significant difference (P = 0.256). This finding indicates no strong evidence of publication bias among the included incidence datasets. The corresponding funnel plot is shown in Supplementary Figure S13, which shows a relatively symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled effect estimate.

## Discussion
Our systematic review and meta-analysis provide updated global estimates of the prevalence and incidence of TIA, revealing notable geographical and temporal variations. The pooled global prevalence of TIA was 1.95%, with an annual incidence of 54 per 100,000 individuals. These estimates, however, should be interpreted in the context of significant heterogeneity and regional disparities in data availability and diagnostic practices.
According to the subgroup analysis, the prevalence and incidence of TIA increased with age, which was consistent with the results of Xiaofeng Zhang and Lip et al. This may be because elderly individuals often experience a variety of chronic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease, which are all risk factors for TIA. Studies have shown that the prevalence of chronic diseases among elderly people in China is 86.23%, with the three most common being hypertension, arthritis or rheumatism, and stomach or digestive system diseases [ ]. These chronic diseases increase the risk of TIA in elderly individuals. Additionally, blood vessels in elderly individuals are more prone to become atherosclerotic, which is a major cause of TIA.
Our analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in the prevalence or incidence of TIA between males and females. These findings contrast with those of some studies reporting lower stroke/TIA risk in women [ ] and may reflect regional, lifestyle, or health care access differences. However, since this meta-analysis did not directly assess factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, or gender-specific preventive treatment gaps [ ], explanations for potential gender-related disparities remain speculative. Future research should explicitly investigate gender-specific risk factors and health care utilization to clarify these findings.
Our analysis revealed significant regional variations in the epidemiology of TIA, with the highest incidence rate in the Americas (81 per 100,000). Owing to the limited data from Africa and Oceania (only one study per region), reliable regional estimates could not be made, which also restricted our ability to provide a truly “global” weighted average. These findings suggest that the significant differences in the prevalence and incidence of TIA across continents cannot be attributed solely to geographical boundaries; methodological and health care system differences likely play key role. For instance, Vaartjes et al. [ ] reported that the comprehensive stroke registry system in the Netherlands records more TIA cases than countries without integrated primary care networks do, suggesting that health care accessibility and monitoring infrastructure may inflate the incidence rate in resource-rich regions. In contrast, Kamal et al. [ ] emphasized that the lower reported incidence in Pakistan might reflect underdiagnosis in rural areas where neuroimaging is scarce and symptom awareness is low. Pathophysiological differences (e.g., higher intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis in Asian patients than in those with extracranial carotid artery disease in Western populations [ ]) may also influence the presentation and diagnosis of TIA. However, this meta-analysis did not stratify by aetiologic subtype; thus, this remains an untested hypothesis. Future studies should incorporate subtype stratification to clarify whether regional differences reflect true epidemiological variation or diagnostic heterogeneity.
Subgroup analyses over time revealed that the prevalence of TIAs increased between 1974 and 2010 but decreased in recent years. This is consistent with the results mentioned in Kleindorfer’s study [ ]. One possible reason is that effective secondary prevention strategies are important means of reducing recurrence, disability, and death in patients. The field of secondary prevention of ischaemic stroke and TIA has been enriched with abundant evidence-based medicine, and guidelines and consensus statements have been issued or updated in various countries [ ]. In addition, the shift from time-based to tissue-based definitions has reduced overdiagnosis. Studies have shown that the use of DWI-MRI results in the reclassification of some TIA patients as stroke cases, which reduces the estimated annual incidence of transient ischaemic attacks in the United States by 33% and increases the estimated annual incidence of stroke by 7% [ ]. Therefore, the incidence of TIA is lower than that reported in previous studies conducted before the tissue-based definition was established. The impact of different diagnostic criteria on the epidemiology of TIA is significant. Studies on prevalence have shown that the prevalence rate diagnosed by “imaging” is significantly lower than that based only on “symptoms”. This highlights the importance of standardized diagnostic approaches for cross-study comparability. In contrast, diagnostic criteria did not affect incidence estimates, possibly because of differences in how cross-sectional (prevalence) and longitudinal (incidence) measures capture TIA frequency.
It is worth noting that the incidence rate of TIA revealed by this systematic review differs from that reported in some studies based on specific populations and using high-intensity active surveillance. Morgenstern et al. [ ] reported an exceptionally high crude annual incidence rate of 239 per 100,000 in Mexico, which is significantly higher than the global pooled incidence rate of 54 per 100,000. This discrepancy can mainly be attributed to the significant differences in research methodology. This study employed an extremely strict combined active and passive surveillance system, which achieved much higher completeness of case detection than conventional passive surveillance or studies relying on medical records did; thus, the results were closer to revealing the true incidence rate of TIA in the population. Additionally, the study focused on Mexican Americans, a specific high-risk group that has been proven to have a higher underlying risk of cerebrovascular disease. This case strongly indicates that routine surveillance data likely underestimate the true burden of TIA by a substantial margin. Future studies aimed at accurately assessing disease burden should adopt more comprehensive and active surveillance strategies as much as possible.

## Limitations
This review benefits from a comprehensive literature search; a rigorous methodological approach; and the inclusion of large, population-based surveys, which enhances the representativeness of our findings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, significant heterogeneity persisted across the included studies, even within subgroups, indicating the influence of unmeasured factors. Second, the variability in TIA diagnostic criteria (e.g., symptom-based vs. imaging-based definitions) directly impacts the comparability and pooling of results. Third, as observational studies, the included studies are appropriate for estimating prevalence and incidence but may have been subject to residual confounding and incomplete case ascertainment. Fourth, the restriction of the scope to studies published in English or Chinese may have introduced language bias by excluding relevant research in other languages. Fifth, the uneven geographical distribution of data—with limited studies from Africa, Oceania, and parts of Asia—constrains the global generalizability of our estimates. Finally, evidence of potential publication bias was detected among prevalence studies, although sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary findings.

## Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis study offers a contemporary overview of the global epidemiology of TIA, revealing a pooled prevalence of 1.95% and an incidence of 54 per 100,000 person-years. Our findings underscore that the reported frequency of TIA is profoundly shaped by diagnostic criteria, with imaging-based definitions yielding significantly lower prevalence estimates. Age is a paramount risk factor, while gender differences appear to be minimal. Significant geographical and temporal trends were identified, although these are complicated by heterogeneous data sources and potential under-ascertainment. Moving forward, we recommend the following: (1) where feasible, future research should adopt tissue-based (imaging) diagnostic criteria or, at minimum, clearly report the diagnostic approach used to enhance comparability across studies; (2) public health initiatives should prioritize TIA awareness and screening for older adults and in regions with high estimated burden; (3) investment in enhanced surveillance systems, particularly in underrepresented regions such as Africa and Oceania, is needed to obtain a more accurate and equitable global picture of the burden of TIA.
