ZhouWenkai ChenCan QiJiaxing ChenMengsha YuanZhenglin MiaoJiani ChenJiaxin JiangDaixi YangMengya DuYuxia CaoKexin WuXiaoyue YouYue ChenDingmo QuRongrong https://orcid.org/0000-0002-0147-297XYangShigui Seasonal influenza, according to the WHO, is estimated to cause 1 billion infections and 300 000–6 50 000 respiratory deaths annually. The most devastating influenza pandemic in human history resulted in the deaths of 40 million people worldwide. Respiratory pathogens primarily spread through droplets and aerosols. Many pathogens can also survive outside the body for extended periods, on surfaces such as contaminated objects or faeces, increasing the likelihood of hand-based transmission through interpersonal contact (eg, handshakes) or daily touch. 
Although mask-wearing has been widely studied, handwashing remains under-researched despite its comparable efficacy. This study instead focuses on the equally crucial yet understudied aspect of handwashing behaviour. Beyond its crucial role in infection control, the practice of handwashing is vital to global health and socioeconomic development. Practising handwashing can effectively remove or disrupt pathogens on the hands, reducing the risk of transmission. During the early stages of an epidemic or pandemic, when specific pathogen detection methods and targeted vaccines are unavailable, non-pharmaceutical interventions like handwashing play a critical role in mitigating transmission risk. Existing studies have revealed that daily habitual handwashing (defined as self-reported or observed compliance with recommended handwashing frequency and technique) can reduce COVID-19 incidence by nearly 50% and lower the risk of community-acquired respiratory infections by 16%. In contrast, key-moment handwashing, which focuses on targeted hygiene practices immediately after suspected exposure (eg, after handshaking, touching contaminated surfaces). During VRID pandemics/epidemics, both habitual daily handwashing and opportunistic key-moment handwashing are highly effective, yet distinct, strategies for reducing disease spread.
Current research on handwashing behaviour and VRIDs has several limitations. Primarily, most studies were temporally concentrated within the COVID-19 period, failing to provide comparative analyses of handwashing compliance across different epidemics/pandemics of VRIDs. Second, while existing investigations have examined individual-level determinants of handwashing behaviour (eg, gender and educational attainment), there remained a significant gap in research examining the potential macro-level factors that influence compliance with handwashing during VRID epidemics/pandemics. Third, existing studies have not systematically compared adherence patterns and determinants between habitual daily handwashing and opportunistic key-moment handwashing during VRID epidemics/pandemics.
In this study, our aim was to assess global and regional handwashing adherence during VRID epidemics/pandemics, while exploring factors for facilitating handwashing adherence and its impact on incidence and death of COVID-19. This study can provide evidence-based support for differentiated hand hygiene interventions targeting populations with distinct characteristics.

## Method

## Study design
Our systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following the standardised guidelines for systematic reviews. We completed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses checklist ( ). Study protocols were formerly registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024499090).

## Definition of handwashing adherence
This study assessed the global and regional rates of daily handwashing, key-moment hand washing behaviour and acceptability of handwashing behaviour. The definition of daily habitual handwashing was operationally defined according to previous research and the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guideline, incorporating three measurable criteria: (1) self-reported frequency exceeding five times per day, (2) self-reporting habitual frequency as ‘often’ or higher or (3) practising handwashing for more than 20 s per occurrence. Key-moment handwashing was defined as hygiene practices performed following exposure to pathogens incorporating universally applicable scenarios: (1) touching contaminated objects, surfaces or public installations; (2) when hands are visibly dirty; (3) after handshaking; (4) returning from public spaces; (5) when outside the home; (6) after blowing nose, coughing or sneezing; (7) before meals or handling food; (8) after using the toilet; (9) after touching garbage. Handshake avoidance was defined as the complete abstention of any handshaking behaviour during the epidemics/pandemics. All behavioural outcomes were quantified as proportions, calculated by dividing the number of adherent individuals by the total study population.

## Search strategy and selection criteria
A comprehensive literature review was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and SCOPUS, covering each database from inception to 5 January 2024, with an update on 2 April 2024. The search strategy included terms related to handwashing, (“influenza” or “influenza” or “respiratory infectious disease” or “COVID-19” or “respiratory syncytial virus”) and (“adheren*” or “accept*” or “willing*”) and (“cross-sectional stud*” or “observational stud*”) without language restrictions. The complete search strategies were displayed in . Inclusion criteria were: (1) observational studies (excluding trials and interventions); (2) availability of raw data for synthesising handwashing related outcomes; (3) focusing on the aforementioned VRIDs; (4) explicitly described the use of probabilistic sampling methods (eg, simple random sampling, stratified sampling and multistage sampling) to ensure representativeness of results. We excluded several types of studies from our analysis: case reports/studies, modelling and simulation studies, studies that only presented graphical summaries without clear statistical evaluations or outputs, ecological studies offering descriptive summaries without detailed data, non-empirical studies (such as commentaries, editorials and government reports), as well as other reviews. Studies reporting both daily handwashing, key-moment handwashing behaviour and acceptability of handwashing were treated as separate datasets for analysis. Studies involving healthcare workers, such as physicians, nurses, pharmacists and laboratory technicians, were excluded, aiming to focus on handwashing in the general and other populations.

## Data attraction and quality assessment
After screening titles and abstracts for relevance, four independent researchers (WZ, JQ, MC and JM) reviewed the full texts of the selected articles and extracted key information. Prior to formal data extraction, all research team members participated in a standardised training programme to ensure consistency in data extraction procedures and quality assessment of included studies. The extracted data included: (1) basic information: article title, author details, publication year, journal name, country (region) of study population, Sociodemographic Index (SDI) level, continental information (Europe, Asia, North America, South America and Africa), study design, study period, sampling method, sample size and mean age of participants; (2) The raw data for calculating acceptability of handwashing behaviours, daily handwashing and key-moment handwashing and (3) factors influencing handwashing adherence, including age groups (≤14 years, 15–59 years, ≥60 years), gender, setting (urban or rural), study population (general population, students, elderly, pregnant women, etc), type of hand hygiene materials used (plain water, soap or hand sanitiser) and period of different VRID epidemics/pandemics. For studies meeting the inclusion criteria with missing or incomplete data, especially where variables were lacking for subgroup analyses, we supplemented unavailable parameters using relevant country-level indicators from authoritative sources, such as the SDI levels, per capita income and comparable demographic or socioeconomic metrics. All the studies and citations were managed in EndNote (V.X21). We adapted the quality assessment scale recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, and since we included observational studies, we evaluated the representativeness of the samples in the included studies, as greater representativeness in the original studies leads to smaller bias in the rates. Any uncertainties related to screening, data extraction or quality assessment were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting the study’s principal investigator (Professor SY) to ensure consensus and high-quality decision-making.

## Data analysis
The pooled adherence rates for daily hand washing, willingness to practise hand washing and avoidance of handshaking behaviours were analysed using logistic transformations. Key moment handwashing rates, which did not meet the normality assumptions required for other transformations, were analysed using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation. After pooling the estimates, the combined point estimates and their 95% CIs were then back transformed into proportions and plotting. The I2 statistic was used to assess the heterogeneity which was calculated by the DerSimonian-Laird method. 
Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify factors influencing handwashing adherence, including gender, age, study population, type of VRIDs, prevalence periods, type of hand hygiene materials used, SDI levels and settings. All subgroup results will be presented in forest plots. The overall results for handshake avoidance will be presented alongside those for daily hand washing. Sample sizes were divided into high, medium-high, medium-low and low tertiles, using the 25th, 50th and 75th percentile scores. Sensitivity analysis, which omitted the studies with low sample sizes (low tertiles) and low-quality scores (<5 points), was conducted to evaluate the robustness and reliability of overall pooled rates.
After obtaining overall estimates of handwashing-related outcomes, a univariate correlation analysis was conducted between adherence rates for key moment handwashing and potential influencing factors. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme provides data on Water Supply, Sanitation and Hygiene. The Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker provides official information on government policy indicators in response to COVID-19, including overall Government Response Index, Containment and Health Index, Stringency Index and Economic Support Index, each with specific definitions and calculation methods. Due to overlap among the indicators selected for the four government-level variables, we included two completely independent indicators (Policy Stringency Index and Economic Support Index) in analysis. Univariate Pearson correlation was conducted between adherence rates for key moment handwashing and each indicator. Information on deaths from COVID-19 from different countries in 2020 and 2021 was obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 database. Country-level covariates encompassing sociodemographic factors were sourced from the World Bank public database. Indicators significantly correlated in univariate analysis were included in generalised linear models (GLMs) to obtain adjusted effect estimates on COVID-19-related outcomes. We also compared the impact of daily habitual handwashing and key-moment handwashing on COVID-19 related outcomes in countries with low handwashing adherence (rates less than 80%). All the data analysis and visualisation were performed by R V.4.3.3.

## Patient and Public Involvement
This study was a systematic analysis based on publicly available data. Our work involved neither the diagnosis nor treatment of any specific condition nor any primary data collection or direct interaction with participants. Therefore, no patients or members of the public were involved in formulating research questions or outcomes, collecting or analysing the data, interpreting the results or writing the manuscript.

## Result

## Searching results
The workflow of this study was depicted in . After searching four electronic databases, we identified 9887 records, and after excluding duplicates, 6435 unique articles remained. Four independent researchers excluded 4182 articles unrelated to the research objectives based on their titles and abstracts. The remaining 2253 articles were subject to full-text screening, and 108 articles were included in the meta-analysis, generating 227 datasets. Among these, 126 datasets were reported for daily frequent handwashing, 79 for handwashing at key moments, 12 for handwashing acceptability and 10 for compliance with handshaking avoidance behaviour. According to our refined grading system, the overall quality of the studies was medium. A detailed search strategy was shown in . ## Adherence to handwashing by countries/regions
The specific results on adherence to handwashing across different countries or regions were presented in . The global and regional pooled rate of daily handwashing during VRID epidemics/pandemics was 72.23% (95% CI 66.95% to 76.95%). The highest adherence rate was observed in Ireland (97.64%, 95% CI 97.03% to 98.13%). Additionally, the pooled rate of handwashing during VRID epidemics/pandemics was 65.11% (95% CI 59.74% to 70.28%) ( ). The highest pooled rates with handwashing at key moments were observed in Poland (80.03%, 95% CI 64.66% to 89.77%). Furthermore, the global pooled rate of handwashing acceptability was 90.01% (95% CI 83.73% to 94.05%) ( ). Nigeria reported the highest acceptability rate (97.38%, 95% CI 94.84% to 98.68%).
 

## Factors associated with daily frequent handwashing
The results of subgroup analysis on daily frequent handwashing were displayed in . Men (71.87%, 95% CI 79.59% to 80.90%) exhibited lower adherence rates compared with women (78.16%, 95% CI 67.62% to 87.13%). Individuals aged 60 years and older demonstrated better adherence compared with individuals aged 15–59 years (77.56% vs 67.64%). The general population showed lower adherence compared with police officers (73.58% vs 94.01%), with public transportation workers exhibiting the lowest compliance (18.15%). Regarding different handwashing materials, the adherence of using hand sanitisers was lower compared with the adherence of handwashing with plain water (51.50% vs 75.25%). Pooled rate was lowest in the African region (59.46%, 95% CI 50.73% to 67.68%), particularly in regions with low SDI scores (51.20%, 95% CI 43.76% to 58.59%). Participants who strictly adhered to avoiding handshaking behaviour during VRID epidemics/pandemics were 54.50% (95% CI 37.43% to 70.58%).
 

## Factors associated with handwashing at key moments
The subgroup analysis results of handwashing adherence at key moments were displayed in . The age group of 15–59 years showed higher adherence rates compared with those under 14 years old and those aged 60 years and above (71.67% vs 46.03% vs 37.50%). The pooled handwashing adherence rate was the lowest after handshaking (36.40%, 95% CI 18.49% to 56.52%), with handwashing after touching garbage (88.19%, 95% CI 84.06% to 91.76%), after visible dirty (87.40%, 95% CI 78.90% to 93.97%) and after returning from outside home/public spaces (75.06%, 95% CI 65.68% to 83.31%) showing the higher adherence. Ageing population, mothers and public transportation workers exhibited poor performance, with compliance rates of 22.86% (95% CI 16.77% to 29.58%), 33.08% (95% CI 23.61% to 43.30%) and 59.71% (95% CI 54.96% to 64.38%), respectively. During the COVID-19 pandemic, compliance was significantly higher compared with the H1N1 influenza pandemic of 2009–2010 and normal influenza seasons (72.02% vs 31.33% vs 35.52%). The handwashing adherence at key moments was the lowest in the African region (54.35%, 95% CI 42.29% to 66.16%) and in low SDI regions (55.02%, 95% CI 43.82% to 65.98%).

## Factors associated with acceptability of handwashing
The data on the acceptability of handwashing were shown in . Overall, acceptability of handwashing was relatively high, with North America and Europe exhibiting higher acceptability rates at 94.15% (95% CI 89.75% to 96.73%) and 93.88% (95% CI 90.38% to 96.17%), respectively. The acceptability during the COVID-19 pandemic surpassed that of regular influenza seasons (90.20% vs 88.24%). Police officer (97.38%, 95% CI 94.84% to 98.68%), public transportation workers (97.60%, 95% CI 95.60% to 98.70%) and outpatients (94.04%, 95% CI 92.19% to 95.48%) showed higher willingness, with the lowest acceptability observed in low SDI countries (86.97%, 95% CI 73.64% to 94.10%).

## Sensitivity analysis
When all the studies were included in the meta-analysis, the pooled rate of frequent handwashing was 72.30% (95% CI 67.50% to 76.63%) ( ). When the studies with low sample sizes were omitted, the overall frequent handwashing rate was estimated to be 75.45% (95% CI 69.97% to 80.22%) ( ); the 95% CIs of the pooled rates overlapped. When the studies with low quality scores were omitted, the overall frequent handwashing rate was estimated to be pooled at 72.57% (67.55% to 77.07%) ( ); the 95% CIs of the pooled rates overlapped. This indicated that the results of pooled rates were robust and reliable.
 

## The impact of handwashing at key moments on the incidence and death of COVID-19
The results of the univariate Pearson correlation analysis between influencing factor and rate of key-moment handwashing behaviour were displayed in . The Government Economic Support Index, Stringency Index, coverage of handwashing facility with soap and water in households and improved latrine coverage showed significant positive correlations with handwashing at key moments, while coverage of handwashing facility without soap and water showed negative correlation with key-moment handwashing (p<0.1). We incorporated national-level indicators, including socioeconomic status (gross domestic product (GDP) per capita), population age structure (proportion aged 65 years and above), health system resource capacity (number of physicians per 100 000 population) and the intensity of governmental pandemic control measures (Stringency Index), into the GLM ( ). Model diagnostics revealed that all included variables exhibited acceptable levels of collinearity (variance inflation factor (VIF)<5 for all variables) and indicated that none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance. The results indicated that a higher rate of handwashing at key moments has been found to significantly reduce the COVID-19 incidence (β=−151.1, p=0.010), death of COVID-19 (β=−0.066, p<0.001) and other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths (β=−0.112, p<0.001). In countries with relatively low handwashing adherence (rate <80%), our comparison of two handwashing strategies revealed that handwashing at key moments significantly reduced COVID-19 incidence (β=−400.5, p=0.020) ( ).
 

## Discussion
Our study found that during VRID epidemics/pandemics, a notable discrepancy persisted between the acceptability of handwashing and its actual implementation, with the latter being relatively low. Public transportation workers consistently underperform in both daily habitual and key-moment handwashing practices. More than half of the participants persisted in engaging in handshaking, with the practising rate of handwashing following handshaking remaining alarmingly low. Handwashing at key moments could significantly reduce the incidence and death of COVID-19 and other COVID-19 pandemic-related deaths.
A substantial gap remained between the acceptability of handwashing practices and their actual implementation, with the latter being relatively low. Previous studies consistently report gaps between attitude and practice regarding hand hygiene, with influential factors at the individual level including educational attainment and personal income levels. Environmental and health resources also significantly influence hand hygiene behaviours. In many low- and middle-income countries, limited access to essential facilities like soap and water substantially impedes handwashing practices. Religious and cultural factors also influence hand hygiene practices to varying degrees. Many religions, including Islam, incorporate handwashing at several critical moments—such as before and after meals and after handling any soiled items—considering it a sacred act. 
Public transportation workers exhibit poor performance in both daily habitual and key-moment handwashing, significantly increasing the risk of contracting VRIDs. Recent data from California during the COVID-19 pandemic indicate that the morbidity and mortality rates among these workers were 1.5 times higher than those recorded across all industries. A parallel analysis in the UK, involving 14.3 million adults, revealed that taxi and bus drivers experienced the highest COVID-19 death rates within the occupational spectrum. Due to frequent interactions with diverse groups of people, public transportation workers face increased exposure to VRIDs, heightening their susceptibility and elevating the risk of pathogen transmission. Public transportation workers’ low handwashing adherence can be partly attributed to the lack of immediate and adequate handwashing facilities. Unlike healthcare settings where alcohol-based hand hygiene products are widely available, access to such facilities on buses and at transit stops is often limited or non-existent, and the quality of existing facilities is generally poor. Additionally, public transport operators frequently work under tight schedules with minimal breaks between routes, leaving little time for proper hand hygiene practices. Moreover, handwashing adherence is strongly influenced by behavioural intention. Many public transportation workers may not perceive their hands as a major vector for disease transmission during routine tasks—such as handling fares or touching controls—which contributes to lower compliance rates. Furthermore, organisations often prioritise operational efficiency over preventive health measures. Inadequate health education and the absence of mandatory, regular training result in hand hygiene being frequently overlooked, highlighting the need for targeted educational interventions. Considering these findings, it is critical to prioritise and tailor prevention strategies to safeguard public transport workers in the event of future VRID epidemics/pandemics. Proposed measures should include the strategic placement of hand sanitisers within public transport vehicles, complemented by comprehensive hygiene education campaigns that are specifically designed to bridge the intention-behaviour gap. These efforts should be integrated into broader occupational health frameworks to ensure sustainability and institutional support. More than half of the research subjects persisted in engaging in handshaking behaviour, with the handwashing adherence following handshaking remaining relatively low. The handshake, a simple social action, is filled with meanings that go beyond its physical representation. A simple touch of hands can directly convey a range of social emotions, improving negotiation outcomes by expressing prosocial intentions. During interpersonal contact, contaminated hands play a crucial role in the transmission of infectious diseases, specifically through direct contact with an infected individual or indirectly by touching surfaces contaminated with transient micro-organism. Nevertheless, handwashing practices following handshakes are inadequate. Current research on the factors influencing handwashing behaviour after handshakes is limited. Possible influencing factors include the accessibility of convenient handwashing facilities, and the social implications that handwashing after a handshake might convey a sense of disgust or rejection to the other party. Moving forward, it is essential to invest in enhanced public awareness campaigns and effective health education initiatives focused on handshaking behaviours and subsequent handwashing practices.
Environmental health and hygiene resources, particularly the availability of basic handwashing facilities in households, play a critical role in shaping handwashing behaviour. Improved access to functional facilities equipped with soap and water facilitates consistent handwashing practices. However, more than 2 billion people worldwide still lack such basic handwashing facilities, a gap that remains especially pronounced in low-income countries. According to the Hygiene Service Ladder, service levels progress from no facility to limited service and eventually to basic service. Evidence suggests that limited hand hygiene services may be nearly as ineffective as having no facilities at all in promoting handwashing behaviour. Our findings indicate that in low- and middle-income countries, health policies aimed at upgrading limited services to basic services may yield greater improvements in handwashing adherence at key times than those focused solely on expanding limited-service coverage. Furthermore, our study demonstrates that latrine improvement is associated with increased handwashing compliance at key moments. Improved sanitation facilities often include integrated handwashing stations, which encourage and simplify handwashing after toilet use or exposure to contaminants. Nevertheless, the high cost of constructing such facilities remains a major barrier to adoption, with higher-income households disproportionately benefiting from improved infrastructure. Although some countries, such as Ethiopia, have introduced subsidies for public toilet upgrades, these rarely extend to household-level facilities. There is thus a pressing need for more financial support and flexible implementation strategies to enhance accessibility at the household level. Public health campaigns play an essential role in raising awareness and reinforcing the importance of handwashing. These campaigns, when culturally adapted and consistently implemented, could effectively increase knowledge, shift attitudes and promote behaviour change across diverse regions. When combined with improved infrastructure, such initiatives help mitigate the impact of socioeconomic inequalities and ensure that handwashing becomes a sustainable and widely adopted practice.
Key-moment handwashing is a vital but often neglected practice, as people frequently touch their faces or transfer pathogens to surfaces without realising it. Soap and water effectively remove micro-organisms by breaking down the oils that harbour pathogens, underscoring the need for improved health education to promote adherence. Our analysis indicates that in regions with generally low handwashing adherence, promoting targeted handwashing strategies at key moments (such as after contact with contaminants or before eating) may demonstrate higher cost-effectiveness and feasibility compared with habitual handwashing campaigns. This is primarily because such strategies feature clear requirements and context-specific scenarios, making them easier to implement and adopt in resource-limited settings and more likely to be remembered and adopted by residents. However, their effectiveness highly depends on the accurate identification of key transmission pathways and targeted public education. In low-adherence areas, designing interventions for handwashing at key moments by incorporating local cultural practices (eg, providing handwashing facilities before communal meals or religious rituals) could significantly enhance their acceptance and impact. The effectiveness of handwashing strategies could be influenced by cultural and infrastructural factors across different epidemics/pandemics. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, there were substantial global disparities in access to water, soap and handwashing facilities, which directly constrained the implementation and outcomes of handwashing strategies. Additionally, cultural beliefs and social norms (such as varying understandings of disease severity or the emphasis placed on collective vs individual health) profoundly shaped individuals’ willingness to accept and adhere to handwashing recommendations. Compared with outbreaks like H1N1, the heightened global attention during COVID-19 may have partially transcended certain cultural barriers. However, in some regions, effectiveness may have diminished due to information overload or hygiene fatigue. The lack of infrastructure remains a persistent challenge that likely undermined the impact of any handwashing strategy in areas where it was most needed. Future epidemic response strategies should place greater emphasis on adapting to local contexts, combining hardware infrastructure development with culturally sensitive communication approaches to enhance the resilience and effectiveness of handwashing interventions.

## Limitations
Several inherent limitations in our study need to be unveiled. First, to ensure the representativeness of our results, we included literature employing probability sampling methods, which may lead to the exclusion of studies on certain diseases. Second, as an observational analysis based largely on self-reported data, the findings are inherently limited by the quality of the original data, including recall biases, social desirability biases and potential heterogeneity in how outcomes were defined and assessed across included studies. These limitations may affect the reliability and validity of the pooled results and restrict the ability to establish causality. Third, we incorporated national-level indicators such as GDP per capita, physician density and policy stringency indices. These variables do not fully capture subnational heterogeneity in healthcare infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions or local implementation of public health measures. These unmeasured geographical variations may affect both handwashing behaviour and pandemic outcomes and may limit the generalisability of the associations we observed across different regions. Fourth, due to data limitations, our model relied on early pandemic data and could not compare the role of handwashing across different VRIDs. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive datasets and indicators to address this gap and could further explore the behavioural mechanisms underlying handwashing adherence—such as the role of social norms, the influence of digital health interventions and the impact of environmental factors.

## Conclusion
Our study discovered a significant gap between the acceptability to practice hand hygiene and its actual implementation during viral respiratory pandemics. Public transportation workers exhibit poor performance in handwashing behaviours. Handshaking persisted as a common behaviour, yet handwashing rates following this behaviour were alarmingly low. Augmented key moments in handwashing adherence are associated with reduction in incidence and death of COVID-19, offering crucial insights for policy formulation and public health guidelines to better prepare for future outbreaks of respiratory viruses and influenza seasons. Further research could also explore behavioural mechanisms underlying handwashing adherence, such as the role of social norms, the influence of digital health interventions or the impact of environmental factors.
