## Abstract

## Background 
Percutaneous laser disc decompression (PLDD) is a minimally invasive treatment option for carefully selected patients with lumbar disc herniations (LDH) whose condition does not respond to conservative therapy but who may not require surgical decompression. Although PLDD has been increasingly adopted, reported outcomes vary widely across studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of PLDD in adults with LDH. 

## Methods 
A systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted through November 2025. Adult PLDD studies reporting clinical outcomes were included. Random-effects models pooled continuous and binary outcomes. Primary outcomes were visual analog scale (VAS) improvement and clinical success. Secondary outcomes were Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) change, reoperation rates, and complications. Heterogeneity was assessed with I2, and risk of bias with the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions. 

## Results 
Fifteen publications reporting 14 unique PLDD cohorts were included. Eight studies (n=506) reported VAS outcomes, showing a pooled pain reduction of 4.33 points on a 0-10 scale (95% confidence interval [CI] 3.03-5.64; I2=98.8%). Three studies (n=128) reported ODI outcomes, with a pooled improvement of 20.1 points, but with substantial uncertainty (95% CI −21.7 to 61.9; I2=99.6%). Five studies (n=298) reported clinical success, yielding a pooled success rate of 73.8% (95% CI 55.9%-86.2%; I2=91.0%). The pooled reoperation rate across eight cohorts (n=650) was 10.3% (95% CI 6.3%-16.5%; I2=65.8%), and the pooled complication rate across ten studies (n=750) was 1.3% (95% CI 0.4%-3.9%; I2=1.0%). Most studies had moderate risk of bias due to non-randomized, single-arm designs. 

## Conclusions 
PLDD is associated with meaningful pain reduction and moderate clinical success with a very low complication rate in selected patients with LDH. Functional outcomes and reoperation rates varied across studies. Further high-quality comparative research is needed to better define long-term results and optimal patient selection.

## Introduction
Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is one of the most common causes of low-back pain affecting approximately 1%-3% of adults each year ). Although most patients show improvement under conservative management, 2%-10% of patients continue to experience persistent radicular pain or functional impairment , ). For these individuals, surgical intervention has long been considered the standard of care. Surgical procedures such as microdiscectomy or lumbar endoscopic decompression achieve symptom relief in 80%-90% of cases , ). However, these procedures require anesthesia, involve soft-tissue and bony manipulation, and carry risks including dural tears, recurrent herniation, and postoperative instability ). This has caused a growing interest in minimally invasive alternatives for patients in whom conservative therapy fails but who may not require open or endoscopic decompression.
Percutaneous laser disc decompression (PLDD) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative that bridges the gap between conservative management and operative decompression ). The technique involves the percutaneous introduction of a laser fiber into the nucleus pulposus under fluoroscopic guidance, which allows controlled thermal vaporization of disc material and reduces intradiscal pressure ). With this method, PLDD alleviates nerve root compression while preserving surrounding soft tissues and spinal stability ). The procedure is typically performed under local anesthesia, requires only a small percutaneous entry point, and is associated with minimal perioperative morbidity and short recovery times ). Given its expanding global adoption, PLDD has become an appealing option for selected patients with contained LDH who seek less invasive treatment.
Despite its expanding use, the evidence supporting PLDD remains inconsistent. Studies differ substantially in patient selection, laser parameters, and follow-up duration, and prior reviews often mixed PLDD with non-laser intradiscal procedures or included stenosis cases, limiting their applicability to contained LDH. These inconsistencies highlight the need for an updated synthesis for PLDD outcomes. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of PLDD by quantifying pain and disability improvement, clinical success, complication rates, and the need for reoperation.

## Materials and Methods

## Study design
This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 recommendations ). The review aimed to synthesize current evidence on the clinical effectiveness and safety of PLDD for LDHs. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration identification: CRD420251233871). All phases of the review were performed independently by two different reviewers who resolved disagreements through consensus.

## Literature search
A systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was performed from database inception up to November 24, 2025. Structured search strategies were developed using combinations of MeSH terms and keywords related to PLDD and LDH. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in the . Reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible publications. Only full-text human studies published in English were considered. Biomechanical, cadaveric, technical, and nonclinical reports, in addition to conference abstracts and studies evaluating alternative intradiscal therapies without extractable PLDD-specific outcomes, were excluded.

## Eligibility criteria
Studies were eligible for inclusion if they examined adults with symptomatic LDH treated using PLDD. The review was limited to studies published from 2013 onward because this period reflects the modern era of PLDD, characterized by advances in laser technology, improved procedural technique, and more uniform patient selection. These developments have yielded more standardized outcome reporting suitable for quantitative synthesis. Eligible studies included prospective or retrospective cohort studies, comparative clinical studies, and case series with at least ten patients. PLDD performed with any laser system or procedural technique was acceptable, but only if the study reported at least one extractable clinical outcome, such as visual analog scale (VAS) scores, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) values, or binary end points including treatment success, reoperation, or complication rates. A minimum follow-up duration of three months was required.
Studies were not excluded from the systematic review solely on the basis of evaluating non-standard PLDD indications or comparator interventions. However, studies were excluded from quantitative pooling when they did not report extractable PLDD-specific outcomes. For example, studies assessing PLDD for lumbar spinal stenosis or those investigating alternative intradiscal therapies were not eligible for meta-analysis. Additional exclusions applied to studies lacking separable PLDD data within mixed-intervention cohorts and to small case series with fewer than ten patients.
Most included studies explicitly limited enrolment to patients with contained lumbar disc herniation, defined radiologically by preservation of the posterior annulus and absence of free disc extrusion. However, several studies did not clearly report disc containment status. These studies were included if they otherwise met eligibility criteria and evaluated PLDD for lumbar disc herniation, but lack of explicit containment reporting was considered a potential source of clinical heterogeneity.

## Study selection and data extraction
Two reviewers independently screened all records at the title/abstract and full-text stages, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Data were extracted into a standardized spreadsheet. Clinical outcomes were collected as means with standard deviations, and binary end points were recorded when available. Follow-up duration was extracted for all studies. VAS and Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) scores were harmonized to a 0-10 scale. For comparative studies, only outcome data from the PLDD arm were used.
To avoid duplication of patient data, all included studies were screened for overlapping recruitment periods, identical sample sizes, and matching demographic or baseline characteristics. When multiple publications reported outcomes from the same cohort, only the most complete or longest follow-up dataset was retained for quantitative synthesis, whereas earlier or secondary reports were used solely for supplementary qualitative information. A known overlap was identified between Brouwer et al. ) (2015) and Brouwer et al. ) (2017). Therefore, only the more complete dataset was included in the meta-analysis.

## Procedural description
All included studies performed PLDD through a percutaneous posterolateral approach under fluoroscopic guidance. After local anesthesia, a spinal needle was advanced into the nucleus pulposus, followed by insertion of the laser fiber for controlled intradiscal vaporization. ## Risk-of-bias assessment
The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool ). Although a small number of studies described allocation procedures suggestive of randomization, none met the criteria for low-risk randomized controlled trials owing to limitations in allocation concealment, blinding, or outcome assessment. Therefore, ROBINS-I was applied uniformly across all included studies to ensure consistent evaluation. Each domain was assessed independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus. Overall judgments were categorized as low, moderate, or serious risk of bias. Potential reporting bias was considered separately through qualitative evaluation of selective outcome reporting, and publication bias was explored using visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry when applicable.

## Statistical analysis
All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1). Continuous outcomes, including VAS and ODI, were pooled as mean difference (MD) using random-effects models to account for clinical and methodological differences among studies. When standard deviations were missing, they were calculated from other reported summary statistics. Change scores were used when available. If not, final follow-up values were used.
Binary outcomes were pooled as proportions using a Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variances, particularly in studies with small samples or zero events. Pooled proportions were back-transformed using the inverse Freeman-Tukey method. Random-effects models were applied throughout using the meta package (functions metaprop and metamean) and the metafor package (rma). Fixed-effect estimates were generated for comparison but were not used as primary results.
Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 and τ2 statistics. Given the high degree of methodological and clinical variability, random-effects models were considered the most appropriate analytical approach. For outcomes with very high heterogeneity, pooled estimates were interpreted cautiously. Meta-regression analyses were performed to explore potential moderators but were considered exploratory owing to the limited number of available studies (k≤8).

## Results
A total of 506 records were identified through database searching, and an additional 10 studies were located through manual screening. After removal of duplicates and eligibility assessment, 15 publications reporting 14 unique PLDD cohorts were included. A detailed summary of the included studies is presented in , , - ). The study selection process is summarized in . Eight studies reported VAS outcomes; three reported ODI, and up to ten reported binary end points. All included studies evaluated PLDD for LDHs, and the median follow-up was 24 months (range 3-43.5 months).
 

## Visual analog score
Eight studies (n=506) reported baseline and follow-up VAS values. All cohorts indicated statistically significant reductions in pain, with mean improvements that ranged between 1.99 and 6.38 points. The random-effects model showed a pooled decrease of 4.33 points on a 0-10 scale (MD 4.33; 95% confidence interval [CI] 3.03-5.64; p<0.001). However, heterogeneity was very high (I2=98.8%), so the true effects likely differ substantially across studies, and the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution. Consistent with this variability, the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.45 to 8.22 points, indicating that pain reduction after PLDD may be modest in some settings and substantial in others ( ).
 When analyses were stratified by pain domain, four studies reported radicular (leg) pain and showed a pooled mean VAS improvement of 5.55 points (95% CI 4.06-7.03) under a random-effects model, with substantial heterogeneity (I2=97.0%). The corresponding prediction interval ranged from 2.28 to 8.81 points, indicating clinically meaningful pain reduction across most settings. In contrast, four studies reporting back pain showed a smaller pooled improvement of 3.11 points (95% CI 1.62-4.59), with a wider prediction interval (−0.18 to 6.39) and similarly high heterogeneity (I2=96.8%). These findings suggest that variability in reported pain outcomes may partly reflect differences in pain domain assessed rather than a uniform treatment effect.
When stratified by follow-up duration, pain reduction after PLDD was observed across short-term (≤6 months), mid-term (6-12 months), and long-term (>12 months) follow-up intervals. Under a random-effects model, pooled VAS improvement was 4.13 points (95% CI −1.22 to 9.48) at ≤6 months, 4.89 points (95% CI 4.64-5.14) at 6-12 months (single study), and 4.62 points (95% CI 0.18-9.06) at >12 months ( ). Heterogeneity remained high within each subgroup, and no statistically significant differences between follow-up strata were observed under the random-effects model. These analyses were considered exploratory given the small number of studies per subgroup.
 Meta-regression exploring follow-up duration (β=0.04 per month, p=0.43; R2=14.8%) and baseline VAS (β=0.16, p=0.78; R2=0.9%) did not identify significant moderators. These findings are consistent with the wide prediction intervals and follow-up-stratified analyses, suggesting that variability in pain outcomes is likely multifactorial rather than driven by any single measured variable. As such, these analyses were considered exploratory and limited by the small number of contributing studies (k=8).
In the subset of studies that included a comparator, between-group findings were heterogeneous and involved diverse alternative interventions, precluding formal pooling; therefore, comparative results were interpreted descriptively.

## Functional improvement
Three single-arm PLDD cohorts (n=128) reported ODI outcomes. Two studies indicated functional improvements of approximately 10 points, whereas another study showed a larger reduction of nearly 40 points. This reflects the differences in baseline disability across cohorts. The pooled analysis suggested an average ODI improvement of 20 points. However, the CI was wide and crossed zero (MD 20.1; 95% CI −21.7 to 61.9). Heterogeneity was high (I2=99.6%), so functional outcomes between studies were inconsistent. Consistent with this variability, the 95% prediction interval ranged from −63.4 to 103.6 points, suggesting that functional improvement after PLDD may vary widely between clinical settings.
Given the very small number of studies, extreme heterogeneity (I2=99.6%), and substantial variability in baseline ODI, this pooled estimate is highly uncertain and should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. A descriptive interpretation of individual study results is therefore more appropriate for understanding functional outcomes after PLDD.

## Clinical success
Five studies (n=298) reported dichotomous measures of treatment success after PLDD. Definitions of clinical success were inconsistent across studies, and in several cases were not explicitly reported ( ). Individual study success rates ranged from 38.8% to 87.5%, with most cohorts reporting favorable outcomes. The pooled success rate under a random-effects model was 73.8% (95% CI 55.9%-86.2%), which indicates that meaningful clinical improvement after PLDD was achieved in the patients. Heterogeneity was substantial (I2=91.0%), which reflects the expected variations in outcome definitions, follow-up duration, and patient selection across studies. This pooled estimate should be interpreted as a descriptive summary of reported success rates rather than a precise or uniform measure of treatment efficacy.

## Reoperation rate
Eight cohorts (n=650) provided data on reoperations. Reoperation rates varied between 5% and 31%, with the highest rate reported in a single small cohort ). The pooled reoperation rate was 10.3% (95% CI 6.3%-16.5%), corresponding to approximately one in ten patients requiring additional lumbar surgery after PLDD. Moderate heterogeneity was present (I2=65.8%). This is probably due to the differences in reoperation thresholds, surgical referral patterns, or follow-up duration among included studies.

## Complication rate
Ten studies (n=750) reported perioperative or postoperative complications. Only PLDD-for-LDH cohorts were included in this analysis. Most studies documented zero complications, and only minor adverse events were observed in the remaining cohorts. The pooled complication rate was 1.3% (95% CI 0.4%-3.9%), with minimal heterogeneity (I2=1.0%), indicating safe outcomes across studies. Although these findings suggest a low reported rate of procedure-related morbidity, they should be interpreted in the context of heterogeneous complication reporting and the predominance of retrospective observational data.

## Risk of bias
Overall risk of bias across the included studies was generally rated as moderate, mostly owing to the predominance of non-randomized and single-arm observational designs and the absence of methodologically robust randomized controlled trials. The most frequent concerns involved confounding, patient selection, and incomplete reporting of baseline characteristics, all of which are common limitations in small clinical cohorts of minimally invasive spine interventions. Even though no study was excluded on the basis of methodological quality, the ROBINS-I assessments indicated that the certainty of evidence should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for outcomes derived from uncontrolled designs. Nevertheless, the overall methodological quality was considered sufficient to allow inclusion in this review.

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis shows that PLDD is associated with meaningful pain reduction with a favorable safety profile in selected patients with contained LDH. Across 15 studies, PLDD is associated with a meaningful reduction in pain, with a pooled VAS improvement of 4.33 points on a 0-10 scale. These numbers exceed the minimal clinically important difference for lumbar radiculopathy and approach the pain relief typically reported after minimally invasive surgical decompression ). However, the wide prediction interval and extreme heterogeneity observed in pooled analyses indicate that the magnitude of pain reduction varies substantially across clinical settings and patient populations. Functional improvement followed a similar direction, although ODI results varied widely across studies, likely owing to the differences in baseline disability, chronicity of symptoms, and disc morphology. Furthermore, pain reduction was consistent across follow-up intervals ranging from 3 to 43 months. Notably, stratified analyses suggested greater and more consistent improvement in radicular pain than in axial back pain, which aligns with the proposed intradiscal decompression mechanism of PLDD and may partly explain the observed heterogeneity. Nevertheless, subgroup analyses by follow-up duration remained exploratory and underpowered, and persistent heterogeneity suggests that durability of benefit may depend on additional unmeasured factors such as disc morphology and symptom chronicity. Although functional improvements were less consistent, the overall pattern suggests that PLDD can reduce radicular pain with minimal morbidity in appropriately selected patients.
Importantly, most included cohorts either explicitly limited enrolment to contained lumbar disc herniations or did not report disc morphology in sufficient detail to exclude non-contained cases. From a practical standpoint, the included evidence suggests that PLDD is most appropriately considered for carefully selected patients with radiologically confirmed contained lumbar disc herniation who experience persistent radicular symptoms despite an adequate course of conservative management. Most cohorts excluded patients with severe or progressive neurologic deficits, marked spinal instability, or large extruded or sequestrated disc fragments. Accordingly, optimal candidates in clinical practice are those with preserved neurologic function, predominantly radicular pain, and imaging findings consistent with annular containment, for whom a minimally invasive intradiscal decompression strategy may reasonably be considered before operative decompression.
Dichotomous outcomes reinforced the clinical picture of PLDD, with approximately 74% of patients who met the definitions of treatment success, whereas the reoperation rate remained low at approximately 10%. In addition, the procedure showed a very low complication rate of approximately 1%, which supports PLDD's good safety profile.
Because most included studies were single-arm cohorts, pre-post improvements may reflect natural history, regression to the mean, and contextual/placebo effects. Accordingly, findings are presented descriptively rather than as evidence of comparative efficacy.
Earlier systematic reviews of PLDD consistently showed the limited and heterogeneous nature of the available evidence. Singh et al. , ) reviewed 10 observational PLDD cohorts (n=2,447) and reported clinical success rates ranging from 56% to 87%, but were unable to pool VAS, ODI, reoperation, or complication outcomes owing to inconsistent reporting and methodological heterogeneity. Their review concluded that PLDD appeared safe but that evidence for its effectiveness remained limited because of non-standardized techniques, lack of controlled studies, and variable follow-up , ). In contrast, the present analysis focuses on post-2013 studies with more consistent reporting, enabling quantitative synthesis across continuous and binary outcomes and offering updated estimates of pain relief, functional change, and safety in a wider PLDD population.
Radoš et al. ) performed a systematic review of PubMed-indexed PLDD studies published over the preceding five years, identifying 10 small cohorts (n=536) and concluding that PLDD generally reduces radicular pain with few complications, but also mentioning limitations in methods and prospective data. Their review did not perform quantitative pooling of VAS, ODI, reoperation, or success rates owing to heterogeneity in reporting and mixed study designs ). In contrast, the present meta-analysis integrates a broader post-2013 evidence base and offers a more detailed assessment of clinical effectiveness and safety.
Several studies that were not included in this review further support the role of PLDD in modern LDH management. A 2021 network meta-analysis evaluating eight surgical and percutaneous interventions found that PLDD and endoscopic discectomy had one of the lowest complication rates across all modalities ). The mean VAS improvement that is reported in this review is in line with the 4-6-point reductions often reported after microdiscectomy or surgical discectomy, which typically achieves a success rate between 78% and 95% at 1-2 years postoperatively ). However, these procedures are not directly interchangeable and should be interpreted cautiously because the present analysis is based predominantly on single-arm studies and does not establish comparative effectiveness between PLDD and surgical decompression.
Microdiscectomy is generally reserved for patients with larger herniations, clear neurologic deficits, or failure of less invasive options, whereas PLDD is intended for carefully selected cases with contained herniation. PLDD outcomes are also comparable for endoscopic discectomy and long-term discectomy cohorts (good/excellent results approximately 79%), which potentially suggests that less invasive procedures may achieve broadly similar results ). In addition, compared with other percutaneous intradiscal techniques, such as nucleoplasty or mechanical decompression, PLDD also appears equivalent in efficacy while avoiding chemical agents and relying on a controlled photothermal mechanism ). Although the included studies differ in patient characteristics and methodological design, the degree of pain reduction observed with PLDD appears to fall within the general range reported for other minimally invasive decompression techniques.
This review synthesizes what is, to our knowledge, the largest and most contemporary body of PLDD evidence published since 2013 and provides the first pooled estimates of both continuous and binary outcomes in a clearly defined LDH population. By applying uniform eligibility criteria, extracting standardized end points, and separating non-PLDD and non-LDH studies from quantitative synthesis, the review reduces some of the clinical heterogeneity that limited earlier analyses. The use of structured risk-of-bias assessment and predefined statistical methods further supports transparency and reproducibility.
At the same time, several limitations must be acknowledged. Most of the included studies were non-randomized, single-arm cohorts, which allows confounding by indication, selection bias, and unmeasured prognostic factors. Sample sizes were generally modest; follow-up durations varied widely, and outcome definition was not uniform across all studies. These issues likely contribute to the very high statistical heterogeneity seen in the pooled VAS and ODI analyses. Therefore, the summary estimates should be interpreted as approximate averages rather than precise effect sizes. However, rather than indicating that this is an analytic failure, this heterogeneity also reflects genuine clinical diversity in patient selection, outcome definitions, and technical parameters across contemporary PLDD practice.
Functional outcomes were reported in only a small subset of cohorts, making conclusions about disability improvement less certain than those about pain relief. In addition, publication bias cannot be excluded. Small, negative, or unsuccessful PLDD series may be under-represented in the literature.
Future research should focus on higher-quality comparative studies and randomized trials that directly evaluate PLDD against other decompression techniques in well-defined patient subgroups. In particular, trials that stratify patients by herniation containment, baseline disability, and dominant pain domain may help clarify which subgroups derive the greatest benefit from PLDD. Despite the limitations of the available evidence, this systematic review shows that PLDD can be associated with meaningful and durable pain relief with a very low complication rate in carefully selected patients with contained LDH. When viewed alongside other minimally invasive options, these findings support the consideration of PLDD as a safe, mechanism-preserving alternative for individuals who fall between conservative management and operative decompression.
