However, its perioperative safety and outcomes have not been clearly defined across published studies. A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and a registered protocol. PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched for studies reporting perioperative outcomes of U-RATS for anatomic lung resection in adults. Primary outcomes included conversion rate, morbidity, and mortality. Secondary outcomes were operative time, estimated blood loss, length of hospital stay, and lymph node yield. Random-effects models were applied to pool estimates. Sixteen studies encompassing 579 patients were included. The pooled conversion rate was 1.34% (95% CI: 0.42–4.15%), with no observed heterogeneity. The pooled postoperative morbidity rate was 5.99% (95% CI: 4.46–7.91%), and the pooled mortality rate was 0.21% (95% CI: 0.07–0.64%). The pooled mean operative time was 171.3 min, the estimated blood loss was 51.9 mL, and the postoperative length of stay was 4.9 days. Across 11 studies, the pooled lymph node yield was 16.6 nodes (95% CI: 14.0–19.2; I² = 89.8%). Current evidence suggests that U-RATS is a safe and feasible approach for anatomic lung resection, with low conversion and complication rates and acceptable perioperative outcomes comparable to established minimally invasive techniques.

## Introduction
Minimally invasive techniques have revolutionized thoracic surgery over the past three decades, leading to a paradigm shift from traditional open thoracotomy to video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and, more recently, to robotic-assisted approaches [ , ]. The first VATS lobectomy was reported in 1992 by Roviaro et al. in Italy, marking a seminal advancement in the treatment of lung cancer [ ]. Clinical trials have confirmed that VATS offers significant benefits over open surgery, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery [ ]. As a result, multi-port VATS has become a preferred standard for anatomic lung resections in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer [ ].
Robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) emerged in the early 2000s to overcome certain limitations of conventional VATS [ ]. The da Vinci® surgical system was first applied to pulmonary lobectomy by Melfi and colleagues in 2002, demonstrating the technical feasibility of robot-assisted lobectomy [ ]. Compared to VATS, RATS provides three-dimensional visualization, enhanced dexterity via wristed instruments, and tremor filtration, which can facilitate fine dissection and complex reconstruction [ , ]. Growing evidence has established RATS as an effective and safe alternative for lung cancer surgery, with large meta-analyses indicating perioperative outcomes comparable to or better than VATS, including less intraoperative blood loss, lower conversion-to-open rates, and shorter postoperative length of stay [ ].
In parallel with robotic advances, thoracic surgeons pursued further reduction in surgical trauma through uniportal VATS (u-VATS), which concentrates all instrumentation through a single intercostal incision [ ]. This approach, refined in the 2010s, reduces chest wall trauma and may decrease postoperative pain and analgesic requirements compared to multiportal techniques [ ]. The clinical success and patient benefits observed with uniportal VATS provided a strong impetus to adapt the single-incision philosophy to robotic surgery [ ].
Uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (U-RATS) has accordingly emerged as the latest innovation in minimally invasive thoracic surgery, merging the single-incision approach with robotic technology [ ]. Early pioneers demonstrated that anatomic lung resections could be accomplished robotically through one incision using either adapted multi-arm systems (e.g., da Vinci Xi) or dedicated single-port platforms (e.g., da Vinci SP®) [ , ]. Over the past few years, centers across Asia, Europe, and North America have reported initial experiences with U-RATS, consistently finding the approach technically feasible and safe, with low conversion rates, acceptable operative times, minimal blood loss, and postoperative recovery on par with multiportal RATS [ , ]. Proponents of U-RATS emphasize its potential advantages, including reduced tissue trauma and intercostal nerve injury (owing to a single incision), superior cosmetic results, and the ability for surgeons already familiar with uniportal techniques to transition more readily to robotic surgery [ , ].
Despite the enthusiasm surrounding U-RATS, high-level evidence on its perioperative outcomes and safety profile is still in an early stage. Notably, the first human cases of U-RATS were performed only in late 2021, and to date, the literature has been limited to small cohort studies, single-center case series, and a few multicenter retrospective analyses [ ]. No coherent, large-scale body of data yet exists to definitively characterize the perioperative risks and benefits of U-RATS in comparison to traditional multiport RATS or VATS [ ]. Furthermore, questions remain regarding the learning curve of U-RATS, patient selection criteria, and its generalizability beyond high-volume specialized centers. Considering these gaps in knowledge, a rigorous evaluation of the cumulative evidence is needed to inform surgeons, patients, and policymakers about the role of U-RATS in thoracic surgical oncology. Accordingly, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess the perioperative outcomes and safety profile of U-RATS for anatomic lung resection in adults, synthesizing available data to determine whether this approach delivers on its promise of enhanced recovery and equivalent oncologic efficacy.

## Methodology

## Literature search and study selection
This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to a predefined protocol registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251271117) and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ , ]. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from January 2020 to 2025 to capture the emergence of the technique. The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords, including (“Robotics“[MeSH] OR “Robotic Surgical Procedures“[MeSH]) AND (“Thoracic Surgery“[MeSH] OR “Lung Resection“[tiab]) AND (“Uniportal“[tiab] OR “Single Port“[tiab] OR “Single-Incision“[tiab]). All identified records were uploaded to Rayyan, a web-based systematic review platform, for collaborative screening and deduplication [ ].
Inclusion criteria required studies of adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing anatomic lung resection (lobectomy or segmentectomy) via a Uniportal Robotic-Assisted Thoracic Surgery (U-RATS) approach. While pneumonectomy is also an anatomic lung resection, it was excluded because it represents a distinct surgical category with substantially higher perioperative risk and morbidity compared with lobectomy/segmentectomy [ ]. Studies must have reported on at least one pre-specified perioperative outcome (conversion rate, operative time, estimated blood loss, or length of stay). Case series with fewer than 10 patients, review articles, non-human studies, conference abstracts without full data, and publications without extractable quantitative outcomes were excluded.

## Screening and data extraction
The screening process was conducted in two phases to ensure methodological rigor. Initially, 171 records were identified through database searching (PubMed: n = 20; Web of Science: n = 145; Cochrane Library: n = 6). After removing 24 duplicate records, 147 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. This phase excluded 99 irrelevant studies, leaving 48 articles for full-text assessment. During the eligibility phase, these 48 full-text articles were evaluated against the predefined inclusion criteria. 32 studies were excluded, with the primary reasons being: 19 studies were irrelevant (e.g., not focused on U-RATS for anatomic resection), 8 full texts could not be retrieved despite attempts, and 5 were case series with fewer than 10 patients. Ultimately, 16 studies met al.l inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [ – ]. Figure presents the PRISMA flow diagram detailing the complete screening process.

 

A standardized data extraction form was developed in Microsoft Excel to comprehensively document all relevant data. This form captured study characteristics, including the first author, publication year, country, study design, and funding sources. Patient demographics such as age, sex, BMI, key comorbidities (e.g., COPD), and clinical stage were systematically recorded. Intervention details about the U-RATS technique were extracted, specifying the robotic system (e.g., Da Vinci Xi), incision location and length, and the type of anatomic resection performed (lobectomy or segmentectomy). For outcomes, primary data included the number of conversions to multiportal RATS, VATS, or open surgery. Secondary outcomes—operative time, estimated blood loss, and postoperative length of stay—were extracted as mean and standard deviation. For studies reporting these continuous outcomes as median with interquartile range or range, the mean and standard deviation were estimated using validated statistical methods. Data on postoperative morbidity and mortality were also extracted for safety analysis.

## Quality assessment and risk of bias
The methodological quality of included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series, selected as the appropriate tool for single-arm meta-analyses where outcomes are pooled from individual cohorts without comparative analysis. The JBI checklist evaluates 10 domains: clarity of inclusion criteria, standardized and reliable measurement of the condition, valid methods for condition identification, consecutive and complete inclusion of participants, clear reporting of participant demographics and clinical information, clear reporting of outcomes and follow-up results, clear reporting of site demographics, and appropriateness of statistical analysis. Each item was scored as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable.” Studies were classified as high quality (≥ 7 “Yes” responses), moderate quality (4–6 “Yes” responses), or low quality (≤ 3 “Yes” responses). Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.x with the meta package (version 7.0–0) for meta-analysis, the metafor package for additional analyses and diagnostics, and the readxl package for data import. Forest plots were generated using the meta package and exported using ggplot2 and the gridExtra packages.

## Data transformation
For continuous outcomes (operative time, estimated blood loss, postoperative length of stay, and lymph node yield), studies reported summary statistics in heterogeneous formats. To enable pooling of all available data, medians were converted to estimated means using validated methods. For studies reporting median with interquartile range (Q1, Q3), the method described by Wan et al. was applied, estimating the mean as (Q1 + median + Q3)/3 and the standard deviation as (Q3 − Q1)/1.35. For studies reporting median with range (minimum, maximum), the method described by Luo et al. was applied, with formulas adjusted based on sample size. These transformations were performed systematically for all continuous outcomes before meta-analysis.

## Effect measures
For the primary outcome of conversion rate and other proportion-based outcomes (postoperative morbidity rate, mortality rate), pooled proportions were calculated using the metaprop function with the generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) method and logit transformation to stabilize variances, with back-transformation for interpretation. The Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied as a sensitivity analysis for outcomes with studies reporting zero events. For continuous secondary outcomes, the raw mean was pooled using the metamean function with inverse-variance weighting. Results were reported as pooled proportions or pooled means with corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

## Heterogeneity assessment
Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic with corresponding p-value, the I² statistic interpreted as low (0–40%), moderate (41–60%), substantial (61–75%), or considerable (> 75%), and τ² (tau-squared) as the between-study variance estimate. The random-effects model was employed for all analyses using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator for τ², given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies.

## Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses
Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity for outcomes with sufficient data (≥ 3 studies per subgroup). The following subgroup analyses were performed: (1) robotic platform (da Vinci SP system versus da Vinci Xi system versus other platforms); (2) incision approach (intercostal approach at the 5th–7th intercostal space versus subcostal approach below the costal margin versus mixed); (3) study design (prospective studies versus retrospective studies versus case series); (4) geographic region (East Asia including China, Taiwan, and South Korea versus Europe versus international multi-center studies); and (5) sample size (studies with ≥ 30 patients versus studies with < 30 patients, to account for reported learning-curve effects on perioperative outcomes in robotic lobectomy) [ ]. Between-subgroup heterogeneity was assessed using the test for subgroup differences (Q-test) with corresponding P-values, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially omitting each study and recalculating the pooled estimate to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall result and to identify potentially influential studies. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry, with results interpreted cautiously when fewer than 10 studies were included in the analysis.

## Publication bias
Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots for all primary and secondary outcomes. For outcomes with 10 or more included studies, Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry was performed. For outcomes with fewer than 10 studies, publication bias assessment was interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power. The trim-and-fill method was planned as a sensitivity analysis if significant asymmetry were detected.

## Statistical significance
A two-sided p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses, and 95% confidence intervals were reported for all effect estimates. All analyses were performed on an intention-to-treat basis using the total number of patients who underwent U-RATS as the denominator for proportion-based outcomes.

## Result

## Characteristics of the included studies
This systematic review included 16 studies published between 2022 and 2025, encompassing a total of 579 patients who underwent uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (U-RATS) for anatomic lung resection. The majority of studies originated from Asia, including China [ , , , , ], Taiwan [ , , , ], and South Korea [ , , ], with additional contributions from Switzerland [ ], Italy [ ], and multi-center collaborations across Spain, Romania, Portugal, India, and China [ , ]. Study designs were heterogeneous: four were prospective studies [ , , , ], eight were retrospective cohorts [ , , , , – , ], and four were case series [ , , , ]. Sample sizes ranged considerably from 5 patients [ ] to 112 patients [ ]. Patient demographics were relatively consistent across studies, with mean or median ages ranging from 59 to 66 years and male representation varying from 26% to 80%. Body mass index, where reported, ranged from 21.6 to 24.8 kg/m². The Da Vinci Xi was the most frequently utilized robotic platform (11 studies) [ , – , , , – ], followed by the Da Vinci SP system (4 studies) [ , , , ], and the novel SHURUI single-port serpentine-arm robotic system in one study [ ] [Table ].

 

 

Surgical approaches varied across the included studies, with incisions placed either via a subcostal approach [ , , , ] or through the intercostal space, most commonly at the 5th to 7th intercostal level [ – , , , – ]. Incision lengths ranged from 3 to 7 cm, with most studies reporting incisions of 4 to 5 cm. Lobectomy was the predominant procedure, performed in the majority of patients across most studies, while segmentectomy was frequently reported in several series [ , , , , ]. The overall conversion rate was low, with 9 of 579 patients (1.6%) requiring conversion to an alternative approach; the highest conversion rate of 18.2% was observed in a study specifically evaluating large tumors exceeding 5 cm [ ]. Operative times varied widely, with median or mean values ranging from 76 min [ ] to 210 min [ ]. Estimated blood loss, where reported, ranged from 15 mL [ ] to 110 mL [ ]. Postoperative length of stay ranged from 3 to 7 days across studies [ – ]. Postoperative complications occurred in 0% to 36.4% of patients, with prolonged air leak, pneumonia, atrial fibrillation, and pleural effusion being the most reported morbidities. Thirty-day or in-hospital mortality was low, with only 3 deaths reported among 579 patients (0.5%), occurring in three separate studies [ , , ] [Table ].

 

All 16 studies were rated as high quality [ – ], with 14 studies achieving 10/10 and two studies scoring 9/10 [ , ]. The two studies with “Unclear” ratings on Q4 (consecutive inclusion) had small sample sizes but did not explicitly confirm consecutive patient enrollment [Table ] (Figs. , ).

 

Across 16 studies (n = 579 patients), the pooled conversion rate was 1.34% (95% CI: 0.42–4.15%; I² = 0.0%) with no heterogeneity between studies [ – ]. Similarly, the pooled morbidity rate was 5.99% (95% CI: 4.46–7.91%; I² = 32.1%) with low heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses stratified by study design, geographic region, robotic platform, and sample size did not reveal any statistically significant differences in the pooled estimates.

 

Across 16 studies (n = 579 patients), the pooled mortality rate was 0.21% (95% CI: 0.07–0.64%; I² = 0.0%), demonstrating excellent short-term survival with complete homogeneity across studies [ – ]. Only three mortality events were reported in the entire cohort. The prediction interval (0.06–0.70%) suggests consistently low mortality rates in future U-RATS implementations. Subgroup analyses stratified by study design, geographic region, robotic platform, and sample size did not reveal any statistically significant differences in the pooled estimates (Fig. ).

 

Across seven studies (n = 201 patients), the pooled operative time was 171.3 min (95% CI: 146.0–196.5; I² = 99.6%), with considerable heterogeneity reflecting variations in surgical complexity and learning curves [ , , , , , , ]. Across five studies (n = 118 patients), the pooled estimated blood loss was 51.9 mL (95% CI: 21.4–82.4; I² = 96.7%), indicating minimal intraoperative bleeding [ , , , , ]. Across 16 studies (n = 579 patients), the pooled postoperative length of stay was 4.9 days (95% CI: 4.2–5.5; I² = 98.3%), demonstrating rapid postoperative recovery [ – ]. Across 11 studies (n = 485 patients), the pooled lymph node yield was 16.6 nodes (95% CI: 14.0–19.2; I² = 89.8%) with high heterogeneity between studies [ , , – , , , , ] (Fig. ).

 

Subgroup analyses for operative time revealed no statistically significant differences based on robotic platform (P = 0.09), incision approach (P = 0.06), study design (P = 0.05), or geographic region (P = 0.69); however, sample size was a significant source of heterogeneity (P = 0.02), with studies including fewer than 30 patients demonstrating longer operative times compared to larger studies, likely reflecting the learning curve effect. The pooled mean postoperative length of stay was 4.85 days (95% CI: 4.21–5.50), with no heterogeneity observed (I²=0%, P = 0.84). Subgroup analyses for length of stay demonstrated no statistically significant differences across robotic platform (P = 0.24), incision approach (P = 0.34), study design (P = 0.51), geographic region (P = 0.80), or sample size (P = 0.48) (Fig. ).

 

Funnel plots (Fig.  ) for conversion rate, operative time, and postoperative length of stay demonstrated overall symmetry. The funnel plot for morbidity rate exhibited mild asymmetry, with smaller studies clustering toward higher complication rates, which may indicate potential publication bias or small-study effects. Egger’s test revealed no statistically significant evidence of publication bias for any of the outcomes: conversion rate (t = -1.296, P = 0.22), morbidity rate (t = -1.565, P = 0.14), mortality rate (t = -0.952, P = 0.36), operative time (t = 0.404, P = 0.70), estimated blood loss (t = 1.128, P = 0.34), length of stay (t = 0.437, P = 0.67), and lymph node yield (t = -0.033, P = 0.97) (Table ).

 

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates and identify potentially influential studies. For the conversion rate, the pooled estimate remained stable across all iterations, ranging from 0.72% to 1.57% when individual studies were sequentially omitted, with heterogeneity consistently at 0%, indicating robust findings. For the morbidity rate, the pooled proportion ranged from 14.05% to 16.46%; omitting Chen et al. [ ] resulted in the largest change (16.46%) with a substantial reduction in heterogeneity (I² = 2.7%), suggesting this study contributed most to between-study variance. For operative time, the pooled mean ranged from 165.1 to 179.6 min; omitting Paradela et al. [ ] yielded the highest estimate (179.6 min) with reduced heterogeneity (I² = 90.4%), while omitting Gonzalez-Rivas et al. [ ] also notably reduced heterogeneity (I² = 96.0%). For length of stay, the pooled mean ranged from 4.67 to 5.00 days; omitting Lin et al. [ ] produced the lowest estimate (4.67 days), while omitting Gonzalez-Rivas et al. [ ] reduced heterogeneity to 94.9%. Overall, no single study substantially altered the direction or statistical significance of the pooled estimates for any outcome, confirming the robustness of the meta-analytic findings. However, the persistently high heterogeneity observed for operative time and length of stay, even after sequential study omission, suggests that between-study variability is attributable to multiple sources rather than any single outlying study.

## Discussion
Uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (U-RATS) appears to be a safe and effective evolution of minimally invasive lung resection, combining the advantages of a single-incision approach with robotic technology. In our systematic review and meta-analysis, perioperative outcomes with U-RATS were largely comparable to those of established multiport robotic (RATS) and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) techniques. The overall conversion rates to open surgery or alternate approaches were low (one to M-RATS and two to U-VATS, with no conversions to thoracotomy), reflecting the feasibility of U-RATS in experienced hands [ ]. Our findings echo recent clinical reports demonstrating that U-RATS can be performed without compromising operative safety or efficiency [ ]. This initial evidence affirms that U-RATS achieves its intended goal of minimizing invasiveness while maintaining surgical precision.
When comparing specific surgical outcome metrics, U-RATS performed on par with, and in some aspects better than, the conventional approaches. Conversion to thoracotomy did not significantly differ between robotic and thoracoscopic techniques in aggregate analyses, and the observed conversion rate for U-RATS (ranging 0%–6.9% in early series) is in line with modern multiport RATS and VATS results [ , ]. Operative times were also comparable across modalities; while one single-center comparison noted a modest increase in mean duration for robotic versus VATS lobectomy (≈ 180 vs. 160 min), larger studies and meta-analyses have found no significant difference in surgical duration between the techniques [ , ]. Notably, the uniportal robotic approach may confer some intraoperative advantages. A recent propensity-matched analysis reported significantly lower median intraoperative blood loss with U-RATS (50 mL) compared to multiport RATS (100 mL) [ ]. This aligns with broader evidence that robotic lobectomy can achieve reduced bleeding relative to VATS [ ]. Postoperative complication rates were low with U-RATS and at least equivalent to other approaches; in the aforementioned analysis, U-RATS had about half the incidence of complications of the multiport technique (5.2% vs. 10.4%) [ ]. Length of hospitalization and chest tube duration showed minimal differences between platforms, with one study noting a roughly one-day longer median chest drainage and stay in the U-RATS group than in the multiport group, potentially reflecting a learning-curve effect [ ]. Overall, these findings indicate that U-RATS can deliver similar operative efficiency and safety profiles as conventional RATS and VATS, with a trend toward less intraoperative blood loss and no increase in perioperative risk.
In terms of oncologic efficacy, the uniportal robotic approach does not compromise oncological completeness and may enhance certain aspects of cancer surgery. Lymph node dissection yields with U-RATS were at least on par with those of multiport RATS and VATS in the studies analyzed. In fact, one propensity-matched study found that U-RATS retrieved significantly more lymph nodes than uniportal VATS (with a higher average node count in the U-RATS group) [ ]. This advantage is consistent with results from randomized trials and meta-analyses of robotic surgery. For example, early results of a multicenter trial (RAVAL) showed that robotic lobectomy harvested a greater number of lymph nodes and nodal stations than VATS lobectomy, reflecting the superior visualization and instrument articulation of the robot [ ]. A 2023 meta-analysis of prospective studies likewise demonstrated that RATS is associated with significantly more mediastinal nodal stations dissected compared to VATS (mean difference ~ 1 station) and a trend toward higher total lymph node counts [ ]. Importantly, these improvements in nodal yield did not come at the expense of oncologic quality. Rates of complete resection and pathological upstaging were statistically similar between robotic and thoracoscopic approaches in comparative studies [ ]. Taken together, the evidence suggests that U-RATS can achieve at least equivalent oncologic outcomes to conventional minimally invasive surgery, and perhaps even improve lymph node clearance for more thorough staging.
Concerning patient-centered outcomes, U-RATS offers an excellent postoperative recovery profile that mirrors the benefits of other minimally invasive approaches. By limiting the surgical access to a single intercostal incision, U-RATS was anticipated to reduce postoperative pain and enhance recovery. Our review found no indications of increased pain with the uniportal robot technique. In a direct comparison of uniportal approaches, postoperative pain scores and opioid requirements were similar between U-RATS and uniportal VATS, indicating that the robotic single-port platform does not worsen the acute pain burden [ ]. In fact, some evidence suggests robotic surgery can provide pain outcomes comparable to or even better than multiport methods. A recent propensity-matched study of patient-reported outcomes noted that multiport RATS was associated with greater early postoperative pain and activity limitation during hospitalization compared to a uniportal VATS approach, potentially due to the difference in number and size of incisions [ ]. However, these short-term differences in symptoms disappeared after discharge, as both groups had similar pain levels and functional status by a few weeks post-surgery [ ]. Health-related quality of life (QoL) outcomes likewise show no long-term detriment from the robotic approach. In the RAVAL randomized trial, patients who underwent robotic lobectomy reported a slightly higher 12-week QoL score on a standard index (mean EQ-5D utility 0.85 vs. 0.80, P = 0.02) than those who had VATS, and overall “health utility” scores were statistically comparable in the short term [ ]. Other studies have also found that by 1–3 months after surgery, physical and mental health recovery is equivalent between RATS and VATS patients [ ]. Therefore, from the patient perspective, U-RATS maintains the key benefits of minimally invasive thoracic surgery – low pain, quick recovery, and favorable quality of life – with the added potential benefit of a single incision (which may also confer a cosmetic advantage). As with other advanced robotic procedures, the adoption of U-RATS is influenced by both surgeon experience and institutional infrastructure; a recent meta-analysis reported that baseline proficiency in robotic lobectomy is achieved after a mean of 25.3 ± 12.6 cases and given the substantial capital investment and maintenance costs associated with robotic platforms, structured training pathways and institutional support remain important considerations for its broader implementation [ , ].
Despite promising results, current evidence on U-RATS is limited by its reliance on retrospective studies and small prospective series, mainly from high-volume centers, which may hinder generalizability. The technique remains early in its global adoption, and a learning curve likely affects outcomes. Moreover, long-term oncologic data, such as survival and recurrence rates, are lacking. Although lymph node yield was reported in 11 studies, the number of lymph node stations and resection margin status were not consistently reported, which limits definitive assessment of oncologic completeness. Reporting of resected lobes or segments, segmentectomy complexity, and chest tube duration was inconsistent and frequently aggregated at the cohort level; therefore, stratified analyses were not performed to avoid selective reporting and potential bias. Considerable heterogeneity in patient selection, surgical complexity, and perioperative protocols further complicates interpretation. Most importantly, the absence of randomized trials means confounding cannot be ruled out, underscoring the need for more robust, long-term studies.

## Conclusion
Uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (U-RATS) has emerged as a technically feasible and safe innovation for anatomic lung resection, demonstrating low conversion and complication rates, minimal blood loss, and acceptable operative times. Its performance appears comparable to multiport RATS and VATS, with potential advantages in cosmetic outcomes and reduced tissue trauma. Oncologic parameters such as lymph node yield are maintained, and patient-centered outcomes, including pain control and recovery, are favorable. These encouraging early results suggest U-RATS may become an important component of minimally invasive thoracic oncology, warranting continued clinical adoption and further evaluation.
