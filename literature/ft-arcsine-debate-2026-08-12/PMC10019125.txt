It is estimated that the global prevalence of last-minute cancellations across all specialties is 18% [ ]. There is significant variation between high-income countries, with reported rates of 2%–40%, and in low- to middle-income countries, with cancellation rates of up to 73% [ ].
In England, National Health Service Improvement publishes quarterly data on last-minute elective cancellations. The rate of last-minute cancellations was stable at around 1.1% both before the COVID-19 pandemic and in the last quarter of 2021 (once reporting resumed) [ ]. However, this figure contrasts with existing literature looking at cancellation rates in the United Kingdom (UK). A large UK-based cohort study in 2017 reports last-minute cancellation rates of 13.9% across all specialties, while a smaller earlier study found a 5% cancellation rate [ , ]. Last-minute cancellations are an issue for urology departments; UK figures predating the pandemic report rates of cancellation ranging between 3.6% and 23% in elective urology lists [ ].
The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated the issue of surgical cancellations over the last 2 years. The elective activity was reduced, with an estimated 33% reduction in surgical activity in the UK across 2020 and estimated global cancellations of 72% during the first peak of the pandemic [ , ]. Elective operating has since resumed, although at reduced capacity. Waiting times for most operations have increased, and there is a growing backlog of patients awaiting surgery for both benign and malignant diseases [ ]. Other factors that slow the recovery of elective services include increased difficulty in face-to-face assessments, the need for preoperative COVID testing, and preoperative self-isolation.

## Consequences of cancellations
Last-minute cancellations lead to multiple, unwanted consequences for patients. Delays in care have physical, emotional, and financial implications [ , ]. The disease requiring surgical treatment may also be progressing, potentially leading to worse outcomes, more complicated surgery, and psychological distress from the delay. Patients or their carers may need to take time off work to attend appointments and surgery, with a potential loss of income in case of cancellations. The current requirement to self-isolate prior to surgery further exacerbates this issue.
For hospitals, last-minute cancellations result in significant financial consequences. The poor use of resources leads to increased waiting times as well as considerable added costs. The mean cost of 1 minute of operating theatre time has been estimated to be approximately 37 US dollars in the USA, and a Finnish study estimated that a single cancelled operation resulted in a loss of almost 2500 euros [ , ].

## Causative factors and strategies to reduce cancellations
Several factors contribute to last-minute cancellations, varying between specialties, hospitals, and countries [ ]. Literature on these is extensive, and a recent meta-analysis studying causative factors categorized them as hospital-related, workup-related, and patient-related causes [ ]. Concerning elective urology activity, key causative factors identified include procedural factors, inadequate patient fitness, and infections detected on the day of operation [ , , ].
Literature studying interventions aimed at reducing cancellations are more limited, perhaps owing to the difficulty in addressing cancellations effectively. Innovative strategies include the use of stochastic optimization approaches and machine learning for theatre scheduling to predict which cases are more likely to get cancelled [ ]. Other studies use more traditional quality improvement methodologies, obtaining varying results. A cardiac surgery unit in the Netherlands managed to halve its cancellation rates by introducing serial changes to its preoperative assessment and scheduling model [ ]. A urology unit in the UK, on the other hand, only managed to obtain modest improvements after introducing a reminder telephone call made by secretaries using a proforma ahead of surgery [ ]. To our knowledge, the vast majority of existing studies on the topic predate the pandemic, with only a single manuscript investigating cancellation rates in the context of post-pandemic service recovery. One cohort study conducted in a paediatric tertiary centre reports a reduction in same-day cancellations since recovering elective services, attributing this improvement to a change in preoperative assessment which began including preoperative COVID testing [ ].

## Surgical preassessment
Current National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidelines recommend that every patient undergoing elective surgery undergoes a generic nurse-led preassessment to determine pending investigations or treatments required [ ]. The generic preassessment includes the assessment of the following:
Past medical and surgical history
Drug history and allergies to inform medication discontinuation
Venous thromboembolism risk assessment
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and COVID-19 status
American Society of Anesthesiologists status
Examination of the cardiorespiratory system
Requirement for investigations (e.g. electrocardiogram, midstream urine, baseline bloods)
Discharge and communication needs
In endourological surgery, it is of particular importance to screen and treat patients for urinary tract infections (UTIs), as they are a contraindication for surgery.
We performed a root cause analysis of last-minute cancellations locally, finding that non-cessation of anticoagulants and untreated UTIs were significant causes of last-minute cancellations. To address this issue, a specialist urology nurse-led clinic was introduced to review urine culture results preoperatively.
Our study aimed to determine whether the introduction of a specialist nurse clinic resulted in a reduction of last-minute cancellations of elective urological surgery as part of our elective recovery plan following the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Materials and methods
The study took place at the urology department of the Princess Royal University Hospital, London, UK between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2021. About 65% of our urology patients are males, while 35% are females (mean age 60 years old, SD 18). Our department is led by six consultants who carry out mainly endourology and inguinoscrotal operations for benign and malignant diseases. The majority of our procedures are carried out as day surgery.
At the time of writing, our waiting time averages 6–8 months for non-oncological procedures, and our current policy is that cancelled patients are rebooked within 27 days.
To appreciate the extent of last-minute cancellations at our unit, we carried out a retrospective analysis of all urological elective surgery lists between 1 January 2021 and 30 June 2021. We reviewed the total cases booked and compared these numbers to the number of cases carried out to completion to assess our last-minute cancellation rates. By using patients’ electronic records, we reviewed the factors that led to the cancellations.
From 1 July 2021, we set up a dedicated virtual urology preassessment clinic led by three Band 7/8 specialist urology nurses for 6 hours a week, screening on average 24 patients per week. Our nurses assessed elective theatre lists on a case-by-case basis and reviewed patients’ electronic records 2 weeks in advance of their planned date for surgery.
In this virtual clinic, nurses reviewed the following:
Any urine cultures sent from preassessment
Any outstanding investigations which formed part of preoperative planning
COVID-19 and MRSA status
Whether patients were prescribed any anticoagulation requiring discontinuation prior to surgery
If urine cultures were found to be positive, patients and their general practitioners were contacted by telephone and email to inform them of the UTI. They were prescribed sensitive antibiotics, with a minimum of 2 days of ‘lead-in’ medication deemed adequate for asymptomatic bacteriuria. Similarly, if positive for MRSA, appropriate decolonization regimes were instituted. If patients required anticoagulation discontinuation, they were contacted on the phone and given a reminder of when to stop medications. Those patients deemed unfit for surgery or positive for COVID-19 were postponed and adequately optimized, while other patients from waiting lists were backfilled into vacant slots. These slots were used specifically for procedures that did not require urine cultures, such as inguinoscrotal surgery, and therefore could be booked with less notice.
A new guideline was created by the urology team and distributed to preassessment staff from 1 August 2021. Booking teams were asked to contact patients asking them to provide a urine sample for culture at least 2 weeks ahead of surgery, while urology nurses continued to review results and arranged prescriptions of antibiotics for UTIs.
This analysis continued in a prospective fashion from 1 July 2021 to 31 December 2021 to determine whether improvements were sustained after changes to our practice were introduced. The primary outcome measure was to reduce the number of cancelled operations as a percentage of total cases booked for each month to <5% in our urology department, which is regarded as the standard of an efficient theatre [ ]. A two-tailed Student’s t-test after Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine transformation was employed to assess whether any changes in cancellation rates were statistically significant [ ]. The study was reported in accordance with the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) criteria [ ]. No ethical concerns were identified.

## Results
Between 1 January 2021 and 30 June 2021, a mean of 40 cases was booked for a month, with cancellations rates of 9.57/40 (23.92%). A review of these patients’ notes revealed that several factors were involved in the cancellations ( ). While some issues with patient fitness on the day were felt to be unpredictable, other factors such as the need for further investigations were deemed modifiable in advance of the date for surgery.
 After the implementation of the new urology clinic from 1 July 2021, 124 cases were booked in July 2021, representing an increase in case numbers due to the recovery of elective services post-pandemic restrictions. All patient records were reviewed. Of the 124 patients booked, 18 patients were started on antibiotics, and another 6 were contacted regarding other reasons, e.g. MRSA treatment or anticoagulation stoppage. Four patients were cancelled on the day, obtaining a cancellation rate of 4/124 (3%).
The specialist urology nurse-led preassessment continued through the rest of 2021. There was a sustained and statistically significant reduction in cancellation rates ( ): between 1 July 2021 and 31 December 2021 cancellations averaged 4.2/97.5 (4.3%, P < .001).

## Discussion

## Statement of principal findings
Our study shows that a specialist preassessment clinic can be implemented to sustainably reduce the rates of last-minute cancellations in elective urological activity. Average cancellation rates fell from 23.92% to 4.3% following the introduction of our specialist nurse-led preassessment, despite higher monthly operative numbers. Potential cancellations were avoided by contacting patients directly by telephone, starting them on antibiotics, and reminding them of preoperative instructions. Of note, despite ongoing waves of the pandemic, only two patients were cancelled due to contracting COVID-19 right before their surgery.

## Strengths and limitations
Running a specialist preassessment may appear to be an unnecessary duplication of tasks. However, a review of our generic preassessment highlighted that occasionally the timing of the preoperative review was too close to the date of surgery. We also found that urine cultures for urology patients were not sent consistently or reviewed in a timely fashion due to constraints on preassessment services.
Due to staffing issues among urology nurses, only 83 notes (out of 88 patients originally booked) were reviewed in November, leaving five patients who had undergone the generic preassessment only. In this month, four patients were cancelled: three patients were found to be unsuitable for day surgery, requiring an inpatient stay, while the fourth patient was cancelled as the previous list had overrun. While this latter cancellation was unavoidable, patient fitness for day surgery should have been identified preoperatively. Overall, cancellations for November were still low at 4.5%; however, this incident highlights the need for ongoing specialist urology nurse-led preassessment.
There are limitations to our study. While our results are encouraging, running an additional specialist clinic increased the burden on our urology nurse specialist services, raising concerns about the sustainability of the project. Our nurses did not have a dedicated schedule to run the specialist clinic and agreed to come in during their ‘off’ days or on the weekends to review elective surgery lists. This additional activity was remunerated as additional hours; however, it did not form part of their job plan. Our aim moving forward is to alter job plans for specialist nurses so that they are allocated dedicated time for elective lists review. This may require the recruitment of additional staff, and consequently a cost to our department. However, given the cost of cancellations estimated by Turunen and colleagues, we anticipate that the cost of additional human resources will be offset by the savings obtained with reduced cancellations [ ].
Cost analysis was not performed as part of this project due to challenges in obtaining accurate figures for cancellations. We relied on assumptions from existing literature to estimate the cost of a surgical cancellation. Our figures might be different from the data obtained from other countries or even hospitals within the UK, and as such, it would be worthwhile obtaining a quantitative estimate of the cost of surgery in our department.
Despite these limitations, this study is reproducible in other urology departments across the country, provided there is capacity and budget for additional specialist clinics.

## Interpretation within the context of the wider literature
It is difficult to infer whether the success of our intervention was due to the specific instructions given during the specialist preassessment, or whether it was merely the introduction of an additional patient encounter—acting as a reminder—that resulted in a reduction in cancellations. Lack of attendance to a planned preoperative clinic visit has been shown to be a reliable predictor of surgical cancellations [ ]. As highlighted by Dexter et al., the implication of such a finding is that it is difficult to discern whether the benefits of preoperative assessments are due to the assessments themselves, or rather are just a marker of engagement with health care providers [ ].
The designation of the staff member contacting the patient may play a role; however, further research is needed to determine whether that is the case. We believe that a non-specialist nurse or other health care professionals (HCPs) would not be able to run a specialist preassessment as effectively as a urology specialist nurse; only minimal improvements in cancellations were obtained in a similar study carried out in South London where a secretary, rather than a specialist nurse, contacted patients ahead of urological surgery [ ].

## Implications for policy, practice, and research
Given the above discussion, future studies could look at whether patient contact by various HCPs such as nurses, doctors, pharmacists, and secretaries has a differential effect on cancellations. It would also be desirable to involve patients in further iterations of this project, particularly looking at whether they found it helpful to be contacted, and whether other means of contacting them—e.g. text and email—are as helpful as a phone call.
Other surgical specialties may consider introducing specialist preassessment clinics as there are shared aspects of preassessment, e.g. anticoagulation or MRSA. However, other aspects such as the need to treat UTIs are unique to urology. Ahead of introducing any changes, we would recommend auditing local factors affecting cancellations are there may be differences compared to our findings.

## Conclusions
In the context of elective surgical recovery, the reduction in last-minute cancellations is of paramount importance to address the backlog created during the COVID-19 pandemic. Following the introduction of our specialist preassessment, average cancellation rates fell from 23.92% to 4.3%. Our data suggest that the implementation of a preoperative nurse-led UTI clinic and direct contact with the patient resulted in a statistically significant sustained reduction in last-minute cancellations in our elective surgical activity.
