## Abstract

## Objective
Bacterial gastroenteritis is a significant public health concern, capable of causing severe infections. Among the various pathogens involved, those belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family are the most frequently isolated and associated with gastrointestinal disorders. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of common diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae in Iran over the past two decades, from 2000 to 2023.

## Methods
A comprehensive systematic search was conducted across multiple databases, including EMBASE, HINARI, MEDLINE, PubMed, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library. The focus was on observational published studies reporting the prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae in Iran during 2000 and 2023. The criteria did not restrict patient demographics such as age, gender, health conditions, or occupation. This meta-analysis employed a 95% confidence interval (CI) for analysis. Evidence of heterogeneity was determined using an I² value greater than 50%. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis and meta-regression analysis were performed. Statistical analyses were executed using R version 4.3.2 along with the meta package. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
Out of 3,701 papers reviewed, 56 studies met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. The overall pooled prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae species from 2000 to 2023 was 14.0% (95% CI: 0.11–0.17). Subgroup analysis revealed Shigella spp. had the highest prevalence at 18.0% (95% CI: 0.13–0.24; I²=99%), followed by diarrheagenic Escherichia coli at 11.0% (95% CI: 0.09–0.15; I²=97%), Salmonella spp. at 9.0% (95% CI: 0.05–0.17; I²=99%), and Yersinia spp. at 2.0% (95% CI: 0.00-0.10; I²=94%). Prevalence trends showed Shigella spp. increasing from 4% (95% CI: 0.03–0.08) in 2000–2004 to 36% (95% CI: 0.20–0.55) in 2021–2023. Diarrheagenic E. coli (DEC) showed no clear pattern, ranging from 5% (95% CI: 0.01–0.24) to 17% (95% CI: 0.07–0.36). Salmonella spp. exhibited more significant fluctuations, rising from 6% (95% CI) in both 2000–2004 and 2005–2008 periods to 20% (95% CI: 0.03–0.66) in 2009–2012 and 30% (95% CI: 0.11–0.60) in 2017–2020. Yersinia spp. was only determined in 2000–2004 with a pool prevalence of 12% (95% CI: 0.00-0.91). Sub-species analysis revealed Shigella sonnei was the most prevalent species among Shigella spp. in Iran, accounting for 42% of cases (95% CI: 0.33–0.52). Regarding DEC species, Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) and Enterotpathogenic E. coli (EPEC) had the highest rate at 15% (95% CI). Furthermore, a pool prevalence of 2% (95% CI: 0.00-0.89) was reported for Yersinia enterocolitica among diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae in Iran.

## Conclusion
This meta-analysis provides valuable insights into the prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae in Iran over the past two decades. The findings highlight the significant impact of these pathogens on public health, with Shigella spp. showing the highest prevalence and increasing trends. Further research should investigate the factors contributing to the prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae, including genetic diversity of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae isolates, molecular mechanisms underlying the virulence of these pathogens, or antibiotic resistance patterns.

## Introduction
Gastroenteritis is a serious digestive tract condition characterized by inflammation within the digestive system [ ]. Gastroenteritis can be categorized based on symptom duration and severity. This condition manifests as either acute or chronic gastroenteritis, depending on the nature of the symptoms experienced by patients. Acute gastroenteritis, characterized by short-term symptoms, typically requires immediate medical attention. The primary treatment approach for this form of the illness is rehydration therapy, aimed at replenishing fluids lost due to diarrhea and vomiting. In cases of severe dehydration or when oral rehydration proves ineffective, intensive intravenous rehydration may be considered as a more aggressive treatment option [ ]. In contrast, chronic gastroenteritis is often caused by chronic inflammation that severely damages the stomach lining [ , ]. These conditions present with a spectrum of symptoms which include diarrhea, dehydration, vomiting, nausea, a fever, and abdominal discomfort. Diarrhea is a key symptom [ ] and is defined by the onset of loose or fluid stools that appear three or more times within a 24 h [ ]. Globally, acute infectious diarrheal diseases are a major cause of morbidity and mortality. It is implicated in between 1.5 and 2.5 million deaths each year, making diarrhea the second foremost cause of death among children under the age of five [ ]. Acute gastroenteritis affects more than 350 million people each year, with nearly 48 million cases caused by foodborne bacteria, in developed countries such as the United States [ ]. Viral, bacterial, or parasitic infections are the main causes of gastroenteritis, with viruses causing about 70–80% of cases. Bacterial infections account for about 10–20%, and parasites are also associated with acute infectious gastroenteritis [ ]. While bacterial gastroenteritis is generally not fatal in developed countries, it still claims about 300 lives annually. However, in more severe cases, bacterial pathogens assume greater importance [ ]. Viral gastroenteritis infections as the most common cause of gastroenteritis, are characterized by clinical diarrhea without blood or mucus. This condition typically resolves on its own within a few days, with treatment focusing on hydration replacement, often through oral rehydration solutions [ ]. Gastroenteritis related to parasites may persist until treated appropriately and may require supportive care or specific antiparasitic medications, depending on the organism involved [ ]. In contrast, bacterial gastroenteritis can lead to more severe outcomes, including severe bloody diarrhea, fever, and potentially life-threatening complications, particularly in vulnerable populations such as the elderly, young children, and those with compromised immune systems [ ]. Moreover, bacterial gastroenteritis is often associated with foodborne outbreaks, which can impact public health on a larger scale. Treatment for bacterial gastroenteritis frequently involves antibiotic prescription, though effectiveness may be limited due to antibiotic resistance. Given these factors, timely identification of bacterial gastroenteritis infections is crucial for optimizing outcomes and preventing long-term sequelae in patients [ , ]. Interestingly, recent evidence also revealed the link between bacterial gastroenteritis (infectious diarrhea) and the development of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) ranging from 4 to 32% [ ].
The primary mechanisms underlying bacterial gastroenteritis encompass three main pathways. Firstly, enteric pathogens or minimally invasive bacteria induce excessive fluid secretion in the proximal small intestine, leading to profuse diarrhea. Secondly, inflammatory or cytotoxic damage to the ileal or colonic mucosa can result in the production of blood and pus, complicating the clinical presentation. Lastly, in cases such as typhoid fever, certain bacteria penetrate the intestinal mucosa, gaining access to the reticuloendothelial system [ ]. These distinct mechanisms contribute to the varied manifestations and severities of bacterial gastroenteritis, emphasizing the complex interplay between the pathogen, host immune response, and gastrointestinal tract function. Gastroenteritis pathogens are classified into three primary categories based on their mechanisms of action and disease presentation [ ]. Secretory gastroenteritis is caused by Vibrio cholerae, ETEC (Enterotoxigenic Escherichia (E.) coli), Clostridium perfringens, Bacillus cereus, and Staphylococcus aureus. These bacteria produce toxins that stimulate intestinal secretion, leading to watery diarrhea without significant inflammation. Inflammatory gastroenteritis, characterized by bloody diarrhea and tissue damage, is primarily associated with Shigella, STEC (Shiga toxin-producing E. coli), Salmonella (excluding Typhi and Paratyphi), Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Clostridium difficile, and Campylobacter. These pathogens induce an inflammatory response in the intestinal lining. Finally, invasive gastroenteritis involves organisms that penetrate the intestinal wall, including Salmonella Typhi and Paratyphi, as well as Yersinia enterocolitica. These pathogens invade the intestinal mucosa, potentially causing systemic infections [ – ].
Among major foodborne bacterial pathogens, the family Enterobacteriaceae is well represented by several groups, including Salmonella spp., E. coli, Shigella spp., and Yersinia spp. This family encompasses a diverse array of pathogens that are frequently associated with gastrointestinal infections [ , ]. Research on diarreagenic Enterobacteriaceae pathogens in Iran is primarily cross-sectional and somewhat limited in scope. Most studies focus on a few specific etiologic bacteria or individual pathogens causing diarrhea, rather than examining the broader range of Enterobacteriaceae species. The detection of the most common diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae species responsible for gastrointestinal infections in a country like Iran with its diverse geography can play a vital role in fighting the spread of antibiotic-resistant pathogens.
Herein, this meta-analysis review aimed to investigate the frequency of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae pathogens occurrences in Iran from 2000 to 2023. Specifically, the objectives were to: (1) Determine the overall prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae, (2) Identify the specific diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae strains responsible, and (3) Track the prevalence over time to inform public health interventions.

## Methods

## Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted on the prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae species between 2000–2023 in Iran. Potentially relevant studies were identified through a literature search of EMBASE, HINARI, MEDLINE, PubMed, Google Scholar, and Cochrane Library Science Direct. Key search terms included various combinations of “Prevalence, “Epidemiology” OR “Incidence” [MeSH Major Topic] AND Iran, “Gastroenteritis” OR “Gastrointestinal Agents” OR “Diarrhea”, “Bacteria” OR “Enterobacteriaceae” OR “diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae” OR “Salmonella” OR “Shigella” OR “Escherichia” OR “Gram-Negative Bacteria” OR “Yersinia” OR Enteropathogen OR “Enteric Pathogen” OR “Diarrheal Pathogen” AND “Title and Abstract”. Boolean operators like “and,” “or,” and “not” were strategically used to refine the search. The articles search was focused on published studies with epidemiological and/or clinical data. All records were managed in Endnote Version X 21.3 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA). The limited language was English and the limit of the study group was human.

## Eligibility criteria and study selection
In selecting articles for our study, we specifically focused on clinical scenarios related to diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae in Iran. We reviewed abstracts from our initial search, using carefully defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. For inclusion, we selected original studies if they met the following criteria: (1) conducted in Iran, (2) had a cross-sectional study design, (3) reported the prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae or concurrent bacterial co-infections (viral, fungal, or protozoan), and (4) published in the English language. As for exclusion, we excluded studies that were not fully accessible after reviewing their titles and abstracts, as we were unable to assess their quality without access to the full texts. Additionally, we excluded systematic reviews, case reports, case notes, editorials, letters, meta-analyses, review literature, short communications, conference papers, book chapters, articles lacking precise isolate counts, and articles where the initial number of the statistical population under investigation was unknown. Studies that only reported the prevalence of Enterobacteriaceae without mentioning any specific bacterial genus within this family were excluded from the analysis. Our criteria did not place any restrictions on patient demographics, including age, gender, underlying health conditions, or occupation.
Two researchers (MDS and SBJ) independently gathered all relevant information from eligible articles. Any discrepancies in the data were resolved through consensus and discussion with a third author (FE). From each article, we collected details about the primary author, publication year, study location, sample sizes (total number of gastroenteritis cases), and the number of each gastroenteritis-associated bacterium isolated from clinical specimens. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist [ ]. The criteria for evaluating the studies included the appropriateness of the sample frame for the target population, proper sampling of study participants, sufficient sample size, detailed description of study subjects and setting, thorough data analysis, use of valid methods for condition identification, standardized and reliable measurement of the condition, appropriate statistical analysis, and adequate response rate. Each study was given a score based on specific parameters aligned with the review objectives, with a score of 0 for “No and not reported” and 1 for “Yes”. Total scores ranged from 0 to 9. Ultimately, the mean score for each paper was assessed independently.

## Data synthesis and statistical analysis
We calculated the pooled prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae isolates in each study. Subgroup analyses by focusing on bacterial sub-species, and various periods (2000–2004, 2005–2008, 2009–2012, 2013–2016, 2017–2020, and 2021–2023) were conducted.
This analysis utilized a 95% confidence interval (CI), and an I² value greater than 50% as evidence of heterogeneity [ ]. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup and meta-regression analyses. Statistical evaluations were performed using R version 4.3.2 with the meta package, employing a p-value of < 0.05 as statistically significant. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, with Egger’s test applied to evaluate plot asymmetry. We utilized the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation method to convert proportions before conducting a random-effects meta-analysis using conventional inverse-variance weighted methods.

## Results

## Identified studies
A total of 3701 articles were initially identified. Following the removal of duplicate entries and those focusing on co-infections [ , ], the study without specifying the abundance of particular species or failing to attribute bacterial samples to specific provinces or cities, the analysis was narrowed down to 56 studies [ – ]. These studies exclusively involved human samples. Due to the scarcity of data prior to the year 2000, which lacked sufficient quantity to be referenced, this period was excluded from the study’s analysis [ , – ]. ## Meta-analysis

## Prevalence of common Enterobacteriaceae pathogens linked to gastroenteritis
We conducted a meta-analysis to assess the prevalence of common Enterobacteriaceae pathogens across Iran from 2000 to 2023. Forest plots were developed to visualize the data. The overall pooled prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae species during this period was found to be 14.0% (95% CI: 0.11–0.17) (Fig. ). Funnel plot analysis (Fig. ) revealed an asymmetrical distribution of studies in Iran (I²=99%, p = 0). Subgroup analysis identified Shigella spp. as having the highest prevalence at 18.0% (95% CI: 0.13–0.24; I²=99%), followed by DEC at 11.0% (95% CI: 0.09–0.15; I²=97%), Salmonella spp. at 9.0% (95% CI: 0.05–0.17; I²=99%), and Yersinia spp. at 2.0% (95% CI: 0.00–0.10; I²=94%).
 We performed a sensitivity analysis by identifying and excluding studies that significantly influenced the heterogeneity and effect size of the data. As a result, the overall pooled prevalence was reported as 14.0% (95% CI: 0.11–0.17, p-value = 0.0016) (Supplementary File 1).

## Risk of bias
The linear regression test of funnel plot asymmetry was conducted to assess potential publication bias in the meta-analysis. The test results indicate a statistically significant asymmetry in the funnel plot, suggesting a possible risk of bias due to publication bias. Specifically, the t-statistic of 3.21 (df = 168, p = 0.0016) indicates that the null hypothesis of no asymmetry can be rejected at a very high level of confidence (p < 0.0016). This suggests that there is statistically significant evidence of asymmetry in the funnel plot. The bias estimate of 3.4928 (SE = 1.0888) provides quantitative information about the degree of asymmetry. A positive bias estimate indicates that smaller studies show more extreme effect sizes than would be expected by chance alone, which could suggest publication bias favoring studies with more extreme results.

## Subgroup analysis of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae during timeframes
The prevalence of common Enterobacteriaceae pathogens was monitored across six distinct time periods between 2000 and 2023. Shigella spp. showed variable pool prevalence throughout all six periods, increasing from 4% (95% CI: 0.03–0.08) in 2000–2004 to 36% (95% CI: 0.20–0.55) in 2021–2023. The between-study variability for Shigella was 9% (95% CI: 0.05–0.17). In contrast, DEC did not exhibit a clear pattern across the years, ranging from 5% (95% CI: 0.01–0.24) to 17% (95% CI: 0.07–0.36). The between-study variability for E. coli was 11% (95% CI: 0.09–0.15). Salmonella spp. showed more significant fluctuations, with prevalence increasing from 6% (95% CI) in both 2000–2004 and 2005–2008 periods to 20% (95% CI: 0.03–0.66) in 2009–2012 and 30% (95% CI: 0.11–0.60) in 2017–2020. No records were found for Salmonella in the 2013–2016 and 2021–2023 timeframes. The between-study variability for Salmonella was also 9% (95% CI: 0.05–0.17). Yersinia spp. was only determined in 2000–2004 with a pool prevalence of 12% (95% CI: 0.00–0.91) and had minimal between-study variability of 2% (95% CI: 0.00–0.10).

## Distribution of enterobacteriaceae species
A detailed analysis of bacterial sub-species revealed Shigella sonnei to be the most prevalent species among Shigella spp. in Iran, accounting for 42% of cases (95% CI: 0.33–0.52). The pooled prevalence for Shigella flexneri was 33% (95% CI: 0.23–0.46), while Shigella boydii and Shigella dysenteriae showed lower rates of 6% (95% CI: 0.03–0.10) and 2% (95% CI: 0.01–0.04), respectively. Interestingly, twelve studies reported a combined pool prevalence of 7% (95% CI: 0.03–0.14) for Shigella spp. without specifying sub-species (Figs. and ). Regarding DEC species, the analysis revealed varying levels of prevalence across different strains. Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) and Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC) had the highest rate at 15% (95% CI: 0.10–0.22) and 15% (95% CI: 0.09–0.23), respectively followed by Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) at 14% (95% CI: 0.08–0.23). Other strains included Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) at 7% (95% CI: 0.03–0.14), and Enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC) at 6% (95% CI: 0.02–0.17) (Figs. and ). Salmonella species were found to be the third most common diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae. Salmonella enterica exhibited the highest prevalence among Salmonella species at 22% (95% CI: 0.09–0.46), making it the most prevalent species overall. However, ten studies only reported Salmonella spp. without specifying the particular species, resulting in an overall pool prevalence of 5% (95% CI: 0.03–0.09) (Figs. and ). Limited data were available on Yersinia enterocolitica, with three studies reporting a combined pool prevalence of 2% (95% CI: 0.00–0.89). Additionally, two studies indicated prevalence of Yersinia spp. at 1% (95% CI: 0.00–0.01) without specifying the particular species (Figs. and ).
 

## Discussion
Gastrointestinal infections are a common cause of diarrhea, leading to significant morbidity and mortality worldwide [ , ]. Gastroenteritis can manifest in both acute and chronic forms, and it may be triggered by viral or bacterial infections, with parasitic causes being less common [ ]. Bacterial pathogens are responsible for roughly 20–40% of gastroenteritis cases that lead to diarrhea. The escalating antimicrobial resistance among these pathogens poses a significant challenge to effective therapeutic interventions, resulting in increased instances of treatment failures [ , ]. Geographical variations influence the etiology of acute diarrhea. In developing countries, there is a significant incidence of mortality among infants and young children attributable to gastrointestinal infections. This concerning trend is exacerbated by the persistent threat of Vibrio cholerae outbreaks, which continue to represent a major public health challenge [ ]. In Europe, prevalent bacterial pathogens associated with gastrointestinal illnesses encompass Campylobacter spp., Salmonella spp., EPEC, and EAEC. Additionally, Clostridium difficile has been recognized as a significant etiological agent responsible for gastrointestinal diarrheal diseases [ , ]. Shigella spp. is the predominant cause of diarrhea in Ecuador, sub-Saharan Africa, and South Asia [ , ]. Recent research in China has uncovered that Shigella, Salmonella species, DEC, Campylobacter jejuni, and Aeromonas species are the country’s most prevalent causes of diarrhea [ , ].
This study examined the incidence of Enterobacteriaceae pathogens causing gastroenteritis infections in Iran over a 20 years for the first time. According to a thorough review of 56 publications from 2000 to 2023, the prevalence of diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae in Iran is 14.0% (95% CI: 0.11–0.17).
Heterogeneity across studies was high (I2 = 99%). Since specific tests to assess heterogeneity in proportional meta-analysis are unavailable, researchers currently rely on the I2 statistic [ ]. While initially developed for comparing data across studies, the I² statistic is frequently applied to estimate heterogeneity in meta-analyses of proportions. This measure quantifies the proportion of variability in study results due to differences between studies rather than sampling error [ ]. Several factors contribute to heterogeneity in public health research, including variability in timing, geographic location, residential areas, age groups, and methods for identifying Enterobacteriaceae pathogens. This heterogeneity can be attributed to the nature of proportional data, which may show limited variance even in studies with small sample sizes. Consequently, high I² values in proportional meta-analyses do not necessarily indicate inconsistent data. To investigate heterogeneity, researchers commonly employ chi-squared tests and Tau-squared statistics.
Gastrointestinal infections caused by Shigella spp. (18.0%, 95% CI: 0.13–0.24) rank first in prevalence in Iran. Shigella species exclusively infect humans, causing acute shigellosis characterized by fever, watery or bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain, tenesmus, and stool containing abundant leukocytes, blood, and mucus [ ]. According to the Centers for Disease Control reports, approximately 80–165 million Shigella infections occur annually worldwide, resulting in about 6 million deaths, with the most severe forms being epidemic shigellosis caused by Shigella dysenteriae and endemic shigellosis caused by Shigella flexneri [ ]. While Shigella sonnei and Shigella boydii infections typically cause relatively mild gastrointestinal symptoms, they are still significant pathogens that can lead to morbidity and mortality, especially in vulnerable populations. Furthermore, the prevalence of Shigella dysenteriae and Shigella boydii infections is rare, while infections caused by Shigella flexneri and Shigella sonnei are more common in developing and developed countries, respectively [ , ].
In the study by Nyarkoh R et al. (2024) conducted in Africa [ ], the pooled prevalence of Shigella spp. among 116 data points was found to be 5.9% (95% CI: 4.9–7.0%), which is much less than prevalence of it in Iran. Systematic reviews conducted in South and Southeast Asia revealed comparable prevalence rates of gastrointestinal infections, with South Asia reporting 7% (95% CI: 6–7%) and Southeast Asia showing 4% (95% CI: 4–5%) [ , ]. A study conducted by Salleh MZ et al. (2023) across 14 Asian countries analyzed data from 36 studies and estimated the pooled prevalence of Shigella spp. in Asian patients to be 8.0% (95% CI: 5.5–10.5). When categorized by species, Shigella flexneri was the most frequently isolated, accounting for 49% (n = 9,483) of the 19,346 total reported cases of Shigella infections in Asia. This was followed by Shigella sonnei at 43% (n = 8,389), Shigella boydii at 5% (n = 923), and Shigella dysenteriae at 3% (n = 551) [ ]. Our comprehensive study reveals that Shigella sonnei was the most prevalent species in Iran during 2000–2023, accounting for 42% of cases (95% CI: 0.33–0.52). It was closely followed by Shigella flexneri at 33% (95% CI: 0.23–0.46). The prevalence of Shigella boydii and Shigella dysenteriae was significantly lower, at 6% (95% CI: 0.03–0.10) and 2% (95% CI: 0.01–0.04) respectively, indicating rare gastroenteritis infections caused by these species. Our analysis shows an increasing trend in the prevalence of Shigella spp.-linked gastroenteritis in Iran over the past two decades. In low-income countries in Africa, Asia, and South America, Shigella flexneri is the primary cause of shigellosis, though approximately 25% of cases are attributed to Shigella sonnei [ , ]. In contrast, Shigella sonnei is the predominant pathogen in most cases of shigellosis in wealthy countries, primarily in Europe and North America [ ]. Global socioeconomic advancements are driving a shift in the causative agents of shigellosis, with Shigella sonnei increasingly replacing Shigella flexneri in diagnostic cases, particularly in transitioning nations [ ]. Shigella sonnei can be distinguished from Shigella flexneri in several key ways. It possesses a distinctive O-antigen identical to that of one serogroup of the environmental bacterium Plesiomonas shigelloides. Additionally, Shigella sonnei produces a group IV capsule, which enhances its survival in the environment and fitness within hosts. Unlike Shigella flexneri, Shigella sonnei has developed antibacterial mechanisms to outcompete and displace gut commensal bacteria. Furthermore, Shigella sonnei exhibits a poorer adaptation to an intracellular lifestyle compared to Shigella flexneri. These differences contribute to Shigella sonnei emerging as the primary cause of shigellosis in high-income countries, particularly as socioeconomic conditions improve globally [ ]. In addition, the World Health Organization have recognized the significant threat posed by antibiotic-resistant strains of Shigella sonnei, demanding new approaches [ ].
Based on the pooled prevalence results from our study, the second most frequently identified Enterobacteriaceae pathogens associated with gastroenteritis in Iran is DEC a bacterium that is widely found in the commensal flora of warm-blooded animals’ and humans’ guts. Nevertheless, while the majority of E. coli are benign, some are harmful and can result in serious gastrointestinal disorders [ , ]. The epidemiology is shifting globally due to a rising incidence of gastroenteritis linked to DEC [ ]. By capturing genomic elements that contain genes coding for virulence components required to produce infection, pathogenicity is gained [ ]. EPEC, ETEC, EIEC, EAEC, and EHEC are the several types of DEC [ ]. Few large-scale studies have been conducted in Iran that offer an objective assessment of the distribution of the main E. coli pathotypes throughout the nation [ ]. Moreover, there is limited research available focusing on adolescents and adults, with the majority of existing studies concentrating on infections in newborns or children under the age of five [ , – ]. Small sample sizes, restricted sampling sites, and/or the examination of only a few E. coli pathotypes are some of the methodological constraints of studies carried out in Iran [ , , ]. According to a nationally representative survey conducted in Iran (2021), 50.5% of severe diarrhea cases were associated with a pathogenic strain of E. coli, and 75.0% of these cases tested positive in cultures for the bacteria. It is well-established that EHEC primarily affects children under the age of five [ ]. The results of our study, which spans over two decades and includes samples without age restrictions, indicate that the most prevalent pathovars in Iran, based on their incidence, are EAEC at 15.0% (95% CI: 0.10–0.22), EPEC at 15.0% (95% CI: 0.09–0.23), ETEC at 14.0% (95% CI: 0.08–0.23), EIEC 7% (95% CI: 0.03–0.14), and EIEC at 6% (95% CI: 0.02–0.17). These findings are consistent with studies conducted by Eybpoosh et al. (2021) [ ] and Aslani et al. (1998 and 2003) [ , ].
EAEC has emerged as an increasingly recognized cause of persistent diarrhea, particularly among children in developing countries [ ]. The 2011 German outbreak of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O104:H4 highlights the epidemiologic and genomic plasticity of this pathotype [ ]. The high prevalence of EAEC in Iran during 2000–2023 is comparable with the prevalence of 34.4% reported by Onanugaet al., in Nigeria [ ], 14.7% in South India [ ], 9.5% in Burkina Faso [ ], and 7.86% in Lambaréné, Gabon [ ]. DEC, particularly the strains producing heat-stable toxins such as EPEC and ETEC, is among the few pathogens associated with moderate to severe cases of diarrhea [ ]. EPEC is one of the major pathogens which is responsible for infantile diarrhea in developing countries. A systematic review and meta-analysis was to provide an updated prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant DEC in Asia (2022) [ ] revealed Although ETEC constituted the largest proportion of DEC recorded in the included studies, the pooled prevalence of EPEC was the highest in Asia, at 56.5% (95% CI: 39.1–73.9). A study conducted in 2018 at an Iranian health center reported a notable prevalence of EPEC at 25% [ ]. Another study in 2019 estimated the prevalence of EPEC in Iranian adults to be 9.9% [ ]. Our meta-analysis did not stratify results by age, but the findings suggest a notably higher prevalence of EAEC in Iran compared to other regions. The estimated prevalence rate of 28% (95% CI: 0.02–0.89) indicates a significant burden of this pathogen in Iranian populations.
ETEC strains are a significant cause of diarrhea among travelers, and they contribute to considerable morbidity and mortality in children living in developing countries [ ]. ETEC is one of the most commonly documented pathotypes of DEC in Africa [ ]. The GEMS study, which emphasized the important role of ETEC in moderate-to-severe diarrhea in young infants, is in line with these findings. Furthermore, ETEC was the most isolated isolate in Asia, accounting for 49% of the 4426 DEC cases that were reported [ ]. Each year, this pathogen is responsible for 280 to 400 million episodes of diarrhea in children under five years old and significantly impacts adults in developing countries, with an estimated 400 million cases occurring annually among individuals over the age of 15 [ ]. Several studies conducted in Iran have also indicated a high prevalence of E. coli isolates, particularly ETEC, across various regions [ , , , – ]. According to the report by Gupta et al., ETEC was identified as the causative agent in a median of 13% of diarrheal cases among children in developing countries [ ]. Previous studies have indicated that ETEC is a common cause of diarrhea in infants under the age of two [ ]. However, the global rise of drug-resistant strains has become a significant challenge in treating diarrheal infections caused by DEC, as it limits the availability of effective antimicrobial medications. In recent years, for example, ETEC has been reported to develop resistance to many first-line antibiotics, including ampicillin, nalidixic acid, tetracycline, sulfonamides, and azithromycin [ , ]. This phenomena occurs not only in Asia but also in other parts of the world [ – ]. Determining their burden within a population is therefore essential to developing focused interventions to lower the incidence of diarrheal mortality.
Salmonella species were found to be the third most common diarrheagenic Enterobacteriaceae in Iran. The results of our study indicate Salmonella enterica exhibited the highest prevalence among Salmonella species at 22% (95% CI: 0.09–0.46), making it the most prevalent species overall. In contrast, Salmonella spp. caused the largest number of diarrheal cases in individuals aged 5 and older across the entire African region (261,298,052 cases, 95% UI 117,282,855–369,845,611), primarily due to contamination of African water sources [ ]. The pooled prevalence of Salmonella spp. in South Asia showed a significant random model effect of 14.47% (95% CI: 10.17–20.19) with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 99.8%), while overall antimicrobial resistance reached 70% (95% CI: 63.0–76.0) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 23.6%). This resistance rate increased from 53 to 77% between 2010 and 2020. Subgroup analysis revealed the highest prevalence in Bangladesh (34.24%, 95% CI: 23.73–46.56), followed by Pakistan (26.15%, 95% CI: 15.04–41.47), and Nepal (10.36%, 95% CI: 3.73–25.63), with India having the lowest prevalence (P-value < 0.0001) [ ]. The difference in the prevalence of salmonellosis in Iran may be attributed to several factors including temperature, humidity, and other weather-related factors [ ], food habits [ ], and availability of migratory birds [ ].
The bacterium Yersinia, belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family, exhibits a remarkably low incidence in cases of gastroenteritis associated with this bacterial group. This observation is supported by three studies conducted between the years 2000 and 2004. According to our findings, a comprehensive global analysis of published studies between 2000 and 2019 revealed the pool prevalence rates for Yersinia enterocolitica in gastroenteritis cases were 1.97% (1.32–2.74%) in the culture method and 2.41% (1.07–4.22%) in the molecular method [ ] that is in line with prevalence of it in Iran (2%; 95% CI: 0.00–0.89).
The present review has several limitations that warrant attention. Firstly, the estimated prevalence of gastroenteritis-related Enterobacteriaceae (14.0%, 95% CI: 0.11–0.17) in Iran may not accurately represent the true incidence. Several factors contribute to this potential inaccuracy. One major limitation stems from the heterogeneity among the studies included in the analysis. Nearly all of the included studies utilized stool cultures for isolating Shigella, DEC, Salmonella, and Yersinia. While stool culture remains the standard method for isolating many Enterobacteriaceae species, it has been criticized for its relatively low sensitivity and lack of specificity. These limitations may lead to underestimation of the actual prevalence. Additionally, our systematic review comprised predominantly health-based studies, with fewer community-based studies represented. The scarcity of community-based studies suggests that the overall estimation of gastroenteritis-related Enterobacteriaceae prevalence reported may not accurately reflect the entire Iranian population.

## Conclusion
This research offers a detailed examination of the prevalence of gastroenteritis-related Enterobacteriaceae in Iran over the past two decades. By examining specific species and strains, the study offers valuable insights into disease trends and prevalence changes over time. However, it is important to note that reported prevalence rates may be influenced by reporting biases, limited healthcare access in certain areas, and variations in diagnostic capabilities across different regions of Iran. Additionally, the study highlights the need for more accurate identification of bacterial strains to assess public health threats effectively. It also emphasizes the potential implications of gaining foundational knowledge on the prevalence of microorganisms linked to gastroenteritis in preventing unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions and reducing antibiotic resistance in Iran. Nonetheless, the absence of trend analysis over time and the lack of a control group make it challenging to determine the significance of observed prevalence rates in relation to the general population. Moreover, the focus on hospitalized patients may not accurately represent the overall prevalence in the general population, especially for less severe cases.
