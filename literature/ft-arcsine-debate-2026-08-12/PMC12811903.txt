## Abstract

## Background
Vitamin D deficiency has been widely evidenced to be associated with promoting cancer progression. Herein, we performed a meta-analysis and systematic review of studies assessing vitamin D status in oral leukoplakia (OLK) and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC).

## Methods
This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251113481) and followed PRISMA guidelines. We searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science, with manual searches of reference lists through August 2025. Meta-analyses of proportion deficiency were performed using random-effects models.

## Results
A total of seven studies met inclusion criteria, involving a total of 317 healthy controls, 45 OLK patients, and 168 OSCC patients. Vitamin D deficiency rates were 45% (threshold: 30 ng/mL) and 65% (35 ng/mL) in healthy controls, exceeding 90% in OLK/OSCC patients at both thresholds. Compared with healthy controls, a significant difference was observed in the forest plot in OLK patients (odds ratio (OR) = 36.84, 95% CI: 7.35–184.51, I2 = 0%), and in OSCC patients (OR = 32.43, 95% CI: 4.40–238.96, I2 = 35.5% at 30 ng/mL; OR = 4.67, 95% CI: 2.31–9.45, I2 = 0% at 35 ng/mL). Serum vitamin D concentrations were consistently lower in OSCC patients than healthy controls (pooled weighted mean differences: -15.35 ng/mL, 95% CI: -19.33 ng/mL to -11.36 ng/mL, I2 = 66.4% ).

## Conclusion
This systematic literature review and meta-analysis demonstrated that patients with OLK or OSCC exhibit significantly higher rates of vitamin D deficiency and lower serum concentrations compared to healthy controls. Therefore, the assessment of vitamin D status may be considered in clinical practice for individuals with OLK or OSCC.

## Introduction
Vitamin D, a fat-soluble secosteroid hormone, is primarily synthesized in the skin upon exposure to sunlight, with supplementary sources including dietary intake and supplementation [ ]. The major serum circulating metabolite, 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], serves as the gold standard biomarker for evaluating vitamin D status due to its relatively long half-life and stability [ ]. Vitamin D plays an indispensable role in calcium and phosphate homeostasis, thereby maintaining optimal mineral availability essential for bone health and skeletal mineralization [ ]. Mechanistically, 1,25-Dihydroxyvitamin D exerts its effects by binding to the vitamin D receptor (VDR), subsequently regulating osteoblast differentiation and promoting the expression of bone-specific proteins, including osteocalcin [ ]. Beyond bone metabolism, accumulating evidence has revealed widespread VDR expression across diverse cell types, including immune, epithelial, and cancer cells, suggesting broader roles in both physiological regulation and disease processes [ , ]. Despite its importance, vitamin D deficiency remains highly prevalent; for instance, it affects up to 80% of the population in some Middle Eastern countries [ ], partly attributable to factors such as increased sunscreen use, prolonged indoor living, and greater skin coverage [ ]. Notably, prospective studies have demonstrated that higher circulating 25(OH)D concentrations are associated with a reduced risk of multiple diseases [ ], highlighting vitamin D deficiency as a potentially modifiable risk factor.
Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality globally, imposing substantial health and socioeconomic burdens [ ]. Epidemiological studies suggest an inverse association between serum vitamin D levels and the risk of various cancers, including oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [ ]. Supporting this, Udeabor et al. reported a higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency among OSCC patients and identified a positive correlation between vitamin D deficiency and OSCC risk [ ]. Meanwhile, Huang et al. demonstrated that vitamin D enhances the efficacy of cisplatin chemotherapy, indicating its potential role as an adjuvant in OSCC therapy [ ]. However, conflicting findings from a Mendelian randomization study by Dudding et al. found that vitamin D supplementation in the general population may not significantly lower oral cancer incidence [ ]. These discrepancies underscore the need for further research to clarify the context-dependent and population-specific effects of vitamin D in OSCC. Notably, increased evidence also indicates that vitamin D might play a potential role in the risk of OSCC development from oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) [ ].
OPMDs are oral mucosal abnormalities associated with an elevated risk of malignant transformation, with oral leukoplakia (OLK) and oral lichen planus (OLP) being the most clinically relevant subtypes [ ]. A recent meta-analysis of seven studies revealed that serum vitamin D levels were significantly lower in OLP patients compared to healthy controls [ ], implying that vitamin D deficiency may be implicated in OPMDs pathogenesis. OLK represents the most prevalent and clinically consequential form [ ]. Gumus R et al. demonstrated in 4NQO-induced OSCC animal models that vitamin D deficiency accelerates the malignant transformation of OLK to OSCC, whereas dietary vitamin D supplementation enhances VDR expression and suppresses tumor progression [ , ]. However, another study showed that vitamin D deficiency alone may not be sufficient to initiate carcinogenesis [ ]. Hence, given these inconsistencies, the present study aims to systematically evaluate vitamin D deficiency in patients with OLK and OSCC, and to determine whether serum vitamin D levels differ significantly between OLK or OSCC patients and healthy controls.

## Materials and methods

## Search strategy
The protocol of this meta-analysis has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251113481). The study was performed as the Cochrane Handbook steps and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The electronic search was performed until August 2025 using PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science and conducted in the in PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Manual Search (Table S1). In addition, the bibliographic references of retrieved studies were manually tracked down to identify potentially suitable studies.

## Eligibility criteria
The included studies were required to meet the following criteria: (1) participants diagnosed with OLK or OSCC; (2) availability of serum vitamin D measurements; (3) reporting of either mean and standard deviation of serum vitamin D levels or the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency; and (4) publication in English. Studies were excluded if they involved in vitro or animal experiments, were small case series with fewer than five patients, were conference abstracts, reviews, or commentaries without original data, or if relevant raw data could not be extracted.

## Data extraction
Screening of studies and data extraction were conducted independently by two qualified reviewers (J. L and R. L). Initially, they examined the titles and abstracts of retrieved articles to assess eligibility. Subsequently, the full texts of potentially relevant studies were reviewed for inclusion. The reviewers cross-checked their screening results, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third reviewer (Y. L). When necessary, corresponding authors of the included studies were contacted via email to obtain additional detailed information. The following data were extracted from the finally included studies: study setting, design, patient age, diagnosis, the number of vitamin D deficiency, serum vitamin D concentration results, and risk of bias considerations.

## Outcome assessment
The primary outcome was the incidence or risk of vitamin D deficiency in patients with OLK or OSCC. The proportion of vitamin D deficiency was defined as the number of samples with serum levels below the threshold divided by the total number of sample diagnosed as OLK or OSCC. To ensure that the confidence interval (CI) remain within the valid range of 0% to 100%, the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied in the meta-analysis of the proportion of vitamin D deficiency. In contrast, other meta-analyses used untransformed data.

## Risk of bias in individual studies
The risk of bias for each study design was assessed using corresponding evaluation tools. Cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, and case-control studies were appraised with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal tools (2020 version). The respective checklists for these study types consist of 11, 8, and 10 questions, respectively. Each question in the checklist can be answered as “yes, no, unclear or not applicable”. Two independent researchers (J. L and R. L) evaluated each study. Any discrepancies between their judgments were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consultation with a third researcher (Y. S). To determine the methodological quality of each study, the number of “yes” responses was summed to calculate a total score in the Table  . The overall percentage of “yes” answers was used to classify the risk of bias as low (74%–100%), moderate (50%–74%), or high (0%–49%). Detailed criteria for answering each question in the checklist are provided in the Table S2.
 

## Data synthesis and statistical analysis
The meta-analysis was conducted using STATA 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) under a random-effects model. Results are presented in forest plots, which show either the pooled prevalence of vitamin D deficiency, the odds ratio (OR), or the mean difference in vitamin D levels between the OLK or OSCC group, and the healthy control group, along with their corresponding 95% CI. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and categorized into four levels: negligible (I²<25%), low (25%–49%), moderate (50%–74%), and high (≥ 75%). In cases of significant heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50%), meta-regression was performed to explore potential sources. Publication bias was evaluated statistically using Egger’s and Begg’s tests. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Study selection
A total of 6248 articles were identified through the search strategy. After removing duplicates, 5321 articles were screened for eligibility. Of these, 74 articles underwent full-text screening. Following a detailed review and application of the eligibility criteria, 64 articles were excluded, and 7 were ultimately included in this review (Fig.  ).
 

## Study characteristics
The characteristics of the seven included studies are summarized in Table  . Among these, two were cohort studies, two were cross-sectional studies, and three were case-control studies focusing on OSCC; an additional two cross-sectional studies involved OLK. Diagnosis of OLK or OSCC was confirmed through clinical and histopathological examination. Regarding the techniques used to measure vitamin D concentrations, three studies employed enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, one used electrochemiluminescence immunoassay, one utilized high-performance liquid chromatography, and one study did not specify the detection method. In the evaluation of the risk of bias, three studies had a low risk of bias (one cohort study and two cross-sectional studies) and two studies were rated as having a moderate risk of bias (one cohort study and two case-control study). Meanwhile, one case-control study had a high risk of bias (Table S2).

## Meta-analysis and risk of bias in studies
In total, the analysis included 317 healthy controls, 45 patients with OLK, and 168 patients with OSCC. Subgroup meta-analysis of the overall vitamin D deficiency rates showed: among healthy controls, the deficiency rate was 45% (95% CI 13%–79%; I² = 89.9%) in the 30 ng/mL threshold subgroup and 65% (95% CI 36%–90%; I² = 93.2%) in the 35 ng/mL subgroup (Fig.  a); in OLK patients, the deficiency rate was 97% (95% CI 87%–100%; I² = 48.2%) (Fig.  b); in OSCC patients, the deficiency rates were 97% (95% CI 92%–100%; I² = 0.0%) in the 30 ng/mL subgroup and 92% (95% CI 83%–98%; I² = 41.7%) in the 35 ng/mL subgroup (Fig.  c).
 Two studies comparing vitamin D deficiency rates between healthy controls and OLK patients were included in the meta-analysis. The forest plot indicated a statistically significant difference between the two groups (OR = 36.84, 95% CI: 7.35–184.51, I² = 0.0%) (Fig.  a). Six studies comparing vitamin D deficiency rates in healthy controls and OSCC patients were also included. A significant difference was observed in the forest plot in the 30 ng/mL subgroup (OR = 32.43, 95% CI: 4.40–238.96, I² = 35.5%) and in the 35 ng/mL subgroup (OR = 4.67, 95% CI: 2.31–9.45, I² = 0.0%) (Fig.  b). Egger’s and Begg’s tests detected no significant publication bias (P = 0.344 and P = 0.260, respectively) (Fig. S1a, b).
 Four studies in healthy controls and five studies in OSCC patients recorded the concentration of vitamin D, and the overall vitamin D concentration was 34.03 ng/mL (95% CI 27.55 ng/mL–40.52 ng/mL, I² = 98.4%) in healthy controls (Fig.  a) and 15.21 ng/mL (95% CI 11.80 ng/mL–18.60 ng/mL, I² = 95.8%) in patients with OSCC (Fig.  b). Since the study by Orell-Kotikangas H et al. did not report the concentration of serum vitamin D in healthy controls, four studies included in the meta-analysis of the difference in the concentration of vitamin D between healthy controls and patients with OSCC. The forest plot indicated a statistically significant difference between the two groups (weighted mean difference (WMD): -15.35 ng/mL, 95% CI: -11.36 ng/mL to -19.33 ng/mL, I² = 66.4%) (Fig.  c). Egger’s and Begg’s tests detected no significant publication bias (P = 0.258 and P = 0.462, respectively) (Fig. S1c, d).
 

## Discussion
OSCC is the most common malignant tumor in the head and neck, while OLK represents a common precancerous lesion with the potential for malignant transformation into OSCC [ , ] Over the past two decades, vitamin D has gained increasing recognition for its importance in disease progression and cancer-related mortality [ ]. Given the increasing number of recent studies focusing on the relationship between vitamin D and OLK or OSCC [ , – ], we conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of seven studies to clarify the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and OLK or OSCC, with particular emphasis on OLK.
In our study, the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency among patients with OLK and OSCC was more than 90% when serum vitamin D thresholds were set at either 30 ng/mL or 35 ng/mL. Interestingly, the higher heterogeneity (I²) in the reported proportion of vitamin D deficiency among OSCC patients was 41.7% for studies using a 35 ng/mL threshold, compared to 0% for those using a 30 ng/mL threshold. This difference in heterogeneity was largely attributable to the extreme result reported by Udeabor SE et al., in which the limit of the 95% CI was 72%-92% —a value that aligned precisely with the overall pooled estimate [ ]. Meanwhile, in this study, the method used for detecting serum vitamin D was not reported, which may have contributed to potential heterogeneity. In contrast, the overall pooled prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in the general population was notably lower than in the OLK or OSCC groups. However, considerable variation was observed based on the diagnostic threshold: 45% at 30 ng/mL and 65% at 35 ng/mL, with both estimates accompanied by significant heterogeneity. Several factors may account for this heterogeneity. First, deficiency rates varied substantially. At the 30 ng/mL threshold, for example, estimates spanned from below 20% (Samanta I et al.) to above 70% (Družijanić A et al.), pointing to notable methodological and/or population-level variations [ , ]. Second, geographic variation strongly influences vitamin D status, with prevalence reported at 40% in a European cohort versus 24% in an American cohort [ , ].
Although vitamin D deficiency shows variability in the general population, our meta-analysis demonstrated a markedly elevated pooled OR for vitamin D deficiency in OSCC patients compared to healthy controls. Using a 30 ng/mL threshold, the pooled OR for OSCC was 32.43, whereas the 35 ng/mL threshold yielded an OR of 4.67. This difference of ORs between the two subgroup was partly driven by the extremely high ORs reported in Indian cohorts [ , ], suggesting potential regional or ethnic effects. Notably, for OLK, the pooled OR was 36.84 — higher than that for OSCC — indicating that vitamin D may be more critical in OLK development. Despite the absence of statistical heterogeneity (I² = 0%), the pooled OR had an extremely wide 95% CI (7.35–184.51). This can be explained by the inclusion of only two studies and the study by Muneshwar R et al., which observed a 100% deficiency rate in merely 30 OLK patients [ ], contributed to this imprecision, indicating that the need for larger sample sizes in future studies to obtain more precise estimates. Furthermore, tobacco exposure has also been shown to affect serum vitamin D levels while most included studies did not report this information. For instance, a recent study found that OLK patients who smoke exhibit significantly lower serum vitamin D levels than non-smoking OLK patients [ ].
Our analysis also revealed that the pooled serum vitamin D concentration in OSCC patients was 15.21 ng/mL, which was substantially below the recommended threshold of 30 ng/mL and healthy controls with 34.03 ng/mL [ ]. Significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 95.8%), likely reflecting non-overlapping confidence intervals across studies (e.g., Družijanić et al. 10.23–12.73 ng/mL, Muneshwar et al. 11.14 ng/mL–12.82 ng/mL, Udeabor et al. 18.74 ng/mL–22.10 ng/mL). A recent study further supports these findings, showing that serum vitamin D levels < 25 ng/mL were associated with a 1.65-fold increased risk of OSCC [ ]. These suggest a significant association between vitamin D deficiency and an elevated risk of OSCC. Our meta-analysis also found a significant reduction in vitamin D levels among OSCC patients compared with controls (WMD = -15.35 ng/mL) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 66.4%). Several factors may account for this heterogeneity. Firstly, the study by Mostafa BED et al. reported an extreme range of 95% CI (-57.94 ng/mL to -23.22 ng/mL), which the upper limit of the 95% CI was substantially lower than the pooled estimate (-15.35 ng/mL) [ ]. Second, some studies did not report essential methodological details, such as the assay used for serum vitamin D measurement or the timing of sample collection, and methodological and/or population-level variations. Third, most studies failed to report the presence of chronic conditions (e.g., cardiovascular or autoimmune diseases) or recent medication use, which could introduce potential confounding bias [ ]. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis by Egido-Moreno et al. similarly reported reduced vitamin D levels in OLP patients (WMD= -6.20 ng/mL) [ ], suggesting OLK patients may exhibit comparable reductions. Therapeutically, supplementation strategies have been explored. For instance, Verma et al. showed that calcitriol (active vitamin D) at 100 IU reduced the incidence of high-grade dysplasia and OSCC in 4NQO-induced animal models [ ]. Clinically, vitamin D₃ is superior to vitamin D₂ in raising serum levels and may be the preferred form of supplementation [ ]. Beyond direct supplementation, other strategies such as solar UVB exposure, vitamin D-fortified foods, and diet may also help improve vitamin D status [ ].
Several limitations should be considered in this study. First, the number of available studies was relatively small, particularly those focusing on OLK. Some studies did not report serum vitamin D concentrations or apply consistent threshold definitions. Meanwhile, some ORs had extremely wide CIs (e.g., 7.35–184.51), indicating low precision in our meta-analysis. Second, although most studies assessed vitamin D status using serum concentrations, the assays and diagnostic criteria for deficiency varied considerably across studies [ ], which may have introduced potential bias. Therefore, more randomized controlled trials employing standardized measurement methods are needed to minimize confounding bias. Third, the lack of detailed clinicopathological information related to OLK or OSCC may have contributed to heterogeneity. Importantly, it remains unclear whether vitamin D deficiency contributes causally to OLK or OSCC or whether the disease itself reduces vitamin D levels. Given these constraints, the available evidence does not allow further statistical elaboration of the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and the initiation or progression of OLK or OSCC. Thus, the results of this analysis should be interpreted with caution in clinical contexts.
In conclusion, this systematic literature review and meta-analysis demonstrated that patients with OLK or OSCC exhibit significantly higher rates of vitamin D deficiency and lower serum concentrations compared to healthy controls. Therefore, the assessment of vitamin D status may be considered in clinical practice for individuals with OLK or OSCC. To clarify the relationship between serum vitamin D and OLK/OSCC, future studies warrant higher-quality evidence, notably from randomized controlled trials.
