## Background
The high prevalence of caesarean section (CS) is a global concern. Relaparotomy is needed when conservative managements are not effective in CS complications. This study aimed to systematically review and meta-analyze the mortality rate and risk factors of relaparotomy after CS.

## Methods
Based on a pre-registered protocol, we searched 11 databases. The MOOSE guideline was followed, and the reporting was in accordance with the PRISMA statement. A Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to transform the raw data, and a random-effects meta-analytic model was applied. The quality of the evidence was evaluated by Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.

## Results
The qualities of the pooled results were assessed to be “very low” based on the inclusion of observational studies. A total of 40 studies were retrieved. The mortality rate in patients after relaparotomy was 7.24% (95%CI 4.10-11.03%). Significant gap existed between high-resources and low-resources countries. In developed areas this rate was 0.56% and in places of low resources this rate was 16.90%. Higher CS rate correlated with higher mortality. Over 70% of the relaparotomies followed emergent CS. Risk factors associated with relaparotomy included higher maternal age, CS performed in the second stage of labor, twin pregnancy, preterm birth (OR 2.72, 95%CI 1.87–3.95) and preeclampsia (OR 3.24, 95%CI 1.47–7.14).

## Conclusion
Our findings demonstrated the high mortality rate in patients who had relaparotomy after CS. Interpretation of the results should be with caution due to GRADE evidence level. During a time when there is an imbalance in medical resources in different countries, interventions to reduce CS rate, good prenatal care and intensified post-operative management are important.

## Introduction
Caesarean birth has been found to be associated with elevated risks for maternal morbidity and mortality compared to vaginal birth [ – ]. However, the incidence of caesarean section (CS) has continued to increase in recent decades and has become a global concern. Caesarean section complications included hemorrhage, sepsis and intrabdominal organ injuries [ – ]. If conservative treatment was not effective, a second laparotomy seemed to be a reliable choice. The World Health Organization (WHO) “Near-Miss Approach for Maternal Health” was published by the WHO in 2011 against the backdrop of high maternal mortality rates globally, particularly in low-resource settings. The aim was to address inconsistencies in the quality of maternal care. In this document, laparotomy was defined as “ a critical intervention under life threatening circumstances” [ ]. But in some hospitals, especially in hospitals in low-resources countries, improper surgical techniques, inadequate aseptic precautions, and suboptimal postoperative care contributed to severe CS complications [ , ]. Therefore, deliberate decisions on relaparotomy in these situations needed to be made.
Relaparotomy after CS was associated with an increased risks of peripartum hysterectomy, transfusion of blood products, maternal near-miss events, and even maternal mortality [ – ]. According to the literature, less than 1% of individuals require relaparotomy after a cesarean section, and this rate is even lower in developed countries. The main reason for relaparotomy is postpartum hemorrhage, followed by infection as the second leading cause [ , – ]. Although relaparotomy after CS is rare, the existing evidence supporting the overall mortality rate of relaparotomy and risk factors have not been summarized. Furthermore, the influence of economic level and local CS rate on relaparotomy have not been reviewed. The differences of relaparotomy in developed and undeveloped areas has not been explored. Therefore, the current systematic review aimed to summarize the mortality rate of relaparotomy after CS. Risk factors for relaparotomy were also explored.

## Methods
We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with meta-analyses of observational studies in epidemiology (MOOSE) guideline [ ]. In addition, our reporting items were in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement for reporting systematic reviews [ ]. The study protocol was registered and is available at PROSPERO (registration No: PROSPERO 2021 CRD42021265325. Available from: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42021265325).

## Search strategy and data sources
A systematic search was performed in six English-language databases and trial registration centers (Medline, Embase, Web of Science, Google scholar, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and ClinicalTrials.gov) and four Chinese databases (the Chinese Biological Medical Literature database, the Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure database, Chinese Medicine Premier (Wanfang database), and the Chinese Journals Full-text Database (VIP)) without language restriction. The World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform was searched for unpublished trials. Additionally, we added three databases compared with the original planned search range to include more potential related studies. The initial search was conducted in June 2022. The updated search was performed in July 2023. The search strategy in Medline and Embase is included in the Supplement file. This study used the most recent publication if multiple publications in a single medical center were identified. Information was carefully checked to prevent repeated entry and the authors were contacted if necessary. The supplementary data from previous publications were documented and analyzed if necessary. Furthermore, manual checks of the reference lists within both the original literature and the reviews were carefully performed to identify eligible studies.

## Study selection and risk of bias assessment
We included studies reporting on relaparotomy following CS. The study population comprised all patients who underwent CS, in which the relaparotomy incidence or associated mortality rate was reported. Only observational studies reporting relevant outcomes were included. Case reports, reviews, and studies lacking extractable data were excluded. The primary outcomes of interest were mortality rate due to relaparotomy and the overall relaparotomy incidence after CS. Secondary outcomes were risk factors for post-CS relaparotomy. Subgroup analyses included the economic development of study locations, annual delivery volume of the hospitals, and CS rates of the hospitals. As most relaparotomy were performed after emergent CS. We specifically examined these factors’ impact on emergency CS cases. Studies identified through the literature search were first identified by examining the abstracts. If eligible, the full text was independently reviewed by two authors (DS and JH) to ensure the applicability of the current analysis. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion.
Two reviewers (JH and XT) independently assessed each of the included studies for quality by using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies [ ]. The JBI appraisal checklist is based on 9 items. The responses were scored 0 for “Not appropriate and Unclear” and 1 for “Yes”; total scores ranged 0–9. Disagreements were discussed and resolved as a group.

## Data collection and analysis
EndNote X9 (Thomson ISI Research Soft, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Microsoft Excel were used to screen the titles and abstracts. Two reviewers (DS and JH) independently assessed the relevant articles, and the inclusion of a full-text article was determined by both reviewers. Any discrepancies between the two investigators were resolved through the adjudication of a third reviewer or discussion among all the reviewers. A specified data collection form was designed and piloted to capture all variables of interest. The authors of the specific studies were contacted if there were missing data of interest. Discrepancies between two independent reviewers collecting information were resolved by re-extraction of data from the original research and discussion between the two reviewers.
 Before pooling the prevalence estimates, the variance of the raw prevalence from each included study was transformed by using the Freeman-Tukey double arc-sine transformation [ ]. I2 statistics were used to estimate heterogeneity among the studies. Studies with an I2 value of < 25%, 25-50% and 50-100% were considered to have low, moderate, and high methodological heterogeneity, respectively [ – ]. A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. Due to the likelihood of high heterogeneity, a random-effects meta-analysis was used [ ]. To assess the associations of various sample characteristics and the prevalence of relaparotomy, meta-regression was performed. Subgroup analysis was based on various sample characteristics including differences in areas of different economic level, sample size, studies of hospitals with different CS rates, studies of hospitals with different annual delivery numbers, and time frames of each study. According to WHO recommendations, the CS rate should not exceed 15% [ ]. However, the CS rate was much higher than that in real world. Because the CS rate in most developed countries is below 30% [ , – ]. In this analysis, countries with CS rate above 30% were considered to be with high CS level. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for each meta-analysis was used to test whether single studies had a disproportionally excessive influence. Publication bias was calculated if more than 10 estimates were available by visual inspection together with Egger linear regression test. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was applied based on the Cochrane guidelines [ ]. All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, v.3.3.0; http://www.r-project.org/).

## Results

## General characteristics of the studies
The selection process is shown in Fig.  . Overall, a total of 3028 studies were isolated from the 11 databases and reference lists in the literature. After the removal of duplicate studies, 1826 studies were excluded after screening the titles and abstracts. The remaining 35 articles were excluded after reviewing the complete texts (Fig.  and Table ). For the qualitative synthesis, 40 studies were included [ – , – , , , – ].

 

The studies included in this systematic review are summarized in Table  (Table  ). Mortality rates after relaparotomy were reported in twenty-four studies [ , , – , , , – , , – , – , , , ].Thirty eight studies reported the overall relaparotomy rate. Eleven studies reported the risk factors for relaparotomy [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. Some studies lacked the information of interest, and the methodologies were not described in detail. Those authors were contacted through email, but only a few of the authors replied. Most of the authors did not respond [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. For studies with possible overlapping participants, the most recently published studies were included. The results from the risk of bias assessment revealed that most of the articles had adequate quality with a low risk of bias (Fig.  and Table ). However, as our pooled results were based on observational studies, and most of the results were with high level of heterogeneity, the overall certainty of the evidence were judged to be “very low” by GRADE approach. The most common reasons for downgrading the certainty of evidence were the study design, inconsistency, and imprecision (Table ).

 

 

The studies we included cover most regions worldwide, encompassing Asia (India, Israel, Bangladesh, China, Nepal, Turkey), Africa (Egypt, Sudan, South Africa, Ethiopia, Rwanda, Ghana), and Europe (Poland, Serbia, France, and the Netherlands). Unfortunately, no studies from the Americas were included. Twelve studies were conducted in high-resources countries, twenty-two studies were conducted in middle-resources countries and six studies were conducted in low-resources countries. Most of the relaparotomies were performed within one week after CS. Most of the studies were conducted at a local referral center, and the annual deliveries in these hospitals were approximately 10,000 deliveries (or greater). The CS rate was calculated according to the data provided in the included studies, which were unevenly distributed (Table  ). In these studies, the reasons for undergoing the initial CS varied. The lowest CS rate in these studies was slightly above 10%, while in some regions, the rate was significantly higher, exceeding 30%. Consequently, the rates of relaparotomy and mortality also varied greatly, reflecting substantial differences in economic development and regional disparities. Most cases of relaparotomy occurred in women of comparatively advanced maternal age, or those with higher parity. No racial differences in relaparotomy incidence have been identified. The most common indication for relaparotomy was still the suspicion of hemorrhage.

## Synthesis of the results

## Mortality and incidence of relaparotomy
The mortality rate of relaparotomy after CS was reported in 24 studies. The pooled mortality rate was 7.24% (95%CI 4.10-11.03%) (Fig.  ). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that no single study changed the overall pooled results (Figure ). No publication bias was observed (Figure ). A mixed-effects meta-regression was applied on seven variables (area, year, sample size, study design, CS rate, annual delivery and economic development level of country). The meta-regression analytical model explained over 50% of the variance of relaparotomy incidence among these studies (R-squared = 52.83%, P < 0.01). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the pooled mortality rates in high-resources, middle-resources, and low-resources countries were 0.56% (95%CI 0.01-2.04%), 7.55% (95%CI 3.95-11.98%), and 16.90% (95%CI 11.33-23.24%), respectively. In studies with a high CS rate, the pooled mortality rate was 9.85% (95%CI 5.31-15.39%). In studies with a low CS rate, the pooled mortality rate was 4.81% (95%CI 1.49-9.49%). A total of 6.59% of patients after relaparotomy died from severe hemorrhage (6.59%, 95%CI 2.68-11.78%) (Figure ). A total of 2.71% of patients died from severe septicemia or septic shock (2.71%, 95%CI 0.71-5.58%) (Figure ). Other reasons for death included cardiac arrest, severe organ injury, eclampsia and etc. Over one tenth of patients who had relaparotomy (10.65%, 95%CI 6.82-15.14%) had a third laparotomy (Fig.  ).

 

 

The overall incidence of relaparotomy after CS were reported in 38 studies. The pooled prevalence was 0.36% (95%CI 0.26-0.46%) by using random-effects model. Publication bias was found based on the funnel plot and Egger test (Harbord-Egger test, P < 0.01). After trim-and-fill method was applied, the pooled relaparotomy prevalence was 0.17% (95% CI, 0.09-0.27%). Suspicion of hemorrhage remained the most common indication for relaparotomy, whether it was due to vaginal bleeding or suspected intraabdominal bleeding and hematoma. More than 75% of relaparotomies were performed due to signs indicative of hemorrhage (76.72%, 95%CI 66.66-85.56%). A total of 42.30% of the relaparotomies were performed due to suspicion of intrabdominal bleeding or hematoma (42.30%, 95%CI 33.37-51.46%). A total of 14.26% of relaparotomies were performed due to infection or intrabdominal abscess formation (14.26%, 95%CI 9.63% 19.51%).

## Impact of emergency Cesarean section on relaparotomy
Twenty-two studies reported relaparotomy after emergent CS. We found that over 70% of the relaparotomies were performed after emergent CS (Fig.  ). Sensitivity analyses revealed that the proportion of relaparotomy after emergent CS varied from 70.75% (95%CI 61.73-79.05%) to 75.03% (95%CI 66.80-82.49%), no single study changed the overall pooled results, and the heterogeneity was not changed. No publication bias was found (Figure S5).

 

Subgroup analyses were performed according to the differences in annual delivery numbers of the study hospitals and the CS rate of included studies. In studies with an annual delivery number below 10,000, over 60% (66.81%, 95%CI 49.09-82.50%) of relaparotomies was performed following emergent CS. But in those hospitals with a larger annual delivery number, over three quarters of relaparotomies followed emergent CS (77.13%, 95%CI 67.12-85.87%). In hospitals with a high CS rate, the proportion of relaparotomy after emergent CS was higher. Over three quarters of relaparotomy were performed following emergent CS (76.52%, 95%CI 61.74-88.79%). In hospitals with a low CS rate, this proportion was decreased to 68.22% (95%CI 56.20-79.16%).

## Risk factors
A total of 11 studies investigated the relaparotomy risk related with maternal age. We found patients in the relaparotomy group had a more advanced maternal age (MD 0.88, 95%CI 0.08–1.69). No publication bias was found in the analysis of maternal age and relaparotomy risk (Harbord-Egger test, P = 0.71). Three studies reported the association of overweight and relaparotomy, the results from this limited number of studies revealed there is no increased risk for relaparotomy in overweight patients (Table  ). Due to the limitation of included studies, we could not find whether women who were classified as underweight (BMI < 18.5) had higher risk for relaparotomy. Patients in the relaparotomy group had an increased parity (MD 0.55, 95%CI 0.06–1.04).There were no association of relaparotomy with nulliparity and smoking.

 

CS performed in the second stage of labor significantly elevated the possibility of relaparotomy. Pooling data indicated this could significantly increase relaparotomy risk by more than 2 times. Compared to women who did not have relaparotomy, the initial CS duration was longer in relaparotomy group (MD 13.98, 95%CI 9.35–18.62). Previous CS history did not correlate with increased risk for relaparotomy. Operation at night did not elevate relaparotomy rate, either. The birthweight in patients of relaparotomy group and non-relaparotomy group were comparable (MD -80.01, 95%CI -258.02-98.01).
Pregnancies with multiple gestations inevitably augmented the possibility for relaparotomy. The risk is elevated by nearly 2 times. In patients with hypertensive disorders, especially in patients with preeclampsia, relaparotomy risk was dramatically increased, which elevated to more than 3 times of that in patients without preeclampsia. Pooling data from limited studies did not find increased risk for relaparotomy in patients taking anticoagulation medications or patients diagnosed with thrombocytopenia or hemophilia. But operations performed in preterm pregnancies did increase the risk for relaparotomy.

## Discussion

## Main findings
This current meta-analysis summarized the mortality rate and risk factors of relaparotomy after CS. Information and data were collected from 11 databases. Due to the inclusion of observational studies, the GRADE of these results was classified as “very low” quality. Hence, interpretation of our results should be with caution.
The pooled mortality rate of relaparotomy after CS was 7.24%. No publication bias was found. According to the WHO statement, there was no further decrease in maternal or neonatal mortality when the national caesarean birth rates increased over 10% [ ]. This high maternal mortality rate suggested alarming adverse outcomes related to increasing CS rates. There were significant differences in mortality rate in different areas. Mortality rate in developed countries was only 0.56%, but in countries of low resources, this rate was 16.9%. Hemorrhage was still the leading cause of mortality, followed by severe infection. Over one tenth of patients who had relaparotomy had a third laparotomy. Over 70% of relaparotomies were performed after emergent CS. In hospitals with high CS rate and larger annual delivery number, this rate was even higher.
Risk factors of relaparotomy included higher maternal age, increased parity and CS performed in the second stage of labor. CS performed in the second stage of labor increased relaparotomy risk by more than 2 times. Previous CS history and operation at night are not risk factors for relaparotomy. CS in twin pregnancies, CS in pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia and CS in preterm pregnancies significantly increased relaparotomy risk. Especially in patients with preeclampsia, relaparotomy risk was elevated to more than 3 times compared with patients without preeclampsia. In preterm CS, this rate was 2.72 times of that in CS of full-term group.

## Interpretation
Considering the high rate of relaparotomy related mortality, this systematic review and meta-analysis underscores the need for reducing CS rate and shortening the gap between high and low resources areas. Bearing in mind the imbalance in medical resources distribution in different areas, this meta-analysis also demonstrated a significant gap between areas of different economic development levels. This significant gap between developed and undeveloped countries revealed that more attention should be paid to pregnant women in low-resources areas and efforts to narrow the gap are urgently needed.
Our study summarized the mortality rate of relaparotomy after CS for the first time. We also found that rate for relaparotomy following emergent CS was high. This high rate correlates with high CS rate and the annual delivery number of the hospital. But we could not differentiate whether the relaparotomy was performed on elective situations or emergent situations. B Lamme et al. analyzed 8 studies exploring relaparotomy in patients susceptible of peritonitis [ ]. They compared planned and on-demand relaparotomy strategies in patients with secondary peritonitis. Their findings demonstrated benefits from the on-demand relaparotomy strategy. In clinical practice, on-demand relaparotomy strategy is more practical because the situation may deteriorate in a short time. Whether the on-demand strategy of relaparotomy could also provide more benefits in patients after CS needs to be analyzed in future. Although we cannot determine the precise timing of relaparotomy, reducing the rate of emergency CS is an effective strategy to decrease relaparotomy occurrences, as the majority of these surgeries occur following emergency CS. Therefore, accurately assessing patients’ eligibility for trial of labor and actively managing pregnancy complications are significant for ensuring successful vaginal deliveries and reducing emergency CS rates.
This study found several demographic and obstetric characteristics of patients were related with relaparotomy. These qualitative portions of the analysis discovered a demand for better antenatal care and labor managements. From this meta-analysis, we still found emergent CS, especially CS in the second stage of labor, put great threats to the fetus as well as the mothers, which is also an important reason for hemorrhage. Furthermore, we found that hemorrhage is the primary cause of relaparotomy and the leading cause of relaparotomy correlated mortality. This indicates that hemorrhage remains a critical factor contributing to maternal mortality and morbidity worldwide. Obstetricians should maintain heightened vigilance and proficiency in the accurate identification and management of both vaginal and intra-abdominal hemorrhages, including hematomas. Effective identification and proactive conservative management of post CS hemorrhage may reduce the need of relaparotomy to some extent. Additionally, several risk factors were possibly related to relaparotomy. Surgeon’s experience, duration of an operation, adhesions, anticoagulation therapy, and other complications during CS are possible perioperative risk factors of relaparotomy. But due to the limitation in study number, we did not analyze the anthropic factor and possible influencing medical interventions. But we found patients with preeclampsia had higher risk for relaparotomy. This indicates the importance of post-operation management in this group of patients.

## Strength and limitations
The results of the analysis of the current study should be interpreted with caution because of the following limitations. First, due to the limitations in the evidence level from observational studies, the findings were determined to be of “very low” quality by the GRADE approach. Second, most of our included studies were carried out in developing countries in Asia and Africa, lack of information in developed countries reflected the imbalance in the distribution of medical resources and the unevenness in the medical development level. Third, heterogeneities were high in most of the analyses. Results from meta regression analyses explained the heterogeneity to a certain extent, but this high heterogeneity reflect the differences in demographic characteristics and gaps in medical resources between countries. Fourth, the planned search database and quality assessment tool were changed compared with our predefined protocol. To include more potential studies, more databases were added.
Although these limitations should be carefully considered, their existence does not render the current systematic review and meta-analysis inappropriate. As the first meta-analyses on CS related relaparotomy, our study summarized the mortality and risk factor of relaparotomy. Our protocol only included studies according to the inclusion criteria. The overall sample size was large enough for the analyses and the magnitudes of the estimates were high enough to be meaningful. The qualities of the included studies were assessed by using the JBI critical appraisal checklist. Most of our included studies were performed in a local referral center, and pregnant women in the research period were all included. The sample frame and sampling method in the included studies were appropriate with a low risk of bias. Electronic medical records databases were used in most of the studies to retrieve the clinical information, which is a guarantee of the accuracy of their reporting data to some extent.

## Conclusions
 This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that the mortality rate of relaparotomy after CS is high. Several factors corelated with relaparotomy after CS. During a time when there is an imbalance in medical resources in different areas, interventions to reduce CS rate, good antenatal care to prevent severe pregnancy complications, and intensified post-operative management may be the feasible ways to prevent relaparotomy. Future studies with more data in highly developed countries and in extremely low-resource countries may improve the current results.
