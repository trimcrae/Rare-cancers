## Background:
Posterior tibial plateau fractures can lead to significant posttraumatic instability if not treated properly. It remains unclear which surgical approach achieves better patient outcomes. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to assess postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing anterior, posterior, or combined approach for posterior tibial plateau fractures.

## Methods:
The PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, and Scopus were searched for studies published before October 26, 2022, comparing anterior, posterior, or combined approaches for posterior tibial plateau fractures. This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines. Outcomes included complications, infections, range of motion (ROM), operation time, union rates, and functional scores. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Meta-analysis was conducted with STATA software.

## Results:
In total, 29 studies with a total of 747 patients were included for quantitative and qualitative analysis. Compared with other approaches, the posterior approach for posterior tibial plateau fractures was associated with a better ROM and shorter operative time. The complication rates, infection rates, union time, and hospital for special surgery (HSS) scores were not significantly different between surgical approaches.

## Conclusions:
The posterior approach for posterior tibial plateau fractures offers advantages such as improved ROM and shorter operative time. However, there are concerns regarding prone positioning in patients with medical or pulmonary comorbidities and indications in polytrauma cases. Further prospective studies are needed to determine the optimal approach for these fractures.

Tibial plateau fractures involving the posterior column are associated with high-energy injury and inherent instability. A thorough understanding of the fracture pattern is essential for surgical planning. The Schatzker classification is based on plain radiography . It does not encompass the posterior quadrant of the tibial plateau. Luo et al. developed the 3-column theory in 2010, as seen in computed tomography (CT) axial plane images and 3-dimensional reconstructions with a better understanding of the posterior column - . The posterior column of the tibial plateau is further divided into 2 subcolumns, the posteromedial and posterolateral , . These types of fractures may cause significant posttraumatic instability of the knee if not treated well.
Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) remains the gold standard of definitive treatment for posterior tibial plateau fractures; however, conventional reduction techniques are difficult to implement because of fracture location, morphology, and associated soft tissue injuries. The goals of surgical intervention are to restore joint congruence, achieve mechanical axis, improve function, and achieve mobilization. Yet, current recommendations still need to be made concerning the most effective surgical approach.
Posterior tibial plateau fractures can be challenging to treat because of difficulty in reducing the fracture and maintaining proper displacement using anterior approaches because the knee is in a flexed position. However, posteromedial and posterolateral surgical approaches often require extensive dissection, which can result in scarring, inflammation, and limited range of motion (ROM).
By contrast, the posterior approach allows for direct visualization of the fracture, leading to a more anatomic reduction and improved joint congruity. However, the posterior approach requires the patient to be in a prone position, which can present challenges in exposure and protection of the posterior neurovascular structures. The unfamiliar anatomy and the risk of injury to the popliteal artery and other structures make this approach more challenging and require experienced surgeons.
Previous literature has proposed additional surgical techniques, including femoral or fibular osteotomy approaches, both of which aim to increase visualization of both posterolateral fracture fragments and intraarticular pathologies such as meniscal tears. Some studies also suggested arthroscopic-assisted techniques during the procedure - . No current recommendations exist regarding the optimal surgical approach to address these complex fracture patterns. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare anterior approaches vs. posterior approaches in patients with posterior tibial plateau fractures.

## Methods

## Research Protocol and Search Question
The search protocol was based on the following PICO question: For patients with posterior column tibial plateau fractures (population of interest), does the anterior approach, compared with the posterior approach (comparator), have lower complication rates and better functional outcomes (outcome)? The review protocol was developed as indicated by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement guidelines and was registered with PROSPERO in October 2022 (CRD42022367689).

## Search Strategy and Study Selection
We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, and Scopus for articles in a systematic approach using the combination of keyword and medical subject heading adjusted for each database. The most recent search was performed on October 26, 2022. A recursive search using bibliographies of obtained articles was performed additionally. We added and contact the original authors if the study was relevant.
We performed a 2-step review of all articles. Initially, 2 reviewers independently evaluated titles and abstracts by the search for eligibility. Second, 2 independent reviewers assessed the full text of relevant articles for qualification. All disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion.

## Data Collection and Quality Assessment
Two reviewers extracted the following data: study characteristics (author, year of publication, region of study, data source, study design, and period of study), study arms, sample size, and patient characteristics. Data on preoperative displacement, fracture patterns, fracture dislocations, associated soft tissue injuries, quality of reduction (assessed using the radiographic Rasmussen score) , secondary procedures, and surgeon experience level were collected.
The risk of bias and study quality was assessed by 2 reviewers independently using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, while the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) assessment was performed to evaluate confidence in effect estimates , . All discrepancies were resolved by discussion.

## Statistical Analysis and Quantitative Data Synthesis
Dichotomous outcomes, including complication and infection rates, were summarized using the pooled proportion of events, with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated by the Wilson method . The Freeman-Tukey transformation was used to stabilize variance to include studies with a zero event rate . Continuous outcomes, including the ROM, operative time, and union time, were calculated using the weighted averages with standard deviations. The effect size from each study and the 95% CIs are displayed in forest plots.
Data from each study were pooled using the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model . The random-effects model provides more conservative estimates with wider CIs than fixed-effects models because it assumes that the analysis includes only a sample of all possible studies. In addition, the model accounts for both within-study variability (random error) and between-study variability (heterogeneity) . Heterogeneity is a concern with meta-analyses and refers to a high degree of variability in study results. Heterogeneity was detected using χ2 and I2 tests. All analyses were conducted in Stata SE, version 17 (StataCorp LLC).

## Results

## Literature Search and Selection Process
A total of 139 articles were identified through database searching, and there were 4 additional records identified through other sources. After the removal of duplicates and screening for titles and abstracts, 33 full-text articles were reviewed for eligibility, with 29 studies included in the meta-analysis (Fig. ).

Our meta-analysis included 12 case series and 17 cohort studies (5 prospective, 12 retrospectives) with a total of 747 patients. Table I reports the characteristics of included studies. Four studies reported information on preoperative displacement. Seven studies reported information on fracture dislocation or associated soft tissue injuries. Eleven studies reported the quality of reduction with the radiographic Rasmussen score. Sixteen studies reported surgeon or institutional experience level. ## Complication Rate
Figure reports the result of complication rates in this study. The complication rate of the anterior approach group was 8% (95% CI, 0.02-0.16). The complication rate of the posterior approach group was 15% (95% CI, 0.05-0.29). The complication rate of the anterior combined posterior approach group was 13% (95% CI, 0.03-0.26). The overall complication rate was 11% (95% CI, 0.06-0.17, I2 = 74.17%). There were no significant differences in the 3 groups (p = 0.298). The detailed description of complications is reported in Appendix 4 (http://links.lww.com/JBJSREV/A970). No studies included reported popliteal artery nor other vascular injuries associated with the approach. The combined group had 1 case of common peroneal nerve injury reported, whereas none were reported in the other groups included in the study .
 

## Infection Rate
Figure reports the result of infection rates in this study. The infection rate of the anterior approach group was 2% (95% CI, 0.00-0.04). The infection rate of the posterior approach group was 2% (95% CI, 0.00-0.08). The infection rate of the anterior combined posterior approach group was 3% (95% CI, 0.00-0.07). The overall infection rate was 2% (95% CI, 0.01-0.04, I2 = 0.00%). There were no significant differences in the 3 groups (p = 0.885).
 

## Range of Motion
Figure reports the result of the range of motion (ROM) in this study. The ROM of the anterior approach group was 127.31° (95% CI, 121.09-133.52). The ROM of the posterior approach group was 130.25° (95% CI, 125.52-134.98). The ROM of the anterior combined posterior approach group was 121.45° (95% CI, 117.67-125.23). The overall ROM was 126.00° (95% CI, 123.05-128.94, I2 = 13.82%). There were significant differences between the 3 groups (p = 0.01).
 

## Operation Time
Figure reports the result of operation time in this study. The operation time of the anterior approach group was 143.48 minutes (95% CI, 133.42-153.54). The operation time of the posterior approach group was 70.92 minutes (95% CI, 41.66-100.18). The operation time of the anterior combined posterior approach group was 129.71 minutes (95% CI, 119.0-140.42). The overall operation time was 124.22 minutes (95% CI, 108.47-139.96, I2 = 61.05%). There were significant differences in the 3 groups (p < 0.01).
 

## Time to Union
Figure reports the result of time to union in this study. The time to union of the anterior approach group was 13.40 weeks (95% CI, 11.96-14.84). The time to union of the posterior approach group was 13.02 weeks (95% CI, 11.86-14.19). The time to union of the anterior combined posterior approach group was 15.06 weeks (95% CI, 13.32-16.80). The overall time to union was 13.92 weeks (95% CI, 13.03-14.80, I2 = 48.23%). There were no significant differences in the 3 groups (p = 0.16).
 

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the effectiveness of anterior and posterior approaches in treating posterior tibial plateau fractures. The study found that the posterior approach resulted in greater postoperative ROM and shorter operative times compared with the anterior approach. However, there were no significant differences in complications, infections, union time, or HSS scores between the approaches. This is the first meta-analysis to compare various approaches for treating posterior tibial plateau fractures.
Posterior tibial plateau fractures are complex periarticular fractures that require restoration of the bone anatomy of the proximal tibia to avoid joint incongruence and limit joint mobilization. Posterior tibial plateau fractures may result in severe injury to the soft tissue structures and neurovascular bundles. Dissection of soft tissue structure around the knee is complicated with neurovascular structures, knee capsule, meniscus, ligaments, and tendinous attachments. To properly treat these fractures, it is important to restore the anatomy and limit movement of the knee joint to prevent further damage and promote healing. However, due to the complex nature of the soft tissue around the knee, surgical repair or reconstruction may be necessary to fully address the injury and ensure proper ROM after surgery. Our results showed that the posterior approach led to better ROM compared with other methods. This may be due to the ability to better preserve the soft tissue structures and neurovascular bundles during the surgery, resulting in less scarring and inflammation, and therefore allowing for a greater ROM postoperatively . In addition, the posterior approach may be better suited for addressing the specific anatomy and patterns of injury that can occur in posterior tibial plateau fractures. This approach allows for better visualization of the articular surface and allows for a more anatomic reduction of the fracture, which in turn leads to improved stability and better joint congruity . These factors may contribute to the improved ROM seen with the posterior approach. However, it is important to note that the decision on the surgical approach should be individualized and based on the specific characteristics of the fracture, the patient's condition, and surgeon experience.
Our research revealed that posterior approaches resulted in shorter surgical duration. There are several potential reasons why a posterior approach may have less operative time than an anterior or combined approach for posterior tibial plateau fractures. One reason is that the posterior approach allows for more direct access to the fracture site, which can make the surgery more efficient. In addition, the posterior approach generally requires less needed reduction with clamps or bone tamps of surrounding tissue due to the gravity while in the prone position, which can also contribute to a shorter operative time . Furthermore, the posterior approaches may require more experience compared with anterior approaches, and the results may be influenced by the learning curve . The increased operative time observed in anterior approaches could be attributed to less experienced surgeons selecting the approach that offers limited visualization, resulting in longer operative times with increased soft tissue dissection . Anatomical reconstruction and stable fixation of the articular surface in comminuted tibial plateau fractures can be challenging, particularly in anterior approaches where visualization may be poor . Although extending the anterolateral or anteromedial approach with an osteotomy of the femoral epicondyle has been suggested by some authors, it may not be sufficient in certain cases , . In such cases, dissecting the meniscocapsular fibers can allow for central subluxation of the meniscus, significantly improving articular visualization and enabling anatomical reduction even in highly complex fractures .
A posterior combined anterior approach for the treatment of posterior tibial plateau fractures can lead to longer union time compared with an isolated posterior or anterior approach. This is because the surgery is more extensive and can cause more trauma to the tibial plateau and the surrounding soft tissues, which can lead to a longer recovery time. In addition, the posterior combined anterior approach may require more extensive fixation, which can also contribute to a longer healing time. Healing time may vary based on the main driving risk factors including patient baseline comorbid status, fixation technique, and vascular/soft tissue injury .
Bullock and Norris analyzed the current incidence of postoperative infections for Orthopaedic Trauma Association/Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen type C fractures of the tibial plateau. They showed a 6% of deep infections in these types of fractures . Norris et al. found that surgical site infections were more common around the proximal tibia than other knee injuries, possibly due to the thin soft tissue in that area. Henkelmann et al. demonstrated double the infection rate of 12.3% for postoperative proximal tibial fractures in their meta-analysis . Yet, the study included open fractures and did not specifically identify the presence of posterior column fracture or surgical approach. The most common bacteria culture was Staphylococcus aureus with or without resistance, followed by Pseudomonas, and Staphylococcus epidermidis, which correlates with the literature . Our study found an overall infection rate of 2% for posterior tibial plateau fractures, with a higher rate of 3% for combined anterior and posterior approaches. This may be explained by the possibility of prolonged surgery among the combined approaches and the need to shift from supine to prone or prone to supine surgery. The posterior approach may have the advantage of direct exposure, easy reduction of the fracture site, and maintenance of a generous posterior soft tissue envelope. Still, it needs the protection of the neurovascular structure. These structures include the common peroneal nerve, tibial nerve, popliteal artery, and vein. Therefore, developing an ideal approach to expose the posterior tibial plateau is challenging. Chan, Chiu, and Cheng et al. suggested using arthroscopic-assisted reduction and internal fixation as a surgical method for treating posterior tibial plateau fractures , , . The advantages of this technique include improved visualization and access to the fracture site, potentially reducing the risk of complications such as malunion or nonunion. It also allows for more accurate reduction and fixation of the fracture, which can lead to better outcomes and faster recovery. However, the disadvantages of this technique include the need for specialized equipment and training, as well as the potential for increased surgical time and cost. In addition, it may not be suitable for all types of posterior tibial plateau fractures. Luo and Sun et al. introduced the posteromedial inverted L-shaped approach for the posterior column tibial plateau fractures , . The fracture site was exposed by elevating the medial head of the gastrocnemius, popliteus, and soleus, with the neurovascular bundle retracted laterally for protection. Although the disadvantage of this approach was the limited visualization in obesity or muscular patients. A direct posterior midline S-shaped approach was proposed by Brunner et al. and Connolly et al., the approach allowed retraction of the medial head of gastrocnemius and elevation of the origins of popliteus and soleus for better exposure of posterior column fragments , .
However, Lin et al. noticed that the posterior approach might have some disadvantages for patients with polytrauma, especially patients with chest trauma and C-spine injury, because a prone position may be commonly needed . They found that the prone position may not be suitable for polytrauma patients with chest and cervical injuries and recommended alternative positions, such as the lateral or supine position, may be more appropriate for these patients to avoid compressing the lungs or causing further injury to the cervical spine. Moreover, imaging techniques such as CT and magnetic resonance imaging, are essential to accurately assess the extent of the injuries and plan the most appropriate treatment course, to avoid further harm, and improve treatment outcomes. Based on the results of this study, the posterior approach may be considered as the preferred surgical approach for posterior tibial plateau fractures in cases of single-system posterior trauma without polytrauma or significant soft tissue injuries, and with a surgeon who is comfortable performing the approach. Further studies are needed to determine the optimal approach for this type of fracture.

## Strengths and Limitations
Although this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis of surgical approaches to address posterior tibial plateau fractures, several limitations must be addressed. First, the approach decision may be mainly chosen by fracture pattern, soft tissue viability, body habitus, and surgeon's experience. Second, patient cohorts consist of patients with both isolated injury and polytrauma, which may attribute to fracture healing potential and surgical timing , . Third, the level of studies included varied from observational to prospective studies; however, strict inclusion criteria were used to maximize the accuracy of results. Using the PRISMA guidelines, expansive search terminology in 2 languages increased the quality of studies that met inclusion criteria. Fourth, the postoperative protocols for ROM and weight-bearing status were not accounted for because both factors attributed to postoperative stiffness and incomplete arc of ROM. In addition, compliance with the postoperative protocol must be considered, especially among patients with trauma. Finally, other important parameters that could affect the complications, such as simple and complex types of fractures, injury mechanisms including crushing injury or fall injury, and methods of reduction including closed vs. open, were not all provided in the studies included.
Our sample of studies mainly consisted of case series and cohort studies, which can lead to lower overall evidence quality. However, randomized controlled trials are infrequently conducted to compare surgical approaches, especially in tibial plateau fractures. It should be noted that there may be some bias present in this meta-analysis because of the nature of the included studies. To address these limitations, future studies should aim to conduct high-quality research with strong study designs that minimize potential methodological biases. Potential methodological biases in studies on surgical approaches for tibial plateau fractures include selection bias, reporting bias, performance bias, detection bias, publication bias, small sample sizes, and heterogeneous patient populations. Selection bias may occur when there are systematic differences between the characteristics of patients undergoing different surgical approaches. Reporting bias may occur when study results are not fully reported or only positive results are reported. Performance bias may occur when patients or surgeons are not blinded to the surgical approach being used, and detection bias may occur when outcome assessors are not blinded to the surgical approach being used. Publication bias may occur when studies with negative or null results are less likely to be published. Small sample sizes may make it difficult to draw meaningful conclusions, and heterogeneous patient populations may make comparisons between studies more challenging. In addition, future studies should also include longer-term follow-up to evaluate the incidence of secondary procedures, such as implant retrieval, heterotopic ossification, residual instability, and arthritis, to better inform clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes. It may be necessary to consider the potential issues related to revision surgery, such as hardware removal and the need for a later total knee arthroplasty. Furthermore, it may be important to investigate the rate of popliteal artery injuries and nerve issues arising from the posterior approach. Surgeon comfort with this approach should also be considered. These issues should be addressed in future studies to provide a comprehensive understanding of the posterior approach and its potential drawbacks.

## Conclusion
The posterior approach for posterior tibial plateau fractures is associated with a better ROM, shorter operative time, than anterior or combined procedures. Additional prospective studies are required to identify the appropriate surgical approach best to achieve fracture fixation of posterior column tibial plateau fractures. However, it should be noted that the prone position required for the posterior approach may not be suitable for all patients, particularly those with medical and pulmonary comorbidities. Furthermore, in certain indications such as polytrauma, the posterior approach may not be the preferred method of treatment.
