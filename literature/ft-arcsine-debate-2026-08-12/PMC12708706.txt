## Abstract

## Background
Single-incision laparoscopic endorectal pull-through (SILEP) has emerged as an alternative to conventional laparoscopic surgery for Hirschsprung disease (HSCR), offering improved cosmetic outcomes with comparable efficacy. However, its adoption remains limited, and evidence supporting its long-term safety and functional outcomes is sparse. This study presents our institutional experience with SILEP, alongside a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating perioperative outcomes, postoperative complications, bowel function, and cosmesis.

## Patients and Methods
A prospective study was conducted at our institution involving 93 pediatric patients diagnosed with HSCR, who underwent SILEP between 2020 and 2021. Demographic data, perioperative details, and postoperative outcomes, including bowel function score (Rintala) and cosmetic results (Manchester Scar Scale), were prospectively collected and analyzed. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis were performed following PRISMA guidelines, pooling data from nine studies comprising 420 patients.

## Results
The mean operative time was 70.0 ± 30.3 min, significantly shorter than the pooled estimate from the meta-analysis (166.44 min; 95% CI 137.74–195.14, p < 0.001). No intraoperative complications or conversions were recorded. The mean hospital stay was 5.23 ± 2.69 days, consistent with prior reports. Enterocolitis occurred in 16.1% of cases, comparable to the pooled estimate (12%; 95% CI 7–21%). Among patients aged ≥ 4 years (n = 64), 84% achieved good to excellent bowel function scores. The mean Manchester Scar Score was 5.73 ± 1.38.

## Conclusion
SILEP is a safe, effective, and cosmetically superior approach for HSCR, with favorable perioperative outcomes and functional results. Our findings, supported by meta-analysis data, reinforce the feasibility of SILEP as a viable alternative for HSCR management.

Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) is a congenital disorder characterized by the absence of ganglionic cells in the distal bowel, resulting in functional intestinal obstruction [ ]. It affects approximately 1 in 5000 live births and, if left untreated, can lead to severe complications such as enterocolitis, bowel perforation, sepsis, and even death [ – ]. Surgical intervention is the definitive treatment, requiring resection of the aganglionic bowel segment and restoration of intestinal continuity. Historically, HSCR has been managed using open surgical techniques, including well-established pull-through procedures such as the Swenson, Soave, and Duhamel procedures [ ].
With advancements in minimally invasive surgery, laparoscopic-assisted endorectal pull-through has become the preferred approach for HSCR due to its advantages, including reduced surgical trauma, faster recovery, and improved cosmesis [ – ]. Conventional laparoscopic surgery (CLS), first introduced by Georgeson [ ], has been widely adopted for HSCR treatment and typically involves three to four trocar incisions [ ]. More recently, single-incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS) has emerged as a refinement of minimally invasive techniques, aiming to minimize scarring and postoperative pain by accessing the abdominal cavity through a single umbilical incision [ , , ].
Muensterer et al. were the first to describe the single-incision laparoscopic endorectal pull-through (SILEP) technique for HSCR in 2010, demonstrating its feasibility and safety [ ]. Follow-up comparative studies by Tang et al. [ ], Xia et al. [ ], and Li et al. [ ] have shown that SILS offers superior cosmetic outcomes compared to CLS, while maintaining comparable surgical efficacy.
In Vietnam, Nguyen et al. were the first to introduce SILEP at the Vietnam National Children’s Hospital in 2011, reporting favorable short-term outcomes in a small cohort of patients [ ]. Subsequent studies by Tran et al. [ ], Nguyen et al. [ ], and Dao et al. [ ] further confirmed the feasibility and safety of SILEP in a larger cohort of patients. However, these studies were limited by short follow-up durations and publication in local Vietnamese-language journals.
Despite its advantages, SILEP remains underutilized for HSCR, especially in infants and younger children. Moreover, there is a scarcity of literature on SILEP in English, with fewer than ten published studies reporting its outcomes [ , – , – ]. To date, no large prospective study has systematically assessed the safety, functional outcomes, and cosmetic results of SILEP using standardized tools. This study aims to bridge this gap by presenting our experience with a large cohort of patients and assessing medium-term follow-up outcomes. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to comprehensively evaluate the safety and efficacy of SILS for HSCR. The analysis focuses on perioperative and postoperative outcomes, including operative time, conversion rates, complications, hospital stay, bowel function, and cosmetic results. These findings will contribute to evidence-based decision-making regarding the surgical management of HSCR.

## Methods

## Prospective study

## Study subjects
This study included pediatric patients diagnosed with HSCR and treated with single-incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS) at the Department of Surgery, Vietnam National Children’s Hospital, between January 2020 and December 2021.

Inclusion criteria:

Age: Newborns to children under 16 years old.
Diagnosis: HSCR confirmed through clinical symptoms, preoperative barium enema imaging, intraoperative frozen section biopsy, and postoperative histopathology demonstrating the absence of ganglion cells.
Surgical Approach: Patients who underwent SILS using a periumbilical technique without additional abdominal incisions.

Exclusion criteria:

HSCR-associated with severe enterocolitis, bowel obstruction, or peritonitis at the time of surgery.
History of previous unsuccessful HSCR surgery.
Cases requiring multistage surgery or colostomy before definitive pull-through.
Parents or legal guardians who declined study participation.

## Study design
This prospective observational cohort study adhered to the ethical principles of biomedical research and was approved by the Ethics Committee in Biomedical Research of Hanoi Medical University (Approval No. HS50/BB-HĐĐĐ, dated April 10, 2017). The study was registered on clinicaltrials.gov with ID NCT06874686. Informed consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians before the intervention. The study complied with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [ ]. Demographic data, perioperative events, and postoperative outcomes were systematically and prospectively recorded.

## Surgical procedure
Preoperatively, all patients underwent colonic irrigation with normal saline for 3 to 4 days to reduce bacterial load and optimize surgical conditions. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation and caudal epidural analgesia.
Conventional laparoscopic instruments were used to perform the SILEP, following the technique previously described by Nguyen et al. [ ]. A 1.5–2 cm periumbilical skin incision was made, through which three separate fascial punctures were created. A 5 mm laparoscope was positioned centrally, while two working ports, a 5 mm port on the left and a 3 mm port on the right, were inserted at the lateral edges of the incision, as far apart as possible. The fascial incisions could be extended beneath the skin without the need for additional skin extension. This configuration effectively minimized instrument collision while optimizing visualization, surgical control, and cosmetic outcomes.
During laparoscopic surgery, two intraoperative extramucosal biopsies were routinely performed: the first from the narrowed aganglionic segment to confirm the absence of ganglion cells, and the second from the proximal transition zone to confirm the presence of ganglion cells. Frozen section evaluation was performed intraoperatively by a senior pediatric pathologist to ensure appropriate selection of the pull-through level. Dissection was carried out downward to approximately 1 cm below the peritoneal fold, ensuring sufficient length for a tension-free anastomosis. The colonic mesentery was further dissected to allow adequate mobilization of the colon. Finally, the modified Soave endorectal pull-through technique was performed, following the approach described in previous studies [ , ].

## Postoperative management and follow-up
Following surgery, all patients were closely monitored for recovery, bowel function, and complications. A 24Fr rectal tube was placed postoperatively to decompress the bowel and prevent obstruction. Oral fluids were introduced after six hours, and milk feeding resumed within 12 to 24 h, depending on the patient’s recovery status. The rectal tube was removed following the first bowel movement.
To prevent anastomotic stenosis, anal dilation was initiated 15 days postoperatively. It was performed once daily for one month, followed by twice weekly for another two months. Parents were trained to continue home-based anal dilation for 3 to 6 months, using dilators ranging from #12 to #15, depending on the child’s age.
Postoperative complications are classified using the Clavien–Dindo system, which categorizes surgical complications by severity, ranging from minor deviations (Grade I) to life-threatening conditions requiring intensive care (Grade IV) or resulting in death (Grade V) [ ]. Postoperative bowel function was evaluated using the Rintala Bowel Function Score (BFS) [ ], which assesses seven parameters: fecal continence, awareness of the urge to defecate, defecation frequency, fecal soiling, accidental defecation, constipation, and social impact. Each parameter is scored from 0 to 3, except for defecation frequency, which ranges from 1 to 2, with total scores ranging from 0 to 20 [ ]. Scores of 18–20 indicate excellent bowel function, 12–17 suggest good function, 7–11 reflect fair function, and ≤ 6 signify poor function, requiring further intervention [ ]. Soiling was defined as the involuntary passage of small stool quantities between voluntary bowel movements, while incontinence referred to a complete loss of bowel control.
Cosmetic outcomes were assessed using the Manchester Scar Scale [ ], which evaluates five aesthetic factors, with total scores ranging from 5 to 28, where lower scores indicate better cosmetic results. Patients were followed up two weeks postoperatively and then at regular 2- to 3-month intervals. Follow-up data were collected from outpatient visits and telephone interviews.

## Literature review and meta-analysis

## Study design and registration
A comprehensive literature search on the safety and efficacy of single-incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS) for HSCR, including perioperative and postoperative outcomes, bowel function, and cosmetic results, was conducted in September 2024. This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ ], with the full PRISMA checklist provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. The methodology strictly followed the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Interventions (Version 5.1.0) [ ], and the protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42025640336).

## Literature-search strategy
A systematic search for studies on SILS for Hirschsprung disease was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library in September 2024. The search terms included [“single-incision laparoscopic surgery” OR “SILS” OR “SILEP” OR “single-port” OR “single-trocar” OR “reduced port”] AND [“Hirschsprung disease” OR “Hirschsprung’s disease” OR “HSCR” OR “HD”]. The reference lists of included articles were manually searched to identify additional relevant studies. Studies addressing single-incision laparoscopic procedures for HSCR were systematically retrieved.
Publications consisting of case reports, abstracts with limited data, letters to the editor, and commentaries were excluded. When multiple publications included overlapping patient cohorts, the most recent study with the largest sample size was selected unless outcome measures were distinct or evaluated at different time points. Studies meeting eligibility criteria but excluded from the meta-analysis were summarized narratively.

## Eligibility criteria
Studies were included in the analysis if they investigated pediatric patients (≤ 18 years old) with a diagnosis of HSCR who underwent SILEP or modified SILS procedures. Studies with or without a control group were considered eligible. To ensure comparability and robust outcome assessment, only studies reporting at least one of the following key outcomes were included: perioperative outcomes (operative time, blood loss, intraoperative complications, conversion to open surgery, and length of hospital stay), postoperative functional outcomes (time to return to feeding, defecation frequency, constipation, soiling, incontinence, and enterocolitis), and cosmetic outcomes (evaluated using the Manchester Scar Scale, SCAR Scale, or patient/parents-reported outcomes).
Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies, and case series. Exclusion criteria were applied to review articles, case reports, editorials, surgical technique papers, and non-original studies. Studies without accessible full-text or original data, those not reporting predefined outcome measures, and those published before 2000 were excluded. Additionally, non-English publications were omitted from the review to maintain consistency in data interpretation.

## Study selection and data extraction
Study selection was conducted in two phases. First, two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles to assess their relevance based on predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then obtained for further evaluation. Any discrepancies regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary. The entire selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig.  ) [ ], which details the number of studies screened, excluded, and included, along with reasons for exclusion.
 Data extraction was performed using a pre-designed electronic data extraction form. Extracted variables included study characteristics (author, year, country, study design, sample size), patient demographics (age at surgery, sex distribution), surgical approach (standard SILS, modified techniques, additional ports, conversion to open surgery), perioperative outcomes (operative time, blood loss, complications, length of stay), postoperative functional outcomes (bowel function, constipation, incontinence, enterocolitis), cosmetic outcomes, follow-up duration, and assessment methods.

## Quality assessment
The quality of non-randomized studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates study design, comparability, and outcome measures [ ]. Furthermore, the methodological rigor of this systematic review was independently evaluated by two senior reviewers using the Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) tool, ensuring adherence to best practices in systematic review methodology [ ].

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software version 4.3.0 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were made using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test, depending on the data distribution. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) for normally distributed data or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for non-normally distributed data. The Mann–Whitney U test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Fisher’s exact test, or Student’s t test were applied as appropriate for comparing continuous variables between groups. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
For the meta-analysis of proportions, the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation [ ] was applied to stabilize variance when estimating pooled proportions for conversion rates, blood loss, length of hospital stay, complication rates, and postoperative functional outcomes. Pooled estimates were reported as percentages with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).
Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. Thresholds for interpreting I2 were as follows: 0–25% = low heterogeneity, 26–50% = moderate heterogeneity, and > 50% = substantial heterogeneity. When I2 exceeded 25%, a random-effects model was employed to account for between-study variability. In cases of low heterogeneity (I2 ≤ 25%), a fixed-effects model was used [ ].
For continuous variables, the weighted mean difference (WMD) was calculated, and a random-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was substantial. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding studies deemed to have a high risk of bias, allowing assessment of the robustness of pooled estimates. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test and funnel plots. However, due to the relatively small number of included studies, formal statistical testing for publication bias was not conducted [ ].

## Results

## Prospective study outcomes
Between January 2020 and December 2021, a total of 93 pediatric patients underwent SILEP for HSCR at Vietnam National Children’s Hospital. All 93 enrolled patients were included in the final analysis. No patients were excluded post enrollment. Patients’ demographic data and perioperative results are summarized in Table  .
 The operative duration showed no statistically significant difference based on the location of the aganglionic segment (p = 0.989), suggesting that surgical complexity and duration were consistent regardless of the affected colonic region.
Table demonstrates postoperative outcomes, cosmesis, and functional assessment. Re-operation was required in five patients (5.4%) due to Clavien–Dindo III complications. These included one case (1.1%) of bowel obstruction caused by adhesions, one case (1.1%) of anal fistula, and two cases (2.2%) of rectal mucosal prolapse. Only one patient (1.1%) required a repeat endorectal pull-through due to anastomotic stenosis. Other postoperative complications included enterocolitis in 16.1% of cases, constipation in 6.5%, and soiling in 8.6%, while no cases of fecal incontinence were reported (Table  ).
 At the time of the last follow-up, 64 patients (69%) met the criteria for bowel function score evaluation (age ≥ 4 years). Among them, 44% achieved excellent bowel function (BFS 18–20), 40% had good function (BFS 12–17), and 16% had fair function (BFS 7–11). No patients had poor bowel function (BFS ≤ 6). The mean bowel function score was 16.4 ± 4.21. Over time, bowel movement frequency decreased from 4 to 15 times per day in the early postoperative period to 1–3 times per day at 12 months post surgery. Cosmetic outcomes were excellent, with a mean Manchester Scar Score of 5.73 ± 1.38.

## Literature review and meta-analysis

## Study characteristics
The systematic literature search identified 241 potentially relevant articles from electronic databases, as illustrated in Fig.  . Following the application of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 224 articles were excluded, leaving 17 studies for full-text review to assess eligibility. Among these, eight studies were excluded due to reasons such as limited data availability (abstract-only publication), duplication of patient cohorts, reporting on different surgical approaches, or lacking the necessary outcome measures for inclusion. After a thorough full-text review, nine studies met the final inclusion criteria (Fig.  ).
Among the included studies, eight were retrospective cohort studies, and one was a prospective case series. Notably, one study presented two distinct patient groups undergoing SILS for HSCR, differing only in incision location; therefore, both cohorts were analyzed separately in the meta-analysis [ ]. Additionally, in Zhu et al.’s study [ ], six patients were excluded from the original dataset due to a diagnosis of Intestinal Neuronal Dysplasia, which could have confounded the outcomes of HSCR patients. The study by Aubdoollah et al. [ ] was also included, even though it reported a modified approach using a hybrid single-incision laparoscopic surgery (H-SILEP) technique that involved an additional trocar-less entry site.
In total, the meta-analysis incorporated 420 pediatric patients from nine cohorts published between 2010 and 2022. These patients underwent SILS or modified SILS procedures for HSCR. The characteristics of each included study are summarized in Table  .
 

## Meta-analysis findings

## Demographics

## Gender distribution
Among the 420 patients included in the cumulative analysis, the proportion of male participants ranged from 64 to 95%, with low heterogeneity (I2 = 29%; Cochran Q test, p = 0.18). The pooled estimate of the male proportion across the ten cohorts was 77% (95% CI 71–82%). Similar pooled estimates in both random and fixed effect models suggest that the results are relatively consistent across studies. The confidence interval suggests a 95% probability that male patients are dominant compared to their female counterparts in the included studies.

## Age at surgery
The mean age at surgery across nine cohorts, as estimated using a random-effects model, was 9.26 months (95% CI 4.25–14.28). Significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 96%; Cochran’s Q test, p < 0.01), indicating substantial variability in age distribution across studies. This variability may be attributed to differences in healthcare systems, diagnostic practices, and surgical referral patterns. Notably, a large study by Meng et al. [ ] reported older patient cohorts, likely due to delayed diagnosis or logistical barriers in their settings. Conversely, Muensterer et al.’s study featured younger patients, reflecting a trend toward earlier surgical intervention in certain specialized centers [ ].

## Perioperative outcomes

## Operative time
The mean operative time varied significantly across studies, ranging from 96 min [ ] to 226 min [ ]. The pooled estimate of the mean operative time, calculated using a random-effects model, was 166.44 min (95% CI 137.74–195.14) (Fig.  ). Heterogeneity analysis indicated high variability (I2 = 99%; Cochran Q test, p < 0.01). The longer operative times in studies by Meng et al. [ ] and Xia et al. [ ] likely influenced the overall pooled estimate, suggesting that technical complexity, surgeon experience, and patient age may contribute to variability in surgical duration.
 

## Conversion to conventional laparoscopic or open surgery
The pooled estimate for conversion to open surgery was 0% (95% CI 0.00–0.02, Cochran Q test, p = 1, I2 = 0%), indicating no observed heterogeneity. This confirms SILS as a feasible and reliable approach without an increased risk of conversion.

## Blood loss
Blood loss was reported for 380 patients across the included studies. The pooled mean blood loss was 28.49 mL (95% CI 11.46–45.51; Cochran Q test, p < 0.01, I2 = 99%), with high heterogeneity. The study with the largest sample size (Meng et al., n = 109) reported a mean blood loss of 53.38 mL (95% CI 45.08–61.68) [ ], likely contributing to the overall heterogeneity.

## Length of hospital stay
The mean length of hospital stay ranged from 4.5 [ ] to 14.6 days [ ]. The pooled estimate, calculated using a random-effects model, was 9.61 days (95% CI 7.24–11.98), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 99%; Cochran Q test, p < 0.01).

## Time to return to feeding
Only one study (Yan et al.) reported on the time to return to feeding following SILS for Hirschsprung disease [ ]. In this study, the average time to return to feeding among 18 patients was 3.1 ± 1.8 days [ ].

## Mortality and severe morbidity
No mortality or severe morbidity was reported among 420 patients, underscoring the safety of SILS for HSCR. Even in larger cohorts [ , , ], no life-threatening complications were observed.

## Bowel function score
Among the nine included studies, three utilized validated scoring systems to assess postoperative bowel function [ , , ]. Nguyen et al. employed the seven-question Rintala questionnaire, in which all 40 patients achieved excellent scores [ ]. The other two studies [ , ] applied El-Sawaf’s bowel function questionnaire, which categorizes outcomes as Excellent (0–10), Good (11–20), Fair (21–30), and Poor (31–40) [ ]. In Meng et al.’s study, 84.31% of patients had Excellent outcomes, 13.73% were classified as Good, 4% as Fair, and none as Poor [ ]. Similarly, Xia et al. reported 84.9% of patients as Excellent, 15% as Good, and 0% as Fair or Poor [ ].

## Defecation frequency
Defecation frequency was reported in six studies [ , , , – ]; however, due to variations in reporting time points, a meta-analysis was not conducted. Muensterer et al. observed multiple daily bowel movements postoperatively in six patients but did not document follow-up data [ ]. Yan et al. reported a mean early postoperative defecation frequency of 8.1 ± 1.9 bowel movements per day in 18 patients [ ]. Xia et al. [ ] and Zhu et al. [ ] described a downward trend in defecation frequency over time, decreasing from 10 to 1–4 bowel movements per day and from 20 to 1–2 bowel movements per day, respectively, at the final follow-up. Similarly, Aubdoollah et al. [ ] and Tang et al. [ ] reported a progressive reduction in defecation frequency at 1-week, 1-month, and 3-month follow-up intervals. These findings consistently demonstrate an initial phase of hyperdefecation that gradually stabilizes over time following SILS for Hirschsprung disease.

## Constipation
A total of 404 patients were assessed for constipation during long-term follow-up, with 29 cases reported. The pooled prevalence of constipation was estimated at 7% (95% CI 5–10%, Cochran Q test, p = 0.38, I2 = 7%), indicating minimal heterogeneity among the included studies. Notably, five studies [ , , , , ] reported no cases of constipation during long-term follow-up, further reinforcing its low prevalence in this patient population.

## Incontinence
Long-term fecal incontinence was assessed in 300 patients. The pooled prevalence of incontinence was 2% (95% CI 1–4%, Cochran Q test, p = 0.86, I2 = 0%), indicating no heterogeneity across studies, confirming SILS as an effective technique for preserving continence in HSCR patients.

## Soiling
A total of 316 patients were evaluated for soiling symptoms during follow-up. The pooled prevalence of soiling was 7% (95% CI 3–16%, Cochran Q test, p = 0.11, I2 = 47%), indicating moderate heterogeneity among studies. The largest study (n = 109) reported a soiling prevalence of 14% (95% CI 8–22%) [ ], whereas Nguyen et al. [ ] reported no cases of soiling in their 40-patient cohort. This variability may reflect differences in follow-up duration, patient populations, or reporting methodologies.

## Enterocolitis
A total of 414 patients were assessed for enterocolitis prevalence during follow-up. The pooled estimate was 12% (95% CI 7–21%, Cochran Q test, p = 0.01, I2 = 59%), indicating moderate variability among studies. The largest cohort (n = 109) reported an enterocolitis prevalence of 18% (95% CI 12–27%) [ ], while Xia et al. [ ] documented the highest rate at 38% (95% CI 24–53%). The observed heterogeneity (I2 = 59%) suggests some inconsistency in the reported enterocolitis rates, potentially due to variations in prophylactic strategies, postoperative care, and follow-up durations across studies.

## Cosmesis
Two studies employed scar scoring systems to evaluate cosmesis following SILS. Nguyen et al. [ ] used the Manchester Scar Scale, with all patients achieving the maximum score of 5. Li et al. [ ] utilized the SCAR scale, which assesses linear scars based on eight parameters using photographic evaluation, with scores ranging from 0 (best) to 15 (worst), and achieved a median SCAR score of 0. Additionally, six studies [ , – , , ] assessed scar appearance through subjective evaluations by patients, guardians, and physicians, all of which reported highly satisfactory cosmetic outcomes with no visible scars.

## Discussion
Single-incision laparoscopic surgery (SILS) represents a major advancement in minimally invasive surgical techniques, aiming to reduce surgical trauma while maximizing cosmetic benefits. Originally applied in procedures such as appendectomy and cholecystectomy, SILS has expanded to more complex gastrointestinal, urological, and thoracic surgeries [ ]. Within the field of pediatric surgery, SILEP has emerged as an alternative to conventional laparoscopy for HSCR, offering comparable surgical outcomes with superior cosmetic results [ – ]. Although SILEP is effective for short-segment HSCR, its utility in long-segment or total colonic disease is limited. These cases may require additional ports or a hybrid approach due to the technical challenges in mobilizing proximal bowel segments through a single incision.

## Patient demographics and perioperative characteristics
Our study included 93 patients, with a notable male predominance (85 male, 8 females), reflecting the well-established male-to-female ratio of HSCR, as also observed in the meta-analysis, where the pooled estimate of male proportion across studies was 77% (95% CI 71–82%) with low heterogeneity. The mean age at surgery in our cohort was 3.27 months, which is younger than the meta-analysis estimates of 9.26 months (95% CI 4.25–14.28) but falls within the reported age range in prior studies at the same institution [ ]. This suggests a trend toward earlier surgical intervention, which has been linked to improved long-term bowel function outcomes [ ].
The mean operative time in our study was 70.0 ± 30.3 min, significantly shorter than the pooled estimate from the meta-analysis (166.44 min; 95% CI 137.74–195.14, p < 0.001). Our results suggest that SILEP can be performed efficiently, particularly as surgical expertise with this approach increases. Previous studies have noted that SILS is technically more demanding due to instrument crowding and limited visualization [ ]. However, the use of optimized port positioning and technical refinements may contribute to a shorter learning curve and faster operative times, as demonstrated in our study. Aubdoollah et al. [ ] reported an average operative time of 116 min using a hybrid single-incision approach, incorporating an additional trocar-less instrument placement. In contrast, our findings suggest that a purely periumbilical technique utilizing three trocars is sufficient.
The variation in operative time across studies can be attributed to surgeon expertise, patient anatomy, and institutional protocols [ , , ]. Importantly, we recorded no intraoperative complications or conversions to open surgery, further supporting the technical feasibility and reproducibility of SILEP. These findings are consistent with the meta-analysis, which reported a pooled conversion rate of 0% (95% CI 0.00–0.02).
The most common aganglionic segment location was the rectum (71%), followed by the sigmoid colon (25.8%) and left colon (3.2%), consistent with epidemiological data suggesting that short-segment HSCR is the predominant form [ ]. Despite the anatomical variation in the location of the aganglionic segment, we observed no significant difference in operative time between cases involving the rectum, sigmoid colon, or left colon, a finding consistent with prior studies [ ].

## Postoperative recovery and complications
The mean hospital stay in our study was 5.23 ± 2.69 days, which is shorter than the pooled estimate from the meta-analysis (4.5–14.56 days). Our findings closely align with Nguyen et al. [ ] (mean = 4.5 days) and previous reports on SILEP and are generally shorter than hospital stays associated with CLS [ ]. This relatively short length of stay highlights the benefits of a minimally invasive approach, including reduced postoperative morbidity and faster recovery. However, as seen in both our study and the meta-analysis, hospital stay duration varied significantly across studies (range: 4.5–14.56 days; I2 = 99%), likely due to differences in institutional perioperative management strategies and variations in healthcare infrastructure [ ].
The reoperation rate in our study was 5.4%, comparable to rates reported in previous studies, which documented low but non-negligible rates of secondary surgical intervention [ – ]. Of the five patients requiring reoperation, one patient (1.1%) underwent redo pull-through surgery due to anastomotic stenosis, a complication consistent with previously reported risk factors such as ischemia, inadequate mobilization, or anastomotic tension [ ]. The rates of rectal mucosal prolapse (2.2%) and bowel obstruction (1.1%) align with those reported by Xia et al., reinforcing the need for careful postoperative monitoring to identify and manage complications early [ ].
Enterocolitis is one of the most significant postoperative complications following HSCR surgery, and our findings suggest that SILEP does not increase its incidence compared to CLS or open surgery [ ]. We observed an incidence of 16.1%, which was consistent with the meta-analysis estimate of 12% (95% CI 7–21%) and lower than the 25–37% prevalence found in some conventional laparoscopic series [ ]. Several studies suggest that preserving a short rectal muscular cuff may help reduce the risk of enterocolitis by preventing fecal stasis and improving colonic motility [ ]. Our approach incorporated a 1.5–2 cm rectal cuff and the removal of dilated, poorly functioning colonic segments, which may have contributed to the relatively lower enterocolitis rates observed.

## Functional outcomes
Bowel function was evaluated using the Rintala Bowel Function Score (BFS), with 69% of patients eligible for assessment (≥ 4 years old). Among them, 44% achieved excellent function (BFS 18–20), 40% had good function (BFS 12–17), and 16% had fair function (BFS 7–11), with no poor outcomes (≤ 6). These results are highly comparable to those reported by Meng et al. [ ] and Xia et al. [ ], where 85% of patients had excellent to good function using El-Sawaf’s questionnaire.
Defecation frequency normalized over time, transitioning from 4 to 15 bowel movements/day in the early postoperative period to 1 to 3 times/day at 12 months. Similar defecation trends were observed by other authors [ , ], reinforcing that postoperative hyperdefecation is a transient phase following pull-through procedures.
The constipation rate in our study (6.5%) was consistent with the meta-analysis estimate (7%), and most cases improved with dietary modifications and anal dilation [ ]. Soiling, observed in 8.6% of cases, was slightly higher than the meta-analysis estimate (7%) but showed improvement over time, consistent with reports from Li et al. [ ] and Aubdoollah et al. [ ].
The rarity of incontinence (pooled prevalence: 2%, 95% CI 1–4%) further supports the ability of SILEP to preserve sphincter function when performed correctly [ , ]. While these findings are reassuring, longer follow-up studies are necessary to assess the durability of bowel function outcomes.

## Cosmetic outcomes
The mean Manchester Scar Score in our study (5.73 ± 1.38) is in line with Nguyen et al. [ ], where all patients achieved a maximum score of 5, indicating superior cosmetic outcomes. Li et al. [ ] also reported excellent cosmesis using the SCAR scale, with a median score of 0. Additionally, subjective evaluations in previous studies have consistently rated SILEP as superior to CLS in terms of cosmesis [ , ].
In conclusion, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first prospective analysis of SILEP involving a substantial cohort of patients, providing medium-term follow-up data. It employs validated tools for the rigorous assessment of bowel function and cosmetic outcomes. Furthermore, it uniquely contextualizes these findings within the broader spectrum of existing research through a comprehensive meta-analysis.

## Limitations
Despite the promising results of our study and the findings from the meta-analysis, several limitations must be acknowledged.
First, our institutional data were derived from a single-center cohort with a moderate sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Although the average follow-up duration of 43.4 months provides valuable insight into medium-term outcomes, it remains insufficient to comprehensively evaluate long-term bowel function, especially as continence and social independence evolve over time in pediatric patients.
Second, postoperative pain was not systematically assessed using validated scoring systems, which precludes direct evaluation of one of the often-discussed differences between single-incision and multiport laparoscopic approaches.
Third, although cosmetic outcomes were assessed using the Manchester Scar Scale in our prospective cohort, we did not collect comparable scar assessment data for patients who previously underwent conventional laparoscopic pull-through at our institution. This limits our ability to conduct an internal comparative analysis of cosmetic results between surgical approaches.
Fourth, while a comparison with conventional multiport laparoscopic pull-through performed at our center would have strengthened internal validity, these historical cases lacked standardized documentation of bowel function, cosmetic outcomes, and complication grading. As a result, a direct institutional comparison was not feasible. To contextualize our findings, we instead conducted a meta-analysis of published studies, while acknowledging the inherent heterogeneity across those studies.
The meta-analysis itself has inherent limitations, including high heterogeneity across studies due to variations in surgical technique, perioperative care, and outcome definitions. Bowel function assessments were inconsistent, with different scoring systems used, making direct comparisons difficult. Furthermore, most included studies were retrospective, with short follow-up durations and a lack of standardized reporting on long-term complications such as enterocolitis and constipation. The absence of randomized controlled trials introduces potential publication bias, as studies with positive outcomes may be more frequently published. To establish SILEP as a definitive alternative to conventional laparoscopy, future research should focus on large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies with long-term follow-up and standardized outcome assessments.

## Conclusion
SILEP appears to be a safe, feasible, and effective surgical option for the treatment of HSCR, with encouraging perioperative outcomes, satisfactory functional recovery, and favorable cosmetic results. In our prospective series, operative time was efficient, no intraoperative complications or conversions were observed, and most patients experienced an uncomplicated postoperative course with a relatively short hospital stay. The incidence of enterocolitis was within the expected range, and bowel function outcomes, as assessed by the Rintala score, were predominantly good to very good among eligible patients. Cosmetic outcomes were highly rated by caregivers and clinicians, with low Manchester Scar Scale scores suggesting minimal visible scarring.
These findings, further supported by our meta-analysis, reinforce the viability of SILEP as a minimally invasive alternative in HSCR management. However, further multicenter studies with longer follow-up and standardized outcome reporting are warranted to strengthen the evidence base and assess long-term functional durability.
