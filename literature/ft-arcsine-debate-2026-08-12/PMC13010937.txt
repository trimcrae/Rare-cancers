## Abstract

## Background
Drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) is a major public health challenge in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), worsened by high HIV prevalence and limited healthcare infrastructure. Bedaquiline, a cornerstone DR-TB treatment, is linked to QT interval prolongation, a risk factor for life-threatening arrhythmias. This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the incidence of QT prolongation in bedaquiline-treated DR-TB patients in SSA.

## Methods
Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and African Journals Online (2013–2024). Studies reporting QT intervals in DR-TB patients receiving bedaquiline in SSA were included (QTc > 450 ms men/>460 ms women). A random-effects model with Freeman-Tukey transformation estimated pooled incidence. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses explored heterogeneity (I2) and bias, with funnel plots assessing publication bias. Data were analyzed using Stata 18.0.

## Results
From 616 records, 23 studies (6673 bedaquiline-exposed patients) were included; 19 studies with 6158 patients contributed to the meta-analysis. Pooled incidence of QTc prolongation (>450 ms in men or >460 ms in women) was 15.0% (95% CI 9.0–22.0%, I2 = 97.1%). RCTs reported higher incidence (35.6%) than observational studies (13.1%; p = 0.001). HIV co-infection (prevalence 26.4%) showed a non-significant trend toward higher incidence (22.0% vs 11.0%, p = 0.07) and QT-prolonging drugs (52.8%) increased risk.

## Conclusion
QT prolongation is significant in SSA’s bedaquiline-treated DR-TB patients, necessitating enhanced ECG monitoring and integrated TB-HIV care.

1.

## Introduction
Drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB), which includes isoniazid-resistant TB, rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB), multidrug-resistant (MDR-TB), and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB), poses a significant challenge to global health . Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is disproportionately affected as the disease burden is amplified by high HIV prevalence and limited healthcare infrastructure . In 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that SSA accounted for over 25% of the 10.8 million global TB cases, with approximately 3.9% of new cases and 18% of previously treated cases classified as MDR-TB or rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB) . The co-infections of DR-TB and HIV, which compromise immune responses and complicate treatment regimens, have led to elevated mortality rates, with an estimated 1.5 million TB-related deaths in 2023 . Effective therapeutic options for DR-TB are critical to curbing this epidemic, however, the introduction of novel agents like bedaquiline has brought both hope and new safety concerns . This necessitates rigorous evaluation of their adverse effects in high-burden settings.
Bedaquiline, a diarylquinoline approved by WHO in 2013, has transformed DR-TB treatment by offering an oral alternative to injectable agents like kanamycin and capreomycin , which were associated with severe toxicities such as ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity . By targeting mycobacterial ATP synthase, bedaquiline disrupts energy production in Mycobacterium tuberculosis, achieving high efficacy against resistant strains , with cure rates exceeding 80% in optimized regimens . Its adoption across Sub-Saharan Africa, including in countries like South Africa, Nigeria, and Uganda, has been pivotal, with over 200,000 patients treated globally by 2024 (World Health Organization, 2024). However, bedaquiline’s cardiac safety profile, particularly its association with QT interval prolongation, a risk factor for life-threatening arrhythmias like torsades de pointes, remains a concern , especially in resource-constrained settings where electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring is often limited . This adverse effect is driven partly by the M2 metabolite of bedaquiline , highlighting the necessity for region-specific data to guide safe treatment protocols.
The incidence of QT prolongation in bedaquiline-treated patients varies widely across studies, reflecting differences in patient populations, monitoring practices, and concomitant medications. For instance, a South African cohort reported a 7.2% incidence of clinically significant QT prolongation, while a multicenter trial noted rates as high as 15% when bedaquiline was combined with other QT-prolonging drugs like clofazimine or delamanid , . Factors such as HIV co-infection, older age, and polypharmacy are known to exacerbate this risk , , . Prior global reviews have addressed bedaquiline’s safety but lack a focused synthesis of Sub-Saharan African data, where unique epidemiological and healthcare challenges prevail . This gap is critical because local data are essential for tailoring WHO guidelines and national TB programs to mitigate cardiac risks while maximizing treatment benefits.
Given the widespread use of bedaquiline in SSA for over a decade, we posit that the incidence of QT interval prolongation varies significantly. However, there is a paucity of synthesized data on its incidence and predictors in SSA’s DR-TB population. This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore aimed to estimate the pooled incidence of QT interval prolongation among patients with drug-resistant TB receiving bedaquiline-based regimens in Sub-Saharan Africa and to explore associated factors.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Study design
This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024560368).

2.2.

## Search strategy
A comprehensive search was performed across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and African Journals Online (AJOL) for studies published between January 2013 and May 2024, aligning with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) bedaquiline approval for DR-TB treatment (World Health Organization, 2013). Search terms included Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms such as “Bedaquiline,” “MDR-TB,” “XDR-TB,” “QT prolongation,” and “Sub-Saharan Africa,” with wildcards (e.g., “drugresistant,” “QTprolongation”). A study team member (R.O.) with expertise in systematic reviews piloted the search against known studies to ensure accuracy. Only studies in English or French were included, reflecting the common languages in SSA. Databases were last searched on 31 May 2024. A search string has been attached as a .

2.3.

## Eligibility criteria
Studies were selected using the PEO (Patient, Exposure, Outcome) framework:
•Patient (P): Patients diagnosed with DR-TB in Sub-Saharan Africa, defined as resistance to one or more first-line anti-TB drugs, including isoniazid-resistant TB, multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), or extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB, per WHO’s 2021 definition: MDR/RR-TB resistant to any fluoroquinolone and one Group A drug . Studies that included only isoniazid-monoresistant TB without rifampicin resistance were excluded; none of the finally included studies contained such patients.
•Exposure (E): Treatment with bedaquiline, alone or in combination regimens.
•Outcome (O): Reported incidence of QT interval prolongation, defined as QTc > 450 ms for men or > 460 ms for women, measured via at least one electrocardiogram (ECG).
Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, case-control studies, and case series with ≥10 participants. Studies published before 2013, case reports, case series with <10 cases, reviews, editorials, animal studies, or those lacking ECG data were excluded.

2.4.

## Study selection
Duplicates were removed using Covidence software. Two independent reviewers (M.O.O., I.P.) screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review against eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and by a third reviewer (R.O.). To prevent patient overlap, studies were cross-checked for study site, period, and population details, prioritizing the most comprehensive or recent dataset.

2.5.

## Data Extraction
Data were extracted by two reviewers (M.O.O. and I.P.) using a pre-designed Microsoft Excel 2016 macro form, with duplicate entry to ensure consistency. Extracted items included:
•Study Characteristics: Author, year, country, design, data collection period.
•Patient Characteristics: Sample size, age (mean/median), sex, HIV status, electrolyte levels (e.g., potassium, magnesium).
•Treatment Details: Bedaquiline dosage, duration, concomitant medications, use of QT-prolonging drugs (e.g., fluoroquinolones, clofazimine).
•Outcomes: Incidence and severity of QT prolongation, time to onset (within 4 weeks to capture M2 metabolite peak, with longer-term data if available), QTc distribution, and change from baseline.
Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (R.O.).

2.6.

## Risk of bias assessment
Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers (M.O.O. and J.N.O.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for RCTs and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies. Studies were graded as low, moderate, or high risk, with high-risk defined as having critical flaws (e.g., inadequate QT monitoring, >20% loss to follow-up). Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (R.O.). All eligible studies were included, with sensitivity analyses to assess high-risk studies.

2.7.

## Data synthesis and analysis
Analyses were conducted using STATA 18.0 (StataCorp, 2023) and Microsoft Excel 2016. The primary outcome, pooled incidence of QT prolongation, was estimated using a random-effects model to minimize small-study bias. Incidence proportions underwent Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variance. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic (>50% indicating significant heterogeneity). Subgroup analyses explored heterogeneity sources. Sensitivity analyses excluded moderate/high-risk studies or outliers to test robustness. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals.

2.8.

## Patient and public involvement
None.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Study selection
The systematic search identified 616 studies from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and African Journals Online (AJOL). After removing 202 duplicates (7 manually identified and 195 by Covidence software), 414 studies were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 357 were deemed irrelevant, and 57 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Thirty-four studies were excluded for reasons including the following:
•Wrong setting (n = 18),
•Conference abstracts (n = 8),
•Wrong publication type (n = 3),
•Wrong study design (n = 2),
•Missing full text (n = 1),
•Pediatric population (n = 1), and
•Wrong outcomes (n = 1).
Ultimately, 23 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis and meta-analysis. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram ( ).
 
3.2.

## Study characteristics
The 23 included studies, conducted between 2013 and 2023, comprised 8 prospective cohort studies, 1 prospective observational study, 10 retrospective cohort studies, and 4 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), all performed in Sub-Saharan Africa. The studies were primarily conducted in South Africa (n = 17), with others in Uganda (n = 1), Democratic Republic of the Congo (n = 1), Ethiopia (n = 1), Niger (n = 1), and multi-country settings (Ethiopia, Kenya, Lesotho, South Africa; n = 2). Sample sizes ranged from 28 to 2296 participants, with a total of 8169 participants across studies, of whom 6673 received bedaquiline. Follow-up durations varied from 6 to 24 months (median 12 months, IQR 6–18 months), 6 studies did not report follow-up duration. Bedaquiline was administered at the standard WHO-recommended dose (400 mg daily for 2 weeks, followed by 200 mg three times weekly for 22 weeks) in 18 studies; 5 studies did not specify dosage. Concomitant medications included levofloxacin, clofazimine, linezolid, and delamanid, with 7 studies not reporting co-medications. Study characteristics are detailed in .
 
3.3.

## Patient characteristics
The median age of participants ranged from 3.9 to 36 years (study-level median 35 years, IQR 31–36). Sex distribution was reported in 21 studies, with 54.3% male participants (3518/6478). HIV co-infection was prevalent, reported in 20 studies, with 1650/6249 participants (26.4%) being HIV-positive. Diabetes mellitus was less common, reported in 5 studies, affecting 368/2641 participants (13.9%). Baseline ECG assessments were reported in 20 studies, with median baseline QTcF values ranging from 396 to 430 ms (study-level median 404.6 ms, IQR 397.5–408 ms). Most studies (n = 20) excluded participants with baseline QTcF > 450 ms. Details in .

## Risk of bias assessment
Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for RCTs (n = 4) and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies (n = 19). Of the RCTs, three [ , , ] were classified as low risk across all domains, while one raised some concerns due to potential issues with randomization and protocol deviations. For cohort studies, 10 achieved high quality (NOS score ≥ 7, low risk), 5 were moderate (NOS score 5–6), and 4 [ , , , ] were of low quality (NOS score < 5, high risk), primarily due to inadequate QT monitoring [ , ] or substantial loss to follow-up exceeding 20% [ ]. Detailed risk of the bias assessments is summarized in .
 
3.5.

## Pooled incidence of QT prolongation
Of the 23 studies identified, 19 provided sufficient data to calculate the incidence of QT prolongation, totaling 6158 bedaquiline-treated participants and 588 incident cases. Using a random-effects model with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation (DerSimonian-Laird method), the pooled incidence of QT prolongation was 15.0% (95% CI: 9.0–22.0%, I2 = 97.1%, p < 0.001). Individual study incidences ranged from 1.3% [ ] to 40.0% [ , ]. 3.6.

## Subgroup analyses
Study Design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs, n = 2: Dooley 2021, Motta 2024) had a pooled incidence of 35.6% (95% CI: 28.5–43.0%, I2 = 0.0%), compared to 13.1% (95% CI: 10.0–16.6%, I2 = 96.0%) for observational studies (n = 17; p = 0.001 for subgroup difference).
HIV Status: Studies with ≥ 30% HIV-positive participants (n = 9) showed a pooled incidence of 22.0% (95% CI: 13.0–31.0%, I2 = 92.6%) versus 11.0% (95% CI: 8.0–15.0%, I2 = 96.9%) in studies with < 30% HIV prevalence (n = 9; p = 0.07). The trend toward higher incidence with HIV co-infection was not statistically significant.
Concomitant QT-Prolonging Drugs: Studies without reported clofazimine or delamanid use (n = 14) had a higher pooled incidence of 17.0% (95% CI: 14.0–21.0%, I2 = 96.9%), compared to 6.0% (95% CI: 4.0–9.0%, I2 = 10.5%) in studies with clofazimine or delamanid use (n = 5; p < 0.001).

3.7.

## Sensitivity analyses
Excluding studies with high incidence (>35%; [ , , ]) yielded a pooled incidence of 13.0% (95% CI: 10.0–16.0%, I2 = 96.0%, p < 0.001).
Sensitivity analysis excluding studies with high risk of bias (n = 4: [ , , , ]) yielded a pooled incidence of 17.0% (95% CI: 13.0–21.0%, I2 = 96.6%, p < 0.001), slightly higher than the overall estimate (15.0%, 95% CI: 12.0–18.0%) ( ).
 
3.8.

## Additional outcomes
Severity of QT Prolongation: Severity was reported in 10 studies, with mild prolongation (QTcF 450–480 ms) comprising 70.4% of cases, moderate (480–500 ms) 22.1%, and severe (>500 ms) 7.5%.
Time to QT Prolongation: Six studies reported a median time to onset of 3.5 months (IQR: 2.5–4.5 months).

4.

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 studies, encompassing 6673 bedaquiline-treated patients with DR-TB in SSA, estimated a pooled incidence of QT interval prolongation of 15.0%. This finding highlights the critical role of bedaquiline in DR-TB management while emphasizing its cardiac safety challenges in a region burdened by high HIV prevalence [ ] and limited healthcare infrastructure [ ]. The high heterogeneity reflects variability in study designs, ECG monitoring protocols, and patient characteristics, posing challenges for synthesizing pharmacovigilance data in resource-constrained settings.
The pooled incidence aligns with global estimates for bedaquiline-treated cohorts, ranging from 10 to 20% [ , ], but is lower than rates reported with bedaquiline-delamanid combinations (20–44%) . The pooled incidence was lower than that reported by Olayanju et al. (2020), who reported a 27.8% incidence of QT prolongation across their cohort in South Africa, with 19.5% in the bedaquiline-only group and 44.1% in the bedaquiline-delamanid combination group , reflecting the higher cardiotoxic potential for delamanid [ ]. Pai et al. (2022) observed a lower incidence (2.5%) of QTc prolongation in bedaquiline-treated patients, compared to our pooled incidence, with the patients mostly receiving concomitant levofloxacin (96.6%) or clofazimine (91.4%) [ ]. This highlights the influence of co-administered QT-prolonging drugs.
Key predictors of QT prolongation were identified through subgroup analyses. Studies with ≥30% HIV-positive participants reported a higher incidence, which is consistent with evidence that HIV and ART, particularly efavirenz, exacerbate QT prolongation via drug interactions or electrolyte imbalances [ ]. However, this was not statistically significant. The high prevalence of HIV (26.4%) among the people being treated for DR-TB shows the need for integrated TB-HIV care. Surprisingly, studies reporting clofazimine or delamanid use showed a lower incidence (6.0% vs. 17.0%), possibly due to incomplete reporting of concomitant medications in the included studies or differences in study populations. Clofazimine’s association with increased QTcF aligns with its known cardiotoxic potential [ ]. Therefore, careful ECG monitoring is recommended when clofazimine is co-administered with bedaquiline to mitigate the risk of clinically significant QT prolongation. The median time to QT prolongation coincides with the accumulation of bedaquiline’s M2 metabolite, known to prolong QT intervals , supporting the recommendation by WHO for intensive monitoring within the first 4–6 weeks of initiating Bedaquiline .
Sensitivity analysis showed an increased pooled incidence of 17.0% from 15%, after the exclusion of studies with high risk of bias, suggesting the impact of methodological variability in high-RoB studies. Publication bias, indicated by funnel plot asymmetry, suggests that small studies with lower QT incidence are underreported, potentially inflating the pooled estimate and skewing the perceived cardiotoxic risk of bedaquiline in SSA. These findings highlight the need for standardized reporting in SSA’s pharmacovigilance systems to ensure consistent data collection and accurate assessment of drug safety.
The significant clinical implications for SSA stem from limited ECG access and inconsistent monitoring, as evidenced by studies lacking baseline assessments ( ), which compromises the ability to detect and manage QT prolongation in bedaquiline-treated patients. In Uganda, a study reported that only 23% of the regional referral hospitals had an ECG machine, with only 33% functional [ ]. National TB programs should prioritize equipping treatment centers with functional ECG machines and establish robust maintenance systems, alongside implementing WHO-recommended monthly ECG monitoring protocols, to ensure consistent and effective cardiac safety monitoring for patients receiving bedaquiline. Integrated TB-HIV care is essential, prioritizing ART regimens with minimal QT effects and regular electrolyte monitoring .

5.

## Limitations
This review has several important limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of the findings. First, substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 95.8%) persisted despite extensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses. This likely reflects differences in definitions of QTc prolongation (e.g., >450/460 ms vs. >500 ms), ECG correction formulas (Fridericia vs. Bazett), monitoring frequency (weekly in most RCTs vs. monthly or symptom-driven in programmatic cohorts), concomitant use of other QT-prolonging drugs, and duration of follow-up. Second, four large multicountry cohorts overlapped in time and geography with several national or single-site reports. Although we excluded known duplicate publications, some degree of undetected patient double-counting cannot be fully ruled out, which may have slightly inflated the precision of the pooled estimate. Third, we restricted inclusion to studies published in English or French, potentially missing relevant reports in other local languages. Lastly, in many routine programmatic settings, ECG monitoring was performed infrequently or only when clinically indicated, which almost certainly led to under-reporting of mild or transient QTc prolongation (>450/460 ms but < 500 ms). The true incidence in real-world practice may therefore be higher than the 15% estimated here.
Despite these limitations, the consistency of the pooled estimate across multiple sensitivity analyses and the inclusion of both trial and real-world evidence strengthen confidence in the overall conclusion that clinically significant QTc prolongation occurs in approximately one in seven bedaquiline-treated patients in Sub-Saharan Africa.

6.

## Conclusions
This systematic review and meta-analysis reveal a notable incidence of QT interval prolongation (15.0%) among bedaquiline-treated DR-TB patients in Sub-Saharan Africa, emphasizing the need for vigilant cardiac monitoring in this high-risk region. The findings highlight the influence of HIV co-infection and co-administered QT-prolonging drugs like clofazimine, alongside challenges posed by limited ECG access and inconsistent pharmacovigilance. To optimize bedaquiline’s safety and efficacy, national TB programs should prioritize equipping treatment centers with functional ECG machines, implement standardized monthly monitoring protocols, and integrate TB-HIV care with minimal cardiotoxic antiretroviral regimens. Future research should focus on SSA-specific trials to strengthen pharmacovigilance systems to ensure accurate detection and management of cardiac risks, supporting the WHO’s End TB Strategy by 2035.
