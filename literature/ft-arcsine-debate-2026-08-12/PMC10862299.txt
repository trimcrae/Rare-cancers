## Abstract

## Objectives
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the magnitude and determinants of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) among patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) in Ethiopia.

## Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis.

## Participants
Age 15 and above all patients with diabetes with the diagnosis of DKA in Ethiopia

## Data source
PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Science Direct, HINARI, Google Scholar and grey literatures were accessed to find relevant articles. Studies that have been conducted and reported in English language, articles with an available full-text, and observational studies were included. The task of searching sources was carried out from all stated electronic databases performed during 15 April–29 April 2023.

## Primary and secondary outcome measures
Eligible studies were critically appraised by three independent reviewers for methodological quality in the review using standardised critical appraisal instruments from Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for observational studies. After the finally extracted studies were exported, systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information (JBI SUMARI) (JBI, Adelaide, Australia) and STATA V.17 software. Sensitivity tests were done, and funnel plot inspections with Egger’s test were used to check for publication bias.

## Result
From a total of 19 studies with 6498 study participants, the pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia was 30.92% (95% CI 29.96 to 31.89) with a significant statistical heterogeneity (I2=99.2, p=<0.001). Sensitivity analysis suggested that three studies showed deviations from the estimated pooled prevalence. A funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test indicated the absence of a publication bias.

## Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia was 30.92%. Besides, different behavioural and clinical determinants of DKA among patients with DM were identified. However, further studies should be conducted, particularly on the possible determinants of DKA, and different stakeholders should be engaged to minimise its burden.

## Introduction
Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a serious acute complication of diabetes mellitus (DM) characterised by hyperglycaemia, ketoacidosis and ketonuria that develops when the body can not produce enough insulin. Although 10%–30% of patients with type 2 DM can also develop DKA, it mainly occurs in patients with type 1 DM (T1DM). This is due to the insulin deficiency feature of T1DM, which means that blood sugar can not enter the cells to be used as energy. As a result, the liver breaks down fat for fuel to produce acids called ketones, which leads to DKA. Globally, with an estimated 1.5 million deaths directly caused by diabetes, it was the ninth leading cause of deaths in 2019, and its prevalence has been rising more rapidly in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) than in high-income countries (HICs). The global prevalence and incidence of DKA among T1DM per 1000 people were 0–128 and 0–263, respectively. In developing countries, the mortality rate due to DKA ranges 6% to 24% and 2% to 5% in developed countries. 
The most common precipitating factors provoking the release of counter-regulatory hormones for the development of extreme hyperglycaemia and subsequent DKA are infection, non-adherence to insulin treatment, acute major illnesses and antipsychotic agents. Being very thirsty and urinating a lot more than usual are early symptoms of DKA with slow development. Since patients with a known history of diabetes account for the majority of DKA occurrences, it can be avoided by health education, complication screening and frequent random blood glucose follow-ups. 
In LMICs, including Ethiopia, the health system’s capacity to manage DKA was very limited. Therefore, it is likely not properly diagnosed and treated, leading to delays or misdiagnosis that might increase the risk of complications and mortality. Besides, the few available centres are unable to adequately maintain a reliable system of insulin supply and extensively investigate their hospitalised patients. Despite the fact that many patients with DKA were seen in the emergency units of Ethiopian hospitals and health facilities, the burden of DKA among patients with DM is not well studied in Ethiopia. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the burden of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia.

## Methods
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for systematic reviews of prevalence and incidence with the updated guideline of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) ( ).

## Search strategy
The existence of similar published systematic reviews and meta-analyses on this topic was checked to prevent duplications. The search strategy aimed to locate both published and unpublished studies. International databases, PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Science Direct, HINARI, Google Scholar and grey literatures were accessed to find relevant articles. Medical Subject Headings for ‘Diabetic Ketoacidosis’, ‘Diabetes Mellitus’ and ‘Ethiopia’ were used, incorporating associated keyword terms and phrases. This approach was applied both independently and in combination, employing Boolean operators ‘OR’ and ‘AND’ to systematically search for eligible articles. The reference list of all included sources of evidence was screened for additional studies. The task of searching sources was carried out from all stated electronic databases performed during 15 April–29 April 2023 ( ).

## Eligibility criteria
The inclusion criteria for this review and meta-analysis were, age 15 and above, all patients with diabetes with the diagnosis of DKA in Ethiopia, studies that have been conducted and reported in English language, articles with an available full text and observational studies. However, gestational DM, studies with a duplicate published literature; and studies that could not extract important outcome data were excluded.

## Study selection
Retrieved and identified citations were collated and exported into EndNote V.X9.3.3 (Clarivate Analytics, Pennsylvania) with removing duplications. A pilot test, titles and abstracts were screened by three independent reviewers for assessment against the inclusion criteria. Potentially relevant studies were retrieved in full and their citation details imported into the JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information (JBI SUMARI) (JBI, Adelaide, Australia). The full text of selected citations was assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by three independent reviewers. Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers at each stage of the selection process were resolved through discussion.

## Outcome measurements
The primary outcome of this systematic review and meta-analysis was the pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia. The outcome of some studies was extracted the prevalence of DKA from both types of DM without identifying the burden in each groups. Hence, studies that reported the prevalence of DKA among patients with type 1 and/or type 2 DM were considered. The second outcome of this systematic review and meta-analysis reported the determinants of DKA among patients with DM.

## Assessment of methodological quality
Eligible studies were critically appraised by three independent reviewers for methodological quality in the review using standardised critical appraisal instruments from JBI for observational studies. Any disagreements that arose during the appraisal were resolved through discussion. The results of the critical appraisal were reported in narrative form and in a table. All studies that met a certain quality threshold underwent data extraction and synthesis.

## Data extraction
Data were extracted from studies included in the review by three independent reviewers using the standardised data extraction tool available in JBI SUMARI. The extracted data included specific details about the condition, populations, study methods, measured outcome and description of the main result; besides, specific details about the exposure included study methods and outcomes of significance to the review question and specific objectives. Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers were resolved through discussion.

## Data synthesis
The extracted data were pooled in statistical proportional meta-analysis using JBI SUMARI and STATA V.17. Effect sizes were expressed as a proportion with 95% CIs around the summary estimate. Adjusted OR (AOR) with its upper and lower bounds were extracted for significant variables. Statistical analyses were performed using the Freeman-Tukey transformation with a random effects model. The existence of heterogeneity was assessed using I2 and its corresponding p value. A value of 0%, 25%, 50% and 75% was used to declare the heterogeneity test as no, low, medium and high heterogeneity, respectively. A subgroup analysis was conducted to manage heterogeneity among studies with a random effects model using different measurements, including region, sample size and study design. The possible risk of publication bias was examined by the inspection of the funnel plot and statistically using Egger’s regression test with p value <0.05 indicated as the presence of publication bias. Besides, sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of each study on the pooled estimated prevalence of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia by excluding each study step-by-step with a random effects model. Tables, figures and narration were used to show the result.

## Result
A total of 178 studies were retrieved and 19 studies were found eligible and included in the final analysis ( ).
 Of the 19 included studies, majority of studies were from the Oromia region (n=10) followed by three studies from each Addis Ababa and Southern Nations and Nationalities People Region (SNNPR). Most of the studies were cross-sectional (n=16). The highest and the lowest prevalence of DKA among patients with DM were reported in Oromia region with a cross-sectional study design, 92.6% and 6.2%, respectively ( ).
 

## Pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM
In this meta-analysis, from a total of 19 studies among 6498 study participants, the pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia was 30.92% (95% CI 29.96 to 31.89) with a significant statistical heterogeneity (I2=99.2, p=<0.001) ( ).
 

## Sub-group analysis by region
There was a significant heterogeneity among the studies (I2=99.2, p=<0.001). As a result, a subgroup analysis was done by region, study design and sample size. The highest pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM was reported in Addis Ababa (38.17%, 95% CI 36.18 to 40.16) with a significant heterogeneity (99.7%, p=<0.001). Insignificant and no heterogeneity (I2=0, p=0.35) with 26.43% (95% CI 23.5 to 29.37) pooled prevalence was reported in Amhara region ( ).

## Determinants of DKA among patients with DM
Three studies were identified to assess the determinants of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia. A study conducted in Dilla identified that 18–25 age groups had three times higher risk of DKA development than above 25-year-old age groups (AOR=3; 95% CI 2 to 10; p=0.005). Patients with prior infection had 46 times higher risk of DKA development than those without (AOR=46; 95% CI 12 to 84; p=0.02). 
The unmatched case–control study design conducted in North Wollo and Waghimra Zone found that patients with T1DM were two times more likely to develop DKA than those with type 2 DM (AOR: 2.01; 95% CI (1.11 to 3.63)). Patients who drank alcohol had three times higher risk of developing DKA than those who did not (AOR: 2.99; 95% CI (1.46 to 6.12)). DM patients who did not receive diabetic education were 2.87 times more likely to develop DKA than patients who received diabetic education (AOR: 2.87; 95% CI (1.44 to 5.72)). Similarly, patients who discontinued their medication were 4.31 times more likely to develop DKA than those who did not discontinue their medication (AOR: 4.31; 95% CI (1.92 to 9.68)). The odds of DKA among patients with DM who had comorbidities were 2.57 times higher than those without (AOR: 2.57; 95% CI (1.37 to 4.84)). Four times higher risk of developing DKA among patients who did not have a regular follow-up in the diabetic clinic than those who had regular follow-up (AOR: 4.19; 95% CI (2.28 to 7.71)). 
Age and hypertension identified the determinants of DKA among patients with DM in Hawassa, Ethiopia, suggested that below 34-year-old age groups had 78% lower risk of DKA development than above 34-year-old patients. Those patients with hypertension showed 2.78 times risk of DKA than patients without hypertension. 

## Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis was carried out to determine the burden of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia from 19 studies, which were published between 1999 and 2023. The result revealed that the pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia was 30.92% (95% CI 29.96 to 31.89) with a significant statistical heterogeneity (I2=99.2, p=<0.001). This finding is lower than a study conducted Tanzania, 38.3% of DKA prevalence in 2022 despite the fact that the nation DKA trend was decreased over time, from 89.9% in 2005. Another retrospective study conducted in Benin teaching hospital found the frequency of DKA in newly diagnosed adults to be 77.1%. This frequency is higher relatives to our study, and this might be due to the Benin study, included adults b/n 15–35 years of age and because the study period was 15 years. In Iraq, from 2013 to 2014, a retrospective data reported 40% of the prevalence of DKA. The differing in magnitude might be explained by differences in study population, the back-ground prevalence of diabetes in the given population, presence or absence of family history of T1DM, socioeconomic status, delayed diagnosis and treatment as well as the definition of DKA used in the particular study. However, our finding is higher than a retrospective study conducted in USA with a data of 12 years (2007 to 2019) that found the prevalence of DKA was 15.22%. Similarly, a cross-sectional study from 70 US endocrinology centres reported 4.8% prevalence of DKA in adult patients with DM. A population-based study with a database extraction during 2005 to 2013 reported that the prevalence of DKA among adult DM patients in Japan was 0.2%. These lower reports of DKA prevalence in the regions may be due to an increased awareness of the condition and hence earlier detection would lower the disease incidence. Besides, a better healthcare provision, high health expenditure and a better educational status among the patients could be listed as contributed factors for the lower prevalence of DKA in the regions.
Due to a significant heterogeneity among the studies (I2=99.2, p=<0.001), a subgroup analysis by a region reported that the highest pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM was in Addis Ababa (38.17%), followed by Oromia (32.76%), Amhara (26.43%) and SNNPR (25.91%). This might be due to the period of the case admissions as the three studies from Addis Ababa were during 1987–1994, 2005–2009 and 2010–2013. This might be due to the number of people with diabetes globally rose from 108 million in 1980 to 422 million in 2014 as the prevalence has been rising more rapidly in LMICs than in HICs. Similarly, patients’ lack of awareness, poor healthcare services and poor living standard of patients with DM in Ethiopia might increase the admission of DKA. Hence, as the time becomes advanced, patients would be aware of the disease and early detection of the complications might decrease the rate of hospital admissions. Despite the pooled prevalence of DKA among patients with DM was similar in cohort studies (31.76%, 95% CI 28.27 to 35.25) and cross-sectional studies (31.21%, 95% CI 30.18 to 32.24), a lower significant heterogeneity (I2=89, p=<0.001) was identified among the cohort studies than the cross-sectional studies (I2=99.4, p=<0.001). This might be due to cohort studies are more evidence suggestive and better in terms of data quality than cross-sectional studies. 
Only three studies were found that have reported the determinants of DKA among DM. Determinants that were listed for the high risk of DKA occurrences among patients with DM were age groups older than 34 years, patients with prior infection, patients with T1DM, patients who drank alcohol, patients with DM who did not receive diabetic education, patients who discontinued their medication, patients with comorbidities, patients who did not have a regular follow-up in the diabetic clinic and hypertension. These findings were similar with the study conducted in Thailand, China and USA, where infections and non-compliance with treatments were the major determinants of DKA occurrences among patients with DM.
While acknowledging the strengths of our systematic review, including the engagement of three reviewers, a thorough search strategy using JBI critical appraisal instruments, adherence to the PRISMA guideline and certain limitations should be acknowledged. Inherent limitations in the available literature, including inconsistent reporting across studies, have hindered the comprehensive breakdown of DKA prevalence among distinct types of DM and other epidemiological surveys. The diversity in data presentation has constrained our ability to provide detailed insights into DKA rates based on diabetes types and other similar epidemiological parameters. Moreover, the exclusion of unavailable full-text articles may impact the estimated pooled prevalence, and the pooling of prevalence, despite high heterogeneity, poses a drawback. Additionally, the inability to pool data on determinants of DKA due to undocumented or unreported information in the studies hinders a comprehensive understanding of the reasons for the DKA burden among patients with diabetes in Ethiopia. Despite these constraints, our systematic review strives to synthesise existing evidence while underscoring the pressing need for standardised reporting in future studies to address these specific gaps.

## Conclusion
DKA mortality was found to be high in Ethiopia. The frequency of DKA among the adult population in the Ethiopian healthcare system has not been well documented, and the evidence is scarce. The current systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that the prevalence of DKA among patients with DM in Ethiopia is 30.92%. The Ministry of Health in Ethiopia, along with policymakers, programme implementers, non-governmental organisations, health institutions and healthcare providers, must recognise this issue and are encouraged to collaborate in order to mitigate the DKA burden among patients with diabetes in Ethiopia. Furthermore, studies should be conducted, particularly the possible determinants of DKA. To detect the source of heterogeneity, we recommend researchers investigate the burden of DKA among the particular types of DM despite the fact that it is expected the high prevalence of DKA among patients with type I DM.
