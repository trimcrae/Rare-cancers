## Abstract

## Background
Asymptomatic malaria during pregnancy is a significant public health concern in malaria-endemic regions, which worsens the various effects of malaria on the mother and fetus and increases maternal and neonatal mortality. To date, no meta-analysis has been conducted on asymptomatic malaria in pregnant women in Ethiopia. Thus, we aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria and its associated factors in pregnant women in Ethiopia.

## Methods
PubMed/Medline, Google Scholar, Web of Science, Cochrane, AJOL, and Ethiopian University repositories were systematically searched to identify studies reporting the prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia. A random effects model was used to perform the analysis. The heterogeneity of the studies was assessed with the I-squared tests, and subgroup analyses were performed to identify the sources of heterogeneity.

## Results
Ten articles with 3277 study participants were included in this review. The pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia was 7.03% (95% CI: 6.23–9.12); I2 = 81.2%). In the species-specific pooled prevalence estimate, Plasmodium falciparum prevalence was 5.34% (95%CI: 3.38–7.3; I2 = 87.8%), and Plasmodium vivax prevalence was 1.69% (95%CI: 1.2–5; I2 = 91.5%).Not using insecticide-treated bed nets [OR = 7.36, 95% CI (2.75, 19.73)], being primi-gravida [OR = 1.86, 95% CI (1.23, 2.82)]; lack of health education about malaria prevention [OR = 6.86, 95% CI (2.90, 11.44)] were predictors of asymptomatic malaria infection during pregnancy.

## Conclusion
This study revealed that asymptomatic malaria was prevalent among pregnant women in Ethiopia. This suggests that relying merely on reported symptoms may result in missed malaria cases. Therefore, regular screening and treatment protocols for malaria are recommended in antenatal care. It is also crucial to ensure that pregnant women have access to insecticide-treated bed nets and other effective malaria prevention measures.

1.

## Introduction
Malaria, a disease caused by the plasmodium parasite is transmitted to human being via mosquito bites ( ). It is a serious public health problem worldwide, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where most malaria infections and death occurs. By 2021, nearly half of the world's population is at risk of malaria, with 247 million new cases and 619,000 deaths. In the African region, it accounts for about 95% of cases and 96% of deaths with 234 cases and 593,000 ( ).
Pregnant women are highly vulnerable to malaria infection due to changes in their immune system, which puts them at greater risk for severe complications such as severe anemia, placental malaria, premature delivery, and low birth weight ( ). Malaria during pregnancy remains a significant public health concern worldwide, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where it is highly prevalent. The prevalence of malaria infection during pregnancy varies depending on the region and the intensity of transmission. In areas with high transmission, the prevalence of malaria infection among pregnant women averages between 30 and 50%, while in lower transmission areas, the prevalence ranges from 10 to 29% ( ). In Africa region, an estimated 13.3 million pregnant women had malaria infection ( ).
Although, malaria during pregnancy has significant adverse effects on maternal and child health outcomes, particularly in malaria-endemic areas such as Sub-Saharan Africa ( ). Placental malaria infection in SSA leads to an estimated 900,000 low birth weight deliveries each year due to both intrauterine growth retardation and preterm delivery. Malaria is also responsible for an estimated 200,000 infant deaths and 100,000 neonatal deaths annually ( ).
Plasmodium species in malaria-endemic areas are often found asymptomatically in many individuals and pregnant women are particularly vulnerable. In asymptomatic parasitemia, the person carries Plasmodium parasites in their bloodstream, but due to partial immunity, the parasites are incapable of inducing symptoms in the affected individual ( ) The prevalence of asymptomatic malaria during pregnancy in Sub Sahara Africa is 26.6% ( ). In Ethiopia the prevalence of asymptomatic malaria in the general population is 6.7% and among pregnant women it ranges from 2.83% to11.2% ( ; ; ; ; ).
Asymptomatic individuals can be a reservoir for transmission by Anopheles mosquitoes and may progress to symptomatic disease ( ). Asymptomatic cases have low health seeking behavior due to absence of developing disease and hence not treated. As a result, malaria has various effects on the mother and fetus and increases maternal and neonatal mortality caused by malaria and malaria-related ( ; ).
Ethiopia launched malaria elimination program by 2030 and implemented efforts such as early diagnosis, prompt treatment, and vector control measures ( ). However, this program will be hampered by asymptomatic cases acting as reservoirs of infection and sources of transmission. Moreover, the national prevalence of asymptomatic malaria during pregnancy is not well understood in Ethiopia, and there is a need to evaluate current evidence. Therefore, we aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection and its determinants among pregnant women. The findings of this study can inform the development of effective strategies to control and eliminate malaria in pregnant women, ultimately improving maternal and child health.

2.

## Methods
A systematic review and meta-analysis was performed to estimate the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria and its associated factors among pregnant women in Ethiopia. We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines ( ) (Supplementary file 1). The protocol of the development of this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO under reference number (CRD42023413981).
2.1.

## Searching strategy
All exhaustive literature searches in Medline/PUBMED, Google Scholar, Science Direct, Cochrane library, AJOL and Ethiopian University research repositories was carried out. All studies conducted asymptomatic malaria and/or its associated factors among pregnant women in Ethiopia were retrieved. The searching terms were “magnitude”; “prevalence”; “proportion”; ‘burden”; “asymptomatic malaria infection”; “plasmodium vivax”; “plasmodium falciparum”; “pregnant women”; “associated factor”; “determinants” and Ethiopia were used as the primary search keywords. Boolean operator terms were searched separately and together accordingly as “OR” or “AND” or AND NOT or AND, NOT. Gray literature from the research online library repository and manual search of references were also searched. The search included all articles published until April 2023.

2.2.

## Eligibility criteria
In this systematic review and meta-analysis; all studies conducted across all regions of Ethiopia on apparently healthy pregnant women with no symptoms/signs suggestive of malaria; all studies conducted on the prevalence of asymptomatic malaria and/or its associated factors among pregnant women; and all studies published in English language were included. Our study excluded the following studies: studies that didn't provide information on the primary outcome of interest; Studies evaluating symptomatic malaria infections in pregnant women, but extracting data exclusively for asymptomatic malaria infections when both symptomatic and asymptomatic malaria infections were reported; and non-primary studies like reviews and case reports.

2.3.

## Outcome measurements
This systematic review and meta-analysis has two main objectives. The first objective was to estimate the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia. Following the generation of prevalence and standard error of prevalence, it was calculated using the metan-prevalence standard error command. Microscopy and/or rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) were used to confirm asymptomatic malaria in the primary studies. The second objective was to identify factors associated with asymptomatic malaria among pregnant women in Ethiopia. A Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was used to extract data for this outcome in the form of two by two tables. Using the findings from the original studies, the log odds ratio (OR) for each factor was calculated. Factors included in this study were health education about malaria prevention (yes/no), Indoor residual sprays (IRS) last 12 months (yes/no), ITN (ever used/never used), and presence of stagnant water (yes/no).

2.4.

## Quality assessments and data extraction
The quality of the included studies was evaluated by two independent authors using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies ( ). Two impartial reviewers critical appraised each study. Data was extracted from Microsoft Excel using standardized extraction checklists by two data extractors (GAK and GAA). Combining search results from databases and removing duplicate articles was done using reference management software (Endnote version X7.2). Following the screening process, studies were excluded based on their titles and abstracts. Based on the prearranged inclusion and exclusion criteria, the full text of the remaining articles was evaluated for eligibility. For fist the outcome prevalence asymptomatic malaria the format for data extraction included the authors' names, years of publication, research locations, regions, sample sizes, prevalence asymptomatic malaria and quality rating. For the second outcome factors associated with asymptomatic malaria infection (health education about malaria prevention (yes/no), IRS in the last 12 months (yes/no), ITN (ever used/never used), and presence of stagnant water (yes/no)) data were extracted in a format of two by two tables (a, b, c, and d). Discussions among the reviewers were used to resolve disagreements. If the independent reviewers couldn't reach an agreement, a third reviewer was consulted to resolve the conflict. Studies that received a final quality rating checklist score of 50% or higher were then included (Supplementary file 2).

2.5.

## Data synthesis and analysis
After all relevant data extracted, data were exported from Microsoft Excel spreadsheet software to the STATA 14.1 for further analysis. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the inverse variance (I2) test. I-squared value represented moderate (30–60%), substantial (60–90%), and considerable (90–100%) heterogeneity. The pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women was estimated based on the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation method with the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model because of the high degree of heterogeneity (I2 > 82.1%, P-value <0.001). Both Egger's, and Begg's tests were carried out to look for publication bias. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was also performed to evaluate the effect of small studies on asymptomatic malaria. A random effect meta-analysis was used to estimate the pooled odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Study selection
A total of 564 articles were retrieved regarding asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia. Of the identified studies, 385 were expelled because of duplications and irrelevancies, and 140 were removed after abstract and title screening. But 39 studies met the eligibility criteria, and 28 full title articles excluded with reason (studies conducted outside Ethiopia, studies insufficient data, study population not pregnant women and studies did not report the outcome interest). Finally, 10 studies were included in this study ( ).
 
3.2.

## Characteristics studies
A total of ten cross-sectional studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. To estimate the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection a total of 3277 of asymptomatic pregnant women were involved ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Four of the studies were institution-based cross-sectional studies, while six were community-based cross-sectional studies. The publication period spanned from 2013 to 2023. The minimum and maximum sample sizes were 212 and 498 in the South Nations, Nationalities, and People's Region (SNNPR) and Oromia, respectively. Of the included studies, three were from the Amhara ( ; ; ), three from the Oromia ( ; ; ), one Benishangul gumuz ( ) and three from SNNP ( ; ; ) ( ).
 A total of five articles reported that using insecticide treated bed net utilization, three studies were reported that pregnant women who reside close to stagnant water three studies reported health education about malaria prevention practice during pregnancy; two studies reported IRS in the last 12 months and three studies reported gravidity which was significant associated factors of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia.

3.3.

## Pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women
Ten studies involving 3277 asymptomatic pregnant women reported the prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection. The random effect pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection during pregnancy in Ethiopia was 7.03% (CI 5.05, 9.01), with significant heterogeneity observed across the studies (I2 = 82.1%, p value <0.001) ( ). According to species-specific pooled prevalence estimate, plasmodium falciparum was 5.34% (95%CI: 3.38–7.3; I2 = 87.8%) and that of plasmodium vivax 1.69% (95%CI: 1.2–5; I2 = 91.5%). In terms of individual studies the highest (11.6%) and the lowest (2.83%) prevalence of asymptomatic infection of malaria was reported from studies in Oromia and Amhara region.
 
3.4.

## Subgroup analysis
We observed a high heterogeneity observed across the studies (I2 = 82.1%, p value <0.001), As a result, subgroup analysis was performed based on the study area (regions where the studies conducted) and study setting. As per region, the highest pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection during pregnancy was observed in SNNPR 7.49% (95% CI =69.82, 79.30) and the lowest was in Amhara 6.43% (95% CI = 32.82, 62.41) ( ). In the context of study setting, the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection during pregnancy was 6.39% (95% CI = 2.82, 9.96) in facility-based studies and 7.49% (95% CI =4.95, 10.02) in community based studies ( ).
 
3.5.

## Publication bias
To assess potential publication bias funnel plot was visually inspected, which was statistically supported by the Begg's and Egger's tests. The funnel plot showed as asymmetrical distribution of the included studies in a large inverted funnel indicated the possibility of a publication bias ( ). This was supported by Begg's and Egger tests revealed possibility publication bias among the studies included to estimate the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia, with p - values of (p < 0.001) to address this publication bias we performed trim and fill analysis and the result indicated that the presence of three unpublished studies.
 
3.6.

## Sensitivity analysis
Sensitivity analysis was used to assess the effect of a single study on the asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia by excluding each study one at a time. The findings revealed that no single study made a statistically significant difference in the pooled asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia ( ).
 
3.7.

## Determinants of asymptomatic malaria infection during pregnancy
To identify factors associated with asymptomatic malaria infection during pregnancy, variables such as gravidity, ITN utilization, IRS in the last one year, living near to stagnant water and health education about malaria prevention extracted from the included studies. Finally, three variables were identified as independent predictors of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women: gravidity, ITN utilization, and health education about malaria prevention.
In this study, the pooled odds estimate showed that a pregnant women who did not utilize ITN were 7.36 times more likely to develop asymptomatic malaria infection than a pregnant women who utilized (OR = 7.36, 95% CI = 2.75, 19.73). The pooled odds of developing asymptomatic malaria infection were 1.86 times higher among prime-gravida women as compared to those who are multigravida (OR = 1.86, 95% CI = 1.23, 2.82). Likewise, the pooled estimate indicated that pregnant women who had not got counseling and health education about a malaria prevention during pregnancy were 6.86 times more likely to be infected by asymptomatic malaria than those who did not (OR = 6.86, 95% CI = 2.90, 11.44) ( ).
 

4.

## Discussion
The current systematic review and meta-analysis were performed to determine the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection in pregnant women. Malaria elimination campaigns are challenged by asymptomatic malaria infection since asymptomatic people do not show overt clinical symptoms and are essentially reservoir of disease. Particularly in pregnancy, asymptomatic malaria infection has detrimental effects on both the mother and the fetus ( ).
This study demonstrated that the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia was 7.03%. The result is in line with global prevalence (10.2%) ( ), Cameroon (10.14%) ( ) and primary studies in Colombia (8%) ( ). However, it was higher than primary studies in India (4.12%) ( ), Bangladesh (2.3%) ( ): and lower than a meta-analysis in SSA (26.1%) ( ), a meta-analysis in Nigeria (23.4%) ( ) and primary study in Burkina Faso (23.9%) ( ). Several factors could explain the difference, including the number of studies, the diagnostic approach, and the endemicity class. For instance, the studies in African countries such as Nigeria has the highest burden of malaria in the world, accounting about 27% of the global malaria cases ( ), and a primary study conducted in Burkina Faso was employed during the high transmission season. Overall, this finding implies that the need of including laboratory diagnosis of asymptomatic pregnant women for malaria as part of antenatal care follow-up for early detection and treatment to prevent these negative outcomes.
In the subgroup analysis, the pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria among pregnant women varied across region of Ethiopia. The pooled proportion of pregnant women who had asymptomatic malaria infection was 7.49% in SNNPR, 6.72% in Oromia and 6.43% in Amhara. The differences between regions may be due to differences in temperature, environmental factors, malaria preventive strategies, and mosquito population density. Therefore, compared with areas with lower mosquito populations, malaria transmission is anticipated to be higher in areas with high mosquito populations ( ).
As part of this meta-analysis, the researchers also sought to identify factors associated with asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women in Ethiopia. This study showed that asymptomatic malaria infection was 1.54 times higher in primigravida women than multigravida women. The finding is in agreement with the meta-analysis study in SSA ( ) and studies conducted in stable malaria transmission areas, which have shown that asymptomatic malaria infection is reduced with increasing gravidity ( ). There may be an explanation for this in that a reduction in malaria prevalence is associated with anti-adhesion antibodies against CSA-binding parasites. The high malaria infection rate in primigravida women is due to the lack of antiadhesion antibodies, which makes these women more susceptible to malaria infection than multigravida women ( ; ). Moreover, the decreased occurrence of asymptomatic malaria infection with increased gravidity may be related to exposure-acquired immunity.
Furthermore, the results showed that not using ITN effectively increased the odds of developing malaria during pregnancy. The results of this study are consistent with those of previous studies. For instance, studies from Nigeria ( ) and Kenya ( ) showed a significant reduction in malaria infection due to the use of ITN during pregnancy. The association between ITNs and malaria may be explained by the fact that ITNs effectively minimize human-mosquito contact, thus preventing malaria-related illnesses ( ). This review revealed the use of insecticide-treated bed nets during pregnancy can significantly reduce the incidence of asymptomatic infection malaria, which is known to have harmful effects on both the mother and the developing fetus. This highlights the importance of implementing interventions that not only focus on treating malaria infection but also on preventing its occurrence among pregnant women.
This review also found that pregnant women who lack counseling and health education about malaria prevention had 10.63 times higher chance of developing asymptomatic infection than those who had. This finding is comparable with a meta-analysis from SSA countries ( ) have also revealed a positive associations between asymptomatic malaria infection and health education on various aspects of malaria among pregnant women in these countries. Therefore, to encourage the adherence of pregnant women to malaria control programs, behavioral change interventions should focus on sociocultural factors and universal education by prioritize community education about malaria prevention and control, including the use of insecticide-treated nets, in order to reduce the burden of asymptomatic malaria. ( ).
4.1.

## Strength and limitation of the study
There was an exhaustive literature search conducted for both published and unpublished studies and the quality of each study was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies quality assessment tool. This will contribute to the quality of the review to produce conclusive findings. However, one should use the findings with caution due to some limitations of this review. First, studies included in this meta-analysis are not from all Ethiopian regions. Given these aforementioned inherent limitations, the result of this study should be interpreted with caution.

5.

## Conclusion and recommendation
In conclusion, this study's findings showed that pooled prevalence of asymptomatic malaria infection among pregnant women is found to be high in Ethiopia. Being primigravida, not using ITNs, and lack counseling or health education about malaria prevention were contributing for developing asymptomatic malaria infection during. To avoid these unfavorable effects, it is advised to screen for and treat asymptomatic malaria early and frequently throughout pregnancy. Community education regarding malaria prevention and control, including the use of insecticide-treated nets and indoor residual spraying, should also be given priority. By putting these steps in place, we can lessen the impact of asymptomatic malaria during pregnancy and enhance the health of both mothers and their offspring.
