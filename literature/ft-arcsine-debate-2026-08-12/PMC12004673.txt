## Abstract

## Background
It is well known that, to be effective, vaccination programs require a high and consistent population uptake. Nevertheless, vaccine coverage is often insufficient. University students occupy a unique position within the broader population: they are highly mobile and socially active individuals who serve as influential figures among their peers and younger age groups, making them an ideal target for educational campaigns.

## Methods
This review (CRD42022309724) quantified the vaccination intention rates for routine immunizations in university students and systematically examined the determinants of vaccination acceptance. Cross-sectional studies that analyzed vaccination intention for at least one routine immunization using multivariable logistic regression were included. We used random-effects models to calculate the pooled proportions of vaccination intention. Determinants were categorized into contextual factors (such as socio-demographic, economic and cultural factors), individual/group factors (such as knowledge, awareness, peer opinions, infection risk perceptions) and vaccine-specific issues (such as vaccine effectiveness and vaccination policies).

## Results
Thirty-six articles were included: 29 on the human papilloma virus (HPV) vaccine and seven on the influenza vaccine. The pooled proportion of students intending to get vaccinated was 54% (95% CI: 47–60%) for HPV and 43% (95% CI: 26–61%) for influenza. The strongest positive determinants were individual/group factors, such as higher knowledge and awareness, pro-vaccine peer opinions, positive personal experience with vaccinations, and higher risk perception for HPV infection, and higher risk perception for infection and previous vaccination history for influenza. By contrast, contextual influences played a minor role in both vaccinations, while vaccine-specific concerns, particularly about costs and availability, negatively influenced intention for both vaccines.

## Conclusions
Given the suboptimal rates of vaccination intention towards HPV and influenza vaccine that we found, addressing key determinants, such as awareness of infection risks and perception of vaccination benefits, is essential to increase vaccination acceptance in university students and to achieve high vaccination coverage.

## Introduction
Vaccines have contributed to the decline in mortality and morbidity of many vaccination-preventable diseases [ ] and represent one of the most cost-effective tools in primary prevention [ ]. However, although it is known that to be effective vaccination programs need a high and constant population uptake [ ], vaccine coverage is often sub-optimal. For example, despite efforts to implement global strategies to eliminate cervical cancer, which include the aim of fully vaccinating 90% of girls aged 15 against human papilloma virus (HPV) by 2030 [ ], in 2022 the worldwide coverage for this population was only 15% [ ]. In addition to a global hesitancy towards vaccination that has grown in the last decade [ ], the COVID-19 pandemic contributed to a reduction in routine vaccination coverage due to the spread of a lack of confidence in the security of and need for vaccines and the temporary closure of vaccination services [ – ].
In this context, it is essential to understand the factors influencing vaccination intention. Experts have focused on the behavioral and social drivers of vaccination, with the premise that beliefs and experiences related to vaccination might be modified to increase vaccine acceptance [ ]. In fact, vaccination choices depend strongly on the motivation of an individual [ , ]. Such factors may vary for different vaccines, countries and population subgroups [ ]. Among the latter, university students represent a population worth exploring: on the one hand, they are a mobile, socially active group and thus easily able to transmit infections [ , ], while on the other hand students are at a time in their lives when their personalities are developing and they become responsible for their own health choices [ , ]. Furthermore, given their societal multiplier function within the university setting, they can influence their peers [ , , ], which makes this population an optimal target for educational campaigns.
A growing body of scientific literature has focused on the factors that influence vaccination acceptance in university students, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic [ , ]. Factors such as a higher perceived risk of infection and better knowledge about vaccination benefits were found to correlate with an increased intention to vaccinate against COVID-19, similarly to what was found in the general population [ ]. However, the same factors were found to be either positive or negative determinants to vaccination intention in different cases [ ]. A smaller number of studies have focused on influenza [ , ] and only one systematic review has focused on HPV vaccination in male students [ ], highlighting that various factors influence vaccination decisions, including knowledge about vaccines, accessibility, recommendations from others [ ], perceived benefits, and susceptibility [ ]. Given these differences in the findings, particularly regarding the types of determinant analyzed and their role in vaccination intention, together with the absence of a comprehensive synthesis of these issues to date, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis (i) to quantify the intention of university students to accept routine vaccinations; and (ii) to investigate the role of determinants in predicting vaccination acceptance. Our goal was to contribute to the understanding of the factors driving vaccination behavior in this population subgroup, thereby helping to address vaccination gaps.

## Methods
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [ , ]. The protocol for the review was registered with PROSPERO (identifier CRD42022309724). As this study did not involve the collection of primary data, the protocol was not subjected to institutional review board approval, and obtaining informed consent was not necessary.

## Search strategy, study selection and inclusion criteria
The search was conducted on PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science databases from inception to 24 October 2023. The main keywords used to search the database included “students”, “vaccine”, “immunization”, “hesitancy”, “attitude”, “acceptance” and synonyms. The string was adapted to fit the search criteria of each database (Supplementary Table ). Duplicate articles were removed. The title and abstract of retrieved records were screened and records that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Full texts of possibly relevant articles were obtained and evaluated independently by two researchers. Discrepancies were addressed through discussions involving a third author. Reasons for exclusion were recorded. The search was supplemented by scanning the reference lists of the retrieved articles.
Any study with the following characteristics was included: (i) reported in English, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, or French based on the co-authors’ language abilities; (ii) had a cross-sectional design; (iii) recruited students in a university setting (i.e., any student enrolled at a tertiary education facility such as a university, college, or comparable postsecondary institution); (iv) quantified the proportion of unvaccinated students willing to be vaccinated for at least one of the twenty-two vaccines recommended for routine immunization by the World Health Organization (WHO) [ ]; and (v) analyzed the determinants of the vaccination intention of non-vaccinated students by multivariable logistic regression.
We excluded studies that investigated vaccination intention in association with willingness to pay; unapproved vaccines or vaccines still in development; intention to receive booster doses of previously received vaccines (i.e., already received in childhood; annually administered influenza vaccines were not considered a booster dose); studies that also included vaccinated students in the regression analysis or that analyzed vaccination determinants by methods other than multivariable logistic regression.

## Data collection, synthesis and quality assessment
For each record included, two reviewers extracted the following information using an ad hoc standardized data extraction form: (i) first author; (ii) year of publication; (iii) country; (iv) geographical area (according to the United Nations definition [ ], six geographical areas were considered: Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, Northern America and Oceania); (v) type of vaccine considered; (vi) total number of students; (vii) number of unvaccinated students; (viii) student characteristics (age, gender, area of study); (ix) vaccination intention, including type of measurement and coding; (x) prevalence of unvaccinated students willing to be vaccinated (proportion or raw data, depending on data availability); and (xi) determinants of vaccination intention (i.e., adjustment factors in the multivariable regression model used for unvaccinated students).
Studies were grouped according to the type of vaccine. If a study presented several regression models, we chose the one with the largest number of independent variables. Determinants were grouped into four macro categories (i.e., contextual influences, individual/group influences, vaccine-specific issues and unclassifiable factors), according to the Vaccine Hesitancy Determinants Matrix developed by the Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (SAGE) Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy [ ]. The matrix is provided in Supplementary Table .
Each determinant was then classified according to two criteria: (i) the direction, i.e. positively (+) or negatively (-) associated with vaccination intention; and (ii) the statistical significance, hence statistically significant (S), not statistically significant (NS), or mixed (S/NS), in those cases when the variable had multiple modalities in which at least one was statistically significant and at least one was not statistically significant.
Two independent authors performed a quality assessment of the articles included using the version of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale adapted for cross-sectional studies [ , ]. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Articles were considered of high quality when the total score was ≥ 7, fair quality if the score was ≥ 5 and < 7, and poor quality if the score was less than 5.

## Statistical analysis
Based on data availability, the vaccination intention rate in non-vaccinated students was calculated as follows: the number of students intending to get vaccinated was considered as the numerator, with the total number of non-vaccinated students taken as the denominator (including undecided students, if any). However, if specified in the study, students who did not answer the question about intention to vaccinate (i.e., missing responses) were not included in the denominator.
The restricted maximum likelihood method [ ] with a Freeman–Tukey transformation [ ] was used to calculate the pooled estimated proportions of vaccination intention towards each vaccine separately. The following variables were considered for stratified meta-analyses: geographic region (i.e., Asia, Europe, North America or Africa), vaccination status at recruitment (i.e., vaccinated and unvaccinated, or all unvaccinated), gender (i.e., both genders, females only, or males only), study area (i.e., health and non-health, health only, or not specified), study quality (i.e., poor/fair or good) and original number of answer options to the vaccination intention question (i.e., dichotomous, or multiple options later dichotomized). The I2 metric was used to assess heterogeneity [ ].
A meta-regression analysis was deemed feasible when at least ten studies were included for each vaccination. Specifically, a random effects meta-regression analysis using the restricted maximum likelihood method [ ] and Freeman–Tukey-transformed proportions [ ] was performed to explore the association between study characteristics and the pooled proportion of students intending to get vaccinated [ , ]. We ran univariable and multivariable analyses that included the covariates that might influence the intention rate based on literature review. The final model consisted of the following variables: geographic region, vaccination status at recruitment, students’ gender, students’ area of study, study quality, and original number of answer options.
Lastly, the potential presence of publication bias was investigated by visual inspection of funnel plots for meta-analyses of proportions and through Egger’s test [ ].
All analyses were performed using STATA (StataCorp LLC, 4905 Lakeway Drive, College Station, TX, USA), version 18.0. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
Overall, the search identified 14,940 records. After removal of duplicates and screening by title and abstract, 237 reports were considered eligible for full-text analysis, of which 201 were excluded for various reasons, resulting in 36 articles to be included in the systematic review (Fig.  ).
 

## Characteristics of the studies included
Of the 22 routine vaccines considered, the articles included in the systematic review focused on only two, i.e. those against HPV (n = 29) [ , – ] and influenza (n = 7) [ , – ].

## HPV vaccination intention
The 29 articles on vaccination against HPV were published from 2008 to 2022 (Table  ). The majority of studies were conducted in Asia (n = 17), of which 12 were in China; six studies were conducted in Europe, half of which were in Italy; five studies were conducted in North America, mostly in the United States of America (n = 4), and only one study was conducted in Africa (Nigeria). Only seven studies had a large sample size (i.e., more than 1000 students). About 45% of the studies (n = 13) included students from mixed areas of study, encompassing both healthcare and non-healthcare fields, while five records focused on healthcare students and six studies did not report such information. Most studies recruited students none of whom had been previously vaccinated for HPV (n = 22). Mean age ranged from 19 to 22.7 years. Thirteen studies included students of both genders, while twelve studies targeted females only and the remainder males only. The majority of studies (n = 16) investigated the vaccination intention of students in one question with only two possible answers (yes/no), while in the other records more options were initially available to the respondents. The number of determinants considered in multivariable regression models ranged from two to 22. Of the 29 studies, 22 (69%) were considered of high quality, eight (28%) of fair quality, and only one of poor quality (Table  , Supplementary Table ).
 

## Influenza vaccination intention
The seven articles on influenza vaccination were published from 2011 to 2020 (Table  ). The majority of studies (n = 4) were conducted in Europe, two in Asia and only one in North America. Three studies included students from mixed areas of study, while the remainder recruited participants from the healthcare field. Only one study had a large sample size, enrolling more than 1000 students. Almost all studies (n = 6) recruited students none of whom had been previously vaccinated for influenza. Mean age ranged from 20.4 to 23.4 years. All studies included students of both genders. The number of determinants considered in individual regression analyses ranged from 3 to 15. Out of seven studies, four were considered of high quality while the other three studies were of fair quality (Table  , Supplementary Table ).
 

## Meta-analysis of vaccination intention rates

## HPV vaccination intention
Overall, the pooled proportion of students intending to get vaccinated against HPV was 0.54 (95% CI: 0.47–0.60), with high heterogeneity (I2: 99.3%) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). According to stratified analyses, the pooled proportions of HPV vaccination intention varied slightly across different geographic regions, giving 0.53 (95% CI: 0.44–0.61, I2: 99.4%) for Asia, 0.59 (95% CI: 0.41–0.76, I2: 99.6%) for Europe, 0.46 (95% CI: 0.39–0.53, I2: 81.7%) for North America and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.69–0.78) for Africa (p < 0.01) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). As for vaccination status at recruitment, studies recruiting only unvaccinated participants showed a higher pooled proportion of HPV vaccination intention compared to those including initially both vaccinated and unvaccinated students (0.56, 95% CI: 0.49–0.63, I2: 99.2% and 0.46, 95% CI: 0.31–0.61, I2: 99.0%, respectively), but this difference was not significant (p = 0.25) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). By contrast, studies conducted on both male and female students had a significantly higher pooled proportion of HPV vaccination intention (0.63, 95% CI: 0.53–0.72, I2: 99.5%), compared to studies on females (0.48, 95% CI: 0.40–0.56, I2: 98.0%) and males only (0.41, 95% CI: 0.25–0.58, I2: 98.4%) (p = 0.03) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). The stratified analyses by study area showed no sub-group differences (p = 0.39): the proportion of participants willing to undergo HPV vaccination was 0.50 (95% CI: 0.37–0.63, I2: 99.5%) for health and non-health students, 0.60 (95% CI: 0.51–0.69, I2: 95.0%) for health students only, 0.59 (95% CI: 0.45–0.73, I2: 99.6%) for non-health students only, and 0.52 (95% CI: 0.46–0.57, I2: 87.9%) for those studies in which the information was not reported (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Records of poor/fair study quality had a higher pooled proportion of HPV vaccination intention (0.57, 95% CI: 0.45–0.69, I2: 98.7%) compared to those considered of good quality (0.52, 95% CI: 0.44–0.60, I2: 99.4%), but the difference was not significant (p = 0.50) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Lastly, stratifying records by the original number of answer options also did not result in a meaningful difference (dichotomous: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.49–0.66; multiple options: 0.47, 0.38–0.55, p = 0.07) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ).
 In the meta-regression analysis, compared to Asia, Africa was the only geographic region to have a higher pooled proportion of HPV vaccination intention (β: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.01–1.40) (Table  ). As for vaccination status at recruitment, studies that recruited only unvaccinated participants had a higher pooled estimate than those that initially included both vaccinated and unvaccinated students (β: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.04–0.76). Regarding gender, studies including both female and male participants showed a higher pooled proportion of HPV vaccination intention than those considering female students only (β: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.09–0.71). By contrast, the students’ area of study, study quality and the original number of answer options did not appear to significantly influence the pooled estimate.
 Finally, there was no evidence of publication bias according to the funnel plot analysis (Supplementary Fig.  A) or Egger’s test (p = 0.503).

## Influenza vaccination intention
The overall pooled proportion of students intending to get vaccinated against influenza was 0.43 (95% CI: 0.26–0.61) with high heterogeneity (I2: 99.3%) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Stratified analysis by geographic region showed that studies conducted in Europe had a higher pooled proportion of influenza vaccination intention (0.56, 95% CI: 0.37–0.74, I2: 98.9%) than studies conducted in Asia (0.24, 95% CI: 0.02–0.62, I2: 99.2%) or in North America (0.33, 95% CI: 0.25–0.42), but this difference was not significant (p = 0.08) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Similarly, the only study that considered both vaccinated and unvaccinated participants at recruitment had a proportion of influenza vaccination intention among unvaccinated students of 0.33 (95% CI: 0.25–0.42), while the other studies had a pooled proportion of 0.45 (95% CI: 0.25–0.66, I2: 99.4%); however, the test of group differences found them not to be significant (p = 0.30) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Neither was a difference found (p = 0.49) in the pooled proportion of influenza vaccination intention between records including health and non-health participants (0.47, 95% CI: 0.18–0.78, I2: 99.4%), studies with health students only (0.40, 95% CI: 0.29–0.52, I2: 95.1%), or the only study that did not specify this information (0.33, 95% CI: 0.15–0.48) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Conversely, studies of poor/fair study quality had a significantly higher pooled proportion of influenza vaccination intention (0.62, 95% CI: 0.41–0.81, I2: 97.4%), compared to those of good quality (0.30, 95% CI: 0.15–0.48, I2: 99.0%) (p = 0.02) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), while the original number of answer options did not appear to be statistically relevant (dichotomous: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.25–0.66; multiple options: 0.33, 0.25–0.32, p = 0.30) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ).
 Lastly, neither funnel plot analysis (Supplementary Fig.  B), nor Egger’s test (p = 0.519) showed evidence of publication bias.

## Determinants of vaccination intention

## HPV vaccination intention
A total of 259 determinants of HPV vaccination intention were found: of these, 21% (n = 56) were contextual influences, about 54% (n = 141) were individual and group influences, 13.5% (n = 35) were vaccine/vaccination-specific issues and the remaining 10% were deemed unclassifiable determinants (Table  ). The contextual influences category showed overall the highest proportion of determinants not associated with HPV vaccination intention (75%). Ethnicity, nationality, religion, parents’ education level, parents’ residence, parents’ occupation, relationship status, religious institutional approval, and source of information were not significantly associated with HPV vaccination intention in any of the studies included. On the contrary, increased media influence, female gender and non-heterosexual orientation showed the highest rates of totally or partially positive associations (40.0%, 60.0% and 66.7% respectively).
 Individual and group influences showed the highest proportion of determinants that were completely or partially positively associated with HPV vaccination intention (51.7%). The students’ knowledge and awareness of HPV or HPV vaccines were the most extensively studied factors, both showing a substantial proportion of significant positive associations with vaccination intention, ranging from 45.0% for awareness to 48.8% for knowledge. Similarly, the “personal or community experience with vaccinations” subgroup exhibited high rates of significant positive association with the outcome, ranging from 50.0 to 75.0%. The “attitudes and beliefs towards health, diseases, prevention, and vaccines” subgroup showed positive rates between 50% and 100%, except for sexual education (33.3%), active sexual behavior (28.6%), and smoking, that instead was always non-significant (100%). Additionally, the “acquaintances’ opinions or recommendations” subgroup was found to be positively associated in 60–100% of the studies.
Vaccine-/vaccination-specific issues showed the highest proportion of negatively associated determinants (22.9%), mainly in relation to the vaccine cost (75%), the presence of logistical barriers, such as not having time or limited appointment slots (60%), and adverse side effects (100%). By contrast, vaccine benefits and effectiveness, vaccine safety and recommendation from healthcare professionals showed completely or partially significant positive associations with the outcome (46.2%, 50% and 55.6%, respectively).
Among the others determinants (i.e., those unclassifiable according to the matrix), main area of academic study was mostly non-significant (77.8%), like increased academic level, which was found not to be significant in 44.4% of cases and to have a weak but negative association with the outcome in the other 44.4% of cases.

## Influenza vaccination intention
A total of 61 determinants were identified for influenza vaccination intention: of these, approximately 14% (n = 8) were in the contextual influences category, about 64% (n = 39) in the individual and group influences category, 10% (n = 6) in the vaccine-/vaccination-specific issues category, and the remaining 12% were unclassifiable determinants (Table  ). Contextual influences exhibited the highest proportion of determinants that were not associated with vaccination intention (75%) and showed completely or partially positive associations in only about 12.5% of cases. Among the socio-demographic and economic factors, gender, ethnicity and finances did not show any significant association with the outcome; student residence (Rome vs. cities of northern Italy) showed some degree of positive association (50%), while geographical area had a contrasting effect.
 Individual and group influences had the highest proportion of positively associated determinants (around 54%). Interestingly, knowledge was investigated only twice, and in both cases did not show a significant association with vaccination intention. By contrast, among the “personal or community experience with vaccination” determinants, previous influenza vaccination was the most extensively studied factor and showed a considerable proportion of positive associations with vaccination intention (80.0%), a value higher than “living in an environment with a positive attitude towards influenza vaccination” (50%). Attitudes towards health, diseases, prevention and vaccines showed completely or partially positive associations with the outcome, with rates between 50% and 100%, apart from a few items related to the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., concern about the pandemic), cues to action and having a chronic condition, which were always not significant (100%). Likewise, the “health system and providers– trust and personal experience” category was mainly not significant, with the exception of the intention to recommend influenza vaccination (100% positive) and the risk perception of influenza as a future health professional (50% partially positive). On the contrary, a higher perception of vaccine effectiveness was positively associated with vaccination intention (100%).
As for the few determinants related to vaccine- or vaccination-specific issues, they showed an equal proportion of positively associated (50%) and non-significantly associated (50%) factors. Specifically, awareness that vaccines would be available and freely provided was always a positive determinant of vaccination intention (100%); vaccine benefits and effectiveness were positively associated in 33.3% of cases and non-significantly associated in the remaining 66.7%, while the presence of logistical barriers was not significantly associated with the outcome (100%).
Finally, regarding the university-related factors, unclassifiable according to the matrix, the area of study (medicine vs. other) and higher year of study exhibited a certain degree of positive association, ranging between 50.0% and 100%.

## Discussion
Our systematic review of studies in students of their intention to accept routine immunizations, which focused on HPV and influenza only, found some variability in the underpinning determinants and showed that the proportion of students willing to undergo vaccination was unsatisfactory.
Most studies on the HPV vaccine are relatively recent, which aligns with the HPV vaccine’s initial licensing in 2006 [ ]. However, scientific interest in identifying determinants of vaccination intention has grown in recent years, even though this increase in interest is not uniform across countries, as highlighted by a lack of geographical representation in our review. Indeed, most studies were conducted in China, where the HPV vaccine was introduced in 2016 and has not yet been integrated into the national immunization program [ ]. This probably reflects stakeholders’ interest in studying these determinants to better inform immunization strategies. Regarding the population investigated, a considerable number of studies focused on female students, probably because of the WHO’s initial guidelines, which prioritized vaccinating females [ ]. By contrast, studies on influenza were fewer in number than those on HPV, which may suggest a relatively lower scientific interest in analyzing the determinants of vaccination intention for influenza, in line with the fact that young people are not the primary target for this vaccination [ ]. However, given the widespread and severe health threat posed by influenza, the necessity of annual vaccination, and the potential added protective effects for the communities in which university students are embedded, evaluating vaccination intentions within this population remains a relevant scientific and public health issue [ ].
With regard to the pooled proportions of vaccination intention, only approximately half of students demonstrated a positive attitude towards HPV and influenza vaccination, meaning that some resistance persists in this sub-group population. This is concerning because, although our results only approached statistical significance (but did not reach it), the use of a binary yes-or-no approach to assess vaccination intention (common in many of the included studies) often leads to an overestimation of actual intention [ , ]. However, students showed higher vaccination intention for HPV than influenza, likely due to their low perceived risk of severe influenza outcomes as they believe they are less likely to contract the infection or, if they do, to develop only mild forms of the disease [ , ]. As for HPV vaccination, we found a few study characteristics that could influence the pooled estimate. For example, African students appeared to have the highest vaccination intentions. However, this result is limited by data from a single study on unvaccinated females and it may be explained by contextual factors, such as the introduction of the first national HPV vaccination program [ ]. As for gender, our findings do not fully align with the literature, which generally indicates that females tend to have a higher vaccination intention compared to males [ ]. Nevertheless, it is worth mentioning that our result emerges as a study characteristic, while in other studies this predictor is usually found at the individual level, making this comparison challenging. Another significant factor was vaccination status at recruitment: studies originally including both vaccinated and unvaccinated students showed lower intentions, possibly reflecting a saturation effect by which individuals more inclined to vaccinate tend to do so early; those remaining are likely less motivated or more resistant to the idea of vaccination. As for other study characteristics, such as study quality and students’ field of study, the lack of association with the outcome is, on the one hand, reassuring, as it suggests that in this case methodological rigor has little or no effect on the proportions obtained. On the other hand, it is alarming, as it implies that studying in a healthcare-related field does not meaningfully increase awareness and knowledge of vaccinations. By contrast, study quality was the only variable that produced a subgroup difference in influenza vaccination intention. Due to the limited number of studies, drawing conclusions from this result is difficult, but it surely underscores the importance of conducting more high-quality research on the topic using a standardized and reproducible approach.
As for the determinants of HPV vaccination intention, among the contextual influences it was found that being female or non-heterosexual seemed to have the greatest influence, probably due to the high levels of awareness and risk perception in these categories [ ]. For a similar reason, among the individual and group factors, awareness and general knowledge appeared to motivate vaccination intention. Although less-well studied, health literacy (HL), which is strongly linked to knowledge and awareness, was also a significant positive predictor, supporting extensive prior research showing that HL is associated with preventive behaviors, such as adherence to cancer screening [ ], vaccination intention [ ] and vaccine uptake [ ]. Therefore, in alignment with evidence from a systematic review on HPV vaccination among male college students [ ], there is a critical need to strengthen students’ knowledge of HPV and its vaccination through targeted educational interventions [ ].
Our study also confirmed that previous personal vaccination experiences and an acute perception of the risks associated with HPV infection affect vaccination decisions. Along with experiences and recommendations from acquaintances, these factors underscore the importance of group opinion and social environment in this age group, factors that can be targeted by educational campaigns [ ]. In addition, as previously reported [ ], the influence of healthcare professionals’ opinions seemed to have a supportive role in vaccination intention, even though it was less pronounced. Hence, training healthcare professionals to raise their awareness of vaccinations is essential to ensure they provide comprehensive and clear communication to the public [ ]. Conversely, logistical barriers, such as insufficient time to attend a vaccination clinic or the limited availability of vaccination slots, together with cost concerns, discouraged vaccination intention, a finding that highlights the critical importance of addressing these obstacles and facilitating easier access to vaccination services to ensure higher participation rates and improve equality of access [ ].
Similarly to the HPV vaccine, contextual factors had a low impact on influenza vaccination intention. In addition, only a few determinants associated with the academic aspect showed some degree of positive influence in this category, a finding that instead was in contrast to HPV vaccination. This difference may stem from the greater awareness and training healthcare students receive on influenza vaccination, as they are often educated on its importance not only for their personal safety but also to reduce the risk of transmitting infections to vulnerable patients. Among individual and group factors, it is well-known that previous influenza vaccination history plays a central role in influencing intention, as do a positive attitude toward influenza vaccination and a higher perception of the risks associated with the disease [ ]. Furthermore, as expected, those with greater awareness of COVID-19 generally demonstrated a higher intention to receive the influenza vaccine [ ]. Lastly, it is important to note that for the influenza vaccine having fewer concerns about vaccination costs and availability is fundamental to obtain higher intention. Therefore, it remains crucial for policymakers to provide free vaccines and easy-to-access vaccination services to motivate participation and reduce inequalities [ ].
This review has some strengths and limitations. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to provide a quantitative synthesis of university students’ willingness to undergo routine vaccinations, particularly HPV and influenza. In addition, by investigating the determinants arising from adjusted analyses, we were able to describe the factors that might influence vaccination behavior, using as reference the SAGE Working Group Vaccination Matrix, and underscore similarities and differences between the two vaccinations. Conversely, the limitations of our review mostly relate to the primary studies included. First, given the cross-sectional nature of the primary studies, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Second, when analyzing the determinants of vaccination intention, we relied solely on the study results, that may be influenced by the study power and sample size. Third, because we adopted a narrative approach to investigate the determinants, focusing solely on their direction and statistical significance, we were unable to provide information on their effect sizes and confidence intervals. However, this approach was necessary given the large number of determinants identified and the impossibility of conducting separate meta-analyses for each one. Fourth, since most studies were from Asia or Europe (depending on the vaccine), further research should be conducted in other regions to improve the generalizability of the findings. In addition, since we did not find any study addressing vaccination intention towards routine vaccinations other than HPV and influenza, for example with respect to the opportunity to catch up missed childhood vaccinations (e.g., measles or rubella), our results cannot be generalized to other vaccinations. Moreover, we only included studies that investigated vaccination intention using multivariable logistic regression analyses; thus, since we excluded some literature that did not meet our inclusion criteria, we are aware that our pooled proportions may only be indicative estimates of the true vaccination intention rates in this population. Although this approach was deemed necessary to make the analysis of determinants as comparable as possible and to maintain consistency among the studies included, we acknowledge that it also affects the interpretation of the determinants. Finally, the high heterogeneity observed in the meta-analyses, combined with the inclusion of studies of poor/fair quality, complicated the interpretation of the findings - especially in relation to influenza vaccination, where the subgroup analysis revealed differences based on study quality. Nevertheless, by specifically focusing on university students, we were able to provide valuable information that should enable policymakers to address immunization gaps and plan future educational campaigns in this population subgroup.

## Conclusions
Our systematic review has shown that the intention of university students to receive routine vaccines, specifically against HPV and influenza, is suboptimal. However, several positive predictors of vaccination intention have been identified, including knowledge and awareness of vaccinations, risk perception, personal experience with vaccinations, the influence of group opinion, and recommendations by healthcare professionals to have the HPV vaccination, as well as risk perception and a history of previous vaccinations for influenza. Conversely, concerns about vaccine costs and availability were negative determinants for both vaccinations. Addressing these factors is essential to increase vaccination intention in this group and to achieve high vaccination coverage.
