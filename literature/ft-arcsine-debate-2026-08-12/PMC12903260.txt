## Abstract

## Background
mRNA and dendritic cell vaccines are emerging immunotherapies for solid tumors. However, their relative immunogenicity and clinical efficacy have not been directly compared. We aimed to evaluate the comparative immune response, tumor response, safety, and survival outcomes of mRNA versus dendritic cell vaccines in patients with solid tumors.

## Methods
We performed a systematic review and network meta-analysis of clinical trials assessing mRNA and dendritic cell cancer vaccines in patients with solid tumors. We included 60 unique studies (67 trials) with a total of 1777 patients. Outcomes evaluated included immunogenicity (immune response), tumor response (objective response rate and disease control rate), safety (incidence of mild and severe adverse events), and survival (overall and progression-free survival). The review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (No. CRD420251012772; registered 17 March, 2025).

## Results
mRNA vaccines elicited significantly stronger immune responses than dendritic cell vaccines. mRNA vaccine recipients also experienced a higher incidence of adverse events, including mild and severe events. By contrast, dendritic cell vaccines achieved significantly higher objective response and disease control rates. No significant differences in overall survival or progression-free survival were observed between the two vaccine groups. Despite moderate between-trial heterogeneity, the findings were consistent and robust across analyses.

## Conclusions
This comprehensive network meta-analysis provides the first comparative evaluation of mRNA versus dendritic cell vaccines in solid tumors. It indicates that while mRNA vaccines induce more potent immunogenicity, dendritic cell vaccines confer better tumor control, with no observed differences in survival outcomes. These findings fill a critical evidence gap and provide an exploratory synthesis highlighting potential strengths and limitations of each vaccine type. Given that most included studies were early-phase, small-sample trials, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and interpreted with caution.

## Introduction
In recent decades, vaccines against cancer have emerged as a potential cure for cancer [ ]. These vaccines activate the immune system to recognize and kill cancer cells, allowing for more precision and lower toxicity [ ]. Dendritic cell (DC) and messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines are two examples of cancer vaccines. DCs are antigen-presenting cells that can present tumor antigens to T cells to activate cell-mediated immunity [ ]. DC vaccines function by capturing and processing tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and subsequently migrating to lymph nodes, where they prime naïve T cells to differentiate into tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [ ]. Numerous clinical trials have demonstrated that DC vaccines may improve outcomes for patients with solid tumors compared to placebo or standard chemotherapy [ ].
mRNA vaccines have garnered more attention in recent years due to their use during the COVID-19 pandemic [ ]. mRNA tumor vaccines deliver synthetic target antigen mRNA sequences which can activate immunity [ ]. Delivery of these vaccines includes cell-based vehicles (e.g., DCs), direct injection of mRNA, and mRNA with viral or non-viral vehicles (i.e., lipid nanoparticles). Compared to DC vaccines, mRNA vaccines may be more economical, simpler, and safer in the preparation and synthesis process [ ]. Early-phase clinical trials of mRNA vaccines have already been conducted, demonstrating favorable immunogenicity and prolonged survival in some patients [ , ].
Although both DC and mRNA vaccines have already been studied to some extent, direct comparisons of immunogenicity, safety, and clinical efficacy remain limited. Thus, we conducted a network meta-analysis to compare the therapeutic effects of DC and mRNA vaccines in patients with solid tumors.

## Methods

## Search strategy and study selection
This network meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline [ ] and was registered on PROSPERO (No. CRD420251012772). The protocol of the meta-analysis was not prepared. PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science were systematically searched from inception until December 31, 2024. Search terms included combinations of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to cancer (“cancer”, “carcinoma”, “tumor”, “neoplasm”, “malignancy”), vaccines (“vaccine”, “vaccines”, “vaccination”), and specific vaccine types (“mRNA”, “messenger RNA”, “dendritic cell”, “dendritic cell-based”). Additional trials were identified through manual screening of the reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies.
Eligible studies for this analysis met the following inclusion criteria: (1) population: patients with cancer (solid tumors only), regardless of cancer stage; (2) intervention and control: clinical trials evaluating either mRNA or DC vaccines as a monotherapy or in combination with other cancer therapies (e.g., chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or targeted therapy); (3) study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or prospective, non-randomized clinical trials with clearly defined treatment groups; (4) outcomes: included studies had to report the primary outcome (immune response) defined as antigen-specific immune activation measured by validated methods (e.g., T-cell responses, antibody titers, cytokine assays); secondary outcomes included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and safety (vaccine-related adverse events per the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [CTCAE] criteria). Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) non-mRNA or non-DC vaccines; (2) preventive vaccines or vaccines for infectious diseases; (3) studies on hematological malignancies or non-tumor diseases; (4) preclinical studies, animal studies, case reports, reviews, retrospective or observational studies, or editorials/commentaries; (5) abstract-only publications without full-text availability; (6) studies lacking quantitative data on any of the predefined outcomes; (7) studies published in a non-English language.
Two independent reviewers (T.L., H.C.) first screened titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text assessments of potentially eligible studies were independently performed by the same two reviewers. Any discrepancies in study selection were resolved through consensus-based discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer (L.D.) when necessary. If multiple publications described results from overlapping patient populations or datasets, only the most comprehensive or latest study reporting outcomes relevant to this meta-analysis was included. The detailed selection flow diagram is presented in Fig.  .
 

## Outcomes and data extraction
The primary outcome of this analysis was immune response, defined as a measurable antigen-specific immune activation indicating the proportion of patients with a post-vaccine T-cell response (CD4+ and CD8+). Secondary outcomes included (1) safety, measured by the incidence and severity of adverse events graded according to the CTCAE criteria; (2) objective response rate (ORR), defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete or partial tumor response according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria; (3) disease control rate (DCR), defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response, partial response, or stable disease; (4) overall survival (OS), defined as the time from randomization or treatment initiation to death from any cause; and (5) progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from randomization or treatment initiation to disease progression or death from any cause.
Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (Y.S., J.H.) using standardized data extraction forms, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer (N.L.). Extracted data included trial characteristics (authors, year of publication, study design, sample size, cancer type), patient demographics (age, sex), treatment details (type of vaccine, delivery method, dose), and outcome measures, which were collected at the trial-arm level. All studies were evaluated using RoB 2 [ ] (for randomized controlled trials) or the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [ ] (for non-randomized trials).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3) within RStudio (version 2024.12.1 + 563). Network meta-analyses were conducted using the netmeta and metafor packages. Given anticipated clinical heterogeneity across trials, random-effects models were employed for all analyses. For proportion-based outcomes, such as immune response, ORR, DCR, and safety, data were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine method. OS and PFS were analyzed using 95% CIs.
Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically with Egger linear regression tests. Egger’s test was run on mixed-effects models that included vaccine type as a moderator. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding studies with small sample sizes (< 10 patients) or older publication dates (> 10 years prior) to examine the robustness of pooled estimates. Network meta-analyses were performed according to vaccine type (mRNA vaccine vs. DC vaccine).
Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the random-effects model with restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimation. The magnitude of heterogeneity was evaluated by calculating the between-study variance (τ2) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) via the profile-likelihood method. Model convergence and stability of variance estimation were confirmed by examining profile-likelihood curves for unimodal and smooth characteristics.

## Results

## Study characteristics
Our systematic search initially identified 60 studies, of which 67 trials (some of the included studies contributed multiple independent trial arms) with a total of 1777 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in this network meta-analysis. Among these trials, 28 studies evaluated mRNA vaccines (707 patients), and 32 studies evaluated DC vaccines (1080 patients). The trials were published from 2001 to 2024. The median sample size was 16 patients for mRNA vaccine studies and 19 patients for DC vaccine studies. The median patient age was 60 years in the mRNA vaccine group and 56 years in the DC vaccine group. Among trials reporting sex distribution (mRNA vaccine, n = 32; DC vaccine, n = 32), the median proportion of male participants was 64.2% for mRNA vaccine trials and 59.6% for DC vaccine trials. The most commonly treated malignancy was melanoma in both mRNA vaccine studies (46.4%) and DC vaccine studies (21.9%). The trials included in this study were listed in Supplementary Table [ – ].

## Risk of bias
Of the 60 included studies, 3 were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [ , , ], all of which were assessed using RoB 2 tool and classified as having a low risk of bias. The remaining studies, assessed using the NOS, demonstrated generally good methodological quality, with NOS scores ranging from 6 to 9 points, indicating moderate to high quality overall.

## Publication bias
To evaluate potential publication bias, we performed Egger’s regression test on the immune response effect estimates. The test yielded a t-value of 1.21 (df = 59, p = 0.23), indicating that there was no statistically significant asymmetry in the funnel plot (Fig.  ). This suggests that publication bias is unlikely to have substantially influenced the pooled effect estimates for immune response.
 

## Heterogeneity estimation and stability
Moderate heterogeneity was observed for immune response (τ² = 0.58; 95% CI, 0.34 to 1.53; τ = 0.76), progression-free survival (τ² = 0.52; 95% CI, 0.23 to 1.24; τ = 0.72), and objective response rate (τ² = 1.48; 95% CI, 1.17 to 5.28; τ = 1.22). Severe adverse events (τ² = 2.88; 95% CI, 1.52 to 4.00; τ = 1.70) and mild adverse events (τ² = 2.51; 95% CI, 1.61 to 4.85; τ = 1.58) demonstrated higher heterogeneity, indicating substantial variability across trials, potentially due to differences in vaccine type, administration schedules, and reporting thresholds. In contrast, lower heterogeneity was noted for disease control (τ² = 0.20; 95% CI, 0.09 to 0.92; τ = 0.45) and overall survival (τ² = 0.22; 95% CI, 0.08 to 0.82; τ = 0.47). Profile-likelihood curves (Table , Figure ) were consistently smooth and unimodal for all outcomes, confirming robust and stable estimation of between-study variance, despite the relatively limited number of included studies.

## Immune response
Data from 62 independent study arms (32 mRNA vaccine and 30 DC vaccine study arms) were synthesized to compare the immunogenicity of mRNA versus DC vaccines. Weighted immune response rates (IRR) were computed using the aggregate number of responders and total participants in each arm. The mRNA vaccine group exhibited a weighted IRR of 74.9% (95% CI, 70.6% to 78.9%), which was significantly higher than the IRR of 63.9% (95% CI, 60.4% to 67.4%) observed in the DC vaccine group (p < 0.001, Fig.  ). To adjust for variability in sample sizes across study arms, a binomial generalized linear model (GLM) was employed. The model revealed that the log-odds of mounting an immune response in the reference group (DC vaccine) was 0.57 (SE = 0.076, p < 0.001), corresponding to an estimated odd of 1.77. The mRNA vaccine group demonstrated an additional log-odds of 0.52 (SE = 0.13, p < 0.001). Exponentiating this coefficient yielded an odds ratio (OR) of 1.69 (95% CI, 1.30 to 2.19), indicating that the odds of achieving an immune response were 69% higher with mRNA vaccines relative to DC vaccines. Furthermore, the aggregated data, when subjected to a chi-square test (χ² = 14.882, df = 1, p < 0.001), confirmed these significant differences.
 

## Safety
The most common adverse events (AEs) were flu-like symptoms, fatigue, and fever for both vaccines. In comparing the safety profiles of mRNA and DC vaccines, adverse events were stratified into mild (Grade 1–2) and severe (≥ Grade 3) categories. The weighted mild AE rate was 54.0% (95% CI, 50.4% to 57.5%) for the DC vaccine group versus 84.6% (95% CI, 81.5% to 87.4%) for the mRNA vaccine group. This difference was highly significant, as evidenced by a chi-square statistic of 142.8 (df = 1, p < 0.001, Fig.  a). Consistent with this finding, the binomial GLM revealed that mRNA vaccines were associated with significantly increased log‐odds of experiencing mild AEs compared to DC vaccines (estimate = 1.55, SE = 0.134, z = 11.5, p < 0.001), with a model intercept of 0.159 (SE = 0.0724, p = 0.028). The 95% confidence interval for the mRNA vaccine coefficient ranged from 1.288 to 1.815, indicating a robust association.
 The weighted severe AE rate was 3.42% (95% CI, 2.29% to 4.91%) in the DC vaccine group and 10.4% (95% CI, 8.20% to 12.9%) in the mRNA vaccine group. A chi-square test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant (χ² = 28.17, df = 1, p < 0.001, Fig.  b). Moreover, the GLM for severe AEs produced an intercept of − 3.34 (SE = 0.192, p < 0.001) for the DC group and a coefficient of 1.18 (SE = 0.230, z = 5.16, p < 0.001) for the mRNA group. The 95% confidence interval for the mRNA coefficient spanned from 0.746 to 1.65.

## Survival outcomes
Across the 21 studies investigating DC vaccines, the mean OS was 19.5 months (SD = 11.0) and the median OS was 19.0 months (range, 3.47 to 41.0 months). Across the 20 studies investigating mRNA vaccines, the mean OS was 21.2 months (SD = 11.1) and the median OS was 18.2 months (range, 7.9 to 51.0 months). Statistical comparisons using an independent samples two-tailed t-test (t = -0.47, df = 38.89, p = 0.638) and a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (W = 188.5, p = 0.584) revealed no significant differences in OS between the two groups (Fig.  a-b). Similarly, PFS was comparable between groups; the DC vaccine group (n = 13 studies) exhibited a mean PFS of 8.87 months (SD = 5.43) and a median PFS of 7.87 months (range, 1.97 to 18 months), while the mRNA vaccine group (n = 17 studies) demonstrated a mean PFS of 7.44 months (SD = 8.40) and a median PFS of 4.00 months (range, 1.80–26 months). Again, an independent samples two-tailed t-test (t = 0.54, df = 28, p = 0.600) and a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (W = 148, p = 0.120) revealed no significant differences in PFS between the two groups (Fig.  c-d). Collectively, these findings suggest that OS and PFS are comparable between mRNA and DC vaccines.
 

## Clinical response
Pooled ORR and DCR differed significantly between the vaccine groups. The DC vaccine group achieved a pooled ORR of 30.6% (224 responders among 731 patients), which was significantly higher than the ORR of 15.7% observed in the mRNA vaccine group (42 responders among 267 patients, Fig.  a). A binomial GLM confirmed this difference, with the intercept (reflecting the log-odds for the DC vaccine) estimated at -0.817 (SE = 0.080, p < 0.001) and the coefficient for the mRNA vaccine estimated at -0.86 (SE = 0.19, z = -4.63, p < 0.001; 95% CI: -1.24 to -0.51). The chi-square test also indicated a significant difference (χ2 = 21.49, df = 1, p < 0.001). For DCR, the DC vaccine group demonstrated a pooled DCR of 63.1% (386/612 patients) compared to 43.8% in the mRNA vaccine group (155/354 patients, Fig.  b). The corresponding GLM produced an intercept of 0.532 (SE = 0.083, p < 0.001) and a coefficient for the mRNA vaccine of -0.782 (SE = 0.14, z = -5.78, p < 0.001; 95% CI: -1.05 to -0.52), with the chi-square test yielding χ2 = 33.13 (df = 1, p < 0.001). These results suggest that the DC vaccine exhibits a significantly superior response profile in terms of both ORR and DCR.
 

## Sensitivity analysis
In the overall meta-analysis (k = 62), the pooled logit‐transformed effect estimate for immune response was 0.68 (95% CI, 0.40 to 0.96; p < 0.001), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 66.84%). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of these findings. When studies with small sample sizes (< 10 patients) were excluded (k = 39), the pooled estimate increased to 0.84 (95% CI, 0.46 to 1.23; p < 0.0001) although heterogeneity was higher (I² = 80.01%). Excluding studies published more than 10 years prior (k = 39) yielded a similar effect estimate of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.49 to 1.11; p < 0.001) with a reduction in heterogeneity (I² = 57.00%). Finally, when both studies with small sample sizes and older studies were excluded (k = 24), the pooled effect increased to 1.08 (95% CI, 0.64 to 1.52; p < 0.001; I² = 74.35%). These sensitivity analyses indicate that the overall findings are robust, with the exclusion of small and/or older studies yielding consistently higher effect estimates for immune response.

## Network meta-analysis
The meta-regression results demonstrated that mRNA vaccines elicited a significantly higher immune response (β = 0.64, 95% CI, 0.11 to 1.16, p = 0.018), indicating superior immunogenicity. Although the pooled ORR and DCR were numerically higher for mRNA vaccines, these differences did not reach statistical significance (ORR: β = − 0.63, p = 0.123; DCR: β = -0.37, p = 0.239). For safety outcomes, mRNA vaccines were associated with a significantly higher incidence of mild AEs (β = -1.80, 95% CI, -2.79 to -0.81, p < 0.001), while differences in severe AEs were not statistically significant (β = -0.47, p = 0.237). Regarding survival, there was no significant difference in OS between the two groups (mean difference = -3.44 months, p = 0.433); however, PFS was significantly shorter for the mRNA vaccine group (mean difference = -4.50 months, 95% CI, -6.90 to -2.10, p < 0.001). Overall, these findings suggest that mRNA vaccines demonstrate superior immunogenicity; however, this immunological advantage did not translate into superior clinical efficacy outcomes, with comparable OS, lower ORR and DCR, and significantly shorter PFS compared to DC vaccines.

## Discussion
In this study, we compared DC vaccines and mRNA vaccines for cancer treatment. mRNA vaccines activated T cells in a greater proportion of patients compared to DC vaccines, indicating greater immunogenicity; in fact, the odd of eliciting an immune response was 69% higher with mRNA vaccines. Conversely, DC vaccines demonstrated a better clinical response, with a higher pooled ORR and DCR compared to mRNA vaccines. Regarding survival outcomes, no significant differences were observed between the two vaccine types for OS and PFS. Notably, mRNA vaccines demonstrated an increased incidence of both mild and severe AEs. Therefore, although mRNA vaccines are immunologically promising, their clinical benefit remains uncertain and requires further investigation.
The superior immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines aligns with its inherent mechanism of action. Through intracellular translation of loaded tumor antigens, mRNA vaccines directly activate adaptive immunity, leading to potent antigen-specific T-cell responses. This makes mRNA vaccines promising candidates for both monotherapy and combination therapy, alongside standard chemotherapy or immunotherapies like PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. In addition to cell-mediated immunity, some studies suggest that mRNA vaccines also activate humoral immunity, with increased antigen-specific antibody levels detected in solid tumor patients [ , ]. However, most studies in this meta-analysis did not analyze antibody levels because the mRNA used in these studies did not target a specific antigen. Finally, mRNA vaccines also stimulate cytokine secretion [ , ], further supporting the strong immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines.
Although mRNA vaccines had greater immunogenicity than DC vaccines, this did not translate to better clinical outcomes, such as superior ORR and DCR. However, studies have still demonstrated that mRNA vaccines have a significant clinical impact and that immune response is highly correlated with clinical efficacy. Mackensen et al. used mRNA vaccines combined with CAR-T therapy to treat refractory solid tumors, which showed better DCRs compared to CAR-T monotherapy (66.67% vs. 33.33%) [ ]. Mu et al. transfected mRNA into DC cells to treat prostate cancer. The PSA slope was found to decrease in all patients, and the cell-mediated immune response activated by mRNA vaccines was found to be antigen-specific [ ]. Rittig et al. found a similar immune response in patients with renal cell carcinoma [ ]. In contrast, Cafri et al. found an increased immune response in 3 out of 4 patients with metastatic gastrointestinal cancer who received mRNA vaccines but no clinical response [ ]. Interestingly, while the total number of T cells increased, there was no corresponding rise in antigen-specific T cells, which suggests that clinical response is related to the antigen-specific immunity activated by mRNA vaccines. Future studies should distinguish whether increased frequencies of T cells are antigen-specific or not, which could help us better understand the relationship between immunogenicity and clinical efficacy.
Similarly, the greater immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines did not translate to improved survival outcomes such as OS and PFS. Although several studies for both mRNA and DC vaccines did not reach median OS or PFS when follow-up ended [ , , , , , ], our sensitivity analysis excluding these studies did not change the results. Nevertheless, given the limited follow-up and the potential underestimation of survival outcomes, further trials with extended follow-up periods are needed for a comprehensive assessment of the survival benefit associated with these vaccines.
Vaccine safety is another critical consideration. mRNA vaccines were associated with a significantly higher incidence of mild AEs than DC vaccines. In addition, the incidence of severe AEs associated with mRNA vaccines was higher, which emphasizes the importance of careful patient monitoring. Continuous surveillance for severe AEs, careful patient selection, and optimization of vaccine delivery (i.e., dose, delivery method [ ], etc.) are still essential.
A significant limitation of this study was the heterogeneity of the included trials. Given the diversity of the included trials, the substantial heterogeneity observed for several endpoints (e.g., adverse events, ORR, and PFS) significantly undermines the stability and robustness of our pooled estimates. Although sensitivity analyses indicated some consistency in results, the substantial between-study variability means that our findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.
Additionally, immune response outcomes combined various biologically distinct assays (T-cell activation, antibody titers, cytokine assays), and due to limited available data, subgroup analyses by assay type were not feasible. This further contributes to outcome heterogeneity and represents an important limitation. We acknowledge that the small sample sizes of most trials and their single-arm designs are inherent limitations that weaken the level of evidence; similarly, because most studies did not report hazard ratios or sufficient data to reconstruct them, our OS and PFS analyses necessarily relied on descriptive pooling of reported median survival times rather than time‑to‑event effect measures. This approach cannot fully account for censoring or survival dynamics and should be interpreted with caution.
We recognize that subgroup or additional meta-regression analyses, such as those by tumor type, trial phase, or use of checkpoint inhibitors, could be valuable. However, given our primary objective of broadly comparing mRNA and dendritic cell vaccines across all solid tumors, we did not perform these analyses due to significant variability in the number of studies per tumor type and vaccine type. For example, certain tumor types were predominantly investigated with either mRNA or dendritic cell vaccines, making direct tumor-specific subgroup comparisons unreliable. Furthermore, the limited and heterogeneous use of checkpoint inhibitors across studies did not permit robust subgroup analyses or meta-regression.
However, in the absence of larger controlled trials, our analysis provides the most comprehensive and systematic evidence synthesis currently available for mRNA and DC cancer vaccines. Encouragingly, our findings remained consistent despite these constraints: the profile-likelihood τ² curves were smooth and unimodal for all endpoints (indicating stable between-study variance estimates) and sensitivity analyses (e.g., excluding the smallest or earliest trials) did not meaningfully alter the pooled results. Thus, while these results should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the heterogeneity and early-phase nature of the data, the overall stability of the estimates lends confidence to our conclusions. Looking ahead, the emergence of data from larger, multicenter randomized controlled trials reporting standardized time-to-event outcomes will be crucial to validate and refine our findings, further enhancing the accuracy and generalizability of the current evidence.
There are several key opportunities for future research. First, the relationship between vaccine-induced immunity, especially antigen-specific immunity, and clinical response needs further investigation, as this could potentially improve the clinical performance of mRNA vaccines. Second, combination therapies warrant further research, as vaccine-based therapies may augment the impact of existing treatments [ , , , ]. Third, future studies should investigate the optimal antigens, vaccine formulation, delivery methods, doses, and administration schedules for each cancer type to best balance safety and clinical efficacy. Lastly, large-scale, randomized clinical trials for both vaccines are still needed.

## Conclusions
This comparative analysis of mRNA versus DC vaccines in solid tumors reveals that mRNA vaccines are more immunogenic but also associated with a higher incidence of adverse events, whereas DC vaccines achieve superior tumor control with fewer side effects. Importantly, these divergent effects did not translate into a survival advantage for either approach: no significant differences in overall survival or progression-free survival were observed between the two vaccine modalities.
Clinically, these exploratory findings highlight potential strengths and trade-offs of mRNA and dendritic cell vaccine strategies. The potent immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines could be explored further in settings requiring robust immune activation is a priority, while the favorable safety and tumor control profile of DC vaccines suggests their potential utility for patients requiring a less intensive approach. However, given the early-phase and exploratory nature of the available evidence, our results are not sufficient to directly inform clinical practice or patient-level decisions. Instead, they underscore the critical need for future well-designed, randomized controlled trials to rigorously evaluate personalized vaccine strategies, optimal patient selection, and potential combinatorial approaches. Such future studies will provide more definitive guidance for the next generation of cancer vaccine therapies in solid tumors.
