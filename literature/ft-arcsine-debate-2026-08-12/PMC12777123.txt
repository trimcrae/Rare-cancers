## Abstract

## Objectives
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the impact of disc repositioning surgeries on condylar bone regeneration.

## Materials and methods
The study protocol adhered to the PRISMA 2020 checklist and Cochrane guidelines (CRD42024501867). The study included both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and nonrandomized studies of interventions (NRSIs) (both observational and clinical trials) that reported radiological outcomes of condylar bone regeneration after disc repositioning surgeries were included. The focus was on joints exhibiting new bone formation and changes in condylar height. A comprehensive search across six databases (Embase, MEDLINE, CENTRAL, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus) was conducted to identify relevant studies up to February 2025. Additionally, reference lists of eligible articles were manually reviewed. The methodological quality were evaluated using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS). The risk-of-bias assessment of the included NRSIs was conducted using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. Random-effect meta-analyses were performed using STATA software with the metaprop and metan commands.

## Results
Sixteen studies with a total of 1,457 patients met the inclusion criteria, including one RCT. Meta-analyses revealed that 89.3% (95% confidence interval (CI): 79.4%-96.5%) of TMJs demonstrated new condylar bone formation post-disc repositioning surgery. For patients under 21 years of age, this figure rose to 95.9% (95% CI: 91.1%-99.2%). On average, disc repositioning surgeries increased condylar height by 1.54 mm (95% CI: 1.18–1.89 mm), with a greater increase of 1.75 mm (95% CI: 1.41–2.10 mm) observed in patients under 21 years. Considerable heterogeneity was found in most meta-analyses.

## Conclusions
Disc repositioning surgeries appear to promote condylar bone regeneration, with more pronounced benefits observed in younger patients. However, the considerable heterogeneity among included studies and limited quality of current evidence necessitate cautious interpretation and call for future longitudinal studies with standardized protocols and long-term follow-up to confirm these preliminary results and establish more robust conclusions.

## Clinical relevance
This systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that restoring the disc position might be beneficial for condylar bone regeneration.

## Background
Temporomandibular joint disc displacements (TMJ DDs) are among the most prevalent TMJ disorders [ ]. Based on the position of the articular disc during mandibular movement, DDs can be categorized into two types: DD with reduction (DDwR) and DD without reduction (DDwoR) [ ]. DDs may result in symptoms such as joint clicking, joint pain, and restricted mandibular movement [ ]. If left untreated, DDs, especially DDwoR, can progressively worsen over time, potentially leading to condylar resorption [ , ]. Condylar resorption can significantly affect facial appearance, causing conditions such as retrognathia and facial asymmetry. It may also impair oral function, thereby diminishing a patient’s quality of life [ , ]. Consequently, determining the most effective treatments for patients with symptomatic DDs continues to be a key priority for both general dentists and dental specialists.
The TMJ exhibits a remarkable biological capacity for adaptive remodeling- defined as morphological changes in existing bone structure in response to altered mechanical stress. Stress alterations surrounding the condyle, such as those induced by disc displacements, occlusal changes, and oral parafunctions, can trigger either condylar resorption or formation as adaptive responses [ ]. Under normal circumstances, TMJ articular discs act as “cushions”, alleviating stress on the condyle and maintaining the joint’s stability. However, disc displacement disrupts this equilibrium, redistributing stress around the condyle and potentially resulting in condylar resorption. As a result, improving the mechanical environment surrounding the condyle is considered a promising strategy to prevent further resorption and facilitate condylar bone regeneration - the formation of new bone tissue with restored structure [ ].
Various treatments are available for patients with DDs. Non-surgical interventions, such as physiotherapy, pharmacotherapy, and occlusal splint therapy, primarily aim to alleviate symptoms and restore oral function. However, their efficacy in managing progressive DDs is limited [ , ]. Furthermore, anterior repositioning splints (ARS) are predominantly effective for Class II malocclusions, restricting their wider applicability [ ]. For DD patients who do not respond adequately to non-surgical treatments or experience progressive condylar resorption, surgical intervention is often recommended [ ]. Disc repositioning surgeries focus on restoring the position of the articular disc, thereby achieving a more stable and normal spatial relationship between disc and condyle compared to other treatment approaches. Research has demonstrated that disc repositioning surgeries can reduce clinical symptoms and dentofacial deformities in patients with DDs [ , ]. Condylar bone regeneration has been regarded as a favorable and essential clinical sign for reconstructive process of the condyle. Bone regeneration can help restore the joint integrity, preventing or mitigating the complications of joint resorption, such as malocclusion and facial deformities, which is essential for maintaining normal orofacial function. Additionally, it facilitates joint stabilization in its new functional position, contributing to long-term joint health [ , , ]. Previous studies have investigated the potential role of disc repositioning in condylar bone regeneration, with varying degrees of post-surgery condylar regeneration reported [ , ]. Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive evidence delineating the exact extent to which disc repositioning surgeries facilitate bone regeneration. To date, robust evidence regarding the extent to which disc repositioning surgery promotes new condylar bone formation in DD-affected joints remains insufficient. Therefore, this study aims to critically and comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of disc repositioning surgery in fostering condylar bone regeneration. It seeks to investigate whether restoring the disc-condyle relationship, via disc repositioning surgery or alternative TMJ disc repositioning treatments, can promote condylar regeneration. The null research hypotheses were: (a) disc repositioning surgeries cannot promote condylar bone regeneration in patients with DDs, and (b) there is no factors such as age influencing condyle regeneration after disc repositioning surgeries.

## Materials and methods

## Protocol and registration
This systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO database (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO; registration number: CRD42024501867). The review’s reporting adheres to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [ – ]. The PRISMA 2020 checklist is presented in Appendix 1.

## Eligibility criteria
The inclusion criteria were developed following the PICOS framework with the specifications outlined below:

P (population): The population consisted of individuals diagnosed with DDwR or DDwoR. 
I (intervention): The intervention included any form of articular disc repositioning surgery, with or without the use of a postoperative splint. 
C (comparison or control): For comparison or control, there were no restrictions regarding the presence of an independent control group. In studies with such a group, comparisons were made with patients who received no treatment, a placebo, or alternative treatments. For studies lacking an independent control group, the condylar bone status in DD joints prior to disc repositioning surgery needed to be documented and served as a point of comparison. 
O (outcomes): The outcomes focused on the occurrence and extent of condylar bone regeneration in DD joints following disc repositioning surgery. This included examining the number and proportion of joints showing regeneration, as well as quantitative changes in condylar morphology, such as height and width.
S (study design): Regarding study design, RCTs and NRSIs, including single-arm studies, were included.

Studies were excluded if they involved patients with systemic diseases that could impact the effectiveness of disc repositioning surgery or patients receiving treatments unrelated to disc repositioning surgery or essential interventions during the treatment process. Studies reporting on populations that included both normal and abnormal joints in patients with disc displacement, rather than focusing exclusively on affected joints, were also excluded. Additionally, studies not published in English and publications in the form of editorials, expert opinions, or abstracts were not considered.

## Information sources and search
A comprehensive search was performed across six electronic databases (Embase, MEDLINE, CENTRAL, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus) up to February 2025 to identify published studies. The search term include (1) P (Patient): temporomandibular joint disorders, temporomandibular joint disc displacement, temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis, disc-condyle position (2). I (Intervention): disc repositioning surgeries, occlusal splint, orthodontic treatment, anterior repositioning splint, stabilization splint, disc repositioning (3). O (Outcome): bone/condylar regeneration, bone/condylar remodeling, temporomandibular joint regeneration, condylar height change, mandibular length. A combination of free-text keywords and subject terms was employed to locate relevant studies, with no restrictions on the publication year. A detailed outline of the search strategies used for each database and total hits per database is provided in Appendix 2. Furthermore, the search was supplemented by manually reviewing the reference lists of the included articles to identify additional potentially relevant studies. Reference management and deduplication were conducted using EndNote software (EndNote 21, Clarivate™, Philadelphia, PA).

## Study selection
Two reviewers (JQL and ZYS) independently selected the papers by sequentially screening the titles, abstracts, full texts, and reference lists of the included studies. Following the full-text assessment, the results were cross-checked for consistency. Interobserver agreements were evaluated by Cohen’s κ value. During the review process, any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (AUY).

## Data extraction
The following information was extracted from the selected studies and organized into a pre-defined table.

General information: first author, publication year, and country or region where the study was conducted.
Study design.
Patients’ characteristics, including total number of participants, age, gender, DD type.
Detailed treatment and number of target joints in both the control group and intervention group.
Follow-up time.
Outcome measures, including the number or proportion of joints showing condylar bone regeneration post-surgery, as well as quantitative changes in condylar morphology (e.g., condylar height, width, etc.), analyzed separately for the control and intervention groups.

## Methodological quality and risk-of-bias assessment
The methodological quality was assessed using the MINORS [ ]. This index comprises twelve items, with the first eight tailored for non-comparative studies. For studies including a control group, all twelve items were applied. Each item was scored as 0, 1, or 2, corresponding to “not reported”, “reported but inadequate”, and “reported and adequate”, respectively. The total score for each study was calculated by summing all item scores, with a maximum score of 16 for non-comparative studies and 24 for comparative studies. The risk-of-bias assessment of the NRSIs was conducted using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [ ], in which the risk-of-bias was assessed based on seven domains: [ ] bias due to confounding [ ], bias in selection of participants into the study [ ], bias in classification of interventions [ ], bias due to deviations from intended intervention [ ], bias due to missing data [ ], bias in measurement of outcomes, and [ ] bias in selection of the reported result. Each study was graded as low, moderate, serious, critical or no information”. The risk-of-bias plots was created by robvis [ ].
Two reviewers (JQL and ZYS) independently evaluated the included studies. Any disagreements in the quality assessment were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (AUY).

## Data synthesis and statistical analysis
Due to the limited number of studies involving a control group receiving either natural course observation or placebo treatment, the data synthesis focused on joints that underwent disc repositioning surgery. The data were synthesized both qualitatively and quantitatively. The qualitative data analysis primarily focused on study characteristics, such as categories of DD, types of disc repositioning surgeries, follow-up duration. Two primary effect measures were quantitatively analyzed: the proportion of DD TMJ cases with condylar bone regeneration following surgery, and the mean changes in condylar height (in mm) post-surgery. As all the included studies used magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and same unist to measure the condylar regeneration. The quantitative measures were not standardized before pooling.
For studies reporting multiple follow-up periods, data from the longest follow-up period were used in the meta-analysis. Standard error (SE) calculations varied based on the type of data. For proportion, SE = SQRT (proportion*(1-proportion)/sample size). For condylar height changes, SE = standard deviation/SQRT (sample size). In studies where the preoperative and postoperative standard deviations (SD) of condylar height were available but the SD of condylar height changes was not, an estimated SD was derived according to the guidelines outlined in Chap. 16.1.3.2 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [ ]. Specifically, the correlation coefficient (Corr) was initially calculated using data from studies that have reported the SD of preoperative condylar heights (SD1), postoperative condylar height (SD2), and the SD of condylar height changes (SD3), employing the formula: Corr = (SD12 + SD22 – SD32)/(2*SD1*SD2). Given the potential correlation between condylar bone regeneration results and follow-up periods, the correlation coefficient was then categorized into “>13 months” and “=<12 months” based on follow-up time in the included studies, and the mean value of each category was used for subsequent calculations. The estimated SD (SDest) was computed using the correlation coefficient and the SD of condylar height before (SDpre) and after (SDpost) disc repositioning surgery, using the formula: SDest = SQRT (SDpre2 + SDpost2 - (2*Corr*SDpre*SDpost)). Studies lacking reports on SDpre and SDpost were excluded from the meta-analysis.
In addition, considering the data availability in the included studies, a meta-analysis of the results for patients under the age of 21 was conducted to evaluate the potential influence of age on surgical outcomes. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA 18 (StataCorp, TX, USA) with a random-effects model utilizing the DerSimonian & Laird approach based on expected heterogeneity and the intention of generalization of results. The metaprop and metan commands were employed for analyzing proportions using Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation approach and mean changes in condylar height, respectively. No continuity correction was conducted. Forest plots were generated to visually depict effect sizes (ESs) along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and 95% predictive intervals. Heterogeneity between studies was quantified using the chi-squared test with p value of < 0.10 or I² statistic of >50% indicating considerable heterogeneity [ ]. If heterogeneity was not considerable, then fixed-effects models would be also performed as sensitivity analysis.

## Results

## Study selection
The PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. ) presents the process of identifying eligible studies for meta-analysis. A total of 510 records were retrieved through electronic searches. After removing duplicate entries, the titles and abstracts of 274 articles were assessed for eligibility, resulting in the exclusion of 230 articles (Cohen’s κ = 0.946). Of the 44 articles subjected to full-text review, 31 were excluded (Cohen’s κ = 0.797), with the reasons for exclusion detailed in Fig. . Additionally, three studies were included following reference list checks of the included studies and a secondary search in February 2025 [ – ]. Ultimately, 16 articles were selected for qualitative and quantitative synthesis, comprising one RCT [ ] and 15 NRSIs [ , – , – , – ], including nine single-arm studies (SASs) [ , – , , , , , ].
 

## Study characteristics

## Overview of study characteristics
The detailed characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table [ , – , – ]. All the studies, published between 2019 and 2024, were conducted in China. The 16 studies included a total of 1,457 patients of both genders, spanning a wide age range (from at least 10 to 70 years old), with a higher proportion of female participants. The cohort comprised cases of both DDwR and DDwoR, with instances of unilateral and bilateral DDs. The follow-up period across the studies varied significantly, ranging from 3 months to more than 5 years.
 

## Study design
Among the 16 studies, there was diversity in the control and intervention groups. The only RCT included utilized a control group that underwent open suturing surgery, while the intervention group underwent open suturing surgery combined with a postoperative occlusal splint [ ]. In 6 NRSIs with comparison groups, Liu et al. [ ] employed untreated joints with DDs (natural course) as the control group, while Ding et al. [ ] used joints with DD treated with stabilization splints as the control group. Zhu et al. and Lu et al. compared the effects of two different disc repositioning surgeries, namely mini-screw anchorage surgery and open suturing surgery, on condylar remodeling [ , ]. The remaining 2 studies evaluated the impact of functional orthodontic treatment on DD patients with dentofacial deformities following disc repositioning surgery [ , ]. In contrast, the 9 SASs did not have specified control groups, and the intervention groups underwent disc repositioning surgery [ , – , , , , , ].

## Variations in DD subtypes and surgical techniques
Although all the subjects in the included studies were patients with DDs, the DD subtypes varied across the articles. These subtypes included DDwR [ ], DDwoR [ , , , – ], unilateral DD [ , , ], and bilateral DD [ , , , ]. Some articles did not specify the subtype and included a mix of DD types [ , , ]. The types of disc repositioning surgeries also differed. Specifically, 5 studies used arthroscopic disc repositioning surgery [ , , , , ], 6 employed mini-screw anchorage surgery [ , , , , , ], and 3 adopted open suturing surgery [ , , ]. Two studies utilized both mini-screw anchorage surgery and open suturing surgery [ , ].

## Methodological quality and risk-of-bias assessment of studies
The results of the methodological quality assessment are presented in Table . All 16 included studies had clearly defined aims and unambiguous endpoints that aligned with the study objectives [ , – , – ]. The studies also featured sufficiently long follow-up periods. Except for one study [ ], the remaining 15 studies recruited patients consecutively. Of the 16 studies, only 3 were prospective [ , , ], while 10 were retrospective [ , , , , , , , – ]. The design of the remaining 3 studies was not specified as either prospective or retrospective in nature [ , , ]. Only 2 studies adequately reported the use of “blind evaluation” during assessments [ , ]. While the remaining studies mentioned their evaluation methods, they provided insufficient details regarding blind evaluation.
 Regarding loss to follow-up, 1 study reported a rate of less than 5% [ ], whereas four studies documented rates greater than 5% [ , , , ]. However, the loss to follow-up could not be identified in the remaining 11 studies [ , , , , – ]. In terms of prospective sample size calculation, only 3 studies adequately reported this aspect [ – ]. Among the 7 studies that included a comparison group, all selected appropriate control groups, conducted adequate statistical analyses, and ensured that the comparison groups were contemporary and had comparable baselines [ , – , – ].
As for the risk-of-bias assessment of the six NRSIs [ , , , – ], all the studies had an overall moderate risk of bias and showed low risk in: bias in classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias in measurement of outcomes and bias in selection of the reported result. Four studies showed moderate bias in selecting the participants, contributing most to the overall moderate of risk of bias [ , – ] (Fig. ). Overall, the quality of the included articles was quite similar, we did not exclude any particular studies to perform sensitivity analysis in this study.
 

## Meta-analysis
Meta-analyses were conducted to evaluate two outcomes. Eight studies [ , , , , , , , ] were included to calculate the proportion of TMJs with new condylar bone formation (%) following disc repositioning surgery, while 12 studies [ , , , – , ] were used to assess changes in condylar height (mm) post-surgery. Due to significant heterogeneity among the 16 included studies, a random-effects model (DerSimonian & Laird approach) was applied for the meta-analysis. The results indicated that 89.3% (95% CI: 79.4% to 96.5%) of TMJs with DDs demonstrated condylar regeneration following disc repositioning surgery (Fig. a). For patients younger than 21 years, the proportion of cases with condylar regeneration increased to 95.9% (95% CI: 91.1% to 99.2%) (Fig. b).
 Regarding changes in condylar height, the analysis revealed that disc repositioning surgery resulted in an average increase in condylar height of 1.54 mm (95% CI: 1.18 mm to 1.89 mm) (Fig.  a). This increase was more pronounced in patients under 21 years of age, with an average gain of 1.75 mm (95% CI: 1.41 mm to 2.10 mm) (Fig.  b).
 Considerable heterogeneity was found in all meta-analyses (p < 0.10 and I² statistic of > 50%). Due to considerable heterogeneity, results should be interpreted in caution.

## Discussion

## Our findings
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to explore the effects of TMJ disc repositioning surgeries on condylar bone regeneration in TMJ DDs. By conducting a comprehensive literature search, rigorous screening process, and data synthesis, the two null hypotheses were rejected, demonstrating condylar bone regeneration in TMJ DDs after disc repositioning surgery, and younger patients exhibiting notably greater regenerative outcomes.

## Key benefits of disc-repositioning surgery
Various therapeutic strategies are available for managing patients with DDs, encompassing both surgical and non-surgical interventions. However, no clear consensus has been reached regarding the most effective approach for treating DDs [ ]. Given the potential for early-stage DDwR to resolve spontaneously, allowing patients to transition from a symptomatic to an asymptomatic state, surgical intervention should be delayed unless symptoms show progressive worsening [ ]. Emerging evidence has linked DDwoR to subsequent condylar bone resorption, with prolonged DDwoR reducing responsiveness to therapeutic interventions. Consequently, a growing consensus advocates timely treatments to mitigate the risk of pathological progression [ ]. Non-surgical and minimally invasive approaches are generally recommended as first-line treatments for both DDwR and DDwoR due to their low risk compared to surgical treatments [ , ]. However, Non-surgical strategies, such as physiotherapy and medication, often fail to restore the absorptive function of the articular discs, with unimproved disc-condyle misalignment recognized as the main contributing factor. Although arthrocentesis can effectively reduce TMJ pain, it may inadvertently exacerbate disc deformation and displacement [ ]. While certain non-surgical methods have been shown to improve or even re-establish the disc-condyle relationship, the success rate is variable and may carry risk of exacerbating displacement [ , – ]. As a result, these splints are mainly used to maintain the disc position after it has been corrected by other methods [ ]. Mandibular manipulation is more successful in recent-onset DDwoR cases, due to better tissue elasticity and fewer joint issues, but its effectiveness decreases in long-standing cases [ , , ]. In contrast, disc repositioning surgery effectively restores the disc-condyle relationship, which may also support condylar regeneration.

## Interpretation of the results and future clinical implications
Undifferentiated mesenchymal cells on the condyle surface enable lifelong TMJ bone remodeling [ ]. Stress changes around the condyle can cause bone resorption or formation [ , ]. Restoring the disc’s position to reduce biomechanical overloading may enhance condylar regeneration, as shown by our meta-analysis where 89.3% of TMJs with DD demonstrated new bone formation and a 1.54 mm increase in condylar height after disc repositioning. Age-related declines in regeneration capacity, attributed to reduced progenitor cells, lead to diminished regeneration in adults compared to adolescents [ – ]. Notably, the percentage of new bone regeneration rose to 95.9%, accompanied by a 1.75 mm increase in individuals under 21 years. Overall, our study demonstrates that disc repositioning surgeries effectively promote condylar bone regeneration in joints with DDs, while emphasizing the importance of re-establishing the articular disc-condyle relationship as a key objective in managing TMJ DDs. However, given the invasive nature of these surgeries, investigating non-invasive or less invasive alternatives to restore the disc position offers a promising direction for future research. For example, mandibular repositioning or functional mandibular advancement appliances can change the position of the mandible, which may alter or even restore the position of articular disc. Nowadays, their long-term success of disc reduction was inconsistent across different studies, with variability in the direction of condylar movement being one of the potential reasons [ ]. Therefore, future research may focus on modifying these non-invasive interventions to maximize the restoration of the disc-condyle relationship. In addition, the DD patients may suffer from functional abnormalities, which greatly impairs their life quality. While the primary aim of our review was to synthesize the available evidence on bone regeneration, the effects of disc-repositioning surgery on functional improvement (e.g., long-term pain reduction, masticatory function, and joint stability) should be investigated and reported in the future research.

## Limitations
This study underscores importance of restoring disc-condyle relationship in treating DD patients, not only provide synthesized evidence to demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of disc repositioning surgeries in joints with DD but also establishing a strong theoretical foundation for exploring other potential alternative TMJ disc repositioning treatments to promote condylar bone regeneration in DD joints. It should be noted that our study has certain limitations that require further improvement in the future. Firstly, the overall quality of evidence from the included studies was suboptimal. Of the 16 studies reviewed, only one was an RCT, six were NRSIs, and the remaining nine were SASs that compared pre-treatment and post-treatment outcomes. Most studies (nine) were retrospective. Moreover, only one study included a control group that followed the natural course of the condition [ ]. Consequently, our study extracted data on changes before and after disc repositioning surgery for quantitative analysis, which limited the ability to perform publication bias and sensitivity analyses. Future research efforts must prioritize rigorously designed RCTs that incorporate robust comparative analyses. Secondly, the considerable heterogeneity in TMJ DDs and surgical procedures across studies restricts the generalizability of our meta-analysis findings, and possible reasons are as follows: TMJ DDs can be classified into DDwR and DDwoR with varying degrees of severity, as well as unilateral or bilateral DDs. These subtypes can exhibit different postoperative prognoses. However, in our meta-analysis, some studies included multiple DD subtypes, which complicates efforts to analyze outcomes specific to each subtype. Additionally, the distinct shapes and elasticity of displaced articular discs require tailored disc repositioning surgeries. For instance, arthroscopic surgery is suitable for displaced discs with a normal shape and intact posterior ligament, whereas open surgery is often used for discs with poor elasticity and abnormal shapes [ ]. Consequently, considerable heterogeneity was observed, and the findings of our meta-analysis may have limited applicability to different DD subtypes and surgical approaches. Therefore, future studies incorporating stratified subgroup analyses based on specific types of DD and disc repositioning surgeries are needed. Finally, all the studies included in our analysis originated from China, raising concerns about potential overlapping patient data across studies, which could introduce bias into the results.

## Conclusions
Our study indicates a high probability of successful bone regeneration following disc repositioning surgeries, with notably better outcomes observed in younger patients. The findings suggest that re-establishing proper articular disc-condyle relationships may support condylar bone formation and represents an effective treatment paradigm for TMJ with DDs. Limited by the considerable heterogeneity among included studies and lack of high-quality studies, the interpretation of pooled results should be in caution. More high-quality studies are urgently needed in the future to improve the evidence strength.
