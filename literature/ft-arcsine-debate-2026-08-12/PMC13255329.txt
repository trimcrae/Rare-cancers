## Abstract

## Introduction
Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a serious complication following cardiac surgery. Despite existing investigations, the true incidence rates and risk factors remain incompletely defined.

## Methods
We systematically searched PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, and Web of Science databases, incorporating 21 eligible studies (n = 794,662). A random-effects model was applied to perform a meta-analysis assessing the incidence of VTE and associated risk factors after cardiac surgery.

## Results
The pooled incidence of VTE after cardiac surgery was 10.2% (95% CI: 7.6%–13.1%), with incidences of DVT and PE reported at 10.6% (95% CI: 7.6%–14.0%) and 1.6% (95% CI: 0.8%–2.6%), respectively based on random‑effects models. Subgroup analyses revealed lower VTE rates in studies conducted in the Americas than in those from Eurasia (6.5% vs. 19.3%). Studies using antithrombotic prophylaxis, including combined therapy (19.6%) or pharmacological prophylaxis alone (23.2%), also showed lower VTE rates than studies without prophylaxis, although these differences were not statistically significant. Studies employing universal screening (100% screening rate) detected significantly higher VTE rates compared with those without (23.1% vs. 3.9%).

## Conclusions
In conclusion, VTE incidence after cardiac surgery remains high, but reported rates are heavily influenced by screening intensity. The clinical significance of screen‑detected events is uncertain. Future research should distinguish asymptomatic from symptomatic VTE and evaluate the cost‑effectiveness of targeted versus universal screening.

## Introduction
Cardiovascular diseases remain a leading causes of global mortality and disability, and cardiac surgery plays an irreplaceable role in saving lives and improving quality of life [ – ]. With advances in medical technology and surgical techniques, the safety of cardiac surgery has markedly improved. However, postoperative complications, particularly venous thromboembolism (VTE), continue to significantly impact patient outcomes [ ]. VTE, encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is characterized by an insidious onset and potentially devastating consequences. Its occurrence not only increases mortality but may also lead to long-term disability and heightened healthcare costs [ ].
Despite continuous refinement of perioperative anticoagulation strategies, the incidence of VTE following cardiac surgery remains persistently high. Studies have indicated that the risk of VTE after cardiac surgery is considerably higher than after general surgery, with the risk of DVT being approximately three times higher in cardiac surgery patients [ ]. This elevated risk may be attributed to factors such as intraoperative cardiopulmonary bypass, vascular endothelial injury, prolonged postoperative immobilization, and inherent hypercoagulability in many cardiac patients [ , ]. However, reported VTE incidence rates vary widely across studies, ranging from 2% to 15% in some studies [ , ] to over 20% in others [ ]. Such heterogeneity likely reflects differences in study design, sample size, screening methodologies (e.g., routine imaging vs. symptom-driven diagnosis), and regional variations.
Notably, missed or delayed diagnosis of VTE can lead to catastrophic outcomes. PE, the most severe manifestation of VTE, carries a mortality rate between 10% and 30% [ , ]. Larger PEs commonly cause shortness of breath and chest pain. Very large PEs can impair the heart’s ability to pump blood to the lungs, leading to hypotension, fainting, or even death [ ]. Meanwhile, DVT may result in post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), characterized by chronic limb pain, swelling, and even ulceration, severely impairing quality of life [ ]. Therefore, early identification of high-risk patients and implementation of targeted preventive measures are crucial. Current clinical prevention strategies include pharmacological anticoagulation (e.g., low-molecular-weight heparin, direct oral anticoagulants) and mechanical prophylaxis (e.g., intermittent pneumatic compression) [ ]. Nevertheless, discrepancies remain among clinical guidelines regarding the strength of recommendations and the optimal duration of anticoagulation after cardiac surgery [ – ]. Against this background, the present study aims to comprehensively evaluate the incidence and risk factors of VTE following cardiac surgery through a systematic review and meta-analysis.

## Materials and methods

## Data sources and search strategy
This research protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42025648080). A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science databases, following the keywords based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (www.prisma-statement.org). The search strategy utilized various combinations of the following keywords: “coronary artery bypass surgery”, “cardiac surgery”, “heart operate”, “heart transplantation”, “venous thrombosis”, “thromboembolism”, “pulmonary embolism”, “cohort”, “retrospective”, and “case control”. The search period ranged from database inception to February 13, 2025. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1.
The primary outcome was the incidence of VTE (composite of DVT and PE) after cardiac surgery. Secondary outcomes included: (1) incidence of DVT and PE separately; (2) VTE incidence by geographic region (Americas vs. Eurasia); (3) VTE incidence by surgical type (CABG, valve surgery, heart transplantation, complex procedures); (4) VTE incidence by screening strategy (100% imaging screening vs. <100% imaging screening or not reported); and (5) VTE incidence by prophylaxis strategy (pharmacological alone, mechanical alone, combined, or none).

## Study selection
‌Inclusion Criteria‌:
(1) Case series, cohort studies or case-control studies, clinical trials or randomized controlled trials (RCTs).
(2) Study populations undergoing cardiac surgery, including coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgery, valve surgery, or complex procedures (defined as CABG + valve surgery ± additional procedures).
(3) Diagnosis of venous thrombosis based on imaging techniques or International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/ICD-10) codes.
(4) Postoperative venous thrombosis, DVT, and/or PE reported as the primary outcome.
(5) Sufficient data reported to calculate the incidence of venous thrombosis after cardiac surgery.
‌Exclusion Criteria‌:
(1) Letters, editorials, reviews, or meta-analyses.
(2) Animal studies, studies without involving cardiac surgery, or case reports with ⩽10 patients.
(3) Studies involving patients under 18 years of age, studies focusing on pregnancy with cardiac surgery, or studies involving surgeries other than cardiac procedures.
Study screening and inclusion were conducted independently by two reviewers (Zuqing Xu, Wenhui Li) in two stages, including title/abstract analysis followed by full-text assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (Yusong Shen). Screening was performed using EndNote X9. In cases where multiple articles included overlapping patient population (i.e., duplicates), the study with the more comprehensive cohort or the later enrollment period was selected for inclusion.

## Data extraction
A standardized data extraction form was used to collect relevant study information, including author names, publication year, study design, country, sample size, mean age, gender percentage, receipt of thromboprophylaxis, type of VTE, thrombosis screening rate, and identified risk factors for VTE after cardiac surgery. Two authors independently performed data extraction (Rong Chang, Zuqing Xu), and discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

## Quality assessment
The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the risk of bias in eligible observational studies [ ]. NOS is based on selection (4 items), comparability (1 item), and outcome (3 items). The Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool was employed to evaluate the quality of randomized controlled trials (RCT) studies [ ]. This tool is based on seven items: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants/personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. For each item, the risk of bias was rated as low, high, or unclear. Two reviewers (Xiangbo Liu, Xiaoying Yang) independently assessed the risk of bias, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

## Data analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 17.0. For the meta-analysis of proportions, we used the metaprop command with a random-effects model because heterogeneity was anticipated. The Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to stabilize variances and normalize the distribution of proportions. Exact confidence intervals for individual studies were calculated using the cimethod(exact) option, which is based on the binomial distribution and ensures that the confidence limits remain within the [0, 1] range. Pooled estimates and their 95% confidence intervals were then back-transformed to the original proportion scale for presentation. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic; an I2 > 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity, justifying the use of a random-effects model [ ]. The between‑study variance (τ²) was also reported. To evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates, we performed a leave‑one‑out sensitivity analysis by sequentially removing each study and recalculating the pooled incidence using the same random‑effects model. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and further quantified by Egger’s regression test; asymmetry was considered significant when p < 0.10. Given the high heterogeneity anticipated, we calculated 95% prediction intervals for the primary outcome (VTE incidence). In subgroup analyses, a random-effects model was conducted to pool subgroups, and forest plots were generated separately for VTE, DVT, and PE. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05.

## Results

## Search results
We retrieved 9,812 articles from the databases, and an additional 9 articles were identified through journal websites and manual searching, resulting in a total of 9,821 records. After screen and applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 21 studies were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig.  ).
 

## Characteristics of the included studies
The characteristics and outcomes of the 21 included studies (n = 794,662) are summarized in Table [ , , – ]. The studies comprised retrospective cohort studies (n = 12), prospective studies (n = 6) and RCTs (n = 3). CABG (n = 9) and heart transplantation (n = 3) were the predominant surgical procedures evaluated. Among the 18 observational studies assessed using the NOS, six studies were rated as low risk of bias, eleven studies as moderate risk, and one study as high risk. For the three randomized controlled trials evaluated with the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, one trial was judged as low risk of bias, while the remaining two trials showed moderate risk of bias. No RCT was rated as high risk. Detailed domain-level scores for each study are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1.
 

## Incidence of VTE, DVT, and PE after cardiac surgery
All 21 studies (n = 794,662) reported the incidence of VTE after cardiac surgery. The pooled analysis demonstrated an overall VTE incidence of 10.2% (95% CI: 7.6%– 13.1%) (Fig.  ). The τ² value was 0.041, and the 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.0% to 52.7%, indicating substantial uncertainty due to between‑study heterogeneity. To assess the influence of any single study on the pooled VTE incidence, we performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis by sequentially removing each study and recalculating the pooled estimate using the same random-effects model applied in the primary analysis (Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation with exact confidence intervals). The resulting point estimates ranged from 8.3% to 11.3%, all of which remained within the 95% confidence interval of the primary pooled estimate (7.6%–13.1%). These findings indicate that no individual study disproportionately influenced the overall effect and confirm the robustness of the primary result (10.2%; 95% CI, 7.6%–13.1%). The funnel plot showed significant asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 2), and Egger’s test was statistically significant (p = 0.010). Nineteen studies reported the incidence of DVT, with a pooled incidence of 10.6% (95% CI: 7.6%–14.0%) (Fig.  ). Nine studies reported the incidence of PE, with a pooled estimate of 1.6% (95% CI: 0.8%– 2.6%) (Fig.  ).
 

## Subgroup analysis
Subgroup analyses revealed significant geographical disparities in VTE incidence after cardiac surgery (p = 0.049) (Table , Supplementary Fig. 3&4). The pooled VTE rate was 6.5% (95% CI: 4.8%–8.6%) in studies from the Americas (15 studies, n = 621,430) and increased markedly to 19.3% (95% CI: 6.8%–36.3%) in studies from Europe and Asia (6 studies, n = 173,232). Studies with smaller sample sizes (< 500 patients) reported significantly higher VTE rates (19.1%) compared to large-scale studies (≥ 500 patients; 5.1%, p < 0.001) (Table , Supplementary Fig. 5&6). No statistically significant difference in VTE risk was observed across different surgical types (p = 0.783), although heart transplant patients (3 studies, n = 3,147) showed a marginally higher DVT incidence (12.1%; 95% CI: 5.7%–20.3%) compared to CABG patients (10.0%; 95% CI: 5.0%–16.5%) (Table , Supplementary Fig. 7&8). In exploratory subgroup analyses based on prophylaxis type, we observed numerically lower DVT incidence with combination therapy (19.6%; 95% CI: 15.2–24.4%) compared to pharmacological prophylaxis alone (23.2%; 95% CI: 8.7–42.1%), but the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table , Supplementary Fig. 9&10). Notably, studies implementing 100% imaging screening adherence detected VTE at a significantly higher rate (23.1%) compared to studies relying < 100% imaging screening or not reported (3.9%, p < 0.001) (Table , Supplementary Fig. 11&12). In all studies with 100% screening, DVT was diagnosed by routine lower extremity duplex ultrasonography performed between postoperative day 3 and 7 (or at discharge). Only one study [ ] performed routine chest imaging (CT) for PE, yielding a 21% PE detection rate; the remaining studies relied on symptom‑driven PE testing.
 

## Risk factors for venous thrombosis after cardiac surgery
A total of eight studies (n = 56,420) reported independent risk factors for VTE after cardiac surgery using multivariable regression models. However, substantial heterogeneity across studies precluded a meta-analysis of effect sizes. Specifically, the included studies differed in: (1) the definitions of risk factors (for example, obesity was defined as BMI > 30 kg/m² in some studies and BMI > 25 kg/m² in others); (2) the covariates included in the multivariable models; and (3) the follow-up duration used for VTE assessment. Given this heterogeneity, we performed a narrative synthesis of risk factors that were consistently identified as independent predictors across multiple studies. The direction and magnitude of these associations are summarized in Table  . However, pooled odds ratios and forest plots were not generated because of the lack of harmonized effect sizes and model specifications. Multivariate analyses demonstrated that advanced age, a preoperative history of DVT or PE, prolonged postoperative mechanical ventilation (> 3 days), and chronic renal insufficiency were significantly associated with an increased risk of VTE. Additionally, obesity and prolonged bed rest were established as critical risk factors (Table  )‌.
 

## Discussion
VTE, as a major global public health issue, has experienced a substantial increase in disease burden over the past two decades. A large observational study involving 22 million individuals in the United Kingdom demonstrated a continuous rise in VTE incidence from 2000 to 2019, with a 28% increase during 2017–2019 compared to 2000–2002 (IRR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.18–1.39) [ ]. These findings underscore VTE as a critical challenge in comprehensive cardiovascular disease management. Our meta-analysis revealed an overall VTE incidence of 10.2% after cardiac surgery, with DVT accounting for 10.6%, a significantly higher rate than observed in the general population.
Although in this study the pooled incidence of PE after cardiac surgery was relatively low at 1.6%, the potential severity of PE cannot be overlooked. Untreated acute PE carries a mortality rate of up to 30%, while early interventions such as anticoagulation or thrombolysis can reduce this to below 10% [ ]. However, the pooled PE incidence of 1.6% should be interpreted with caution, as it likely underestimates the true burden of PE after cardiac surgery. First, most large-scale studies included in our meta-analysis were retrospective cohort studies based on administrative databases, such as the Nationwide Inpatient Sample and ICD-9/10 coding. In these data sources, PE events that were not coded as discharge diagnoses—for example, incidentally detected subsegmental PEs or PEs in patients who died before diagnostic evaluation—would have been systematically missed. Second, as noted in the ESC guidelines, up to 50% of fatal PEs remain undiagnosed before death [ ]. These fatal events are unlikely to be captured in administrative datasets unless autopsy was performed and the diagnosis coded. Third, the use of symptom‑driven testing in non‑systematically screened studies means that only clinically overt PEs were detected, while asymptomatic or mild PEs were ignored. Taken together, administrative data and non‑systematic screening protocols likely substantially underestimate the true incidence of PE after cardiac surgery. Future studies should employ prospective designs with systematic imaging screening and rigorous ascertainment of both fatal and non‑fatal PE to provide more accurate estimates.
Studies implementing 100% screening protocols (e.g., ultrasound or CT imaging) detected significantly higher VTE rates (23.1% vs. 3.9%) compared to studies using symptom-driven diagnoses. Notably, in non-systematically screened studies, the PE detection rate was as low as 1.0% (95% CI: 0.5%-1.7%), whereas the sole study by Vitor B Viana et al. [ ]), which employed 100% imaging screening, reported a PE detection rate of 21.0%, with all cases achieving favorable outcomes through early anticoagulation. Although this finding may initially suggest a strong effect of screening strategy on VTE detection, it more fundamentally reflects case-ascertainment bias. Universal screening inevitably identifies asymptomatic, subclinical distal deep vein thromboses that would otherwise remain undiagnosed in routine clinical practice. The more than 5-fold difference likely overestimates the true effect of screening, as it conflates diagnostic intensity with the actual incidence of VTE.
Notably, the incidence of VTE after cardiac surgery was significantly higher in Europe and Asia compared to the Americas. Several factors may contribute to this disparity. Screening practices: American studies predominantly relied on symptom-driven screening [ , ], while European and Asian studies (e.g., Alvarez-Alvarez et al. [ ]) adopted systematic postoperative ultrasound protocols, resulting in increased detection of asymptomatic thrombi. Anticoagulation Strategies: North American guidelines recommend early combined prophylaxis (e.g., low-molecular-weight heparin plus intermittent pneumatic compression) [ ], whereas Asian studies (e.g., Ambrosetti et al. [ ]) reported lower utilization rates of anticoagulant (62%), potentially elevating thrombotic risks. Patient heterogeneity: Higher incidence of chronic kidney disease (OR = 2.17) and obesity (OR = 1.67) in European and Asian cohorts, combined with a younger average age in American populations (62 vs. 67 years) [ ], may reflect baseline differences in thrombotic risk profiles. However, the observed geographic disparity should be interpreted with caution because of structural imbalances in study characteristics. Most large-scale studies from the Americas were retrospective registry-based analyses that relied on administrative codes for VTE ascertainment, reflecting non-systematic screening. In contrast, smaller studies from Europe and Asia more often used prospective designs with universal imaging screening. This imbalance alone may account for much of the apparent regional difference.
Despite guideline recommendations favoring combined pharmaco-mechanical prophylaxis [ ], our analysis found no significant difference in VTE rates between different prophylactic strategies (p > 0.05). More importantly, confounding by indication is highly likely. Clinicians tend to prescribe more intensive prophylaxis strategies, such as combined pharmacological and mechanical prophylaxis, to patients at higher baseline risk of VTE, including those of advanced age, those with obesity, those with a history of VTE, or those undergoing more complex surgical procedures. As a result, comparisons between prophylaxis groups are inherently biased, because higher-risk patients are disproportionately represented in the more intensive prophylaxis groups. This bias may attenuate or even reverse the observed treatment effect, which could explain why no significant difference was identified between strategies. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that prophylaxis is ineffective or that different strategies are equivalent. This represents an important limitation of the present study, and randomized controlled trials or well-designed observational studies with propensity score adjustment are needed to provide more reliable comparisons of prophylaxis strategies in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.
Additionally, this study identified multiple risk factors for VTE, including a history of preoperative thrombosis, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and renal insufficiency. Notably, prolonged mechanical ventilation (> 3 days) was strongly associated with VTE risk (OR = 2.89), likely due to long-term immobilization and inflammatory responses. The abnormal position of central venous catheter (OR = 2.50) also emerging as an important modifiable risk factor, highlighting the necessity for standardized catheter operation. Variation in the weighting of risk factors across studies—e.g., Kainuma et al. [ ] emphasizing preoperative history, versus Schwann et al. [ ] focusing on postoperative complications such as atrial fibrillation—reflects differences in study population and surgical complexity, necessitating individualized VTE prevention strategies.
Another noteworthy finding of this meta-analysis is the significantly higher pooled incidence of VTE reported in studies with smaller sample sizes (< 500 patients) than in larger cohort studies (19.1% vs. 5.1%). Several factors may account for this disparity, and its clinical implications warrant discussion. First, smaller studies, particularly prospective single-center trials, often use mandatory systematic screening protocols, such as routine postoperative Doppler ultrasonography for all participants, which increases the detection of asymptomatic or subclinical deep vein thrombosis that would typically be missed in routine clinical practice. In contrast, larger retrospective studies mainly rely on administrative databases or ICD codes and therefore tend to capture only symptomatic or severe events requiring medical attention [ , ]. Second, publication bias may also contribute, as smaller studies reporting unexpectedly high or novel event rates are more likely to be submitted and accepted for publication than those reporting negative findings or lower event rates.
The limitations of this study include: (1) Significant heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I² > 90%), likely due to differences in screening methods, types of surgery, and geographical variations; (2) Incomplete reporting of anticoagulation protocols in some studies limited a more detailed analysis of prevention strategies, a limitation also noted in other systematic reviews of cardiac surgery interventions [ ]; (3) The majority of included studies were retrospective, carrying an inherent risk of selection bias; (4) In most included studies, we were unable to distinguish between symptomatic and asymptomatic VTE events, nor could we differentiate proximal from distal DVT. This limitation reduces our ability to assess the clinical relevance of screen-detected thrombi and may have introduced detection bias.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, this meta-analysis provides robust evidence regarding the incidence and risk factors of VTE post-cardiac surgery. Variations in screening rates substantially influence reported VTE incidence. However, the clinical significance of screen-detected events remains uncertain. Future studies should distinguish asymptomatic from symptomatic VTE and evaluate the cost-effectiveness of targeted versus universal screening strategies.
