## ABSTRACT

## Context
It is well recognised there is a high prevalence of pituitary dysfunction in childhood brain tumours survivors who are treated with radiotherapy. In contrast, the potential for survivors of brain tumours arising in adulthood to develop radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism may be underestimated. The aim of this systematic review and meta‐analysis is to determine the pooled prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism in survivors of primary non‐pituitary brain tumours arising in adulthood.

## Design
A systematic literature search of five databases was performed. Two reviewers independently screened potential articles and then extracted data from accepted studies. A random effects meta‐analysis was performed to assess the pooled prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism for each hormone axis. Forest plots were created to graphically represent these estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Between study heterogeneity was quantified using the I
2 statistic.

## Results
Fourteen studies comprising a total of 580 participants were included in the review. The pooled prevalence of hypopituitarism was 48% (95% CI 36.7−59.4; I
2 = 81%) with significant heterogeneity between study results. The pooled prevalence of growth hormone (GH) deficiency was 35.2% (95% CI 24.2−47.0; I
2 = 81.4%), gonadotropin deficiency 20.9% (95% CI 14.4−28.1, I
2 = 63.1%), adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH) deficiency 16.0% (95% CI 11.4−21.1; I
2 = 42.6%) and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) deficiency 18.2% (95% CI 12.3−24.8; I
2 = 63.5%). Heterogeneity among study results was further evaluated through a meta‐regression.

## Conclusion
Radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism is common, affecting almost half of survivors with a primary non‐pituitary brain tumour arising in adulthood. Protocolised endocrine surveillance is essential in this cohort to identify and treat pituitary hormone deficits in a timely manner. These patients report decreased quality of life which may be due, in part, to undiagnosed pituitary dysfunction.

## Introduction
As survival from brain tumours arising in adulthood continues to improve, increasing numbers of patients will experience radiotherapy late effects [ , ]. Radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism is one of the most common late effects in brain tumour survivors. Hypopituitarism is characterised by a progressive and irreversible loss of one or more anterior pituitary hormones in the years following treatment [ ]. It is hypothesised that radiation induces cell damage and vasculopathy resulting in hypopituitarism, however the exact pathophysiology has not been determined. The development of early onset hypopituitarism, in particular growth hormone (GH) deficiency, has been attributed to hypothalamic damage, while pituitary atrophy due to absent hypothalamic stimulation is thought to be a contributing factor to later onset hypopituitarism [ , ]. Early direct pituitary damage can also occur at radiation doses exceeding 60 Gray (Gy) [ ]. Radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism can exacerbate symptoms commonly experienced by brain tumour survivors including lethargy, sexual dysfunction and impaired wellbeing [ ]. It also confers additional morbidity including subfertility, metabolic dysfunction and cerebrovascular disease [ , ]. Patients with hypopituitarism have an increased mortality risk [ , ]. A meta‐analysis comprising over 23,000 patients with hypopituitarism reported a standardised mortality ratio (SMR) of 1.55, with a higher rate in women (SMR 2.29) compared to men (SMR 1.50) [ ].
Considerable research has been undertaken in childhood brain tumour survivors relative to adults. This effort reflects the heightened radiosensitivity of the hypothalamic‐pituitary axis (HPA), the pressing need to optimise linear growth by assessing and treating GH deficiency (GHD) and improved survival in childhood brain tumour survivors compared to adults [ ]. This research has facilitated the development of evidence‐based guidelines, providing recommendations on radiation dose thresholds necessitating endocrine surveillance and the frequency and duration of testing required in childhood cancer survivors [ , , ]. In contrast, there are no equivalent guidelines for survivors of brain tumours arising in adulthood [ ]. Delayed or missed diagnoses of hypopituitarism and its associated complications may result from inadequate endocrine surveillance [ , ]. A 2011 systematic review reported the pooled prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism in adults with either a brain tumour or nasopharyngeal cancer was 66% [ ]. One weakness of this review was its generalisability to patients currently receiving treatment with more modern radiotherapy techniques. Another weakness was the combined analysis of patients with brain tumours and nasopharyngeal cancer. A separate analysis is warranted given these diseases' distinct anatomical locations, radiation doses and treatment fields. Numerous studies have been published in recent years, enabling a more targeted analysis without nasopharyngeal cancer survivors. The primary aim of the current systematic review is to assess the pooled prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism in survivors of primary non‐pituitary brain tumours arising in adulthood.

2.

## Methods
A systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [ ]. The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on the 27 August 2023 and published online (CRD 42023451385).
2.1.

## Primary and Secondary Outcomes
The primary outcome of the review was to determine the pooled prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism in survivors of primary non‐pituitary brain tumours arising in adulthood.
Secondary outcomes included identification of (i) risk factors associated with the development of hypopituitarism and (ii) radiotherapy dose thresholds below which hypopituitarism did not occur.

2.2.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
The review included original, peer‐reviewed studies that reported the prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism in survivors (aged > 16 years at the time of treatment) of primary non‐pituitary brain tumours arising in adulthood. Eligible studies included randomised controlled trials, observational studies, patient registry data and case series (minimum of 10 participants). Conversely, case reports, conference abstracts, trial protocols, narrative and systematic reviews were excluded. There was no restriction in terms of publication date, however only English language articles were considered for inclusion. In studies with serial assessments of pituitary function, data from the middle time point was included in the analysis. If articles reported findings from overlapping patient cohorts, the article with the largest number of patients was chosen.

2.3.

## Search Strategy
A search strategy was created in consultation with an endocrinologist and information specialist. Five databases including PubMed, EMBASE, CINHAL, SCOPUS and CENTRAL were systematically searched on the 13 June 2023. The following terms were searched; ‘radiotherapy’ OR ‘radiation’ OR ‘irradiation’ AND ‘hypopituitarism’ OR ‘pituitary dysfunction’ OR ‘pituitary hormone dysfunction’ OR ‘endocrine dysfunction’ OR ‘pituitary hormone deficit’ OR ‘pituitary insufficiency’ OR ‘hormonal deficit’ OR ‘hypothalamic dysfunction’ OR ‘pituitary deficiency’ AND ‘brain tumour’ OR ‘intracranial tumour’ OR ‘cranial tumour’ OR ‘intracerebral tumour’. The full search strategy is available in Table  .

2.4.

## Data Management
Studies identified in the systematic literature search were exported into Endnote X9 where duplicate were articles removed. Two reviewers (D.M.D. and M.T.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of retrieved studies according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This process was then followed by full text screening (D.M.D. and M.T.) of potential articles to determine their suitability for inclusion in the review. Discordant opinions were resolved through discussion, the senior author adjudicated when a consensus could not be reached. The reference lists of eligible studies were also screened to identify potential studies. The rationale for excluding studies during the full text review was documented in the PRISMA diagram (Figure  ).
 Data were extracted from accepted studies by two reviewers (D.M.D. and M.T.) using a piloted data extraction form. Discrepancies in the extracted data were addressed by reviewing the original article. The following data were extracted from each study:

1.Study: first author, year of publication, study design, study location(s), recruitment source.
2.Population: number of participants, age, gender, tumour histology, HPA infiltration, time interval following radiotherapy to endocrine assessment, proportion of patients treated with surgery and chemotherapy. The radiotherapy dose (in Gy) and the target area being measured (e.g., dose to tumour, HPA) was be recorded.
3.Outcome: endocrine surveillance regimen, prevalence of hypopituitarism and individual pituitary hormone deficits, diagnostic cut‐offs, risk factors associated with the development of hypopituitarism and radiotherapy dose thresholds. Hypopituitarism was defined as the development of any anterior pituitary hormone deficit and panhypopituitarism defined as the development of GH, gonadotropin, ACTH and thyroid deficiency.

2.5.

## Risk of Bias Assessment
The methodological quality of studies was appraised using the Agency for Research and Health Quality (AHRQ) methodology checklist for cross‐sectional and prevalence studies [ ]. The AHRQ checklist consists of 11 questions, with ‘yes’, ‘no’ or ‘unclear’ responses. Studies were classified as having good, moderate or poor methodological quality based on scores of 8−11, 4−7 and 0−3, respectively [ ]. Two reviewers (D.M.D. and M.T.) conducted each assessment independently and discussed any discrepancies until a consensus was reached.

2.6.

## Data Synthesis
A narrative synthesis of the data was initially undertaken based on the pre‐determined themes of study selection, study characteristics, prevalence of hypopituitarism, risk factors and dose thresholds for developing hypopituitarism. A meta‐analysis was then performed to determine the pooled prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism for each hormone axis [ ]. A random effects model with a Freeman‐Turkey double arcsine transformation was chosen given the anticipated heterogeneity across studies. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I
2 statistic, a cut‐off of 50% used to define significant heterogeneity [ ]. Results were presented in forest plots with 95% confidence intervals (CI), calculated using the exact method. A univariable and multivariable meta‐regression analysis was undertaken to identify sources of heterogeneity [ ]. Year of study publication, time interval following radiotherapy and dose of radiotherapy were selected as predetermined variables for inclusion in the meta‐regression. A minimum of 10 data points were required for each variable to be included in the meta‐regression. Recruitment source (selected vs. unselected cohorts) was considered as an alternative variable as there was insufficient data in a usable format to include dose of radiotherapy [ ]. Selected cohorts comprised patients with symptoms of hypopituitarism and those referred to endocrinology services, whereas, unselected cohorts included patients recruited from cancer registries or consecutive patients from neurosurgical and radiation oncology clinics. Despite some studies being categorised as comprising unselected cohorts, the authors acknowledge the unavoidable presence of selection bias, as participants must still fulfil individual study inclusion and exclusion criteria. A p < 0.05 was used to determine significant heterogeneity that influenced the prevalence of hypopituitarism. All analyses were performed with Stata V18 (Texas, United States), using the metaprop command [ ].

## Results
3.1.

## Study Selection
The systematic literature search identified 2852 articles. After removing duplicates, 1767 underwent title and abstract screening. Fifty articles proceeded to full text screening, of which 14 were included in the review. The reasons for excluding studies based on their full text screening were documented in the PRISMA diagram (Figure  ).

3.2.

## Characteristics of Included Studies
The 14 accepted studies comprised a total of 580 participants who underwent radiotherapy for a primary non‐pituitary brain tumour arising in adulthood. Characteristics of these studies are outlined in Table  . Patients were recruited from a wide range of sources. The histological diagnosis was reported in all patients in 11 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , ] and in another study was only reported in those with hypopituitarism [ ]. Two studies did not provide a histological diagnosis [ , ]. Meningiomas were the most common tumour, accounting for 47.7% of reported cases, followed by gliomas (44.9%, n = 220), medulloblastomas (2.8%, n = 13), pinealomas (2.4%, n = 11) and ‘other brain tumours’ (2.2%, n = 10). Two‐ or three‐dimensional radiotherapy (2D‐RT, 3D‐RT) was used in 9 studies [ , , , , , , , , ], intensity modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) in 1 study, mixed radiotherapy modalities in three studies [ , , ], while radiotherapy modality was not reported in another study [ ]. In total, 64% (n = 371) of patients received 2D/3D‐RT, 14% (n = 97) received IMRT, 10% (n = 59) received proton beam therapy (PBT), less than 1% (n = 3) received stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) while the remaining 11% (n = 50) were classified as ‘other’ or not reported. There was significant variation in studies' reporting of radiation doses. Five studies reported the radiation dose to the tumour only [ , , , , ], three studies reported the dose to the HPA [ , , ], two studies reported the radiation dose to the tumour and pituitary gland [ , ], two studies reported the dose to the tumour and the HPA [ , ] one study reported doses to the hypothalamus and pituitary gland separately [ ] and one study did not report details of radiation doses [ ]. In total, the median dose of radiotherapy delivered to the tumour and pituitary gland ranged from 43.0 to 62.3 Gy and 36.1 to 55.8 Gy, respectively [ , , , ]. The median time interval between completing radiotherapy and undergoing endocrine assessment ranged from 6 months to 13 years [ , ]. Ten studies recruited unselected cohorts, primarily from radiotherapy treatment registries [ , , , , , , , , , ], while four studies recruited selected cohorts, typically patients who had symptoms of hypopituitarism or were attending endocrinology clinics [ , , , ]. Pituitary hormone assessments differed markedly between studies. Dynamic testing was performed in all participants in nine studies [ , , , , , , , , ], a proportion of patients underwent dynamic testing in four studies [ , , , ] and baseline samples were measured in one study [ ].
 3.2.1.

## Primary Outcome
3.2.1.1.

## Hypopituitarism
The number of patients assessed and prevalence of hypopituitarism in each study is outlined in Table  . Hypopituitarism (any hormone deficit) was reported in 11 studies and ranged from 33% to 70% [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. The pooled prevalence of any degree of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism in survivors of primary non‐pituitary brain tumours arising in adulthood was 48.0% (95% CI 36.7−59.4; I
2 = 81%) (Figure  ). There was significant heterogeneity between studies which was further examined in a meta‐regression. The univariable meta‐regression identified a significant association between year of study publication, recruitment source and time interval following radiotherapy with the prevalence of hypopituitarism (Table  ). Studies that were published earlier, had longer follow up intervals and recruited selected cohorts had a higher prevalence of hypopituitarism. In the multivariable meta‐regression, longer time interval and recruitment of selected cohorts were independently associated with a higher prevalence of hypopituitarism.
 
3.2.1.2.

## Growth Hormone Deficiency
The GH axis was assessed in 11 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , ], with dynamic testing performed in 10. There was significant variation between studies in the tests utilised to assess GH status. The insulin tolerance test (ITT) was undertaken in two studies [ , ], the GH releasing hormone and arginine test (GHRH + AST) [ , , ] undertaken in three studies, the glucagon stimulation test (GST) [ ] in 1 and combination of these tests were performed in the remaining four studies [ , , , ]. One study used an IGF‐1 level below the normal reference range to diagnose GHD [ ]. GH cut‐offs ranging from 3 to 5 mcg/L were used to define GHD with the ITT, 2.5−3 mcg/L with the GST, while BMI specific cut‐offs were used in the GHRH + AST test. One study reported GH cuts‐offs for partial and severe GHD with the GST and ITT [ ] of 7 and 3 mcg/L, respectively [ ]. Data for severe GHD were included in the meta‐analysis as this cut‐off has greater clinical significance [ ]. GHD was the most common pituitary hormone deficiency in 8 of 11 studies [ , , , , , , , ]. The pooled prevalence of GHD was 35.2% (95% CI 24.2−47.0; I
2 = 81.4%) (Figure  ) with significant heterogeneity between study results. Studies that were published earlier and had longer time intervals following radiotherapy were associated with a higher prevalence of GHD in both the univariable and multivariable meta‐regression (Table  ).

3.2.1.3.

## Gonadotropin Deficiency
Twelve studies assessed the prevalence of gonadotropin deficiency, nine measured baseline gonadotropin and sex hormones levels [ , , , , , , , , ] while three performed either a gonadotropin releasing hormone [ , ] or clomiphene simulation test [ ]. Ten studies reported their criteria for diagnosing gonadotropin deficiency in pre‐ and post‐menopausal women [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. The overall pooled prevalence of gonadotropin deficiency was 20.9% (95% CI 14.4−28.1; I
2 = 63.1%, Figure  ). Six studies [ , , , , , ] stratified their results by sex and three by menopausal status [ , , ]. No study reported a significant difference in the prevalence of gonadotropin deficiency based on either sex or menopausal status. There was significant heterogeneity in the overall prevalence of gonadotropin deficiency between studies. Earlier published studies were associated with a higher prevalence in the univariable but not multivariable regression after adjusting for time interval following radiotherapy and recruitment source (Table  ). There was no association between recruitment source or time interval following radiotherapy and the prevalence gonadotropin deficiency. The prevalence of primary hypogonadism was reported separately in three studies, ranging from 7% to 13.6% [ , , ] but was not pooled due to the small number of studies.

3.2.1.4.

## ACTH Deficiency
The adrenal axis was assessed in 12 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , ]. A range of dynamic tests including the ITT, GST, short synacthen test (SST) and corticotrophin releasing hormone (CRH) test were performed. An early morning cortisol and a 24 h urinary free cortisol (UFC) where each used in one study to diagnose adrenal insufficiency [ , ]. The pooled prevalence of adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH) deficiency was 16.0% (95% CI 11.4−21.1; I
2 = 42.6%), without significant heterogeneity between studies (Figure  ). Time interval following radiotherapy was associated with the prevalence of ACTH deficiency in both the uni‐ and multi‐variable meta‐regression after adjusting for year of publication and recruitment source (Table  ).

3.2.1.5.

## TSH Deficiency
The thyroid axis was evaluated in all 14 studies. Baseline measurements of TSH, free thyroxine (T4) and/or free triiodothyronine (T3) were measured in 11 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , ], a thyrotropin releasing hormone (TRH) test was performed in two studies [ , ] and a TRH and T3 resin uptake test were performed in another study [ ]. The pooled prevalence of TSH deficiency was 18.2% (95% CI 12.3−24.8; I
2 = 63.5%) with significant heterogeneity between results (Figure  ). The prevalence of TSH deficiency was associated with recruitment source in the uni‐ and multi‐variable meta‐regression, with a higher prevalence in unselected cohorts. The prevalence of primary hypothyroidism was reported in seven studies, ranging from 0% to 19% [ , , ].

3.2.1.6.

## Panhypopituitarism
Panhypopituitarism was assessed in seven studies [ , , , , , , , , ] and had a pooled prevalence of 6.4% (95% CI 3.5−9.9; I
2 13.2%, p = 0.33), without significant heterogeneity between studies (Figure  ). A meta‐regression was not performed given the limited number of studies in this analysis.

3.2.1.7.

## Hyperprolactinaemia
The presence of hyperprolactinaemia was reported in 11 studies and had a pooled‐prevalence of 20.4% (95% CI 9.6−33.5; I
2 = 88.7%, p = < 0.01). Significant heterogeneity between study results was again demonstrated.

3.2.2.

## Secondary Outcomes
3.2.2.1.

## Risk Factors for the Development of Radiotherapy‐Induced Hypopituitarism
Radiation dose to the pituitary and time interval following radiotherapy were reported as independent predictors of hypopituitarism in two studies [ , ]. In another study of patients with skull‐based meningiomas, the risk of hypopituitarism was increased nearly threefold with tumours exceeding four centimeters and 3.6‐fold when pituitary radiation doses exceeded 50 Gy [ ]. Finally, the planning target volume (PTV) was an independent risk factor for hypopituitarism in a separate study of patients with skull‐based meningiomas [ ]. Sex and age at radiotherapy (in adults) were not identified as significant risk factors for predicting hypopituitarism.

3.2.2.2.

## Dose Thresholds for the Development of Radiotherapy‐Induced Hypopituitarism
No studies in the review reported an absolute radiotherapy dose threshold below which hypopituitarism did not occur. Although a clear threshold was not identified, Partoune et al. reported the risk of hypopituitarism was low in patients who received a pituitary radiation dose of less than 45 Gy [ ]. In a subgroup of 20 patients, the prevalence of GH and TSH deficiency was 5% and 7% respectively with no cases of ACTH or gonadotropin deficiency, after a median follow up period of 7.5 years [ ].

3.2.2.3.

## Risk of Bias Assessment
Ten studies were appraised to be of high methodological quality [ , , , , , , , , , ] while four studies were deemed to be of moderate quality [ , , , ]. Recruitment of consecutive patients was a key distinguishing feature between high and moderate quality studies. Studies of moderate quality were also more likely to report patient response rates, the proportion of incomplete patient data and the time period in which patients were recruited. Detailed descriptions of the critical appraisals for each study are available in the Table  .

## Discussion
This systematic review demonstrates a high prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism, affecting almost half of brain tumour survivors who received various modalities of radiotherapy. The likelihood of developing hypopituitarism was independently associated with recruitment of selected cohorts (i.e., patients in endocrinology clinics) and longer time intervals following radiotherapy, as hormone deficits progressively emerged in the years following treatment. The prevalence of individual pituitary hormone deficiencies varied markedly. GH was the most commonly affected pituitary hormone axis, 35.2% of survivors were diagnosed with GHD. By comparison, the prevalence of gonadotropin, ACTH and TSH deficiency were 20.9%, 15.6% and 18.2%, respectively. These findings underscore the heightened radiosensitivity of the somatotroph axis relative to other hormone axes. The review identified an unexpectedly high prevalence of TSH deficiency as the thyroid axis is usually considered to be the most radioresistant [ ]. One potential explanation for this may be the inclusion of studies utilising the dynamic TRH test which is rarely used in clinical practice to diagnose secondary hypothyroidism. This test can detect subtle pituitary dysfunction in euthyroid patients (i.e., normal circulating free T4 levels). This increases the prevalence of TSH deficiency, however, is of uncertain clinical significance [ , , ].
The previous systematic review by Appelman‐Dijkstra et al. reported higher rates of hypopituitarism, GH, gonadotropin, ACTH and TSH deficiency at 66%, 45%, 30%, 22% and 25%, respectively, compared to this review [ ]. There are several potential explanations for this higher prevalence. Firstly, 75% of participants in the previous review had a nasopharyngeal carcinoma while only 25% had a brain tumour [ ]. Nasopharyngeal carcinomas are located in close proximity to the pituitary gland and are typically treated with higher doses of radiotherapy (60−70 Gy) compared to most brain tumours (50.4−60 Gy). The prevalence of hypopituitarism declined from 66% to 54% after excluding patients with a nasopharyngeal carcinoma, although this was not statistically significant [ ]. Secondly, two studies in the previous review included some participants who were treated with radiotherapy in childhood. Their inclusion may have increased the prevalence of hypopituitarism as the HPA, and particularly the GH axis, is more radiosensitive in childhood and adolescence [ ]. Finally, studies utilising newer modalities of radiotherapy including IMRT, SRS and PBT may have contributed to the lower rates of hypopituitarism reported in this review. These techniques allow improved tumour targeting of radiotherapy. For instance, IMRT utilises multiple radiation beams of varying intensity to create steep dosimetric gradients around the tumour [ ], while PBT utilises the Bragg peak phenomenon to deliver highly concentrated radiation to the tumour while minimising the entrance dose and effectively eliminating the exit dose [ , ]. These newer modalities reduce radiation exposure to surrounding tissues such as the HPA, potentially reducing the risk of developing hypopituitarism [ ]. Studies comparing radiotherapy modalities have started to emerge. In one study comprising 118 childhood medulloblastoma survivors, children treated with PBT had a reduced risk of gonadotropin deficiency, hypothyroidism and were less likely to require endocrine replacement therapy compared to age, gender and radiotherapy dose matched children treated with photon‐based therapy [ ]. Further studies with longer follow periods are required to investigate this further.
Two studies in this review demonstrated the pituitary radiation dose was an independent predictor of hypopituitarism [ , ]. The extent and speed of onset of hypopituitarism are also dose dependant [ ]. Isolated GHD commonly occurs in adults treated with low‐moderate doses of radiotherapy (<40 Gy) [ , ]. In contrast, deficiencies of the gonadal, adrenal and thyroid axes are uncommon when pituitary radiation doses are less than 45 Gy. Partoune et al. reported TSH deficiency occurred in only 7% of patients, with no cases of ACTH or gonadal deficiency, 7.5 years after receiving a pituitary radiation dose of less 45 Gy [ ]. In contrast, intensive radiotherapy schedules (>60 Gy) which are largely reserved for skull base and nasopharyngeal tumours, frequently result in the rapid onset of multiple hormone deficiencies [ , , ]. No studies included in this review reported absolute dose thresholds for developing pituitary hormone deficiencies. However, a study by Kyriakakis et al. (ineligible for inclusion in this systematic review and meta‐analysis as it was a subgroup analysis of an accepted study) reported that GH, gonadotropin, ACTH and TSH deficiency did not occur below hypothalamic‐pituitary doses of 10, 30, 32 and 40.8 Gy, respectively, after 8 years of follow up [ ]. Modern radiotherapy techniques allow calculation of the both pituitary and hypothalamic doses separately and combined. Future studies should report both the hypothalamic and pituitary radiation doses to determine which is most important for developing early and late onset hypopituitarism and to define radiation dose thresholds above which hypopituitarism occurs. Establishing dose thresholds would facilitate targeted endocrine surveillance in those at risk of developing hypopituitarism whilst avoiding unnecessary testing in others.
This review highlights that survivors of brain tumour arising in adulthood require systematic endocrine surveillance in dedicated endocrine late effect clinics [ ]. Endocrine surveillance should be individualised taking into account patient's risk factors for hypopituitarism including the pituitary radiation dose, time interval following radiotherapy and whether they may be candidates for GH replacement. A multidisciplinary discussion involving oncology and radiation oncology is essential to determine the safety and appropriateness of GH therapy. Patients who are not candidates for GH replacement due to the presence of recurrent, progressive or high‐grade disease or simply patient preference, should be screened with a baseline pituitary profile and synacthen test. Alternatively, a morning cortisol exceeding 300 nmol/L is sufficient to exclude adrenal insufficiency [ ]. Dynamic testing with either an insulin tolerance or GSTs should be reserved for GH replacement candidates as these tests are time consuming, labour intensive and may expose to patients to either symptomatic hypoglycaemia or the risk of nausea and vomiting [ ]. The macimorelin test may be a better tolerated alternative, however, it has not yet been validated in adult brain tumour survivors. Furthermore, the ITT is contraindicated in a substantial proportion of brain tumour survivors who have a seizure disorder. IGF‐1 levels lack sensitivity to diagnose GHD in brain tumour survivors [ , ]. GH responses to the ITT (which assesses the entire HPA axis) decline markedly in the first 5 years following radiotherapy, whereas GH responses to GHRH + arginine are largely stable within the first 5 years after radiotherapy and decline thereafter. This provides further evidence of the early hypothalamic insult followed by pituitary atrophy due to absent hypothalamic stimulation [ ]. There is currently no consensus for how long survivors of adult‐onset brain tumours should be followed up, however, expert consensus guidelines recommend at least 15 years surveillance in childhood cancer survivors [ ]. It seems reasonable to adopt a similar approach for adults given the paucity of evidence in this cohort. Pituitary function should be assessed annually. Kyriakakis et al. reported a low likelihood of developing hypopituitarism after 10−15 years. This raises the possibility that the frequency of screening can be reduced over time, however, further studies are required to assess this further.
This is the first systematic review to report the prevalence of radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism solely in survivors of brain tumours arising in adulthood. Half of the studies in this review were published within the past 12 years. In contrast to the review by Appelman‐Dijkstra et al. most studies in this review undertook dynamic assessments of the GH and ACTH axes using validated tests in all study participants, which is essential to accurately assess the prevalence of hypopituitarism. However, a small proportion patients underwent dynamic assessments that are no longer used in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, 10 of the 14 studies were judged to be of high methodological quality. The primary limitation of this review was the significant heterogeneity between studies which was further evaluated in a meta‐regression. Time interval following radiotherapy and selected cohorts were independently associated with a higher prevalence of hypopituitarism in the multivariable meta‐regression. Although a majority of studies had follow up intervals exceeding 5 years, the inclusion of studies with shorter time intervals may underestimate the long‐term risk of hypopituitarism in brain tumour survivors. Included studies also have differing selection biases. Studies with selected cohorts may have included patients with symptoms of hypopituitarism while unselected cohorts were subject to testing bias (i.e., only those whose HPA was included in the radiation treatment field were tested rather than all brain tumour survivors). It was not possible to include radiotherapy dose as a variable in our meta‐regression due to insufficient usable data. It is highly likely that radiotherapy dose accounted for some of the heterogeneity in this review. Other potential sources of heterogeneity such as variations in patient characteristics, endocrine testing regimens, assays and diagnostic cut‐offs between studies were not included in the meta‐regression. For example, adolescence and young adults may have different underlying histological diagnoses, treatment regimens and have an inherent higher risk of developing hypopituitarism that older adults, however, all were combined in this meta‐analysis. Finally, a majority of the studies in the review utilised older radiation modalities such as two and three‐dimensional radiotherapy which are no longer used in clinical practice. Only one study used IMRT exclusively and three studies utilised mixed modalities including IMRT). Therefore, our results may overestimate the risk of hypopituitarism in patients treated with newer radiotherapy modalities including IMRT and PBT. Further studies of adequate size and duration are required to evaluate the risk of hypopituitarism with these modalities.
4.1.

## Conclusion
Radiotherapy‐induced hypopituitarism is prevalent, affecting almost half of survivors with primary non‐pituitary brain tumours arising in adulthood. The development of hypopituitarism was independently associated with the time interval following radiotherapy and recruitment of selected cohorts. Ongoing systematic endocrine surveillance is essential given the high prevalence of this condition and the morbidity associated with untreated hypopituitarism. Further studies are required as a priority to determine the long‐term risks associated with modern radiotherapy modalities and the optimal approach to endocrine surveillance.
