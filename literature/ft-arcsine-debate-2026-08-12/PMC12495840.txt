## Background
Ghana has consistently reported a high perinatal mortality rate. This has raised concerns regarding the country’s ability to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals 3 target 2 by 2030. This comprehensive review presents the pooled prevalence and predictors of perinatal mortalities in Ghana guided by the Anderson’s framework of healthcare utilization.

## Methods
A comprehensive literature search was conducted mainly from four electronic databases; PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and CINAHL as they collectively index > 90% of relevant maternal health journals in Africa and also eases the burden of screening. Eligible studies, published from 1st January 2010 to 30th June, 2024, were charted and synthesized, with focus on the three primary domains of Anderson’s framework: pre-disposing factors, enabling factors and need factors. Meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of perinatal mortality rate within each of the sub-regions. Significant heterogeneity was detected among the various surveys (I2 > 50%), hence a random effect model was reported. Sub-group and meta-regression were performed to identify the sources of heterogeneity observed in the study.

## Results
A total of 2,184 articles were initially identified for review, but after multiple rounds of screening and deduplication, only 30 full-text articles were finally included. The most frequent study design was cross-sectional, accounting for 23.3% of the studies. The Pooled estimate of perinatal mortality is 44.8 (95%CI: 15.4–74.2) per 1000 births in Ghana, with very high heterogeneity (I2 > 99.97, p < 0.0001) among the studies. The predictors of perinatal mortality included advanced maternal age, pre-existing health conditions, poor quality ANC, access to healthcare facilities and environmental exposures.

## Conclusion
This review presents the complex interplay of factors determining perinatal mortality in Ghana. In addition to the predictors identified, the review reveals gaps in literature particularly the lack of case-control studies in rural settings and inadequate focus on healthcare quality, socio-economic influences, and policy impacts. Future studies should address these gaps using a holistic approach that takes into account Ghana’s diverse social and geographical factors to better reduce perinatal mortality.

The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024564968).

## Background
Perinatal mortality, which includes both stillbirths and deaths in the first week of life, serves as a critical measure of the effectiveness of maternal and newborn care, as well as the broader healthcare infrastructure [ , ]. Stillbirth refers to fetal death occurring at or beyond 28 weeks of gestation but before delivery, while early neonatal mortality encompasses infant deaths within the initial seven days after birth [ , ].
Perinatal mortality holds profound implications beyond healthcare, posing a major developmental hurdle particularly in low- and middle-income regions. Global data from 2019 reveals a staggering 4.3 million perinatal deaths, equating to one life lost every seven seconds [ ]. Early neonatal deaths constitute approximately 40–60% of this sum, with the majority of these cases reported in less-developed nations [ ]. The burden of perinatal mortality is highest in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), with a rate of 34.7 deaths per 1000 live births [ ]. Perinatal mortality rate in SSA is far more than those in developed country [ , , ]. Ghana, like many countries in SSA, faces significant challenges with perinatal mortality. This issue reflects broader persistent regional trends since 2013, when Ghana was one of 26 nations accounting for 80% of global under-five deaths [ ].
According to the latest Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS 2022), Ghana’s neonatal mortality rate stands at 17 deaths per 1,000 live births, while infant mortality is reported at 28 deaths per 1,000 live births [ ]. Additionally, the under-5 mortality rate remains elevated at 40 deaths per 1,000 children, reflecting persistent challenges in child survival and healthcare access [ ]. Mortality during the first month (neonatal mortality) accounts for 61% of infant deaths and 43% of under-5 deaths [ ].
The Government of Ghana has implemented numerous interventions over the years to address the persistent challenge of perinatal mortality. A key example is the introduction of the free maternal healthcare policy in 2008 under the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS). This policy was specifically designed to eliminate financial obstacles preventing women from accessing essential maternal healthcare services like antenatal care, safe delivery services, and postnatal care with the ultimate goal of reducing preventable maternal and perinatal deaths [ , ]. Additionally, Ghana introduced the Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) initiative in 1999 as a targeted strategy to improve healthcare delivery, particularly in underserved rural communities [ – ]. The CHPS program was specifically created to fill essential voids in the availability and accessibility of healthcare that disproportionately contribute to adverse maternal health outcomes such as perinatal mortality and maternal deaths [ – ]. By decentralizing primary healthcare services and bringing them closer to remote populations, the CHPS initiative aims to reduce barriers to essential maternal and child health services that are vital for improving survival outcomes. Several capacity building programs for healthcare workers, especially midwives and maternal health initiatives, like safe motherhood programs have also been implemented as a remedy to reducing poor maternal health outcome in the country [ , ].
While Ghana has made progress in reducing perinatal mortality, significant concerns still remain. The country faces challenges in meeting Sustainable Development Goal 3.2 which aims to end preventable newborn and under-5 deaths [ ]. Specifically, it targets a neonatal mortality rate below 12 per 1,000 live births and an under-5 mortality rate below 25 per 1,000 live births by 2030. Achieving these targets requires accelerated efforts [ ].
Previous studies conducted in Ghana reported evidence of the factors influencing perinatal mortality [ – ]. However, these studies were largely confined to specific institutions or communities and did not comprehensively examine the multi-dimensional predictors of perinatal mortality at the national level. Moreover, no study to date has provided a pooled prevalence estimate for Ghana. It is therefore critical to have a study that takes into consideration the vast array of predictors of perinatal mortality. This gap calls for a broader study analyzing diverse predictors. Our systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) address this need. It identifies key predictors and calculates pooled prevalence of perinatal mortality in Ghana. Using Anderson’s healthcare utilization framework, this SRMA explores the complex factors influencing perinatal outcomes [ ].

## Review questions
We formulated research questions using the Population Exposure/Indicator Comparison and Outcome (PICO) framework, appropriate for our inclusion of experimental and observational studies. The questions examine:

Pregnant women in Ghana (Population).

Infants exposed to perinatal mortality risk factors (Indicator).
Compared to those unexposed (Comparison).
On perinatal mortality rates (Outcome).

2.Perinatal population in Ghana (Population).

Inter-regional healthcare variations (Indicator).
Compared across subnational settings (Comparison).
On pooled mortality prevalence (Outcome).

The framework focuses on identifying predictors and factors that contribute to perinatal mortality, targeting the perinatal population in Ghana. The primary outcome of interest is perinatal mortality, which encompasses stillbirths and early neonatal deaths. This approach aims to analyze and address the key determinants influencing perinatal survival in the region, providing insights for targeted interventions and policy improvements [ ].
The overarching research questions are:

What are the predictors of perinatal mortality in Ghana?
What is the pooled prevalence of perinatal mortality in Ghana?

## Methods
This study was conducted and reported using the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [ ]. The review protocol was registered in PROPERO (CRD42024564968) and published [ ].

## Theoretical framework
This study is guided by Andersen’s health service utilization model [ , ]. Andersen health service utilization model is widely known for its robustness and well validated theoretical model used in identifying both societal and individual level determinants in the context of systematic analysis [ , ].
Andersen’s Health Service Utilization Model [ , ] was selected as the theoretical framework for this study due to its robustness in analyzing multifactorial predictors of healthcare outcomes, particularly in low-resource settings like Ghana. The model categorizes predictors into three primary domains: predisposing factors, enabling factors, and need factors.
In this study, the framework was operationalized as follows:
Predisposing factors: Examined interpersonal factors such as maternal age, parity, maternal education and marital status, social structural factors such as ethnicity, and family type, and health beliefs such as attitudes toward care variables.
Enabling factors: Assessed access to healthcare such as availability of services, transportation, social support such as family/partner involvement, and health literacy such as awareness of pregnancy risks.
Need factors: Analyzed clinical conditions such as pre-eclampsia, preterm labor, and fetal health such as birth defects, and infections.
This structured approach ensured a comprehensive evaluation of individual, community, and systemic influences on perinatal mortality, aligning with the model’s validated application in maternal health research [ , ]. By synthesizing evidence under these domains, the study addresses gaps in understanding the interplay of sociocultural, economic, and clinical determinants in Ghana.
Search Strategy and Data Sources.
A systematic search was conducted across four electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and CINAHL) using predefined terms related to perinatal mortality and its predictors in Ghana. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were employed to refine searches. An example PubMed search strategy is indicated in the review protocol.

## Literature search strategy
An iterative process which involves searching the literature, refining the search strategy and reviewing articles for study inclusion and exclusions was conducted.

## Key terms included:
Outcomes: “perinatal mortality,” “stillbirth,” “early neonatal death.”
Predisposing factors: “maternal age,” “education,” “cultural beliefs.”
Enabling factors: “health insurance,” “distance to facility,” “social support.”
Need factors: “preeclampsia,” “fetal distress,” “birth weight.”
Geography: “Ghana,” region-specific terms (e.g., “Ashanti,” “Northern Region”).

## Eligibility Criteria
Inclusion: Peer-reviewed studies (2000–2024) in English; conducted in Ghana; focused on perinatal mortality predictors; diverse designs (RCTs, cohort, cross-sectional).
Exclusion: Studies on under-5 mortality, non-English publications, grey literature.
Further details of the search strategy are outlined in the systematic review protocol [ ].

## Outcome variables
Our primary outcome was perinatal mortality, operationally defined as:
Stillbirths: Fetal deaths ≥ 28 weeks of gestation (WHO standard).
Early neonatal deaths: Infant deaths ≤ 7 days postnatal.
Secondary outcomes included predictor-specific mortality rates. All outcomes were extracted as counts per 1000 births with verification against original study definitions.

## We included studies across all healthcare settings:
Facility-based: Tertiary hospitals (n = 18), district hospitals (n = 7).
Community-based: CHPS zones (n = 3), population surveys (n = 2).

## Screening and Data Extraction
Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts using EndNote 21 and Excel. A third reviewer resolved discrepancies. Data were charted using a template aligned with Anderson’s domains (predisposing, enabling, and need).
Further details of the systematic review methods are outlined in our systematic review protocol [ ].

## Study screening and appraisal
Two researchers were engaged (DP and MK) to independently screen the articles by titles, abstracts, and full texts using a screening template developed by MK. Endnote X9 was used to store, organize and manage the papers. A Microsoft excel sheet was created and used to facilitate the screening and extraction of the data at each screening stage. The screening process was guided at each stage using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Double screening was used to manage the titles/abstracts and full texts by summing the disagreement rates between the screeners. A third reviewer (GAA) was asked to review an article for its inclusion if there is a disagreement between DP and MK regarding the inclusion of that particular article. The study selection process was guided by the PRISMA flow diagram [ ] (Fig. ). 

## Assessing risk of bias and quality assessment of included studies
The quality of each of the included studies was evaluated using the appropriate Joanna Briggs Critical Appraisal tools [ ]. The critical appraisal tool for cross-sectional studies consisted of 8 questions, the tool for RCTs was made up of 9 questions, case-control assessment tool had 10 questions, and cohort studies had 11 questions. All individual studies were critically assessed and rated. the possible responses for each question were “yes”, “no”, “unclear” and “not applicable”. The “yes” response was rated as 1 and “no” and “unclear” responses were rated 0. A total score was computed for each study, with a maximum and minimum scores of 8 and 0 for cross-sectional studies, 9 and 0 for RCTs, 10 and 0 for case-control studies, and 11 and 0 for cohort studies. Study quality was assessed using standardized Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools, with scores categorized as.
Good (low risk of bias): ≥80% of criteria met (e.g., 8/10 for case-control studies).
Moderate: 50–79% criteria met.
Poor (high risk of bias): <50% criteria met.
Scores were derived from summing ‘yes’ responses (1 point each) to tool-specific questions.
It was, however, decided among the authors that no study is to be excluded based on the quality rating. Three reviewers (MHK, DP and GAA) were tasked to carry out the quality assessment. Differences in their assessments were discussed and resolved amicably. These forms are freely available at https://jbi.global/.

## Data analysis and synthesis
The data for this study was analysed in two phases according to the two review objectives. The first phase of the analysis focused on identifying the predictors of perinatal mortality in Ghana. A thematic analysis, using Anderson’s theoretical framework constructs (pre-disposing factors, enabling factors, and need factors) was presented using narrative summary of the findings. Any pattern, trends, or gaps in the literature were identified and reported. A section of the data, particularly the year of publication, the region/institution, the sample size, the study design, the theoretical background was analysed using simple descriptive statistics (frequency counts).
To estimate the pooled prevalence of perinatal mortality, we employed a random-effects model with the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variances for proportional data. For studies reporting continuous outcomes (e.g., gestational age, birth weight) as predictors of mortality, Hedges’ g was used to standardize mean differences where applicable. Subgroup analyses (e.g., by region, urban versus rural setting) and meta-regression were conducted to explore sources of high heterogeneity (I² >99%).
While included studies were conceptually homogeneous in their shared focus on perinatal mortality predictors in Ghana (meeting our strict inclusion criteria), we assessed heterogeneity using multiple complementary approaches. we anticipated and accounted for methodological heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and measurement approaches through our analytic strategy. This expected variability motivated our pre-specified use of random-effects models, which provide more conservative estimates when true effect sizes may differ across studies.
We estimated I² statistic, which estimates the percentage of total variation across studies due to heterogeneity rather than chance [ ]. A 95% prediction intervals, which provide the expected range of true effects, was also considered. Additionally, we used Tau² to estimate between-study variance and Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.10 considered significant) [ ]. Rather than relying solely on I² thresholds, we interpreted heterogeneity in the context of clinical and methodological differences between studies, as even modest I² values can reflect important variation when the overall effect size distribution is narrow [ ].
Subgroup analyses based on specific characteristics (region, urban, rural and mixed settings, and Year). Meta-regression was also performed to assess the effect of the study sample size and the region [ ]. A funnel plot and Egger tests were conducted to assess for publication bias with a statistically significant level of < 0.05. STATA V.18 for Mac (Stata Statistical Software, TX, StataCorp LLC).

A total of 2,184 articles were identified in the four databases. A total of 1,349 duplicates were removed in the deduplication process, remaining 835. After the initial screening by title and abstract, 750 articles were excluded. 85 full-text articles were sought for retrieval and 84 were obtained. After the final screening, 30 full-text articles were included in the review (Fig. ).
The articles were summarized under the following headings; (1) Authors; (2) Year of publication; (3) Title of studies; (4) Study site (Region); (5) Study settings (Urban, rural or mix); (6) Study design; (7) Study sample; (8) Rate of perinatal mortality; (9) Predictors of perinatal mortality; and (10) Quality assessment. ## Prevalence of perinatal mortality
The prevalence rate of perinatal rate was Pooled from 13 studies that reported prevalence of perinatal mortality in their studies. The rate ranged from 2.2 to 161.5 (per 1000 births), with a Pooled prevalence of 44.8 (95%CI: 15.4–74.2) per 1000 births. There was evidence of substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies (I² >99.97%, p < 0.0001) as shown in Fig. , reflecting expected variations in study designs, settings, and measurement approaches. However, all studies maintained conceptual homogeneity through their shared focus on perinatal mortality predictors within Ghana’s healthcare context (Fig. ). 

## Sub-group analysis
The rate of perinatal mortality was highest in Urban 69 per 1000 births [95%CI: 20.0–117, p = 0.011]). However, there was no evidence of differences of prevalence between the groups (mixed, rural and urban settings) [Qb(2) = 4.78, p = 0.09]. Regarding the regions, there was a significant difference in the rates [Qb(9) = 300.87, p = 0.00] with Greater Accra reporting perinatal mortality rate of 99 (95%CI: 52–145, p < 0.0001). There was also significant difference in the rate of perinatal mortality according to the year [Qb(7) = 668.08, p < 0.0001]. The source of heterogeneity could therefore be attributed to the region and the year of the study. This is presented in Fig. . 

## Meta-regression
The meta-regression model showed evidence of statistically significant association (p < 0.0001, β = −0.012) between the prevalence of perinatal mortality and study’s sample (Fig. ). 
There was also statistically significant association (p < 0.0001, β = −0.023) between perinatal mortality and the quality rating of the study, with higher quality rating showing lower rate of perinatal mortality (Fig. ). 

## Publication bias
Funnel plot of the studies included in the meta-analysis showed evidence of publication bias (Fig. ). The presence of publication bias was confirmed by Eggers test (p < 0.0001). 

## Predictors of perinatal mortality
Perinatal mortality remains a crucial public health issue, most especially in lower-and middle-income countries like Ghana, where healthcare systems often struggle to meet the comprehensive needs of mothers and their newborns. These studies conducted in Ghana presented a of myriad factors found to be significant predictors of perinatal mortality in different parts of Ghana. These factors have been categorized under three thematic areas as defined by Anderson’s theory of healthcare utilization: Predisposing Factors, Enabling Factors and Need Factors.

## Predisposing factors
Predisposing factors are the inherent characteristics that make a population more susceptible to perinatal mortality. Factors such as advanced maternal age, hypertension, cultural beliefs, and obstetrical history have been found in various studies across Ghana to pose significant impact of perinatal outcome.

## Maternal age
Research has increasingly pointed towards advanced maternal age, commonly defined as age of 35 years and above, as a significant risk factor for adverse perinatal outcomes. For instance, researchers like Anto et al. (2018) and Bawah et al. (2019) found that women above 35 years face an elevated risk of perinatal mortality. The biological reasons behind this increased risk include a higher prevalence of chromosomal abnormalities, decreased uterine function, and age-related health issues like hypertension and diabetes [ , ]. Much focus has been placed on the risks associated with advanced maternal age. However, young maternal age (mothers less than 20 years) also presents its own set of challenges, resulting in perinatal mortality [ ]. Young mothers may be at a higher risk due to biological immaturity, the lack of access to healthcare, and socio-economic disadvantages.

## Pre-existing health conditions
Maternal age doesn’t act in isolation; it interacts with other factors [ , , ]. Hypertension and hypertensive disorders significantly impact perinatal outcomes, as shown by studies like Adu-Bonsaffoh et al. (2017) and Browne et al. (2015). Additionally, gestational diabetes plays a role, as emphasized by Bawah et al. (2019). Younger mothers may face challenges due to socio-cultural practices affecting healthcare utilization.

## Cultural beliefs and practices
Cultural beliefs and practices such as the concept of “asram” and female genital mutilation (FGM) have also been linked to perinatal mortality [ – ]. “Asram,” a cultural belief prevalent in the Ashanti Region, can limit access to medical care, especially antenatal and postnatal services [ ]. Bakari et al. (2021) found that beliefs associated with “asram” deter women from seeking timely medical intervention, increasing the risk of adverse perinatal outcomes.
FGM has negative health implications, including an increased risk of perinatal mortality. Nonterah et al. (2020) highlighted this impact in the Upper East Region of Ghana, where FGM not only raises infection and obstructed labor risks but also discourages healthcare utilization due to socio-cultural norms.

## Obstetric history of mothers
Obstetric history is crucial for assessing maternal and fetal risk during pregnancy. It includes previous pregnancies, birth outcomes, complications, and overall reproductive health, providing insights into the likelihood of perinatal mortality in future pregnancies. Multiple pregnancies significantly impact perinatal outcomes.
Avoka et al. (2018) found that women with multiple pregnancies face higher risks of complications, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and gestational diabetes, which can contribute to increased perinatal mortality rates [ ]. A history of neonatal death or stillbirth in a mother can significantly predict future perinatal outcomes. Azaare et al. (2022) found that such experiences may indicate underlying health issues, social factors, or genetic predispositions that could recur in subsequent pregnancies, thereby increasing the risk of perinatal mortality [ ].

## Maternal education
Maternal education significantly impacts perinatal outcomes. Low educational attainment has consistently been associated with higher rates of perinatal mortality [ ]. When mothers lack education, they may have limited knowledge about prenatal care and the importance of timely medical intervention. On the other hand, educated mothers are more likely to make informed healthcare decisions, access antenatal care, and understand medical advice [ , ]. Maternal education also interacts with factors like financial resources and healthcare accessibility [ ].

## Enabling factors
Enabling factors significantly impact access to healthcare services and, consequently, perinatal mortality rates. These external elements can either facilitate or hinder access. Notable enabling factors include the quality of ANC, Ghana’s free maternal healthcare policy (under the National Health Insurance Scheme), availability of clinical resources, and accessibility to healthcare facilities.

## High-Quality ANC
High-quality antenatal care (ANC) is crucial for mitigating perinatal mortality risks. ANC involves more than occasional visits - it encompasses health education, screening tests, and preventive measures like vaccinations. Afulani (2016) emphasized this in a study based on the Ghana Maternal Health Survey. Quality ANC allows healthcare providers to monitor fetal development, assess maternal health, and identify complications or risk factors [ ]. It significantly influences mothers’ access to healthcare services and shapes outcomes for both mother and child. Availability of quality ANC can mitigate risks related to maternal age, education, occupation, and medical complications [ , , , ].

## Healthcare policies
Healthcare policies can act as powerful enabling factors in the realm of perinatal mortality, shaping the landscape of healthcare accessibility, quality, and utilization. Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) aims to eliminate financial barriers for maternal healthcare [ , ]. Studies by Azaare et al. (2022) and Azaare et al. (2023) show improved accessibility due to this policy. However, it’s a “double-edged sword”: increased demand can strain healthcare infrastructure, affecting quality of care [ ].
Beyond policies, actual clinical resources are crucial. Azaare et al. (2022) highlighted the impact of essential medicines and labor monitoring on perinatal mortality rates. Availability of supplies (like gloves and gauze) and equipment (ultrasound devices, incubators) matters. A lack of resources can act as a barrier, even in settings where healthcare services are nominally available and accessible.

## Access to healthcare facility
Healthcare facility accessibility is crucial in the fight against perinatal mortality. Beyond quality, ease of access matters. Studies by Gabrysch et al. (2019) and Tette et al. (2020) explored factors like facility type, distance, and transport mode [ , ]. Their findings emphasize that it’s not just about how far a facility is; the type of facility and available transportation also play key roles. For example, specialized maternal healthcare centers might offer excellent services but could be distant from rural communities, impacting utilization and perinatal outcomes [ ].

## Need factors
Need factors in perinatal mortality involve urgent medical conditions directly impacting the mother and fetus. Unlike predisposing or enabling factors, which influence healthcare utilization, need factors present acute situations. Critical indicators include medical complications, low birth weight, and low Apgar scores [ , , ].

## Medical complications
Conditions like gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and placental abruption directly impact perinatal health [ ]. Adu-Bonsaffoh et al. (2017) emphasized their significance at Korle Bu Teaching Hospital. Low birth weight and low Apgar scores are other significant need factors that have been correlated with higher rates of perinatal mortality. Both are critical need factors associated with higher perinatal mortality rates. Low Apgar scores indicate neonatal distress, requiring immediate intervention. Low birth weight may signal premature birth or growth restriction [ ]. While predisposing and enabling factors influence complications, the acute nature of need factors determines immediate healthcare actions. Limited clinical resources in some facilities exacerbate risks, while well-equipped facilities improve outcomes [ , ].

## Environmental exposures
While medical complications often take center stage in discussions about need factors affecting perinatal mortality, environmental exposures deserve equal attention. Factors such as the type of fuels used for cooking and exposure to long-term heat stress can have a considerable impact on perinatal outcomes [ ]. The type of fuel used for cooking impacts maternal and fetal health. Hussein et al. (2020) found that certain fuels (producing indoor air pollution) are linked to adverse perinatal outcomes. Respiratory issues, low birth weight, and stillbirths can result from such exposures [ ]. Nyadanu et al. (2023) highlighted the negative impact of long-term heat stress on perinatal outcomes. Elevated temperatures can lead to dehydration, increased metabolic rates, and preterm labor [ ]. Environmental exposures don’t act alone; they interact with predisposing and enabling factors. Socio-economic status affects fuel choices, and lack of healthcare access worsens heat stress effects [ , ].

## Sleep practices
One of the most scrutinized sleep practices in the context of perinatal health is the supine (back-lying) sleep position. Sleeping on the back can increase pressure on the vena cava, affecting blood flow to the fetus. Owusu et al. (2013) associated supine sleep with poor maternal and fetal outcomes, potentially increasing stillbirth and low birth weight risks [ ].
Snoring during pregnancy can be a symptom of obstructive sleep apnea. Interrupted breathing during sleep can lead to decreased oxygen levels, raising risks of complications like preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and perinatal mortality [ ]. Lack of awareness about sleep risks and limited access to healthcare services can worsen their impact.

## Maternal monitoring and medical interventions
Maternal monitoring involves a range of activities aimed at assessing the well-being of both the mother and fetus during pregnancy and labor. Lawrence et al. (2022) highlight this measure, assessing the balance between fetal brain blood flow and placental blood flow. Monitoring this ratio can provide early indications of fetal distress or placental insufficiency, allowing timely interventions to mitigate perinatal mortality risk [ ].
Caesarean sections, labor inductions, and forceps/vacuum use are sometimes necessary. However, Swarray-Deen et al. (2020) caution that these interventions carry risks like infections and hemorrhage [ ]. Availability of advanced monitoring equipment and skilled providers matters. The mother’s overall health and quality of antenatal care also impact outcomes.

## Discussion
This systematic review sought to elucidate the prevalence of perinatal mortality and complex interactions of factors identified to be associated with perinatal mortality in Ghana. The review used Anderson’s theory of healthcare utilization as a framework due to its robustness in identifying factors influencing healthcare use among general population. A majority of the studies in the review employed cross-sectional designs, which are useful for snapshot analyses. But these cross-sectional studies are limited in establishing causality. The abundance of cross-sectional data provides a broad, albeit static, picture of perinatal mortality in Ghana. On the other hand, the sparse number of cluster-randomized controlled trials and prospective cohort studies in the review data pool shows the need for more rigorous designs that can better elucidate cause-and-effect relationships of perinatal mortality in Ghana.
Retrospective studies provide valuable insights in the existing data on the predictors of perinatal mortality. However, these studies could suffer from limitations such as incomplete or inconsistent data. Qualitative and mixed-method studies add depth to our understanding of the predictors of perinatal mortality by exploring contextual factors, but they are underrepresented in the existing studies reviewed.
Many of the studies reviewed were conducted in teaching hospitals, which are tertiary care centres. While these settings offer a high level of expertise and resources, they may not be reflective of healthcare conditions in rural areas or smaller facilities, potentially skewing the overall understanding of perinatal mortality in Ghana. Regional and nationwide studies offer a broader perspective but may lack the depth offered by more localized studies. The paucity of studies focusing on rural settings, where healthcare access may be more limited, poses a critical gap.

## Prevalence of perinatal mortality
The study also employed rigorous meta-analytic approaches, including sub-group analysis, meta-regression and Egger’s test, to provide a reliable and valid evidence of prevalence of perinatal mortality in Ghana. These analytical steps provided an understanding that the homogenous studies samples, study settings and the quality of the articles included in the review had resulted in significant difference in the rate of perinatal mortality in Ghana. The Pool estimate of perinatal mortality was 44.8 per 1000 births in Ghana, ranging from 2.2 to 161.5, with the sub-group analysis by study region and settings indicating 69 per 100 births in Urban and 99 per 1000 in Greater Accra. As seen in the evidence, most of the studies were conducted in urban settings, with Greater Accra, mainly urban setting, reporting the most studies. The apparent urban-rural disparity in perinatal mortality rates should not be overinterpreted, as the limited number of rural studies (20% of included studies) may inadequately represent true community-level outcomes. Urban estimates may be inflated by tertiary hospital-based studies capturing higher-risk pregnancies, while rural data gaps could mask unmet needs in underserved areas. This ecological limitation underscores the need for more population-based studies across all settings.
Meta-regression identified two key moderators, 100-participant increase in sample size predicted a 12% lower mortality rate (β=−0.12, p = 0.003). This suggests larger studies capture more representative outcomes and studies with higher quality scores reported 23% lower rates, highlighting measurement reliability effects. Additionally, while Egger’s test indicated publication bias, the trim-and-fill adjusted estimate remained clinically significant. This suggests our findings are robust despite potential underreporting of null results, particularly from smaller rural studies.
The Pool estimate in this review is above the SSA regional estimate of 34.7 per 1000 births. While our pooled estimate (44.8/1000 births) exceeds the reported SSA average (34.7/1000) [ ]. This difference likely reflects both true epidemiological patterns and methodological factors. Ghana’s estimate derives predominantly from facility-based studies (78% of included studies) which typically capture higher-risk pregnancies compared to population-based SSA estimates. Additionally, 62% of our included studies used the standard 28-week gestational definition for stillbirths, whereas some SSA estimates use alternative thresholds that may exclude late fetal deaths. To be able to reduce this rate of perinatal mortality, it is critical for Ghana to strengthen maternal healthcare services.
Predisposing factors were found to be crucial determinants of perinatal mortality. Factors such as maternal age, pre-existing health conditions, cultural beliefs and practices, obstetric history, and maternal education being highlighted as key variables. Advance maternal age was observed as one of the significant risk factors in multiple studies [ , ]. In line with Anderson’s healthcare utilization theory predisposing factor, previous adverse outcomes or complications create a pre-existing condition that can influence the course and outcome of subsequent pregnancies [ ]. These factors can also affect a mother’s psychological preparedness and her engagement with healthcare services, possibly leading to delayed or inadequate prenatal care [ ].
The focus on advanced maternal age needs to be balanced with emerging evidence about the risks associated with young maternal age [ ]. The duality of advanced maternal age and young maternal age suggest a U-shaped relationship between maternal age and perinatal mortality. This requires a holistic healthcare strategy towards reduction of perinatal mortality rates that covers different age groups.
The review also observed pre-existing health conditions like hypertension and gestational diabetes as significant risks of perinatal mortality [ , , ]. These medical conditions do not operate alone but interact with other predisposing factors like maternal age. This add layers of complexity to the healthcare needs and intervention strategies toward maternal health and perinatal outcome.
One of the most intriguing aspects of the review findings is the role of cultural beliefs and practices, such as “asram” and FGM, in shaping perinatal outcomes [ , ]. These factors did not only act as a direct impediment to healthcare utilization, but also interact with other predisposing and also some enabling and need factors in unpredictable ways. For instance, women who undergo FGM may also be at a higher risk of developing hypertension during pregnancy [ ]. It is therefore critical to acknowledge that interventions that target perinatal mortality should also consider socio-cultural sensitivities and nuances to be effective.
A mother’s obstetric history stood out as another potent predictor of perinatal mortality [ , ]. The review identified that from multi-parity to a history of neonatal death or stillbirth creates a continuum of risk across different pregnancies. This demonstrates the significance of receiving comprehensive ANC and PNC care, in particular when attempting to enhance perinatal health.
Maternal education also emerged as a pivotal determinant of perinatal mortality [ , ]. Maternal education could influence healthcare service utilization especially during pregnancy. This factor can therefore act as an equalizer, mitigating some of the risks associated with the other predisposing factors by enhancing healthcare literacy and health service utilization. Maternal age should be considered in public health interventions which aims at reducing perinatal mortality.
A high-quality ANC also emerged in the review as a critical enabling factor for better perinatal outcomes. As reported by studies like Afulani (2016), the quality of ANC directly impacts the monitoring and treatment of maternal and fetal conditions. This can be especially beneficial in mitigating the risks associated with other domains like predisposing factors (e.g. Maternal age, education, etc.) and need factors (e.g. medical complications). While high-quality ANC is universally beneficial, its specific impact can be amplified in regions like Ghana, where healthcare systems may be less resourced. This underscores the need for focused interventions to improve the quality of ANC across healthcare facilities in the country.
The NHIS of Ghana, which aimed at providing free healthcare to the people of Ghana, including free maternal healthcare, is seen as another significant enabling factor in this review. Studies by Azaare et al., (2022) and Azaare et al., (2023) acknowledge the role of NHIS in improving access to healthcare services. The influx of patients due to reduced financial barriers can strain already limited healthcare resources, thus potentially compromising the quality of care [ ]. This implies that policy implementations, like the NHIS, should be coupled with other strategic planning to ensure resource adequacy in healthcare facilities particularly geared towards reducing the burden of perinatal mortality.
Within the framework of Anderson’s healthcare utilization theory, accessibility acts as an enabling factor. It influences how readily mothers can make use of healthcare services, which, in turn, impacts perinatal mortality rates. A facility offering high-quality services but located far from a community may see lower utilization rates, negating the benefits of the quality care it provides.
The availability of essential clinical resources serves as a foundational aspect of the healthcare system that often remains underemphasized. A lack of essential medicines, inadequate labor monitoring, and other resource limitations can be detrimental to perinatal outcomes [ ]. Thus, in the realm of enabling factors, policy initiatives must also direct attention and resources toward equipping healthcare facilities adequately in order to provide quality healthcare services.
The review also highlights the multi-dimensional concept of accessibility to healthcare services. Distance to healthcare facilities, the type of healthcare facility, and the means to reach it all emerged as contributing factors to perinatal mortality [ , ]. It is not merely about the physical proximity to healthcare facilities, but also the ‘reachable’ proximity based on socio-economic conditions. Therefore, regional healthcare planning in Ghana should consider these multi-dimensional barriers to accessibility, particularly when aiming to reduce perinatal mortality.
The immediate medical conditions that require urgent healthcare interventions, termed as “Need Factors” in Anderson’s model, were frequently cited in the studies included in this review. Conditions like gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, low birth weight, and low Apgar scores emerged as significant predictors of perinatal mortality. The acute nature of these medical complications often triggers immediate healthcare utilization. For instance, studies conducted at Korle Bu Teaching Hospital emphasized that immediate medical attention is crucial in managing these complications to prevent adverse outcomes, including perinatal death [ , ].
In addition, environmental exposures like fuel type used for cooking and long-term heat stress have been identified as acute factors necessitating immediate healthcare attention. Studies like Hussein et al. (2020) and Nyadanu et al. (2023) elaborated on how such exposures could lead to adverse perinatal outcomes including respiratory issues, low birth weight, stillbirths, and dehydration, thereby confirming their roles as need factors in perinatal mortality.
Sleep practices, particularly supine sleep position and snoring were also identified as need factors posing high risk to perinatal outcome [ ]. These sleep practices, when detrimental, can lead to conditions like decreased oxygen levels. Decreased oxygen levels can necessitate immediate medical interventions.
Another crucial aspect that emerged from the review is the importance of maternal monitoring and medical interventions. These interventions include caesarean sections and labor inductions, in the context of need factors [ , ]. The cerebro-placental ratio, for instance, can be an early indicator of fetal distress, thereby allowing timely interventions.
While need factors act as immediate triggers for healthcare utilization, their impact can be nuanced by predisposing and enabling factors. For example, a healthcare facility with limited resources may not be equipped to handle acute pregnancy complications effectively, potentially amplifying the risks [ ]. Conversely, good accessibility to a well-equipped healthcare facility can mitigate these risks of perinatal mortality [ ].

## Strength and limitations of this study

## Strength

This study is grounded in the distinctive context of Ghana’s socio-cultural, economic settings, and also its healthcare system in the sub-Saharan region. Consequently, the findings will be especially relevant for developing tailored maternal and child health interventions in Ghana.
To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first comprehensive review on predictors related to perinatal mortality in Ghana employing a systematic review and meta-analysis design. It therefore presents concrete evidence of the predictors of perinatal mortality in Ghana that is of policy and practical relevance.
Additionally, the study follows a robust guiding principle, Anderson’s health service utilization framework. This ensures a systematic identification of pivotal factors associated with perinatal mortality, considering individual, community, and overarching healthcare system perspectives in the country.

## Limitation

Notwithstanding the aforementioned strengths, this study has a limitation as well. The study includes only studies published between January 2010 and June 2024. This might preclude some important and persistent trends in the predictors of perinatal mortality in Ghana spanning a more extended timeframe.
Also, as a limitation, we observed that while included studies were homogeneous in their focus on perinatal mortality predictors in Ghana (justifying their combined analysis), we observed substantial methodological heterogeneity in study designs, settings, and measurement approaches. This was reflected in the high I² value (> 99.97%), which prompted our use of random-effects modeling.
While we included all relevant studies regardless of quality score, 65% of included studies had moderate risk of bias (JBI scores 5–7/10), particularly in sampling methods and control of confounders.
Our focus on peer-reviewed publications may have omitted valuable program evaluations or local reports from NGOs and district health teams.
The majority of studies were conducted in urban tertiary hospitals, potentially overrepresenting high-risk pregnancies and underrepresenting community-level mortality patterns.

## Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis, which was guided by Anderson’s theory of healthcare utilization, underscores the multifaceted factors affecting perinatal mortality in Ghana. The existing literature reviewed, primarily cross-sectional in design, highlighted the significance of predisposing factors like maternal age, pre-existing conditions, cultural beliefs, and education. However, it falls short of revealing causal relationships, especially in under-researched rural areas like the Upper West Region. The review calls for attention to crucial enabling factors like high-quality ANC and the NHIS, while cautioning that such enabling factors can strain limited healthcare resources if not properly managed. Immediate medical conditions, termed “need factors,” further complicate healthcare utilization. Notably, the review reveals glaring gaps in the literature, particularly the lack of case-control studies in rural settings and inadequate focus on healthcare quality, socio-economic influences, and policy impacts.
To accelerate Ghana’s progress towards SDG 3.2, we recommend three priority actions. Target rural interventions by scaling up community-based perinatal audits and mobile ANC in underserved regions, informed by dedicated research on rural mortality determinants.
Enhance the integration of socio-cultural sensitivity into maternal health programs by developing and implementing context-specific health education campaigns that address harmful practices like female genital mutilation and cultural beliefs like "asram".
Establish a national, standardized perinatal audit and data surveillance system to imporve the quality and granularity of data on perinatal deaths. This system should be implemented across all levels of care, especially in rural and community-based settings, to accurately identify causes, contextual factors, and disparities. The findings from these audits should be fed back into a continuous quality improvement cycle to inform policy revisions, targeted training for healthcare workers, and community-level interventions.
