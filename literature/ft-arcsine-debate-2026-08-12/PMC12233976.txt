## Abstract

## Background
Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), a chronic inflammatory skin disease affecting intertriginous areas, presents a recurring and debilitating challenge. Even though recent efforts have been made to estimate the overall HS prevalence, variations in screening modalities and missing data from the Global South warrant further investigation. Understanding the HS prevalence is crucial for treatment approaches and pathogenesis. Thus, we aimed to estimate a global HS prevalence based on studies using homogeneous validated questions.

## Methods
This systematic review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO and adhered to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. Searches in PubMed, Embase, and CINAHL were performed on August 9, 2023. Original reports assessing the HS prevalence in adults using the following two questions were included: “Do you have recurrent boils of the skin?” and “Have you for the past 6 months had 2 or more boils/abscesses in any of the below locations: in the axilla, in the groin, around your genitals, on the buttocks, several locations i.e., first the buttocks then the axilla, etc.?” A pooled prevalence with 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated with a random-effects model.

## Results
Eight studies qualified for inclusion, of which the prevalence estimate ranged from 0.7% to 6.4%. The meta-analysis encompassing 49,971 participants revealed a global HS prevalence of 2.5% (95% CI, 1.8–3.5%).

## Conclusion
This meta-analysis suggests a global HS prevalence of 2.5% using a validated questionnaire. Due to heterogeneity and sparse data from the Global South, this estimate should be interpreted with caution.

## Introduction
Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), also known as acne inversa, is a chronic inflammatory skin disease that is recurring and debilitating. It is characterized by painful, deep-seated, inflamed lesions assumingly caused by occlusion of hair follicles, which can progress to abscesses that rupture and suppurate and lead to tunnels resulting in persistent cycles of inflammation, healing, and scarring. HS primarily affects the intertriginous skin areas, such as the axilla, inframammary area, inguinal, perianal, gluteal, and pubic regions [ ]. Patients with HS are generally overweight and, compared to the general population, more often smoke [ ]. Women are more frequently affected than men [ ]. Treatments include topical antiseptics or antibiotics, intralesional steroid injections, systemic antibiotics, and biologics. In some cases, surgical treatment may be necessary [ ].
Studies estimating the HS prevalence and incidence rates have been conducted across the world both as hospital-based and population-based studies. The diagnostic techniques used to identify cases with HS in the literature vary, ranging from self-reported assessments to medically verified diagnoses, or by retrieving treatment codes through automated queries within medical information systems. Consequently, this diversity in diagnostic modalities has contributed significantly to the variability observed in the reported prevalence of HS [ ]. HS is affecting between 0.04% and 4.10% of the sampled populations, according to previous estimates [ ]. Prevalence and incidence estimates hailing from the Global South are rare, which might be due to underreporting and diagnostic difficulties by nonspecialists [ ].
Recently, a systematic review and meta-analysis reported an overall prevalence of HS of 0.40%. The included studies, however, used different techniques to identify cases of HS in the given populations [ ]. The Global Prevalence of Hidradenitis Suppurativa Atlas (GHiSA) is a nonprofit organization that is trying to gain precise and homogenous national prevalence rates by executing explorative, cross-sectional, descriptive studies in more than 58 countries using a simple validated questionnaire developed by Vinding et al. [ ] in 2014 [ , ]. In GHiSA, the questionnaire is used to screen for HS, and the diagnosis is subsequently clinically verified in patients who screen positive on the questionnaire.
Globally, patients experience an average diagnostic delay of 7.2 years, which might be due to the lack of recognition of the disease but also to the relatively mild symptoms experienced by some patients [ ]. This may explain part of the variation seen in current prevalence estimates. Defining the prevalence of the disease and its distinctive features is a necessary condition to better understand the pathogenesis and strengthen individual treatment approaches for HS. Therefore, this review aimed to determine the overall global HS prevalence assessed using two simple questions developed by Vinding et al. [ ] as these are validated and used by GHiSA.

## Methods
This systematic review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [ ]. The study was registered on PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO) with registration number: CRD42023453106 [ , ].
We used the following PICOS model (P: population, I: intervention, C: control, O: outcomes, S: study design) [ ] when constructing the eligibility criteria in the search strategy: P: adult populations, I: none, C: none, O: prevalence of HS, and S: none. Our inclusion criteria were studies assessing the prevalence of HS in an unselected adult population by using two validated questions by Vinding et al. [ ]. Studies using a questionnaire with the questions “Do you have recurrent boils of the skin?” and “Have you for the past 6 months had 2 or more boils/abscesses in any of the below locations: in the axilla, in the groin, around your genitals, on the buttocks, several locations i.e., first the buttocks then the axilla, etc.?” in any language were included. Studies were excluded if they used other questions/questionnaires or a modification of the two before-mentioned questions to assess the prevalence of HS. Furthermore, studies assessing the prevalence of HS in specific patient groups such as in patients with inflammatory bowel disease or in a mixed pediatric and adult patient population were excluded. Likewise, studies that did not investigate the prevalence as an outcome were excluded. Case-control studies, case reports, and studies that did not report original data such as reviews were also excluded. There were no restrictions on other outcomes being assessed in the studies. No time or language restrictions were applied in the current review, and no attempt to include ongoing trials was made.
The search was carried out in PubMed, Embase, and CINAHL (PubMed: 1966 – search date, Embase: 1974 – search date, CINAHL: 1981 – search date). The search was conducted on August 9, 2023, and the same search terms were used in the databases. The search terms were (((Hidradenitis Suppurativa) OR (acne inversa)) OR (Verneuil's disease)) AND (((epidemiology) OR (incidence)) OR (prevalence)).
We utilized Covidence, a web-based review software platform, as a blinded screening tool [ ]. After duplicate removal, two reviewers (J.A.Z., M.A.S.H.) independently screened the title and abstract at first and then full text according to the eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies in both screening phases were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached within the author group. When more than one publication from the same study population was found, the publication with the largest study population was included and reported. Data from the included studies were extracted by both reviewers independently into predesigned spreadsheets and cross checked. Data items included author name, publication year, country, number of cases, population size, prevalence estimate, confidence interval (CI), and for patients with HS, the following items were retrieved: sex, age, BMI, and smoking status.
One author assessed the methodological quality of the included studies by using the Joanna Briggs Institute Prevalence Critical Appraisal Tool [ ], which includes assessing the use of an appropriate sample frame, appropriate participant sampling, adequate sample size, description of study subjects and setting, sufficient coverage of the sample, valid and reliable diagnostic measurement as well as relevant use of statistics and response rate adequacy. This tool was used based on the findings of a systematic review in 2022 that considered it to be the most appropriate tool to assess quality in prevalence studies [ ].
The primary outcome of this review and meta-analysis was the prevalence of HS regardless of whether the included studies assessed HS as a primary or exploratory outcome. To measure prevalence, the studies needed to report the number of participants identified as HS cases and the total sample size investigated in the study.
A meta-analysis of proportions was undertaken of studies that met the above-described criteria. The proportions were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine method. An inverse variance method was used in a random-effects model with DerSimonian-Laird estimates, due to expected interstudy population differences. A pooled proportion, i.e., an overall prevalence of HS, was reported with 95% CI. The results were presented in a forest plot. Two sets of subgroup analyses were performed: on studies investigating the prevalence in the same country and one on studies using the similar sampling techniques such as general population studies. Inconsistency was determined using I2-test. Values of ≤25, 50, and ≥75% equaled low, moderate, and high inconsistency, respectively. p values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio version 2023.09.1 + 494 and R version 4.3.1.

## Results
A total of 2,222 records were identified in the searched databases (PubMed: 903, Embase: 1,106, CINAHL: 213). After removing duplicates, 1,526 titles and abstracts were screened, which resulted in 49 records being full-text screened, which were all in English. Of these, 8 papers published between 2014 and 2023 were included in the final qualitative and quantitative analyses [ , – ] (shown in ). The population size, in which the prevalence of HS was investigated, ranged from 502 to 27,765, and the prevalence rates ranged from 0.67% to 6.44%. Half of the papers investigated prevalence rates in countries belonging to the Global South – see . A total of 15% of all prevalent cases of HS were men, and the average age of the patients with HS ranged from 29 to 49 years. On average, the patients with HS were overweight – see .
 The 8 papers were all included in the meta-analysis as they provided data from unique datasets without overlapping data and included 49,971 participants. The meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model and yielded an overall HS prevalence of 2.5% with 95% CI between 1.8% and 3.5%. There was a substantial statistical heterogeneity in the meta-analysis, I2 = 95% (shown in ). Two papers investigated the prevalence in a Danish population, and two papers investigated the prevalence in a Ghanaian population. Our subgroup analyses revealed a pooled prevalence of 1.9% (95% CI, 1.7–2.2%) in Denmark and 0.7% (95% CI, 0.4–1.1%) in Ghana (shown in a, b). When looking at sampling techniques, studies that used data from general population studies revealed a pooled prevalence of 2.9% (95% CI, 1.9–4.0%) and in studies that screened relatives to patients visiting a hospital department the pooled prevalence was 1.5% (95% CI, 0.6–2.9%) (shown in c, d).
 Using the Joanna Briggs Institute Prevalence Critical Appraisal Tool for prevalence studies, we found that most studies had an appropriate sample frame, but in most cases, it was unclear if the sampling of participants was done appropriately. In one-third of the studies, it was unclear whether the sample size was adequate; however, all studies had sufficient descriptions of study subjects and study settings. In one-fourth of the studies, it was unclear if the data analysis was conducted with sufficient coverage of the sample. All studies used valid methods to identify HS and measured it in a standard, reliable way. Likewise, all studies used appropriate statistical analyses. Only in one-fourth of the studies was response rate assessed as appropriate; in most of the studies this was unclear – see .
 Six of the 41 excluded full-text records used a different questionnaire or a modified version of the Vinding et al. [ ] questionnaire, and another 12 did not report prevalence as an outcome, although they provided data that could be used to estimate prevalence [ – ]. In a post hoc qualitative analysis, we have summarized findings from some of these papers, which are presented in . Only the first publication was included in the table if two or more studies used the same data set. There is a substantial overlap and reuse of data in the eight Danish papers using the validated Vinding et al. [ ] questions, which might explain why the range in prevalence estimates in these eight papers is small. The remaining six studies do not have any overlapping data, and the range is prominent from 0.4% to 8.9%.
 

## Discussion
The present systematic review and meta-analysis found an overall global HS prevalence of 2.5% (95% CI, 1.8–3.5%). The finding is higher than the prevalence found in a previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Jfri et al. [ ] which estimated the HS prevalence to be 0.4%. The previous study also revealed that studies based on clinical samples resulted in a pooled prevalence of 1.7%, whereas in population-based studies the pooled prevalence was found to be 0.3%. The prevalence estimates presented in it were based only on studies investigating HS in the Global North and included pediatric and adult populations. Furthermore, in 11 out of 16 studies included in the meta-analyses by Jfri et al. [ ] HS cases were diagnosed by physicians and they reported that questionnaire-based studies had the highest prevalence estimates.
In our full-text screening, we identified several studies that presented data that could be used to calculate the prevalence of HS. However, these papers did either not report on the prevalence or did not use Vinding et al. [ ] questions. Findings from these papers are summarized in ; however, caution is warranted as there is an overlap in the data shown, and therefore, statistical analyses could not be made from these data.
Even though half of the studies included in our analysis hailed from the Global South, they only accounted for 8% of the pooled number of participants in the analysis. Three studies were from sub-Saharan West African countries, and one was from Singapore. Due to the sparse data from the Global South, our global HS prevalence estimate must be interpreted with caution. The GHiSA initiative is facilitating this, and future results from GHiSA will result in reliable, reproducible, uniform data which will provide evidence for a robust global HS prevalence estimate. As with the prevalence, knowledge regarding risk factors for HS stems from European, US, and Australian studies. In general, smoking and overweight are regarded as risk factors for HS [ ]. However, in the two included studies from Ghana, patients with HS did not smoke, and in the Nigerian study, the authors argue that smoking is not a common practice in Nigeria and, therefore, opine that smoking would not have been a noteworthy risk factor in their population [ , , ]. Furthermore, none of the studies from the Global South reported a significant difference in BMI between the HS group and the control group [ , – ].
There are some methodological limitations of the current review. First, the potential bias in the actual process of searching, selecting, and extracting data is a limitation as it can introduce subjectivity and errors, potentially leading to a biased representation of the available research. To eliminate this bias, two reviews independently selected and extracted data as stated in the methods section. Second, there is a risk of publication bias, as we did not search trial registers for unpublished trials, which could potentially result in literature being overlooked and leading to either inflated or deflated prevalence estimates. Third, the statistical heterogeneity observed in our meta-analysis can be attributed to various factors, including population characteristics, sample size differences, sampling techniques, and geographic disparities. Some studies clinically verified the HS diagnoses in patients who screened positive for HS, which might contribute to the heterogeneity. Understanding and addressing heterogeneity is crucial for obtaining accurate prevalence estimates and for the development of effective treatment strategies. Therefore, we performed subgroup analyses focusing on countries and sampling techniques. The pooled prevalence rates in these analyses ranged from 0.7% to 2.9%. Lastly, due to the low number of studies included, it resulted in a wide CI, and even though we set stringent inclusion criteria, i.e., the use of validated questions, statistical heterogeneity was present in the meta-analysis.
This systematic review and meta-analysis found an overall global HS prevalence of 2.5% (95% CI, 1.8–3.5%) assessed by validated questions. There is a high degree of statistical heterogeneity in the studies included, and the data from the Global South are still sparse. Thus, this study highlights the need for more uniformly acquired data internationally to calculate a precise and robust global HS prevalence, which GHiSA is aiming toward, and to increase our understanding of the characteristics of HS.
