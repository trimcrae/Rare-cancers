## Abstract

## Background
One‐third of the global rheumatic heart disease burden lies in sub‐Saharan Africa, where 17% of patients with severe rheumatic heart disease die within 3 years without valve surgery. Surgery is often considered uneconomical, although this assumption is not grounded in cost‐effectiveness analyses.

## Methods
We evaluated the cost‐effectiveness of mechanical valve replacement, bioprosthetic valve replacement, and valve repair compared with medical management for patients with severe rheumatic heart disease in Rwanda. Using a Markov model, we simulated disease progression, incorporating transition probabilities from a meta‐analysis of regional observational studies. Costs were calculated using microcosting, and health effects were measured in disability‐adjusted life‐years (DALYs). Cost‐effectiveness was defined by a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of United States dollars (USD) $2307 per DALY, which is 3 times Rwanda's gross domestic product per capita. A 3% discount rate for costs and DALYs was applied.

## Results
Surgical strategies extended life expectancy from 7 to 11 to 13 years. Mechanical and bioprosthetic valve replacements were cost‐effective compared with medical management. Mechanical valve replacement was most efficient, averting 5.64 discounted DALYs for a lifetime discounted cost of USD $10 539.34, with an incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio of USD $1704 per DALY. Bioprosthetic valve replacement averted 5.27 discounted DALYs but incurred higher costs (USD $10 873.06). Valve repair only averted 3.30 DALYs despite being least expensive (USD $8790.19). Repair became the most efficient surgical strategy if valve degeneration rates dropped <3.7% annually.

## Conclusions
Mechanical valve replacement is the most efficient surgical strategy for severe rheumatic heart disease in sub‐Saharan Africa, challenging assumptions that surgery is uneconomical. Reducing surgery costs and optimizing anticoagulation could further enhance cost‐effectiveness.

## Clinical Perspective

## What Is New?

Mechanical valve replacement is shown to be the most efficient surgical intervention in a health economic model for rheumatic heart disease in Rwanda, averting the most disability‐adjusted life‐years compared with medical management, and at a cost acceptable to the local health care system.

## What Are the Clinical Implications?

Sensitivity analyses revealed that valve repair may become the most efficient surgical strategy if postoperative valve degeneration rates are low. A prospective cohort study of both bioprosthetic valve replacement and valve repair surgery recipients in Rwanda would help ascertain what these rates are among a contemporary cohort of patients, providing definitive evidence for or against this surgical strategy in a resource‐limited setting.

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) disproportionately affects countries in sub‐Saharan Africa. In 2019, the region alone accounted for 11 of the 33 million prevalent cases worldwide. Patients progress from asymptomatic disease to symptomatic disease because chronic inflammation from untreated streptococcal pharyngitis causes irreversible damage to heart valves. This damage can result in congestive heart failure, atrial arrhythmias, and thromboembolism. At severe stages of the disease, patients struggle with basic activities of daily living, resulting in >1 million disability‐adjusted life‐years (DALYs) lost. Without surgical intervention to correct diseased valves, the short‐term case fatality rate is 17% in 3 years. 

There is a long waiting list of patients requiring cardiac surgery for RHD in African countries due to the dearth of programs that can provide cardiac surgery. In 2020, there was 1 cardiac center per 38 million people in sub‐Saharan Africa, compared with 1 center per 120 000 people in North America. As a result, although 60% of patients in Uganda with RHD required invasive valvular intervention, a mere 8% received surgery. Medical management is often the only available strategy for patients, because surgery is either unavailable or seen as a resource‐intensive intervention incompatible with the constraints of limited health care budgets. , Establishing cardiac surgery centers to provide surgery requires investment in training surgeons and support staff, as well as the creation of regional supply chains to source affordable perioperative equipment. Although these costs are not trivial, failure to provide the means to intervene in advanced stages of RHD is a death sentence. In contrast, surgical replacement or repair of the damaged valves can offer significant gains in survival and quality of life.
There are 3 main types of open valvular surgery performed for RHD, each with different sequelae to consider. Mechanical valve replacement offers a highly durable solution but requires life‐long anticoagulation administration and management that can be particularly challenging in resource‐limited settings. Inadequate anticoagulation management can be lethal, leading to stroke, major bleeding, and valve thrombosis. In contrast, bioprosthetic valve replacement does not require long‐term anticoagulation but is susceptible to high rates of valve degeneration in young patients, with an estimated 33% of patients requiring reoperation in 10 years. Valve repair, while avoiding many of the adverse outcomes seen in valve replacements, is often technically challenging due to the complexity of the lesions in RHD and can be prone to rapid valve degeneration if attempted. , However, it has been performed in 20% of mitral valve surgeries in Africa and at comparable rates to bioprosthetic valve replacement in Rwanda. , Therefore, valve repair remains an important surgical approach to evaluate in the decision‐analysis model.
Currently, there is no known formal assessment comparing the cost‐effectiveness of surgical intervention strategies against watchful waiting with medical management in Africa. However, this information would provide valuable evidence to inform investments into national cardiac surgery programs worldwide. , As such, the objective of this study was to conduct a type of cost‐effectiveness analysis (CEA) comparing 3 surgical strategies to medical management using DALYs as the measure of effectiveness, known as a cost‐utility analysis. We focus our analysis on Rwanda due to its high prevalence of RHD, long history of valvular surgery in partnership with foreign teams, and desire to increase investment in its domestic cardiac surgery program.

## METHODS

## Model Overview
The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its online supplementary files. In this study, we assessed medical costs and benefits from the perspective of a Rwandan public payer. We created a model to simulate the progression of a patient cohort through medical and varied surgical treatment strategies for severe RHD. We chose a Markov model over a microsimulation approach, because most of the existing data lack the granularity needed for microsimulation to accurately predict the effects of specific patient‐level characteristics on transition probabilities. We started the model with patients at age 25 years, because this is the average age that patients in Rwanda receive surgery. Patients can experience various complications according to the transition probabilities noted in Table  , impacting their lifetime health care costs and quality of life. For each strategy, we included the most common and clinically significant complications. All health states are mutually exclusive. In addition, patients had an age‐specific baseline mortality rate for Rwanda, which was incorporated into all strategies (Table  ). The model is run for 60 years until age 85 years, after which the background age‐specific probability of dying is 100%. We used 1‐month cycle lengths and 3% discounting for future costs and DALYs. Because Rwanda has no formal willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) threshold, we followed the guidelines from the World Health Organization to use 3 times the gross domestic product per capita as a benchmark for the WTP, which equates to United States dollars (USD) $2307 per DALY using the most recent estimate of gross domestic product in 2022. , 

 

## Model Structure
We considered the possible treatment strategies for severe RHD, which include watchful waiting with medical management, mechanical valve replacement, bioprosthetic valve replacement, and valve repair. Medical management was the baseline strategy in the model. The health states in the medical management arm consist of managed RHD, congestive heart failure, stroke, and death. Patients in the managed state are either asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic (New York Heart Association class I or II) and have no complications. In contrast, patients in the congestive heart failure health state are in New York Heart Association class III or IV. Although atrial fibrillation is an important complication in RHD, we did not include it as a separate health state because it has significant overlap with congestive heart failure. , However, one of the major morbidities from atrial fibrillation is stroke. To make sure this is accounted for, we included the contribution of stroke from atrial fibrillation in our overall stroke rate.
The health states in patients receiving mechanical valve replacement also include managed RHD, congestive heart failure, stroke, and death. Additionally, patients can experience valve thrombosis and major bleeding as transient complications that can be treated with medical management or surgery. Patients with valve thrombosis receiving surgical treatment would undergo a valve reoperation with a second mechanical prosthesis. Health states in patients undergoing bioprosthetic valve replacement and valve repair are managed RHD, valve degeneration, stroke, and death. Valve degeneration is the dominant cause of heart failure in these strategies, so we did not separate congestive heart failure into a separate health state to avoid double counting. Given varying definitions for valve degeneration, in our analysis we considered patients in the valve degeneration state as needing intervention. They can ideally receive reoperation if available or can be suboptimally managed with medical treatment. We assumed all patients who undergo reoperation will have a mechanical prosthesis. As a result, all reoperated patients will transition to the mechanical valve arm and be subject to its associated complications. A summary schematic of the model for each strategy is shown in Figure  through .
 There are some key assumptions made in our model. To enable comparability among patients, we assumed that medical and surgical patients start at the same severity of heart failure before any intervention. We assume that after surgery, patients with congestive heart failure enter the asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic RHD managed stage. All patients in the surgical arm are eligible for any type of surgery. Because reoperation in case of valve thrombosis or valve degeneration currently occurs at exceedingly low frequencies in sub‐Saharan Africa, we assumed the capacity to perform reoperations exists at the levels suggested by published literature. If a patient requires reoperation, then they receive mechanical valve replacement. To calculate the medical costs, we assumed that medications, laboratory results, and imaging necessary for treatment according to expert opinion and established guidelines were available and administered to patients. Last, RHD most commonly affects the mitral valve. However, in low‐ and middle‐income countries such as Rwanda, patients typically present late in the disease course, and ≥2 valves may be affected and require surgical intervention. Published data on RHD surgery in sub‐Saharan Africa suggest approximately 50% and 40% receive single and double valve operations, respectively. To account for this nuance, our transition probabilities and costs assume patients receive an average of 1.5 valves per operation.

## Model Inputs
Table  provides a detailed summary of all model input parameters and their sources. Transition probabilities are presented as monthly values and were obtained from published observational studies. These studies were identified in the PubMed and African Journals Online databases using keywords “rheumatic heart disease”/“heart valve disease,” “valvular surgery”/“valve repair”/“valve replacement,” “surgical outcomes,” and “Africa”/“low‐ and middle‐income country.” Additionally, the references in relevant articles were used to identify more publications. The search was conducted reiteratively between November 2023 and March 2024. To ensure that the search was comprehensive, articles were cross‐checked with a recent systematic review that identified all articles on open heart valvular surgery in low‐ and middle‐income countries published between January 2012 and September 2022 from the Embase and Cochrane libraries. The relevance of identified articles was performed through an initial abstract screen by the first authors. Articles with small total sample sizes (n<80), inadequate follow‐up (<80%), or those that did not provide the relevant data required were excluded. Two cardiothoracic surgeons who are currently operating or have operated in Rwanda evaluated the clinical appropriateness of studies that passed the initial screen. We prioritized observational studies from Rwanda and other sub‐Saharan African countries where possible. If no studies were available, we identified studies from other low‐ and middle‐income countries with comparable patient demographics. A summary of the studies that were used is provided in Table  . For medical management, we relied on the REMEDY (Global Rheumatic Heart Disease Registry) study. This is the largest prospective study of RHD, in which 12 out of 14 participating countries were in sub‐Saharan Africa. For surgical strategies where there were multiple appropriate studies for 1 parameter, we performed a meta‐analysis of the available probabilities using a random‐effects model and double arcsine transformation for proportions (Table  ). Hartung‐Knapp adjustment was applied to account for expected heterogeneity among a small number of studies. Heterogeneity testing was performed with a significance level set to α=0.05. The analysis was performed in RStudio version 2023 12.1. Rarely were quality studies available from sub‐Saharan Africa or comparable low‐income settings. In these cases, we used relevant international meta‐analyses for these parameters. This was only required for 2 parameters, the probability of death after receiving medical management or surgery for valve thrombosis, respectively. The probabilities of relevant individual studies were used to inform the minimum and maximum values possible in low‐resourced settings for the sensitivity analyses. For minimum or maximum values that could not be found in the literature, a wide range was used to reflect this uncertainty.
Surgery and complication costs were calculated from microlevel costs for medications, laboratory tests, imaging, and procedures obtained from King Faisal Hospital. This hospital was chosen as the source of financial information, because it is the only tertiary level hospital in Rwanda that performs cardiac surgery. Microlevel costs were combined into intervention‐level estimates based on expected resource usage. Aggregate costs per intervention are presented in Table  and converted from Rwandan francs into USDs using the exchange rate on January 1, 2024 (USD $1=Rwandan francs 1262). Detailed microlevel costs are available in Tables  through . We used 50% to 150% of the intervention‐level cost in the sensitivity analyses except for acute and chronic stroke costs that were derived from published literature.
Health utility scores that describe morbidity of different health states were obtained from the Institute of Health Metrics and Evaluation Global Burden of Disease Study 2019. The 95% CIs from this study were used for the sensitivity analysis. Half‐cycle correction was applied when running the model.

## Model Validity
To ensure internal validity of our model, we examined the logical consistency of results after varying key parameters that have known relationships with cost or outcomes. We also used edge cases where probabilities were set to 0% or 100% to ensure the model behaved as expected. Model versions were run by 2 independent analysts to ensure they produced the same results. Data validity was fostered by choosing model assumptions consistent with the Rwandan setting and literature from Rwanda or that shared similarities in resource availability or patient population. Last, the model went through reiterative critique by experts in health economics, the local cardiothoracic surgeon, and international cardiothoracic surgeons with extensive operative experience in Rwanda. Their critical feedback refined early versions of the model, informed key health states, and ensured that inputs and results were consistent with reality. They also reviewed assumptions to make sure they were appropriate. This study was approved by the Stanford Institutional Review Board (eProtocol 68 620) and the Rwanda National Ethics Committee (institutional review board number 00001497). Because this study uses secondary data, no informed consent was required.

## Statistical Analysis
We performed 1‐way sensitivity analyses of parameters that were either uncertain or likely to impact the model results. For parameters that affected the cost‐effectiveness result, we identified the value at which this change would occur. Given the potential effect of our model assumptions on the decision, we also conducted a scenario analysis by varying these assumptions and assessing their impact on the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER) of the various strategies. We varied the starting age, considered time horizons <60 years, used a worse utility after surgery to simulate suboptimal surgical outcomes, and adjusted the probability of getting a reoperation after valve degeneration. The latter is an important consideration in Rwanda, where reoperations are currently infrequent but could improve over time. For the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, we used β distributions for probabilities and health utilities. The distributions were defined by a mean and SE corresponding to the base value and the SE calculated in the meta‐analysis. If there was a singular appropriate study, SE was directly calculated from the probability and sample size of the studies. For health utilities, the SE was derived from the 95% CI provided by the Global Burden of Disease Study. Given that costs were directly calculated, we used a triangular distribution for costs defined by the most likely minimum and maximum costs. The distributions were inspected to ensure they matched the behavior and expected range of the parameter. The analysis was performed with 10 000 simulations.

## Model Outputs
The Markov model was constructed and analyzed using TreeAge Pro (health care version) version 2024. The primary outcomes of this analysis were the ICERs between medical management and the surgical strategies. We first report the ICERs of each strategy compared with medical management as a baseline to address the question of whether any of the strategies are cost‐effective at the WTP threshold. Subsequently, we compared all strategies to each other simultaneously on the cost‐effectiveness plane to identify the optimal strategy at the WTP threshold. A checklist for cost‐effectiveness analysis in global surgery is included in Table  to compare the research methodology with recommended standards.

## RESULTS
Table  shows that all surgical strategies accrued fewer DALYs compared with medical management. Although medical management was the least costly strategy, with total discounted lifetime cost of USD $933.67, it produced 21.39 discounted DALYs. Additionally, medical management was also associated with an average life expectancy of 7 years from the starting age compared with 11 to 13 years for surgical strategies. In contrast, mechanical valve replacement surgery produced 15.75 discounted DALYs for a lifetime cost of USD $10 539.34. The ICER compared with medical management was USD $1704 per DALY, which is below the WTP threshold of USD $2307 per DALY. Compared with medical management, bioprosthetic valve replacement but not valve repair was cost‐effective, with ICERs of USD $1884.91 per DALY and USD $2382.47 per DALY, respectively.
 When comparing surgical strategies, bioprosthetic valve replacement accrued more DALYs and at a higher cost than mechanical valve replacement. Valve repair had the lowest discounted total lifetime costs at USD $8790.19 but had the lowest incremental effectiveness with 18.09 discounted DALYs. Overall, mechanical valve replacement was the most economical surgical strategy, because it optimized incremental cost and effectiveness beneath Rwanda's WTP threshold. All 4 strategies are plotted on the cost‐effectiveness plane in Figure  .
 

## Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis
When the uncertainty of all parameters was considered simultaneously, the order of mean incremental effectiveness was still mechanical valve replacement, bioprosthetic valve replacement, and then repair (Table  ). However, valve repair had wide 95% uncertainty intervals for both costs (USD $4756.56–USD $12 972.87) and DALYs (11.20–23.72), although the mean ICER compared with medical management was the lowest among the surgical strategies by a small margin at USD $1164.21 per DALY (95% uncertainty interval, $1145.65–$1183.26). The mean ICERs for mechanical valve replacement and bioprosthetic valve replacement were USD $1287.99 per DALY (95% uncertainty interval, $1276.53–$1299.63) and USD $1294.18 per DALY (95% uncertainty interval, $1283.18–$1305.31). At the WTP threshold, the probabilistic sensitivity analysis showed that medical management, mechanical valve replacement, valve repair, and bioprosthetic valve replacement were cost‐effective 0.5%, 35.1%, 46.4%, and 19.0% of the time, respectively (Figure  ).
 

## Sensitivity Analyses
For mechanical valve replacement, there were 3 parameters that increased the ICER compared with medical management above the WTP threshold as shown by the tornado diagram in Figure  . These included the cost of mechanical valve replacement, the probability of postoperative long‐term stroke, and the probability of postoperative congestive heart failure. Increased costs of mechanical valve replacement and higher probabilities of long‐term stroke or heart failure increased the ICER. Increased RHD burden indicated by higher probabilities of baseline and new‐onset congestive heart failure among medically managed patients decreased the ICER, making mechanical valve replacement more cost‐effective than medical management. When compared with mechanical valve replacement, bioprosthetic valve replacement becomes a cost‐effective alternative at the WTP threshold if the probability of perioperative stroke decreases from 5.2% to 0.7% or if the costs of the initial surgery were to decrease from USD $8860 to USD $7634.
 For valve repair surgery, when the valve degeneration rate of repair decreases to ≤0.65% per month, the ICER compared with medical management became cost‐effective at the WTP threshold (Figure  ). Similarly, valve repair was no longer a suboptimal strategy at lower rates of valve degeneration. At the threshold value of ≤0.31% valve degeneration per month, valve repair became the optimal surgical approach (Figure  ).
 In our scenario analyses, mechanical valve replacement was no longer cost‐effective compared with medical management at the WTP threshold if the time horizon was ≤21 years (Figure  ). Similarly, if patients receive mechanical valve replacement at the age of ≥37 years, it is no longer cost‐effective (Figure  ). If we assume worse health utility after surgery, the ICER of mechanical valve replacement versus medical management increased to USD $3292 per DALY, which is above the WTP threshold. In this scenario, mechanical valve replacement was still the most efficient surgical strategy (Figure  ). Last, if we assume that patients do not have access to repeat surgeries in bioprosthetic valve replacement and valve repair surgery, both strategies become less expensive but with fewer DALYs averted. Bioprosthetic valve replacement will cost USD $10 060 for 17.1 discounted DALYs, and valve repair will cost USD $6828 for 20.7 discounted DALYs. In contrast, with 100% access to repeat surgeries, valve repair becomes the favored approach over mechanical valve and bioprosthetic valve replacement, with an ICER of USD $1456 per DALY (Figure  ). When the probability of receiving a reoperation is ≥24%, valve repair becomes cost‐effective compared with medical management (Figure  ).

## DISCUSSION
This is the first known CEA for RHD in sub‐Saharan Africa that evaluates surgical strategies compared with medical management. Due to this sparsity of existing literature, there has been limited use of economic evaluations in cardiac surgery resource allocation in Rwanda despite the high prevalence of RHD and the advanced presentation of this disease to the health care system. Historically, valvular surgery was performed by visiting surgical teams, and the types of surgeries performed were influenced by factors such as age, sex, disease severity, surgeon skill, and the discretion of visiting operative teams. , However, over time, with pivotal support from the Rwandan government, the health care workforce has continued to gain independence. In 2020, Rwanda's first cardiac surgeon began operating, and by 2022, he successfully led 3 mitral valve replacement surgeries alongside a full local team. As such, it has become the government's priority to continue expanding surgical capacity for RHD, and the results of this CEA grounded within the realities of complex trade‐offs associated with different surgical strategies can be used as an evidence‐based guide for future investment. Additionally, it highlights approaches that will decrease costs and increase effectiveness of surgery.
Prior CEAs for RHD in Africa have primarily focused on comparing primary and/or secondary prevention strategies. , , , Although prevention is an important strategy, there is a simultaneous need to build capacity for patients with severe RHD who need surgery to survive. Our study found that compared with medical management, mechanical valve replacement and bioprosthetic valve replacement are cost‐effective. All surgical interventions led to significantly greater life expectancy compared with medical management. Although watchful waiting with medical management is inexpensive, it has abysmal outcomes, with an average life expectancy of 7 years after symptoms of congestive heart failure. This life expectancy may be even lower among patients in the most severe stages of heart failure, a level of severity commonly observed among surgical candidates in Rwanda. Valve repair was the most inexpensive strategy but was not cost‐effective compared with medical management at the WTP threshold due to high rates of valve degeneration and subsequent mortality. However, despite these complications, it still averted more DALYs than medical management.
The prevailing assumption has been that surgery is not cost‐effective in sub‐Saharan Africa; however, this study directly challenges that notion and presents concrete strategies to further improve cost‐effectiveness. For example, the cost of mechanical valve replacement significantly affected its cost‐effectiveness. Initially, the cardiac surgery program in Rwanda received equipment and valves directly from US‐based suppliers through foreign visiting teams. , , However, now that the domestic capacity to perform surgery has increased, local supply chains are being established to reduce the cost of surgical supplies. For example, the cardiac program in Rwanda has been able to source less expensive valves at USD $1200 instead of USD $3500, representing significant reductions in the initial cost of mechanical valve replacement. Additionally, the increase and consistency in case volume will facilitate economies of scale and bolster economic bargaining power, lowering the cost of surgery even further. For example, cardiac surgery centers in East Africa have formed a collaborative and successfully negotiated less expensive perfusion supplies, showing the transformative impact of regional collective action. Using these strategies across sub‐Saharan Africa will serve to make cardiac surgery scalable, bridging the inequitable gap in essential treatment.
It is imperative to keep the rates of postoperative complications low to maximize the effectiveness of surgery. In our sensitivity analysis, when mechanical valve replacement resulted in stroke and congestive heart failure incidence rates >2.9% and >2.3% per year, respectively, it was no longer cost‐effective compared with medical management. This is concerning, given that the base case stroke rates were not much lower than this threshold at 2.4% per year. Most postoperative patients in Rwanda have public health insurance and receive their follow‐up care in public district hospitals closer to their place of residence. However, patients may still need to travel long distances to these health centers and are not always able to attend monthly international normalized ratio (INR) testing appointments for anticoagulation monitoring due to financial and time limitations. Additionally, INR testing strips and reagents have historically been out of stock in rural health centers. In 2020, only 30% of postoperative patients were within their target INR range. Despite these limitations in maintaining therapeutic range, the durability of mechanical valves still offered advantages over bioprosthetic valve replacement and valve repair surgeries. However, these advantages can be further increased by preventing disabling strokes. There are already unique initiatives taking place in Rwanda in service of this aim such as nurse‐led anticoagulation clinics partnering with community health workers who visit patient homes if they miss follow‐up appointments. , Through collaborative efforts between the Rwanda Biomedical Center within the Ministry of Health and nongovernmental organizations, supply chains for anticoagulation medication and INR monitoring equipment have also strengthened over time to prevent stock‐outs. , Last, Rwanda has established a national cardiac registry that should enhance tracking of postoperative patients so that they are not lost to follow‐up and can receive timely intervention for any complications. Of note, mechanical valve surgery is not cost‐effective at time horizons of ≤21 years. With a shorter time interval, insufficient DALYs have been averted to compensate for the high initial costs of surgery, emphasizing both the humanitarian and health economic importance of supporting patients throughout their lifetime so that they do not die prematurely from preventable long‐term complications.
In our analysis, repair and bioprosthetic valve replacement accrued more DALYs compared with mechanical valve replacement due to mortality and morbidity associated with valve degeneration, making them less favorable surgical strategies. This contrasts with a CEA on surgical strategies for RHD conducted in India. In that analysis, the complications of mechanical valve replacement made repair and bioprosthetic valve replacement more favorable. This difference is likely because they use significantly lower rates of valve degeneration compared with our study. Their valve failure rates were a maximum of 1% and 5.4% per year for bioprosthetic valve replacement and repair, respectively, compared with valve degeneration rates of 2.3% and 8.1%, respectively, per year in this study. Young patients with valvular interventions tend to have high inflammatory activity leading to elevated rates of degeneration. The lower rates of valve degeneration in repair patients in India may reflect differences in disease burden and surgical experience with performing challenging repairs. Additionally, the authors highlighted that their bioprosthetic valve replacement population received new generation Carpentier‐Edwards valves, which may have better long‐term performance. , 

The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that if valve degeneration rates for valve repair decrease to <3.7% annually, it becomes the preferred strategy. Successful repair would be a beneficial strategy in the region, because it costs less than valve replacement and does not require long‐term anticoagulation. This is exemplified by the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, which showed that the comparative cost‐effectiveness of valve repair is highly variable. In instances where valve degeneration rates are low, costs of valve repair surgery are at a minimum, and there are high bleeding‐ or thromboembolic‐related complications associated with poor INR management, valve repair may be the preferred choice. However, achieving such low rates in this setting may not be feasible, necessitating repeat interventions. Additionally, there are other challenges to consider, such as the need for formal echocardiography surveillance systems and the capacity for repeat surgeries, which is just starting to grow. As shown by our scenario analysis, if there are no repeat operations, patients either die or live with a suboptimal quality of life, further decreasing the cost‐effectiveness of valve repair. As a result, it may be both unethical and a poor allocation of resources to perform these surgeries without the infrastructure to monitor and intervene on degenerated valves. Instead, efforts to prevent anticoagulation‐associated complications may be more fruitful.

## Limitations
The main limitation of this study is that the input parameters for transition probabilities are derived from observational studies rather than clinical trials due to the absence of direct, comparative studies on this topic. We sourced transition probabilities directly from published literature in Rwanda but often had to extend our search to other countries in the region to fill in the data gaps. However, we selectively included studies from the sub‐Saharan African region such that estimates for Rwanda are likely to fall within the sensitivity ranges used in this study. Moreover, most published data do not provide the level of granularity necessary to account for patient‐level heterogeneity in transition probabilities, which limits our ability to use more nuanced decision‐analysis models, such as a microsimulation model. We chose the Markov model to ground our analyses with solid input data while still providing meaningful, informative outputs. Nevertheless, the analysis would be enhanced by replacing base case values in our model with actual outcomes from a prospective study of medically managed and postoperative RHD in patients in Rwanda using a more granular decision‐analysis model.
Additionally, we used health utilities from the Global Burden of Disease study, but Rwandan patients may have different health preferences that result in lower postoperative health utility scores. In our scenario analysis, we assessed the impact of a lower utility score. If the postoperative health disutility is 0.26 as reported by older UK patients after valvular surgery, the ICER of mechanical valve replacement compared with medical management is above the WTP threshold. Directly assessing quality of life with a survey using a culturally congruent instrument will enhance the analysis.
We made some assumptions that are a departure from reality to streamline the model. For example, we assumed that patients could not have 2 complications at the same time. Although it is possible for a patient to have both stroke and congestive heart failure, the probability of having 2 concurrent complications is relatively low enough to omit. We excluded atrial fibrillation as a health state to avoid double counting complications due to its significant overlap with congestive heart failure. We also assumed that all patients are eligible for all 3 surgery types. However, factors such as surgeon or patient preference, patient clinical characteristics, and the capacity to perform a particular surgery may affect the choice in a nonrandom manner. Last, we did not consider nonmedical costs and benefits in this CEA, such as gains or losses in productivity, technological advancement, and training opportunities for health care personnel.

## CONCLUSIONS
Cardiac surgery for RHD is a worthwhile investment. Although the initial cost of mechanical valve replacement for RHD can be high, the quality‐of‐life benefits are so remarkable that they compensate for these expenses. Although bioprosthetic valve replacement and repair surgery were found to be economically suboptimal compared with mechanical valve replacement, the surgical landscape is constantly evolving with advancements in technology and expertise that may make it possible to reach low valve degeneration targets. Additionally, as countries scale up echocardiographic screening, the proportion of patients with lesions amenable to successful repair may also increase. This CEA arms policymakers in Rwanda and the wider region with data that highlight the urgent need to continue allocating resources toward building and growing sustainable domestic cardiac surgery programs. This should also include investment in anticoagulation management and postoperative rehabilitation to maximize the long‐term benefits of surgery.
