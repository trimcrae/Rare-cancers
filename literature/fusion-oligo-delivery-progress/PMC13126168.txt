## Abstract
Alternative splicing (AS) is a fundamental process that
dramatically
expands the proteomic diversity of eukaryotes. The precise regulation
of AS is governed by the complex interplay between cis-regulatory elements in the pre-mRNA and trans-acting
protein factors. Recently, RNA secondary structures have emerged as
critical players in the regulatory landscape. Among these, G-quadruplexes
(G4s), which are stable four-stranded structures formed by guanine-rich
(G-rich) sequences, have been identified as potent regulators of splicing
outcomes. This review synthesizes the current understanding of the
role of RNA G4s in AS, focusing on three interconnected pillars: (1)
the characteristic sequence features and genomic distribution of G4-forming
motifs near splice sites revealing a significant strand-specific
enrichment that suggests a conserved regulatory function; (2) the
multifaceted interactions between G4 structures and RNA-binding proteins
which can act as splicing enhancers or repressors by binding to single-stranded
G-tracts or structured G4s; and (3) the profound impact of G4-stabilizing
or -destabilizing small molecules, which can be harnessed to deliberately
re-engineer splicing patterns in genes linked to cancer, neurodegeneration,
and other diseases. By integrating findings from genomic analyses,
biochemical studies, and chemical biology approaches, this review
aims to expound G4 structures as dynamic, druggable nodes in the splicing
regulatory network, offering novel mechanistic insights and promising
therapeutic avenues.
Keywords: alternative splicing, g-quadruplex, G4-related
proteins, G4 ligands

## Introduction
Most protein-coding genes in eukaryotes
are interrupted by introns,
which must be precisely removed from premature RNA (pre-mRNA) through
the process of splicing. This essential
reaction is catalyzed by the spliceosome, a massive and dynamic ribonucleoprotein
complex composed of five small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (U1, U2,
U4, U5, and U6 snRNPs) and numerous associated proteins. The spliceosome recognizes conserved sequence
elements at the intron-exon boundaries: the 5′ splice site
(5′ss) with a GU dinucleotide, the 3′ splice site (3′ss)
with an AG dinucleotide, a branch point sequence containing an adenine
residue for the first transesterification reaction, and a polypyrimidine
tract (PPT) upstream of the 3′ss. Through two transesterification
steps, the intron is excised as a lariat and the exons are ligated
to form the mature mRNA. In the first
step, the 2′ hydroxyl group of the conserved adenosine in the
branch point attacks the phosphodiester bond at the 5′ss, resulting
in the release of the upstream exon with a free 3′–OH
group and the formation of a lariat intermediate. In the second step,
the 3′–OH group of the upstream exon attacks the phosphodiester
bond at the 3′ ss, leading to exon ligation and the release
of the intron lariat. The excised intron is subsequently degraded
by cellular nucleases. Through these two transesterification reactions,
exons are precisely and efficiently joined to generate mature mRNA
molecules.
The splicing process is complex and highly regulated.
The complexity
of eukaryotic genomes, however, is not fully captured by constitutive
splicing. A widespread phenomenon known as alternative splicing (AS)
allows a single pre-mRNA to be processed in multiple ways, leading
to the inclusion or exclusion of exons or the retention of introns.
−

 This process, which occurs in approximately 92–94% of human
genes, is a primary driver of proteomic
diversity and functional complexity. AS is classified into several
types, including exon skipping, mutually exclusive exons, alternative
5′ss and 3′ss usage, and intron retention. The regulation of AS is a finely tuned process
dependent on the interplay between core splice site signals, auxiliary cis-regulatory elements, and trans-acting
factors. These auxiliary signals, termed splicing-regulatory elements
(SREs), including exon splicing enhancers (ESEs), exon splicing silencers
(ESSs), intronic splicing enhancers (ISEs), intronic splicing silencers
(ISSs) ( A).
The trans-acting factors most notably are serine/arginine-rich
(SR) proteins that generally promote splicing and heterogeneous nuclear
ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) that often repress it.
 Beyond linear sequence motifs, there is growing
appreciation for
the role of the RNA secondary structure in modulating the accessibility
of these elements to the splicing machinery.
−

 RNA structures can mask or expose splice sites and
protein-binding sites, adding a dynamic layer to the splicing code.
The diversity of RNA secondary structures can be comparable to that
of proteins, and their structures are mainly determined by the nucleotide
sequence. A hairpin (also known as a stem-loop) is the simplest RNA
secondary structure and is a motif frequently involved in AS. In a
hairpin structure, a single-stranded RNA molecule folds back on itself,
with locally inverted complementary sequences pairing to form a double-stranded
region (stem), while the unpaired region forms a loop. There are several
other secondary structures derived from the hairpin structure. The
internal loop, where unpaired nucleotides interrupt the stem of the
hairpin on both strands, while flanking regions remain paired. The
bulge, where unpaired nucleotides exist on only one strand within
the stem region of the hairpin. The pseudoknot structure is an interesting
motif in which an RNA hairpin loop and the loop nucleotides form a
Watson–Crick base pair with an adjacent strand. And when this
happens, these two duplexes stack on top of each other to form one
contiguous duplex, and their intervening strands snake through the
major and minor grooves.
These double-stranded complementary-pair-based
structures play
a crucial role in regulating pre-mRNA AS. Hairpins can directly influence
exon skipping through a “looping-out” mechanism, where
complementary base-pairing interactions between intronic regions cause
the intervening exon to be looped out, thereby promoting its exclusion.
,
 RNA hairpin structures can also act as RNA “bridges”
to facilitate long-range interactions, bringing distal regulatory
elements into close proximity with their target exons without the
participation of auxiliary proteins. For example, more than half of
RBFOX2 binding sites are located over 500 bp away from regulated exons,
and their regulatory effect depends on the formation of long-range
hairpin structures. Specific regions
of the hairpin also influence RNA-binding protein (RBP) binding. Enhancer
or silencer elements often exhibit stronger effects when located within
the loop compared to the stem, and the stem region may inhibit RBP
binding.
−

Aberrant AS regulation can lead to a variety
of diseases, including
cancer, neurodegenerative syndromes, and respiratory diseases. In recent years, with the rapid development of RNA biology
and genomics technologies, the role of AS in disease has been increasingly
revealed, making it a hot topic in drug development and precision
medicine research. Small-molecule splicing modulators (SMSMs) are
promising pharmacologic agents that can be applied in AS-related diseases.
There are mainly three types of SMSMs. The one regulates the splicing
process by targeting the formation of the spliceosome, such as SF3B1,
by blocking the assembly of snRNPs or disrupting the stability of
snRNPs. The SMSMs targeting SF3B4 have
been systematically studied to reveal a central pharmacophore represented
by a conjugated diene flanked by two functional moieties. The other
one regulates the splicing process by modulating splicing factors
(SFs), such as RBM39 degraders or SR
protein phosphorylation regulation. The
first two types of SMSMs both intervene in aberrant splicing processes
by targeting a splicing-related protein. The third approach directly
targets the aberrant splice sites of the mRNA to correct and compensate
for the abnormal splicing. This is also the drug target of interest
and focus in this work and will be introduced in subsequent sections.
Among the various RNA secondary structures, G-quadruplexes (G4s)
have garnered significant interest.
,
 Unlike the
hairpin structure, the formation of G4 structures does not rely on
traditional base complementary pairing. In nucleic acid sequences
rich in regularly repeating guanines, four guanine bases can form
a planar structure via Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding between each pair,
known as a G-quartet ( B). Multiple G-quartets stack via π–π interactions
to form a G-quadruplex. A typical G4
structure consists of three stacked G-quartets. However, recent studies
indicate that G-rich RNA can form more complex structures, including
G4s comprising three noncanonical G-quartets with irregular loops
or bulges, which significantly expanded
the abundance of potential G4 sequences on the genome and transcriptome.
G4s are stabilized by monovalent cations, particularly K+. Depending on the length and orientation of the loops, the number
of G-quartets comprising the G-quadruplex, and the nature of the central
cation, G-quadruplexes can adopt various topological conformations. For instance, DNA G-quadruplexes can form parallel,
antiparallel, or hybrid structures. In contrast, RNA G-quadruplexes,
constrained by the anticonformation of the glycosidic bond, typically
form parallel structures and exhibit higher thermal stability.
While DNA G4s in gene promoters and telomeres have been extensively
studied, RNA G4s (rG4s) are increasingly recognized for their roles
in post-transcriptional regulation, including mRNA localization, stability, translation, and, pivotally, splicing. One of the most distinctive features of G4, compared to
other nucleic acid secondary structures, is the presence of the large
G-quartet plane. This feature enables a large number of proteins to
interact with G4s in vitro and in vivo. These proteins control key
steps in numerous physiological or pathological pathways by recognizing
and acting upon G4s. Also, this characteristic allows for the design
of small molecules featuring similarly large aromatic planar surfaces
that can specifically recognize G4s through interactions with the
G-quartet. Such small molecules are termed G-quadruplex ligands (G4
ligands).
,
 These ligands typically bind to G4s via
end-stacking interactions with the G-quartet plane, groove binding,
or interactions with loop regions.
G4 in pre-mRNA plays a crucial
role in the regulation of AS. G4
may facilitate the splicing process by recruiting spliceosome-associated
RNA-binding proteins to specific splicing sites or may repress the
binding of AS factors by spatially masking single-stranded cis-regulatory elements. The objective of this article is
to provide a comprehensive review of the current evidence linking
rG4 structures to the regulation of AS. We will review three core
themes: first, the genomic and sequence evidence for G4 motifs as
conserved cis-regulatory elements near splice sites;
second, the diverse mechanisms by which G4s and their binding proteins
influence splicing decisions; and third, the transformative potential
of small molecules that target G4s to manipulate splicing for research
and therapeutic purposes. By integrating these perspectives, we aim
to establish a coherent framework for understanding G4s as actionable
players in the complex world of pre-mRNA splicing.

## The Colocalization of AS Sites and the G-Quadruplex
Genome-wide bioinformatic surveys have consistently revealed that
putative G-quadruplex-forming sequences (PQSs) are not randomly distributed
but are significantly enriched in functionally important regions,
including gene promoters, untranslated regions, and, most relevantly
for this review, near splice sites. The
potential for DNA or RNA to form a G4 structure is encoded in its
primary sequence, specifically the presence of G-tracts, typically
three or more consecutive guanines (G ≥ 3) separated by short
loops ( ). As
previously mentioned, the definition of AS sites is regulated by a
combination of cis-acting elements and trans-acting factors. The sequence features surrounding alternative splice
sites are critical determinants of the splicing efficiency and whether
an exon is incorporated into the mature mRNA. Bioinformatics analyses
of cis-acting elements in the splicing process have
identified relatively conserved sequence motifs. G-tracts play a central
role in the splicing of many critical cellular and viral genes, typically
by recruiting splicing regulators of the hnRNP family
−

 ( A). Studies
have shown that the G-tract is a hallmark feature of intronic splicing
enhancers. For instance, an intronic GGG repeat was shown to enhance
the splicing of an alternative intron of the chicken β-tropomyosin
pre-mRNA and in the human growth hormone ( B). Also, hnRNP H and F bind to G-rich sequences in exon 3B
of the proteolipid protein gene (PLP), which was
reported to regulate the AS ratio between the exon 3B-included PLP transcript and the exon 3B-skipped DM20 transcript ( C). Although the formation of a G4 was not
directly verified in these studies, when comparing these splicing-related
G-tract sites with the reported rG4-seq data, it is not difficult to find that these sites highly overlap with
the high-score G4 sites, strongly suggesting that the observed effects
might be mediated by higher-order structures rather than by single-stranded
motifs alone.
 The regulation of AS by G-tracts is relatively
conservative. The
work of SUSAN and colleagues has found that GGG repeats capable of
forming classical G4 structures play a decisive role in the definition
of 5′ and 3′ splice sites, which help define exon–intron
boundaries and overall splicing efficiency. In this work, the functional significance of these G-tracts was
demonstrated experimentally. An RNA oligonucleotide containing the
G-tracts motif (C GGG CC GGG A) was demonstrated to specifically inhibit
the splicing of α-globin intron 2 in an in vitro minigene assay.
A follow-up study provided evidence that these G-triplets might affect
the binding of U1 snRNP to the 5′ss through potential base-pairing
interaction. Hai et al. identified a
16-nucleotide G-tract element as the key responsive sequence through
which the apoptosis inducer Ro-31-8220 regulates AS of the Bcl-x gene. A genome-wide screening revealed 3374 exons in the human genome
containing the GGGGNNNNNGGGG motif. The authors experimentally validated
that G-tract-containing exons in several genes (e.g., RARG, LCAT,
and ALAS1) underwent splicing changes upon Ro-31-8220 treatment.
Furthermore, a landmark genome-scale analysis by Georgakopoulos-Soares
et al. provided compelling evidence for this colocalization. Their study found that a remarkable 31% of human
genes harbor at least one putative G4 motif within 100 nt of a splice
site. These motifs were significantly enriched near both 5′ss
and 3′ss, with a 4.06-fold enrichment at the 5′ss and
a 2.44-fold enrichment at the 3′ss. The bias was markedly more
pronounced on the nontemplate strand (the strand identical in sequence
to the pre-mRNA), showing a 5.56-fold enrichment at the 5′ss
compared to only a 2.38-fold enrichment on the template strand. This
strand asymmetry strongly implies a functional role for these sequences
at the RNA level, specifically during pre-mRNA processing such as
splicing, rather than at the DNA level during transcription. Furthermore,
this G4 enrichment was more evident near weak or suboptimal splice
sites. This suggests that G4 structures may serve as auxiliary regulatory
elements to reinforce or modulate the definition of splice junctions
that are not strongly defined by their core consensus sequences alone,
thereby adding structural information to the splicing code.
This genomic evidence is supported by a more targeted analysis
of promoter regions (TSS ±1 kb) across 18,217 human RefSeq genes. This study also highlighted a significant G-rich
bias on the nontemplate strand, particularly at the 5′ end
of the first intron. A key strength of this analysis was its attempt
to isolate the signal for potential G4 structures from those of other
known G-rich motifs (such as SP1 binding sites, CpG islands, and hnRNP
A/H binding sites). Even after computationally masking known linear
G-rich regulatory elements, there are approximately 16% of genes retained
with G-rich characteristics in this region. What is more, these regions
often featured multiple G-runs (≥4 per 100 bp), a hallmark
of high G4-forming potential. The conservation of these G-rich elements
across mice, chickens, and frogs underscores their potential evolutionary
and functional significance. These findings collectively suggest that
the regulatory function of these regions may depend more on their
structural conformation (i.e., the ability to form a G4) than on their
primary sequence alone.
In summary, the genomic landscape is
replete with G-rich sequences
strategically located near the splice sites. Their strand-specific
enrichment on the nontemplate strand, their prevalence near weak splice
sites, and their evolutionary conservation strongly support a model
wherein the formation of rG4 structures constitutes a widespread,
evolutionarily conserved layer of cis-regulatory
information for splicing control.

## Detailed Mechanisms and Functional Consequences of G4-Mediated
Splicing Regulation
The formation of G4s in pre-mRNA can
act as a decisive molecular switch, regulating AS. The effect is determined
by its precise location, its structural stability, and the specific trans-acting factors it recruits or excludes. This section
delves deeper into the mechanistic nuances and functional outcomes
elucidated by studies of specific genes and systems. presents the current findings
on the effects of G4s and their associated proteins and small molecules
on AS.
 

## G4s Act as Splicing Enhancers to Promote Exon Inclusion
A paradigm for this function is found in the FMR1 gene. Didiot et al. discovered that two G-quadruplex structures
within the FMRP binding site of FMR1 mRNA function
as potent ESEs. Crucially, their activity
was dependent on the formation of the G-quadruplex structure itself
( A). These
G4-ESEs participate in regulating AS of the FMR1 gene
by promoting exon inclusion. This creates a fascinating autoregulatory
loop, as the FMRP protein itself binds specifically to G4 RNA and was found to inhibit the splicing of exons
near the G4 structure, suggesting FMRP may fine-tune its own production
by modulating the splicing outcome initiated by binding its own mRNA
structure.
 The role of G4s as enhancers is also evident in
the CD44 gene, where a G4 structure located in the
intron adjacent to variable
exon 8 (V8) of CD44 (designated I8) regulates the
AS of this exon. Mutation of the I8-G4
structure resulted in a reduced retention of this exon. By replacing
the I8 sequence in a minigene construct with various other rG4-forming
sequences, the authors demonstrated that it is the G4 structure at
this site rather than the specific sequence of I8 that underlies this
phenomenon. Subsequently, through RNA pull-down assays, they found
that hnRNP F binds to the G4 structure in I8 and promotes the splicing
of the cassette exon in a G4 structure-dependent manner. Analysis
of CLIP-seq data revealed that among 13,616 hnRNP F binding regions,
3,304 contain potential G4 sites, accounting for 24% of total hnRNP
F binding sites, indicating that hnRNP F is a potential G4-binding
protein. The authors further confirmed the interaction between hnRNP
F and I8-G4 through I8-G4 pull-down assays. Knockdown of hnRNP F by
shRNA led to a reduced level of exon inclusion. Moreover, the addition
of TMPyP4 inhibited hnRNP F-induced inclusion of the CD44 v8 minigene
exon. These results further suggest that hnRNP F-mediated exon inclusion
depends on the formation of the G-quadruplex structure.

## G4s Act as Splicing Silencers to Induce Exon Skipping
Conversely, the G4 structures can also function as negative regulatory
elements, leading to the skipping of exons ( B). The effect often depends on the specific
proteins that engage with the structures. The role of G4s as a silencer
is evident in the BACE1 gene, where a G4 in exon
3 serves as a recruitment platform for hnRNP H. This interaction positively
regulates the production of the full-length, active BACE1 isoform while suppressing a shorter, inactive isoform generated
from an internal 5′ss. The functional
significance is profound, as knockdown of hnRNP H reduces full-length BACE1 and the secretion of Aβ, pointing to a direct
role for this G4-hnRNP H axis in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis.
The EWSR1 gene provides a compelling example where
a G4 structure is involved in promoting exon skipping to generate
an oncogenic fusion protein. hnRNP H1
binds to a G-rich sequence in intron 8 that forms a parallel rG4,
and this binding promotes the skipping of exon 8, yielding the in-frame EWS-FLI1 fusion mRNA. In this study, the
authors discussed in detail the thermal stability of the G4 sequence
involved in splicing regulation. CD melting experiments confirmed
that the melting temperatures (Tm) of this G4 structure
exceed 80 °C, indicating that the G4 structure possesses high
stability under physiological temperatures. The thermodynamic stability
of G4 structures varies depending on their sequences. For example,
in another study, two G4 sequences with Tm of 48.2
(±0.5) and 55.3 (±0.5) °C were found to influence the
AS of the CD133 gene. It is evident that the Tm values
of these three G4 sequences differ significantly. Usually, the stability
of G4 is determined in vitro without considering the influence of
proteins. Therefore, we cannot accurately evaluate the regulatory
role of G4s in AS, relying on the thermodynamic stability of G4 structures.
A similar skipping mechanism is observed in the RBM3 gene, where hnRNP H1 binds a G-rich sequence with G4 potential near
a “poison exon”, promoting its exclusion and thus upregulating
RBM3 expression. This study also found
enhanced binding of hnRNP H1 at low temperatures, suggesting that
environmental cues can modulate G4 stability or protein-RNA interactions
to influence splicing decisions. In this study, the authors found
that hNRNP H1 exhibits a strong interaction with the GGGG motif near
the 5′ splice site of the poison exon. Interestingly, at 32
°C, the binding of hNRNP H1 to the G-rich sequence was approximately
doubled compared to that at 37 °C. Moreover, this interaction
is crucial for skipping the poison exon at 32 °C. Removal of
this GGGG motif disrupts the rG4 structure and significantly increases
the inclusion rate of the poison exon, but this effect is not observed
at 37 °C. However, in this study, the authors did not conduct
measurements on the G4 thermodynamic stability at the two temperatures
or the thermodynamic and kinetic constants of its binding to the protein.
These aspects could be further investigated in subsequent studies.

## G4s Modulate the Splicing of Introns
The retention
of introns often leads to the production of mRNA containing PTCs.
NMD can recognize and degrade PTCs within the open reading frame so
as to prevent the accumulation of truncated protein products, which
could otherwise be toxic to the cell. G-quadruplexes formed by G-rich
sequences in intron 3 of the TP53 gene regulate the
AS of intron 2, thereby influencing the production of p53 mRNA isoforms
,
 ( C). Specifically,
the formation of G4 facilitates the correct excision of intron 2,
leading to an increase in the fully spliced FSp53 mRNA, which encodes
the full-length p53 protein and a decrease in the p53I2 mRNA that
retains intron 2 and encodes the N-terminally truncated Δ40p53
isoform. The fluorescence resonance energy transfer melting assay
showed that the treatment with the G4-stabilizing ligand 360A resulted
in an increased melting temperature (Tm), at which
fluorescence emission ceases due to unfolding of the G-quadruplex.
A 3-fold upregulation of FSp53 mRNA and a 40% reduction in p53I2 mRNA
levels were found with the 360A treatment, demonstrating that G4 stabilization
promotes accurate splicing of intron 2. This study highlights the
important role of the RNA secondary structure in long-range splicing
control and provides new insights into the concept of spatial conformational
regulation of pre-mRNA splicing.
Zizza et al. identified G-rich
sequences within introns 3 and 7 of the CD133 gene
capable of forming G4 structures. They demonstrated that stabilization
of these structures by G4 ligands such as RHPS4-induced aberrant splicing,
specifically intron retention in introns 3­(b) and 7, leading to the
production of transcripts containing PTCs. The CD melting assay showed that RHPS4 increased the Tm of 3­(b) G4 of about 17 and 34 °C at 2:1 and 4:1 ligand/DNA
ratio, respectively. And, the ligand-induced increase in the Tm of the intron 7 G4 was of about 12 and 23 °C at
2:1 and 4:1 ratios, respectively. This resulted in a significant downregulation
of CD133 protein expression and consequently impaired key cancer stem
cell properties, including self-renewal, migration, tumor initiation,
and metastatic potential in colon cancer.

## Co-transcriptional Folding and Kinetic Competition
A crucial consideration in G4-mediated splicing regulation is the
timing of G4 formation relative to transcription and splicing ( A). The study by
Brighton Samatanga et al. provided pivotal insight: hnRNP F binds
to nascent RNA during transcription, preventing G4 folding and ensuring
the G-tract remains accessible for splicing factors. If hnRNP F is added after transcription, when G4 has already
formed, it cannot bind or disrupt the structure. This demonstrates
a kinetic competition between protein binding and RNA folding. If
a G4 folds too quickly or too stably, it may become inaccessible to
proteins such as hnRNP F that function as splicing enhancers, potentially
leading to aberrant splicing outcomes. This model emphasizes that
the functional effect of a G4 sequence is not absolute but depends
on the dynamic interplay between its folding kinetics and the availability
of its cognate binding proteins.
 The dynamic nature of the rG4 structure is crucial
to its role
in AS regulation. Natasha J. Caplen and colleagues, through competitive
binding assays between hnRNP H1 and the G4-specific antibody BG4,
observed that increasing concentrations of hnRNP H1 completely abolished
BG4 binding, while increasing BG4 only partially inhibited hnRNP H1
binding. This indicates that hnRNP H1
is capable of binding both unstructured G-rich tracts and prefolded
G4 structures. In a follow-up study, the same research team provided
further experimental validation, demonstrating that hnRNP H1 facilitates
the transition of the EWSR1 exon 8 G-rich sequence
from a G-quadruplex to a non-G4 state through a noncatalytic destabilization
mechanism, thereby promoting exon skipping and modulating splicing. Based on experimental data including EMSA with
8-aza-7-deazaguanosine substitutions, CD spectroscopy, and FRET assays,
hnRNP H1 noncatalytically destabilizes the G4 structures formed by
G-rich sequences in EWSR1-exon 8, promoting a structural
transition from a G4 to a non-G4 state. This conformational change
facilitates the skipping of this exon during AS. These findings underscore
that the structural dynamics and thermodynamic stability of G-tract-containing cis-elements significantly influence the binding of splicing
regulators. It is plausible that under various physiological conditions
or in response to pathological stimuli or drug treatments, structural
alterations in these cis-regulatory elements could
modulate the selection of adjacent splice sites.

## Interplay with Other Regulatory Layers
G4s do not function
in isolation. An emerging layer of complexity is their interplay with
the epitranscriptomic mark N6-methyladenosine (m6A). Research has shown that early m6A deposition determines
the fate of transcripts in terms of splicing kinetics and AS ( B). For example,
using a nascent transcriptome-wide m6A sequencing method
(TNT-Seq), scientists discovered that early cotranscriptional m6A deposition near splice junctions promotes rapid splicing.
In contrast, m6A modifications within introns are associated
with slowly processed introns and AS events.
,
 There is a significant colocalization between putative G4 motifs
and m6A modification sites in human pre-mRNAs, particularly
near splice sites. This suggests a potential
synergy, where G4 structures might act as “structural signals”
to guide m6A deposition, which in turn could recruit reader
proteins that influence splice site selection. The finding that m6A motifs (“SAG”) often contain a single-nucleotide
loop (frequently an A), a feature also common in G4s, further supports
a potential mechanistic link. In HIV-1
RNA, the colocalization of m6A sites with G4s and the observation
that m6A mutation leads to hyper-splicing suggesting that
both features collaborate to fine-tune splicing efficiency. Future work is needed to disentangle whether
G4s recruit methyltransferases or if m6A modifications
stabilize or destabilize G4 structures to impact splicing factor binding.
Jana Kralovicova et al. demonstrated that G4 structures in INS intron 1 participate in a conformational equilibrium
with adjacent stem-loop structures, collectively modulating the splicing
efficiency of the intron.
,
 The presence of G4
influences local RNA secondary structure dynamics, thereby indirectly
regulating the binding of proteins such as hnRNP F/H and ultimately
affecting the level of intron 1 retention. This study highlights the
dynamic balance between G4 and other RNA secondary structure conformations,
constituting an important layer of splicing regulation.

## G4-Related Proteins and Their Impact on AS
The functional
consequence of a G4-forming sequence in pre-mRNA
is not intrinsic to the structure itself but is largely determined
by its interaction with specific RNA-binding proteins. These interactions
can stabilize or destabilize the G4, recruit or block the spliceosome,
and ultimately dictate whether a particular exon is included or skipped.
The hnRNP family, particularly hnRNP H and F, is a central protagonist
in this G4-mediated regulatory drama.

## hnRNP H and hnRNP F
hnRNP H and its closely related
paralogue hnRNP F are among the most well-characterized proteins that
interact with G-rich sequences. They typically bind to G-tracts via
their quasi-RNA recognition motifs and often function to promote exon
inclusion. As mentioned above, hnRNP
F and H are frequently recruited to G-rich sequences to regulate AS,
as seen in BACE1,
PLP, and TP53
 ( A). This recruitment can facilitate the assembly of
the spliceosome. It is worth mentioning that the function of G4 is
not always straightforward and can be determined by the competition
between different hnRNPs. This is exquisitely illustrated in the regulation
of CD40 AS, where hnRNP
F binds near the 5′ss of intron 6, promoting the inclusion
of exon 6; in contrast, hnRNP A1 and A2/B1 exert an opposite effect,
inhibiting exon 6 inclusion. In hnRNP F-deficient B cells, hnRNP A1/A2B1
binds more readily, tipping the balance toward exon skipping. It is
proposed that these proteins compete for binding to overlapping or
adjacent G-tracts on the pre-mRNA. This illustrates that the final
splicing outcome depends on the delicate balance between competing
hnRNPs that recognize similar sequence motifs but exert antagonistic
effects.
 The sequestration of functional hnRNP H by pathological
G4 structures
is also implicated in the disease. GGGGCC is located between the two
putative first noncoding exons of the gene C9ORF72 (C9), which is overexpressed in the brains of patients with amyotrophic
lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). GGGGCC
repeats form RNA G4s that aberrantly sequester hnRNP H, leading to
the dysregulated splicing of multiple hnRNP H target transcripts. It was found that compared with patients without
overexpression, the fibroblasts and astrocytes of C9 ALS patients
had an increase in rG4 and were more frequently colocalized with hnRNP
H. Importantly, they discovered multiple dysregulated splicing of
known hnRNP H target transcripts in the brains of C9 patients and
proved that this was related to the increase in insoluble hnRNP H/rG4
aggregates. This study suggests that the isolation of hnRNP H mediated
by rG4s may be a significant contributor to neurodegenerative diseases
of C9 ALS/FTD.

## The Critical Role of RNA Helicases
rG4s are stable
structures that can potentially impede access of the spliceosome or
other regulatory proteins. RNA helicases, such as DDX5 and DDX17,
are essential enzymes that resolve G4 structures, thereby facilitating splicing ( B). Work by Etienne Dardenne et al. found
that knockdown of DDX5/DDX17 or hnRNP H/F led to exon skipping events,
and these skipped exons were significantly enriched in downstream
G4 structures. Stabilizing G4s with the
ligand TMPyP4 induced the same skipping pattern, suggesting that unresolved
G4s act as structural barriers to splicing. DDX5/DDX17, through their
unwinding activity, are thought to melt these G4s, allowing proteins
such as hnRNP H/F to access their single-stranded G-tract binding
sites and promote exon inclusion. This model is consistent with the
findings that DDX5 resolves a G4 in the COL2A1 promoter, and the loss
of DDX5 leads to aberrant splicing of FN1 and PLOD2. These reports suggest
a broader role of G4 helicase in maintaining splicing fidelity.

## Other G4-Binding Proteins That Regulate AS
Wilms’
tumor 1-associating protein (WTAP) complex was reported
to regulate AS and polyadenylation through binding to potential G4-forming
sequences. Indirect regulation is also
possible. For instance, the long noncoding RNA MALAT1 contains three G4 structures in its 3′ end that specifically
bind the nucleolar protein Nucleolin (NCL), recruiting
it to nuclear speckles. Within these
speckles, NCL interacts with multiple SR proteins
(SRSF2, SRSF3, SRSF5, and SRSF11), which are core splicing regulators.
Thus, G4s in MALAT1 can influence the subnuclear
localization of a protein that modulates the activity of key splicing
factors, thereby indirectly influencing global splicing patterns.
The reported proteins specifically bind to G4, which, in addition
to participating in the regulation of AS, also indicates that G4 may
be related to AS regulation. Through the overexpression or knockdown
of certain G4-binding proteins and detection of changes of AS events
in the cells, various AS proteins have been found to participate in
the regulation of AS through the mediation of G4. For instance, AFF
family proteins (including AFF2/FMR2, AFF3/LAF4, and AFF4/AF5q31)
were reported to specifically bind to the G-quadruplex RNA structure
and regulate AS, particularly by inhibiting the inclusion of exons
containing the G-quadruplex. In the experiments
for identifying G4-related proteins, various proteins related to AS
have also been discovered. For instance, Zhou et al. enriched a series
of proteins involved in pre-mRNA splicing through the G-quadruplex-interacting
proteins specific biotin-ligation procedure (PLGPB), and found that
a splicing factor SF3B4 acts as a G4 binding protein. The G4 ligand pyridostatin (PDS) was demonstrated to inhibit
the binding of SF3B4 to G4s which are located in the vicinal ve, and
promoted the inclusion of downstream exons in MCF10A cells.

## The Potency and Advantages of Developing SMSMs Targeting the
G4 Structure
Therapeutic approaches for AS-related diseases
include the intrathecally administered antisense oligonucleotide (nusinersen),
the AAV9-based gene therapy (onasemnogene abeparvovec-xioi), and the
SMSMs mentioned earlier. Compared with other therapies, small-molecule
drugs offer the advantages of broad tissue distribution and the ability
to cross the blood–brain barrier, making early intervention
more feasible. In this section, we mainly focus on the reported SMSMs
targeting RNA secondary structures and discuss the potential and advantages
of discovering SMSMs targeting G4 structures.

## SMSMs Targeting the AS-Related Hairpin Structure
Risdiplam
is an mRNA splicing modulator for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA).
−

 Studies have confirmed that initiating treatment with SMA before
symptom onset significantly improves outcomes, which has further accelerated
the development of SMSMs for SMA in several countries. Branaplam is
another small-molecule drug developed for SMA therapy, whose development
was previously discontinued due to market competition. However, in
2022, the development of branaplam was resumed for the treatment of
Huntington’s disease (HD). In
the treatment of HD, branaplam increases the level of inclusion of
pseudoexon 50 in the huntingtin (HTT) gene. Unlike the increased level
of inclusion of exon 7 in SMA therapy, which enhances the expression
of functional protein, the inclusion of pseudoexon 50 in HTT introduces
a PTC, thereby reducing the level of expression of the toxic mutant
HTT (mHTT) protein that causes neuronal dysfunction. Both of these
small molecules were discovered through a high-throughput screening
campaign. Subsequent studies have shown that risdiplam-like compounds
specifically stabilize the RNA duplex structure formed by the exon
7’s 5′ss of the human survival of motor neuron 2 gene
and the U1 snRNP complex.

Some
bacteria and fungi regulate AS through riboswitches, which are RNA
structures responding to metabolites. The thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP)
riboswitch regulates AS in fungi such as Neurospora
crassa. The TPP riboswitch is located within the intron
of 5′-UTR, which contains a TPP binding aptamer and an expression
platform. At lower TPP concentrations, the aptamer region base-pairs
with the proximal 5′ss to mask it and promotes the use of a
distal splicing site, resulting in an mRNA that lacks upstream open
reading frames (uORFs) and allows translation of the main ORF. Conversely,
when the TPP concentration is high, TPP binds to the aptamer to induce
a conformational change in the aptamer, releasing the proximal 5′ss,
which leads to the production of an mRNA containing uORFs. Therefore, developing small molecules that target
splicing-related RNA secondary structures represents a promising therapeutic
strategy for AS-related diseases.

## Stabilization and Disruption of G4s as a Regulating Strategy
G4-stabilizing ligands can produce dramatic splicing shifts in
disease-relevant genes. In situ protonated ligands bear protonable
amino appendages, which become positively charged at physiological
pH. Their binding to G4 structures is achieved via π–π
stacking between the aromatic core of the ligand and the G-quartet,
coupled with electrostatic interactions between the protonated side
chains and the grooves of the G4 ( ). Carika Weldon and colleagues demonstrated
two G4s form in the Bcl-X pre-mRNA
and modulate its AS.
,
 These two G4s are located near
a proximal 5′ss and a distal 5′ss, respectively. They
found that specific G4-stabilizing ligands, such as GQC-05, enhance
G4 formation, thereby suppressing the usage of the proximal 5′ss
responsible for the antiapoptotic isoform Bcl-XL, while promoting the selection of the distal 5′ss
that generates the pro-apoptotic isoform Bcl-XS. These results indicate that the G4s at these two sites
play contrary roles in the regulation of AS.
 PDS and its derivatives are G4 stabilizers. In
previous studies,
it was reported that they could induce a shift in the melting temperature
of DNA and RNA G4s.
,
 The analysis of high-throughput
sequencing results under PDS and K+ treatments observed
an enrichment of peaks relative to the 3′ss and 5′ss,
and the enrichment degree under PDS treatment was more significant
than that under K+ treatment.

Among the reported SMSMs targeting G4 structures, the most
common
type is N-methylated aromatic ligands. In this class of ligands, the
nitrogen atoms on the aza-aromatic moieties are methylated, conferring
a permanent positive charge that is pH-independent ( ), for instance, the TMPyP4,
RHPS4, and phenDC3 that we discussed earlier. The landmark study was
conducted by Dennis et al. using the G4 ligand 12459 on the hTERT gene, which encodes the catalytic subunit of telomerase. Stabilization of a G4 in intron 6 of hTERT pre-mRNA
dramatically altered its splicing pattern, causing a near-complete
loss of the active (+α, + β) transcript and a significant
up-regulation of an inactive (-β) isoform. This effect was specific,
as 12459-resistant cell lines maintained their original transcript
ratio, suggesting that tumors could develop resistance by adapting
their splicing regulatory pathways. This was the first direct evidence
that small molecules could modulate AS by targeting intronic G4s,
opening up a new frontier in anticancer drug discovery.
The TP53 gene provides another key example in
which G4 stabilization can have therapeutic benefits. The G4 ligand
360A and PhenDC3, which stabilizes G4s in intron 3 of TP53, led to a 3-fold increase in the FSp53 mRNA and a 40% decrease in
the p53I2 mRNA (Δ40p53).
,
 This demonstrates that
G4 stabilization can promote productive splicing, in this case, favoring
the tumor-suppressive isoform of p53. Similarly, 360A was shown to
increase the splicing efficiency of PAX9 intron 1.

Conversely, molecules that disrupt G4
structures can also alter
splicing in a therapeutically beneficial manner ( ). A high-throughput screen
using a minigene reporter with a G4 near a variable exon identified
emetine and cephaeline as G4-destabilizing compounds. Treatment with these molecules increased the rate of exon
skipping. It was reported that I-8 RNA of the CD44 gene forms the
G4 structure, and the addition of TMPyP4 perturbed the characteristic
CD spectrum of the I-8 RNA in a dose-dependent manner even in the
presence of 100 mM K+. Furthermore, the disruption of G4
by TMPyP4 inhibits the binding of hnRNP F, which leads to a decrease
of splicing.

The ligand ability
of G4 structures presents a unique and powerful
opportunity for pharmacological intervention. Small molecules that
selectively stabilize or disrupt G4s can be used as chemical tools
to probe G4 function and, more importantly, as therapeutic agents
to manipulate pathological splicing patterns in diseases such as cancer
and neurodegeneration. The effect of a ligand is highly specific and
context-dependent. Different G4 ligands exhibit varying efficacies
in stabilizing the same G4 structure, and the same ligand can have
different effects depending on the cellular context and the specific
G4 that it targets. This highlights the critical importance of “ligand-structure
matching” for achieving desired biological outcomes and the
need for continued development of highly specific G4 ligands. In the
previous discussion, we learned that G4 can adopt various structures,
which lay a structural foundation for the specific targeting of G4.
Compared with hairpin structures, the greatest advantage of G4 lies
in its significant structural difference from the genomic DNA double
helix, making it a potentially more specific target. Nucleic acid-targeting
drugs often face challenges of off-target effects due to the limited
structural differences between their targets and other double-stranded
DNA.
,
 The most distinctive feature of G4 compared
to other nucleic acid secondary structures is the large planar G-quartet.
To enhance specificity, G4 ligands typically possess larger aromatic
planar systems to better discriminate between G4 and double-stranded
DNA. They also incorporate sterically hindered groups to prevent intercalation
into double-stranded structures, while positively charged groups interact
with the loops or grooves of G4 to further stabilize the binding. We can already elucidate the fine structures
of G4 at the molecular level through methods such as X-ray crystallography
and nuclear magnetic resonance, which promote the rational design
of G4-targeting small molecules. To date, the database for discovering
and studying G-quadruplex ligands (http://www.g4ldb.com) has reported over 4,600 G4 ligands.

## Other G4 Regulatory Methods Used for the Modulation of AS
Other G4 regulatory approaches could be useful in cases where abnormal
splicing events cause diseases. Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD)
is caused by frameshift or nonsense mutations in the dystrophin gene.
Correction of this erroneous splicing pattern at the mRNA level is
a promising therapeutic approach. A G4-disrupting strategy involving
the use of G-rich antisense oligonucleotides (G-ASOs) was reported
in the DMD model. This G-ASO contains
an antisense oligonucleotide strand fully complementary to the target
RNA sequence, enabling specific targeting of the splicing regulatory
site, and a G-rich sequence designed to form a hybrid G-quadruplex
with a distal G-tract. The G-ASO effectively promoted the skipping
of pre-mRNA exon 23, leading to an increase in the expression of partially
functional dystrophin from the genetic material in DMD mice. G-ASO
utilizes steric hindrance to interfere with splice factor binding,
which is more effective than the double-stranded complementary pairing
occupancy of ASOs. Neither the G-rich sequence nor the standard ASO
alone exhibited this restorative function. Another advantage of this
approach lies in the fact that the hG4 structure can still form effectively
even when the G-rich region in the target RNA is up to 70 nucleotides
from the antisense binding site. This demonstrates that G-ASOs possess
high structural flexibility, overcoming the limitation of traditional
ASOs, which typically require precise targeting near the splice site
sequence. Furthermore, the series of strategies employed to enhance
the efficacy of the G-ASO sequence holds a significant reference value
for subsequent research on G4-based regulation of AS. For instance,
modifying the G-ASOs with 2′-OMe [-OCH3] increased
the stability of the ASO sequence and its binding affinity to the
target sequence. Additionally, introducing bulges between the GGG
tracts in the G-rich sequence reduced intermolecular G-quadruplex
self-assembly.

## Conclusion and Future Perspectives
The extensive evidence
reviewed establishes RNA G4s as versatile,
dynamic regulators of AS. Their strategic enrichment near splice sites,
particularly on the nontemplate strand, positions them as structural
elements in the splicing code. G4 structures influence splicing decisions
through multiple mechanisms: they can serve as structural scaffolds
for protein recruitment, create kinetic barriers during cotranscriptional
folding, exert long-range effects on splice site recognition, and
respond to environmental cues through structural transitions. However,
as evidenced in this review, these G4 structures can function as either
splicing activators or repressors. A unified and definitive mechanistic
model to explain these divergent outcomes remains elusive, underscoring
the need for further investigation to elucidate the underlying principles
and specific characteristics governing G4-mediated splicing regulation.
The influence of specific G4 sequences on AS is typically investigated
using minigene assays, an approach that has validated the regulatory
capacity of various G4 sequences and their positional effects on local
splicing events. In conventional minigene experiments, control designs
often involve nucleotide substitutions that disrupt the G-tracts.
Since these G-tracts have been demonstrated to serve as binding sites
for splicing regulators such as hnRNP family proteins, it becomes
challenging to discern whether observed splicing effects originate
from the linear G-tract sequence or the formation of G4 structures.
A more refined strategy employs synthetic nucleotides with 8-aza-7-deazaguanosine
substitutions, which effectively disrupt G4 folding while largely
preserving the native G-tract sequence. It is also crucial to recognize
that G4 structures do not operate in isolation. Other secondary structural
elements, such as stem loops, where specific positioning at the apical
region can impair 5′ss recognition. Therefore, the integrated
effects of G4s and coexisting RNA structures must be considered. Given
that the functional consequence of G4-mediated regulation arises from
a complex interplay between G4 structural features and the cellular
complement of trans-acting factors, future studies
examining the thermodynamic stability and kinetic properties of G4–protein
complexes may provide valuable insights into the intrinsic mechanisms
of G4-dependent splicing control. Furthermore, understanding the complex
kinetic competition among protein binding, RNA folding, and helicase
activity in real-time within the cellular nucleus is a daunting but
necessary task.
G4-targeting small molecules represent powerful
chemical tools
and potential therapeutics. By shifting the structural equilibrium
of pre-mRNA, they can deliberately alter splicing patterns to suppress
oncogenes or correct pathogenic splicing events.
,
 The ability of small molecules to manipulate this system by stabilizing
or destabilizing G4s opens up a promising therapeutic frontier. The
successful redirection of splicing in genes like hTERT,
TP53,
,

Bcl-x,
,

CD44,
,
 and CD133
 provides proof-of-concept for targeting
RNA secondary structure to manipulate splicing for therapeutic benefit.
The discovery of both G4-stabilizing and G4-destabilizing compounds
expands the toolkit available for pharmacological splicing modulation.
However, significant challenges remain. A primary hurdle is improving
the specificity of G4 ligands to avoid off-target effects on the multitude
of G4 present in the transcriptome. Efforts are required to enhance
delivery of chemical molecules via novel chemical modifications and
conjugation to delivery-assisting moieties. It is also worth noting
that when the G4 ligand is used in cells, it may not only bind to
the G4s at this site but also affect the binding and expression of
some splicing-related proteins, which in turn will have an impact
on the results of AS.
Recent studies have highlighted the application
of antisense oligonucleotides
(ASOs) to induce exon-skipping in therapeutic research.
,
 In these studies, ASOs were used to block the binding of splicing
factors to critical cis-regulatory elements. However,
conventional ASO therapeutics often suffer from low efficacy, and
the greater steric hindrance produced by G4 may improve the efficiency.
Strategy combining ASOs with RNA/DNA heteroduplex G-quadruplex structures
(G-ASO) to regulate pre-mRNA AS is a direction worthy of in-depth
study. In addition, the attempt of reported
G4 regulating methods in AS may show potential, such as the CRISPR-Cas
system, G4-based aptamers, and G4 antibodies.
,

Future research should focus on several key challenges: (1)
elucidating
the dynamic interplay between G4 folding, protein binding, and other
regulatory layers like m6A modification in living cells;
(2) the development of more sophisticated tools for studying G4–protein
interaction in living cells will provide deeper insights into the
dynamic nature of G4-mediated regulation; (3) developing higher-specificity
G4 ligands to target individual structures within the complex transcriptome;
and (4) developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting G4, for
diseases characterized by splicing dysregulation. As these challenges
are addressed, the study of G4s in splicing will continue to reveal
fundamental principles of gene regulation and open new avenues for
treating diseases characterized by splicing dysregulation.
