Early diagnosis of cancer can significantly contribute
to improving
therapeutic outcomes and enhancing survival rates for cancer patients.
Polymer-based biosensors have emerged as a promising tool for cancer
detection due to their high sensitivity, selectivity, and low cost.
These biosensors utilize functionalized polymers in different parts
of the body to detect cancer biomarkers in biological samples. This
approach offers several advantages over traditional detection methods,
including real-time monitoring and noninvasive detection while maintaining
high sensitivity and accuracy. This review discusses recent advances
in the development of polymer-based biosensors for cancer detection
including their design, fabrication, and performance. The essential
characteristics of biosensing devices are presented, along with examples
for natural and synthetic polymers commonly utilized in biosensors.
Furthermore, strategies employed to tailor polymers to improve biosensing
applications and future perspectives for the application of polymer-based
biosensors in cancer diagnosis are also highlighted. Integrating these
advancements will illuminate the potential of polymer-based biosensors
as transformative tools in the early detection and management of cancer.

## Introduction
Cancer remains a significant
global health concern, and early detection,
accurate monitoring, and tracking of treatment response are essential
for improving patient outcomes. , Early cancer detection
leads to more effective therapy and a significant increase in survival
rates. However, more than 50% of cancer
cases are not discovered until they are in advanced stages. Identifying cancer cells at an early stage is
crucial in ensuring timely and effective treatment. , Hence,
it is imperative to develop innovative methods for the early detection
of cancer. − surface enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) is an
impressive technique that develops Raman scattering signals through
the management of metallic nanostructures, permitting the detection
of low-abundance biomolecules correlated with cancer. This technique
improves sensitivity and specificity, making it a successful approach
for the early diagnosis of cancerous changes at the molecular level. Differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) is a technique
that employs a series of voltage beats to an electrode while determining
the resulting current, which is verified at specific intervals. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) engages in sweeping
the potential of the electrode in a repeated manner and evaluating
the resulting current. This technique offers information on the redox
performance of analytes. Electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) determines the impedance of a system
across a range of frequencies, presenting insights into the electrochemical
processes following the electrode interface. Cancer biosensors offer a potential route for the accurate and sensitive
identification of cancer biomarkers, which can be utilized to identify
cancer in its early stages, track the disease’s progression,
and assess the efficacy of treatments, as shown in .
 Biosensors are analytical devices used to detect
the presence,
determine the concentration, or both of a specific analyte using a
biological component. The detection of
these analytes serves a wide range of applications, including clinical
diagnostics, food analysis, and environmental monitoring. Depending on the target, biosensors employ various biorecognition
molecules that are immobilized on a transducer surface, such as antibodies,
complementary nucleic acid probes, or others. The prognosis and effectiveness of treatment for many cancer types,
as well as the monitoring targets for adjuvant cancer treatment, currently
depend on specific tumor markers. Biosensors
offer several advantages over other available analytical techniques,
including being manageable, cost-effective, simple to use, selective,
sensitive, and less time-consuming. The
components of biosensors include an analyte, a bioreceptor, a transducer,
electronics, and a display. The bioreceptor
provides specificity for analyte detection when immobilized onto the
surface of the detection component. When
these bioreceptors interact with the target analyte, they produce
physicochemical changes that the transducer can convert into measurable
signals that can be detected and quantified. They can detect very low limit of detection (LOD) analytes, thus
providing a useful alternative to traditional detection methods such
as ELISA, RT-PCR, Western blot, and flow cytometry that require several
steps for detection, bulky equipment, and highly expensive costs. − The sensing properties of biosensors (e.g., linear detection range,
LOD, sensitivity, and stability) depend on the material employed to
immobilize the bioreceptors. Biosensors
can be classified according to transducers into various types including
electrochemical, piezoelectric, optical, mechanical, and thermal sensors.
Each type of biosensor utilizes a different mechanism to convert the
recognition event into a measurable signal, allowing for the detection
and quantification of analytes in diverse samples, as depicted in .
 Polymer-based sensors have made significant strides
in achieving
higher tissue compatibility and biosafety when used as implantable
electronic sensors in vivo. In 1970, a groundbreaking
innovation emerged in the field of medical monitoring. A balloon-based
pressure sensor was successfully implanted into the heart to monitor
left ventricular pressure. This remarkable
development showcased the exceptional qualities of polymers, which
include high flexibility, self-repairability, stretchability, and biodegradation. These extraordinary
characteristics have opened new frontiers in medical technology and
paved the way for future advancements in the field. By combining device
engineering and functional integration, these sensors have been able
to detect multiple stimuli with an even greater accuracy. These advancements
offer new possibilities for improving health outcomes and enhancing
our understanding of the human body. Extensive
reviews have described the types of biosensors and the different detection
methods utilized. 
However, studies
that provide a comparison between natural and
synthetic polymers in biosensing applications are limited. This review offers insight into the main natural
and synthetic polymers used in biosensing applications for cancer
detection. In this comprehensive review, we delve into the fascinating
realm of polymer-based biosensors and their groundbreaking applications
in the field of cancer detection. First, an intricate analysis of
the essential characteristics of biosensing devices is introduced,
along with the innovative strategies employed to tailor polymers to
meet these stringent requirements for biosensing applications. Second,
the ingenious methods utilized to optimize the functional interfaces
and device structures using these cutting-edge polymer materials are
highlighted. Lastly, a detailed examination of the latest advancements
in cancer detection is presented, showcasing the remarkable potential
and implications of various polymer-based biosensors.

## Crucial Features of Diverse Biosensing Devices
Biosensors harness the power of label-based and label-free detection
strategies. Label-based detection relies on the unique properties
of labeled compounds to pinpoint specific targets. While reliable, these biosensors often require a combination
of specialized sensing elements and immobilized target proteins. On the other hand, label-free methods enable
the detection of unlabeled or challenging-to-tag molecules, thus expanding
the medical and environmental applications of biosensing. , This groundbreaking approach has been made possible through the
convergence of biotechnology, bioengineering, electrical engineering,
and electronics engineering.
Biosensors are cutting-edge tools
that revolutionize biomarker
analysis. They offer a host of advantages
that make them highly beneficial: user-friendly interfaces, affordability,
lightning-fast results, and unwavering reliability. These remarkable
devices also boost the ability to perform multianalyte testing, making
them invaluable in the field of cancer diagnosis. Biomarkers allow the use of multiarray sensors to detect
multiple markers simultaneously, enabling efficient and accurate diagnosis.
Moreover, biosensors incorporate sample preparation techniques right
into their systems. Just like glucose sensors equipped with microfluidics
and membrane separation systems, they effortlessly handle blood samples.
2.1

## Biosensors Based on Electrochemical Reactions
Electrochemical sensors are a category of device known for their
high sensitivity, cost-effectiveness, and reliable execution in detecting
various biological species and diseases. They have been successfully applied in the analysis of several diseases
such as diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and cancer. Chemical
biosensors incorporate a biological investigative component, including
enzymes, nucleic acids, proteins, tissues, antibodies, and cells. This component selectively reacts with the analyte
to produce an electrical signal, which is proportional to the amount
of target analyte. Electrochemical biosensors
can be categorized into two types based on the investigative method:
biocatalytic-based and affinity-based biosensors. Biocatalytic-based
sensors use enzymes (enzymatic biosensors) or cells (cytosensors)
to generate electroactive species that recognize the target analyte.
In contrast, affinity-based biosensors rely on a specific interaction
between the analyte and biological elements, such as antibodies or
nucleic acids, to produce electrical signals with high sensitivity
and selectivity. 

2.2

## Biosensors Based on Visual Detection
Indeed, optical biosensors stand out due to their utilization of
an optical measurement approach. These sensors employ optical transducers
for their measurements, which are closely integrated with the biosensing
element. The key performance metrics
and operational principles of optical biosensors vary significantly
based on the specific type of optical transducer employed. Hence,
it is crucial to categorize optical biosensors according to the transducers
that they utilize. This classification enables a better understanding
of their capabilities and facilitates the selection of the most suitable
sensor for a given application. Another
way to classify optical biosensors is by labeling them as either label-based
or label-free. In label-based biosensors, a fluorescent or colorimetric
label is used to detect an analyte by observing changes in intensity
or color. 

2.3

## Biosensors Based on Changing Physical Phenomena
Physical biosensors can detect biological occurrences by tracking
alterations in physical phenomena, such as mass. Mass-based biosensors can indeed be classified into two
main groups: piezoelectric sensors and magnetoelectric sensors. The piezoelectric effect has been known since
the 19th century. However, its widespread technological applications
began in the 20th century. It was recognized
that anisotropic crystals, which lack a center of symmetry, can generate
an electric dipole when subjected to mechanical pressure. This electric
dipole is also known as piezoelectricity. Conversely, the effect can
be reversed when the anisotropic crystal is deformed due to voltage
imposition. While mechanical deformation
is a simple situation, oscillation is more commonly used in practical
applications, such as analytical devices. In oscillation, an alternating
voltage is applied to the crystal, which then induces mechanical oscillation. , In analytical applications, measuring the frequencies of oscillations
is a standard practice. The interaction between either the crystal
alone or an electrode that delivers an electrical impulse on the crystal
surface can be utilized to determine the analyte. , 
Pioneering the way in energy harvesting, piezoelectric nanogenerators
(PENGs) have an incredible ability to harness mechanical energy from
the surrounding environment or even the human body. Not only can they
convert these mechanical signals into electricity, but they also possess
the remarkable capability to provide valuable insights into pathophysiological
changes. By transforming these changes into electrical signals, PENG
devices have the potential to provide crucial information, thus serving
various healthcare applications and beyond. PENGs have a remarkable ability to operate without the need for
a contact separation process between two materials. Additionally,
the piezoelectric material used in PENGs exhibits desirable characteristics
such as a high dielectric constant, low dielectric loss, and a high
electromechanical coupling coefficient. These advantageous properties
make PENGs an excellent choice for powering medical sensors. Magnetoelectric sensors are remarkable substances
that possess the unique ability to bring together magnetization and
electric polarization, initiating a powerful connection that unlocks
a multitude of possibilities for functional devices. Magnetoelectric
sensors combine ferroelectric and magnetic orderings within a single-phase
material system.

## Natural Polymer-Based Biosensors
Biosensors
require functional materials to detect specific biological
analytes. However, these materials may not always maintain the necessary
biocompatibility for proper biorecognition. To address this issue,
natural biopolymers have been explored as a potential solution due
to their ability to easily form composites with functional materials
and improve biocompatibility. Biopolymers
such as chitosan, cellulose, hyaluronic acid, and gelatin, as well
as their derivatives, have a crucial role in the development of effective
biosensor systems.
3.1

## Chitosan
Chitosan (Cs) is a natural
polysaccharide that has been exploited for a broad range of biomedical
applications, , including drug delivery, gene delivery, and biosensors. Cs is characterized
by high permeability and mechanical strength, biocompatibility, cost-effectiveness,
pH-dependent solubility, nontoxicity, availability, good adhesion,
and easy chemical modification. The linear chain of chitosan contains
reactive amino and hydroxyl groups that confer excellent mass transfer
properties, beneficial hydrodynamic properties for enzyme immobilization,
and a unique film-forming ability. Thus,
Cs is considered one of the most attractive biopolymers for fabricating
sensors ( ). Cs has a variety of functional groups,
including hydroxyl and amino groups, which are prone to chemical reactions
and can easily cross-link with various nanomaterials, which in turn
facilitates the fabrication of Cs nanocomposite-based biosensors. , The presence of these groups also assists in the immobilization
of various biomarkers that can be used as sensing components for the
identified substances. For example,
Nasrollahpour and his co-workers utilized Cs moieties to hold luminol,
AgNPs, and antibodies strongly onto the electrode, which increases
the sensitivity of the biosensor to detect prostate specific antigen
(PSA). Although the electrical conductivity
of Cs is very low due to strong bonds that prevent the hydrogen atoms
in the chitosan monomer from being mobilized in response to an electric
field ( ), it was found to enhance the electrical
conductivity and electrocatalytic activity characteristics of electrochemical
biosensors when mixed with multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs). , Cs can also be used in the form of dots to produce emissions in
the presence of an analyte ( ). Wang et al. employed Cs-based dots as electrochemiluminescence
probes for the detection of KRAS gene mutants and used phenylboronic
acid (PBA) as a regulator, which improves the luminescence efficiency
of Cs-based dots by 12.1-fold. A nanocomposite
composed of graphene nanoribbon and chitosan was utilized to detect
sarcosine in blood through a matrix for covalently immobilizing sarcosine
oxidase. 
 
3.2

## Cellulose
Cellulose is a well-known
natural biopolymer composed of glucose residues linked by β-(1
→ 4) glycosidic linkages. , It exhibits
considerable potential as a sustainable sensing material due to its
excellent biocompatibility, strong mechanical strength, high degradation
efficiency, and regeneration capabilities. Cellulose-based materials are known for their fibrous and porous
structures, making them highly compatible with immobilizing receptors
or transducer surfaces. These surfaces might include metal oxides,
nanoparticles, and enzymes. The inherent
structural and functional properties of cellulose and its derivatives
make it an exceptional material for developing sensors. Biosensors based on cellulose exhibit distinct
and desirable qualities, including high sensitivity, precision, rapid
response, cost-effectiveness, easy dispersibility, excellent absorbance
capacity, porosity, nontoxicity, hydrophilicity, large surface area,
and compatibility for integrating biologically active substances ( ). Furthermore, cellulose is one of the key components of
paper-based biosensors. , Pereira et al. employed
cellulose paper that has endured chemical modification using (mercaptopropyl)methyldimetoxisilane
as a substrate for attaching the thiolated aptamer, which functions
as a biological detection layer for osteopontin (OPN). Cellulose is produced not only from plants but
also from algae, bacteria, tunicates, and animals. To construct inexpensive immunosensors, Bondancia et al.
developed an immunosensor to detect P53 using bacterial nanocellulose
electrodes (BNC) ( ). BNC substrates exhibited superior mechanical resistance in both
aqueous and nonaqueous media compared to plant cellulose.

## Hyaluronic Acid
Hyaluronic acid (HA)
is a hydrophilic anionic polysaccharide polymer found naturally in
the human body and in other living organisms. The repeated units of N-acetylglucosamine and glucuronic acid in HA play a vital
role in numerous physiological processes. − HA possesses
an impressive list of desirable qualities, such as biocompatibility,
nonimmunogenicity, nontoxicity, non-inflammatory nature, and biodegradability
( ). The chemical
properties of HA are vital for designing organic–inorganic
nanocomposites for various biomedical and sensor applications ( ). The functional
groups of HA (i.e., carboxylic acid, N-acetyl moieties,
and hydroxyl) allow for its combination with other chemicals. , HA is a significant component of the tumor microenvironment with
both carcinogenic and pro-tumorigenic properties in addition to a
high affinity for cluster-determinant 44 (CD44) receptors in cancer
cells, which makes them attractive targets for active targeting based
on CD44 overexpression in cancer cells compared with normal body cells. , Jawaid et al. developed an electrochemical biosensor for the detection
of CD44 proteins based on the natural affinity of HA to CD44. Recently, a biosensor based on the fluorescent
emission of amine-functionalized graphene quantum dots (af-GQD) grafted
with HA was fabricated for detecting human cancer cells rich in CD44
receptors. Giang et al. also utilized
carbonized dots HA as a fluorescent probe to target specific cells
for cancer diagnosis. 
 
3.4

## Gelatin
Gelatin is a natural polymer
derived from animal-origin collagen through partial hydrolysis. Owing
to its abundance and affordability, gelatin has been widely utilized
in various industries, including food processing and pharmaceuticals. As an eco-friendly, biodegradable, and biocompatible
nontoxic polymer, gelatin has gained attention as a versatile dispersive
and stabilizing agent. The ability of gelatin to form films makes
it an ideal matrix for nanoparticles and molecules, enabling the fabrication
of biosensors. The amino groups of gelatin
allow it to immobilize biorecognition elements without negatively
impacting the sensitivity and stability of the biosensors ( ). This unique property
makes gelatin a valuable resource for developing advanced biosensing
technologies. − Thin film gelatin-based hydrogels have been used
as potential sensor substrates, which are hydrolyzed in the presence
of trypsin ( ). Feng et al. developed a distance-based
biosensor using a stimuli responsive gelatin hydrogel combined with
a CRISPR/Cas12a system and the target-triggered strand displacement
amplification (SDA) reaction. The target-triggered SDA reaction initially
converted the target miRNA into plenty of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA).
Then, dsDNA activated the collateral cleavage activity of CRISPR/Cas12a,
which released trypsin from magnetic beads. The released trypsin hydrolyzed
gelatin and thus increased the permeability of gelatin-treated filter
paper and produced a visible distance signal on a cotton thread. Gelatin can be used as a substrate in biosensors
with nanocomposites composed of gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) and AuNPs
to detect matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) based on MMP-9 gelatinase
activity.

## Alginate
Alginate is a natural anionic
polymer usually found in brown seaweed and capsules of bacteria such
as Azotobacter sp. and Pseudomonas sp. It is employed in numerous biomedical applications due to its
biocompatibility, low toxicity, affordability, and mild gelation when
divalent cations such as Ca2+ are added. , Different types of alginate salts are used depending on their properties
and desired applications. Biomedical
applications include tissue engineering, drug delivery, and biosensors. Alginate-based matrices have been employed
to detect a variety of molecules. − Attia et al. developed
a biosensor embedded in a thin film of sodium alginate polymer for
the early diagnosis of liver cancer by detecting alpha-fetoprotein
(AFP) ( ). Alginate
was utilized to immobilize AFP monoclonal antibodies through the connected
epichlorohydrin’s epoxy ring opening and to encapsulate 3,3`′-bis(dimethyl[6,6′`]bi[benzo[b]phenoxazinyl]-3,3′`-ylidene) ammonium chloride [phenoxazinyl]; then,
AFP concentrations were determined by tracking fluorescence intensity. Three-dimensional porous Ca-alginate beads
possess a large surface area to volume ratio, allowing biorecognition
molecules to be trapped and fluids to enter and exit. Thus, Chia et
al. employed calcium alginate beads to encapsulate antibodies that
recognize the CA125 and HER2 proteins ( ). Antibodies were conjugated with fluorescence-emitting
dyes to quantify breast cancer biomarkers. Due to the ability of sodium alginate hydrogel to encapsulate cells,
Berthuy et al. developed a novel fully automated technique for the
immobilization of antibodies and cells on a surface plasmon resonance
imaging (SPRi) biochip, allowing for the analysis of multiple cell
populations and the early detection of PSA and β-2-microglobulin
(B2M) on the same chip.

## Synthetic Polymer-Based Biosensors
The arrangement of materials resulting from the immobilization
process on electrode surfaces or the transducer layer is the most
significant factor that influences the performance of any biosensor.
The surface architecture between the biorecognition element and the
analyte is crucial in determining the sensitivity and stability of
the biosensor. Modifying the performance capability of the transducer
surface through the architecture can significantly affect the performance
of the biosensor. The use of polymers
can improve the electrical conductivity, optimize the transducer surface,
and serve as matrices for binding biomolecules. Furthermore, they
can be engineered to act as synthetic receptors with high chemical
affinity and selectivity for the detection and binding of specific
molecules. These synthetic receptors, known as molecularly imprinted
polymers (MIPs), form polymeric matrices with imprinted recognition
sites based on the template of a target molecule. Therefore, the significance
of polymers in the development of biosensors cannot be overstated. − The creation of biosensor surfaces has made extensive use of polymers,
which can be natural or synthetic and have conductive or nonconductive
character. The output current values
can indeed be influenced by the shape of the electrode. The choice
of electrode shape is determined based on several factors such as
surface area, field distribution, and material properties. Surface
area, where different shapes can provide varying surface areas, directly
affects the electrochemical reactions occurring at the electrode surface.
A larger surface area typically enhances the current output. Field
distribution, where the geometry of the electrode influences the electric
field distribution, can impact the efficiency of charge transfer processes.
Material properties as the choice of materials used in the electrodes,
in conjunction with their shape, can optimize conductivity and reaction
kinetics. , 
Advancements in printing
technologies enable the fabrication of
electrode materials with precise control over their shape and thickness
at relatively low temperatures. These innovations present new opportunities
for creating electrodes using paper substrates. For instance, a printing
method was employed to deposited ZnCl2 and polyvinyl alcohol
(PVA) onto filter paper, where the paper’s porosity enhances
the adhesion of the materials to its surface. Electrochemical analysis
of the electrodes revealed that current generation fluctuated with
varying humidity levels in the cell, indicating effective performance
for self-powered applications. Although bending influenced the shape
of the electrode materials, ECBS demonstrated stable sensor performance
even when the bending angle reached 180°. 
4.1

## Synthetic Conductive Polymers (CPs)
Owing to delocalized π electrons within the molecule, CPs possess
distinct characteristics such as electrical conductivity, low optical
transition energy, low ionization potential, and absorbance and light
emission. For this reason, they are used, among others, in sensors,
organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), or solar cells. 
4.1.1

## Polypyrrole
Polypyrrole (PPy) is
a heterocyclic positively charged synthetic polymer with excellent
electrical conductivity, good biocompatibility, and low cost ( ). It is used in different biomedical applications including
biosensors, tissue engineering, and drug delivery. The high electrical conductivity and electron transfer rate of PPy
enhance the analytical signal in electrochemical biodevices. Furthermore,
PPy provides stable film production via electropolymerization, making
it easy to produce and offering attractive biomolecules with biocompatibility
for anchoring ( ). For example, Oliveira et al. developed electrochemical flexible
geno-sensors based on PPy and graphene quantum dots (GQDs) for screening
the PML-RARα fusion oncogene for diagnosing acute promyelocytic
leukemia (APL) in children. As another
approach for diagnosing leukemia, PPy can be used for screening the
BCR/ABL fusion gene through immobilizing hybrid nanocomposite composed
of chitosan and zinc oxide nanoparticles (Chit-ZnO NP). When PPy is overoxidized, oxygen-based groups,
such as carbonyl and carboxyl groups, are enriched in the resulting
film, which enhances its accumulative properties. Therefore, Abdel-aal
et al. used a PPy overoxidized film on the large surface area of carbon
dot nanotubes to provide a highly applicable and sensitive method
for determining tryptophan (Trp) in cancer patients. Additionally, PPy was applied as an ultrasensitive signal
transducer in a nanostructured biosensor composed of electrosynthesized
PPy and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) to detect the P53 tumor suppressor
gene and HPV. An impedimetric biosensor
composed of reduced graphene oxide combined with amino-substituted
PPy (rGO-PPyNH2) was developed to detect calreticulin (CALR).
The amino groups in PPyNH2 provide supportive immobilization
of anti-CALR biomolecules, as well as exceptional properties such
as excellent stability, large surface area, biocompatibility, and
unique electrochemical properties. 
 
4.1.2

## Polyaniline (PANI)
Polyaniline
(PANI) is a conductive polymer with various applications, such as
sensors, photonic devices, and optoelectronic devices. PANI possesses high electronic conductivity
resulting from the π-conjugated electron system, low ionization
potential, high affinity for electrons, and large faradaic reaction
transfer and is environmentally stable, resistant to pH changes, and
easy to synthesize. PANI has some limitations (e.g., low solubility
in aqueous media due to its hydrophobic nature, low mechanical strength,
agglomeration, and adhesion issues) that prevent stable film formation
( ). These limitations
can be overcome by using PANI in nanomaterial composites to produce
a stable electrode modifier composite. For example, an impedimetric
immunosensor has been fabricated based on a silver-doped copper oxide-linked
polyaniline (Ag-CuO@PANI) nanocomposite for the sensitive detection
of mucin 1 (MUC1). To gain the same
advantages of enhancing conductivity and producing an electrical signal,
Li et al. electrodeposited PANI onto the bare electrode to detect
aminopeptidase N (APN). PANI was also
utilized as a sensing stage in DNA biosensors based on an extended-gate
field effect transistor (EGFET) to detect μDNA-224. The presence of free amino groups at the end
of the PANI polymer chain reduces the sensor’s linear range
and limits the ability of biomolecules to be immobilized covalently.
To overcome this drawback, composites of PANI, silver nanoparticles
(AgNPs), and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were utilized to enrich the
immobilization matrix (EIM) to immobilize the capture antibody to
detect alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). At the end of the PANI polymer chain,
free amino groups are found. Carboxylic
acid-tethered polyaniline (CPANI) was utilized instead of the standard
PANI as an immobilization matrix for the detection of carcinoembryonic
antigen (CEA) and AFP ( ). CPANI possessed a 5-fold wider linear range compared to
PANI. Moreover, the carboxylic group
of CPANI enhanced the redox activity in the buffer media, enhanced
electroactivity, and increased the degree of biomolecular immobilization. 
 
4.1.3

## Polyethylene Glycol (PEG)
Polyethylene
glycol (PEG) is a synthetic polymer with excellent tolerance, biocompatibility,
and high solubility in aqueous media. Owing to these properties, PEG is suitable for different biomedical
applications, including drug delivery, gene delivery, and biosensors. PEG can enhance the solubility of bioconjugates
and reagents and is a suitable linker for bioconjugation and biorecognition
molecules. PEG was utilized as a linker to develop an aptasensor based
on gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) for HER2 detection. The biocompatibility and antifouling properties of PEG
could enhance the photoelectrochemical (PEC) responses in biosensors , ( ). Liu et
al. developed a sensitive and selective sensor that detects ERBB2
in serum through engineered peptide membrane interfaces. RBCM was
palmitoylated (PP) to form RBCM-PP, which served as a recognition
interface, while PEG was used as a glycoprotein to anchor the membrane
to the surface to enhance the hydrophilic ability and stability of
the cell membrane ( ). In an aqueous solution, black
phosphorus (BP) QDs agglomerate spontaneously and rapidly oxidize,
producing signals with low electrochemiluminescence (ECL) efficiency
and instability. To overcome this problem, PEG was employed as a protective
agent to prevent the rapid oxidation of BP QDs in aqueous solutions
and prevent their spontaneous agglomeration, which resulted in efficient
and stable ECL signals for detecting mucin 1 (MUC1). PEG-based mixed self-assembled monolayers (mSAMs) have
been recognized as an effective method for modifying field effect
transistor (FET) channels for biosensing under high ionic strength
conditions. Additionally, PEG can act as a spacer molecule to keep
the bioactivities of the recognition factors intact while separating
them from the surface. mSAMs were used to determine and manage the
intermolecular distance of DNA probes during DNA/RNA hybridization
and maximize the signal change by DNA/RNA complexes in high ionic
strength conditions by manipulating the Debye length ( ). In addition, PEG-mSAMs prevent
DNA probes from directly contacting sensing channels, thus enhancing
the accuracy of the sensors. 
 

4.2

## Synthetic Nonconductive Polymers
Certain synthetic nonconductive polymers contain functional groups
that hold potential as matrices for physical entrapment and as a source
of covalent coupling during the immobilization process. These polymers
offer advantages such as excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity,
and selectivity, which greatly enhance the performance of biosensors.
Among the various synthetic polymers, polystyrene-based composites
and Nafion-based composites are the two primary types of materials
extensively utilized in the development of biosensors. 
4.2.1

## Nafion
Nafion is a sulfonated tetrafluoroethylene-based
fluoropolymer that has been widely exploited as a membrane material
for several applications including sensors, batteries, and fuel cells
( ). Nafion has
high ionic conductivity, biocompatibility, chemical stability, and
permselectivity, making it a potential candidate for biosensing applications. It is used as an immobilization matrix to improve
the stability and activity of enzymes, antibodies, DNA molecules,
and other biomolecules on the electrode surface. To increase the adhesion strength of the materials and
to avoid peeling, Hu et al. have used Nafion to stably immobilize
the immunosensor ( ). Nafion, as a binder, was utilized to fabricate
redox amino acid-encoded green fluorescent protein on the electrode
surface to detect zinc ions in a sensitive and selective manner. To enhance the shedding of AuNPs onto electrode
surfaces, electrode materials consisting of Nafion-AuNPs mixtures
that exhibited good film-forming characteristics were fabricated.

## Molecularly Imprinted Polymers (MIPs)
In the field of biotechnology, there is a growing need for analytical
platforms that are fast, accurate, and cost-effective. To meet this
demand, molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) have been reviewed as
a synthetic strategy and biomimetic design ( ). MIPs are cross-linked polymer matrices
that contain molecular recognition sites. These recognition sites
are generated by synthesizing the polymers in the presence of a target
template, which allows for the creation of cost-effective detection
modules for target molecules. Recent research has improved MIP technology
and broadened its scope by integrating nanomaterials and enhancing
detection techniques with novel applications. The spatial arrangement
of MIPs is influenced by various interactions, such as hydrogen bonds,
van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions,
which are initiated by the prepolymer/template combination ( ). Once the template
molecules are removed from the cross-linked polymeric matrices, many
cavities with specified chemical end groups can be easily created
based on complemental templates defined by size, shape, and chemical
functionality.

## Limitations and challenges of Polymer-Based
Biosensors
Biosensors assembled from synthetic and natural
polymers for cancer
detection show several limitations and weaknesses. One significant
issue is stability; both types of polymers can degrade over time,
which adversely affects the biosensor’s execution and reliability.
The polymer matrix can be sensitively affected by environmental factors
such as temperature, pH, or humidity, which can lead to instability
of the biosensor. Furthermore, many
biosensors have a restricted lifetime due to the instability of the
biorecognition elements, forcing frequent replacements. An alternative
concern is the capability for cross-reactivity, where nonspecific
binding with other biomolecules may result in false positives, thus
dropping the specificity of the polymer-based biosensor. Increasing
production costs due to the complexity of fabrication and functionalization
of polymer-based biosensors can be involved and may need procedures
and techniques. Likewise, natural polymers might be more sensitive
to environmental considerations, such as microbial contamination,
that can compromise their performance. Combining polymer-based biosensors
with existing recognition approaches can also be challenging, requiring
further optimization. Adjusting agreements
for new biosensor technologies is often a complex and lengthy process
that can hinder their presence in the market. Scalability exhibits
another challenge, as scaling up manufacture while keeping quality
and performance can be challenging.

## Functionalized Polymer-Based Biosensors
Functionalized polymers are an excellent choice for biosensing
applications due to their unique combination of characteristics, including
high probe loading capacity and low nonspecific binding. , Functional polymers are synthesized and characterized for their
application in biosensors for various biomedical applications, including
studying biological processes, biomolecule interactions, biomarker
discovery, molecular diagnosis, development of serological assays,
genotyping, and detection of DNA mutations. Polymers have advantages over metallic and ceramic materials for
biosensing applications, such as mild synthetic conditions, scalable
and large processing area, chemical and structural diversity, flexibility,
low operating temperature, and biocompatibility. The application of CPs in surface engineering of biosensors
has gradually increased, owing to their beneficial properties. CPs
have diverse functions in the design of biosensors, serving as an
immobilization matrix, a signal transduction system, or even a component
that recognizes analytes in catalytic and affinity biosensors. One
significant advantage of CPs is their capacity to improve the mechanical
durability of electrode surfaces. Moreover, these materials demonstrate
considerable potential for incorporation into electronic devices due
to their ability to switch between semiconducting and conducting states,
their regulated electrochemical conductivity via suitable doping/dedoping,
and the capacity to modify mechanical properties through chemical
modification, controlled doping, and the creation of composites with
additional materials. − Commonly used polymeric materials in biosensor
devices include polystyrene (PS), polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), polyethylene
terephthalate (PET), polycarbonate (PCL), cyclic olefin copolymer
(COC), and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA). The incorporation of chemical functionalities into polymers can
be achieved by the technique of doping. Dopants have the capability
of being incorporated inside the polymer matrix, and the process of
doping can be carefully manipulated to achieve reversibility. Emphasizing
the significance of the dopant selection is crucial, as it can influence
the biocompatibility of conducting polymers. A study conducted by
Wallace et al. investigated the impact of various biological substances,
including dextran sulfate, chondroitin sulfate, and alginate, when
utilized as dopants on the biologically important characteristics
of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene). The factors
such as nanoroughness, modulus, wettability, and electrical impedance,
have a subsequent impact on biological processes, specifically cell
adhesion. 
In reports of Bangar
et al., the biofunctionalization of polyproline
(PPy) nanowires was explored using glutaraldehyde (GA) and N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC).
It is commonly employed for the covalent immobilization of biomolecules.
The comparison of GA and EDC chemistries revealed that EDC is the
most effective, leading to the functionalization of a single PPy nanowire
surface with cancer antigen (CA125) antibody, creating a nanoimmunosensor
for CA125 biomarker detection. Additionally,
surface immobilization of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) using EDC has
been employed in biosensor applications with polyaniline nanowires.
Lee et al. have employed polyaniline (PANI) nanowires in the creation
of a biosensor, incorporating surface immobilization of monoclonal
antibodies (mAbs) targeting specific proteins using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide
(EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS). Modifying the
surface is essential for nanowires to improve their in vivo suitability for various biomedical applications such as tissue engineering,
wound dressing, immobilized enzymes, and drug delivery. 
Similarly, Xie et al. have engineered
poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)
(PEDOT) nanowire devices for protein detection purposes. These nanowires
were constructed with carboxylic acid side chain functional groups
(poly EDOT-COOH), enabling bioconjugation through an electric field-assisted
technique. The modified PEDOT nanowires demonstrated properties of
a p-type field effect transistor (FET) and could be utilized for the
identification of specific target protein molecules. In addition, semiconducting polymer dots (Pdots) have been
developed as optical probes for biosensing applications. Facile surface
and intraparticle modifications enable Pdots to detect various biological
parameters, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), typical metal ions,
pH values, temperature, and various biomolecules.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, the integration
of synthetic and natural polymers
in biosensors for cancer detection represents a groundbreaking approach
with immense potential for improving early diagnosis and treatment
outcomes for cancer patients. The unique properties of synthetic polymers,
such as tunable functionalities and high sensitivity, complement the
biocompatibility and sustainability offered by natural polymers, creating
powerful synergy in biosensor design. This combination enables the
detection of cancer biomarkers with exceptional accuracy, selectivity,
and efficiency. The versatility of polymer-based biosensors allows
for real-time monitoring, noninvasive detection, and precise identification
of cancer-related molecules in biological samples. However, the stability
and longevity of biosensors present significant challenges that can
impede their real-world applications. To address these issues, continued
research efforts aimed at optimizing polymer characteristics, improving
sensor performance, and exploring novel detection strategies will
be crucial in enhancing stability and reducing the oxidation of target
molecules. The successful development and application of these biosensors
in cancer diagnostics underscore their significance in advancing personalized
medicine and enhancing patient care. With ongoing advancements, these
biosensors hold great promise in revolutionizing cancer diagnosis
and contributing to better therapeutic outcomes for individuals affected
by this disease.
