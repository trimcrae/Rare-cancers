## Highlights
•NPM1 mutations are the most common genetic abnormalities in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), affecting downstream oncogenic pathways.
•lncRNAs play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis, prognosis, and chemoresistance of NPM1c+ AML.
•Key lncRNAs, such as HOTAIRM1, HOXB-AS3, CRNDE, HOXBLINC, LONA, IFEX9, XLOC_109,948, and HOTTIP have expression levels closely associated with clinical outcomes in NPM1c+ AML.
•lncRNAs regulate molecular mechanisms in AML by interacting with proteins, RNA, and DNA, influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, and viability.
•Aberrant expression of lncRNAs serves as potential biomarkers for AML, offering new directions for personalized therapy.
Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, NPM1, Long noncoding RNAs

## Abstract
In the landscape of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) research, mutations in nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) are the most prevalent genetic alterations. The leukemogenic mutant variant, NPM1c⁺, is associated with a distinct gene expression profile linked to leukemia, but the downstream oncogenic pathways remain only partially understood. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are RNA molecules with known regulatory roles in human development and disease. Research implicates many lncRNAs in hematopoiesis and leukemogenesis, revealing correlations between their expression and clinical parameters in AML patients. While NPM1c⁺ AML exhibits a distinct lncRNA signature, it remains contentious whether these molecules are bona fide drivers or passenger events, and how their context-dependent functions can be therapeutically exploited.
This review focuses on lncRNAs in NPM1c⁺ AML, highlighting their roles in pathogenesis, prognosis, and chemoresistance. By systematically elucidating the role of lncRNAs as pivotal factors in the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of NPM1c⁺ AML, this study addresses a gap in the existing literature. Our analysis of specific lncRNAs, such as HOTAIRM1, HOXB-AS3, CRNDE, HOXBLINC, LONA, IFEX9, XLOC_109948, and HOTTIP, enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying AML in the context of NPM1c⁺. These findings lay the groundwork for developing targeted therapies and improved prognostic tools for NPM1c⁺AML.

## Introduction
Leukemia, a blood cancer, is mainly divided into myeloid and lymphoid types [ , ]. The global incidence of AML is rising. In 2016, 85,000 people died from AML worldwide, and this number is expected to double by 2040 [ , ]. Research has identified key drivers of AML, including genetic mutations and, more recently, non-coding RNAs [ , ]. AML classification has moved from being based on cell appearance to incorporating molecular features. The older French–American–British (FAB) system has been largely replaced by the World Health Organization (WHO) classification ( ) [ , ]. The 2022 WHO and European Leukemia Net (ELN) guidelines now recognize NPM1-mutant AML (NPM1c⁺AML) as a distinct disease [ , ]. NPM1c⁺ is the most common genetic subtype in adult AML, found in 30–35 % of cases and up to 60 % of patients with normal chromosomes. It is rare in children [ , , , ]. NPM1c⁺ usually occurs alongside other mutations in genes like DNMT3A, TET2, or FLT3, following a stepwise development pattern [ , , ]. Current guidelines require testing for both NPM1 and FLT3 mutations to determine patient risk. While NPM1c⁺ alone often means a better outlook, its combination with FLT3-ITD or DNMT3A mutations leads to worse outcomes [ , , , ]. NPM1c⁺ is also a key marker for tracking minimal residual disease (MRD), allowing for early relapse prediction [ , ].
 Because NPM1c⁺ is so central to AML, understanding how it affects non-coding RNAs, especially lncRNAs, is a major research focus. This review summarizes current knowledge on lncRNAs that are altered in NPM1c⁺ AML, focusing on how they contribute to leukemia development, their impact on prognosis, and their potential as therapeutic targets. By combining different research perspectives, we aim to clarify the role of lncRNAs in this AML subtype and highlight areas for future precision medicine approaches.

## Structure and functions of NPM1
Nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1), also known as B23, NO38, or numatrin, is a highly phosphorylated nucleolar protein whose expression is markedly induced by cellular stress and transformation [ , ]. The NPM1 gene, located at 5q35, spans ∼25 kb and contains 12 exons, encoding a multifunctional protein involved in ribosome biogenesis, centrosome duplication, rRNA processing/export, chromatin remodeling, DNA repair, stress response, and liquid–liquid phase separation ( ). Wild-type NPM1 shuttles dynamically between the nucleus and cytoplasm but is predominantly retained in the nucleolus [ , ].
 In AML, NPM1 mutations almost exclusively occur in exon 12, typically involving a 4-bp insertion that causes a frameshift. This disrupts the C-terminal nucleolar localization signal (NoLS)—most commonly by loss of tryptophan residues W288/W290—and simultaneously generates a novel nuclear export signal (NES), resulting in aberrant cytoplasmic accumulation of the mutant protein (NPM1c⁺) [ , ]( ). All NPM1c⁺ variants produce a stable, oncogenic isoform associated with a unique transcriptional signature: upregulation of HOX/MEIS1 genes and loss of CD34 expression. Murine models expressing humanized NPM1c⁺recapitulate this profile and develop myeloid leukemia [ , , ]. Mechanistically, cytoplasmic mislocalization impairs apoptosis by inhibiting caspase-6/8 and disrupts normal myeloid differentiation [ ].
 

## Structure and functions of lncRNAs
AML is highly heterogeneous, driven by intricate genetic and epigenetic interactions [ , ]. While early research focused on protein-coding genes—advances in next-generation sequencing have revealed that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) dominate the transcriptome (∼97 %) [ , ]( ). Among these, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs; >200 nt) have emerged as key regulatory molecules despite lacking protein-coding capacity [ ]. As of 2024, GENCODE has cataloged 19,370 human lncRNAs. Though generally expressed at lower levels and less evolutionarily conserved than mRNAs—initially misclassified as transcriptional noise [ ]—lncRNAs exhibit greater tissue and developmental specificity than many proteins, underpinning their functional relevance [ , ]. They regulate diverse cellular processes: modulating transcription via cis/trans mechanisms, organizing nuclear architecture, controlling mRNA stability and translation, and scaffolding cytoplasmic signaling complexes [ , , ]. Although predominantly nuclear, growing evidence supports critical cytoplasmic roles for lncRNAs as well [ ]
 

## LncRNAs associated with AML
Dysregulated lncRNAs contribute significantly to AML pathogenesis and progression. For example, low MBNL1-AS1 expression correlates with poor prognosis [ ], while CD27-AS1 promotes leukemic cell proliferation and invasion via the miR-224–5p/PBX3 axis [ ]. A recent meta-analysis confirms that lncRNA aberrations are associated with reduced overall and relapse-free survival in AML patients [ ], Functionally, lncRNAs act as versatile regulators of chromatin architecture, transcription, and RNA stability—modulating processes such as epigenetic remodeling, enhancer-promoter looping, and mRNA decay [ , , , , ]. Their expression is itself shaped by DNA methylation, histone modifications, and other ncRNAs, further linking them to disease heterogeneity and clinical outcomes [ , , ]. LncRNAs play a critical role as either "oncogenes" or "tumor suppressor genes" in hematological malignancies [ , ]. Although they represent highly attractive drug targets, their low evolutionary conservation, complex functional mechanisms, and tissue-specific expression pose fundamental challenges for target validation and drug delivery. To date, no lncRNA-targeting drug has been approved globally, and the entire field remains in the preclinical research stage. Current research efforts are focused on validating the pathogenicity of lncRNAs and screening specific inhibitors. Notably, NPM1c⁺ AML exhibits a distinct lncRNA signature [ , ], underscoring the context-dependent nature of their roles. This review synthesizes current knowledge on NPM1-associated lncRNAs (see ), aiming to clarify their mechanistic and translational implications in NPM1c⁺ AML biology.
 

## Important role of lncRNA in NPM1c⁺ AML

## Epigenetic regulators: architects of the chromatin landscape

## HOXBLINC
Disruption of HOXA/B gene regulation is a critical mechanism in the etiology of leukemia and the dysregulation of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) [ ]. HOXBLINC is a long non-coding RNA located upstream of the HOXB gene cluster and is significantly upregulated in NPM1c⁺- AML, where it modulates HOXA/B transcription via epigenetic remodeling [ , ]. Functionally, HOXBLINC recruits the Setd1a/MLL1 complex to deposit H3K4me3 at HOXA/B promoters, sustaining high expression of NPM1c⁺-signature genes (e.g., MEIS1, RUNX1), thereby enhancing self-renewal of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells and promoting leukemogenesis [ , , ]. Knockdown of HOXBLINC in OCI-AML3 cells impairs viability, induces apoptosis, and attenuates NPM1c⁺-driven leukemic initiation; murine models further demonstrate that HOXBLINC-transgenic and NPM1c⁺ knock-in HSPCs exhibit highly overlapping transcriptomic and chromatin architectures [ ], supporting its role as a core co-driver in NPM1c⁺ AML ( . A-1).
 Evidence Gaps &Translational Challenges:The HOXBLINC–MLL1 axis is a functional oncogenic module in NPM1c⁺ AML and a promising theoretical therapeutic target. However, current evidence has important limitations. Most mechanistic studies rely on NPM1c⁺ knock-in mouse models and the OCI‑AML3 cell line, with limited validation in primary patient cells, which reduces clinical relevance. Key upstream regulatory mechanisms remain unclear: it is unknown whether NPM1c⁺ directly binds the HOXBLINC promoter or indirectly activates it by remodeling HOX cluster chromatin. The functional relationship between HOXBLINC and the nearby lncRNA HOXB‑AS3, which also binds MLL1, is also unresolved, as direct comparative knockout studies are lacking. While targeting this axis (e.g., with antisense oligonucleotides) is theoretically possible, clinical translation faces challenges including inefficient nuclear delivery, potential off-target effects, and a lack of suitable preclinical models for testing efficacy and resistance. Therefore, before advancing HOXBLINC–MLL1 as a therapeutic target, these knowledge gaps must be addressed in more physiologically relevant models, and translational hurdles must be overcome.

## HOTTIP
HOTTIP (HOXA transcript at the distal tip) , a lincRNA transcribed from the 5′ tip of the HOXA locus that coordinates the activation of multiple 5′ HOXA genes in vivo [ ]. HOTTIP plays a significant role in regulating the development and progression of various malignancies. Studies suggest that HOTTIP, through its aberrant overexpression, promotes tumor cell proliferation, reduces apoptosis, and enhances migratory capacity, thereby driving tumor development [ , , ]. Specifically in mixed-lineage leukemia gene (MLL, KMT2A) rearranged (MLLr+) or NPM1c⁺ AML, its overexpression is associated with poor prognosis [ ]. In NPM1c⁺ AML, the NPM1c⁺ drives dysregulated HOTTIP expression by remodeling chromatin three-dimensional architecture and hijacking oncogenic transcriptional programs. Specifically, NPM1c⁺ binds to active chromatin regions, interferes with CTCF-mediated chromatin topology, aberrantly activates the genomic region harboring the HOX gene cluster, and consequently leads to the co-upregulation of HOTTIP. HOTTIP dysfunction further drives aberrant posterior HOXA gene expression by altering topologically associating domains (TADs) in the AML genome: it directly interacts with and regulates a subset of CTCF-binding sites, recruits the CTCF/cohesin complex and R-loop-associated regulators to form R-loops, thereby strengthening CTCF boundaries, facilitating TAD formation, and ultimately driving oncogenic transcription and leukemic development [ , , ]. Concurrently, HOTTIP regulates the recruitment of the WDR5/MLL complex to coordinate active chromatin modifications and HOXA gene expression, forming a feedback loop that sustains the leukemic state [ , ]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that HOTTIP loss attenuates leukemogenesis in transplanted mice, while reactivation of HOTTIP in CTCF-boundary-attenuated AML cells restores leukemic TADs, transcription, and leukemogenesis. Aberrant Hottip expression in mice also abnormally promotes hematopoietic stem cell self-renewal by altering hematopoietic gene-associated chromatin signatures and transcriptional programs, leading to AML-like disease ( . A-2) [ , ].
Evidence Gaps &Translational Challenges: In critically assessing HOTTIP's role in NPM1c⁺ AML, several limitations in current studies need careful consideration. First, the proposed R-loop-dependent mechanism is mainly validated by RNase H1 overexpression, which can itself trigger a DNA damage response, complicating the interpretation of results. Second, although HOTTIP is highly expressed and linked to poor prognosis in NPM1c⁺ AML, a direct causal relationship between the NPM1c⁺ mutation and HOTTIP upregulation has not been established. Its overexpression may simply be a side effect of the broader activation of the HOXA gene cluster. Additionally, existing research has largely ignored the potential role of the neighboring antisense transcript HOTTIP-AS, which could influence chromatin structure and confound mechanistic conclusions. Therefore, future work should use more precise genetic tools to confirm the R-loop mechanism, clarify the direct regulatory link with NPM1c⁺, and account for the contribution of nearby non-coding RNAs.

## miRNA sponges and modulators of signaling pathways

## CRNDE
Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) is defined by the PML/RARα fusion oncogene [ ]. However, this lesion alone is insufficient for leukemogenesis, necessitating cooperating factors. Through RNA-seq analysis, Ma and colleagues identified the long non-coding RNA CRNDE (Colorectal Neoplasia Differentially Expressed) as significantly upregulated in both APL and NPM1c⁺ AML, but absent in the pre-leukemic phase of APL [ ]. CRNDE is nuclear-localized, non-protein-coding, and gives rise to multiple isoforms—including the evolutionarily conserved LINC00180. Although first discovered in colorectal cancer, accumulating evidence indicates that CRNDE exerts oncogenic activity by modulating proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and migration across malignancies [ , ]. Functional studies demonstrate that CRNDE knockdown induces myeloid differentiation (evidenced by increased CD11b⁺ cells), suppresses proliferation in APL and FAB-M3 AML cell lines (e.g., NB4, OCI-AML3), and prolongs survival in APL mouse models [ , ]. Transcriptomic profiling reveals that CRNDE regulates a network enriched for myeloid differentiation pathways and key hematopoietic transcription factors, including CEBPA, CEBPE, and NOTCH2. The role of CRNDE in AML exhibits remarkable context-dependency. In APL, CRNDE is markedly upregulated and cooperates with the PML/RARα fusion oncogene to drive leukemogenesis. Mechanistically, CRNDE acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) for the miR-181 family, thereby derepressing NOTCH2 and enforcing a differentiation block [ ]. In NPM1c⁺ AML, high CRNDE expression is linked to better outcomes, but how it works is unknown. Studies show it correlates with the NPM1 mutation and may block cell differentiation, yet no specific molecular pathway has been identified. This shift in prognostic meaning indicates that CRNDE's role depends heavily on the leukemia cell's genetic and molecular context ( . B) [ ].
Evidence Gaps and Translational Challenges: The core "molecular switch" governing the functional transition of CRNDE between APL and NPM1c⁺ AML remains unknown. Direct evidence supporting a physical or functional interaction between CRNDE and PML/RARα is limited, and most functional validations rely on cell line models, lacking robust confirmation in more physiologically relevant in vivo systems. Furthermore, studies have not adequately accounted for the confounding effects of common co-occurring mutations, such as FLT3-ITD, introducing significant uncertainty regarding the efficacy of CRNDE as an independent therapeutic target. Therefore, future research must prioritize elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the context-dependent actions of CRNDE and rigorously evaluate its translational potential using more accurate models that recapitulate the complexity of the native disease microenvironment.

## Protein scaffolds and mediators of nucleocytoplasmic shuttling

## HOTAIRM1
Research indicates that lncRNAs play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis, prognosis, and chemotherapeutic drug resistance of AML [ , ]. HOTAIRM1 is a myeloid-specific lncRNA transcribed from the intergenic region between HOXA1 and HOXA2. Lacking protein-coding potential, it modulates the epigenetic state of the HOXA gene cluster by interacting with the UTX/MLL or PRC2 complexes, and mediates chromatin loop dissociation to suppress transcription of central HOXA genes [ , ]. In NB4 cells, HOTAIRM1 expression increases during all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA)-induced granulocytic differentiation; its knockdown impairs differentiation, sustains a proliferative state, and attenuates ATRA-mediated activation of maturation-associated genes [ , ]. indicating its role as a key regulator of hematopoietic differentiation.
Clinically, HOTAIRM1 is markedly overexpressed in NPM1c⁺ AML patients and strongly associated with adverse prognosis [ ]. NPM1c⁺ AML drives the upregulation of HOTAIRM1 expression primarily through KLF5-dependent transcriptional activation, induced by the mutation itself, and the hijacking of the transcriptional program of the HOX gene cluster. Functionally, HOTAIRM1 exerts its oncogenic effects via a spatially coordinated dual‑compartment mechanism: in the nucleus, it acts as a scaffold to promote the assembly of the MDM2–EGR1 complex, leading to accelerated proteasomal degradation of the tumor suppressor EGR1, thereby inhibiting differentiation and activating autophagy and cell proliferation; in the cytoplasm, it functions as a competing endogenous RNA that sequesters miR‑152‑3p, thereby relieving the inhibition of its target ULK3 and further amplifying the autophagy signal. This HOTAIRM1‑centered dual‑functional axis collectively contributes to the pathogenesis of NPM1c⁺ AML, providing important insights into the molecular mechanisms of this leukemia subtype and suggesting HOTAIRM1 as a potential therapeutic target [ ]. Consistent with these mechanisms, in vivo studies demonstrate that knockdown of HOTAIRM1 significantly attenuates disease progression and extends survival in murine models of NPM1c⁺ AML ( . C-1) [ ].
Moreover, high HOTAIRM1 expression is significantly associated with reduced 5-year overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS), as well as increased 5-year cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR). Notably, the combination of HOTAIRM1 and miR-196b serves as an independent prognostic biomarker, retaining predictive power specifically within the NPM1c⁺ subgroup [ ], highlighting its translational potential.
Evidence Gaps &Translational Challenges: Current studies have confirmed that HOTAIRM1 is highly expressed in NPM1c⁺ AML, is partially regulated upstream by mutant NPM1, and promotes leukemia cell proliferation downstream through mechanisms such as degrading EGR1 and sponging miR-152–3p However, significant gaps remain in this field. Mechanistically, its complete upstream regulatory network and potential other functional variants have not been elucidated. In terms of translational application, directly targeting HOTAIRM1 presents technical challenges, and its downstream pathways may exhibit redundancy, which could compromise the efficacy of single-target approaches. More importantly, existing research primarily focuses on the NPM1c⁺ subtype; its role and clinical value in other AML subtypes lack validation, and its correlation with patient treatment response has not been confirmed by prospective studies. Therefore, the full biological functions of HOTAIRM1, its generalizability across subtypes, and its translational potential still require more in-depth mechanistic exploration and systematic clinical research to clarify.

## HOXB-AS3
lncRNAs are key regulators of cellular function and disease. One such lncRNA, HOXB-AS3, is located within the HOXB gene cluster and is found in both the cytoplasm and nucleus [ , ]. It is specifically upregulated in patients with NPM1c⁺ AML compared to those with wild-type NPM1 or healthy cells, driven by the NPM1c⁺mutation itself [ ]. Functionally, reducing HOXB-AS3 expression in leukemia cells (OCI-AML3) alters the cell cycle, decreasing the proportion of cells in the S phase and increasing those in the G2/M phase. While apoptosis remains largely unchanged, the colony-forming ability of these cells is significantly impaired after HOXB-AS3 knockdown [ , ]. Conversely, overexpressing HOXB-AS3 promotes leukemia cell proliferation, indicating that its levels modulate the proliferative state of leukemia stem cells [ , ]. HOXB-AS3 exerts its effects by interacting with the RNA-binding protein EBP1 [ ]. EBP1 is involved in ribosome biogenesis and interacts with NPM1 [ , , ]. HOXB-AS3 expression influences the EBP1-NPM1 interaction and complex formation at the rDNA locus, thereby regulating ribosomal RNA (rRNA) transcription to sustain AML proliferation. Preclinically, knocking down HOXB-AS3 significantly extended the survival of mice transplanted with primary human AML cells, demonstrating anti-leukemic efficacy [ ]. This positions HOXB-AS3 as a promising, though rare, example of a lncRNA target for AML therapy. Further proteomic analyses have also linked HOXB-AS3 to other cellular complexes, such as the DSHB complex involved in DNA repair ( . C-2)[90].
Evidence Gaps &Translational Challenges: Assessing the potential of the long non-coding RNA HOXB-AS3 as a therapeutic target for NPM1c⁺ AML reveals both promise and challenges. Current research has elucidated its core mechanism, and preclinical inhibition experiments have demonstrated promising anti-leukemic effects. However, translating this target into clinical application still faces multiple obstacles. First, its mechanism of action is not fully understood; beyond the known EBP1-NPM1 axis, whether other key interacting molecules exist requires further exploration. Second, its functional specificity in different cellular subsets, such as leukemia stem cells, and the influence of the bone marrow microenvironment remain insufficiently studied. Furthermore, the clinical correlation between HOXB-AS3 expression levels and patient prognosis awaits validation through large-scale cohort data.

## LONA
In a study using RNA sequencing of AML patients, Gourvest and colleagues identified the long non-coding RNA LONA, which is overexpressed in NPM1c⁺ AML and shows distinct functions depending on whether NPM1 is mutated or wild-type. LONA, an lncRNA linked to leukemogenesis and tumor invasiveness, is normally cytoplasmic in AML cells with wild-type NPM1 (which itself is nuclear). However, when mutant NPM1 accumulates in the cytoplasm, LONA is upregulated and moves to the nucleus—either through active translocation or impaired nuclear export [ ].
Ex vivo assays show that reducing LONA induces apoptosis, lowers cell viability, and increases chemosensitivity, regardless of NPM1 status. In contrast, LONA overexpression blocks myeloid differentiation—but only in the context of NPM1 mutation: in wild-type NPM1 cells, cytoplasmic LONA promotes differentiation, whereas in NPM1c⁺ cells, nuclear LONA inhibits it. Both in vitro and in vivo data suggest that LONA-overexpressing cells rely on microenvironmental interactions to boost proliferation and resist endogenous AraC chemotherapy [ , , ].
Beyond differentiation, LONA regulates genes involved in cell–matrix and cell–cell interactions. Upon entering the nucleus, it controls key myeloid differentiation genes such as THSB1, MAFB, and ASB2 [ ], and also modulates adhesion molecules, signaling factors, and chemokines like CXCL11[95]. Together, Gourvest et al. uncovered a novel leukemogenesis mechanism driven by the coordinated nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of mutant NPM1 and LONA, offering new insights into AML biology ( . C-3).
Evidence Gaps &Translational Challenges:The study by Gourvest et al. first revealed the unique role of long non-coding RNA LONA in NPM1c⁺ AML. However, key mechanisms remain unclear, hindering clinical application. Major gaps include the unknown signals driving LONA into the nucleus, the unclear NPM1-LONA interaction, and the incomplete understanding of how LONA inhibits differentiation in the nucleus. These gaps challenge drug development targeting this pathway. A further complication is LONA's dual functionality: it has opposite effects in different cell types, meaning non-selective inhibition could harm normal cells. This necessitates precise targeting strategies. Additionally, LONA's pro-cancer effects may depend on the tumor microenvironment, suggesting targeting cancer cells alone may be insufficient.

## lncRNAs with limited functional annotation

## IFEX9 (FTO-lncRNA)
Noting notable expression levels of IFEX9 in one-third of NPM1c⁺AML cases, Saioa Arza-Apalategi and colleagues have investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the presence or absence of this LncRNA [ ]. The cellular localization suggests a nucleus residence for IFEX9, which, according to protein-coding predictive algorithms, lacks the catalytic domain characteristic of FTO (Fat Mass and Obesity-associated protein) and does not possess the capacity to encode proteins within the cytoplasmic milieu. Distinct transcriptomic profiles were observed in M1 (positive) and M4-M5 (negative) subtypes of NPM1c⁺, with IFEX9-positive cases exhibiting an enrichment of a program indicative of stem/progenitor cells, characterized by Cadherin and Wnt signaling pathways. Conversely, IFEX9-negative cases correspond to a more mature subgroup within NPM1c⁺ cases, as indicated by the overexpression of numerous myeloid cell surface markers, cytokines, chemokines, and transcription factors (such as CD14, IL-6 and IRF8) [ , ]. It is noteworthy that while IFEX9 is highly expressed in the majority of NPM1-positive cases, there is no difference in prognosis between cases that are IFEX9-positive and those that are IFEX9-negative. Uncovering the molecular complexity underlying the presence or absence of IFEX9 and determining its potential contribution to the pathogenesis of the disease will provide valuable insights ( . d-1).
Evidence Gaps &Translational Challenges: The paradox of IFEX9 is that its high expression correlates with better survival in univariate analysis but does not emerge as an independent prognostic factor in multivariate models. This can be explained by distinguishing its biological significance from its clinical utility. Biologically, high IFEX9 expression identifies a molecular subtype enriched with stem/progenitor cell features. Clinically, its prognostic signal may be masked by stronger factors such as NPM1 or FLT3-ITD mutations. Therefore, IFEX9 is more suitable as a biomarker for molecular subtyping rather than as an independent prognostic indicator. Its functional role in leukemia remains unclear, and its independent prognostic value lacks sufficient validation, which limits its clinical translation.

## XLOC_109,948
Studies have linked dysregulated lncRNAs to recurrent genetic mutations in AML and their impact on clinical prognosis [ , , ]. Using RNA-Seq data from 40 CN-AML patients, Etienne De Clara identified a distinct lncRNA expression profile in NPM1c⁺ cases. Among these, XLOC_109,948 was one of the most highly expressed [ ]. Predictive analysis indicates it is nuclear-localized and non-coding. Clinical data analysis showed that patients with high XLOC_109,948 expression had significantly worse overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Stratifying patients by both XLOC_109,948 expression and NPM1c⁺ status revealed that those with low XLOC_109,948 and NPM1c⁺ formed a favorable subgroup in terms of event-free survival (EFS), OS, and DFS. These findings suggest XLOC_109,948 could be a useful biomarker for risk stratification in CN-AML, especially in NPM1c⁺ patients.
Further experiments showed that knocking down XLOC_109,948 sensitizes NPM1c⁺ OCI-AML3 cells to chemotherapy. Specifically, its downregulation increased apoptosis in cells treated with Ara-C or ATRA [ ]. This implies that measuring XLOC_109,948 levels might help predict chemotherapy response. Together, these insights highlight the potential of lncRNAs like XLOC_109,948 as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets in AML ( . d-2).
Evidence Gaps &Translational Challenges: Research on the long non-coding RNA XLOC_109,948 in AML faces major gaps. Its exact molecular role, including whether it functions as a ceRNA, what downstream pathways it affects, and which proteins it interacts with, remains unknown. Current studies are confined to knockdown experiments in a single cell line, which severely limits mechanistic and translational insights. As a result, despite its strong association with clinical outcomes, XLOC_109,948 has not yet been validated as a druggable target, and no targeted therapies currently exist. Therefore, XLOC_109,948 should be regarded as a high-priority candidate for in-depth functional and mechanistic investigation rather than a proven therapeutic target.

## Limitations and future directions
Although lncRNAs have been recognized as important regulators in the pathogenesis of NPM1c⁺ AML, much of the current work remains based on correlative expression analyses. A systematic and causal understanding of how lncRNAs reprogram transcriptional and epigenetic states in the context of NPM1c⁺—and contribute to leukemic transformation—is still limited. Notably, the genetic heterogeneity of AML, together with dynamic interactions between leukemic cells and the bone marrow microenvironment, reduces the general applicability of individual lncRNA markers and presents a substantial challenge to clinical translation.
The field is now at a pivotal juncture, transitioning from phenotypic association toward mechanistic causality. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated functional perturbations, high-throughput lncRNA screening, and integrative multi-omics approaches are catalyzing a paradigm shift—enabling the identification of high-confidence “core driver” lncRNAs and the dissection of their functional interactomes with miRNAs, chromatin-modifying complexes, and key signaling nodes. Concurrently, advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics are overcoming traditional limitations in resolution and dimensionality, revealing the cell-type-specific expression dynamics of lncRNAs across leukemic hierarchies and their spatial organization within the bone marrow niche, thereby providing unprecedented spatiotemporal insights into clonal evolution and therapy resistance.
With the advancement of mechanistic understanding, early translational efforts are emerging: antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small-molecule inhibitors, and RNA-targeting degradation technologies have demonstrated promising efficacy against oncogenic lncRNAs in preclinical studies [ , , ]. Notably, combination strategies that pair lncRNA inhibition with conventional chemotherapy are being actively investigated to overcome therapeutic resistance and improve selectivity. Nevertheless, clinical translation continues to face persistent challenges: the safe and efficient delivery of therapeutic molecules to specific diseased tissues remains a core technical bottleneck. Additionally, potential safety concerns such as off-target effects and immunogenicity cannot be overlooked, underscoring the need to develop conformation-selective intervention approaches.
Looking ahead, establishing a closed-loop research framework that integrates mechanistic dissection, functional validation, targeted intervention, and clinical evaluation is essential. Developing large-scale, prospective, multicenter cohorts will enable rigorous assessment of the clinical utility of lncRNA expression signatures in early detection, molecular subtyping, risk stratification, and dynamic monitoring of measurable residual disease (MRD), thereby supporting the transition of lncRNAs from candidate biomarkers to standardized diagnostic and therapeutic tools. At the same time,the development of appropriate delivery platforms and the innovation of precise targeting strategies are critical for advancing high-confidence lncRNA targets into Phase I/II clinical trials. Ultimately, only through coordinated progress in deep mechanistic research, cutting-edge technological development, and clinically oriented translation can lncRNAs realize their full potential as a novel class of druggable non-coding drivers in precision oncology for hematologic malignancies.

## Conclusions
Collectively, in the context of NPM1c⁺, AML exhibits a distinctive expression profile of lncRNAs. Studies have delineated the mechanisms by which specific lncRNAs—such as HOTAIRM1, HOXB-AS3, CRNDE, HOXBLINC, LONA, IFEX9, XLOC_109,948, and HOTTIP as referenced in this study—influence the pathobiologic processes of NPM1c⁺ AML. These molecules specifically modulate gene expression by interacting with proteins, RNA, or DNA, regulating cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and viability. Moreover, lncRNAs play a significant role in facilitating or inhibiting key signaling pathways, including autophagy, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis, which offering novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of AML under conditions of NPM1c⁺. At present, a dearth of comprehensive data characterizes our understanding of the etiology, progression, diagnostic modalities, therapeutic approaches, and prognostic indicators of NPM1c⁺ AML. To elucidate the multifaceted elements that shape the course of this malignancy, rigorous and meticulous investigation is of paramount importance. The realm of lncRNAs emerges as a compelling frontier within this domain, offering a wealth of untapped potential. Upcoming research must focus on the complex role of lncRNAs in AML, particularly their functional implications in the context of NPM1c⁺. The dissection of lncRNAs' involvement promises to unveil novel perspectives on the pathophysiological underpinnings and prognostic nuances of NPM1c⁺ AML, while the identification of lncRNAs that are differentially expressed in NPM1c⁺ AML provides a foundation for the development of targeted therapies that could potentially enhance treatment efficacy and selectivity, ultimately improving patient care and quality of life. In summary, lncRNAs pave a promising path towards personalized treatment for NPM1c⁺ AML.
