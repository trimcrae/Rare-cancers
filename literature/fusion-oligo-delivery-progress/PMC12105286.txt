## Introduction
Cancer is the second-leading cause of mortality worldwide. Several standard medicines and chemotherapies have combated cancer [ ]. Furthermore, bone cancer (BC) is pretty infrequent. These complicated malignancies typically affect the long bones and pelvis [ ]. Moreover, BC is divided into osteosarcoma (OS), chondrosarcoma (CHS), and Ewing sarcoma (EWS) [ , ]. There are many different kinds of BC. Some start in the bone itself, called a primary tumor, while others spread to the bone from somewhere else in the body, called a secondary or spreading tumor [ , ]. There are two types of primary BC: benign and malignant. Each has biological patterns, clinical signs, and treatment reactions [ , ]. Regarding primary cancerous BC, OS is by far the most common. It tends to happen to teens and young adults who are growing quickly. This kind of tumor is defined by cancer cells that make osteoid tissue, which is young bone [ ]. Children and teens are most likely to get EWS, a hazardous type of cancer. It has tiny, round, blue cells that make it stand out. It usually appears in the hip, femur, humerus, ribs, and spine [ ]. Conversely, CHS is a malignant neoplasm that generates cartilage and is predominantly seen in adults, with the pelvis, femur, and shoulder girdle being the most common manifestation sites [ ]. OS accounts for about 35% of all primary invasive BC. CHS is at 30%, and EWS is at 16%. However, the occurrence rates vary widely worldwide. For example, OS is more common in some Asian countries than in Western countries [ ].
When breast BC to other body regions, it typically originates in the pelvis and spine. This may result in motor dysfunction, neurological manifestations in the impacted tissue, discomfort, fractures, and further symptoms [ ]. The present clinical management of BC involves extensive surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, which are generally employed in conjunction [ , ]. However, some tumor cells may stay in the area after removal, and some BC are not sensitive to radiation treatment and often are not sensitive to chemotherapy. This can cause the cancer to return or spread after surgery [ , ]. Interestingly, the current chemotherapy methods used to treat OS often fail. This is mainly because they hurt healthy cells and tissues without targeting cancer cells, cancer cells may develop ways to resist drugs, and it's hard to get anticancer drugs to the tumor cells where they need to go [ , ]. Bone is a common place for cancer cells to spread in people with advanced cancer, and it's also home to some rare but difficult primary tumors [ ]. Treatments for bone sarcomas and metastases have slowed, so researchers have begun looking into additional and different approaches to standard therapies [ ]. So, a different way to treat BC and other malignant cancers is effective and safe [ ].
Nanotechnology has changed significantly in the last few years and can be used in many ways in healthcare. Nanoscale materials have a good chance of curing cancer because they are safe and don't cause many problems [ ]. Also, nanoparticles (NPs) are tiny particles (1–100 nm in diameter and width) that have different qualities than their micron-scale cousins (1–100 μm) [ ]. Nanotechnology has created new and improved materials used in medicine, especially in healthcare [ ]. Metal nanoparticles (MNPs) are flexible nanoscale particles used often to find and treat cancer. MNPs can kill cancer cells by stopping the cell cycle and causing death. MNPs also stop tumor formation, spread, and inflammation, which prevents cancer growth [ , ]. Regarding metallic oxide NPs, ferroferric oxide (Fe3O4) and iron oxide were the most studied nanomaterials in OS. These NPs were mainly used for heat treatment because they could turn magnetic field energy into heat [ , ]. Nanomedicines based on iron oxide NPs (IONPs) have been used recently for improved cancer-related mortality detection, diagnosis, therapy, and control. Furthermore, using surface-engineered IONPs for targeted drug delivery may lessen the severity of side effects while increasing therapy efficacy. IONPs are a potential drug delivery technology for cancer treatment because of their superparamagnetic behavior and efficient size control capabilities. Essential characteristics, including size, shape, and superparamagnetic properties, should be optimized in their preparation processes. Additionally, their surface characteristics must improve the bloodstream's colloidal stability and half-life. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently approved using several IONPs to treat anemia and as contrast agents in bioimaging [ ]. Many dangerous compounds that are harmful to the environment and individuals are used in the chemical synthesis of IONPs. However, the most recent development in their green synthesis may eliminate the need for outside chemicals by offering naturally occurring capping and reducing agents for iron ion reduction [ ]. Furthermore, the unique physicochemical features of metal-based NPs bestow potential biological effects and pose unforeseen hazardous dangers to the human body simultaneously. To safely use metal-based NPs in people, researchers need a thorough understanding of NP toxicity. Hopefully, the convergence of related disciplines will considerably enhance the development of nanotoxicity research, making the deployment of metal-based NPs in humans safer [ , ].
The biosynthesis of NPs employs an environmentally benign, green-chemistry-based strategy that includes bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, yeast, algae, and plants. Plant extract-based NP synthesis provides an eco-friendly, clean, nontoxic, and cost-effective technique for synthesizing NPs of various shapes, sizes, and morphologies [ , ]. MNPs can be made using the biosynthesis route, which is better than some physicochemical methods because it can produce particles of better sizes and shapes. Using plant extracts to make NPs is better than using microorganisms because it doesn't need complicated steps like isolating, identifying, growing, and maintaining microorganisms [ – ]. For instance, because of their high surface area to volume ratio and intrinsic superparamagnetic characteristic, magnetic iron oxide NPs (MIONPs) have been a significant focus of study interest in biological applications. Several physical and chemical techniques have been devised for their synthesis, including electrochemical synthesis, thermal breakdown, solvothermal reduction, and coprecipitation. However, most of these approaches face environmental risks and operational challenges. As a result, current research is focused on synthesizing them from various biological or green resources, such as plant extracts, microorganisms, algae, glucose, starch, clays, etc. Because plant extracts can quickly decrease iron ions and are readily available in various forms, they are often appropriate for green synthesis [ ].
Additionally, using plants to make NPs is less expensive, faster, and easier to scale up for large-scale production [ ]. Nanotechnology is an exciting and growing area that includes many subfields, such as NPs, nanostructures, and nanomaterials [ ]. Biosynthesized green NPs are showing promise as tools for diagnosing and treating cancer [ ]. Recently, green synthesis to make MNPs for cancer treatments has come up, mostly because they are easier to work with in the lab [ ]. Alhagi maurorum, for instance, is a plant with various medicinal uses. It is a member of the Alhagi genus, Fabalesorder, Fabaceae family, and Plantae kingdom. Alhagi maurorum has been used since ancient times to treat various illnesses affecting the gastrointestinal, immunological, respiratory, hepatic, cardiovascular, urinary, and genital systems. To the best of our knowledge, no comparative study has yet been conducted on the anti-OS properties of zinc oxide (ZnO) NPs produced by Alhagi maurorum leaf aqueous extract, Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, and Alhagi maurorum leaf aqueous extract against OS cell lines in cellular models. Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, Alhagi maurorum leaf, and ZnO NPs were studied for their ability to inhibit OS cell lines (G-292, clone A141B1, MG-63, HOS, Hs 707(A).T, and Saos-2). ZnO NPs' half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) against the HOS, MG-63, G-292, clone A141B1, Saos-2, and Hs 707(A).T cell lines were 234 ± 0, 285 ± 0, 327 ± 0, 372 ± 0, and 341 ± 0 µg/mL, respectively. Following a clinical investigation, Zn NPs containing an aqueous extract from Alhagi maurorum leaves can be an effective medication for human BC [ ].
MNPs have several uses as an anticancer treatment for BC. Thus, we reviewed the key characteristics and workings of different MNPs in cancer therapy. Next, we reviewed the limits and adverse consequences of the traditional procedures used to produce MNPs. Green synthesis is a workable way to lessen MNPs' adverse effects. Therefore, this work examined several green synthesis techniques for creating MNPs. Next, we examined how different MNPs made using green synthesis affected various BC, such as OS, CHS, and EWS. Lastly, we discussed the approach's benefits, drawbacks, and prospects for treating BC. Additionally, this study has given additional attention to the majority of research from the most recent years (2020–2025). However, reviews of earlier studies have supplemented part of the study on the green synthesis of MNPs for treating BC. This study has focused on the prospective use of MNPs using the green synthesis approach to treat BC. By creating a green synthesis technique for MNPs, researchers may aid in treating BC in the future.

## Anticancer mechanism of metal nanoparticles in bone cancers by ROS generation and apoptosis
Utilizing NPs has tremendous potential for drug delivery systems (DDSs) and medical applications, and promises in the fight against cancer and other disorders. Because of their tiny size, chemical makeup, structure, and large surface area, MNPs have exceptional physical and chemical characteristics that make them both economical and practical. Their better targeting capabilities, reduction of gene activity, and increased drug delivery efficiency have all been shown to have a substantial and positive influence on cancer therapy. Functionalized MNPs increase treatment precision by precisely depositing energy into malignancies via targeted ligands. Beyond their therapeutic effectiveness, MNPs are valuable instruments for seeing cancer cells, aiding diagnostic procedures. Using MNPs in therapeutic systems revolutionizes cancer care by enabling regulated medication release and simultaneous cancer diagnostics and therapy [ ]. Various MNPs have been developed to combat cancer. Silver (Ag), gold (Au), zinc (Zn), and copper (Cu) are the most efficacious [ ]. This NP's ability to fight cancer is due to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) inside cells. This sets off the autophagic, apoptotic, and necrotic death processes [ ]. The effect of NPs varies depending on the kind of cancer, demonstrating that the cellular response is also significant in addition to the unique features of NPs [ ]. Due to their nano size, these particles may quickly enter biological systems, meaning they might have a wide range of consequences on vulnerable species. Increased oxidative stress, aberrant apoptosis, altered gene expression, and pro-inflammatory effects of engineered NPs (ENPs). ENPs’ in vitro reactions to various cells also vary greatly, which might be related to comparable features of the ENPs' size, forms, aggregation, ENP kinds, surface modifications, surface charges, dosages, etc. These elements may impact the treated cells' ENP pathways [ ].
AgNPs can kill cancer cells and have a lot of promise as a tumor killer [ ]. For instance, OS is commonly referred to as osteogenic sarcoma. It is the predominant form of cancer. The dysfunction of the tumor suppressor p53 is prevalent in human malignancies and complicates the efficacy of medicines in inducing apoptosis in cancer cells. Significant concerns over this process have prompted scientists to develop a new class of anticancer drugs capable of inducing apoptosis independent of p53. Due to the distinctive deadly features of AgNPs, researchers investigated their potential to eradicate cancer cells lacking sufficient tumor suppressors. Researchers examined the effects of AgNPs on OS cells with varying p53 genetic backgrounds. Kovács et al. utilized 5 nm and 35 nm citrate-coated AgNPs since particle size may influence the chemical mechanisms by which AgNPs induce cellular mortality. Researchers identified that both varieties of AgNPs targeted mitochondria and induced cell death in U2Os cells possessing wild-type p53 and Saos-2 cells lacking p53. These data indicated that AgNPs can eliminate OS cells regardless of their p53 status, leading to the death of cancer cells devoid of p53. Consequently, AgNPs represent viable alternatives for novel chemotherapeutic strategies [ ].
In another research, the morphology and quantity of AuNPs significantly influence their cytotoxicity. Steckiewicz et al. demonstrated that AuNP stars pose the most threat to human cells. Researchers indicated that cancer cells have a higher susceptibility to destruction by AuNPs. Additionally, AuNP rods and stars induced OS cells to synthesize increased Bax protein levels while decreasing Bcl-2 protein levels. AuNPs were seen to penetrate the cell membrane and alter the ultrastructure of the cells. The findings unequivocally indicated that the cytotoxicity of AuNPs was contingent upon their morphology. The AuNP stars (about 215 nm), exhibiting the most significant anti-cancer efficacy, also had the highest cytotoxicity among the NPs examined. Conversely, AuNP spheres (about 6.3 nm), which appeared to be the least hazardous, had the poorest anti-cancer efficacy [ ].
It is essential to look into new ways to treat OS that can effectively stop the spread. Bioactive NPs like ZnO NPs can effectively stop OS growth. However, how they work and affect the spread of disease is still unclear. First, the researchers made ZnO NPs that were widely spread out. RNA-Seqs showed DEGs were more common in wingless/integrated (Wnt) and hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) signaling pathways in OS cells treated with ZnO NP. He et al. also showed that Zn2+ released from ZnO NPs decreased the production of β-catenin through the HIF-1α/BNIP3/LC3B pathway, part of the mitophagy process. When ZnO NPs and ICG-001, a β-catenin inhibitor, were used together, they effectively stopped OS lung spread and increased lifetime. Also, the tissue microarray of OS patients showed much higher levels of β-catenin mRNA, which showed that β-catenin plays a part in the development of OS. Investigators showed that ZnO NPs can stop OS spread by breaking down β-catenin in the HIF-1α/BNIP3/LC3B-mediated mitophagy pathway. It also showed that ZnO NPs could be used in many ways to treat metastatic OS [ , ].
Aloy et al. evaluated gadolinium oxide NPs (GdoNP, AGuIX®) in CHS to assess their efficacy as a radiosensitizing agent in vitro, employing spheroid CHS models to observe cellular interactions with the extracellular matrix and in vivo, utilizing a nude mouse model with a distinct tumor xenograft. Detecting proteoglycans in the extracellular matrix (ECM) of spheroids indicated the applicability of the three-dimensional model. Applying GdoNP with single or split irradiation increased cell death in 2D and 3D preparations. A single or fractionated dosage of 4 Gy, combined with an IT or IV injection of GdoNP, significantly inhibited tumor development in live organisms. Only the IT intervention prolonged the lifespan of the mice. The radiosensitizing impact of GdoNP was associated with a notable reduction in invasion and migration capabilities. In vivo, it was associated with decreased expression of PTX3, a protein involved in the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition pathway. This indicates that GdoNP may influence metastasis development. These findings constitute the first proof of concept for GdoNP's radiosensitizing action in CHSs. A multicenter, randomized Phase 2 investigation was also facilitated to evaluate the efficacy of GdoNP in conjunction with radiation for treating inoperable, painful musculoskeletal tumors [ ].
The Wnt signaling system regulates certain essential biological functions. As an intracellular signal transducer, the beta-catenin-dependent Wnt signaling (Wnt/β-catenin) pathway regulates the expression of critical developmental genes. Numerous malignancies are often linked to the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Recently, there has been a lot of interest in using the Wnt/β-catenin pathway as a possible therapeutic application to treat BCs [ , ]. Researchers created evenly distributed ZnO NPs and showed they could prevent migration, invasion, and the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), which are malignant activities linked to OS metastases. Wnt and HIF-1 signaling pathways were revealed to be enriched in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in OS cells treated with ZnO NP, according to RNA-Seqs. Researchers also demonstrated that the HIF-1α/BNIP3/LC3B-mediated mitophagy pathway caused the downregulation of β-catenin expression when Zn2+ was liberated from ZnO NPs. A prolonged survival period and a synergistic inhibitory impact on OS lung metastasis were shown by ZnO NPs in combination with the β-catenin inhibitor ICG-001. Furthermore, OS patients' tissue microarrays (TMA) revealed significantly greater β-catenin expression, suggesting that β-catenin plays a part in the development of OS. By breaking down β-catenin in the HIF-1α/BNIP3/LC3B-mediated mitophagy pathway, researchers demonstrated that ZnO NPs may prevent OS metastasis. However, they also revealed the broad potential of ZnO NPs in clinical OS therapy with metastasis [ ].
Because HIF-1α controls the tumor microenvironment (TME), scientists investigated the relationship between mitophagy and HIF-1α regulation in ZnO NP-induced OS inhibition in vitro and in vivo. After killing four standard human OS cell lines, they discovered that ZnO NPs increased the levels of the HIF-1α protein. Researchers demonstrated that NP-induced cell death was improved by further HIF-1α overexpression by a hypoxia inducer, CoCl2, or in an environment with 1% hypoxia, but that NP-induced cell death was prevented by contemporaneous HIF-1α suppression by a hypoxia inhibitor, YC-1, or HIF-1α siRNA. Researchers found that NP-induced cancer cell death followed an HIF-1α-BNIP3-LC3B-mediated mitophagy route and that HIF-1α interacted with the autophagy–Zn2+–ROS–autophagy cycle axis. It was shown that prolyl hydroxylase inhibition by both ROS and Zn2+ was responsible for the NP-upregulated production of the HIF-1α protein. Furthermore, the in vivo test verified ZnO NPs' safety and therapeutic efficacy on a model of OS in nude mice. The scientists elucidated the upstream regulatory mechanism of autophagy triggered by the NPs and furthered their in vivo anticancer potency [ ].
Current approaches to treating OS concentrate on using combination therapy to prevent osteolysis and cancer cell development. To improve the treatment of orthotopic OS, researchers first created novel “carrier free” core–shell metal-drug NPs that sensitize radiation and decrease osteoclast activity. Ferric ions self-assembled with zoledronate (ZOL) to generate the core. With an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) concentration of more than 60 weight percent and the insoluble components of ZOL and Fe, the resulting NPs may effectively cause cell death and are simple to synthesize on a large scale. Furthermore, the surface-modified hyaluronic acid and integrated ZOL may enable the NPs to evade skeletal absorption and raise the ZOL concentration at the tumor location. The released ZOL reduced osteoclast activity around the tumors to stop osteolysis once they had gathered there. In the meantime, the Fenton-like reaction-sensitized radiation killed the tumor cells that produced effective free radicals from HA@FeZOL. The results supported the viability of using osteolysis inhibition and sensitized radiation in conjunction with HA@FeZOL-mediated synergistic treatment to treat orthotopic OS. Thus, this easy-to-use nanoplatform offers a novel combinatorial approach to OS treatment and creates a space for metal-drug NPs [ ].

## Metal nanoparticles for optimizing DNA damage-based bone cancer therapy
The current therapy options targeting DNA damage have obvious limits in practical usage. The nucleus of cancer cells contains DNA, and to reach the nucleus, most agents that might damage DNA must first pass through a series of obstacles, such as the cell membrane, lysosomes, and nuclear envelope. There aren’t enough drug concentrations within the cell to cause DNA damage because of the efflux activity of transport proteins on cancer cell membranes. MNPs offer a potent method for successfully generating DNA damage to treat cancers. The optimization of size and surface changes of MNPs allows for responsive release and accurate transport to nuclei. The capacity to enter the cell nucleus and cause DNA damage is remarkable. Also, MNPs may build up a DNA damage burden in cancer cells by simultaneously interfering with normal cellular physiological processes and DNA damage repair pathways. When these processes come together, they kill cancer cells and produce many tumor antigens, such as DNA fragments, which activate the immune system. MNPs, with their dual capabilities of inducing DNA damage and triggering immune-mediated anti-tumor responses, provide a potentially flexible approach to tumor therapy [ ].
Researchers evaluated the efficacy of AgCl-NPs and Ag/AgCl-NPs against EWS cells. Human cells derived from the RPE-1 cell line (pigmented retinal epithelium) were utilized to demonstrate non-tumorigenic cells. The RPE-1 cells exhibited minimal impact from introducing AgCl-NPs or Ag/AgCl-NPs. There was a marginal decrease in cell quantity and viability, accompanied by a modest increase in the apoptosis rate. Nonetheless, there was no impairment of the lysosomes, alterations in ROS generation, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, or modifications in microfilaments or cellular regions. Upon treatment of A673 tumor cells with AgCl-NPs, their quantity and viability decreased by 65.05% and 99.17%, respectively. Upon treatment with Ag/AgCl-NPs, the figures decreased by 65.53% and 92.51%, respectively. The production of ROS and the mitochondrial membrane potential dramatically diminished after treating A673 cells with AgNPs. This increased the mortality rate of the population. Administration of the highest concentration of AgCl-NPs resulted in lysosomal damage in A673 cells. Researchers indicated that both AgCl-NPs and Ag/AgCl-NPs exhibited anticancer activity with minimal to no impact on healthy cells. This indicated their potential application in cancer therapy [ ].
Sha et al. showed that titanium dioxide (TiO2) NPs killed HeLa cells successfully in vitro through photocatalysis. This suggests TiO2 NPs could stop OS and CHS from reoccurring by killing bone tumor cells. Researchers looked into what TiO2 NPs might do to two types of cancer cells in the lab: U-2 OS and SW 1353 (CHS). TiO2 NPs killed cells in a way that depended on the amount and time they were present compared to the NP-free control. At 24 h, the median concentration of TiO2 NPs that stopped the growth of SW 1353 cells was 211.3 ± 15.2 µg/mL, and for U-2 OS cells, it was 5408.8 ± 45.9 µg/mL. It was more effective for TiO2 NPs amounts above 1 µg/mL to kill SW 1353 cells than U-2 OS NPs at all exposure times. The fact that ROS levels went up and GSH levels went down showed that TiO2 NPs killed cancer cells by causing oxidative stress. These findings indicated that TiO2 NPs might help reduce or stop the return of OS and CHS [ ].
Vijayakumar et al. demonstrated a straightforward technique for synthesizing safe AuNPs utilizing sodium borohydride as a reducing agent and chondroitin sulfate (CS) derived from shark cartilage as a cost-effective and water-soluble binding agent. The synthetic CS-Au NPs were purple and mostly spherical, with an average diameter of 40.768 nm. MTT indicated that CS-AuNPs markedly reduced the viability of human OS cells (MG63) at 100 μg/mL while exhibiting no effect on mouse embryonic fibroblast cells at the same dose. The CS-Au NPs were determined to be detrimental as they induced damaged mitochondria to produce more ROS. Treatment of MG63 cells with CS-Au NPs resulted in the formation of ROS and alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential. The investigation of apoptosis utilizing ethidium bromide dual staining and flow cytometry revealed that elevated CS-Au NPs markedly augmented the number of cells that had undergone death, as seen by the acridine orange/ethidium bromide labeling. Flow cytometry further demonstrated that CS-Au NPs impeded the apoptotic process in MG63 cells [ ].

## Metal nanoparticles as a drug delivery system for bone cancer cells
Most anticancer medications have an off-target impact that causes nonspecific cell death, making delivering these medicines to tumors exceedingly tricky. The cancer therapy age sees the exploration of multifunctionalized MNPs as a potential novel carrier system. Scientists studied metallic NPs for their ability to reduce anticancer drugs’ off-target effects by actively and passively targeting tumor cells [ , ]. The enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect and the photocatalytic activity of upconversion NPs (UCNPs) have also garnered significant interest in cancer therapy. Nanotechnology has recently made strides, demonstrating its effectiveness in cancer treatment [ ]. Finally, anti-metastatic osteolysis relies on anticancer therapy, which is insufficient without targeted delivery to the osteolytic region or in combination with anti-bone resorption medications. Optimized targeted delivery systems often use NPs that bind to specific ligands to successfully transport anticancer or anti-osteolysis medications to metastatic bone. Targeted delivery techniques limit the drug's distribution to the bone, tumor, or osteolytic location by systemic administration methods, including intravenous injection. Also, these targeted nanocarriers’ physicochemical properties dictate how they're used. To evade macrophage and renal clearance, nanocarriers should ideally be smaller than 200 nm but not exceed 400 nm. Smaller NPs (10–70 nm) are necessary to get through the sinusoidal capillaries in the bone. Additionally, compared to big NPs (approximately 320 nm), small neutral NPs (about 150 nm) were seven times more efficient in localizing in bone marrow. Bones were a common target for nanocarriers with a 70 to 100 nm diameter because of the longer blood circulation time they provide [ ].
Because MNPs increase the therapeutic index of medications via site specificity, avoid multidrug resistance (MDR), and effectively distribute therapeutic agents, their use has garnered much interest and offered significant advancements, particularly in medicine. MNPs provide many benefits when used in DDSs. These include enhanced stability and half-life of the drug carrier in circulation, necessary biodistribution, and passive or active targeting into the target location [ ].
Rahman et al. used doxorubicin (DOX) as a model drug to create Ag oxide doped mesoporous bioactive glass (Ag2O-MBG) NPs with controlled release. Using Pluronic 123 as an internal template, Ag2O-MBG NPs were made using the sol–gel technique. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to analyze the composition, shape, morphology, and size of the produced Ag2O-MBG NPs. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) and Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) methods were used to calculate the surface area and pore size. Using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, it was shown that hydroxycarbonate apatite (HCAp) formed while submerged in simulated bodily fluid (SBF). In vivo tissue histopathology and the MTT test revealed no adverse effects from the as-synthesised Ag2O-MBG NPs. The drug loading concentration (0.2–1.0 mg/mL) and pH (6.4–8.4) of the release medium were shown to impact the release of DOX, which was encapsulated with an 84% efficiency rate. For around two weeks, at a somewhat acidic pH of 6.4, a 93% DOX leak was observed. Significant inhibitory effects on the viability of the MG-63 OS cancer cells were detected at 11.88 μg/mL of DOX-Ag2O-MBG NPs. These characteristics demonstrated the Ag2O-MBG NPs system's efficacy in treating BC and promoting bone tissue regeneration [ , ].
To overcome CHS's high chemo- and radioresistance, researchers employed polyethylene glycol-encapsulated IONPs to regulate the administration of the chemotherapy doxorubicin (IONPDOX) and increase the cytotoxicity of proton radiation treatment. Cell survival was also assessed in 3D SW3153 spheroids treated with low Linear Energy Transfer (LET) protons with and without the IONPDOX chemical. IONPDOX enhanced radiosensitivity after proton irradiation at both LETs, correlated with DNA damage expressed as MN or γH2AX. The IONPDOX-low-LET proton combination had a more significant fatal impact than the IONPDOX-high-LET protons. CHS cell biology changes were represented in hyperspectral pictures and spectral profiles, highlighting novel spectroscopic indicators of cancer therapeutic effects. These data indicate that the proposed therapy combination can potentially enhance CHS management [ ].

## Photothermal and photodynamic effects of metal nanoparticles in bone cancer
Using NPs, such as AuNPs, photothermal therapy (PTT) absorbs near-infrared (NIR) light and transforms it into heat. Hyperthermia brought on by local heat attacks cancer cells specifically while preserving healthy tissue. In cases of BC, when conventional therapies like radiation might harm good bones, this focused strategy is beneficial. Treatment results are improved when PTT and immunotherapy are used together. AuNPs may cause dying cells to release tumor antigens by producing heat. Dendritic cells then pick up these antigens and deliver them to T cells, strengthening the immune response against malignancies. Compared to when each medication is administered alone, research shows that the immune system is activated when immune checkpoint inhibitors and AuNPs are combined. Tumor destruction is enhanced, improving tumor regression and survival. Additionally, photosensitizers used in Photothermal therapy (PDT) generate ROS when exposed to light. Because they may produce ROS, MNPs like TiO2 and ZnO work well as photosensitizers. PDT causes oxidative stress in BC, killing tumor cells and releasing dangerously relevant molecular patterns (DAMPs), further eliciting an immune response. ZnONP studies have shown that PDT may boost T and natural killer (NK) cells while destroying malignancies. PDT may boost immune activation when combined with immune checkpoint inhibition, which results in a more potent anti-tumor response and longer-lasting protection against recurrence [ , ].

## Conventional metallic nanoparticle production methods
MNPs are synthesized by methodologies classified into bottom-up and top-down techniques [ ]. The top-down technique involves the reduction of the comparable macroscopic substance through physical or chemical processes [ , ]. During this procedure, atoms or molecules are meticulously deposited by controlled processes, and the subsequent self-organization can yield nanostructures [ ]. The primary issue with the top-down approach is that the resultant surface structure is imperfect. The large aspect ratio of MNPs means that surface structural faults can substantially influence their ultimate physical and chemical properties [ ]. The bottom-up method builds a structure atom-by-atom, molecule-by-molecule, or cluster-by-cluster. The metal ion is brought to a zero-valent state, and the atoms stick together to make NPs. Nanostructured blocks are created using this method and combined using chemical or biological processes. The bottom-up method has a clear advantage in making it more likely that MNPs will have fewer flaws and a more uniform chemical makeup [ ].
Scientists have documented many chemical and physical ways to produce MNPs. Chemical approaches include the use of harsh chemical additions to create MNPs. These approaches are simple, convenient, cost-effective (for large-scale production), and rapid to implement, with no need for specialized equipment [ ]. Physicochemical methods for synthesizing MNPs encompass the utilization of microwaves, ultrasound, irradiation, and mechanical grinding, among other techniques, to get the desired outcome. These systems utilize energy to maintain elevated pressures and temperatures [ ].
There is a more scientific way to make different kinds of nanomaterials, especially metal oxide nanoparticles (MONPs), called the sol–gel process. The molecular precursor, usually a metal alkoxide, is mixed with water or alcohol. It is then heated and stirred to turn it into a gel. Since the gel made from hydrolysis or alcoholysis is still wet, it must be dried correctly based on the traits and uses it is meant to have. Using alcohol as an example, the cooling process is done by setting it on fire. The gels are turned into a powder and then heated to a high temperature. The sol–gel method is cheap, and because the reaction temperature is low, it's easy to control what chemicals are in the end products [ ].
During inert gas condensation, metal or inorganic materials are vaporized from a source while an inert gas atmosphere is present. The dissolved atoms of the metal or inorganic material then quickly condense on a cold surface to form MNPs. One of the first and fastest ways to make MNPs and NCs is to use inert gas condensation. The Inert Gas Condensation method for making MNPs has a lot of benefits. This method can be used to create a lot of different kinds of NPs. This method lets you choose from a range of NP sizes. Several things, such as the temperature, the pressure of the cylinder, and the amount of harmless gas pumped into it, can change the size of the NPs made [ ]. The inert gas condensation process is used to manufacture a wide range of nanomaterials, including metallic materials, alloys, semiconductors, MNPs, and their oxide NPs [ ].
Chemical precipitation can change the NPs’ sizes to fit their needs. Lubricant can help keep the pieces from sticking together. Electrodeposition plates are strong and have a smooth material surface. The hypersonic plasma particle depositing (HPPP) method can also be used to make NPs and put them down [ ].

## The side effects of the metallic nanoparticle and their risks to the body
Numerous studies have demonstrated that metals and MONPs can adversely affect the immune system. Various types of metals and MONPs might adversely affect the immune system. These encompass inflammation, oxidative stress, autophagy, and apoptosis. Immunometabolism is a crucial mechanism that dictates the function and destiny of immune cells. These NPs may serve as a primary target for inducing immunotoxic effects in live organisms. When assessing the immunotoxicity of metals and MONPs, it is crucial to examine their degradation and metabolic alterations. So, the main focus of the current study is also on how different kinds of immune cells and NPs work together in the immune system. Researchers should also look at the immunological properties of nanomaterials because there isn't a lot of research on immunotoxicity. It is essential to understand all of their immunological characteristics [ – ].
So, when metal and MONPs are being made, their composition and shape must be considered. Many things must be regarded as complex problems in this field. One of the tasks for the future will be to put metals and MONPs into groups based on the results of toxicology studies. Due to the immune system's complexity in living things, more experiments should be done to understand better how metals and MONPs affect the immune system. This is also missing from present research and needs to be looked into. Also, using metal and MONPs as tools to change the biology of immune cells should be thought about more. More molecular studies should be done to figure out how to make NPs less harmful. This can improve the healing benefits of NPs and make them last longer [ ]. Because of the higher amounts of ROS generated when exposed to NPs, MNPs are genotoxic. Not all MNPs can make free radicals directly, but most can. This is because they can release metal ions and combine with water molecules. MNPs can also cause more free radicals to be made and cells to die. This can start reduction/oxidation (redox) processes that keep making ROS in the body, creating a positive feedback loop. Chronic oxidative stress happens when MNPs are exposed. This is caused by too many inflammatory factors like nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and signal transducer and activator of transcription proteins (STATs), problems with mitochondria, and higher calcium levels inside cells [ , ].
Nanotechnology-based methods might be able to help treat breast cancer bone metastasis (BCBM) in ways that surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy can't. NP-based drug transport makes cancer treatment more prosperous and safer for normal cells. More importantly, NPs can be made to release more than one drug, which is becoming more popular in tumor treatment. Many tailored NPs have been made to help find and treat BC. NPs could change BCBM treatment by quickly moving drugs or genes by lengthening the circulation time, making them more bioavailable, lowering the immune system's ability to identify them, and making the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents more accurate. NPs also gather at tumor sites because of the vascular EPR effect [ – ]. One covert method of drug targeting, nanocarriers, can access cancer cells by endocytosis. Via the application of particular ligands that identify tumor biomarkers, actively targeted NPs can penetrate tumor tissue and attach to cancer cells via the EPR effect. However, most nanomedicines are still in the in vitro study phase, and there aren’t any available ways to make them yet. The tumor variety concerning the EPR effect and longer circulation duration is vital to address. However, none can ensure the nanomedicine reaches the tumor site [ ]. Using the EPR effect alone is insufficient, especially for cancers that don’t get enough blood flow. NPs also have trouble getting into tumors and are quickly removed by the reticuloendothelial system. So, more studies need to be done to create NP-based carriers that can help drugs get into the area around the tumor. Nanotherapies are thought to be a good way to send healing drugs to cancer cells that have spread to other parts of the body [ ].
The intricacy of the formulation's structure and physicochemical qualities poses a substantial challenge to large-scale manufacture. The necessity for complex and challenging synthesis methods to produce these NPs at a larger scale constrains their potential for clinical translation, since corporations emphasize quality and cost. These variables should be routinely assessed. Moreover, MNPs have been associated with potential clinical harm. Consequently, comprehensive safety must be ensured before human utilization. Pharmaceutical regulatory agencies strongly recommend that sponsors meticulously assess any modifications in the medication, its synthesis method, or medicinal product formulation at any clinical development phase. These methodologies aid sponsors in evaluating whether alterations may directly or indirectly affect product safety. Data on the biological activity and toxicities of free medicines, with the behavioral patterns of distinct MNPs and their interactions with biological entities, facilitate the prediction of NP toxicity. The influence of drug release rate on both target and off-target concentrations of bioavailable drugs facilitates the prediction of safety and toxicity. Due to the increased circulation half-lives and drug retention lengths associated with nanoencapsulation, targeted toxicity studies in suitable animal models would yield a more precise assessment of acute and chronic toxicity. Additional assessment methods encompass biodistribution, target site accumulation, pharmacokinetics, local distribution at the site of action, localization in healthy tissue, drug release kinetics, and therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, real-time imaging helps elucidate the interactions of MNPs with various organs and tissues upon in vivo administration. Addressing the issues above with MNPs and resolving them will facilitate enhanced clinical translation of MNPs for their biological application in cancer treatment [ ].
The air we breathe contains various naturally occurring and artificially produced NPs. We will also likely contact them via our food, water, and healthcare systems. Animal and cell research in toxicology highlight concerns about potential immune system side effects, inflammation, pulmonary illness, and problems associated with oxidative stress. Still up in the air is the question of whether or not such exposure is conceivable in the workplace or environmental settings, and the dosages typically required to generate these effects. Information about exposure is also necessary to calculate risks. Concerns regarding nomenclature, the appropriateness of current test methods, and the classification of nanomaterials as new substances under chemicals regulations like REACH (registration, evaluation, authorization and restriction of chemicals) are among the issues that current legislation fails to address regarding NPs and nanomaterials [ ]. Furthermore, the 5% or less oral, cutaneous, or inhalational absorption may rise with decreasing sizes, negative charge, and suitable coatings. Due to nonspecific absorption by reticuloendothelial cells, metallic NPs may be disseminated throughout the body, with the primary accumulation occurring in the liver, spleen, and lymph node. These NPs can potentially stay in the body for at least six months. Coating with neuropeptides that may pass the blood–brain barrier (BBB) enhances the ability of metallic NPs (≤ 100 nm) to traverse the BBB. Surface composition and the embryonic/placental development stage determine placental transfer, which may be improved by coating with biocompatible compounds like polyethylene glycol or ferritin. Renal and biliary excretion is usually limited because they accumulate in tissues. However, renal clearance might be significantly enhanced with reduced sizes and specialized coatings like glutathione (GSH) [ ]. It is essential to assess the potential toxicity of MNPs to other organs and systems, even though the liver and spleen are the primary targets for intravenous administration [ ].
The frequent developmental concerns show the significance of treating metallic NPs with prudence, since their effects are highly unpredictable. Some nanomaterials that were formerly thought to be compatible because of the safety of many compounds may be harmful in their nanoforms, even if research disagrees on the degree and mechanism of toxicity. Considering the pharmacokinetic properties of various NP forms necessitates an exhaustive investigation and a database of health risks associated with multiple NPs. Most of the literature on nanotoxicity has been on building databases and libraries to investigate the harmfulness of different NPs empirically and using computational methods to forecast the toxicity of nanoformulations, including metals. This data may pave the path for more NP in vitro and in vivo studies. Additional studies on NPs should focus on how they accumulate, connect with cells and receptors, influence signaling networks, and exert their widespread phagocytosis activity. One approach to safeguard these “new building materials” in areas like diagnosis and therapy is to learn how they interact with biological systems [ ].

## Green synthesis in metallic nanoparticle production and advantages in cancer
Green synthesis methods are a novel strategy that makes use of biological molecules. These approaches provide environmentally benign and physiologically compatible alternatives to traditional chemical synthesis procedures [ ]. Consequently, there is a growing interest in developing green biosynthetic methods utilizing plant extracts and microbes [ ]. Microorganisms and flora have evolved intricate mechanisms to extract metal ions from the environment and facilitate their remediation. They emit substantial biomolecules such as phytochemicals, enzymes, and proteins that can decompose metal ions into their fundamental forms as NPs during synthesis [ ]. Green synthesis is an eco-friendly process that employs plant extracts rather than dangerous chemicals or extreme temperatures. To minimize adverse effects on the environment and guarantee the safety of the produced NPs, it is common practice to use non-toxic and biocompatible ingredients throughout the synthesis process [ ]. Synthesizing industrial-scale NPs by conventional physical and chemical pathways is faster. Still, it always involves heat stations or hazardous chemicals as surfactants to keep the NPs stable, which requires a high containment facility and a stringent dispersion policy. On the other hand, green synthesis emphasizes the experimental setup of technology that is both cost-efficient and highly successful, while being reasonably safe for humans and the environment. The goal is to reduce chemical waste and ensure operations are sustainable so the economy may grow. The bio-ingredients used to make green synthesis NPs are plant and microbial (bacteria, fungi, and algae) derivatives, which are nontoxic, less harmful, and environmentally benign. For instance, phytochemicals comprising ketones, aldehydes, flavones, amides, terpenoids, carboxylic acids, phenols, or ascorbic acids derived from leaves are used in the green production of MNPs or MONPs. These parts may turn metal salts into MNPs. So far, NPs of varying sizes and shapes have been synthesized from plant extracts. It is preferable because it does not restrict the size and form of the NPs during synthesis and has a much quicker rate than the microbial method [ ]. The energy consumption can be reduced by 30%, costs can be reduced by up to 40%, and manufacturing output may be increased by 50% when compared to traditional synthesis. Cancer therapies may become more accessible, particularly in areas with limited resources, if they were thus inexpensive [ , ]. One of the best economic options for large-scale production of AuNPs with well-specified size and shape is the green synthesis process based on plants. Since plants don’t need to go through the trouble of keeping microbial cultures alive, they are a better choice than microorganisms like bacteria and fungi for NPs production [ ]. Green synthesized NPs are more biocompatible since they come from natural sources. When applied to cancer treatments, this quality can reduce side effects and increase patient success rates [ , ]. In addition, lowering organic agents and the type of biological entities with varying concentrations affect NP size and form [ ].
For instance, plasmonic NPs (PNPs) have several uses in nanotechnology, which is driving the demand for improved production techniques. Researchers shed insight into the potential of microbial metabolites as a potent anti-cancer drug and a tool for controlling the size and shape of PNPs during synthesis. The quick formation of PNPs with significant control over shape and size makes pyoverdine and pyocyanin promising candidates for PNP synthesis. While there is a lot of enthusiasm for studying the biological synthesis of NPs because of their potential environmental benefits, there are also some significant downsides to consider. Biological production of NPs utilizing microorganisms is very sluggish compared to wet-chemistry procedures. Predicting response output is also challenging since controlling the experimental settings is hard. To reduce batch-to-batch fluctuations and get a consistent size and shape of NPs, it isn’t easy to use phytoextract-based biological synthesis due to the inhomogeneous composition of the phytochemicals, even if it can kinetically compete with chemical synthesis approaches. Biologically produced NPs may not be the best option in specialty applications like optoelectronics and catalysis, where precise control over NP size and form is essential. Biological synthesis has produced anisotropic NPs, but unlike chemical or physical synthesis, it does not yet provide for control over size and shape characteristics like aspect ratio. The low output capacity is another issue with biological NP synthesis. Because of the complexity of NPs synthesis (in the case of microorganisms) and the many unexplained reaction factors, scaling up NP production via biological synthesis techniques would be problematic. Instead of live microbial cells, redox-active biomolecules may provide reaction control on par with chemical approaches. A comprehensive study is necessary to learn more about the reaction stoichiometry, growth, and capping processes of NPs. Consistent progress in biological synthesis techniques might pave the way for large-scale manufacturing and make customized NPs synthesis a reality [ ].
Some plant extracts used in green manufacturing have antioxidant capabilities that might enhance the therapeutic effects of the NPs and help fight cancer-related oxidative stress [ , ]. For instance, researchers demonstrated that AuNPs containing an aqueous extract of Curcumae Kwangsiensis Folium (Leaf) exhibited anti-human ovarian cancer, antioxidant, cytotoxic, and environmentally friendly synthesis and characterization effects. Using the 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) test with butylated hydroxytoluene as the positive control, researchers investigated the antioxidant capabilities of Au chloride, aqueous extract of Curcumae Kwangsiensis Folium leaves, and AuNPs. AuNPs had the most effective antioxidant effects. At a dosage of 153 µg/mL, they could block 50% of the DPPH molecules. Perhaps the antioxidant properties of AuNPs produced by the aqueous extract of Curcumae Kwangsiensis Folium leaves explain their promising anti-human ovarian cancer potential [ ].
NPs that are safe and can be used in medicine are often made this way because they use fewer harmful chemicals and have less of an effect on the environment. Green synthesis produces most of the characteristics of natural sources. It offers an exciting, long-lasting, cheap, and scalable alternative to traditional chemical methods, especially in targeted cancer treatment [ ]. Chemotherapy and other conventional cancer medicines often have serious side effects and can't target only specific cells. On the other hand, green-synthesized MNPs can be made with specific size, form, and surface qualities, making them more effective at killing cancer cells while causing less damage to normal cells [ ]. These NPs use the EPR effect to target tumors, sticking to and building up in tumor cells because of their abnormal blood vessel structures. Surface functionalization also makes it possible for these NPs to link directly to cancer cell markers, which improves their ability to target and treat cancer. Creating green-synthesized MNPs is a big step toward more effective and long-lasting cancer treatments. It marks the start of a new era in the fight against this challenging disease [ ].
Researchers show that biosynthesized b-AuNP and b-AgNP can deliver an anti-cancer drug to cancer cells. NPs made from Butea monosperma leaf extract are fully described using several analytical methods. When DOX is delivered through NP-based DDSs, it significantly slows the growth of cancer cells (B16F10 and MCF-7) compared to the original drug. As a result, experts are sure that biosynthesized NPs will soon help develop nanomedicine-based cancer therapies [ ]. Besides plant-mediated synthesis, several microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, and viruses, have been recorded to produce diverse MNPs [ ]. Distinct microorganisms have varying capabilities in converting diverse metal ions into NPs. This is due to many factors associated with microbial physiology and the inherent properties of metals [ ]. Microscopic organisms have developed distinctive mechanisms to eliminate certain metals, enabling them to reduce elevated concentrations of those ions. The redox activity of biomolecules produced by bacteria for stability and reduction differs in their efficacy in lowering metal ions [ ]. Excess free metal ions, beyond tolerable levels, have detrimental effects via oxidative stress, limiting the concentration range and permitting microbial transformation activity [ ]. Thus, microbes generally have the inherent ability to change metals, creating MNPs at the cellular level during their typical reaction to metal stress [ ]. Nevertheless, recently, the microbial production of NPs has become a fascinating area of study because it has some benefits over other methods. Depending on the type of bacteria, the NPs are made inside or outside cells. In the past few years, AgNPs were made from a cell-free sample of Pantoea ananatis, an endophytic bacterium [ ].

Other species of fungi, such as Penicillium notatum and Fusarium oxysporum, are used to synthesize Platinum, Si, and Ti NPs [ ]. Actinomycetes are also used to make extracellular enzymes and secondary metabolites. They can also biosynthesize NPs because they are very good at making many beneficial chemicals and have a lot of protein. In another study, researchers prepared the AgNPs using the Actinobacteria Rhodococcus National Collection of Industrial Microorganisms (NCIM) 2891 with a size range of 10 nm and spherical shape [ ].

They can kill some harmful bacteria, like S. aureus. A study took a yeast strain called Candida lusitaniae from a termite's gut and showed that it could make AgNPs with a diameter of 2–10 nm [ , ].
Plants provide a variety of components and biochemicals that can be used as stabilizing and reducing agents in the synthesis of green NPs. ## Bacterial synthesis of metal nanoparticles
Microbes' extraordinary capacity to adapt to stressful environmental circumstances leads to the production of decreased metal ions. Thus, supernatants of different bacteria, including Arthrobacter gangotriensis, Arthrobacter kerguelensis, Pseudomonas Antarctica, Pseudomonas proteolytic, and Pseudomonas meridiana, function as microbial cell factories and are used as reducing agents in the production of AgNPs [ ].
Extracellular enzymes produced by various bacteria and fungi may convert metals and metalloids to their corresponding NPs. Extracellular enzymes, like nitrate reductase, may aid in the conversion of specific donors, such as hydroxyl groups, into metallic AgNPs by facilitating the passage of electrons from these donors to Ag + . Functional groups, including f –OH, –SH, and –NH2, found in microbial proteins have been shown to aid in the stability of NPs by offering metal ions binding sites and subsequently reducing them into NPs on the cell wall or in the periplasmic space. Proteins may sometimes serve as the primary capping or reducing agents during the creation and stabilization of NPs. Through electron transport between cytoplasmic components (such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)/nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), vitamins, and organic acids, intracellular enzymes such as cytochrome oxidases are also known to aid in the reduction of metal ions to NPs. Periplasmic reductase may directly reduce M+ to M, bio-reduction in the cytoplasm or periplasm generates M from M+, or bioconversion of M2+ to M+ in the cytoplasm can result in M creation. These are the three ways intracellular reductase can start the manufacture and stability of NPs [ ].

## Mycosynthesis of metal nanoparticles
Compared to plant materials and bacteria, fungi are more favorable because their mycelial mesh can tolerate flow pressure, agitation, and other conditions in bioreactors or different chambers. They are simple to handle, synthesize, and grow meticulously. Due to its ease of use, lack of additional downstream processing, and shorter turnaround time than intracellular synthesis, the fungal-mediated extracellular synthesis approach has garnered much attention. Furthermore, adjusting pH, temperature, substrate concentration (metal ions), and reaction time may all affect the size and form of extracellularly produced NPs. While intracellular synthesis might produce NPs with precise size and form, product recovery and harvesting are time-consuming and costly. As a result, extracellular synthesis techniques may be the best option for producing NPs on a wide scale for various commercial uses. Crucially, fungi also release many proteins and secondary metabolites outside of cells. As a result, the fungal biomass may more readily reduce metal ions, which would hasten the creation of NPs [ , ]. Several parameters must be considered when ascomycetes or basidiomycetes synthesize NPs. These include the source of the fungus or fungal metabolites, the simplicity of production, the possible cost of synthesis, and consumer approval of using mycosynthesis by certain fungal species. When manufacturing organic chemicals, ascomycetes are well-known and used in industry. For example, Aspergillus niger produces citric acid, whereas Penicillium sp. produces antibiotics. However, several Ascomycetes species are harmful to both plants and animals, and the care required when using NPs made from these fungi may harm the NPs' price. Furthermore, NPs made with pathogenic fungi or just fungi from the same species may cause illogical and unreasonable reactions in prospective medical, veterinary care, and agriculture purchasers. This might make it more difficult for mycosynthesized NPs to be accepted. These factors suggest that basidiomycetes could be significant. Their usage may be much more acceptable since most of the basidiomycetes used to synthesize NPs are either edible or medicinal. Because of physiologically active fungal biomolecules that are adsorbed on the NPs' surface and absent from other synthesized NPs, NPs made with these fungi may sometimes have specific advantageous characteristics. Additionally, because a lot of edible fungi are produced on substrates like sawdust, the wasted mycelia of Basidiomycetes may be an inexpensive and plentiful supply of biomolecules necessary for mycosynthesis [ ].
Researchers produced Talaromyces purpureogenus AgNPs (Tp-AgNPs) utilizing mycelial extract of the endophytic fungus Talaromyces purpureogenus (MEEF), and their antibacterial, anti-proliferation, and cell injury healing properties were described and examined. Results from the V-visible spectrophotometer (UV–Vis) showed a significant absorption peak between 390 and 420 nm, indicating that Tp-AgNPs had been synthesized successfully. EDX and field emission transmission electron microscopy (FETEM) findings showed that the Tp-AgNPs were round and triangular, with an average size of 25 nm, in line with particle size analysis (PSA). According to FTIR analysis, the production of Tp-AgNPs included several functional compounds from MEEF. The XRD result showed the nature of Tp-AgNPs as a superior crystal. At the least inhibitory dose of 13.98 μg⋅mL−1 for Gram − bacteria and 16.12 μg⋅mL−1 for Gram + bacteria, the Tp-AgNPs dramatically reduced the development of bacterial pathogens. Additionally, in A549, Tp-AgNPs (2 μg⋅mL−1) showed a potent anti-proliferation activity. Remarkably, normal NIH3T3 cells were not cytotoxically affected by Tp-AgNPs. Furthermore, the NPs demonstrated a powerful ability to mend cell wounds. Investigators used the endophytic fungus T. purpureogenus to biosynthesize AgNPs with potent antibacterial, anticancer, and cell wound healing activities [ ].

## Yeast-mediated synthesis of metal nanoparticles
Yeasts are intriguing microbes that have lately caught the interest of several researchers who want to learn more about their potential in producing NPs with various applications. Numerous studies have shown various yeast species' intracellular and extracellular synthesis of MNPs and MONPs. Ag, Au, selenium (Se), Zn sulfide, palladium, manganese dioxide, TiO2, and Se sulfide are examples of these NPs. Biomedical properties of yeast-mediated NPs include antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer properties. Research has shown that NPs produced by yeast are harmless and safe. Fewer studies have examined the use of yeast in NPs biosynthesis than those using bacteria and fungi. This suggests that this field is viable for further scientific advancements in NP biosynthesis and uses [ ]. Several investigations have been conducted on the use of yeast in manufacturing MNPs. However, one of the main strategies for using biological material was achieved by using the eukaryotic systems Candida glabrata and Schizosaccharomyces pombe [ ].

Additionally, the mice's health was greatly enhanced by including 0.3–0.8 mg/kg Se-NPs in the meal. Researchers showed that the yeast-derived Se-NPs, when added to animal feed as a trace element, improved the animals’ resistance to oxidative stress and infectious illnesses. Consequently, Se-NPs may be used as an alternative to antibiotics in animal husbandry [ ].

## Green synthesis of AgNPs in bone cancer treatment
AgNPs can penetrate biological barriers and build up in tissues, which may result in genotoxicity, inflammation, and oxidative stress. They pose dangers, including cytotoxicity and immunotoxicity, by interfering with DNA integrity, mitochondrial functioning, and cellular processes. Changes in metabolic pathways and organ damage have been linked to prolonged exposure. Thus, for safe biomedical uses, it is essential to comprehend their biodistribution, long-term consequences, and mechanisms of action. AgNPs have been shown to have cytotoxic effects in human health investigations, especially in HaCaT cells, while low amounts (< 10 μM) seem safe. Higher doses, however, cause a considerable amount of cell death. As a result, studies highlight the need for area-specific criteria and consistent toxicity assessment techniques. Green synthesis techniques are beneficial for lowering toxicity hazards [ ].

Ormocarpum cochinchinense, also known as "elumbotti," is a bush used for a long time to fix bones in places like Tamil Nadu. Hydroxyapatite (HA) is a well-known biomaterial that is well-suited to living things and is often used by dentists and orthopedists. Synthesized AgNPs were joined with HA powder to improve drug distribution. The bio-conjugate’s ability to kill microbes and cells was tested. Researchers will test the bioconjugate’s in vitro action in the MG-63 OS cell line. The bioconjugate that was made can be used to treat OS, bone fractures, and inflammation [ ].
Rolim et al. elucidated the antibacterial and cytotoxic properties of an NC composed of Ag chloride and biogenically synthesized AgNPs by Arthrobacter sp., a non-pathogenic bacterium indigenous to Antarctic soil. Researchers obtained spherical NPs coated with biomolecules derived from Arthrobacter sp. The mixture was effective against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including the multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) KPC 37 strain. In conclusion, it can be said that a lot of work has been done in the last ten years to create green synthesis. Since green synthesis is economical, environmentally friendly, and efficiently scaled up for large-scale synthesis, it advances chemical and physical processes. The most effective little functional materials are created by nature in beautiful and creative ways. The drive to develop environmentally friendly processes was sparked by growing knowledge of green chemistry and the use of green routes to manufacture MNPs, particularly AgNPs. It is possible to use organisms of any size and form to synthesize NPs, from simple bacteria to very sophisticated eukaryotes. Comparatively speaking, the creation of microorganisms and large-scale formulation residues is challenging. Researchers focused on using plants because of the low synthesis rate and the small number of size and shape distributions created. ## Green synthesis of ZnO NPs in bone cancer treatment
ZnO NPs are one of the many types of NPs that are now being studied further because of their strong antifungal, antibacterial, and anticancer effects. Adults need Zn as a nutrient, and ZnO NPs are safe to provide in vivo. ZnO NPs are now mainly employed to treat cancer since they are cytotoxic to various cancer cell types. ZnO NPs have a significant role in developing cancer drug delivery therapy strategies. Additionally, it is soluble, non-toxic, stable, inexpensive, and very practical, providing simple access to the intended action locations. ZnO NPs work well by weakening the mitochondria, which increases ROS, lipid peroxides, and DNA damage. ZnO NPs increase the cytotoxicity against cancer cells by interfering with the protein equilibrium and influencing several critical cellular functions, such as DNA damage, replication, apoptosis, electron transport chain activity, and cellular homeostasis [ – ]. Furthermore, by encouraging their disintegration, autophagy causes the uptaken NPs to liberate Zn ions. These internal Zn ions and those first liberated from the extracellular NPs enter the cells and target the mitochondria, producing ROS. The ROS then stops cell growth by preventing the S phase and causes apoptosis via both intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms, which results in cell death. More significantly, whereas blocking late-stage autophagy inversely increases apoptosis, suppressing early-stage autophagy improves cell survival by eliminating apoptosis. On the other hand, apoptosis suppression increases autophagy but has a limited effect on restoring cell viability. Notably, combining apoptosis and late-stage autophagy inhibitors significantly improves cell survival [ ]. When activated, synoviocytes—resident macrophages of the joint—release various inflammatory mediators, including oxygen/nitrogen free radicals (ROS/NOS) and the cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α. Because ROS and RNS have unpaired electrons, they may seriously damage cell structures and lead to oxidative stress. In this sense, the development of pain, inflammation, and illness is influenced mainly by these inflammatory mediators and ROS/RNS. The transcription factor NF-kB regulates the expression of these cytokines. Therefore, focusing on cytokines and ROS may be a suitable method to manage the course of the illness. Rats treated with ZnO NPs also showed a reduction in clinical severity and inflammation. This was linked to a decline in the levels of inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, COX-2), biomarkers (such as RF, a-CCP, and CRP), and transcription factor NF-kB activity [ ]. Furthermore, because of their unique physical and chemical characteristics and their highly selective absorption by cancer cells, ZnO-NPs have become the perfect multifunctional NPs for cancer therapy. In addition to ZnO-NPs' intrinsic toxicity to cancer cells via the disruption of Zn-dependent protein function, their photo-electrochemical characteristics can make cancer cells more vulnerable to UV radiation [ ]. Furthermore, ZnO NPs have attracted much interest lately due to Zn's anti-cancer qualities. According to some research, ZnO NPs may improve the efficacy of chemotherapy medications by focusing on the key processes behind cancer chemoresistance. According to these findings, ZnO NPs may increase DNA damage and apoptosis, decrease ABC transporter function, and diminish stemness in cancer cells, all boosting chemosensitivity [ ]. Furthermore, because of its low cost, easy manufacturing, non-toxicity, high drug-loading capacity, programmable drug release ability, targeted distribution, and customizable structure, ZnO is used in modern DDSs. Targeted DDSs have effectively used porous ZnO structures, including porous nanotubes, nanobelts, nanorods, and porous cages [ ]. Furthermore, green synthesis creates biocompatible and less toxic ZnNPs than their chemically manufactured counterparts. Reducing harm to good tissue is critical in treating BC, and this factor is especially important [ ].
Usually, bone growth is needed to treat degenerative spine conditions. AO/EB labeling showed that ZnO NPs caused apoptosis in MG-63 cells when RR was applied. It was also found that ZnO NPs led to more ROS being made and less MMP being made. The research suggests that ZnO NPs from RR have potent anticancer effects and cause death in MG-63 cells by increasing the production of ROS. So, ZnO NPs from RR could be a possible drug target for several cancer cell lines [ ].

In the antioxidant assay, NPs exhibited an IC50 of 115 μg/mL, whereas butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) had an IC50 of 96 μg/mL against DPPH free radicals. The IC50 values of the NPs were 278 and 256 μg/mL for the A-673 and HS-729 cell lines, respectively. A dose-dependent reduction in the viability of cancer cell lines occurred in the presence of ZnNPs. The efficacy of novel NPs in combating cancer appears to stem from their antioxidant properties. Given the efficacy demonstrated by this combo in clinical studies, it may be a viable alternative to chemotherapeutic agents [ ].
OS is the most common mesenchymal bone neoplasm, and it can happen to people of any age. However, older people have a mortality rate that is 2–8 times lower than that of teens. As nanomedicine and multi-targeted drug delivery have improved, the clinical medicines used to treat cancer are slowly becoming less valuable. ## Green synthesis of FeNPs in bone cancer treatment
Although IONPs mediated by plant extracts have been shown to have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, their practical usefulness is limited by their toxicity to healthy cells. Over the last several decades, zero-valent iron (ZVI) NPs have been widely employed for environmental decontamination. In a typical Fenton reaction, hydrogen peroxide, ferric, and ferrous ions combine to produce hydroxyl radicals, potent oxidants that may break down various organic pollutants. ZVINPs' superior ROS-inducing capabilities have led to new reports that they may be bactericidal and anticancer agents. Last but not least, the biomolecules in the extract are self-sufficient to stabilize the biologically produced NPs. Therefore, no agglomeration occurs, and no additional capping agents are needed. ## Novel green synthesis of metallic nanoparticles in bone cancer treatment
Because of their highly adjustable porosity, structure, and adaptability, MOFs and their derivatives offer essential solutions for detecting, separating, and remediating electromagnetic and water pollution and improving catalysis and energy storage efficiency [ , ]. In the MOF family, MOF/biomass, bioMOFs, and their variations have recently become essential. The effectiveness of MOFs has increased with the addition of proteins and biomass [ ].
Compared to MOFs, bioMOFs have many advantages: they are highly biocompatible, have strong self-assembly capabilities, are non-toxic, and are easily recyclable; have a variety of rigid and flexible structural designs, as well as different binding sites that allow for multiple coordination modes with metal ions; and bioMOFs made with chiral biomolecules exhibit unique recognition and separation capabilities. BioMOF derivatives with a higher heteroatom content have more active sites, significantly improving electrochemical performance [ ]. The applications of bioMOF have recently gained importance in several research fields, including targeted medication delivery, tumor treatment, and imaging. ## Future direction
Conventional surgery plus neoadjuvant chemotherapy is still the most common way to treat OS, even though it has some problems, such as side effects, a higher risk of illness from weakened immune systems, and a higher chance of recurrence. NP-based treatments could do a lot more than what is currently possible. Focusing on using magnetic nanotechnology for OS, many problems need to be solved to increase patient survival after surgery, get targeted anti-cancer drugs to sites where tumors have spread, control the body's response and side effects, and fight long-term problems like drug resistance and aggressive metastatic recurrence. Magnetic NPs (MaNPs) have been looked into because they might be useful in health and cancer care. Because of their naturally good properties, superparamagnetic iron oxide has become very popular in imaging and therapy. The ability of ligands to target may be affected by how they are arranged in space and how easily they can join other molecules. A lot of research has been done to find out how MaNPs are taken up, how long they stay in the body, how quickly they are metabolized and eliminated, how toxic they are, what happens to them over time, how well they work for targeting tumors, how well drugs are delivered, how well they work for imaging tissues, and many other uses related to cancer. Along with the effects of iron oxide nanosystems, the main problem that needs to be looked at is how to put scientific study into real-world clinical and business uses. It took so long to get to the clinical stage partly because scientists don't fully understand how biological systems work with nanosystems, how hosts react, and how physical parameters affect pharmacokinetics, metabolic routes, and excretion. Coating and functionalizing surfaces with natural polymers and biomaterials has reduced harmful effects and improved therapeutic targeting. However, neurotoxicity, medication effectiveness, and immune responses must be studied in a living organism. For preliminary tests, more research should be done to determine how ligands target cells [ ].
MNPs have shown a beneficial and prevalent role in cancer therapy during the last 20 years by offering better medication delivery and targeting. Furthermore, their functionalization with targeted ligands provides superior control over energy deposition in malignancies. These MNPs have also been investigated as a diagnostic tool for visualizing cancer cells. Their primary benefits include the ability to be used for both simultaneous diagnosis and treatment and the provision of tailored medication release, revolutionizing cancer treatment and management. Researchers emphasized the diverse uses of different metallic particles in the overall treatment of cancer. Because of their unique relationship with light, these NPs make it possible to follow the nanometal-drug combination within the body. As a result, they provide cancer patients receiving chemotherapy with an effective treatment that is comparable to traditional medication administration. Despite these facts, various bottlenecks make commercializing and translating them clinically tricky. Bulk production, biological concerns, safety, governmental regulations, and general cost-effectiveness about already offered therapies are a few of these [ ].
The FDA clearance procedure presents significant obstacles to using MNPs in cancer treatments, which have not yet been resolved despite the abovementioned preclinical developments. Due to the few candidates that have passed clinical assessment for medical purposes, the FDA has not issued comprehensive recommendations regarding the translation of metallic NPs. A thorough evaluation of each component's safety is required for NP regulation, which results in more expensive research than traditional small-molecule treatments. To reduce barriers to clinical advancement for the companies conducting these studies, the Nanotechnology Characterization Laboratory helps investigators and the FDA form collaborations. Additionally, preclinical toxicological tests are given to qualified candidates without putting any financial strain on the investigator. However, the high cost of creating these formulations and the absence of a precedent for authorizing metallic NPs have deterred researchers from aggressively seeking clinical translation. Few funding options and research incentives are available for efforts to further the clinical translation of MNPs, despite researchers' eagerness to do so. Despite decades of research and major public funding, the first Major FDA clearance for a medicine using MNPs has not been obtained. The emphasis of metallic NP treatments over biodegradable NP delivery technologies, such as polymeric or liposomal techniques, has proven more challenging to defend in light of recent developments. Several prominent companies that focus on clinical translation have indeed shifted to using non-metallic particles in translational medicine research [ , ].
The relationship between disease pathology and human disease heterogeneity is necessary when discussing biological problems. Moreover, MNPs’ physicochemical characteristics have to be the main emphasis to overcome biological barriers and improve medication targeting to sick tissue while reducing the accumulation of pharmaceuticals in non-specific organs. Regretfully, these elements have received less attention in the effort to comprehend the connections between patient biology and MNP behavior broadly. This may be one of the leading causes of the failure seen in clinical trials when promising MNPs were translated. These biological hurdles may discourage pharmaceutical companies from investing in metallic nanomedicines. A comprehensive assessment of preclinical data regarding therapeutic effectiveness, safety, biodistribution, and pharmacokinetics in suitable tumor animal models is desperately needed to overcome this obstacle. Instead of depending on a single animal model, which only reflects a limited association with human physiology, the trials must employ many animal models of cancer to get consistent findings [ , ]. Different biological sources, such as plants, microbes, and even algae, in various sizes and shapes, might be used to create MNPs. Most investigations showed that biogenic MNPs are genotoxic, despite a few reports of their antigenotoxic effect. Conducting genotoxicity tests on MNPs to determine any possible harm is crucial. Many studies have shown green synthesis techniques for MNPs that use biological processes as an environmentally friendly strategy over the last ten years. Nevertheless, there are presently few and very inconsistent laboratory investigations on the genotoxicity of biologically produced MNPs. As a result, it isn't easy to compare and extrapolate from genotoxicity data. Researchers highlighted that MNP genotoxicity might still occur even if MNPs are biosynthesized utilizing natural sources. We might avoid possible harm if we had a better grasp of the genotoxicity processes of biogenic MNPs [ ].
AgNPs and AuNPs are two of the most essential MNPs because they can be used in chemistry, medicine, and sensor technology [ ]. AuNPs are safe to use in treating bone diseases because they are biocompatible, and their surface chemistry can be changed to control how they connect with bone cells and bioactivity precisely. Also, AuNPs' excellent imaging properties make it possible to see bone structures clearly, and their PTT effectiveness gives doctors specific ways to treat bone illnesses like tumors. AuNPs can also be fixed and functionalized to allow controlled drug release, which improves the effectiveness of therapy. However, the fact that AuNPs are more complicated and costly may stop many people from using and producing them. Also, possible toxicity issues, dose adjustment, and the fact that AuNPs can’t hold as much drug as other NPs should be carefully thought through. Another thing that needs to be looked at is surface stability, if the traits and bioactivity of AuNPs are to stay the same over time. Even with these problems, more studies and improvements can help make AuNPs work better to treat bone diseases. As research continues, AuNPs have the potential to completely change how bone diseases are treated and how bones grow back. AuNPs could make a big difference in bone disease therapy by handling their limits and taking advantage of their unique benefits, leading to better patient outcomes and quality of life. Overall, AuNPs offer benefits and drawbacks for bone disease regeneration. Their flexibility, biocompatibility, imaging capabilities, controlled release potential, and ability to interact well with photothermal treatment make them a viable nanomaterial for the future of treating bone ailments [ ].
There are several different ways to make AgNPs and AuNPs. The chemical, photochemical, and electrical reduction routes comprise a small part. Plant products are seen as the most straightforward, most effective, least expensive, and safest way to do things [ , ]. A lot of good work has been done on making NPs using plant extracts as stabilizers and lowering agents, and the long time it took to do the cellular synthesis outweighed the value of having a pure output. Noni (Morinda citrifolia) has long been known for its many health benefits [ ]. It is a small plant that stays green all year and is in the Rubiaceae family. AgNPs and AuNPs are made in green production, are stable, inexpensive, and can be used repeatedly. Long praised as a medicine, Morinda citrifolia is the most effective way to reduce and defend against AgNPs and AuNPs. The prepared NPs can stop the growth of infectious bacteria and ROS, such as free radicals. There was a potent and concentration-dependent cytotoxicity of the manufactured NPs against human OS cells [ ].
It was essential to figure out how plant-based bioengineered NPs can interact with parts of cancer cells [ ]. Green biosynthesized NPs appear to be a unique tool for prognostic biomarkers and medication administration in tumor cells [ ]. Depending on how they work, they can either hurt tumor cells or protect healthy cells. This is because of the unique properties of NPs and the antitumor and antioxidant qualities found in plants [ ]. However, during the biosynthesis process, extra care should be taken with the plant species, products, and harmful doses of some phytocompounds that are used. Any extra metal or trace element released by metal and MONPs biosynthesized NPs can cause more reactive stress, which is linked to a higher chance of cancer. Plant-based nanosystems should be improved to protect healthy cells so that they can target cancer cells more precisely [ ].
IONPs have become appealing instruments for cancer theranostics because of their magnetic responsiveness, configurable surface properties, and ability to enhance therapeutic and diagnostic precision [ ]. This study primarily focuses on improving the environmental sustainability of IONPs by utilizing plant-derived materials rich in polyphenols. These polyphenols function as natural reducing agents and assist in combating cancer. These green-synthesized IONPs have potential in targeted drug administration, regulated release, and enhanced imaging using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), potentially resulting in durable and efficacious cancer treatment. The review examines recent in vitro experiments over the past five years, demonstrating that green-synthesized IONPs are safe, biocompatible, and effective in combating cancer. Significant issues in plant-based synthesis are examined comprehensively, including potential solutions to enhance the repeatability of the process [ ].
AuNPs, for instance, have a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) effect that lets light interact with them. This makes them useful for biological uses like cancer imaging and therapy, specific drug delivery, and SPR. The size of Au-NPs is essential for what they can be used for. Small particles are suitable for immunohistochemistry and biomarkers, medium-sized particles are good for diagnosing with DNA, imaging, and biosensor technologies, and bigger particles are ideal for forensic studies. Because they have an SPR effect, Au-NPs can also kill tumor cells through active and passive targeting. They are also very stable and don't cause much harm, which makes them useful for imaging and treating cancer. Greenly made Au-NPs are becoming increasingly important for finding and evaluating cancer. They are not immunogenic, have high permeability, minor damage, and are very stable. They can also help with screening diagnoses and giving medications to the right places. Biosensors and imaging devices that use Au-NPs made chemically or in the environment can find specific types of cancer, which helps with early detection and raises the survival rate. Au-NPs show great potential for BC study, treatment, and diagnosis [ ].
NPs made using green methods are also an area that is growing very quickly. Green-synthesized NPs could be used because they kill bacteria and free radicals better than NPs made in other ways. They could be a big help in the fight against drug-resistant cancer and other diseases related to healthcare, among other things [ , ].
Chemotherapeutic drugs are used to treat cancer, but they can damage other parts of the body and make it hard for patients to follow their treatment plans [ ]. This makes it challenging to deliver therapeutic drugs to tumor cells with accuracy. Advancements in medical research are being boosted by the development of nanotechnology, which presents fantastic solutions to cope with such life-threatening disorders [ ]. NPs synthesized for medical applications must be biocompatible and low- or non-toxic. As a result, green NPs production is emerging as an alternative to physical and chemical approaches [ ]. Recently, the green synthesis of NPs (GMNP) has gotten much attention because it has many benefits over traditional chemical and physical processes, such as being cheaper, easier, more eco-friendly, biocompatible, and more useful [ ]. Several biomolecules from microorganisms and plants have been successfully employed to manufacture MNPs and have been widely documented [ ].
Because these NPs have unique physical, chemical, electronic, and biological qualities, they are widely used as an alternative for cancer theranostics (treatments and diagnoses). So, making MNPs that could be useful for cancer diagnosis cheaply and reasonably for the environment is becoming a new area of scientific study [ ].
Researchers showed that plants’ regional and seasonal patterns and makeup are complicated. This means that green synthesis is limited by the time and place it is made and that problems with low purity and low output exist [ ].
To make the physical and chemical combination, dangerous or harmful chemicals are used, which could cause cancer or damage the environment [ ]. As a result, a greener, cleaner, and more trustworthy strategy is required. Green synthetics have emerged as a remedy to the restrictions above [ ]. Green synthesis is now chosen because it prevents or minimizes waste, lowers the amount of derivatives and pollution, and uses better, non-toxic solvents [ ]. This method doesn't just use plant sources to break down metal salts. The biomolecules also cover the NPs made or work as end-cap and reducing agents in situ. In addition, their participation in the formation process lowers the nanomaterial's toxicity, stops it from clumping together, and boosts its antibacterial properties, which could lead to a positive feedback loop [ ].
Various testing parameters, including reactant content, reaction duration, pH, temperature, salt concentration, and many others, significantly impact the form, size, and rate of response of the NPs that were created [ ]. The parameters could also change the polydispersity of the materials being made, so more studies need to be done on them. Also, most of these green synthesis ideas are still theoretical, so they still have problems that must be fixed [ ]. These include controlling crystal growth, shape, and size, as well as the stability and clustering of NPs. In addition, the separation and cleaning of NPs are essential factors that need more research [ ]. As researchers look into the possible future directions for the green production of MNPs, researchers expect to see several trends and progress. The main goals are to find new green ingredients, improve synthesis methods for better efficiency and scalability, and make multipurpose NPs fit specific uses. Green-synthesized MNPs will likely lead to significant steps forward and new ideas in medicine, the environment, and technology. NPs can be used in many areas of science, which helps many regions and improves daily life. Nanotechnology will continue to go from strength to strength, with fast progress and wide use in many areas. With ongoing success in green manufacturing methods and the wide range of uses for MNPs, nanotechnology is expected to continue to grow and lead to transformative changes and improvements in many areas [ ].
Integrating green synthesis into cancer therapy is a significant paradigm shift toward medicines with improved efficacy and fewer environmental impacts, bringing hope to the battle against BC [ ].
In recent years, tremendous strides have been made in studies on NPs and how they might be used. The GMNP has been found in many studies using different organic sources, like yeast, bacteria, plants, and fungi. However, some problems still make it hard to scale it up and use it in other ways. Here are some of the most significant issues seen during the synthesis: (1) To change the NPs' size and form, researchers need to do in-depth optimization studies on the reactants (plant extract, microorganism inoculum, fermentation medium makeup, etc.) and process factors; (2) More research needs to be done on improving different physical properties of NPs for specific uses; (3) It is essential to thoroughly study how each molecule of plant extract and biological component of microorganisms contributes to the creation of NPs; (4) Green synthesis ways to make more NPs for business use must be a top priority; and (5) Improving the yield and stability of NPs while reducing reaction time is needed by finding the best values for different reaction factors [ ]. Resolving these issues might render green synthesis methods more economical and competitive with conventional techniques for large-scale NP manufacturing. Moreover, the separation and purification of NPs from the reaction mixture are critical aspects that require examination. A comprehensive toxicological assessment of NPs on flora and fauna is necessary before their further application across diverse sectors. Alongside wild-type strains, genetically modified microbes that may generate increased quantities of enzymes, proteins, and macromolecules may enhance NP synthesis and stability. Moreover, strengthening the metal accumulation capacity and tolerance of genetically engineered microorganisms should provide a promising approach for producing and using MNPs using green synthesis methods [ ].
Furthermore, preserving reproducibility and consistency in these synthesis procedures remains a significant obstacle. Inconsistencies in the characteristics and functionality of NPs have been attributed to several variables, including differences in plant species, growing environments, and extraction techniques. Furthermore, it is more difficult to compare and validate results from different research when different experimental methods and analytical techniques are used. Reproducibility is guaranteed, and meaningful comparisons across studies are made easier by standardization procedures and developments in analytical techniques, such as standardized characterisation approaches and data reporting processes [ , ]. Furthermore, many reviews point out differences in the final product's characteristics, such as its sizes, forms, or composition, as well as changes in the mechanism that are often seen after end usage. Variations in plant species, growing circumstances, and extraction techniques may be blamed for these uneven and varied nanoparticle properties and performance across several trials. Furthermore, different experimental procedures and analytical methods make comparing and validating findings from other investigations more difficult. In general, inconsistent results, limited reproducibility, difficulties in comparability, compromised product qualities, a barrier to industrial applications, and higher research and development costs result from the lack of standardization and protocol challenges in producing plant-assisted NPs [ ].
Production and quality control at good manufacturing practice (GMP) facilities are crucial for biopharmaceuticals, which employ human cells or tissue-derived substances as medications [ ]. Reliable goods are manufactured and introduced to the market. The good laboratory practice (GLP) rule governs nonclinical safety research quality control. The goal of the rule is to encourage scientists to plan and conduct their research in a manner that supports the validity and quality of the test results. If produced by current GMP and GLP criteria, novel DDS such as nanomedicine have been authorized for clinical use [ ]. Furthermore, a crucial step for the industry's success is the development of dependable and reasonably priced methods for the large-scale biogenic production of MNPs [ ]. Early in the development process, the difficulties of closing the translational gap between laboratory and industry practice should be considered. These problems must be resolved to facilitate market penetration and guarantee subsequent regulatory compliance. This enables the effective transmission of the information generated at the bench to the patient’s bedside. A successful transfer requires establishing large-scale NP production that complies with GMP. Commercial batch size and GMP regulations should be considered when designing these procedures. The excellent GMP compatibility and infinite batch size of continuous production technologies make them stand out among NP manufacturing techniques. Aside from the selection of production techniques, the quality of the particles by pharmaceutical standards is another crucial factor. Particle characteristics, including size and distribution, must be well managed to prevent batch-to-batch fluctuations. By adjusting production settings and continuously monitoring them using in-process controls, continuous manufacturing techniques allow for managing crucial quality aspects [ ].
Although green synthesis of nanoscale metals has a lot of promise, it has limitations regarding material selection, synthesis conditions, and the application and quality control of the final product. Adopting industrial production and widespread use of green-synthesised nanoscale metals is hampered by these factors. Furthermore, the storage of metals at the nanoscale must be considered. The cost of storing or preserving nanoscale metals will significantly drop if they can be maintained at room temperature. The stability of nanoscale metals affects how they are stored. The NPs may be kept more cheaply the more stable they are. As previously stated, AgNPs made from Acorous calamus rhizome remained steady for 24 h; however, those made from Carissa carandas fruits only displayed the most significant UV–Vis absorption peak for 4 h before declining over time. This suggested that after four hours, its stability might be impacted. Cu NPs produced using leaf extract from Thymus vulgaris L. and supported on bentonite may be stored for 20 days in an inert environment. For 15 days, the Cu NPs created using aqueous guava extract were stable at room temperature. PEG 6000 was used as a capping agent to stabilize the metal colloid over a 15-day storage period since the Cu NPs are readily oxidized to CuO. Various synthesis materials and techniques may impact the stability of the products. To create more stable goods, researchers need to choose the right materials and techniques [ ].
Materials, including metals, polymers, and other carbon allotropes not found in the body, are used to create NPs. Exposure to these exogenous substances raises questions about immunological activation, inflammation, and toxicity. Direct synthesis of NPs from host-derived compounds may lessen these reactions. It is still challenging to combine patient-derived macromolecules into structures that can be tuned in size and form to the same extent as their external counterparts. This novel type of customized nanomaterial has the potential to completely transform patient care and play a crucial role in the therapeutic and diagnostic toolkit [ , ]. Furthermore, the effectiveness of nanoparticle therapies may be impacted by individual differences in responsiveness to treatments brought on by genetic, metabolic, or environmental variables. Discussing customized medicine strategies and how they may be combined with nanoparticle therapies would be beneficial. Receiving informed permission may be complicated in several situations because patients engaging in drug testing in personalized medicine must be aware of the possible risks involved. Additionally, the expense of developing a precision medication is relatively high, and it will need financing for years with a scary uncertainty of success, much like the manufacture of nanomedicine. The tools required to develop nanomedicine and sequence a person's DNA also raise the price. Therefore, tailored medicine must be included in standard healthcare before being introduced to the market. To improve knowledge and comprehension of a patient's unique conditions and lower the likelihood of failure, it is necessary to comprehend the disease at the molecular and genetic level, interpret the genetic testing required for the patient, and then design the medication [ ]. Individual differences in responsiveness to treatments brought on by genetic, metabolic, or environmental variables may impact the efficacy of NPs-based treatments. It would be beneficial to discuss customized medicine strategies and how they might be used with green NP therapy to treat BC.

## Conclusion
Researchers showed that different MNPs, including Ag, Au, Zn, iron, Cu, Ti, and MOF, can treat cancerous tumors, including OS, CHS, and EWS. However, most of these tests were done in animals first, and they didn't go very far in the real world because MNPs have some problems, like being poisonous and making the immune system react badly. They also concluded that even though there are many chemical and physical ways to make NPs, they are expensive, must be made at high temperatures and under a lot of pressure, and are bad for the environment because they could cause hazards or be carcinogenic or toxic. Because of this, cleaner synthesis routes that use different types of biomass (like plants, mushrooms, algae, or bacteria) are getting more attention. MNPs made from green materials can change how BC is treated because they are safe, effective, and can do many things. More studies and clinical trials are needed to prove they work and get governmental approval for wide medical use. However, green MNPs have problems with being stable and getting approval from regulators. Green-synthesized MNPs have been studied for their potential use in treating BC, but these studies have some issues that need to be fixed before we can move forward. Green-synthesized MNPs have been studied as a possible way to treat BC, but these studies have some problems that need to be fixed before we can move forward. A lot of research was done on OS. Also, most studies have been on AgNPs and AuNPs that were made using green synthesis. Different kinds of BC should be studied. The role of green-synthesized MNPs in CHS and EWS must be studied in a focused and correct way. Novel MNPs, like MOFs, can also work against different types of BC. Researchers should consider doing more in-depth studies on new green-synthesized MNPs fighting BC. In the future, the clinical treatment of BC may benefit from using MNPs made using the green synthesis approach. Necessary research will be carried out, including customized therapy using green-produced MNPs to treat BC. Significant difficulties also arise when green-synthesized NPs are produced widely after being synthesized in a lab. The practical use of green MNPs to treat BC will need research into cost, scalability, and quality preservation during mass manufacturing. Furthermore, depending on the biological materials and techniques used, the production of green-synthesized MNPs might differ significantly. For the practical use of these NPs in BC therapies, it is crucial to address the need for standardized techniques to guarantee repeatability and consistency in nanoparticle features, such as size, shape, and surface charge.
