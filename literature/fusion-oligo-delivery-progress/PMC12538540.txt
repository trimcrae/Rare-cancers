## Abstract
Effective siRNA delivery in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is limited by preferential hepatic accumulation. To address this, a lipopolymer (PEI‐C) is developed by conjugating lipid to polyethylenimine and formulated lipopolymer nanoparticles (LPNPs) via complexation with siRNA. The siRNA delivery efficiency of LPNPs is evaluated in vitro in t(4;11)‐positive ALL cells (RS4;11 and SEM) as well as “normal” peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from human donors and bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) from mice. Molecular effects are assessed by quantifying target mRNA silencing and downstream apoptosis. In vivo biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy are examined in mouse models. LPNPs demonstrated significantly higher siRNA uptake than commercial reagents in PBMCs and BMSCs, reaching siRNA uptakes of 87.2% and 93.0% in RS4;11 and SEM cells, respectively. Molecular analyses revealed effective silencing of KMT2A::AFF1 mRNA (≈80% in RS4;11), accompanied by BCL2 downregulation and increased apoptosis. In vivo, LPNPs showed efficient siRNA biodistribution to leukemia‐associated organs (spleen and bone marrow) and significantly reduced leukemia burden in a systemic RS4;11 xenograft mouse model and improved survival. These findings suggest that PEI‐C‐formulated LPNPs present a promising avenue for therapeutic siRNA delivery in ALL, effectively targeting leukemia‐associated organs, and warrant further exploration in clinical studies.
Keywords: KMT2A::AFF1, leukemia, lipopolymer nanoparticle, LPNP, MLL‐AF4, siRNA

This study introduces a new lipopolymer nanoparticle (LPNP) system that efficiently delivers siRNA to leukemia cells. The LPNPs silence the leukemia fusion gene KMT2A::AFF1, induce apoptosis, and decrease leukemia burden in mice. These results demonstrate the potential of LPNPs as a targeted siRNA therapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia.

## Background
Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a genetically heterogeneous malignancy characterized by a variety of genetic alterations, among which the lysine methyltransferase 2A (KMT2A, formerly MLL) gene rearrangements (KMT2Ar) stand out as particularly aggressive and clinically challenging ones.[
 
]
KMT2A is crucial for embryonic development and hematopoiesis;[
 
] however, this gene forms aberrant fusions with over 100 partner genes,[
 
] most commonly with AFF1 (also known as AF4), and generates the potent fusion oncogene KMT2A::AFF1 (t(4;11)(q21;q23), formerly MLL‐AF4), implicated in over half of KMT2Ar‐driven ALL cases.[
 
]
KMT2Ar ALL accounts for more than 70% of new ALL diagnoses in infants, 5 to 6% of pediatric cases, and 15% of adult cases.[
 
] Clinically, KMT2Ar ALL is associated with hyperleukocytosis, high incidence of central nervous system involvement, aggressive disease course, early relapses, and poor prognosis.[
 , 
] In a recent study by Richard‐Carpentier and colleagues, the authors analyzed ALL patients treated at their institution between 1984 and 2019. Among 50 KMT2Ar‐positive ALL patients, 84% were KMT2A::AFF1‐positive, and 16% had other fusion partners, with a 5‐year overall survival rate of just 18%.[
 
] In a separate report, authors studied 7 infants with KMT2Ar‐positive ALL, of which they found five had KMT2A::AFF1 fusions and two had KMT2A::MLLT3 fusions.[
 
] The recent U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of the menin inhibitor, Revumenib (Revuforj), which blocks the interaction of menin with the KMT2Ar protein, represents an advance in KMT2Ar leukemia management;[
 
] however, no therapies specifically targeting KMT2Ar fusion genes have so far been approved. Furthermore, Phase 2 clinical data for Revumenib showed safety concerns, with 54% of patients experiencing grade 3 or higher adverse events, including differentiation syndrome, QTc prolongation, and febrile neutropenia.[
 
]
KMT2A::AFF1 functions by directly interacting with BCL2‐specific enhancers, which orchestrates histone modifications, including H3K27 acetylation and H3K79 methylation, ultimately driving the transcription of anti‐apoptotic genes like BCL2 and MCL1.[
 , 
] Critically, its persistent expression is indispensable for ALL maintenance, as silencing KMT2A::AFF1 has the potential to prevent ALL proliferation and halt disease progression.
The clinical management of KMT2Ar ALL remains a formidable challenge due to its aggressive nature, therapy resistance, and poor prognosis.[
 
] Pediatric patients with KMT2A::AFF1‐positive ALL frequently present with high leukocyte counts and central nervous system involvement, complicating treatment efforts, while adult cases, though less prevalent, are often associated with additional genetic alterations, exacerbating resistance and leading to dismal outcomes.[
 , 
] The fusion product KMT2A::AFF1 drives leukemia progression by altering gene expression, conferring resistance to standard chemotherapy, and promoting early relapse, leaving current treatment regimens largely ineffective and troubled with toxicity.[
 , 
] This resistance stems from the fusion protein's intricate mechanisms involving epigenetic modifications and protein‐protein interactions, which are poorly addressed by conventional small‐molecule inhibitors.[
 
] Therefore, there has been a continuous push to develop personalized therapeutic strategies to specifically target the molecular drivers of KMT2A::AFF1/t(4;11) ALL. Tailored RNA interference (RNAi) using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) shows significant promise in this context due to its ability to precisely target and modulate gene activity.
The siRNAs are a promising class of synthetic oligonucleotide‐based RNAi therapeutics that hold promise for improving disease management through the targeted silencing of genes of interest. However, their therapeutic potential is hindered by challenges in systemic delivery, including rapid degradation in the bloodstream, nonspecific binding leading to off‐target effects, and poor cellular uptake due to its difficulty of crossing the cell membrane.[
 
] The siRNAs often become trapped in non‐productive intracellular sinks, e.g., endosomes, preventing them from reaching the cytoplasm,[
 , 
] and may cause unwanted immune responses.[
 , 
] These limitations can be addressed with the development of safe and efficient delivery systems for siRNA. Although six FDA‐approved siRNA therapeutics are currently available,[
 
] none of them have been developed for malignant diseases or diseases with extra‐hepatic involvement. Addressing hematopoietic diseases, particularly those arising from bone marrow‐derived leukocytes, is particularly challenging as these are considered “hard‐to‐transfect” cells.[
 
] Effective delivery systems are essential for stabilizing siRNAs, improving cellular uptake, and ensuring targeted delivery to diseased cells. Liposomes and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are widely investigated for siRNA delivery, which consists of a lipid layer encapsulating a hydrophilic nucleic acid payload to facilitate cellular delivery. However, their broader applicability is limited by their fast clearance by the liver and unwanted immunogenic responses, as they can trigger the production of IL‐1 cytokines in human immune cells, particularly IL‐1β, a response influenced by both the nucleic acids and lipids used.[
 , 
] Unlike these, polymeric systems have been alternatively studied as non‐viral delivery carriers for oligonucleotides, with polyethylenimine (PEI)‐derived lipopolymers being widely investigated. These cationic polymers, available in various architectures and molecular weights, have been formulated into diverse nanoparticle systems. While nanoparticles formed with high‐molecular‐weight (MW) PEI (≈25 kDa) exhibit enhanced nucleic acid delivery efficiency, they are associated with less than desired biocompatibility.[
 
] In contrast, low‐MW PEIs (≈0.6 –1.2 kDa) offer better biocompatibility but are ineffective for siRNA delivery.[
 
]

Lipopolymers, composed of lipid‐conjugated low‐MW PEI, have recently emerged as a promising platform for overcoming these challenges, providing protection, enhanced uptake, and targeted delivery to specific tissues. By complexing siRNA with these lipopolymers, we formulated LPNPs (lipopolymer/siRNA nanoparticles) that have been explored for modulating gene expression across various cancer models,[
 , , , , , , , 
] including ALL.[
 , 
] Notably, our lipopolymers, PEI‐A, PEI‐B, and PEI‐C exhibited promising results in delivering STAT5A‐targeting siRNA in ALL, with PEI‐C achieving better outcomes.[
 , 
] However, STAT5A is expressed in both leukemic and normal tissues and functions in both diseased and healthy conditions.[
 , 
] To enhance our approach by making it more targeted, we aimed to specifically target a fusion oncogene that is unique to leukemia cells. Prior work using LNP to deliver siRNAs against fusion genes like RUNX1::ETO in AML shows high potential of this strategy.[
 
] More recently, we demonstrated that targeting KMT2A::MLLT3 with our LPNPs reduces leukemia burden in an AML model.[
 
]

In this study, we advance this concept by focusing on KMT2A::AFF1, a hallmark of KMT2Ar ALL and a more selective target in ALL. A key advantage of this approach is the potential for cancer‐specific treatment with minimal effects on normal tissues. Our strategy involves the use of LPNP, employing our lipopolymer, PEI‐C, with siRNAs that specifically bind the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion site (siKMAF), selectively silencing the fused gene. We investigated the potential of PEI‐C to deliver siRNA in ex vivo human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and mouse bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs). We successfully silenced KMT2A::AFF1 using PEI‐C formulated siKMAF LPNPs in ALL cell lines tested in vitro, resulting in increased apoptosis and reduced colony formation. Additionally, we explored the combined efficacy of PEI‐C‐mediated siRNA therapy and the BCL2 inhibitor Venetoclax (Vent), demonstrating enhanced anti‐leukemic activity. Furthermore, we characterized the biodistribution of siRNA delivered via LPNPs, which showed preferential distribution in organs associated with leukemia and improved extrahepatic delivery in a murine model. In our previous research, we tested PEI‐C formulated LPNPs in a local xenograft model of ALL.[
 
] Here, we conducted a systematic murine cell line‐derived preclinical model and observed positive therapeutic outcomes with PEI‐C formulated siKMAF LPNPs. This research highlights the translational potential of PEI‐C‐mediated siRNA delivery and provides compelling evidence for its development as a precision therapy for KMT2Ar t(4;11) ALL.

## Results and Discussion
2.1.

## Physicochemical Characteristics
To evaluate the transfection potential of PEI‐C in ALL cells, we first characterized the physicochemical properties of PEI‐C formulated LPNPs. A schematic representation of the PEI‐C‐based LPNP formulation with the siRNA is illustrated in Figure
 . Given that the efficiency of siRNA delivery is regulated by physicochemical parameters of the nanoparticles, we examined key attributes including PEI‐C/siRNA binding affinity, siRNA release kinetics from LPNPs, LPNP size, and surface charge.
 To assess the siRNA‐binding capacity of PEI‐C, we quantified its binding efficiency, a crucial determinant of stable complex formation and intracellular delivery. PEI‐C exhibits a robust affinity for siRNA, with a binding capacity (BC50) of 0.99 (w/w), ensuring stable complexation even at low polymer concentrations (Figure  ). However, in comparison to unsubstituted PEI1.2 (BC50 = 0.083 w/w), PEI‐C requires a higher polymer concentration to achieve equivalent siRNA complexation. In addition to binding capacity, post‐endosomal escape siRNA dissociation efficiency from LPNP is of critical importance in achieving effective gene silencing. Therefore, we evaluated siRNA release kinetics by challenging LPNPs with heparin that facilitates the displacement of electrostatically bound PEI‐C and siRNA. PEI‐C based LPNPs exhibit a DC50 value of 40.7 U mL−1, indicating that half of the complexed siRNA is released at this heparin concentration (Figure  ). In contrast, unmodified PEI1.2‐based nanoparticles require a significantly higher heparin concentration (DC50 = 72.7 U/mL) for equivalent siRNA displacement, highlighting that PEI‐C modification enhances siRNA release while maintaining sufficient complex stability.
Particle size is another determinant of nanoparticle biodistribution and cellular uptake. Nanoparticles smaller than 10 nm are subject to rapid renal clearance, whereas those exceeding 200 nm may activate the complement system and trigger immune recognition.[
 
] To ensure optimal nanoparticle dimensions for systemic stability and cellular uptake, we measured the hydrodynamic diameters of LPNPs formulated at PEI‐C/siRNA ratios of 5.0 (r5.0), 7.5 (r7.5), and 10.0 (r10.0). Our findings showed that the nanoparticle sizes were uniform across all formulation ratios, with measured diameters of 193.6 nm for r5.0, 177.2 nm for r7.5, and 183.2 nm for r10.0. The (%) mean polydispersity index (PDI) values were 15.5 (r5.0), 19.8 (r7.5), and 17.2 (r10.0) (Figure  ; Figure , Supporting Information). These results indicate that the compaction efficiency of LPNPs remains largely unaffected by variations in the PEI‐C/siRNA ratio. We next analyzed the ζ‐potential of the LPNPs, a key parameter influencing cellular membrane interactions. Across all PEI‐C/siRNA ratios tested, ζ‐potential values remained consistently positive at 25.4 mV (r5.0), 25.7 mV (r7.5), and 27.2 mV (r10.0) (Figure  ). The sustained cationic charge facilitates efficient electrostatic interaction with anionic cellular membranes, promoting endocytosis and intracellular delivery. TEM imaging of LPNPs formulated at r7.5 further corroborates these findings, revealing spherical nanoparticle morphologies consistent with the hydrodynamic sizes (Figure  ).
In summary, these findings established the appropriate potential of PEI‐C based LPNPs for siRNA delivery, including efficient complexation, controlled release, optimal nanoscale dimensions, and stable surface charge, all of which are crucial for effective intracellular gene silencing in ALL cells.

2.2.

## Efficient siRNA Delivery by LPNPs
Effective intracellular delivery of siRNA is essential for successful siRNA‐mediated gene silencing. Since our LPNPs have a suitable nanoparticle size of ≈200 nm, we aimed to evaluate the siRNA delivery efficiency of PEI‐C in various in vitro and ex vivo models. We first evaluated two ALL cell lines, RS4;11 and SEM, comparing LPNP‐mediated delivery to that achieved by a leading commercial reagent RNAiMAX. In RS4;11 cells, LPNPs demonstrated high siRNA delivery efficiency, with uptake rates of 85.5% and 87.2% at r5.0 and r7.5, respectively, markedly surpassing the 13.7% achieved with RNAiMAX (Figure
 ). A similar trend was observed in SEM cells, where LPNPs facilitated siRNA delivery at efficiencies of 93.0% (r5.0) and 85.4% (r7.5), in contrast to the 12.2% obtained using RNAiMAX (Figure  ). Microscopic investigations on the siRNA uptake in ALL cell lines by using confocal microscopy also confirmed the results from the flow cytometry analysis (see Figure
 ).
 Recognizing the importance of primary cells for translational research, we next investigated siRNA delivery in BMSCs ex vivo, freshly isolated from murine femurs. BMSCs play a pivotal role in the bone marrow niche, where they provide survival signals to leukemic cells, promoting chemoresistance through cell‐cell adhesion[
 
] and reducing sensitivity to apoptotic cell death in ALL.[
 , , 
] PEI‐C formulated LPNPs achieved siRNA uptake efficiencies of 55.0% (r5.0) and 51.2% (r7.5), significantly higher than the RNAiMAX (15.4%) (Figure  ).
We next assessed the siRNA delivery efficiency of PEI‐C in human PBMCs ex vivo. Although we attempted to isolate PBMCs from mice, we could not obtain enough cells for flow cytometric evaluation and thus decided to use human‐derived PBMCs as a model for circulating mononuclear cells. PBMCs are selected for their role as the milieu for leukemic blasts, which suppress normal hematopoiesis, and siRNA‐mediated disruption of these blasts can restore normal blood counts. Furthermore, following systemic nanoparticle administration, PBMCs are among the first cells to interact with the LPNPs, making them a critical target for evaluation. PBMCs were obtained and isolated from six healthy volunteers. PEI‐C LPNPs facilitated siRNA uptake in the PBMC populations, with efficiencies of 13.4% (r5.0) and 12.1% (r7.5), where the RNAiMAX reagent achieved only 4.4% uptake (Figure  ). This analysis encompassed the full spectrum of PBMC subpopulations, including lymphocytes, monocytes, and dendritic cells, rather than focusing on a specific subset.
Beyond assessing siRNA uptake in PBMCs, we also evaluated the cytotoxicity of PEI‐C‐formulated LPNPs in normal cells using the MTT assay. PBMCs isolated from healthy human donors were transfected with PEI‐C/siRNA LPNPs. While PEI‐C alone caused slight growth inhibition, neither siRNA alone nor the PEI‐C/siRNA LPNPs significantly affected cell viability, indicating that the LPNP formulations are biocompatible and well‐tolerated by primary human cells (Figure , Supporting Information).
These findings confirmed that the PEI‐C‐mediated siRNA delivery was highly effective in ALL cells, demonstrated robust performance in siRNA delivery to primary BMSCs and PBMCs, and, interestingly, outperformed a widely used commercial transfection reagent. The enhanced delivery in RS4;11, and SEM cells highlights the strong affinity of PEI‐C‐based nanoparticles for ALL cells, likely due to optimized nanoparticle size and charge properties. Our findings extend beyond conventional immortal cell line models to include clinically relevant primary cells. While primary cells inherently present a more challenging barrier for nanoparticle‐mediated transfection due to slower proliferation rates,[
 
] our data provide compelling evidence that PEI‐C effectively overcomes these barriers, particularly in BMSCs. The variability observed in PBMC transfection efficiency across individual cohorts highlights the influence of inter‐individual differences, an inherent challenge in primary cell studies,[
 
] which should be addressed in future clinical applications. One constraint of this study was the inability to assess siRNA uptake in human BMSCs due to limited access, as well as in murine PBMCs because of insufficient cell yield after collecting blood from mice. This restriction hinders direct comparative analysis between species. Future research can optimize nanoparticle formulations to improve PBMC transfection efficiency while minimizing variability. Additionally, mechanistic studies that explore the different uptake of LPNPs among PBMC subsets could provide deeper insights into their interactions with circulating cells.

2.3.

## siKMAF LPNPs Silence KMT2A::AFF1 and BCL2

Following the confirmation of efficient siRNA internalization in ALL cells via PEI‐C LPNPs, we next assessed the molecular consequences of siKMAF LPNP treatment on KMT2A::AFF1‐positive cells. Quantification of target mRNA levels was performed using RT‐qPCR in RS4;11 and SEM cells treated with siKMAF LPNPs (40 nm siRNA, r7.5.0). We selected this condition based on an MTT‐based cell growth inhibition study. Among the tested siRNA concentrations (20, 40, and 60 nm) and weight ratios (5.0:1.0, 7.5:1.0, and 10.0:1.0), 40 nm siRNA provided the most effective inhibition with minimal cytotoxicity, while the 7.5:1.0 ratio offered the best balance of efficacy and safety (Figure , Supporting Information). To analyze the time‐dependent dynamics of gene silencing, transcript levels were measured at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h following treatment. In RS4;11 cells, a modest reduction in KMT2A::AFF1 mRNA was detectable as early as 6 h post‐treatment, with maximal suppression (≈80%) observed at 24 h, followed by a partial re‐expression at 48 h (Figure
 ). SEM cells exhibited a similar pattern, maximum knockdown (≈50%) at 24 h, followed by a comparable rebound at 48 h (Figure  ). RS4;11 cells showed a greater reduction in KMT2A::AFF1 expression than SEM cells at the 24‐h time point, possibly due to differing susceptibilities of the two cell lines to siRNA‐mediated silencing.
 To delineate the downstream molecular impact of KMT2A::AFF1 suppression, we further examined the expression of the anti‐apoptotic gene BCL2, a known transcriptional target of KMT2A::AFF1. Mechanistically, KMT2A::AFF1 promotes BCL2 transcription via DOT1L‐mediated histone H3K79 methylation[
 
] Consistent with KMT2A::AFF1 silencing, BCL2 transcript levels were significantly reduced in RS4;11 cells (≈30% knockdown at 24 h; Figure  ). Similarly, in SEM cells, BCL2 expression decreased by ≈25% at the 24‐h timepoint (Figure  ). The silencing kinetics of KMT2A::AFF1, akin to that of BCL2, supports the functional linkage between these genes.
Expanding on our siRNA uptake results, we next evaluated the functional gene silencing efficiency of PEI‐C‐based LPNPs in direct comparison with RNAiMAX. Since we saw a maximal knockdown at 24 h, we focused on this single time point rather than conducting a full time‐course (6, 12, 24, and 48 h). RS4;11 and SEM cells were transfected with siKMAF complexed with either PEI‐C or RNAiMAX, and KMT2A::AFF1 mRNA levels were measured by RT‐qPCR after 24 h. Consistent with the uptake data, PEI‐C LPNPs resulted in greater knockdown than RNAiMAX in both cell lines. This enhanced silencing was also reflected in downstream targets BCL2 and HOXA9, further supporting the superior performance of the PEI‐C delivery system (Figure , Supporting Information).
To validate the specificity of our RNAi strategy, we employed two distinct siKMAF sequences targeting the unique fusion breakpoints present in RS4;11 (KMT2A exon 10–AFF1 exon 4, e10–e4) and SEM (KMT2A exon 9–AFF1 exon 4, e9–e4) cells. Both siRNAs effectively suppressed KMT2A::AFF1 expression in their respective target cell lines, emphasizing the precision of this approach in selectively silencing fusion transcripts. This target‐specific mechanism underscores the potential of siRNA therapy for overcoming drug resistance, as RNAi‐escape mutations could be mitigated through rational sequence modifications. Analysis of transcript levels indicated that peak silencing occurred 24 h after transfection, with gene expression rebounding by 48 h. While this transient knockdown may be sufficient to initiate apoptosis, a more prolonged silencing effect could be advantageous for sustained therapeutic efficacy. One possible strategy to achieve that could perhaps be through multiple dosing; however, this warrants further comprehensive investigation. Besides, we employed conventional unmodified 21‐mer siRNAs in this study; future studies incorporating chemical modifications may enhance siRNA stability and extend the duration of target suppression.
The ability to selectively target leukemic fusion genes represents a major advantage over conventional chemotherapy, offering a leukemia‐specific strategy with minimal impact on normal hematopoiesis.[
 
] A key advantage of targeting leukemic fusion genes is the potential for cancer‐specific treatment with minimal effects on normal tissues, where the target fusion gene does not exist. Our findings lay the foundation for further refinements in siRNA‐based therapeutics aimed at targeting oncogenic fusion transcripts with precision and robust efficiency.

2.4.

## Silencing KMT2A::AFF1 Induces Apoptosis and Disrupts ALL growth
To investigate whether the knockdown of KMT2A::AFF1 translates into apoptotic induction in leukemia cells, we evaluated the levels of apoptosis in RS4;11 and SEM cells. These cells exhibit resistance to apoptotic triggers, largely driven by their dysregulated expression of pro‐survival factors such as BCL2. Cells were treated with siKMAF LPNPs, incubated for 72 h, and subsequently analyzed using FITC‐Annexin V and PI staining to distinguish viable cells (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻) from late apoptotic/necrotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) via flow cytometry. KMT2A::AFF1 knockdown significantly induced apoptosis in both RS4;11 and SEM cells, with siKMAF LPNP treatment causing late apoptosis rates of 19.93% and 15.97%, respectively (Figure
 ). The untreated and CsiRNA LPNP‐treated groups displayed no significant differences in apoptosis, directing the biocompatibility of the LPNP delivery system. In line with apoptosis induction, a decrease in the viable cell population was observed. The RS4;11 and SEM cell lines exhibited 62.70% (Figure  ; Figure , Supporting Information) and 72.73% (Figure  ; Figure , Supporting Information) viable cells, respectively, following siKMAF LPNP treatment.
 We then assessed the apoptosis induction levels following treatment with siKMAF LPNPs to determine whether siKMAF LPNPs in combination with Venetoclax potentiates BCL2 inhibition. Venetoclax is a BH3 mimetic that selectively inhibits BCL2, which was recently shown to be efficacious in t(4;11) ALL in a 46‐year‐old male with relapsed ALL (KMT2A::AFF1‐positive).[
 
] Venetoclax combined with other chemotherapies, e.g., Chidamide and Azacitidine, resulted in a complete remission in this patient, supporting its use in combination regimens for relapsed/refractory cases. We observed over 60.10% apoptosis induced in Venetoclax monotherapy (10 nm) in RS4;11 cells (Figure  ). Its combination with siKMAF LPNPs substantially increased apoptosis to 71.67%, whereas Venetoclax‐CsiRNA combination did not have a significant effect (63.03%). We observed a similar effect on SEM cells; Venetoclax‐siKMAF combination resulted in a significant increase in apoptosis rate (32.40%), compared to Venetoclax alone (20.76%) and Venetoclax‐CsiRNA combination (22.63%) (Figure  ). These effects were paralleled by reductions in cell viability. In RS4;11 cells, the viable population declined from 23.23% with Venetoclax alone to 12.87% with Venetoclax and siKMAF LPNP combination (Figure  ). In SEM cells, viability dropped from 66.90% to 52.16% with the combination therapy (Figure  ).
To further assess the impact of KMT2A::AFF1 silencing on leukemia cell growth, we performed clonogenic assays to evaluate the long‐term proliferative capacity of RS4;11 and SEM cells. Treatment with siKMAF LPNPs alone reduced colony formation by 57.7% in RS4;11 cells and 19.0% in SEM cells compared to the untreated cells (Figure
 ). Venetoclax alone inhibited colony formation by 31.0% in RS4;11 and 25.7% in SEM cells. Particularly, Venetoclax‐siKMAF combination significantly enhanced inhibition, reducing colony numbers by 93.5% in RS4;11 and 53.0% in SEM cells (Figure  ). In contrast, the Venetoclax‐CsiRNA combination resulted in only a modest effect. The suppression of clonogenic potential extended to distinct hematopoietic progenitor subtypes in both cell lines, including burst‐forming unit‐erythroid (Figure  ) and colony‐forming unit‐granulocyte, macrophage (Figure  ), which signifies a broad inhibitory effect across leukemic progenitor populations.
 These findings illuminate the promising therapeutic potential of siKMAF LPNPs in promoting apoptosis and curtailing the growth of leukemia cells, both as an independent treatment and alongside Venetoclax. Mechanistically, the combined inhibition of KMT2A::AFF1 and BCL2 disrupts pro‐survival signaling pathways, increasing the susceptibility of cells to apoptosis. We opted for a 10 nm concentration for Venetoclax monotherapy, as this minimal dose has already been reported to effectively induce apoptosis via caspase‐3/7 activation in ALL cells.[
 
] Given the growing interest in BCL2 inhibitors for leukemia management, our data provide primary evidence that siKMAF LPNPs could further sensitize KMT2A::AFF1‐positive leukemias to Venetoclax. This presents a valuable opportunity for advancing combination therapy development, which is essential for improving treatment outcomes, particularly in aggressive leukemia subtypes, by increasing therapeutic effectiveness while tackling resistance mechanisms.[
 , 
] Although Venetoclax displayed varying levels of apoptosis induction between the RS4;11 (high sensitivity) and SEM (low sensitivity) cell lines, the combination with siKMAF LPNP consistently yielded enhanced efficacy compared to Venetoclax alone in both cell lines. The consistency observed in apoptosis induction and colony formation inhibition across biological replicates highlights the therapeutic potential of siKMAF LPNP.

2.5.

## LPNPs Target Extrahepatic Tissues In Vivo
We further investigated the in vivo efficacy of PEI‐C‐mediated therapeutic siRNA delivery. Considering the inherent differences between in vitro and in vivo systems, we first sought to determine the optimal route of administration by evaluating siRNA biodistribution in key leukemia‐associated organs using a healthy mouse model. To accomplish this, we administered a single dose of siRNA formulated in LPNP (1 mg kg−1 body weight) to Balb/c mice (Figure
 ). Stem‐loop PCR quantification revealed that the IV route resulted in superior siRNA biodistribution compared to IP and SQ administrations. Specifically, the liver tissue exhibited siRNA concentrations of 823 pM (IV), 594 pM (IP), and undetectable (SQ), presumably 0 pM, at 24 h of post‐treatment. Particularly, the IV route showed significantly higher siRNA distribution in leukemia‐associated major targets compared to liver, with concentrations of 1943 pM in the spleen and 1452 pM in the bone marrow. The highest siRNA levels were seen in lungs (3266 pM) following the IV administration (Figure  ). The SQ route exhibited poor siRNA biodistribution consistently, resulting in the exclusion of this route of administration from further experiments.
 Since multiple siRNA injections are common for efficacy in preclinical studies, we conducted a second study with 5 consecutive dose administrations using another cohort of Balb/c mice via IV and IP routes. Consistent with the single‐dose study, the IV route again outperformed the IP route, with significantly higher siRNA levels detected in extrahepatic organs such as spleen (8434 pM), and bone marrow (2323 pM) as compared to the liver (2278 pM; Figure  ). Interestingly, the distribution profile shifted slightly in the multi‐dose experiment, with the spleen exhibiting higher siRNA accumulation than the lungs. Across both single and multiple injections, siRNA levels in the kidney were higher following the IP administration than the IV, suggesting more rapid clearance via the IP route. These findings demonstrate that the lipopolymer PEI‐C formulated LPNPs effectively address a critical challenge in siRNA therapeutic development, achieving effective extrahepatic siRNA delivery to leukemia target tissues via systemic IV administration.
Although our study primarily focused on the LPNP for ALL treatment, its impact on other hematopoietic conditions remains promising. These findings indicated that the PEI‐C formulated lipopolymer LPNPs have the potential to evade first‐pass entrapment in the liver and distribute higher siRNA amounts to the spleen following IP administration. This suggests that the high binding capacity of the lipopolymers with siRNA could significantly improve their circulation time within the bloodstream and enhance their uptake in spleen cells. To further advance our understanding, it would be beneficial to explore the precise mechanisms of uptake in the spleen. Although our current nanoparticles do not utilize an active, ligand‐directed targeting mechanism, future modifications may enable targeted delivery. The specificity in the LPNP‐mediated delivery is expected to arise from the siRNA that targets the fusion regions of oncogenes, which are present in cells undergoing uncontrolled proliferation. Additionally, recognizing how disease state or treatments like radiation and chemotherapy can influence this process is crucial for optimizing therapeutic applications. The biodistribution study has shown significant siRNA deposition at the lungs, raising the possibility of siRNA‐based therapies for leukemic diseases with pulmonary involvement and/or other respiratory diseases. A more comprehensive pharmacokinetic study beyond the 24‐h time point would provide more in‐depth insights into the stability, biodistribution, circulation time, and clearance dynamics of siRNA. Addressing these areas could further optimize LPNP‐based siRNA therapeutics and broaden their clinical applicability in leukemia and other medical conditions.

2.6.

## siKMAF LPNP Reduce Systemic ALL Leukemia Burden and Improve Survival
Building on the preferential biodistribution of siRNA to leukemia‐targeted tissues, particularly the spleen and bone marrow following IV administration, we investigate the therapeutic potential of siRNA delivered with the PEI‐C formulated LPNP in an ALL‐xenograft model. Translating siRNA therapeutics from in vitro to in vivo settings always poses substantial challenges due to systemic barriers, including rapid siRNA degradation, clearance from circulation, and limited biodistribution.[
 
] We developed a systemic ALL model by intravenously injecting RS4;11 cells into the tail vein of immunocompromised mice, following a previous report indicating that the median survival for NOD mice engrafted with RS4;11 cells was 45.5 days, which serves as a benchmark for assessing disease progression[
 
] We randomly assigned mice to receive either CsiRNA or siKMAF LPNPs via IV administration (Figure
 ). Leukemia burden is monitored by flow cytometric analysis of human CD45 and CD19 expression in mouse peripheral blood. By day 24, we first detected leukemia cells circulating in the peripheral blood, which led to the initiation of treatment. At day 44, siKMAF‐treated mice exhibit a significant reduction in leukemia burden relative to the CsiRNA group (p = 0.0093; Figure  ). We analyzed the body weight of mice up to the leukemia burden reached ≈30%; our findings showed no significant difference in body weight between the control and treatment groups (Figure  ), confirming the safety of the system. Following the final treatment, mice were monitored for subsequent survival. The siKMAF‐treated group demonstrated a significant improvement in survival rate compared to the CsiRNA group (p = 0.0300; Figure  ). After calculating mice age at death (in days) for both groups, we saw a significant improvement in the siKMAF‐treated group over the CsiRNA group (p = 0.0470; Figure  ).
 Existing studies on siRNA delivery for the therapeutic development of ALL explore various non‐viral siRNA delivery systems, such as Turbofect,[
 
] HiPerFect,[
 , , 
] Lipofectamines,[
 , , 
] OligoFectamine,[
 
] as well as mechanical approaches including electroporation.[
 , , , , , , 
] However, only a few studies have translated in vitro outcomes into preclinical models to evaluate in vivo delivery efficiency of the vector. One of the studies used an LNP formulation to deliver siRNA targeting the TCF3::PBX1 fusion gene through IP administration (2.5 mg kg−1 dose, 10 injections), which resulted in reduced engraftment of ALL cells.[
 
] However, LNPs require specialized preparation techniques and pose risks of immunogenicity. In contrast, our LPNPs, formulated with the PEI‐C, enable siRNA delivery using a straightforward room‐temperature incubation process that does not require specialized equipment/expertise. Compared to the LNP‐based approach, our method achieved significant leukemia burden reduction with a lower siRNA dosage (1 mg kg−1) and fewer injections (7 vs 10). Another study used electroporation to introduce siRNA targeting the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion gene into the SEM cell line before transplanting it into SCID mice, resulting in improved survival rates.[
 
] Despite this, electroporation faces practical challenges for clinical delivery of siRNA therapies due to its technical constraints.[
 
]

While our experiment demonstrated the therapeutic potential of siKMAF LPNPs in vivo, several areas need further investigation to refine and expand our findings. Our analysis primarily focuses on hCD45⁺ and hCD19⁺ leukemia cells in peripheral blood as one measure of therapeutic efficacy; however, evaluating bone marrow engraftment would provide deeper insights within the hematopoietic niche. Additionally, because we focused on mouse survival outcomes, we were unable to collect organ samples to assess in vivo gene silencing; future studies are required to explore these aspects. Moreover, this study used cell‐line‐derived xenografts; validation in t(4;11) patient‐derived xenograft models will represent a crucial next step to establish the clinical relevance of our approach. From a translational development perspective, the immunogenicity and biocompatibility of PEI‐C‐formulated LPNPs remain critical considerations. Our previous findings on PEI‐C formulated LPNPs show minimal immune activation in human PBMCs, with reported levels of IL‐6, IFN‐γ, and TNF‐α being less altered ex vivo, and favorable in vivo biocompatibility following SQ administration, as demonstrated by unaltered hematological and biochemical markers.[
 
] Here, we conducted a serum biochemistry‐based biocompatibility study following IV administration of PEI‐C/CsiRNA LPNPs in healthy BALB/c mice. To avoid off‐target effects, we used CsiRNA. Serum analysis revealed no significant differences in toxicity markers between LPNP‐treated and untreated mice (Figure , Supporting Information). Key biomarkers assessed included hemolysis index, serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (kidney), creatine kinase (muscle), and liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase), along with bilirubin. Comparable levels across all markers indicated no hepatic, renal, or muscle toxicity, confirming that the PEI‐C/siRNA LPNPs are systemically well‐tolerated in vivo. However, further safety evaluations, both at complete blood counts and histopathological levels following IV administration, are required to support future clinical translation of our current approach by confirming the systemic tolerability of siKMAF LPNPs and ensuring their suitability for clinical application in leukemia therapy.

3.

## Conclusion
Building on our previous studies, this study expands the therapeutic potential of LPNP delivery of siRNA therapies to suppress t(4;11) ALL, both as a standalone therapy and in combination with chemotherapies. By achieving robust gene silencing and synergistic anti‐leukemic effects in vitro, our approach represents a significant advancement in RNAi‐based therapeutics. Most importantly, the evidence that LPNP enables extrahepatic biodistribution of siRNA in spleen and bone marrow, along with significant reduction of leukemia burden and improved survival in a systemic ALL xenograft model, provides high translational potential to this strategy. Future studies aimed at optimizing formulation parameters and validating efficacy in clinically relevant models will be instrumental in making this strategy enter the clinic for high‐risk leukemia patients.

## Experimental Section

## siRNA
Unlabeled scrambled control siRNA (CsiRNA: sense: 5′‐GCGUAUUAUACGCGAUUAACG‐3′; antisense: 5′‐GUUAAUCGCGUAUAAUACGC‐3′) and 5′‐carboxyfluorescein (FAM)‐labeled scrambled siRNA (sense: 5′‐/56‐FAM/CAGUCGCGUUUGCGACUGGUUTT‐3′; antisense: 5′‐AACCAGUCGCAAACGCGACUGTT‐3′) were custom synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA). Two distinct siRNA sequences specifically targeting KMT2A::AFF1 fusion transcripts were obtained from a previous study for use in SEM and RS4;11 cells separately.[
 
] The siRNA for SEM cells (siKMAF) used (sense: 5′‐AAGAAAAGCAGACCUACUCCA‐3′; antisense: 5′‐UGGAGUAGGUCUGCUUUUCUUUU‐3′), and the siRNA for RS4;11 cells (siKMAF) used (sense: 5′‐ACUUUAAGCAGACCUACUCCA‐3′; antisense: 5′‐UGGAGUAGGUCUGCUUAAAGUCC‐3′). The FLT‐3 specific siRNA used for the biodistribution study had a sense sequence of 5′‐AUGCUACCAAUUCAAGUGAAGAUTA‐3′ and antisense of 5′‐UUUACGAUGGUUAAGUUCACUUCUAAU‐3′, previously validated for biodistribution studies (all siRNAs from IDT). Lyophilized siRNAs were reconstituted in RNase‐free water to prepare stock solutions as per the manufacturer's instructions. Working siRNA concentrations (0.14 µg/µL) were obtained using nuclease‐free duplex buffer (Cat. #11‐05‐01‐12, IDT, Coralville, IA).

## LPNP Formulation with PEI‐C/siRNA and Transfection
The lipopolymeric delivery reagent PEI‐C was obtained from RJH Biosciences Inc. (Edmonton, AB). It was derived from branched PEI with a MW of 1.2 kDa with lipid substitution as described earlier.[
 , , 
] Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Cat. #13778075, ThermoFisher Scientific, Life Sciences Solutions, Carlsbad, CA) was included in siRNA uptake studies as the reference transfection reagent. To make complexes, PEI‐C, was diluted to a working concentration of 1 µg/µL using nuclease‐free water (Cat. #11‐05‐01‐04, IDT, Coralville, IA). LPNPs were formulated by mixing siRNA, PEI‐C, and serum‐free RPMI 1640 medium (Cat. #11875093, Life Technologies Corporation, Grand Island, NY), followed by brief vortexing and incubation for 30 min at room temperature. The siRNA amount in the LPNPs was determined based on the desired final siRNA concentration (e.g., 40 nM for cell treatment), while the PEI‐C quantity was adjusted to achieve the desired lipopolymer/siRNA weight ratio (e.g., 5.0:1.0 or 7.5:1.0). The cells were transfected using a reverse transfection protocol. LPNPs were first added to cell culture plates, followed by seeding the appropriate number of cells onto the preloaded plates. Cells were incubated at 37 °C until the specified experimental endpoint, after which they were harvested for downstream analyses. Transfections using RNAiMAX were performed following the manufacturer's instructions in a similar manner.

## Cells
The KMT2A::AFF1‐positive cell line RS4;11 was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Rockville, MD, USA). Another KMT2A::AFF1‐positive cell line, SEM, was kindly provided by Prof. Olaf Heidenreich (Princess Máxima Center for Pediatric Oncology, Utrecht, the Netherlands). Both cell lines was maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U mL⁻¹ penicillin, and 100 µg mL⁻¹ streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. Subculturing was performed upon reaching 80% confluency (every ≈4 days), where cells were centrifuged at 600 rpm for 5 min and seeded at 20% of the original density for further expansion. Cells were discarded after 15 passages.
Male Balb/c mice aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Laval, QC, Canada). To isolate BMSCs from mice, the animals were first humanely euthanized in compliance with approved ethical protocols. Both femurs were aseptically removed, cleaned of muscle and connective tissue, and rinsed with sterile phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, Cat. #P7059‐1L, St. Louis, Ewing, MO, USA) to minimize contamination. The ends of the femur were then carefully clipped to expose the bone marrow cavity. Bone marrow was flushed out using a 25G needle attached to a syringe filled with RPMI 1640 medium enriched, ensuring gentle aspiration to preserve cell viability. The flushed bone marrow suspension was passed through a 70 µm cell strainer to remove debris and large particulates. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and the cell pellet was resuspended in fresh MEM α medium (Minimum Essential Medium α; Cat. # 12571063, Life Technologies Corporation, Grand Island, NY, USA, supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were plated in tissue culture‐treated flasks and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Non‐adherent cells were removed by replacing the medium after 48 h, allowing the adherent stromal cells to proliferate and form a monolayer, and preparing siRNA transfection immediately. This study complies with protocols approved by the Health Sciences Laboratory Animal Services (HSLAS) at the University of Alberta, ensuring rigorous adherence to ethical and regulatory standards for animal experimentation.
The PBMCs were isolated from whole blood samples obtained from six healthy human volunteers through Canadian Blood Services, following ethical approvals from the University of Alberta and Canadian Blood Services Ethics Boards. The whole blood was diluted 1:1 with Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and carefully layered over 15 mL of Ficoll‐Paque Plus in a 50 mL tube. The samples were centrifuged at 400 g for 40 min at room temperature. After centrifugation, the upper plasma layer was gently removed, ensuring the lymphocyte‐enriched interface remains undisturbed. The lymphocyte layer was transferred to a new tube and washed three times with fresh HBSS, each involving centrifugation at 100 g for 10 min. Following the final wash, cells were resuspended and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, PAA Laboratories Inc., Etobicoke, ON, Canada) and 40 IU/mL interleukin‐2 (IL‐2; PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

## siRNA Binding and Dissociation Analysis
The siRNA binding affinity of PEI‐C was assessed using a fluorescence‐based dye exclusion assay employing SYBR Green I (Cat. #S7563, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, OR, USA) as described previously.[
 
] To establish the binding profile, PEI‐C solutions (1.0 µg µL−1) were serially diluted in nuclease‐free double‐distilled water (ddH₂O) in microcentrifuge tubes to achieve final lipopolymer concentrations ranging from 0 to 0.056 µg µL−1. To each sample, 2 µL of CsiRNA (0.14 µg µL−1 in water) was introduced, and the mixtures were gently vortexed to facilitate the complex formation, yielding PEI‐C/siRNA weight ratios from 0 to 5.0 (w/w). These complexes were then incubated at an ambient temperature for 30 min to ensure complete binding interactions. Following incubation, each complex was further diluted with 100 µL of SYBR Green I (1× in TAE buffer) to facilitate fluorescence‐based quantification. The samples (100 µL per well) were transferred to black 96‐well microplates, and fluorescence intensity was recorded using a microplate fluorometer (λEX = 485 nm, λEM = 527 nm). The quantity of unbound siRNA within each complex was determined by using blank siRNA as a reference control. The polymer binding efficiency was quantitatively expressed as BC₅₀, defined as the PEI‐C/siRNA ratio necessary to achieve 50% siRNA complexation.
To evaluate the siRNA release dynamics from the LPNPs, a dissociation assay was conducted using heparin as a polyanionic competitor. In brief, PEI‐C/siRNA LPNPs were formulated in ddH₂O and incubated at room temperature, followed by the addition of heparin at final concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 U mL−1. The complexes were incubated for 1 h to allow competitive displacement, after which the extent of siRNA release was quantified using the SYBR Green I exclusion assay, as described previously.[
 
] The degree of siRNA unpacking under anionic conditions was expressed as DC₅₀, representing the heparin concentration (U mL−1) required to achieve 50% dissociation of the complexes.

## Physicochemical Characterization of LPNPs
To assess the size distribution and surface charge characteristics of the LPNPs, particle size and ζ‐potential measurements were performed using dynamic light scattering by following the protocol reported earlier.[
 
] LPNPs formulated with PEI‐C and siRNA at weight ratios of 5.0:1.0 and 7.5:1.0 (w/w) were initially prepared in 50 µL of PBS at room temperature. The complexes were incubated for 30 min to ensure complete nanoparticle formation before being further diluted in 950 µL of PBS. The prepared samples were then transferred into Univettes, and the hydrodynamic diameter (particle size) and surface charge (ζ‐potential) were determined using a Litesizer 500 particle analyzer (Anton Paar). These physicochemical properties provide critical insights into the colloidal stability, siRNA loading efficiency, and overall structural integrity of the LPNP formulations.
To further investigate the morphology and structural characteristics of LPNPs, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed. LPNPs formulated at a 7.5:1.0 (w/w) ratio were prepared as described above and placed on a 5% (w/v) quaternized chitosan solution. The mixture was then applied to a TEM grid and air‐dried at room temperature for 10 min. The morphology of the LPNP complexes was visualized using a FEI (Morgagni) TEM equipped with a Gatan Camera (Advanced Microscopy Facility, Biological Sciences Building, University OF Alberta).

## Ex Vivo and In Vitro siRNA Uptake—Flow Cytometry
The uptake of siRNA using LPNP was assessed in human PBMCs, mouse BMSCs, and the t(4;11) cell lines RS4;11 and SEM using FAM‐labeled scrambled siRNA. Cells were transfected with LPNP complexes prepared at two PEI‐C/siRNA weight ratios (5.0:1.0, denoted as r5.0, and 7.5:1.0, denoted as r7.5). Transfections were performed using 40 nm siRNA, and cells were harvested after 24 h for analysis by flow cytometry. Following incubation, cells were collected in microcentrifuge tubes, washed three times with HBSS (pH 7.2), and resuspended in 200 µL of buffer before transferring to 96‐well plates. The cell‐associated FAM‐labeled siRNA was quantified using a BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) at a core facility (Faculty of Medicine & Dentistry, University of Alberta) by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and the percentage of FAM‐positive cells. The MFI values of treated cells were normalized to those of untreated controls and plotted to evaluate uptake efficiency.

## Ex Vivo and In Vitro siRNA Uptake—Confocal Microscopy
Glass coverslips (15 mm diameter) were sterilized by spraying with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry in a sterile cell culture hood. To enhance cell adhesion, coverslips were placed in a 6‐well plate, and 25 µL of 0.1% poly‐L‐lysine was added to the center of each slip. The plate was left undisturbed in the hood for 1 h. After incubation, coverslips were thoroughly washed by gently dipping them in distilled water at least 10 times and left to dry before further use. LPNPs were prepared in 400 µL of serum‐free RPMI medium at PEI‐C:siRNA ratios of r5.0 and r7.5, with a final siRNA concentration of 40 nm. The nanoparticle suspension was transferred to a 12‐well plate, followed by the addition of 650000 cells suspended in 1.2 mL of culture medium to each well. Cells were incubated for 24 h under standard culture conditions. Following incubation, transfected cells were harvested into sterile 1.5 mL tubes and washed twice with HBSS. Hoechst 33258 (1 µg mL−1; Cat. # H1398, Invitrogen Rockford, lL, USA) was added to stain live cell nuclei, and the suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Cells were subsequently washed and resuspended in 50 µL of HBSS. A 40 µL drop of the stained cell suspension was carefully placed at the center of each cover slip and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h to allow adherence. Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides for imaging. Confocal microscopy was performed using a Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (Olympus FLUOVIEW FV3000) equipped with a 20 × 1.3 NA oil plan‐apochromatic objective at the Advanced Microscopy Facility, Biological Sciences Building, University of Alberta.

## MTT Assay
The anti‐proliferative activity of PEI‐C‐formulated LPNPs was evaluated in t(4;11) acute leukemia cell lines, RS4;11 and SEM, using the MTT assay. Cells were seeded at a density of 120000 cells/mL in 48‐well plates (final volume: 300 µL) and reverse‐transfected with 100 µL of freshly prepared LPNPs containing either siKMAF or CsiRNA. Cells were incubated for 72 h post‐transfection. To optimize delivery conditions, different siRNA concentrations (20, 40, and 60 nm) and PEI‐C/siRNA weight ratios (5.0:1, 7.5:1, and 10.0:1) were tested. Based on growth inhibition efficacy and minimal cytotoxicity, the combination of 40 nm siRNA and a 7.5:1 PEI‐C/siRNA ratio was selected for further experiments. For viability analysis, 50 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg mL−1 in 1× HBSS; Alfa Aesar, MA, USA) was added to each well, followed by 3 h of incubation. Formazan crystals were collected by centrifugation, dissolved in 150 µL of DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (ELx800).
To evaluate the biocompatibility of LPNPs, freshly isolated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; n = 2 donors) were transfected with various formulations: PEI‐C alone, siRNA alone (CsiRNA, siKMAF‐RS4;11, or siKMAF‐SEM), and PEI‐C/siRNA LPNPs using a fixed siRNA concentration of 40 nm and a 7.5:1 PEI‐C/siRNA ratio. After 72 h of incubation, cell viability was measured using the MTT assay as described above. The results were used to assess cytotoxicity and confirm the safety profile of the LPNP formulations in normal human cells.

## Real‐Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR)
The suppression of KMT2A::AFF1 and its downstream target BCL2 was evaluated by quantifying mRNA transcript levels following treatment with LPNP complexes using RT‐qPCR at multiple time points. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop Lite spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the SensiFAST cDNA synthesis kit (Cat. #BIO‐65054, Meridian Life Science, Inc.). RT‐qPCR was conducted with 100 ng of cDNA on a StepOnePlus real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) utilizing the SensiFAST SYBR Hi‐ROX master mix (Cat. #BIO‐92005, Meridian Life Science), with ROX serving as the passive reference dye. Specific primer sequences used for the analysis were detailed in the Cells and Culture section. The PCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 65 °C for 30 s. Melt‐curve analysis was performed at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and a gradual temperature increase of +0.3 °C per second to 95 °C. The ΔΔCt method, using β‐actin as the housekeeping gene, was applied to quantify gene expression levels relative to the scrambled negative control siRNA‐treated group. Forward and reverse primers (IDT, Coralville, IA) used for the amplification of specific transcripts were: i) KMT2A::AFF1 (Forward: 5′‐ACAGAAAAAAGTGGCTCCCCG‐3′, Reverse: 5′‐TATTGCTGTCAAAGGAGGCGG‐3′), ii) BCL2 (Forward: 5′‐GGAGGATTGTGGCCTTCTTT‐3′, Reverse: 5′‐GCCCAATACGACCAAATCCGTTGA‐3′), and iii) 18S rRNA (Forward: 5′‐GAACGTCTGCCCTATCAAC‐3′, Reverse: 5′‐GCTCCCAAGATCCAACTAC‐3′). Transcript levels were quantified using the comparative threshold cycle (ΔΔCt) method, and the abundance of target transcripts was normalized relative to the negative control group treated with CsiRNA LPNPs.

## Apoptosis Study
The apoptotic cell percentages in treatment groups were determined using the FITC‐Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) staining protocol.[
 
] Briefly, RS4;11 and SEM cells were transfected with siRNA LPNPs for three days with/without Vent (Venetoclax/ABT‐199, Cat. #A12500‐50, Adooq Bioscience LLC, Irvine, CA, USA). The cells were then harvested, washed with HBSS, and resuspended in an apoptosis binding buffer provided by the manufacturer (Cat #556547, BD Biosciences). The cells were stained with FITC‐Annexin V and PI, followed by a 15 min incubation in the dark at room temperature. The proportions of apoptotic cells (FITC‐annexin V+, PI+) in the treatment groups were analyzed using a BD LSR Fortessa‐SORP flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and compared to the untreated control group.

## Colony Forming Assay
The effect of KMT2A::AFF1 suppression on colony‐forming potential was evaluated one day after treatment with siKMAF LPNP and/or Vent. Viable cells were counted using a hemocytometer by the trypan blue exclusion method. A total of 1500 RS4;11 cells and 1000 SEM cells were seeded in 500 µL of human methylcellulose base media (Cat. #HSC005, R&D Systems, MN, USA), and the mixture was transferred into the inner wells of a 24‐well plate. To maintain moisture, the outer wells were filled with sterile HBSS. Plates were incubated in a humidified atmosphere (95% air, 5% CO₂) at 37 °C for 14 days. On day 14, colonies were counted under a microscope manually and visualized by staining with 100 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg mL−1; 3‐[4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl]‐2,5‐diphenyl tetrazolium bromide; Alfa Aesar, Ward Hill, MA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 2 h. Images of individual wells were then acquired for documentation and analysis.

## In Vivo siRNA Biodistribution
Male Balb/c mice aged 6–8 weeks, obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Laval, QC, Canada), were selected as the model for in vivo biodistribution studies due to their well‐characterized physiology.[
 
] We aimed to characterize the biodistribution of siRNA delivered through LPNPs into various organs, providing critical insights into delivery efficiency and organ‐specific targeting. To perform this, PEI‐C derived LPNPs were formulated with a previously validated siRNA targeting FLT3 (25 µg per dose; 1 mg kg−1 body weight) as the delivery vehicle. The formulations were administered via intravenous (IV), intraperitoneal (IP), and subcutaneous (SQ) routes according to the detailed regimen outlined in Figure  . All animal procedures were conducted under institutional‐approved SOPs, including monitoring using a standardized scoring system for animal health and pain assessments, ensuring the maintenance of animal well‐being throughout the experimental timeline. Two separate study designs were employed to evaluate the impact of both single and repeated dosing regimens on siRNA biodistribution. In the first study, mice received a single injection of LPNPs via each of the three routes (IV, IP, and SQ). In the second study, mice received multiple LPNP injections (five doses administered every third day) through IV and IP routes. In both studies, animals were humanely euthanized 24 h after the final dose to allow for the collection of vital organs, including the liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs, and heart. Organ samples was analyzed for siRNA levels using a previously reported technique, stem‐loop quantitative PCR (qPCR).[
 
]

## In Vivo siRNA Biodistribution—Stem‐loop RT‐qPCR
To generate a standard curve for quantifying siRNA copies, a series of standards with defined siRNA concentrations was prepared. RNA extracted from untreated (sham‐treated) organ samples was diluted to a concentration of 10 ng µL−1 and added to the standards (5 µL/sample) to mimic the tissue background. The siRNA was added to achieve final concentrations ranging between 0.0005 and 0.3125 µm in the standards. For cDNA synthesis, 1 µL of RNA sample (10 ng µL−1) was mixed with 2 µL of a 5× stem‐loop primer specifically designed for siRNA detection (5′‐GTC GTA TCC AGT GCA GGG TCC GAG GTA TTC GCA CTG GAT ACG ACT AAT CT‐3′). The mixture was vortexed briefly, centrifuged, and subjected to denaturation at 85 °C for 5 min, annealing at 60 °C for 5 min, and held at 4 °C. A master mix containing TaqMan reagents (dNTPs, MultiScribe RT enzyme, RT buffer, and RNase inhibitor; TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit, ThermoFisher, Cat #4366596) was prepared. A volume of 7 µL of the master mix was added to each RNA‐primer sample, followed by incubation at 16 °C for 30 min, 42 °C for 30 min, and 85 °C for 5 min. The cDNA was stored at −20 °C until further use. Stem‐loop RT‐qPCR was performed with a program optimized for detecting mock siRNA. Each reaction contained 1 µL of cDNA (sample or standard), TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher, Cat #4304437), a specific MGBEc probe for the siRNA (5′‐/56‐FAM‐CAC TGG ATA CGA CTA ATC TTC/3MGB‐NFQ/), a forward primer for the mock siRNA (5′‐CAC GCA ATG CTA CCA ATT C‐3′), and a universal reverse primer (5′‐CCA GTG CAG GGT CCG AGG TA‐3′). Ct values were measured and extrapolated against the standard curve to quantify siRNA concentrations.

## In Vivo Biocompatibility
To evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility of PEI‐C/siRNA complexes, healthy male BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks (Charles River Laboratories, Laval, QC, Canada) were IV injected with either RPMI medium (serving as an untreated control, UT) or PEI‐C/siRNA complexes at a siRNA dosage of 1 mg kg−1. Treatments were administered every third day for a total of five doses. 24 h after the final treatment, whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture and immediately centrifuged at 4 °C to isolate serum. Serum biochemistry analyses were performed by IDEXX Reference Laboratories Ltd. (Edmonton, AB, Canada).
