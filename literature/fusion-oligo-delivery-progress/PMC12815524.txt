ADCs are currently the most widely used class of conjugate drugs in clinical oncology, with 15 ADCs approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of various malignancies.[ , ] ADCs present several significant advantages over traditional chemotherapy. Through the precise targeting of cancer cells, ADCs increase therapeutic efficacy while minimizing damage to healthy tissues.[ ] This targeted approach effectively reduces systemic side effects by decreasing off-target toxicity, establishing ADCs as a superior therapeutic modality for the treatment of various malignancies.
However, ADCs face limitations due to their physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties, which impede their ability to effectively cross normal vasculature and penetrate tumor tissues.[ ] The high molecular weight and protein-like characteristics of ADCs further restrict their tumor penetration, despite the potential benefits from the bystander effect. Additionally, ADCs often exhibit significant immunogenicity, driven by the properties of their linkers and payloads, the specifics of the conjugation process, and the possibility of aggregation.[ ] The complex structure of ADCs also contributes to the high costs of manufacturing, which can affect optimal patient outcomes.
Compared to ADCs, peptide-drug conjugates (PDCs) offer distinct clinical advantages. They are easier to synthesize and modify, enabling rational drug design that can improve bioavailability, affinity, and stability while reducing immunogenicity. Moreover, PDCs demonstrate a superior ability to penetrate solid tumor cells, making them more broadly applicable in clinical research.[ ]
Small molecule-drug conjugates (SMDCs) represent another promising category of targeted therapeutics. The distinction between PDCs and SMDCs is often less clear, as peptidic ligands themselves can be classified as small molecules. Beyond peptides, however, SMDCs can utilize a variety of small molecule ligands, including folate, arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) peptides, hormone analogs, and bisphosphonates.[ ] When small molecules are used as ligands, SMDCs offer several advantages over peptide-based PDCs, including improved tissue penetration, easier synthesis, a wider range of targets, and faster cellular uptake. While SMDCs offer advantages in tissue penetration and synthesis, they also have drawbacks, including reduced targeting specificity, shorter half-life, potential for drug resistance, and limited receptor-mediated internalization.[ ] In contrast, PDCs provide unique benefits in targeting specificity and stability, making them a promising alternative in targeted cancer therapy.

## Design and Development of PDCs
A PDC consists of three main components: a peptide segment, a small molecule linker, and a cytotoxic payload [Figure ]. Peptides in PDCs are essential for directing the cytotoxic component to specific cancer cells by targeting proteins that are overexpressed in these cells, thereby concentrating the drug at the tumor site. The cytotoxic payload must be released only within cancer cells to minimize off-tumor toxicity. Compounds with sub-nanomolar half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values, such as camptothecin (CPT) derivatives, auristatin, and doxorubicin (DOX), are ideal for this purpose. Appropriate linkers improve PDC stability and ensure a sufficient in vivo half-life for effective targeting.[ ]
 

## Selections of targeting moieties of PDCs
The targeting moieties of PDCs, typically peptides, recognize cancer cells by binding to specific receptors or antigens that are overexpressed on the surface of these cells. These receptors are often unique to, or more abundant in, cancer cells compared to normal cells, enabling the PDC to selectively target and deliver the cytotoxic payload directly to the tumor, thereby minimizing damage to healthy tissues. In clinical research, the targeting moieties of PDCs are composed of several to tens of amino acids, which are mainly categorized into two types.[ ] The first type aims to enhance cellular penetration by drawing inspiration from transmembrane amino acid sequences found in certain viruses and proteins, which provide strong cell penetration capability. These sequences are known as cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C270]. This type of peptide forms a PDC drug by linking a small molecule linker to a drug, thereby increasing the drug’s cell penetration and improving its ability to destroy cancer cells. The second type involves a targeting moiety that binds to specific cell surface molecules, leading to endocytosis. The peptides in the targeting moiety of these PDCs are referred to as cell-targeting peptides (CTPs) [Table ]. This class of peptides, when used in PDC drugs, increases the drug’s targeting efficiency, causing it to accumulate within tumor cells, reducing damage to normal tissues, and minimizing toxic side effects. The design objective for the targeting moiety is to raise the intracellular concentration of cytotoxic drugs in tumor cells, while minimizing their concentration in the blood or other normal tissues, thereby reducing adverse drug reactions and improving drug tolerance. However, the use of CPPs is limited by their low cell specificity. In contrast, CTPs function similarly to mAbs while overcoming some of the limitations associated with mAbs.[ ]
 Several PDCs have been approved or are currently in clinical trials, with targets chosen based on specific therapeutic regimens [Table ]. 177Lu-DOTATATE (C65H90N14O19S2; [177Lu]-DOTA0, Tyr3-octreotate) is an approved therapeutic agent, as illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C270). This drug combines a somatostatin analog with a radioactive nuclide, linked through the chelator DOTA (1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid), which features a central 12-membered tetraazacyclododecane ring.[ ] And 177Lu-DOTATATE has shown effectiveness in treating neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), demonstrating extended disease-free survival and highlighting the potential of PDCs in targeted cancer therapies.[ ] Another example is NGR015, a PDC currently in clinical trials and specifically designed to target tumor vasculature by conjugating human tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) with the Asn-Gly-Arg (NGR) tumor-homing peptide. In a phase III trial, NGR-hTNF was evaluated in combination with the best investigator choice therapy for patients with advanced malignant pleural mesothelioma.[ ] These examples illustrate the therapeutic benefits of PDC therapy in the treatment of various malignant tumors.
 Furthermore, the advent of new technologies is advancing the development of innovative PDC drug models. Target design for PDCs is becoming more sophisticated, incorporating structures such as the triple helix bundle, cyclic peptides (Bicycle toxin conjugates), and dendrimeric peptide complexes. These advancements improve the drug’s penetration and targeting efficiency. Additionally, receptor-targeting strategies for drug delivery through modified PDCs are evolving [Figure ], featuring various peptide chain configurations, such as linear, cyclic, dual-target, and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified structures, designed to optimize interactions with specific receptors. Collectively, these technological innovations and advanced design strategies are significantly improving the clinical efficacy of PDCs.[ , ]
 

## Dendrimeric peptides
Dendritic polymers are oligomers characterized by repeating linear connections of dendritic units, forming a macromolecular structure that typically consists of a core, polymer backbone, and dendritic side chains. These polymers are monodispersed and feature a highly branched architecture. Dendrimer-peptide conjugates modify the surface of dendrimers with functional peptide components that target receptors or surface antigens, enabling the development of “smart nanoparticles” specifically designed to target cancer cells.
Dendritic PDCs are particularly effective in delivering DOX to cancer cells. When PEG-modified dendritic peptides are combined with DOX through a linker, they form copolymers that release the drug during the cell division of overexpressed histone protease B cells. In breast cancer-bearing mice, these dendritic peptide-DOX conjugates significantly reduced tumor size compared to DOX alone.[ ] Similarly, gemcitabine conjugated to dendritic peptides showed encouraging outcomes in colorectal cancer treatment. Additionally, the tyrosine-isoleucine-glycine-serine-arginine (YIGSR) peptide, when combined with dendritic polymer peptides, selectively targeted HCT-116 cells, facilitating intracellular drug release and inducing apoptosis.[ ]
Dendrimer-peptide conjugates, in conjunction with phosphorylated Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (p-Nrf2), can influence the early microenvironment in attention deficit disorder.[ ] These conjugates neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS), activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway, and restore cellular antioxidant capacity, thus offering protective effects.[ ] This approach holds promise for reversing early stage cellular damage in Alzheimer’s disease and opens new possibilities for the treatment of brain disorders.[ ]

## Bicyclic toxin peptides
Bicyclic peptides, typically composed of 9–20 amino acids and containing three cysteine (Cys) residues, allow binding to small molecules while maintaining a rigid structure. These peptides offer the pharmacokinetic benefits of small-molecule drugs with low immunogenicity. Their structure improves their ability to penetrate tumor cell barriers and facilitates faster renal clearance. Currently, Bicycle therapeutics is developing three bicyclic peptide-toxin conjugates: BT1718, BT5528, and BT8009.[ ]
BT1718 is an innovative anticancer drug that uses a bicyclic peptide to deliver the toxin derivatized maytansine1 (DM1), specifically targeting membrane type I matrix metalloproteinases. Upon binding, the toxin is released, leading to apoptosis in tumor cells. BT5528 has also demonstrated promising tumor-suppressing effects by targeting ephrin-A type 2 receptor (EphA2), which regulates cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation and is overexpressed in cancers like colorectal, urothelial, and lung cancer.[ ] Another drug, BT8009, targets Nectin-4, a type I membrane protein involved in cell proliferation and differentiation, which is overexpressed in cancers such as urothelial carcinoma and breast cancer. BT8009 has shown significant antitumor activity.[ ]

## Triple helix peptides
Triple helix peptides form a three-helix bundle created by folding two types of polypeptides: affinity bodies and albumin-binding domain-derived affinity proteins (ADAPTs).[ ] This structure demonstrates a high affinity for tumor cell receptors. Despite their larger molecular weight compared to typical peptides, triple helix peptides can be chemically synthesized. They can be chemically linked to nucleophiles, allowing for the creation of PDCs that specifically target and destroy tumor cells. A peptide-polymer conjugate with a unique triple helix structure was developed by covalently attaching PEG to the exterior of the helix bundle. The hydrophilic nature of PEG supports peptide self-assembly, provides protection against hydrolysis, and improves thermal stability. This design also allows for chemical modifications, enabling precise control over the peptide’s spatial structure and aggregation.[ ]

## Strategies for selecting PDC linkers
The selection of appropriate linkers plays a vital role in regulating the release of active drugs in prodrug systems. Various types of linkers, each with different stability and release mechanisms, are available for practical use.[ ] An ideal linker should remain sufficiently stable during systemic circulation to prevent premature drug release, while enabling efficient payload liberation in response to tumor-associated stimuli after cellular internalization. However, many linkers are susceptible to premature degradation before cellular internalization, either in plasma or in the extracellular tumor microenvironment. Therefore, rational linker design is essential to balance systemic stability with tumor-specific drug release.
Linkers are generally categorized into four types: enzymatically cleavable (e.g., esters, amides, and carbamates), acid-labile (e.g., hydrazones and carbonates), reductively cleavable (e.g., disulfides), and non-cleavable (e.g., thioethers, oximes, and triazoles) [Supplementary Figures 2–4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C270].[ ] For PDC design, pH-sensitive linkers, such as those with ester or hydrazone bonds, are often chosen for their stability in the bloodstream and ability to dissociate in the acidic environment of tumors.[ ] Disulfide bonds are another option for targeted release at tumor sites, utilizing the high concentration of reduced glutathione found in cancer cells. Furthermore, PDCs can adopt strategies used in ADCs, such as employing histone-sensitive Val-Cit linkers that react to enzymes overexpressed in tumors.[ ]
One successful example of linker design is the conjugation of anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) peptide (AHNP) with DOX using matrix metalloproteinase-2-sensitive (MMP-2-sensitive) linkers, which primarily use ester and amide bonds. This MAHNP-DOX conjugate effectively targeted HER2-positive cancer in mouse models, demonstrating greater efficacy than DOX alone.[ ] Another study showed that aminoformate or carbonate linkers improved PDC stability, allowing selective degradation by prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and effectively targeting PSA-secreting tumor cells, such as CWR22Rv1 prostate cancer cells.[ , ] Several studies indicate that the stability of linkers in vivo follows this order: amide >carbamate >ester >carbonate.[ ] Additionally, cytotoxic prodrugs like desacetylvinblastine hydrazide ethylamide (DDAE) or monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) have been attached to tripeptides (Ala-Ala-Norleucine) via aminoformate or amide bonds. Although these prodrugs lacked targeting peptides, they could be conjugated with specific moieties to improve tumor specificity, leading to improved tumor suppression in vivo.[ ]

## Strategies for selecting the payload in PDCs
In PDCs, the selection of the cytotoxic component is a critical decision that often mirrors the choices made in ADCs. However, due to differences in the design of the targeting moiety and linker, the selection of the cytotoxic agent must be customized to account for factors such as intracellular targets, half-life, and in vivo kinetics. Additionally, the final PDC construct must exhibit low toxicity and limit off-target effects, both essential for effective cancer treatment [Table ]. Cytotoxic agents commonly used in PDCs include small-molecule chemical compounds, such as microtubule inhibitors, DNA-damaging agents, protein synthesis inhibitors, along with radioactive isotopes and boron neutron capture therapy agents [Supplementary Figure 5, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C270]. Innovative agents like toxic proteins, cytokines, proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs), and oligonucleotides are also being explored for future integration into PDC therapeutics.[ – ] Several PDCs utilizing docetaxel are currently undergoing clinical trials, targeting the sortilin 1 (SORT1) receptor in patients with advanced solid tumors that have not responded to standard treatments.[ ] These trials, including TH1902 and TH1904, have been granted FDA approval for Phase I.[ ] Another strategy involves conjugating 10-hydroxycamptothecin (HCPT) with a peptide to improve drug delivery to tumor cell nuclei via intravenous injection.[ ] In a separate study, the peptide derivative Nap-FFKYp was linked to paclitaxel (PTX), forming a self-assembling gel in water that showed tumor suppression in cellular models.[ ] CPT analogs such as irinotecan, topotecan, belotecan, and trastuzumab deruxtecan have already received clinical approval.[ ] A more recent PDC combined CPT with a Tau-derived peptide (VIQVIC), which self-assembled into nanotubes, prolonging drug release and increasing the ability of the drug to penetrate tumors.[ ] Further innovations include the use of reduced linker ethyl disulfide carbonate (etcSS), conjugated with CPT, and modified with an internalizing RGD peptide (iRGD). This combination formed an amphiphilic hydrogel (diCPT-etcSS-iRGD) with hydrophobic diCPT cores and hydrophilic cyclic iRGD peptides on the surface, improving solubility and increasing the drug’s penetration into tumors by targeting neuropilin-1. This design effectively combined two CPT molecules with ethyl disulfide carbonate and iRGD.[ ] Although all PDCs are still in clinical phases with varying outcomes, these studies highlight both the potential and challenges involved in PDC drug development. Moreover, triple-modified peptide conjugates containing boron atoms have demonstrated strong activity and selectivity for human Y1 receptor subtypes. Modifications to these conjugates have improved solubility and boron loading, making them promising candidates for targeted cancer therapies.[ ]

## Design Strategies for PDC Drug Delivery Systems
The effectiveness of PDCs is influenced by the selection of the targeting moiety, linker, and cytotoxic agent. In some cases, PDCs operate similarly to ADCs by targeting specific cells, undergoing internalization, and then being broken down to release the cytotoxic drug.[ ] Alternatively, lysis may occur outside the cell, followed by the internalization of the cytotoxin. The targeting moiety plays a critical role in this process, whether it involves cell-penetrating or non-CPPs. Non-CPPs typically bind to overexpressed receptors on tumor cells, initiating receptor-mediated endocytosis. Once inside, PDCs dissociate from the receptors in early sorting endosomes. The receptors are then recycled to the cell surface, whereas the PDCs are sorted into the late endosomal pathway for eventual fusion with lysosomes. In the lysosomes, the acidic environment or specific enzymes cause the breakdown of PDCs and the release of the cytotoxic agent. However, a major challenge for PDCs is their instability in circulation and rapid renal clearance. Ensuring stability in circulation is crucial to prevent the premature release of the cytotoxic drug and to minimize systemic exposure.[ ] This instability, along with quick renal clearance, remains a significant obstacle for PDC development.[ ]

## Innovative Formulation Strategies to Improve PDC Stability

## Nanoparticle technology
One approach to addressing the instability of PDCs is by coupling them with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs).[ ] Due to their favorable physicochemical properties, safety, ease of structural modification, and extended half-life, AuNPs are highly effective additives for improving PDC stability. Research has shown that PDCs targeting lymphoma cells demonstrated a 490-fold increase in half-life when combined with PEG-coated AuNPs, resulting in selective PEG-AuNP-PDC43.[ ]
Another strategy involved developing a nanoparticle-based system to improve PDC stability, incorporating photothermal therapy (PTT), a non-invasive cancer treatment using near-infrared (NIR) light. In this system, hollow copper sulfide nanoparticles (HCuS) encapsulated the PDC (cRGD-SMCC-DM1), which enters cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Once inside, the PDC was broken down in lysosomes, releasing DM1. The PDC/P@HCuS nanosystem exhibited antitumor activity for up to three weeks after the initial NIR laser irradiation.[ ] Thus, improving the pharmacokinetics of PDCs using nanomaterials holds great potential for clinical applications.

## Hydrogel technology
Currently, there is significant interest in supramolecular hydrogels formed by low molecular weight gels through non-covalent interactions in water-based assemblies.[ ] These hydrogels can be triggered by slight changes in the body’s microenvironment, making them highly valuable for biomedical applications. Utilizing this natural process to generate supramolecular hydrogels in vivo or in situ presents promising benefits. For example, one research group developed a series of peptide-based amphiphilic (PA) hydrogels for drug delivery. In one study, they linked nabumetone to a peptide via a hydrazone bond, creating a gel that releases nabumetone in the acidic environment of tumors. This hydrogel takes advantage of the pH difference between tumor tissue and healthy cells, enabling selective drug release at the tumor site while minimizing release in healthy tissues, which also helps improve the drug’s stability in circulation.[ ]

## Self-assembly technology
Self-assembled PDCs are an emerging field, in which nanostructures are formed through conjugation, resulting in unique physicochemical properties. These structures offer precise control over drug release, either timed or in response to specific stimuli. By preventing premature metabolism and rapid clearance, self-assembled PDCs improve the drug’s ability to reach its target site.[ ] For instance, self-assembled PDCs based on CPT and PTX have shown high drug-loading capacity and excellent stability.[ ] These PDCs retain the active targeting benefits of PDC drugs while also utilizing passive targeting mechanisms, directing the drug to the tumor site and increasing accumulation through enhanced permeability and retention effects.[ ]
By mimicking the self-assembly of biological macromolecules like collagen fibrils, the integration of enzyme catalysis and small molecule self-assembly (EISA) provides an efficient method to form nanofiber networks and create hydrogels under different conditions.[ , ] In one study, researchers developed hydrogels by linking PTX to a phosphorylated naphthalene Phe-Phe-Lys-Tyr precursor, producing a PTX-containing supramolecular hydrogel for chemotherapy.[ ] After treatment with alkaline phosphatase (ALP), the phosphate group was cleaved, resulting in the spontaneous formation of a semitransparent hydrogel. Notably, both the precursor naphthalene-phenylalanine-phenylalanine-lysine-phosphotyrosine (PTX NapFFKYp) and the hydrogel scaffold demonstrated significant anticancer activity, indicating that the drug’s potency remained intact.[ ]

## Selection of amino acid types
For extended applications like controlled drug release and intracellular imaging, the biological stability of peptides is often insufficient. This is primarily because peptides made of L-amino acids are easily degraded by various endogenous proteases. To address this challenge, Li et al[ ] substituted L-amino acids with D-enantiomers to create a D-peptide-based hydrogel. Interestingly, despite the natural resistance of D-peptide derivatives to proteolysis, the molecules were still able to form hydrogels in the presence of ALP. When further combined with PTX, the resulting hydrogel was biologically stable and capable of continuously releasing free PTX for up to one month.[ ]

## Circular peptide technology
Linear peptides offer a wide range of targeting moieties with various lengths, structures, and physicochemical properties. However, their conformational flexibility often limits their binding selectivity and affinity. Additionally, their inherent instability and low molecular weight lead to rapid kidney clearance. To overcome this limitation, linear peptides have been extensively modified into cyclic peptides.[ ] Compared to linear peptides, cyclic peptides exhibit significantly improved receptor affinity and selectivity.[ ] The synthesis of cyclic peptides is typically achieved through N- to C-terminus cyclization or fragment condensation techniques.
One example is Cyclo-GCGPep1, a cyclic peptide with a strong affinity for HER2 receptors, which was used to create PDCs with CPT. This conjugate showed potent anti-proliferative effects against HER2-positive cells, such as SK-BR-3 and NCI-N87, while preserving CPT’s pro-apoptotic and Topo I inhibitory functions. Moreover, the conjugate demonstrated enhanced targeting and penetration in tumor spheroid models compared to CPT alone.[ ]
Another case involves a dual-action covalent conjugate that combines a cyclic peptide derived from the αVβ3 integrin-targeting diketopiperazine (DKP) ligand with a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) antagonist.[ ] The peptide c (DKP-RGD) displayed high affinity for αVβ3 integrins and inhibited microtubule formation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). This conjugate also targeted VEGF receptors using peptidomimetic inhibitors, offering a distinct method from traditional antiangiogenic therapies. Both ligands maintained their bioactive conformations during kinetic studies, and in vitro experiments confirmed strong receptor binding.[ ]
Additionally, an NGR peptide was linked to DOX to form a PDC targeting the CD13 receptor on tumor cells. The inclusion of a cleavable glycine-phenylalanine-leucine-glycine (GFLG) linker enabled the selective release of DOX triggered by Cathepsin B, an enzyme overexpressed in the tumor tissue. In vivo studies showed that the conjugates were well-tolerated, with no notable hepatotoxicity or changes in body weight, and they exhibited selective, non-toxic effects compared to free DOX.[ ]

## PEG modification of PDC side chains
PEG modification of PDC side chains is a proven method to improve drug stability and targeting. By selecting the appropriate molecular weight of PEG, the half-life of PDC drugs can be extended, thereby improving their effectiveness.[ ] For instance, the short half-life of the hydroxy-methyl-3 (HM-3) peptide requires patients to undergo twice-daily injections, which is inconvenient. Increasing the drug’s half-life is crucial to ensure its efficacy until it reaches its target site. In one case, modifying HM-3 at the N-terminal with methoxy-poly (ethylene glycol)-aldehyde (mPEGAld) resulted in a 5.86-fold increase in half-life.[ ] Similarly, Bayer’s PEG-adrenomedullin (PEG-ADM) was used to treat acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), leading to improved respiratory function.[ ] Another study linked an E-selectin-binding peptide to SN38 using PEG, creating PEGylated peptide-targeted drugs. These PDCs effectively targeted both the tumor vascular system and the tumor itself, enhancing drug permeability and retention. The results demonstrated that PEG modification prolonged the survival of HCT116 tumor-bearing mice and inhibited B16-F10 lung tumor formation, showing better results than SN38 and irinotecan alone.[ ] This development introduces a promising approach to designing tumor vascular-targeted PDC nanomedicine, potentially applicable to other chemotherapeutic agents.
In another example, a cyclic peptide scaffold based on 4-aminoproline, characterized by secondary and tertiary amine functionalities and derivatized with PEGylated chains, was designed to selectively target αVβ3 integrins. This cyclotetrapeptide was covalently linked to the anti-angiogenic agent sunitinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that interacts with the VEGFR2 receptor. Experimental data revealed that this PEG-conjugated drug showed significant anti-angiogenic activity both in vitro and in mouse models, outperforming sunitinib alone.[ ]

## Marketed and Investigational Products: Expanding Applications and Enhanced Biological Content
PDC drugs have become a major focus in research, with numerous PDCs progressing to clinical trials globally [Table ]. One notable example is ANG1005, developed by Angiochem, which shows a significant advantage in crossing the blood–brain barrier by targeting the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP-1) receptor through the use of angiopep-2 (Ang2) as the targeting moiety. This PDC combines Ang2, PTX, and a succinyl linker, exhibiting strong antitumor activity in glioblastoma models and excellent tolerability in humans.
 ANG1005 achieved a 4.5-fold increase in brain uptake compared to PTX and demonstrated higher cytotoxicity across various cancer cell lines, including glioblastoma, lung cancer, and ovarian cancer. In a Phase I clinical trial involving 63 patients with recurrent malignant glioma, ANG1005 successfully delivered PTX across the blood-brain barrier, reaching therapeutic levels at the tumor site. While its toxicity profile was similar to that of PTX, the drug exhibited enhanced efficacy against recurrent glioblastoma. Phase II trials in patients with recurrent high-grade glioma and brain metastases from breast cancer also produced positive results, and ANG1005 is now in Phase III clinical trials, with results forthcoming.
Following the success of ANG1005, other molecules using similar approaches have been explored. For instance, Cybrexa Therapeutics developed CBX-12, which is currently in phase I/II trials.[ ] CBX-12 features a pH-sensitive targeting moiety linked to the topoisomerase inhibitor exatecan. This drug works by inhibiting topoisomerase, preventing the recovery of DNA supercoiling during replication, transcription, and chromatin remodeling.[ ] By targeting the acidic environment of tumors, CBX-12 integrates into cancer cell membranes and releases its cytotoxic payload via glutathione reduction. A phase I/II trial involving 112 patients with advanced or metastatic refractory solid tumors is underway to assess the safety, dose escalation, and efficacy of CBX-12 (NCT04902872).
Bicycle Therapeutics has developed a small, structurally constrained bicyclic peptide (1.5 kDa) made up of 9–20 amino acids with three cysteine residues. These cysteines allow the peptide to maintain a rigid conformation through small molecular linkers.[ ]
These examples highlight the growing importance of PDCs in treating challenging cancers, such as glioblastoma and metastatic solid tumors. By utilizing advancements in targeting moieties like Ang2 and pH-sensitive peptides, along with innovative linker technologies, PDCs are improving drug delivery and therapeutic effectiveness. While clinical results have been promising, ongoing trials are crucial for assessing long-term efficacy and safety across different patient populations. The increasing focus on PDCs suggests they could become a key component in the future of personalized cancer treatment.[ ]

## Challenges of PDCs
PDC drugs also face limitations, largely due to the characteristics of their peptide-based targets, which share common drawbacks with peptides themselves. One major issue is that PDCs have poor stability in circulation and are rapidly cleared from the body, leading to the premature release of cytotoxic drugs throughout the system. This challenge limits the selection of linkers and toxins available for use in PDCs. As a result, PDCs require more frequent administration compared to macromolecular drugs, contributing to the slower progress in PDC research. The half-life of hydrophilic peptides is determined by soluble enzymes in the body, particularly exopeptidases, which are key to the chemical stability of peptides in plasma and on cell membranes. Exopeptidases are categorized into aminopeptidases and carboxypeptidases, which target the N-terminal and C-terminal ends of peptides, respectively. Rapid renal clearance and short half-lives have hindered in vivo studies of peptides, and these factors also impact the druggability of peptide-based drugs. Kidney glomerular capillaries are about 8 nm, and peptides with molecular weights under 25 kDa are filtered through the glomerulus without being reabsorbed by the tubules. Moreover, while peptide-targeting moieties have low immunogenicity, their specificity is somewhat lower than that of mAbs, posing a challenge in selecting targeting peptides.[ ] Additionally, the synthesis of PDCs is technically complex and expensive. The conjugation process requires precise control to maintain consistent drug-to-peptide ratios and proper functionalization of both drug and peptide components. These complexities create challenges for large-scale production and make the development process economically challenging.[ ]

## Future Outlook and Prospects
Targeted therapy using anticancer drugs continues to be one of the most promising directions in cancer research.[ , ] Peptides, which are intermediate in size between macromolecular and small-molecule drugs, provide distinct advantages: they can effectively target peptide receptors that are overexpressed in tumors, and they are relatively easy to synthesize and modify structurally.[ ] The development of natural peptide ligand analogues has become a key area of interest in PDC research, as these analogues exhibit high affinity and specificity for target tissues, facilitate rapid internalization, and possess low immunogenicity.[ ] Technological advancements have addressed previous limitations of PDCs, such as their short half-lives and poor stability, through peptide modifications and improved formulations. These innovations have also made it easier to attach therapeutic agents to peptides efficiently. More traditional chemotherapeutic drugs are increasingly being incorporated into PDCs and are advancing through clinical trials. In January 2018, Pluvicto became the first PDC to receive FDA approval for clinical use, demonstrating the potential of PDCs in cancer therapy. Moreover, next-generation PDCs are currently being developed with smart linkers to ensure precise drug release, and with diverse non-radioactive payloads to improve safety profiles and broaden therapeutic applicability. As a result, peptide- and receptor-mediated drug delivery systems are likely to drive future innovations in cancer treatment.
