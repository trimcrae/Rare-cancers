## Abstract

MYC genomic amplification and pathway activation are associated with aggressive behavior and poor prognosis in osteosarcoma. However, a discordance exists between copy-number gains and transcriptional output from MYC in osteosarcoma, and defining these mechanisms is critical to understanding and intercepting persistent MYC signaling. In this study, we showed that cytoplasmic mRNA poly(A) sustains MYC activation in osteosarcoma. Multiomics profiling and single-cell transcriptomics identified TENT5A, a noncanonical RNA-binding poly(A) polymerase, as selectively upregulated in MYC-activated tumors and enriched in proliferative, stem-like populations. Biochemical and genetic evidence demonstrated that TENT5A directly bound MYC mRNA via its PAP/OAS1 domain, extended its poly(A) tail, and stabilized the transcript, thereby reinforcing MYC-driven stemness and chemoresistance. Gain- and loss-of-function assays, orthotopic xenografts, and patient-derived organoids confirmed that elevated TENT5A enhanced tumor-initiating capacity and reduced chemotherapy sensitivity. Pharmacologic inhibition of TENT5A disrupted MYC mRNA stabilization, shortened poly(A) tails, and reversed chemoresistance in preclinical models. These findings delineate a posttranscriptional RNA stabilization pathway that reconciles the disconnect between MYC genetic alterations and transcriptional activity and nominate the RNA-binding protein TENT5A as a therapeutically tractable target in osteosarcoma.

## Significance:
Enhanced MYC mRNA stability mediated by TENT5A-induced poly(A) elongation supports cancer stem cell maintenance in osteosarcoma, highlighting TENT5A inhibition as a potential strategy to block MYC activity in cancer.

## Introduction
Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents. Despite advances in multimodal therapy combining chemotherapy with surgical resection, long-term survival has remained stagnant for more than three decades ( ). Approximately 30% of patients develop recurrence or metastasis, and the 5-year survival rate drops below 20% in chemoresistant disease ( ). The pronounced clinical and molecular heterogeneity of osteosarcoma complicates treatment, underscoring the urgent need for precise, subtype-specific therapeutic strategies ( ). Therefore, identifying individualized therapeutic targets and developing rational treatment strategies remain crucial to overcoming chemotherapy resistance in osteosarcoma.
The oncogene MYC plays a central role across diverse cancers ( ), maintaining cancer stem cell (CSC)–associated phenotypes such as self-renewal, therapy resistance, and enhanced tumor initiation ( , ). CSCs drive tumor progression ( ), chemoresistance, and metastatic dissemination ( , ). Persistent MYC activation thus contributes to relapse and poor clinical outcomes. In osteosarcoma, previous studies, including our own, have linked MYC amplification (AMP) and elevated pathway activity with aggressive behavior and poor prognosis ( – ). However, copy-number gains do not consistently translate into transcriptional activation, suggesting that additional regulatory layers beyond DNA copy number sustain MYC activity ( , ). Defining these mechanisms is critical to understanding persistent MYC signaling in osteosarcoma.
Recent evidence highlights mRNA polyadenylation as a crucial posttranscriptional modification controlling transcript stability, nuclear export, and translation ( , ). Dysregulated poly(A)-tail dynamics can stabilize oncogenic transcripts and promote tumor progression ( ). In cancer, aberrant polyadenylation may extend transcript half-life and reinforce oncogene activity, providing a potential genome-to-transcriptome coupling mechanism for MYC. Noncanonical poly(A) polymerases, particularly the TENT5 family, mediate cytoplasmic polyadenylation and selectively stabilize target mRNAs in a tissue-dependent manner ( – ). Among them, TENT5A has been implicated in osteoblast differentiation and bone development ( , ), yet its role in malignancy, especially osteosarcoma, remains unclear ( ).
Given the oncogenic importance of MYC, direct pharmacologic inhibition has proven challenging due to its nuclear localization and intrinsically disordered structure ( – ). This limitation motivates indirect approaches, including interventions at posttranscriptional control points ( , ). In parallel, patient-derived organoids (PDO) have emerged as physiologically relevant models that recapitulate CSC plasticity, intratumoral heterogeneity, and drug response ( – ). Organoid-based lineage tracing and single-cell studies reveal dynamic transitions between CSC-like and differentiated states under microenvironmental cues ( ) and demonstrate MYC-dependent reprogramming of differentiated tumor cells into CSC-like states ( ). These systems, therefore, provide powerful preclinical platforms for evaluating therapies that target regulatory networks sustaining MYC-driven stemness.
In this study, we investigate whether TENT5A-mediated cytoplasmic polyadenylation constitutes a posttranscriptional axis that functionally couples MYC copy-number alterations to transcript stabilization and sustained CSC-associated programs in osteosarcoma. We hypothesize that TENT5A engages MYC mRNA via its PAP/OAS1 domain to extend poly(A) tails, thereby enhancing transcript stability and reinforcing stemness phenotypes. Through integrated multiomics and single-cell profiling, complemented by functional assays in cell lines, orthotopic xenografts, and PDOs, we delineate the contribution of TENT5A to MYC mRNA stability and assess whether pharmacologic interruption of this genome-to-transcriptome coupling mitigates chemoresistance.

## Materials and Methods

## Experimental model and study participant details
Our study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2021KY103). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (or their legal guardians when applicable) prior to inclusion and/or sample collection. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with recognized ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki.
This study includes two retrospective cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S1; ). The first cohort includes clinical features and chemotherapy response stratified by MYC status of a total of 43 patients with osteosarcoma, with written consent from Shanghai General Hospital, comprising 19 females and 24 males, aged between 10 and 66 years. All patients received standard-of-care methotrexate, doxorubicin (DOX), and cisplatin (MAP) neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by curative resection at Shanghai General Hospital according to institutional practice ( ). We therefore refer to the regimen throughout as “standard MAP neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus surgery,” consistent with the prior report. The second cohort includes clinical characteristics of eight patients with osteosarcoma, with written informed consents from Shanghai General Hospital, from whom PDX organoid (PDXO) models were derived. This includes MYC AMP status, age, primary lesion location, recurrence, metastasis, chemotherapy treatment, and post-chemotherapy disease progression, comprising four females and four males, aged between 13 and 30 years. This study also includes a patient with a giant cell tumor of bone and a patient with synovial sarcoma, with written consent from Shanghai General Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The inclusion criteria comprised patients with osteosarcoma who received standard treatment (surgical resection and chemotherapy). The exclusion criteria included patients with osteosarcoma who did not undergo standard treatment, those lost to follow-up, and patients whose treatment was discontinued for any reason or who voluntarily withdrew from treatment. Detailed information is provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S6. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with recognized ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki.
 The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data used in this study were derived from GSE152048 (n = 6) and GSE162454 (n = 7). All samples are primary osteosarcoma specimens with samples taken before and after chemotherapy. The samples were categorized into MYC AMP and MYC wild-type (WT) based on their MYC condition ( ).
The specimens of the Shanghai General Hospital Osteosarcoma (SGH-OS) cohort in this study (n = 77) were extended from our previous study (GSA: HRA003260, OMIX002042), including clinical information, copy-number array data for genomic copy-number variation, and bulk RNA-seq data for transcriptomics. The tissue microarrays (TMA; this study) were created using tumor-containing paraffin blocks selected based on the pathologist’s observation of matched tissue. The two TMAs were extended from our previous study ( , , ). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Pathologic diagnoses of all the patients with osteosarcoma admitted to Shanghai General Hospital were independently examined by three pathologists. Clinical information and gene expression related to patient survival (GSE42352) for survival analysis were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with recognized ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Animal experiments
All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines provided by the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanghai General Hospital. The Clinical Center Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, approved all animal study protocols used in this study (IACUC: 2023AW067). Female nude mice (strain: BALB/cJGpt-foxn1nu/Gpt; Charles River Laboratories; RRID: IMSR_GPT: D000521), aged 6 to 8 weeks, were used throughout the study.
All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, maintained in a controlled environment at 20°C to 22°C, with a 12-hour light–dark cycle and humidity ranging from 50% to 70%. For the establishment of an orthotopic tibial osteosarcoma xenograft model, osteosarcoma cells suspended in 25 μL PBS containing 10% FBS were injected into the proximal tibial marrow cavity using a 27-gauge needle, following procedures described in our previous study ( ). Tumor growth was monitored by caliper measurements, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = Length × Width2/2. The tumor burden was assessed based on maximum tumor burden: 2,000 mm3; mice were randomized when tumors reached 80 to 120 mm3; treating personnel were blinded to group assignments, as approved by the Clinical Center Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. The maximal tumor size was confirmed to not be exceeded in our study, and the survival curves were produced according to the survival of mice and the time to reach maximum tumor size. Tumor samples were collected from the central region of the resected masses. Humane endpoints included ulceration, >15% body weight loss, impaired mobility, euthanasia by CO2 followed by cervical dislocation, and isoflurane used for painful procedures.
To evaluate the effect of TENT5A expression on the efficacy of DOX treatment in vivo, the osteosarcoma mouse xenograft was developed by intramedullary injection of osteosarcoma cells (5 × 105 cells in 25 μL PBS supplemented with 10% FBS) into the marrow space of the proximal tibia of nude mice with a 27-gauge needle, as in our previous studies ( ). Tumor volumes were measured prior to randomization and during treatment to monitor disease progression and therapeutic response. Treatment was as follows; DOX, 5 mg/kg intraperitoneally, twice weekly; Ro-3306, 5 mg/kg intraperitoneally, twice weekly; vehicle (0.3% DMSO). To evaluate the effect of TENT5A overexpression and MYC knockdown in vivo, as well as Ro-3306 and DOX combined treatment, the subcutaneous tumor-bearing mouse model was established by injecting osteosarcoma cells (5 × 105 cells in 25 μL PBS supplemented with 10% FBS) into the lateral abdominal region of nude mice using a 27-gauge needle. Tumor volumes were measured prior to randomization and during treatment to monitor disease progression and therapeutic response.
To perform an in vivo limiting dilution assay, the osteosarcoma mouse xenograft was developed by intramedullary injection with osteosarcoma cells (1 × 105, 1 × 106, and 1 × 107 cells in 25 μL PBS supplemented with 10% FBS) into the marrow space of the proximal tibia of nude mice with a 27-gauge needle. The mice were evaluated by monitoring tumor volume to quantify osteosarcoma burden.

## Construction and cultivation of organoid models
The tissue of tumor or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models was digested into single cells, and self-assembly was performed to construct organoid models. The culture methods from our previous research were applied for cultivation and drug treatment, and cell viability assays were performed after about 7 days. For organoid handling, we used a storage solution consisting of a buffered salt base supplemented with low serum, antibiotics, and HEPES; aliquoted into sterile tubes; and kept at 4°C. A washing solution of PBS with antibiotics was prepared fresh before use. For dissociation, we prepared an RPMI-based enzyme mix containing collagenase II and DNase I with a small amount of serum and antibiotics, made immediately prior to use to preserve activity; dissociation was quenched with a DMEM/F12-based stop solution containing serum and antibiotics. Organoids were maintained in a DMEM-based culture medium formulated with antibiotics, serum, glutamine supplement, N-2, B-27, nonessential amino acids, insulin–transferrin–selenium, ascorbic acid, a Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor, and a standard growth factor cocktail (e.g., EGF, bFGF, IGF1, a TGFβ family member, and a Wnt ligand), with small-molecule pathway modulators as appropriate; media were sterile-filtered and stored at 4°C for short-term use. For cryopreservation, a serum-free freezing medium supplemented with a ROCK inhibitor was prepared immediately before freezing to maximize post-thaw viability. All solutions were prepared under standardized procedures to ensure consistency and reproducibility. The specific construction and cultivation methods can be referenced from our earlier studies (bioRxiv 2024.08.11.607471).

## Preparation and analysis of bulk RNA-seq
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The integrity and purity of RNA were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA purity of specimens was checked using the kaiaoK5500 Spectrophotometer (Kaiao). RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies). A total amount of 2 μg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy mRNA Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, barcoded, and converted to DNA nanoballs for sequencing. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot cluster generation system using the HiSeq PE Cluster Kit v4-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (RRID:SCR_017981, BGI) in Wuhan Benagen Technology Co., Ltd., and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.
The raw data were first processed with FastQC to filter out adapters and low-quality sequences. Paired-end reads were aligned to the human GRCh38 genome or mouse mm10 genome using STAR (RRID:SCR_004463, version 2.7.6a; ref. ). Reads with good mapping quality (> 30) that aligned to genomic exons were counted using featureCounts (GRCh38 or mm10 Ensembl 93; ref. ) to generate a table with counts for each gene. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2 (RRID:SCR_015687; ref. ) with the IfcShrink function. Genes with a fold change ≥2 and false discovery rate (FDR) P value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO; http://geneontology.org/, RRID:SCR_002811) enrichment analyses for differentially expressed genes were performed using the R package clusterProfiler version 3.8 ( ). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA, RRID:SCR_003199; http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/; ref. ) was performed on a list of genes ranked from high to low DESeq2 estimated fold change using the GSEAPreRanked function with the enrichment statistic classic, 1,000 permutations, and a normalized P value < 0.05. The gene set variation analysis (GSVA) scores for each sample were calculated using the GSVA function (RRID:SCR_021058). The resulting GSVA scores represent the relative enrichment of the gene sets in each sample. These scores were used in downstream analyses to identify pathways that are differentially activated or suppressed under different conditions. Related gene sets were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/, RRID:SCR_016863; refs. , ). The expression data were preprocessed and normalized before performing GSVA. The calcPhenotype function (RRID:SCR_023872) was used to fit the GSDC2 database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) with the RNA-seq data in the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments Osteosarcoma (TARGET-OS) cohort to obtain a drug response prediction matrix.

## Preparation and analysis of Tail Iso-seq
We performed Oxford Nanopore long-read, isoform-resolved transcriptome sequencing with signal-based poly(A) tail estimation (Tail Iso-seq; refs. – ).
Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and purity were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry (NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Input for library construction was 1 μg total RNA per sample unless otherwise stated. Where indicated by the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) protocol, poly(A)+-enriched or ribo-depleted RNA may also be used [10 ng poly(A)+ RNA or 500 ng total RNA per sample]. All workspaces and tools were prepared for RNase-free handling prior to setup. Libraries were prepared using the ONT PCR-cDNA workflow with the cDNA RT Adapter (CRTA), which is a double-stranded adapter carrying a poly(T) overhang that anneals to the very end of the RNA poly(A) tail. This design promotes full-length reverse transcription and enables accurate poly(A) length estimation in downstream signal-based analysis. For each sample, RNA was combined with CRTA (1 μL) and annealing buffer (1 μL; total 12 μL), incubated at 60°C for 5 minutes, and cooled to room temperature. Ligation was then performed by adding NEBNext Quick Ligation Reaction Buffer (3.6 μL), T4 DNA ligase (1.4 μL), and RNaseOUT (1 μL) to a final volume of 18 μL, mixing thoroughly, and incubating for 10 minutes at room temperature. To the ligation reaction, we added Lambda exonuclease (1 μL) and USER enzyme (1 μL; total 20 μL) and incubated for 5 minutes at 37°C to process the CRTA bottom strand and enable priming. Reactions were cleaned with RNAClean XP beads (36 μL per reaction) and washed twice with Short Fragment Buffer, followed by elution in 12 μL of nuclease-free water. The eluate was then mixed with reverse transcription (RT) primer (1 μL) and 10 mmol/L dNTPs (1 μL; 14 μL total) and incubated for 5 minutes at room temperature to allow priming upstream of the poly(A) tail. Strand-switching reverse transcription was set up by adding Maxima H Minus 5× RT buffer (4.5 μL), RNaseOUT (1 μL), and Strand-Switching Primer II (2 μL; total 21.5 μL); mixed; and equilibrated for 2 minutes at 42°C, followed by the addition of Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (1 μL; final 22.5 μL). cDNA synthesis and strand switching proceeded at 42°C for 30 minutes, with enzyme inactivation at 85°C for 5 minutes. Reactions were held at 4°C or taken forward immediately. Full-length cDNA was selectively amplified with LongAmp Hot Start Taq 2× Master Mix and Barcode Primers (BP01–BP24) when multiplexing. Per-sample PCRs (25 μL) contained 5 μL of reverse-transcribed sample and a unique barcode primer; cycling used 95°C for 30 seconds, then 10 to 18 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds, 62°C for 15 seconds, and 65°C for 60 seconds per kb, followed by 65°C for 6 minutes and a 4°C hold (14 cycles are recommended as a starting point and can be adjusted). Residual primers were removed with Thermolabile Exonuclease I (1 μL; 37°C for 5 minutes; 80°C for 2 minutes). Amplicons were purified with AMPure XP beads (18 μL), washed twice with 70% ethanol, and eluted in 12 μL of elution buffer. Library quantity/size was assessed by Qubit and bioanalyzer (or equivalent) for quality checks. Equimolar pooling of barcoded samples was performed to ∼50 fmol total in 31 μL of elution buffer prior to adapter addition. Sequencing adapters were added by diluting the Rapid Adapter (RA) with Adapter Buffer (ADB; 1.5 μL RA + 3.5 μL ADB), then adding 1 μL of the diluted RA to 32 μL of pooled library and incubating for 5 minutes at room temperature. PromethION flow cells were primed with Flow Cell Tether (30 μL) and Flow Cell Flush (1,170 μL). Immediately before loading, the library was mixed as Sequencing Buffer (100 μL) + Library Beads (68 μL) + DNA library (32 μL) and 200 μL were loaded to the inlet port after priming. Runs were started in MinKNOW according to ONT guidance. Sequencing was performed on an ONT PromethION instrument for 48 to 72 hours, with standard ONT device checks and run monitoring.
The raw sequencing data were base-called using Guppy software (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) and quality-controlled using NanoPlot software (Wick and colleagues, 2018). The cDNA reads were aligned to the reference genome using minimap2 software (https://github.com/lh3/minimap2; ref. ). The transcript isoforms were identified and quantified using FLAIR software ( ). The alternative splicing events were detected and classified using SUPPA2 software (https://github.com/comprna/SUPPA/; ref. ). The poly(A) tail lengths were measured using tailfindr (https://github.com/adnaniazi/tailfindr; ref. ). The novel transcripts were annotated using gffcompare (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/gffcompare.shtml) and BLAST software (Altschul and colleagues, 1990). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2 (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html) with the IfcShrink function. Genes with fold change ≥2, probability ≥0.80, and FDR P value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. GO (http://geneontology.org/) enrichment analyses for differentially expressed genes were performed using the R package clusterProfiler version 3.8 (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html; ref. ). GSEA (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/; ref. ) was performed on a list of genes ranked from high to low DESeq2 estimated fold change using the GSEAPreRanked function with enrichment statistic classic; 1,000 permutations; and normalized P value < 0.05. ## Nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction
Subcellular fractionation of cultured cells into cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions was performed using the Thermo Scientific NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #78833) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with the following steps. Adherent cells were detached with trypsin-EDTA, collected by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 minutes at 4°C, and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Cells were counted, and approximately 1 to 2 × 106 cells (corresponding to ∼20 μL packed cell volume) were transferred into a prechilled 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and pelleted at 500 g for 2 to 3 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was removed completely. The dry cell pellet was resuspended in 200 μL ice-cold Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent I supplemented with a 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Tubes were vortexed at maximum speed for 15 seconds and incubated on ice for 10 minutes. Next, 11 μL of ice-cold Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent II was added; the tube was vortexed for 5 seconds, incubated on ice for 1 minute, and then vortexed again for 5 seconds. Samples were centrifuged at 16,000 g for 5 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant (cytoplasmic fraction) was carefully transferred to a new prechilled tube and kept on ice. The remaining pellet (containing nuclei) was gently resuspended in 100 μL of ice-cold Nuclear Extraction Reagent containing a 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Samples were vortexed for 15 seconds and then incubated on ice for 40 minutes with brief vortexing (15 seconds) every 10 minutes. Following incubation, tubes were centrifuged at 16,000 g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant (nuclear fraction) was collected into a fresh prechilled tube. Both cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts were stored at –80°C until further use.

## Histone extraction
Histones were extracted from cultured osteosarcoma cells using the Abcam histone extraction kit (ab113476) with the following streamlined procedure: Adherent cells (∼1 × 107) were harvested by trypsinization, washed once in cold PBS, and pelleted (1,000 g, 5 minutes, 4°C). Pellets were resuspended in Pre-Lysis Buffer (kit) at 1 × 107 cells/mL, incubated on ice for 10 minutes with occasional mixing, and then spun (3,000 g, 5 minutes, 4°C). The supernatant was discarded. Pellets were lysed in Acid Extraction Buffer (200 μL per 1 × 107 cells) on ice for 30 minutes, vortexing briefly every 10 minutes. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 minutes (4°C), and the acid-soluble supernatant containing histones was collected. Supernatants were neutralized by adding Balance-DTT Buffer (0.3× volume); then, protein concentration was measured against BSA standards. Histone extracts were aliquoted and stored at –80°C until use.

## Protein–mRNA docking
RNA sequences were derived from the NCBI database. Protein sequences were derived from the UniProt database (RRID:SCR_002380). Secondary structure was predicted with RNAfold (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi, RRID:SCR_024427) to obtain the minimum free-energy structure. Each secondary structure was converted to a 3D model with RNAComposer (http://rnacomposer.ibch.poznan.pl/, RRID:SCR_026636). The TENT5A protein structure was predicted with AlphaFold3 (RRID:SCR_025454). Regions with Predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) ≥70 were prioritized. Relative placement confidence was evaluated using the predicted aligned error (PAE) matrix. Where necessary, poorly resolved loops were trimmed or treated as flexible without altering residues in the catalytic PAP/OAS1/NTase core. Protein–RNA docking was performed under identical parameters and fixed random seeds across all targets and controls. We report PAE/pLDDT maps, interface contact residues, and protein surface electrostatics for visualization. For each RNA, docking results are summarized as median score ± IQR across repeats; figures display a single representative complex for illustration only. In this study, docking is used solely to propose a geometrically feasible interface model that is consistent with functional data; it is not used to claim sequence motif recognition nor to serve as stand-alone evidence of direct substrate selectivity.

## Results

## Biological pathways linking MYC genomic alteration to transcriptional activation in osteosarcoma
To investigate upstream mechanisms driving MYC activation in osteosarcoma, we employed an integrative workflow incorporating clinical cohort profiling, single-cell transcriptomics, genomic and transcriptomic analyses, TMAs, and functional validation ( ). The SGH-OS cohort included 43 patients who all received standardized preoperative MAP (high dose) chemotherapy, with tumor specimens collected before surgery ( ). MYC genomic AMP was detected in 57.7% of cases and was significantly associated with unfavorable outcomes, including higher rates of recurrence and metastasis.
 Patients were further stratified by next-generation sequencing and bulk RNA-seq into MYC gain (n = 20) versus MYC WT (n = 23) and independently into MYC-activated (n = 14) versus MYC-unactivated (n = 29) groups. Comprehensive clinical variables (age, sex, lesion site, recurrence, metastasis), homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) score, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and alterations in cancer-relevant loci (TP53, RB1, BRCA2, NSD1, MDM2, CDKN2A/B, CCNE1) were integrated to evaluate potential confounders (Supplementary Fig. S1A; Supplementary Table S1). Baseline characteristics and treatment regimens were comparable across MYC-defined groups (Supplementary Table S1). Among genomic features, only TP53 deletion showed a modest yet significant association with poor chemotherapy response (P < 0.05), whereas other alterations and HRD/TMB were not associated (Supplementary Fig. S1B). By contrast, MYC gain and pathway activation were robustly linked to inferior radiographic responses by RECIST version 1.1 and MRI. Specifically, MYC gain and MYC-activated patients more frequently experienced disease progression [e.g., patient P2; seven progressive diseases, five stable disease (SD)], whereas MYC WT and MYC-unactivated patients more often exhibited lesion shrinkage (e.g., patient P40; 15 SD, 6 partial responses; ; Supplementary Fig. S1C). These observations support the hypothesis that MYC pathway activation is a key determinant of reduced chemotherapy responsiveness in MYC-gain osteosarcoma.
To define molecular programs accompanying MYC activation, we integrated genomic and transcriptomic data from the TARGET-OS and SGH-OS datasets. GSEA consistently revealed significant enrichment of RNA metabolism pathways—particularly mRNA polyadenylation and 3′-end processing—in tumors with MYC AMP or elevated MYC transcriptional activity ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Single-sample GSEA and GSVA confirmed these associations across MYC-defined subgroups in both cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S2B and S2C). Correlation network analysis further demonstrated coordinated transcriptional regulation among RNA-processing genes within these enriched pathways ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2D). To pinpoint candidate upstream regulators linking MYC activation to polyadenylation, we correlated MYC activation signatures with gene-level expression of curated polyadenylation genes. TENT5A emerged as the top candidate, displaying strong, consistent positive correlations with MYC target gene sets (MYC_TARGETS_V1/V2) and multiple RNA-processing pathways in both TARGET-OS and SGH-OS ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S3A–S3D). TENT5A expression was markedly higher in osteosarcoma compared with other soft-tissue sarcomas (Supplementary Fig. S3E), and high TENT5A expression was significantly associated with poor overall survival in the TARGET-OS cohort (Supplementary Fig. S3F). Collectively, these findings identify enhanced mRNA polyadenylation as a defining transcriptomic hallmark of MYC-activated osteosarcoma and highlight TENT5A as a putative regulator coupling poly(A) dynamics to MYC-driven transcriptional activation and adverse clinical outcomes.

## mRNA polyadenylation–mediated MYC activation associates with and sustains stemness in proliferative osteosarcoma cells
Given the enrichment of mRNA polyadenylation signatures in MYC-activated osteosarcoma, we asked whether these features localize to tumor cell states with distinct functions. We analyzed scRNA-seq datasets (n = 13) from the GSE152048 ( ) and GSE162454 ( ) clinical cohorts, encompassing primary osteosarcoma specimens collected before and after chemotherapy. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction resolved the tumor microenvironment into eight lineages—osteoblasts, chondrocytes, fibroblasts, T/NK cells, monocytes, macrophages, osteoclasts, and endothelial cells—stratified by treatment sensitivity and lineage identity ( and ). Within the osteoblastic lineage, subclustering revealed transcriptionally distinct groups. Two subclusters (O2 and O3) displayed high MKI67/PCNA expression, consistent with enhanced proliferation ( ). Trajectory inference placed O2 and O3 at the proliferative terminus but with divergent programs: O2 corresponded to a proliferating, stem-like osteoblast population, whereas O3 represented proliferating nonstem osteoblasts ( ). Along pseudotime, MYC activation scores were highest in O2, suggesting that MYC signaling is preferentially sustained in the stem-like proliferative state ( ). Consistent with this, both MYC activation scores (MYC, E2F1, CCND1, CDC25A, CDKN1A) and stem-like proliferation scores (SOX2, NANOG, CD44, ALDH1A1, BMI1, MYC, E2F1, LIN28B, MKI67, PROM1) showed positive correlations with poly(A) signature scores derived from curated mRNA polyadenylation genes (TENT5A, SRSF3, SRSF1, PEX2, NDUFB6, MRPL13, HNRNPK, ETF1, ELAVL1, EFTUD1, RIOX1, BUD23; see ; and ). TENT5A expression increased along the same trajectory and closely tracked the poly(A) score, implicating TENT5A as a candidate driver of this transcriptional state.
 We next evaluated TENT5A–MYC coexpression in clinical material using TMAs from our prior cohort ( ). IHC revealed coelevated TENT5A and MYC expression in tumors from metastatic or poor-outcome patients, with a significant positive correlation between their H-scores ( ; Supplementary Fig. S4A; Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). An independent retrospective cohort of 37 patients with osteosarcoma treated with standard therapy corroborated these findings (Supplementary Fig. S4B; Supplementary Tables S4 and S5; ref. ). IHC confirmed significantly higher TENT5A and MYC levels in metastatic compared with nonmetastatic tumors (Supplementary Fig. S4C and S4D). Quantitative analysis demonstrated elevated TENT5A protein abundance in metastatic cases (Supplementary Fig. S4E), and logistic regression identified high TENT5A expression as an independent predictor of metastasis (OR = 6.04, P = 0.0152; Supplementary Fig. S4F). Together, these data indicate that mRNA polyadenylation—particularly mediated by TENT5A—is linked to MYC activation and aligns with a proliferative, stem-like osteoblastic state, supporting a model in which poly(A)-dependent regulation helps sustain stemness programs in osteosarcoma.

## TENT5A-mediated cytoplasmic polyadenylation stabilizes MYC mRNA in osteosarcoma
Given the association between TENT5A expression, MYC activity, and stem-like transcriptional programs, we asked whether TENT5A directly regulates MYC posttranscriptionally. We hypothesized that TENT5A-mediated cytoplasmic polyadenylation stabilizes MYC transcripts. To assess paralog conservation, AlphaFold3 structural predictions ( ) revealed conserved NTase and PAP/OAS1 domains across all four TENT5 paralogs (TENT5A–D), consistent with prior studies (Supplementary Fig. S5A; ref. ). However, functional silencing of TENT5B, TENT5C, or TENT5D did not significantly alter MYC expression (Supplementary Fig. S5B). In both SGH-OS and TARGET-OS datasets, only TENT5A showed consistent positive correlations with MYC_TARGETS_V1/V2 GSVA scores (Supplementary Fig. S5C). In cell-based assays, OE TENT5A increased MYC protein levels, whereas knockdown reduced them (Supplementary Fig. S6A), confirming a specific role for TENT5A in MYC regulation. The cell proliferation rate was accelerated following OE TENT5A (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Functionally, OE TENT5A reduced DOX sensitivity, whereas TENT5A depletion enhanced DOX-induced cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. S6C). Colony-formation and EdU assays corroborated these findings, demonstrating increased proliferation and survival under both basal and chemotherapeutic conditions (Supplementary Fig. S6D–S6F). Unlike TENT5C, which is reported to mitigate endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in myeloma ( ), OE TENT5A in osteosarcoma modestly elevated canonical ER stress markers (IRE1, XBP1, PERK, ATF6, CHOP) without triggering apoptosis, suggesting adaptive ER stress reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. S6G and S6H).
We next explored the underlying mechanism. Across six osteosarcoma cell lines (U2OS, SJSA, Saos-2, HOS, MG63, and 143B), MYC protein levels did not consistently track genomic copy number or mRNA abundance, and transcriptional activity varied independently, supporting posttranscriptional regulation. PCR-based poly(A) tail (PAT) assays revealed heterogeneous MYC tail lengths ( ). Transcriptome-wide Tail Iso-seq of patient samples further demonstrated right-shifted poly(A) length distributions and higher 3′-UTR mapping in MYC-activated cases, with significantly longer MYC tails than in MYC-unactivated cases ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7A). In cell models, enforced TENT5A expression induced widespread tail elongation, with MYC among significantly lengthened transcripts versus vector ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7A). RNA-FISH showed increased MYC mRNA signal with OE TENT5A and decreased signal with knockdown ( ). PAT assays corroborated these results, and RNase H digestion following oligo(dT) hybridization confirmed that the observed size shifts reflected genuine poly(A) tails (Supplementary Fig. S7B–S7D).
 To test whether TENT5A acts posttranscriptionally, we inhibited transcription with actinomycin D. In TENT5A-overexpressing cells, MYC poly(A) tails remained extended, whereas control cells exhibited rapid tail loss (Supplementary Fig. S7E). Actinomycin D chases with RT-qPCR further showed a prolonged MYC mRNA half-life upon OE TENT5A and accelerated decay upon knockdown ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7F). Subcellular fractionation localized this activity to the cytoplasm, in which MYC tails were selectively preserved in TENT5A-overexpressing cells; in controls, cytoplasmic MYC mRNA underwent progressive deadenylation and degradation ( ). IF/RNA-FISH revealed cytoplasmic colocalization of Flag–TENT5A with MYC mRNA ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7G), and RIP-PCR validated direct binding of TENT5A to MYC mRNA ( ). To assess MYC dependency, rescue experiments demonstrated that MYC knockdown abrogated TENT5A-induced MYC upregulation without affecting TENT5A mRNA levels, supporting unidirectional regulation. Furthermore, because MYC cooperates with RUNX2 in osteosarcoma proliferation and chemoresistance ( , ), we tested whether TENT5A influenced RUNX2 indirectly via MYC. Indeed, OE TENT5A increased RUNX2, but this effect was abolished by MYC knockdown ( ; Supplementary Fig. S7H and S7I).
Finally, we used AlphaFold3-guided docking as a reference to inform mutagenesis, not as definitive structural proof. Using COL1A1/2 mRNAs as positive and U7 snRNA as negative controls, the modeling suggested a putative RNA engagement surface within the second PAP/OAS1 domain, with V367 as a candidate contact residue (Supplementary Fig. S7J). Although these predictions provide a structural reference, they remain speculative and primarily serve to guide our subsequent mutagenesis experiments. To validate, we generated three TENT5A mutants: Δ37–111, Δ308–392, and V367S. All were stably expressed (Supplementary Fig. S7K). RIP-PCR revealed that the Δ308–392 and V367S mutants exhibited markedly reduced MYC mRNA binding compared with WT and Δ37–111 ( ), and IF/RNA-FISH confirmed diminished colocalization ( ). Taken together, these data support a model in which TENT5A stabilizes MYC transcripts via cytoplasmic polyadenylation through a defined RNA engagement surface within the PAP/OAS1 domain. This posttranscriptional mechanism provides a functional genome-to-transcriptome coupling that sustains MYC expression and contributes to proliferative, chemoresistant phenotypes in osteosarcoma.

## TENT5A promotes MYC-dependent tumor stemness with increased tumor-initiating capacity in osteosarcoma
To define the functional consequences of TENT5A-mediated stabilization of MYC, we profiled transcriptomic changes and stemness-associated phenotypes following OE TENT5A. GSEA and GSVA revealed enrichment of MYC_TARGETS_V1/V2 together with Hallmark pathways related to cell-cycle progression, mRNA polyadenylation, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, and stemness-associated signaling ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S8A–S8C). Among these, MYC_TARGETS_V1/V2 were the most strongly upregulated signatures, reinforcing the functional link between TENT5A and MYC pathway activity. ECM pathways—including collagen biosynthesis, remodeling, and matrix organization—were also enriched (Supplementary Fig. S8D), consistent with prior findings that TENT5A stabilizes collagen transcripts in osteoblasts ( ), indicating TENT5A may promote tumor invasion and metastasis through its role in ECM remodeling. In osteosarcoma, however, our data suggest a context-dependent shift in substrate preference, with TENT5A engaging oncogenic targets such as MYC rather than differentiation-associated RNAs. At the phenotypic level, OE TENT5A increased canonical stemness markers (SOX2, OCT4, ALDH1A1), whereas TENT5A knockdown reduced their expression ( ; Supplementary Fig. S8E). Tumor-sphere assays under basal conditions and following DOX exposure showed that TENT5A maintains stem-like states under chemotherapeutic stress ( ; Supplementary Fig. S8F–S8H). Importantly, MYC knockdown reversed TENT5A-induced upregulation of stemness markers ( ), indicating MYC dependence. To test whether TENT5A enhances MYC-driven transcription of stemness genes, we first identified MYC-binding motifs within the SOX2 and ALDH1A1 promoters using JASPAR, with AlphaFold3-based models serving as structural hypotheses ( ). CUT&Tag assays then demonstrated increased MYC occupancy at these promoters in TENT5A-overexpressing cells ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S9A). Multiplex IF further demonstrated coexpression and spatial colocalization of MYC, SOX2, and ALDH1A1, supporting direct MYC-mediated regulation of stemness genes (Supplementary Fig. S9B).
 We next assessed in vivo relevance using PDXs and orthotopic tibial implantation models. OE TENT5A promoted tumor growth and increased SOX2, OCT4, and ALDH1A1 expression in both settings (Supplementary Fig. S10A–S10D, S10F, and S10G). In orthotopic tumors, DOX efficacy was diminished in the TENT5A-overexpressing group (Supplementary Fig. S10F–S10J). Histology and IHC confirmed elevated proliferation and stemness marker expression (Supplementary Fig. S10E, S10K, and S10L). To directly assess tumor stemness in vivo, limiting dilution assays were performed by transplanting varying cell numbers (107, 106, or 105) into tibial xenografts. OE TENT5A significantly increased tumor formation frequency and estimated CSC frequency compared with controls ( and ) and was associated with a greater lung metastasis burden, particularly at lower inoculation doses ( ). These tumors consistently exhibited high MYC expression along with increased PCNA, MKI67, SOX2, ALDH1A1, and OCT4 ( ). Histopathologic analysis revealed no overt toxicity in major organs (Supplementary Fig. S11A). Finally, to establish MYC dependency in vivo, we generated subcutaneous xenografts combining OE TENT5A with MYC knockdown. MYC depletion abrogated TENT5A-driven tumor promotion and reversed the upregulation of proliferation and stemness markers (Ki67, ALDH1A1, SOX2, RUNX2; ; Supplementary Fig. S11B). Collectively, these data demonstrate that TENT5A promotes MYC-dependent stemness, enhancing tumor initiation, chemoresistance, and metastatic potential in osteosarcoma.
 

## TENT5A inhibition reverses MYC-driven chemoresistance in osteosarcoma
Given the role of TENT5A in stabilizing MYC mRNA and the difficulty of directly targeting MYC, we next sought pharmacologic strategies to inhibit TENT5A and overcome MYC-driven chemoresistance. Cross-dataset drug-response analyses (GDSC2, CCLE, TARGET-OS) identified Ro-3306, a CDK1 inhibitor ( ), as a promising candidate compound ( and ). Experimentally, Ro-3306 reduced viability at low micromolar concentrations across multiple osteosarcoma cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S12A–S12C). To determine whether this effect was specific to Ro-3306 or generalizable to CDK1 inhibition, we tested additional structurally distinct CDK1 inhibitors (CDK1-IN-2, avotaciclib, Butyrolactone I). Only Ro-3306 consistently decreased TENT5A and MYC protein levels ( ; Supplementary Fig. S13A). Compared with MYC-targeting agents (BRD4 inhibitor JQ1; CDK9 inhibitors AZD4573 and atuveciclib), Ro-3306 uniquely shortened the MYC poly(A) tail, indicating selective disruption of TENT5A-mediated stabilization (Supplementary Fig. S13B and S13C). Cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) supported target engagement of Ro-3306 with TENT5A (Supplementary Fig. S13D), and partial rescue by MG-132 implicated proteasome-mediated degradation of TENT5A (Supplementary Fig. S13E). Mechanistically, Ro-3306 reduced MYC mRNA stability, as shown by decreased transcript levels, accelerated RNA decay, and shortened poly(A) tails. Ro-3306 treatment led to reduced MYC and TENT5A expression, along with downregulation of cell-cycle proteins (CCND1, CCNE1, CCNB1) and CDK1. OE TENT5A partially rescued MYC protein levels, indicating that Ro-3306 inhibits MYC stability through TENT5A ( ; Supplementary Fig. S14A and S14B). Overexpression of TENT5A rescued the migration and proliferation inhibition caused by Ro-3306 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S14C). AlphaFold3 modeling provided structural hypotheses for a potential interaction surface within the NTase regulatory domain of TENT5A ( ), serving as a supportive reference for these findings.
 Transcriptomic profiling revealed that Ro-3306 reversed TENT5A-induced programs. PCA and GSVA showed transcriptomic shifts opposite to those seen with OE TENT5A, particularly affecting MYC-linked and stemness pathways ( ; Supplementary Fig. S14D). Pathways associated with RNA stability, chemoresistance, and ECM remodeling were consistently downregulated (Supplementary Fig. S14E). Integration of Tail Iso-seq and RNA-seq identified 652 overlapping genes with both shortened poly(A) tails and decreased expression among 4,695 differentially expressed transcripts and 1,523 poly(A)-shortened transcripts ( ). GSEA on the Hallmark gene sets, ranking by poly(A) tail change, demonstrated strong negative enrichment for MYC_TARGETS_V1 [normalized enrichment score (NES) = −2.60, FDR < 0.001] and MYC_TARGETS_V2 (NES = −2.74, FDR < 0.001), with scatterplots of log2 fold changes in mRNA expression and poly(A) length indicating that MYC-related Hallmark pathways are significantly inhibited ( and ). A further 5,347 transcripts exhibited expression changes without tail alteration, consistent with indirect effects downstream of MYC ( ). Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) GSEA of this subset revealed negative enrichment for proliferation-related terms, including positive regulation of stem cell proliferation, stem cell proliferation, and mesenchymal cell proliferation ( ). Additional GSEA showed that Ro-3306 suppressed bone and mesenchymal developmental programs (Supplementary Fig. S14F) and downregulated Wnt and Notch signaling (Supplementary Fig. S14G), two pathways critical for stem cell maintenance. Epigenetic regulator pathways were also enriched, in line with a dose-dependent reduction in both activating (H3K9ac, H3K27ac) and repressive (H3K27me2, H3K27me3) histone marks after treatment (Supplementary Fig. S14H and S14I). These changes indicate global chromatin remodeling antagonistic to osteosarcoma stemness.
Functionally, Ro-3306 decreased ALDH1A1, OCT4, and SOX2 expression and impaired tumorsphere formation in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. S15A and S15B). TENT5A knockdown phenocopied these effects, whereas OE TENT5A partially mitigated them (Supplementary Fig. S15C–S15E). Combination therapy with Ro-3306 and DOX showed strong synergy (CI < 1) in reducing viability, colony formation, EdU incorporation, and spheroid growth (Supplementary Fig. S15F–S15I). Enhanced cell death and reduced sphere formation were especially evident in TENT5A-overexpressing cells (Supplementary Fig. S16A–S16D). Consistently, the combination diminished MYC and stemness markers (ALDH1A1, SOX2, OCT4) and impaired proliferation/self-renewal (Supplementary Fig. S17A–S17C). These pharmacodynamic effects were influenced by TENT5A status. Ro-3306 monotherapy had greater inhibitory effects in TENT5A-overexpressing cells compared with TENT5A-low controls. By contrast, Ro-3306 plus DOX yielded comparable suppression in both contexts, such that residual expression levels converged between TENT5A-high and TENT5A-low groups (normalized to vehicle controls). Consistently, tumorsphere formation was markedly reduced by the combination in both settings ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S17D and S17E). Together, these findings establish Ro-3306 as a functional inhibitor of TENT5A-dependent polyadenylation, capable of shortening MYC poly(A) tails, destabilizing MYC mRNA, and restoring chemosensitivity in osteosarcoma.

## TENT5A inhibition demonstrates therapeutic potential in MYC-activated osteosarcoma PDOs
To evaluate the translational relevance of TENT5A-mediated regulation of MYC, we established PDXO models from patients with osteosarcoma stratified by MYC copy-number status (MYC gain vs. MYC WT; ). Clinical variables—including lesion site, recurrence, metastasis, treatment history, and post-chemotherapy outcomes—were curated for each case (Supplementary Table S6). Patients with MYC gain (n = 4) exhibited poorer responses to chemotherapy, shorter follow-up, and higher recurrence/metastasis rates compared with MYC WT counterparts (n = 4; ; Supplementary Fig. S18A). Histologic analysis revealed elevated TENT5A protein in MYC gain PDXOs, which correlated with reduced tumor shrinkage on post-chemotherapy MRI ( ; Supplementary Fig. S18B and S18C). For therapeutic evaluation, PDXOs were treated with DOX, Ro-3306, or the combination. MYC gain PDXOs exhibited limited sensitivity to DOX monotherapy but significantly reduced viability with the combination ( ; Supplementary Fig. S18D). Mechanistically, combination therapy shortened MYC mRNA poly(A) tails and reduced transcript abundance, consistent with impaired TENT5A-mediated mRNA stabilization ( and ). Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) confirmed reduced MYC and MKI67 after treatment ( ). Expression of stemness markers (ALDH1A1, SOX2, OCT4) and the proliferation marker PCNA was also decreased ( ; Supplementary Fig. S19A). These molecular effects aligned with dose-dependent reductions in TENT5A and MYC protein following Ro-3306 exposure (Supplementary Fig. S19B). In vivo, a subcutaneous xenograft model demonstrated significant tumor volume reduction with Ro-3306 plus DOX, without overt systemic toxicity ( ; Supplementary Fig. S19C). IHC revealed that tumors from the combination group exhibited markedly reduced expression of RUNX2, TENT5A, MYC, MKI67, and stemness markers (ALDH1A1, SOX2; and ).
To extend clinical relevance, Kaplan–Meier analyses across mixed sarcoma cohorts and TCGA-SARC consistently linked elevated TENT5A expression with unfavorable prognosis (Supplementary Fig. S19D–S19F). Organoids derived from giant cell tumor of bone and synovial sarcoma also responded to Ro-3306 and DOX with reduced MYC expression and viability (Supplementary Fig. S19G–S19I). Pan-cancer analyses further demonstrated broad upregulation of TENT5A across diverse malignancies (Supplementary Fig. S20A–S20C). Survival analysis of the TARGET-OS and TCGA-SARC cohorts revealed that patients with high coexpression of TENT5A and MYC had significantly shorter overall survival compared with other groups (Supplementary Fig. S20D), indicating that combined high expression of TENT5A and MYC may serve as a prognostic marker for poor survival and potential chemotherapy resistance in osteosarcoma. Consistent synergy between Ro-3306 and DOX was observed in multiple cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S21A and S21B), suggesting that TENT5A-dependent regulation may represent a more generalizable therapeutic axis. Together, these findings indicate that in MYC-activated osteosarcoma, TENT5A contributes to chemoresistance and stemness by stabilizing MYC mRNA and that pharmacologic inhibition of TENT5A with Ro-3306 sensitizes tumors to chemotherapy, underscoring the translational promise of targeting this pathway.

## Discussion
Osteosarcoma is a clinically aggressive malignancy marked by substantial genetic heterogeneity and layered transcriptional and posttranscriptional control. Although MYC activation typifies aggressive osteosarcoma, it frequently occurs without corresponding genomic AMP, implying additional regulatory mechanisms ( , ). Here, we identify TENT5A, a noncanonical cytoplasmic poly(A) polymerase, as a posttranscriptional regulator that stabilizes MYC mRNA and drives stemness, chemoresistance, and tumor initiation. TENT5A promotes cytoplasmic polyadenylation of existing MYC transcripts while suppressing their physiologic deadenylation, thereby enhancing transcript stability independently of ongoing transcription. These findings were consistently validated across independent osteosarcoma cohorts, establishing a robust genome-to-transcriptome coupling of MYC activity.
Our approach complements, rather than replaces, the classic TAIL-seq framework for global tail-length profiling ( ). TAIL-seq (Illumina short-read with 3′-end enrichment) set the rigor for spike-in calibration, global distribution estimates, and quantitative benchmarking. By contrast, our long-read, isoform-resolved strategy (ONT, “Tail Iso-seq”) captures full-length transcript context (splicing/alternative polyadenylation, APA) and enables tail inference at the isoform level within the same experiment, allowing direct linkage of poly(A) dynamics to MYC isoforms and pharmacodynamic responses. Together, these methods offer orthogonal lenses. TAIL-seq excels at gene-level global distributions and non-A modifications, whereas long reads excel at isoform specificity and comeasurement with expression/APA that converge on TENT5A–MYC biology in osteosarcoma ( – ).
Functionally, CSCs underpin recurrence, metastasis, and therapy resistance through self-renewal, lineage plasticity, and stress tolerance ( , ). Our single-cell and experimental data indicate that TENT5A-high states align with proliferative, stem-like programs characterized by elevated MYC activity. Metabolic inputs have been associated with MYC-driven dedifferentiation, suggesting that microenvironmental cues could potentiate a TENT5A–MYC stemness axis ( ). Although our focus is osteosarcoma, the association of TENT5A with adverse outcomes in additional sarcoma cohorts raises the possibility that this posttranscriptional mechanism extends to other MYC-driven malignancies in which TENT5A is expressed.
Across osteoblast biology and osteosarcoma, MYC serves as a nodal regulator that links growth control to lineage commitment. Prior work shows that MYC activation suppresses osteoblast maturation programs and, in osteosarcoma, can cooperate with RUNX2 to promote proliferation, stem-like features, and chemoresistance ( ). Mechanistically, MYC engages transcriptional elongation and chromatin coactivators and rewires cellular metabolism, biasing cells away from terminal osteogenic differentiation ( ). Within this framework, our data position TENT5A upstream as a stabilizer of MYC mRNA via cytoplasmic polyadenylation, creating a state in which sustained MYC output drives network-level effects on stemness targets. Consistent with reported MYC–RUNX2 cross-talk, RUNX2 increased with OE TENT5A, but this increase was lost upon MYC knockdown, indicating MYC-dependent rather than autonomous differentiation effects.
Physiologically, TENT5A supports ECM production and ER homeostasis in osteoblasts ( ), and recent studies suggest that TENT5A-dependent readenylation is governed primarily by subcellular localization/ER anchoring rather than fixed sequence motifs ( ). Our osteosarcoma data fit a two-tier model: (i) ER-proximal transcripts or those recruited to ER-associated translation gain access to TENT5A, and (ii) among accessible RNAs, a subset—exemplified by MYC—exhibits features compatible with functional stabilization (tail extension, half-life increase) and coherent pharmacodynamic responses when the TENT5A axis is perturbed. These effects align with our RNA-seq and Tail Iso-seq readouts, which show downregulation of MYC programs and shortening of MYC tails upon TENT5A blockade. The same framework also explains context-dependent substrate selection, wherein TENT5A stabilizes oncogenic transcripts in cancer, contrasting with TENT5C’s reported role in restraining ER stress in myeloma. Divergent functions among TENT5 paralogs likely reflect differences in substrate availability, cofactor landscapes, microenvironmental cues (e.g., hypoxia, ECM remodeling), and posttranslational modifications that remodel the TENT5A interactome, RNA-binding preferences, enzymatic kinetics, or subcellular localization ( , ). Our study reveals a context-dependent shift in TENT5A substrate preference: in normal osteoblasts, TENT5A mainly polyadenylates and stabilizes differentiation-associated mRNAs such as COL1A1, whereas in osteosarcoma, it preferentially targets the oncogenic MYC mRNA. The mechanisms underlying this switch remain to be defined. One possibility is that osteosarcoma-specific RNA-binding proteins recruit TENT5A to MYC mRNA by recognizing its 3′ untranslated region, thereby enhancing polyadenylation and stabilizing the transcript. Another nonexclusive possibility is that posttranslational modifications of TENT5A (e.g., phosphorylation or acetylation) alter its RNA-binding affinity and interaction partners, promoting selective engagement with MYC mRNA in osteosarcoma cells. Although these hypotheses require further experimental validation, they provide a conceptual framework for future mechanistic studies.
These insights provide a mechanistic rationale for the discordance between MYC copy number and transcriptional output: Posttranscriptional stabilization by TENT5A can maintain high MYC abundance and activity even when genomic AMP alone would not predict it. Therapeutically, although direct MYC inhibition remains challenging, modulating RNA stability offers an indirect route to attenuate MYC function ( , ). In our models, pharmacologic TENT5A inhibition curtailed MYC poly(A) tails, accelerated MYC mRNA decay, and reversed MYC-linked stemness programs; CETSA and structure-informed hypotheses support direct engagement of TENT5A, and transcriptome-scale profiling shows a broad reversal of TENT5A-induced signatures. Notably, these changes occur alongside chromatin state remodeling associated with osteosarcoma stemness, consistent with on-target perturbation of an RNA-stability axis rather than forced terminal differentiation. More broadly, our findings offer a lens on why MYC hyperactivation does not always elicit the anticipated cytotoxicity from RNA-turnover stress ( ). In TENT5A-high tumors, posttranscriptional stabilization of MYC may buffer against RNA-metabolic stress, enabling survival despite elevated transcriptional drive. This buffering model suggests a wider role for TENT5A in tuning the balance between MYC activity and cellular stress responses.
Our work opens several avenues. First, delineating the upstream regulation of TENT5A—epigenetic, metabolic, or microenvironmental—may refine biomarker-guided patient stratification. Second, deeper mapping of the MYC–stemness feedback may reveal additional therapeutic nodes. Third, given that Ro-3306 is a known CDK1 inhibitor, it is possible that CDK1-related mechanisms also contribute to its effects on MYC stabilization. The development of TENT5A-selective inhibitors is a priority; high-throughput screening, structure-guided design, and RNA-binding interface mapping could yield molecules that selectively disrupt TENT5A–MYC coupling or inhibit TENT5A’s polyadenylation activity. In the interim, our data establish TENT5A as a compelling therapeutic axis. Emerging RNA-based technologies, particularly mRNA-vaccine platforms, may offer innovative routes to modulate TENT5A-regulated pathways and attenuate MYC-driven stemness and chemoresistance. If successfully translated, such strategies could complement chemotherapy and broaden treatment options for aggressive osteosarcoma.
This study has certain limitations inherent to preclinical work. Although validated across cell lines, xenografts, and organoids, these models cannot fully capture clinical complexity. Moreover, although our focus was on TENT5A-dependent polyadenylation, MYC regulation is multifactorial and likely integrates epigenetic, metabolic, and microenvironmental cues. Addressing these layers and advancing toward clinical testing of TENT5A-targeted strategies will be crucial for translation.
