## Graphical abstract
B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) involves the uncontrolled proliferation of immature neoplastic B-cell lymphoblasts within the bone marrow, gradually disseminating to other parts of the body. It comprises of multiple diverse molecular subtypes, each characterized by distinct gene expression signature and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) profile. This altered expression of lncRNAs disrupts various signaling pathways, promoting enhanced cell proliferation and survival, ultimately leading to B-ALL oncogenesis.

## Introduction
Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a major malignancy of the blood and is defined as pernicious proliferation of a certain population of immature blood cells, afflicting both children and adults [ ]. This neoplastic multiplication starts in the bone marrow due to error in maturation process of hematopoietic progenitor cells, leading to fatal damage to the immune system [ ]. Mutations, genomic translocations, copy number variations and aberrant splicing are key features of ALL that disrupt the normal maturation of hematopoietic progenitor cells [ ]. ALL is the most common type of cancer in pediatric populations, accounting for around 25 % of cancer cases in children under the age of 15 [ ]. It is further classified into two major types: B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia [ ].
B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) represents a significant category of ALL, constituting ∼80 % of ALL cases [ ]. It arises from the proliferation of immature B-cell lymphoblasts, which subsequently spread to various parts of the body, overwhelming the healthy blood cells. B-ALL can be further sub-divided into various subtypes such as BCR-ABL1, ETV6-RUNX1, hyperdiploidy, etc., each resulting from different genetic alterations and chromosomal aberrations. Over recent decades, the survival rate for pediatric patients with ALL has ameliorated to exceed ∼90 % [ ], but the survival rate for adult patients is still dismal. Intriguingly, the survival rate for pediatric patients abates to around 50 % after the first relapse, and this survival rate keeps on diminishing post second and third relapse [ ]. In developing and low-income countries, the cure rates are discouraging, with high incidences of relapse and resistance to chemotherapy. One of the major impediments in B-ALL treatment is the molecular heterogeneity within its subtypes, which alters the intracellular signaling cascades and leads to varied responses to conventional chemotherapy. Hence, there is an urgent need to design more precise, effective, and targeted treatment options for B-ALL by identifying new druggable targets that can ameliorate the current treatment options.
Approximately 70 % of the genome is transcribed, but only about 2 % consists of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) that translate into proteins [ ]. These mRNAs are crucial in regulating various cellular and biological processes. The remaining transcripts, once considered insignificant, are now known to be functionally relevant non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) that do not encode proteins. These ncRNAs are categorized into two groups based on their size: small non-coding RNAs (sncRNAs: <200 nucleotides) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs: >200 nucleotides) [ ]. The current GENCODE annotation includes 17,958 lncRNAs and ∼7569 sncRNAs, making up roughly 30 % and 13 % of all human genes, respectively. Major types of sncRNAs include microRNA (miRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), and small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA), each playing distinct roles in transcription regulation [ ].
LncRNA constitute a substantial portion of ncRNAs and play a crucial role in regulating key processes such as transcription, translation, and intracellular signaling [ ]. They are transcribed by RNA polymerase II and like mRNAs, they also undergo 7-methyl guanosine capping at their 5′ end, polyadenylation at their 3’ end, constitutive splicing, and other post-transcriptional modifications [ ]. However, lncRNAs have fewer exons, generally lack open reading frames (ORF), have lower evolutionary conservation, and have lower expression in different tissues when compared to their mRNA counterparts [ , ]. Based on their genomic location, they are classified into five main types, namely: intronic, intergenic, sense, anti-sense, and bi-directional lncRNA ( ) [ ]. Intergenic lncRNAs are transcribed from the region situated within two protein-coding genes, while intronic lncRNAs are expressed from within the introns of protein-coding genes. Sense lncRNAs are transcribed from the same strand as protein-coding genes, including exons from these genes. Conversely, antisense lncRNAs are expressed from the opposite strands of protein-coding genes. Bi-directional lncRNAs share a promoter with protein-coding genes, but are transcribed in the opposite direction [ ]. A notable characteristic of lncRNAs is their capability to form stable secondary structure or higher-order complexes, which facilitates their interactions with various nucleic acids and proteins [ ]. 
Current complications in B-ALL treatment, such as drug-induced cytotoxicity, chemoresistance, and late relapse, can be addressed effectively through targeted therapy. Transcriptomic profiling studies have revealed that abnormalities in lncRNA expression and mutations significantly contribute to malignancies, including B-ALL [ ]. Pre-clinical studies using siRNAs, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small molecules, and natural compounds targeting specific lncRNAs involved in B-ALL progression have demonstrated immense promise. Therefore, understanding B-ALL pathology and the role of lncRNAs in B-ALL progression can help identify novel therapeutic modalities, improving treatment outcomes for different subtypes. In this review, we discuss the various B-ALL subtypes and the crucial role of lncRNAs in B-ALL progression through interconnected signaling pathways, emphasizing their clinical significance. We also provide detailed insights into ongoing research on lncRNAs as biomarkers and therapeutic targets, highlighting associated challenges and future prospects.

## B-ALL and its subtypes
B-ALL is best characterized by a myriad of recurrent genetic alterations which halt the development of normal b-lymphoblasts [ ]. Different genomic aberrations like chromosomal translocations, gain or loss in chromosome numbers, copy number variations and oncogenic tyrosine kinase activity give rise to a variety of highly heterogenous B-ALL subtypes [ , ]. These subtypes exhibit different prognosis, treatment outcomes and disease-free survival in the affected patients. Some of the frequently observed genetic translocations in B-ALL subtypes are summarized below:
ETV6-RUNX1: This subtype is mostly observed in pediatric patients, making ∼25 % of all pediatric B-ALL cases and ∼1–4 % of adult cases [ ]. It arises from the oncogenic activity of the ETV6-RUNX1 fusion, which occurs due to the genomic juxtaposition of the ETV6 gene located on chromosome 12 with the RUNX1 gene located on chromosome 21 [ ]. This ETV6-RUNX1 fusion (also referred as TEL-AML1 fusion) has been observed to take place very early, in utero, and later it goes on to activate the B-ALL malignancy. Patients diagnosed with this subtype generally have a good prognosis and initial treatment outcome, but late relapse has been observed in around 20 % cases [ ].
Hyperdiploidy: Like ETV6-RUNX1, this B-ALL subtype is also more commonly observed in children than adults. It is found in approximately 25–30 % of childhood B-ALL cases and is caused by an elevation in the number of chromosomes from the typical 46 to a range of 51–65 chromosomes [ ]. Chromosomes 4, 10, X, 14, 17 and 21 undergo increase in number. Although most hyperdiploid patients exhibit good prognosis and treatment outcome, around 20 % cases suffer from late relapse [ ].
BCR-ABL1: This subtype is also known as Ph+ B-ALL and results due to the integration of ABL1 gene situated on chromosome 9 with BCR gene located on the chromosome 22, engendering a BCR-ABL1 fusion protein having properties of a tyrosine-kinase [ ]. The chromosome 22 housing the new BCR-ABL1 fusion protein is also referred as philadelphia chromosome (Ph) and the patients having this translocation are also referred to as Ph + patients. The prevalence of this subtype rises with age, with approximately 3 % of pediatric B-ALL cases and around 25 % of adult B-ALL cases exhibiting this particular subtype [ ]. Initially, this subtype was linked to unfavorable treatment results; however, the incorporation of tyrosine kinase inhibitors like imatinib mesylate and dasatinib, along with conventional chemotherapy, has significantly enhanced treatment outcomes.
Ph like or BCR-ABL1 like: Individuals with this subtype possess a gene expression profile similar to patients with Ph + B-ALL; however, they do not exhibit the BCR-ABL1 fusion. Consequently, they are referred to as Ph-like or BCR-ABL1-like. This subtype is distinguished by numerous genomic alterations, with upregulation of the CRLF2 gene being observed in approximately 50 % of cases [ ]. It has been identified in around 12 % of pediatric B-ALL cases and approximately 20–27 % of adult B-ALL cases and is linked to a poor prognosis and unfavorable treatment outcomes.
TCF3-PBX1: This subtype arises from the fusion between the TCF3 gene located on chromosome 19 and the PBX1 gene on chromosome 1, resulting in the formation of a fusion TCF3-PBX1 gene with oncogenic properties [ ]. This subtype is found in ∼6 % of pediatric B-ALL, ∼ 1–3 % of adult B-ALL cases and is correlated with inferior outcome & unfavorable disease-free survival.
MLL-AF4: This subtype is a result of conjugation between the MLL gene located on chromosome 11 with the AF4 gene located on chromosome 4, giving rise to MLL-AF4 fusion gene having oncogenic functions [ ]. This subtype manifests as a rapid and aggressive phenotype with an unfavorable treatment prognosis. MLL-AF4 is more common in infants as it has frequency of around 50 % in infants <6 months and frequency of ∼10 % of adult ALL cases [ ].
iAMP21: iAMP21 is a B-ALL subtype which results due to additional copies of RUNX1 gene present on chromosome 21 [ ]. This subtype has a frequency of ∼2–5 % in pediatric B-ALL cases and is linked with dismal prognosis and poor survival [ ].
B-others (B common): Notably, a significant percentage of B-ALL patients are negative for all the above-mentioned genetic aberrations and are marked by the presence of unique genomic alterations like MEF2D, DUX4 and PAX5 rearrangement among others [ ]. Patients with these distinctive genetic changes are classified as B-others, and predicting the prognosis for these individuals is challenging due to the diversity of genomic alterations they exhibit. This subtype represents approximately 30 % of all B-ALL cases [ ].
Advances in genomics and transcriptomics have revealed diverse and previously unrecognized functions of lncRNAs, positioning them as promising prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in hematologic malignancies. The clinical success and FDA approval of the lncRNA PCA3 for prostate cancer detection substantiates the feasibility of lncRNAs as diagnostic and prognostic tools. In B-ALL, several lncRNAs have been associated with poor prognosis and glucocorticoid resistance, and their inhibition has been shown to resensitize leukemic cells to prednisone, underscoring their potential as clinically actionable targets. These findings suggest that, akin to PCA3 in prostate cancer, lncRNAs could serve as biomarkers for prognosis, subtype classification, relapse risk assessment, and minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring in B-ALL. Importantly, their non-coding nature offers unique opportunities for targeted therapies using ASOs or siRNAs, approaches that may limit off-target effects and reduce treatment-related toxicities. In contrast, current chemotherapies, targeted agents, and immunotherapies face challenges such as acquired mutations, antigen loss, and therapy-induced resistance. Targeting lncRNAs, with their high cell-type specificity and ability to regulate multiple oncogenic pathways, provides a complementary strategy to overcome these limitations and represents an untapped avenue in the evolving treatment landscape of B-ALL.

## LncRNAs and B-ALL pathogenesis
LncRNAs participate in the regulation of transcriptional and post-transcriptional gene expression by functioning as decoys, scaffolds, guides, or signaling molecules ( ). The main role of lncRNAs as a decoy is to block a defined molecular or cellular pathway by directly binding with proteins and inactivating their function [ ].For example, lncRNA PANDA is known to inactivate nuclear transcription factor NF-YA, which in turn blocks the expression of target apoptotic genes of NF-YA, thereby blocking NF-YA apoptotic pathway [ ]. Furthermore, lncRNAs can also guide protein molecules to reach their target sites and exert their effects. For example, lncRNA TCF7 is observed to recruit chromatin remodeling complex SWI/SNF to promoter site of TCF7 which further upregulates TCF7 expression thereby activating Wnt signaling pathway [ ]. Additionally, as molecular scaffolds, lncRNAs help in assembling ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes which can be transcriptional activator or suppressor depending on the nature of RNA and proteins involved. For example, lncRNA TERC has been observed to act as a scaffold which assembles a telomerase complex having the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) protein, and other accessory proteins [ ]. LncRNAs can also act as signals of transcriptional regulation in response to a myriad of stimuli. Signal lncRNAs are cell type specific for instance, lncRNA XIST that is expressed during early stage of female development and is involved in X-chromosome inactivation [ ]. 
Numerous studies involving comprehensive analysis of the transcriptomes of B-ALL patients have shown that lncRNAs intricately contribute to B-ALL, playing pivotal roles in disease initiation and progression [ ]. These lncRNAs affect various signaling pathways that contribute to disease progression ( ). A list comprising of lncRNAs, B-ALL subtype in which they are implicated, their mechanism of action and clinical significance, along with other information is summarized in . Major lncRNAs and their reported roles are summarized below: 
TCL6: TCL6 is linked with the pathophysiology of ETV6-RUNX1 positive B-ALL subtype. Study involving B-ALL patients samples found that TCL6 is highly upregulated in the ETV6-RUNX1 subtype of B-ALL and within this subtype patients with lower levels of TCL6 are associated with worse disease-free survival indicating prognostic significance of TCL6 [ ]. This study proposed that TCL6 is linked with ETV6-RUNX1 positive B-ALL via the AKT pathway [ ]. Other than B-ALL, TCL6 has also been implicated in progression of renal carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In renal carcinoma patient specimens TCL6 is reported to be downregulated and cell lines study shows that it regulates renal carcinoma progression via the src-AKT pathway, likewise, in HCC patients also TCL6 has been observed to be downregulated and in-vitro study shows that TCL6 is involved in HCC progression by regulating PTEN-PI3K-AKT signaling network [ , ].
TUG1: TUG1 is a novel lncRNA linked with B-ALL tumorigenesis. A study showed that TUG1 is aberrantly upregulated in Ph negative B-ALL patients compared to normal healthy samples [ ]. This study also observed that the high levels of TUG1 in Ph negative patients correlates with poor disease free survival, abnormally high bone marrow blasts and spread of cancer in central nervous system [ ]. Besides B-ALL, TUG1 has also been linked with progression of various other malignancies such as HCC, renal cell carcinoma and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). In HCC patient samples, TUG1 is upregulated and cell lines study shows that TUG1 advances HCC progression by regulating transcription factor KLF2, likewise, in renal carcinoma patients also TUG1 has been observed to be upregulated and is involved in tumor dissemination by regulating expression of YAP protein, according to cell line studies [ , ]. Furthermore, in AML patients also TUG1 is upregulated and cell lines-based study has shown that it promotes AML malignancy via MAPK/ERK signaling pathway [ ].
ZEB1-AS1: ZEB1-AS1 has been shown to promote B-ALL pathogenesis by activating IL-11/STAT3 signaling cascade [ ]. One study observed that upregulation of ZEB1-AS1 in B-ALL patients is correlated with overproduction of tumor microenvironment protein IL-11 and depletion of ZEB1-AS1 leads to notable downregulation of IL-11 which further suppresses leukemic cell proliferation [ ]. Additionally, in B-ALL cell lines, this study observed that increased levels of ZEB1-AS1 correlates with STAT3 initiation, suggesting a possible role of IL-11/STAT3 cascade in B-ALL progression. Apart from B-ALL, ZEB1-AS1 has also been linked with progression of some other malignancies. For instance, in HCC patients, ZEB1-AS1 is upregulated, and it stimulates tumor formation by exerting inhibitory effect on its target miR-23c as observed in cell-line based study [ ]. In a study done on intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (IHCC) patients, ZEB1-AS1 upregulation is linked with poor overall survival and worse prognosis [ ]. Additionally, according to in-vitro study ZEB1-AS1 interact with miR-200a through which it promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition in IHCC [ ]. In colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, ZEB1-AS1 is reported to be upregulated and it promotes tumorigenesis via miR-205-YAP1 axis, as observed from cell-line based study [ ].
TEX41: TEX41 is another class of lncRNA upregulated in B-ALL patient samples, specifically in MEF2D subtype [ ]. This upregulation was observed throughout the diagnosis timeline in B-ALL patients whereas post chemotherapy the levels of TEX41 were considerably low, suggesting that TEX41 can act as a prognostic marker to check the efficacy of treatment [ ]. Moreover, knockdown of TEX41 in B-ALL cells induces cell cycle arrest at G2-M phase, suggesting TEX41 as a potential therapeutic target [ ].
CRNDE: CRNDE is a novel lncRNA that is highly expressed in B-ALL patients [ ]. In silico and in-vitro studies have revealed that CRNDE is involved in B-ALL pathogenesis via CRNDE/miR-345-5p/CREB axis. CRNDE knockdown shows diminished cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis in B-ALL cells suggesting that CRNDE is a potential therapeutic target [ ]. Apart from B-ALL, upregulation of CRNDE in other malignancies such as CRC, multiple myeloma (MM) and tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is correlated with poor overall survival. In CRC patients, CRNDE is upregulated, and in-vitro study has found that CRNDE promotes CRC oncogenesis and chemoresistance by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling network [ ]. Further, CRNDE is significantly elevated in MM patient samples and according to in-vitro study it stimulates MM malignancy by regulating IL-6 signaling cascade [ ]. Likewise, in TSCC, CRNDE is known to be upregulated and cell-line based study has observed that it advances TSCC malignancy by modulating PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling cascade [ ].
BALR-2: BALR-2 is another class of lncRNA reported to promote B-ALL tumorigenesis. Microarray and qRT-PCR analysis in patient samples found that BALR-2 is elevated in different B-ALL subtypes compared to normal samples and its increased expression is linked with poor overall survival suggesting its prognostic importance [ ]. In-vitro study has observed that BALR-2 overexpression leads to increased cell proliferation whereas its knockdown reduces cell growth, promotes cellular apoptosis and upregulates glucocorticoid receptor signaling cascade suggesting that BALR-2 is a potential regulator of this pathway [ ]. In patients where prednisone is given as a line of treatment of B-ALL, high expression of BALR-2 is linked with reduced efficacy to prednisone while its knockdown increases sensitivity towards prednisone treatment [ ].
BALR-6: BALR-6 is upregulated in B-ALL patients specifically in MLL subtype [ ]. In-vitro study has observed that siRNA driven knockdown of BALR-6 thwarts cell proliferation and increases apoptosis. Moreover, knockdown of BALR-6 in B-ALL cell lines causes dysregulation of specificity protein 1 (SP1) mediated transcriptome, suggesting that BALR-6 might regulate B-ALL pathogenesis through this protein [ ].
CASC15: CASC15 is known to be elevated in B-ALL patients and its cell lines with highest expression observed in ETV6-RUNX1 subtype [ ]. It has been observed that overexpression of CASC15 reduces cell proliferation and cancer growth whereas its knockdown reduces apoptosis in B-ALL cell lines [ ]. Moreover, knockdown of CASC15 in B-ALL cell lines reduces the expression of transcription factor SOX4 and its downstream target genes suggesting the role of CASC15-SOX4 axis in B-ALL tumorigenesis [ ]. CASC15 expression is also linked with the progression of other malignancies, such as lung cancer, colon cancer and HCC. CASC15 has been reported to be upregulated in lung cancer patients and it stimulates tumor proliferation via HIF-1α/CASC15/SOX4/Wnt axis as observed from in-vitro experiments. Similarly, in colon cancer patients also CASC15 is observed to be upregulated and in-vitro study has shown that it activates Wnt/β-catenin pathway resulting in increased tumor proliferation [ , ]. Additionally, another study showed that CASC15 is upregulated in HCC tumor specimens and its high expression is correlated with poor survival and prognosis [ ].
RP11–137H2.4: This lncRNA is reported to be elevated in pediatric B-ALL patient samples. siRNA mediated silencing of RP11-137H2.4 inhibits cell migration, proliferation in B-ALL cell lines and also enhances responsiveness to glucocorticoids [ ]. Notably, its knockdown in B-ALL cell lines, downregulates cell cycle genes, while genes related to TNFα/NF-kβ, MAPK signaling cascade are upregulated, suggesting the potential role of these pathways in tumorigenesis [ ].
DUXAP8: DUXAP8 is upregulated in B-ALL patients resistant to chemotherapy and its overexpression increases cell proliferation and reduces apoptosis in B-ALL cell lines [ ]. In-vitro study has observed that DUXAP8 interacts with miR-29a, PIK3CA forming DUXAP8/miR-29a/PIK3CA axis and promotes doxorubicin resistance in B-ALL by activating PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway [ ]. Besides B-ALL, DUXAP8 has been implicated in other malignancies like HCC, melanoma, and cervical cancer. DUXAP8 is reported to exhibit elevated levels in both HCC and melanoma patient samples, and its elevated expression is correlated with unfavorable overall survival outcomes in patients [ , ]. According to in-vitro study, in HCC, DUXAP8 promotes oncogenesis via miR-490-5p-BUB1 axis whereas in melanoma DUXAP8 promotes oncogenesis through miR-3182-NUPR1 axis [ , ]. Further, DUXAP8 has elevated levels in cervical cancer patients also, and it stimulates tumor dissemination via miR-1297-RCN2 axis as observed from in-vitro experiment [ ].
H19: H19 is significantly upregulated in B-ALL patients compared to healthy controls, and its overexpression is also evident in B-ALL cell lines [ ]. Notably, the overexpression in B-ALL cell lines diminishes following treatment with chemotherapy drugs [ ]. Studies conducted on cell lines have revealed that post-chemotherapy treatment, the p53 expression is increased while c-Myc levels decreases, subsequently leading to H19 downregulation suggesting the potential regulatory role of these genes in modulating H19 expression [ ]. Other than B-ALL, H19 is implicated in various other cancers such as CRC and breast cancer. In CRC, H19 is significantly upregulated, and in vitro studies have demonstrated it promote cell invasion, migration, and metastasis by activating the Raf-ERK signaling pathway [ ]. Additionally, elevated levels of H19 are observed in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients, and in vitro studies have elucidated its role in promoting breast cancer tumorigenesis via the p53-TNFAIP8 pathway [ ].
GAS5: GAS5 acts as a suppressor of glucocorticoid receptor (GR) and is known to be upregulated in pediatric B-ALL patients [ ]. Study has demonstrated that GAS5 is upregulated in pediatric B-ALL patients compared to normal patients at the time of diagnosis; however, there is a significant decrease in GAS5 expression post-induction therapy, suggesting its potential as a marker of treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the study also demonstrated that patients with high GAS5 expression are more susceptible to relapse and experience poorer treatment outcomes compared to those with lower GAS5 expression, highlighting the clinical significance of GAS5 in B-ALL [ ]. Other than B-ALL, GAS5 is also implicated in CRC and breast cancer. In CRC tissues, GAS5 is downregulated, and in vitro experiments have demonstrated that its overexpression inhibits tumor proliferation by suppressing the expression of miR-182-5p [ ]. Additionally, GAS5 is downregulated in TNBC patients, and low expression of GAS5 is linked with poor survival. In vitro study has shown that GAS5 overexpression hinders tumor proliferation and enhances apoptosis in TNBC cells by downregulating miR-196a-5p [ ].
Linc-PINT: Linc-PINT is downregulated in primary B-ALL patient samples [ ]. A cell-line study demonstrated that overexpression of linc-PINT is associated with reduced cell proliferation and increased apoptosis. Additionally, the study also revealed that increased linc-PINT expression induced the expression of HMOX1 gene, contributing to reduced cell viability [ ]. Beyond B-ALL, linc-PINT is implicated in various other cancers such as nasopharyngeal cancer (NPC) and melanoma. In NPC patient samples, linc-PINT is downregulated, and its decreased expression is linked with a poor response to radiotherapy. In vitro studies have shown that overexpression of linc-PINT enhances the sensitivity of NPC cells to radiotherapy by promoting DNA damage [ ]. Furthermore, linc-PINT is downregulated in clinical samples of melanoma, and in vitro studies have suggested that its overexpression inhibits tumor progression by recruiting the transcriptional activator EZH2 gene [ ].
While it is clear that lncRNAs have a critical role in B-ALL oncogenesis, it is essential to develop a comprehensive understanding of their clinical significance and mechanisms of action. The inherent heterogeneity of B-ALL manifests in distinct gene expression patterns, prognosis, and responses to treatment among its subtypes. Consequently, targeting lncRNAs therapeutically in one subtype may not translate effectively to another subtype. Since lncRNAs lack protein-coding capacity, their expression levels are closely linked to their molecular functions. Determining their subtype-specific expression patterns may provide information that can be leveraged for therapeutic purposes. Studies performed by Yan et al. and Zhao et al. demonstrated that the expression profiles of lncRNAs can effectively distinguish between various breast cancer subtypes, suggesting the potential for similar distinctions in B-ALL subtypes [ , ]. Overall, changes in lncRNA expression across B-ALL subtypes may harbor underlying disease phenotype signatures, underscoring the importance of investigating subtype-specific expression patterns to inform targeted therapeutic approaches.

## LncRNA inhibitors
An array of lncRNAs have been identified with dysregulated expression in various tumors, establishing them as pivotal contributors to tumorigenesis. This discovery has paved the way for the exploration and examination of innovative lncRNA-based therapeutics for potential treatment. Various forms of lncRNA-based therapeutics for cancer treatment have undergone testing in preclinical models ( ) and are elaborated below:
4.1

## Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs)
Numerous preclinical studies have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting lncRNAs. For instance, Xu et al. reported that siRNA-mediated knockdown of lncRNA TINCR reduced cell proliferation and migration, enhanced apoptosis, and decreased mTOR expression in colon cancer cell lines [ ]. Further, a study demonstrated that DANCR siRNA nanoparticles efficiently decrease the expression of the upregulated oncogenic DANCR lncRNA in triple-negative breast cancer cell lines and mouse models [ ]. Wang et al. demonstrated that silencing of XIST with siRNA leads to increased apoptosis, reduced proliferation, and lower tumorigenesis in AML cells due to suppression of MYC gene expression [ ]. Moreover, siRNA targeting NEAT1 results in decreased proliferation, invasion, and migration of infantile hemangioma (IH) cells through activation of the NF-κB signaling cascade [ ]. siRNA-mediated silencing of CASC9 lncRNA results in increased apoptosis and suppressed proliferation in CRC cells by decreasing the expression of AKT3 protein [ ]. Effect of siRNA mediated silencing of lncRNAs has also been tested in B-ALL, where siRNA driven knockdown of BALR-2 and BALR-6 lncRNAs involved in B-ALL progression leads to reduced cell proliferation and increased apoptosis in B-ALL cell lines [ , ]. Similarly, siRNA driven silencing of lncRNA RP11-137H2.4, implicated in B-ALL progression, has demonstrated decreased cell proliferation in B-ALL cell lines [ ]. Furthermore, shRNAs, which operate via similar RNA interference mechanisms, have been tested against lncRNAs involved in B-ALL. For example, the knockdown of CRNDE using shRNA results in increased apoptosis and reduced cell proliferation in B-ALL cell lines [ ]. Likewise knockdown of ZEB1-AS1 using shRNA decreases cell proliferation by downregulating the IL-11 expression in B-ALL cell lines [ ].
Despite having remarkable therapeutic potential, siRNAs face challenges such as low cell permeability, instability, and off-target effects. To address these issues, the utilization of drug delivery carriers conjugated with aptamers, which can efficiently attach to target molecules, represents a significant advancement. For example, Liu et al. designed a nanodrug delivery vehicle conjugated with CL4 aptamers, encapsulating DARS-AS1 siRNA and doxorubicin [ ]. This system effectively targeted lncRNA DARS-AS1 in TNBC cells, impeded tumor growth, and enhanced doxorubicin sensitivity by inhibiting TGF-β/Smad3 signaling-mediated autophagy [ ].

4.2

## Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)
ASOs are chemically modified synthetic single stranded oligonucleotides, typically 15–25 nucleotides in length [ ]. These molecules exhibit high specificity for their complementary RNA targets, leading to the degradation of the target RNA through RNase H-mediated breakdown of the DNA-RNA duplex. To overcome limitations associated with siRNAs, ASOs have undergone various chemical modifications, including phosphorothioate ASOs, 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-MOE) ASOs, peptide nucleic acids (PNAs), and locked nucleic acids (LNAs). These modifications enhance cell permeability, stability, and target specificity [ , , ].
ASOs have also been tested for their therapeutic effects on lncRNAs in various pre-clinical models. Recently, Feichtenschlager et al. demonstrated that a GapmeR ASO targeting lncRNA AC004540.4 reduced tumor growth in MAPK-dependent melanoma by destabilizing the oncogene hnRNPA2/B1 [ ]. Similarly, Tian et al. showed that ASO-LLNLR-299G3.1, delivered via peptide-fused nanoparticles, targeted lncRNA LLNLR-299G3.1 and suppressed oncogenesis in a mouse model of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [ ]. In another study, an LNA GapmeR ASO targeting lncRNA HIF1A-As2 inhibited KRAS-mediated lung cancer oncogenesis [ ]. Research indicates that an LNA-based ASO targeting lncRNA AC104041.1 reduces tumorigenesis in cell lines associated with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) by inhibiting the β-catenin signaling cascade [ ]. Additionally, an ASO targeting MALAT1 was effective in impeding tumor growth and metastasis in a mouse model of breast cancer [ ]. NEAT1 silencing via LNA GapmeR ASO promotes apoptosis and suppresses cell proliferation in a murine MM model, highlighting its anti-tumor potential [ ]. Inhibition of lncRNA HOTAIR using a PNA cross-linked with a pH-low insertion peptide (pHLIP) reduces its interaction with EZH2, thereby decreasing tumorigenesis in ovarian and breast cancer cells [ ]. Furthermore, treatment with a 2′-deoxy-2′-fluoro-beta-D-arabinonucleic acid (2′F-ANA) modified ASO targeting TEX41, an lncRNA involved in B-ALL progression, results in reduced cell proliferation and cell cycle arrest, suggesting potential therapeutic benefits for B-ALL [ ].
A major breakthrough in ASO therapy occurred with the approval of Andes-1537, an ASO targeting antisense non-coding mitochondrial RNA (ASncmtRNA) [ ]. Andes-1537, developed by Andes Biotechnology, inhibits cancer progression by inducing apoptosis and downregulating genes such as cyclin B1 and cyclin D1. Phase 1 clinical trials demonstrated good tolerance and efficacy, particularly in patients with cholangiocarcinoma and pancreatic cancer [ ]. Recent study by Araya et al. further supported the efficacy of Andes-1537, showing that suppression of ASncmtRNAs led to increased apoptosis and reduced cell proliferation in primary cultures from patients with primary and metastatic renal carcinoma. This underscores the potential of ASOs as a therapeutic strategy for otherwise incurable tumors [ ].

4.3

## Small molecules and natural compounds
LncRNAs possess complex tertiary structures that allow them to specifically interact with small molecules and natural compounds. Despite the challenge of designing small molecules targeting lncRNAs due to the lack of well-defined 3D structures and limited sequence information, several studies have utilized computational approaches to suggest potential small molecules targeting lncRNAs. Chen et al. identified a small molecule AC1Q3QWB (AQB) through high-throughput virtual screening. AQB disrupts the interaction between lncRNA HOTAIR and EZH2, reducing cell proliferation in endometrial cancer (EC) cell lines [ ]. Combining AQB with the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat upregulated tumor suppressor genes in EC cells and also showed effectiveness in breast cancer models [ , ]. Donlic et al. have demonstrated that DPFp8, a diphenylfuran derivative, binds with high affinity to the MALAT1 triple helix. They went on to design DPFp8 analogs that exhibited better binding [ ]. Similarly, another compound MTC07, has been shown to bind to targetable pockets of MALAT1 [ ] while FDA-approved small molecules niclosamide and Tyrphostin9 reduce MALAT1 levels by activating GSK3β [ ]. AC1NOD4Q (ADQ) has been shown to disrupt the HOTAIR-EZH2 interaction, leading to reduced tumorigenesis in breast cancer models [ ]. Simvastatin inhibits lncRNA SNHG29, reducing PD-L1 expression and tumorigenesis in CRC cell lines [ ]. Similarly, YK-4-279 blocks lncRNA HULC, decreasing TWIST1 expression and tumor growth in Ewing sarcoma cell lines [ ].
In recent years natural compounds have also been used to target lncRNAs. For instance, a recent study observed that ellagic acid treatment leads to increased apoptosis and modulation of numerous lncRNAs in CRC cells [ ]. Curcumin inhibits lncRNA H19, reducing proliferation and increasing apoptosis in gastric cancer cells [ ]. Curcumin analog Da0324 decreases LINC01021 expression, elevating p53 and reducing cell migration and proliferation in gastric cancer cell lines [ ]. Resveratrol diminishes NEAT1 expression in multiple myeloma cells, deactivating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and reducing tumorigenesis [ ]. Quercetin, a naturally existing flavonoid, reduces MALAT1 expression in prostate cancer cells, leading to decreased proliferation and migration, and inhibiting the PI3K-AKT pathway [ ].
Recently, Vahedi et al. developed a dual nanoparticle platform to deliver linc-RoR siRNA and curcumin in breast cancer cells, enhancing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest while reducing linc-RoR expression, demonstrating the potential of combinatorial therapeutic approaches [ ].

## Conclusions, challenges and future perspectives
lncRNAs have emerged as crucial regulators of B-ALL landscape. Numerous lncRNAs have been identified as dysregulated in B-ALL, providing newer insights into the molecular mechanisms driving disease progression. Their ability to regulate gene expression and cellular processes, combined with their presence in bodily fluids, makes them particularly valuable for early cancer detection and monitoring. The inception of high-throughput sequencing technologies has aided in the discovery of numerous lncRNAs that contribute to a variety of cancers, including B-ALL [ , ].
Despite the advancements in our understanding, targeting LncRNAs for B-ALL therapeutics presents multiple challenges. The sheer complexity of lncRNA networks suggests their plausible and multifaceted contribution to the immense subtype heterogeneity observed in B-ALL patients. The dynamic nature of lncRNA expression in different B-ALL subtypes and disease stages requires comprehensive exploration. Our understanding of the functional characterization of specific lncRNAs and their interactions with other molecular players inside cells is still evolving. A notable limitation in lncRNA expression profiling is their lowly expressed transcripts, which makes them undetectable and can obscure the accurate detection of differential gene expression by closely resembling sampling or transcriptional noise [ , , ]. These transcripts must be filtered out during RNA-Seq analysis. In recent years, advancements in RNA-Seq methodologies have helped address this issue and enhance the quantification of lncRNAs in clinical specimens. One such method is CaptureSeq, a targeted RNA-Seq technique gaining popularity for quantifying specific genomic regions [ ]. CaptureSeq employs customized oligonucleotide probes to selectively capture desired transcripts while excluding others thereby helping in quantification of lowly expressed transcripts and identification of greater number of lncRNAs compared to conventional RNA-Seq approaches [ , , , ].
Moreover, lack of well-defined structural data for lncRNAs makes it difficult to design effective small molecule inhibitors [ , , ]. Structure-based drug discovery (SBDD) has proven effective in expediting the identification of anticancer therapies by pinpointing potent small molecules or ligands capable of binding to specific functional regions of the target. However, SBDD relies heavily on having a well-defined 3-D structure of the target, which remains a hurdle in case of lncRNAs. Furthermore, the lack of comprehensive sequence databases for lncRNAs, stemming from insufficient experimental sequencing data hinders predicting structures based on sequences using computational tools. Computational structure prediction tools require a substantial number of defined structures for comparative analysis to be effective, thus proving ineffective for lncRNAs. Despite these challenges, computational tools for predicting lncRNA structures are emerging, with some success in identifying small molecule inhibitors [ , , ]. For instance, Li et al. utilized the MC-Fold/MC-Sym tool to predict HOTAIR structure, followed by small molecule identification using Autodock [ ]. They identified a novel small molecule AC1Q3QWB which effectively impeded HOTAIR-mediated tumorigenesis in breast cancer cells [ ]. Identifying inhibitors that can selectively modulate the activity of B-ALL-associated lncRNAs while avoiding unintended off-target effects and potential disruptions to normal cellular processes will be a boon to realizing the effectiveness of lncRNAs as therapeutic targets. lncRNAs have different domains that interact with different proteins. For example: 5′-domain of HOTAIR interacts with the PRC2 complex protein while the 3′ domain interacts with the LSD1 protein. This information needs to be taken into account while designing inhibitors against HOTAIR in order to reduce the risk of off-target effects [ ].
At present, primary concern regarding the therapeutic targeting of lncRNAs using siRNAs and ASOs is their low target specificity and tendency to affect unintended targets, potentially leading to unintended alterations in phenotype [ , , ]. To address this challenge, enhancing target specificity and mitigating off-target effects can be achieved through the use of efficient delivery vehicles for siRNAs and ASOs. Among these, liposomes have emerged as popular delivery vehicles for oligonucleotides due to their low immunogenicity/toxicity and high drug stability. Recently, Meng et al. demonstrated enhanced efficacy of liposome-enclosed HIF1α-AS2 siRNA in targeting the HIF1α-AS2 lncRNA implicated in CRC [ ]. Additionally, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have shown promise as effective carriers for delivering drugs, facilitating the transport of various oligonucleotides with increased bioavailability and reduced toxicity. Recently, Connerty et al. demonstrated that LNP-encapsulated siRNA targeting the lncRNA LINC01257 effectively reduced cell proliferation in AML cells [ ]. Further research exploring the use of additional peptides, small molecules, aptamers, ligands, and antibodies in conjunction with liposomes and LNPs is being explored to enhance the efficacy of these therapies [ ].
Despite the challenges, the future for lncRNA research is immensely promising. The rapid emergence of advanced technologies, such as single-cell RNA sequencing, has begun enabling the dissection of heterogeneity within B-ALL populations/subtypes and will begin providing insights into lncRNA dynamics at the single-cell level in various subtypes. Recently, Hayashi et al. introduced RamDA-seq, a total RNA-Seq technique designed for single-cell analysis, enabling the quantification of different types of lncRNAs at single-cell level [ ]. Moreover, given their potential as cancer biomarkers studies are being performed to quantify lncRNAs present within exosomes [ ]. However, due to the low abundance of lncRNAs within exosomes, efficient techniques akin to CaptureSeq will need to be developed for accurate quantification. Several therapeutic agents targeting lncRNAs have demonstrated promise in clinical trials. For example, Andes-1537, an ASO targeting ASncmtRNAs, has demonstrated immense promise in phase I clinical trials [ , , ]. Additionally, an siRNA targeting lncRNA DDX11-AS1 has recently been patented for liver cancer treatment [ ]. lncRNAs also offer significant advantages over proteins as biomarkers due to their potential for early disease detection. Protein markers are typically identified at advanced disease stages, whereas dysregulated lncRNAs present in the bloodstream or other bodily fluids can facilitate early, non-invasive cancer detection [ ]. For instance, lncRNA PCA3 is widely used as a biomarker for prostate cancer detection from body fluids [ ]. Similarly, lncRNA H19 is a biomarker for diagnosing breast cancer due to its elevated presence in the serum and plasma of affected individuals [ ]. Since lncRNAs do not translate into proteins, their expression levels are closely linked to their molecular functions, offering heightened sensitivity and specificity as biomarkers [ ].
Integrative approaches combining genomics, epigenomics, and functional studies will enhance our understanding of lncRNA regulatory networks. Additionally, exploring the crosstalk between lncRNAs, their networks and other biomolecules, such as coding genes, microRNAs, and proteins, will provide a holistic view of their regulatory functions in disease progression. Longitudinal studies tracking lncRNA expression changes in various subtypes, disease burden, progression and treatment response will contribute to the identification of subtype and stage specific lncRNAs that can serve as prognostic markers and therapeutic targets. Taken together this will help develop precise and innovative treatment strategies to therapeutically target specific dysregulated lncRNAs for further preclinical and clinical investigations.
In summary, lncRNAs are promising targets for B-ALL diagnostics and therapeutics. Advancements in our understanding of their mechanisms of action, drug delivery technologies, and targeted therapeutic approaches will help realize their potential in clinical settings. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the effects, clinical significance, and underlying mechanisms of various lncRNAs associated with different B-ALL subtypes. We have also discussed the recent research advances and the challenges associated with lncRNA expression profiling, identifying potential therapeutics, and the ongoing advancements in this field. A thorough understanding of these topics will undoubtedly contribute to the development of innovative strategies for B-ALL treatment.
