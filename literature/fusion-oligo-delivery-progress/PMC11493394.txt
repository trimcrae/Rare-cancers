## ABSTRACT
In 2020, the top 10 causes of death among children and adolescents between the ages of 1 and 19 years old included cancer, congenital anomalies, heart disease, and chronic respiratory disease; all these conditions are potentially treatable with medical intervention. However, children exhibit specific physiological and developmental characteristics that can significantly impact drug pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety profile. These factors illustrate the importance of a heightened focus on pediatric drug development. Traditional drugs lack proper circulation, permeability, targeting, accumulation, and release, and they often require dose adjustments or modifications, which can result in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes and increased risks of adverse effects in pediatric patients. Nanomedicines have emerged as efficient drug delivery systems because of their unique properties, which can improve the solubility and stability of drugs by encapsulating them in different forms of nanoparticles. This review discusses the challenges of pediatric therapy, and the current state of nanomedicines for pediatric diseases in terms of Food and Drug Administration‐approved nanomedicines, the types of diseases treated or diagnosed, and preclinical studies that have the potential to be translated to the clinic. In summary, nanomedicine holds significant potential for addressing the unique and pressing challenges associated with diagnosing and treating pediatric diseases.

This review highlights the unique challenges in pediatric drug therapy and emphasizes the potential of nanomedicines in addressing these challenges, detailing their properties, Food and Drug Administration‐approved applications, and preclinical studies that may translate to clinical practice in treating pediatric diseases.

Liposomes are a highly biocompatible type of NP considered to be the most successful in clinical use. Their structure comprises of a self‐assembled phospholipid bilayer with a size ranging from 30 to 1000 nm scale (Liu, Chen, and Zhang  ). Liposomes can be unilamellar (one bilayer) or multilamellar (multiple bilayers) with a central aqueous compartment. They can be loaded with a hydrophobic drug in the bilayer and a hydrophilic drug in the center (aqueous compartment) (Andra et al.  ). The outer layer of the liposome can further be functionalized to increase the efficiency and compatibility of the drug which makes it an efficient drug delivery system. Notably, the prominence of liposomes in the field of nanomedicine was marked by the approval of Doxil, a liposomal formulation of doxorubicin, in 1995 (Yellepeddi, Joseph, and Nance  ). This became the first nanomedicine to receive approval, serving as a pivotal example of the potential of such formulations. Of note, the half‐life of Doxil in pediatrics patients was about half that of adults (36.4 h vs. 73.9 h) (Yellepeddi, Joseph, and Nance  ). This difference was attributed to the reduced plasma protein concentration in pediatric patients compared to adults which altered its pharmacokinetics.

Polymeric NPs range in size from 1 to 1000 nm with different morphologies. NPs developed from natural polymers are used to deliver various hydrophobic and hydrophilic chemotherapeutic drugs. Based on the morphology, polymeric NPs can be nanocapsules or nanospheres. Nanocapsules consist of an oily core and a polymeric shell. Nanospheres have a polymeric network of the shell continuing inside. The drug is either dissolved in the core of the nanocapsules, entrapped inside the shell of nanospheres, or adsorbed by the polymeric membrane. The release profile of the drug from the core is controlled by the polymeric coat (Adhikari  ).

Solid lipid NPs are a versatile class of nanocarriers developed as an alternative to traditional lipid‐based systems, such as liposomes (Madkhali  ). Comprising a solid lipid core, these NPs offer advantages in terms of improved drug stability, controlled drug release, and enhanced protection of encapsulated bioactives. Typically, Solid lipid NPs fall within a size range of 50–1000 nm. Notably, Solid lipid NPs have gained immense attention during the COVID‐19 pandemic, as they were used in the formulation of Pfizer‐BioNTech and Moderna vaccines. Their unique characteristics, such as lipid composition and structure, play a pivotal role in the effective delivery of messenger RNA (mRNA) into human immune cells, ensuring the intended therapeutic effect.

Inorganic NPs are generally based on metals and non‐carbon sources such as gold (Au) NPs, metal salts, quantum dots, superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs and silica NPs (Liu et al.  ). The metal ions released by these NPs are used in treatments and as diagnostic tools. Researchers have gained interest in inorganic NPs due to their large surface area, controllable surface chemistry, and their optical and magnetic properties (Liu et al.  ). Although, they improve the treatment and diagnostic efficiency, their limitations on toxicity and solubility still prevail.

Dendrimers are a type of organic NP that are well‐defined, have high molecular mass, are highly branched, and have a low polydispersity index (Jain et al.  ). Their structure ranges from 1 to 10 nm in size. They usually have a tree‐like 3D structure with three distinct components: a center core, an inner shell (branches), and an outer shell (functional groups). All bonds in the structure of a dendrimer emerge radially from the center core and contribute to the branching which provides flexibility and mono‐dispersibility. The lipophilicity of dendrimers makes them suitable therapeutic agents for passing the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Additionally, dendrimers usually resemble biological structures such as—the fifth‐generation polyaminodoamine dendrimers—which resemble hemoglobin in size and shape (Kaurav et al.  ).

Nanofibers are a type of NP developed from a variety of materials based on their application. They usually measures below 100 nm and have one dimension in the nanoscale (diameter) and one dimension which is significantly larger (length) (Ece  ). The type of nanofiber can vary depending on the nature (natural or engineered); structure (hollow, core‐shell, porous, or nonporous); and composition (organic, inorganic, carbon‐based, or composite). Compared to other NPs, nanofibers have the advantage of a high aspect ratio (diameter/length) and surface area (Ece  ). These unique and promising properties have gained the interest of researchers to develop nanofibers for regenerative tissue and organ engineering, wound healing (nano‐scaffolds) and drug delivery (Nayl et al.  ).
Protein NPs have garnered substantial attention due to their exceptional biocompatibility, biodegradability, and versatility in medical applications. These NPs, derived from natural proteins such as viral capsids, ferritin, and albumin, serve as highly effective drug delivery platforms. Their unique structural properties allow for precise delivery and targeting of therapeutic agents, making them particularly useful in immunotherapy and vaccine development. Recent advancements in protein NPs have demonstrated their potential in developing multivalent vaccines and targeted cancer therapies. The biocompatibility and ease of modification of protein NPs facilitate the creation of sophisticated delivery systems that can enhance the stability and bioavailability of drugs. Furthermore, protein NPs can be genetically modified or covalently conjugated with various molecules to augment their functionality, enhancing both therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles (Xu et al.  ). These developments highlight the growing role of protein NPs in advancing nanomedicine, offering new avenues for treatment across a range of diseases, including cancer and infectious diseases.

## Potential Impact of Nanomedicine for Pediatric Application
Nanomedicine holds significant potential for addressing unique challenges associated with treating pediatric diseases (Figure  ). Children exhibit specific physiological and developmental characteristics that can significantly impact drug pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety profiles (G. L. Kearns et al.  ). Traditional drugs lack proper circulation, permeability, targeting, accumulation, and release (Lu et al.  ). They often require dose adjustments or modifications, which can result in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes and increased risks of adverse effects in pediatric patients (G. L. Kearns et al.  ). Nanomedicines have emerged as potential drug delivery systems because of their unique properties, which can improve the solubility and stability of drugs by encapsulating them in different forms of NPs (Mitchell et al.  ). The structure, surface charge, and functionality of the NP can be tuned to enhance permeability and retention of the drug at the target site (Yao et al.  ), reducing off‐target effects, and improving the safety profile (Lu et al.  ; Singh et al.  ). These features are particularly crucial, as minimizing toxicity is paramount in the treatment of pediatric patients (Irvine and Dane  ; Yang et al.  ).
 Extensive pharmacokinetic studies in adults have shown that NPs can improve drug bioavailability and efficacy in clinical trials by controlling biodistribution and clearance. (Cabral et al.  ) However, in pediatric patients, these effects are not fully understood due to the dynamic and developing nature of their bodies. Children have different rates of organ development, enzyme activity, and plasma protein levels compared to adults, which can influence the pharmacokinetics of nanomedicines. For example, the biodistribution of NPs may vary significantly due to differences in tissue and vascular permeability, body weight, and metabolic rates. Studies have shown that pediatric patients often require different dosing regimens to achieve therapeutic effects similar to those seen in adults (Yellepeddi, Joseph, and Nance  ). This variability necessitates thorough pharmacokinetic studies tailored to pediatric populations to ensure that nanomedicines are both effective and safe for children. Addressing these challenges through in‐depth studies within appropriate populations will be essential for the successful application of nanomedicine in pediatric healthcare (Omidian and Mfoafo  ).
Clearance mechanisms of the body, like the kidney, liver, and spleen, significantly impact the targeting and toxicity of nanomedicines. The size of NPs is a critical factor. Larger NPs, above 200 nm, accumulate in the liver and spleen, while those less than 5 nm are kidney‐filtered. Therefore, NPs sized 10–100 nm are preferred for drug delivery, balancing efficacy with reduced off‐target toxicity (Yang et al.  ).
Nanotechnology‐based drug delivery systems can improve patient compliance by reducing the frequency of administration, providing sustained or controlled drug release, prolonging drug retention and time of action at disease site, and offering alternative routes of administration (e.g., transdermal, nasal, or oral) (Ventola  ). This helps reduce the need for multiple dose administrations while increasing safety (reduced adverse side effects), stability, bioavailability (concentration of drug at the site of action), biodegradability, drug loading, and delivery efficacy with an improved drug‐release profile (Hami  ; Adepu and Ramakrishna  ; Corma, Botella, and Rivero‐Buceta  ; Sheng et al.  ). This holds significant relevance for pediatric groups, given that children frequently exhibit lower compliance and might struggle with ingesting or enduring specific medication compositions (Hack and Chow  ; Winnick et al.  ).
Moreover, these nanoformulations can be functionalized with target molecules and polyethylene glycol (PEG) molecules on the surface to evade the mononuclear phagocyte system and prolong circulation time (Yang et al.  ). They can also be characterized to recognize stimuli or receptors to modify the release profile or accumulation of the drug at target site. NPs can be formulated as complex systems for combining multiple therapies to maximize the therapeutic efficacy and safety (Mitchell et al.  ) including real time tracking of drug accumulation and its effect on the system. Furthermore, nanomedicines can overcome biological barriers that typically limit the effectiveness of conventional drugs, such as the BBB (Abballe et al.  ). This trait is especially beneficial when addressing pediatric brain tumors, as ensuring therapeutic agents traverse the BBB is crucial (Pardridge  ).

 2.3.1

## Vaccines
Vaccines are one of the most successful interventions in the history of medicine and have played a pivotal role in the control and prevention of infectious diseases. In the context of pediatric populations, vaccines not only protect the individual child but also contribute to herd immunity, thus reducing the overall incidence of disease (Greenwood  ; Sampath et al.  ). Nanotechnology has provided new opportunities for vaccine development. For example, nanocarriers like liposomes, protein‐based NPs, and inorganic NPs have been used to enhance the immune response to antigens as well as increase the stability of encapsulated agents, thus improving vaccine efficacy (Kon, Elia, and Peer  ; Wen et al.  ; Zhao et al.  ). Furthermore, nanotechnology‐based vaccines can be designed to target specific cells, such as dendritic cells, that are crucial in inducing an effective immune response (Hills and Howarth  ; Mohsen et al.  ).
In the context of vaccine response, it is worth noting that the immune response to vaccines can vary between children and adults. Children's immune systems are still developing and might react differently to certain antigens compared to mature adult immune systems. While some vaccines might elicit a stronger and more robust response in children, others might require booster doses to achieve the desired level of immunity (Kollmann et al.  ; Simon, Hollander, and McMichael  ). Additionally, certain side effects or immune reactions may be more prevalent in one age group compared to the other. Factors like previous exposure to the pathogen or other vaccines, the maturation state of the immune system, and age‐specific differences in the number or functionality of immune cells can all influence this differential response (Olin et al.  ; Saso and Kampmann  ). Hence, when developing and testing vaccines, especially those using novel platforms like nanotechnology, it is crucial to consider and study these age‐specific differences to ensure both safety and efficacy across all age groups.
2.3.1.1

## 
COVID‐19 Vaccines
The recent COVID‐19 pandemic has further highlighted the importance of vaccines in global health and disease prevention. Particularly noteworthy is the rapid development and widespread deployment of nanotechnology‐based vaccines for SARS‐CoV‐2, the virus that causes COVID‐19. Each of these vaccines, including mRNA vaccines, viral vector vaccines, protein subunit vaccines, and inactivated vaccines, utilize a different method to elicit an immune response against the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus (Hadj Hassine  ). These vaccines are celebrated as a milestone, showcasing the pinnacle of nanomedicine's success and highlighting the adaptability and potency of nanotechnologies in the realm of vaccinology.
2.3.1.1.1

## mRNA Vaccines (Pfizer‐BioNTech and Moderna)
Two of the first vaccines to receive Emergency Use Authorization from the FDA for COVID‐19 were mRNA vaccines developed by Pfizer‐BioNTech and Moderna. Instead of introducing a weakened or inactivated vaccine subunit into our bodies, mRNA vaccines inject a small piece of the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus's genetic code, which instructs cells to build the virus's spike protein. Once these proteins are produced and displayed on cell surfaces, they trigger an immune response. At the heart of mRNA vaccines lies nanotechnology: the mRNA is packaged within lipid NPs for delivery. These lipid NPs protect the mRNA from degradation, help it enter cells, and contribute to a stronger immune response. These NPs, typically around 100 nm in size, are small enough to facilitate effective cellular uptake but large enough to avoid rapid renal clearance, striking a balance between efficacy and safety (Mulligan et al.  ; Patel et al.  ; Xia  ).
For pediatric applications, these mRNA vaccines have shown promising results. The Pfizer‐BioNTech vaccine (BNT162b2) was approved for children at least 6 months of age on June 17, 2022 (U.S. Food and Drug Administration  ). As of June 24, 2022, the Moderna vaccine (mRNA‐1273) has received an endorsement from the CDC Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices for its use in children aged 6 through 17 years, in addition to its already recommended use in children 6 months through 5 years (U.S. Food and Drug Administration  ). The lipid NP technology makes these vaccines safer and more effective in children due to their increased stability, decreased immunogenicity, and precise delivery. Although efficacious, it is worth noting that the approved vaccines required significant dose reduction when translated from adult (12 years and above—30 μg) to preteen (5–11 years—10 μg) and even further to baby/child (6 months—4 years—3 μg) (Figure  ) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration  ).
 
2.3.1.1.2

## Viral Vector Vaccines (Johnson & Johnson and AstraZeneca‐Oxford)
Although mRNA vaccines were the first nanomedicines against COVID‐19, they were not the last. Adenovirus vector vaccines like the Johnson & Johnson's Janssen COVID‐19 vaccine and the AstraZeneca/Oxford vaccine also utilize nanotechnology. These vaccines use a harmless virus to deliver a piece of the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus genome, specifically the gene for the spike protein, into cells, prompting an immune response (Patel et al.  ; Sharifian‐Dorche et al.  ). The pediatric use of these vaccines is still under investigation. Future versions of these vaccines might include nanoemulsion adjuvants to enhance immune response and potentially lower necessary dosage levels, which would be particularly beneficial for pediatric populations (Cao et al.  ; Reber and Katz  ).

2.3.1.1.3

## Protein Subunit Vaccines (Novavax)
Novavax's vaccine is a protein subunit vaccine, which involves introducing harmless pieces of the virus (not the whole virus) to stimulate an immune response. In this case, the spike proteins of the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus are bioengineered and presented to the immune system along with an adjuvant, a substance that enhances the immune response (Keech et al.  ; Parums  ; Tian et al.  ). This vaccine takes advantage of the NP technology, with the spike proteins assembled into NPs around 27 nm in size (Tian et al.  ). These NPs mimic the structure of the virus, which can trigger a robust immune response (Guebre‐Xabier et al.  ). This approach could be particularly beneficial for children, as it removes the risk of administering a live virus. This vaccine has been authorized for emergency use by the FDA for adolescents between the ages of 12 and 17 (Underwood et al.  ).

2.3.1.1.4

## Inactivated Vaccines (Sinovac, Sinopharm)
Sinovac and Sinopharm use inactivated SARS‐CoV‐2 virus to provoke an immune response (Hotez and Bottazzi  ; Xia et al.  ). Inactivated vaccines have been widely used for a variety of diseases in pediatric populations due to their long history and known safety profile (Pollard and Bijker  ). Sinovac is conducting clinical trials in children and adolescents, with early results appearing promising (Xia et al.  ).

2.3.1.2

## Vaccines for Other Diseases
Engerix‐B, a nanotechnology‐enhanced vaccine, is a highly effective tool in the prevention of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. This hepatitis B vaccine uses recombinant DNA technology to produce the hepatitis B surface antigen, which is then adsorbed onto aluminum hydroxide, a type of NP, to enhance the immune response. Engerix‐B has been shown to have high efficacy in healthy newborns, children, adolescents, and adults after the complete vaccine schedule (Keating and Noble  ). Clinic studies show within 1–2 months after the final dose of Engerix‐B, 96%–100% of infants, children, and adolescents aged 0 through 19 years, 78%–87% of adult subjects, and 30%–44% of subjects aged 40 years and older achieved a protective antibody response (antibody concentrations ≥ 10 mIU/mL against HBsAg) (Drugs.com  ). Despite its effectiveness, like any vaccine, Engerix‐B can cause side effects, although not everybody gets them. Most side effects are mild and short‐lived. Common side effects include site soreness (22%) and fatigue (14%) (Drugs.com  ). These are typical side effects of all injectable vaccines and are often a sign that the body is building protection against the virus. Thus, while Engerix‐B represents a significant advancement in HBV prevention, it is crucial to monitor vaccine recipients for possible adverse reactions and to use the vaccine with caution in individuals with a history of severe allergic reactions to any component of the vaccine.
Recent advancements in NP‐based vaccines, particularly mRNA vaccines, have revolutionized the field of vaccinology and opened new avenues for both vaccines and therapeutic applications. Protein subunit vaccines, for instance, can mimic the structure of the virus to trigger robust immunity without the risks associated with live virus vaccines. Viral vector vaccines can potentially include nanoemulsion adjuvants to enhance immune responses and reduce required dosages, which is particularly beneficial for pediatric populations. mRNA vaccines, such as those developed by Pfizer‐BioNTech and Moderna, utilize lipid NPs to deliver genetic material encoding the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein. These lipid NPs protect the mRNA from degradation, facilitate cellular uptake, and enhance the immune response. This method allows for quick adaptation to emerging viral variants by simply altering the mRNA sequence, making it a highly versatile platform for future vaccine development.
However, there are notable concerns and challenges associated with NP‐based vaccines. One significant issue is the instability of mRNA in aqueous solutions, which necessitates storage at ultralow temperatures. For instance, Pfizer's vaccine needs to be kept at −70°C and Moderna's at −20°C (Cheng et al.  ). This requirement complicates distribution, particularly in resource‐limited settings that may lack the necessary infrastructure, thus impacting global accessibility. Additionally, while lipid NPs are effective in delivering mRNA, they can provoke inflammatory responses in organs such as the liver and spleen, raising concerns about potential long‐term effects. Further research is needed to optimize these delivery systems to minimize adverse reactions and improve safety profiles.
In pediatric applications, these NP‐based vaccines have shown efficacy and safety, but they require careful consideration of dosing and potential side effects. The need for dose reductions in younger age groups, as seen with the Pfizer‐BioNTech vaccine, highlights the importance of tailored formulations for children to avoid adverse reactions while maintaining efficacy.

2.3.2

## Cancer
Pediatric cancer poses a unique set of challenges that distinguish it significantly from adult oncology (Houghton and Kurmasheva  ). Pediatric cancers frequently arise from genetic alterations in cells that can occur extremely early, occasionally predating birth. In contrast to many adult malignancies, those in children are not predominantly influenced by lifestyle choices or environmental exposures. Notably, the predominant cancer types in children differ from adult variants, manifesting in distinct regions and necessitating specialized treatments.
Among the most common types are hematological cancer, central nervous system malignancies, and bone cancer (Yang et al. ). Leukemia is the most common cancer in children, accounting for about 28% of all pediatric malignancies (Yang et al. ). The two primary subtypes are acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). ALL, the most prevalent type, makes up around 75% of pediatric leukemia cases and is characterized by the overproduction of immature lymphocytes, or lymphoblasts. It predominantly affects children between the ages of 2 and 5 and has a relatively favorable prognosis with modern treatments. AML, on the other hand, is less common but tends to be more aggressive. It involves the rapid proliferation of myeloid precursors and presents a more challenging therapeutic scenario due to its heterogeneity and higher relapse rates. Brain and spinal cord tumors are the second most common type of pediatric cancer, making up about 26% of all pediatric cancers. Medulloblastoma, one of the most frequent malignant brain tumors in children, arises in the cerebellum and often requires a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation for treatment. It occurs at any age, but most likely to happen in childhood, and is rare in adults. Osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma represent the primary bone cancers in the pediatric population. Osteosarcoma is the most common type of bone cancer among children and adolescents, with a poor prognosis for patients with metastatic or nonresectable primary disease (Rodríguez et al.  ). These malignancies are frequently diagnosed during adolescence, corresponding with periods of rapid bone growth. Neuroblastoma originates from immature nerve cells and primarily affects children under the age of five. Its prognosis varies significantly depending on the age of the patient and disease stage at diagnosis. Wilms tumor, a nephroblastoma, is a common renal cancer in children, usually diagnosed in those under the age of six.
The application of nanomedicine in the field of pediatric oncology has the potential to overcome some of the limitations associated with conventional chemotherapy, such as nonspecific toxicity, drug resistance, and poor bioavailability. NP‐based drug delivery systems can enhance the therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of chemotherapeutic agents, showing potential in improving the treatment of pediatric cancers (Yohan et al.  ).
Vyxeos is a liposomal formulation that encapsulates two chemotherapeutic agents, daunorubicin and cytarabine in a predetermined 1:5 M ratio, is primarily designed for adults and pediatric patients diagnosed with therapy‐related AML or AML accompanied by myelodysplasia‐related changes. This unique combination has demonstrated a synergistic effect against leukemic cells. Notably, compared to the conventional 3 + 7 daunorubicin and cytarabine regimen, Vyxeos has shown a statistically significant enhancement in overall survival for patients with newly diagnosed AML with myelodysplasia‐related AML. This marks a critical advancement, given the typically poor prognosis of these patients and their pressing unmet medical needs. The optimized delivery of daunorubicin and cytarabine provided by the liposomal formulation of Vyxeos exemplifies the potential of nanomedicine in augmenting drug efficacy (Alfayez et al.  ; Lancet et al.  ; Tzogani et al.  ).
Oncaspar (pegaspargase) is a PEGylated form of the enzyme L‐asparaginase, which is used as a part of multi‐agent chemotherapeutic regimen for the treatment of ALL in pediatric and adult patients. The PEGylation process involves the covalent attachment of PEG to the enzyme, which allows for a decreased rate of clearance and a longer duration of action, reducing the frequency of injections required (Avramis and Panosyan  ; Heo, Syed, and Keam  ).
Another application of nanomedicine in pediatric oncology lies within the realm of mitigating the side effects of cancer treatments. Dantrolene sodium is used for the treatment of malignant hyperthermia, a potentially fatal reaction to certain medications used during general anesthesia. While it is not a cancer treatment per se, it plays a critical role in ensuring the safety of pediatric patients who are undergoing cancer treatments that require anesthesia (Ratto and Joyner  ; Rosenberg et al.  ; Rosenberg et al.  ). Ryanodex is a nanosuspension designed for fast and efficient administration. Compared with other formulations of dantrolene sodium, the lyophilized formulation allows Ryanodex to be reconstituted at a higher concentration to achieve a faster response (Eagle Pharmaceuticals  ).
Neulasta (pegfilgrastim) is a remarkable example of nanomedicine in the management of pediatric cancer treatment side effects. It is a pegylated form of the granulocyte‐colony stimulating factor filgrastim, meaning that PEG chains are attached to the filgrastim molecule. This modification results in a longer circulating half‐life and reduces the frequency of administration, thereby enhancing the overall therapeutic effect. Neulasta is employed to prevent neutropenia, a common side effect of chemotherapy characterized by a dangerously low number of neutrophils—the frontline white blood cells that fight off bacterial and fungal infections. By stimulating the bone marrow to produce more neutrophils, Neulasta enhances the immune response and decreases the infection risk, thereby improving the overall safety profile of chemotherapy in pediatric patients (Piedmonte and Treuheit  ).
Emend (aprepitant) is another crucial nanomedicine in pediatric care, used in the prevention of chemotherapy‐induced nausea and vomiting (CINV), a common side effect in children undergoing chemotherapy (Hesketh et al.  ). As a neurokinin‐1 receptor antagonist, it is often used in combination with other antiemetic medications to optimize the management of CINV (Roila et al.  ). This NP formulation of aprepitant enhances its bioavailability and extends its duration of action. This improved drug performance contributes significantly to the quality of life for pediatric patients undergoing intensive treatments by more effectively managing CINV (Pingale et al.  ).
The application of nanomedicine in pediatric oncology has indeed shown potential in overcoming many of the limitations associated with conventional chemotherapy, such as nonspecific toxicity, drug resistance, and poor bioavailability. The encapsulation of chemotherapeutic agents in NPs can enhance therapeutic efficacy and improve safety profiles. For instance, Vyxeos and Oncaspar are excellent examples of how nanotechnology can be utilized to optimize drug delivery and reduce the frequency of treatments, thus improving patient compliance and outcomes.
However, despite these promising advancements, there are notable limitations and challenges remains. One significant concern is the variability in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics among pediatric patients, which complicates the dosing and safety profiles of NP‐based drugs. Children's developing bodies may react differently to these treatments compared to adults, requiring careful consideration and tailored dosing regimens to avoid adverse effects. Moreover, while nanomedicine can mitigate some side effects of cancer treatments, it is not without its own risks. For instance, the long‐term biocompatibility and potential toxicity of NPs remain areas of active research. Some studies have raised concerns about the accumulation of NPs in organs such as the liver and spleen, which could lead to unforeseen health issues over time. Therefore, ensuring the safety, efficacy, and accessibility of these innovative treatments will be critical in maximizing their benefits and improving health outcomes for pediatric cancer patients worldwide.

## Infectious Diseases Other Diseases
Infectious diseases continue to pose significant health challenges globally, particularly among pediatric populations (Li and Wang  ). Children are particularly vulnerable to infectious diseases due to their developing immune systems and often present different symptoms and complications compared to adults (Simon, Hollander, and McMichael  ). The application of nanomedicine in this field promises to revolutionize the delivery of antimicrobials, enhancing their efficacy and reducing toxicity.
Pegasys (peginterferon alfa‐2a) and Pegintron (peginterferon alfa‐2b) are potent examples of the application of nanomedicine in treating infectious diseases, specifically Hepatitis C. Both are interferon proteins, which are naturally produced by the immune system to combat viral infections. Interferons work by binding to interferon receptors on cell surfaces, setting off a chain of intracellular signals that lead to the transcription of antiviral genes (Pestka  ). Pegasys and Pegintron are PEGylated versions of interferon alfa, which means they are covalently attached to a molecule of PEG. This modification significantly extends their half‐life in the body, allowing for less frequent dosing. It also reduces the immunogenicity of the protein, reducing the likelihood of an immune response to the drug (Keating  ; Zeuzem  ). Although these medicines are primarily used in adults, they have been studied and, in some cases, used off‐label in the pediatric population (Aziz  ; Nardin et al.  ; Peginterferon Alfa  ).
In the fight against systemic fungal infections, AmBisome and Abelcet have shown promising results. AmBisome and Abelcet are liposomal formulations of amphotericin B, a broad‐spectrum antifungal medication. Amphotericin B works by binding to ergosterol, a component of fungal cell membranes, and creating pores that disrupt the membrane's integrity, leading to cell death. However, conventional amphotericin B has significant toxicity, including nephrotoxicity and infusion‐related reactions (Hamill  ; Laniado‐Laborín and Cabrales‐Vargas  ). AmBisome and Abelcet encapsulate amphotericin B in liposomes, which are spherical vesicles composed of phospholipid bilayers. These liposomes serve as a vehicle, delivering the drug directly to the site of infection while minimizing exposure to healthy tissues. These modifications significantly reduce the toxicity of amphotericin B, making these drugs more tolerable for patients. Both drugs are FDA‐approved for use in children, and both drugs have been used in the pediatric population (Groll et al.  ).

2.3.3.2

## Hematological Disease
Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is a common pediatric health issue often managed through oral iron supplements. However, some children may not adequately respond to or tolerate these treatments, necessitating the use of intravenous iron preparations. DexFerrum and INFed (low molecular weight iron dextran), Dexferrum (high‐molecular‐weight iron dextran), Ferrlecit (sodium ferric gluconate), and Venofer (iron sucrose) represent nanomedicine solutions in this context (Auerbach and Rodgers  ). These agents exhibit different properties due to variations in their molecular structures and sizes, leading to differences in their physicochemical and pharmacokinetic characteristics. The iron‐carbohydrate complexes in these formulations aim to minimize the adverse effects associated with free iron and to improve the iron bioavailability, thereby effectively addressing the condition (Fütterer et al.  ; Lyseng‐Williamson and Keating  ).
Anemia is a common complication among children with chronic kidney disease. Mircera is a PEGylated NP of epoetin beta, a recombinant form of erythropoietin. The nanoformulation of epoetin beta significantly extends its half‐life, from 7 to 20 h to around 135 h, while also allowing for controlled release (Abdellatif and Alsowinea  ).
Hemophilia A and B are congenital bleeding disorders characterized by deficiencies in clotting factors VIII or VIX, respectively, leading to excessive bleeding. The traditional treatment for hemophilia is replacement of the clotting factors, which has a short‐half life and therefore requires frequent intravenous administrations. Adynovate and Rebinyn are PEGylated Factor VIII and Factor VIX NPs, respectively, for treatment of hemophilia. These recombinant nanoformulations have been found to reduce clearance and prolong half‐life of the administered coagulation factor, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy (Chen et al.  ; Nederlof et al.  ).

2.3.3.3

## Neurological and Psychiatric Disorders
Nanomedicine also holds promise for pediatric populations suffering from neurological and psychiatric disorders. For instance, Invega (paliperidone) and Focalin XR (dexmethylphenidate), which are used in the management of schizophrenia and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder, respectively, employ NP technology for a prolonged drug‐release mechanism (Stein, Weiss, and Hlavaty  ). This enhances medication compliance, a significant challenge in pediatric populations, by reducing the frequency of dosage.

2.3.3.4

## Endocrine and Pulmonary Disorders
Fensolvi (triptorelin), a twice‐yearly injectable gonadotropin‐releasing hormone agonist, has gained FDA approval for the treatment of pediatric patients 2 years of age and older with central precocious puberty (CPP). Fensolvi for injectable suspension is a sterile polymeric matrix formulation of leuprolide acetate for subcutaneous use. This medication is encapsulated within a biodegradable poly(DL‐lactide‐co‐glycolide) polymer gel formulation, serving as a drug delivery system that provides sustained drug release. Upon injection, the gel transforms into a biodegradable solid which remains just beneath the skin for 6 months, creating a steady and consistent source of medication for the body. This technology helps inhibit the waves of GnRH that induce early puberty, reduces the frequency of administration, and thereby improves patient compliance, which effectively manages CPP in pediatric patients (Tolmar  ).
Curosurf (poractant alfa) is another unique example of nanomedicine used in the pediatric population, particularly for neonates. It is a pulmonary surfactant, a naturally occurring substance consisting of phospholipids and proteins that reduce surface tension within the alveoli in the lungs, thereby preventing their collapse and facilitating gas exchange. Curosurf is used for the treatment and prevention of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), also known as hyaline membrane disease, a common condition in preterm neonates characterized by surfactant deficiency. The drug is a nanoscale liposomal formulation, which mimics the natural lung surfactant, and has been shown to improve lung function and reduce the mortality associated with RDS (Sardesai et al.  ).

## Nanomedicines Approved Outside United States for Pediatric Use
Nanomedicines that are approved for pediatric use abroad but not in the United States offer further insight into the future of this field, as these drugs have the potential to become FDA approved. Nanotop, Nanocoll, and Nanoalbumon are nanocolloids of human serum albumin that have gained approval by the European Medicines Agency (EMA). These drugs serve as contrast agents for positron emission topography and single‐photon emission topography scans and are radiolabeled with Technetium‐99. They are useful in visualizing cancer, as they can be used for the intraoperative detection of the sentinel lymph node in cancer, thereby allowing for clinicians to determine if a patient's cancer has spread to the lymph nodes. These drugs can also be used to evaluate pulmonary function in patients with lung damage (Marenco et al.  ).
Nanocis is a technetium‐labeled rhenium sulfide nanocolloid approved by the EMA as a contrast agent for diagnostic imaging. It is administered via a subcutaneous injection into the desired anatomical area and is used for lymphoscintigraphy and gastroesophageal scintigraphy for studies of lymphatic drainage in cancer and the gastroesophageal reflux, respectively (Medvedeva et al.  ). Another contrast agent approved by the EMA is Hepatate, a technetium‐labeled stannous fluoride nanocolloid. Like Nanocis, Hepatate is used for lymphoscintigraphy and gastroesophageal studies. It also can act as a contrast agent for imaging the liver and spleen (Durbin Pharamceuticals  ).
Mifamurtide is a liposomal modulator of innate immunity approved by the EMA to treat osteosarcoma in pediatric populations. Mifamurtide activates macrophages and monocytes, thus increasing tumoricidal activity. When used in combination with chemotherapy, mifamurtide has been found to improve both overall survival and disease‐free survival (Nardin et al.  ). The nanoformulation of mifamurtide confers advantages such as increased blood circulation time, increased tumor uptake and reduced toxicity (Rodríguez et al.  ).

## Summary
Nanomedicine represents a transformative approach in pediatric healthcare, offering substantial benefits across various disease categories. Nanomedicine can revolutionize treatment protocols by enabling targeted drug delivery, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. The use of liposomal formulations like Doxil and Abelcet has already shown promise in reducing toxicity in pediatric patients, highlighting the potential for NPs to improve outcomes and quality of life for children. And the reduced side effect profile of NP‐based therapies enhances their suitability for long‐term use in children. Additionally, the flexibility in the design of NPs offers unprecedented opportunities to enhance drug delivery efficiency and specificity, which is crucial for treating complex pediatric conditions. The ability of NPs to improve drug solubility and stability, such as Dexferrum and Pegintron, can enhance the efficacy profiles. The controlled or prolonged release properties of NPs, such as Fensolvi, Invega, and Mircera, can optimize drug delivery, ensuring consistent therapeutic levels and reducing the burden of frequent dosing. The ability to administer treatments less frequently can improve patient compliance and overall treatment outcomes, and it is also beneficial for chronic conditions requiring long‐term management. Similarly, NP‐based vaccines have demonstrated improved immune responses and stability, which are crucial for effective immunization in children. The rapid development of nanotechnology‐based COVID‐19 vaccines underscores the potential of this technology to address pediatric infectious diseases more effectively.
Overall, the unique properties of nanomedicines provide an unparalleled opportunity to address the specific physiological and developmental challenges of pediatric patients, paving the way for more effective and safer therapeutic interventions.

## Ongoing Clinical Trials
In spite of the aforementioned advancements in nanomedicines for pediatrics, the need for pediatric drugs remains largely unmet. Clinical trials are a necessary step in ensuring the safety and efficacy of novel drugs and offer insight into the future of this field. There are numerous clinical trials underway in the United States for pediatric nanomedicines that could potentially become approved by the FDA for pediatric use in the future (Table  ). These drugs offer promising advancements that address the unique challenges of treating pediatric diseases and could act to reduce the disparity between pediatric and adult drug development and availability.
 One promising ongoing clinical trial is the use of convection‐enhanced delivery (CED) for treating diffuse midline gliomas. The trial is investigating the CED of MTX110, a water‐soluble Panobinostat NP, and gadolinium for the treatment of newly diagnosed diffuse midline gliomas. In this trial, CED will be utilized to deliver MTX110 and gadolinium in a pump over a span of 9–11 days for pediatric patients aged three to 18 years old (U.S. National Library of Medicine  ). CED involves a microcatheter inserted directly into the interstitial spaces of the brain, thereby bypassing the BBB, allowing for a high concentration of the drug therapy to be delivered directly to the tumor site, and minimizing systematic absorption. This delivery method utilizes bulk flow, which relies on pressure differentials instead of concentration gradients. This eliminates the need for high concentrations of drug in order for delivery to occur (Mehta, Sonabend, and Bruce  ). This method leverages the properties of nanomedicine to bypass the BBB, a significant hurdle in treating brain tumors. The use of CED to deliver Panobinostat NPs directly to the tumor site minimizes systemic absorption and maximizes therapeutic efficacy. This approach exemplifies the precision and targeted capabilities of nanomedicine, which are crucial for treating complex pediatric conditions with minimal side effects.
Another clinical trial aims to use Ferumoxytol, an iron‐oxide NP, in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in order to detect kidney transplant rejection, which has the potential to provide a noninvasive alternative to biopsies. This study entails giving subjects an intravenous injection of 5 mg Fe/kg of Ferumoxytol as a contrast agent to visualize macrophage infiltration in kidney transplants. Macrophages can phagocytose iron oxide NPs, which can be seen in magnetic resonance images and thereby allow for allograft rejection to be quantified (U.S. National Library of Medicine  ). The ability of iron oxide NPs to enhance MRI provides a noninvasive and highly sensitive method for monitoring organ transplant health. This advancement not only reduces the need for biopsies but also allows for earlier detection and intervention, improving patient outcomes.
Nab‐rapamycin's application in treating recurrent or refractory solid tumors showcases the versatility of nanomedicine in oncology. Nab‐rapamycin, also known as nab‐sirolimus, is an albumin‐bound NP that acts as an inhibitor of the mammalian target of rapamycin signaling pathway, which regulates cellular metabolism and growth (Feng et al.  ). Nab‐rapamycin is being used to treat recurrent or refractory solid tumors in a Phase I trial. In this trial, participants are between the ages of 12 months and 21 years and receive a combination treatment of rapamycin along with the chemotherapy drugs temozolomide (TMZ) and irinotecan hydrochloride. Nab‐rapamycin is delivered intravenously over 30 min on days 1 and 8, initiating cycle 1. Beginning in cycle 2, TMZ and irinotecan hydrochloride are delivered orally on days 1 through 5 along with rapamycin. Each cycle lasts 21 days and can be repeated up to 35 times (U.S. National Library of Medicine  ). The safety and efficacy of nab‐rapamycin is also being evaluated in a Phase 2 basket trial. In this clinical trial, patients 12 years and older with malignant solid tumors containing inactivating mutations in the TSC1/TSC2 genes are administered an IV infusion of nab‐rapamycin (U.S. National Library of Medicine  ). Previous studies that found that nab‐rapamycin leads to greater intratumoral drug accumulation and growth inhibition of cancer cells compared to its oral form, offering a promising treatment for pediatric cancer patients (Wagner et al.  ). The albumin‐bound NP formulation improves drug solubility and stability, facilitating better intratumoral accumulation and reduced systemic toxicity. This is particularly beneficial for pediatric cancer patients, who are often more susceptible to the adverse effects of conventional chemotherapy. The ability to combine nab‐rapamycin with other chemotherapeutic agents in a controlled manner further enhances its therapeutic potential.
In addition to these clinical trials in the United States, there are several trials either in progress or recently completed abroad. The iron hydroxide adipate tartate (IHAT)‐GUT study in the United Kingdom and the Gambia is a Phase II investigation on the use of IHAT, an Fe(III) oxo‐hydroxide NP, to treat IDA in children between the ages of 6 months and 35 months old. Iron supplementations to treat anemia are typically administered orally but are associated with side effects such as bloody diarrhea and gut inflammation. These adverse events are especially burdensome in countries with a high prevalence of enteric infections and poor infection control. This study therefore compared the non‐inferiority of IHAT to ferrous sulfate in terms of treating iron deficiency and decreasing diarrhea risk. Participants either received a 12.5 mg dose of IHAT or ferrous sulfate daily, or a placebo (Pereira et al.  ). The investigators determined that IHAT achieved non‐inferiority in its ability to treat iron deficiency and lowered the incidence rates of diarrhea. They have plans to move on to a Phase III trial (Mohammed et al.  ). This approach addresses common side effects associated with traditional iron supplements, such as bloody diarrhea and gut inflammation, particularly problematic in regions with high enteric infection prevalence. While the study's findings that IHAT is non‐inferior to ferrous sulfate and lowers diarrhea incidence are promising, Phase III trials must ensure consistent results across diverse populations and investigate long‐term effects.
A clinical trial in Egypt is recruiting participants for a study on the use of rectal dexmedetomidine niosomes (DEX‐NANO) for postoperative analgesia in pediatric cancer patients following bone marrow biopsy or aspiration. This study aims to investigate the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of DEX‐NANO in children aged 3–7 years old. Then, 1 μg/kg of DEX‐NANO will be administered to participants via a rectal suppository 30 min prior to anesthesia and serum concentrations of DEX will be compared to participants who received DEX intravenously or rectally (U.S. National Library of Medicine  ). This clinical trial highlights nanotechnology's potential to improve pain management with less invasive methods. Although rectal administration offers a viable alternative to intravenous methods, concerns about absorption variability and patient comfort need addressing.
Another study in Egypt explored the use of a voriconazole‐loaded self‐nanoemulsifying drug delivery system that forms oil‐in‐water nanoemulsions to treat tinea versicolor infections. Voriconazole is a commonly used antifungal medication but its use is limited by its unfavorable pharmacological properties, including low solubility and nonlinear pharmacokinetics. In prior studies, NP‐based delivery systems of Voriconazole have been found to improve solubility and promote controlled release of the drug (de Almeida Campos et al.  ). In this Phase II study, which included both children and adults, participants received a gel nanoformulation of Voriconazole once or twice daily, or were given a placebo, and clinical improvement of the infection and patient satisfaction were assessed (U.S. National Library of Medicine  ). This study shows nanotechnology's capacity to enhance solubility and controlled release of antifungal medications. While early Phase II results indicate clinical improvement and patient satisfaction, long‐term studies are necessary to monitor adverse reactions and formulation stability.
These trials reflect the broader trend of integrating nanotechnology into pediatric drug development. The unique properties of NPs, such as their ability to be engineered for specific delivery, reduced toxicity, and enhanced bioavailability, are being harnessed to address the unmet needs in pediatric medicine. However, the ethical considerations of using novel nanotechnologies in vulnerable pediatric populations must be carefully managed, ensuring informed consent and minimizing potential risks. Despite the advancements highlighted by these trials, several limitations and challenges remain. The small sample sizes often seen in pediatric trials can limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the ethical considerations surrounding pediatric trials, such as obtaining informed consent and ensuring minimal risk, complicate the research process.

## Preclinical Studies and Future Directions
Preclinical studies offer valuable insight into nanoformulations that may undergo clinical trials to treat pediatric diseases in the future. Preclinical studies are a necessary step before beginning clinical trials and entail testing the drug using animal models to determine dosing and toxicity.
One prominent example of preclinical studies for pediatric nanomedicines are those investigating the use of nanotherapies for Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). The current standard treatment for DMD includes glucocorticoids, but the long‐term use of these drugs is associated with many side effects that reduce the overall quality of life for DMD patients. Furthermore, gene therapy methods to restore the mutated dystrophin gene have been faced with pharmacokinetic and drug delivery challenges. Nanomedicine therapies aim to overcome these obstacles (Nance et al.  ). Many preclinical studies have focused on using antisense oligonucleotides (AONs) to treat DMD, which modulate alternative splicing to allow for expression of the dystrophin gene that is mutated in DMD. Polymeric encapsulations of AONs can prevent rapid drug clearance and degradation of the oligonucleotides and allow for controlled release of the drug. In a mouse model of DMD with a nonsense mutation in exon 23 of the dystrophin gene, polymeric AONs were found to enhance exon‐skipping efficiency compared to nonencapsulated AONs (Kim et al.  ). Rimessi et al. found that cationic PMMA NPs loaded with AONs restored dystrophin expression in striate muscle in mdx mice, whereas an identical dose of naked AON had no observable effect (Rimessi et al.  ).
Lipid NPs have also been used to deliver AONs. Afzal et al. developed a nanolipodendrosome carrier that improved muscle mass in mdx mice, reduced markers of inflammation, and did not demonstrate significant toxicity (Afzal et al.  ) Bubble liposomes, which are PEG‐modified liposomes that contain contrast gas, used in combination with ultrasound exposure have been found to improve uptake of AONs and subsequently increase functional dystrophin expression (Negishi et al.  ) Studies using viral NPs to treat DMD have also achieved positive results. Muir et al. transduced dermal fibroblasts with a lentivirus carrying the dystrophin gene using the inducible MyoD‐ER(T) system, which were then engrafted into dystrophic skeletal muscle and found to induce dystrophin expression (Muir et al.  ).
Another noteworthy preclinical study involves the use of nanotechnology in Ewing Sarcoma treatment. Baldwin et al. developed a NP formulation of talazoparib (NanoTLZ) in an attempt to modify the toxicity profile of a combination treatment with talazoparib (TLZ) and TMZ (P. Baldwin et al.  ). TLZ, a potent PARP inhibitor that enhances DNA damage was evaluated as a treatment for BRCA‐deficient cancers (P. Baldwin et al.  ; Belz et al.  ; Yang et al.  ; Zhang et al.  ). The combination of TLZ and TMZ has shown potency in Ewing Sarcoma treatment, but exhibited toxicity in both preclinical and clinical trials, necessitating a substantial reduction of TMZ dosage. The nanoformulation approach resulted in decreased gross toxicity and increased maximum tolerated dose compared to orally administered TLZ. Importantly, the dosage of TMZ did not need to be reduced when combined with NanoTLZ, which indicates that the nanotechnology‐based delivery system can significantly mitigate the systemic toxicity associated with the combination of oral TLZ and TMZ. This research presents an exciting prospect in the application of nanomedicine for pediatric cancer therapy, highlighting the potential benefits of NP drug delivery systems in addressing therapeutic challenges in pediatric treatments (P. Baldwin et al.  ).
Advancements in nanotechnology have also been explored for improving oral bioavailability and palatability of pediatric drug formulations. Deng et al. developed a NP‐based orodispersible pediatric drug delivery platform with the intention to enhance the bioavailability and taste of poorly water‐soluble and unpalatable therapeutics (Deng et al.  ). Two unpalatable Biopharmaceutics Classification System II/IV compounds, lopinavir (LPV) and Ritonavir (RTV), were encapsulated in Eudragit E PO NPs, yielding over 98% encapsulation efficiency and confirming the taste‐masking effect. The lyophilized LPV and RTV NPs rapidly dispersed in water within 7 s, showing potential for improved patient compliance due to ease of administration (Deng et al.  ). Furthermore, the developed LPV/RTV NP formulations demonstrated increased oral bioavailability in a rat model, outperforming the commercial LPV/RTV tablet, Kaletra. This research presents a potential breakthrough in pediatric medication formulation, which often grapples with challenges of drug palatability and oral bioavailability (Deng et al.  ).
In the realm of pediatric anti‐helminthic treatment, praziquantel, the drug of choice for schistosomiasis, faces challenges of low aqueous solubility and unpleasant taste, which may hinder its bioavailability and pediatric compliance (Gonzalez, Ramírez Rigo, and Gonzalez Vidal  ). In an attempt to overcome these challenges, Gonzalez et al. developed a dry nanosuspension of praziquantel, employing a combination of high‐pressure homogenization and spray drying for extemporaneous use in a more palatable vehicle. After the homogenization process, a significant reduction in particle size was achieved, and the NPs were microencapsulated using a spray drying technique. The reconstituted dried powders maintained the particle size distribution in the nanometric range and demonstrated an improved dissolution performance. This development of NP‐loaded powders can potentially enhance the administration of praziquantel to preschool‐age children.
The application of CRISPR‐Cas9 for cancer therapy has been a challenge due to low editing efficiency and potential toxicity of existing delivery systems (Selvakumar et al.  ; Xu et al.  ). Addressing this, Rosenblum et al. developed an efficient lipid NP delivery system for Cas9 mRNA and single guide RNAs, demonstrating promising results in both orthotopic glioblastoma and disseminated ovarian tumor models (Rosenblum et al.  ). They achieved high gene editing efficiency (up to ~70% in glioblastoma and ~80% in ovarian tumors) with these targeted lipid NPs, leading to significant inhibition of tumor growth and increased survival rates. This opens new possibilities not only for cancer treatments but also for targeted gene editing in noncancerous tissues.
Preclinical studies are critical in evaluating the potential of nanomedicines for pediatric applications. Recent advancements have shown promising results, particularly in the use of NPs for targeted drug delivery. For instance, studies have demonstrated that polymeric NPs can effectively deliver AONs to treat DMD, enhancing exon‐skipping efficiency and restoring dystrophin expression in muscle tissues. Similarly, lipid NPs have been developed to deliver AONs, showing improved muscle mass and reduced inflammation in animal models. These studies highlight the potential of nanotechnology to address specific challenges in pediatric diseases, such as drug stability and targeted delivery.
The future of nanomedicine in pediatric healthcare is poised for transformative advancements, focusing on enhancing drug delivery, reducing toxicity, and improving patient compliance. One promising direction is the development of multifunctional NPs that combine diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, enabling real‐time monitoring of treatment efficacy and disease progression. Moreover, advancements in CRISPR‐Cas9 delivery systems using lipid NPs could revolutionize gene therapy for pediatric genetic disorders, offering precise and efficient gene editing with reduced side effects. The exploration of novel materials and surface modifications aims to improve the biocompatibility and stability of NPs, addressing long‐term safety concerns.

## Conclusion
Nanomedicine has the potential to revolutionize the treatment landscape of pediatric diseases. As we have reviewed, nanomedicine has already had a significant impact on the management of a wide array of pediatric conditions, from therapeutics and vaccines for cancer to infectious diseases, and holds the promise to overcome many pediatric‐specific challenges in drug treatment. The advancements in nanotechnology have already demonstrated the ability to enhance drug delivery, improve therapeutic efficacy, and reduce the adverse effects of conventional treatments. Innovative strategies, such as stimuli‐responsive systems and controlled release systems, are being studied extensively. These systems offer the potential to address current challenges in pediatric treatment, including improving drug bioavailability and reducing drug toxicity.
Additionally, the integration of nanomedicine with other emerging technologies, such as gene editing tools like CRISPR‐Cas9, could pave the way for groundbreaking treatments for genetic and other complex pediatric disorders. The ability of nanomedicine to provide personalized and targeted therapies represents a transformative advancement in pediatric healthcare. The use of NPs to deliver drugs precisely where they are needed, while minimizing systemic exposure, offers significant benefits in terms of efficacy and safety. For instance, the development of multifunctional NPs that combine diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities can enable real‐time monitoring and tailored treatment adjustments, further enhancing patient outcomes.
Despite these promising developments, the number of FDA‐approved nanomedicines for pediatric use remains limited. This gap underscores the critical need for continued research and development to fully realize the potential of nanomedicine in pediatric applications. In the same time, several challenges remain to be addressed. Regulatory hurdles, ethical considerations, and the high cost of developing and manufacturing NP‐based therapies pose significant barriers to widespread adoption. Ensuring equitable access to these advanced treatments, particularly in resource‐limited settings, is also a critical consideration. Overcoming these challenges will require collaborative efforts from researchers, clinicians, regulatory bodies, and industry stakeholders.
The potential of nanomedicine to significantly improve pediatric healthcare has already been demonstrated. Its ability to provide targeted drug delivery, improved efficacy, reduced toxicity, better pharmacokinetics, and improved compliance presents a promising future for the management of pediatric diseases. While challenges exist, the ongoing research and active clinical trials globally provide a hopeful outlook for the future of nanomedicine in pediatric healthcare. The field is burgeoning with possibilities, and as our understanding and technology progresses, we can expect an increasing number of nanomedicine applications to benefit pediatric patients in the near future.
