## Abstract

## Background
Fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC), a rare and fatal liver cancer lacking effective drug therapy, is driven by the DNAJ-PKAc fusion oncoprotein. However, the underlying mechanism of DNAJ-PKAc’s role in FLC tumour growth remains enigmatic.

## Objective
We sought to determine the protein kinase-mediated signalling networks that drive growth and proliferation in FLC.

## Design
We integrated a combination of newly established preclinical models of FLC and an unbiased polypharmacology-based approach to identify downstream kinases involved in DNAJ-PKAc-mediated FLC cell growth. We validated our findings in multiple patient-derived mouse models and patient tumours.

## Results
Functional screening, coupled with computational analysis, highlighted Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) as vital for FLC cell viability. Genetic and pharmacological PLK1 inhibition significantly reduced FLC cell growth, inducing apoptosis. Further studies showed DNAJ-PKAc’s centrosomal presence and direct interaction with PLK1, revealing a novel mechanism that promotes PLK1 activation and mitotic progression. Clinical-grade PLK1 inhibitors effectively suppressed FLC tumour growth across multiple preclinical models, including patient-derived xenograft and an orthotopic model of FLC, suggesting promising therapeutic avenues.

## Conclusion
Our findings underscore the role of DNAJ-PKAc in rewiring signalling networks and highlight valuable clinical implications for PLK1-targeted therapies for FLC.

Keywords: cancer, pharmacology, hepatobiliary cancer

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC), a rare and lethal form of liver cancer lacking effective drug therapies, is predominantly driven by the DNAJ-PKAc fusion oncoprotein; however, the precise mechanism through which DNAJ-PKAc contributes to FLC tumour growth has remained elusive.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
We elucidate a new molecular mechanism by which the DNAJ-PKAc oncoprotein fuels FLC’s aggressive growth.
Through systematic screening approaches, we have identified the crucial role of Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) in driving FLC growth.
Both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of PLK1 exhibited remarkable efficacy in restraining FLC growth across various patient-derived in vitro and in vivo models.
Furthermore, our mechanistic investigations have unveiled that DNAJ-PKAC localises to the centrosome, where it engages in a high-affinity interaction with PLK1.
Our research advances the mechanistic understanding of how DNAJ-PKAc oncoprotein promotes FLC pathogenesis and offers a promising avenue for the development of effective treatments.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
The ongoing clinical progress of various PLK1 inhibitors makes our discoveries particularly relevant.
Our in vivo results provide convincing proof of concept from both animal models and human patient tumour tissues and position us promisingly to move forward into testing PLK1 inhibitors in human clinical trials for FLC treatment.

## Introduction
 Fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC) is a life-threatening variant of liver cancer affecting adolescents and young adults. FLC has a dismal overall 5-year survival rate of ~30%, and no effective therapy currently exists. These otherwise healthy individuals exhibit no early warning signs resulting in advanced disease at the time of diagnosis. Consequently, their disease cannot be completely removed surgically, compelling these individuals to be reliant on systemic therapy. In this regard, FLC is refractory to all chemotherapies and targeted agents. Thus, overcoming ‘chemoresistance’ in FLC represents an unmet clinical need and a high-priority research goal.
Genetic and functional studies have established that FLC is driven by a fusion oncoprotein DNAJ-PKAc. DNAJ-PKAc consists of the chaperonin-binding domain of heat shock protein 40 (DNAJ) fused to the C-subunit of protein kinase A (PKAc). The fusion protein is uniquely expressed in FLCs and retains most, if not all, of the biochemical properties of the wild-type PKA. Studies in mice bearing a homologous fusion implicated that the kinase domain of DNAJB1-PRKACA is necessary for tumour initiation, yet paradoxically, protein kinase activity alone is not sufficient for tumourigenesis. Additionally, cellular and biochemical studies suggest that DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein acts as a scaffold to assemble a unique set of proteins, which results in rewiring of the cellular signalling and oncogenesis. However, the details of the underlying mechanism by which DNAJ-PKAc drives FLC tumour growth remain incompletely understood.
To identify new signalling networks that drive growth and proliferation in FLC, we applied an unbiased systems-based computational approach to discover downstream kinases that are functionally relevant to FLC pathogenesis. The approach, called kinome regularisation (KiR), uses phenotypic screening of a set of well-characterised inhibitors, which collectively inhibit >90% of the kinome, and elastic net regression to compute and deconvolute the kinases that are responsible for a given cellular phenotype. , Here, we applied KiR functional screening and identified 11 kinases that are predicted to be essential for the growth of FLC cells. Follow-up studies confirmed that knockdown of eight kinases (out of 11) led to a statistically significant decrease in the FLC cell viability compared with scrambled small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfected control. Of these, knockdown of Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) led to the most prominent reduction in cell viability in FLC cells. Furthermore, we showed that genetic and pharmacological inhibition of PLK1 selectively reduced growth in several independent patient-derived FLC cells and organotypic tissues. Subsequent studies revealed the centrosomal localisation of DNAJ-PKAc and its direct interaction with PLK1 enhanced mitotic progression. Finally, we demonstrate that multiple clinical-grade PLK1 inhibitors effectively suppress FLC tumour growth in various patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Overall, we propose that the DNAJ-PKAc fusion rewires the network connectivity to promote reliance on PLK1 signalling, which is essential for FLC growth and progression. These findings hold significant clinical implications for potential targeted therapies in treating FLC by exploiting the PLK1 signalling pathway.

## Results

## Discovery of kinases important for FLC cell growth
To identify new signalling networks that drive growth and proliferation in FLC, we applied an unbiased kinome-wide approach (KiR) to discover downstream kinases that are functionally relevant to FLC pathogenesis. The KiR approach leverages the fact that most kinase inhibitors (KIs) are not highly specific and tend to affect multiple targets. Unlike highly specific inhibitors that may target few kinases, our computationally selected set of 32 KIs collectively inhibits over 90% of the kinome, allowing for a more comprehensive analysis of kinase function. By using machine learning to integrate drug response data, KiR identifies the most functionally relevant kinases driving a phenotype rather than relying on a single inhibitor’s activity, which may not fully capture redundancy or compensatory pathways. A key advantage of the KiR approach over genetic or RNA interference (RNAi)-based methods is that small molecule inhibitors can be readily adapted for use in both primary and difficult-to-transfect cell lines, as well as in in vivo models. This method has been extensively validated and applied across various biological systems, including cancer cell migration, cancer cell growth, COVID-19, malaria, herpesvirus and cytokine signalling. Here, we treated previously established AML12-FLC cells (AML12-Clone 14 ) and wild-type AML12 controls with a set of 32 computationally chosen, broad-specific KIs that collectively target a broad range of kinases ( , ). Each inhibitor was examined at eight concentrations, and its effect on cell growth was scored using a real-time growth assay. To generate these data, live-cell images were taken every 2 hours until control (vehicle) treated cells reached 100% confluence, which controls for the effect of varying doubling times of different cell lines. Together, we generated 93 888 microscopy images that were processed and analysed for KiR modelling.
 To identify a set of kinases that affect cell growth, we used elastic net regularisation, a technique that regresses a single target variable (quantified cell growth) against a set of predictor variables (previously determined activities of individual kinases). We used a standard ‘leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV)’ to identify a set of informative kinases at the absolute minimum of the least-mean-square error. The optimisation profile shows that removing certain variables initially boosts model accuracy, suggesting that for some kinases, inhibition levels act as proxy identifiers for specific drugs. These highly specific variables contribute to overfitting by making the model overly reliant on patterns that don’t generalise well beyond the training data ( ). There is a defined optimal point (red line) with the least mean squared errors representing the 11 most ‘informative kinases’ out of >300 kinases that affect the proliferation of AML12-FLC cells ( ). Next, to validate the role of the kinases that we predicted to be important in the growth of FLC cells, we knocked down the expression of 11 kinases in both AML12-FLC and parental control cells and measured their effect on cell viability. Cell viability was determined by cell counting 72 hours post-transfection. Transient transfections of pooled siRNA resulted in a minimum of 50% reduction in the expression of each of the measured kinases, as quantified by quantitative PCR ( ). Our data revealed that the knockdown of eight out of the eleven kinases led to a statistically significant (p<0.05) reduction in AML12-FLC cell viability compared with control cells transfected with scrambled siRNA ( ). Among these, the knockdown of Plk1, Ulk1 and Cdk6 resulted in a reduction of more than two-fold in cell viability ( ). In contrast, although knockdown of Cdk6, Frk, and Plk1 also reduced cell viability, none of these kinases caused a two-fold or greater decrease in viability in parental AML12-WT cells. ( ). Thus, these findings corroborated the predictions made by KiR and substantiated the significance of various kinases, notably PLK1, in sustaining the viability of AML12-FLC cells while remaining biologically insignificant in parental control cells.

## Suppression of PLK1 reduces FLC cell proliferation
To further validate the role of PLK1 kinase in mediating the growth of FLC cells, we depleted its levels by RNAi and measured the effect of PLK1 suppression on cell growth by live-cell imaging. As described above, AML12-FLC cells were transiently transfected with pooled siRNA targeting Plk1 or scrambled control. Cells were replated 72 hours post-transfections in 96-well plates containing reduced serum (1% fetal bovine serum (FBS)), and cell growth was monitored using the Incucyte imaging system. Knockdown of Plk1 significantly reduced AML12-FLC cells’ growth, while scrambled control did not affect cell growth ( ). Consistently, knockdown of PLK1 in patient-derived FLC4 cells ( ) also led to a significant decrease in cell viability. Together, these data suggest that PLK1 kinase is essential for FLC cell growth.

## FLC cells are hypersensitive to PLK1 inhibition
Next, we asked if pharmacological inhibition of PLK1 could also inhibit FLC cell growth. Our data showed that AML12-FLC cells are more sensitive to PLK1 inhibition than their parental AML12 control cells ( ). These data are also consistent with a previous study showing human normal hepatocytes, QSG-7701, and Chang liver cells as well as mouse normal liver cells are less sensitive to PLK1 inhibition. Furthermore, PLK1 inhibitor treatment also reduces the growth of patient-derived FLC4 cells in a dose-dependent manner, with clinical-grade onvansertib (EC50 25 nM) and plogosertib (EC50 8 nM) outperforming BI-2536 response ( ). In contrast, the EC50 values for inhibiting the growth of HepaRG, an immortalised untransformed hepatocyte cell line, are >100-fold higher ( ). Similar levels of hypersensitivity to clinical-grade PLK1 inhibitors were also observed in three independent patient-derived FLC cell lines ( ). It is well-established that many tumour types without the DNAJ-PKAc fusion oncoprotein still exhibit elevated PLK1 activity compared with normal tissues. Therefore, we investigated whether the increased sensitivity of FLC cells to PLK1 inhibitors, compared with untransformed hepatocytes, is due to their higher mitotic index. To address this, we first assessed the efficacy of plogosertib in FLC cells and compared it with a panel of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines ( ). We selected mostly mesenchymal-like HCC cell lines with comparable doubling times for a fair comparison ( ), as both tumour types originate in the liver. Our data demonstrate that the presence of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion significantly increases the sensitivity of FLC cells to plogosertib, as shown by notable differences in dose-response between FLC and HCC cells. Specifically, in HCC cells lacking the DNAJ-PKAc fusion, the EC50 for onvansertib ranges from 600 nM to 1900 nM. In contrast, FLC cell lines with the fusion exhibit an EC50 between 3 nM and 15 nM. We then explored whether the expression of DNAJ-PKAc could enhance the sensitivity of HCC cells to PLK1 inhibitors. A recent study revealed that the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated introduction of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion oncoprotein into HepG2 cells activates a transcriptional programme akin to that observed in FLC. Therefore, we examined whether HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA cells that express the DNAJ-PKAc fusion (H33 clone) exhibit heightened sensitivity to PLK1 inhibitors. Our data demonstrate increased susceptibility of H33 cells to onvansertib (EC50, a dose that produces 50% of its maximum effect, 20 nM), in contrast to parental HepG2 cells (EC50 300 nM), while exhibiting minimal to no effect on primary human hepatocytes ( ). We next examined whether the expression of DNAJ-PKAc in untransformed cells could confer sensitivity to PLK1 inhibitors. We found that both onvansertib and plogosertib significantly suppressed growth in parental HEK293 cells at high micromolar concentrations. However, at low nanomolar concentrations (EC50 observed in FLC cells), these inhibitors had no effect on HEK293 cell growth ( ). In contrast, expression of DNAJ-PKAc in HEK293 cells induced significant sensitivity to both PLK1 inhibitors, even at low nanomolar concentrations, consistent with our findings in HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA cells. Interestingly, expression of DNAJ-PKAc-K27H (a kinase-dead PKAc mutant) or DNAJ-PKAc-H32Q (a DNAJB1 variant with impaired HSP70 binding) did not confer this sensitivity to PLK1 inhibition ( ). Furthermore, overexpression of PRKACA alone results in a slight increase in PLK1 inhibitor sensitivity, whereas DNAJB1 alone has no effect. These results suggest that while PRKACA contributes to PLK1 dependency, the fusion oncoprotein amplifies this effect, reinforcing its role in driving tumour cell sensitivity to PLK1 inhibitors. In alignment with the growth-suppressing effects of the PLK1 inhibitors, we also observed an increase in apoptosis as measured by live cell cleaved caspase 3 reporter assay ( ). Therefore, while high PLK1 activity is common in many cancer cells, the presence of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion markedly enhances sensitivity to PLK1 inhibition.
 Next, we investigated whether the hypersensitivity to PLK1 inhibitors in FLC cells is dependent on the PKA activity. We treated FLX1 cells with a low dose (500 nM) of the selective PKA inhibitor, BLU2864, and then measured the dose response of onvansertib and plogosertib. Our data indicate that the PKA inhibitor significantly reduced the efficacy of the PLK1 inhibitors, suggesting that PLK1 sensitivity in FLC cells is dependent on PKA activity ( ). Finally, as expected, inhibition of PLK1 resulted in a reduction in the expression levels of several FLC-associated marker genes, including SLC16A14, MUC13 and CREB3L1 ( ). Together, these data suggest that DNAJ-PKAc fusion alters the network wiring to create a hypersensitivity towards PLK1 inhibition in FLC cells.

## PLK1 activity is increased in FLC tumours and FLC cells
PLK1 is a critical cell-cycle regulator frequently overexpressed in various human cancers. We investigated whether PLK1 activity is specifically elevated in FLC by using a combination of previously generated expression profiling and newly generated bulk RNA-sequencing profiling from FLC tumours. Analysis using the Reactome PLK1 activity 87-gene signature (R-HSA-2565942) revealed that PLK1 activity is significantly higher in FLC samples compared with non-tumour liver samples ( ). Similar results were obtained from independent cohorts at Fred Hutchinson (FH), Cornell and the The Cancer Genome Atlas. Further analysis of the metastatic FLC cohort also showed significant enrichment of the PLK1 signature, suggesting that this activity is maintained through tumour progression ( ). Furthermore, the induction of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion oncoprotein into primary human hepatocytes through CRISPR-Cas9-mediated expression led to significant enrichment of the PLK signature ( ). Further examination of this signature revealed increased expression of PLK1 itself and several of its downstream targets, including AURKA, NEK2 and CCNB1/2 in FLC samples compared with non-tumour livers ( ).
 Next, we sought to confirm the protein level changes in PLK1 and its downstream targets. We measured the levels of PLK1 and its mitotic targets in AML12-FLC and patient-derived FLC cell lines. Our data show that the expression of the DNAJ-PKac fusion oncoprotein led to an increase in PLK1 levels and its downstream targets such as Aurora A, phosphorylated translationally controlled tumour protein (TCTP) and total TCTP levels in AML12 cells ( ). Consistently, we observed an increase in PLK1 and its downstream targets, including Aurora A, TCTP and phospho-CDC25C, in patient-derived FLC cells compared with untransformed HepaRG cells ( ). Additionally, depleting PKA, which predominantly reduced DNAJ-PKAc levels in FLX1 cells, led to a decrease in the total levels of PLK1 and its downstream targets, TCTP and Aurora A ( ). Next, we investigated whether the functional activity of DNAJ-PKAc is crucial for regulating PLK1 levels in FLC. Overexpression of DNAJ-PKAc in HEK293 cells resulted in a marked increase in PLK1 levels ( ). However, this increase was absent when we overexpressed DNAJ-PKAc-K27H (a kinase-dead form of PKAc) or DNAJ-PKAc-H32Q, a DNAJB1 variant with impaired HSP70 binding ( ). These results strongly suggest that the functional DNAJ-PKAc is essential for driving the increase in PLK1 levels.
To further explore PLK1 levels across different cell cycle phases in FLC cells, we specifically measured the levels of PLK1 during both interphase and mitotic phases in FLX1 cells, comparing these with HepaRG cells, which serve as non-tumorous controls. We used nocodazole treatment to synchronise the cells into the mitotic phase in both FLX1 and HepaRG cell lines. Following synchronisation, we performed protein lysis and subsequent western blot analysis to analyse the protein levels. Our analysis showed that PLK1 levels in FLX1 cells during the mitotic phase were elevated by approximately 1.5-fold to 2-fold compared with their levels in HepaRG cells during the same phase ( ). Interestingly, the levels of cyclin B1, another cell cycle-related protein, remained unchanged between FLX1 cells and HepaRG cells, indicating that the observed increase in PLK1 is specific and not a general feature of cell cycle progression in these cells ( ). This differential expression suggests a unique regulation of PLK1 in FLC cells that could be pivotal during mitosis. A similar increase in PLK1 levels was observed in the mitotic fractions of FLC cells compared with the mitotic fractions of a panel of HCC cells ( ). Furthermore, we assessed the postmitotic levels of PLK1 in AML12-FLC and AML12-WT cells. After treating these cells with nocodazole to synchronise them into the mitotic phase, they were replated and collected at specified time points. Our data revealed that even 30 min after removing nocodazole, the levels of PLK1 and its downstream substrate, TCTP, remained significantly higher (p<0.05) in AML12-FLC cells compared with the parental controls ( ). In contrast, the levels of cyclin B1 decreased at the same rate in both AML12-FLC and AML12-WT cells. These findings underscore the distinct regulatory dynamics of PLK1 in FLC cells, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target while highlighting that the behaviour of other cell cycle proteins like cyclin B1 remains consistent across different cell types.

## DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein is recruited to centrosomes
PLK kinases are key regulators of centrosome maturation, mitosis and cell division. Previously, it was shown that AKAP12-mediated recruitment of PLK1 to the centrosome contributes to the fidelity of symmetric cell division. Thus, we hypothesised that the heightened sensitivity of the FLC cells to PLK inhibition may stem from (1) the localisation of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein to the centrosome, (2) its interaction with centrosomal PLK1 complex, (3) enhanced activation of PLK1 and (4) increased mitotic progression. To establish that DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein is localised in the centrosomes in FLC cells, we used density-gradient centrifugation to purify centrosomes from FLC cells as described previously. In this method, cells are treated with nocodazole and cytochalasin B to depolymerise microtubules and actin, consequently weakening the association of the centrosome with the nuclear envelope and helping to release the centrosome ( ). Furthermore, using a discontinuous sucrose gradient provides a faster and more robust method of isolating pure centrosomes. Western blot analysis of the centrosome fractions obtained from patient-derived FLC1 cells revealed substantial levels of the predominant DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein ( ). Additionally, a low-level endogenous PKA within the centrosome was detected, consistent with findings from previous studies. Blots were further probed with γ-tubulin (positive control) and GAPDH (negative control), confirming the purity of the fractions. In accordance with these results, confocal imaging confirmed the co-localisation of γ-tubulin, PLK1 and PKAc in patient-derived FLC1 cells ( ). PLK1 is activated by phosphorylation at Thr210 during mitosis. We next explored whether the centrosomal localisation of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein influences the activity of PLK1. We isolated the total amount of PLK1 from the centrosome fraction of AML12-WT and AML12-FLC cells using immunoprecipitation. Using unphosphorylated casein as a PLK1 substrate and the kinase assay kit, we measured PLK1 activity in the centrosome fraction. Our findings indicate that PLK1 activity at the centrosome is ~twofold higher in AML12-FLC cells compared with AML12-Wt cells ( ). Together, these data support the recruitment of DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein at centrosomes, offering insights into potential mechanisms underlying the heightened PLK1 inhibitor sensitivity observed in FLC.
 

## Centrosomal DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein interacts with PLK1
To ascertain the physical interaction of DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein with PLK1 within the signalling complex at the centrosome, we conducted co-immunoprecipitation studies using nocodazole-treated, centrosomal fractions isolated from FLC cells under non-denaturing conditions (without DTT). Our data showed that DNAJ-PKAc physically associates with PLK1 in centrosome fractions from patient-derived FLC1 cells ( ). Furthermore, using purified recombinant proteins (constitutively active His-tagged full-length PLK1 and GST-His-tagged as a negative control) along with extracts from centrosomes, we showed that DNAJ-PKAc and AKAP12 associate with purified PLK1 ( ). Although low-level endogenous PKAc binding was also observed, DNAJ-PKAc and phosphorylated-DNAJ-PKAc was the predominant entity in the PLK1 complex. Finally, we investigated whether the DNAJ-PKAc chimaera directly binds to activated PLK1. Using recombinant chimaera and PLK1 protein, our results demonstrate that the chimaera oncoprotein can directly bind to PLK1 with a measured Kd of 146 nM using Bio-layer interferometry ( ). In contrast, weak binding of PLK1 with PKAc alone was observed with a Kd >10 μM. Thus, our biochemical studies reveal a robust and strong interaction between DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein and PLK1 within the centrosomal signalling complex, suggesting the pivotal role of DNAJ-PKAc in modulating PLK1-related processes in FLC. Finally, we explored if there are FLC-specific PLK1 substrates by analysing several published datasets. First, we examined the overlap between phosphorylated proteins identified in FLC tumours, 439 proteins and PLK1 inhibitor-sensitive phosphoproteins, 393 proteins ( ). PLK1 inhibitor-sensitive phosphoproteins were determined from mitotic HeLa cells treated with BI-4834, a PLK1 inhibitor, compared with a dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) control. The FLC tumour phosphoproteome was identified based on statistically significant increases in phosphorylation levels (>1 label-free quantification(LFQ)) compared with non-tumour liver. Our analysis revealed 44 overlapping proteins, which were enriched for pathways involved in cytoskeleton organisation, G2/M cell cycle regulation and autophagy ( ). Since some of these phosphoproteins may originate from non-tumour cells within the FLC microenvironment, we sought to identify proteins specifically driven by DNAJ-PKAc fusion expression. To do this, we analysed the overlap between phosphoproteins found in FLC tumours and those in HEK293 cells expressing either DNAJ-PKAc or WT-PKAc, identifying 82 shared proteins. Among these, 13 proteins also overlapped with PLK1 inhibitor-sensitive phosphoproteins, including AKAP12, SQSTM1, NUMA1, HSP90, AHNAK and CTNND1. Enrichment analysis of these proteins again pointed towards autophagy and centrosome localisation, suggesting a potential role for these PLK1-mediated proteins in FLC tumour biology. Together, our findings suggest that the centrosomal localisation of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein, alongside its interaction with PLK1, contributes to the enhancement of PLK1 activity, which in turn promotes accelerated mitotic progression.

## PLK1 inhibition suppresses FLC tumour growth
Our in vitro and ex vivo data demonstrated the potent reduction of FLC cell growth and viability of FLC tumour tissues by PLK1 inhibitors. Next, we aimed to validate the clinical relevance of targeting PLK1 in FLC by assessing the efficacy of clinical-grade PLK1 inhibitors in FLC PDX models. PDX models, which rely on FLC fusion oncogene for tumour growth in vivo, serve as robust systems for investigating mechanistic impacts and therapeutic effectiveness of PLK1 inhibition. Recently, we established three distinct PDX models derived from FLCs, confirmed to harbour the DNAJB1-PKAc fusion, with tumour histology closely mirroring the human disease. First, we evaluated a single-agent treatment with BI-2536 (50 mg/kg) or onvansertib (50 mg/kg), or plogosertib (40 mg/kg) in the FLC4 or FLX1 PDX models. Our data indicated that PLK1 inhibition mediated by three different PLK1 inhibitors, BI-2536, onvansertib and plogosertib, significantly suppressed FLC tumour growth and reduced tumour weight ( ). Histological analysis of PLK inhibitor-treated tumours showed marked decrease in Ki-67 staining, indicating reduced proliferative capacity ( ). Among these, plogosertib exhibited the most potent effects, significantly suppressing growth and exerting a cytostatic effect on FLC tumours ( ). Consistently, following a 2-week treatment with plogosertib and subsequent monitoring for up to 30 days, there was a significant increase in survival in the FLX1 model. Mice were treated with plogosertib for 14 days and then monitored until either death or sacrifice due to excessive tumour burden. Most vehicle-treated mice developed large tumours and had to be sacrificed at the humane end point, with a median survival of 15 days. In contrast, mice treated with plogosertib had a median survival of 31 days ( ). These findings highlight the efficacy of plogosertib as a potent PLK1 inhibitor, offering a promising therapeutic approach.
 

## Orthotopic model of FLC
FLC tumours are situated within the liver parenchyma, an environment prone to drug metabolism and transformation. To gain deeper insights into the impact of PLK1 inhibition on FLC tumours within this context, we developed an orthotopic FLC preclinical model that closely mimics the human disease. This model involves the orthotopic implantation of patient-derived FLC cells into the mouse liver, creating a natural interaction between the tumour cells and the adjacent stroma. Notably, onvansertib has demonstrated synergistic activity with irinotecan (a chemotherapy agent) in colorectal cancers. Both drugs boast favourable safety profiles, and their combined administration is manageable by patients. Importantly, a recent study indicated that FLC tumours exhibit sensitivity to irinotecan. Accordingly, we posit that a synergistic effect between PLK1 inhibitors and irinotecan could be evident in FLC and set out to test the efficacy of a combination of plogosertib and irinotecan in an orthotopic model of FLC. Intrahepatic FLC tumours were induced through intrasplenic injection of genetically engineered bioluminescent FLC1025 cells into the left lobe of the liver in male NSG mice aged 8–10 weeks. One week after injection, the baseline bioluminescent signal was established, and mice were assigned to either vehicle or treatment groups. Mice were subjected to plogosertib alone (40 mg/kg/day), irinotecan alone (40 mg/kg once every 7 days) and a combination thereof for 2 weeks ( ). Our results demonstrate that plogosertib monotherapy and irinotecan monotherapy significantly suppressed liver tumour growth (p<0.05) ( , ). Histopathological examination at the end of the study revealed that the combination treatment completely halted liver tumour growth. Gross analysis and H&E staining demonstrated tumour nodules in the livers of control animals (9/9), whereas no discernible tumours were observed in the livers of animals treated with the combination (0/7). This observation was corroborated by Ki-67 staining, which also exhibited a substantial reduction in staining intensity in response to combination therapy ( ). In summary, our subcutaneous and orthotopic FLC preclinical models emphasise the potential effectiveness of plogosertib and irinotecan treatments, both individually and in combination, in reducing liver tumour growth. This provides a promising pathway for addressing FLC within the complex environment of the liver parenchyma.

## Inhibiting PLK1 reduces the viability of FLC tumours in ex vivo organotypic tissue slices
Organotypic tumour slice culture offers a direct replication of the tumour microenvironment, which is highly representative of the human disease. We have successfully refined conditions to maintain freshly cut 250 μm thin slices produced by a vibratome and ensure a dependable basis for comparing samples with a high degree of reproducibility ( ). With viability and tissue architecture maintained for at least 7 days in culture, the system affords great flexibility and ease of testing the effects of drug combinations. Furthermore, we also optimised the delivery of small molecules using an active flow-based perfusion system enabling pharmacological studies. Real-time assessment, facilitated by bioluminescence-based reagents, permits the quantification of viability in tumour tissue slices ( ). To date, we have successfully created slice cultures for ex vivo testing in over 100 cases of human cancers of different histology. Using organotypic tumour slices prepared from seven independent human FLC tissues and real-time bioluminescence-based reagents, we confirmed a significant decrease in FLC tumour tissue viability on treatment with three different PLK1 inhibitors ( ). In contrast, the viability of slices prepared from the non-tumour liver from the same patients was not affected by the PLK1 inhibitor treatment. This trend was consistently observed as we detected a significant decrease in viability within PLK1 inhibitor-treated tissues sourced from three independent FLC PDX models ( ), further underscoring the potential of PLK1 inhibition as a promising therapeutic approach for FLC.
 

## Discussion
FLC tumours are characterised by greater chemo-resistance compared with classic HCC, resulting in a limited array of viable therapeutic options for FLC. Despite recent clinical endeavours targeting distinct cell signalling pathways in FLC, success has been very limited. FLC tumours exhibit heightened mTORC1 activity that, via the proto-oncogene Myc, propels protein translation. Notably, attempts using the mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor, everolimus, failed to manifest clinical efficacy in FLC, treatment with aromatase inhibitors (letrozole/leuprolide) yielded no apparent benefit. Although upregulated in FLC, Aurora kinase A (AURKA) encountered limited response with the selective inhibitor ENMD-2076, achieving only a 3% partial response. In concurrence, Alisertib, an Aurora kinase inhibitor, restrained the growth of both AML12 WT and AML12-FLC cells, indicating potential indiscriminate toxicity. The failure of these drug trials provides an impetus for the FLC research community to deploy an alternative strategy that focuses on the molecular events immediately downstream of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion kinase. Thus, our central hypothesis is that drugging DNAJ-PKAc binding partners offers a fresh new strategy to develop therapeutics for FLC.
Kinases represent a key group of enzymes that are involved in most cellular responses to stimuli. Furthermore, kinases represent relevant targets for existing therapeutics for cancer, inflammation and other diseases. To identify kinases that are important for the growth of DNAJ-PKAc-expressing cells, we employed a previously developed approach, called KiR. , Previously, this approach revealed host regulators in liver-stage malaria and COVID-19 as well as molecular drivers of bone metastases, highlighting its versatility. Through KiR screening and follow-up investigations, we identified several kinases, including PLK1, CDK6 and ULK1, that may contribute to the growth of FLC cells. Among these, we chose to focus on extensively validating the role of PLK1, as it had the most pronounced effect on FLC cells while showing minimal impact on normal hepatocytes. Additionally, the availability of several PLK1 inhibitors in clinical trials further motivated us to explore PLK1 as a potential therapeutic target in FLC. We successfully confirmed the critical involvement of PLK1 in DNAJ-PKAc-mediated cell growth in vitro, as well as the viability of FLC patient tumour tissues and tumour growth in vivo. Our data are also consistent with an independent high-throughput investigation of therapeutic agents in FLC models, where the first-generation PLK1 inhibitor BI-2536 was identified as effective in several FLC models. 
The heightened sensitivity of FLC cells to PLK1 inhibitors can be attributed to several interconnected factors, primarily involving the dysregulation of TP53 and the centrosomal localisation of the DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein. Dysregulation of TP53: previous studies have shown that untransformed cells with functional TP53 can effectively resist PLK1 inhibition due to p53’s ability to allow centrosome separation, facilitating the completion of mitosis and re-entry into the cell cycle with a normal 2N chromosome complement. In this study, we discovered that FLC cells are hypersensitive to PLK1 inhibition, leading us to examine whether TP53 is dysregulated in FLC. Although p53 mutations are a common oncogenic factor in HCC, they are infrequent in FLC. Notably, recent findings indicate that in FLC, the activated, phosphorylated form of p53 predominantly resides in the cytoplasm rather than the nucleus. Our analysis confirmed a significant enrichment of the aberrant TP53 hallmark signature in FLC samples compared with non-tumour liver tissue ( ). Consistently, we observed that FLC cells exhibited elevated levels of total and phosphorylated TP53 in whole cell lysates, dependent on PKA activity. Importantly, reintroducing wild-type TP53 diminished the cells’ response to PLK1 inhibitors, highlighting the potential role of TP53 dysregulation in modulating PLK1 inhibitor sensitivity in FLC.
DNAJ-PKAc fusion and centrosomal localisation: previous studies have demonstrated that AKAP12-mediated recruitment of PLK1 to the centrosome contributes to the accuracy of symmetric cell division. This prompted us to hypothesise that the heightened sensitivity of FLC cells to PLK inhibition originates from their reliance on a centrosomal AKAP12-PLK1 complex. Subsequent biochemical and cell biological investigations highlighted the centromeric localisation of DNAJ-PKAc fusion proteins, revealing their presence at the centrosome where they interact with PLK1 within a signalling complex. Besides AKAP12, several other AKAP proteins, such as pericentrin, are also found at the centrosome. It is conceivable that the DNAJ-PKAc chimaera may interact with multiple anchoring proteins. Our findings demonstrate for the first time the centrosomal localisation of DNAJ-PKAc, which, coupled with its interaction with PLK1, leads to enhanced PLK1 activation and accelerated mitotic progression. Together, these findings establish a complex interplay between the centrosomal localisation of a fusion protein and the dysregulation of TP53, both of which contribute to the unique vulnerability of FLC cells to PLK1 inhibition. This intricate regulatory network provides new insights into the mechanisms driving PLK1 sensitivity in FLC, offering potential avenues for targeted therapeutic interventions.
Limitation of the study: while our findings identify PLK1 as a potential therapeutic target in FLC, several aspects require further investigation. First, the specific molecular mechanisms, including potential FLC-specific substrates of PLK1 and the impact of DNAJ-PKAc on PLK1-regulated processes at the centrosome, are still not fully understood. Additionally, the regulation of PLK1 expression at the mRNA level and its implications for FLC are not well understood. Lastly, the structural-functional interactions between PLK1 and the DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein have yet to be elucidated. Addressing these gaps is crucial for advancing PLK1-targeted therapies and fully leveraging PLK1’s therapeutic potential in FLC.
Overall, our findings demonstrated that using clinical-grade PLK1 inhibitors, specifically second-generation and third-generation ones, decreased the growth of FLC tumours in various PDX models. Onvansertib, developed by Cardiff Oncology, is currently undergoing phase II clinical trials for several solid tumours including metastatic colorectal cancer (NCT05593328, NCT05383196, NCT05450965), while plogosertib from Cyclacel Pharmaceuticals is being assessed in phase II for its potential in treating multiple solid tumours and lymphoma (NCT05358379). Notably, both onvansertib and plogosertib have completed phase I safety trials and have been shown to be well-tolerated, both when used alone and in combination with chemotherapy. Significantly, our studies employed these clinically tested compounds and demonstrated their superior performance compared with the first-generation BI-2536 in patient-derived FLC cells. Finally, a recent study showed FLC tumours are sensitive to TOPO1 inhibition, particularly to irinotecan, in multiple PDX models. Our results, which show that a combination of PLK1 inhibitors and irinotecan achieved substantial growth suppression in an orthotopic model, suggest a strategy to reduce irinotecan doses to prevent toxicity. Altogether, our in vivo findings provide compelling proof-of-concept evidence from animal models and human patient tumour tissues, positioning us favourably to advance into human clinical trials for FLC treatment.

## Methods

## Cell lines
Mouse hepatocyte cell line AML12 and AML12-FLC (AML12-clone 14) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.04 µg/mL dexamethasone, 0.1% gentamicin, 1 µg/mL recombinant human insulin, 0.55 µg/mL human transferrin and 0.5 ng/mL sodium selenite. Patient-derived FLC4, FLX1 and FLC1025 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1 µg/mL recombinant human insulin, 0.55 µg/mL human transferrin, 0.5 ng/mL sodium selenite, 50 ng/mL hepatocyte growth factor and 50 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor 10. HepaRG cells were obtained from Thermo and cultured in Williams’ Medium E supplemented with 10% FBS and 1X GlutaMax. All cell lines were maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. HepG2 and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA (H33) cells were maintained in RPMI1640 culture medium (RPMI1640 medium (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gemini Bio Products, Sacramento, California, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin-glutamine (Life Technologies) at 37°C and 5% CO2. Primary human hepatocytes were isolated fresh from human donor liver transplant specimens. 
PLK1 inhibitors: BI-2536 was obtained from Selleckchem, onvansertib was acquired from Cardiff Oncology and plogosertib was obtained from Cyclacel Pharmaceuticals.

## Kinase inhibitor screening
KI screening was performed as described previously. The effect of computationally selected 32 KIs on cell growth was studied using a live-cell imaging assay. Briefly, 5×103 cells were plated on 96-well tissue culture-treated plates, allowing cells to adhere overnight, and treated with KIs suspended in DMSO. Drug dilutions were made using threefold serial dilutions in the complete media, and each dose was administered to the cells in triplicate. Microscopy images were taken using an Incucyte imaging system with a 10× objective lens. Confluence as the percentage of the total area covered by cells was quantified using the Incucyte ZOOM 2016B software. The percentage cell confluence at 500 nM calculated using the full dose-response curves for each of the inhibitors was used as a response variable for regression.

## KiR modelling
Elastic Net-regularised KiR models and the list of predicted key kinases or key functional nodes were generated as previously described. Briefly, a panel of 427 kinase inhibitors previously had their pairwise effects on 298 human kinases profiled. The result is a quantitative drug-target matrix, where each entry is a percentage between 0 and 100 that represents residual activity (as a per cent of control, uninhibited activity) in the presence of that inhibitor. The kinase inhibition profiles of each inhibitor and the quantitative responses to those inhibitors were used as the explanatory and response variables, respectively, for elastic net regularised multiple linear regression models. Custom R scripts (available at: https://github.com/FredHutch/KiRNet-Public) employing the glmnet package were used to generate the final models. LOOCV was used to select the optimal value for the penalty scaling factor λ ( ). Models were computed for 11 evenly spaced values of α (the relative weighting between LASSO and Ridge regularisation) ranging from 0 to 1.0 inclusive. Kinases with positive coefficients in at least one of these models (with the exception of α=0, which always has non-zero coefficients for every kinase) were considered hits ( ). Model accuracy was assessed via the LOOCV error as well as the root-mean-squared error of the predictions for the tested inhibitors.

## Centrosome isolation
Centrosome-enriched fractions from patient-derived FLC or AML12-FLC cells were prepared using sucrose gradient fractionation following previously established protocols. Briefly, 2–5 15 cm dishes of cells were cultured to 70%–80% confluence, and the medium was replaced with complete medium containing 5 μg/mL nocodazole and 5 μg/mL cytochalasin B. The cells were then incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 1 hour. After two washes with ice-cold HEPES buffer (HB) (20 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), pH 7.8; 5 mM potassium acetate; 0.5 mM MgCl2; 0.5 mM Dithiothreitol, DTT), cells were incubated with 20 mL of HB buffer at 4°C for 10 min. The cells were then gently scraped from the culture plates, transferred to a 15 mL Dounce homogeniser and homogenised using 10 strokes of the tight-fitting pestle. The lysate was subsequently transferred to a 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 3000 rpm at 4°C for 5 min to pellet nuclei. The supernatant was collected, and the pellet was washed with 10 mL of ice-cold HB buffer. After another centrifugation step, the supernatants were combined, and 0.1% Triton X-100 was added. The lysate was centrifuged again, and the supernatant was loaded onto a sucrose gradient in Ultra-Clear Beckman tubes. The gradient was centrifuged at 26 000 rpm in SW32Ti at 4°C for 1 hour. Fractions of 0.5 mL were collected. To confirm the centrosome fractions, samples from the 40%–60% sucrose range were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and immunoblotting using γ-tubulin antibody (a centrosomal marker).

## Confocal imaging
FLC cells were cultured on Lab-Tek II chamber glass slides (Nalge Nunc, Naperville, Illinois, USA) or on 24-well glass bottom dishes (MatTek). Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, washed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked for 60 min with PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin (w/v). Cells were immunostained with the appropriate antibody, followed by immunostaining with Alexa Fluor 488-labelled goat-anti-rabbit antibody (Molecular Probes, Eugene, Oregon, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Fluorescent micrographs were obtained using a Zeiss 780 confocal laser-scanning microscope. Individual channels were overlaid using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

## Small interfering RNA transfection
All siRNA were obtained from Dharmacon (Thermo). All siRNAs were used at a final concentration of 3–6 nM. siRNA transfections for assessing cell growth changes were performed in 12-well plates using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Organotypic tissue slice preparation
Organotypic tumour slices were prepared from FLC PDX tumours or directly from patient tumours, as described previously. Briefly, tumour slices (250 µm thick, 6 mm in diameter) were prepared using the Leica VT1200S vibratome. These slices were cultured at the air-liquid interface using cell culture inserts in slice culture medium consisting of Williams’ Medium E supplemented with 12 mM nicotinamide, 150 nM ascorbic acid, 2.25 mg/mL sodium bicarbonate, 20 mM HEPES, 50 mg/mL additional glucose, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% (v/v) Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (ITS) supplement, 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor, 40 IU/mL penicillin and 40 µg/mL streptomycin. Following a 24-hour incubation, the slices were exposed to various PLK1 inhibitors at various concentrations for a 72-96 hour period. The overall viability of the tumour tissue was assessed using RealTime-Glo (Promega, G9711) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescent signal was captured before (day 0) and after drug treatment (days 3–7) using the BioTek Synergy plate reader.

## Pull-down assays and western blot analysis
Centrosome extracts for immunoprecipitation (IP) and His6 pull-down assays were prepared following the same procedure as described above, with the exception that DTT was excluded from the lysis buffer. Total protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay. For IP experiments, the centrosome fractions were mixed either with an anti-PLK1 antibody or IgG control overnight at 4°C. Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) was then added and the slurry incubated for 1 hour on a rotating shaker at 4°C. For His6 pull-down assay, centrosome extracts were incubated with purified His-tagged PLK1 or GST-His control proteins bound to Ni-NTA Agarose (Qiagen, Valencia, California, USA). The mixture was gently rotated on a shaker for 2 hours at 4°C to allow binding. For both the pull-down and IP experiments, the agarose beads were washed four times with lysis buffer and then boiled for 5 min in NuPAGE SDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen) prior to analysis by western blot analysis. For quantitative immunoblots, primary antibodies were detected with IRDye 680-labelled goat antirabbit IgG or IRDye 800-labelled goat antimouse IgG, each at 1:5000 dilution. Bands were visualised and quantified using the Odyssey Infrared Imaging System.

## Mitotic cell fractions
Mitotic cells were collected using cell synchronisation and mitotic shake-off procedure as previously described. Briefly, 2.5×106 AML12-WT and AML12-FLC cells were seeded in T75 flasks. The following day, the cells were treated with 1 µM nocodazole for 8 hours. Mitotic cells were detached by applying strong strokes and examined under a microscope. Once all mitotic cells were in suspension, they were collected into a tube, washed with PBS and lysed with 2% SDS lysis buffer. The cells were also replated for either 30 min or 60 min to collect extracts postsynchronisation. The remaining adherent cells, which were asynchronous interphase cells, were also collected, washed and lysed.

## Animal studies
PDX model: typically, human FLC fragments (5×5×5 mm max size) were subcutaneously engrafted onto a cohort of ~5–10 anaesthetised NSG mice as the primary graft. To implant the tumour, a small (about 5 mm size) skin incision was made at the flank, a pocket was created and the tumour was inserted. The skin was closed (incision was made only for fragment implanting, not cell injection) with a wound clip, or tissue glue. The mice were monitored daily for 2–3 days after implantation, and the xenografts were then allowed to establish. They were subsequently followed and measured at least twice weekly. When tumours reached 100–150 mm² in volume, mice were treated with various agents. Mice were treated with BI-2536 or onvansertib at 50 mg/kg, administered by oral gavage 5 days a week, or irinotecan at 40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, once a week, or the same volume of vehicle including saline (BI-2536, irinotecan) and 0.5% methylcellulose/0.1% Tween-80 (onvansertib), respectively. At the end of the study, mice were sacrificed; tumours were extracted and used for downstream biochemical and histological analysis.
Orthotopic model: to establish FLC tumours in the liver, patient-derived FLC cells (FLC1025) were injected intrasplenically. Briefly, a 1 cm incision was made in the left upper abdominal wall to expose the spleen; 1–1.5×106 FLC luciferase cells in 50 μL PBS and 100 μL additional PBS were gently injected into the spleen. Splenectomy was performed 10 min after injection using surgical cautery tip (Gemini Cat. No. 59017, Illinois, USA). The abdominal incision was closed with 4–0 absorbable plain gut suture and 9 mm wound clips. Tumour growth was monitored by IVIS imaging. Treatment started when IVIS signal reached >100-fold above background. Mice were treated with plogosertib at 40 mg/kg in 5% mannitol via oral gavage, 5 days a week, or irinotecan at 40 mg/kg in saline intraperitoneally, once a week, or a combination of both, or vehicle control ( ). At the end of the study, body weight, liver weight and liver area were measured. Livers were fixed in 10% formalin for histological analysis. Histology: images of entire tissue sections were obtained using the TissueFAXS system (TissueGnostics, Austria) with a 10× objective lens in brightfield mode. The percentage of the Ki-67-positive area relative to the scanned whole tissue area was quantified using HistoQuest software (TissueGnostics, Austria).
