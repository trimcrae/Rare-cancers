Most proteins (greater than ~40 kDa), undergo facilitated transport through nuclear membrane pores via receptor-substrate interactions with members of the IMP family of proteins [ ]. This interaction is commonly facilitated by short sequences called nuclear localization signals (NLSs) within cargo. The individual cargo binding properties of IMPα (7 in human) and IMPβ (20 in human) proteins are both distinct and overlapping, and each displays a unique cellular expression profile [ , ]. This provides a layer of intracellular regulation between the processes of protein translation and nuclear access, which ensures nuclear factors enter the nucleus at a time appropriate to their function [ ]. Nuclear export is also an active process, mediated predominantly by the exportins, a family of RanGTPase-dependent export mediators [ ].
In “classical nuclear transport”, NLS-bearing cargo first bind to an IMPαprotein which in turn enables IMPα binding to the IMPβ1 protein; the latter facilitates nuclear pore translocation. Alternatively, in non-classical nuclear transport, IMPβ protein binds directly to NLS-containing cargo. The roles of IMPβ proteins vary; some exclusively import or export proteins, while others operate as bi-directional transporters. IPO5 (previously also known as RanBP5, KPNB3, and IMP beta 3) is an IMP β protein, that comprises 24 HEAT repeats [ ] and is highly conserved between mouse and human. Except for IMPβ1, IPO5 like most other IMP βs, exhibits no binding affinity for IMPα, instead binding directly to substrates specifically for nuclear import [ ].
Individual nuclear transport proteins may chaperone a broad range of proteins into the nucleus, but they also display binding preferences for specific cargoes relating to tissue- or cell-specific roles. To date, several important functions have been identified for IPO5, including as the major import carrier for Stella in post-fertilization embryos [ ]. Because Stella mediates protection of imprinted loci within the maternal pronucleus, this interaction is important for embryonic viability. This Stella-IPO5 interaction also has the potential to influence formation of primordial germ cells and epigenetic patterning in early embryogenesis [ ]. The discovery that decreased IPO5-mediated IQGAP1 transport reduces β-catenin activity, and subsequently reduces WNT-directed gene expression in X. laevis embryos, is relevant to the steps in embryogenesis and testis development that rely on WNT signaling [ ]. The identification that IPO5 preferentially transports BMP signaling SMADs (SMAD1/5/9, but not the activin/Nodal/TGFβ SMADs 2/3) [ ] is highly important in the context of cell fate scenarios where elevated BMP or reduced activin signaling determines transcriptional outputs, such as the SNAI1 transcription factor that controls epithelial to mesenchymal transition in cancers [ ]. Decreased IPO5 expression impacts TGFβ signaling in the TCam-2 testis cancer seminoma cell line, modulating signaling outcomes when activin and BMP4 are present [ ]. Fundamental roles for IPO5 in RNA processing include both RNA-splicing protein transport [ ] and translational control [ ]. This translational role has particular relevance to the testis, where IPO5-mediated nuclear delivery of Musashi 1 (Msi1) releases the Msi2 mRNA for translation into its encoded protein, Musashi 2, which is itself a regulator of RNA translation in spermatids [ ]. Further roles for IPO5 in cell cycle control and cellular behavior are emerging, particularly in pathological states, with increased IPO5 expression resulting in higher proliferation by a range of cancers [ ], and with its downregulation resulting in a dramatic reduction in tumor volume [ ] or decreased EMT [ ].
IPO5 is under tight spatio-temporal regulation, indicative of developmentally specific roles. Within the fetal murine gonad, IPO5 exhibits sex-specific expression and subcellular localization [ ]. Immediately after the period of sex determination IPO5 localizes to the nucleus in ovarian germ cells but is cytoplasmic in testicular germ cells (gonocytes or prospermatogonia). Subsequently, IPO5 remains readily detectable in testicular germ cells beyond embryonic day (E) 16.5, but exhibits a concurrent decrease in ovarian germ cells [ ]. During the first postnatal wave of murine spermatogenesis, IPO5 transcripts and protein levels show a distinct increase. Peak levels correlate with the emergence of post-meiotic germ cells which are the most abundant cell type in the adult testis [ , ]. This profile is maintained in the adult mouse and is conserved in adult human spermatogenesis [ ]. Testicular somatic cells, particularly Leydig cells, also express Ipo5 transcripts in the developing and adult testis, however protein levels are low to undetectable. This evidence implicates IPO5 expression and activity in several distinct spermatogenic steps.
We have identified the critical requirement for IPO5 in embryonic development and testicular differentiation in this study using a series of conditional IPO5 null mutant mouse models that target 3 developmental stages, expanding on our work on the role of the IMPs in embryonic stem cell differentiation [ ] and the published association of IPO5 with the earliest stages of differentiation [ ], we show here that the deletion of IPO5 leads to pre-implantation embryonic loss. We also demonstrate the essential role of IPO5 in the male germline during both fetal and postnatal development. The fetal germ cell-specific IPO5 conditional knockout leads to a loss of testicular germ cells, while post-natal germ cell-specific IPO5 null mutants exhibit severe meiotic and spermiogenic defects. Finally, through immunoprecipitation (IP) and mass spectroscopy analyses, we identify a series of IPO5 binding proteins in embryonic testis, and in meiotic, and haploid spermatogenic cells. These findings add to our understanding that regulated nucleo-cytoplasmic transport drives cell fate, with the IMP IPO5 serving crucial functions in embryonic development and male germ cell biology.

## Materials and methods

## Mice
All mouse lines (including wildtype C57/BL6 J for IP) were housed at the Monash University Central Animal House, at the Monash Medical Centre Animal Facility, or at the Newcastle University Animal Facility in accordance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes, with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and food and water available ad libitum. This study was approved either by the Monash University Animal Ethics Committee or Newcastle University Animal Ethics committees, as appropriate.

## Generation of a conditional IPO5FL/FL mouse line and three Cre-recombination-directed mutants
To delineate the role of IPO5 during specific developmental periods, we first generated an Ipo5FL/FL conditional mouse line. The introduction of flanking flox sites by homologous recombination allowed Cre-induced excision of exons 9 and 10 of Ipo5, targeting HEAT repeats 8 and 9 and the RanGTP-binding region ( , and detailed in Supplemental Materials and Methods).
The Ipo5FL/FL line was subsequently crossed to 3 mouse lines containing Cre-recombinase encoded behind specific promoters in a 2-stage breeding series (outlined in Supplemental Materials and Methods). Breeding outcomes indicated that genomic modification to the IPO5 locus did not impact overall viability, and each germline cross (outlined in in Supplemental Materials and Methods) produced litters of generally similar size ( ), despite the partial loss of certain genotypes as outlined in Results. These crosses resulted in the global (CMVCre), fetal germ cell (VasaCre) and post-natal male germ cell (Stra8Cre) deletion of IPO5. Mice were genotyped to determine the genomic status of both the Ipo5FL/FL allele and the promoter-Cre (see ). This facilitated determination of both somatic and germline genotypes, as outlined in Results.

## Tissue collection and fixation
Mice were collected for analysis at specific post-natal ages and whole-body weights recorded. Gonads were dissected, weighed, then either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; overnight) or Bouin’s fixative (4–5 h) and paraffin embedded prior to sectioning by Monash University Monash Health Research Precinct (MHRP) Histology Facility staff. For frozen sections, PFA fixed tissues were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose (1–3 days), embedded in OCT (Sakura), snap frozen on dry ice and then stored at −80°C prior to being sectioned.

## Histological and immunohistochemical/immunofluorescence staining of gonadal tissues
Slides with paraffin-embedded sections were treated with standard dewaxing and rehydration methods, including 3 min washes in a graded ethanol series. For immunohistochemistry, testis sections were exposed to hydrogen peroxide, blocked for 1 h, then incubated with primary antibody ( in Supplemental Materials and Methods). The following day, sections were washed, and primary antibody binding detected using biotinylated IgG secondary antibodies ( in Supplemental Materials and Methods), appearing as a brown precipitate following development with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently sections were mounted and imaged. For immunofluorescence, paraffin and frozen sections were blocked, then permeabilized prior to being incubated overnight at room temperature with the indicated primary antibodies ( in Supplemental Materials and Methods). The following day, slides were washed and appropriate secondary antibodies applied ( in Supplemental Materials and Methods). Samples were counterstained with DAPI prior to mounting. Slides were scanned at the MHTP Histology Facility using the Olympus VS120® Virtual Slide Microscope System. Imaging of immunofluorescence staining for IPO5/XPO2 co-localization was performed on an Axio Imager A1 epifluorescent microscope (Carl Zeiss MicroImaging) and photographed using an Olympus DP70 microscope camera (Olympus).

## Imaging and counting of testis age series
Slide scans were analysed using QuPath image processing software [ ]. For Sertoli cell quantification, 25–50 intact cords were analysed per section, cord perimeters were measured, and SOX9-positive cells within the cords were counted. To account for differences in cord size and shape, SOX9+ cells were normalized to cord perimeter in each cord cross section. For spermatogonial subpopulation quantification, spermatogonia inside the cords were identified on whole testis cross-sections as follows: cells with PLZF+ nuclei (undifferentiated spermatogonia, PLZF+/KIT-) were detected using the Cell detection tool, and all PLZF+ cells additionally KIT+ were then identified using the Single Measurement Classifier tool (progenitor spermatogonia, PLZF+/KIT+). Cells with KIT+ cytoplasm were counted manually (PLZF-/KIT+, differentiated spermatogonia) ( , bottom panel). Each spermatogonial subpopulation is represented as a proportion of all PLZF+ and/or KIT+ spermatogonia.

## Quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR
Whole testis RNA extracted using the RNeasy extraction kit (Qiagen) was treated with DNaseI according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Following reverse transcription, SYBR green-based qRT-PCR was performed; gene details and primer sequences are presented in Supplemental Materials and methods. Reactions were performed in triplicate, and the resulting relative quantification values averaged. All PCR products were verified by sequencing. Relative standard curve analysis was performed using SDS 2.0 software (Applied Biosystems). Values for each transcript were normalized to murine ribosomal RN18S RNA (Rplp0) and are presented as relative fold-change from control samples. Three independent experiments were performed for each treatment, with error bars indicating standard error of the mean (SEM). Unpaired 2-tailed student’s t-tests, Mann–Whitney post-test, were performed with GraphPad Prism™ software to determine statistical significance.

## Spermatocyte and spermatid isolation for importome analysis
Spermatocytes and spermatids were isolated from 2 adult (>PND 60) manually dissociated testes onto a 2%–4% continuous bovine serum albumin (BSA) gradient [ ]; a detailed protocol is presented in Supplemental Materials and Methods. Purified germ cells were identified by Arachis hypogaea lectin staining of the developing acrosome for spermatocytes and spermatids, and cells were categorized based on both size and lectin staining pattern: spermatocytes exhibited relatively diffuse staining localized to a region representing the early stages of acrosome development, and spermatids were scored based on distinct labeling of the developing and elongating acrosome. Spermatocyte fractions were 65%–70% pure with contaminating cells largely consisting of early spermatocytes. Spermatids were 85%–95% pure with contaminating cells consisting of late spermatocytes.

## Protein extraction and immunoblotting
Protein was extracted from isolated spermatocytes and spermatids using RIPA buffer, followed by detection on Western blots, as previously described and detailed in Supplemental Materials and Methods [ ]. Labeled antibodies were detected with Amersham ECL Western Blotting Detection reagents according to the manufacturer’s instructions (GE Healthcare UK, Buckinghamshire, United Kingdom), with the results recorded using a cooled charge-coupled device camera system (Fuji-LAS-4000; Fujifilm Life Science Systems, Tokyo, Japan). Membranes were stripped of primary and secondary antibodies using Western Re-probe (G-Biosciences) to allow re-probing. Signal intensities of protein bands were quantitated where required using Fujifilm Multigauge software, v3.0.

## IP
IP to identify IPO5 binding partners was performed on whole embryonic E13.5 testes and isolated spermatocytes and round spermatids from adult mouse testis (AdMT), using IPO5 antibody, or rabbit/mouse IgG control, bound to Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen). The IPO5 antibody was cross-linked to Dynabeads, and antibody-bound beads were incubated with lysate. Bound proteins were eluted. For isolated spermatocyte and spermatid proteins, half the eluant was run on an SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and gels stained with Coomassie Blue. Selected protein products were then excised and analysed. From whole E13.5 testes, total IP lysates were sent for analysis. All analyses were performed via mass spectrometry (1D nano-LC ESI MS/MS [nano-liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry]) (E13.5: Hunter Medical Research Institute, Newcastle and Bio21, Victoria; isolated spermatocytes and spermatids: Australian Proteome Analysis Facility), as previously described [ ]. Bioinformatics was performed by the respective platforms, and positive IP results were then classified as unique IDs. The other half of the denatured protein from adult germ cells was separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and membrane transfer for immunoblot confirmation of potential targets, (e.g. XPO2).

## Results

## IPO5 protein is detected in germ cell cytoplasm in embryonic and early post-natal testes
Using immunofluorescence, expression of IPO5 protein was examined in WT embryonic (E13.5, E15.5), early post-natal (PND 0, 3 and 6) and AdMT ( ). Sections were co-stained with GILZ, marking gonocyte, spermatogonia and early-mid pachytene spermatocytes cytoplasm [ ] and GFRA1, an established marker of self-renewing SSC-enriched cells [ , ]). At all ages, IPO5 is detected in germ cell cytoplasm consistent its role in nuclear import [ , ]. IPO5 is robustly expressed in germ cells at E13.5 and E15.5. At birth (PND 0), almost all GILZ+ spermatogonia are IPO5+/GFRA1-. At PND 3, IPO5 is reduced, but is co-expressed in GILZ+/GFRA1+ spermatogonia, demonstrating an overlap in expression during establishment of the nascent spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) population. By PND 6, IPO5 is low/not detectable in GILZ+ spermatogonia regardless of GFRA1 expression. In the AdMT, IPO5 signal is very low in GFRA1+ spermatogonia, but in contrast, is highly expressed in pachytene spermatocytes, round spermatids and early elongates consistent with previous reports [ ]. Overall, these data show that IPO5 levels are dynamically regulated during testis development with age, cell and stage specific expression consistent with sc-RNAseq data [ ]. Cells with a high stem cell potential (GFRA1+) are IPO5 low or negative, possibly indicating that IPO5 is not important for the maintenance of adult SSCs in steady-state spermatogenesis, however this remains to be determined.
 

## IPO5 is indispensable for embryogenesis
To determine whether IPO5 is broadly required for murine development, the newly generated Ipo5FL/FL line was crossed with a CMVCre line (active during early embryonic development) to produce a global knockout of IPO5. IPO5 heterozygous (Ipo5HET) offspring generated from these crosses were mated (3 mating pairs in total), and the genotypes of resulting progeny were analysed. From the 14 litters produced (from 3 continuous mating pairs, each yielding 4–5 litters), there were no IPO5 null (Ipo5KO) offspring ( ), demonstrating the global knockout of IPO5 is embryonic lethal. In summary, the absence of IPO5 leads to embryo loss, suggesting IPO5 has critical roles to play in early development. In addition, given the lower-than-expected incidence of heterozygous IPO5 offspring (there should be double the number of HET vs WT offspring), it appears likely that mono-allelic IPO5 expression may compromise embryogenesis in some embryos in this global knockout model. In conjunction with evidence from the VasaCre knockouts ( , and next section below), it appears that the IPO5 null state leads to embryonic loss in the early post-implantation period. This suggests that IPO5 has critical roles to play in development.
 

## IPO5 is specifically required for male germ cell survival
Our previous studies showed that IPO5 protein exhibits a gender-specific expression and subcellular localization pattern in the embryonic gonad [ ]. Thus, to examine the impact of IPO5 loss specifically on male and female fetal germ cells, Ipo5FL/FL mice were crossed with the VasaCre mouse line. This well-characterized Cre line exhibits germ cell-specific Cre excision from E15.5 onward [ ] typically observed in >95% of both XY and XX germ cells at birth, with minimal ectopic recombination reported [ ]. The 2-stage strategy (detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods) was used to generate fetal germline IPO5 knockout offspring. Because Vasa mRNA is present in the ovary [ ], males were used at all stages to transmit the VasaCre allele to avoid producing a global knockout in the embryos. Briefly, Ipo5FL/FL: VasaCreWT/WT females were crossed with Ipo5WT/WT:VasaCreWT/Cre males, and the resulting male offspring harboring an Ipo5WT/FL:VasaCreWT/Cre genotype were crossed back to Ipo5FL/FL:VasaCreWT/WT females, with the assumption that half of the sperm would carry a null Ipo5 allele. While all four expected genotypes were detected in the resulting offspring, it was apparent that these crosses generated significantly fewer than expected Ipo5FL/FL:VasaCreWT/Cre mice ( ). Therefore, although this VasaCre construct has been reported to have minimal ectopic expression, this particular cross did demonstrate ectopic activity. As shown in , VasaCre-mediated IPO5 knockout resulted in lower-than-expected germline KO genotypes; the ratio of live born KO pups (Ipo5KO/FL:VasaCreWT/Cre) was 0.5, in contrast to the expected 1. The loss of HET pups noted in the CMVCre line was not observed in this model. These observations indicate the ectopic recombination of the IPO5 floxed allele early in embryogenesis led to loss of approximately 50% of Ipo5FL/FL:VasaCreWT/Cre mice through ectopic global recombination; this loss is similar to outcomes from the global knockouts from CMVCre crosses. In support of this, recombination of the floxed allele in some Ipo5FL/WT:VasaCreWT/Cre mice was confirmed by detection of the ectopically recombined allele in tail-derived gDNA. However, in the absence of ectopic recombination, we were able to generate mice that showed specific deletion of IPO5 in the germline. Excitingly, the Ipo5FL/KO:Cre/WT males generated no pups when mated for 6 weeks with WT females, while Ipo5KO/FL:Cre/WT females mated with either Ipo5 WT or HET males passed on the KO allele, indicating that the oocytes were fertile (discussed below).
In Ipo5FL/FL × VasaCre (second generation) adult mice, testis weights were significantly reduced (by ~75%) compared to WT and HET littermates ( ). In contrast, adult ovary weights were not different ( ), suggesting loss of IPO5 in E15.5 fetal germ cells has a male-specific effect. Likewise, testis:body weight ratios were significantly affected ( ), but ovary:body weight ratios were unchanged ( ). Representative images of testes from Ipo5FL/FL × VasaCre HET and KO mice illustrate the size difference ( ). To assess the basic fertility of these mice, two KO males and two KO females were mated with WT mice. The KO males produced copulatory plugs, indicating normal mating behavior, however no litters were produced despite continuing mating opportunities. In contrast, the two germline KO females produced pups that all harbored the recombined IPO5 allele (data not shown), indicating that a lack of IPO5 from the late fetal stage onwards does not impact female germ cell viability or fertility. Preliminary histological analyses revealed that Ipo5WT and Ipo5HET testis sections were grossly normal, however, Ipo5KO testes exhibited a complete lack of spermatogenesis with almost no germ cells ( ). Although tubules were well-formed, they appeared to contain only Sertoli cell nuclei and cytoplasm. As expected, ovaries were grossly normal ( ).
 Transcriptional analysis of Ipo5 × VasaCre WT, HET, and KO testes confirmed the complete absence of Ipo5 transcripts 3′ to the recombination site in KO samples ( ). Additionally, germ cell-specific transcripts, Ddx4 (Vasa, Mvh), and the pre- and post-meiotic germ cell transcripts, Stra8 and Prtm1 respectively, were all undetectable, indicative of complete germ cell absence ( ). Our data indicates that Ipo5 levels in the testis are tightly regulated, with no difference in Ipo5, Ddx4, Stra8 and Prtm1 measured between WT and HET testes ( ). Immunohistochemical detection of DDX4 confirmed the almost complete absence of germ cells in KO testes compared with Ipo5 × VasaCre WT and HET ( , DDX4). A small proportion of tubules, particularly around the periphery of the testis, did contain some DDX4+ germ cells located on the basement membrane ( , DDX4 inset), which were also positive for the proliferation marker PCNA ( ). This localization suggests they are likely spermatogonia (including SSCs). Most tubules lack PCNA staining indicating the absence of proliferative germ cells, and the presence of SOX9+ cells confirms the presence of Sertoli cells. In summary, an absence of IPO5 in the fetal testis leads to the catastrophic loss of male germ cells. The few persisting germ cells that survive into the adult testis appear to be cycling, however, they do not appear to differentiate beyond the spermatogonial stage.
 

## IPO5 is required for male germ cells during the first wave of spermatogenesis
Since females lacking IPO5 from E15.5 (Ipo5 × VasaCre) are fully fertile, and IPO5 mRNA and protein is strongly detected in meiotic and post-meiotic male germ cells [ ], our next series of experiments examined the impact of IPO5 loss in the early post-natal spermatogenesis. The Ipo5FL/FL mice were crossed with the temporal/tissue/gender-specific Stra8Cre transgenic line, generating the Ipo5 × Stra8Cre line. In the male germline, Stra8 is expressed in a highly restricted pattern, detected only in pre-meiotic post-natal germ cells from day 3 onwards [ ]. The Stra8Cre line [ ] is well-defined, with males reported to exhibit greater than 95% recombination in germ cells. In addition, although XX germ cells express endogenous Stra8 through meiotic prophase I during embryonic life, the exogenous promoter fragment is not active in the ovary [ ].
In a similar process to that employed for the VasaCre line, Ipo5FL/FL female mice were crossed with male Stra8CreWT/Cre mice to produce stage 1 offspring. Ipo5FL/WT:Stra8CreWT/Cre males from this generation were then backcrossed to female Ipo5FL/FL mice. From these crosses, half of the resulting males should carry the null allele. The four expected genotypes are presented in . These outcomes show the Stra8Cre construct exhibits less ectopic expression compared with VasaCre, as litters resulting from this cross presented with all four potential genotypes at expected ratios at weaning ( ).
 To determine the impact of IPO5 loss on the first wave of spermatogenesis, PND 14 testes from Ipo5 × Stra8Cre WT and HET males were compared to KO ( ). At this age, KO testis weights were significantly lower ( ). In histological sections some tubules appeared morphologically normal, however meiotic and more mature germ cells were absent from others. Tubules were scored based on the relative proportion of meiotic germ cells (from 0%, no meiotic germ cells, to 100%, normal spermatogenesis) ( and ). Notably, the disruption to meiotic progression correlates with the elevation of IPO5 protein observed as pachytene spermatocytes emerge [ ].
 IPO5 was detected using immunofluorescence analysis of PND 14 Ipo5 × Stra8Cre WT testes in spermatogonia and meiotic germ cells ( ). In KO testes, IPO5 expression was absent from some tubules (white dotted line), however others contained IPO5+ spermatogonia and meiotic cells that had evaded recombination ( ). In these tubules, spermatogenesis appeared grossly normally ( ). These tubules provide an excellent internal control, contrasting with the many tubules lacking IPO5 protein in which spermatogenesis is severely disrupted. The presence of DDX4+ cells at the basement membrane in KO testes indicates that despite the loss of IPO5, a robust complement of spermatogonia remains ( ). The number of SOX9+ Sertoli cells remained unchanged in the KO testis compared to WT ( ) further emphasizing the recombination was germ cell specific. Next, the proportion of undifferentiated spermatogonia (PLZF+/KIT-), early differentiating spermatogonia (PLZF+/KIT+), and late differentiating spermatogonia (PLZF-/KIT+) was quantified. A significant increase in undifferentiated spermatogonia accompanied by a decrease in early and differentiated spermatogonia was observed in KO testes, indicating a block in differentiation occurs in the absence of IPO5 ( ). Lastly, the number of cleaved caspase 3 (CC3+) cells/tubule was increased in Ipo5 × Stra8Cre KO testes, consistent with activation of apoptotic pathways causing germ cell loss. Together these data show that IPO5 impacts spermatogonial differentiation at PND 14 and is also essential for meiotic germ cell viability as evidenced by the dramatic disruption to meiotic initiation in its absence.
To determine how IPO5 deletion in the Ipo5 × Stra8Cre mice affects adult spermatogenesis, testes from WT, HET and KO males at 6- and 12-weeks of age were assessed. Testis weight and testis:body weight ratios were significantly decreased (~ 37%) in KO testes at 6-weeks ( and ) and 12-weeks ( and ) compared to WT and HET testes. Examination of testis cross-sections found spermatogenesis was disrupted, with extensive regions of germ cell loss, and vacuoles within Sertoli cell cytoplasm evident in multiple KO tubules ( ). Immunofluorescence was performed to examine the extent of IPO5 deletion. All testis sections from WT mice exhibited IPO5 expression throughout the seminiferous epithelium, while IPO5 appeared mosaic in KO testis tubules correlating with the significant absence of germ cells. However, despite the extensive loss of germ cells, some regions contained IPO5+ germ cells ( ) and were able to support complete spermatogenesis thus indicating the presence of a functional niche. To quantitate the extent of IPO5 recombination and check for the presence of a truncated IPO5 protein, a Western blot of IPO5 proteins in WT, HET, and KO Ipo5 × Stra8cre testis lysates was performed ( ). Full-length IPO5 protein (IPO5 FL) was detected at ~124 kDa in all samples but present at markedly reduced levels in the KO testis lysate, consistent with incomplete recombination in these testes. Accordingly, a truncated product (IPO5 TR) of around 30 kDa, corresponding to the expected outcome following recombination was observed in KO testes ( ). We conclude that the substitution of IPO5 FL by IPO5 TR, lacking the RanGTP-binding domain, leads to spermatogenic failure in Ipo5 × Stra8cre testes. We interpret this to show that the loss of IPO5 transport functionality prevents sperm production. This is further supported by examination of post-testicular sperm in the caput and cauda epididymis which demonstrated a severe reduction in KO testis sperm output ( ) and the fact that the very few offspring generated from KO male matings harbored an intact locus.
 To assess fertility, four Ipo5 × Stra8Cre knockout males were mated with wildtype females. These matings resulted in formation of copulatory plugs in the females, indicating normal mating behavior. Extended breeding opportunities for 3 of the Ipo5 KO males resulted in only 1 live pup, which was cannibalized immediately after birth and thus could not be genotyped. A fourth male generated a litter of 8 pups, however these pups were Ipo5 FL/WT, indicating that the sperm contributing to these offspring did not contain the recombined floxed allele. This supports our observation that recombination directed by this construct is often incomplete. In summary, it is evident that adult male fertility is dependent on the presence of germline IPO5 in the spermatogonial population onward, and its loss has a catastrophic impact on spermatogenesis, and ultimately prevents the generation of spermatozoa.

## IPO5 binds to distinct cargoes through germline development
Given the role of IPO5 as a nuclear transport factor and the requirement for IPO5 in male germline cells, we used IP to identify IPO5 binding proteins at two stages of development: during fetal life and in post-mitotic cells of AdMTs. In E13.5 whole mouse testis extracts, the only germ cell type present is the gonocyte, or pro-spermatogonium, which is surrounded by supporting Sertoli cells within the seminiferous cords, and these are embedded in a matrix comprised of Leydig, peritubular and vascular cells as well as macrophages and fibroblasts. Six unique putative binding proteins (in addition to IPO5) were identified in these samples ( and ). Between E13.5 and birth, IPO5 is readily and preferentially detected in the cytoplasm of male germ cells, indicating it is available for cargo transport into the nucleus ( ). One of the binding proteins, splicing factor proline- and glutamine-rich (SFPQ), binds to both RNA- and DNA and was previously identified as a nuclear protein in postnatal germline cells [ ]. Using indirect immunofluorescence, SFPQ was barely detectable in E13.5 mouse testes but yielded a robust signal in germ cell nuclei of E15.5 and PND 0 testes ( ). In adult testes, SFPQ was nuclear in spermatogonia, and co-expressed with cytoplasmic IPO5 in meiotic cells ( ). The other four additional binding proteins identified include two nuclear riboproteins, HNRNPH1 and HNRNPA3, the 60 ribosomal protein RPL29, and the RNA-binding protein EWSR1.
 To identify IPO5 binding partners in two functionally distinct adult spermatogenic cell populations, enriched populations of pachytene spermatocytes and round spermatids were subjected to IP ( and ). Unique putative positive IDs were identified for 8 proteins in isolated spermatocytes and 9 in round spermatids ( ), including IPO5. Of particular interest was the finding of exportin 2 (XPO2) in both. Using reciprocal co-IP and Western blot, the interaction between XPO2 and IPO5 was confirmed ( ), and the presence of both proteins in pachytene spermatocytes and round spermatids was also evident in adult testis cross-sections by IF ( ). However, the differences in the IPO5 binding partners for these two cell types ( ) reinforces the understanding that IPO5 transports distinct cargo as spermatogenesis progresses, and functional evidence for many shows they are involved in key stages of male germline development and/or required for male fertility.
 

## Discussion
The outcomes of this study provide in vivo evidence of functional links between regulated IPO5 protein expression, gender-specific germ cell development and fertility. By creating and analyzing a series of tissue- and temporal-specific Cre recombinase lines, the impact of IPO5 loss at critical stages of both embryogenesis and male-specific germ cell development has been revealed, and new evidence for additional IPO5 binding partners provided.
Based on the lack of live births of IPO5 KO mice arising from constitutive IPO5 deletion using CMVCre, these in vivo results demonstrate IPO5 is essential for embryogenesis. The importance of IPO5 in early development was previously documented using siRNA-based knockdown to create in vitro-derived mouse embryos; these exhibited little nuclear Stella and were non-viable [ ]. Importantly, methylation of the majority of the maternal genome and at least two paternally imprinted loci in these in vitro-derived embryos was significantly lower in the absence of IPO5, implicating IPO5 in the regulation of epigenetic status during early embryogenesis, a fundamental process controlling growth and differentiation in the early post-fertilization embryo [ ]. In addition, a specific subset of IMPs, including IMPβ1 and IPO5, exhibit transiently increased expression during the first four days of ESC differentiation [ ], and the IMPβ1-null mouse line also exhibits lethality at the pre-implantation stage. Such findings extend previous discoveries relating to the functional importance of related IMPα proteins, which are also tightly regulated during development and determine cell fate in vitro [ ]. Collectively, these data reinforce the understanding that the highly regulated expression of IMPs play significant roles in driving early cell fate decisions.
Both in fetal testes and during postnatal spermatogenesis, there are pronounced changes in the expression and subcellular localization of individual IMPs in germ cells [ ]. The present analyses using VasaCre and Stra8Cre to excise IPO5 at ~E15.5 and PND3, respectively, unequivocally demonstrate that the presence of IPO5 in male germ cells is important for specific stages of their development. Excision in the fetal gonad (VasaCre) leads to a complete absence of male, but not female, germ cells, while postnatal deletion (Stra8Cre) impairs spermatogonial differentiation and severely disrupts germ cell progression into meiosis.
It is through analysis of binding partners that potential roles can be deduced, as for Stella [ ]. In this study we identify additional potential IPO5 binding proteins, and most have established roles in male fertility that fit with the regulated and high level of IPO5 expression at specific stages of germline development. SFPQ is of particular interest due to its roles in regulating gene function, repair of double-strand DNA breaks, RNA splicing, and initiation of nuclear paraspeckle formation. Its robust co-expression with IPO5 in fetal and adult male germ cells highlights this as a likely binding partner of functional significance. In the first studies of SFPQ in the testis, it was identified as an androgen receptor co-activator in Sertoli cells as they matured and developed androgen-responsiveness [ ]. Our previous survey of its distribution in testis [ ] along with its best-known binding partners, NONO and NEAT1, showed its localization in nuclei of postnatal germline cells; these findings are now extended to include the fetal testis. This and other candidates will require further validation, including examples such as HNRNPH1 and EWSR1 were identified in the E13.5 testis and are essential for fertility in mouse models [ , ]. Localization of HNRNPH1 appears to be exclusive to Sertoli cells in the E17.5 testis [ ], and its role in androgen receptor-mediated gene expression in Sertoli cells was also reported. Thus we speculate the whole testis IP performed here using the E13.5 testis identified both somatic and germline IPO5 binding partners.
Our assessment of IPO5 binding partners in the meiotic and post-meiotic germ cells of the adult testis, pachytene spermatocytes and round spermatids respectively, identified a suite of binding partners, with XPO2 (also called CAS), the only one common to both. XPO2 facilitates protein nuclear export of IMP⍺ proteins [ ]. We speculate that XPO2 binding to IPO5 restricts its capacity to recycle IMP⍺ proteins to the cytoplasm. This would be similar to the role previously described for IPO5 as anchoring the full-length interleukin-33 protein (FLIL33) within the nucleus; this interaction appears non-essential for FLIL33 nuclear transport but protects it from proteasomal degradation and thereby governs IL-33-mediated inflammatory responses [ ].
In both humans and rodents, several IMP⍺ proteins are localized to the nucleus in the same germline cells in which we see high levels of IPO5 [ , ]. This may reflect their involvement in the extensive changes in nuclear protein composition, for example the histone to transition protein to protamine transitions that are central to these stages of spermatogenesis. The binding of IPO5 to histone proteins documented here indicates an involvement in these fundamental processes required for sperm formation.
IPO5 was previously identified as a binding partner in murine spermatogonia for the RNA binding protein, Musashi-1 (MSHI1) [ ]. Outcomes from this study led to a model in which IPO5 is linked to meiotic processes, another highly regulated step in germline maturation. During spermatogonial exit from the mitotic cell cycle, IPO5 binds to MSI1 in the cytoplasm, directing it to the XY chromatin domain unique to meiotic spermatocytes. This results in Msi2 RNA release from translational repression by MSI1 which permits meiotic progression [ ]. This model invokes a role for IPO5 during the cell cycle transition when the nuclear envelope breaks down and then reforms. A similar phenomenon has been described for IPO5 in mitotic cell cycle studies. In MelJuSo cells, IPO5 delivers the 750 ka 20S proteasome components into the nucleus within 20 min following nuclear reformation at the end of mitosis [ ]. Importantly, the level of nuclear proteasome delivered increases following ectopic over-expression of IPO5, providing mechanistic evidence of the functional impact of changing IPO5 levels. Changes in IPO5 protein stoichiometry have also been shown to mediate neural differentiation of embryonic stem cells [ ], demonstrating that these observations are not limited to the germline.
The present study highlights the intriguing potential for IPO5 to bind to many different proteins required for spermatogenic progression and/or that are required for male fertility, highlighting targets for validation. In spermatocytes, the binding of Histone H4 is in agreement with results from an earlier in vitro study; nuclear transport by IPO5 of newly synthesized monomeric histone subunits H4 and H3 was demonstrated in vitro to precede histone-chaperone binding [ ]. Additional potential binding partners in spermatocytes include DNA heterogeneous nuclear riboprotein (HNRPF) [ ], HNRNPU [ ], HSP7C (encoded by Hspa8) [ ], and ODPAT [ , ]. Absence of ODPAT results in male infertility. There are currently no functional data relating to fertility regarding the transitional endoplasmic reticulum ATPase, TERA (also known as Valosin Containing Protein [VCP]) [ ].
Most of the potential IPO5 binding proteins identified in round spermatids are directly implicated in governing male fertility, including the ATP synthase F1 subunit (ATPG) [ ], cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor subunit 5 (CPSF5), also known as Nudix hydroxylase 21 [ ], insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) [ , ], and outer dense fiber of sperm tails 2 (ODF2) [ ]. Histone H1t (H1T) is among the testis-specific subtypes present in the nuclei of spermatocytes and spermatids as they undergo extensive chromatin remodeling and replacement by transition proteins. As a poor condenser of chromatin, H1T has been implicated in repressing retroposons in pachytene spermatocytes and IPO5 was previously identified as an H1t-associated protein [ , ].
The diminished survival of heterozygous CMVCre × IPO5FLFLembryos, monitored at E12.5, provides intriguing evidence that IPO5 dosage influences cellular function. In HEK293T cells and NHLFs cell lines, deletion of IPO5 using shRNA or clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9 constructs were not informative because the cells lacking IPO5 were non-viable [ ]. This may be important in the context of human fertility, where no IPO5 loss-of-function variants (nonsense, frameshift, splice) were identified by exome sequencing of the ESTAND cohort (Estonian Andrology) comprised of ~640 infertile men and 321 fertile men [ , ]. In an analysis of rare variants, four men were predicted to carry heterozygous IPO5 missense variants located in DNA sequence positions that are 100% conserved from zebrafish to human. These are predicted to be Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS) by the ACMG guidelines, with high CADD scores (26–32) suggestive of a possible functional consequence (personal communication: Professor Maris Laan and Anna-Grete Juchnewitsch). These findings support the hypothesis that IPO5 is essential for life, while further analysis of this and other databases is likely to elucidate the clinical significance of IPO5 variants for male fertility.
These new results reinforce the concept that distinct nuclear import factors are required at specific stages of mammalian embryonic and adult germ cell development, with IPO5 clearly playing a critical role at all stages from the gonocyte to post-meiotic germ cells to drive progressive cell fate choices. We identified several proteins as potential IPO5 binding proteins which were previously demonstrated to be essential for mammalian gametogenesis and male fertility. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of understanding how the levels and localization of IPO5 and its binding partners levels are regulated at distinct stages and cell types within the testis, a focus for our future research.
