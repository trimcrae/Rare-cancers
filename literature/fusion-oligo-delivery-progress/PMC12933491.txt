## Abstract
Endogenous RNAs orchestrate cellular processes through
precise
spatiotemporal regulation, and live-cell imaging of these molecules
is essential for dissecting their roles in gene expression, disease
progression, and cellular homeostasis. While traditional methods rely
on exogenous labeling or fixation, fluorescent RNA aptamer-based sensors
have emerged as transformative tools for visualizing endogenous RNAs
in their native context. These sensors allow real-time tracking of
mRNA localization, miRNA activity, and RNA–protein interactions
with minimal disturbance. However, challenges such as the requirement
for exogenous fluorogenic dyes, limited brightness, photostability,
and target specificity in complex cellular environments hinder their
wider application. This review provides a comprehensive overview of
recent advances in fluorescent RNA aptamer-based sensors, discussing
the design principles, mechanisms of fluorescence activation, and
their application in live-cell RNA imaging. We also address the current
limitations and future directions for improving these sensors, highlighting
their transformative potential in RNA biology and their implications
for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
Keywords: RNA Aptamers, Imaging, Biosensors, RNA Dynamics, Fluorogenic Dyes

## Introduction
RNA molecules are critical
in cellular and gene expression regulation. They adopt specific structures to impact transcription,
RNA processing, translation, and turnover. RNA types include mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, and regulatory RNAs, with mRNA-encoding proteins and other RNAs involved
in chromosomal organization, DNA repair, and metabolic regulation.
,
 Some RNAs also have catalytic activity, similar to enzymes. RNA structures play a significant role in various
physiological and pathological processes. The dynamic behavior of
endogenous RNAs, such as mRNA trafficking to specific subcellular
compartments or miRNA-mediated gene silencing, underpins critical
biological events ranging from embryogenesis to cancer metastasis.
For instance, the asymmetric distribution of β-actin mRNA in
migrating fibroblasts regulates cell polarity, while dysregulated
miRNA-21 expression in tumors drives oncogenic signaling. Imaging the localization and dynamics of RNAs
helps uncover their function and reveals novel mechanisms at single-cell
resolution. RNA imaging has highlighted the importance of post-transcriptional
mechanisms like mRNA localization, but challenges remain due to technological
limitations and RNA biology complexity. While protein imaging is routine,
RNA imaging in live cells has been less explored, though recent advances
in RNA binding proteins and chemical biology have improved visualization
tools. Notwithstanding these advancements, unresolved challenges persist
in achieving high-fidelity real-time visualization of endogenous RNA
within intact mammalian systems, alongside comprehensive monitoring
of spatiotemporal RNA dynamics and functional interactions in native
biological contexts.
Current RNA imaging techniques are primarily
divided into fixed
and live-cell RNA imaging. Unlike FISH-based fixed-cell imaging, live-cell
RNA imaging provides temporal information, enabling the analysis of
gene expression regulation and dynamic RNA processes.

 This approach establishes a multidimensional analytical framework
for investigating RNA lifecycle processesspanning biosynthesis,
spatial distribution, regulatory mechanisms, intracellular retention,
and disposal pathwaysin diverse cellular populations.
,
 In vivo RNA imaging technologies fall into two main categories:
fluorescent protein-based systems, such as the MS2/MCP,

When tracing

the proposal and realization of creating ’RNA mimics of GFP’.
Using SELEX against the GFP fluorophore derivative 3,5-difluoro-4-hydroxybenzylidene

Mango aptamers that bind thiazole orange (TO) derivatives, the orange/red SRB-2/SR-DN system,

## Fluorescent RNA Aptamers: Design and Mechanism
Fluorescent RNA aptamers are single-stranded RNA molecules that
bind specific fluorogenic ligands, enhancing fluorescence. These aptamers
are valuable tools for RNA detection, imaging, and biosensing applications.
The design of fluorescent RNA aptamers typically involves in vitro
selection methods such as SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by
Exponential Enrichment) to isolate sequences with desired binding
and fluorescence properties. The mechanism
of fluorescence enhancement in RNA aptamers often relies on base quadruples
(tetrads) to constrain the photoexcited chromophore. Structural studies reveal that these quadruplexes form
rigid binding pockets. The planar chromophore of the dye stacks between
the faces of these tetrads or within a cavity formed by them. This
constrained environment significantly restricts rotational and vibrational
freedom of the dye molecule in its excited state, a major pathway
for nonradiative energy loss. This restriction minimizes internal
conversion and promotes radiative decay, leading to the dramatic fluorescence
enhancement characteristic of these aptamers. This structural feature is shared among unrelated fluorogenic RNAs,
highlighting its importance in fluorescence activation. Additionally,
some aptamers can be designed as ″structure-switching signaling
aptamers,″ which undergo conformational changes upon ligand
binding, resulting in increased fluorescence.

2.1.

## Principles of RNA Aptamer Design for Fluorescence
RNA aptamers designed for fluorescence applications typically employ
structure-switching mechanisms and specific binding interactions to
generate detectable signals. These aptamers can be rationally designed
or selected through in vitro evolution to bind nonfluorescent dyes
and activate their fluorescence, making them valuable tools for biosensing
and molecular imaging ( ). The design principles of
fluorescent RNA aptamers often involve the use of base quadruples
(tetrads) to constrain the photoexcited chromophore, as revealed by
3D structural studies of fluorogenic RNAs crucial for efficient fluorescence
activation.

 For fluorescence-based applications, RNA aptamers
can be designed
to interact with a fluorophore or a fluorescent dye that enables them
to emit light upon excitation. The aptamers are usually modified by
attaching a fluorophore at specific positions within the RNA sequence,
often through covalent linkage at the RNA’s 3′ or 5′
ends. Alternatively, RNA aptamers can be designed to include specific
fluorescent probe structures, such as the 2-aminopurine (2-AP) nucleotide,
which fluoresces upon binding to a specific target molecule. The optimization of RNA aptamers for fluorescence
typically involves the balancing of factors such as binding affinity,
selectivity, and fluorescence intensity to achieve optimal performance
in real-time imaging. Understanding the
precise structural basis of dye binding and fluorescence activation,
as revealed by crystallography and NMR studies, is crucial for rational
engineering efforts aimed at improving these parameters.

2.2.

## Mechanism of Fluorescence Activation upon
RNA Binding
Fluorescence activation upon RNA binding involves
complex mechanisms that vary depending on the specific RNA-fluorophore
system. The fluorescence mechanism of RNA aptamers largely depends
on incorporating fluorophores that either emit fluorescence directly
upon excitation or undergo conformational changes that activate fluorescence. Different RNA aptamers employ distinct mechanisms
for fluorescence activation ( ).

Fluorophore-quencher pairs: Some RNA aptamers
are designed to bring a fluorophore and a quencher nearby when bound
to their target, leading to fluorescence quenching in the free state.
Upon target binding, the aptamer undergoes a conformational change
that separates the fluorophore from the quencher, restoring fluorescence. This mechanism is commonly seen in molecular
beacons, where the RNA aptamer functions as a “sensor”.

Fluorophore stabilization:
In other cases,
the fluorophore is embedded within the aptamer structure so that its
fluorescence is initially suppressed due to interactions with the
RNA itself or neighboring bases. Binding to a target induces a structural
rearrangement that releases the fluorophore from its quenching environment,
leading to a fluorescence ″turn-on″ effect. This design is helpful in bioimaging for detecting
specific cellular biomarkers or monitoring molecular interactions
in live cells.

Aptamer-target-induced
conformational changes: RNA aptamers may undergo large-scale
conformational changes upon
binding to a specific target. These structural transitions can lead
to the exposure or de-exposure of fluorophores embedded in the aptamer,
causing fluorescence to either increase or decrease, depending on
the nature of the binding molecules. This mechanism is central to
the functionality of RNA-based sensors in imaging applications.

 The common thread among these mechanisms, underpinned
by structural
insights, is the aptamer’s ability to impose a specific, rigid
conformation on the fluorophore upon binding. This conformational
constraint, often facilitated by interactions within pockets formed
by base quadruples or other tertiary structures, effectively suppresses
nonradiative decay pathways that dissipate energy as heat. Consequently,
the probability of radiative decay increases dramatically, enabling
sensitive detection.

3.

## Fluorescent RNA Aptamer-Based Sensors for Imaging
Endogenous RNA
Imaging endogenous RNA within live cells has
become an essential
tool for understanding the dynamics of RNA biology in real time. The
ability to visualize RNA molecules directly offers insights into critical
cellular processes, including transcription, splicing, transport,
localization, and degradation. Fluorescent RNA aptamers have enabled
the detection and visualization of endogenous RNA species with high
spatial and temporal resolution ( ).
 3.1.

## Imaging Endogenous mRNAs
The ability
to track endogenous mRNAs in live cells is critical for understanding
their roles in cellular polarization, stress responses, and disease.
Traditional methods such as MS2-GFP require genetic modification,
which may alter RNA dynamics. Fluorescent RNA aptamers overcome this
limitation through direct, label-free imaging.
3.1.1.

## Aptamer-Initiated Fluorescence Complementation
Method (AiFc)
Green fluorescent protein (GFP) emits green
fluorescence, and primarily used in advance bioscience research as
in vivo reporter protein. GFP contains a 4-hydroxybenzimidazolinone
(HBI) fluorophore. HBI and denatured GFP obtained by chemical synthesis
are nonfluorescent and only upon refolding the folded GFP protein
specifically contact the fluorophore, thereby preventing intramolecular
motion and restoring GFP fluorescence. 2011, Jaffrey et al. designed
a new HBI derivative 3,5-difluoro-4-hydroxybenzylidene imidazolinone
(DFHBI) was screened for its corresponding RNA aptamer by exponential
enrichment ligand phylogenetic techniques (SELEX) and named ″Spinach″
because of its green color of luminescence. Spinach was shown to fuse with the 3′ end of 5S, a noncoding
RNA, and effectively label it, resulting in the observation of green
fluorescence in cells shortly after Spinach transcription. Following
the discovery of Spinach and DFHBI, the system was continuously refined.
Additional DFHBI analogs with distinct fluorescence spectra were developed,
and various Spinach aptamer variants. Broccoli, a 49nt variant of Spinach, emerged as an improved version
due to its reduced dependence on magnesium ions, higher intracellular
folding efficiency, and increased brightness, which has gained significant
attention ( ).
 To use fluorescent aptamers to image endogenous
mRNAs, researchers
have explored a variety of applications. The strategy of disassembling
the intact RNA aptamer to make the fluorescence disappear and then
designing it to bind to the target RNA close to each other to restore
the aptamer structure and thus direct the fluorescence to turn on
is very effective. The ″Split-Spinach″ concept was proposed
by Rogers et al. in 2015, who found that Spinach aptamers could be
split in half at a suitable site in a third stem, and fluorescence
could be restored after assembly. However,
although he showed that splitting Spinach aptamers is a potential
tool for monitoring artificial or natural RNA, he did not apply it.
2018, Wang et al. reported an aptamer-initiated fluorescence complementation
method (AiFc) for RNA imaging. They split
an intact RNA aptamer into two parts at suitable sites, each part
tandem with a part of the complementary sequence of the target RNA,
and only in the presence and hybridization of the target RNA can the
two luminescent RNA aptamer parts be drawn together thereby restoring
them to their fluorescent form. They split Baby Spinach and Broccoli
by screening cleavage sites and successfully used them for imaging
several different endogenous mRNAs. The AiFc splitting strategy essentially
reduces the presence of background signal, and the hybridization region
of the target RNA can be replaced with a variety of RNAs, significantly
increasing its versatility ( ). To use AiFc to other fluorescent RNA aptamers should
potentially expand its application in RNA multicolor imaging.
 
3.1.2.

## Inert Pepper System (iPepper)
Pepper-based
fluorescent RNA aptamer sensors have emerged as powerful tools for
imaging and detecting various targets in live cells. These sensors
exhibit superior brightness, photostability, and signal-to-background
ratio compared to other fluorescent light-up RNA aptamers (FLAPs). The Pepper aptamer folds into a unique monomeric
non-G-quadruplex tuning-fork-like architecture, which allows for efficient
fluorophore binding and activation. Chen
et al. developed the fluorescent dye HBC, featuring a rigid electron
acceptor and a strong electron donor. HBC remains nonfluorescent in
solution but regains fluorescence upon binding to the Pepper RNA aptamer.
By modifying donor–acceptor properties, they created HBC analogs
emitting fluorescence from green to red, all compatible with Pepper.
Their experiments demonstrated Pepper’s potential for real-time
RNA tagging and imaging, offering improved stability, higher binding
affinity, and enhanced fluorescence compared to previous aptamers.

Wang et al. introduced the inert Pepper
system (iPepper), modifying the original aptamer by adding complementary
sequences at its 5′ and 3′ ends ( ). This design enables fluorescence activation
only in the presence of target RNA. They also developed iPepper tandem
arrays, significantly enhancing fluorescence intensity and enabling
effective imaging of low-expression endogenous mRNAs.

 
3.1.3.

## INSIGHT System
To amplify the signal
while preserving the localization information of the target mRNAs,
Ren et al. designed a genetically encoded in situ amplification strategy
called INSIGHT. By dividing the broccoli
into two parts, using a hybridization chain reaction (HCR) related
design, so that the system trigger cascade hybridization of the hairpin
structure only in the presence of the target RNA binding, thus activating
the amplified fluorescent signal, and at the same time the designed
broccoli strand will be associated with the target RNA strand to achieve
real-time localization of the target RNA. The INSIGHT system has been
demonstrated to apply to RNA imaging of bacterial cells and living
mammalian cells ( ).
 
3.1.4.

## Co-expressing RNA Sensor Based on SRB-2
The SRB-2 aptamer, initially identified against sulforhodamine
B, exhibits exceptional adaptability in attaching to a range of dyes
displaying diverse hues, leading to either an enhancement or suppression
of fluorescence. This characteristic
positions SRB-2 as a promising candidate for fluorescence microscopy
and biological analyses. By conducting a thorough examination of fluorophore
structural components and dissociation constants, researchers have
successfully engineered a novel, vivid, orange fluorescent turn-on
probe termed TMR-DN. This probe facilitates nonwash live-cell RNA
imaging, featuring a notably enhanced signal-to-background ratio.
In a study by Sunbul et al., a sensor was created in which specific
RNA aptamers engage with fluorophore-quencher conjugates to regenerate
fluorescence. The researchers employed sulfonyl rhodamine B (SR) and
its corresponding aptamer, SRB-2, in conjunction with dinitroaniline
(DN) as a triggering agent, thereby establishing the SRB-2/SR-DN system.

Ying et al. designed an RNA sensor based
on a single promoter system for mRNA ratio imaging. By coexpressing
SRB-2 aptamer and GFP in a single transcript, they avoided the issues
of signal misalignment between dual promoters ( ). To ensure proper fluorescence, they incorporated
a substrate site cleaved by Dicer enzyme and an mRNA-stabilizing triplex
to prevent rapid GFP mRNA degradation. This system improved proportional
imaging and provided a more reliable approach for RNA aptamer-GFP
coexpression.

 

3.2.

## Detecting miRNAs in Live Cells
MicroRNAs
(miRNAs) regulate gene expression at post-transcriptional levels,
and their dysregulation is linked to diseases such as cancer. Fluorescent
aptamers offer a powerful tool for real-time miRNA detection.

3.2.1.

## Sensor “Pandan”
To
enable the imaging of natural RNA without genetic manipulation, researchers
have explored modifications to fluorescent RNA aptamers. Aw et al.
introduced additional stem-loops into Spinach and changed its 3′
and 5′ ends to include two sequence-variable stem-loops encoding
complementary sequences of the target microRNA. Only after binding target microRNA, the reconstruction
of the RNA scaffold can be reconstituted for DFHBI binding and fluorescence
emission, which finally triggers a substantial enhancement of fluorescence.
They named this new circular bimolecular sensor ″Pandan″
and accomplished the detection of miRNA in vitro. Although Pandan did not complete the detection of intracellular
miRNAs, this hybridization-induced structural repair of RNA aptamers
and, thus, restoration of fluorescence provided a new idea for aptamers
uses.

3.2.2.

## FASTmiR Probes
Huang et al., designed
FASTmiR by using similar strategy of hybridization-induced structural
repair as Pandan. They accomplished rapid
quantification of microRNAs in vitro and direct detection and localization
of microRNAs in vivo by generating a tandem repeat sequence of a sensor
stabilized by a three-way junction (SWJ) based on Spinach. The system
has been validated to complete detection using both miR122 and miR171
as targets, demonstrating its potential to detect various microRNAs
with modified binding site region sequences specifically.

3.2.3.

## Sensor Based on the SRB-2/SR-DN System
Based on the SRB-2/SR-DN system, Ying et al. reported a microRNA
sensor for live mammalian cell imaging, incorporating a complementary
sequence of the target microRNA into the SRB-2 aptamer. This disrupted
the aptamer’s structure, preventing SR-DN fluorescence recovery
until the target microRNA was present. The aptamer was protected with
a tRNA structure to ensure stability in cells. This system enabled
the correct folding of SRB-2 and fluorescence restoration only in
the presence of the target microRNA. They
also designed a coexpression system with RNA sensors and GFP for proportional
imaging of microRNAs in tumor cells.

3.3.

## Visualization of Other Noncoding RNAs
3.3.1.

## CHARGE Circuit
Karunanayake et
al. designed genetically encoded RNA-based catalytic hairpin assembly
circuit, CHARGE, for sensitively detecting low-abundance RNA in cells. They divided Broccoli into two nonfluorescent
parts, which were bound to the ends of two catalytic hairpin assemblies
(CHA), and the target RNA served as a catalyst. The presence of the
target RNA causes the two catalytic hairpins to assemble and simultaneously
restore the fluorescence of Broccoli ( ). In contrast, the target RNA catalyze the
activation of tens to hundreds of Broccoli fluorescence, enabling
highly sensitive detection of sRNA, and the circuit has been demonstrated
in bacterial cells. However, although the CHARGE circuit is highly
sensitive, but cannot provide information about the location of the
target RNA because the target RNA is shed after the end of catalysis.
 
3.3.2.

## Corn-Based Fluorescent Light-Up Biosensor
Song et al. synthesized DFHO, an RFP fluorophore mimic, based on
RFP’s structure.
,
 DFHO, containing a
N-acyl imine substituent, demonstrated lower background fluorescence,
low cytotoxicity, and enhanced photostability, making it ideal for
cellular applications. A fluorescent RNA aptamer, named ″maize″
due to its yellow fluorescence, was developed through SELEX and truncation
analysis. A distinctive feature of the Corn aptamer is its dimeric
structure, which is crucial for DFHO fluorescence activation. Based
on this, Gu et al. designed the RIFC probe, which splits Corn into
two parts, each containing the target RNA’s recognition sequence. When the target RNA is present, the aptamers
self-assemble and activate DFHO fluorescence. Verified for imaging
microRNAs in living cells, the RIFC probe is versatile and can be
adapted to image various RNAs by replacing the target sequence, making
it a ″plug-and-play″ tool. Corn-based fluorogenic RNA
aptamer sensors offer significant advantages in cellular imaging and
biosensor applications. The ability to convert Corn into a small-molecule-regulated
fluorescent biosensor, as demonstrated with the S-adenosylmethionine
(SAM) sensor, and its use in split aptamer systems showcase the versatility
of this aptamer.

Qian Zhang et
al. designed a biosensor using a cyclic cleavage reaction to amplify
RNA aptamer-Corn for the sensitive detection of lncRNA. By employing
magnetic beads (MBs) for target enrichment and separation, they achieved
highly sensitive quantification of lncRNA at femtomolar levels, with
the ability to distinguish tumor cells from normal ones. The approach
could be easily adapted to detect various lncRNAs by altering the
capture probe sequences, offering a new tool for molecular diagnostics. These developments provide new opportunities
for real-time visualization and mechanistic analysis of nucleic acids
in living cells, with potential applications in tumor-associated RNA
detection and RNA/RNA interaction monitoring ( ).
 

3.4.

## Multiplexed RNA Imaging with Dual-Color Aptamers
Dual-color fluorescent RNA aptamer sensors offer significant advantages
for RNA detection and imaging, including ratiometric analysis, improved
sensitivity, and the potential for multicolor imaging. These systems
have shown promise in various applications, from real-time studies
in cell and synthetic biology to the detection of specific RNA targets
in living cells.
3.4.1.

## FRET-Enabled Dual-Color System
Early generation RNA quantification systems relied on coexpressed
GFP mRNA under unified transcriptional control as calibration modules.
Nevertheless, the photophysical constraints and spatial resolution
limitations inherent to GFP-based references prompted the development
of next-generation RNA imaging platforms. Contemporary solutions employ
dual-output RNA aptamer systems that implement FRET-enabled ratiometric
detection, significantly improving sensitivity through coordinated
fluorophore pairing.

Pioneering
work by Zhang et al. engineered a conformation-switching aptamer-fluorophore
assembly exhibiting cyan-to-NIR spectral conversion upon target binding,
permitting quantitative NIR-based RNA surveillance with single-cell
resolution across heterogeneous expression gradients. The progression toward multimodal detection has enabled
strategic construction of chimeric aptamer assemblies. By implementing
Watson–Crick mediated fusion of Mango-IV and iSpinach aptamers,
investigators achieved cross-aptamer FRET activation, laying the groundwork
for multiplexed RNA tracking systems that resolve colocalized transcripts
with spatial precision.

3.4.2.

## Dicer-Mediated Proportional Imaging
Dou et al. developed a ratiometric biosensing platform leveraging
complementary fluorescent aptamers for microRNA quantification in
mammalian systems. Their design incorporated Mango aptamer as a stable
calibration unit paired with target-responsive SRB-2 aptamer, interconnected
via a Dicer-sensitive dsRNA bridge enabling endogenous enzyme-mediated
signal modulation. The sensor restores fluorescence in both Mango
and SRB-2 upon target microRNA binding and Dicer cleavage, enabling
proportional imaging ( ). This strategy minimizes interference and provides a new
approach for RNA imaging in living cells, offering potential for building
more RNA imaging platforms.

 

3.5.

## Super-Resolution Imaging with Fluorescent
RNA Aptamers
Super-resolution (SR) microscopy techniques
break the diffraction limit of light, enabling visualization of cellular
structures and biomolecule dynamics at the nanoscale. Applying these
techniques to RNA imaging holds immense promise for uncovering the
true spatial organization and dynamics of transcripts, such as within
stress granules or at transcription sites. However, this imposes stringent
requirements on the fluorescent probe: high photon output, excellent
photostability, and efficient labeling. While early FRAPs struggled
to meet these demands, recent advances have yielded aptamers capable
of SR imaging. The SiRA (Silicon Rhodamine-Binding Aptamer) aptamer, binding the cell-permeable dye SiR, demonstrated
exceptional photostability and single-molecule brightness, enabling
robust dSTORM imaging of mRNA distribution and dynamics in fixed and
living cells with ∼ 20 nm resolution. SiRA revealed the nanoscale
organization of NEAT1 lncRNA within paraspeckles. The exceptional
brightness and photostability reported for Okra and Clivia suggest these newer
aptamers are also highly promising candidates for pushing the boundaries
of live-cell RNA SR imaging, potentially enabling longer trajectories
and higher localization precision for single RNA molecules. Combining
SR-capable aptamers with advanced microscopy techniques will be crucial
for dissecting the nanoscale architecture and dynamic interactions
of the RNA transcriptome.

4.

## Advantages over Traditional RNA Imaging Methods
Fluorescent RNA aptamer-based sensors offer several advantages
compared to traditional RNA imaging methods, such as in situ hybridization
and RNA probes.
4.1.

## Real-Time, Dynamic Imaging of Endogenous RNA
Fluorescent RNA aptamer-based sensors enable real-time, continuous
monitoring of endogenous RNA molecules in live cells. Unlike traditional
methods that often rely on fixed samples and provide only static one-time
observations, RNA aptamers allow dynamic observation of RNA behavior,
interactions, and localization within their native cellular context.

4.2.

## High Specificity and Sensitivity
RNA aptamers can be engineered for high specificity, enabling precise
targeting of specific RNA sequences. This reduces nonspecific binding
and improves imaging accuracy, particularly for low-abundance or transient
RNA species. In comparison, traditional methods often lack the sensitivity
to detect such targets.

4.3.

## No Amplification Required
Unlike
traditional RNA probes, which typically require amplification techniques
to enhance the signal, RNA aptamers do not need any amplification
steps. This simplifies the imaging process, reduces potential experimental
errors or false signals, and offers a more direct and reliable method
of detecting RNA.

4.4.

## Enhanced Versatility and Modifiability
RNA aptamers can be easily modified to improve their properties,
such as fluorescence intensity, photostability, and stability. This
flexibility allows for the customization of aptamers for various applications,
from single-molecule imaging to multiplexed RNA detection, making
them a versatile tool in RNA biology research.

5.

## Challenges, Limitations, and Future Directions
While fluorescent RNA aptamers hold immense promise, their application
to endogenous RNA imaging faces unique hurdles. To address the challenges
associated with RNA aptamer-based sensors, several strategies are
being explored.
5.1.

## Specificity Issues and Technology Integration
Many endogenous RNAs share high sequence similarity or exist as
splice variants. Current aptamers often lack single-nucleotide resolution,
leading to cross-reactivity. Also, RNA folding states vary with cellular
conditions, potentially occluding aptamer-binding sites or generating
false signals.
Integrating RNA aptamer-based sensors with emerging
technologies could significantly enhance future advancements in these
sensors. For example, coupling RNA aptamers with gene-editing techniques
could enable targeted imaging of specific RNA species in genetically
modified organisms. Additionally, integrating RNA aptamers with single-cell
sequencing technologies could provide unprecedented insights into
the spatiotemporal dynamics of RNA expression at the single-cell level,
offering new ways for understanding cellular heterogeneity and gene
regulation.

5.2.

## Sensitivity Amplification and Signal Enhancement
Transcripts like transcription factor mRNAs or rare noncoding RNAs
exist at ultralow levels (<10 copies/cell). Existing aptamer-dye
systems struggle to achieve sufficient signal-to-noise ratios without
amplification. Background noise also is a very important question.
Autofluorescence from cellular components and nonspecific dye-RNA
interactions obscure target-specific signals, particularly in densely
packed organelles like nucleoli.
Developing RNA aptamers with
enhanced optical properties is crucial to further expanding their
utility for imaging. This includes designing aptamers that exhibit
red-shifted fluorescence, allowing for deeper tissue penetration and
reduced autofluorescence interference. Optimizing photostability,
brightness, and signal-to-noise ratios will make these sensors more
reliable and practical for complex imaging environments.

5.3.

## Delivery and Stability Optimization
Current transfection methods may alter RNA expression profiles or
induce stress responses, skewing imaging results. Moreover, aptamer
degradation by ribonucleases limits long-term observation.
A
significant challenge remains the optimization of aptamer stability
in vivo. RNA aptamers are prone to degradation in biological environments,
which limits their long-term use in diagnostic and therapeutic settings.
Strategies to enhance the chemical stability of aptamers and protect
them from enzymatic degradation are critical. Furthermore, developing
efficient and targeted delivery systems will be essential to ensure
the effective and precise targeting of biomolecules without causing
off-target effects or degradation.

5.4.

## Multiplexing and Super-Resolution Imaging
Most aptamer-dye pairs emit in the green spectrum, hindering simultaneous
imaging of multiple RNAs. Also, current dual-color systems require
stringent spectral unmixing and lack modularity for diverse RNA targets.
The rational design of next-generation fluorogenic probes requires
molecular engineering of dyes with enhanced sensitivity, superior
photostability, and tunable spectral properties to advance RNA-targeting
biosensing systems. Crucially, concurrent expansion of aptamer libraries
through directed evolution and computational design will optimize
specificity and enhance binding affinities for diverse pathophysiological
biomarkers. This synergistic optimization of probe-aptamer pairs translates
to diagnostic platforms with improved clinical resolution while expanding
their multiplexing capacity across complex biological matrices.

5.5.

## Clinical Translation and Diagnostic Potential
Fluorescent RNA aptamer-based sensors show immense promise for
clinical diagnostics, offering precise, real-time monitoring of biomolecular
interactions. These sensors could detect diseases early, facilitate
personalized treatment strategies, and enable noninvasive diagnostics.
However, translating laboratory research to clinical application demands
rigorous validation in complex biological systems, particularly in
human clinical trials.

6.

## Conclusion
Fluorescent RNA aptamer-based
sensors are revolutionizing the study
of endogenous RNAs by enabling real-time, label-free imaging of mRNA
dynamics, miRNA activity, and RNA-protein interactions in live cells.
These tools have illuminated previously inaccessible phenomena, such
as the phase separation of stress granule-associated mRNAs or the
spatiotemporal regulation of oncogenic miRNAs in tumor microenvironments.
While challenges in sensitivity, multiplexing, and in vivo compatibility
persist, emerging strategiesfrom CRISPR-coupled targeting
to NIR-optimized fluorophorespromise to bridge these gaps.
As these technologies mature, they will not only deepen our understanding
of RNA biology but also pave the way for clinical innovations, such
as intraoperative RNA-guided surgery or personalized therapeutics
based on real-time RNA profiling. The next decade will undoubtedly
witness fluorescent aptamers transitioning from bench-side curiosities
to cornerstone tools in both basic research and translational medicine.
