## Significance
Approved RNA interference (RNAi) therapeutics marked groundbreaking advances. Yet, the clinical translation of RNAi for cancer treatment remains unrealized—largely due to the predominant delivery of siRNAs to hepatocytes. Our study uncovers a novel crosstalk mechanism that shapes the metastatic liver niche, offering a compelling opportunity to transform this apparent limitation into a therapeutic advantage. By selectively targeting NPY expression in hepatocytes, rather than in tumor cells, we introduce a paradigm for the treatment of hepatic metastases.
Keywords: metastasis, metastatic niche, NPY, liver metastasis, cancer

## Abstract
RNA interference (RNAi) therapeutics represent breakthrough discoveries, but their use in cancer remains limited due to hepatocyte-specific targeting. Cancer metastasis is regulated by complex crosstalk between tumor cells and niche-derived factors. However, the molecular mechanisms enabling metastatic seeding and outgrowth in the liver remain incompletely understood, representing a major clinical challenge. We identified neuropeptide Y (NPY) as a promotor of liver metastasis. Hepatocyte-derived NPY attracts metastatic tumor cells to the liver niche. Subsequent microenvironment activation induces TGFβ, promoting a vicious cycle of perimetastatic NPY secretion and liver metastasis. Concomitantly, cancer cells upregulate the NPY-5 receptor (Y5R) which is correlated with liver metastasis. NPY-Y5R crosstalk drives chemotactic migration via cAMP and ERK signaling. Moreover, NPY-Y5R activation dephosphorylates checkpoint kinase 2 to promote clonogenicity and proliferation of cancer cells. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are a promising drug delivery vehicle for siRNAs. LNPs carrying siRNA pools targeting NPY were designed, and preclinical studies provided evidence for efficacy for the treatment of liver metastasis. Our findings transform the limitation of hepatocyte specificity of RNA interference into a therapeutic advantage, introducing a paradigm for the treatment of hepatic metastases.

RNA interference (RNAi) is revolutionizing pharmaceutical development, and highly modified siRNAs can silence any target in hepatocytes with unprecedented potency ( ). However, mainly due to hepatocyte-specific targeting, their use in cancer remains limited.
Cancer-related mortality is, above all, caused by metastasis ( ). The liver is the most common site for secondary tumors arising from various types of solid malignancies including melanoma, colorectal, pancreatic, and breast cancers ( , ). Successful liver colonization depends on dynamic interactions between disseminated cancer cells and the hepatic microenvironment ( – ). Key contributors include niche-derived cytokines such as transforming growth factor β (TGFβ), facilitating stromal remodeling and immune evasion ( ), as well as few chemokine–G-protein-coupled receptor signaling axes identified to date ( , , ). However, the molecular mechanisms enabling metastatic seeding and outgrowth in the liver remain incompletely understood, representing a major clinical challenge ( , , ).
Neuropeptide Y (NPY) is a sympathetic nervous system (SNS) related cotransmitter ( , ). Moreover, NPY and its G-protein-coupled receptor subtypes constitute a conserved, chemokine-like system which is involved in various biological functions ( – ). With respect to the liver, NPY was identified by our group as a crucial target in cirrhosis and related complications including portal hypertension ( , , ) and hepatocellular carcinoma ( ). Several studies confirmed that NPY affects chronic liver disease via promotion of fibrosis ( ), the metabolic syndrome ( , ), adipocyte–macrophage crosstalk ( ), and cholesterol synthesis ( ). However, unlike its role in chronic liver disease and evidence supporting that NPY affects tumor pathobiology, its overall role in cancer dissemination remained underexplored ( ). In particular, the impact of the NPY system on liver metastasis was unclear ( ).

## Results

## NPY Is Expressed by Hepatocytes in the Metastatic Liver Niche.
A preclinical mouse model using splenic injection of syngeneic B16 melanoma cells ( ) was employed to explore liver metastasis ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–D). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), differential gene expression (DGEA), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of metastatic liver tissue (MET) confirmed induction of metastatic melanoma-related signatures as defined in humans ( ) and mice ( ). These included proliferation-, migration-, and differentiation-associated gene set categories (Dataset S1), like DNA damage response and related ATM-CHK2-E2F signaling, melanocyte-inducing transcription factor (MITF) signatures, TGFβ signaling and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) ( and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 E–H, S2, and S3 A and B and Dataset S2). In particular, NPY-related processes (GPCR, MAPK/ERK, cAMP, chemokine, and SNS signaling) were also enriched ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Considering our previous studies revealing that NPY affects chronic liver disease ( , , , ) and its unclear role in cancer dissemination ( ), these findings provided a rationale for a comprehensive mechanistic dissection of NPY’s impact on liver metastasis. First, the expression of NPY was characterized in detail. NPY mRNA levels and NPY/gp70 ratios (glycoprotein 70 (gp70) are a sensitive marker for murine tumor burden ( )) were specifically induced in METs ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). However and unexpectedly, immunofluorescence (IF), immunohistochemical (IHC), qRT-PCR, and quantitative ELISA analyses revealed that metastatic melanoma cells both in mice and humans do not express NPY. Instead, NPY was exclusively expressed by perimetastatic hepatocytes and by isolated hepatocytes in vitro ( and and SI Appendix, Figs. S4 C–E, S5 A–C, and S6 A and B, and Table S1). Screening of human-derived single-cell RNA-seq data (GSE149614) confirmed that hepatocytes are the only and specific cellular source of NPY within the liver ecosystem (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–D). These findings were confirmed in various cancer types including colorectal (CRC), pancreatic (PC), and breast cancer (BC) applying mouse models, organoids, and patient-derived samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C–H and Table S2). In summary, NPY is expressed by hepatocytes in the metastatic liver niche.
 

## TGFβ Drives Perimetastatic NPY Induction.
Next, we aimed to determine the mechanism of metastasis-related NPY induction. In cirrhotic livers, NPY is regulated by TGFβ ( ). In contrast, in metastatic livers, NPY was specifically induced only in hepatocytes in close proximity to the tumor invasion front ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). We hypothesized that metastasis-related microenvironment activation and associated TGFβ induction ( , ) might trigger nearby NPY expression. RNA-seq confirmed activation of the metastatic microenvironment, including TGFβ-associated processes, EMT, and hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation (SI Appendix, Figs. S1H and S9A and Dataset S2). Likewise, both TGFβ and metastasis-related alpha smooth muscle actin (αSMA) ( ) [which is produced by activated HSC ( )] were induced in metastatic livers (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B–D) and their expression levels correlated significantly in mice and humans across various cancer types ( and SI Appendix, Figs. S9E, S10, and S11 and Table S2). Because of the strong upregulation of TGF-β in the vicinity of metastases, IHC staining alone was insufficient to pinpoint the cellular source of the cytokine. Notably, also hepatocytes appeared to display positive staining ( and SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S11), which may be misleading, as hepatocytes can only absorb and store TGF-β without substantially producing it ( , ). Consistent with this, single-nucleus RNA-seq of the metastatic microenvironment applying the mouse model identified HSCs, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, and myeloid cells as the principal sources of TGF-β1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A–D). Mechanistically, recombinant TGFβ induced NPY in hepatoblast-derived organoids and PHHs ( ). In vivo, the TGFβ receptor inhibitor Galunisertib blocked metastasis-related NPY induction ( ). These data were supported by consensus SMAD2/3 binding sites in human and murine NPY promoters and by mechanistic data defining NPY as a TGFβ-SMAD2/3-regulated hub protein ( ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A–C). Together, TGFβ drives perimetastatic NPY induction.

## NPY Promotes Liver Colonization and Chemotactic Cancer Cell Migration.
Next, we aimed at exploring NPY’s functional role in liver metastasis. We showed that NPY is upregulated in cirrhosis ( ), yet cirrhotic livers are considered to be protected from metastatic infiltration ( ). Therefore, it remained unclear whether metastasis-related NPY induction promotes or inhibits metastasis. We found that liver metastasis was significantly reduced in NPY−/− mice ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B).
 In contrast to specific NPY overexpression in metastatic livers, systemic NPY levels were unchanged in patients and mice with liver metastasis ( and SI Appendix, Table S3). Suggesting a liver-directed chemogradient as described for the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis ( ), we found that chemotaxis was enhanced in the metastasis model (SI Appendix, Fig. S14C and Dataset S2), and synthetic NPY induced chemotactic migration of B16 melanoma cells ( ). Moreover, the chemoattractive effects mediated by liver lysates from NPY+/+ mice were significantly reduced applying liver lysates from NPY−/− mice, which was rescued by synthetic NPY ( ). Dose-dependent chemotactic migration and invasion was confirmed in further melanoma cells and various CRC, PC, and BC cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 D–Q). In summary, hepatocyte-derived NPY promotes liver colonization and chemotactic cancer cell migration.

## Liver Metastatic Cancer Cells Overexpress NPY-5 Receptor.
Our next aim was to determine the responsible receptor mediating NPY-induced liver metastasis. In the mouse model cell line (B16), NPY-5 receptor (Y5R) was identified to be upregulated compared to syngeneic benign melanocytes ( ). Likewise, Y5R was overexpressed in METs ( ), and IHC and IF confirmed that Y5R was markedly expressed by B16 cells in vivo ( ), which was validated applying a further melanoma mouse model ( ). Human melanoma cells also showed increased Y5R expression, which remained preserved in xenografts ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S15 A–C). Marked Y5R expression was confirmed in clinical melanoma samples ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S15 D–H and
Table S2) and in various other cancer types (CRC, BC; PC) applying cell lines, mouse models, and patient tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 A–I).
 Addressing the prognostic impact of Y5R, liver metastasis was found to correlate with enhanced Y5R expression in melanoma ( and SI Appendix, Table S4). Particularly, high-risk melanoma subtypes for liver metastasis formation ( – ) revealed pronounced Y5R expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S17A). Likewise, the emergence of liver metastasis was associated with higher Y5R expression in CRC tissues compared with nonmetastatic and nonliver metastatic cohorts (SI Appendix, Fig. S17B and
Table S5). The robustness of these findings was confirmed after inclusion of additional melanoma, PC and BC cohorts (SI Appendix, Fig. S17C). Furthermore, marked Y5R expression correlated with advanced tumor stages (SI Appendix, Table S5) and poor overall survival in CRC ( ), which was reaffirmed applying TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) derived data ( ). Thus, Y5R appeared as a promising candidate receptor potentially mediating NPY-mediated liver metastasis.

## Y5R Drives NPY-Induced Liver Metastasis.
Small molecule–mediated Y5R inhibition (Y5Ri) blocked NPY-induced chemotactic migration of melanoma cells ( ), while a specific Y5R agonist ( ) enhanced migration (SI Appendix, Fig. S18A). Y5Ri also prevented migration induced by NPY-containing hepatocyte supernatants or liver lysates, respectively ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S18B), with similar effects observed in various melanoma, PC, BC, and CRC cell lines (SI Appendix, Figs. S18 C–K and S19 A).
 Next to migration/invasion, the establishment of metastasis requires sufficient colony formation capacities (including clonogenicity and proliferation) ( ). Y5Ri reduced clonogenicity in melanoma, CRC, PC, and BC cells ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S19 B–F). In mice, Y5Ri treatment markedly decreased liver metastasis ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S20 A–D). In vivo, Y5Ri slightly increased apoptosis markers ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S21A) and significantly reduced Ki67 expression, which was confirmed in melanoma cells and CRC organoids ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S21B), and by Y5Ri-mediated dose-dependent inhibition of proliferation ( ). Also in clinical samples, higher Y5R expression correlated with increased Ki67 in liver metastases ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S21 C and D).
Together, specific crosstalk between microenvironment-derived NPY and Y5R expressed by cancer cells drives liver metastasis.

## Y5R Drives Metastasis Via Modulation of cAMP, ERK, and CHK2 Signaling.
Next, we explored downstream signaling of NPY-Y5R crosstalk. NPY antagonizes cAMP signaling via Gi proteins ( ). In the murine liver metastasis model, cAMP-associated processes were enriched, especially among NPY-related terms ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) analysis confirmed NPY and Y5R agonist–induced cAMP inhibition in melanoma cells ( ), while functionally, NPY-driven migration was blocked by concomitant chemical cAMP induction ( ), consistent with cAMP-mediated inhibition of migration across various cell types ( , ).
 Beyond cAMP inhibition, ERK activation is a key driver of cancer cell motility ( ). ERK/MAPK-related pathways were enriched in the mouse model of metastasis ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), and phospho-ERK (pERK) was particularly prominent in invasive tumor cells near NPY-expressing hepatocytes ( ), but reduced in NPY−/− mice ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S22A). Synthetic NPY and the Y5R agonist activated ERK in various melanoma cells and organoids ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S22 B–D). In several types of human liver metastases and primary tumor tissues, pERK levels correlated with Y5R ( and SI Appendix, Figs. S22E and S23), and ERK inhibition blocked Y5R-driven migration ( ). Taken together, NPY-Y5R promotes migration via cAMP inhibition and ERK activation.
Beyond migration, NPY-Y5R enhanced proliferation and clonogenicity, with minimal impact on apoptosis. Although pERK correlated with Ki67 in human metastases (SI Appendix, Figs. S23 and S24A), ERK or cAMP modulation had limited effects on proliferation and clonogenicity (SI Appendix, Fig. S24 B–I), suggesting additional, yet unknown downstream effector(s). Unbiased phosphokinase profiling revealed dephosphorylation of checkpoint kinase 2 (CHK2, CHEK2) which was associated with a dephosphorylation of p53 upon Y5R activation in melanoma cells ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S25A). As part of the DNA damage response pathway comprising ATM-CHK2-E2F1-TP53 signaling ( ), CHK2 is a suppressor of metastasis, promoting G2/M arrest and apoptosis in various cancer types including melanoma and CRC ( – ). Gene sets related to DNA damage response, G2/M cell cycle checkpoint, and ATM-E2F signaling were enriched in the mouse model of metastasis (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 H and S3 A and B). Y5R agonist treatment confirmed dose-dependent reduction of pCHK2 in human melanoma and CRC cells and organoids ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S25B). Conversely, Y5Ri upregulated pCHK2 in cancer cells and organoids ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S25C). In human metastases, pCHK2 was poorly expressed and inversely correlated with Y5R ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S25D and
Table S2). Moreover, pCHK2 was induced in hepatic metastases of NPY−/− and Y5Ri-treated mice ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S26 A and B). Functionally, chemical CHK2-E2F1 activation suppressed proliferation and clonogenicity ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S26 C and D), which was reversed by coadministered high-dose Y5R agonist (SI Appendix, Fig. S26E). Thus, NPY-Y5R crosstalk was identified to regulate CHK2, alongside cAMP and ERK pathways ( ). These data further support NPY-Y5R as a promising therapeutic target in liver metastasis.

## Y5R Is the Principal Receptor Mediating NPY-Driven Metastatic Traits.
Besides Y5R, NPY can activate NPY-1 receptor (Y1R) and NPY-2 receptor (Y2R) ( ). However, unlike Y5R, neither Y1R nor Y2R showed significant upregulation in metastatic versus non-METs in the mouse model (SI Appendix, Fig. S27A), nor were they markedly overexpressed in human melanoma cell lines compared to melanocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S27B). Functionally, NPY-induced chemotactic migration and clonogenicity remained unaffected by selective inhibition of Y1R (Y1Ri) or Y2R (Y2Ri) in melanoma cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S27 C and D). Beyond melanoma, Y1R and Y2R were expressed across various human and murine colorectal (CRC), pancreatic (PC), and breast cancer (BC) cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S27E), yet pharmacological inhibition of either receptor did not impact NPY-induced migration or clonogenicity in CRC cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S27 F and G). Likewise, NPY-driven clonogenicity in PC and BC cell lines was unaffected by Y1Ri or Y2Ri (SI Appendix, Fig. S28 A–C), and Y2Ri failed to impair chemotactic migration in these models (SI Appendix, Fig. S28 D–F). Collectively, these findings identify Y5R as the dominant receptor mediating NPY-dependent metastatic features, while Y1R and Y2R appear largely dispensable across various cancer types.
However, only in some PC and BC cells, Y1R, alongside Y5R, induced chemotactic migration (SI Appendix, Fig. S28 D–F). This was confirmed by a recent study describing that inhibition of Y1R in PC can reduce their migration and metastatic potential ( ). Y1R was also detected in some Y5R-negative CRC-derived liver metastases (SI Appendix, Fig. S28 G and H) and correlated with poor prognosis in CRC (besides Y5R) (SI Appendix, Fig. S28I). Moreover, Y5Ri induced upregulation of Y1R and NPY in metastatic livers as well as systemic NPY levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S28 J and K), suggesting potential Y1R- and/or NPY-mediated resistance mechanisms upon Y5R inhibition in clinical settings. Notably and in contrast, NPY receptor expression remained unchanged in the absence of the ligand, i.e., in NPY−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S29A). Given these findings and the specific induction of NPY by perimetastatic hepatocytes, targeting the liver niche rather than Y5R or other NPY receptors (on cancer cells) appeared to be an attractive therapeutic concept.

## RNA Interference Targeting NPY in the Liver Niche Represents an Effective Strategy to Control Metastasis.
Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are a promising drug delivery vehicle for siRNAs ( ). In a preclinical therapeutic approach, application of LNPs carrying siRNA pools targeting NPY (NPYi) (SI Appendix, Tables S6 and S7) enabled hepatocyte-specific ( – ) in vivo knockdown, significantly reducing NPY in liver tissues without affecting other types of liver cells (e.g., HSCs), extrahepatic tissues, or systemic NPY levels ( and SI Appendix, Figs. S29 B–F and S30 A–H). Moreover, LNP-RNAi-mediated NPY inhibition showed no signs of toxicity, and no changes in behavior, body weight, extrahepatic and systemic NPY levels, serum levels of liver transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase (SI Appendix, Fig. S30 I and J). Resembling NPY-induced chemotactic migration, NPYi markedly decreased NPY liver–serum ratios ( ). NPYi also reduced macroscopic liver metastasis ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S30K), and IHC confirmed reduced numbers and sizes of metastases as well as downregulation of Ki67 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S31A). Addressing the downstream signaling pathways identified in this study, NPYi confirmed reduction of pERK and enhanced pCHK2 levels in vivo ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S31 B and C). Notably, reaffirming our findings in NPY−/− mice and in contrast to Y5Ri, NPYi did not induce upregulation of NPY receptors (SI Appendix, Fig. S31D).
 So far in our study, the NPY system was targeted prior to liver metastasis formation, but its effectiveness in established metastases (palliative approach) was unclear. Therefore, NPYi treatment was started on day 4 (when early colonies are established) and after performing splenectomy following intrasplenic tumor cell injection (SI Appendix, Fig. S32 A and B). Also in this setting, NPYi markedly reduced metastasis (SI Appendix, Figs. S32 C–E and S33 A–D). A detailed analysis of metastases by size showed that NPYi reduced both number and area across all categories, confirming that next to migration, NPY affects the proliferation of established metastasis (SI Appendix, Fig. S33 E–G). Together, RNA interference targeting NPY in the liver niche represents an effective strategy to control metastasis.

## Discussion
This study introduces a biological concept, revealing a specific molecular crosstalk mechanism within the liver metastatic ecosystem. Moreover, our study identifies the CHK2–E2F1 axis as a downstream effector of NPY-Y5R signaling, affecting clonogenicity and proliferation of liver metastatic cancer cells.
Considering the cell-type specificity of LNP-formulated RNAi drugs ( – ), one question is whether targeting hepatocyte-derived NPY might affect other cell types, especially immune cells, within the metastatic ecosystem. LNP-mediated NPYi did not affect NPY expression in other types of liver cells, systemic NPY, other organs, or in primary tumors (SI Appendix, Figs. S29 B–F, S30 A–H, and S33 H–J). Moreover, genetic NPY knockout, NPYi, and Y5Ri did not significantly affect the number of CD3-, CD4-, or CD8-positive immune cells or the expression of various further cytokines/immune cell markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S34 A–D).
Still, NPY represents an immunomodulatory factor ( ). Several lines of evidence suggest an immunosuppressive role of the NPY system. NPY decreases NK cell activity ( ) and exerts anti-inflammatory effects by promoting a TH2-lymphocyte and a M2-macrophage polarization ( , ). Given these “immune checkpoint–like” functions, inhibition of NPY combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors may provide synergistic therapeutic benefit. Future studies should delineate the precise role and net impact of NPY on immune cell function within the liver metastatic microenvironment.
While previous studies have shown oncogenic functions of NPY and its receptors in some types of cancers, a recent review concluded that NPY’s overall role in cancer dissemination remained unclear ( ). In particular, so far NPY signaling was found to affect bone metastasis in Ewing sarcoma xenografts ( , ). However, this mechanism was basically different compared with our findings, since the ligand NPY was not produced in the bone niche, but in an autocrine manner by Ewing sarcoma cells. Very similarly, a most recent study described autocrine NPY effects in pancreatic cancer (PC) ( ). This study showed autocrine NPY expression in PC and that inhibition of Y1R (not Y5R) decreased the metastatic potential of PC cells.
In conclusion, a specific niche-cancer-cell crosstalk in metastatic organs mediated by the NPY system was unknown before. Moreover, the potential impact of the NPY system on metastatic sites beyond bones remained elusive ( ). Next to the liver, the lung represents the major site of metastasis in most solid cancer types. Since TGFβ appears to be a principal regulator of NPY in hepatocytes, this might be also relevant regarding lung metastasis, because also in the lung, TGFβ was shown to play potential roles in metastasis ( , ). However, to the best of our knowledge, no detailed data exist regarding NPY expression in lung tissue. We performed IHC analysis of human metastatic lung tissues derived from patients with melanoma, CRC, and PC patients, revealing moderate and in some cases marked expression of Y5R and/or Y1R (SI Appendix, Fig. S35 A and B and
Table S8). However, and in contrast to liver metastasis, the emergence of lung, bone, peritoneal, and brain metastasis was not associated with enhanced Y5R expression in primary tumor tissues of CRC patients (SI Appendix, Fig. S17 B and C and
Table S5). Moreover, NPY was not specifically induced in human perimetastatic lung tissues, but appeared to be expressed by alveolar macrophages which was independent from lung metastasis (SI Appendix, Fig. S35 C–E and
Table S8). Interestingly, NPY production by adipose tissue macrophages was shown previously ( ), but NPY expression by alveolar macrophages has not been described before and its potential impact should be addressed in future studies. Together, our study provides robust evidence for the existence of organ-specific factors in different metastatic niches, outlining that liver-derived NPY appears as a site-specific contributor of liver infiltration and colony formation.
From a therapeutic perspective, specific targeting of NPY in cancer cells would not be applicable with, e.g., RNAi, and our data suggest that targeting NPY receptors in cancer cells might induce resistance mechanisms. In contrast and unexpectedly, our study reveals that specifically in the liver niche, metastatic cancer cells of diverse cancer types do not express NPY, while liver-infiltrating cells take advantage from hepatocyte-derived NPY. This niche-cancer-cell crosstalk was previously unknown, as well as the concept to target NPY rather than its receptors and to target hepatocytes rather than directly tumor cells, respectively, to inhibit hepatic metastasis.
RNAi therapeutics represent breakthrough discoveries ( ), with six siRNA drugs approved for hereditary and metabolic diseases ( , ). In contrast, the concept of RNAi for cancer treatment remained unexplored in clinical studies, mainly because siRNAs typically target hepatocytes. We propose an unprecedented approach of targeting hepatocytes in the liver niche, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment.

## Materials and Methods
The presented in vivo studies mainly focused on the murine spleen–liver metastasis model to investigate mechanisms of hepatic metastasis. Different therapeutic approaches were used to selectively inhibit NPY–Y5R interaction (small molecule inhibitor, knockout models, siRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles). Cellular properties such as proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation were assessed in vitro through established functional assays, while mRNA and protein expression were analyzed by qRT-PCR, western blotting, IHC, and ELISA. In addition, bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, as well as reanalysis of public datasets, were performed to validate findings. Detailed information about materials, protocols, and analyses is provided in SI Appendix.
