## Summary
The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and the autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) are the primary mechanisms for protein degradation, yet the molecular mechanisms linking them remain unclear. We show that loss of the UPS shuttle-protease DDI2 in diverse human and murine cells leads to a proteotoxic stress response driven by the intracellular accumulation of the secretory protein CCN1. Misfolded CCN1 is normally extracted from the endoplasmic reticulum by a DDI2–p97 complex and directed to lysosomes for degradation. In the absence of DDI2, CCN1 builds up, produces reactive oxygen species, and triggers compensatory autophagy; CCN1 knockout or ROS scavenging attenuates this response. Loss of DDI2 also impairs CCN1–LAMP1 colocalization, suggesting that DDI2 functions as a selective cargo receptor linking the UPS and ALP. These findings reveal a stress-responsive DDI2–CCN1 axis that reshapes proteostasis and highlight DDI2 as a potential therapeutic vulnerability in proteasome-dependent cancers.

## Introduction
The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy are two major intracellular pathways that regulate protein degradation and maintain cellular quality control. Their functional interplay is essential for cellular homeostasis, and dysregulation of either system contributes to the pathogenesis of various diseases, including cancer. , 
The UPS primarily targets short-lived and misfolded proteins for degradation by tagging them with polyubiquitin chains and directing them to the proteasome. When proteasomal degradation is impaired, ubiquitin-conjugated proteins accumulate, leading to proteotoxic stress and activation of apoptotic pathways. This principle underlies the therapeutic efficacy of proteasome inhibitors in cancer treatment. Many ubiquitinated substrates are shuttled to proteasomes via ubiquitin-binding factors, including DNA-damage inducible 1 homolog 2 (DDI2). In addition to its role as a ubiquitin shuttling factor, DDI2 possesses a retroviral aspartyl protease-like domain that enables it to function as a protease. , , When proteasome activity is limited, DDI2 recognizes proteins with elongated ubiquitin chains and facilitates their degradation. Consequently, DDI2 depletion leads to the accumulation of slowly migrating ubiquitinated proteins. 
In vivo, DDI2 knockout (KO) in mice results in embryonic lethality due to insufficient proteasome expression, proteotoxic stress, and activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR), underscoring its essential role in proteostasis. 
Macroautophagy, the most extensively studied form of autophagy, protects cells from cytotoxic stress by degrading long-lived proteins and insoluble protein aggregates. This process involves four key steps: initiation and nucleation of the phagophore, expansion and closure of the autophagosome, fusion with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, and cargo degradation with the recycling of the breakdown products. Microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B light chain 3B (LC3B) is a key marker of autophagosomes, contributing to their formation and the sequestration of cellular components for lysosomal degradation. , Various regulatory proteins and pathways, including those influenced by secreted factors, modulate the autophagic response. Notably, cellular communication network factor 1 (CCN1), an extracellular matrix-associated protein, has emerged as an important modulator of autophagy through its interactions with integrin receptors. , CCN1 is a key protein within the CCN family, a group of six secreted factors involved in diverse cellular processes, including tissue repair and inflammation. The CCN1 gene is expressed in connective tissues, blood vessels, skin, and bone, reflecting its broad role in angiogenesis and wound healing. It influences numerous cellular functions by binding to multiple receptors across different cell types and activating related signaling pathways. , In addition to its physiological roles, CCN1 is also implicated in pathological processes, including cancer, where it can exert context-dependent effects such as promoting or inhibiting tumor growth, regulating apoptosis, autophagy, and the inflammatory microenvironment. , 
While DDI2’s role in the UPS is well recognized, its contributions to broader aspects of proteostasis remain unclear. We previously showed that DDI2 depletion sensitizes cancer cells to proteasome inhibition. Since DDI2 facilitates the degradation of polyubiquitinated proteins, its loss may mimic proteasome impairment. Given that the proteasome dysfunction often triggers compensatory autophagy, , , , there is a strong rationale to investigate whether DDI2 depletion affects autophagic processes. In this study, we demonstrate that DDI2 loss upregulates autophagy via the intracellular accumulation of CCN1. Moreover, our findings unexpectedly suggest that DDI2 may function as an autophagy receptor by mediating the delivery of CCN1 to the autophagy/lysosomal degradation pathway.

## Results

## DNA-damage inducible 1 homolog 2 depletion induces autophagy
Previous studies have shown that DDI2 depletion replicates proteasome impairment, sensitizing cancer cells to proteasome inhibitors by causing the accumulation of highly ubiquitinated proteins. Given the delicate balance between the autophagy machinery and the UPS—where the dysfunction of the UPS activates compensatory autophagy —we sought to investigate whether DDI2 plays a role in regulating autophagic activity.
To address this, we employed CRISPR technology to generate NIH-3T3 murine fibroblast cells with a knockout of the DDI2 gene. After validating the knockout cell line ( A), we assessed the impact of DDI2 depletion on autophagy using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to visualize morphological changes in NIH-3T3 DDI2KO cells compared to controls. TEM, widely used for observing intracellular microstructures such as autophagosomes and lysosomes, revealed the accumulation of multiple autophagosomal structures containing cytoplasmic components in DDI2-deficient cells – structures that were absent in control cells ( B). Additionally, an increased number of both early and late autolysosomes was observed in DDI2KO cells, indicating enhanced autophagic activity.
 To further validate these morphological observations, we performed autophagic flux analysis. During autophagy, pro-LC3 is cleaved by ATG4 to generate cytosolic LC3-I, which is subsequently lipidated to form LC3-II. Lipidated LC3-II associates with autophagosomal membranes and facilitates cargo recruitment through interactions with autophagy receptors. Elevated LC3-II levels can result either from autophagy inhibition (due to impaired LC3-II degradation) or from autophagy activation (via increased LC3-II production). To distinguish between these possibilities, we treated cells with an autophagy inhibitor that blocks LC3 degradation, allowing us to discern enhanced autophagosome formation from impaired degradation. For this purpose, we generated a panel of cell lines from various origins—including NIH-3T3, MRC5 human fetal lung fibroblasts, HAP1 human chronic myeloid leukemia cell line, MIA PaCa-2 pancreatic cancer cells, and EW16 and ES1 Ewing sarcoma cells—either deficient in DDI2 or serving as controls. Following treatment with chloroquine (CQ), a lysosomal inhibitor that blocks lysosome-autophagosome fusion, LC3B-II protein levels were measured by Western blotting ( C). The analysis revealed elevated LC3B-II levels in both CQ-treated and untreated DDI2-deficient cells compared to their respective controls, suggesting that DDI2 deficiency induces autophagy activation through increased autophagosome formation and accumulation of LC3B-II protein.
To explore the cause of the increased autophagic activity observed in DDI2-deficient cells, we performed a multiplexed total proteome-wide analysis using tandem mass tags (TMTs) to compare DDI2KO cells with controls. As expected, DDI2 was depleted in the KO cells, and a list of differentially upregulated or downregulated proteins was generated. Notably, we identified a significant accumulation of CCN1 with an existing link to autophagy in DDI2-deficient cells ( A). To orthogonally verify this observation, we measured CCN1 protein levels with and without autophagy inhibition by CQ across multiple cell types. Consistently, CCN1 protein levels were elevated in DDI2-deficient cells across all examined cell lines corroborating the findings of the proteome analysis ( B). Interestingly, CCN1 levels also increased in response to CQ treatment, suggesting that this protein could be a substrate of the lysosome ( B). Further validation came from immunofluorescence staining of MRC5 control and DDI2KO cells, where confocal microscopy revealed a significant increase in CCN1 fluorescence intensity in DDI2KO cells compared to controls ( C).
 To determine whether DDI2 regulates CCN1 at the transcriptional level, we performed real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of CCN1 mRNA in the presence or absence of CQ treatment in NIH-3T3, MRC5, and MIA PaCa-2 DDI2KO cells and their controls. Interestingly, DDI2 deficiency did not affect CCN1 transcription ( D), indicating that regulation occurs at the protein level. Collectively, these findings support a model in which DDI2 deficiency leads to the accumulation of CCN1 protein—likely through post-transcriptional mechanisms—without altering CCN1 transcription.

## Cellular communication network factor 1 is essential and sufficient to induce autophagic flux
Previous studies have demonstrated that the secretory protein CCN1 plays a role in autophagy induction via CCN1/α6β1 signaling. To further explore the role of DDI2 in autophagy regulation through CCN1, we knocked down CCN1 using CCN1-specific siRNA in NIH-3T3 DDI2KO cells and generated a double-knockout MIA PaCa-2 cell line lacking both CCN1 and DDI2 (CCN1KO/DDI2KO). Autophagic flux was then assessed following CQ-mediated autophagy inhibition. The efficiency of CCN1 knockdown was confirmed by immunoblotting ( A). Strikingly, the autophagy induced by DDI2 depletion in DDI2KO cells was markedly diminished upon CCN1 knockdown, as evidenced by a reduction in LC3B-II protein levels ( A). These results suggest that CCN1 is necessary for the induction of autophagy in the absence of DDI2.
 Next, we evaluated whether increasing CCN1 levels alone could enhance autophagic flux. To this end, we overexpressed CCN1 in NIH-3T3, MRC5, and MIA PaCa-2 wild-type (WT) cells and assessed autophagic flux by immunoblotting following CQ treatment. Compared to WT cells, CCN1 overexpression led to a significant elevation in LC3B-II protein levels ( B), demonstrating that CCN1 alone is sufficient to induce autophagy.
Previous studies have shown that CCN1 overexpression increases apoptotic activity in hepatic stellate cells, while in esophageal cancer, CCN1 has been reported to neutralize tumor necrosis factor (TNF), thereby suppressing TNF-induced apoptosis in malignant cells. , Notably, CCN1 has also been shown to promote pancreatic cancer cell growth both in vitro and in vivo, and to enhance resistance to tumor necrosis factor-α-induced apoptosis. Therefore, we sought to further characterize the biological role of CCN1 by comparing the effects of endogenous versus exogenously overexpressed CCN1 on autophagy and apoptosis in MIA PaCa-2 DDI2-deficient cells. After overexpressing CCN1 in DDI2KO cells, we first measured autophagy flux, which revealed a further increase in autophagy induction in CCN1-overexpressing DDI2KO cells compared with DDI2KO cells alone ( C). Since autophagy can have cytoprotective effects that promote cancer cell survival, we next evaluated apoptotic activity to determine the functional consequence of the increased autophagy in this model. Hence, cleaved caspase-3 levels were measured following treatment with the proteasome inhibitor carfilzomib (CFZ). Interestingly, MIA PaCa-2 DDI2KO cells with only endogenous CCN1 showed increased apoptotic activity, whereas CCN1 overexpression reversed this effect, resulting in reduced caspase-3 cleavage to levels comparable to control cells and lower than DDI2KO cells ( D). The assessment of cell viability ( E) demonstrated consistent findings, DDI2KO cells showed reduced viability, while CCN1 overexpression in DDI2KO cells rescued this phenotype, restoring viability to near control cells. Taken together, the reduced apoptosis and increased viability observed under CCN1 overexpression in DDI2KO cells suggest that elevated CCN1 shifts the balance toward pro-survival signaling and that the enhanced autophagy observed in the absence of DDI2 likely represents a cytoprotective response.

## Intracellular cellular communication network factor 1 accumulates in response to DNA-damage inducible 1 homolog 2 depletion
It is well established that extracellular CCN1 induces autophagy through binding to integrin receptors on the cell surface. , However, whether the intracellular accumulation of CCN1 can similarly activate autophagy has not been addressed. Since our previous experiments demonstrated that CCN1 induces autophagy in the absence of DDI2, we next investigated the role of intracellular CCN1 in this process.
Brefeldin A (BFA) is commonly used to block the secretion of proteins, such as CCN1, by inhibiting the formation of transport vesicles at the Golgi apparatus. This treatment effectively prevents the transport of CCN1 from the ER to the cell surface, leading to its intracellular accumulation. To confirm the efficacy of BFA in blocking CCN1 secretion, we pre-treated MRC5 wild-type cells with BFA and performed confocal immunofluorescence microscopy. As shown in A, BFA treatment resulted in a significant increase in intracellular CCN1 staining, indicating effective inhibition of CCN1 secretion.
 Next, we evaluated autophagic flux by immunoblotting after treatment with BFA, CQ, or a combination of both in MRC5 DDI2KO and MIA PaCa-2 DDI2KO cells, alongside their respective controls. The results confirmed that CCN1 accumulated in both control and DDI2KO cells, with notably higher accumulation observed in DDI2KO cells following BFA treatment ( B). Importantly, an increase in autophagic flux was detected in DDI2KO cells compared to controls after BFA treatment, consistent with our earlier findings ( C and C). To confirm that this effect is specific to CCN1, we generated MIA PaCa-2 CCN1KO cells and measured autophagic flux under the same conditions. Notably, CCN1 knockout led to a decrease in autophagic flux following BFA treatment ( C). Signal peptides (SPs) are short amino acid sequences that direct protein secretion and translocation across membranes. To genetically retain CCN1 within the cell, we disrupted its secretion by deleting the signal peptide sequence responsible for its export in the MRC5 cell line, thereby generating CCN1 ΔSP (delta signal peptide) cells ( D). WT CCN1-overexpressing cells were generated as a positive control. We then measured autophagic flux in these cells and, interestingly, found that autophagy activity remained higher in CCN1 ΔSP cells compared to controls. In WT CCN1 cells, LC3B-II levels were more abundant than in CCN1 ΔSP cells, likely due to greater expression of CCN1 ( E). These results demonstrate that the intracellular accumulation of CCN1 can regulate autophagy.
Previous reports have identified CCN1 as an oxidative stress-related molecule, with high levels of CCN1 promoting the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). , ROS, in turn, can activate stress-responsive pathways such as AMPK, which inhibit mTOR and promote autophagy. , Based on these observations, we hypothesized that scavenging ROS production would diminish the autophagy observed in CCN1 overexpressing (OE) cells ( B). To test this hypothesis, MRC5 control and CCN1OE cells were treated with BFA to block CCN1 secretion, and N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) was used to scavenge ROS. Autophagic activity was then assessed by the Western blot analysis of LC3B-II protein levels. Strikingly, NAC treatment significantly reduced CCN1-induced autophagy in CCN1OE cells ( F), underscoring the essential role of ROS in mediating CCN1-induced autophagy.

## Misfolded cellular communication network factor 1 is directed from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cytosol by DNA-damage inducible 1 homolog 2
The ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway eliminates misfolded proteins by extracting them from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) lumen/membrane and retrotranslocating them into the cytosol for degradation. The p97/valosin-containing protein is a key ATPase in this process, facilitating the retrotranslocation of these substrates to prevent their accumulation in the ER lumen. , Given that elevated intracellular levels of CCN1 can induce ER overload and stress, we investigated whether DDI2 played a role in regulating misfolded CCN1 via the ERAD pathway.
To begin, we assessed whether p97 contributes to the processing of misfolded CCN1. We compared CCN1 protein levels in control cells and cells treated with the p97 inhibitor CB-5083. Immunoblotting analyses of MRC5 DDI2KO and MIA PaCa-2 DDI2KO cells, along with their respective controls, revealed that p97 inhibition robustly increased CCN1 accumulation ( A), indicating that p97 is essential for the degradation of misfolded CCN1.
 Based on these results, we next tested the hypothesis that DDI2 associates with p97 to facilitate the retrotranslocation of CCN1 to the cytosol. DDI2, an aspartyl protease structurally similar to the HIV-1 retroviral protease (RVP), typically interacts transiently with its client proteins due to the dynamic nature of its active site. To capture these interactions more effectively, we introduced a D252A mutation in the catalytic domain of DDI2, creating a protease-dead mutant (dRVP-DDI2) that retains bound client proteins. We expressed FLAG-tagged dRVP-DDI2 in HEK293 cells, immunoprecipitated the mutant protein, and probed for interactions with p97 and CCN1 via immunoblotting. RAD23A, a known DDI2 interactor, was considered as a positive control. Remarkably, both p97 and CCN1 co-precipitated with DDI2 ( B), suggesting a potential association.
To further verify this interaction, we performed colocalization analyses to determine whether DDI2 and CCN1 reside in close proximity within cells. Immunofluorescence microscopy of MRC5 and MIA PaCa-2 wild-type cells revealed significant colocalization of DDI2 and CCN1, as indicated by the merged yellow staining ( C). Together, these findings provide compelling evidence that DDI2 interacts with both p97 and CCN1, implicating DDI2 in the retrotranslocation of misfolded CCN1 from the ER to the cytosol.

## DNA-damage inducible 1 homolog 2 facilitates the lysosomal degradation of misfolded cellular communication network factor 1
Lysosomal protein degradation represents the final step in the autophagy pathway. During this process, autophagosomes fuse with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, where misfolded proteins are degraded. Misfolded proteins destined for autophagic degradation frequently colocalize with lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1), a well-established lysosomal marker located on the lysosomal membrane that enables the visualization and identification of lysosomes within cells. , Here, we aimed to determine whether DDI2 facilitates the degradation of CCN1 via autophagy.
To confirm whether CCN1 is trafficked to lysosomes, we first performed co-immunoprecipitation experiments in HEK293 cells with or without overexpressed CCN1 ( A and 6B). The results revealed that CCN1 interacts with LAMP1 even in the absence of CCN1 overexpression. In addition, immunofluorescence staining combined with confocal microscopy was employed to assess the colocalization of CCN1 and LAMP1 in MRC5 and MIA PaCa-2 wild-type cells. Consistent with the co-immunoprecipitation findings, CCN1 exhibited strong colocalization with LAMP1, as evidenced by the yellow puncta in the merged fluorescence images ( C). These results confirm that CCN1 is indeed trafficked to lysosomes.
 Next, we sought to address the role of DDI2 in the lysosomal degradation of CCN1. We hypothesized that DDI2 directs misfolded CCN1 to the lysosome for autophagic degradation. To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed V5-tagged CCN1 in MRC5 DDI2KO cells, which allowed for the specific detection of CCN1 ( D). Confocal immunofluorescence microscopy was then used to examine the colocalization of CCN1 and LAMP1 in the absence of DDI2. Notably, the abundance of yellow puncta in DDI2-depleted cells was considerably reduced compared to controls ( E), suggesting that CCN1 is less efficiently recruited to lysosomes when DDI2 is absent. Together, these findings indicate that DDI2 plays a crucial role in directing intracellular misfolded CCN1 toward lysosomal degradation, thereby facilitating its clearance via the autophagy pathway.

## Discussion
The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy function as interconnected quality control mechanisms essential for maintaining cellular proteostasis. The dysregulation of either pathway can lead to the accumulation of misfolded or damaged proteins, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of various diseases, including neurodegeneration, cancer, and metabolic disorders. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular crosstalk between these systems is therefore critical for identifying therapeutic targets.
Significant progress has been made in elucidating the dual role of DDI2 as both a protease and a shuttle factor within the UPS. , , , Previous studies, including our own, have shown that DDI2-depleted cells exhibit hypersensitivity to pharmacological proteasome inhibitors , , and accumulate highly ubiquitylated proteins, effectively mimicking the effects of proteasome impairment. This proteotoxic stress underscores the critical role of DDI2 in maintaining proteostasis. In our current study, we reveal a function of DDI2 in protein quality control that extends beyond its canonical role in the UPS, highlighting its involvement in the regulation of autophagy.
Our data demonstrate that DDI2 deficiency activates the compensatory autophagy pathway, presumably to facilitate the degradation of accumulated proteins. Proteomic analyses identified CCN1—a secretory protein—as a prominent target whose intracellular levels markedly increase in the absence of DDI2. Mechanistically, we found that DDI2 regulates CCN1 post-transcriptionally, implicating a role in protein stability rather than in gene expression. While extracellular CCN1 has previously been implicated in autophagy induction through CCN1/α6β1 signaling, , , , our study is the first to demonstrate that intracellular, misfolded CCN1 can independently trigger autophagy. Although additional pathways may contribute to autophagy activation under proteotoxic stress, our findings suggest that CCN1 accumulation alone is sufficient to initiate this response, underscoring its pivotal role in cellular quality control. We also found that although endogenous CCN1 contributes to apoptotic susceptibility in DDI2-deficient cells, exogenous CCN1 overexpression instead promotes a shift toward pro-survival signaling by enhancing cytoprotective autophagy and reducing apoptosis.
To further elucidate the mechanism underlying CCN1-induced autophagy, we examined the role of ROS, given the well-established link between CCN1 and oxidative stress. Elevated intracellular CCN1 levels induce endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and activate the unfolded protein response (UPR), resulting in ROS production. , This ROS accumulation contributes not only to cellular senescence and apoptosis but also to the induction of autophagy. Consistent with this, we observed that ROS scavenging significantly attenuated CCN1-induced autophagy, underscoring the central role of ROS in mediating autophagic responses to misfolded CCN1.
Further insight into the disposal of misfolded CCN1 was obtained by examining the role of p97, a critical ATPase that facilitates the retrotranslocation of misfolded secretory proteins from the ER to the cytosol. We hypothesized that misfolded intracellular CCN1 might be a substrate for p97, with its translocation from the ER to the cytosol being mediated through interaction with DDI2. In support of this, we found that CCN1 accumulates upon p97 inhibition and confirmed that DDI2 interacts with both p97 and CCN1. These findings suggest that DDI2 functions as a critical regulator in the disposal of ER-resident misfolded CCN1 molecules, potentially acting at the intersection of proteasomal and autophagic pathways.
ER stress not only induces autophagy but also promotes autophagosome formation, during which p97 targets misfolded proteins within the ER for lysosomal degradation. Consistent with this model, we observed that CCN1 interacts and colocalizes with LAMP1, a lysosomal marker, indicating that its degradation occurs via the lysosome. Autophagy is distinguished by its selectivity in protein degradation, a process mediated by cargo receptor proteins that bind and guide specific substrates to autolysosomes. Importantly, our findings revealed that DDI2 deficiency reduces the colocalization of CCN1 and LAMP1, thereby underscoring the essential role of DDI2 in this selective degradation process.
Our study elucidates a previously unrecognized molecular mechanism in which DDI2 mediates the quality control of CCN1 through autophagy-dependent degradation ( ). By directing misfolded CCN1 to the autophagosome-lysosome pathway, DDI2 acts as a potential autophagy receptor that links the UPS and autophagy systems. This insight into DDI2’s role in proteostasis advances our understanding of protein quality control and suggests that DDI2 may represent a promising target for the development of mechanism-based therapeutic strategies for proteostasis-related diseases, including cancer. Further investigations are required to validate DDI2’s role as an autophagy receptor and to explore its broader implications in cellular homeostasis and disease.
 

## Limitations of the study
The study has several limitations. Much of the evidence is derived from cellular models using knockout and reconstitution approaches, which, although informative, do not fully recapitulate physiological conditions. Confirming these mechanisms in an in vivo system would substantially strengthen the conclusions. Furthermore, since DDI2 has additional functions, including a role in DNA repair, the possibility that secondary genetic alterations or underlying genomic instability influenced the observed phenotypes cannot be excluded. Applying complementary strategies—such as whole-genome sequencing to identify potential confounding mutations—would help clarify DDI2’s specific contribution and increase the reliability of the results.

## Method details

## Plasmid constructs
The pQCXIP-6xHis-Flag-DDI2 (Flag-DDI2) construct, which expresses human DDI2, was generously provided by Lan Huang (University of California Irvine, Irvine, CA, USA) and has been previously described. To investigate murine DDI2, we cloned wild-type DDI2 (Flag-WT-DDI2) and a protease-dead mutant with a D252A substitution in the RVP domain (Flag-dRVP-DDI2) into the pQCXIP-6xHis-Flag vector. For CRISPR-based gene editing, 20-mer guide RNAs targeting the coding sequences of mouse DDI2 (TGTTGTGATTCTACGACAGA) and human DDI2 (CATAAGAAGCCAATGATCTG) were cloned into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector (a gift from Feng Zhang, Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA; Addgene plasmid). Additionally, the lentiCRISPR-EGFP-sgRNA1 plasmid was obtained from the same source (Addgene plasmid). To study CCN1, we employed the CCSB-Broad LentiORF-Mutant CYR61 plasmid from Horizon Discovery/Dharmacon for overexpression of CCN1. For CRISPR-mediated knockout of CCN1, we used the Edit-R Human CYR61 Lentiviral sgRNA. As a non-targeting control for CCN1 knockout experiments, we applied the Edit-R Lentiviral sgRNA Non-targeting Control, also from Horizon Discovery/Dharmacon. Both Human CCN1-WT and CCN1 ΔSP were cloned into pGenLenti plasmid using EcoRI and BamHI restriction sites with FLAG tag at the C-terminus. Human CCN1 ΔSP lacks the 24-amino-acid N-terminal signal peptide present in Human CCN1-WT.

## Viral transduction and generation of stable cell lines
To generate viral particles, 5.5 million HEK293T cells were plated in 10-cm culture plates in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, one day prior to transfection. For retroviral production, 4 μg of retroviral transfer plasmid, 3.5 μg of pUMVC, and 0.4 μg of pCMV-VSV-G were transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 3000, following the manufacturer's instructions. For lentiviral production, 4 μg of lentiviral transfer plasmid, 2 μg of pCAG-HIVgp, 1 μg of pHDM-G, and 1 μg of pCAG4-RTR2 were used. The media supernatant containing viral particles was collected at 48- and 72-hours post-transfection, and viral particles were then precipitated with PEG-it solution (Systems Bio). The concentrated viral particles were aliquoted and stored at −80°C.
The day prior to infection, target cells were plated in six-well plates at approximately 60% confluency. For stable overexpression or knockout cell generation, one aliquot of viral particles, along with freshly added polybrene (4 μg/mL), was used to infect cells plated in serum-free media. The media was replaced with complete media the following day. Cells infected with LentiORF-Mutant CYR61 were selected using 15 μg/mL blasticidin, cells infected with Lentiviral Human CCN1 sgRNA were selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin, and cells infected with retroviral WT-DDI2 or protease-dead (dRVP)-DDI2 were selected using 1-2 μg/mL puromycin for 10 days.

For immunofluorescence studies, secondary antibodies included anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (160324; Jackson ImmunoResearch), anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (A-21422; Invitrogen), and anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (1608397; Life Technologies).

## siRNA transfections
NIH-3T3 DDI2KO cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, and streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. ON-TARGET plus mouse CYR61 siRNA and GAPDH control siRNA were purchased from Dharmacon and transfected into the cells at a final concentration of 50 nM using DharmaFECT-1 transfection reagent, following the manufacturer's protocol. Seventy-two hours post-transfection, cells were treated with CQ (60 μM) for 24 hours or left untreated and subsequently harvested for Western blot analysis.

## Western blot analysis
Cells were plated in six-well plates and treated when they reached approximately 70% confluency. For total cell lysate preparation, cells were scraped from culture plates, washed with cold PBS, and pelleted by cold centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Pierce) and incubated on ice for 30 minutes. The lysates were then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C. Total protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. For Western blot analysis of conditioned media, cells were cultured in FBS-free medium. After media collection, cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 1,500 RPM for 3 minutes. Proteins in the media were precipitated by incubation with 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) for 45 minutes on ice, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 RPM for 30 minutes. The protein pellet was washed with cold acetone and resuspended in 2X Laemmli protein sample buffer.
For Western blot analysis of cell lysates, samples were prepared by mixing cell lysates with 4X Laemmli protein sample buffer containing 2-mercaptoethanol on the day of lysis. Samples were then boiled for 7 minutes before loading. Protein samples (15 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto an Immobilon-P PVDF membrane. After transfer, the membranes were blocked for 1 hour with 5% non-fat dry milk in tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T; 50 mM Tris-Base, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20), then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C. Membranes were washed three times with TBS-T, incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour, washed again, and then incubated with ECL Western blotting substrate. Detection was performed using the LiCOR Odyssey Fc Imaging System. Quantification of Western blot signals was carried out using Image Studio V5.5 software. ## Co-immunoprecipitation assay
HEK293 cells were transiently transfected with flag-tagged DDI2 (flag-DDI2), LentiORF-Mutant CYR61, or an empty vector. After transfection, the cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100), and supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Pierce). The lysate was incubated on ice for 30 minutes and then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C. Protein concentration was quantified using the Bradford assay.
The protein concentrations of all samples were adjusted to equal levels, with the volume set to 300 μL. A 25 μL aliquot of the lysate was saved as the input sample, representing the total cell lysate. The remaining lysate supernatants were used for immunoprecipitation using either anti-FLAG beads or A/G beads, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Anti-FLAG beads (40 μL) were pre-blocked with 5% BSA for 1 hour prior to use. Typically, 500 μg of total protein (as determined by Bradford assay) was used for each immunoprecipitation, which was incubated overnight. Then, the beads were washed three times with IP buffer, eluted in Laemmli buffer, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting. The Western blot protocol was performed as previously described.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy
For immunofluorescence experiments cells were seeded onto 12-mm glass coverslips in 12-well culture plates. Prior to fixation, the cells were washed with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes at room temperature (RT). After fixation, the cells were rinsed with PBS and blocked for 1 hour in a blocking solution containing 5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C with the designated primary antibodies. The following day, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 hours at RT. For nuclear staining, the cells were incubated with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) in the dark for 3 minutes at RT. After three additional PBS washes, the cells were mounted onto glass slides using Vectashield mounting medium. Visualization was performed using a Zeiss Laser Scanning Microscope (LSM 700) fitted with a 63x oil-immersion objective lens. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

## Quantitative RT-PCR
Cells were plated in six-well plates and allowed to reach 70% confluency before treatment. They were then treated with 60 μM CQ for 24 hours. Following the treatment, cells were washed once with PBS and collected by scraping in 1 mL PBS. The cell lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 1 minute at 4°C, and the PBS was aspirated. RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen), with an additional DNase treatment (Qiagen) to remove genomic DNA.
One microgram of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis using iScript Reverse Transcriptase (Bio-Rad). Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) reactions were prepared with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) and run on a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Depending on the experimental conditions, either 18S rRNA or GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. The forward and reverse primers utilized for the qPCR reactions are listed as follows: Mouse CCN1 (5′-GGA TGA CCT CCT CGG ACT CG-3′ and 5′-GTT CGG TGC CAA AGA CAG GAA G-3′), Human CCN1 (5′-GAA GCG GCT CCC TGT TTT TG-3′ and 5′-CGG GTT TCT TTC ACA AGG CG-3′), Human CCN1 (5′-ACA GCA GCC TGA AAA AGG GC-3′ and 5′-TCA CAC TCA AAC ATC CAG CGT-3′), Human GAPDH (5′-GGT GGT CTC CTC TGA CTT CAA CA-3′ and 5′-GTT GCT GTA GCC AAA TTC GTT GT-3′), Mouse GAPDH (5′-AACTTTGGCATTGTGGAAGG-3′ and 5′-GGATGCAGGGATGATGTTCT-3′) and Human 18S rRNA (5′-ATG GCC GTT CTT AGT TGG TG-3′ and 5′-CGC TGA GCC AGT CAG TGT AG-3′). Statistical analysis of the data was performed using Student’s unpaired t-test.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
NIH-3T3 cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 2.0–2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer for 1 hour at room temperature. After fixation, the cells were stored at 4°C in the fixative solution until they were processed for dehydration. Dehydration was carried out using a graded series of acetone concentrations (50%, 70%, 80%, and 95%), with each step lasting 10 minutes, followed by three washes in 100% acetone for 10–15 minutes each. The dehydrated cells were then infiltrated with a mixture of acetone and EMbed 812 resin (Polysciences, Inc.) at a 1:1 ratio overnight, followed by infiltration with pure EMbed 812 resin for 8 hours and subsequently overnight. Polymerization of the samples was performed in an oven at 55–60°C for 48 hours.
Ultrathin sections, approximately 80–100 nm thick, were prepared using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems) and mounted on grids. Sections were stained sequentially with 5% uranyl acetate and Reynold’s lead citrate. TEM imaging was performed using a JEOL JEM 1400Plus TEM (JEOL USA, Inc.) equipped with a Gatan One View digital camera (Gatan Inc.), maintained at 4°C. Images were acquired with Digital Micrograph version 3.3 software. ## Proteomics sample preparation and analysis
Cell pellets from DDI2KO and control cells, with or without 60 μM CQ treatment, were washed three times with PBS, frozen, and shipped to the Department of Cell Biology at Harvard Medical School. Proteomics sample preparation and analysis were performed using the TMT Mosaic v2.0 platform. The workflow began with protein extraction from the samples, followed by quantification, reduction, alkylation, precipitation, and enzymatic digestion. Peptides generated from digestion were labeled using Tandem Mass Tag (TMT) reagents and pooled into a single multiplexed sample. These multiplexed samples were loaded into a custom-built capillary column, where peptide separation was performed based on their hydrophobicity and charge. Ionized peptides were subsequently analyzed via tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), with precursor ions separated by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) during the MS1 scan. Fragmentation of selected ions was performed using collision-induced dissociation (CID) or higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). The resulting MS/MS spectra were processed and matched to a protein database using a Sequest-based in-house software pipeline. The web app VolcaNoseR was used to generate and label volcano plots. The proteomics datasets were deposited at ProteomeXchange PRIDE: PXD 060474.

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay. Briefly, 10,000 cells were seeded in 100 μL of medium per well in 96-well plates and allowed to attach overnight. The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (CellTiter-Glo, Promega), and luminescence was measured after 48 hours using a POLARStar Optima microplate reader.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Unless otherwise indicated, all quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Šídák’s post hoc test, or by Student’s unpaired t-test. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ∗ p < 0.05, ∗∗ p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ p < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗ p < 0.0001.
