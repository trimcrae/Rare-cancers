## Abstract
Engineering cells to express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) represents a novel approach in cancer immunotherapy, demonstrating remarkable efficacy in the treatment of hematologic malignancies while also encountering numerous challenges. Against the backdrop of the widespread application of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, the integration of mRNA technology to produce CAR cells and enhance CAR therapies marks the cutting edge of cancer treatment innovation, offering potential solutions to the challenges faced by traditional CAR therapies. This convergence offers distinct advantages. It enables the generation of CAR cells both in vitro and in vivo without transgene integration, thereby achieving transient expression that reduces the risk of various side effects. Meanwhile, this approach entails lower production costs. This method may therefore serve as a novel and promising alternative to existing therapies in the future. In this article, we review the latest advancements and clinical applications of mRNA-based CAR therapies, which utilize mRNA technology to generate CAR-T cells. Additionally, we explore the diverse therapies enabled by mRNA technology, such as gene editing and vaccines, and their combination with CAR therapies. By analyzing their challenges and prospects, we aim to provide new insights into comprehensively improving the therapeutic efficacy of CAR therapies and expanding their clinical application.
Keywords: mRNA technology, CAR therapy, CAR-T cells, CAR mRNA, Delivery system, Therapeutic efficacy, Safety, Future prospects

## Introduction
Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) Therapy constitutes a state-of-art immunotherapeutic modality grounded in the principles of genetic engineering. This groundbreaking strategy entails the genetic reprogramming of a patient’s immune cells, primarily T cells, to express CARs, thereby enhancing their ability to recognize and eliminate malignant cells [ ]. Although viral transfection, as a conventional genetic engineering technique, enables stable CAR gene expression [ ] and has exhibited remarkable efficacy in the treatment of hematologic malignancies [ ], several critical challenges remain unresolved: (i) Therapeutic limitations are manifested in treatment resistance, disease relapse, and notably suboptimal outcomes in the management of solid tumors; (ii) Adverse events associated with CAR therapy encompass cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), on-target off-tumor toxicity (OTOT) [ ], and even the depletion of the CAR cells [ , ]. Moreover, CAR gene integration may induce aberrant gene expression, elevating the risk of secondary primary malignancies or delayed toxicities such as B cell dysplasia; (iii) Practical constraints arise from the limited accessibility of conventional CAR-T cells, attributable to the intricate, time-consuming, and cost-prohibitive manufacturing processes, which hinder their widespread clinical adoption [ ]; (iv) Significant hurdles persist in extending CAR-based therapies to non-oncological domains, including autoimmune disorders, infectious diseases, and cardiovascular pathologies.
mRNA technology embodies a cutting-edge molecular therapeutic platform that harnesses specific mRNA molecules as therapeutic agents, exploiting host cells as endogenous bioreactors to facilitate translation and post-translational modifications, thereby enabling precise modulation of cellular gene expression and protein synthesis. This innovative approach facilitates the targeted delivery of mRNA to specific cell populations, where the encoded proteins are subsequently translated and functionally expressed within the target cells through sophisticated delivery systems. The therapeutic potential of mRNA is underscored by several distinctive advantages: it circumvents the risk of host genome integration, possesses a transient in vivo half-life, exhibits remarkable flexibility, and can be rapidly manufactured. In contrast to conventional protein-based therapies, mRNA demonstrates enhanced cellular uptake efficiency and superior capacity to traverse biological barriers, thereby effectively remodeling the cellular microenvironment. These unique characteristics render mRNA technology a promising candidate for addressing the current limitations of CAR-T therapy.
In this comprehensive review, we systematically examine the application of mRNA technology in the generation of CAR-T cells, and explore the synergistic integration of diverse mRNA-derived therapeutic modalities, including gene editing technologies and vaccines, with CAR therapy. Herein, we focus specifically on mRNA-based CAR therapy, elucidating its delivery systems and translational applications to demonstrate its ability to overcome the challenges of conventional CAR therapies while preserving their advantages. This integrated approach offers innovative strategies to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of CAR therapy and expand its applicability across diverse clinical settings.

## mRNA-based CAR cell preparation and delivery system
In contrast to traditional viral vector-based CAR therapy, mRNA-based CAR therapy leverages the direct translation of mRNA into proteins within the cytoplasm, bypassing the need for viral integration into the host genome, thereby mitigating the risks associated with genomic integration [ ]. Moreover, the transient expression characteristic of mRNA can reduce the side effects induced by sustained CAR expression driven by viral vectors, such as persistent cellular activation and CRS [ ]. Additionally, the in situ generation of CAR-T cells through the infusion of CAR mRNA into patients holds the potential to significantly lower treatment costs, circumventing the expensive and complex ex vivo manufacturing processes required for viral-based therapies.

## In vitro preparation of CAR cells and delivery of CAR mRNA
The conventional CAR-T cell preparation process comprises four sequential steps: 1) Leukapheresis: Isolation of T lymphocytes from the patient’s peripheral blood; 2) T Cell Activation: Stimulation of T cells using antibody-coated magnetic beads; 3) Genetic Modification: Transfection of the CAR gene into T cells via viral vector-mediated gene transfer; 4) Expansion and Infusion: Ex vivo expansion of the engineered CAR-T cell products followed by reinfusion into the patient.
In current clinical practice, approximately 94% of CAR-T cell therapies employ viral vectors for gene delivery, with lentiviral vectors constituting 54% of CAR-T products and retroviral vectors representing 41% [ ]. Lentiviral vectors offer notable advantages, including high transduction efficiency and long-term stable expression achieved through integration into the host cell genome. However, this integration carries the inherent risk of insertional mutagenesis, which may potentially lead to oncogenesis [ ]. Retroviral vectors, on the other hand, demonstrate better transduction efficiency and safety profiles. Their well-established production protocols and cost-effectiveness have rendered them the most extensively utilized viral vector system in CAR-T cell therapy to date [ ]. While adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors exhibit low immunogenicity and pathogenicity [ , ], along with the ability to achieve stable gene transduction without tumorigenic risks [ ], their application in CAR-T therapy remains limited. This is primarily due to their restricted packaging capacity, typically not exceeding 5 kb, which poses challenges for accommodating the larger gene fragments required in CAR-T cell engineering [ ]. The intrinsic tropism of viral vectors enables highly efficient in vitro production of CAR-T cells, facilitating stable integration of transgenes into the host cell genome and ensuring sustained expression of CAR genes.
In mRNA-based CAR-T cell production, step 3) entails the delivery of CAR mRNA into T cells using either electroporation or biomaterial-based methods (most commonly viral vectors) to enable the expression of CAR on the surface of immune cells. Alternatively, CAR-T cells can be generated directly in vivo through targeted mRNA nanocarriers, circumventing the intricate procedures required for in vitro gene manipulation [ ] (Fig. A). The selection of an appropriate delivery method is a pivotal factor in the production of mRNA-based CAR-T cells, as it directly impacts the yield, functionality, and safety of CAR-T cells [ ]. Since viral vectors enhance the immunogenic response of host cells and reduce cell viability, and most viral vectors can deliver only one oligonucleotide cargo at a time, they exhibit significant limitations in the delivery of large CAR genes or in co-delivery with other RNA or protein components. Consequently, more advanced delivery systems, such as electroporation and nanomaterial-based platforms, have emerged as superior alternatives (Table ).
 

## Electroporation
Compared to viral vectors, a unique advantage of electroporation is its versatility as a cell delivery method, capable of transporting both oligonucleotides and proteins. Furthermore, transfection of CAR mRNA into cells via electroporation demonstrates superior transfection efficiency and cytotoxic activity compared to viral transfection [ – ]. Optimizing this CAR mRNA delivery strategy demands precise adjustment of the electroporation parameters and mRNA dose, typically applying a voltage of ~ 300 V and an mRNA concentration of 40–50 μg/mL [ , ]. However, the harsh conditions generated by high-voltage pulses during electroporation may significantly impair biological functions. Studies have revealed a marked increase in intracellular fluorescent calcium levels post-electroporation, an indicator of cellular stress, alongside delayed cell doubling times [ ]. Another study highlighted that electroporation induces abnormal gene expression levels and cytokine production in primary immune cells, thereby compromising the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T cells [ ]. As an emerging technology, nanoneedles offer relatively simple operation and achieve higher delivery efficiency of CAR DNA [ ] and non-CAR mRNA [ ] in vitro with minimal cellular perturbation compared to electroporation. It is believed that nanoneedles may emerge as a superior method for CAR mRNA delivery in the future.

## LNP
Although electroporation remains the predominant method for producing mRNA-based CAR cells, its high technical requirements and significant cellular damage limit its large-scale application [ ]. Nanomaterials such as lipid nanoparticles, polymers, and extracellular vesicles are increasingly emerging as promising candidates for CAR mRNA delivery.
As the most widely utilized carrier for in vitro delivery, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have undergone extensive design optimization and modification in numerous studies, leading to their successful application in various contexts [ – ]. LNPs are typically composed of four key components: cholesterol, cationic lipids, neutral helper lipids, and PEGylated lipids [ ]. Innovative modifications have further enhanced LNP functionality. For instance, replacing cholesterol with bile acids to produce BA-LNPs has been shown to reduce hepatocyte targeting while promoting delivery to other cell types, including T cells, B cells, and epithelial cells in vitro [ ]. Cationic lipids play a central role in LNP design, as they form complexes with negatively charged nucleic acids (such as mRNA or siRNA) to encapsulate them. Additionally, they facilitate LNP-cell membrane fusion and promote cellular uptake by interacting with the negatively charged cell membrane. LNPs based on C14-4, one kind of cationic lipids, have demonstrated the ability to deliver CAR mRNA to human primary T cells [ ]. Using the Design of Experiments method, the B10 formulation (C14-4 : DOPE : Cholesterol : PEG = 40: 30: 25: 2.5) was identified as optimal, exhibiting reduced cytotoxicity and potent cancer cell-killing efficacy [ ] (Fig. C). The B10 formulation also displays excellent stability and effectively delivers mRNA to various lung cell types, particularly endothelial and epithelial cells [ ]. Further advancements include the development of 9322–016 B LNP, cardiolipin-mimic LNP and 76–017 Se LNP, which deliver chemically modified CAR mRNA to primary macrophages, T cells and CD8+ T lymphocytes, respectively, achieving significant efficacy [ , ] (Fig. C). Furthermore, novel functional components can be incorporated into lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) without altering their conventional core structure. In one study, the introduction of arachidonic acid (ARA)—a pro-inflammatory fatty acid—as a functional constituent enabled high transfection efficiency (83.76%) in primary M2-polarized bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). The resulting CAR-M cells demonstrated strong and specific phagocytic activity against tumor cells in vitro [ ]. Activated lipid nanoparticles (aLNPs), designed to mimic antigen-presenting cells (APCs), integrate the T cell activation properties of magnetic beads with the efficient transfection capabilities of LNPs. This innovative approach enables the simultaneous activation and transfection of primary human T cells in a single step. The resulting mRNA CAR-T cells have demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing tumor burden in mouse xenograft models [ ]. Currently, numerous optimization strategies are being explored for the design and preparation of LNPs, highlighting their versatility and potential for being a novel alternative.strategy.
 

## Other biomaterials
In addition to the widely employed viral vectors, electroporation, and LNPs, other biomaterial-based platforms mediating CAR mRNA delivery in vitro are currently under active investigation.
CART (charge-altering releasable transporter) is a synthetic polymer carrier whose charge can dynamically adapt to external conditions. This charge-altering mechanism not only enhances delivery efficiency but also reduces toxicity associated with persistent cationic charges. Among CART formulations, CART BDK-O7:N7:A13 represents an optimized oligomer that demonstrates higher efficiency and lower cytotoxicity compared to electroporation. CART has been successfully employed to deliver mRNA encoding anti-human CD19/4-1BB/CD3 CAR, enabling the efficient generation of cytotoxic human CAR NK cells without requiring prior cell activation or altering the NK cell phenotype [ ].
Chol-PAsp(DET/CET), an amphiphilic polyaspartamide derivative, is a non-viral polymeric carrier specifically engineered for enhanced mRNA transfection in natural killer (NK) cells. In the human NK-92mi cell line, it mediated significantly higher transfection efficiency for both reporter genes (e.g., luciferase, EGFP) and therapeutic genes (e.g., GreenLantern-NKG2D) than control carriers in the same series, including non-cholesterol-modified PAsp(DET/CHE) and double-cholesterol-modified Chol-PAsp(DET)-Chol. This superior performance establishes Chol-PAsp(DET/CHE) as a promising platform for CAR mRNA delivery to NK cells [ ].
CPP (cell-penetrating peptide), also referred to as PTD (peptide transduction domain), is a short peptide typically less than 30 amino acids in length. It possesses the ability to traverse biological barriers and transport cargo of varying sizes into cells [ ]. Beyond its role as a tool for recombinant protein transduction in protein replacement therapy for conditions such as thalassemia, cardio-cerebral myopathy, myocardial infarction, and peritoneal fibrosis [ – ], PTD has demonstrated significant potential in the in vitro delivery of mRNA for treating mitochondrial diseases [ ]. Notably, NK-92 cells engineered via PTD-mediated delivery of CAR-T1E exhibited high target cell death efficiency, ranging from 25% to 33%, in co-culture experiments, demonstrating potential for treating HSC-3 (oral squamous cell carcinoma) and MCF-7 (breast metastatic adenocarcinoma) [ ].
Exosomes, one kind of extracellular vesicles (EVs) secreted by cells, facilitate the intercellular transfer of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Their potential as drug delivery vectors, including for RNA, has been extensively explored [ ], with specific evidence supporting their role in maintaining cerebrovascular stability, reversing chemotherapy resistance, and improving cognitive function in Alzheimer’s disease mouse models [ – ]. Leveraging these promising outcomes and the inherent properties of exosomes, a research team has developed a refined packaging method to adapt exosomes for mRNA-based CAR therapy. This approach has successfully generated CAR T cells expressing anti-CD3/CD28 single-chain variable fragments (scFv) and demonstrated effective cancer cell killing in subsequent experiments [ ].
Virus-like particles (VLPs), composed of viral capsid proteins without genetic material, retain natural cargo-loading and cell-targeting capacities. They present distinct advantages as delivery vectors: surpassing AAVs by avoiding packaging limits, immunogenicity, and insertional risks; exceeding lentiviral vectors by eliminating genomic integration and complex production; and improving upon LNPs through enhanced targeting accuracy, formulation simplicity, and stability. Additional strengths include structural homogeneity, precise stoichiometry, RNase resistance, and engineerable surfaces for flexible targeting. In vitro, anti-CD3-CCMV VLPs generated CAR-positive expression in 66.3% of live single T cells after co-incubation, a rate significantly higher than that in control groups [ ].

## In vivo preparation of CAR cells and delivery of CAR mRNA
Compared to the in vitro delivery of CAR mRNA for CAR cell production, intravenous infusion of CAR mRNA offers a streamlined alternative that circumvents the complex procedures required for ex vivo manufacturing. This approach significantly simplifies the process, shortens preparation time, reduces treatment costs, and enhances scalability for large-scale applications. Intravenous infusion leverages autologous cells within the stable internal environment as a natural production platform. On one hand, it capitalizes on the inherent adaptability of autologous cells to the microenvironment, transforming them into highly targeted therapeutic protein producers to amplify treatment efficacy. On the other hand, it mitigates the risks of cell variation and contamination possible in in-vitro culture, thereby improving treatment safety. This innovative delivery method holds great promise as a future breakthrough in immunotherapy. However, unlike the direct infusion of CAR mRNA into target cells in vitro, in vivo delivery of CAR mRNA critically depends on the protection of delivery carriers and precise targeting guidance. The delivery vector must not only shield CAR mRNA from nuclease degradation in vivo but also ensure efficient targeting of specific immune cells while maintaining stability and low immunogenicity. Therefore, as the foundation and critical guarantee for the effective function of CAR mRNA, the design and optimization of in vivo delivery carriers have garnered significant attention in recent research, emerging as a core focus for realizing this innovative therapeutic strategy.

## LNP
The safety and efficacy of LNPs in the human body remain the most critical considerations for clinical applications. The successful deployment of LNP vectors in mRNA vaccines for COVID-19 has provided valuable insights and confidence for their use in delivering other therapeutic mRNAs in vivo. However, the first-pass effect associated with LNP-mediated in vivo delivery remains a significant challenge. This phenomenon leads to the majority of CAR mRNA being metabolized or degraded by the liver before reaching target cells. On one hand, this reduces delivery efficiency and limits therapeutic efficacy; on the other hand, the degradation products of mRNA may trigger immune responses and exacerbate treatment-related side effects. To address these issues, numerous studies have focused on optimizing LNP design to enhance delivery efficiency, thereby ensuring therapeutic effectiveness [ , , , – ].
Various design-optimized LNPs have significantly enhanced the in vivo delivery efficiency of CAR mRNA, enabling targeted delivery to T cells [ , , , , ], macrophages [ , , ], or selective homing to infected organs while bypassing the liver [ , ]. As anticipated, higher in vivo delivery efficiency correlates with improved therapeutic outcomes. For instance, the proportion of effector memory CD8+ T cells in CAR and Siglec-GΔITIMs LNP-treated mice was 2.29 times higher than in PBS-treated mice, demonstrating sustained anti-tumor effects. This enhanced efficacy may be linked to alterations in the immune microenvironment, as injections of aCD3-LNPs stimulated myeloid cells to secrete chemokines and T-helper cytokines. Transfected T cells localized to tumor tissues and tumor-draining lymph nodes, improving the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Notably, in vivo delivery of LNPs extends beyond cancer therapy, showing promise in other disease areas. For example, AngII/PE-injured mice treated with CD5/LNP-FAPCAR leveraged the transient expression of mRNA to reduce fibrosis and normalize left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes [ ]. Additionally, CRV/LNP-RNA treatment fully restored sepsis in mice infected with a lethal dose of MRSA [ ].
In vitro, the transient expression of TROP2 CAR on monocytes induces antigen-dependent cytotoxicity and cytokine release. Intravenous injection of TROP2 CAR mRNA/LNP generates TROP2 CAR myeloid cells, which demonstrate the ability to migrate to tumors and exert direct anti-tumor effects [ ]. This highlights the potential of directly engineering myeloid cells in vivo to kill tumor cells and coordinate adaptive immune responses, providing a promising direction for clinical research in solid tumor therapy. Importantly, no adverse events were observed during in vivo delivery of CAR mRNA using LNPs [ – , ], underscoring the safety and therapeutic potential of this approach.

## Other biomaterials
Delivering CAR mRNA using biodegradable polymer nanocarriers poly(beta-amino ester)s(PBAEs) offers dual advantages of mRNA protection and precise T cell targeting [ , ]. In mouse models, repeated infusions of these polymer nanoparticles have been shown to induce sufficient host T cells to express tumor-specific CAR or virus-specific TCR, leading to disease regression. This approach achieves effects comparable to the large-scale infusion of in vitro-engineered lymphocytes while maintaining high safety, making it applicable for treating ovarian cancer, melanoma, and glioblastoma [ ]. However, since nanoparticle formation primarily relies on electrostatic interactions, PBAEs are often limited to delivering anionic cargoes. While this is well-suited for highly negatively charged nucleic acids, it may restrict the co-delivery of other types of cargo. To address this limitation, researchers have developed a novel hyperbranched PBAE that incorporates both cationic and anionic charges. By modifying polymer end groups with carboxylic acid ligands (similar to amino acid precursors), this innovation not only leverages electrostatic interactions but also enhances nanoparticle assembly through increased hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic effects. This strategy strengthens the interaction between the polymer and proteins, expanding the versatility of PBAEs for therapeutic applications [ ].
In addition to its application in in vitro CAR mRNA delivery [ ], mixed lipid CART (such as BDK-O7:N7:A13) has demonstrated significant potential in in vivo delivery within mouse models. This formulation achieved efficient luciferase mRNA expression in the spleen, with transfection efficiency in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells exceeding 1.5%, a notable improvement over single-lipid CART [ ], highlight the promising capability of CART for in vivo delivery of CAR mRNA. To address the challenge of strong polyanion association, further optimizations have been made by incorporating guanidinylated serine moieties, resulting in GSer-CART. This advanced formulation exhibits highly selective mRNA delivery to the lung and spleen [ ].
Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), engineered with surface anti-CD206 single-chain antibodies (scFv), enabled the in situ generation of CAR macrophages in vivo. These engineered sEVs demonstrated superior biocompatibility over LNPs, minimizing complement activation and immune clearance risks. They also promoted enhanced endosomal escape, leading to higher intracellular mRNA release efficiency—approximately 73% of macrophages successfully synthesized CAR protein after 24 hours of culture. In a murine model of lung cancer metastasis, inhalation of CARmRNA@aCD206-sEVs yielded striking therapeutic outcomes: a significant reduction in both the number and size of lung tumor nodules, extension of median survival from 30 days (control) to over 60 days, and a 100% survival rate [ ].
Furthermore, a study utilizing anaerobic bacteria [ ] and employing natural membranes or membrane proteins as raw materials has adopted a biomimetic design strategy to produce hybrid vesicles [ ]. These vesicles inherit multiple functionalities from their source cells, including stability in blood, immune stealth capabilities, intrinsic cell-targeting properties,the ability to cross biological barriers, and protection of internal genetic cargo from RNase degradation and phagocytosis [ ]. Furthermore, they address key limitations of natural vesicles, such as low production yield and limited drug-loading capacity, while additionally providing a broader and more controllable size range along with high cargo versatility. Additionally, while liposomes may exhibit potential toxicity/immunogenicity and are prone to degradation and clearance during in vivo circulation, hybrid vesicles achieve longer circulation times and demonstrate superior stability in pathological environments. These suggest that biomimetic vesicles could serve as a viable strategy for the optimization of sEVs and a promising candidate for in vivo CAR mRNA delivery.
Virus-like particles (VLPs), an emerging platform for CAR mRNA delivery, have shown strong therapeutic potential. In a murine model of B-cell lymphoma, treatment with T-FVLPmCAR led to significant suppression of tumor growth, with inhibition rates ranging from 83.5% to 99.0%. Complete tumor regression was achieved in a subset of mice receiving higher doses. Concurrently, no notable adverse effects were observed, supporting the favorable biocompatibility and safety profile of T-FVLPmCAR [ ].
Numerous nanoparticles hold potential for future in vivo CAR mRNA delivery, many of which have already been utilized for mRNA vaccine administration. These nanoparticles can be categorized into four main groups: 1) Lipid-based nanoparticles, e.g., LNPs, liposomes, and lipoplexes; 2) Polymer-based nanoparticles, e.g., diethylaminoethyl dextran, polyethyleneimine, dendrimers, polymeric micelles, and poly(CBA-co-4-amino-1-butanol); 3) Nanoemulsions(NEs), e.g., squalene-based cationic NEs; 4) Protein nanoparticles, e.g., comprising the ferritin family and polypeptides; 5) Inorganic nanoparticles, e.g., gold NPs, silica NPs, quantum dots, and iron oxide NPs [ ].

## Comparative platforms and future direction
Viral vectors and electroporation represent two mature technologies for CAR mRNA delivery. Sustained CAR expression is a common feature shared by all viral vectors, with LV and RV primarily used for in vitro delivery and AAV mainly employed for in vivo applications. Electroporation serves as a benchmark for in vitro mRNA delivery, achieving high transfection efficiency in primary T cells (50–90% [ ]), though it is often accompanied by significant cytotoxicity. As an emerging technology, nanoneedles demonstrate a transfection efficiency up to three times that of conventional electroporation under specific conditions [ ], while also exhibiting advantages in targeting specificity ( > 90%) and cell viability ( > 90%).
Several emerging non-viral delivery vectors have also been developed. Synthetic or bio-derived nanocarriers—such as LNPs, EVs, and VLPs—possess distinct characteristics for CAR mRNA delivery in vitro and in vivo, though their performance varies significantly. In vitro, the transfection efficiency of these carriers in primary immune cells is generally low (typically below 30% [ , , , ]) and lower than that achieved with viral vectors [ , ]. In vivo, following intravenous administration, delivery efficiency is further reduced (often reported below 5%). Among them, VLPs and CART demonstrate slightly higher in vivo delivery efficiency than LNPs [ , ]. Notably, the route of administration can fundamentally alter delivery outcomes. For instance, a study on pulmonary metastases utilizing inhalable EVs successfully achieved CAR expression in over 20% of pulmonary macrophages [ ], underscoring the advantage of localized administration in bypassing systemic barriers.
In terms of targeting specificity, all these platforms can be enhanced through surface ligand modifications (e.g., antibodies, peptides). Regarding safety profiles, while certain LNP components (e.g., ionizable cationic lipids) may pose potential toxicity risks, one study on in vivo CAR mRNA delivery using LNPs observed no significant toxic reactions. CART, EVs, and VLPs have all demonstrated favorable tolerability in vivo [ , , , ].
Furthermore, several other carriers are currently applied primarily in either in vitro or in vivo settings. These include CPP [ ] and Chol-PASp (DET/CHE), used in vitro, and PBAEs, used in vivo. While no significant safety concerns have been reported for these carriers [ ], their delivery efficiency and targeting specificity require further investigation. Both Chol-PASp (DET/CHE) and PBAEs can be functionalized with targeting ligands to enhance their targeting capability, indicating promising potential for broader in vivo applications [ ].
Green nanomaterials also hold great promise as a crucial development direction for mRNA delivery systems. These materials exhibit environmentally friendly properties throughout their entire life cycle, from synthesis and application to degradation, while also featuring sustainable sources, low toxicity, and biodegradability, making them ideal nanocarrier candidates. Specifically, EVs, naturally derived biological carriers, and biomimetic vesicles, based on EVs with moderate incorporation of liposome components, are primarily prepared by green physical methods for cell processing; VLPs, whose core component is protein, can be mass-produced through sustainable biomanufacturing technologies. Notably, all three types of carriers demonstrate low toxicity in vivo and can be effectively cleared by the body, highlighting their significant safety advantages. From the perspective of application scenarios, green nanomaterials are particularly suitable for clinical settings with stringent safety requirements, such as CAR therapy for pediatric patients or CAR therapy regimens requiring multiple infusions, and thus possess broad development potential.
In general, viral vectors enable stable CAR protein expression and are suitable for hematologic malignancies, which require long-term CAR expression. Technologies such as electroporation and nanoneedles are efficient and versatile tools for in vitro mRNA delivery, suitable for personalized therapies, but they require a balance between efficiency, toxicity, and cost. For “off-the-shelf” CAR therapies, in vivo direct reprogramming is an ideal approach. In this context, LNP technology is mature and easily scalable, making it a pioneering platform for systemic administration; VLP and CART have greater potential for cell-specific targeting in their design; and EVs, as natural carriers, are more stable in the blood and better at crossing biological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier than LNP, with higher safety [ ]. While in vivo delivery systems are not yet as mature as in vitro technologies, they hold greater development potential due to their comparable or superior CAR mRNA delivery efficiency, enhanced safety profile, and reduced preparation costs. These advantages position in vivo delivery as a promising frontier for advancing CAR mRNA therapy. Future advancements will rely on the rational selection, engineering optimization, and even combination of these platforms based on the target disease, location of target cells (systemic or local), and required pharmacokinetics (transient or sustained expression).

## Co-delivery of multiple molecules with CAR mRNA

## Enhancing cytotoxicity
Multimolecular co-delivery of CAR mRNA represents a critical strategy for enhancing mRNA-based CAR therapy. Compared to UCB-NK, CD19 CAR-NK, and CD20 CAR-NK cells, mRNA-based CD19/CD20 dual-targeted CAR-NK cells exhibit increased specific cytotoxicity against acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and elevated release levels of cytokines such as perforin, IFN-γ, and IL-15 [ ]. Additionally, dual CAR NK-92 cells generated through electroporation of CD19-/BCMA-CAR mRNA demonstrated over 60% of the killing efficiency of single antigen-expressing NK cells [ ]. Although research on double or multiple CAR mRNA co-delivery remains limited, existing studies consistently suggest that non-single CAR mRNA co-delivery may yield higher clinical benefits. However, whether this approach is accompanied by additional side effects requires further experimental investigation and observation.
In addition to dual/multiple CAR mRNA co-delivery, combining CAR mRNA with other molecular co-delivery strategies can further enhance the cytotoxicity against target cells. Previous studies have demonstrated that interleukins and interferons can synergistically induce anti-tumor effects [ , ]. For instance, a research team used mRNA electroporation to co-express IL-12/IFNα2 or IL-12/IL-18 in CAR T cells, observing enhanced pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironments, reduced T cell exhaustion [ ], and increased efficacy against mouse melanoma [ ]. siRNA is another molecule frequently co-delivered with CAR mRNA. For example, CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) cells produced by the in situ delivery of MRSA-targeting chimeric antigen receptor (SasA-CAR mRNA) and CASP11 siRNA (a key MRSA intracellular evasion target) recruited mitochondria to accumulate nearby, enhancing the elimination of intracellular MRSA through in situ ROS production [ ]. Additionally, the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) signaling pathway, a major inhibitor of T cell activity in the tumor microenvironment, can be targeted by co-delivering siRNA to knock down PD-1 expression, maintaining T cell activation while successfully preparing CAR-T cells [ ]. Other studies have improved the anti-tumor capabilities of CAR-M cells by co-delivering modified mRNA. For example, the phagocytic inhibition caused by the binding of Siglec-G on macrophages to CD24 on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells was alleviated by co-delivering Siglec-GΔITIMs mRNA to macrophages [ ]. Furthermore,a bicistronic mRNA has been designed, with its CAR portion encoding HLA-G-CAR and its BiTE portion encoding a PD-L1/CD3ε bispecific antibody. This dual-targeting strategy not only directly attacks HLA-G-positive tumor cells but also recruits other T cells to target PD-L1-positive tumor cells via BiTE. This approach more effectively induces the dissolution of tumor cells with high expression of both HLA-G and PD-L1, prolonging the survival time of mice [ ] (Fig. ).
 

## Improving safety
mRNA-based CAR therapy inherently contributes to mitigating CAR-related adverse reactions, including CRS. Furthermore, combining CAR mRNA with multi-molecule co-delivery strategies may further reduce the incidence of such adverse effects. For example, co-loading DNA encoding CAR19 + shIL-6 into LNPs targeting CD3 enabled the simultaneous expression of CD19-specific CAR in T cells and silencing of IL-6 in vivo. Compared to the group receiving only CAR19 DNA, this approach resulted in lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby reducing the risk of CRS [ ]. While the co-delivery of mRNA encoding CAR19 + shIL-6 is theoretically capable of achieving similar outcomes, specific experimental validation is still required to confirm its efficacy.

## Delivery challenges for mRNA-based CAR therapy

## The delivery quantity of CAR mRNA
CAR therapy requires higher mRNA doses and repeated administrations to achieve sustained therapeutic effects [ ]. Clearly, many studies have confirmed that both anti-tumor activity and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling effects increase with the elevation of mRNA dose [ , ]. However, studies on in vivo CAR cell production have found that higher mRNA doses, 2.0 mg/kg LNPs, lead to increased cytokine release both early and late after administration, significant T cell exhaustion, and hepatotoxicity compared to lower doses, 0.5 mg/kg LNPs, suggesting that lower doses may be safer [ ]. Another study also reported that one cynomolgus monkey showed immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like syndrome after the third 1.5 mg/kg dose [ ], a recognized adverse effect of CAR T cell therapies [ ]. In clinical trials, higher mRNA doses initiated adverse effects as well, such as prolonged myalgia [ ] and fever [ ]. For safety considerations, although the 2.0 mg/kg dose exhibits stronger anti-tumor activity, a dose of 1.0 mg/kg, demonstrating “high CAR delivery efficiency and no toxicity” in cynomolgus monkeys, has became a clinical candidate dose (NCT05969041 clinical trial) [ ], which provides insights for dose design in clinical translation.The therapeutic efficacy can be compensated for through repeated dosing. Currently, there is limited research exploring the relationship between in vivo CAR mRNA delivery doses and the therapeutic outcomes of mRNA-based CAR therapies. We still anticipate more studies to investigate effective and safe in vivo mRNA delivery doses.

## The stability of CAR mRNA
The encapsulation of CAR mRNA by carriers such as LNPs protects the mRNA from degradation by extracellular RNases, ensuring its stability before cellular uptake. During cellular entry, CAR mRNA, along with the delivery carrier, is endocytosed by target cells to form endosomes. The delivery carrier facilitates the escape of CAR mRNA from the endosomes into the cytoplasm, where it is translated into CAR proteins and inserted into the cell membrane to exert its function. The duration of CAR expression on the T cell surface is closely related to the activity of intracellular CAR mRNA [ ]. Once all transfected CAR mRNA becomes inactive, CAR will no longer be present on the cell membrane, and the CAR cells will cease to function. Therefore, enhancing the activity and stability of CAR mRNA in the cytoplasm holds the potential to maintain therapeutic efficacy while ensuring safety through low-dose CAR mRNA delivery, extending the intervals between repeated administrations, and reducing treatment costs.
Enhancing mRNA stability primarily involves three strategies. The first is modifying the mRNA structure. Conventional linear mRNA consists of key structural elements, including the 5’ cap, 5’ untranslated region (UTR), coding sequence (CDS), 3’ UTR, and poly(A) tail. Each of these regions can be precisely engineered to improve mRNA performance in therapeutic contexts [ ]. Modifying the 5’ cap (e.g., incorporating anti-reverse cap analogs [ ]) and selecting or engineering specific 5’ and 3’ UTRs [ ] can significantly enhance mRNA translation while improving stability. Fine-tuning the poly(A) tail length can balance mRNA stability and translation efficiency, contributing to sustained CAR protein expression [ ]. The second strategy involves altering nucleotides. Modified nucleotides such as 5-methylcytidine (m5C), N6-methyladenosine (m6A), pseudouridine ( ), 5-methoxyuridine (5moU), and 1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ) are commonly used for mRNA optimization. These modifications not only enhance protein translation efficiency but also reduce innate immune activation by mRNA [ – ]. The third strategy is optimizing codon usage. The efficient decoding of codon information in the open reading frame (ORF) is closely linked to mRNA protein expression efficiency [ ]. Advances in artificial intelligence have enabled rapid exploration of optimal mRNA sequences [ ]. These methods can be applied to enhance the persistence of CAR protein expression, offering the possibility of extending the effective therapeutic duration of a single dose (Fig. A).
Recent studies have explored the use of circular RNA (circRNA) as a novel delivery form for CAR, offering a promising approach to enhance CAR stability. Circular RNA, a single-stranded RNA with a closed-loop structure formed through reverse splicing, represents a class of non-coding RNAs [ , ] with potential diverse biological functions. Emerging research has revealed that certain circRNAs possess protein-coding capabilities, opening new avenues for protein translation [ – ]. Unlike linear mRNAs, circRNAs lack the terminal motifs required for exonuclease-mediated degradation [ ], making their unique closed-loop structure inherently more stable. Many studies have directly quantified the half-lives of RNA molecules. Endogenous circRNAs in MCF10A cells display prolonged stability (median half-life: 18.8–23.7 h), contrasting with the shorter half-lives of linear isoforms (4.0–7.4 h) [ ]. This advantage is even more pronounced in other human cell lines: in HEK293 cells, the half-life of circRNA (~80 hours) is nearly twice that of linear mRNA (~43–45 hours); strikingly, in HeLa cells, circRNA exhibits an exceptional half-life of 116 hours, far exceeding the 44–49 hours observed for linear mRNA [ ]. Collectively, these data point to a conclusion: the circular architecture confers robust protection against mRNA degradation, with engineered circRNA exhibiting markedly superior molecular stability—demonstrating median half-lives extended beyond 2-fold compared to linear counterparts. Studies have demonstrated that circRNAs constructed using self-splicing introns exhibit robust and stable protein expression in eukaryotic cells [ ]. Furthermore, compared to linear mRNA, circRNA enables higher expression density of anti-CD19 CAR protein within cells, greater cytotoxicity, and enhanced anti-tumor efficacy against NALM-6 tumors [ ]. Circular mRNA-driven CAR expression remains detectable until day 7, in contrast to linear mRNA, which decays by day 4. Notably, cmRNA-CAR-T cells exhibited no significant toxicity against CD19-negative cells (K562), suggesting that prolonged expression duration does not increase off-target effects. The ‘high-yield, scarless’ Hi-Scarless-PIE system developed in this study also addresses the limitations of conventional PIE platforms—either ‘scar sequences triggering immunogenicity’ or ‘scarless designs causing low yield [ ], highlighting its potential as an advanced form of CAR delivery (Fig. B). In summary, circRNA avoids the risks of permanent transgene expression inherent in conventional CAR-T, while appropriately prolonging mRNA expression, achieving a more favorable efficacy-safety balance. The ideal duration for optimal balance may be disease-dependent, which remains a key question for future research.

## Selection of CAR mRNA delivery targets
CAR mRNA can be delivered to a wide range of immune cells to mediate anti-cancer responses, encompassing traditional CAR-T cells (CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells) as well as novel cell types such as CAR-NK, CAR-NKT, CAR-M, CAR-Treg, and CAR-γδT [ ]. Among these, we focus on three of the most significant: CAR-T cells, CAR-M cells, and CAR-NK cells.
T cells express numerous surface-specific molecules, and modifying LNPs with antibodies targeting these molecules enables in vivo T cell-targeted CAR delivery. For instance, CD3 [ , ], CD28 [ ], and CD5 [ ], which are widely expressed on various T cells, as well as CD4 on helper T cells [ ] and CD8 on cytotoxic T cells [ ], are all viable T cell targets. Multi-target strategies can enhance the efficiency of T cell-targeted CAR mRNA delivery. For example, a study found that activation lipid nanoparticles (aLNPs) with a CD3:CD28 ratio of 1:10 exhibited optimal performance [ ]. Beyond LNPs, PBAE polymers modified with anti-CD3 or anti-CD8 antibodies [ ], and exosomes decorated with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies [ ], have also achieved efficient CAR mRNA delivery to T cells. In addition to antibody modifications, altering the lipid composition of LNPs can improve delivery efficiency. For example, replacing cholesterol with bile acids [ ] or using C14-4 as a cationic lipid [ ] can enhance T cell targeting. Furthermore, LNPs formed with 76-O17Se as a cationic lipid have been shown to stably encapsulate mRNA, protecting it from degradation. The incorporation of selenium atoms may significantly enhance the membrane-penetrating ability of LNPs, demonstrating higher mRNA delivery efficiency in human primary CD8+ T cells compared to other lipids [ ]. SORT LNPs, by incorporating a fifth lipid (e.g., 18:1 PA, palmitoyloleoyl phosphatidylcholine), enable targeted delivery to splenic T cells [ ] (Fig. C).
CAR-Ms may offer advantages over CAR-Ts because macrophages infiltrate the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ ], downregulate the proportion of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), activate adaptive immunity, and exhibit lower non-tumor targeting toxicity due to their limited circulation time [ ]. LNPs are also commonly used for in vivo macrophage-targeted delivery. Macrophages and monocytes naturally function to clear oxidized lipids and lipoproteins, giving them a high affinity for oxidized lipids. Researchers have designed a class of oxidized ionizable lipids (oLNPs) to leverage this natural affinity for enhanced macrophage targeting. Certain oLNPs, such as C14–O2, demonstrate significant tropism for macrophages and monocytes in vivo [ ]. Altering the lipid composition of LNPs, such as using a formulation of 9322-O16B/Cholesterol/DOPE (16:10:1, w/w), can also achieve efficient mRNA delivery to macrophages [ ]. Additionally, PBAE can be modified with ligands to target receptors on macrophages. For example, dimannose on the surface of nanocarriers can specifically bind to the mannose receptor (CD206) on M2-type macrophages, enabling targeted delivery to M2 macrophages [ ]. The released mRNA is translated into corresponding proteins (e.g., IRF5 and IKKβ) within the cells, which can reprogram macrophages from the pro-tumor M2 phenotype to the anti-tumor M1 phenotype. PPZ-A10 LNPs adsorb specific plasma proteins, allowing them to target HCC-associated macrophages. Studies have found that the protein corona of PPZ-A10 LNPs is enriched with key proteins such as albumin and vitronectin (Vtn), which interact with macrophage receptors to promote LNP uptake [ ]. Conjugating the MΦ-targeting peptide CRV to the surface of LNPs enables in situ transfection of MΦs after intravenous injection, inducing CAR expression [ ] (Fig. C).
Although preclinical studies and Phase I trials have reported that NK cell-based therapies are safer (with minimal side effects and fewer rejection reactions), their clinical efficiency and short cell lifespan raise concerns about their broad clinical adaptability [ ]. Currently, NK cell-targeted CAR mRNA delivery is primarily achieved through in vitro electroporation, and some studies have used CART BDK-O7:N7:A13 oligomers as carriers for in vitro delivery [ ]. To date, there have been no studies on delivering CAR mRNA to in situ NK cells to produce CAR NK cells.

## Application of mRNA-based CAR therapy

## Application scenarios for mRNA-based CAR therapy
To accelerate the clinical translation of mRNA-based CAR therapy, it is crucial to define its appropriate application scenarios. Diseases considered for this approach should ideally meet the following criteria regarding their targets: First, the target should be specifically expressed on the cell surface, with ideal targets exhibiting significantly higher expression levels in pathological tissues/cells compared to healthy tissues (e.g., a > 10-fold difference) [ – ]. The expression levels can typically be assessed via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) [ ], immunohistochemistry (IHC) [ ], and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) [ ]; Second, the selected target antigen should be recognized as a key player in the core pathway of the disease. Examples include CD19 in B-cell survival signaling [ , , ]，and Fibroblast Activation Protein (FAP) in cardiac fibrosis [ , ]; Third, the temporal window of target expression should cover the functional duration of mRNA-encoded CARs. Stable targets like BCMA in multiple myeloma are suitable. For targets with fluctuating expression, such as the intermittent expression of EGFR in glioma [ , ]，or compensatory upregulation of CD22 following CD19 downregulation in hematological malignancies [ , ], adjusting the dosing interval may be necessary.Another critical consideration is whether the disease’s therapeutic requirements align with the inherent characteristics of mRNA-based CAR (MC) therapy. For oncological indications, MC therapy offers a milder and more controllable strategy to address tolerance issues associated with conventional CAR-T therapy. For non-oncological diseases, the transient nature of the condition aligns well with the transient cytotoxicity of MC therapy, holding promise for achieving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing collateral damage. Theoretically, any disease fitting the target and pathological profiles described above could be considered for MC translation. However, due to the limited depth of research for many diseases, only a select number of conditions have currently been explored for MC application, which are introduced below.

## mRNA-based CAR therapy for cancer treatment

## Preclinical studies on mRNA-based CAR therapy for hematologic malignancies
mRNA-based CAR therapy was initially applied in the treatment of hematological malignancies. While BCMA and CD19 remain the most commonly used targets in this field, numerous novel targets have entered preclinical studies (Table ). In this domain, the therapy predominantly employs in vitro electroporation to prepare CAR cells, most of which demonstrate significant therapeutic efficacy. For instance, single mRNA delivery of CAR CD33 [ ], CAR CD117 [ ], or CAR-T cell immunoglobulin domain and mucin domain-3 (TIM-3) [ ] has shown remarkable effects in treating acute myeloid leukemia (AML), while CAR CD5 [ ] has proven effective against acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), and CAR CD19 [ , , ] has demonstrated efficacy in B-cell malignancies in mouse models. To further improve the therapeutic outcomes for hematological tumors, dual-targeted CAR cells using mRNA also holds great promise. For example, co-delivery of CAR mRNA targeting CD20 [ ] or BCMA [ ] alongside CD19 CAR mRNA via electroporation significantly improves the cytotoxicity of CAR-NK cells against B-cell malignancies [ ] and addresses the risk of tumor escape caused by antigen heterogeneity in ALL [ ]. Beyond its therapeutic efficacy, mRNA-based CAR therapy also offers the advantage of low risk for side effects, including CRS and ICANS, in the treatment of hematological malignancies [ , , , , , ]. In conclusion, the existing researches on mRNA-based CAR therapy for hematological tumors provide a robust foundation for its clinical application (Fig. ).
 

## Preclinical studies on mRNA-based CAR therapy for solid tumors
Multiple injections of RNA-engineered T cells represent a novel approach in adoptive cell therapy, offering a flexible platform [ ] for solid tumor treatment and demonstrating significant anti-tumor efficacy in numerous studies [ , , ] (Table ). For localized solid tumors, local injection has been shown to enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR cells compared to intravenous administration. For instance, local injections of mRNA-engineered IL-12 CAR-T cells [ ], IL-12/IL-18 CAR-T cells [ ], CXCR1-overexpressing CAR-NK cells [ ], and multifunctional NKG2D CAR-T cells co-expressing mIL-12 and mIFNα2 [ ] into mouse models of peritoneal carcinoma [ ], melanoma [ ], human SKOV3 ovarian peritoneal carcinoma [ ], and in situ glioma [ ] have all elicited robust tumor rejection. Except local injection, a recent study has renovated sEVs to deliver anti-MSLN CAR mRNA to macrophages in lungs by inhalation, which can reach the target region without liver accumulation, showing great efficacy and safety in murine lung metastatic models of multiple solid tumors (such as melanoma) [ ]. As mentioned earlier, macrophages infiltrate the tumor microenvironment (TME). CAR macrophages (CAR-Ms) have been shown to remodel the TME by increasing the proportion of M2-like macrophages while reducing M1-like polarization [ ]. Furthermore, CAR-Ms secrete more pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α) and inhibit the production of immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-10) [ ]. Through antigen presentation and cytokine recruitment, CAR-Ms activate CD8+T cells and promote their infiltration into the TME [ ]. Simultaneously, CAR-Ms facilitate the differentiation of naïve T cells into memory T cells [ ]. In conclusion, CAR mRNA delivery can transform immunosuppressive TME into immunostimulatory. The high anti-tumor activity observed in these studies underpins prolonged survival benefits, as evidenced by research on diffuse midline glioma treated with mRNA GPC2 CAR-T cells [ ] and recurrent/metastatic Ewing’s sarcoma (ES) treated with anti-MCAM-CAR-NK cells [ ], both of which significantly extended mouse survival. Notably, some peritoneal cancer models reported nearly 100% survival following mRNA CAR therapy [ ]. In addressing immunosuppression, a hepatocellular carcinoma study revealed that mice treated with LNPs carrying GPC3-specific CAR mRNA and CD24-Siglec-G blocker mRNA exhibited a 10-fold increase in macrophage-mediated cancer cell engulfment compared to controls, effectively reducing tumor burden and improving survival [ ]. These promising therapeutic outcomes provide substantial evidence for advancing mRNA-based CAR therapies into clinical trials for solid tumors. Moreover, in various solid tumor mouse models, no significant adverse events were observed following treatment with mRNA-based CAR cells, though further validation in future clinical trials remains necessary (Fig. ).

## Clinical trials of mRNA-based CAR therapy
Currently, clinical trials of mRNA-based CAR therapy primarily focus on cancer treatment, with most mRNA-based CAR-T cell therapy trials in phase 1, while some projects, such as Descartes-08 (NCT06799247), have progressed rapidly and completed phase 3 clinical trial registration. These trials mainly target hematological tumors and solid tumors, with mesothelin (MSLN) being the most common therapeutic target. Other frequently used clinical targets include CD19, NKG2DL, c-MET, BCMA, and CD123. Although novel CAR mRNA delivery platforms have recently entered clinical testing, electroporation remains the dominant method for cell preparation. (Table ). In terms of patient tolerance, existing clinical trials have demonstrated favorable outcomes [ – , , , ]. Although one patient with mesothelioma experienced a serious adverse event (SAE) minutes after a single infusion but recovered rapidly following appropriate management [ ]. In another trial, adverse events (AEs) were transient (2 cases of grade 1 and 1 case of grade 2) and did not require intervention [ ]. While one clinical trial reported a complete clinical recovery in a patient with solid tumors [ ], the therapeutic effects of CAR-T or CAR-NK cells generated via in vitro electroporation and subsequent infusion were generally below expectations. Most patients who did not succumb to the disease were categorized as having stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) [ , , ]. Notably, Myeloid Therapeutics company has conducted a first-in-human mRNA CAR therapy clinical trial using MT-302, an intravenously administered LNP-formulated mRNA designed to reprogram circulating myeloid cells in vivo to express a TROP2-targeting CAR for advanced epithelial tumors [ ]. Using the same strategy, the company has developed MT-303, a GPC3-directed CAR mRNA product for treating hepatocellular carcinoma [ ]. These pioneering attempts showed great efficacy and safety but more validation is needed. Importantly, most clinical trials reported no cases of CRS, ICANS, or OTOT [ , , , ]. In one single trial where CRS was observed, it was limited to grade 1 and resolved with intravenous administration and symptomatic treatment [ ], indicating that current technologies do not induce severe toxic reactions and maintain a high safety profile. Overall, the tolerance and safety results from current clinical trials provide substantial evidence supporting the practical application of mRNA-based CAR therapy. However, further improvements in therapeutic efficacy are necessary to fully realize its potential.
 

## mRNA-based CAR therapy for myocardial fibrosis
During myocardial injury, fibroblast activation drives myocardial fibrosis [ ]. Currently, the primary target of CAR cell therapy for myocardial fibrosis is fibroblast activation protein α (FAP), which is highly expressed in activated cardiac fibroblasts but shows minimal expression in quiescent fibroblasts [ ]. FAP-CAR cell therapy has been shown to eliminate activated fibroblasts in mouse models of heart disease, significantly reducing cardiac fibrosis and improving cardiac function [ – ]. However, a major concern is the indefinite persistence of engineered T cells, similar to the conventional CAR-T cell therapy currently used in clinical oncology [ ]. Since fibroblast activation is essential for normal wound healing in multiple tissues, prolonged anti-fibrotic CAR-T cell activity may pose risks during future injuries [ ]. The CAR-T cells engineered by in vivo delivery of mRNA offers a transient anti-fibrotic CAR therapy solution. CD5, naturally expressed by T cells and a subset of B cells, is not essential for T cell effector function [ , ]. In a study, CD5-targeted LNPs encapsulating FAP-encoding CAR mRNA (CD5/LNP-FAPCAR) were intravenously injected into mice with cardiac injury and fibrosis. After 48 hours, 17.5–24.7% of T cells expressed FAP CAR, with expression observed across all major T cell subsets. By one week, FAP CAR expression was undetectable in splenic T cells, confirming its transient nature. These transient FAP CAR-T cells significantly improved cardiac function without causing notable non-cardiac organ toxicity [ ]. Moreover, recently a study reported that FAP CAR macrophages generated in vivo by LNP within CAR mRNA can effectively alleviate myocardial fibrosis and improve cardiac function following myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury with no toxic reactions observed in the heart or other major organs [ ]. This approach provides a novel strategy to address the limitations of traditional persistent CAR therapies and introduces a conceptual innovation for managing diseases requiring short-term targeting, such as infectious diseases, metabolic disorders, and autoimmune conditions (Fig. ).

## mRNA-based CAR therapy for sepsis
Sepsis, the most severe manifestation of acute infection, is associated with poor prognosis and a high mortality rate [ , ]. Persistent methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infection and subsequent host immune paralysis are primary contributors to sepsis-related deaths [ – ]. Therefore, restoring and revitalizing immune function is crucial for completely eradicating MRSA and ultimately overcoming fatal sepsis. However, existing clinical interventions for sepsis have largely failed to effectively reestablish immune homeostasis, highlighting the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies.
Given the ability of CAR cell therapies to specifically target malignancies and enhance anti-tumor immune responses [ – ], studies have explored their potential in infectious diseases, laying the groundwork for their application in sepsis [ – ]. However, the persistent cytotoxic effects of traditional CAR cells are difficult to reconcile with the risks of CRS and subsequent immune suppression. In contrast, mRNA-based CAR therapies can rapidly clear pathogens and reduce excessive immune activation, demonstrating higher therapeutic potential for sepsis treatment. Based on these considerations, one study utilized CRV/LNP-RNA to co-deliver SasA (Staphylococcus aureus surface protein A)-CAR mRNA and CASP11 siRNA to MΦs in situ, enabling transient in situ reprogramming of MΦs to generate CAR-Ms with enhanced bactericidal efficacy. This approach effectively countered MRSA immune evasion, significantly improving the survival rate of experimental mice (80%) without observed toxicity [ ], highlighting the potential of mRNA-based CAR therapies for treating similar infectious diseases (Fig. ).

## mRNA-based CAR therapy for cellular senescence
Cellular senescence, characterized by irreversible proliferation arrest triggered by various stressors, is a hallmark of chronic inflammatory diseases. Senescent cells secrete a pro-inflammatory and proteolytic mixture known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [ ]. Current clinical trials targeting aging primarily focus on chemical senolytic agents, such as dasatinib and quercetin, which eliminate senescent cells by disrupting their anti-apoptotic environment [ , ]. However, these senolytic drugs carry potential off-target toxicity [ ], necessitating more specific therapeutic approaches. CAR-T cells, with their precisely targeting capabilities, have shown promise in addressing cellular senescence. For instance, uPAR (urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor) CAR-T cells have been demonstrated to eliminate senescent cells in cancer models and reduce fibrosis and liver injury markers in liver disease models. While preliminary analyses indicated limited uPAR expression in vital tissues, high doses of uPAR CAR-T cells induced CRS-like symptoms in mice, including elevated serum cytokines, hypothermia, weight loss, and death [ ]. The transient nature of mRNA-based CAR therapy offers a potential solution to mitigate the on-target/off-tissue adverse events associated with persistent activation of conventional CAR-T cells and CRS [ ]. One study has successfully reduced liver fibrosis and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) in mouse models by delivering circular PL40 mRNA encoding human uPAR to engineer CAR cells, confirming its safety [ ]. Given the broad pathological impact of cellular senescence, mRNA-based CAR therapy holds significant clinical potential for addressing senescence-related inflammatory diseases (Fig. ).

## mRNA-based CAR therapy for autoimmune diseases
CAR-T cell therapy has emerged as a promising approach for treating various autoimmune diseases through two key mechanisms focusing on two different types of cells: pathogenic B cells that drive autoimmunity, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) that exhibit insufficient control over aberrant immune responses [ ].
The first approach involves using CAR-T cells to target B cells, thereby reducing antibody production [ ]. This strategy has demonstrated significant efficacy, favorable tolerance [ , – ], and safety [ , , ] in treating various B cell-related autoimmune diseases. The second approach employs CAR-Treg cells that precisely migrate to inflammatory sites and suppress local immune responses [ ]. CAR-Treg cells have shown effectiveness in preventing type 1 diabetes [ , ] and human skin graft rejection [ , ]. Compared to CAR-T cell therapy, CAR-Treg cells are associated with the lowest risk of CRS [ , ]. However, this therapy carries certain risks. For instance, in the presence of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and the depletion of IL-2 due to Treg cell combination, Treg cells may lose Foxp3 expression [ , ]. Additionally, engineered Treg cells also have the potential to convert into effector T cells (ex-T cells), not only losing their immune regulatory function but also possibly exacerbating immune responses [ – ]. mRNA-based CAR delivery offers a potential solution to mitigate these risks. The transient expression of CAR may prevent the unintended killing of normal cells if CAR-Tregs transform into ex-T cells. Additionally, maintaining a controlled number of CAR-Tregs in the body without repeated infusions reduces the likelihood of IL-2 depletion. Furthermore, co-delivering shIL-6 DNA or mRNA can reduce local inflammatory factor stimulation, preventing the adverse convertion of CAR-Tregs through multiple mechanisms (Fig. ).

## Combination of mRNA delivery alone with CAR therapy
Overexpressing certain proteins in CAR cells using mRNA can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce side effects. For instance, the delivery of mRNA encoding reverse transcriptase, such as TERT, transiently increases telomerase activity in CD19 CAR-T cells, leading to enhanced proliferation and delayed replicative senescence. This results in CAR cells exhibiting stronger anti-tumor activity [ ]. Stat5b*, a mutant form of Stat5b, can be constitutively activated independent of tyrosine phosphorylation and has been shown to improve T cell engraftment and therapeutic outcomes. Delivering Stat5b* mRNA promotes the survival and expansion of T cells in the host while reducing the risk of CRS, highlighting the potential of activating the Stat5b signaling pathway to enhance CAR-T cell engraftment and function [ ] (Fig. A).
 

## Integration of gene editing technologies with CAR therapy
Combining CRISPR technology with mRNA-based CAR therapy represents another innovative approach. One common method of integrating CRISPR with CAR therapy involves delivering ribonucleoprotein complexes (RNPs) composed of Cas protein and sgRNA via electroporation. This method has been used to knock out numerous targets [ ]. In addition to RNPs, Cas9 mRNA can be co-delivered with sgRNA via electroporation. From a manufacturing perspective, mRNA synthesis is relatively mature and cost-effective. Compared to the complex assembly and preparation of RNPs, mRNA can be rapidly and abundantly synthesized using in vitro transcription technologies with easier quality control. This scalability may become particularly advantageous for large-scale clinical applications of gene therapy in the future. Cas9 protein is generated through mRNA translation and then forms a complex with sgRNA to edit target genes within cells. This approach offers a potential solution for comprehensively enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of CAR cells while controlling adverse effects (Fig. B).
Co-delivery of Cas9 mRNA and sgRNA enables CAR-T cells targeted knockout of key immunosuppressive molecules in CAR-T cells, including PD1 [ ], TGFβRII [ ], and PRDM1—a transcriptional regulator promoting T cell exhaustion [ ], enhancing therapeutic efficacy. To control adverse effects, knockout of TCR [ ] or TRAC [ , ] in CAR-T cells can reduce allogeneic reactivity and prevent graft-versus-host disease (GvHD). Studies have shown that CAR-T cells with these knockouts exhibit enhanced anti-tumor activity without significant toxicity [ – ]. However, one research using electroporation to deliver RNPs has demonstrated that knockout of the inhibitory gene CTLA-4 results in higher proliferation capacity, stronger cytotoxicity, and better maintenance of surface CAR expression, potentially due to the release of CD28 co-stimulatory signaling inhibition [ ]. Additionally, knockout of PD-1, another inhibitory gene, did not consistently improve outcomes in a CRISPR single-knockout study [ ]. Combined knockout of the PD1 and TCR genes significantly enhances the anti-tumor activity of CAR cells and prevents GvHD but may compromise persistence [ ]. Moreover, the benefit of PD1 knockout appears affinity-dependent, with low-affinity CAR-T cells deriving greater improvement [ ]. Further research is needed to optimize CRISPR-mediated PD-1 knockout in CAR-T therapy. Beyond targeting inhibitory receptors, CRISPR knockout of other genes, such as CALHM2 (a calcium channel affecting NK cell activation) [ ] and GM-CSF (a cytokine whose excessive secretion contributes to CRS) [ , ], may also enhance the therapeutic efficacy of CAR cells.
In addition to CRISPR-based systems, other nucleases are also widely used to improve the production and functionality of CAR cells. For example, mRNA encoding the engineered nuclease TRC1-2 can be delivered into cells via electroporation and translated to knock out the TRAC gene. Subsequently, the CAR gene can be inserted into the TRAC locus by AAV6 vector transduction, significantly improving the safety of allogeneic CAR-T cells [ ]. In another application, electroporation of megaTAL nuclease mRNA followed by AAV delivery of an HIVspecific CAR template facilitates replacement of the CCR5 gene, simplifying the production of HIV-resistant CAR-T cells [ ]. To address the issue of off-target editing caused by the low fidelity of CRISPR-Cas9, Cas-CLOVER, a high-fidelity, site-specific endonuclease with low off-target activity, was developed. This system has been used to produce allogeneic CAR-T cells enriched with TSCM cells [ ]. Transposases also show promise in CAR cells production. For instance, delivery of SB100X transposase mRNA by electroporation enables translation of the enzyme, which then catalyzes genomic integration of a CAR transgene delivered via engineered AAV vectors containing the corresponding transposon construct, sustaining prolonged and elevated CAR expression [ ]. Collectivetly, these gene-editing technologies demonstrate unique advantages and application prospects in CAR-T cell production, providing versatile and powerful tools to comprehensively improve the efficacy of CAR therapy (Fig. C).

## The application of mRNA vaccine combined with CAR therapy
The efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors is significantly limited by multiple barriers. The dense tumor architecture, composed of stromal cells and the extracellular matrix forms a physical barrier that impedes CAR-T cell infiltration, while the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment constitutes a functional barrier that attenuates CAR-T cell activity and persistence [ , ]. Moreover, CAR-T cell populations decline due to insufficient proliferation signals [ – ], which is compounded by a phenomenon known as antigen transfer via pinocytosis. In this process, CAR-T cells internalize target antigens from tumor cells, reducing antigen density on the tumor surface and triggering fratricidal interactions among CAR-T cells. This leads to their progressive depletion and diminished cytotoxic function [ ]. Additionally, the loss of tumor antigens, heterogeneity of tumor antigen, and phenotypic plasticity in solid tumors collectively contribute to immune evasion and tumor relapse following CAR-T therapy [ , ].
Patient-specific neoantigen-targeting vaccines represent a promising personalized therapeutic approaches to enhance the precision of T cell therapies, strengthen immune responses against unique tumor-specific antigens (TSAs), and counter tumor antigen heterogeneity [ – ]. Notably, in general, the enhancement of CAR-T cell function through vaccine-delivered antigens relies on the TCR molecules on the surface of CAR-T cells to recognize antigens presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) via MHC molecules [ , ]. We speculate that CAR cells with prior TCR knockout intended to avoid graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) may fail to benefit from such indirect activation pathways. Additionally, vaccines can activate dendritic cells (DCs) to present multiple tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), thereby activating endogenous T cells in diverse immunogenic environments to target various tumor antigens. This approach may compensates for the limited target specificity of CAR-T cells and reduces the risk of tumor immune evasion. Furthermore, vaccines can induce a memory-like phenotype in CAR-T cells, sustaining their activity and enabling long-term tumor surveillance to prevent tumor recurrence [ , ]. Beyond traditional antigen delivery, recent studies have demonstrated promising results in in situ reprogramming of tumor tissues via vaccines. Reprogrammed tumor cells can remodel the immunosuppressive TME, activate existing memory effector T cells, and reduce the risk of immune escape [ – ]. For example, tumor cells reprogrammed by viral delivery of transcription factors not only lose their proliferative and tumorigenic potential but also transform into highly efficient and persistent dendritic cells (DCs) that support T cell-mediated tumor killing by presenting antigens. Remarkably, reprogramming as few as 2% of tumor cells has been shown to trigger tumor regression and establish long-term immune memory, preventing tumor recurrence [ , ]. Future studies may explore combining in situ TME reprogramming with the direct tumor-killing capacity of CAR-T cells, potentially yielding synergistic antitumor effects. In conclusion, the synergistic interaction between vaccines and CAR-T cells holds great potential to enhance the adaptability, efficacy, and persistence of CAR-T therapy. This innovative approach opens new avenues for the treatment of solid tumors, offering a transformative strategy to overcome current limitations and improve patient outcomes.
mRNA vaccines offer several advantages: first, they pose a lower risk of genomic integration compared to viral vector vaccines delivering antigen DNA; they exhibit stronger immunogenicity than peptide vaccines, eliminating the need for adjuvants; and they have a simpler production process compared to DC-based vaccines, which require labor-intensive ex vivo cell isolation, assembly, and reinfusion [ ]. Second, mRNA vaccines do not require customized screening, neoantigen identification, or lengthy production pipelines, enabling large-scale manufacturing and rapid deployment. Additionally, they demonstrate high technical compatibility with traditional CAR therapies [ ], making them highly promising for the combination with CAR therapies.
mRNA vaccines encoding TAAs enhance the activation and proliferation of CAR-T cells by stimulating DCs and other APCs to efficiently process and present TAAs [ – ]. By promoting the secretion of key inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ，they also promote CAR-T cell infiltration, functionality, and persistence by activating multiple immune signaling pathways, such as the T cell receptor signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and antigen processing and presentation pathway [ ]. With advancements in nanotechnology and molecular medicine, mRNA vaccine delivery technology has matured. For instance, mRNA vaccines delivered via LNPs have been shown to boost the efficacy of T cells targeting tumor cells [ – ], triggering robust immune responses while minimizing off-target toxicity [ ]. CLDN6, a TAA critical for tumor cell tight junctions, is highly expressed in solid tumors such as testicular germ cell tumors and high-grade ovarian serous adenocarcinoma. mRNA vaccines designed to enhance the expansion and efficacy of CLDN6-targeting CAR-T cells have demonstrated promising results in solid tumor treatment [ , ]. In vitro experiments have demonstrated that CLDN6-LPX can be taken up by DCs and fully expressed on their surface, enabling direct recognition by the CAR molecules of CLDN6 CAR-T cells. This interaction induces the secretion of the cytokine IFN-γ and promotes proliferation, significantly enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of CLDN6 CAR-T cells [ ] (Fig. D). However, whether the full expression of CLDN6 on the DC surface could lead to CAR-T cells directly attacking DCs, potentially causing DC depletion, requires further investigation. mRNA vaccine-mediated antigen expression on the surface of APCs can mimic the activation of CAR-T cells through direct CAR-mediated target recognition, inducing robust activation of CAR-T cells. Further research is needed to validate this strategy for other solid tumor targets in combination with CAR therapies and to identify optimal antigen candidates to broaden the applicability of combined mRNA vaccine and CAR-T regimens.
The use of mRNA vaccines for in situ tumor reprogramming has also garnered growing interest. Unlike mRNA vaccines encoding TAAs, those used for reprogramming do not encode intrinsic tumor antigens but instead enable tumor cells to present a universal antigen “tag”. This strategy has been shown to remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and enhance cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity across multiple tumor models, including melanoma, breast cancer, and colon cancer [ ], demonstrating its potential for tumor reprogramming. As an emerging technology, mRNA vaccines for tumor reprogramming have not yet been validated in combination with CAR therapy. However, we speculate that if this technology can be employed to induce presentation of specific CAR target antigens on solid tumor cells in conjunction with CAR therapy, it could offer a novel strategy to address the challenges of solid tumor treatment (Fig. D).
Currently, the clinical translation of mRNA vaccines combined with CAR therapy remains limited to oncology. The sole ongoing clinical trial (BNT211-01) utilizes the mRNA vaccine developed by Reinhard et al. [ ] to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CAR-T cells alone versus CAR-T cells combined with CARVac in patients with relapsed/refractory (r/r) CLDN6+ solid tumors [ ]. Among 21 evaluable patients, the disease control rate was 67% (14/21). In the higher dose level (DL2) cohort, the objective response rate (ORR) for the combination treatment group (CAR-T + CARVac) was 57% (4/7), compared to 25% (1/4) for patients treated with CAR-T cells alone, suggesting that the combination therapy may yield better efficacy at higher doses. Regarding safety, managed toxicity was observed in 10 of the 22 patients (46%), including CRS, and one patient (5%) experienced grade 1 ICANS.
However, the combination of mRNA vaccines and CAR therapy is not without limitations. Suboptimal treatment protocols may lead to simultaneous adverse events associated with mRNA vaccines (such as local injection site reactions and flu-like symptoms) and CAR-T cell administration (such as CRS and neurotoxicity) [ – ]. Therefore, further clinical researches are essential to refine strategies for CAR cell administration and vaccination timing. To mitigate the adverse effects of CAR-T cell therapy, a coordinated management strategy incorporating glucocorticoids, IL-6 antibodies, and IL-1 receptor antagonists can be employed at appropriate stages throughout the vaccination and treatment course [ ].

## Conclusions and prospects
Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in treating B cell malignancies [ – ]. Currently, the U.S. FDA has approved six CAR-T cell products for conditions such as non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and multiple myeloma, significantly improving the survival rates and quality of life for patients with hematological cancers [ , ]. However, the application of CAR therapy to solid tumors and non-cancer diseases remains challenging. The precision of mRNA technology positions it as a powerful tool to enhance CAR therapy. We primarily summarize two approaches for integrating mRNA technology with CAR therapy: mRNA-based CAR therapy and mRNA-derived therapies combined with CAR therapy.

## Manufacturing of mRNA-based CAR therapy
Using mRNA as the delivery form of CAR genes offers dual advantages: it avoids the risk of gene insertion associated with traditional CAR therapy and provides CAR cells with transient cytotoxic activity, effectively reducing the toxic reactions associated with conventional CAR therapy and expanding its application. The development of delivery vectors and delivery strategies are two critical steps in the preparation of mRNA-based CAR cells. Currently, ex vivo electroporation remains the most widely used approach; however, it often compromises T cell viability, thereby limiting the quality of the final CAR-T product. And since in vivo delivery of CAR mRNA to prepare CAR cells bypasses the cumbersome steps of traditional CAR therapy and substantially lowers treatment costs, recent studies have increasingly focused on in vivo delivery. The success of in vivo mRNA delivery relies heavily on the carriers. Beyond improving targeting efficiency, the design and optimization of carriers can enhance the overall efficacy of in vivo delivery. Studies have confirmed that modified LNPs can transport mRNA across the blood-brain barrier [ , ], illuminating new application scenarios for mRNA-based CAR therapy. One promising application leverages the phagocytic capacity of CAR macrophages (CAR-M); by delivering CAR mRNA across the blood-brain barrier, in situ generation of CAR-M cells could potentially offer a non-invasive therapeutic strategy for Alzheimer’s disease, avoiding the need for invasive cranial infusion [ ]. Nevertheless, the efficiency of in vivo delivery remains generally low. A key bottleneck for widely used carriers such as LNPs is their inefficient endosomal escape. Strategies to overcome this limitation include the rational design of novel ionizable lipids and optimization of helper and PEG-lipid ratios [ ]. In addition, exploring natural carriers such as EVs or VLPs, as well as adopting localized administration routes, may further enhance delivery efficiency to target cells [ ]. Furthermore, scalable production is challenged by the difficulty in ensuring batch-to-batch consistency in key parameters (e.g., size, encapsulation, potency), necessitating continuous processes, modular platforms, and automated closed systems to boost efficiency and cut costs. CAR mRNA is the core component of delivery, and its modification is essential to ensure stability and safety. Circular RNA (circRNA) represents a promising alternative for CAR delivery with enhanced structural stability, yet its research remains far less mature than that of mRNA. The delivery of dual CAR mRNA or co-delivery with other RNAs shows potential in enhancing cytotoxicity and safety. However, potential interactions between co-delivered molecules [ , ] necessitate deeper investigation into their design and distribution. Furthermore, a limitation lies in the payload capacity of delivery vectors. For instance, the most commonly used LNPs have an average mRNA loading of 2.8 mRNA molecules per nanoparticle, with most loaded nanoparticles containing 2 or 3 mRNA molecules [ ]. Currently, there are limited researches on the maximum cargo capacity of various non-viral delivery vectors. While improving delivery vectors could enable larger mRNA payloads, whether this would lead to increased immunogenicity or other side effects requires further study, since there are chances that mRNA molecules activate the innate immune system. Mitigation strategies may include: modifications to the mRNA backbone itself, optimizing in vitro transcription production and purification processes, and the use of innate immune inhibitors. However, it is noteworthy that in current research, most side effects caused by strong immunogenicity are either self-limiting [ , ]; or can be properly managed (e.g., through symptomatic treatment) [ , ]. Compared to the suffering and burden imposed by the diseases, these side effects are considerably minor. Therefore, despite the risk for adverse effects, mRNA-based CAR therapy demonstrates immense potential in treating tumors and other non-oncological diseases requiring transient therapeutic efficacy. In the future, artificial intelligence and machine learning may also be applied to the design of delivery vectors and payloads to address the limitations in clinical translation mentioned above.
Moreover, research on delivery vectors remains at an early stage, and the initiation of clinical trials using biomaterials for CAR mRNA delivery is significantly hindered by the absence of a mature regulatory framework. The novelty and complexity of these therapeutic products mean that a clear regulatory pathway has yet to be established, presenting a critical barrier to their clinical translation.

## The clinical translation of mRNA-based CAR therapy
The clinical translation of mRNA-based CAR therapy is guided by a fundamental principle: matching the duration of CAR expression to the requirements of the target disease. This dichotomy is evident when comparing its application in oncology versus non-oncological fields.
For hematological malignancies and solid tumors, durable CAR activity is typically desired to prevent relapse. While mRNA CAR therapy shows comparable efficacy to traditional methods in hematological cancers, its transient nature can be a limitation in solid tumors due to the immunosuppressive TME and tumor antigen heterogeneitys [ ]. This can addressed through strategies like repeat dosing or local injection to enhance persistence. Moreover, the mRNA platform may offer a unique strategic advantage: the agility to rapidly re-target CARs in response to antigen escape, a process far more efficient than re-generating viral-based CAR-T products.
While cancer therapy often demands the complete eradication of malignant cells, the treatment of non-oncological diseases (e.g., myocardial fibrosis, autoimmune diseases, severe asthma) aims instead to modulate the underlying pathology, seeking to control or reset the dysfunctional biological state [ ]. Although CAR therapy has shown potential in treating myocardial fibrosis, severe asthma, autoimmune diseases, infectious diseases, metabolic disorders, and other conditions [ , , , – ], the uncontrolled cytotoxicity of traditional CAR cells poses risks to long-term patient outcomes. Here, the transient expression of mRNA CAR is a key therapeutic advantage. It provides a controlled, self-limiting intervention that can effectively reduce fibrosis or control infection while significantly mitigating the risks of long-term toxicity (e.g., OTOT, CRS) and excessive disruption of homeostasis [ , , , ].
Thus, the ongoing clinical development of mRNA CAR therapy, including the exploration of different administration methods (intravenous, local, oral, patches), is fundamentally centered on improving its potency in oncology and harnessing its transient expression for safety in chronic diseases.
The clinical translation of mRNA CAR therapy urgently requires the establishment of a standardized system covering production, quality control, and safety assessment. At the production and quality control level, by-products such as double-stranded RNA generated during the in vitro transcription of mRNA are potent activators of the innate immune system, leading to reduced mRNA stability and expression [ ]. Therefore, it is essential to establish standardized purification processes to remove these impurities. At the same time, key physicochemical attributes of the delivery vectors—such as particle size and encapsulation efficiency—as well as their functional potency must be maintained with high consistency across different batches. The lack of unified manufacturing and testing standards can directly lead to inaccurate dosing, variable efficacy, and even unpredictable toxicities in clinical trials, making the results difficult to interpret and reproduce. Regarding safety monitoring, although the transient expression of mRNA reduces long-term risks, acute adverse reactions such as cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity still require close surveillance. To this end, it is necessary to develop standardized toxicity grading and management guidelines. Based on these harmonized safety data, the frequency and dosage of repeated administrations can be scientifically determined, thereby achieving an optimal balance between efficacy and safety.

## mRNA-derived therapies combined with CAR therapy
The therapeutic efficacy of traditional CAR cells in solid tumors has largely been disappointing. The incorporation of mRNA vaccines holds promise for addressing challenges such as tumor antigen escape, poor T cell infiltration, and tumor immune microenvironment (TME) suppression faced by traditional CAR therapies. mRNA vaccines leverage antigen-presenting cells (APCs) for antigen presentation, which can assist in activating CAR cells to enhance therapeutic outcomes. Currently, only one clinical trial [ ] has explored this combination, showing favorable patient outcomes, but further clinical trial results are needed to confirm the benefits of this approach. Beyond antigen presentation, the application of vaccines in tumor reprogramming has also yielded intriguing results. Viral-based vaccines delivering transcription factors can reprogram tumor cells into APCs, enabling surface delivery of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and inducing tumor regression, laying a strong foundation for in situ tumor reprogramming technology. mRNA vaccines delivering universal antigens can induce tumor cells to express antigen tags, triggering robust activation of endogenous T cells [ ]. This raises the possibility of using mRNA to induce tumor cells to express target antigen tags in combination with CAR therapies targeting these antigens, potentially addressing tumor antigen escape and heterogeneity. This innovative approach warrants further exploration.
To further address the issue of tumor immune microenvironment (TME) suppression in CAR therapies, mRNA-based strategies have emerged as promising tools for TME modulation (Fig. E). Among these approaches, mRNA-mediated cell phenotype switching and cytokine profile transformation are the most promising strategies for TME improvement. For instance, delivering TriMix mRNA composed of CD40L, CD70, and the constitutively active form of TLR4 (caTLR4) [ , ] or other T cell-activating mRNA molecules [ ] can shift tumor-associated dendritic cells (TADCs) toward a T cell-activating phenotype. Interferon regulatory factor 5 (IRF5) and IKKβ (a kinase that induces IRF5 phosphorylation and activation) have been identified as ideal master regulators for tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) polarization. Delivering mRNA encoding these molecules can reprogram TAMs into a potent pro-inflammatory phenotype, promoting T cell activation, proliferation, and infiltration [ , , ]. Currently, mRNA delivery of tumor-suppressive cytokines to various cell types has been achieved, inducing cytokine profile transformation [ , – ]. Given the complexity and cost associated with multi-cytokine preparations, using mRNA to express tumor-suppressive proteins such as P53 to indirectly regulate multiple cytokine levels may hold greater potential [ ]. Additionally, delivering mRNA encoding matrix-degrading enzymes (e.g., collagenase) or chemokines (e.g., CXCL9/10) represents another potential strategy to enhance CAR-T cell infiltration into solid tumors. However, these mRNA-based TME-modulating approaches still require further validation in the context of CAR therapies.
Although the integration of mRNA technology with CAR therapies is still in its early stages and faces numerous unresolved challenges, we anticipate that as research into CAR and mRNA technologies advances, their combined application will become an inevitable trend and will play a significant role in the treatment of various diseases.
