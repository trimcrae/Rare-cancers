## ABSTRACT
The content and composition of milk fat are critical determinants influencing milk flavor, nutritional value, and economic significance. Buffalo milk is characterized by its high‐fat content and complex lipid profile, characterized by elevated levels of health‐beneficial fatty acids such as linoleic acid, α‐linolenic acid, and arachidonic acid. However, the molecular regulatory mechanisms governing milk fat synthesis in buffaloes remain incompletely elucidated. This study employed transcriptomic analysis of milk fat globules (MFGs) from buffaloes exhibiting high and low milk fat content, identifying 15 949 annotated genes, including 234 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly associated with cell proliferation and differentiation, glyconeogenesis, and reproductive system development. Notably, the expression of IGFBP4, AGPAT4, GPAT3, GPR84, and PC exhibited positive correlations with buffalo milk fat content, identifying them as potential candidate genes regulating milk fat synthesis. Proteomic profiling identified 1678 proteins, including 53 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Enrichment analysis indicated that DEPs were primarily involved in nucleotide metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and TGF‐β signaling. Integrated analysis revealed potential interactions involving the IGFBP4 and PC genes, as well as the ACO1, TMED7, and APRT proteins, highlighting IGFBP4 as a pivotal regulator of milk fat synthesis. Functional validation demonstrated that overexpression or knockdown of IGFBP4 in buffalo mammary epithelial cells (BMECs) significantly modulated cell proliferation and altered the expression of key milk fat synthesis‐related genes (FABP3, LPL, SCD, ACACA, and FASN), indicating that IGFBP4 can promote de novo fatty acid synthesis and intracellular lipid storage while inhibiting exogenous fatty acid uptake. Collectively, this study provides novel mechanistic insights into the regulation of milk fat synthesis in buffaloes and establishes a foundation for enhancing lactation traits through targeted genetic breeding strategies.
Keywords: buffalo, IGFBP4, milk fat, proteome, RNA‐seq

Milk fat in buffaloes, with beneficial fatty acids, has unclear regulatory mechanisms. This study used transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of high/low milk fat buffaloes, identified (234 DEGs, e.g., IGFBP4) and proteomics (53 DEPs). Functional validation by overexpression or knockdown of IGFBP4 in buffalo mammary epithelial cells (BMECs) validated IGFBPs role in cell proliferation and fatty acid synthesis/storage, offering insights for genetic breeding to improve lactation traits.

## Introduction
Globally, buffaloes rank as the second‐largest milk‐producing species. Buffalo milk is rich in fat, protein, vitamins, and trace elements, while exhibiting relatively low cholesterol, conferring excellent nutritional value. The content and composition of milk fat are critical factors affecting its flavor, nutritional value, and economic significance. The higher concentrations of fat and protein in buffalo milk impart superior coagulability and richer sensory properties to its dairy products during processing. Therefore, milk fat content represents not only a key competitive indicator in the dairy industry but also a primary breeding target [ ]. However, milk fat synthesis is a complex biological process involving multiple genes, proteins, and signaling pathways. Although certain key genes implicated in milk fat synthesis have been characterized, the molecular mechanism specifically regulating buffalo milk fat remains incompletely characterized.
Milk fat is primarily composed of triglycerides, and its synthesis involves multiple critical steps, including fatty acid biosynthesis, triglyceride assembly, milk fat globule (MFG) formation, transport, and secretion. These processes predominantly occur in mammary epithelial cells and the alveolar structure, requiring coordinated enzymatic activity and integrated regulation of signaling pathways. Fatty acids in mammary epithelial cells serve as the foundation for milk fat synthesis, originating from either de novo synthesis within these cells or uptake from the bloodstream [ , ]. The precursor substrates for de novo fatty acid synthesis are acetyl‐CoA and malonyl‐CoA, whose interconversion is catalyzed by acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC)—the rate‐limiting enzyme in fatty acid biosynthesis [ ]. Research indicates that signaling pathways, including MAPK, JAK–STAT, NF‐κB, and Akt/mTOR, are key regulators of milk fat synthesis [ , , ]. Numerous cytokines and hormones regulate lipid metabolism through activation of the JAK–STAT pathway [ , ]. Activated STATs subsequently modulate lipid metabolism directly by regulating the expression of enzymes such as AOX and FAS [ , ]. In ovine mammary tissue, JAK2 is predominantly expressed, and JAK2‐1 induces the expression of FASN, SCD, PPARG, and LPL, concomitantly increasing triglyceride content [ ]. Although primarily associated with immune responses and inflammation, the NF‐κB pathway also governs mammary epithelial cell proliferation during glandular development through regulation of COX‐2 gene expression [ , ]. The Akt/mTOR pathway promotes fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis, along with MFG formation and secretion. mTORC1 activates the transcription factor SREBP1 (sterol regulatory element‐binding protein 1), facilitating its nuclear translocation to enhance genes involved in fatty acid synthesis. It also elevates DGAT1 and DGAT2 expression via SREBP1, promoting triglyceride accumulation [ ]. Furthermore, Akt/mTOR signaling enhances the activity of key enzymes such as FASN and ACC that are crucial for fatty acid synthesis. Valine stimulates AKT/mTOR signaling to augment milk fat synthesis in porcine mammary glands, while activated Akt promotes lipid synthesis in mammary tissues during pregnancy and lactation [ , ]. Additionally, Akt/mTOR signaling may regulate cytoskeletal reorganization to maintain cellular architecture and secretory capacity, thereby ensuring proper milk fat secretion [ ].
The insulin‐like growth factor‐binding protein (IGFBP) family critically regulates the bioactivity of insulin‐like growth factors (IGFs) through both IGF‐dependent and ‐independent mechanisms, participating in cellular proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and apoptosis [ ]. Current research on IGFBPs predominantly focuses on animal growth, metabolic regulation, and mammary pathologies, with limited investigation into their roles in lactation traits. For instance, IGFBP3 regulates muscle satellite cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation, promoting myotube formation and muscle repair [ , ], while also modulating adipocyte metabolism by influencing lipid synthesis and storage pathways [ ]. IGFBP6 modulates lipid homeostasis through the regulation of lipogenic enzyme activity and fatty acid transport [ ]. During early mammary development, IGFBP2 interacts with IGFs to promote ductal epithelial cell proliferation and migration, thereby guiding proper ductal branching and morphogenesis [ ]. Furthermore, IGFBP2 stimulates mitotic activity in invasive mammary carcinoma cells [ ]. IGFBP3 maintains controlled mammary cell proliferation by limiting IGF‐1 activity, ensuring orderly ductal development [ ]. Notably, IGFBP5 significantly impacts milk fat synthesis by interacting with transcription factors to regulate key genes involved in fatty acid synthesis and triglyceride assembly [ ]. It also modulates the mammary microenvironment through extracellular matrix interactions, influencing lipid metabolism via PI3K/Akt signaling to regulate lipogenic enzyme activity and lipid transport [ , ].
Here, we aimed to systematically screen the candidate molecules influencing buffalo milk fat synthesis through integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. Comparative profiling of milk from high‐ versus low‐milk‐fat buffaloes revealed distinct transcriptional and proteomic signatures, with preliminary characterization of IGFBP4‐mediated regulation of genes associated with milk fat synthesis in mammary epithelial cells. These findings may advance the understanding of molecular mechanisms underlying buffalo milk fat synthesis, enhance functional genomic annotations, and provide valuable molecular references for strategic molecular breeding to improve buffalo lactation performance.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Experimental Animals and Sampling
Milk samples were collected from crossbred buffaloes (Murrah × local swamp buffalo, n = 6) on the Buffalo Breeding Farm of the Buffalo Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Nanning, Guangxi, China). All animals were managed under identical feeding and housing conditions. The sampling protocol was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Review Committee of Guangxi University, Nanning, China (Approval number: GXU‐2022‐192). Milk composition (protein, fat, lactose, and total solids) was analyzed using an infrared milk analyzer (MilkoScan FT‐120, FOSS Electric A/S, Hillerod, Denmark). Composition data from approximately 500 lactating buffaloes were tracked over two consecutive lactations. Based on this longitudinal dataset, the top three buffaloes exhibiting consistently high milk fat content (HF group; 12.09% ± 1.03%) or low milk fat content (LF group; 3.27% ± 0.92%) were selected. Selected buffaloes were healthy, in their second or third parity, aged 5–8 years, and in mid‐lactation (100–200 days postpartum). Milk samples were collected manually into the centrifuge tubes (Vazyme, Shanghai, China) and were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The milk samples were transported to the laboratory at 4°C and stored at −80°C until further use.

2.2.

## RNA Extraction and Quality Control
RNA was extracted from MFGs for RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) and gene expression analysis, referring to a previous report [ ]. Briefly, milk samples were centrifuged at 7000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to isolate the fat layer. The collected fat layer (500 μL) was homogenized in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and centrifuged. After lipid removal from the top layer, the aqueous phase was separated using chloroform (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). RNA was precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol (Fuyu Fine Chemical Co. Ltd., Tianjin, China), and dissolved in nuclease‐free, DEPC‐treated water (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). RNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop ND‐2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Only RNA samples exhibiting an A260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 and distinct ribosomal bands with no detectable degradation on the gel were selected for library construction.

2.3.

## RNA‐Seq Analysis
cDNA library preparation and sequenced on an Illumina platform by referring to a previous study [ ]. A fragment analyzer was used to determine RNA quality, and SOAPnuke (v1.5.6) was used to filter raw data. Using the HISAT2 software (v2.2.1), clean data were mapped to the buffalo reference genome [ ]. Gene expression was measured using RSEM software. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high‐fat (HF) and low‐fat (LF) milk groups were identified using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) with significance thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log2 (fold change)| > 1.5. Venn diagrams comparing milk fat groups were generated using an online tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). Heatmaps and volcano plots were created with the R packages heatmap (v1.0.12) and ggplot2 (v3.3.6), respectively. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using ClusterProfiler (v4.0.5) with Gene Ontology (GO) terms (http://www.geneontology.org) and KEGG pathway annotations (http://www.kegg.jp). Terms with an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

2.4.

## Protein Extraction and LC–MS/MS Data Collection
The total protein of the buffalo milk was extracted using the SDT lysis buffer (4% (w/v) SDS, 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.6), 0.1 M DTT). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit. To maximize proteome coverage, proteins were digested using the filter‐aided sample preparation (FASP) method, followed by peptide fractionation with reversed‐phase high performance liquid chromatography (RP‐HPLC). In brief, samples were denatured at 95°C for 5 min and cooled to room temperature. After adding 200 μL of 8 M urea, samples were transferred to 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) ultrafiltration devices and centrifuged (25°C, 14 000 × g, 30 min). This step was repeated once, with flow‐through discarded. Proteins were alkylated with 100 μL of 100 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) in darkness at RT for 30 min. Subsequent washes were performed twice with 100 μL 8 M urea (14 000 × g, RT, 30 min) and twice with 100 μL 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) buffer (14 000 × g, RT, 30 min). Proteins were digested overnight at 37°C with trypsin (enzyme: substrate ratio 1:50). Peptides were fractionated using a Pierce High pH Reversed‐Phase Peptide Fractionation Kit. Spin columns were equilibrated with 0.1% triethylamine (TEA) acetonitrile. Samples were loaded and eluted with a stepwise acetonitrile gradient (5%, 10%, 12.5%, 15%, 17.5%, 20%, 22.5%, and 25%). Fractions were lyophilized and reconstituted in 40 μL of 0.1% formic acid (FA). Peptide concentration was measured by A280 absorbance. Analysis was performed using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker). Peptides were separated on a C18 column (75 μm × 250 mm, 1.6 μm particles; IonOpticks Aurora Series) at 300 nL/min with a nonlinear gradient from 2% to 40% buffer B (0.1% FA in 60% acetonitrile) over 120 min. MS data were acquired in positive ion mode (1.5 kV capillary voltage) with m/z 100–1700 scanning range using PASEF mode (10 MS/MS scans per cycle, 1.17 s cycle time). Dynamic exclusion was set to 24 s. Raw files were analyzed using MaxQuant (v1.6.14) against the 
Bubalus bubalis
 UniProt database with standard settings (1% FDR, trypsin specificity, variable oxidation, and fixed carbamidomethylation).

2.5.

## Protein Identification, Quantification, and Functional Analysis
The obtained peptide raw data were normalized, and significantly DEPs were screened based on Fold Change (FC) and p‐value. The screening criteria for DEP are |FC > 1.5| or |FC < 0.67|, and p < 0.05. CELLO (v2.4) software was used to evaluate the subcellular localization of the protein, and KEGG pathway enrichment and GO functional annotation were performed based on the KAAS online platform and GO database. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant during the GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. Protein domain analysis was performed using the Pfam database using the InterProScan software package (v‐5.25–64.0). Protein–protein interaction networks were constructed using the STRING database (v11.5, minimum interaction score: 0.400) and visualized by the Cytoscape software (v3.9.1).

2.6.

## Interaction Network Analysis Between DEGs and DEPs
Protein–protein interactions between DEGs and DEPs were analyzed using STRING v12.1 (https://string‐db.org) with 
Bos taurus
 as the reference species and a medium confidence score (0.400). Co‐enrichment pathways were identified through KEGG analysis to evaluate the functional convergence of DEGs and DEPs. Interaction networks were visualized in Cytoscape v3.9.1.

2.7.

## IGFBP Gene Family Characterization
Nucleotide and amino acid sequences of buffalo IGFBP genes were retrieved from NCBI (Table  ). Phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA v11.0. Conserved motifs were identified with MEME Suite v5.5.8, and gene structures were annotated using GSDS 2.0 (https://gsds.gao‐lab.org). Domain architecture was integrated using TBtools v2.056.

2.8.

## Sequence Characteristics of Buffalo IGFBP4 Protein
Physicochemical properties of buffalo IGFBP4 were predicted using ExPASy ProtParam. Secondary structure was modeled with SOPMA (https://npsa‐prabi.ibcp.fr), and tertiary structure was predicted using Phyre2 v2.2 in intensive mode. Subcellular localization was determined with WoLF PSORT. Hydrophobicity profiles were generated using ProtScale; signal peptides were predicted with SignalP 6.0, and conserved domains were annotated via NCBI CD‐Search.

2.9.

## Isolation, Culture, and Purification of Buffalo Mammary Epithelial Cells
The buffalo mammary epithelial cells (BMECs) were cultured and purified referring to a previous study [ ]. In brief, buffalo mammary gland tissue was collected from a local butchery, and the acinus portion was extracted. High‐resistance PBS (containing 400 IU mL−1 penicillin and streptomycin) was used to wash the tissue and transport it to the laboratory. The tissue pieces were then cut into small pieces (1–2 mm) and cultured in an incubator (38.5°C). DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin was used for cell culture. The BMECs were purified through differential trypsinization, and BMECs at 3–4 generations were used in the following studies.

2.10.

## siRNA Synthesis and Transfection
The IGFBP4‐targeting siRNA conjugated with FAM green fluorescent dye with a control sequence was commercially synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) (Table  ). The siRNAs were transfected into BMECs using an RNA transfection reagent (SANGON, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The fluorescence was detected 24 h after transfection, and cells were harvested 72 h after transfection for q‐RT‐PCR detection. A random siRNA sequence was transfected into the BMECs as the negative control.

2.11.

## Buffalo IGFBP4 Overexpression Vector Construction and Transfection

IGFBP4 was cloned from BMECs according to the GenBank sequences and inserted into the psfGFP plasmid vector and defined as the psfGFP‐IGFBP4 expression vector (Table  ). The psfGFP‐IGFBP4 vector was transfected into BMECs using an electro‐transfection method. Approximately 5 × 106 BMECs were electroporated in 200 μL electroporation buffer containing 10 μg psfGFP‐IGFBP4 using a Bio‐Rad GENEPUISERXCE II Type Electroporator in 2 mm gap cuvettes under 190 V for 15 ms. The fluorescence was detected 24 h after transfection; cells were harvested 72 h after transfection for q‐RT‐PCR detection. The blank psfGFP vector was used as the negative control.

2.12.

## Single‐Strand cDNA Synthesis and qRT‐PCR Analysis
The BMECs were collected and total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. Genomic DNA was eliminated using DNase I, and the first‐strand cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Synthesized cDNA was stored at −20°C until further use. The primers used in this study were designed using Oligo 7.0 software (Table  ) and synthesized by GenSys Biotech (Nanning, China). Using the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Shanghai, China), qRT‐PCR was performed following the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence data were acquired using the fluorescence ratio PCR instrument (Roche, Shanghai, China). More than three biological and technical replicates were maintained. The relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, and GAPDH served as the reference gene.

2.13.

## Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Assay
The effects of IGFBP4 overexpression and interference on the proliferation of BMECs were determined by CCK‐8 assay (Tiandz, Beijing, China). BMECs were obtained at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h after transfection, and 6000 cells/well were inoculated into 96‐well plates. Each well was added with 10% CCK‐8 solution. After incubation at 37°C for 4 h, the absorbance (OD) at 450 nm wavelength of each well was measured by a versatile microplate reader (Infinite M200PRO TECAN, Tecan, Switzerland).

2.14.

## Statistical Analysis
All experiments or detections were repeated more than three times. Statistical analysis was performed using Student's t‐test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) with DUNCAN's multiple range test (DMR) using SPSS 29.0 software (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). The data are shown as mean ± SEM, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
3.1.

## RNA‐Seq Analysis of High Milk Fat and Low Milk Fat Samples
Using RNA‐seq, we identified 15 949 genes in the data, including 444 and 966 genes that were specifically expressed in the HF and LF samples, respectively (Figure  ). Comparative analysis revealed 234 significantly DEGs were identified between the two groups, comprising 62 downregulated and 172 upregulated DEGs in the HF group (Figure  ). Further GO analysis showed that the DEGs were mainly enriched in biological processes including cytokine‐mediated signaling pathway and regulation of cell proliferation, as well as molecular functions such as cytokine receptor activity and cytokine binding (Figure  ). KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of DEGs in pathways related to lipid synthesis and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction (Figure  ). Notably, among the genes enriched in the fat synthesis pathway, the expression of AGPAT4, GPAT3, GPR84, IGFBP4, and PC were found significantly upregulated in HF samples (Figure  ), suggesting their potential regulatory roles in milk fat synthesis in buffalo.
 
3.2.

## Proteome Analysis of High Milk Fat and Low Milk Fat Samples
Mass error analysis revealed high‐quality peptide identification, with the majority of ion mass deviations distributed within 10 ppm (Figure  ). MS/MS spectra identification confidence was high, as reflected by Andromeda scores exceeding 60 for 78.52% of peptides, with a median score of 87.94 (Figure  ). Log2‐transformed protein abundance ratios between groups were predominantly distributed between −2.2 and 2.2 (Figure  ). We identified 1678 proteins across milk samples, with 1540 detected in both HF and LF groups, while 34 were unique to HF and 81 to LF (Figure  ). Comparative analysis identified 53 significantly differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), comprising 35 downregulated and 18 upregulated in the HF group (Figure  ).
 
3.3.

## Functional Enrichment Analysis of DEPs and DEGs–DEPs Interaction Network Analysis
Subcellular localization analysis showed that most of the proteins were localized to the extracellular space and cytoplasm (Figure  ). Domain analysis demonstrated that a significant proportion of DEPs contained Aconitase family (aconitate hydratase), Lipocalin/cytosolic fatty acid binding protein family, Aconitase C‐terminal domain, and TBC (Tre2/Bub2/Cdc16) domains (Figure  ). GO analysis indicated that in biological processes (BP), DEPs were predominantly associated with the enzyme‐linked receptor protein signaling pathway, Golgi organization, nucleotide salvage, regulation of peptide hormone secretion, response to vitamin, activin receptor signaling pathway, and positive regulation of dephosphorylation. For cellular components (CC), DEPs were mainly enriched in three membrane component‐related terms and COPII‐coated vesicles, which facilitate transport from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus. Among molecular functions (MF), significantly enriched terms included transferase activity, growth factor activity, metal cluster binding, aconitate hydratase activity, and purine phosphoribosyltransferase activity—functions critical for enzymatic processes in lipid synthesis (Figure  ). KEGG analysis revealed DEPs enrichment in nucleotide metabolism, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and TGF‐β signaling pathway (Figure  ). We identified six key candidate proteins significantly upregulated in HF samples and enriched in lipid metabolism pathways: Cytoplasmic aconitate hydratase (ACO1), adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT), follistatin (FST), phosphatidylcholine–sterol acyltransferase (PLA2G15), butyrophilin subfamily 1 member A1 (BTN1A1), and transmembrane p24 trafficking protein 7 (TMED7) (Figure  ), suggesting their potential roles in milk fat synthesis.
 Protein–protein interaction network analysis revealed extensive interconnectivity between DEGs and DEPs (Figure  ). Notably, strong correlations were observed between GPAT3 and AGPAT4 mRNA, as well as between ACO1/TMED7 proteins and PC mRNA, indicating potential posttranscriptional regulation of milk fat synthesis. Furthermore, IGFBP4 mRNA showed significant correlations with PC mRNA and ACO1, TMED7, and APRT proteins, positioning IGFBP4 as a central regulatory hub that coordinates milk fat synthesis through dynamic modulation of IGF signaling, integration of precursor metabolism, regulation of energy supply, and optimization of secretory efficiency. Based on these findings, we subsequently characterized the sequence features and functional role of AGPAT4 in BMECs.

3.4.

## Sequence Characteristics of Buffalo IGFBP Gene Family
Bioinformatic analysis identified 12 conserved motifs within buffalo IGFBP family proteins (Figure  ), with IB and thyroglobulin type‐1 domains exhibiting the highest conservation (Figure  ). IGFBP1‐6 shared similar domain architectures, featuring an N‐terminal IB domain and a thyroglobulin‐1 domain in the C‐terminal in all proteins (Figure  ). In contrast, IGFBP7, a distinct member of the IGFBP superfamily, displayed divergent domain organization. Specifically, buffalo IGFBP4 contained only 3 motifs while retaining an N‐terminal IB domain and a thyroglobulin type‐1 domain in the C‐terminal. The IGFBP4 protein comprises 258 amino acids (C1184H1914N364O363S25) with a predicted molecular weight of 27.86 kDa, theoretical isoelectric point of 7.10, mammalian reticulocyte half‐life of ~30 h, and instability index of 46.03. Secondary and tertiary structure predictions indicated a composition dominated by random coils and α‐helices (Figure  ). Subcellular localization predicted extracellular secretion, supported by a high‐probability (99.97%) type I signal peptide cleavage site between residues 21–22 (Figure  ). Hydropathy analysis further revealed predominant hydrophilicity (Figure  ).
 
3.5.

## Effect of IGFBP4 Interference on BMECs
Transfection of siRNAs targeting buffalo IGFBP4 into BMECs resulted in green fluorescence 24 h posttransfection (Figure  ). q‐RTPCR analysis confirmed significant knockdown of IGFBP4 expression (Figure  ). Concomitantly, IGFBP4 knockdown significantly reduced transcript levels of CD36, FABP3, LPL, SCD, PLIN2, XDH, ACACA, FASN, ACSL1, ACSS2, and VLDLR—lipogenic genes governing milk fat synthesis and transport (Figure  ). CCK‐8 assays demonstrated significant inhibition of BMECs proliferation following IGFBP4 knockdown (Figure  ). These findings indicate that IGFBP4 depletion suppresses milk fat synthesis via transcriptional repression of key metabolic genes and impairs BMEC proliferation.
 
3.6.

## Effect of IGFBP4 Overexpression on BMECs
The IGFBP4 coding sequence was cloned from BMECs (Figure  ) and inserted into the BamHI restriction sites of the psfGFP expression vector (Figure  ). Vector integrity was verified by sequencing and restriction digestion (Figure  ). Following transfection, BMECs exhibited green fluorescence at 24 h (Figure  ). qRT‐PCR confirmed successful IGFBP4 overexpression, validating vector functionality (Figure  ). Upon IGFBP4 overexpression, transcript levels of FABP3, LPL, and SCD were downregulated, whereas CD36, PLIN2, XDH, ACACA, FASN, ACSL1, ACSS2, and VLDLR were upregulated (Figure  ). Furthermore, IGFBP4 overexpression significantly enhanced BMEC proliferation (Figure  ). These results demonstrate that IGFBP4 overexpression promotes de novo fatty acid synthesis and intracellular lipid storage, while suppressing exogenous fatty acid uptake and desaturation.

## Discussion
Milk and its derivatives constitute indispensable dietary components. Milk fat provides mammals with energy, fat‐soluble nutrients, and bioactive lipids, while serving as a critical determinant of butter and yogurt quality, conferring significant nutritional and economic value. Its synthesis occurs via lipid droplet biogenesis and secretion in mammary epithelial cells. Milk fatty acids originate from either de novo synthesis within mammary epithelial cells or uptake from circulation [ ]. These fatty acids undergo enzymatic esterification into triacylglycerols in the endoplasmic reticulum prior to secretion as MFGs. This process is hormonally regulated (e.g., insulin and prolactin), and involves energy metabolism, transcriptional activation, and noncoding RNA‐mediated epigenetic regulation [ ]. Consequently, gene regulation underpins dairy lactation performance. Elucidating these molecular mechanisms is critical for optimizing dairy production efficiency.
Milk fat synthesis comprises three stages: (1) fatty acid acquisition (de novo synthesis or uptake), (2) triacylglycerol assembly and transport, and (3) MFG formation and secretion. These stages are precisely orchestrated by complex genomic sequences. Recent studies have identified key genes and biomarkers for milk fat metabolism [ , , ]. For example, comparative RNA‐seq of mammary tissues from cows with divergent milk fat yields revealed critical genes and noncoding RNAs (e.g., FABP3, bta‐miR‐224) involved in milk fat synthesis [ , , ]. Integrated ChIP‐seq and RNA‐seq in goat mammary epithelial cells uncovered promoter binding sites for milk fat regulators (PROX1, PPARGC1A) [ ]. Multi‐omics approaches have further identified potential milk fat biomarkers, including functional miRNAs, metabolic intermediates, and epigenetic marks [ ]. Here, comparative gene expression profiling of high‐ versus low‐fat buffalo milk identified 234 DEGs. These DEGs are implicated in transcriptional regulation (PABPC1L, MAFF), oxidative phosphorylation (PTPN12), ubiquitination (UBE2L6), methylation (ETFBKMT), ribosomal function (NR4A3), lipid metabolism (AGPAT4, GPAT3, GPR84, and IGFBP4), glyconeogenesis (PC), cell cycle regulation (HIRIP3), and vesicle trafficking (TRAPPC9). They likely coordinate milk fat synthesis via posttranscriptional control, epigenetic modification, and energy homeostasis. Enrichment analysis further associated GPAT3 with Jak–STAT signaling, AGPAT4 with NF‐κB signaling, and IGFBP4, GPR84, and PC with cell proliferation, cytokine binding, and glyconeogenesis. Consistent with established roles, JAK–STAT activation by cytokines/hormones modulates lipid metabolism [ , ], whereas NF‐κB regulates mammary cell proliferation via COX‐2 [ ]. Notably, IGFBP4 can function independently of IGFs [ ], e.g., inhibiting sphingosine‐induced apoptosis [ ], suggesting it may promote proliferation and inhibit apoptosis in an IGF‐independent manner, aligning with our findings. Thus, IGFBP4 potentially facilitates mammary development pre‐lactation by stimulating BMEC proliferation.
Proteomic profiling elucidates protein dynamics through quantifying expression, abundance, posttranslational modifications, and structural variation [ ]. Prior studies quantified 1583 proteins in bovine milk using TMT [ ] and 165 in caprine/bovine whey via iTRAQ [ ]. Buffalo milk proteomics has focused on MFGM and whey, identifying 211 whey proteins (clusterin as a marker) [ ] and 520 MFGM proteins [ ]. Comparative analysis across buffalo breeds detected 580 whey proteins [ ], with higher content in Mediterranean versus Murrah/Nili‐Ravi breeds [ ]. MFGM analysis identified 1258 proteins, including 781 novel proteins with antioxidant/antimicrobial potential [ ]. Our proteomic analysis of high/low‐fat buffalo milk identified 1678 proteins, including 53 DEPs enriched in core metabolic and regulatory pathways for milk fat synthesis. Functional network integration categorized these into three modules: (1) Precursor supply and energy metabolism (ACO1, APRT, and LCAT), ensuring acetyl‐CoA, ATP, and phospholipid availability; (2) Signal transduction and secretion regulation (FST, PC, TMED7, and BTN1A1); (3) Transmembrane transport and homeostasis. APRT maintains energy charge via purine salvage. FST and PC may modulate triacylglycerol synthesis via AKT–mTOR signaling [ , ]. TMED7 and BTN1A1 directly facilitate lipid droplet biogenesis and secretion [ , ]. FST and GPR84 influence adipose browning and turnover [ , , ].
Further integrated analysis of DEGs and DEPs (Figure  ) revealed that: Cytoplasmic aconitate hydratase (encoded by ACO1) enriched in the TCA cycle; Adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (encoded by APRT) enriched in purine metabolism and nucleotide salvage; Pyruvate carboxylase (encoded by PC) enriched in glyconeogenesis. Notably, ACO1, APRT, and PC participate in de novo fatty acid synthesis [ , ]. GPAT3 encodes a lysophosphatidic acid acyltransferase, promoting the lysophosphatidic acid synthesis [ ]. AGPAT4 encodes a 1‐acylglycerol‐3‐phosphate O‐acyltransferase catalyzing the conversion of lysophosphatidic acid to phosphatidic acid [ ]. GPAT3 and AGPAT4 were enriched in Jak–STAT and NF‐κB pathways, respectively, suggesting enhanced transcriptional regulation during triacylglycerol biosynthesis. Follistatin (encoded by FST) was enriched in activin‐related processes. PC catalyzes pyruvate carboxylation to oxaloacetate. Thus, FST and PC may regulate milk fat synthesis via AKT–mTOR signaling [ ]. MFGs are encapsulated by polar lipid membranes. BTN1A1 and TMED7 were enriched in membrane‐related processes, facilitating lipid droplet formation/secretion [ , ]. FST and GPR84 promote adipose lipolysis [ , ]. Additionally, we found that IGFBP4 was correlated with the abundances of multiple proteins and genes such as ACO1, TMED7, and APRT. In summary, via transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, we identified a series of candidate molecules influence buffalo milk fat synthesis, which can regulate buffalo milk fat synthesis by affecting biological processes such as de novo synthesis and uptake of milk fat (ACO1, APRT, PC, IGFBP4, and LCAT), synthesis and transport of milk fat (GPAT3, AGPAT4, PC, FST, BTN1A1, and TMED7), and lipid metabolic pathways (FST, GPR84). Among these, IGFBP4 exhibits robust interactions with multiple proteins and genes, potentially serving as a key hub in regulating milk fat synthesis.
 Given IGFBP's potential role, we characterized the IGFBP family structure and IGFBP4 function. IGFBP1‐6 share conserved N‐terminal, variable mid‐, and C‐terminal domains, binding IGF‐1/2 with high affinity to regulate cellular growth and metabolism. IGFBPs modulate intracellular processes via cytoplasmic interactions [ ] and nuclear translocation, affecting transcription. Thyroglobulin type‐1 domains (present in IGFBP1‐6) represent putative functional cores. Thyroid hormones regulate metabolism, influencing adipocyte dynamics. Buffalo thyroglobulin promoter polymorphisms associate with milk fat percentage, and THRSP modulates de novo synthesis and desaturation [ , ]. These findings indicate that IGFBPs are important regulators in milk fat synthesis, which is consistent with our study. In this study, we found that IGFBP4 knockdown in BMECs downregulated lipogenic genes, whereas overexpression enhanced proliferation and upregulated de novo synthesis genes (ACACA, FASN, and ACSL1) while downregulating exogenous uptake (FABP3, LPL) and desaturation (SCD) genes. SCD downregulation negatively correlates with de novo synthesis [ ]. PLIN2 upregulation stabilizes lipid droplets, and XDH upregulation may mitigate oxidative stress during high lipid production [ ]. Thus, IGFBP4 coordinates fatty acid synthesis and storage, identifying it as a key regulator.

5.

## Conclusion
In this study, integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed the molecular regulation of milk fat synthesis in buffalo mammary tissue, identifying IGFBP4 as a key regulator. Genes including IGFBP4, GPAT3, AGPAT4, GPR84, PC, APRT, ACO1, and FST modulate milk fat synthesis via mammary epithelial cell proliferation/differentiation, cytokine binding, and glyconeogenesis. Specifically, IGFBP4 enhances de novo fatty acid synthesis and storage genes while suppressing exogenous uptake genes. This work advances understanding of buffalo milk fat regulation, enriches genomic annotation, and supports molecular breeding of lactation traits.
