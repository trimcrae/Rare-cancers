While coding polymorphisms linked to drug efficacy and adverse reactions are well characterized, the contribution of noncoding regulatory elements remains underexplored. Using CAGE (Cap Analysis of Gene Expression), profiling transcription initiations of mRNAs and enhancer RNAs, we identify candidate cis-regulatory elements (CREs) and assessed their activities simultaneously in HepG2 cells expressing the drug-responsive transcription factor pregnane X receptor (PXR). Comparison with GWAS data reveals strong enrichment of the drug-induced CREs near variants associated with bilirubin and vitamin D levels. Among those bound by PXR in primary hepatocytes, we identify enhancers of UGT1A1, TSKU, and CYP24A1 and functional alleles that alter regulatory activities. We also find that TSKU influences expression of vitamin D-metabolizing enzymes. This study expands the landscape of PXR-mediated regulatory elements and uncovers noncoding variants impacting drug response, providing insights into the genomic basis of adverse drug reactions.

Here the authors map drug-induced cis-regulatory elements by quantitative profiling of messenger and enhancer RNAs with CAGE. Enhancers of UGT1A1, TSKU, and CYP24A1, along with alleles affecting their regulatory activities, are revealed, shedding light on the genomic basis of adverse drug reactions.

## Introduction
Variations in the human genome contribute to the phenotypic differences among individuals. Understanding their impact on drug efficacy and adverse drug reactions (ADRs) is of importance in drug development and administration. Extensive studies in the field of pharmacogenomics have been conducted over decades, leading to the discovery of causal variants that modulate drug responses. For instance, drug-metabolizing enzymes encoded by cytochrome P450 family 2 subfamily C member 9 (CYP2C9) and member19 (CYP2C19) harbor missense polymorphisms (CYP2C9*2, CYP2C9*3, CYP2C19*2, and CYP2C19*3) leading to reduced enzymatic activity – . Most of the genetic variants with established causal relationships to altered drug responses are located in the protein-coding regions, particularly in genes for which the contributions to drug-associated phenotypes are well studied.
Meanwhile, over 90% of the trait-associated genetic variations identified in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are located in noncoding regions , . These variants are shown to be significantly enriched in cis-regulatory elements (CREs), such as promoters and enhancers, which control gene expression either in the proximity of the target genes or from distant locations , – . A well-documented noncoding variant associated with a drug response is the polymorphism at the promoter region of uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferase 1A (UGT1A1), known as UGT1A1*28. The allele causes a decrease of UGT1A1 expression level , consequently leading to an elevated risk of neutropenia in treatment of the anticancer drug irinotecan . Assessment of this polymorphism is now recommended for clinical treatment of patients . This example underscores the importance of comprehending CREs in the noncoding regions of the genome.
The nuclear receptor pregnane X receptor (PXR), or NR1I2, is a drug-activated transcription factor primarily expressed in the liver and the intestine. PXR has a flexible ligand-binding pocket that allows it to be activated by a wide range of prescription drugs, including rifampicin (an antimicrobial drug), dexamethasone (an anti-inflammatory corticosteroid), phenobarbital (a sedative and anti-seizure drug for epilepsy), and tamoxifen (a selective estrogen receptor modulator to prevent breast cancer). Vitamin D deficiency is a well-documented adverse effect of rifampicin and the other PXR activators described above – , while its molecular basis remains to be understood. Since PXR functions as a nuclear receptor regulating the expression of downstream genes, the shared adverse effect may result from unexpected alterations in gene expression triggered by its activation. Upon activation by the drugs, PXR forms a heterodimer with retinoid X receptor α (or NR2B1) and binds to a specific set of CREs throughout the genome, leading to the transcription of genes that encode drug-metabolizing enzymes and drug transporters – . The well-characterized targets of PXR are xenobiotic response enhancer module (XREM) for cytochrome P450 family 3 subfamily A member 4 (CYP3A4) and phenobarbital-responsive enhancer module (PBREM) for UGT1A1 . A minor allele in PBREM (UGT1A1*60 of rs4124874) decreases transcriptional activity of UGT1A1 .
Smith et al. conducted a genome-wide survey of drug-inducible and PXR-binding CREs in human primary hepatocytes. The survey employed epigenetic profiling, in particular ChIP-seq assays targeting PXR, p300, and histone modifications H3K4me1 and H3K27ac. Assessment of conditional occupancy of PXR and chromatin signatures led to approximately 300 potential candidates of drug induced enhancers. Of the two well-characterized enhancers, only XREM was identified. Reporter assays confirmed regulatory activities in 15 out of the 42 selected regions. This pioneering study highlights the role of enhancers on drug treatment, with underscoring the need for further studies to understand enhancers altering drug responses, given the limitation of the approach based on conditional analysis of epigenetic profiles in sensitivity and specificity.
Transcriptome profiles can also be used for CRE identification, which is made possible through the detection of noncoding RNAs transcribed from active enhancers , called enhancer RNAs (eRNAs). Profiling of transcription start sites can be effectively used for enhancer identification . This approach offers unique advantages in the assessment of regulatory activities, because eRNA level can be used as a proxy for CRE activity , and because specificity of enhancer identification is higher in this approach than in epigenetic profiling . A genome-wide atlas of promoters and enhancers in a wide range of primary cells, tissues, and cell lines has been developed in the FANTOM5 project , , by using cap analysis of gene expression (CAGE), a quantitative method to monitor capped 5′ ends of RNAs , . However, drug responsiveness in human hepatocytes was not covered.
In the present study, we aimed to delineate drug-inducible CREs mediated by PXR for a better understanding of drug responses and ADRs. We conducted a genome-wide assessment of CREs in a HepG2 cell line with stable expression of human PXR (ShP51 cells) by using CAGE. By integrating data on PXR-binding sites found in human primary hepatocytes and GWAS results, we narrowed down the candidate regions to the ones directly targeted by PXR and associated with ADR-linked phenotypes. We assessed their impact on molecular phenotypes associated with ADR through evaluating their regulatory activities of ADR associated genes by using CRISPR/Cas9 knockout, luciferase reporter assay, and siRNA knockdown experiments.

## Results

## Genome-wide survey of drug-inducible CREs through quantitative profiling of transcription start sites
The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 is a widely used model system, but it is not necessarily suitable for studying drug induction as it is, owing to its negligible PXR expression. As an in vitro model of human hepatocytes, we chose ShP51 cells, which were generated from HepG2 cells to stably express human PXR . We confirmed upregulation of both CYP3A4 mRNA and XREM eRNA upon treatment with rifampicin in ShP51 cells (Fig.  ). This demonstrated that the model system recapitulates the response to PXR activation, and that activation of both promoters and enhancers can be assessed by RNA quantification. 
We next performed a genome-wide survey of the response of ShP51 cells to rifampicin treatment by using CAGE. We identified 90,871 CRE candidates and found that 2,398 (2.6%) of them were induced by rifampicin treatment with statistical significance at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.1. We confirmed that the identified CREs included promoters of CYP3A4, UGT1A1, and glutathione S-transferase α 1 (GSTA1), as well as the well-studied enhancers, XREM and PBREM (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). We found that 217 (9%) regions were located at promoters of known genes, and the remaining 91% were located in distal regions (Fig.  )
We subsequently examined the impact of the drug-induced CRE candidates by assessing the functional categories (GO terms) of their nearby genes. The analysis revealed an enrichment of relevant biological processes (Fig.  ), such as steroid metabolic processes. The result aligns with the known role of PXR in activating CYP3A4, which encodes a major enzyme responsible for steroid hormone hydroxylation . The enrichment in vitamin metabolic process is consistent with the known ADR of PXR activating drugs, vitamin D deficiency , . Additionally, the enrichment of leukocyte mediated immunity correlated with the fact that ligand-activated PXR exerts immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory effects by antagonizing nuclear factor κB , .
We further assessed the genetic correlation of the candidate CREs with biochemical marker–associated loci identified in UK BioBank’s GWAS studies , . Using the stratified linkage-disequilibrium score regression analysis (S-LDSC) with the summary statistics, we found that the variants associated with the levels of vitamin D and bilirubin in the serum are substantially enriched in the identified CRE candidates (Fig.  ). The enrichment in the bilirubin level–associated loci is concordant with a PXR downstream pathway, as metabolic clearance of bilirubin is driven by UGT1A1 expression mediated by PXR binding to both the promoter and PBREM . The greatest enrichment was found in the association with vitamin D and calcium levels, which was consistent with the GO enrichment analysis of nearby genes mentioned above. The high level of enrichment, such as more than 100-fold, indicated the relevance of the identified CRE candidates as regulatory elements underlying the phenotypes.

## Drug-inducible CRE candidates bound by PXR in human primary hepatocytes
We further narrowed the CRE candidates down to the ones directly targeted by PXR in human primary hepatocytes. We re-processed the data produced by a genome-wide mapping of PXR-binding sites with ChIP-seq . Of the 2,398 rifampicin-induced CRE candidates, we found the overlap with PXR-binding sites (Fig.  ) to be 364 CREs, which included 217 promoters and 147 enhancers (Fig.  ). We refer to these sites as drug-inducible and PXR-binding promoters (DPP; hereafter numbered as DPP1–217) and drug-inducible and PXR-binding enhancers (DPE; hereafter numbered as DPE1–147) (Supplementary Data  ).
We examined the index SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms) in the GWAS catalog database and found them or SNPs correlated with them (linkage disequilibrium as R2 > 0.8) within 103 of the 364 regions, including 64 promoters and 39 enhancers (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). Three of the enhancer candidates (DPE15–17) overlapped SNPs associated with bilirubin levels (Table  ). DPE16 corresponds to PBREM, and DPE15 and DPE17 are enhancer candidates located upstream and at the intron of UGT1A1, respectively (Fig.  ). Six of the enhancer candidates overlapped SNPs associated with vitamin D levels, including ones located downstream of cytochrome P450 family 24 subfamily A member 1 (CYP24A1) (DPE127–129, Fig.  ) and others located upstream of tsukushi, small leucine rich proteoglycan (TSKU) (DPE70–72, Fig.  ). Notably, the enhancer candidates close to CYP24A1 and TSKU were located within super-enhancers (Figs.  and  ). We performed siRNA knockdown experiments targeting PXR, and found a significant reduction in the expression levels of UGT1A1, CYP24A1, and TSKU by depletion of PXR, under the condition of rifampicin treatment (Supplementary Fig.  ). Combined with the observed upregulation of these genes in response to rifampicin (Fig.  ), the result implied that the induced CREs may influence their expressions through PXR. 

## Functional copy of the well-known enhancer of UGT1A1
The activation level of UGT1A1 is known to influence various phenotypes, including the risk of neutropenia in irinotecan treatment and the metabolic clearance of the endogenous toxin bilirubin. We here focused on the investigation of unrevealed noncoding elements that could alter its expression. DPE15 and DPE17 are PXR-bound enhancer candidates in the UGT1A1 locus (Fig.  ), and we unexpectedly found that the genome sequence of DPE15 is 92% identical to that of PBREM (Fig.  ). DPE15 (referred to hereafter as PBREM-like) is located at 14.9 kb upstream of the UGT1A1 promoter, whereas PBREM is located at 3 kb upstream (Fig.  ). PXR response elements within PBREM (direct repeat (DR)-4, PXRE, gtNR-1, and DR-3 ) were mostly preserved in PBREM-like, except that DR-3 had a single nucleotide mismatch to that in PBREM (Fig.  ). Notably, a SNP (rs4124874, UGT1A1*60T>G) has been reported in the position of the mismatch, and the minor allele has been shown to lead to a decrease in regulatory activity .
To investigate the regulatory role of PBREM-like, we generated homozygous deletion mutants of ShP51 cells using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. We confirmed that homozygous deletion of PBREM (ΔPBREM 1 and 2; Fig.  ) significantly decreased both basal and drug-induced expression of UGT1A1 (Fig.  ), consistent with a previous study . Homozygous deletion of PBREM-like (ΔPBREM-like 1 and 2; Fig.  ) showed a significant reduction of UGT1A1 expression as well (Fig.  ). We also observed significant reduction of the UGT1A1 protein levels in these deletion clones (Supplementary Fig.  ), consistent with the corresponding mRNA levels.
As deleting either of the enhancers almost abolishes the expression of UGT1A1, we next asked their combinatorial effect. We found that homozygous deletion of both PBREM and PBREM-like further reduced the expression (Supplementary Fig.  ). Given that our luciferase assays found comparable regulatory activities of PBREM-like with the minor alleles of PBREM (Supplementary Fig.  ), our experiments demonstrate that PBREM-like play a role of an independent enhancer of UGT1A1 as a functional copy of PBREM, beyond its sequence similarity to the well-known enhancer.

## Intronic enhancer of UGT1A1
We subsequently investigated the functional role of DPE17, an enhancer candidate located in the first intron of UGT1A1. We found that homozygous deletion (ΔIntronic enh. 1–3; Fig.  ) substantially decreased both basal and drug-induced expression of UGT1A1 (Fig.  ). We also observed significant reduction of the UGT1A1 protein levels in the deletion clones (Supplementary Fig. ). We further confirmed that its silencing by CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) similarly decreased the expression of UGT1A1 in both ShP51 cells and differentiated HepaRG cells (Supplementary Fig.  ).
We focused on two SNPs associated with bilirubin levels (rs3771341 , and rs4148325 – ) overlapping with the intronic enhancer (Table  ), and additional two SNPs in the proximal region (rs6742078 , , – and rs4148324 ). They comprise three haplotypes (Fig.  ) in the whole population , and all four SNPs were significantly associated with the expression level of UGT1A1 in liver according to the database of GTEx eQTL with a P value < 1e−5 and effect size −0.3 (Supplementary Data  ). We constructed reporter vectors containing a minimal promoter and the +3657/ + 4741 region from UGT1A1 transcription start site, corresponding to the three haplotypes (Fig.  ), and found a significant decrease of luciferase activity in both of the two minor alleles. The results identified an enhancer of UGT1A1 with revealing its haplotypes causes alteration of the expression level.

## Impact of UGT1A1 enhancers on its alternative promoters
The UGT1A locus contain nine alternative promoters producing distinct first exons followed by four shared exons . Among these, five isoforms other than UGT1A1, namely UGT1A3, UGT1A4, UGT1A6, UGT1A7, and UGT1A9, have been shown to be expressed in human liver, human primary hepatocytes, and HepG2 cells , , which is also found in our CAGE data (Supplementary Fig.  ). We asked whether the known and the newly identified enhancers function equivalently as enhancers for these promoters producing distinct isoforms.
We examined the expression levels of the UGT1A1 isoforms in the deletion mutants of PBREM, PBREM-like, and the intronic enhancer (Fig.  ; Supplementary Fig.  ). Deletion of the intronic enhancer reduced the expression of all five UGT1A isoforms, while deletion of PBREM increased expression of the UGT1A isoforms, except for UGT1A1 and UGT1A3 (Supplementary Fig.  ). The deletion effects of PBREM-like were inconclusive due to inconsistencies across the replicates (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results indicated that (i) the intronic enhancer act as an enhancer for all UGT1A isoforms, (ii) PBREM contributes as enhancer to the two most downstream promoters producing UGT1A1 and UGT1A3, while inhibiting the other UGT1A isoforms, and (iii) PBREM-like specifically targets the UGT1A1 promoter. The effects of the three enhancers in the UGT1A locus vary depending on the target promoters (Fig.  ).

## Variants in a drug-induced super-enhancer alter CYP24A1 expression
Vitamin D deficiency by PXR activators is likely explained by upregulation of CYP24A1, which encodes an enzyme facilitating excretion of vitamin D . Although PXR binds to the CYP24A1 promoter in the human hepatocellular carcinoma Huh7 cell line that overexpress human PXR , we found no peaks of PXR ChIP-seq within the CYP24A1 promoter in human primary hepatocytes (Fig.  ). This indicates that PXR involvement in CYP24A1 induction is mediated primarily by distal elements rather than the promoter in vivo.
Of the drug-inducible and PXR-bound enhancer candidates, three (DPE127–129) are located downstream of CYP24A1 (Fig.  ). They comprise a part of CYP24A1 enhancer clusters activated by another nuclear receptor, vitamin D receptor (VDR, or NR1I1), through vitamin D response elements (VDREs) . The enhancer cluster overlaps a super-enhancer defined on the basis of H3K27ac ChIP-seq according to the Super-Enhancer database . Regulatory activity of the enhancer cluster on CYP24A1 was only demonstrated in a cell line derived from colorectal adenocarcinoma , and we assessed the regulatory activity in ShP51 cells. We found that the CRISPRi-based repression of DPE128 significantly reduced the expression of CYP24A1 (Fig.  ), suggesting its regulatory activity in a model system of hepatocytes.
Binding of PXR to VDRE is possible as PXR recognize similar DNA motifs to VDR . Notably, the region contains rs6013892, a GWAS SNP associated with vitamin D levels , and rs158523, genetically correlated (R2 > 0.8) with another GWAS SNP associated with vitamin D levels, rs158528 . We asked whether the two variants alter the PXR-mediated regulatory activities. To address the hypothesis, we performed a luciferase reporter assay in ShP51 cells. The major haplotype (rs6013892-C and rs158523-T) had the strongest transcriptional activity at both basal and drug-induced levels (Fig.  ). The activity was significantly reduced when either of the SNPs was altered and was lowest when both SNPs were altered (Fig.  ). The results identified that the two SNPs have causal effect to alter the regulatory activity.

## Drug-inducible super-enhancer of TSKU alters the expression of vitamin D–metabolizing enzymes
Of the enhancer candidates overlapping SNPs associated with vitamin D levels, three (DPE70–72) were identified at 10.2 kb, 9.4 kb, and 0.5 kb upstream of TSKU which encodes a liver-enriched secreted protein that possibly regulates energy expenditure . PXR was shown to contribute to an increase of TSKU expression in phenobarbital-treated human primary hepatocytes , which is also found in our CAGE data (Fig.  ). A series of drug-inducible CREs were found from 8 kb to 13 kb upstream of TSKU (Fig.  ), which is included in a super-enhancer according to the Super-Enhancer database . We generated two clones of ShP51 cells with homozygous deletion of the 6.7 kbp region (ΔTSKU-up 1 and 2; Fig.  ; Supplementary Fig.  ). We found a significant decrease of TSKU expression in the deletion mutants (Fig.  ), which indicated that a portion of the large region play a role to increases TSKU expression. We observed slight increase of the TSKU protein levels in the deletion clones (Supplementary Fig.  ), which suggested a presence of negative feedback at translational or post-translational levels. We selected four segments of the region (up-1, -2, -3, and -4) (Fig.  ) and assessed their regulatory activity with a luciferase reporter assay (Fig.  ). Although rifampicin increased the activity of all of them, up-1 had the strongest activity in both basal and drug-induced conditions (Fig.  ; Supplementary Fig.  ). These results suggest that up-1 is the main contributor in increasing TSKU expression level. Because up-4 contains a SNP rs1149605 that is genetically associated with vitamin D levels , (Supplementary Fig.  ), we assessed the regulatory activity of the region with a luciferase reporter assay and found no allele-dependency (Supplementary Fig.  ). This is consistent with the result that up-1 is the main component within the super-enhancer.
We investigated whether TSKU itself affect expression of genes involved in the metabolism of vitamin D. Using siRNA, we reduced the TSKU mRNA level to approximately 15% of the original level in ShP51 cells (Fig.  ). Unexpectedly, this reduction led to an increase of CYP24A1 expression, which facilitate excretion of vitamin D, and decreases of CYP27A1 and CYP27B1 expression, while no effect on CYP2R1 (Fig.  ). Given the three latter genes are involved in producing the active form of vitamin D, our results suggested that TSKU upregulates the levels of vitamin D by repression of negatively impacting genes (CYP24A1) and activation of positively impacting genes (CYP27A1 and CYP27B1). These relationships are likely indirect, by considering TSKU have a role as a secreted protein , not as a transcription factor.

## Discussion
In this study, we systematically analyzed drug-inducible CREs to elucidate the genomic basis of both anticipated and unanticipated drug induction. We first explored potential CREs through a quantitative and genome-wide assessment of the transcription initiations by using CAGE. The resulting candidates were substantially enriched in loci genetically associated with several biochemical markers measured in serum. Integrative analysis with a reprocessed dataset of PXR ChIP-seq from human primary hepatocytes led us to identify 103 drug-inducible, PXR binding, and GWAS-associated CREs. Targeted experiments, including CRISPR/Cas9 knockout, luciferase reporter assay, and siRNA knockdown shed light on PXR downstream regulation. The findings included enhancers and super-enhancers, allele dependency of the regulatory activities, and an unexpected regulatory cascade leading to ADR. Despite extensive research on drug-responses over several decades, our study uncovered previously unrecognized regulatory interactions, made possible by the greater sensitivity of our quantitative transcriptome-based approach (Fig.  ). 

## Regulatory elements and alleles associated with drug efficacy
UGT1A1 plays a critical role in detoxification of a variety of xenobiotics, including therapeutic drugs, and metabolic clearance of the endogenous toxin bilirubin. Whereas PBREM has been the only well-studied enhancer of the gene, we discovered two additional enhancers, PBREM-like and the intronic enhancer, both of which substantially affect UGT1A1 expression levels (Fig.  ).
PBREM-like had 92% sequence identity to PBREM, which is also found in rhesus macaque but not in common marmoset (Supplementary Fig.  ). This suggests that PBREM-like originated from a segmental duplication of the genome after the divergence of old world monkeys and new world monkeys. Both of the enhancers are crucial for ensuring proper UGT1A1 expression levels, as demonstrated by the significant decrease of expression observed in the knockout cells (Fig.  ). The segmental duplication event may have contributed to the very high level of expression of UGT1A1. The identification of the intronic enhancer as a functional component is both intriguing and important for understanding genotype-dependent responses to drugs. Variants of this enhancer result in substantially lower regulatory activity (Fig.  ).
The UGT1A locus contains nine active CREs in human hepatocytes, including six promoters corresponding to distinct isoforms (UGT1A1, UGT1A3, UGT1A4, UGT1A6, UGT1A7, and UGT1A9), and three enhancers (PBREM, PBREM-like, and the intronic enhancer). Our results indicate that the interactions among these elements can have both positive and negative effects. The impact of allele differences will be influenced by the interplay among the multiple CREs (Fig.  ). Therefore, a composite model would be required to accurately estimate regulatory outcomes.

## ADR-associated regulatory interactions mediated by super-enhancers
Activation of PXR leads to the upregulation of CYP24A1, which encodes an enzyme facilitating excretion of vitamin D . This upregulation may contribute to vitamin D deficiency, an ADR of PXR activators. We found several CREs induced by PXR that included polymorphisms genetically associated with vitamin D levels. The CREs were located within two super-enhancers near CYP24A1 and TSKU loci (Figs.  and  A).
In contrast to an earlier observation of PXR binding to the CYP24A1 promoter in a tumor cell line , the reprocessed ChIP-seq data obtained from human primary hepatocytes revealed PXR binding within the super-enhancer region rather than the promoter (Fig.  ). This suggests that PXR-dependent activation of CYP24A1 in vivo is primarily mediated by the super-enhancer. Our results demonstrated that DEP128, which is included in the super-enhancer, plays a role as an enhancer of CYP24A1 in hepatocyte-derived cells (Fig.  ), and we also found that two SNPs in DPE128 genetically associated with vitamin D levels alter the regulatory activity (Fig.  ). These results support the contribution of the super-enhancer to drug induction.
The second super-enhancer was located upstream of TSKU, which encodes a hepatokine likely involved in energy expenditure . We found that the drug-inducible super-enhancer contributed to the upregulation of TSKU (Fig.  ). Notably, our siRNA experiment revealed that TSKU upregulates the levels of vitamin D by repression of CYP24A1 gene and activation of CYP27A1 and CYP27B1 genes (Fig.  ). Because TSKU is secreted from hepatocytes, this suppression of CYP24A1 might happen in cells other than hepatocytes and thus may contribute to fine-tuning CYP24A1 expression by coordinating with other regulatory elements, including the super-enhancer adjacent to CYP24A1.
Our genome-wide quantitative assessment revealed a number of drug-inducible, PXR-mediated, and trait-associated enhancers in human hepatocytes. The results not only aligned closely with the anticipated outcomes to drug exposure but also provided insights into the complex regulatory mechanisms underlying drug responses. Our focused experiments further demonstrated that several of these enhancers affect molecular phenotypes associated with ADR, the expression of drug-induced genes associated with ADR, such as neutropenia and vitamin D deficiency. The uncovered enhancers, regulatory alleles, and a molecular cascade leading to ADR contribute a precise understanding of the noncoding elements of the human genome underlying drug responses.

## Methods

## Chemicals and reagents
Restriction enzyme SacI-HF was purchased from New England Biolabs, NEB (Ipswich, MA, USA) and luciferase reporter vectors, pGL4.26 and pGL4.74, from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). The oligonucleotide primers were custom-synthesized by FASMAC Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa, Japan).

## Cell culture
The human hepatocellular carcinoma–derived HepG2 cells that stably express human PXR (ShP51 cells) were kindly provided by Dr. Masahiko Negishi . ShP51 cells and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated enhancer-deleted ShP51 cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (high glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 1× MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator, which provided an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air at a constant temperature of 37 °C.

## Cell treatment
Cells were treated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), or with 10 µM or 100 µM rifampicin dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) for 24 h.

## Plasmid construction
To construct pGL4.26-1A1-Intronic enh. (Ref, Alt1, or Alt2), the UGT1A1 intronic region (+3658/+4757 from the UGT1A1 TSS) was amplified by nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The amplified DNA fragments were purified and cloned into the SacI-digested pGL4.26 vector. To construct pGL4.26-1A1-Intronic enh. with deleted PXR ChIP-seq peak(Δpeak), the UGT1A1 intronic regions (fragment 1: +3658/+4003 and fragment 2: +4307/+4757, both the from the UGT1A1 TSS) were amplified using pGL4.26-1A1 Intronic enh. (Ref) as a template. The two amplified DNA fragments were cloned into the SacI-digested pGL4.26. To construct pGL4.26-TSKU upstream, 5 upstream regions of TSKU (up-1: −15,412/−13,367; up-2: −13,970/−11,658; up-3: −11,677/−10,067; up-4: −10,236/−9003; and TSKU-up: −15,412/−9003 from TSKU TSS) were amplified by nested PCR. Each of the amplified DNA fragments was cloned into the SacI-digested pGL4.26. Mixed human genomic DNA of 10 people (Promega) was used as a template for 1st round PCR amplification with PrimeSTAR MAX DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) in a ProFlex PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primer sets designed and used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data  . To construct pGL4.26-DPE128-PXR ChIP-seq peak (+66,190/+66,358 from CYP24A1 TSS), the double-stranded DNA fragments containing rs6013892 and rs158523 with different alleles were synthesized (gBlocks; IDT, San Jose, CA, USA). To construct pGL4.26-DPE15 (−14,848/−14,415 from UGT1A1 TSS) and pGL4.26-DPE16 (−3490/−3060 from UGT1A1 TSS), the double-stranded DNA fragments containing rs28900379 and rs11695484 or rs4124874 and rs10929302 with different alleles were synthesized. Each of the gBlocks was cloned into the SacI-digested pGL4.26 vector. The sequences of gBlocks (reference sequences) are listed in Supplementary Data  .
All cloning reactions were performed with NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (NEB). The constructs were verified by DNA sequencing (Eurofins Scientific, Luxembourg City, Luxembourg).

## Luciferase reporter assay
Cells were transiently reverse-transfected with the constructed pGL4.26 luciferase reporter vector and pGL4.74 vector using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were treated with 10 μM rifampicin for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were resuspended in a passive lysis buffer, and luciferase activity was measured with an ARVOX3 Perkin Elmer 2030 multiple reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) using the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay system (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was first normalized to Renilla reniformis luciferase activity, which served as an internal control. The resulting value were subsequently normalized to those measured from cells transfected with the empty pGL4.26 vector.

## Design of single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs)
CHOPCHOP (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/) , Benchling (https://www.benchling.com/), and CRISPRtarget (http://crispr.otago.ac.nz/CRISPRTarget/crispr_analysis.html) were used to design high-performance gRNAs for the deletion of DPE15, DPE16, and DPE17 around UGT1A1 and the deletion of TSKU-up. All sgRNAs (Alt-R CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNA) for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion were obtained from IDT and are listed in Supplementary Data  . For CRISPRi-mediated repression of the UGT1A1 intronic region (DPE17) and downstream enhancer of CYP24A1 (DPE128), two gRNAs were designed using CHOPCHOP respectively. The cloning of designed gRNAs into Mammalian Dual-gRNA Expression Lentiviral Vector (pLV-1A1intronic gRNAs, pLV-DPE128 gRNAs) was conducted by Vector Builder (Chicago, IL, USA). sgRNAs for CRISPRi are listed in Supplementary Data  .

## Transfection and generation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated enhancer-deletion mutants
Ribonucleoprotein complexes composed of the designed sgRNAs and Alt-R S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3 (IDT) were electroporated using the NEON transfection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) into ShP51 cells or ΔPBREM-1. Following electroporation, cells were seeded into 10-cm dishes and cultured for 13 days, single-cell clones were hand-picked, grown, and expanded. Genomic DNAs of single-cell clones were extracted using a Wizard SV Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega). Deletion was confirmed by genotyping PCR to amplify the targeted region with Tks Gflex DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio); the primers are listed in Supplementary Data  . The PCR products were loaded on 0.8% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide and visualized under ultraviolet light. The truncated genomic fragments (mutant PCR products) were subsequently purified and analyzed by DNA sequencing (Eurofins).

## CRISPRi for UGT1A1 intronic enhancer (DPE17) and CYP24A1 downstream enhancer (DPE128) in ShP51 cells
pLV-1A1 intronic gRNAs or pLV‐scrambled sgRNAs were co-transfected with the ViraPower Lentiviral Packaging Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) into 293FT packaging cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Viral supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45-μm PVDF syringe filter. Viral titration was conducted using a Lenti-X p24 Rapid Titer Kit (Takara Bio). Lentiviral dCas9-KRAB particles and CRISPRi for DPE128 particles were purchased from Vector Builder. ShP51 cells were transduced at multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. Forty-eight hours after lentiviral infection, cells were cultured with puromycin (0.25 μg/mL) and hygromycin (400 μg/mL) for 10 days. Subsequently, the transduced cells were rifampicin-treated and processed for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR).

## CRISPRi for UGT1A1 intronic enhancer (DPE17) in HepaRG cells
The human hepatoma cell line HepaRG (fully differentiated cryopreserved cells, HPR116-TA10) and all media for the cell culture were purchased from KAC Co., Ltd. (Kyoto, Japan). HepaRG cells were maintained in Medium-620 following the supplier’s protocols in a humidified incubator, which provided an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air at a constant temperature of 37 °C. The medium was changed once every 3–4 days. Lentiviral dCas9-KRAB particles and CRISPRi for DPE17 particles were used to transduce HepaRG cells at MOI of 6. Forty-eight hours after lentiviral infection, cells were treated with 20 µM rifampicin for 48 h. Subsequently, the transduced cells were processed for qRT-PCR.

## siRNA knockdown of TSKU and PXR
ShP51 cells were transfected with control siRNA (Silencer Select Negative Control #1, 4390843) or human TSKU siRNA (Silencer Select standard siRNA s24880, 4392420) or human NR1I2 (Silencer™ Select Pre-Designed siRNA s16909, 4392420) from Thermo Fisher Scientific using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific); 72 h after transfection, cells were processed for qRT-PCR.

## qRT-PCR
Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or a miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands), and quantified by using a Denovix DS-11 spectrophotometer (DeNovix Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). DNA was digested with TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and total RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RT-PCR was performed on CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection Systems (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Kit (NIPPON Genetics, Tokyo, Japan). The list of specific sense and antisense oligonucleotide primers is shown in Supplementary Data  . The expression levels are normalized to GAPDH mRNA.

## Sample preparation for liquid chromatograph-tandem mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS) analysis
The cell pellets were lysed in cell lysis buffer (5% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 50 mM tetraethylammonium bromide (TEAB)) by sonication. After determination of the protein concentration with the bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), proteins were reduced by incubation with 4.5 mM dithiothreitol at 55 °C for 30 min and alkylated in the dark for 15 min with 10 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature. The alkylated proteins were acidified with phosphoric acid to a final concentration of 2.5% before addition of six volumes of suspension trapping (S-Trap) binding buffer (90% methanol, 100 mM TEAB, pH 7.5). S-Trap digestion was performed using S-Trap micro-sample preparation kits (Protifi, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the mixture samples were spun through the S-trap micro-spin column and washed with S-Trap binding buffer. Protein digestion step was performed using 50 mM TEAB with Trypsin/Lys-C protease mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) added to the top of the column and incubated for 1 h at 47 °C. The tryptic peptides were sequentially eluted with 50 mM TEAB, 0.5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and 50% acetonitrile (ACN) and 0.5% TFA. The collected peptides were dried with vacuum centrifuge and resuspended in 0.1% TFA for LC-MS/MS analysis.

## Nano-flow LC-MS/MS
Data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA-MS) analysis was performed on a Vanquish Neo LC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (both Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides (500 ng) were injected into the packed nano-capillary column Aurora Ultimate (0.075 mm I.D. ×250 mm L, particle diameter 1.7 μm) (IonOptics, Victoria, Australia) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min using the following gradient: 1 min, 2.5% B; 2 min, 6% B; 60.3 min, 21% B; 82.6 min, 31% B; 95.6 min, 44% B; 99.6 min, 95% B;104.6 min, 95% B (Buffer A: 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water, and buffer B: 80% ACN, 0.1% FA in water). Column temperature was maintained at 55 °C using a column oven (Reifycs Analytical, Osaka, Japan). For ionization, a spray voltage of 1.8 kV and an ion transfer tube temperature of 275 °C were used. Full MS scans were acquired at 60k orbitrap resolution from 380 to 985 Da and a maximum injection time of 25 ms. The MS2 was performed over a mass range of 380–980 Da using 10 Da windows with an overlap of 1 Da. After MS acquisition, DIA raw data were processed using library-free analysis against UniProt (release 2024_06, human) and the protein levels were quantified by cross-run normalization with DIA-NN (v1.9.1) , followed by missing value imputation with Perseus (v2.0.3.0) . The following parameters were used: trypsin as an enzyme, allowing for up to one missed cleavage site, charge states of 1–4 for peptides consisting of 7–30 amino acids, N-term of Met excision, carbamidomethylation of Cys, oxidation of Met, Acetylation of N-term and an FDR of 1% for precursor identifications.

## Statistical analysis
The difference in means between the two conditions was assessed by a two-sided, unpaired Welch’s t test with the GraphPad Prism10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). The statistical parameters (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001) are presented in individual figures and figure legends. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## CAGE and data analysis
ShP51 cells were treated with 10 μM or 100 μM rifampicin for 24 h. Total RNA was isolated using a miRNeasy Mini Kit. The quality of total RNA was assessed by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to ensure that the RNA integrity number was over 7.0. CAGE libraries were prepared using a CAGE Library preparation kit (DNAform, Kanagawa, Japan). Briefly, cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using random primers. The ribose diols in the 5′ cap structures of RNA were oxidized and then biotinylated. The biotinylated RNA/cDNAs were selected by streptavidin beads (a process known as cap-trapping). RNA was digested with RNaseONE/H, adaptors were ligated to both ends of cDNA, and CAGE libraries of double-stranded cDNA were constructed. The CAGE libraries were validated for quality and size distribution using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and sequenced to the sequencing depth of approximately 54 million reads on average by NextSeq500 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).
Obtained reads were aligned with the reference human genome GRCh38 by using STAR (v2.7.5a) , taking splice junctions of Gencode transcripts (v37) to improve the alignment accuracy. The first position of each uniquely mapped read was counted as the transcription initiation, and the result was used to identify divergently transcribed regions as candidate CREs by using CREate (https://github.com/hkawaji/CREate) (v0.1), bedtools (v2.29.2) , and the utility programs of the UCSC Genome Browser Database (v425) . Read counts starting from the identified candidate regions were subjected to quantitative analysis by using edgeR (v3.30.0) , consisting of normalization based on the relative log expression method and differential analysis based on the exact test (FDR < 0.1 for statistical significance).
Functional enrichment of the upregulated CRE candidates was assessed by Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (v4.0.4) . Partitioning heritability of S-LDSC was computed by LDSC (v1.0.1) with the genomic coordinates of the CRE candidates converted into GRCh37 by the liftOver tool and 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 data (10.5281/zenodo.7768714).

## ChIP-seq data analysis
Data of PXR ChIP-seq performed on human primary hepatocytes (accession numbers: SRR1642056 and SRR1642057 for PXR ChIP-seq and SRR1642055 for the control experiment) were downloaded and aligned with the reference human genome GRCh38 by BWA MEM (v0.7.17) . Alignments with a mapping quality score more than 20 were subjected to peak call by MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) with the default setting . With a threshold of FDR of <0.2 for detecting the binding of PXR to XREM as a positive control, 27,633 peaks for dimethyl sulfoxide and 11,752 peaks for rifampicin treatment were identified.

## Integration with the GWAS catalog database
Genomic coordinates of index SNPs in the GWAS Catalog and common SNPs in The Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser Database . The R2 measure of linkage disequilibrium between SNPs within 200 kbp was calculated by using plink2 (v2.00a2.3LM) and was based on European population data in the 1000 Genomes Project database.

## Determination of haplotypes and estimation of haplotype frequency
The frequency of each haplotype in all populations was obtained from LDlink (data from the 1000 Genomes Project) via LDhap (https://ldlink.nih.gov/).
