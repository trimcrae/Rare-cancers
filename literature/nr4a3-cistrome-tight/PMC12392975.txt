## Summary
Fleshy fruits are commercially and nutritionally important but become more susceptible to pathogens as they ripen. Active defence mechanisms continue to function in ripe fruits but they are influenced by ripening regulators and ripening outcomes. Botrytis cinerea is one of the most important fruit fungal pathogens of tomato and jasmonic acid (JA) is the major defence phytohormone involved in response to B. cinerea infection. Pathogens induce the accumulation of bioactive jasmonoyl‐isoleucine (JA‐Ile), which transmits defence signals through a hub transcription factor (TF) MYC2 that regulates defence responses. This has been clarified in depth in leaves, but how ripening, and in particular the regulators NOR and RIN influence JA‐Ile‐mediated defence responses remains unclear. In this article, we demonstrate that knocking‐out NOR or RIN either singly (CR‐NOR, CR‐RIN) or together (CR‐NOR/RIN) weakens JA‐Ile accumulation in response to B. cinerea infection in tomato fruit. Both NOR and RIN physically interact with MYC2, and 110 of 569 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in B. cinerea‐infected wild‐type (WT) fruits were identified as direct targets of MYC2. These included ethylene response factors (ERFs) such as ERF.A2, B12, C3, F4, G2/PTI6, whose transcripts accumulated in WT but were greatly reduced in either CR‐NOR or CR‐RIN infected fruits. Only MYC2 binding to ERF.F4 was severely impaired in CR‐NOR and CR‐RIN fruits. ERF.F4 transactivates PR‐STH2 and activates defence signalling in B. cinerea‐infected fruit. The increase in susceptibility to infection caused by promotion of ripening by RIN and NOR is thus partially compensated by their positive action in defence signalling.

## Both NOR and RIN physically interact with the hub regulator MYC2 in JA signalling
It has been reported that the hub regulator MYC2 could recruit JAZ proteins as co‐repressors and MED25 as a coactivator in the JA‐Ile‐mediated defence response. Transcripts of nine of the 13 tomato JAZ genes, including JAZ1‐4, 6‐7, 9, 11‐12, were detectable in ripening fruits (Tomato Genome Consortium, ). We investigated their physical interaction with either NOR or RIN using the yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) system. The results showed that NOR could interact with MYC2, while RIN could interact with MYC2 and MED25. Neither NOR nor RIN interacted with any JAZ protein (Figure  ). To confirm these interactions observed in the Y2H system, we performed both the luciferase complementation assay (LCA) and co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP). Full‐length NOR or RIN were cloned to generate NOR‐cLUC and RIN‐cLUC, respectively, and MYC2 and MED25 were cloned to generate MYC2‐nLUC and MED25‐nLUC. These constructs were co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. The results showed that NOR‐cLUC plus MYC2‐nLUC, RIN‐cLUC plus MYC2‐nLUC, and RIN‐cLUC plus MYC2‐nLUC had obvious detectable fluorescence compared with control (Figure  ). Full‐length NOR, RIN, MYC2, MED25 plus C‐terminal tag of MYC, FLAG, GFP, and GFP, respectively, were co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. The results showed that NOR‐MYC co‐immunoprecipitated with MYC2‐GFP and RIN‐FLAG co‐immunoprecipitated with MYC2‐GFP and MED25‐GFP, respectively (Figure  ). Moreover, RIN competitively interfered with the interaction between MYC2 and MED25, while NOR competitively interacted with MYC2 and JAZ1 or JAZ6 in the yeast three‐hybrid (Y3H) system (Figure  ). The interaction between RIN and MYC2 and MED25, and NOR and MYC2 and JAZ1/6 were verified using pull‐down assays (Figure  ). These results indicate that NOR and RIN participate in the JA‐mediated response to B. cinerea infection through protein interaction with MYC2.
 The transactivation capacity of NOR, RIN, and MYC2 for genes involved in the JA‐Ile signalling was tested via transient dual‐luciferase (LUC) reporter assays. Promoters of MYC2, NOR, RIN, and potential target genes were fused to the LUC reporter separately, with a Renilla (REN) as an internal control. NOR and RIN activated the LUC gene driven by the MYC2 promoter (Figure  ) and MYC2 activated the LUC genes driven by either the NOR or the RIN promoters (Figure  ). Moreover, MYC2 activated the LUC gene driven by promoters of functional genes, such as allene oxide cyclase (AOC), allene oxide synthase (AOS), lipoxygenase D (LoxD), oxophytodienoate‐reductase 3 (OPR3), cytochrome P450 94 (CPYP94), and ERF.C3. Furthermore, NOR could activate the LUC gene driven by promoters of AOC, AOS, LoxD, CYP94C1, and ERF.C3, while RIN activated the LUC gene driven by promoters of CYP94C1 and ERF.C3 (Figure  ). Comparison of the expression pattern of these genes in WT, CR‐NOR, and CR‐RIN fruits was also consistent with the participation of NOR and RIN in promoting their expression (Figure  ).

## 
NOR and RIN enhance MYC2 transactivation of ERF.F4

Transgenic plants over‐expressing (OE) MYC2‐GFP in WT, CR‐NOR, and CR‐RIN plants, respectively, were obtained (Figure  ) to identify global direct targets of MYC2 in tomato fruits via CUT&Tag technology. Nuclei of transgenic WT 35S::MYC2‐GFP, CR‐NOR 35S::MYC2‐GFP, and CR‐RIN 35S::MYC2‐GFP fruits were isolated for CUT&Tag assay. In WT 35S::MYC2‐GFP transgenic fruit, a total of 3523 binding peaks of MYC2 located on gene promoters were found, occupying 47.04% of the tomato genome (Figure  , Table  ), with the peaks at about 250 bp upstream from the transcriptional starting site (TSS) (Figure  ). Searching promoter regions (2.0 kb) of these sites identified 594 peaks of MYC2 binding which also had binding motifs for both NAC and MADS (NOR is a NAC and RIN is a MADS gene), 1294 peaks with NAC‐binding motifs, and 99 peaks with MADS‐binding motifs, indicating a high proportion of these targets are regulated by MYC2, NOR, and RIN TFs (Figure  ). In WT 35S::MYC2‐GFP fruit, a total of 6532 genes were identified as direct binding targets of MYC2, with 359 and 673 of them showing differential binding in CR‐NOR and CR‐RIN backgrounds (Table  ), indicating that both NOR and RIN could enhance the binding ability of MYC2 to many of these targets (Figure  ).
 Among the 569 DEGs between WT fruits with and without B. cinerea infection (Figure  ), there were 110 detected as direct target genes of MYC2 in WT fruits (Figure  ). These included genes encoding ERFs, such as ERF.A2, B12, C3, F4, G2/PTI6, whose transcripts accumulated in WT but were greatly reduced in either CR‐NOR or CR‐RIN infected fruits (Figure  ) and were selected as potential candidate targets of MYC2 in fruits. Of these, ERF.C3, F4, and G2 could be directly bound by MYC2, but only the binding ability to ERF.F4 was severely impaired in CR‐NOR and CR‐RIN fruits (Figure  ). Further, two MYC2‐binding motifs in the ERF.F4 promoter were identified, namely, CACGTG and ACCGACA. Consistent with this finding, ChIP‐qPCR assay showed that MYC2 specifically bound to each (Figure  ). Furthermore, MYC2 could transactivate LUC reporter genes driven by the ERF.F4 promoter (2.6‐fold) and the transactivation ability was significantly enhanced (3.5–8.2‐fold) by adding RIN or NOR protein (Figure  ).

The precise role of ERF.F4 in B. cinerea‐infected tomato fruit was analysed using ERF.F4 knockout (CR‐ERF.F4) tomato mutant lines, generated by CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system. Two specific genomic sites were selected for cleavage (Figure  ), and a series of independent transgenic plants were identified by directly sequencing PCR products derived from the genomic DNA nearby the target regions. Four homozygous mutant lines were obtained, including insertion of 1 bp A/T at site 2 (CR‐ERF.F4#1) and insertion of 1 bp A/T at site 1 (CR‐ERF.F4#2) (Figure  ). These manipulations introduced premature stop codons in the ERF.F4 coding sequence, which led to predicted truncated ERF.F4 polypeptides of 73 and 163 amino acids, compared with the 225 amino acids of WT ERF.F4 native protein (Figure  ).
Fruits of CR‐ERF.F4 lines had lower resistance to B. cinerea infection (Figure  ). The expression pattern of DEGs encoding PRs, such as PR‐STH2, PR‐5, and PR‐like increased in WT fruit, but the induction was greatly weakened in either CR‐NOR or CR‐RIN infected fruits (Figure  ). qRT‐PCR experiments indicated that the accumulation of these transcripts encoding PRs normally induced by infection in WT fruits was reduced significantly in CR‐ERF.F4 fruits (Figure  ). Both PR‐STH2 and PR‐5 genes have a GCC‐box (ERF binding motif) (Figure  ), but PR‐like does not. ERF.F4 was shown to be localized in nuclei (Figure  ) and could activate the LUC reporter gene driven by the PR‐STH2 promoter, but not by the PR‐5 promoter (Figure  ). Moreover, ERF.F4 specifically activated the LUC gene driven by the PR‐STH2 promoter but did not show any obvious transactivating ability to the promoters of any of the other tested functional genes involved in fruit ripening and defence signalling, such as aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylic acid synthase (ACS2), ACS4, aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylate oxidase 1 (ACO1), never‐ripe (NR), ethylene insensitive 3 (EIN3), pectin methylesterase (PME2.1), endo‐1,4‐β‐glucanases (CEL), expansion 1 (EXP1), phytoene synthase 1 (PSY1), PSY2, LoxD, AOC, AOS, OPR3, and CYP94C1 (Figure  ).
 

## Discussion
Fruits become more susceptible to pathogens when they ripen, although ripening regulators are important not only for ripening progression but also impact defence responses. In this study, we investigated whether the ripening regulators NOR and RIN play a role in the defence response in B. cinerea‐infected tomato fruit. Our results showed that both NOR and RIN promote the accumulation of JA‐Ile induced by B. cinerea infection (Figure  ). They physically interact with MYC2 (Figure  ), the hub regulator in JA‐mediated defence signalling in B. cinerea‐infected tomato fruit, and enhance downstream MYC2‐transactivation of ERF.F4 (Figure  ). ERF.F4 further transactivates the expression of PR‐STH2 (Figure  ), a known response gene. These results indicate that, in addition to their established roles in regulating ripening (Gao et al.,  , ; Li et al.,  ), NOR and RIN participate in modulating the defence response in ripening fruit (Cantu et al.,  ; Silva et al.,  ; Zheng et al.,  ) by interacting with a multiprotein transcription complex which includes MYC2 (Figures , ).
 

## A MYC2 protein complex affects both pathogen resistance, ripening, and fruit quality
Our evidence shows that MYC2 protein plays a central role in regulating gene transcription in JA‐mediated defence responses in tomato fruits, and this confirms previously published work in Arabidopsis plants and tomato leaves (Zhai et al.,  ). In the defence response pathway, COI1 perceives JA‐Ile and forms a Skp‐Cullin‐F‐box (SCF) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex SCFCol1, releasing MYC2 from the JAZ‐NINJA‐TPL repressor complex (Thines et al.,  ; Zhai et al.,  ). MYC2 does not act alone but interacts with other TFs and co‐regulators, such as MED25 as coactivator and JAZs and TARGETED BHLH (MTB) protein as co‐repressors, and this complex modulates JA accumulation and defence response (Du et al.,  ; Liu et al.,  ). Additionally, MED25 also interacts in tomato fruit with several other factors that modulate both pathogen infection response and ripening, including a B‐box TF SlBBX20, which attenuates JA signalling in response to B. cinerea and also increases fruit yield (Luo et al.,  ), and with the ethylene signalling components EIL1‐4 (Deng et al.,  ). EIL1 also directly activates CYP94C1, encoding an enzyme involved in JA‐mediated fruit resistance to grey mould during ripening (Yang et al.,  ). EIN3 and EIN3‐Like (EILs) are also involved directly in ethylene signalling and regulation of tomato ripening gene expression (Gao et al.,  ; Klee,  , ; Lv et al.,  ). In this study, we demonstrated the interaction between NOR and RIN with MYC2, MED25 and some JAZ proteins (Figure  ) and showed the impact on gene expression. It is known that RIN is indispensable for system‐2 ethylene production (Li et al.,  ), and RIN plus ethylene form a positive feedback loop in fruit ripening regulation (Lv et al.,  ). Moreover, ethylene contributes to pathogen resistance in fruits by activating JA and/or ethylene responses (Blanco‐Ulate et al.,  ). All these findings could be explained by proposing that a regulatory protein complex composed of hub factors RIN‐MYC2‐MED25 governs the expression of genes involved in both fruit ripening and pathogen defence responses.
There is other evidence indicating a role for MYC2 in regulating fruit susceptibility to B. cinerea infection in a ripening‐related way. For example, MYC2 regulates flavonoid and phenolic acids accumulation in tomato fruit, which affects the infection response (Min et al.,  ; Shu et al.,  ). A MYC2‐GAME9 transcriptional complex regulates the transcription of GAME cluster genes controlling accumulation of steroidal glycoalkaloids (SGAs), which are poisonous compounds considered as preformed phytoalexins that are active against pathogens in tomato (Bai et al.,  ). MYC2 also directly functions in fruit quality formation during ripening, and is involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis in sweet cherry fruit (Chen et al.,  ), red‐fleshed apple fruit (Keller‐Przybylkowicz et al.,  ), and is also highly correlated with monoterpenoids accumulation in pomelo (Citrus maxima Merr.) fruit (Xiang et al.,  ). MYC2 also interacts with SlMsrB5, a methionine sulfoxide reductase, and regulates the expression of autophagy‐related genes (SlATGs) and post‐harvest fruit defence responses (Min et al.,  ). Thus, MYC2 contributes to both ripening changes and the defence responses to B. cinerea infection in ripening fruits by interacting with several components of a multiprotein transcription complex.

## 
NOR and RIN regulate multiple ripening genes that contribute to fruit susceptibility to pathogen defence but also upregulate defence responses in conjunction with MYC2

Fleshy fruits normally become more susceptible to pathogen infection when they ripen, due to the complex changes including sugar accumulation, cell wall modification and breakdown, cellular pH, contents of primary and secondary metabolites, ROS status, lipid peroxidation, and protein oxidation, all of which increase susceptibility to infection. However, active defence mechanisms continue to function in ripe fruits and are influenced by ripening (reviewed in Li et al.,  ). Fruits of the naturally occurring tomato ripening mutants nor and rin show defects in the expression of specific ripening‐related genes and fail to undergo multiple changes associated with ripening (Klee and Giovannoni,  ). The nor natural mutant fruits are much more resistant than WT fruits to infection by pathogens (Cantu et al.,  ), while rin fruits show a significantly higher level of disease severity compared with WT, following infection by B. cinerea (Cantu et al.,  ; Silva et al.,  ). In this study, both ripening progression and resistance to B. cinerea infection of tomato fruit were modified by either single or double mutant NOR and RIN knock‐outs (CR‐NOR, CR‐RIN, CR‐NOR/RIN) (Figure  ). These findings indicate that ripening regulators in fleshy fruits are not only important for ripening progression (Li et al.,  , ) but also affect fruit defence responses to pathogens. Thus, mutations in some ripening‐related genetic regulators (NOR and RIN) will enhance resistance, while mutations in others may promote susceptibility to infection.
Ethylene is a hormone required for ripening initiation in tomato (Grierson,  ) and RIN is an indispensable factor for system‐2 ethylene formation (Li et al.,  ), while both RIN and NOR are required for ripening progression (Gao et al.,  ; Li et al.,  ). RIN and NOR also directly regulate expression of multiple functional genes and TFs involved in various aspects of ripening such as ethylene, texture, colour, aroma, taste, and nutritional content (Cao et al.,  ; Fujisawa et al.,  ; Gao et al.,  ). Fruits of Nr natural mutants, which retain ethylene biosynthesis capacity but have a deficiency in ethylene signal transduction (Klee and Giovannoni,  ), are much more resistant to B. cinerea infection (Cantu et al.,  ), and ethylene treatment also induces resistance in Nr fruit (Díaz et al.,  ). Ethylene also contributes to fruit pathogen resistance by activating JA and/or ethylene responses and may also modulate the antagonistic effect of SA on JA signalling (Blanco‐Ulate et al.,  ). Ethylene perception promotes JA signalling in tomato fruit, which is impaired by 1‐methylcyclopropene (1‐MCP), an ethylene action inhibitor (Yu et al.,  ). The ripening changes promoted by NOR, RIN, and other TFs also require ethylene, and many of these changes improve fruit quality but also increase susceptibility to pathogens (Prusky and Romanazzi,  ). Thus, as well as promoting ripening, RIN and NOR play an important role in defence response in B. cinerea‐infected fruit (Figure  ). RIN also modulates disease resistance in roots, where it facilitates the recruitment of microbiota that inhibit the soil‐borne pathogenic bacterium Ralstonia solanacearum (Yang et al.,  ). This suggests a wider role for RIN in ameliorating plant disease in other organs, in addition to fruit.

## Different ERFs regulate response to ethylene and ripening, response to infection, wounding, and other hormones
JA is the major defence phytohormone that acts against B. cinerea infection in tomato, and pathogens induce the accumulation of bioactive JA‐Ile (Du et al.,  ). JA‐Ile level is determined by biosynthesis, and the interconversion between JA and JA‐Ile and some other forms (Zhai et al.,  ). It has been reported that genes such as AOC, AOS, OPR3, and LoxD are involved in JA or JA‐Ile biosynthesis, and genes such as CYP94C1, JAR1, and ILL3 are involved in JA and JA‐Ile conversion (Zhai et al.,  ). LoxD has previously been implicated in defence, and its overexpression is known to increase endogenous JA and resistance to the fungal pathogen Cladosporium fulvum (Hu et al.,  ; Yan et al.,  ). However, the detailed mechanisms of how the protein complex composed of NOR, RIN, and the hub factor MYC2 regulates the expression of genes involved in JA or JA‐Ile de novo biosynthesis and conversions in tomato fruit need further in‐depth clarification.
Ripening regulators NOR and RIN strongly promote transactivation of MYC2 and activation of defence responses (Figures  , ). The MYC2‐NOR/RIN protein complex functions to promote transcriptional activation of both fruit ripening genes and resistance genes. This must also involve interactions with other participants in transcription, including RNA polymerase II (Pol II), general TFs and networks of gene‐specific TFs, interacting with the mediator complex that modulate these complex processes (Chen et al.,  ; Li et al.,  ). ERFs also play a pivotal role in fruit ripening and also in response to pathogens (Li et al.,  ), responding to different hormones and stresses, acting downstream of RIN, NOR, ethylene and MYC2. Tomato ERF.C1 has been reported to form an ethylene‐MPK8‐ERF.C1‐PR module that functions in resisting B. cinerea infection in fruit (Deng et al.,  ), but the question of whether these proteins also complexed with MYC2 was not investigated. We found that MYC2, NOR, and RIN could all transactivate ERF.C1 by 1.6‐, 3.1‐ and 1.8‐fold respectively (Figure  ). Several investigations have shown that ERFs, such as ERF15 and ERF16, are involved in regulating JA biosynthesis and the wound response (Hu et al.,  , ), and SlERF2 (ERF.E1) promotes PR1 accumulation and reinforces B. cinerea infection‐induced JA signalling (Yu et al.,  ). ERFs have also been reported to be involved in other hormones signalling (Li et al.,  ). Pti4 (ERF.A3), which acts downstream of ethylene and ABA signalling, plays a major role in B. cinerea‐infected tomato fruit (Sun et al.,  ). Several other ERFs including A1, B2 (SlERF5), B4, C3, and F5 (SlERF3b) play regulatory roles in the SA, JA, and ethylene signalling pathways in response to B. cinerea infection in tomato (Álvarez‐Gómez et al.,  ; Ouyang et al.,  ). Previously, ERF.F4 has been reported to be negatively correlated with flavonoid biosynthesis and has been proposed to be a candidate regulator in both ripening and B. cinerea‐infected tomato fruit (Li et al.,  ). We found that MYC2 transactivated ERF.F4 and this transactivating ability was enhanced by both NOR and RIN TFs in B. cinerea‐infected fruit (Figure  ). This indicates, first, that ERF.F4 is an important defence response gene and, secondly, that its expression is directly activated by a complex involving MYC2, RIN and NOR.

## Conclusions
Our results show that ripening‐induced defence signalling involves activation of ERF.F4 by a MYC2‐NOR/RIN protein complex in response to B. cinerea infection in tomato fruits. Both ripening regulators NOR and RIN enhance JA‐Ile accumulation in the defence response through physical interaction with MYC2. Together with other co‐regulators, such as MED25 as coactivator and JAZs as co‐repressor, they transactivate downstream defence genes. A model outlining these interactions is shown in Figure  . Understanding the roles of RIN and NOR in defence provides important clues for improving fruit ripening quality and defence against infection. A major challenge for the future is to adopt whole nuclear proteome Co‐IP identification to clarify the role and significance of specific protein complexes underlying the signalling interactions in ripening and defence.

## Experimental procedures

## Plant materials and growth conditions
Wild‐type (WT) tomato (Solanum lycopersicum Ailsa Craig) from TGRC (https://tgrc.ucdavis.edu/), NOR or RIN knockout lines (CR‐NOR, CR‐RIN) were generated previously (Gao et al.,  ; Li et al.,  ), and the double mutant CR‐NOR/RIN hybrid was generated by crossing CR‐NOR and CR‐RIN plants. WT, transgenic, and mutant seedlings were grown in a greenhouse under long‐day conditions (16 h:8 h, light:dark photoperiod) at a temperature of 26 °C.

## Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR)
Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol extraction method, and RNA quality and quantity were confirmed by spectrophotometry (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA, NanoDrop 1000). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qPCR was conducted using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) with a CFX Opus 96 (Bio‐Rad, CA, USA). Relative gene expression values were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen,  ). At least three biological replicates were included, and Primer pairs for qPCR are listed in Table  .

## Plasmid construction and plant transformation
The target sites selected for ERF.F4 mutagenesis via CRISPR‐P program (http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/cgi‐bin/CRISPR) (Figure  ), were cloned into sgRNA cassettes and pYLCRISPR/Cas9‐Ubi‐H binary plasmid by Golden Gate ligation (Ma et al.,  ). Agrobacterium tumefaciens‐mediated transfer of T‐DNA was used for stable transformation of tomato (Kimura and Sinha,  ; Sun et al.,  ). For the genetic analysis, genomic DNA was extracted from young tomato leaves using a Plant Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) and used as a template to amplify the ERF.F4 fragment using PCR and the fragments were verified by sequencing. The homozygous mutant lines of ERF.F4 knockout (CR‐ERF.F4) were selected and cultivated in a greenhouse for further research. For the construction of MYC2‐GFP transgenic overexpression lines (MYC2‐GFP‐OE), the full‐length sequence of MYC2 gene body without stop codon was fused to the GFP gene and then inserted into the pCAMBIA1300‐221 vector. The resulting pCAMBIA1300‐221‐MYC2‐GFP plasmid was transformed into different genetic backgrounds including tomato WT, CR‐NOR, and CR‐RIN using Agrobacterium tumefaciens‐mediated stable genetic transformation as mentioned above, to obtain transgenic WT 35S::MYC2‐GFP, CR‐NOR 35S::MYC2‐GFP, and CR‐RIN 35S::MYC2‐GFP materials. Positive transgenic lines were identified by PCR amplification using primers targeting the Hygromycin B (HYG) resistance gene and the homozygous lines were cultured in a greenhouse for further use.

## Fungal culture and fruit inoculation

Botrytis cinerea (strain: ACCC 36028) was obtained from the Agricultural Culture Collection of China. The fungus was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 26 °C and 90% relative humidity (RH). Spore suspensions were prepared and diluted with sterile water. Fruits with uniform shape, colour, size without physical injuries were harvested at breaker stage and surface‐sterilized with 2% sodium hypochlorite, washed three times with water and air‐dried. Uniform wounds (2 mm width × 4 mm depth) were created at the equator of fruit. Subsequently, 5 μL of B. cinerea suspension (1 × 106 conidia/L) were pipetted into the wound (sterile water as control). Inoculated fruits were stored at 26 °C and 90% RH. The experiment included three biological replicates with 20 fruits per replicate. At 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 days post‐inoculation (dpi), a 1 cm region of pericarp tissues (both around and below the infection site) was sampled and frozen rapidly in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

## 
JA‐Ile level measurement
Extraction and measurement of JA‐Ile were performed following established protocols (Fu et al.,  ; Liu et al.,  ) with slight modifications. Briefly, five volumes of ice‐cold 90% methanol, were supplemented with JA‐d5 as an internal standard. The homogenates were centrifuged twice at 20 000 
g
, and the supernatants were subjected to ultra‐performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis (Heitz et al.,  ).

## 
RNA‐seq and data analysis
Total RNA was extracted using Plant RNA TransZol Kit (TransGen, Beijing, China). A total of 2 μg of purified RNA samples were employed for the construction of RNA‐seq libraries by using the NEBNext ultra‐RNA Library Prepare Kit (NEB, MA, USA). The resulting libraries were subjected to high‐throughput sequencing using an Illumina HiSeq2000 sequencer. Raw sequencing reads containing adapters and low‐quality bases were filtered out using the FASTQ_Quality_Filter tool (available at http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toollkit). The resulting high‐quality clean reads were aligned to the tomato reference genome (build_SL2.4, accessible at ftp://ftp.solgenomics.net/tomato_genome/) using the HISAT2 software (version 2.2.1) (Kim et al.,  ). Uniquely mapped reads were assigned to corresponding genes using Feature Counts (version 2.0.14) (Liao et al.,  ). Gene transcripts were quantified using StringTie and ballgown (Pertea et al.,  ) and normalized as FPKM (fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DEseq2 algorithm with the following thresholds: a fold change ≥2 and an adjusted P‐value <0.05 (Love et al.,  ). Functional annotation and pathway analysis of DEGs were performed using the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes) database (https://www.kegg.jp/).

## Yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assays
CDS of tomato MYC2, JAZ, MED25 were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector, and NOR and RIN were cloned into pGADT7 vector. Yeast two‐hybrid assays were conducted using the Matchmaker GAL4 Two‐Hybrid System (Clontech, CA, USA). Constructs designed to test interactions were co‐transformed into yeast strain Saccharomyces cerevisiae AH109. As a negative control, the empty pGBKT7 vector was co‐transformed in parallel. To evaluate protein interactions, the transformed yeast cells were plated on SD/‐4 (lacking adenine, histidine, leucine, and tryptophan). Interactions were assessed after incubating at 30 °C for 3 days.

## Yeast three‐hybrid (Y3H) assays
The MATCHMAKER GAL4 Two‐Hybrid System (Clontech) was selected for Y3H assay. To generate the pBridge‐MYC2‐MED25 construct, the MYC2 CDS was cloned into the multiple cloning site (MCS) I of the pBridge vector (Clontech), fused to the GAL4 BD domain, and MED25 was cloned into MCS II of the same pBridge vector. NOR or RIN was expressed as the bridge protein to examine their influence on the MYC2‐MED25 interaction. Similarly, for the pBridge‐MYC2‐JAZ1/6 construct, the CDS of MYC2 was inserted into MCS I, and JAZ1 or JAZ6 into MCS II of the pBridge vector. NOR/RIN was expressed as the bridge protein to assess its effect on the MYC2‐JAZ1/6 interaction. The constructs were co‐transformed into yeast AH109, and transformed yeast cells were plated on SD/‐2 and SD/‐4 medium, and growth was observed.

## Luciferase complementation assay (LCA)
For the LCA assay, the CDS of MYC2, MED25 and NOR, RIN were cloned into the binary vector pSPYNE‐35S and pSPYCE‐35S to generate the constructs pSPYNE‐MYC2/MED25 (MYC2‐nLUC, MED25‐nLUC) and pSPYCE‐NOR/RIN (NOR‐cLUC, RIN‐cLUC), respectively. The re‐constructs were transformed into the A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and cultured at 28 °C overnight. The bacterial cells were collected and suspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, pH 5.6, 10 mM MgCl2, and 150 mM acetosyringone) to a final OD600 of 0.8. Then, the Agrobacterium carrying pSPYNE‐MYC2 or pSPYNE‐MED25 and that carrying pSPYCE‐NOR or pSPYCE‐RIN plasmid were mixed in a ratio of 1:1 (v/v) and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves and observed after 2 days using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)
CDS of NOR, RIN and MYC2, MED25 were cloned into pCAMBIA1300‐221‐6MYC, pCAMBIA1300‐221‐3FLAG, and pCAMBIA1300‐221‐GFP vectors to generate the p35S:NOR‐6MYC, p35S:RIN‐3FLAG, p35S:MYC2‐GFP, p35S:MED25‐GFP constructs. These were transferred into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, and injected into N. benthamiana leaves for transient protein expression. Infected leaves were collected 48 h after infiltration, then ground in liquid nitrogen and mixed with three volumes of IP buffer (40 mM Tris–HCl with pH 7.5, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.2% (v/v) Triton X‐100, 1 mM PMSF, 2% Glycerol, and proteinase inhibitor), incubated on ice for 30 min, then centrifugation twice at 16 000 
g
 at 4 °C, 15 min each time. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, 100 μL of the supernatant was taken as the input, and the remaining material was immunoprecipitated with beads conjugated with anti‐GFP antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 4 °C for 3 h. The incubation was washed with IP buffer five times, then mixed with the SDS loading buffer and boiled to recover immunoprecipitated proteins. Finally, samples including input and immunoprecipitated protein were subjected to Western blot assay with the anti‐MYC antibody, anti‐FLAG antibody or anti‐GFP antibody (CWBio, Jiangsu, China). The experiments were repeated three times.

## Pull‐down assay
Coding sequences of MYC2, MED25, JAZ1, JAZ6 were cloned into pGEX‐6P‐1, pET‐32a, pMAL‐c2X vectors to generate pGEX‐GST‐MYC2, pET‐His‐MED25, pET‐His‐JAZ1, pET‐His‐JAZ6, pMAL‐MBP‐RIN, pMAL‐MBP‐NOR constructs. The recombinant vectors were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 cells. GST‐MYC2, His‐MED25, His‐JAZ1, His‐JAZ6, MBP‐RIN, and MBP‐NOR fusion proteins were expressed by adding IPTG and purified by resin columns.
To detect the interactions between RIN, MYC2, and MED25, in vitro pull‐down assays were performed by adding purified GST‐MYC2, His‐MED25, and MBP‐RIN proteins and increase concentrations of His‐MED25 and MBP‐RIN fusion proteins. The GST Bind Resin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to pull down proteins. Briefly, for each reaction, 15 μL aliquots of agarose beads bound with 1 μg GST‐MYC2, His‐MED25 were incubated with 1 μg MBP‐RIN in 1 mL of reaction buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitor) at 4 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, beads were washed three times with washing buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT) and collected. Samples were denatured using SDS loading buffer, and the GST‐MYC2, His‐MED25, MBP‐RIN fusion protein was detected via anti‐GST, anti‐His, and anti‐MBP antibodies, respectively, by immunoblotting. Purified GST, or single fusion proteins were used as negative controls. The effect of NOR on MYC2‐JAZ1 and the effect of NOR on MYC2‐JAZ6 interaction were detected similarly.

## 
CUT&Tag assay and data analysis
This was according to the published protocol (Deng et al.,  ); briefly, transgenic WT 35S::MYC2‐GFP, CR‐NOR 35S::MYC2‐GFP, and CR‐RIN 35S::MYC2‐GFP fruits were harvested at Br + 5 stage. CUT&Tag assays were conducted following the manufacturer's protocol using the Hyperactive In‐Situ ChIP Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme). Primary antibody incubation was performed with an anti‐GFP antibody (Abcam) at 4 °C overnight, followed by 1 h incubation with a Sheep anti‐Rabbit IgG antibody at room temperature. The CUT&Tag libraries were prepared using the Vazyme TruePrep Index Kit V2 and the TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme). PCR products were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, generating ~ 10 Gb of clean data per library. The clean data was converted to binary format, and uniquely mapped reads were filtered using SAMtools (version 1.14) (Li et al.,  ). MYC2‐binding peaks were identified using MACS (version 2.2.7.1) (Zhang et al.,  ), and annotated using ChIPseeker (version 1.22.1) (Yu et al.,  ). Binding motifs of MYC2 were determined using MEME Suite (version 5.4.1) (Bailey,  ).

## 
ChIP‐qPCR assay
ChIP assays were conducted following a previously established protocol (Zhu et al.,  ). Briefly, 10 g of MYC2‐GFP‐OE fruit tissue was cross‐linked using 1% (v/v) formaldehyde under vacuum for 10 min. Tissues were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. Chromatin complexes were isolated, sonicated, and immunoprecipitated using polyclonal anti‐GFP antibodies (Abcam). The ChIP DNA and input DNA were recovered and dissolved in water for subsequent ChIP‐qPCR analysis. For ChIP‐qPCR, the immunoprecipitated DNA was amplified using specific primer pairs (listed in Table  ). Each ChIP signal was normalized to its corresponding input. The fold enrichment of each target promoter was calculated relative to the ACTIN promoter as a control. Data from three independent biological experiments were analysed.

## Dual‐luciferase reporter assay
As previously described (Gao et al.,  ), CDS of NOR, RIN, MYC2, or ERF.F4 were cloned into the pEAQ vector as effectors (Peyret and Lomonossoff,  ). Promoters of target genes were inserted into the pGreenII‐0800‐LUC double reporter vector, fused to the LUC reporter gene. A REN luciferase construct under the control of the 35S promoter in the same vector served as an internal control (Hellens et al.,  ). Effector and reporter plasmids were transfected into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 (harbouring the pSoup plasmid) and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. LUC/REN activities were measured 2–5 days post‐infiltration using dual‐luciferase assay kits (Promega, Wisconsin, USA), with at least eight replicates per assay.

## Promoter sequence and motif assay
Promoter sequences (2.0 kb) and GCC‐box, cis‐element binding site for ERFs (Licausi et al.,  ) were from Sol Genomics Network (https://solgenomics.net/).

## Data analysis
Data analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics and Microsoft Excel (2013) software. Multiple comparisons were subjected to ANOVA analysis using Duncan's multiple range test, and significant differences are indicated with different letters (P < 0.05). Data are displayed as images by GraphPad Prism (Version 8.3.0).
