## Background
Low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma (LGESS) is a malignant stromal tumor characterized by an indolent clinical course, often with late recurrence or distant metastasis after a prolonged period of remission. These tumors can exhibit various lineage differentiations, making diagnosis challenging, especially in cases of remote recurrence. Most of these tumors are driven by fusions involving the JAZF1, SUZ12, and/or PHF1 genes.

## Materials and Methods
A case of metastatic endometrial stromal sarcoma was collected. Clinicopathologic and molecular features were documented.

## Results
A senior lady with a remote history of an unknown uterine neoplasm. Imaging of the chest revealed bilateral, enlarging pulmonary nodules, which were histologically and immunohistochemically characterized as mesenchymal-like neoplastic cells with sex cord and neuroendocrine differentiation. RNA-based next-generation sequencing identified a complex JAZF1::DLG5::PHF1 fusion, confirming a diagnosis of metastatic endometrial stromal sarcoma with sex cord and neuroendocrine differentiation.

## Conclusion
This report underscores the propensity of low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma to metastasize after a prolonged period of remission, in which the tumor exhibited sex cord and unique neuroendocrine differentiation. We also present a complex fusion in which the DLG5 gene acts as a ‘filler’ to maintain the in-frame configuration.

## Introduction
Metastatic low-grade endometrial stromal sarcomas (LGESS) present significant diagnostic challenges due to their indolent behavior and diverse morphologic patterns. Originating from the endometrial stroma, these tumors often exhibit considerable morphologic overlap with other neoplasms [ – ]. Accurate diagnosis of metastatic foci is crucial, as the management typically involves a combination of surgical resection and hormonal therapy [ – ]. 
Molecular alterations frequently identified in LGESS include JAZF1, SUZ12, or PHF1 gene rearrangements [ , ]. PHF1 gene fusions are known to induce sex cord-like differentiation, while JAZF1 gene fusions are rarely seen outside the context of endometrial stromal sarcoma [ , ]. These alterations can be particularly useful in resolving diagnostic ambiguity.
In this article, we present a unique case of metastatic endometrial stromal sarcoma with sex cord differentiation and a neuroendocrine subcomponent, in which a complex JAZF1::DLG5::PHF1 fusion was identified. The DLG5 gene fusion has not previously been described in the literature in the context of endometrial stromal sarcoma.

## Materials and Methods
Clinical data—including patient clinical presentation, anatomic site, available imaging, treatment, and follow-up information—were obtained through chart review. Hematoxylin and eosin-stained slides from the resection specimen were reviewed. Immunohistochemical staining was performed with appropriate controls. Molecular testing was conducted using an RNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) sarcoma fusion panel (Archer FusionPlex Pan Solid Tumor Panel). Reads from cDNA libraries were mapped to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37). Unidirectional gene-specific primers targeting specific exons in 127 genes were used to detect both known and novel oncogenic fusions in solid tumors.

## Results

## Clinical Presentation and Imaging Studies
An elderly lady had a remote history of an unknown uterine neoplasm more than 35 years ago, treated with total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy followed by radiation therapy. She also had prior diagnoses of skin malignancy and invasive breast carcinoma. More recently, she developed colonic malignancy. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis performed at that time revealed two bilateral enlarging pulmonary nodules (Fig. ). She subsequently underwent a wedge resection of one of the pulmonary nodules.

 

## Histopathologic and Immunohistochemical Findings
Microscopic examination (Fig. ) of the resected left lung wedge showed a well-circumscribed, unencapsulated neoplasm with mixed architectural patterns. The predominant morphology consisted of cords of polygonal mesenchymal-like neoplastic cells with intervening densely hyalinized and sclerotic stroma. Additionally, multiple areas of plexiform cords separated by hyalinized stroma, a sertoliform component, sex cord stroma-like growth pattern, and trabecular growth pattern were present. The neoplastic cells in most areas were small to medium-sized and contained moderate amounts of pale eosinophilic cytoplasm, mild nuclear hyperchromasia, inconspicuous nucleoli, and rare nuclear grooves. There was no necrosis or high-grade nuclear atypia. Mitotic activity was low. The resection margins were clear.

 

The tumor exhibited a polyphenotypic immunohistochemical profile (Fig. ). All components were diffusely positive for estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and smooth muscle actin (SMA). The plexiform, sertoliform, and sex cord stroma-like components were weakly positive for cytokeratin AE1/AE3 (CK AE1/AE3) and cytokeratin CAM 5.2 (CK CAM 5.2). Desmin and caldesmon were diffusely positive in plexiform and mesenchymal-like areas and negative in sertoliform, trabecular, and sex cord stroma-like areas. Steroidogenic factor 1 (SF-1) was positive in mesenchymal-like areas and negative in other components. CD10 was positive in the sex cord stroma-like areas and showed patchy positivity in other components. Synaptophysin and chromogranin-A were strongly positive in trabecular areas and negative in other components. Insulinoma-associated protein 1 (INSM1) was weakly positive in all components, including the trabecular areas. All components were negative for p63, HMB-45, Melan-A, SOX10, S100, PAX-8, GATA-3, TTF-1, calretinin, and inhibin.

 

## Molecular Findings
Targeted RNA sequencing revealed a complex in-frame gene fusion transcript involving JAZF1 exon 2 (NM_175061.4), DLG5 exon 2 (NM_004747.4), and PHF1 exon 2 (NM_002636.5) (chr7:28031528::chr10:79628955|chr10:79628887::chr6:33380025). In the predicted chimeric protein, the zinc finger domain (ZnF1) of JAZF1 is fused to DLG5 exon 2 (acting as a filler to keep the fusion in-frame) and downstream exons of PHF1 beginning with exon 2 (which includes the Tudor domain). ## Management and Clinical Follow-up
The patient underwent complete surgical resection of reminder pulmonary nodule with clear margins. Follow-up at 20 months post-surgery showed that she had recovered well. A CT scan demonstrated no evidence of new pulmonary nodules, lung consolidation, pneumothorax, or pleural effusion.

## Discussion
Low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma (LGESS) is a malignant stromal tumor characterized by cells that closely resemble proliferative phase endometrial stroma. It typically demonstrates infiltrative growth and commonly has associated vascular invasion. LGESS can exhibit various lineage differentiations, either focally or extensively, including smooth muscle, fibroblastic/fibromyxoid, sex-cord, osteoclast-like, adipocytic and rhabdoid [ – ]. It typically has relatively indolent behavior with a protracted clinical course, even in patients with recurrent disease. In addition to tumor resection, studies have reported that patients with LGESS respond well to hormonal therapy [ ]. LGESS has been found to metastasize to various locations, with the lungs being the most common site, followed by the pelvis, abdomen, adnexa, lymph nodes, bowel wall, and urinary bladder [ – ]. Unusual metastatic sites, such as the breast and inferior vena cava, have also been documented [ – ]. These unusual metastatic sites can be a diagnostic pitfall, as LGESS may closely mimic other tumors including tumors originating from these sites.
In our study, the metastatic tumor exhibited prominent sex cord–like differentiation. While this pattern is observed with some frequency in primary endometrial stromal tumors particularly LGESS, it is only rarely described in metastases [ ]. Notably, the tumor also demonstrated neuroendocrine differentiation, among other distinctive features. Neuroendocrine differentiation in endometrial stromal tumors is exceedingly uncommon—whether in primary or metastatic disease—and, to our knowledge, has been reported only once, by Maeda et al., who documented a 25-year clinical course of recurrent LGESS that evolved stepwise from conventional morphology to neuroendocrine tumor–like features and ultimately to adenocarcinoma-like differentiation [ ]. These observations underscore the plasticity of endometrial stromal tumors and the potential for significant diagnostic pitfalls.
Although a collision tumor cannot be entirely excluded, it is exceedingly rare; only a single case has been reported in which LGESS co-occurred with endometrial large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC). In that report, the LCNEC component showed myometrial invasion, lymphovascular invasion, and bilateral ovarian metastases, portending rapid recurrence and short survival. Immunophenotypically, the components were distinct: LGESS was positive for CD10, ER, and PR, whereas LCNEC was positive for cytokeratins, synaptophysin, chromogranin, and PAX8, demonstrated a high Ki-67 index, and was negative for CD10, ER, PR, and TTF-1[ ]. In our case, all components were ER and PR positive; CD10 showed variable positivity; TTF-1 was negative; and synaptophysin and chromogranin expression was confined to the neuroendocrine areas. Taken together with the molecular findings, these features support LGESS with neuroendocrine differentiation rather than a collision tumor.
Another diagnostic consideration for this tumor, based on the patient’s history and histomorphological features, is uterine tumors resembling ovarian sex cord tumors (UTROSCT). These are characterized a morphological resemblance to ovarian sex cord differentiation and a polyphenotypic immunoprofile with variable expression of ovarian sex cord–stromal immunohistochemical markers. Recent studies have shown that some UTROSCT have a propensity for late recurrence, metastasis, and unfavorable outcomes, leading to their classification as malignant UTROSCT [ ]. Several molecular alterations have been identified in association with UTROSCT, including ESR1 or GREB1 gene rearrangements with NCOA1/2/3, CTNNB1, NR4A3, and SS18 [ – , – ]. Notably, the vast majority of UTROSCT do not harbor molecular alterations typically seen in endometrial stromal tumors [ ]. In cases where the characteristic molecular profile is absent, diagnosis relies on histomorphology, immunohistochemical profile, and the exclusion of other diagnostic possibilities.
Another entity in the differential is ossifying fibromyxoid tumor, which can harbor PHF1 gene rearrangements. PHF1 fusions are thought to preferentially induce sex cord–like differentiation [ ]. Although this tumor shows both sex cord–like differentiation and PHF1 gene rearrangement, the patient’s clinical history and overall context support a diagnosis of LGESS.
With regard to endometrial stromal tumors with JAZF1, SUZ12, and PHF1 gene fusions: these tumors are characterized by recurrent gene fusions that perturb chromatin-regulating complexes. The most common event is t(7;17)(p15;q21) JAZF1::SUZ12, first described in 2001 as JAZF1::JJAZ1. Functional studies show that this chimera dysregulates PRC2 (altered complex composition/occupancy) and reduces H3K27me3 at target loci—but not a uniform “global loss” by routine IHC—resulting in aberrant transcriptional programs [ , , – ]. The next most frequent class comprises 6p21 PHF1-rearranged tumors; among these, JAZF1::PHF1 [t(6;7)(p21;p15)] and EPC1::PHF1 are recurrent [ ]. Additional PHF1 partners include MEAF6::PHF1, and, more rarely, EPC2::PHF1, BRD8::PHF1, MBTD1::PHF1, and ING3::PHF1. [ – ] PHF1-rearranged tumors are often associated with fibromyxoid and/or sex cord–like differentiation [ ]. Rare JAZF1 partners in endometrial stromal tumors include BCORL1 and CDKN1A (p21), both showing LGESS morphology [ – ]. Beyond JAZF1 and PHF1, several non-JAZF1/PHF1 fusions have been reported in endometrial stromal tumors: MBTD1::CXorf67 (EZHIP) defines a distinct LGESS subset; MEAF6::SUZ12 has been described in LGESS; and EPC1-linked fusions with SUZ12, BCOR, EED, and EZH2 have been reported (often associated with aggressive behavior and/or morphology) [ , – ]. Additionally, fusions involving JAZF1, SUZ12, and PHF1 outside the context of endometrial stromal tumors have been documented in Müllerian adenosarcoma (JAZF1::BCORL1), TSC2-mutant uterine sarcomas with PEComa-like features (JAZF1::SUZ12), ossifying fibromyxoid tumor (most commonly EP400::PHF1), primary cardiac sarcoma (JAZF1::PHF1) and LGESS-like sarcoma in males (JAZF1::GLI3 and JAZF1::PHF1) [ , – ]. Tri-partite or “complex” fusions are exceptionally rare in the literature and in endometrial stromal tumors. In our case, targeted RNA-seq identified a complex JAZF1::DLG5::PHF1 fusion.
Discs large homolog 5 (DLG5) is part of the membrane-associated guanylate kinase (MAGUK) superfamily, which functions as scaffolding molecules at sites of cell-cell contact. The DLG5 gene plays a key role in transmitting extracellular signals to the cytoskeleton and maintaining epithelial cell structure. It influences cancer progression through direct or indirect interactions with β-catenin, E-cadherin, Vimentin, p53, P21, Cyclin D1, TGF-β1, AKT, Hippo, and classic G protein signaling pathways. Additionally, DLG5 has been found to have a dual role in various cancers, including pancreatic adenocarcinoma and glioblastoma [ , ]. However, DLG5 has not previously been associated with endometrial stromal sarcomas. Most likely, the DLG5 sequence functions as a “filler,” keeping the fusion in frame, thereby resulting in a functional protein in the novel JAZF1::DLG5::PHF1 fusion.
In summary, we present a case of metastatic low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma with sex cord and neuroendocrine differentiation, harboring a complex JAZF1::DLG5::PHF1 fusion. To our knowledge, this is the first report within the endometrial stromal tumor spectrum demonstrating a JAZF1::DLG5::PHF1 fusion.
