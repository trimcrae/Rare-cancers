## ABSTRACT
Pleomorphic adenoma (PA) is the most common salivary gland tumor. PAs are characterized by chromosomal rearrangements of 8q12 and 12q14‐15, leading to gene fusions involving the PLAG1 and HMGA2 oncogenes. Here, we performed the first comprehensive study of the transcriptomic and gene fusion landscape of 38 cytogenetically characterized PAs. RNA‐seq identified PLAG1 or HMGA2 fusions in 33/38 cases (87%), of which 15 were novel fusions. Fusions were found also in tumors with normal karyotype, demonstrating that they are generated by cryptic rearrangements. PLAG1 was mainly activated by promoter swapping and HMGA2 by truncation of its 3′‐part. RNA‐seq revealed upregulation of genes involved in extracellular matrix production, WNT‐signaling, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition in PA compared to normal salivary tissue. Principal component analysis identified two PA subclusters characterized by PLAG1‐ and HMGA2‐activation, respectively, that differed in expression of genes involved in the immune system, cell adhesion, and microenvironment remodeling. Moreover, comparative analyses of PA and salivary carcinomas revealed that PA resembles myoepithelial carcinoma. Our study reveals new oncogenic gene fusions and expands our knowledge about the molecular underpinnings of PA.
Keywords: gene fusion, HMGA2, PLAG1, pleomorphic adenoma, transcriptomic profiling

## Introduction
Pleomorphic adenoma (PA) is the most common type of salivary gland tumor and can occur at all ages [ ]. Although it is a benign tumor, PA may occasionally recur and/or undergo malignant transformation to carcinoma‐ex‐PA [ ]. Histopathologically, PAs are characterized by architectural pleomorphism with myoepithelial and epithelial tumor cells in an often myxoid or chondroid stroma. The malignant component of carcinoma‐ex‐PA may be a salivary duct carcinoma (SDC), myoepithelial carcinoma (MECA), adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC), or any other salivary gland carcinoma subtype.
PA was the first benign tumor in which a tumor‐type specific chromosomal translocation was discovered [ ]. Comprehensive cytogenetic studies have revealed specific and recurrent patterns of chromosomal translocations with consistent breakpoints at 8q12 (39%) and 12q14‐15 (12%) [ , , , , ]. There are also subsets of tumors with trisomy 8 (2%), nonrecurrent translocations (17%), and normal karyotype (30%). Molecular analyses of translocation‐positive PAs have shown that they result in fusion genes involving the PLAG1 and HMGA2 oncogenes [ , , , , , ].

PLAG1 encodes a developmentally regulated DNA‐binding zinc finger transcription factor that is primarily expressed during embryogenesis. The expression is low or absent in most adult tissues, including salivary gland tissue [ , ]. Rearrangements of 8q12 lead to substitution of the low‐activity promoter of PLAG1 with a highly active promoter of the fusion partner gene (e.g., CTNNB1 or FGFR1), leading to PLAG1‐activation [ , , ]. HMGA2, a member of the high mobility group gene family, encodes an architectural transcription factor that is mainly expressed in embryonic tissues. Rearrangements of 12q14‐15 result in chimeric fusions consisting of the 5′‐part of HMGA2, encoding the AT hook DNA‐binding domains, and the 3′‐part of different fusion partner genes (e.g., WIF1 or NFIB) [ , , , ]. The rearrangements lead to loss of negative regulatory elements, including binding sites for the tumor suppressor microRNA let‐7, in the 3′‐part of HMGA2 and activation of the gene [ , ]. The tumorigenic properties of both PLAG1 and HMGA2 are well established both in vitro and in transgenic mouse models [ , , , , , ].
To further investigate the underlying pathogenetic mechanisms in PA, we have performed the first comprehensive study of the transcriptomic and gene fusion landscape of these tumors. RNA‐seq analysis revealed the molecular underpinnings of PA with activation of multiple oncogenic pathways as well as numerous novel gene fusion events involving PLAG1 and HMGA2. Comparison of the transcriptomic signatures of PA with those of different malignant salivary gland tumors revealed similarities to MECA and a spectrum of gene expression profiles that reflected the clinical aggressiveness of the tumors.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Tumor Material
Fresh‐frozen tumor tissue from 38 PAs were used in this study. All tumors showed the typical histological features of benign PAs. The clinical, cytogenetic, and molecular characteristics of the tumors are shown in Table  and in Table  .
 
2.2.

## Cytogenetic Analysis
Primary cultures were established from fresh, unfixed tumor specimens of 25 PAs (cases 1, 4, 8, 10, 11, 13, 15, 17–21, 23–29, 31–33, 35–37) as previously described [ ]. Chromosome preparations were made from primary cultures, and these were subsequently G‐banded and analyzed using standard cytogenetic procedures. At least 8 metaphases were karyotyped from each case. The cytogenetic findings from the remaining PAs (cases 2, 3, 5–7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 22, 30, 34, 38, 39) have previously been published [ , , , , , ].

2.3.

## 
RNA Sequencing (RNA‐Seq)
Total RNA was isolated from normal salivary gland (NSG) tissue (n = 7) and fresh‐frozen tumor tissue (n = 38) using the TRIzol reagent and chloroform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for RNA purification. RNA‐seq was performed with the INVIEW Transcriptome Discover package (Eurofins Genomics, Konstanz, Germany). Ribosomal RNA was depleted from total RNA prior to paired‐end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) using the Illumina sequencing platform. Bioinformatic analyses was done at UPPMAX (Uppsala Multidisciplinary Center for Advanced Computational Science, Sweden). Quality control of the raw data was done with FastQC (v0.11.5). Paired‐end reads were aligned to the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 37 (GRCh37/hg19) with STAR (v2.5.3a) and read summarization for exons with FeatureCounts/Subread (v1.5.2). Chimeric transcripts were detected with Star‐Fusion (v1.2.0), FusionCatcher (v1.00), and Arriba (v.1.1.0). The Integrative Genomics Viewer software (IGV, v2.4.10) [ ] was used to further analyze the fusion transcripts manually. The UCSC genome browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu) was used to investigate H3K27 acetylation marks associated with enhancer activity. The fusion gene status of four of the cases was previously published [ ].

2.4.

## Gene Expression Analyses
RNA‐seq count data were normalized using variance stabilizing transformation (VST) with the DESeq2 R package (v1.22.2) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were estimated. DEGs with a log2 fold change > 1 and an adjusted p < 0.01 (Benjamini–Hochberg) were considered significant. Estimation of statistically significant differences between groups was done with Kruskal–Wallis and Mann Whitney tests. The Chi square test was used to estimate the significance of overlap between the PA‐I and PA‐II clusters versus NSG. Hierarchical clustering was done with the R package stats, heatmap visualization with the Morpheus software (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus), and principal component analysis (PCA) with DESeq2. The ToppGene database (http://toppgene.cchmc.org) [ ] was used to identify gene ontologies (GO) linked to DEGs and gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) was done with the GSEA software (v.4.1.0) [ ]. Cell type enrichment was analyzed with xCell [ ]. Venn diagrams were done using the BioVenn website (https://www.biovenn.nl) [ ].

2.5.

## Comparative RNA‐Seq Analysis of Different Subtypes of Salivary Gland Tumors
Publicly available RNA‐seq datasets for ACC (n = 20), MECA (n = 40), and SDC (n = 16) were downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) via accession numbers PRJNA601423, PRJNA390610, and PRJNA361261. These cases, including clinical information, diagnostic criteria, gene fusion status, and gene expression pattern are previously described [ , , , , ]. Reads from all samples were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference human genome, excluding alternative haplotype regions, with HISAT v.0.1.6‐beta1. Alignments were converted to BAM format, sorted and indexed using Samtools v.0.1.192. Gene‐level read counts were quantified using HTSeq v. 0.6.03.
Nearest neighbor analysis was performed by calculating pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients of each PA sample and each of the samples in the above cohorts and considering the most strongly correlated sample. Diffusion map analysis was performed based on log2 (RPKM+1) transformed values using the destiny R package (v.3.4.0). Potential batch effects considered were material type (fresh frozen vs. formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissues), read length, total reads, and sex (Figure  ). Heatmaps of the expression of genes contributing to each of the first two diffusion components were made using ClusterExperiment (v.2.10.1). This was done with respect to the top 100 genes by p values obtained from LOESS regression (using the gam function from the R package of the same name; v.1.20) fitted on the relationships between the expression of each gene and a given diffusion component.

2.6.

## 
RT‐PCR and Nucleotide Sequence Analyses
cDNAs were synthesized from DNase‐treated total RNA using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA). cDNAs were amplified by PCR using gene‐specific primers designed to detect fusion transcripts identified by RNA‐seq. The primer sequences are listed in (Table  ) [ , ]. UBC expression was used as an endogenous control. Gel purification of PCR products was done with the QIAquick Gel Extraction kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), followed by SupremeRun Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics, Konstanz, Germany). Nucleotide sequences were aligned and analyzed with BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) and the SnapGene software (v.6.0) (GSL Biotech LLC, San Diego, CA) to GRCh37/hg19.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## 
RNA‐Seq Identifies a High Frequency of 
PLAG1
 and 
HMGA2
 Fusions in PA

RNA‐seq of 38 PAs identified fusions in 33 cases (87%) (Table  ). PLAG1 fusions were found in 13 cases and HMGA2 fusions in 17 cases (Figure  ). The most common gene fusion was HMGA2::WIF1, generated by intrachromosomal 12q rearrangements, identified in eight cases. One novel PLAG1 fusion (MYC::PLAG1) and eight novel HMGA2 fusions (HMGA2::AICDA, HMGA2::CADM2, HMGA2::AKAP9, HMGA2::FAM186B, HMGA2::SLC6A15, HMGA2::chr4:127,687,378, HMGA2::RP11‐766N7.3, and HMGA2::RP11‐362K2.2) were detected (Table  ). We also found one case each with novel NFIB::CHCHD7, NFIB::chr8:60,205,514, and chr8:57,061,364::CHCHD7 fusions (Table  ).
 In all HMGA2 fusions, the 3′‐part of HMGA2 was replaced with the 3′‐part of the fusion partner gene (Table  ). In five cases, the fusion points were located after the stop codon in the last exon of HMGA2, indicating that the fusions lead to expression of full‐length HMGA2 proteins. In the remaining cases, the fusion points in HMGA2 were located at the 3′‐end of exons 3 or 4, implying that C‐terminally truncated HMGA2 proteins are expressed. In two of these cases, a stop codon was found in frame within the fusion partner sequence close to the fusion point, whereas the other cases showed out of frame fusion partner transcripts.
There were five fusion‐negative PAs (cases 34–38), three of which had activation of HMGA2 and two activation of PLAG1, further supporting the notion that these genes may also be activated by other mechanisms such as for example enhancer hijacking [ ].
We also studied the expression PLAG1 and HMGA2 fusion partner genes in NSG (Figure  ). As expected, most PLAG1 fusion partners were expressed at high levels in NSG, whereas HMGA2 fusions partners were mainly expressed at very low levels.

3.2.

## The Transcriptomic Landscape of PA

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the 45 RNA‐seq samples revealed two distinct clusters corresponding to PA and NSG tissue (Figure  ). Subclustering of the PAs demonstrated that all but one case with HMGA2‐activation (defined as having an HMGA2 fusion and/or overexpression of HMGA2) clustered together. One subcluster included all 8 HMGA::WIF1 positive cases (Figure  ). Tumors in this subcluster showed increased expression of genes encoding nuclear orphan receptors (NR4A1, NR4A2, and NR4A3) and transcription factors that are part of the AP‐1 complex (JUN, JUND, and FOS) compared to other PAs. Downregulated genes included several genes within the epidermal growth factor receptor signaling pathway (EGFR, ERBB4, BTC, EGF, EREG, and TGFA). PLAG1‐activated tumors (defined as having a PLAG1 fusion and/or overexpression of PLAG1) formed two closely related clusters (except for one case clustering together with HMGA2‐activated tumors). PCA of the RNA‐seq samples revealed a distinct separation between PA and NSG tissue (Figure  ). In addition, there were two subclusters within PAs (PA‐I and PA‐II) characterized by PLAG1‐ and HMGA2‐activation, respectively (Figure  ). PLAG1 was significantly overexpressed in the PA‐I cluster compared to NSG and PA‐II tumors (Figure  ). Similarly, HMGA2 was significantly overexpressed in the PA‐II cluster compared to NSG and PA‐I tumors (Figure  ). Interestingly, we also detected a slight increase in PLAG1 expression in tumors in the PA‐II cluster (Figure  ), whereas the expression of HMGA2, with one exception, was limited to tumors in the PA‐II cluster (Figure  ).
 Next, we investigated differentially regulated genes between PA and NSG. HMGA2 and IGF2 (a downstream target of PLAG1) were among the most upregulated genes in PA (Table  ). PLAG1 was also significantly upregulated (p = 1.08 × 10−14) in PA, albeit at lower levels. GO analysis of genes upregulated in PA versus NSG also revealed increased expression of genes associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) production (Figure  ). Notably, eleven genes encoding ECM‐associated proteins (C1QL1, C1QL2, CHRDL2, COMP, CLEC3A, EPYC, ACAN, C1QL4, MUC2, MUC3A, HAPLN1) were among the top 50 upregulated genes (Table  ). In support of these findings, our results also showed that SOX6, a key gene in chondrocyte differentiation and transcriptional activation of genes encoding ECM‐proteins [ ], was significantly upregulated in PA (p = 1.73 × 10−14). Furthermore, several noncoding anti‐sense transcripts (e.g., AL109810.2) of genes that were upregulated in PA (e.g., TRIM67) were highly expressed (Figure  and Table  ), indicating regions with high transcriptional activity. GO analyses further revealed that genes associated with tissue development and WNT‐/cadherin‐signaling pathways were upregulated in PA (Figure  ). GSEA showed enrichment of epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) genes and genes involved in cell cycle regulation (Figure  ). Further analysis of known EMT marker genes [ ] confirmed that CDH1, CDH2, FN1, VIM, SNAI2, and TWIST1 were differentially expressed between PA and NSG (Figure  ). Moreover, xCell cell type enrichment analysis demonstrated a decrease in expression of genes associated with epithelial cells and an increase in expression of chondrocyte genes in PAs compared with NSG (Figure  ). The genes downregulated in PA were mainly associated with NSG functions such as secretion of saliva, antimicrobial peptides, and digestion of dietary carbohydrates (e.g., CA6, PRH2, HTN3, BPIFA2, and AMY1A) (Figure  and Table  ). In addition, multiple genes involved in mitochondrial function were downregulated (e.g., BHLHA15, COX4I1, and ETNPPL) and pathway analysis showed a decreased activity in oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and of the reactive oxygen species pathway (Figures  and and Table  ), indicating possible abnormalities in mitochondrial metabolism.
 To further analyze differences between PA‐I and PA‐II tumors, we studied their clinical, cytogenetic, and gene expression characteristics. There were no differences with regard to patient age (p = 0.25), tumor size (p = 0.22), karyotype (normal versus abnormal; p = 0.28), or the number of chromosomal aberrations (p = 0.36) between the two groups. We then investigated the overlap of up‐ and downregulated genes between PA‐I and PA‐II tumors versus NSG (Figure  ). There was a significant overlap (p < 0.0001) between PA‐I and PA‐II tumors and most genes showing a differential expression to NSG were shared. To identify molecular pathways unique to PA‐I and PA‐II tumors, we used GO analysis to study the nonoverlapping genes of the two clusters. PA‐I tumors specifically showed upregulation of genes involved in ECM organization (e.g., MMP14, NCAN, and EFEMP1) and collagen remodeling (e.g., COL17A1, COL22A1, and P4HA3) compared with PA‐II tumors (Figure  ). Notably, there were no uniquely upregulated pathways in PA‐II tumors. In contrast, PA‐II tumors specifically showed downregulated expression of genes involved in cell adhesion (e.g., NRXN1, CLDN19, and L1CAM) and immune response (e.g., CXCL1, IL10RA, and LILRB1) compared with PA‐I tumors (Figure  ), which lacked uniquely downregulated pathways. xCell analysis showed that there were low levels of infiltrating immune cells in PA and that there was no significant difference in immune scores between PA‐I and PA‐II tumors (p = 0.07). These results indicate that the immune‐related genes that are downregulated in the PA‐II cluster are expressed by the tumor cells. Moreover, xCell analysis revealed that gene expression signatures associated with keratinocytes and fibroblasts were the only two out of 64 cell signatures that were significantly different between PA‐I and PA‐II tumors (Figure  ). GSEA confirmed that PA‐I tumors had increased expression of genes linked to keratinization (e.g., KRT5, SFN, and KRT17) and fibroblast proliferation (e.g., CD248, WNT5A, and FGF10) (Figure  ). Taken together, our results indicate that PA‐I and PA‐II tumors have highly similar transcriptomes but show differences in expression of certain cell type marker genes and genes involved in cell adhesion, microenvironment remodeling, and the immune system.
 
3.3.

## Comparative RNA‐Seq Analysis Reveals Similarities Between PA and MECA

PA may occasionally progress to carcinoma‐ex‐PA, where the malignant component may be a SDC, MECA, ACC, or any other salivary gland carcinoma [ ]. To gain further insights into the molecular similarities and/or differences between PA and different salivary gland carcinomas, we compared RNA‐seq data for our PA cohort with publicly available RNA‐seq datasets for SDC, MECA, and ACC. Notably, nearest neighbor analysis with Spearman correlations showed that all but one of the PAs resembled MECA (Figure  ). In line with this observation, diffusion map analysis showed a clear overlap between PAs and a subset of MECAs, but not with any of the other tumor subsets (Figure  ). This association was seen with both de novo MECA and MECA‐ex‐PA (Figure  ). Salivary gland tumors formed three separate clusters with PAs, the majority of MECAs, and some ACCs at one end of the spectrum, the remaining MECAs and ACCs in the middle, and the majority of SDCs at the other end of the spectrum. These were all clearly distinct from the NSG cluster. Importantly, we found no evident batch effects when comparing these cohorts (Figure  ). Analysis of the top genes contributing to the diffusion components showed that expression of genes involved in cell cycle regulation, chromatin assembly, and ECM organization were significantly associated with the diffusion pattern (Figures  and ). Such genes were progressively upregulated from NSG to PA and further on to MECA, ACC, and SDC along diffusion component 2. Genes involved in ribosome assembly and translation were increasingly activated along diffusion component 1 from PAs at one end of the spectrum to the highest expression in SDCs at the other end (Figures  and ). Genes involved in salivary gland secretion were downregulated in both benign and malignant tumors compared with NSG. Next, we analyzed PLAG1 expression in MECA, ACC, and SDC (Figure  ). PLAG1 expression was positively correlated with PLAG1 fusion status and was most prominent in MECA. PLAG1 was also overexpressed in two PLAG1 fusion‐positive SDCs that presumably represent SDC‐ex‐PA. ACCs, which all lacked PLAG1 fusions, had low levels of PLAG1 expression. Taken together, our results demonstrate similarities in gene expression patterns between PAs and other salivary gland carcinomas, particularly MECA.

## Discussion
The genomic hallmark of PAs are recurrent chromosomal rearrangements leading to activation of key oncogenic transcription factors through gene fusion [ , ]. Here, we for the first time used RNA‐seq to study the global gene expression profile of PA and to further investigate the prevalence and types of gene fusions in a large cohort of these tumors.
Analysis of the global gene expression profiles of PA versus NSG showed that numerous genes involved in ECM production were among the significantly upregulated genes in PA. GO analyses supported these findings by revealing pathways and biological processes involved in ECM organization. In addition, SOX6, encoding a key transcription factor involved in chondrocyte differentiation and regulation of ECM genes [ ], was significantly upregulated in PA. These findings are consistent with the well‐known histopathological and histochemical observations that PAs are ECM‐rich tumors [ ]. Furthermore, several tissue developmental pathways were activated (e.g., cartilage, skeletal, and neuronal) in PA and we also found activation of EMT by pathway analysis. Given the known similarities between several types of salivary and mammary tumors, we further investigated the expression of a set of eight genes involved in EMT in mammary stem cells and mammary tumor‐initiating cells [ ]. We found significant overexpression of six of these, which together with the pathway analysis indicate that PAs indeed show clear molecular evidence of EMT. Our findings are supported by previous studies showing expression of EMT markers at the protein level in PA [ , ]. Notably, the TWIST1 gene was downregulated in PAs. This is in line with previous results linking TWIST1 depletion to an increase in chondroid differentiation, a feature occasionally seen in PA [ ]. Similarly, xCell analysis of PAs showed increased expression of genes associated with chondrocytes. Taken together, our results reflect the molecular underpinnings of the morphology of PA with architectural pleomorphism and a prominent ECM component and provide the first comprehensive transcriptomic evidence of EMT in PA.
Genes involved in WNT−/cadherin‐signaling pathways were also upregulated in PAs. Notably, increased WNT‐signaling has previously also been found in PLAG1 induced salivary gland tumors in transgenic mouse models [ , ]. Interestingly, IGF2, a downstream target of PLAG1, has been implicated in activation of WNT‐induced EMT [ ]. Thus, a similar mechanism might drive cellular pluripotency in PA through PLAG1‐induced IGF2 expression and subsequent activation of WNT‐ and EMT‐pathways. This notion is supported by our finding that IGF2 is highly overexpressed in PA.
Salivary gland tumors are a heterogenous group of neoplasms with often overlapping histological patterns that may complicate the histopathological diagnosis [ , , ]. The present findings point to extensive similarities also at the transcriptomic level between different tumor types. PAs showed resemblance to MECA and several tumors from these two groups had similar expression profiles, irrespective of whether they were de novo MECA or MECA‐ex‐PA. This may, at least partly, be explained by the almost universal activation of PLAG1 or HMGA2 in both PA and MECA [ ] and the strong effects these genes have on the transcriptional program in these tumors. Diffusion map analyses further illustrated that genes involved in cell cycle progression and chromatin remodeling were increasingly upregulated from PAs at one end of the spectrum to SDCs at the other end. This likely reflects an increased aggressiveness of the tumors detectable also at the molecular level, a reasoning that is supported by the clinical follow‐up data publicly available for these tumor types [ ]. A limitation of our cross‐tumoral comparison is the inclusion of only four different subtypes of salivary gland tumors. Further studies, including additional subtypes of both benign and malignant salivary gland tumors, are urged to fully grasp the relationship between these enigmatic tumors.
RNA‐seq analysis revealed fusion events in 33 of the 38 analyzed PAs, including 16 fusions leading to PLAG1‐activation and 17 to HMGA2‐activation. More than half of these were formed by cryptic intra‐ or interchromosomal rearrangements. Importantly, 7 of 10 PAs with cytogenetically normal karyotype were fusion‐positive, two of which had PLAG1 fusions and 5 HMGA2 fusions. Similarly, 5 PAs with nonrecurrent clonal aberrations not involving 8q12 or 12q14‐15 were fusion‐positive; all cases had fusions involving HMGA2. There was also one case with a del(8)(q12q22) and expected involvement of PLAG1 that instead had a cryptic HMGA2::WIF1 fusion. Collectively, these results clearly demonstrate that PLAG1 and HMGA2 fusions are much more common in PA than previously estimated by cytogenetics and this is particularly true for HMGA2 fusions [ , , ]. Notably, we found 12 novel fusions and three fusions that were previously not reported in PA but in other salivary neoplasms, including NCALD‐PLAG1 in MECA [ ] and NTF3‐PLAG1 and FBXO32‐PLAG1 in oncocytic myoepithelioma [ ]. For the majority of these, the mechanisms of activation of PLAG1 and HMGA2 were consistent with previous findings [ , , , ]. Thus, PLAG1 was activated by promoter swapping and HMGA2 by loss of its 3′‐part and flanking sequences, which in most cases were replaced by the 3′‐part of a fusion partner gene. This was apparent in the novel MYC::PLAG1 fusion, where the aberrant transcripts included the noncoding exon 1 of MYC fused to exon 2 of PLAG1 (Figure  ). The fusion is predicted to lead to overexpression of wild‐type PLAG1 proteins, like other previously known PLAG1 fusions in PA. We also found three cases with fusions not directly affecting PLAG1 but involving genes or sequences located in the vicinity of PLAG1 (cases 14–16). These cases had all PLAG1‐activation, presumably caused by enhancer elements juxtaposed close to the PLAG1 locus similar to what was recently described for the NFIB::PLAG1 fusion [ ]. Similarly, we found three cases (cases 31–33) where the fusion partner sequences were enriched for H3K27 acetylation (data obtained from the UCSC genome browser), indicating enhancer hijacking as a possible mechanism of HMGA2‐activation.
In summary, our findings unequivocally show that activation of PLAG1 or HMGA2 by gene fusion or enhancer hijacking is near‐universal in PA and that many of the fusion events are generated by cryptic intra‐ or interchromosomal rearrangements. We demonstrate the transcriptional basis for increased ECM production, EMT, and activated WNT‐signaling in PA. Although two molecular subsets of PAs exist (PLAG1‐ vs. HMGA2‐activated), they share the same activated signaling pathways but show differences in expression of genes involved in the immune system, cell adhesion, and microenvironment remodeling. Our study reveals new oncogenic gene fusions and expands our knowledge about the molecular underpinnings of PA.
