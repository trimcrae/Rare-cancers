These triple-negative breast cancers (TNBC), a heterogeneous distribution of disease subtypes [ ], primarily appear in younger women with African, Asian, Hispanic, or BRCA1 mutant ancestry [ , , ]. Standard of care surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation provide median survival of only 4 years [ ]. While many TNBC patients may respond initially to first- or second-line therapy, resistance arises from mutations that drive higher oncogene expression [ , ].
Approved molecularly targeted drugs for TNBC include poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [ ], topoisomerase inhibitors [ ], immunological checkpoint inhibitors [ ], and antibody-drug conjugates [ ], all targeting proteins. These are only modestly effective in treating small subsets of TNBC patients, such as those with BRCA1/2 mutations [ ] or elevated PD-L1 [ ]. Bevacizumab and EGFR inhibitors failed to significantly improve patients’ overall survival [ ]. Tecentriq, an anti-PD-L1 mAb, was initially approved upon report of one extra month of survival, but was subsequently withdrawn [ ]. Trodelvy, an FDA-approved conjugate of anti-Trop-2 with irinotecan, only increased TNBC overall survival by 3 months [ ]. Thus, a new molecular therapeutic agent with greater efficacy, specificity, and safety will bridge the gap in TNBC therapy. We address this critical need by targeted inhibition of oncogenic microRNAs (miRNAs) to enhance therapeutic precision and effectiveness.
miRNAs are non-coding RNAs that disrupt target messenger RNA (mRNA) translation or induce target mRNA degradation [ ]. Dysregulation of miRNAs plays important roles in various stages of tumor development among different types of cancer [ – ], including breast cancer [ ]. Therapeutically targeting miRNAs offers a powerful approach to overcoming cancer heterogeneity, as each miRNA can simultaneously regulate multiple mRNA networks, thereby influencing diverse cellular pathways in a coordinated manner. Most TNBC cells and adjacent stroma show high expression of microRNA-21 (miR-21) [ , ], correlating with poor disease-free survival [ – ]. Overexpression of miR-21 was universally observed in breast cancer cell lines and tissues [ – ], boosting cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis [ , , ], and drug resistance [ , ]. Highly metastatic 4T1 TNBC cells failed to grow tumors in miR-21 knockout Balb/c mice [ ].
Blocking oncogenic miR-21 can inhibit tumor progression, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis by restoring the expression of tumor suppressor proteins, such as PDCD4 [ ], PTEN [ ], SPRY2 [ ], maspin [ ], and TPM1 [ ]. PDCD4 and PTEN proteins are negative regulators of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. PDCD4 protein expression level was reduced in at least six human tumor types, including lung, brain, kidney, breast, colon, and pancreas [ – ]. PDCD4 regulates eIF4A [ ], p21, CDK4, ornithine decarboxylase, carbonic anhydrase II, and JNK/c-June/AP-1 [ ]. PTEN negatively regulates cell proliferation and survival. Impairment of PTEN regulation is thought to play a role in oncogenic transformation [ ].
Maspin, SPRY2, and TPM1 proteins play major roles in restricting metastasis by controlling cell branching, migration, blood vessel formation, and invasion [ , , ]. Moreover, miR-21 expression can be upregulated in response to cytokine stimulation through transcriptional activation by STAT3 [ ], resulting in resistance to cytokine type I interferon (IFN)-induced apoptosis [ ]. This might correlate miR-21 with resistance to cell death by chemo and cytokine therapy. Since miR-21 contributes to cancer cell survival through multiple modes of action, its suppression will benefit the majority of TNBC subtypes [ ]. A 19-mer anti-miR-21 with particular backbone modifications has been patented for use in kidney disease [ ] and Alport’s syndrome, but it has not yet been applied to TNBC. Our goal is to develop an miR-21 inhibitor specifically tailored for TNBC, addressing an urgent need in combating this aggressive cancer.
To improve cancer-specific delivery of the miR-21 inhibitor, we utilize the insulin-like growth factor receptor (IGF1R) as a gateway to facilitate endocytosis. IGF1R belongs to the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) family, with a ligand-binding domain on the cell surface, a hydrophobic transmembrane domain, and an intracellular tyrosine kinase domain followed by a C-terminal domain in the cytoplasm [ ]. Upon IGF1 binding to one of the extracellular domains of the IGF1R dimer, the cytoplasmic kinase domain is activated, triggering multiple signaling pathways that drive cell division [ ]. Aggressive breast cancers, including TNBC, frequently exhibit elevated IGF1R levels [ ]. Approximately 50% of breast cancer [ ] and 22%–46% of TNBC express IGF1R [ , ]. Most importantly, TNBC cells show strong IGF1R signaling activation, correlating with poor survival [ ].
A variety of nuclease-resistant RNA backbone analogs have been employed as antisense agents, such as phosphorothioate (PS) [ ], methylphosphonate (MP) [ ], 2′,4′-locked nucleic acid (LNA) [ ], 2′,4′-NC-bridged nucleic acid (NC-BNA) [ ], and polyamide nucleic acid (PNA) [ ] (Fig.  ). We previously designed PNA [ ] oligomers with a protease-resistant retro-inverso cyclized d(CSKC) tetrapeptide IGF1 analog at the C-terminus of PNA to direct endocytosis into IGF1R-overexpressing cells [ , ]. We used that IGF1 analog [ ] to selectively deliver radiolabeled peptide nucleic acid (PNA) probes for PET imaging of oncogene mRNAs in IGF1R-overexpressing cells and tumor xenografts [ ]. This approach paves the way for cancer-specific delivery of therapeutic oligonucleotides to TNBC cells overexpressing IGF1R. Currently, no known signaling feedback loop has been identified between miR-21 and IGF1R [ ].
 2′,4′-BNANC (BNA) is a nuclease-resistant derivative of 2′-O,4′-C-methylene-bridged nucleic acid (LNA) [ , ] (Fig.  ). The BNA–DNA–BNA gapmer minimizes hybridization-dependent and hybridization-independent toxicity, with superior hybridization affinity Tm > 80°C [ ]. Kinetic data revealed that the stronger binding resulted from a much slower dissociation rate constant [ ]. The BNA backbone provided much higher stability in human serum [ ]. In vivo, BNA gapmers were highly effective against proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) in low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) protein regulation in western diet-fed C57BL/6J mice [ ]. BNAs displayed low liver toxicity in those mice [ , , ]. Most recently, a BNA gapmer successfully inhibited defective CUG repeats in the 3′UTR of DMPK gene, which contributes to the pathogenesis of myotonic dystrophy type I (DM1) [ , ]. Whole-body PET imaging of healthy rats after administration of [18F]BNA phosphorothioates revealed a circulation half-life of ∼1 h, with predominant accumulation in the liver and kidneys after blood clearance [ ]. An anti-PCSK9 cholesteryl-[18F]BNA phosphorothioate showed a shorter circulation half-life of ∼20 min, with concomitant rapid accumulation in the liver, but not the kidneys [ ].
BNA knockdown oligomers exhibited comparable potency to LNA [ ], with minimal toxicity in mice [ ]. Although LNA showed greater binding affinity compared to other RNA analog modifications [ ] and has been tested for therapeutic development to block miRNAs [ – ], it has been observed to induce hepatotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo, making it impractical for clinical development [ – ].
Here, we describe the design and functional characterization of a 2′-O,4′-C-BNANC (BNA)-modified anti-miR-21 oligonucleotide that suppresses both proliferation and immune checkpoint-associated genes in mouse and human TNBC cells. Mature miR-21 is overexpressed in TNBC and is conserved between mouse and human, allowing preclinical testing of the same inhibitor across multiple animal species. We further developed a bispecific RNA-peptide conjugate by linking the anti-miR-21 BNA oligonucleotide to an IGF1R-targeting peptide, enabling receptor-mediated uptake into IGF1R-overexpressing TNBC cells. This conjugate achieved tumor-specific delivery and inhibited TNBC progression in a syngeneic mouse model. Together, these results support IGF1R-targeted delivery of BNA-based miR-21 inhibitors as a potential therapeutic strategy for TNBC.

## Materials and methods

## BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers
Antisense BNA–DNA–BNA 5-5-5 gapmer phosphorothioates and peptide conjugates (Table ) (Bio-Synthesis, Inc., Lewisville, TX 75057) were assembled by solid-phase coupling of nucleotide phosphoramidites [ , ], 3′ addition of an alkyne linker, and then click chemistry coupling of azidohexyl-peptides [ ]. The gapmers and conjugates were analyzed by reversed-phase liquid chromatography and MALDI-TOF mass spectroscopy as described [ , ]. Calculated gapmer and conjugate masses agreed with manufacturer’s reported masses for BND5412: 5413.6 Da, BND5371: 5372.1 Da, BND6482: 6483.5 Da, and BND6372: 6371.6 Da.
 

## Cell lines and cell culture
Seven human TNBC cell lines, of European and African ancestry, which represent 80% of all TNBC subtypes [ ], were studied: basal-like 1 MDA-MB-468 cells [ ], basal-like 1 HCC1937 cells [ ], basal-like 2 HCC1806 cells [ ], mesenchymal stem-like MDA-MB-231 cells [ , – ], mesenchymal stem–like MDA-MB-157 cells [ ], mesenchymal stem–like MDA-MB-436 cells [ ], immunomodulatory HCC1187 cells [ ], mesenchymal BT549 cells [ ], luminal androgen receptor MDA-MB-453 [ ], and unclassified BT-20 cells [ ], versus a benign fibrocystic breast line, MCF10A [ ], as a negative control. Two murine TNBC cell lines, EMT6 cells [ ] and 4T1 cells [ ], originating in female immunocompetent Balb/c mice, were studied. miR-21-5p is identical in mice and humans. All cell lines were obtained from ATCC and maintained according to ATCC instructions.

## Cell treatment with BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers for western blot and real-time quantitative RT-PCR
TNBC cells were seeded in T-25 flasks in complete medium without antibiotics the day before transfection. BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers were transfected into TNBC cells with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) at a final gapmer concentration of 50 nM for 24–72 h at 37°C under 5% CO2, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At the end of transfection, cells were detached and harvested with cell dissociation buffer.

## Dual-Glo luciferase reporter assay to determine IC50 for miR-21 inhibition
MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells were seeded in 24-well plates to reach 50% confluency in complete medium without antibiotics the day before transfection. Cells were co-transfected with 100 ng of dual-luciferase psiCHECK2 plasmid (C8021, Promega) containing a perfect complement to miR-21 in the 3′UTR of the Renilla luciferase gene, and with a concentration gradient of BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 h, cells were lysed and Dual-Glo reporter assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (E2920, Promega). Luminescence intensity for each sample was measured with a Veritas Microplate Luminometer (Turner BioSystems), and each value from psiCHECK2 Renilla luciferase was normalized by psiCHECK2 firefly luciferase. IC50 was determined by the concentration at 50% elevation of the luciferase signal using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism.
For evaluation of compound delivery in the absence of transfection reagents, cells were initially transfected with 100 ng of the dual-luciferase psiCHECK2 plasmid (C8021, Promega) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 4 h. Following transfection, cells were washed and incubated in fresh culture medium for 2 h to allow recovery. Cells were subsequently treated with increasing concentrations of BND6482 or BND5412 for 24 h. Luciferase activity was then quantified using the Dual-Glo Reporter Assay (E2920, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Cell proliferation by CellTiter-Glo assay after treatment with BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers
TNBC cells were seeded in 24-well plates to reach 50% confluency in complete medium without antibiotics the day before transfection. BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers were transfected into TNBC cells with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) at a final gapmer concentration of 50 nM for 4 days for cell viability measurement over time, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At the end of transfection, cellular viability was measured by quantitating lysed ATP using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega). For cell proliferation IC50 measurements, TNBC cells seeded in 24-well plates were transfected with concentration gradients of BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers for 48 h, followed by CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega). Luminescence intensity for each sample was measured with a BioTek Synergy HT Reader.

## Western blots of cellular proteins in treated cells
Treated cells were detached by cell dissociation buffer and harvested with 1× PBS, then lysed in cell lysis buffer (Invitrogen) with protease inhibitor cocktail (P-2714, Sigma). Lysate protein concentrations were quantified by the Bradford Assay (Bio-Rad). Lysate aliquots containing 30 µg protein were separated on NuPAGE 4%–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen), transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with blocking buffer, and incubated with rabbit anti-PDCD4 (ab80590, Abcam), sheep anti-CD47 (AF4670, R&D Systems), rabbit anti-PD-L1 (13684S, Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-PD-L2 (ab256386, Abcam), rabbit anti-Jak2 (D2E12, Cell Signaling), and mouse anti-β-actin (AM4302, ThermoFisher), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies labeled with horseradish peroxidase (Invitrogen). The resulting protein bands were imaged by luminescence using a SuperSignal West Femto Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific) on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Imaging System and analyzed with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

## Lactate dehydrogenase assay to detect apoptosis in treated cells
Cells in 24-well plates were treated with 50 nM BNA–DNA–BNA gapmers as above. Seventy-two hours later, induction of apoptosis was deduced from lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released into the medium due to cell lysis, using CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega).

## qPCR
Total RNA from treated TNBC cells was extracted using a mirVana miRNA isolation kit (ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA from treated EMT6 tumors was extracted using TRIzol Plus RNA Purification Kit (ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) of miRNAs, 100 ng of purified total RNA were reverse transcribed with a TaqMan miRNA reverse transcription kit (#4366597, ThermoFisher). qPCR of miRNAs was performed with an miRNA Gene Expression Assay (ThermoFisher) on an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus RT-PCR system. Primers specific for miR-21 (Assay ID 000397) and internal control RNA U6 (Assay ID 001973) for both reverse transcription and qPCR were obtained from ThermoFisher. The average absolute values of triplicate samples for the same miRNA were calculated and normalized to U6 RNA, measured by the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method [ ].
For qPCR of tumor suppressor and immune checkpoint mRNAs, 500 ng of purified total RNA were reverse transcribed with a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher, cat#4368814). qPCR of mRNAs was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (ThermoFisher) on an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus RT-PCT system with the following TaqMan primers/probes to detect transcripts: GAPDH (Hs02786624_g1); PDCD4 (Hs00377253_m1); CD47 (Hs00179953_m1); PDL1 (Hs00204257_m1); PDL2 (Hs00228839_m1); and JAK2 (Hs01078136_m1). The average of triplicate samples for the same mRNA was calculated and normalized to the internal control gene GAPDH (Hs03929097_g1) by the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method [ ].
To quantify the copy number of miR-21 per cell, synthetic miR-21 RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the TaqMan miRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (#4366597, ThermoFisher). A standard curve was generated by performing qPCR on five concentrations of synthetic miR-21, ranging from 1.8 × 107 to 3 × 108 copies/L, using the miR-21 TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Assay ID 000397, ThermoFisher). In parallel, total RNA extracted from cells underwent miR-21 qPCR using the same assay. The total miR-21 copy number in each RNA sample was then calculated based on the standard curve. Finally, the copy number per cell was determined by dividing the total miR-21 copy number by the number of cells used for RNA extraction.

## RNA-seq measurements of transcriptome changes of miR-21 target mRNAs in treated cells
Total RNA was extracted from HCC1806 cells 24 h post-transfection with EC90 (10.7 nM) of BND5412, using the mirVana miRNA isolation kit (ThermoFisher) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality assessment, library preparation using the Stranded Total RNA Prep Ligation with Ribo-Zero Plus kit (Illumina), and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) via NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), generating 60 million reads per sample in a 2 × 100 bp paired-end configuration, were conducted at the Cancer Genomics & Bioinformatics Core at Thomas Jefferson University. Samples were multiplexed across two flow cell lanes. FASTQ files were aligned using STAR, with quality checks performed via RSeQC, and aligned reads counted with HTSeq.
Differential gene expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2, and Jensen Disease enrichment analysis was performed using the iDEP platform 2.01 [ ]. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software version 4.3.2 from the Broad Institute to evaluate the enrichment of predefined gene sets in differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq data. Differentially expressed genes were identified by comparing control and treated samples using DESeq2, with adjusted P-value <.05 as thresholds for significance. The ranked gene list was generated based on the log2 fold change of gene expression between treated and control samples. This ranked list was used as input for GSEA. The analysis was conducted using the Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). The NCBI BioProject accession number is PRJNA1300816, accessible at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1300816.

## Confocal fluorescent microscopy
HCC1806 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/ml in 2 ml of growth medium in Ibidi 35 mm dishes and cultured overnight at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Following the initial incubation, cells were treated with 100 nM Cy5-BND6482 in fresh growth medium for 4 h under identical conditions. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with Lysotracker Green (80 nM final concentration) for 30 min. at 37°C. After incubation, the cells were gently washed thrice with Ca2+ and Mg2+-containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and immersed in 2 ml of imaging medium. Live-cell confocal microscopy was performed using a Nikon A1R HD confocal microscope equipped with a 60× objective lens and 3× optical zoom.

## Serum stability of BND6482
BND6482 was incubated in either 50% or 90% FBS, diluted with RPMI 1640 medium at 37°C, to a final concentration of 2 µM. Aliquots (10 µl) were collected at specified time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, and 48 h) and stored at −80°C until analysis. After the incubation period, all samples were resolved using a 15% TBE-urea gel with TBE running buffer. The gel was run at a constant voltage of 180 V for 50 min., followed by staining in 50 ml of 1× SYBR Gold Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (ThermoFisher, S11494), diluted in TBE running buffer, for 25 min. The stained gel was imaged using an iBright FL1500 Imaging System (Invitrogen).

## Orthotopic TNBC allograft treatment
Murine EMT6 TNBC cells [ ], originating in female immunocompetent Balb/c mice, express high miR-21 and median IGF1R relative to other TNBC cells [ ]. Therefore, we chose EMT6 cells to create an orthotopic female immunocompetent Balb/c model for TNBC tumor progression. In groups of 5 syngeneic, immunocompetent 4–8-week-old female Balb/c mice, we implanted 1 × 105 EMT6 cells into a mammary fat pad of each subject under isoflurane anesthesia. Tumors were allowed to grow to 5 mm in diameter. Then we administered BND6482, scrambled control BND6372, or saline vehicle intraperitoneally to each subject at 5 mg/kg twice a week on days 0, 3, 7, and 10 over 13 days. Palpable tumor volumes were measured using calipers with the formula (0.5 × length × width × width), along with dissected tumor weights.
These animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Philadelphia Charles River Animal Accelerator and Development Laboratory IACUC on 23 October 2023, permit 2023–1978. Humane endpoints: we sacrificed each mouse at 14 days, collecting 0.5 ml urine and 0.5 ml blood, then harvested tumors, lungs, heart, liver, kidneys, bladder, and muscle for preservation in liquid nitrogen. Euthanasia: carbon dioxide. We assessed euthanasia 2 min. past unconsciousness by the lack of heartbeat upon placing thumb and index finger on the chest, then the lack of response to a tweezer pinch to a foot. Minimization of animal pain and suffering: analgesics, or euthanasia if necessary, to relieve pain or stress. We observed the mice until they regained consciousness and maintained sternal recumbence. We placed each mouse into a different single-use cage until they were fully recovered. We changed the cages every 3 days to keep the mice clean.

## Fluorescent imaging of biodistribution and tumor uptake
To assess biodistribution, EMT6 tumor-bearing mice received a single intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 5 mg/kg AlexaFluor647-labeled BND6482 or AlexaFluor647-labeled BND5412 at time 0. Mice were then positioned prone under isoflurane anesthesia and imaged using an In-Vivo Multispectral FX PRO optical imaging system. Fluorescence and X-ray images were captured at 2, 24, 48, and 96 h post-injection for BND6482 and at 0 and 24 h for BND5412.
For tumor uptake analysis, EMT6 tumors were allowed to grow to ~5 mm in diameter in 4- to 8-week-old female Balb/c mice. Mice were then administered either a saline vehicle control or AlexaFluor647-labeled BND6482 at 5 mg/kg via IP injection once daily for three consecutive days. On the fourth day, tumors were harvested and imaged using the AlexaFluor647 fluorescence channel on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Imaging System.

## Serum markers of liver and kidney toxicity
Serum markers of liver and kidney toxicity were assessed from blood collected immediately following sacrifice. The blood was drawn after three IP injections of 5 mg/kg BND6482, administered over a 2-week period on days 0, 6, and 12, once the tumors had reached a diameter of 5 mm.

## Histological analysis of thin sections of liver and kidney
Histological analysis of the liver and kidneys was conducted following three IP injections of 5 mg/kg BND6482, administered over a 2-week period on days 0, 6, and 12, once tumors had reached a diameter of 5 mm. Liver and kidney tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and processed for immunofluorescence staining by the Comparative Pathology Core at the University of Pennsylvania School of Veterinary Medicine.

## Statistical Analysis
All experimental measurements were performed independently at least three times. Significance was assessed by Student’s t-test. Tumor growth curves were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10 using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA (factors: treatment group and time), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparison test. One-way ANOVA and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were conducted with GraphPad Prism 10. Prior to applying one-way ANOVA and t-tests, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

## Results

## miR-21 inhibitor design
We incorporated the RNA analog 2′,4′-BNANC (BNA) backbone modification [ ] (Fig.  ) in the five 5′ nucleotides and the five 3′ nucleotides of BND5412 (Fig.  ) (Table  ).
 We chose a length of 15 nt to maximize uniqueness among human RNAs [ ]. The anti-miR-21-5p sequence BND5412 (Fig. ) (Table  ) includes a 15 nt sequence designed to specifically block miR-21-5p (identical in mice and humans). We previously reported the potential for miRNA inhibitors to mimic the effects of endogenous miRNAs [ ]. We observed that conventionally designed miRNA inhibitors closely resembled the complementary passenger strand of the target miRNA within the pre-miRNA hairpin structure [ ]. This finding suggests that a conventionally designed miRNA inhibitor, fully complementary to the target miRNA, could mimic the function of the passenger strand in the pre-miRNA duplex, potentially leading to off-target effects.
Since miRNA inhibitors often incorporate backbone modifications, the off-target effects resulting from mimicking the passenger strand could be amplified by the accumulation of these long-lasting, nuclease-resistant analogs within cells [ ]. We specifically selected the BND5412 sequence 5′-CAGTCTGATAAGCTA-3′ to exclude the seed region of the passenger strand, thereby preventing the anti-miR-21 15-mer from inducing off-target effects by imitating the miR-21-3p passenger strand [ ].

## Pharmacodynamic effects of miR-21 inhibitor on TNBC cell proliferation
We observed an IC50 of 22 nM for the 15-mer BNA miR-21 inhibitor BND5412 (Fig.  ) in suppressing miR-21 activity after transfection into MDA-MB-231 cells, leading to derepression of a luciferase reporter vector with a miR-21 complementary sequence in the 3′-UTR of the luciferase gene (Fig.  ). Transfection with the miR-21 inhibitor BND5412 significantly reduced mature miR-21 levels (Fig.  ) and upregulated the mRNA and protein expression of PDCD4 (Fig.  and C), a known direct target of miR-21 [ ].
 Fifty nanomolar transfection of BND5412 decreased cell proliferation by up to eight-fold in MDA-MB-231, HCC1806, BT-20, MDA-MB-157, MDA-MB-436, BT-549, and HCC1937 (Fig. ). A strong positive correlation was observed between the anti-miR-21 IC50 for inhibiting cell proliferation and the amount of miR-21 per cell in six out of seven TNBC cell lines. The non-tumorigenic breast epithelial cell line MCF-10A was significantly less sensitive to anti-miR-21 BNA treatment (Fig. ). Furthermore, significant apoptosis, as measured by the LDH assay, was induced in TNBC cell lines MDA-MB-231, BT-20, BT-549, HCC1806, and HCC1937 (Fig.  ).
 

## Effects of miR-21 inhibitor on TNBC cell immune checkpoints
A critical aspect of miR-21’s role in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is its ability to modulate immune responses, contributing to immune evasion [ ]. Suppression of miR-21 in highly metastatic tumor models led to tumor regression in immunocompetent mice, but not in immunocompromised mice. The regression was linked to increased infiltration of activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a reduction in PD-L1+ monocytes, highlighting miR-21’s role in modulating immune responses [ ]. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed reduced expression of cell cycle genes and enhanced immune activity upon miR-21 knockdown [ ].
We therefore investigated the effect of miR-21 inhibition by BND5412 on the suppression of several key immune checkpoint proteins. CD47, PD-L1, PD-L2, and JAK2 are critical regulators of the TME and play pivotal roles in modulating anti-tumor immunity. CD47 is a cell surface protein that binds to SIRPα on macrophages to deliver a “don’t eat me” signal, preventing phagocytosis [ ]. Tumors overexpress CD47 to evade immune surveillance, hindering macrophages and dendritic cells from engulfing tumor cells [ ]. PD-L1 and PD-L2 are immune checkpoints that bind to PD-1 on activated T cells, suppressing T-cell function and promoting an immunosuppressive TME [ ]. Tumors upregulate these ligands to evade adaptive immunity [ ]. JAK2 plays a key role in cytokine signaling, essential for immune regulation and inflammation in the TME. Aberrant JAK2 activity, due to mutations or overexpression, drives production of cytokines like IL-6 and VEGF, promoting tumor growth and recruiting immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs and MDSCs [ ]. This disrupts the balance between effector and suppressive immune cells, aiding tumor immune evasion [ ].
Inhibition of miR-21 by BND5412 reduced the expression of immune checkpoint proteins CD47, PD-L1, PD-L2, and Jak2 in human MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig.  ). Furthermore, BND5412 significantly downregulated CD47 mRNA levels in MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-468, BT549, BT20, and HCC1187 cells; PD-L2 mRNA levels in MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, and BT549 cells; PD-L1 mRNA levels in MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and HCC1806 cells; and JAK2 mRNA levels in MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-468, HCC1806, BT549, BT20, MDA-MB-453, and HCC1187 cells (Fig.  ). These eight TNBC cell lines represent six different TNBC subtypes [ ].
 

## RNA expression profile post miR-21 blockade
We conducted RNA-seq analysis on RNA samples from HCC1806 cells transfected with the EC90 concentration of 10.7 nM, determined from the luciferase reporter assay of the miR-21 inhibitor BND5412, alongside vehicle-treated cells. Off-target effects were assessed using GGGenome [ ] by analyzing human spliced RNAs complementary to BND5412 sequence with 0–2 mismatches, insertions, or deletions. Over 300 genes were identified in the search results. To identify transcripts with obvious off-target concerns, we applied a >2-fold change cutoff to identify genes showing large transcriptional downregulation, since such large changes could result in severe off-target toxicity. We understand that miRNAs typically regulate many targets by <2-fold, and coordinated smaller-amplitude changes across multiple genes can still be biologically meaningful. Therefore, this >2-fold filter is not intended to define the full off-target network of miR-21 inhibition, but rather to identify off-target transcripts that could significantly contribute to off-target toxicity. Under this criterion, no transcripts with 0–1 mismatches were downregulated by at least 2-fold. However, eight genes with two mismatches to the anti-miR-21 BNA sequence showed a decrease of at least two-fold (Fig. ). Among these, SH3PXD2A, DIAPH2, PTPRK, MGAT5, and NLGN4X were identified as oncogenes. The remaining genes, ERC1, ATRN, and FHOD3, were not linked to any known disease when downregulated. No gene exhibited off-target effects greater than a four-fold change.
 To assess the global impact of miR-21 on regulated transcripts, a list of predicted miR-21 target genes was obtained from TargetScan [ ]. A Kolmogorov–Smirnov test comparing the cumulative distribution of transcripts between miR-21 targets and non-target genes revealed a significant difference (Fig. ). This suggests that BND5412 treatment exerted widespread on-target effects on miR-21-regulated transcripts.
Gene Set Enrichment Analysis on HallMark genes from MSigDB dataset [ ] on differentially expressed genes identified the top 3 enriched pathways (Fig.  ). The enrichment results showed that miR-21 inhibition by BND5412 activated interferon α and interferon γ responses, both of which have a positive effect on anti-tumor immunity [ , ]. In addition, the KRAS inhibitory pathway was also significantly enriched in BND5412-treated samples (Fig. ). Disease enrichment analysis using the Jensen Disease database on the iDEP platform [ ] revealed significant enrichment of breast cancer among the down-regulated diseases (Fig.  ). Other enriched down-regulated cancers included kidney, liver, melanoma, pancreatic cancer, and malignant glioma. Beyond cancer, enrichment was also observed for downregulation of Alzheimer’s disease, schizophrenia, heart conduction disease, obesity, and metabolic disorders like type 2 diabetes. In contrast, we did not detect enriched upregulated diseases among differentially expressed genes. This suggests that miR-21 inhibition did not broadly activate known disease-related pathways under the current experimental conditions. Taken together, these findings indicate that miR-21 inhibition may have therapeutic potential across a range of diseases beyond cancer.

## miR-21 inhibitor-peptide conjugate for cell-specific delivery
To deliver therapeutic miR-21 inhibitor by receptor-mediated endocytosis preferentially into TNBC cells that overexpress IGF1R (Fig.  ), we conjugated a small peptide analog of IGF1 [ ] to BND5412 via a non-cleavable DBCO linker (Fig.  ) [ , ]. To evaluate the serum stability of BND6482, 2 µM of the compound was incubated in 50% and 90% fetal bovine serum (FBS). At various time points, aliquots were collected and analyzed on a denaturing 15% TBE-urea gel. The results showed that the majority of BND6482 remained intact after 48 h at 37°C in both serum conditions, indicating high stability. Furthermore, the detached BND5412 exhibited no signs of degradation or smaller fragments, suggesting high serum stability of the 15-mer BNA gapmer (Fig.  ).
 One hundred nanomolar Cy5-labeled miR-21 inhibitor-peptide conjugate (BND6482) demonstrated efficient cytoplasmic uptake in HCC1806 cells, as observed through live-cell confocal microscopy after 4 h of incubation (Fig. ). Fluorescent BND6482 partially co-localized with green acidic vesicles, indicated by yellow puncta, while a portion was observed outside these vesicles, suggesting partial endosomal escape. BND6482 uptake in high IGF1R-expressing HCC1806 cells [ ] resulted in dose-dependent derepression of miR-21 luciferase reporter expression (Fig.  ). In contrast, uptake in low IGF1R-expressing MDA-MB-157 cells [ ] did not significantly derepress the luciferase reporter without lipofection (Fig.  ). Under transfection conditions, the peptide-miR-21 inhibitor exhibited ∼50% lower de-repression of the miR-21 reporter than the unconjugated inhibitor (Fig.  ). To assess IGF1R-mediated delivery, we performed miR-21 luciferase assays in HCC1806 cells without lipofection across increasing inhibitor concentrations, with or without the targeting peptide. For cross-compound comparison, each luciferase ratio was normalized to its own 50 nM transfection activity (set to 100%). After 24 h, the IGF1R-targeted conjugate BND6482 showed dose-dependent de-repression and reached ∼60% of its transfection activity, whereas the naked inhibitor BND5412 reached only ∼13% at the highest concentration tested (Fig.  ).
 

## BND6482 biodistribution and uptake in EMT6 allografts
A single intraperitoneal injection of AlexaFluor647-BND6482 at 5 mg/kg in EMT6 allografts in immunocompetent female Balb/c mice demonstrated fluorescent drug distribution from the injection site (left) to the tumor site (right), becoming visible within 2 h and persisting for at least 96 h. Notably, no significant accumulation was observed in the livers or kidneys during this time (Fig. ). In contrast, a single intraperitoneal injection of AlexaFluor647–BND5412 at 5 mg/kg in EMT6 allografts failed to show apparent tumor uptake in 24 h (Fig. ). In the absence of IGF1R-targeting peptide, BND5412 mainly accumulated in liver, kidney, and lung and was excreted via the bladder.
 

## TNBC allograft response to BND6482
Tumor inhibition was observed following daily intraperitoneal dosing of 5 mg/kg AlexaFluor647-labeled BND6482 for 3 days (Fig.  ), with two out of three tumors markedly smaller than those in the vehicle group. Fluorescent imaging confirmed drug accumulation in the extracted tumors from each treated animal (Fig.  ). Subsequently, we conducted a 2-week trial with nonfluorescent BND6482. After EMT6 tumor allografts reached ~5 mm in diameter, mice were treated with saline vehicle, 5 mg/kg scrambled BND6372, or BND6482, administered intraperitoneally twice a week on days 0, 3, 7, and 10 over 13 days. Tumor volumes were measured at each injection point, and on day 13, tumors were harvested and weighed for analysis. Over the two-week treatment period, BND6482 stopped tumor progression, whereas the scrambled control BND6372 failed to suppress growth (Fig. ). After 2 weeks, BND6482-treated tumors exhibited a significant 74% mean reduction in tumor mass relative to the vehicle-treated group (Fig.  ). Furthermore, BND6482 treatment resulted in a decrease in miR-21 level and a corresponding upregulation of PDCD4 mRNA, a known miR-21 target (Fig.  ).

## Liver and kidney toxicity assessment of BND6482
To assess liver and kidney toxicity of BND6482 at the therapeutic dose, immunocompetent female Balb/c mice bearing EMT6 tumors were treated with three intraperitoneal injections of 5 mg/kg BND6482 over a 2-week period on days 0, 6, and 12, once the tumors reached 5 mm in diameter. Blood serum was collected on day 14 and analyzed for hepatotoxicity markers, including liver enzymes (ALP, ALT, AST), bile acids, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT). Compared to the vehicle and scrambled control groups, BND6482-treated mice showed no significant alterations in serum markers indicative of hepatotoxicity (Fig.  ).
 Liver and kidney toxicity in treated mice was further evaluated using fluorescent immunohistochemistry to analyze markers including caspase 3, Ki67, and IBA1. Elevated caspase 3 levels indicate drug-induced cell death in the liver and kidney, signaling toxicity [ ]. Increased Ki67 expression suggests regenerative proliferation of liver cells in response to damage [ ]. IBA1 serves as a marker for activated macrophages, with higher levels reflecting an early immune response to toxic substances, tissue damage, and inflammation [ ]. LNA-containing antisense oligonucleotides with high toxicity were previously shown to significantly induce caspase 3, Ki67, or IBA1 levels in liver and kidney tissues of rats [ ]. Liver and kidney sections from mice treated with BND6482 exhibited no significant increase in caspase 3, Ki67, or IBA1 levels compared to saline-treated controls. This indicates that BND6482 does not induce observable toxicity at the effective dose (Fig.  ). We did not perform matched blood chemistry analysis for the unconjugated miR-21 inhibitor in this study because the objective here was to assess the safety profile of the lead therapeutic peptide-conjugated miR-21 inhibitor. A direct comparison of toxicity-associated markers between the unconjugated and conjugated miR-21 inhibitors will be included in future studies.

## Conclusions and discussion
Lipofection of the anti-miR-21 BNA–DNA–BNA gapmer BND5412 (Fig. ) resulted in sub-nanomolar IC50 values for inhibiting proliferation across seven TNBC cell lines (Fig.  ). IC50 values positively correlated with miR-21 copies/cell (Fig.  ). Among the eight TNBC cell lines tested, MDA-MB-453 exhibited the weakest response to miR-21 inhibition by BND5412. This resistance is likely due to the fact that MDA-MB-453 is not a true triple-negative cell line, as it harbors amplified Her2 signaling [ ] and high androgen receptor (AR) expression [ ]. These factors drive constitutive activation of Her2 and AR pathways, promoting sustained cell growth and survival. While miR-21 inhibition typically suppresses PI3K-AKT pathway by upregulating PDCD4, a downstream target of mTORC1, the persistent activation of Her2 and AR signaling enables continued activation of alternative AKT-driven survival pathways, ultimately diminishing the therapeutic efficacy of miR-21 inhibition. Overall, the miR-21 inhibitor BND5412 significantly slowed proliferation (Fig.  ), elevated tumor suppressor proteins (Fig.  ), reduced immune checkpoint proteins (Fig.  ), suppressed immune checkpoint mRNA expression (Fig.  ), and increased apoptosis (Fig.  ) in multiple cell lines representing six genomic TNBC subtypes.
Inhibition of miR-21 across eight TNBC cell lines resulted in variable effects on PD-L1, PD-L2, CD47, and JAK2 mRNA expression, reflecting differences in genomic subtype (Fig.  ). Mechanistically, the link between miR-21 inhibition and downregulation of CD47, PD-L1, PD-L2, and JAK2 is likely multifactorial. miR-21 is known to influence oncogenic and inflammatory signaling networks, including PTEN/PI3K-AKT [ ], NF-κB [ ], and STAT/JAK pathways [ , ]. These pathways could induce PD-L1, PD-L2, CD47, and JAK2 through pathway crosstalk and feedback. Inhibition of miR-21 could therefore secondarily suppress immune checkpoint genes and survival signaling transcripts without each of those mRNAs being direct miR-21 targets. Additional studies using reporter assays will be required to distinguish causal, miR-21-dependent repression from coincident transcriptional changes.
MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-436 (mesenchymal stem-like) and MDA-MB-468 (basal-like 1) cells [ ] were highly responsive, consistent with their dependence on JAK2/STAT3 signaling [ – ]. In these models, miR-21 suppression relieved repression of negative regulators such as SOCS1/3 [ , ], PIAS3 [ ], PTEN [ ], and PDCD4 [ ], thereby dampening the JAK2/STAT3 axis and reducing transcription of PD-L1, PD-L2, JAK2, and CD47 mRNAs. MDA-MB-436 cells exhibited only slight PD-L1 mRNA reduction, likely due to a low-level 9p24.1 copy gain encompassing JAK2, PD-L1, and PD-L2 [ , ], which maintain higher inducible expression despite miR-21 inhibition. In contrast, HCC1806 (basal-like 2) cells showed modest reductions in PD-L1 and JAK2 mRNAs, reflecting their reliance on EGFR/MAPK and NF-κB rather than STAT3 [ ]. CD47 mRNA downregulation upon miR-21 inhibition was most evident in STAT3-driven lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-468), consistent with direct STAT3 regulation of CD47 transcription [ ] (Fig.  ).
In contrast, BT-549 (mesenchymal), BT-20 and MDA-MB-453 (luminal androgen receptor), and HCC1187 (unstable) cells [ ] showed minimal effects on PD-L1 mRNA and only modest changes in JAK2 mRNA, as checkpoint regulation in these subtypes is largely STAT3-independent, relying instead on PI3K/AKT [ – ], AR/HER2 [ ], or genomic instability–associated pathways [ ] (Fig.  ). PD-L2 mRNA expression remained largely unchanged across the panel, with the exception of MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-436 cells. Baseline PD-L2 mRNA levels were generally low [ ], and expression in MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-453 cells was undetectable, leading to their exclusion from this analysis (Fig.  ).
Together, these findings indicate that miR-21 inhibition alters immune checkpoint-associated transcripts in a manner that is highly context dependent across TNBC subtypes. This raises the possibility that miR-21 blockade could reduce immune evasion programs in specific TNBC contexts, with potential implications for anti-tumor immunity in those settings. However, these studies were performed in cancer cell culture and thus do not test functional immune consequences. Validation in immunocompetent models will be required to determine whether these transcript-level changes translate into meaningful immunological effects in vivo.
Comparing cumulative distributions of transcripts between miR-21 targets and all other genes showed a significant difference (Fig.  ), indicating that BND5412 treatment of TNBC cells had global on-target effects on miR-21 regulated transcripts. No off-target change >4-fold was observed in any gene. RNA-seq enrichment analysis showed that miR-21 blockade activated interferon alpha and interferon gamma responses, both of which have a positive effect on anti-tumor immunity [ , ]. In addition, the KRAS inhibitory pathway was also significantly enriched in miR-21-BNA-treated samples (Fig. ). Transcriptome analysis thus suggested additional unexpected anti-cancer benefits of miR-21 blockade.
Targeted delivery is the biggest challenge for RNA analog therapy. Current delivery approaches using liposomes or nanoparticles are suboptimal for long-term clinical use due to low delivery efficiency, toxicity, and restricted biodistribution to liver and kidneys [ ]. Our designs provide safe, efficient, extrahepatic delivery of nucleic acid therapeutics. Conjugating RNA analogs to receptor-targeting peptide analogs (Fig. ) provides cell-type selective delivery. IGF1R is elevated in aggressive breast cancers, including TNBC [ ]. Quantitative studies of pharmacokinetics and systemic biodistribution are a necessary next step.
We previously designed polyamide nucleic acid (PNA) (Fig.  ) oligomers with a protease-resistant retro-inverso cyclized d(CSKC) tetrapeptide IGF1 analog at the C-terminus of PNA to direct endocytosis into IGF1R-overexpressing cells [ , ]. We found that radiolabeled PNA 12mers with a C-terminal cyclo-d(CSKC) stay in circulation by complexing with IGF1BP [ ], extending the lifetime of the agent in the body, which reduces the necessary dose. Urine from mice injected with a [99mTc]MYCC PNA-cyclo-d(CSKC) showed 83% of the radioactivity in an intact probe peak [ ], illustrating in vivo stability.
We observed receptor-mediated PNA-cyclo-d(CSKC) knockdown of cyclin D1 protein in MCF7 breast cancer xenografts [ ]. We have also used cyclo-d(CSKC) for tissue-specific delivery of various radioimaging agents. Thus, we were able to image CCND1, MYCC, HER2, or KRAS2 mRNAs with nuclide-PNA-cyclo-d(CSKC) PET agents in mice bearing ER+ breast cancer xenografts [ , , , ], Her2+ breast cancer xenografts, illustrating DOX response by reduction of HER2 mRNA PET SUV [ ], pancreas cancer xenografts [ , , , ], and transgenic mice with spontaneous mammary or lung tumors, illustrating response to cisplatin [ ], with single mismatch specificity. Importantly, specific PET imaging was blocked by excess IGF1 [ ].
The miR-21 BNA–DNA–BNA–peptide BND6482 (Fig. ) presents a novel method to inhibit miR-21 by leveraging a peptide ligand for targeted delivery into TNBC cells without the need for lipofection. BND6482 demonstrated dose-dependent efficacy and enhanced miR-21 inhibition in high IGF1R-expressing HCC1806 cells (Fig.  ), while showing no significant effect in low IGF1R-expressing MDA-MB-157 cells. In immunocompetent female Balb/c mice, intraperitoneal administration of AlexaFluor647-labeled BND6482 effectively localized to syngeneic EMT6 allografts, leading to a reduction in tumor mass after 3 days of therapy (Fig.  ). Further, a 2-week course of 5 mg/kg BND6482 halted tumor progression, resulting in a 74% mean reduction in tumor mass (Fig.  ), with no observed liver or kidney toxicity, as confirmed by serum markers (Fig.  ) and histological analysis (Fig.  ). Two-week treatment also reduced miR-21 levels and significantly upregulated PDCD4 mRNA, a miR-21 target (Fig.  ), supporting the hypothesized mechanism of action. These findings suggest that IGF1R peptide-mediated delivery of a miR-21 inhibitor could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for TNBC, and it warrants further investigation.
Using peptides as drug delivery molecules provides precise targeting, biocompatibility, and versatility, which are especially valuable in therapies where selective delivery to specific tissues or cells is critical, such as in cancer treatment [ ]. Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), composed of basic amino acids or derived from protein translocation motifs, have been successfully conjugated to neutral backbone oligonucleotides such as morpholino phosphorodiamidate oligonucleotides (PMOs) and peptide nucleic acids (PNAs) for enhanced delivery [ ]. However, conjugating CPPs to charged oligonucleotides has been less widely adopted due to the charge–charge interactions between the cationic CPPs and anionic oligonucleotides, which complicate synthesis and purification. To overcome these challenges, conjugating receptor-targeting peptides to charged oligonucleotides offers a promising alternative, as these peptide ligands facilitate cellular uptake via receptor-mediated endocytosis, bypassing the need for multiple cationic residues that are typically required for lipid bilayer destabilization. Recently, one study demonstrated a novel approach to targeted delivery of antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to pancreatic β-cells by conjugating ASOs to a GLP-1R peptide agonist. The results show that this strategy enhances ASO uptake in pancreatic islets, leading to effective gene silencing in GLP1R-expressing cells both in vitro and in vivo, without significantly affecting gene expression in the liver or other non-target tissues [ ]. Another study explored a novel approach for tumor-targeted gene silencing using systemic delivery of cRGD-conjugated siRNA, specifically targeting the VEGFR2 gene. The cRGD-siRNA conjugates were shown to selectively enter tumor cells expressing αvβ3 integrin, resulting in efficient gene knockdown, inhibition of angiogenesis, and significant reduction in tumor growth in both zebrafish and mouse models, without inducing immune responses or toxicity [ ].
In this study, we present an RNA-peptide analog strategy targeting IGF1R for cancer cell-specific delivery, aimed at inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis. Tumor suppression observed in a syngeneic mouse model highlights the potential of the anti-miR-21 RNA-peptide analog BND6482, supporting further preclinical proof-of-concept studies to facilitate its clinical translation. Next steps will include in vivo dose response and toxicology studies to define the therapeutic window and assess systemic safety. We also plan to evaluate efficacy in human TNBC xenografts in immunocompromised mice to assess direct, tumor cell intrinsic activity of BND6482. In parallel, we will investigate potential immune-related effects using immunocompetent humanized allograft models that can capture contributions from the tumor–immune interactions.
