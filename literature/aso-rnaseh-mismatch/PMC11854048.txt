## ABSTRACT

## Introduction
The clinical management of the inherited bleeding disorder von Willebrand disease (VWD) focuses on normalizing circulating levels of von Willebrand factor (VWF) and factor VIII (FVIII) to prevent or control bleeding events. The heterogeneous nature of VWD, however, complicates effective disease management and development of universal treatment guidelines.

## Areas covered
The current treatment modalities of VWD and their limitations are described and why this prompts the development of new treatment approaches. In particular, RNA-based therapeutics have gained significant interest because of their ability to reversibly alter gene expression with long-term efficacy. In the field of VWD, small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs) have been explored through various strategies to improve disease phenotypes. These different approaches are discussed as well as their potential impact on reshaping the future therapeutic landscape.

## Expert opinion
Current treatments for VWD often require frequent intravenous administration of VWF concentrates or desmopressin, with only short-term benefits. Moreover, remaining circulating mutant VWF can cause detrimental effects. Allele-selective siRNA-based therapies could provide more reliable and long-term disease correction by specifically targeting mutant VWF. This approach could be applied to a large part of the population aligning with the growing emphasis on personalized treatment and patient-centered care in VWD management.

## Introduction
Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is a genetic bleeding disorder marked by insufficient or defective von Willebrand factor (VWF), a multimeric glycoprotein synthesized in endothelial cells and megakaryocytes ( ) [ ]. VWF is secreted as ultra-large VWF strings into the bloodstream, which are subsequently enzymatically cleaved by ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motif, 13) into smaller subunits [ ]. Following damage to blood vessels and exposure of subendothelial collagen, VWF binds to exposed collagen and unwinds, revealing specific binding sites for the platelet glycoprotein Ib (GPIb) receptor. Through this interaction with collagen and the GPIb receptor, VWF plays a crucial role in the initial stages of hemostasis by promoting adhesion and activation of platelets thereby facilitating formation of a platelet plug at sites of vascular injury [ ]. Apart from its platelet binding function, VWF stabilizes coagulation factor VIII (FVIII) protecting it from proteolysis and premature clearance [ ].

 The prevalence of VWD varies from an estimated 1 in 100 to 1 in 10 000 individuals, with approximately 1 in 1000 manifesting clinically relevant symptoms in a primary care setting [ , ]. Traditionally classified into three types based on the VWF protein phenotype ( ), VWD type 1 constitutes the majority (70–80%) of cases and reflects a partial quantitative deficiency in plasma VWF [ ]. VWD type 2 (around 20% of cases) involves qualitative defects, further categorized into four subtypes based on specific functional abnormalities: type 2A is characterized by defective multimerization of plasma VWF; type 2B is described by the spontaneous interaction of VWF with GPIb without prior activation; type 2 M reflects a decreased binding of VWF to GPIb or collagen and type 2N is characterized by a decreased binding of VWF to FVIII [ ]. VWD type 3, the rarest and most severe form (<5% of cases), is characterized by a complete absence of plasma VWF.
The inheritance pattern for most VWD types is autosomal dominant. VWD type 1 and 2 are predominantly associated with heterozygous missense variants that exhibit a dominant-negative effect [ , ]. Variants causing VWD type 1 are distributed throughout the VWF protein, while those contributing to VWD type 2 tend to cluster in specific domains of the VWF protein. For instance, the A1 domain of VWF, responsible for the interaction with GPIb, harbors most of the gain-of-function variants associated with VWD type 2B. In contrast, VWD type 2N and 3 arise from a recessive inheritance pattern. Type 2N VWD is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous missense variants in the FVIII binding domain or compound heterozygosity for a null allele and a missense variant in the FVIII binding domain, whereas VWD type 3 results in most cases from homozygosity or compound heterozygosity for null alleles [ ].
Diagnosis of VWD consists of an assessment of bleeding history and laboratory phenotypic assays. These assays include the measurement of plasma VWF antigen (VWF:Ag) levels, platelet-dependent VWF activity, FVIII activity, VWF multimer analysis and collagen binding [ ]. The heterogeneity and complexity of VWD, along with the need for highly specialized diagnostic testing, present challenges in achieving a precise and timely diagnosis of VWD. This may contribute to instances of underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis of VWD [ ], thereby complicating the accurate management of VWD. This review will describe the current treatment of VWD and its limitations and will focus on the development of new treatment approaches for VWD in the field of RNA therapeutics.

2.

## Current therapeutic landscape of VWD and its limitations
The management of VWD centers around normalizing circulating VWF and FVIII levels to prevent or stop bleeding events [ , ]. This can be achieved by inducing secretion of endogenously stored VWF and FVIII through the administration of desmopressin or by infusing an exogenous source of VWF concentrate, referred to as replacement therapy, which can be either plasma-derived or recombinant VWF (rVWF). Desmopressin (also known as 1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin or DDAVP) is the preferred treatment for individuals with VWD type 1. Its mode of action involves stimulating the release of VWF and FVIII from endothelial storage sites, resulting in a transient increase in these coagulation factors [ ]. Given this mode of action, desmopressin is not useful for individuals with VWD type 3, as they do not produce any VWF. Furthermore, desmopressin exhibits limited effectiveness in VWD type 2 due to the release of dysfunctional VWF, and its use is contraindicated in VWD type 2B as it may induce thrombocytopenia. Finally, the transient elevation of VWF levels and reduced responsiveness upon repetitive use (known as tachyphylaxis) limit its applicability in certain clinical scenarios [ ].
For VWD type 3 and most cases of VWD type 2, replacement therapy is currently the treatment of choice. It is effective for both on-demand and prophylactic management of bleeding events. Several plasma-derived factor concentrates are commercially available with varying ratios of VWF and FVIII. As most VWD patients have normal FVIII synthesis, monitoring plasma FVIII levels is crucial to assess the potential risk of FVIII accumulation and thrombotic complications associated with concentrate usage [ ]. Most plasma-derived concentrates lack the largest VWF multimers due to exposure of VWF to ADAMTS13 during manufacturing. rVWF addresses this limitation, containing the complete array of multimers, increasing its activity [ ]. A phase 3 clinical trial demonstrated the efficacy and safety of rVWF for both on-demand and prophylactic treatment and highlighted that switching from on-demand treatment with plasma-derived VWF products to rVWF prophylaxis reduced spontaneous bleeding events in patients with severe VWD [ , ]. The recent international guidelines on the management of VWD from 2021 also suggest the use of long-term prophylactic treatment for those patients with severe or frequent bleeds [ ]. Both desmopressin and VWF replacement therapy are usually combined with supportive treatment including antifibrinolytic agents (e.g. tranexamic acid) and hormonal treatment in women with VWD experiencing menorrhagia [ ].
Despite recent advancements regarding rVWF, the overall field of VWD treatment has not progressed drastically over the past years with a remaining unmet clinical need. Moreover, certain treatment challenges persist, such as repeated administration due to the short half-life of VWF, the intravenous administration route, the need for highly specialized treatment centers and more importantly, the continued presence of mutant VWF in the circulation which may have negative effects on hemostatic capacity by itself, even despite substitution therapy with normal VWF. In addition, the management of VWD can no longer be viewed separately from the patient’s disease burden. As highlighted by Denis et al. [ ], the use of health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) tools reveal disparities between the clinical and patient perspectives in VWD. Patients often experience a diminished QoL due to frequent hospitalizations, treatment administration, and periods of sick leaves.
The genetic heterogeneity of VWD further complicates the development of universal treatment decision tools, emphasizing the need for a personalized treatment approach tailored to the specific requirements of individual patients [ ]. Emerging therapies are increasingly focused on addressing the diverse subtypes of VWD. For example, emicizumab, a bispecific monoclonal antibody to factor IX and factor X to mimic FVIII function, has shown promise in addressing FVIII deficiency in VWD types 2N and 3 [ , ]. Similarly, efforts to extend the half-life of VWF using nanobody KB-V13A12 engineered to bind both albumin and VWF have successfully restored functional hemostasis in a mouse model of VWD type 1 [ ]. Another innovative approach involves the aptamer BT200 (rondaptivon pegol), initially developed as an antithrombotic agent [ , ]. This molecule binds to the VWF A1 domain, inhibiting its interaction with GPIba, and has demonstrated to elevate VWF antigen levels and FVIII activity, and prolong the effects of desmopressin in healthy individuals. This makes it a potentially valuable treatment option for thrombocytopenic patients with VWD type 2B. While a detailed discussion of these emerging therapies lies beyond the scope of this review, elaborate discussions have been provided in recent literature [ ].
These innovative therapeutic approaches show potential as alternatives or supplements to current standard treatments. However, they do not fully address the previously mentioned persistent presence of mutant VWF. In this context, the field of RNA therapeutics has gained considerable interest because of their ability to alter gene expression and directly target the genetic root cause of VWD [ ].

3.

## Introduction to RNA therapeutics
3.1.

## From discovery to clinical success
The landscape of RNA therapeutics has been shaped by a series of landmark discoveries that have helped in our understanding and clinical management of both viral infections and genetic disorders. Already in 1978, Zamecnik and Stephenson published on the potential of oligonucleotides in inhibiting the replication of viral Rous sarcoma virus messenger RNA (mRNA) [ ]. The term ‘hybridon’ was coined to describe the tridecamer deoxyribonucleotide utilized in this study, which we now recognize as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs). Notably, it took two decades for the approval of the first ASO therapy, fomivirsen, by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment of cytomegalovirus retinitis [ ]. The discovery of RNA interference (RNAi) beginning in 1990, followed by its explanation in 1998 by Fire et al. further underscored the potential of RNA therapeutics [ , ]. In their investigations involving the Caenorhabditis elegans, Fire et al. demonstrated that the introduction of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), rather than single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), yielded highly efficient and specific interference with gene expression [ ]. Subsequent studies revealed that dsRNA was processed into shorter small non-coding RNA fragments, including small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), inducing RNAi [ , ]. In contrast to the extensive timeline associated with the development of the first ASO therapy, the translation of RNAi discovery and its underlying mechanisms into a therapy was relatively short. In 2002, McCaffrey et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of siRNA-mediated RNAi in suppressing the replication of human hepatitis C viral RNA expressed in adult mice [ ]. This stimulated the development of siRNA candidates for indications ranging from asthma to solid tumors [ ]. These early investigations and clinical trials attracted significant investment from pharmaceutical companies, acknowledging RNAi not merely as a research instrument but as a potent therapeutic asset. As a result, we can now distinguish different types of RNA therapeutics ( ).
 However, alongside these advancements came initial challenges. Research groups highlighted the potential of siRNAs to trigger innate immune responses and encountered issues with delivery, primarily relying on ‘naked’ administration methods [ ]. This prompted a renewed focus on the scientific properties of RNAi therapeutics, particularly emphasizing chemical modifications to enhance specificity and affinity. In 2018, Alnylam Pharmaceuticals’ liver-directed patisiran became the first siRNA treatment to be approved by the FDA for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis [ ]. Currently, there exists a diverse array of experimental delivery methods for RNAi therapeutics including encapsulation in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) or conjugation to N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) with numerous ongoing clinical trials which will not be the focus of this review but which are elaborately summarized by Setten et al. and others [ , ].

3.2.

## RNA therapeutics in bleeding disorders
RNA therapeutics are gaining increasing attention in the field of inherited bleedings disorders as a way to address unmet clinical needs. In particular, the clinical research and management of hemophilia A and B, which are X-linked bleeding disorders characterized by a deficiency of coagulation factor VIII or IX, respectively, represent leading areas where RNA therapies are being actively explored and applied. A recent review series focused on RNA targeting of hematologic disorders has shed light on the latest advancements in RNA therapy for hemophilia. Both this review series and other work reveal a notable divergence in the development of novel therapies for hemophilia compared to those for VWD [ , , ]. Recent advancements have moved hemophilia treatment in a different trajectory where the approval of adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy for both hemophilia A (valoctocogene roxaparvovec) and B (etranacogene dezaparvovec) stands as a significant milestone. Additionally, several early and late-phase clinical trials are exploring the use of RNA therapeutics, such as an siRNA against antithrombin (fitusiran) [ , ]. In contrast, the development of VWD treatment evolved not much further than beyond approval of rVWF. Given the similar challenges in treating both hemophilia and VWD, along with their shared monogenic nature and distinct genotype-phenotype relationship, it encourages examination into what insights from the field of gene therapy can be used in VWD as well as how RNA therapeutics can complement these approaches [ ].

4.

## RNA therapeutics and VWD
4.1.

## RNA therapeutics versus current VWD treatment
RNA therapeutics could offer a promising alternative for treating VWD, because they have the ability to target the genetic root cause of the disease with high specificity unlike desmopressin and VWF replacement therapy, which primarily manage symptoms. Treatment with desmopressin is favored due to its subcutaneous and intranasal administration options and cost-effectiveness compared to VWF replacement therapy with VWF concentrates. A favorable response to desmopressin is characterized by a minimum twofold increase in baseline VWF:Ag levels accompanied by VWF and FVIII levels above 0.50 IU/mL [ ]. Nevertheless, the efficacy of desmopressin varies considerably among patients, with the majority of individuals diagnosed with VWD type 1 demonstrating a positive response, whereas only a small subset of those with VWD type 2 exhibit responsiveness [ ]. In clinical practice, close monitoring of VWF:Ag and VWF and FVIII activity levels is needed both prior to and following initial desmopressin administration. Upon confirming a positive response, desmopressin infusions can be administered at intervals ranging from every 12 to 24 hours, depending on the type and severity of bleeding episodes, but no longer than three consecutive days. RNA therapeutics, on the other hand, could selectively target the molecular defects associated with VWD, with the potential to reduce interpatient variability, be effective in patients unresponsive to desmopressin, and customize treatment to address various subtypes of the condition.
Current VWF replacement therapy entails dosing regimens determined by body weight, VWD subtype and bleeding site, with options for on-demand or prophylactic administration [ ]. Both on-demand and prophylactic treatment create a substantial burden to the patient due to the need for repetitive intravenous administrations at short intervals. Depending on the specific indication (i.e. major or minor surgery), prophylactic treatment may involve daily administration or a single pre-procedural dose, with long-term prophylaxis typically entailing weekly administrations for at least six months, possibly escalating to three times per week depending on bleeding severity, as outlined by Franchini et al. [ ] The associated treatment burden could be alleviated with RNA therapeutics, offering less frequent dosing schedules and potential administration outside specialized settings. Approved RNA therapeutics, including ASOs, siRNAs and aptamers exhibit diverse dosing intervals and administration routes tailored to specific indications [ ]. For instance, the ASO nusinersen, indicated for spinal muscular atrophy, is given intrathecally, with an initial four-dose regimen over two months followed by maintenance doses every four months [ , ]. Similarly, inotersen, indicated for familial amyloid polyneuropathy, is administered subcutaneously once weekly, facilitating home administration [ ]. In the field of siRNAs, inclisiran, aimed at lowering low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, is administered via a single subcutaneous injection every six months following an initial loading dose at zero and three months, patisiran for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis is administered intravenously every three weeks, while givosiran for acute hepatic porphyria offers monthly subcutaneous administration [ , , ]. Although clinical data on RNA therapeutics in VWD is lacking, evidence from the aforementioned approved RNA therapeutics suggests potentially long-term efficacy, reducing the necessity for frequent administrations and hospital visits. Long-term efficacy may also lead to more stable correction of the VWD phenotype in contrast to the high peak and low trough levels of VWF inherent to the current treatment modalities with desmopressin and VWF concentrates. Moreover, already approved siRNAs generally demonstrate a favorable safety profile, demonstrating high specificity and efficiency even at low doses. While off-target effects remain a potential concern due to the choice of delivery system, advancements in chemical modifications and improved delivery strategies have significantly reduced these risks, offering a promising and safe therapeutic approach [ , ].

4.2.

## RNA therapeutics versus developments in VWD gene therapy
The promising properties of RNA therapy, with regards to its potential long-term effectiveness, make it a strong candidate for the treatment of VWD in comparison to existing therapeutic approaches. To really phenotypically cure VWD, however, gene therapy would seem a more appropriate choice. In hemophilia research, progress has been made toward achieving a phenotypic cure through the approval of AAV-based gene therapy. Conversely, gene therapy for VWD has predominantly been studied in in vivo models and has not progressed beyond preclinical studies. Initial attempts by Marx et al. and De Meyer et al. to correct severe VWD in VWF-deficient (Vwf−/−) mice through hepatocyte-directed hydrodynamic injection of high doses of wild-type (WT) murine Vwf (mVwf) complementary DNA (cDNA) demonstrated that VWF expression levels were supraphysiological and declined rapidly, indicating the transient nature of this approach [ , ]. Moreover, the multimeric profile of VWF exhibited imbalances between low-molecular-weight multimers (LMWMs) and high-molecular-weight multimers (HMWMs), likely stemming from non-physiological liver-mediated synthesis of VWF [ ]. Inspired by gene transfer strategies in hemophilia, researchers explored AAV and lentiviral vectors for VWD. The use of AAV vectors faces challenges due to their limited packaging capacity which is insufficient for the 8.4 kb human VWF cDNA. This led to development of dual vector strategies where segmented VWF was packaged into two vectors. These studies resulted in subtherapeutic VWF expression in vitro and failed to yield detectable multimers at variable time points in vivo [ , ]. Although lentiviral-mediated gene transfer of human VWF initially showed promising results with high vector titers and a full spectrum of VWF multimers in vitro, subsequent in vivo studies in Vwf−/−mice failed to achieve sufficient VWF levels for phenotypic improvement [ , ]. Finally, the use of the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR‐associated protein 9 (Cas9) system to target gene expression in the context of VWD has been explored primarily to study cellular processes, and, more recently, to develop mouse and rat models mimicking VWD types 2N and 3, respectively [ ].
While these studies represent advancements in the field of gene therapy for VWD and suggest the theoretical feasibility of such treatments, they underscore that gene therapy is still in its early stages and unlikely to yield a viable treatment in the near future. RNA therapy, on the other hand, has demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential, resulting in the development of numerous experimental tools that can be applied to VWD. Unlike gene therapy, where coexpression of mutant VWF alongside transgenic VWF may perpetuate the phenotypic impact of the mutant variant and cause irreversible effects, RNA therapy has the capability to reversibly correct mutant VWF and prevent its phenotypic manifestations.

5.

## Development of siRNA-mediated treatment for VWD
5.1.

## Why an siRNA approach is attractive for VWD
The previous paragraphs have highlighted that existing treatment options and possible future gene therapy are characterized by unmet clinical needs and practical hurdles. As stressed before, existing therapies fail to address the persistent production of mutant VWF, which leads to secretion of functionally defective VWF multimers. The dominant-negative effect of many VWF variants makes RNA therapeutics an appealing strategy for VWD types 1, 2A, 2B and 2 M ( ). This characteristic of VWD has been used in preclinical studies exploring the use of siRNAs. Theoretically, siRNA induced allele-selective degradation of mRNA of the mutant VWF allele would prevent the synthesis of mutant VWF protein. This, in turn, could increase VWF survival, reduce the formation of defective VWF multimers, and improve the overall hemostatic function of VWF. The subtypes of VWD that are not driven by dominant-negative effects will not benefit from an siRNA approach. For these cases, novel therapies targeting FVIII deficiency or prolonging VWF half-life may represent valuable additions to the treatment landscape, particularly for VWD types 1, 2N and 3 [ ]. The practical applications of an siRNA treatment in dominant-negative VWD will be discussed in the next paragraphs.

5.2.

## siRNAs in VWD: variant-based targeting
The applicability of targeting dominant-negative variants with siRNAs has been successfully studied both in vitro and in vivo in various dominant-negative diseases, including neurodegenerative diseases, and skin and muscle disorders, although clear clinical outcomes have yet to be demonstrated [ ]. In VWD research, the first attempt aimed at counteracting the dominant-negative effect of mutant VWF was described by Casari et al. in 2010 [ ]. This study investigated a previously reported de novo heterozygous variant (p.P1127_C1948delinsR) [ ], which produces an in-frame deleted protein resulting in a severe VWD type 2A phenotype. A heterozygous in vitro model was created by cotransfecting equal amounts of WT and mutant VWF plasmids. To explore the potential rescue of the dominant-negative phenotype, characterized by impaired secretion of HMWMs and reduced collagen-binding activity, a specific siRNA targeting the deletion breakpoint was designed and transfected. Western blot analysis demonstrated a substantial increase in the proportion of WT VWF compared to mutant VWF after siRNA treatment. This increase was associated with a three-fold rise in VWF:Ag levels and an improvement in collagen-binding activity from undetectable levels to 28.0%. Although the multimeric profile showed a clearer presence of HMWMs, quantitative measurement was not conducted. Subsequently, the same in-frame deletion was studied in vivo using Vwf−/− mice expressing equal amounts of WT mVwf and mutant mVwf through hepatocyte-directed hydrodynamic injection [ ]. Consistent with patient and in vitro observations, these mice exhibited an absence of HMWMs. Treatment with an optimized siRNA targeting the deletion breakpoint led to improved multimer profiles, but full phenotypic correction was not achieved, indicating the persistence of the dominant-negative effect.
This proof-of-concept study demonstrates the promise of silencing mutant VWF in vitro and in vivo, despite certain limitations, particularly regarding liver-directed gene transfer and the efficiency of the specific transfection reagent used. An important general limitation of this RNAi approach, where the siRNA targets a specific variant, is the extensive diversity of missense variants associated with VWD. Currently, over 750 unique variants have been reported in VWD [ ]. While some variants are recurrent and present in multiple patients, the majority of patients have distinct, unique VWF variants. Consequently, the siRNAs used in this previous proof-of-concept study would only be applicable to patients carrying that specific gene defect targeted by those siRNAs. Further development of siRNA therapies targeting specific VWD variants would require testing numerous siRNA compounds, with the effectiveness and specificity possibly varying for each patient.

5.3.

## siRNAs in VWD: SNP-based targeting
To advance toward a personalized yet widely applicable therapeutic strategy, we and others have demonstrated the concept, feasibility and potential of allele-selective silencing targeting heterozygous single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) instead of the specific disease-causing variants ( ) [ , ]. This strategy involves directing siRNAs toward the SNP allele situated on the same allele as the dominant-negative variant, thereby inhibiting the expression of the mutant allele while preserving the expression of the normal allele. As this requires information on which SNP allele is in linkage with the disease-causing variant, the choice of siRNA is tailored to the genotype of VWD patients. This approach has previously been explored for dominant-negative diseases, including Huntington’s disease, with studies indicating that employing a combination of five siRNAs targeting three distinct SNP sites could potentially treat three-quarters of Huntington’s disease patients in the United States and Europe [ , ].

 In the field of VWD, the potential of SNP-based targeting has been studied across various experimental models [ , , ]. De Jong et al. selected a panel of four SNPs within the coding region of VWF exhibiting a high minor allele frequency ( ) [ ]. Their analysis revealed that 74% of the general Caucasian population is heterozygous for at least one of these four SNPs. Subsequently, several siRNAs for each SNP were designed and screened for their efficacy and specificity in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells stably producing VWF. To mimic heterozygous expression, HEK293 cells were cotransfected with two VWF plasmids differing in the nucleotide at the position of the SNP, representing the two respective SNP-alleles, one of which will be the allele targeted by the siRNA and the other the untargeted allele. Among several siRNAs tested, the 11 most effective siRNAs were selected based on their impact on VWF:Ag and VWF mRNA levels. These siRNAs were then used to study their potential in the presence of a dominant-negative variant. HEK293 cells were cotransfected with normal VWF and mutant VWF harboring the VWD type 2A p.Cys2773Ser (c.8318 G>C) variant along with a target SNP. This cotransfection led to a severe defect in multimerization, as in patients with this variant, characterized by a decrease in HMWMs and a large multimer index of 50.1%, the latter being used to quantify the increase of large multimers upon siRNA treatment. After siRNA treatment, an improvement in the multimer pattern and index was observed. The most effective siRNA achieved a multimer index as high as 85.3%, nearing the 100% index observed in cells transfected with WT VWF only. This proof-of-concept study showed initial signs of allele-selective silencing of mutant VWF based on a single nucleotide difference between the mutant and normal VWF allele leading to phenotypic correction in vitro. Nevertheless, the HEK293 model used lacks endogenous production of VWF, necessitating an exogenous source of overexpression of plasmids.
To explore SNP-based targeting in a model that closely mimics physiological conditions, endothelial colony forming cells (ECFCs) were isolated from the peripheral blood of both healthy donors and a patient with VWD type 2A [ ]. Cultured ECFCs display endothelial cell-like characteristics and offer a significant advantage over HEK293 cells because of their endogenous production of VWF and its storage in Weibel-Palade bodies. Similar to HEK293 cells transfected with WT VWF plasmids, siRNA transfections in ECFCs from healthy donors heterozygous for the previously selected panel of SNPs resulted in robust knockdown of the targeted VWF allele at the mRNA level and demonstrated their ability to discriminate between VWF alleles based on a single nucleotide difference [ ]. The duration of VWF inhibition was studied using an siRNA targeting total VWF and downregulation was observed for up to 28 days with the strongest inhibition observed six days after transfection. Subsequent investigations of SNP-based targeting of siRNAs in ECFCs from a patient with the VWD type 2A p.Cys1190Tyr (c.3569 G>A) variant showed that inhibition of the mutant VWF allele led to near-normal correction of VWF collagen binding, improvement in the multimeric profile of conditioned medium and a decrease in the endoplasmic reticulum retention of defectively processed VWF [ ]. Conversely, transfection of the siRNA targeted against a SNP-allele located on the WT VWF allele exacerbated the disease phenotype of these patient ECFCs, characterized by an increase in VWF retention in the endoplasmic reticulum.
Following the promising results of these in vitro and ex vivo proof-of-concept studies, our group continued to study this SNP-based siRNA approach in an in vivo setting. Designing such in vivo experiments presents several challenges. As previously described, in the field of VWF and VWD research, in vivo mouse studies have primarily used liver-directed gene transfer strategies to induce mVwf expression in the liver. Under physiological conditions, however, VWF is synthesized and stored in endothelial cells and megakaryocytes. Therefore, an ideal in vivo model should mimic endogenous endothelial expression of VWF which in turn requires an endothelial delivery vehicle for a (SNP-based) siRNA approach. A second challenge specific to the SNP-based siRNA approach is the lack of SNPs in the Vwf gene of mouse inbred strains, as these strains are homozygous for all genetic loci. This genetic uniformity complicates the application of SNP-based siRNA strategies in these models.
To address the first challenge of achieving endothelial expression of Vwf, our group has used a variety of WT and VWD mouse models with endogenous Vwf expression which will be discussed in more detail below [ , , ]. For the endothelial delivery of siRNAs, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) which were developed by Dahlman et al. [ ] and are composed of an ionizable, low-molecular weight polymer, termed 7C1, an siRNA and a lipid poly(ethylene glycol) were used. It was shown in vivo both in mouse models and non-human primates, that 7C1 LNPs were well-tolerated showing no signs of acute toxicity. At a single low dose, these nanoparticles effectively target the endothelium of multiple organ systems, including the endothelial cells of the lungs, heart and kidneys [ , ]. This effective delivery to lung endothelial cells is a favorable characteristic in the context of VWF and VWD, as the lung endothelium significantly contributes to VWF production [ ].
To address the lack of SNPs in the Vwf gene of mouse inbred strains in our in vivo studies, genetic differences between the Vwf genes of two commonly used mouse inbred strains, the C57BL/6J (B6) and 129S1/SvImJ (129S) mouse strain, were used as proxies for human SNPs [ , ]. Jongejan et al. designed strain-selective siRNAs that could discriminate between B6 and 129S Vwf alleles based on a single nucleotide difference [ ]. They identified the most potent siRNAs, demonstrating superior inhibition and strain-selectivity in vitro, for further analysis in vivo [ ]. Homozygous WT-B6 and homozygous WT-129S mice were intravenously injected with 7C1 LNPs complexed with one of the following siRNAs: a scrambled control siRNA (siCtrl), a nonselective siRNA (siVwf) targeting both B6 and 129S Vwf alleles, the strain-selective siVwf.B6 targeting only the B6 Vwf allele or the strain-selective siVwf.129S targeting only the 129S Vwf allele. First, using only the siCtrl and siVwf, the efficient delivery to the lung endothelium was confirmed, showing that the inhibitory effects of siVwf on lung Vwf mRNA and plasma VWF were sustained for 10 days post-siRNA injection. To validate allele-selective inhibition, 7C1 LNPs complexed with siVwf.B6 or siVwf.129S were injected into their corresponding (i.e. siVwf.B6 in B6 mice) and non-corresponding (i.e. siVwf.B6 in 129S mice) strain. Injection of siVwf.B6 into B6 mice resulted in a median reduction of 83.0% in lung Vwf mRNA levels and an 87.7% reduction in plasma VWF levels 96 hours post-siRNA injection which was sustained for at least 10 days. Injection of siVwf.B6 into 129S mice showed only minimal inhibitory effects, as expected. Comparable results on lung Vwf mRNA and plasma VWF levels were observed for siVwf.129S in 129S mice, though the allele-selectivity of this siRNA was less pronounced. Further studies were conducted in a more complex heterozygous setting by crossing homozygous WT-B6 and homozygous WT-129S mice to generate heterozygous B6.129S mice [ ]. It was confirmed that both siVwf.B6 and siVwf.129S were allele-selective, with siVwf.B6 having an overall better performance in the different genetic backgrounds. Specifically in these heterozygous mice, siVwf.B6 treatment resulted in a reduction of 41.0% in total Vwf lung mRNA and a median reduction of 66.0% in B6 Vwf versus 12.0% of 129S Vwf. In addition, Jongejan et al. [ ] explored the effects of allele-selective inhibition following thrombotic and bleeding challenges, reasoning that this SNP-based siRNA approach could be relevant not only for VWD but also for addressing high VWF levels associated with increased risks of arterial and venous thrombosis [ , ]. In this study, it was found that in B6.129S mice subjected to a ferric-chloride induced vascular injury model and a tail-bleeding assay, siVwf.B6 decreased thrombus formation in the mesenteric arterioles without prolonging tail-bleeding times.
To further investigate the therapeutic potential of these allele-selective siRNAs, a VWD type 2B mouse model was used that was developed through knock-in of the severe p.Val1316Met (c.3946 G>A) variant in homozygous B6 (2B-B6) mice [ ]. These homozygous mice displayed a VWD type 2B phenotype similar to that observed in humans, with endogenous endothelial expression of mutant Vwf. To facilitate the allele-selective siRNA approach, homozygous 2B-B6 mice were crossed with homozygous WT-129S mice to generate a heterozygous 2B-B6.129S offspring [ ]. These offspring harbored one normal 129S Vwf allele, one mutant B6 Vwf allele and the strain-specific genetic difference to be targeted by siRNAs. Using this model in combination with the previously studied 7C1 LNPs complexed with siRNAs, it was demonstrated that allele-selective silencing of the mutant Vwf allele was feasible, resulting in inhibition of mutant VWF at the protein level. Treatment with the allele-selective siVwf.B6 in the 2B-B6.129S mice improved the VWF multimeric structure, characterized by an increase in HMWMs and a decrease in LMWMs. This improvement in multimeric phenotype was further supported by an increased affinity of VWF to collagen and normalization of tail-bleeding times in two-thirds of the 2B-B6.129S mice. Overall, this study contributes to the growing evidence supporting the efficacy of allele-selective siRNAs targeting a single nucleotide variation. Although those proof-of-concept mouse studies were performed using siRNAs designed for targeting genetic variations in murine Vwf, the in vitro and ex vivo experiments targeting human VWF sequences showed similar feasibility for SNP-based allele-selective silencing. Future research should therefore prioritize using siRNAs targeting human SNPs in human VWF.

6.

## Conclusion
Despite an unmet clinical need, the field of VWD treatment has not progressed drastically over the past years, unlike related fields such as hemophilia, which has seen the introduction of non-factor replacement bispecific antibody treatment (emicizumab), progress with the approval of AAV-based gene therapy, the development of rebalancing agents like inhibitors of tissue factor pathway inhibitor and an siRNA inhibiting antithrombin (fitusiran). Current VWD treatments aim to normalize circulating VWF and FVIII levels to prevent or stop bleeding events. However, these treatments do not address the undesirable effects of the persistent presence of mutant VWF in the circulation. siRNAs present a promising alternative, as they can reversibly silence circulating mutant VWF in dominant-negative VWD and prevent its phenotypic manifestations. Moreover, the potential long-term inhibitory effect of siRNAs reduces the need for frequent administration, unlike standard treatments as desmopressin and VWF concentrates. The siRNAs can be designed to target specific heterozygous variants but given the genetic heterogeneity and extensive diversity of missense variants associated with VWD, a more promising and more broadly applicable approach involves targeting a common heterozygous SNP located on the same VWF allele as the dominant-negative variant. Proof-of-concept studies using siRNAs in various VWF and VWD related experimental models have shown that allele-selective silencing of VWF is feasible based on one nucleotide difference between alleles. In addition, endothelial targeting of VWF was achieved in vivo using siRNAs complexed in LNPs resulting in a strong and selective knockdown of the targeted allele. Finally, allele-selective siRNAs were able to correct the disease phenotype 1) in vitro, in HEK293 cells expressing mutant VWF harboring the VWD type 2A p.Cys2773Ser variant, 2) ex vivo, in ECFCs from a patient with the VWD type 2A p.Cys1190Tyr variant, and 3) in vivo, in mice heterozygous for the VWD type 2B p.Val1316Met variant. These promising findings offer great potential for developing a customized clinical approach to VWD treatment.

7.

## Expert opinion
The primary objective of current treatments for VWD is to increase VWF and FVIII levels to improve platelet adhesion, aggregation, and fibrin formation to a level sufficient to adequately deal with clinically relevant conditions. While these treatments are generally effective in preventing or stopping unwanted bleeding, they are not without drawbacks. Patients often require frequent, repeated intravenous administration of VWF concentrates or desmopressin with only a short-lasting effect, they may experience side effects, and may face frequent hospitalizations at specialized centers. These factors all impact and reduce HR-QoL among VWD patients, reflecting impairments in both physical and social functioning [ , , ]. More importantly, with current treatments, mutant VWF remains in the circulation, resulting in the competition between mutant and normal VWF and continuous secretion of functionally defective VWF multimers. These functionally defective VWF multimers can cause detrimental effects, despite the presence of normal VWF, including onset or worsening of thrombocytopenia in VWD type 2B, gastrointestinal bleeding as a result of angiodysplasia, increased clearance of VWF and FVIII or a secretion defect. An siRNA-based treatment for VWD would potentially have a long-term corrective effect (maybe lasting for weeks to months) requiring only a low frequency of administration. Such a prolonged treatment could be a very good option for those patients requiring long-term prophylaxis (for example in case of gastrointestinal bleeds due to angiodysplasia). It could also be applied as a single pre-operative administration to cover hemostasis during the entire surgical and post-operative period, leading to shortening of required hospitalization. Also, in case of heavy menstrual bleeding a single administration every month will probably be sufficient. After full development, the novel siRNA treatment could serve as a standalone treatment or be used together with other emerging therapies aimed at addressing FVIII deficiency or extending the half-life of VWF. Collectively, these alternatives will certainly lead to a change of treatment guidelines and may reduce overall healthcare costs related to VWD, but most importantly, it would positively influence the HR-QoL of VWD patients.
So far, the genetic heterogeneity of VWD has presented challenges in developing a universal gene-based treatment approach. In this review, we have shed light on the use of RNA therapeutics in the field of VWD with a particular focus on siRNAs. siRNAs have the ability to target specific variants with high precision, effectively inhibiting mutant VWF and tackling the dominant-negative phenotypic effects observed in most types of VWD. Given the extensive list of disease-causing variants associated with VWD, a variant-specific siRNA approach would require an equally extensive set of siRNAs. Instead, previous research has explored the feasibility of allele-selective silencing of mutant VWF with siRNAs targeting common SNPs in the coding region of VWF. This strategy allows for reversible correction of mutant VWF to prevent its phenotypic manifestations. Moreover, the dominant-negative siRNA-based approach has the potential to target a considerable portion of the population, as up to 75% of the African and Caucasian population is heterozygous for at least one of four selected SNPs with high minor allele frequencies in the VWF coding region [ ]. In addition, in other populations included in the 1000 Genomes Project heterozygosity for these SNPs is estimated to be 55% or higher. Consequently, a limited set of siRNAs could be developed to effectively treat a substantial part of the VWD population. In practice, it needs to be determined for each patient which SNP allele is in linkage with the disease-causing variant, meaning that the choice of the siRNA is tailored to the genotype of the patient.
While the studies performed have shown promising results in vitro, ex vivo and in vivo demonstrating phenotypic improvements, there are hurdles to continue these preclinical investigations toward clinical studies. As siRNA treatments are gene sequence specific it is not possible to test therapeutic siRNA compounds for effectivity and selectivity in small animal models as well as non-human primates because of the interspecies gene variations. Even though some parts of sequences may be homologous between species, targeting specific SNPs or variants will be even more difficult or impossible in other species. Therefore, further development of those allele-selective siRNAs could involve the development of humanized animal models, including fully humanized VWF, carrying the siRNA (therapeutic) target sequences, and optionally, when desired for evaluation of bleeding phenotypes humanized GPIb expression on platelets ( ). However, the creation of such models does not have to be a rate-limiting step for advancing siRNA therapies. Established cell models, such as patient-derived endothelial cells, can be used to evaluate effectivity and selectivity by screening multiple siRNA candidates that differ in their chemical modifications and how these human siRNAs might improve VWD phenotypes caused by different variants. Existing animal models can be used to assess safety and toxicity of both the siRNA and its delivery vehicle. This is important for understanding the long-term duration of the siRNA-mediated VWF inhibition and possible off-target effects as well as identifying the most optimal (LNP) delivery vehicle for endothelial targeting.

 We anticipate that the future of novel treatments for VWD will be driven by the development of allele-selective siRNAs. As the field progresses, there is likely to be an increased emphasis on personalized treatment approaches and patient-centered care, which will enhance HR-QoL for individuals with VWD. Prioritizing therapies that offer more reliable correction of the disease, along with reduced frequency of hospitalizations and treatment administrations, should be a key focus in VWD research.

## Article highlights

The overall field of von Willebrand disease (VWD) treatment has not progressed drastically over the past years with a remaining unmet clinical need.
The genetic heterogeneity of VWD complicates the development of universal treatment decision tools, emphasizing the need for a personalized treatment approach tailored to the specific requirements of individual patients.
RNA-based therapies could offer a promising alternative, as they have the ability to target the genetic root cause of the disease with high specificity unlike current treatments, which primarily manage symptoms.
Allele-selective small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs) are able to correct disease phenotype in different experimental models and have the potential to target a large part of the VWD population.
Future research should focus on further assessing allele-selective siRNAs on efficacy and safety in established experimental models
