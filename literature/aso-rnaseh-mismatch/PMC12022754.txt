CLN7 Batten disease is a late-infantile onset, progressive neurodegenerative disease, leading to vision loss, seizures, dementia, motor dysfunction, and premature death. Due to the condition’s rarity and intronic location of her mutation, Mila had a protracted diagnostic odyssey, eventually requiring whole genome sequencing to identify a critical pathogenic deep intronic variant in CLN7, resulting in activation of a cryptic splice site that truncated the gene. While no traditional treatment options were available to modify the CLN7 Batten disease course, cryptic splicing variants are ideal targets for splice-modulating antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs). At the time of discovering the cryptic splicing variant, another splice-switching ASO, nusinersen, had just been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of spinal muscular atrophy [ ]. Nusinersen is delivered intrathecally and achieves widespread uptake throughout the central nervous system, demonstrating the feasibility of ASO treatment of brain and spinal cord diseases. Building on this breakthrough, a customized ASO therapy—later named milasen—was developed specifically for Mila by Dr Tim Yu at Boston’s Children Hospital within a remarkably short time: the experimental therapy was initiated within 10 months of completing the genetic diagnosis [ ]. Milasen not only improved Mila’s symptoms but also demonstrated that individualized therapy development is possible. This groundbreaking case inspired broader efforts to bring personalized therapies to others with serious, life-limiting, rare genetic diseases.
Several organizations—including the Dutch Center for RNA therapeutics, the n-Lorem Foundation, and various academic medical centers—began developing similar individualized therapy programs [ , ]. Pharmaceutical companies like EveryOne Medicines also expressed interest, seeing the potential of individualized therapies to address previously unmet medical needs.

## The need for a collaborative effort
While each n-of-1 ASO has to be developed for a unique context, requiring specific ASO design and preclinical testing, many processes of ASO development are shared [ ]. Furthermore, one can build on prior lessons learned from individual ASO developments that were successful or not. Thus, open sharing of data and experiences across n-of-1 programs provides a powerful mechanism to accelerate progress, particularly for platform modalities like ASOs. These platforms would create unique opportunities to connect clinical outcomes with sequence- or chemistry-specific toxicity data, informing trends in future drug development. Beyond advancing the science, transparent knowledge sharing fulfills an ethical imperative. Individuals with ultra-rare diseases often face limited or no treatment options, so ensuring that lessons learned from one case can inform others is essential to maximizing the impact of each therapeutic effort.
To address this critical need, the N=1 Collaborative (N1C)—a global platform for knowledge and data sharing—has taken the lead in fostering cross-sector dialogue, bringing together experts from academia, industry, regulatory agencies, and patient communities to drive progress in individualized therapies. Founded as a 501(c)(3) non-profit organization, N1C serves as an independent and neutral platform dedicated to advancing individualized medicines. Rather than directly developing n-of-1 treatments, N1C facilitates progress by providing guidance, frameworks, and shared infrastructure to empower stakeholders. By catalyzing the creation and dissemination of a rigorous, reproducible, and openly available pathway, N1C ensures that researchers, clinicians, drug developers, and patient communities can work together effectively. N1C enables data sharing through its efforts to create open databases of cases of mutations amenable to ASO correction, shared oligonucleotide controls for screening standardization, and regulatory document samples to hasten the formation of data packages for smaller clinical teams. Furthermore, it commits to continuously refining these resources to shape future best practices, driving the field toward sustainable growth and broader accessibility.
This N1C community is organized into working groups that focus on specific aspects of n-of-1 treatment development. N1C working groups have provided updates and learnings through online webinars and publications (https://www.n1collaborative.org/resources). Current N1C working groups include but are not limited to patient identification, preclinical development, data coordination, chemistry, manufacturing and control, institutional implementation, clinical outcomes, and access. The N1C working groups are linked by a Scientific Advisory Board, comprised of experts aligning in each of the key working group areas. In parallel to the scientific and clinical endeavors of N1C, the organization is propelled by its operation team and board of directors to support and make N1C efforts possible. N1C does not directly build therapeutics itself. Instead, the work of N1C aims to serve as collaborative enabler, paving paths for others to develop and dose customized nucleic acid-based therapeutics for patients with orphan diseases safely, rapidly, and at a global scale. N1C held its inaugural meeting in October 2024, showcasing recent collaborative development and brought together members, key stakeholders, and interested parties to plan for the future (https://www.n1collaborative.org/annual-meeting). This review provides context to the concept of individualized treatment development, its challenges and highlights insights from the 2024 Annual Meeting, and examines the scientific and regulatory advancements needed to expand access to customized nucleic acid-based therapies.

## Patient and caregiver perspective
The Annual Meeting kicked off with a presentation from Yiwei She, mother of Leo, a boy with a severe, rare disease caused by a pathogenic variant in TNPO2 that causes a neurodevelopmental disorder, including seizures. While not the focus of the presentation, an individualized ASO has been developed for Leo by Creyon Bio. Yiwei openly shared her struggles to be a good mother to a son who can have seizures leading to respiratory distress, as well as being a good mother to a healthy daughter. She voiced that she feels there is a disconnection between patients who are eligible for treatments developed for a single individual or only a few patients and the drug development system, which is focused on larger groups. Personalized medicine should be more personal and directed at the individuals they are developed for. Instead, procedures for drug development for larger groups are imposed on individualized treatment approaches and they are clearly not fit for purpose. The inflexibility of the system is perceived as “impersonal” by parents of children who lack treatment options.
Many of these themes were elaborated upon in a panel discussion with parents of children with ultra-rare diseases, some of whom were in the midst of an n-of-1 ASO development journey, shared their experiences, openly discussing the daily challenges and realities of their family’s everyday life. For many families, receiving a diagnosis is only the beginning. The hardest part is coming to terms with what it means for the future. At times, it feels impossible to stay upright or care for yourself and other family members, including siblings. These families carry hopes and fears not only for their affected child but also for their siblings, who bear the emotional toll of watching a loved one struggle. Siblings often take on caregiving responsibilities well beyond their years, bearing the weight of endless doctor appointments and the absence of their brother or sister and a parent, with the hospital becoming a second home. They cope with worry and the pressure to grow up too quickly.
Parents also bear immense burdens, managing logistical challenges and the relentless demands of constant care while enduring the emotional and mental toll that affects the entire family. Parents described how they fight to advocate for their child every day, as it is all they can do to hold on to hope. Parents spoke of balancing that hope with the harsh reality that, for many, a treatment or cure may never come.
The nature of n-of-1 treatments brings risks and uncertainties that often extend well beyond standard treatments. Parents on the panel discussed how they have learned to cope with this uncertainty because the potential for improving their child’s quality of life outweighs the risks involved. They are keenly aware of the risk of not treating their child, and many parents have reached a point where they are willing to take on risks others might avoid because, for them, this journey is about more than conventional outcomes. They also stressed their unique perspectives on risk-benefit, emphasizing that their understanding of quality of life differs in ways that families with healthy children may not grasp, where some parents phrased that they have come to accept that death is not the scariest outcome. Parents intimately understand what their children endure and are uniquely positioned to advocate for them in ways that no one else can. When treatments are available, they are often not curative, but even small improvements can significantly ease the hardships faced.
Their stories provided a view that extended beyond most traditional clinical discussions, offering an intimate look into the day-to-day reality of life with a rare disease. The discussion highlighted unmet needs and the profound emotional, mental, and physical burdens carried by these families. Support is essential in the family’s journey, as there are often complex and evolving uncertainties in ultra-rare diseases and n-of-1 therapies. Every child, even one with a chronic or life-limiting condition, deserves a life that allows them to feel as normal as possible. No parent wants to watch their child struggle day after day. To reach more patients and families, as a field, we must challenge ourselves to step out of our comfort zones. Families call on the ecosystem, including researchers, industry, and regulators, to adopt a flexible and dynamic approach that moves the needle forward and aligns with the unique and immediate needs of patients with ultra-rare diseases.
One of the key takeaway messages to emerge from these discussions was the centrality of patients and their families to the evaluation of risks and benefits. With normal drug development, these assessments are made by clinicians and regulators. However, for n-of-1 treatment development, families play a critical role as well. They will bear the burden of the treatment and are best positioned to assess whether it may be worth the potential benefit. Clinicians continue to have crucial responsibilities to communicate clearly about potential treatment effects and to convey the uncertainties of safety and efficacy to avoid false hope and unrealistic expectations. Striking this balance between patient centricity and expert guidance requires a multidisciplinary approach involving families and clinicians but also ethicists and can be facilitated by formalized processes (see also below).

## What is involved in n-of-1 treatment development
From the above, it is clear that there is both a huge need and opportunity for n-of-1 treatments, but developing them is no sinecure and the developer needs to be aware of many aspects. A key feature of n-of-1 treatment development is the use of programmable genetic medicines as a foundational platform for creating individualized therapies. This approach leverages established technologies and learnings from approved drugs to develop custom therapeutics, resulting in significant efficiencies in time, cost, and risk compared to de novo drug development. To date, most examples of platform-based individualized therapies have utilized ASO technology. However, other genetic medicines, such as siRNA, CRISPR gene editing, and ADAR-based RNA editing, hold similar potential to follow this pathway. In these cases, the process is the product, creating a repeatable and adaptable framework for therapeutic development.
A comprehensive review by Jonker et al. [ ] outlines the current state of n-of-1 therapies and introduces a development roadmap for the field. While the therapeutic development process for n-of-1 treatments mirrors traditional drug development—encompassing patient selection, therapeutic design, preclinical evaluation, manufacturing, regulatory submissions, and clinical administration and trial data collection—there are critical differences that demand innovative approaches.
First, the urgency of n-of-1 treatments requires significantly accelerated timelines, as patients often present with severe, rapidly progressing diseases that cannot wait for conventional drug approval or belated incorporation of siloed learnings. Second, the complexity of development necessitates a collaborative approach, bringing together patients and their families, clinicians with expertise in the disease, and drug developers experienced in platform technologies. Finally, the individualized nature of these therapies results in heterogeneous data, which poses challenges for analysis but also offers unique opportunities to learn across programs and improve future therapies.
Despite these challenges, at least 28 patients have been treated with ASOs tailored to their specific gene mutations between 2018 and 2024. Table provides a summary of known cases of n-of-1 or n-of-few therapies dosed to patients. These initial cases demonstrate the viability of custom ASOs, yet thousands, if not millions, of additional patients still require variant-specific therapies. Achieving this goal will require a transformative shift in the scientific and regulatory landscape. Notably, it is challenging to give exact numbers as many rare diseases are underdiagnosed and some mutation types, such as the cryptic splicing variant, are often missed during genetic analysis. So it is possible that millions of patients may be eligible for variant-specific therapies
 As efforts expand, it is becoming evident that moving beyond isolated successes like milasen requires more than individual initiatives—collaboration across sectors is essential to creating a sustainable framework capable of treating many patients. The number of cases advanced in a short timespan is impressive but also points to the vital importance of cross-program coordination to extract generalizable learnings across disparate programs. Finding ways to harmonize trial designs, instituting appropriate safety monitoring protocols, and aligning the selection of outcome measures will be of increasing importance as these efforts scale.

## Geographic regulatory differences
Most of the current individualized ASO treatments are ongoing in the USA. Here, the regulatory pathway is clear, involving submitting an investigational new drug (IND) application to the FDA. The FDA has also issued draft guidance for the development of individualized ASOs by noncommercial entities.
Whereas regulation has been outlined in the United States, involving a centrally regulated review process through FDA with regards to safety and toxicity studies and manufacturing, in Europe, individualized ASO treatment may proceed on a named patient basis [ ]. The exact criteria vary from country to country. The basic principle is that the treatment is done under the responsibility of the treating clinician with approval from the local institutional review board. However, there are exceptions, e.g. in Spain approval from the regulatory authorities is needed before treatment can be initiated. In countries where only local approval is needed, the administrative burden is reduced, and this facilitates upscaling of individualized ASO treatment. At the same time, the lack of regulatory oversight poses a challenge, as academics, who often lead the n-of-1 ASO development, lack experience with clinical development of treatments and would benefit from a regulatory framework.
The 1 Mutation 1 Medicine (1M1M) initiative is an academically driven platform for the development and implementation of ASO treatments. 1M1M strives for a collaborative ecosystem in Europe and beyond. A dialogue has been initiated with the regulators, as the academic partners involved want to benefit from regulatory guidance and expertise [ ]. While there are specific regulations for advanced therapeutic medicinal products, like hospital exemption, these do not exist for ASOs.
1M1M has set up processes for n-of-1 ASO development within Europe. When a potentially eligible patient is identified, e.g. by the European Reference Network for rare neurological diseases, first a case dossier is submitted. This involves basic information that allows a quick screening of whether the pathogenic variant in principle would be eligible for ASO treatment and where the disease would be treatable. Once this is the case, an online gene group meeting is convened. For this, a more extensive dossier has to be prepared with more information on the candidate patient. This dossier is then discussed by expert researchers on the gene, the protein, the pathology and by expert clinicians to assess whether there is enough information to decide on treatment readiness. If the outcome is negative, this can be a permanent no (disease or patient not eligible) or temporary, when more information needs to be collected, for instance, when the pathomechanism is not fully elucidated. If the outcome is positive, a treatment board is convened with a wider group of experts, also involving patient representatives and ethicists. Here the discussion is patient focused, to assess whether everything is in place to develop the treatment for the patient and to implement clinical treatment once it is ready. This will involve discussions on whether the anticipated benefit outweighs the burden of the treatment for the specific patient and on how to measure safety and efficacy in the patient, based also on input from the patient and family. Thus far, 25 case dossiers have been submitted, and six gene group and three treatment board meetings have been held.
Additional national efforts to streamline n-of-1 development are occurring as well, e.g. efforts in Japan and the Netherlands (Dutch Center for RNA Therapeutics in the Netherlands) [ ]. In the UK, a launchpad has been set up that does not only involve a mechanism for development of n-of-1 ASOs but also for reimbursement [ ]. This is a collaboration between Genomics England, the Medicine Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency, Oxford Harrington, ABPI, EveryOne Medicines, and Mila’s Miracle Foundation. The UK Launchpad aims to set up a process for individualized treatment, starting with a pilot for ASO treatments. The focus is on setting up a sustainable and scalable system, with an appropriate regulatory pathway and also including reimbursement and ensuring equity in access. The premise of the UK Launchpad is that a sustainable process for individualized treatment development, reimbursement, and access can only be realized if those involved are prepared to think differently and beyond the processes that are in place for common therapy development and approval. The idea is to treat patients in accredited centers and to learn via a reiterative process. The UK Launchpad aims to address the different aspects that are involved in n-of-1 treatment development, including trial design, ensuring safety, risk tolerance, how to consent, data collection, oversight of the data, reimbursement, and marketing with limited evidence.

## Critical areas for growth

## Diagnosing and identifying patients for customized ASO therapies
Identifying candidates for customized ASOs remains a central challenge in ultra-rare disease therapeutics. For patients and their families, the diagnostic journey can be lengthy, emotionally taxing, and often takes months or even years. Even after a genetic diagnosis is made and a genetic cause identified, not all rare genetic variants are amenable to correction by ASOs. Until recently, information and guidelines on which variants might be suitable for ASO-based treatment were limited or non-existent.
One key barrier has been the limited data on the pathogenicity (or not) of genetic variants outside of the coding region, as clinical sequencing has, until recently, primarily relied on exome sequencing rather than whole genome sequencing, including also intronic sequencing. Additionally, the currently available bioinformatic tools for splicing analysis limit our understanding of which and how variants alter pre-RNA splicing. Further, understanding the pathogenic mechanisms by which a gene variant affects protein function or expression is essential for selecting the correct ASO strategy, but the available information can vary widely based on the variant, gene, and disease.
Despite the challenges, recent work has accelerated the characterization of genetic variants and our ability to identify patients for individualized therapies. ASO-correctable variants include, but are not limited to, those where addressing a mis-splicing event, such as a cryptic splice site, can restore gene expression, where ASO-mediated exon skipping can bypass a frame-shifting or truncating variant in in-frame exons to allow production of a partially functional protein, or where a dominant negative or gain-of-function variant can be corrected using a transcript-lowering agent like a gapmer ASO [ ]. Guidelines have been published to guide clinicians and researchers to identify if and how an ASO may correct various mutations [ , ]. These tools illustrate how varied types of mutations disrupt normal protein or gene function and explain how different modalities of ASOs can potentially correct or improve the final gene product or protein. Within these broad categories lies a vast and complex array of unique nuances, making it essential to evaluate each variant on an individual basis. To facilitate assessment of eligibility the N1C patient eligibility taskforce has produced guidelines. These guidelines, the Variant Assessments towards Eligibility for Antisense Oligonucleotide Treatment (N1C VARIANT), provide a detailed, step-by-step workbook and decision tree characterizing genetic variants through the lens of ASO-based therapies [ ]. This framework aims to enable clinicians with a genetics background, but perhaps not oligonucleotide-based drug design backgrounds, to analyze patients’ variants and assess their ASO amenability.
Some key advancements have been the comprehensive profiling of genetic variants associated with monogenic conditions. Landmark work published in 2023 provided the first framework by which to analyze gene variants within a single disorder, using ataxia–telangiectasia (A–T) as an example [ ]. This work identified that ∼15% of variants across the ATM gene leading to A–T were at least possibly amenable to oligonucleotide correction [ ].
An ongoing challenge is that, even when a variant is identified, its mechanism of action must be well understood to determine the suitability of an ASO approach and to evaluate the success or failure of the intervention. Currently, this requires extensive in vitro validation in the lab, e.g. to confirm mis-splicing or whether a pathogenic variant acts through a gain or loss of function mechanism. Though limitations still remain, the initial breakthrough efforts to compile genetic data across multiple rare diseases, characterize variants, and provide open-access guidelines for assessing ASO eligibility represent leaps for understanding the genetics of ASO-amenable rare disease patients.
Following the publication and release of the N1C VARIANT guidelines, the next phase will focus on implementation and interactive feedback to refine these frameworks. Although not all variants are amenable to ASO correction, advances in sequencing and oligonucleotide therapeutics are paving the way to address more variants. In the coming years, these consensus guidelines will hopefully shorten the time required to identify ASO-eligible patients, ultimately improving patient access to personalized ASO therapies.

## ASO design strategies to correct rare mutations: challenges and considerations
For ASO design within an industry setting, usually an ASO screen is done, first screening the entire exon and flanking regions (splicing modulation) or transcript (gapmers) and then narrowing down with a walk on regions of interest. For exon skipping ASOs, design guidelines that take into account splicing motifs, thermodynamic properties of ASOs and their target and general motifs that should be avoided, have been published [ ] and similar guidelines for gapmers are forthcoming (Watts et al., manuscript in preparation). Using these design guidelines, it may be possible to identify functional ASOs using a smaller screen (e.g. after screening 10–100 rationally selected ASOs versus 1000 broad ASOs), though sometimes more extensive screening will be needed. It should be stated that these values are a general estimate, but each study will vary the number of ASOs tested based on multiple parameters, such as the length of the sequence and chemical modifications needed. Beyond the sequence itself, ASO function, target-binding efficacy, potency, and duration of effect can be altered by modifying the chemical modification pattern [ ]. Whether the workflow to identify a functional ASO is best performed by identifying a potent target sequence and then modifying the ASO chemistry, or instead by screening a combinatorial variety of ASO chemistries and sequences in the initial screen, is not yet clear [ ]. However, it is essential to acknowledge that ASO design will be impacted by its chemistry; therefore, this cannot be taken for granted during the ASO design process. While chemical permutations are possible under the remit of FDA regulation, for the named patient setting that is used in Europe, only ASO chemistries that have been approved for larger groups of patients can be used to develop n-of-1 ASOs.
Notably, the n-of-1 development of ASOs will lead to a vast increase of scale for clinically developed ASOs. Sharing successes and failures between developers will facilitate reiterative learning and will improve future design.
The models and steps needed for preclinical development of n-of-1 ASOs will vary based on the approach. For instance, for in vitro studies, cells that express the target transcripts and ideally where pathological aspects of the pathogenic variants are apparent, are needed. Still, there are commonalities and learnings for the preclinical development based on current knowledge. For splice switching ASO development N1C has published consensus guidelines for in vitro efficiency testing, based on current knowledge and expertise [ ]. ASO effects should ideally be confirmed not only on the transcript level but also at the protein level and at a functional level (e.g. enzymatic activity, reduction of toxic metabolites, or other phenotype) [ ]. When optimizing the ASO in vitro, ASO developers should set a minimal effect size they aim for on the transcript and protein level ahead of the study. Given the time-sensitive development of the ASO for n-of-1 cases, one should aim at an ASO that is good enough (expected to achieve clinical benefit in the patient with a high safety margin) over indefinitely investing time into optimizing ASOs further.
For efficiency tests, in many cases, showing ASO efficiency in a cell model using a clinically validated chemistry or ASO modality type (different sequence only) is sufficient and generating an animal model to test the ASO in vivo is not needed. This is provided ASOs are approved for more common diseases using the same route of administration and targeting the same cells or tissue. That said, pharmacokinetics (biodistribution) must be tested in rodents even if they do not have conserved biology or target sequence. This is because each ASO sequence can have different biodistribution properties. This information can be obtained from the safety/toxicity studies, for which for now animal studies are crucial.
When the ASO needs to reach a tissue for which no clinical evidence yet exists, more extensive animal studies will be required to confirm that the ASO is able to reach the target tissue and can be functional in the target cells. Another exception would be when the patho-mechanism of the mutation is unclear (e.g. toxic gain of function versus haploinsufficiency) and more information or genetic characterization of the mutation is needed before the target is studied in cell models.

## Intellectual property considerations of N-of-few oligonucleotide treatments
When treatments are developed for commercial marketing, patent protection is important. However, n-of-1 ASOs will generally apply to only a limited number of individuals and as such the relevance of patenting the sequences is less clear. In fact, the costs involved with filing and maintenance of patents would increase the development costs of ASOs. At the same time, it is possible that some n-of-1 ASOs may prove to have broader application than initially anticipated, and failing to file for intellectual property (IP) protection could create missed opportunities to support the broader distribution of that drug.
Some institutions may thus insist on patenting ASOs developed under the n-of-1 umbrella. This could pose a challenge when a second patient would benefit from the same, patented ASOs, but this challenge could be solved by offering noncommercial use licenses to other institutions for free or for a token fee.
In addition to IP related to the ASO sequence, the chemical modification or the conjugates used may be patented by companies. Here, the hope is that the companies will be willing to offer a free license for the n-of-1 ASOs within their specific context of use.

## Lessons learned from ASO toxicity studies
Several comprehensive reviews exist on the topic of ASO toxicity [ , ], including Goyenvalle et al. [ ], which includes a summary table of recommended predictive assays for oligonucleotide drug toxicity investigation. Additionally, multiple teams have summarized adverse drug reactions to FDA-approved ASOs [ , ]. Generally speaking, considerations for ASO toxicity are twofold: those that result from the ASO technology in general, and those that are unique to a specific ASO sequence. Several lessons can be learned from the ASO field that are widely applicable, due to the nature of the molecule. Modifications to the bases or backbone of the ASO can increase stability and solubility but can result in toxicity [ ]. Likewise, toxicity can result if the ASO binds to a nucleic acid other than its intended target. These hybridization -dependent and -independent toxicities can be categorized as: immunostimulatory, high organ exposure (liver and kidneys), thrombocytopenia, coagulation, complement activation, and central nervous system toxicities. Additionally, common adverse drug reactions result based on the injection site/delivery mechanism, including injection site reactions and flu-like symptoms (fever, headache, nausea, vomiting) [ ]. Uncommon reactions have also occurred and tend to be specific to the ASO sequence [ ].
With time and more patients, the trends in toxicity of ASOs will be elucidated. Likely though, rare diseases will always require an approach that evaluates the toxicity of individual ASOs. The paradox in rare disease treatment lies in the mismatch between traditional safety evaluation standards and the inherently small patient populations. Current frameworks demand large-scale clinical trials to assess safety and efficacy, yet rare diseases, cannot meet these requirements. The reader is directed to Goyenvalle et al., who summarize preclinical safety assessments for the safety of ASOs. The inability of rare disease to be evaluated by these metrics highlights the urgent need for tailored preclinical and regulatory standards that can predict ASO toxicity before they reach patients and account for the unique challenges of rare diseases in clinical medicine. A new safety model is needed to characterize ASO toxicity based on common design features, bridging the gap between custom ASOs and a standardized safe drug class. Here, reverse translation of ASOs that turn out not to be safe or well tolerated in patients will increase knowledge and hopefully facilitate the design of new, more predictive safety and toxicity models. This approach would create a pathway to safe, individualized therapies without relying on large patient cohorts.
Due to the special case of n-of-1 or few, the typical process of evaluating safety and toxicity do not apply. The N-of-1 IRDIRC Task Force roadmap includes recommendations on safety assessment and the determination of clinical and molecular biomarkers [ ]. For example, to determine clinical efficacy, rather than compare the patient to a cohort of patients, intra-comparison (i.e. comparing vision of a treated eye versus untreated eye) could be used. Molecular biomarkers often times manifest prior to changes in clinical presentation. Ongoing monitoring of a protein, for example, in biofluids (blood, serum, urine, cerebral spinal fluid) could provide trends in protein concentration indicative of toxicity, disease state and therapeutic efficacy. The caveat to the on-going biomarker monitoring strategy, is that it would need to be employed for the entire duration of the treatment, and ideally the data would be shared with the rare disease community. Patient and family expectations should be set at the beginning as discussed previously, and clear endpoints should be established.
In the n-of-1 setting, the example of valeriasen is an important illustration of the challenges of ASO safety and toxicity [ ]. Valeriasen is an RNase H activating ASO that was developed to treat Valeria, who was a patient with severe infantile onset epilepsy syndrome due to variants in the KCNT1 gene. Initially valeriasen was effective at reducing seizure activity in Valeria and a second patient whose treatment commenced after Valeria. Unexpectedly, the ASO resulted in the development of hydrocephalus in both patients. This adverse effect had not been observed in safety studies conducted in healthy rats, but was hypothesized to be specifically due to the pathological state of the human brain. Interestingly, hydrocephalus and inflammation has been observed as an adverse reaction following treatment with other ASOs [ , ], further illustrating the dire need to share safety and toxicity data as the lessons learned from valeriasen may translate to other ASOs.
Currently, safety and toxicity studies are primarily done in rodents and in some cases non human primates. Efforts are ongoing to assess whether 2D or 3D cell models, such as induced pluripotent stem cells that are differentiated into central nervous lineages or embryoid bodies, can be used to predict safety. Here, knowing which ASOs induced toxicity and which ones were well tolerated will facilitate the reverse translation efforts in vitro and in vivo.

## Manufacturing: a considerable challenge for bringing ASOs to patients
The cost of manufacturing oligonucleotides to scale for the purposes of ASO development and clinical validation remains a significant bottleneck in the investigation of oligonucleotide therapeutics. ASOs face the added challenge of requiring significant chemical modifications, which increase cost. In addition to the practical challenges of scaling oligonucleotide synthesis from lab investigation to clinical validation scale, there exists a significant environmental burden as a result of traditional oligonucleotide synthesis, which uses hazardous organic solvents. Though outside of the scope of this review, advancements in the scalability, and green and sustainable synthetic methods are thoroughly and technically reviewed elsewhere [ , ].
The other bottleneck of manufacturing therapeutic oligonucleotides is ensuring the product is produced via good manufacturing practices, which will vary depending on the regulating body and the modality [ ]. All ASOs must meet quality, purity, and sterility standards before use. Additionally, the preparation must be confirmed free of endotoxins, and its biological efficacy should be confirmed in an in vitro model. The amount of ASO needed to confirm quality criteria is relatively small for larger patient groups. For n-of-1 cases, even a few grams are sufficient to perform animal safety studies and to treat a patient for decades. This means that the vast majority of the ASO produced is needed for quality tests rather than for treatment, thus increasing the manufacturing costs significantly.
As is the challenge with ASO design, the cost of manufacturing ASOs combined with the costs of necessary experiments for preclinical investigation is often cost prohibitive for academic labs. Besides the fact that manufacturing is expensive, there is very little grant funding available for rare disease. This barrier alone can stop rare disease ASO development in its tracks. Though more and more companies are offering compassionate in-kind and discounted services, often patients and their families bear the burden of raising enough money to fund investigational studies. There are countless patient groups, initiatives and foundations (including Mila's Miracle Foundation, 1M1M and n-Lorem discussed herein) with the sole goal of raising enough money, often in the millions of dollars, to bring an ASO to market for a single rare disease patient.
The frustrating reality is the already high cost of manufacturing an ASO is relatively inexpensive compared to other costs associated with the drug development pipeline. The Spinraza® (nusinersen) example is probably the most well known to the field and the public. Patients were being charged ∼$750 000 USD for the first year of treatment (4 doses) and then maintenance dosing cost ∼$375 000 USD in following years [ ]. The most expensive factor for n-of-1 drug development and preclinical testing tends to be the safety testing in animals. Thus, ultimately, making ASOs available to patients in a timely and cost-accessible manner requires new scientific advancements (cheaper/greener synthetic strategies, new models, new protocols, and most importantly new ways to ensure the safety of ASOs at lower cost) combined with an altruistic collaborative approach by the scientific community, regulators, and companies. The N1C primarily aims to accelerate this collaboration.

## Local infrastructure and team requirements
N-of-1 trials typically occur in academic medical settings, led by a single dedicated team. This trial involves dosing the patient, monitoring safety, and collecting as much clinical and biological information as possible to assess the drug's effectiveness and detect any signs of efficacy or toxicity. Current discussions about n-of-1 trials are focused on information sharing and process uniformity to use ongoing trials as guideposts to accelerate the process for more patients and at other institutions.
Before a trial can begin, the local institution must also prepare for the logistical and operational demands of running a clinical trial. Multidisciplinary teams are essential to handle the medical, ethical, logistical, and research aspects needed to validate the ASO’s safety, tolerability, and efficacy. A local team might include a principal investigator, clinical coordinator, pharmacist, nursing staff, database coordinator, ethics monitor, compliance officer, regulatory support, procedural team, and data and safety monitoring board (Scott Demarest, N1C Annual Meeting, Montreal Canada, October 2024).
With a team assembled and clinical trial or treatment on the horizon, extensive regulatory submissions are required. At the local academic level, regulatory submissions are a substantial logistical undertaking that is often lengthy, complex, and ambiguous. These submissions include all available knowledge on the proposed therapy, along with any available justification on its anticipated clinical benefit and safety for the patient. The principal investigator must detail the planned outcome measures, safety and monitoring protocols, and proposed dosing regimen supported by relevant preclinical or clinical data. Each element in this paperwork, especially in an urgent n-of-1 setting, is likely a thoughtful, educated guess. Without complete data sets (which is what takes years of validation and preclinical modeling), data-sharing on dosing, aggregate repositories on outcome metrics, and more, are essential for these trial's success.
Beyond clinical trials, customized therapeutics, including ASOs, can also be administered to patients through named patient or compassionate use programs. In this approach, the ASO or treatment is provided and monitored locally by the primary clinician outside of a formal trial. These programs are reserved for cases involving serious, life-threatening conditions where no other therapeutic options are available, and trial enrollment is not possible. Both clinical trials and compassionate use or named patient programs require an IND submission and authorization from national drug regulatory agencies in the USA. In Europe, generally institutional review board approval is required for named patient treatment, though as mentioned in some countries competent authorities need to give approval as well.
Sharing IND proposals has become a way for academics in individualized medicine to challenge the traditional “closed-door” approach often used by large pharmaceutical groups to protect their intellectual assets during therapeutic investigations. Conversations with researchers navigating and running n-of-few clinical trials also reveal that having an institutional champion—someone influential within the institution who supports the trial and team despite potential pushback—is essential to conducting these studies successfully.
Overall, bringing a customized ASO to a patient within their lifetime means that many traditional phases of drug development will occur in parallel. Baseline outcome measures and safety data collection happen concurrently with building a clinical team so that, once approval is granted, the patient can be dosed as quickly as possible. Streamlining processes and providing a clear, step-by-step guide for clinicians is essential to support institutions with limited experience in rare disease clinical trials. This guidance, a focus of N1C working groups, aims to include sample INDs, a defined clinical trial pathway, recommendations on outcomes that are or are not helpful within a certain type of disease, resource-sharing opportunities, and advice on securing funding, which often depends on foundations, nonprofits, or individual donors.

## Outcome measures: testing treatment efficacy in an individualized setting
Advancing therapeutics for rare diseases requires rigorously assessing the safety and efficacy of a customized medicine in an individualized setting. To achieve this, traditional clinical trial designs must be adapted to evaluate treatments on a patient-specific basis [ ]. These restructured n-of-1 trials focus on dose-escalation studies that prioritize safety, within-patient biomarkers, and both clinical and caregiver-reported outcomes, without a primary emphasis on commercialization (as seen in traditional pharmaceutical trials).
The outcome measures that define the success or failure of a trial come from various sources, including clinical outcomes, performance outcomes, biomarker data, molecular data, and subjective reports (from patients or caregivers) [ ]. In n-of-1 trials, three types of outcomes are most important: (i) safety outcomes, (ii) non-clinical outcomes (such as target engagement, fluid markers, and imaging), and (iii) clinical outcomes, including patient-reported outcome measures.
First and foremost is safety. Monitoring safety may look like assessing adverse events, using a dose escalation study design, and engaging with local Data Safety Monitoring Boards for an outside evaluation of clinical study progression. Beyond ASO design and sequence, safety concerns clinically associated with nucleic acid therapeutics may relate to their route of administration. Currently, most ASOs developed for n-of-few neurological disease indications rely on intrathecal administration. Given the history and approval of nusinersen, this route is considered less invasive than intraparenchymal or intracerebroventricular delivery. However, other intrathecally administered ASOs, such as Tominersen, have faced challenges.
In the case of Tominersen, an RNase H-active Gapmer ASO designed to lower total huntingtin levels, high-dose administration unexpectedly led to increased neurofilament light levels and worsened ventricular enlargement in patients compared to placebo controls [ , ]. This outcome illustrates that even with a well-validated target and a known mechanism—one similar to the approved Tofersen for familial SOD1 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [ ]—significant toxicity can still emerge, ultimately leading to the halting of the Phase III trial.
Based on data from recent nucleic acid trials, increased neurofilament light levels and ventricular enlargement have been the most commonly observed indicators of CNS toxicity [ , ]. These cases highlight that despite a well-characterized target, mechanism, and administration route, unexpected clinical outcomes can occur.
Beyond intrathecal delivery, IV and subcutaneous injections have also been explored. The decision on the route of administration during the preclinical-to-clinical transition is typically made by the research team, who evaluate whether ASOs can effectively reach the target tissue with the desired pharmacodynamic properties while minimizing the need for invasive procedures whenever possible.
As ASOs and other nucleic acid therapeutics continue to be developed for a broader range of gene targets and conditions, innovative delivery methods will likely emerge [ ]. While many established administration routes are used in n-of-few trials and compassionate use cases, alternative routes may be considered if supported by preclinical data.
Next, primary, secondary, and exploratory treatment goals must be defined prior to treatment and should include objective, validated, and quantitative clinical assessments. These may be known biomarkers such as neurofilament in the CNS, creatine in the kidney, or liver function tests for the liver (n-Lorem, 2024). These data are often a part of standard clinical care and can contribute to a more generalized clinical understanding in the future. Thus, leaders overseeing n-of-few trials recommend these more universal outcomes to better compare studies head-to-head across sites and diseases.
Third is defining a clinical picture of heterogeneous diseases. Importantly, natural history data is usually absent for rare diseases. Because of this, the solutions proposed by others and summarized here work to think differently about tracing disease progression in patients so that the lack of natural history is not a barrier for treatment. One group leading efforts in capturing clinical progression and defining outcomes is n-Lorem. They use a battery of measures to assess a patient's current disease status and generate predictions about disease progression over 1–5 years. Further, one approach that those organizing rare disease trials recommend is to aggregate data across many n-of-1 diseases in order to group or create guidelines across phenotype clusters (Synofzik, 2024). These data might include recommended outcomes or historical data for specific symptoms like seizures, movement disorders, visual impairment, or developmental delays (Synofzik, 2024). Through this approach, the field can better understand how select clinical pictures change over time or with gene expression correction, leading to more generalizable knowledge.
Importantly, clinical outcomes, metrics, and expectations will not—and likely cannot—be the same across patients. An example is the drug Zebronkysen currently in clinical trials in 2024 for an ultra-rare cause of CLN3 Batten disease, a pediatric neurodegenerative, lysosomal storage disease. This n-of-2 trial involves twin sisters with the same disease and pathogenic variant, but different clinical progression with one ambulant sister, and one sister with a more rapid disease progression who is no longer ambulant. To address the differences, clinical outcomes are tailored for each sister to evaluate the rate of change for each (Michelle Hastings and Yael Shiloh-Malawsky, N1C Annual Meeting, Montreal, Canada, October 2024). This individualized approach—tailoring criteria for success and weighting metrics based on a patient's unique presentation—may become more common in n-of-few trials.
When selecting outcomes, the pinnacle question for clinical teams and regulatory bodies running individualized clinical trials is determining how to measure change (Richard Finkel, N1C Annual Meeting, Montreal, Canada, October 2024). Placebo-controlled trials look for a change or difference at the group level. Without the ability to use large or control cohorts, measuring and detecting change for n-of-few clinical trials is an area of robust innovation and rethinking. Change can be evaluated using multiple metrics including, but not limited to, known disease progression data (number of seizures, ability to feed, or walk, etc.), disease-specific scales (such as ataxia scales, etc) [ ] and biomarkers (within-patient change, against natural history or disease staging) [ ]. Innovations in outcome metrics for individualized medicine trials are focusing on measuring a patient's change or delta from their baseline or expected progression. This approach reduces the need for traditional placebo groups by using each patient's own progression as the comparison metric [ ]. For each of these metrics, as more data is collected (the same ASO is dosed or disease is monitored), the more generalizable these results become for similar diseases. From a pragmatic perspective, given the need to track change in a single patient, collecting ongoing baseline (pre-treatment) data as soon as possible is key for any patient who will likely join a clinical trial.
Lastly, in addition to trial outcome metrics, the clinical trial team must maintain open, frequent discussions with the patient and family regarding continuation or discontinuation of treatment in the trial. The major question here for clinicians and families is determining what “success” looks like in the context of a fatal neurodegenerative condition. Outcomes assess safety and efficacy and may be shared across sites; however, continuation and discontinuation are intimate discussions between the clinical team and patient's family, occurring early and often. Clear milestones and definitions of what success looks like are needed for each family.
With patients and global data-sharing at the forefront, the development of guidelines and aggregated data repositories is an area of active effort of the N1C. Through this, the hope is that investigators globally will have the resources needed to navigate IND submissions, clinical trial design, outcome assessment, and trial data interpretation at a larger, more rapid scale.

## Funding challenges for n-of-few nucleic acid-based therapeutic synthesis, trials, and access
From the several dozen patients treated with n-of-1 or n-of-few ASOs to date, it is clear that developing them is possible from a technical perspective. However, the elephant in the room is that there are significant costs involved with development, GMP production, GLP safety studies, and treatment and management in the hospital setting. As mentioned, money to pay for the development of the ASO is currently raised research grants or philanthropy [ ]. Models for scaling and assuring long-term sustainability of this mode of drug development—not to mention addressing the longer-term costs involved with treatment and clinical management of patients—remain to be developed. Currently these costs are often born by the hospital where patients are treated and the families [ ]. Clearly, paths will be required to navigate the transition from these early investigational efforts to more mature models of sustained (and reimbursed) treatment. Such models will have to address how these treatments are regulated, including devising strategies for how these treatments should be approved.
The UK Rare Therapies Launchpad represents an ambitious early effort to establish a framework for grappling with these issues [ ]. It is attempting to leverage the centralized health care structure of the UK to bring together scientists, clinicians, medical centers, companies, regulatory agencies, and a national payer to negotiate an economically viable model for N=1 treatments in Great Britain. The RTLP has espoused a number of principles that are likely to be critical if this field is to become self-sustaining: (i) the advancement of N=1 treatments for individuals with life-threatening and/or fatal diseases by accredited centers, trusted to be expert in the evaluation, selection, and management of such patients; (ii) a scalable approach to regulatory oversight that allows patients to be treated in parallel with regulatory reporting, as opposed to gated by it (described in the UK RTLP as a “do and tell” model, allowing centers to treat patients with new N=1 drugs without filing an IND/Clinical Trials Application for each patient, as long as the drug development process follows a well-defined and scientifically justified process; (iii) regular auditing of centers to ensure they are following best practices; (iv) iterative learnings from each treatment, whereby results of N=1 therapies are shared and used to inform improvements to best practices, allowing for safer and more cost-effective drug development; and (v) reimbursement of N=1 interventions under a “cost plus recovery” model, akin to medical procedures such as surgical interventions or transplant, rather than marketed drugs. While this ambitious effort remains in its earliest stages, and many of the particulars of this solution may vary per country based on local healthcare structures, it will undoubtedly be instructive.
Other potentially simpler models can also be contemplated. One suggestion that has been contemplated is incentivizing N=1 drug development using a variant of the Priority Review Voucher (PRV) system in the United States, a legislatively mandated incentive whereby pharmaceutical companies can be rewarded for winning drug approvals for otherwise neglected, non-profitable diseases by being granted a transferable “credit” that can be exchanged for expedited FDA review of any other drug development program (including those targeting large-market indications with the potential to generate billions of USD in revenue). These vouchers can have market values in the 80–100 million USD range. A similar economic incentive system could conceivably be adapted to encourage N=1 treatment development, for instance by setting milestones by which a PRV could be awarded for successful deployment of individualized therapies (e.g. granting a voucher for every 10, 25, or 50 patients treated, etc). Another interesting model would be one where companies with expertise in genetically targeted platform technologies like ASOs partner with nonprofit medical centers to provide in kind support of N=1 drug development and treatment for “non-commercializable” indications, helping meet unmet needs of patients while receiving in return access to valuable clinical data on efficiency, safety and tolerability of their platform.

## Towards the future
The future of n-of-few therapies holds immense promise, not only for the individuals they aim to treat but also for the broader landscape of genetic medicines. Extending therapeutic platforms from n-of-many to n-of-1 unlocks the full potential of these technologies, offering hope to patients with ultra-rare genetic variants for whom no existing therapies are available, and de novo drug development is commercially unviable. The science and technology are ready, but the field must now focus on streamlining processes, building robust data-sharing frameworks, and fostering real-time cross-program learning. These efforts are essential to enhance safety, efficacy, and success rates while minimizing redundancies. Clinically, n-of-few therapies provide a lifeline for patients in desperate need of disease-modifying treatments; scientifically, they generate critical insights into the translational outcomes and disease impacts of genetic interventions, creating a unique opportunity to learn while treating.
N-of-few therapies also hold the potential to benefit n-of-many. The small modifications required for each individualized medicine offer a window into understanding the molecular impact of subtle changes to the platform. These insights can inform the development of better drugs for broader populations or enable more efficient therapeutic development.
Although most progress to date has been achieved with ASOs, the tools, frameworks, and lessons being developed extend far beyond ASO technology. They are equally applicable to modalities such as siRNA, CRISPR gene editing, and RNA editing, paving the way for innovation across diverse platforms. By investing in shared infrastructure, fostering collaboration, and embracing adaptable technologies, we can unlock the full potential of individualized medicines and expand their reach to benefit patients on a global scale.
