Neurological and neuromuscular diseases resulting from familial, sporadic, or de novo mutations have devasting personal, familial, and societal impacts. As the initial product of DNA transcription, RNA transcripts and their associated ribonucleoprotein complexes provide attractive targets for modulation by increasing wild-type or blocking mutant allele expression, thus relieving downstream pathological consequences. Therefore, it is unsurprising that many existing and under-development therapeutics have focused on targeting disease-associated RNA transcripts as a frontline drug strategy for these genetic disorders. This review focuses on the current range of RNA targeting modalities using examples of both dominant and recessive neurological and neuromuscular diseases.

Nascent RNA transcripts are single-stranded as they emerge from the Pol II complex but rapidly fold into secondary and higher-order structures as they coordinately bind trans-acting factors, including RNA binding proteins (RBPs), to form structurally distinct ribonucleoprotein complexes (RNPs) ( ; ). For pre-mRNAs, these complexes provide the substrates for cotranscriptional RNA processing, including splicing, polyadenylation, and nucleocytoplasmic export, followed by cytoplasmic messenger RNP (mRNP) remodeling, mRNA localization, and translation into potentially toxic proteins ( ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). During all of these steps, both RNA structures and RNP compositions are highly dynamic, which provides discrete intermediates for therapeutic targeting using a variety of tools, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), microRNAs (miRNAs)/small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), small nuclear RNPs (snRNPs), small molecule compounds, and trans-splicing modules ( ; ; ; ).
For this review, we first describe the RNA metabolic processes amenable to targeting and outline the various molecular tools that are being implemented for therapeutics. We then contextualize these approaches using five examples of neurological and neuromuscular diseases that run the gamut from the trailblazing success of clinically approved drugs for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) to discovery-phase studies for Dravet syndrome. For neurological, neuromuscular, and other diseases not covered here, the reader is directed to excellent recent reviews ( ; ; ; ; ; ).

## Biochemical processes amenable to RNA targeting

## Splicing
Most human genes contain multiple introns that are selectively removed by RNA splicing, which is initiated by the recognition of 5′ and 3′ splice sites (ss) at exon/intron boundaries and the branch point sequence upstream of the 3′ss followed by two sequential transesterification reactions. Splicing is catalyzed by the major and minor spliceosomes, composed of five snRNP complexes as well as >150 other protein factors ( ; ; ). Splicing is primarily a cotranscriptional process initiated by the emergence of the 5′ss from the Pol II RNA exit site and U1 snRNP recruitment via U1-70K binding to Pol II ( ). As transcription proceeds, the 5′ss is retained near the RNA exit site, resulting in an intron loop, and after the 3′ss emerges, the spliceosome is activated, leading to catalysis and intron release. Since both 5′ss and 3′ss consensus sequences are characterized by limited conservation, additional intron (intronic splicing enhancer [ISE] and intronic splicing silencer [ISS]) and exon (exonic splicing enhancer [ESE] and exonic splicing silencer [ESS]) RNA sequence motifs recognized by splicing factors (SFs) are required to activate or repress spliceosome recruitment. This regulatory architecture allows splice site recognition to be a highly flexible process dependent on RNA sequence context and trans-acting factor accessibility, resulting in the expression of the multitude of RNA isoforms required by various cell types ( ). However, SF activities require binding to both exon and flanking intron sequences, so the ability of therapeutic modalities to access targeted RNA sequences discussed in the next section may be compromised by multiple factors such as proximal and distal RNA structures, RBP–RNA interaction kinetics, and Pol II transcription rates. Although this mechanistic complexity makes splicing sensitive to the multitude of mutations that underlie neurological and neuromuscular diseases, it also allows the deployment of a variety of targeting strategies for therapeutic correction.

## 3′ end processing and polyadenylation
Another key RNA processing step amenable to RNA targeting is pre-mRNA 3′ end processing and polyadenylation ( ). Cleavage is coupled to polyadenylation mediated by poly(A) polymerase (PAP) and is regulated by cis elements located both upstream of and downstream from the cleavage site. Cryptic polyadenylation signals exist throughout many Pol II nascent transcripts and, if used, result in premature cleavage and polyadenylation (PCPA) or telescripting. Telescripting was discovered by treating cells with a U1 snRNA antisense morpholino oligonucleotide (AMO) ( ; ) that disrupts U1 snRNP structure and alters U1 snRNP–Pol II interactions, which leads to effects on transcription elongation ( ). Additionally, commonly used 3′ UTR poly(A) sites are cleaved but not polyadenylated if U1 snRNP binds nearby due to interactions between the U1 snRNP 70K protein (SNRNP70) and PAP. The resulting nonpolyadenylated transcript is susceptible to rapid turnover ( ; ).

## RNA localization and turnover
Mutant RNA levels can also be manipulated by the biochemical machineries that control nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA levels. In the nucleus, pre-mRNAs are modified by 5′ end capping with 7-methyl guanosine (m7G) and 3′ end polyadenylation (except for histone RNAs), which inhibits transcript decay by blocking the activities of the nuclear 5′–3′ exonuclease XRN2 as well as the 3′–5′ exonuclease activities of the nuclear RNA exosome ( ). Upon export into the cytoplasm, developmentally regulated intron retention (IR) or disease-associated mutations may result in premature termination codons (PTCs) that are recognized by the nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) machinery during translation to accelerate RNA turnover, though NMD also regulates the levels of RNAs encoding full-length proteins ( ; ; ). RNA turnover kinetics are also routinely fine-tuned by interactions with RNA sequence elements and trans-acting factors, including miRNAs and the wide diversity of RNPs formed following RBP binding ( ; ; ; ; ).

## Translation
The major objectives of RNA therapeutic targeting are to either upregulate the expression of proteins with full or partial functional activity or reduce the expression of mutant proteins that cause cell toxicity and disease. Canonical translational initiation requires recognition of the 5′ m7G cap by eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E), a component of the eIF4F complex, followed by recruitment of the 43S preinitiation complex (PIC) and subsequent 5′ UTR scanning for an AUG, or near-cognate, initiation codon ( ). Environmental (cellular stress and inhibition of cap-dependent canonical translation) and disease-associated (expanded short tandem repeat RNAs) factors trigger alternative translational modes, including internal ribosomal entry site (IRES)- and repeat-associated non-AUG (RAN)-mediated translation ( ; ; ; ). Additionally, developmental events or disease-associated mutations that alter RNA splicing patterns may result in an in-frame PTC that leads to the expression of a truncated protein and/or NMD-mediated RNA turnover.

## Strategies for targeting disease gene transcripts

## Oligonucleotides
Since a synthesized single-strand antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) was used to target Rous sarcoma virus translation nearly five decades ago ( ; ), ASOs have been become the most widely used class of RNA targeting therapeutics ( ; ; ; ). This progress is due in part to backbone and base modifications that increase nuclease resistance, binding affinity, and on-target pharmacological activity while reducing off-target effects. Single-stranded (ss) oligonucleotides have been generally designed to (1) trigger a splicing change by blocking access to 5′ss, 3′ss, or splicing factor binding sites (steric blockers) ( ; , ); (2) promote target RNA turnover by recruiting RNase H (gapmers) ( ; ); (3) silence miRNAs using antagomiRs or anti-miRs ( ; ; ); and (4) enhance expression of wild-type allele RNA transcript levels by inhibiting natural antisense transcripts (antagoNATS) ( ). In addition to ASO-mediated therapeutics, elevated translation of recessive disease or disease-modifying mRNAs has also been achieved using antisense long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) constructs composed of a target-specific antisense binding domain together with an effector domain containing an inverted B2 class SINE element ( ; ; ).
 

## Small RNAs and RNPs
The RNA interference (RNAi) pathway has also been an important target of RNA therapeutics. MiRNA-mediated translational repression results from inhibition of the 43S preinitiation complex combined with increased poly(A) tail shortening ( ). In contrast, RNA slicing, or cleavage with subsequent RNA turnover, occurs when the miRNA–target RNA base pairing is extensive. This cleavage activity forms the basis of siRNA-mediated RNA turnover that is used routinely to promote target RNA degradation in research laboratories while also providing the basis for promoting mutant RNA degradation for a broad range of diseases ( ).
For RNPs, the small nuclear RNPs (snRNPs), particularly the U1 and U7 snRNPs, constitute another class of RNA therapeutics that can be delivered by gene therapy vehicles such as adeno-associated virus (AAV) for prolonged expression. An advantage of snRNPs for polyadenylation and splice site modulation is their natural subnuclear localization ( ). Engineered U1 snRNPs consist of a 5′ end-modified U1 snRNA designed to bind to sequences proximal to a splice site or other region to either promote (e.g., ISS proximal to a 5′ss) or inhibit (e.g., 3′ss or ESE) splicing ( ; ; ). Modified U1 snRNPs have also been used to target polyadenylation sites, resulting in inhibition of poly(A) polymerase following 3′ end cleavage and rapid turnover of the targeted transcript ( ; ). Engineered U7 snRNPs (U7 Sm OPT) target specific RNA sequences and block recruitment of the 3′ end cleavage complex or modulate exon skipping via modifications to both the U7 snRNA 5′ end for RNA targeting and the Sm binding site to alter RNP composition ( ; ; ; ; ).

## Trans-splicing
RNA splicing is most often a cis processing step occurring cotranscriptionally. In contrast, trans-splicing, which was first demonstrated in vitro ( ; ), involves exon joining between two separate RNA transcripts from the same (intragenic), different (intergenic), or bidirectional transcripts from the same locus (cross-strand) to form a chimeric RNA. Trans-splicing occurs in vivo in multiple organisms such as trypanosomes (Trypanosoma brucei) ( ; ), nematodes (Caenorhabditis elegans) ( ), and vertebrates, including humans ( ; ; ; ). As artificial trans-splicing provides a mechanism to repair a disease-associated RNA transcript with a nonmutant version, early studies focused on spliceosome-mediated RNA trans-splicing (SMaRT) using a pre-trans-splicing module (PTM) ( ; ; ). For 5′ and 3′ trans-splicing, the PTM is composed of a binding domain (BD) for target recognition, an artificial intron for splicing, and a cDNA designed to replace the mutant coding sequence, whereas for internal exon replacement, both 5′ and 3′ BDs and introns are required ( ).
The therapeutic promise of spliceosome-mediated mRNA repair in vivo was highlighted more than two decades ago by the phenotypic correction of a mouse hemophilia A knockout model using a PTM containing factor VIII (F8) exons ( ). Since then, a major therapeutic hurdle has been to increase trans-splicing efficiency, though recent CRISPR/Cas-based programmable technologies have shown a marked improvement. For example, CRISPR–Cas13 and Cas7–11 systems have been implemented to allow trans-splicing of larger artificial RNAs for multiple coding region replacement ( ; ). Additionally, RNA-guided trans-splicing with Cas editor (RESPLICE) uses orthogonal CRISPR effectors that colocalize a deactivated Cas protein trans-splicing module (dCasRx TSM) with a cis-splicing interfering module (CIM) to enhance trans-splicing efficiency ( ).

## Small molecule compounds
Recent advances in determining RNA structures have accelerated the development of small molecules to target them. A clinically approved therapeutic strategy involves the use of small molecule modifiers to target nonconsensus splice sites (5′-G−2A−1|G+1U+2R+3A+4G+5U+5-3′) that contain an A−1 bulge nucleotide (bold) that limits U1 snRNP recruitment activity ( ; ). The identification of small molecule modifiers that repair this A−1 5′ss bulge resulted in a significant increase in splicing of the corresponding exon ( ; ). Non-splice-site RNA structures have also been targeted by small molecule modifiers, including unusual secondary and higher-order structures induced by short tandem repeat expansions ( ; ), and mRNA 5′ UTR structures to reduce the levels of a toxic protein, such as α-synuclein ( )

## RNA base editors
As more than half of human pathogenic genetic variants are single-nucleotide mutations (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar), editing of individual RNA bases using programmable editors to correct mutant variants is another RNA-based therapeutic approach that offers several advantages compared with DNA editing, including reversibility due to RNA instability ( ; ). RNA base modifications include adenosine to inosine (A-to-I), catalyzed by adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADAR1 and ADAR2) proteins ( ), and cytosine to uracil (C-to-U) using a modified ADAR2 deaminase domain (DD) and apolipoprotein B mRNA-editing enzyme catalytic subunit 3A (APOBEC3A) ( ; ). RNA targeting of these effector enzymes has been achieved using multiple systems based on either CRISPR, CRISPR-free RNPs, Pumilio and FBF homology (PUF) proteins, antisense RNAs, snoRNAs, or ribozymes ( ).

## Challenges with therapeutic delivery
Efficacious therapeutics must reach the affected cells followed by efficient tissue biodistribution, internalization, release into the cytoplasm, and trafficking to the relevant intracellular target. To address biodistribution and low activity concerns due to limited cell type-specific uptake ( ), ASOs and therapeutic siRNAs have been modified with a variety of cell targeting modules, including receptor ligands such as triantennary N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) for siRNA targeting of liver hepatocytes ( ), cationic or amphipathic cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) for muscle and the brain ( ; ; ; ; ), fatty acids for enhanced muscle delivery ( ), and antitransferrin receptor (TfR1) monoclonal antibodies and Fab fragments for skeletal muscle and the heart ( ; ). An alternative approach to modulate cellular uptake is the development of AAV-mediated delivery of RNA targeting therapeutics ( ). For neurological disease, delivery of long-term therapeutics may be facilitated by the development of AAV capsids that bind the human TfR1 (or other receptors) to facilitate intravenously delivered therapeutic cargos across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to the central nervous system ( ).
For some RNA therapeutics, including ASOs, PMOs, and siRNAs, endosomal escape following endocytosis presents a major barrier, with ≥99% of these RNA therapeutics failing to rapidly escape from endosomes, though this retention has the beneficial effect of providing a long-term intracellular reservoir ( ). This problem has led to the development of endosomal escape domain moieties based on enveloped virus escape mechanisms ( ) or cyclic enhanced endosomal escape vehicle (EEV) peptides ( ).
Another major pharmacodynamic concern is that therapies are generally tested at the discovery and IND-enabling stages using cell and animal models that may be poor substitutes for the binding, uptake, and intracellular trafficking characteristics of the human differentiated tissues they are designed to remediate. Nevertheless, there have been some notable RNA targeting successes, but as discussed in the next section, significant challenges remain to optimize these targeting strategies.

## RNA targeted therapeutics for neurological and neuromuscular disease
There are many neurological and neuromuscular disorders for which RNA targeting therapies are currently being pursued. Due to space constraints, we have selected a subset of diseases to highlight the broad range of therapeutic development (from discovery research to clinical therapies), the different types of strategies being invoked, and the types of underlying mutations that drive both hereditary and de novo disorders.

## Spinal muscular atrophy: protein restoration by 5′ splice site activation via ASO steric blockers and small molecules
RNA targeting therapeutics achieved a notable success for the treatment of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), a rare autosomal recessive neuromuscular disease characterized by progressive degeneration of α-motoneurons, subsequent denervation, and progressive muscle loss ( ). SMA is caused by spinal motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene deletions or loss-of-function mutations leading to a reduction in the levels of the SMN protein ( ). SMN is a core component the SMN complex essential for assembly of snRNP complexes ( , ) but it also interacts with several RBPs, where it may function in additional pathways, including RNA localization and translation ( ; ). The severity of SMA is tempered by the copy number of the paralogous gene SMN2, which is nearly identical in sequence to SMN1 except for an exon 7 ESE C > T transition mutation ( A; ). This mutation inhibits SRSF1 recruitment, leading to increased exon 7 skipping and synthesis of an unstable SMN2Δ7 protein with compromised functional activity ( ; ). These observations led to the realization that tiling across SMN2 exon 7 and its flanking intronic regions with oligonucleotides might uncover a region that, when sterically blocked, would increase SMN2 protein levels ( , ). This groundbreaking work led to the development and subsequent clinical approval of nusinersen, an 18-mer ASO designed to sterically block recruitment of HNRNPA2B1 to SMN2 intron 7 ISS-N1, which therapeutically increases exon 7 inclusion ( A, ASO; ; ; ).
 The success of nusinersen as an ASO steric blocker was followed by a different RNA targeting strategy with the discovery of risdiplam/RG7916, the first-in-class clinically approved small molecule splicing modifier for SMA ( A, small molecule; ). Risdiplam is an orally administered drug that is a chemical derivative of the SMN–C3/C5 compounds that target the unusual SMN exon 7 5′ss, which instead of the consensus (U2-type) 5′ss (5′-A−2G−1|G+1U+2R+3A+4G+5U+5-3′) is modified to 5′-G−2A−1|G+1U+2R+3A+4G+5U+5-3′, with an A−1 bulge nucleotide (bold) that restricts U1 snRNP recruitment. Risdiplam repairs this A−1 5′ss bulge, resulting in significant increases in splicing of the SMN2 exon 7 and functional SMN protein ( ; ). Clinical follow-up reports of the effects of risdiplam treatment for SMA type 1, the most severe form of this disease, report improvements in motor functions and achieving motor milestones ( ). However, significant improvements in respiratory or nutritional outcomes have not yet occurred in SMA patients treated with nusinersen, risdiplam, or combination therapies ( ).

## Duchenne muscular dystrophy: PTC suppression and exon skipping by small molecules, RNA base editors, ASOs, and snRNPs
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), an early-onset degenerative and fatal neuromuscular disease with a live male birth incidence of approximately one in 3600, is caused by single or multiexon deletions, exon duplications, or nonsense mutations in the DMD gene ( ). DMD is a very large (79 exons, ∼2.2 Mb) X-linked gene that encodes the cytoplasmic protein dystrophin. Dystrophin prevents contraction-induced muscle damage by acting as a link between the intracellular actin, intermediate filament, and microtubule networks and the dystroglycan complex embedded in the muscle cell cytoplasmic membrane (sarcolemma) that in turn interacts with the extracellular matrix (ECM) ( ; ; ; ; ). Loss of dystrophin compromises sarcolemma integrity, resulting in repetitive cycles of myofiber necrosis/regeneration, ultimately leading to severe skeletal muscle wasting and death.
Because ∼10%–15% of DMD mutations are caused by nonsense mutations ( ), nonsense suppression is an attractive therapeutic idea. PTC124/ataluren was identified as a small molecule that promotes readthrough of nonsense mutations. Initial follow-up studies questioned ataluren's mechanism of action, as the firefly luciferase (Fluc)-based reporter used to identify ataluren was shown to be stabilized post-translationally by the drug, and additional reporter assays failed to confirm ataluren's stop codon readthrough activity ( ; ). Nevertheless, subsequent cell-based and other studies suggest that ataluren readthrough activity is primarily due to its promotion of near/noncognate tRNA insertions at nonsense codons, but not natural termination codons (NTCs), due to direct ribosome interactions and decoding center alterations ( ; ). This molecular discrimination between PTCs and NTCs may be due to context-specific RNA sequences, translation factors, and mRNA 3′ end proximity. In 2014, ataluren received conditional approval by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for treatment of nonsense mutation DMD (nmDMD) ( ), but to date, the FDA has refused to accept a new drug application (NDA) submission for ataluren. A potential issue with ataluren efficacy is PTC-mediated frameshifting leading to NMD because this decay pathway limits the levels of the mutant DMD mRNA available for ataluren to target. However, mutant DMD RNA levels did not increase over ataluren alone by the combination of ataluren and the FDA-approved NMD inhibitors 5′-aza cytidine ( ) and amlexanox ( ) in patient-derived skin fibroblasts ( ). It will be important to test this strategy in muscle cells, and another issue will be cell toxicity issues that might arise with long-term administration of NMD inhibitors ( ).
RNA base editing using RNA-guided adenosine deaminases is an alternative approach to restore dystrophin expression. Using the combination of the ADAR2 enzyme and ADAR-guided RNAs (adRNAs), which are composed of an antisense region complementary to a target sequence with a cytidine opposite the target adenosine, base editing of the mdx exon 23 nonsense mutation occurred following dual adRNA delivery, but targeting efficiencies were relatively low (1%–3%) ( ). More recently, correction of a mouse Dmd humanized exon 30 knock-in model carrying the DMD nonsense c.4174C > T, p.Gln1992* mutation using single AAV delivery of mini-dCas13X-mediated RNA A-to-I/G base editing (mxABE) resulted in editing rates of 84% in vivo following intramuscular injection, with a corresponding dystrophin expression of 50% of wild type ( ). Potential problems with this approach are that new guide RNAs must be designed for each mutation site, and there is the potential for deleterious effects due to long-term expression of immunogenic Cas proteins ( ; ).
Early studies highlighted the fact that exon skipping occurs naturally during mutant DMD pre-mRNA splicing that resulted in truncated DMD proteins detectable by immunolabeling in “revertant” myofibers ( ; ). Based on this foundational observation, multiple ASO-based therapies that target pre-mRNA splicing using exon-skipping strategies have been clinically approved to address DMD exon deletion mutations and partially restore dystrophin protein expression ( B, ASO). These ASOs are PMOs that target ESEs in exon 45 (casimersen) ( ), 51 (eteplirsen) ( ), or 53 (viltolarsen and golodirsen) ( ; ) and are designed to restore the DMD reading frame and express a truncated and partially functional dystrophin protein similar to Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD), a milder form of muscular dystrophy with exon-skip mutations that maintain the dystrophin reading frame ( B, exon 50 deletion; ; ). The safety profiles of these PMOs were excellent during initial and longitudinal clinical trials (6 years with eteplirsen) and improved long-term ambulation and pulmonary declines compared with DMD natural history controls ( , , ; ). Although these exon-skipping drugs also increased both exon skipping and dystrophin expression, a caveat is that expression of these pseudodystrophin proteins is quite modest (∼1%–2.5%) compared with dystrophin levels in healthy muscle ( ; ). Notably, these exon-skipping drugs failed to receive regulatory approval by the EMA due to concerns with efficacy and small trial sizes, though the FDA did grant approval ( ).
 Another RNA-based therapeutic approach designed to promote exon skipping uses a vectorized strategy that is longer term and does not require ASO repetitive dosing. AAV-mediated expression of modified U7 snRNAs has been effective in restoring dystrophin expression with remediation of muscular dystrophy in the mdx mouse DMD model, which expresses a Dmd exon 23 C > T transition nonsense mutation ( ). AAV-mediated U7 strategies have also successfully restored dystrophin expression in the heart of a larger vertebrate: the golden retriever dog DMD model ( ). Exon duplications also occur in DMD, with exon 2 duplications (Dup2s) being the most frequent (∼11%) ( B, exon 2 duplication; ). An exon-skipping strategy using a self-complementary AAV9.U7.ACCA (scAAV9.U7.ACCA) vector increases dystrophin levels in a Dup2 mouse to generate either full-length (due to skipping of one copy during splicing) or N-terminal truncated (skipping of both copies induces translation via an exon 5 IRES) functional DMD proteins ( ; ; ). This strategy is being tested in a clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04240314), and current data show >70% dystrophin restoration in a 7 month old patient ( ).

## Huntington's disease: promoting RNA turnover using ASO gapmers and small molecules
Huntington's disease (HD) is a progressive autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disease that generally develops in midlife (30–60 years of age) and is characterized by loss of cortex and striatal neurons with progressive loss of motor control (chorea), cognitive issues, and behavioral/personality changes ( ). HD is one of nine short tandem repeat diseases associated with the somatic expansion of a coding region CAG repeat ( ). Current models propose that HD is caused by inheritance of an HTT mutant allele (mHTT) at or above 36–40 CAG repeats, with somatic expansions of uninterrupted CAG repeats primarily in the striatum ( ; ; ). HD presymptomatic phases may occur over years or decades with sequential pathogenic components, including somatic expansion of the inherited mutant allele followed by transcriptional and/or co/post-transcriptional changes that elicit cell degeneration and death ( ; ). Following transcription, mHTT full-length mRNA or an N-terminal exon 1 truncated form, which is generated by use of an intron 1 cryptic polyadenylation site promoted by the CAG expansion, is translated into a polyglutamine (polyGln) protein ( A; ; ). Alternatively, bidirectional transcription occurs across the HTT locus so that RAN translation of HTT sense transcripts ( ) generates polyGln, polyserine (polySer), and polyalanine (polyAla), whereas antisense transcripts initiating in exon 1 ( ) generate polyAla, polycysteine (polyCys) and polyleucine (polyLeu). The synthesis of these aggregation-prone homopolymeric proteins suggests a predominant protein gain-of-function disease mechanism for HD. However, RNA toxicity and CAG repeat-induced splicing dysregulation have also been proposed as important pathogenic events ( ; ).
Currently, it is unclear whether postsymptomatic therapy delivery would prevent or ameliorate HD pathological changes, though early stage neuronal degeneration is reversed in a conditional mouse transgenic HD model when the mHTT transgene expression is turned off ( ). Another question is the potential link between aberrant HTT expression during neurodevelopment and later-onset neurodegeneration ( ). To examine whether reducing both wild-type and mutant HTT RNA levels is an effective therapeutic strategy, ASO gapmer ( A, ASO) and RNAi methodologies have been tested in rodent models and shown to reduce both total HTT RNA levels and neuropathology ( ; ; ). One of these allele nonselective gapmers (tominersen) was assessed in an earlier phase 1/2a clinical GENHD1 trial and showed reduced mHTT with a good safety profile ( ) but was halted in phase 3 due to concerns regarding efficacy and worsened outcomes compared with placebo ( ; ). A potential issue is that reduction of mutant HTT RNA late in the disease course may miss the therapeutic window, and so a subsequent GENHD2 tominersen trial for participants with prodromal and early manifest HD is ongoing (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05686551). A different strategy that has recently shown positive interim data in a phase I/II clinical trial uses an AAV-delivered miRNA to reduce HTT expression (NCT04120493). As the long-term effects of suppressing total HTT expression in humans is unknown, another ASO approach is selective reduction of mHTT levels by targeting HTT repeat-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to the repeat expansion ( ; ). Alternatively, a (CUG)7 antisense oligonucleotide that targets CAG repeats and reduces mHTT in HD animal models ( ) is currently being tested in a clinical trial for several CAG expansion diseases, including HD (NCT05822908).
Similar to the orally bioavailable small molecule approach for SMA, several small molecule compounds are currently in phase I/II clinical trials for HD. In contrast to SMA, which is a recessive loss-of-function disease, the tactic being pursued for HD is to increase inclusion of a pseudoexon (PE) located in HTT intron 49 (exon 50a), which results in a frameshift and NMD-mediated RNA turnover ( A, small molecule). Like SMN2 exon 7, this HTT PE contains a A−1 5′ss bulge. Several studies have reported using branaplam, which is another small molecule splicing modifier originally developed to treat SMA ( ; ); PTC518 ( ); or a pyrazine amide derivative (compound 27) ( ) to reduce overall HTT RNA and protein levels. A caveat with this approach is that HTT exon 1 CAG expansions trigger the use of a cryptic polyadenylation site in the downstream intron 1, resulting in a highly toxic N-terminal truncated HTT protein that should not be affected by downstream inclusion of exon 50a ( ; ). Interestingly, both branaplam and risdiplam not only decrease HTT RNA and protein levels but also reduce the length of somatic CAG repeat expansions in a cell model (RPE1-AAVS1-CAG115), an effect likely due to the splicing of an A−1 5′ss bulge PE in PMS1 intron 5 ( ). This PMS1 connection is interesting because the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) pathway is thought to influence HD onset and progression by promoting CAG expansions, and HD GWAS analysis has identified both PMS1 and PMS2, paralogous genes that encode MMR proteins that form heterodimers with MLH1 (MutLα [MLH1-PMS2] and MutLβ [MLH1-PMS1]), as genetic modifiers of HD age of onset ( ; ; ).
A different approach to HD RNA correction involves trans-splicing to remove mutant exon 1 RNA and replace it with the wild-type exon version ( A, trans-splicing). This strategy for HTT mRNA has been simmering in the background for more than a decade since it was first demonstrated in proof of concept studies ( ). However, trans-splicing approaches have been hampered by low efficiencies that precluded their use. This approach has been recently revisited due to advances in RNA targeting tools such as CRISPR. For 5′ exon trans-splicing, the PRECISE method ( ) uses cleavage of the poly(A) signal in the cargo to stimulate the reaction, resulting in an increase from 2%–3% to ∼35% editing of the HTT transcript in HEK293FT cells when cleavage is driven by the eDisCas7–11 system. Furthermore, cargo cleavage by a cis-ribozyme is even more efficient and has the added benefit of only requiring delivery of the trans-template. However, these efficiency gains were not observed following lentiviral delivery of a ribozyme-mediated cargo, with only ∼4% editing in HD patient fibroblasts and ∼5% editing in induced human cortical neurons. Still, the demonstration of activity in nondividing primary cells is encouraging, and these technologies are beginning to gain momentum as trans-splicing methodologies continue to be refined.

## Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: targeting RNA and RAN toxicity with ASO gapmers and small molecules
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neuromuscular disease with a typical mid to late onset age range that results in upper and lower motor neuron degeneration, leading to paralysis, respiratory failure, and death ( ). The first gene implicated in ALS was SOD1, with >200 pathogenic gene variants accounting for ∼12% familial and 1%–2% of sporadic ALS, making SOD1 mutations the second leading cause of familial ALS (fALS) in European ancestries ( ; ). SOD1 is also a recent success story for RNA targeting of ALS with tofersen, an allele-nonselective 20-mer mixed (5-10-5) backbone gapmer that targets the SOD1 transcript for degradation. Tofersen received accelerated approval by the FDA in April 2023 due to its activity in reducing neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels and phosphorylated heavy chain (pNfH) in serum and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF), together with deceleration of the decline of clinical and respiratory measures ( ; ; ).
A more complex pathogenic picture has emerged from studies on the most frequent genetic cause of ALS (∼40% familial and ∼7% sporadic in European ancestry populations) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD; C9-ALS/FTD) due to GGGGCC (G4C2) repeat expansions in C9orf72 intron 1 ( B; ; ; ). C9orf72 expression is highest in myeloid cells, and G4C2 repeat expansions are associated with reduced C9ORF72 protein levels, suggesting that G4C2 expansions may cause C9orf72 haploinsufficiency in which expression of only the wild-type allele is insufficient to preserve a wild-type phenotype. Arguments against this pathomechanism include the observations that ALS patients carrying C9orf72 missense or truncation mutations have not been reported ( ), and ALS-relevant neurodegenerative phenotypes are not observed in either C9orf72+/− or C9orf72−/− mice ( ; ). For the latter observation, disease-associated biochemical events, such as pseudoexon splicing characteristic of human ALS ( ), can vary between mice and humans, as observed for ALS-linked STMN2 downregulation ( ; ). Additionally, recent studies argue that a C9orf72 loss-of-function mechanism may be an important contributor to C9-ALS/FTD pathology. For example, C9orf72 haploinsufficiency has been reported in human induced motor neurons (iMNs) with reduced C9orf72 expression, resulting in altered vesicular trafficking, lysosomal biogenesis, and enhanced sensitivity to both glutamate and dipeptide repeat (DPR) protein toxicity ( ). Retroviral-mediated restoration of C9ORF72 protein expression restored iMN survival, whereas elimination of C9ORF72 expression from one allele reduced iMN survival to levels similar to that of C9-ALS-FTD patient iMNs. Another issue is that loss of C9ORF72 protein in C9orf72−/− homozygous mice leads to STING-induced elevation of the type 1 interferon inflammatory response. Cumulatively, these results suggest that substantial biallelic loss of human C9orf72 expression should be avoided ( ).
To date, RNA-based therapeutics have primarily emphasized C9-ALS/FTD pathomechanistic models implicating toxic RNA and RAN protein gain-of-function mechanisms, both of which could be resolved by reducing C9orf72 RNA levels using ASO gapmers ( B, ASO). This strategy was initially validated using human C9orf72 fibroblasts and iPSC-derived motor neurons by targeting either only the G4C2 repeat, both the sense G4C2 and antisense G2C4 repeats, or regions of the transcript that do not contain the repeat ( ; ; ). These gapmers reduced characteristic features of C9-ALS/FTD, including RNA foci (containing G4C2 or G2C4 repeats), aberrant gene expression, and susceptibility to excitotoxicity. These early studies and others led to clinical trials of ASO gapmers designed to reduce mutant C9orf72 RNA G4C2 levels following intrathecal injection (Clinical Trials.gov NCT04288856 and NCT04931862). Unfortunately, both trials were eventually terminated due to failures to meet secondary endpoints or provide sufficient clinical benefits, and patients in the high-dose group for one trial trended toward worsened clinical outcomes ( ; ).
One explanation for this clinical failure is that C9orf72 antisense RNAs carrying G2C4 repeats are still expressed and may be upregulated following sense strand downregulation ( ). Additionally, G2C4 antisense repeat RNAs, but not DPR proteins, induce activation of the PKR/eIF2α-mediated integrated stress response pathway that inhibits canonical, while activating RAN, translation ( ; ). To address this issue via an RNAi approach, earlier studies developed duplex G2C4/G4C2 RNAs with A/U central mismatches that were designed to destabilize the G2C4/G4C2 duplex to allow efficient RISC loading, which led to 40%–60% reduction in nuclear RNA foci ( ), whereas single-stranded siRNAs (ss-siRNAs) composed entirely of chemically modified bases were more effective in blocking both intronic sense and antisense transcripts through both RNAi and RNAi-independent (ASO-like) pathways ( B, ss-siRNA; ). An alternative miRNA strategy is based on concatenated constructs expressing two miRNA hairpins (miC) predicted to target both sense and antisense C9orf72 RNAs in the nucleus ( ). AAV5-miC expression in either human iPSC-derived neurons or following bilateral striatal injections into the Tg(C9orf72) line 112 mouse ALS model led to reductions in RNA foci and C9orf72 transcripts ( ).
Small molecule strategies for C9-ALS/FTD have been primarily designed to target the hairpin and G-quadruplex structures formed by r(G4C2) repeats to block its interactions with RBPs and/or prevent RNA structures amenable to RAN translation. For example, early studies revealed that r(G4C2) binding by TMPyP4 porphyrin alters G-quadruplex structures and blocks its interactions with two r(G4C2)-interacting RBPs, HNRNPA1 and SRSF1 ( ), whereas compound 1a decreases polyGA, polyGP, and RNA foci in HEK293 cells expressing a r(G4C2)66 repeat and in C9orf72 iNeurons ( ). A FRET-based screen identified three small molecules (DB1246/1247/1273) that stabilize the r(G4C2) G-quadruplex and decrease sense RNA foci and DPRs in iPSC-derived motor and cortical neurons ( ). Another small molecule, compound 4, selectively targets the r(G4C2) hairpin via binding the 1 × 1 GG internal loop and inhibits HNRNPH1 binding as well as RAN translation and polysome assembly ( ). Finally, CB253 binds and stabilizes an alternative strand slippage-induced 2 × 2 GG internal loop and inhibits RAN translation in r(G4C2)66 transfected HEK293T cells ( ). Another notable finding is a blood–brain-penetrant small molecule (compound 1) that selectively binds r(G4C2) expansions in the C9orf72 mutant intron 1 (mi1) ( B, small molecule). Compound 1 selectively decreases mi1 retention during splicing, which results in increased mi1 turnover mediated by the nuclear RNA exosome as well as decreases of polyGP in C9-ALS/FTD cells (LCLs and iPSC-derived neurons) and of polyGP as well as polyGA aggregates in the brains of BAC transgenic C9-ALS/FTD models ( ).
Fewer studies have examined the structures formed by the antisense r(G2C4) strand and its binding to small molecules even though the crystal structure of [(CCCCGG)3(CCCC)] r(G2C4) is an A-form helix with C:C mismatches ( ), whereas, in a ligand complex with the small molecule ANP77 (2-amino-1,8-naphthyridine dimer), r(G2C4) forms a triplex structure consisting of four RNA chains ( ).

## Dravet syndrome: overcoming haploinsufficiency through antagoNATs and ASOs
Dravet syndrome, originally described as severe myoclonic epilepsy in infancy (SMEI), is a rare developmental epileptic encephalopathy with multiple seizure types resistant to anticonvulsants, developmental delays, cognitive impairment, behavioral disorders, and increased incidence of sudden death ( ). In contrast to the hereditary diseases described in the previous sections, >80% of Dravet syndrome cases are caused by de novo heterozygous frameshift, truncation, and missense variants in the SCN1A gene, which encodes the sodium voltage-gated channel α subunit 1 (Nav1.1) ( , mutant; ). Additionally, intronic mutations, including SCN1A intron 20 mutation c.3969 + 2451G > C, have been identified in Dravet syndrome that trigger 20N splicing, resulting in frameshifting leading to an exon 21 PTC and NMD ( ; ). The c.3969 + 2451G > C mutation has been examined by generation of an orthologous mouse Scn1a+/KI model that reproduces exon 20N inclusion, seizures, early-onset mortality, and ∼50% reductions of both Scn1a RNA and Nav1.1 protein levels ( ). All of these SCN1A mutations lead to severely attenuated inward sodium currents due to reduced expression of SCN1A RNA and protein in inhibitory GABAergic interneurons ( ; ; ).
 Because Dravet syndrome is a neurological disease characterized by haploinsufficiency, several ASO approaches have been tested to determine whether SCN1A wild-type allele expression can be upregulated to a therapeutic level. First, natural antisense RNAs (NATs) regulate the expression of many genes ( ), and SCN1A-AS1, also known as SCN1ANAT, is a multiexon lncRNA gene that fully overlaps the human SCN1A locus. Blocking ASOs (or antagoNATS) targeting the SCN1A-AS1 lncRNA upregulate SCN1A expression in multiple cell lines (4.2-fold in SK-N-AS neuroblastoma cells), including Dravet syndrome patient fibroblasts ( , antagoNAT). Importantly, these effects are gene-specific and do not affect the α subunits of related sodium channels ( ). Intrathecal injection of an active antagoNAT into a Dravet mouse knock-in model (Scn1a+/E1099X), which is characterized by reduced Scn1a RNA levels (50% of wild type), at 7 weeks of age led to a 10%–30% increase in brain Scn1a RNA levels and dose-dependent spontaneous seizure reduction (70% at 20 μg/injection). These positive in vivo effects support the possibility that postnatal correction of SCN1A expression will be an effective therapeutic for Dravet syndrome.
Another approach is an ASO-based strategy, currently in a phase 2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04740476), that is designed to upregulate the expression of the wild-type SCN1A allele by blocking splicing of the SCN1A 20N poison exon ( , ASO) ( ; ). Similar to the strategy used to identify the SMA drug nusinersen, ASO tiling across exon 20N and its flanking introns identified ASO-22 as a lead candidate that significantly increased expression of the productive (−20N) SCN1A RNA isoform in a human neural progenitor cell line without altering the expression of other voltage-gated sodium channel α-subunit genes. Single ICV injections of ASO-22 into Scn1+/− heterozygous postnatal day 2 (P2) mice increased Nav1.1 protein expression in the brain, with an increase in percent survival at P30–P90 while reducing the incidence of seizures and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) ( ). Interestingly, changes in Nav1.1 protein levels were muted when injections were performed at a later developmental stage (P14), highlighting the importance of identifying the therapeutic window for future human clinical trials.

## Conclusions and outlook
Remarkable progress has been made in the past decade in the design, optimization, and preclinical testing of therapies designed to target RNAs implicated in a variety of neurological and neuromuscular diseases. Development and testing of the therapeutic modalities covered in this review will continue and likely expand to address more prevalent disorders, including Parkinson's disease. However, determining the optimal therapeutic approach for a particular target remains challenging, as each system presents unique hurdles related to the affected tissue (delivery and dosage), the specific RNP network at the target site (access), the kinetic window (timing), and persistence (durability).
Cargo delivery and efficacy remain high barriers for many approaches, and the viability of long-term administration of immunogenic enzymes like Cas9 remains unclear. One alternative may be leveraging endogenous pathways, as is possible in the case of RNA base editors. Recruitment of the cellular editing machinery to enable a desired editing event has been demonstrated ( ; ). Encouragingly, recent studies using stereopure-modified oligonucleotides have been able to achieve 50% editing in nonhuman primate hepatocytes ( ). Furthermore, the emergence of engineered tRNA-based therapeutics to correct missense, nonsense, and even some frameshift mutations is an exciting development, particularly because an estimated 11% of all genetic lesions are caused by nonsense mutations ( ). However, engineered tRNA-based nonsense suppressors based on anticodon edited tRNAs to readthrough PTCs have not yet been reported for neurological or neuromuscular diseases ( ; ; ; ). As opposed to small molecule nonsense suppressors that alter the ribosome decoding center and allow binding of near/noncognate aminoacylated tRNAs, engineered tRNAs can be designed to insert the wild-type amino acid at that position, thus maintaining protein structures required for full functional activity ( ). Importantly, this is a therapeutic option for both hereditary and de novo mutations that can be corrected using a relatively small library of engineered tRNAs ( ).
