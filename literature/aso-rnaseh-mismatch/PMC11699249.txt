To realize chronic administration of ASO therapeutics to treat neurodegenerative diseases, new chemical modifications that improve activity and safety profiles are still needed. Furthermore, it is highly desirable to develop a single stereopure ASO with a defined activity and safety profile to avoid any efficacy and safety concerns due to the batch-to-batch variation in the composition of diastereomers. Here, a stereopure PMO-gapmer was developed as a new construct to improve safety and stability by installing charge-neutral PMOs at the wing region and by fully controlling phosphorus stereochemistries. While evaluating the stereopure PMO-gapmers, we observed a significant difference in safety profile among stereoisomers in which only one phosphorus stereochemistry differs. These results further highlight the benefits of developing stereopure ASOs as safe and well-characterized candidates for clinical studies.

Kanatsu and colleagues developed a PMO-gapmer construct and a convergent solution phase synthetic method, leading to the identification of ASO-486-R5, a stereopure PMO-gapmer targeting tau. The varied safety profiles of different antisense oligonucleotide phosphorus stereoisomers underscore the importance of phosphorus stereocontrol in developing safe and effective clinical candidates.

## Introduction
Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are an important class of therapeutics to treat diseases with genetically validated targets. ASOs exhibit therapeutic activity by knocking down the target genes or modulating splicing events. For the knockdown of target genes, gapmer ASOs (or gapmers) inducing RNase H-mediated degradation of the target RNA have been employed. The gapmers have a central DNA gap region for engagement with RNase H, and 5′- and 3′-wing regions of modified nucleotides (e.g., 2′-O-methoxyethylribose [2′-MOE], LNA, cEt) to improve the binding affinity to target genes as well as to increase stability against exonucleases ( ). Furthermore, phosphorothioates are preferentially used as the internucleotide linkages throughout gapmers to improve their stability and pharmacokinetics profiles. While conventional gapmer configurations have been successfully used in approved drugs, there are still concerns regarding the toxicity caused by nonselective protein binding associated with phosphorothioate linkages. Efforts have been made to mitigate safety risks by adding a certain number of phosphodiester linkages, adding a chemically modified nucleotide at a specific position of the gap region, or more recently, introducing charge-neutral linkages. , , , , , , Incorporation of charge-neutral linkages like phosphoryl guanidine in the wing and/or gap region was demonstrated to improve ASO properties. , Phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers (PMOs) are another class of clinically validated, chemically modified oligonucleotides with neutral linkages. Considering the general safety benefits of PMO modification and its resistance to nucleases due to the unnatural phosphorodiamidate linkages, the employment of PMO modification at the wing region of gapmers could be beneficial. However, the use of PMOs has been limited to modulation of splicing events, and its incorporation into gapmer constructs has not yet been reported. 
Replacement of a phosphodiester linkage with a phosphorothioate or phosphorodiamidate linkage makes the phosphorus atom chiral and generates two possible diastereomers at each internucleotide linkage. It has been hypothesized that the phosphorus stereochemistry influences the activity, stability, and safety of ASOs and could be used for fine-tuning of ASO properties. , , , , , However, the investigation of this stereochemical hypothesis has been hampered by two related obstacles: (1) the notion that issues of phosphorous stereochemistry were of secondary or even no importance and (2) the chemistry technologies to stereoselectively and practically construct single molecule ASOs were not available. Recently, advances in stereoselective phosphorylation chemistry have emerged to enable practical synthesis of stereopure ASOs. Such technologies include Stec and colleagues’ oxathiaphospholane chemistry, , , Beaucage and coworkers’ acyloxazaphospholidines, the oxazaphospholidines of Wada et al., , , and Baran and colleagues’ Phosphorus Sulfur Incorporation (PSI) chemistry. , , Additionally, it was reported that the clinical failure of mongersen was traced to inconsistent biological activity exhibited in different clinical batches. , , The batch-to-batch variability was not discerned by the standard small-molecule analytical release testing utilized at that time. Presumably, different compositions of mongersen’s 220 possible phosphorothioate diastereomers was an uncontrolled variable in the Good Manufacturing Practices production of the different clinical batches. The obvious implication is that a controlled synthesis and comparison of single-molecule entities would be essential to scientifically explore and manage the safety and efficacy of ASOs.
Tau is an important target for tauopathies including Alzheimer disease (AD). Tau protein aggregates upon phosphorylation in pathological conditions to result in neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), which is a hallmark of AD pathology. The level of NFT correlates well with cognitive deficit and neuronal loss. In addition, there is strong genetic evidence between tau mutations and frontotemporal dementias. , DeVos et al. reported that intracerebroventricular (ICV) infusion of a tau targeting ASO reduced already formed phosphorylated tau and prevented neuronal loss in PS19 mice. Moreover, Ionis-MAPTRx (BIIB-080), an ASO targeting MAPT, reduced not only tau protein production but also the tau tangles—measured by tau positron emission tomography in a clinical phase 1 study—providing further supporting evidence for the potential of a tau targeting ASO therapy. 
Here, we report a new gapmer construct called PMO-gapmer, consisting of charge-neutral PMO wing regions and a phosphorothioate DNA gap region. We have established a synthesis of stereodefined PMO-gapmers with complete control of phosphorus stereochemistry by combining a new method for controlling PMO stereochemistry with novel applications of phosphorothioate stereocontrol. A solution-phase synthetic method for stereodefined PMO-gapmers features a block coupling of 5′ and 3′ fragments and enables a convergent synthesis. We evaluated a series of stereopatterns in the DNA gap region to select optimal stereochemistries in terms of in vivo activity, stability in brain homogenates, and immunogenicity. ASO-486-R5 exhibits good activity, good stability in human brain homogenates, and no cytokine release in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs), and thus, it warrants further evaluation. Moreover, we have observed remarkable differences in efficacy and safety profiles for specific stereochemical patterns of PMO-gapmers. Specifically, we have found that a single phosphorous stereogenic center of ASO-409-R3 imparts lethality or safety in the same PMO-gapmer sequence.

## Results

## Synthesis of stereorandom PMO-gapmers for sequence screening
Stereorandom PMO-gapmers for sequence screening were prepared by solid-phase synthesis using conventional chlorophosphoramidate chemistry for the PMO wings and a phosphoramidite chemistry for the DNA gap region with phosphorothioate linkages. Since conventional PMO synthesis is conducted in the 5′ to 3′ direction, unconventional 5′-phosphoramidite DNA monomers were used to match the synthetic direction ( ). For the PMO-DNA junctions and DNA-PMO junctions at the gap region 5′ and 3′ termini, a 5′-chlorophosphoramidate DNA monomer and a 5′-phosphoramidite PMO monomer were used, respectively. The developed solid-phase synthesis was successfully implemented to synthesize 65 select PMO-gapmers for in vitro and in vivo screening.

## Synthesis of stereodefined PMO-gapmers
Stereopure PMO wings were prepared by the previously reported synthetic protocols ( ). Stereospecific coupling reactions with the stereopure activated PMO monomers were accomplished in the presence of a non-nucleophilic base in a polar aprotic solvent at room temperature: 1,2,2,6,6-pentamethylpiperidine was selected as the optimal base and 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone (DMI) as the best reaction solvent. The use of N-methylimidazole or N-ethylmorpholine, which were employed in conventional stereorandom PMO synthesis, caused epimerization or generated side products. Good solubility as well as faster reaction rate was observed in DMI. DMI gave better coupling results than other tested solvents, including acetonitrile, N-methylpyrrolidinone and DMSO. Under the optimized conditions, efficient stereospecific coupling reactions were achieved without epimerization. All of the possible 16 PMO dinucleotides were synthesized by the developed method to confirm the integrity of phosphorus stereochemistry during the coupling reactions and determine the absolute stereochemistry ( B). X-ray crystal structure of a 5'-TA-3' dinucleotide was obtained to unambiguously determine the absolute stereochemistry of the phosphorus atom and to deduce the absolute stereochemistry of the activated A monomer given that the presumed mechanism of the coupling reaction involves inversion of configuration at the phosphorus stereogenic center ( A). The phosphorus stereochemistry of the rest of the dinucleotides and the activated monomers were then assigned based on relative 31P NMR chemical shifts in comparison with those of TA dinucleotide and PMO A monomer ( C). , 
To assess the correlation of PMO phosphorus stereochemistry with binding affinity, A, T, C, and G 10-mers having homogeneous phosphorus stereochemistry (i.e., all-Rp and all-Sp) were prepared from the stereopure monomers ( ). Melting temperature (Tm) measurements with the corresponding cRNA 10-mers were conducted for each stereoisomer. All-Sp 10-mers consistently exhibited higher Tm than the corresponding all-Rp 10-mers, thus demonstrating that Sp phosphorodiamidate linkages generally have better binding affinity than the corresponding Rp stereoisomers.
For the synthesis of stereodefined PMO-gapmers, P(V) chemistry was selected to ensure stereochemical integrity during the coupling reactions: the stereopure PMO synthesis was used for the stereopure PMO wings, and PSI chemistry developed by the Baran group was employed for the synthesis of the DNA gap region with stereopure phosphorothioate linkages. , , The coupling reactions with PSI-activated DNA monomers resulted in free phosphorothioates, which were not compatible with chlorophosphoramidate chemistry for PMO synthesis. Thus, the linear synthesis of stereodefined PMO-gapmers, as for the stereorandom PMO-gapmers, was not feasible. Instead, a convergent synthetic method featuring a block coupling of two oligomers, a 5′ fragment and a 3′ fragment, was developed ( A). As the coupling reaction between 3′ PSI-activated monomer and 5′ OH showed better coupling efficiency in a model system, the block coupling of 5′ fragment (5- or 6-mer) having 3′ PSI activation with 3′ fragment (13- or 12-mer) having 5′ OH was selected as the key step. To make the key block coupling reaction more efficient on a practical scale, a solution phase synthesis was devised. 
The synthesis of a 3′ fragment commenced with the stereopure PMO synthesis ( B). After synthesis of the desired length of PMOs, all the protecting groups were removed with ammonium hydroxide, and the morpholine at the 3′ terminal was protected as a bistrifluoromethyl benzamide. The stereopure PMO wing facilitated the precipitation of all the intermediates in the synthesis of 3′ fragment upon the addition of less-polar solvents to avoid column chromatographic purifications of the highly polar intermediates. The DNA gap region was elongated from the deprotected 3′ PMO wing with 3′ PSI-activated DNA monomers to give the desired length of 3′ fragment. For the coupling reactions, 3 equiv of monomers in the presence of 4–15 equiv of 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene (DBU) were used in DMI, which was the preferred solvent to compensate for low solubility of the growing oligomers. It is worth noting that the exclusion of water—by azeotroping with toluene before the coupling reaction and by use of molecular sieves during the reaction—was critical for successful coupling reactions. A mixture of triethylsilane, 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), trifluoroethanol, and dichloromethane was used for the mild deprotection of the 4,4′-dimethoxytrityl protecting group. Repeated precipitations after the coupling and deprotection reactions were performed to obtain the oligomeric intermediates in good purity.
The 5′ fragment was synthesized by coupling of a 5′ stereopure PMO wing with a 3′-tert-butyldimethylsilyl protected DNA 5′-chlorophosphoramidate ( C). The resulting product was desilylated and activated with a selected PSI reagent to give the 5′ fragment for the block coupling. The key block coupling reaction was conducted by use of the 3′ fragment as the limiting reagent in the presence of 3 equiv of 5′ fragment and an excess amount of DBU in DMI at room temperature to give the coupling product in a 30%–80% yield. The obtained crude product was subjected to global deprotection with 28% NH4OH in methanol in the presence of dl-dithiothreitol at 50°C–55°C. The resulting product was precipitated from the mixture by adding acetonitrile and ethyl acetate. The isolated solid was dissolved in water and filtered using an Amicon Ultracel 3K filter to remove unreacted substrate residues. Finally, the product was purified by reverse phase-high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), followed by ion-exchange chromatography, desalted with Amicon Ultracel 3K filter, and lyophilized to give a stereodefined PMO-gapmer.
The reaction progress was monitored by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (MS), and 31P NMR was used to assess the chemical and stereochemical integrity of synthetic intermediates and stereodefined PMO-gapmers. The phosphorodiamidate and phosphorothioate linkages showed unique 31P NMR chemical shifts at 15–20 and 55–60 ppm regions, respectively. Since the stereodefined PMO-gapmers have 7–9 phosphorodiamidate linkages and 8–10 phosphorothioates linkages, individual phosphorus peaks were well resolved. All the stereopure intermediates show different 31P NMR patterns from their stereoisomers. The stereodefined PMO-gapmers showed distinct 31P NMR and HPLC profiles to be used for identification. Under the HPLC conditions used for the analysis, certain stereodefined PMO-gapmers showed clear separation from the other stereoisomer having one different phosphorus stereochemistry (e.g., ASO-486-R5-S vs. ASO-486-R5-R; see the ).

## Selection of candidate sequences
Sequence screening for PMO-gapmers commenced with an in silico screening of 18-mer oligonucleotides targeting both exonic and intronic regions of MAPT sequences homologous in cynomolgus monkeys, rhesus monkeys, and humans ( ). The sequences having self-complementarity of more than 4 bp were filtered out. The identified sequences were further narrowed down by off-target search, allowing up to two mismatches and by removing the sequences targeting disease-causing and nerve system-related genes by Gene Ontology terms.
Since a high-throughput solid-phase synthesis of PMO-gapmers was not established at the time of screening, a 5-8-5 2′-OMe gapmer construct, having similar binding affinity with a PMO-gapmer, was used as a surrogate of PMO-gapmer for the first round of sequence screening. The synthesized 2′-OMe gapmers were subjected to in vitro screening: a human neuroblastoma (SH-SY5Y) cell line was treated with the gapmers in the presence of Lipofectamine RANiMAX for 48 h, and the knockdown results were analyzed by qPCR. The in vitro screening identified 65 active sequences after excluding the ones showing cytotoxicity. The sequences were prepared as stereorandom 5-8-5 PMO-gapmers by solid-phase synthesis and evaluated by in vitro assay using Endo-Porter as the transfection agent ( and ). The 12 most active sequences were selected for in vivo evaluation in adult male human MAPT knockin (hTau KI) mice by single ICV injection. Four lead oligonucleotide sequences were identified by their prominent MAPT gene knockdown activity: ASO-409, ASO-467, ASO-469, and ASO-373 ( ). The four sequences were synthesized as two different PMO-gapmer configurations, 5-8-5 and 4-10-4, and evaluated by in vivo study. The two most active compounds having similar sequences but different configurations, ASO-409 (5-8-5) and ASO-486 (4-10-4), were selected for further evaluation ( ).

## Investigation of optimal phosphorus stereochemistry
Screening for optimal phosphorus stereochemical patterns was conducted with the two selected PMO-gapmer sequences ( and ). The phosphorus stereochemistry at the PMO wing regions was fixed as Sp to have better binding to the target sequences. Considering that Sp phosphorothioate linkages show better stability against nucleases, more Sp linkages and a minimum number of Rp linkages would be preferred in the DNA gap region to have a longer duration of action. Thus, single Rp walk in the DNA gap region was conducted to find the optimal position of the Rp linkage in terms of knockdown activity. Also, SSR (3'-SpSpRp-5') stereopatterns at the gap region were evaluated since Verdine et al. reported that the 3′-SSR stereotriad is recognized by RNase H1 and enhances activity. 
For ASO-409, three PMO-gapmers having SSR patterns and five compounds having one Rp phosphorothioate linkage in the DNA gap region were synthesized and evaluated in a 3-day in vivo study with hTau KI mice ( ). For synthetic convenience, the phosphorus stereochemistry of the phosphorodiamidate linkage at the 5′ PMO-DNA junction was not controlled; a mixture of two stereoisomers, marked “-M,” was used for in vivo study. The DNA gap stereoisomers showed quite different in vivo activities: ASO-409-SSR2-M showed the best activity, which was approximately 5-fold better than that of stereorandom ASO-409. ASO-409-R3-M isomer showed similar activity with ASO-409-SSR2-M, suggesting the Rp linkage at the position between the 7th and 8th nucleotide (G-A) is important for the activity.
Similar stereochemical structure activity relationship (SSAR) studies were conducted with ASO-486, a 4-10-4 PMO-gapmer sequence ( ). Since ASO-486 has 10 nucleotides in the gap region, 4 PMO-gapmers having SSR patterns were designed, and Rp walk was conducted using six compounds having one Rp linkage. Among them, ASO-486-SSR2-M showed the best activity, and ASO-486-R5-M showed similar activity with ASO-486-SSR2-M, suggesting that the Rp linkage at the position between the 8th and 9th nucleotide (G-A) is important for the activity. The relative activity difference between ASO-486-SSR2-M/ASO-486-R5-M and ASO-486-random was smaller than that observed in ASO-409. The results suggested that the impact of phosphorus stereochemistry varies depending on the ASO sequences and configurations.

## Impact of phosphorus stereochemistry on safety
The two most potent ASO-409 stereoisomers (ASO-409-SSR2-M and ASO-409-R3-M) from the 3-day in vivo study were subjected to a 2-month in vivo study with hTau KI mice at 30- and 60-μg doses to evaluate the duration of knockdown activity and preliminary safety profile after single ICV administration (four animals/group). Mortality occurred in three animals in the 60-μg ASO-409-SSR2-M group after 3 weeks ( A). One mortality in the 30-μg ASO-409-R3-M group was suspected to be incidental since all the other animals in the same group did not show any sign of toxicity. The mortality was not caused by the MAPT knockdown: the ASO-409-R8-M group did not show any toxicity, including mortality or significant body weight loss, although ASO-409-R8-M showed MAPT knockdown activity similar to that of ASO-409-SSR2-M after 3 weeks ( B and 5C). To assess the impact of chirality at the phosphorodiamidate linkage of 5′ PMO-DNA junctions on the safety profile, fully stereocontrolled ASO-409-SSR2 and ASO-409-R3 were synthesized and subjected to a 2-month in vivo single ICV dose study at a 60-μg dose ( D). In this study, the ASO-409-SSR2-M group showed results similar to those of the previous study. All the mice in the ASO-409-SSR2-S group also showed mortality after 3 weeks, but in the ASO-409-SSR2-R group, which differs in one phosphorus chirality, only one mouse showed mortality. The single phosphorus stereochemistry exhibited more dramatic differences in safety profile in the ASO-409-R3 groups. All mice in the ASO-409-R3-S group died within 6 weeks, but the mice in the ASO-409-R3-R group did not show any mortality or significant body weight loss in 8 weeks ( E). 

## Identification of ASO-486-R5 as a candidate
The two most potent stereoisomers of ASO-486 (ASO-486-SSR2-M and ASO-486-R5-M) from the 3-day in vivo study were assessed in a 2-month in vivo study with hTau KI mice at 30- and 60-μg doses to evaluate the duration of knockdown activity and potential toxicity after single-dose ICV administration (four animals/group). ASO-486-R4-M, which showed weaker activity in a 3-day in vivo study, as well as a stereorandom ASO-486 were tested in parallel at a 100-μg dose for comparison ( ). Gratifyingly, no mortality was observed after ASO-486 treatment, and all of the tested groups, except the ASO-486-SSR2-M 60-μg dose group, did not show any body weight loss ( ). Both ASO-486-SSR2-M and ASO-486-R5-M showed dose-dependent MAPT mRNA reduction lasting for 2 months. ASO-486-R5-M showed 40% average knockdown in cortex after 3 weeks, which was weaker than that of ASO-486-SSR2-M (59% average knockdown) at a 60-μg dose. However, the knockdown activity of ASO-486-R5-M decreased slowly over time to show similar knockdown activity with ASO-486-SSR2-M after 2 months (32% vs. 40% average knockdown). 
The stability of ASO-486 stereoisomers was evaluated in human brain homogenates ( ). The residual percentage of stereorandom ASO-486 decreased time dependently and was about 50% after 6 days’ incubation. As expected, all the stereodefined PMO-gapmers, including ASO-486-SSR2-M and ASO-486-R5-M, showed better stability profiles than the stereorandom compound. ASO-486-R9-M showed the most noticeable degradation among stereodefined ASO-486 isomers (20% decrease), which was, however, still significantly better than that of the stereorandom compound. The compounds having one Rp and two Rp linkages (SSR isomers) did not show significant stability differences. 
For the evaluation of individual stereoisomers, the stereopure isomers of ASO-486-SSR2-M and ASO-486-R5-M (ASO-486-SSR2-S, ASO-486-SSR2-R, ASO-486-R5-S, and ASO-486-R5-R) were synthesized and evaluated by a 2-month in vivo study ( A). All the stereoisomers showed good knockdown activity in a 3-day in vivo study, with a tendency for Sp junction isomers to show slightly better activity than the corresponding Rp isomers ( ). In a 2-month in vivo study, stereopure ASO-486-SSR2-S and ASO-486-SSR2-R exhibited around 10% body weight loss at a 60-μg dose ( B). In addition, ASO-486-SSR2-S showed increased cytokine release (interleukin-6 [IL-6], IL-8, IL-13 and interferon-γ [IFN-γ]) at high doses in hPBMCs ( ). However, ASO-486-SSR2-R did not show any changes in cytokine release, thus providing further evidence that a single phosphorus stereochemistry change has impacts on the safety profile. Gratifyingly, ASO-486-R5-S and ASO-486-R5-R did not show any body weight changes in a 2-month in vivo study or increases in cytokine release in hPBMCs. Based on the activity, stability, and safety results, ASO-486-R5-S and ASO-486-R5-R were selected as candidates for further development. 

## Discussion
ASOs are an important therapeutic modality for the selective reduction of target genes. The clinical application of ASOs have successfully expanded to CNS diseases to treat spinal muscular atrophy (nusinersen) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (tofersen) by intrathecal injection. To further expand the application of ASOs in the CNS field, improved chemical modifications are desirable to increase the activity, safety profile, and stability that would lengthen the dosing interval after intrathecal injection. Since the stabilization strategies for ASOs have typically replaced the oxygen(s) at the phosphorous internucleotide linkages with other atoms, the issue of stereocontrol has emerged. Thus far, stereorandom ASO modalities consisting of mixtures of thousands to billions of individual components have been evaluated pre-clinically and clinically, with several commercial products as well. Recent publications have highlighted several serious issues with stereorandom ASOs: (1) analytical techniques for traditional small molecules are incapable of detecting, let alone quantifying, single components in a complex stereorandom ASO mixture; (2) batch-to-batch consistency is not possible for producing complex mixtures of stereoisomers; and (3) biological assessments of different batches of the “same” stereorandom ASO reveal significant differences in behavior at the pre-clinical and clinical stage. , , To investigate the full potential of the ASO modality, it is thus desirable to apply small-molecule standards (i.e., single well-characterized molecule with well-defined and consistent biological properties) to this therapeutic platform. Whereas several technologies have recently been developed to assemble phosphorothioate-linked oligonucleotides in a stereocontrolled manner, thus far, no method has been reported to assemble phosphorodiamidate-linked PMOs with any control of stereochemistry at the internucleotide linkage. Recently, stereopure PMO monomers were reported from our lab that enable the precise control of stereochemistry at each stereogenic phosphorous atom. With a view to create single-molecule tau-targeting ASOs with well-defined quality, safety, and efficacy, a goal was set to combine the stereodefined PMO and phosphorothioate methodologies to assemble a chimeric ASO (i.e., PMO-gapmers).
For the evaluation of the therapeutic application of PMO-gapmers, we developed a solid-phase synthesis of stereorandom PMO-gapmers for sequence screening and a convergent solution-phase synthetic method for stereodefined PMO-gapmers. The solution-phase synthesis for stereodefined PMO-gapmers utilizes a block coupling of 3′ fragment with PSI-activated 5′ fragment and repeated precipitation-based purifications. Our solution-phase synthetic protocol consistently gave the desired products in good yield and could be the basis of an efficient method for manufacturing clinical candidates. Considering the low throughput of the solution-phase method, a high-throughput solid-phase synthesis is desirable for efficient screening of stereodefined PMO-gapmers, and the development will be reported in due course.
With optimal sequences for MAPT knockdown, searching for optimal phosphorus stereochemistry was conducted with the stereoisomers selected based on hypotheses in ASO-409 or ASO-486 (217 = 131,072 possible stereoisomers). For the initial screening of stereochemistry, a mixture of two stereoisomers without control of the phosphorus stereochemistry at the 5′ junction of PMO and DNA was used for synthetic convenience. The stereochemistry of the phosphorodiamidate linkages in PMO wings was fixed as Sp to have better binding to the target sequences. Considering that the stereorandom PMO-gapmer has lower Tm than the corresponding MOE-gapmer, Sp linkages at the PMO wing regions were expected to partially compensate for the differences in Tm and achieve optimal binding affinity. For the selection of the stereochemistry in the DNA gap region, it was desirable to have more Sp than Rp linkages to ensure better stability against endonucleases, which would translate to longer duration in in vivo studies. Thus, single Rp walk and SSR walk in the DNA gap region were conducted to find the stereoisomers showing the best activity. Similar strategies have been reported for the identification of the optimal stereochemistry. , After evaluating the activity of all the synthesized stereoisomers, it was found that Rp at specific positions (e.g., at G7-A8 for ASO-409 [ ] and at G8-A9 for ASO-486 [ ]) is crucial to have good knockdown activity. In addition, the presence of a second Rp linkage did not give significant activity gain (e.g., ASO-409-SSR2-M vs. ASO-409-R3-M and ASO-486-SSR2-M vs. ASO-486-R5-M). It is reported that the position of Rp linkage in the gap region guides the RNase H cleavage site, and that selection of Rp linkage position—which reinforces cleavage at one of the native cleavage sites—gives a cleaner cleavage profile and higher knockdown activity. , The RNase H cleavage site analysis showed that ASO-486-SSR2-R and ASO-486-R5-R result in an increased cleavage at the same cleavage site presumably due to the Rp linkage at G8-A9 ( ). ASO-486-SSR2-R showed additional cleavage at the secondary cleavage site due to the additional Rp linkage at C11–C12. However, the cleavage at the secondary site was very weak and did not change overall knockdown activity.
SSAR studies revealed remarkably different safety profiles for two stereodefined PMO-gapmers differing at a single phosphorus stereogenic center. In a 2-month in vivo study to evaluate the duration of activity and safety profile, ASO-409-SSR2-M showed mortality after 3 weeks (no acute adverse event was observed after ICV injection). The quality check of study samples confirmed that the endotoxin level and osmolarity of the samples were in the acceptable range. , The stereopure components of ASO-409-SSR2-M and ASO-409-R3-M were synthesized and subjected to the 2-month study at a 60-μg dose to unambiguously evaluate the safety profile of each component. ASO-409-SSR2-S and ASO-409-R3-S showed similar toxicity with the corresponding mixtures, and turned out more toxic than ASO-409-SSR2-R and ASO-409-R3-R, respectively. Surprisingly, ASO-409-R3-R did not show any toxicity or weight changes after 2 months, although ASO-409-R3-S showed total mortality. These results strengthen the hypothesis that rigorous investigation of single stereodefined ASOs with control of phosphorus stereochemistry will be essential to select safe ASOs within a given sequence.
The mice treated with ASO-409-SSR2-M showed mortality, forelimb dysfunction or abnormal gait, and body weight loss after 3 weeks in 2-month in vivo studies. In histopathologic examination, degeneration and/or necrosis of Purkinje cells in the cerebellum and vacuolation in the pons were observed in all animals treated with ASO-409-SSR2-M at 30 μg and above. In severely affected animals, loss of neurons with infiltration of glia cells were also noted in the pons ( ). ASO-409-SSR2-S and ASO-409-R3-S showed similar target knockdown activity at cerebellum and cortex ( ) to rule out the possibility that disproportionate ASO distribution in different brain regions would have caused the toxicity. The ASOs having the same sequence and chemistry but different stereochemistry did not show any toxicity (e.g., ASO-409-R8-M in ). Thus, it was proposed that the observed toxicity is not due to the ASO chemistry but potentially due to knockdown of off-target genes. In fact, Atp2b2 with 1-base mismatch to ASO-409 was identified as one of the potential causal genes using the RNA-sequence method and in silico mouse off-target search (data not shown). Considering that the target sequence in the mouse Atp2b2 gene does not overlap with human gene sequence, the observed adverse event might be limited to mice.
Toward the identification of development candidates, ASO-486-R5 showed good activity and drug properties. ASO-486-R5-M showed knockdown activity lasting for 2 months. Even though ASO-486-R5-M showed slightly weaker activity than ASO-486-SSR2-M in 3 weeks, it showed similar activity after 8 weeks without any body weight loss. The corresponding fully stereocontrolled ASOs, ASO-486-R5-S and ASO-486-R5-R, showed almost the same binding affinity to the target sequence measured by Tm (60.7°C and 60.5°C, respectively), similar knockdown activity in a 3-day in vivo study ( ), and no body weight change in a 2-month in vivo study at a 60-μg dose. Since the stereopatterns of ASO-486 were selected to have better stability against nucleases, all the stereoisomers including ASO-486-R5-M showed better stability than the corresponding stereorandom mixtures in human brain homogenates. In addition, ASO-486-R5-S and ASO-486-R5-R did not show any increase in cytokine release in hPBMCs. Further studies in mice and non-human primates will be conducted for the evaluation of both compounds toward clinical development.
Our study has several limitations. To assess long-term safety and sustained efficacy, chronic dosing of stereodefined PMO-gapmers would be necessary. Although tested by cytokine release assay in hPBMCs, more extensive immunogenicity testing would be required to evaluate long-term safety in humans. Evaluating the new PMO-gapmer construct’s efficacy and advantages involves testing it on other neurodegenerative or genetic diseases. Additionally, comparison studies with MOE-gapmers would be needed to understand differences in distribution, neurotoxicity, off-target effects, and immunogenicity.
In conclusion, a novel PMO-gapmer was discovered and evaluated as a potentially safer and more stable gapmer construct. Methods were also described that enable the convergent synthesis of stereodefined PMO-gapmers and subsequent SSAR studies. SSAR studies revealed remarkable differences in the safety and toxicity of stereodefined PMO-gapmers differing at a single phosphorus stereogenic center. After series of in silico, in vitro, and in vivo screening, ASO-486-R5-S and ASO-486-R5-R were identified as potential development candidates targeting MAPT for the treatment of tauopathies. Further studies in mice and non-human primates are warranted to evaluate the safety, activity, and duration of ASO-486-R5-S and ASO-486-R5-R.

## Materials and methods

## Synthesis of oligonucleotides
Stereorandom PMO-gapmers for this study were prepared by solid-phase synthesis following the procedures described in the . Detailed procedures for the solution-phase synthesis of stereopure PMO-gapmers (ASO-486-R5-S, ASO-486-R5-R, and ASO-486-R5-M) and 31P NMR and HPLC profiles of selected compounds are provided in the . All other stereopure PMO-gapmers were prepared following the same procedure.

## In vitro screening
The SH-SY5Y cells (European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures) were cultured in DMEM/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (high-glucose) supplemented with non-essential amino acids and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; all materials were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 tissue culture incubator.
Cultured SH-SY5Y cells were transfected using Endo-Porter (Gene Tools, Philomath, OR) with 10–300 nM antisense oligonucleotide. Two days post-transfection, RNA was isolated from the cell lysis using Maxwell RSC Simply RNA Cells/Tissue Kit (Promega, Madison, WI), and cDNA was synthesized. Tau mRNA levels were measured by real-time qPCR using TaqMan probes specific to human MAPT (assay ID: Hs00902194_m1) and human GAPDH (assay ID: HS99999905_m1). GAPDH mRNA levels were used as internal control for normalization. Results are presented as relative mRNA expression to the vehicle control.

## In vivo screening
Adult male human MAPT knockin (hTau KI) mice were used for in vivo evaluations. All surgical procedures were performed under butorphanol, medetomidine, and midazolam anesthesia, and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and carried out in accordance with, as appropriate, the animal experimentation regulations of Eisai Company, Ltd. ASOs (30–100 μg in 10-μL saline solution) were administered to mice by ICV bolus injection (n = 3–12 mice/group) into the left lateral ventricle. A control group was treated with saline with ICV injection. At necropsy, all surviving animals were weighed. Tissues were collected 3, 7, 21, or 56 days after oligonucleotide administration. RNA was extracted from hippocampus, striatum, and cortex and examined for human tau mRNA expression using real-time qPCR analysis. Human tau mRNA levels were measured using TaqMan probes specific to human MAPT (assay ID: Hs00902194_m1) and were normalized by mouse glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh, assay ID: Mm99999915_g1). Results were calculated as normalized human tau mRNA percent inhibition of the vehicle control. The hemibrains were collected from animals treated with ASO-409-SSR2-M at 30 and 60 μg in a 3-week study and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, and paraffin-embedded sections were prepared. Tissue sections were stained with H&E.

## In vitro stability in human brain homogenate
Human brain homogenate was obtained from BioIVT (Westbury, NY). Gender-mixed human brains (five males and five females) were homogenized in 100 mM Tris HCl (pH 8) buffer including 1 mM magnesium acetate and antimycotic agents. The diluted homogenate (10 mg/mL) in the same buffer was incubated for 1 day at 37°C. ASO solution (100 μM in 5 μL) was added to 500 μL of the pre-incubated homogenate and then incubated at 37°C for 144 h. The homogenate samples were sequentially collected at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 144 h during incubation. The collected samples were immediately mixed with Clarity OTX lysis-loading buffer (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) and stored at −80°C. Solid-phase extraction was performed using Clarity OTX 100 mg 96-well plates (Phenomenex) following the manufacturer’s procedure. Samples were mixed with internal standard (IS, an in-house synthesized 18-mer oligonucleotide with different chemistry and sequence from ASO-486) solution and loaded onto a Clarity OTX plate equilibrated with 50 mM ammonium acetate (pH 4.5). After centrifugation, the plate was washed twice with 50 mM ammonium acetate (pH 4.5) in 50% acetonitrile. Analyte was eluted twice with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 9.5) in 50% acetonitrile. The eluted solutions were dried and reconstituted with a solvent consisting of 10 mM triethylamine, 200 mM HFIP, 10% methanol, and 0.02% acetylacetone. The solution was analyzed by ion-pairing LC-MS (Nexera system [Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan] and Q Exactive HF System [Thermo Fisher Scientific]). The peak area ratio (PAR) of each ASO-486 to IS was calculated. The residual percentages of ASO-486 were calculated using the percentage of PARs at 24, 48, 72, and 144 h of PAR at 0 h.

## Preliminary cytokine release assay using hPBMCs
hPBMCs were purchased from AllCells (donor ID: 14078). Upon receiving the live cells, fresh hPBMCs were plated in 96-well plates, with the seeding density at 5 × 105 cells/well in 180 μL RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% FBS. Total 20 μL ASOs and/or positive control (ODN2006: 5′-tcgtcgttttgtcgttttgtcgtt-3′ (24-mer), InvivoGen catalog no. tlrl-2006-1) at 10× concentration were added to the cell plate to have final dosing concentrations at 0, 0.03, 0.1, 3, and 10 μM with 1-well PBMCs per ASO per treatment concentration. Cells were treated for 24 h, the cell plate was briefly spun at 200–500 × g, and supernatant was collected for Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) assay on different cytokine releases in the media post-treatment. MSD V-plex proinflammatory panel 1 ELISA assay was performed following vendor protocol (MSD catalog no. K15049D-1; analytes include IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, and TNF-α) using MESO QuickPlex SQ 120MM MSD reader. For the MSD assay using V-plex proinflammatory panel 1, the assay plate was washed three times with 150 μL/well of wash buffer from the kit. A total of 50 μL media supernatant after ASO or control treatments was added to plate in each well, followed by shaking at room temperature for 2 h. The media was aspirated in the plate and the plate washed three times with 150 μL/well of wash buffer. Then, 25 μL detection antibody solution was added to each well. The plate was sealed with adhesive seal and incubated at room temperature with shaking for 2 h. The assay plate was washed three times with 150 μL/well of wash buffer, then 150 μL of 2× Read Buffer T was added to each well, and the plate was immediately read on the MESO QuickPlex SQ 120MM MSD reader.

## RNase H1 cleavage assay
The RNase H1 cleavage assay was based on published protocol. Human RNase H1 (Abcam, AB153634) was diluted to 10 ng/μL (10×) in buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 30% glycerol, and 10 mM DTT at pH 7.4. RNA probe (5′-FAM-UGUCUAAGGGUCAUCUGCU-3′; IDT, catalog no. 438813816) was mixed in equal ratio with different ASOs at 0.33 μM and annealed in reaction buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM DTT at pH 7.4. Either RNase buffer control or RNase H1 protein (1 ng) was added to each duplex solution and then incubated at 37°C for 10 min using a Bio-Rad thermocycler. The reaction was stopped by adding 10 μL stop solution containing 8 M urea and 120 mM EDTA for every 20 μL of reaction mixture. Samples were heated to 95°C for 5 min and separated on 15% tris borate EDTA (TBE) with urea polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 3450093) at 200 V for 60 min. The signal was detected using a LiCor Odyssey M Imager.
