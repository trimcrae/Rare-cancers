## Cotranscriptional
Capping and mRNA Characterization
Following the preparation
of modified cap analogs, we sought to investigate
their incorporation into mRNA via cotranscriptional capping. Accordingly,
DNA templates containing a class III T7 promoter sequence modified
with an AG initiation site were prepared and linearized ( A). As a model system,
capped mRNA encoding Firefly luciferase (FLuc) were prepared cotranscriptionally
using T7 polymerase. Standard IVT conditions were employed with replacement
of uridine for N1-methyl-pseudouridine. In addition, we initially
included all cap analogs at higher concentrations (8 mM) to promote
the formation of capped transcripts. In all cases, IVT proceeded smoothly
under these conditions in the presence of both novel TNA cap analogs,
resulting in full length mRNA as evident by capillary electrophoresis.
While these results were promising, previous reports have suggested
that chemical modification of trinucleotide cap analogs may hinder
their ability to prime IVT reactions resulting in translationally
inactive uncapped transcripts. B). The method utilized a biotin labeled 2′-OMe gapmer
probe complementary to the 5′-end of the mRNA transcript. To
minimize the number of cleavage fragments obtained from digestion
with RNase H, we designed the probe to contain the minimum amount
of DNA in the gap required for activity (4 nucleotides). Once hybridized,
the mRNA was trimmed using E. coli RNase H, captured
with streptavidin coupled magnetic beads and washed to remove unbound
impurities, producing a small RNA fragment as determined by denaturing
polyacrylamide electrophoresis (PAGE, B). Capped fragments were further analyzed
by high resolution HPLC-MS to provide the identity of the mRNA 5′-end
and was used to estimate the relative capping efficiency. In all cases,
cleavage by E. coli RNase H with this probe consistently
resulted in only two fragments detectable by MS regardless of the
identity of the mRNA 5′-end. Moreover, to ensure uncapped transcripts
were detectable, we performed the capping efficiency assay on samples
containing varying levels of spiked-in uncapped mRNA (see Figures S18 and S19 for details). In all cases,
the correct mass corresponding to each respective cap analog was detected
using this method, confirming TNA analogs are capable of priming T7
mediated IVT reactions.

## Evaluation of TNA Cap Analogs
in a Gene Editing System
Given the ability of TNA modified
cap analogs to support cap-dependent
translation of delivered mRNA, we next explored their potential in
therapeutic applications. In recent years, the CRISPR/Cas9 system
has become an exceptionally promising therapeutic modality with the
potential to treat rare genetic disorders via precise
genome editing.
 ). ). Following, indel analysis was performed and, in all cases,
efficient editing of mTtr was observed. Encouragingly,
the editing system equipped with the novel TNA modified cap showed
comparable editing relative to the native Cap 1 system. This outcome
was not fully reflective of the improvements in translation we observed
in the luciferase assays suggesting editing efficiencies may depend
on additional variables beyond Cas9 protein abundance such as nuclear
localization or changes in protein folding. Moreover, editing efficiency
reflects the assembly of functional Cas9-sgRNA complexes which can
be constrained by the stability and availability of the sgRNA thereby
uncoupling translation gains from editing outcomes. This observation
may however be more pronounced in cell culture models and may differ in vivo with optimized chemically modified sgRNAs. Taken
together, these results in addition to the promising features and
properties of this novel TNA modified cap analog support its potential
as a promising candidate for further investigation in mRNA-based therapeutics.
 

## Conclusion
The investigation of
new chemical modalities remains essential
for the further development of mRNA-based therapeutics. In this study,
we successfully synthesized two novel mRNA cap analogs containing
TNA and evaluated their incorporation into several mRNAs. Notably,
the cap analogs were efficiently incorporated cotranscriptionally
using standard IVT conditions with T7 polymerase and displayed comparable
yields relative to the industry standard 5′-cap analog. Moreover,
the mRNA generated was of high quality as evident by capillary electrophoresis
and both cap analogs displayed capping efficiencies ≥ 95%.
Further, we showed that FLuc mRNAs containing TNA modified caps are
efficiently translated in vitro indicating engagement
of cap binding protein eIF4E. Incorporation of TNA at the first transcribed
nucleotide improved translation efficiency relative to the native
Cap 1 structure in all tested systems, while mRNA containing TNA modified
at m7G was translated less efficiently. In vitro decapping
susceptibility of short, capped RNA oligonucleotides demonstrated
that TNA incorporation in the cap scaffold improves stability toward
decapping enzymes. Molecular modeling simulations of caps in complex
with eIF4E suggest TNA modifications are well accommodated into the
binding pocket of eIF4E and no differences in calculated binding energy
were observed between all analogs. Additionally, no differences in
proinflammatory cytokine IL-6 release was observed when compared to
the standard Cap 1 structure in vitro. Lastly, we
show that mRNA encoding SpCas9 containing TNA modified cap m7G-A

TNA

G codelivered with sgRNA
targeting mTtr induced efficient genome editing in
cultured cells. Given the promising characteristics of this novel
cap analog in the synthesis and application of therapeutic mRNA, we
feel further investigation of TNA as well as additional chemical modification
along the cap scaffold is warranted. In addition, the assembly of
TNA modified cap analogs relies on well-established phosphoramidite
chemistry indicating feasibility of large-scale production using current
oligonucleotide manufacturing platforms. We hope the work presented
here contributes to the expanding repertoire of modified cap analogs
for mRNA-based applications and enhances our understanding of the
positional tolerance of chemical modifications within the cap structure.

## Solid-Phase
Synthesis of pAG Dinucleotides
All dinucleotides
were synthesized on an ÄKTA oligosynt (Cytiva) using standard solid-phase synthesis reagents (See Supporting Information for solid-phase scheme). In all cases,
UnyLinker loaded solid support (394 μmol/g) was used. Removal
of 4,4′-dimethoxytrityl protecting groups and coupling of nucleoside
phosphoramidites was achieved with 15% dichloroacetic acid/toluene
and 4,5-dicyanoimidazole (1 M in acetonitrile) respectively. To generate
phosphodiester linkages, I2 oxidation (0.02 M in pyridine/water)
was used. Standard 2′-OMe and RNA (TBDMS-protected) phosphoramidites
were employed and chemical 5′-phosphorylation was achieved
using commercially available bis­(2-cyanoethyl)-N,N-diisopropyl-phosphoramidite
(Ambeed). All phosphoramidites were dissolved in anhydrous acetonitrile
at a concentration of 0.15 M and coupled for a minimum of 8 min. Deprotection
was performed with ammonium hydroxide (20 mL, 28% aqueous solution)
for a minimum of 12 h at 55 °C. Removal of the TBS protecting
groups consisted of treatment with Et3N · 3HF/DMSO (0.5 mL/2
mL) at 65 °C for 1 h followed by precipitation from cold ethanol.
Crude dinucleotides were dissolved in water and purified using WAX
chromatography (0–0.8 M TEAB, pH 8). Fractions containing pure
fractions were concentrated in vacuo and further purified using RP
chromatography (0–50% ACN) to yield after lyophilization the
dinucleotide as a triethylammonium salt. The identity of the modified
dinucleotide was confirmed by LCMS.

## General Procedure for Cap Analog Synthesis
To a solution
of N7-methylated diphosphate in 10% H2O/DMSO
(100 mg/mL) was added N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide
hydrochloride (EDC-HCl) and imidazole. The reaction was stirred at
room temperature for 5 h. To the resulting reaction mixture was added
MnCl2 (3.0 M solution in water) followed immediately by
the appropriate pAG dinucleotide. The reaction was stirred
overnight at room temperature. The following day, the solution was
quenched with EDTA (0.5 M solution in water) and diluted with water
(20 mL). The resulting solution was brought to pH 6 and purified using
WAX chromatography (0–0.8 M TEAB, pH 8). Fractions containing
pure fractions were concentrated in vacuo and further purified using
RP chromatography (0–50% ACN).

## 
In Vitro Transcription
Chemically
modified cap analogs were incorporated into mRNA by in vitro transcription. Linearized DNA plasmids containing a class III T7
promoter served as templates for transcription (See Supporting Information for sequences). Linearization was performed
using BspQ1 (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s
protocol and purified using DNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo
Research). In vitro transcription was performed using
HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs) with
only minor changes to the recommended protocol. Typical reactions
contained 1 μg of template, NTPs (5 mM), cap analog (8 mM or
4 mM), DTT (5 mM), HiScribe T7 mix (2 μL) in a final volume
of 20 μL. All mRNA were synthesized with complete substitution
of uridine for N1-Me-pseudouridine. Reactions were incubated at 37
°C for 2 h and template DNA was digested using TURBO DNase (2
μL, Invitrogen, RNase-free) at 37 °C for 15 min. Reactions
were diluted to 50 μL with 1 mM EDTA, treated with LiCl (7.5M,
25 μL) and placed at −20 °C for 1 h. The resulting
precipitate was collected by centrifugation and washed with cold 70%
ethanol (500 μL). Reactions were scaled linearly as needed.
Resulting mRNAs were dissolved in 1 mM EDTA and purified by IP-RP-HPLC
on an Agilent 1260 Infinity III system equipped with a COSMOSIL RNA-RP1
column (4.6 mm x 100 mM, Nacalai USA) at 65 °C. Full length mRNA
was eluted from the column with a gradient of 2.5–14% B over
25 min (Buffer A: 100 mM TEAA, pH 7. Buffer B: acetonitrile). Fractions
containing desired mRNA were first concentrated using Pierce protein
concentrators (100 K MWCO, ThermoFisher Scientific), then precipitated
from NaOAc and ethanol. The obtained RNA transcripts were quantified
by UV 260 nm absorbance and full-length integrity was assessed using
automated electrophoresis (RNA ScreenTape, 4200 TapeStation, Agilent
Technologies).

## Capping Efficiency Assay
Full length
mRNA isolated
from IVT reactions were briefly annealed to a biotin labeled 2′-O-Me
gapmer probe and trimmed using E. coli RNase H (see Supporting Information for sequence). Cleavage
reactions were performed on 2.5 μg of mRNA in a reaction containing
gapmer probe (25 pmol), murine RNase inhibitor (5 U, New England Biolabs),
and E. coli RNase H (5 U, New England Biolabs) at
a final volume of 50 μL buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 75 mM KCl, 3
mM, MgCl2, 10 mM DTT, pH 8.3). The cleavage was allowed
to proceed for 2 h at 37 °C, quenched by the addition of 0.5
M EDTA (1 μL) and diluted to 50 μL with binding buffer
(10 mM Tris, 1000 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA). Cleaved fragments were isolated
using streptavidin magnetic beads (S1421, New England Biolabs) with
small modification to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly,
magnetic beads (20 μL) were washed twice with 100 mM EDTA then
once more with binding buffer. Beads were suspended in fresh binding
buffer (50 μL), added to the cleaved sample and mixed with gentle
shaking for 10 min at 25 °C. Captured fragments were washed three
times with binding buffer and eluted using RNase-free water (50 μL)
by heating at 80 °C for 10 min. To verify cleavage and capture
occurred, denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was
performed. Samples were dissolved in formamide, loaded onto the gel
(Novex TBE Gels, 15%, Invitrogen) and ran for 2 h at 180 V. Once complete,
gels were washed, stained with SYBR gold (Invitrogen) and imaged using
a Gel Doc XR+ imaging system (Bio-Rad).
High-pressure liquid
chromatography mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) analysis of cleaved 5′-end
mRNA fragments was performed on a Vanquish Flex HPLC instrument connected
to an Exploris 240 Orbitrap mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific).
Chromatographic separation was established on a Waters ACQUITY Premier
Oligonucleotide C18 column (130Å, 1.7 μm 2.1 × 100
mm column). RNA fragments were eluted from the column by first holding
at 5% B for 1 min followed by a linear gradient to 35% over 6 min
at a flow rate of 350 μL/min, followed by a 2 min buffer B wash
and equilibration of the column. Buffer A: 3 mM dimethylcyclohexylamine,
15 mM hexafluoro-2-methyl-2-propanol in water. Buffer B: 3 mM dimethylcyclohexylamine,
15 mM hexafluoro-2-methyl-2-propanol in acetonitrile. Mass spectra
of the intact RNA fragments were collected in the scan range of 500
to 1500 m/z utilizing a spray voltage
of −2700 V at a resolution of 125,000. Spectra were analyzed
using BioPharma Finder (Thermo Fisher Scientific) determining the
relative capping efficiencies by summing the peak intensities of capped
fragments and dividing them by total RNA species (capped and uncapped
fragments) (See Figures S18–S20 for
MS spectra).

## 
In Vitro Translation in
HeLa Cell Extract

In vitro translation was
performed using the 1-Step
Human High-Yield IVT Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) with minor changes
to the recommended protocol. Briefly, to a reaction containing HeLa
lysate (12.5 μL), accessory proteins (2.5 μL) and reaction
mix (5 μL) was added 50 ng of freshly prepared FLuc mRNA in
a total reaction volume of 50 μL at 30 °C. At each time
point, a 2 μL aliquot was added to a detection buffer containing
Dual-Glo Luciferase reagent (25 μL) and water (23 μL).
Samples were shaken for 5 min in the dark, and firefly luminescence
was read on a Spark multimode reader (Tecan).

## mRNA Expression in Cultured
Cells
Luciferase expression
of in vitro transcribed mRNA was evaluated in cultured
HepG2, and AML12 cells (see Supporting Information for cell culture details). Prior to transfection, cells were seeded
in Nunc MicroWell 96-Well, Nunclon Delta-Treated, flat-bottom microplates
(ThermoFisher Scientific) at densities of 18,000 cells/well (HepG2)
and 16,000 cells/well (AML12) in 100 μL of medium and allowed
to attach for 2–6 h prior to transfection. RNA transfection
was achieved using a custom ionizable lipid complex (ILC) comprising
40% ALC-0315, 38.5% cholesterol, 21% DOPE, and 0.5% DMG-PEG 2000,
formulated in 50 mM acetate buffer at an N/P ratio of 6. Each well
received a total of 50 ng of total mRNA containing a mixture a FLuc
and RLuc in a ratio of 4:1 respectively. At each time point, luminescence
was measured using Dual-Glo Luciferase assay system (Promega) according
to manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, an equal volume of Dual-Glo
Luciferase reagent was added to each well, plates were shaken for
10 min in the dark and Firefly luminescence was read on a Spark multimode
reader (Tecan). Following, an equal volume of Dual-Glo Stop &
Glo Reagent was added to each well and plates were shaken once again
for 10 min prior to measurement of Renilla luminescence. Background
values for nontransfected controls were subtracted from sample wells
and FLuc/RLuc ratios were calculated.

## Decapping Assay
Decapping experiments were conducted
on short, capped RNA oligonucleotides generated by IVT using a sequence
corresponding to the first 35 nucleotides of evaluated mRNAs in this
study (see Figure S21). Capped RNAs were
isolated directly from the IVT reaction using Monarch Spin RNA Cleanup
Kits (500 μg, New England Biolabs). Isolated RNAs were purified
by IP-RP chromatography on an Agilent 1260 Infinity III system equipped
with an Agilent PLRP-S column (1000 Å, 7.5 mm × 150 mm)
at 55 °C. Full length RNA was eluted from the column with a gradient
of 50% B over 30 min (Buffer A: 100 mM TEAA, pH 7. Buffer B: acetonitrile).
Fractions were concentrated on a SpeedVac vacuum concentrator and
precipitated twice from sodium acetate in ethanol. For decapping experiments,
capped RNAs (60 pmol) were dissolved in 120 μL 1X reaction buffer
(50 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NH4Cl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1
mM DTT, 0.1% Poloxamer 188, pH 7.5) and placed at 37 °C for 10
min prior to the addition of mRNA Decapping Enzyme (0.1 U, New England
Biolabs). At each time point, an aliquot (20 μL) was removed
and mixed with an equal volume of 100 mM EDTA in formamide prior to
freezing on dry ice. After removal of the final aliquot, all samples
were thawed, heated at 80 °C for 10 min and analyzed by denaturing
PAGE (Novex TBE Gels, 15%, Invitrogen). Gels were run for 2 h at 180
V, stained and imaged as described above. Gel images were analyzed
using ImageJ software.

## mTTR Editing and Indel Analysis in Cultured
AML12 Cells
Capped SpCas9 and GLuc mRNAs were prepared by
IVT and purified by
IP-RP-HPLC as described above. A previously described highly modified
single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting mTtr was purchased
from BioSpring (see Supporting Information for sequence). For transfection, cells
were seeded in a Falcon 96-well Clear Flat Bottom TC-treated Culture
Microplate (Corning) at a density of 18,000 cells/well in 100 μL
of medium and allowed to attach for 2 h prior to transfection. Transfection
of RNA was achieved using a custom ILC in a ratio of 1:2 by weight
(mRNA:gRNA) at a N/P ratio of 6. mRNA encoding GLuc was included in
all samples at a 3:1 SpCas9:GLuc ratio to serve as a transfection
control. Following a 24-h incubation, the transfection medium was
replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium. Genomic DNA was harvested
72 h post-transfection from 96-well plates for downstream analysis.
Edited cells were harvested and treated with DirectPCR Lysis Reagent
(Viagen Biotech) containing Proteinase K (QIAGEN) to lyse the cells
(10 μg/well), followed by thermal treatment at 50–55
°C for 45 min and 95 °C for 15 min. Locus-specific PCR (PCR1)
was performed using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher
Scientific) with primers appended with MiSeq adapter sequences. The
PCR1 products were then used as templates for indexing PCR (PCR2).
Equal volumes of PCR2 products were pooled and purified with AMPure
XP beads (Beckman Coulter). The library was then sequenced on a MiSeq
(Illumina) platform at 1 × 300 bp with a depth of at least 1,000
reads per sample. The sequencing reads were analyzed using CRISPResso2
to quantify the levels of indels.
