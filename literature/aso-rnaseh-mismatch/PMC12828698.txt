## Abstract
Antimicrobial resistance poses a pressing global health challenge in the 21st century. The rapid increase and prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria will require novel approaches to develop new antibiotics. Major advances in nucleic acid-based therapeutics, particularly antisense technologies, could be one solution for developing precision antibiotics. The selectivity and specificity in the drug design of antibacterial antisense oligomers (ASOs) allows precise gene-specific silencing and ultimately enables targeting of currently undruggable gene products. Our goal here is to comprehensively review the advances in asobiotics (antisense oligomer biotics) leading to therapeutic success, including modifications in the nucleic acid backbone of ASOs, which have improved their properties and progresses in delivery. We will discuss utilization of ASOs against several pathogens, strategies to overcome resistance, and finally future scenarios and prospects for asobiotics as pathogen-specific therapy in the clinic.
Keywords: antisense, asobiotics, antibiotics, resistance, uptake, off-target effects

An overview highlighting the present successes utilizing ASOs as a potential therapeutic to combat several medically important bacterial pathogens and the future scenarios for their application in the clinic.

## Introduction
The global rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) endangers many achievements of modern medicine. In 2021, nearly 7·7 million deaths were attributed to bacterial infections, of which 4.95 million were associated with drug-resistant pathogens; this number is projected to increase in the future. This has created an urgent need for new antibiotics featuring novel chemical scaffolds and alternative mechanisms of action that can withstand AMR (Okeke et al. ). Nucleic acid-based therapeutics, a major drug class owing to their tremendous clinical success and recent regulatory approvals (Egli and Manoharan , Sparmann and Vogel ), can be harnessed as antibacterial therapeutics. Specifically, silencing of essential bacterial genes by antisense oligomers (ASOs), represent a novel and innovative approach to develop new antibacterials. The rapid expansion of sequenced microbial genomes provides a wealth of information that can be exploited to study pathogenesis and identify novel drug targets (Abavisani et al. ). ASOs capitalize on this genomic insight by selectively inhibiting target genes in a sequence-specific manner. Although antisense strategies for inhibiting bacterial growth have been explored for many years, success had been hampered by the poor membrane permeability of the ASOs. However, advances in ASO chemistry have ushered in a new era for the therapeutic use of these molecules.
Chemically, ASOs are synthetic, single stranded oligomers that base pair with RNA using Watson and Crick rules. They are generally short (about 10–15 bases in length) and are composed of modified RNA or DNA bases that confer nucleases stability. An antisense therapeutic is synthesized with a specific base sequence, which enables it to bind a specific mRNA with a complementary sequence in the bacterium of interest (Geller ).
Early ASO studies utilized synthetic deoxyribonucleotides, a chemically unmodified oligonucleotide with a phosphoribose-backbone that resulted in these compounds being susceptible to degradation by nucleases and limited their therapeutic potential (Hofman and Corey ). To improve their properties and overcome these shortcomings, ASO synthesis increasingly incorporated a wide range of chemical modifications. These modifications provided the ability to customize ASOs for different clinical applications. Out of the numerous possibilities of different nucleic acid mimics that could be used, only two have been extensively developed for use against bacteria: peptide nucleic acids (PNAs) and phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers (PMOs) (Egholm et al. , Quemener et al. ). ASOs based on PNA or PMO cannot penetrate the bacterial envelope. Thus, they are reliant on carriers such as cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) for entry into the bacteria cytosol, their site of action. Despite early success in proof-of-concept studies, antimicrobial ASOs have been largely confined to laboratory use. Recently, there has been a resurgence of interest in these compounds, with promising preclinical data including innovations in delivery agents, development of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) pipelines to aid in target identification and applications beyond antibacterial efficacy.
This review highlights the recent advances in antibacterial antisense technologies and how these asobiotics (antisense oligonucleotide biotics; Vogel et al. ) could be used to treat bacterial infections in humans. We begin by outlining innovations in ASO chemistry and delivery strategies, then discuss their gene-silencing activity across diverse bacterial species. We also address key challenges, including off-target effects, toxicity, and resistance development, before highlighting emerging applications beyond antibacterial efficacy. Finally, we share our perspectives on the future potential of this promising technology.

## Chemistry and design of antibacterial ASOs

## PNAs and PMOs are the most commonly used ASO modalities
Given the liabilities of earlier chemistries that limited their therapeutic potential, ASO synthesis increasingly incorporated a wide range of chemical modifications.
PNAs are pseudopeptides in which the ribose-phosphate backbone is replaced by a polyamide backbone consisting of repeats of N-(2-aminoethyl) glycine units with acetyl linkers to the nucleobases (Nielsen ). This structure forms a hydrophilic, uncharged, flexible oligomer with a geometry that enables complementary base pairing with double-stranded DNA or single stranded RNA (Fig.  ). PNAs were first conceived as sequence-specific, DNA-binding molecules for the purpose of inhibiting transcription in eukaryotic cells by sterically inhibiting RNA polymerase and other transcription factors (Larsen and Nielsen ). In bacteria, PNAs have been shown to inhibit translation if they are targeted to either rRNA or mRNA. Their mechanism of action is sequence-specific, and sterically interferes with ribosome function, by blocking assembly, initiation, or translocation of the ribosome past the initiation site (Good and Nielsen ). Unlike some other antisense technologies, such as phosphorothioates, PNAs inhibit bacterial translation in a RNase H-independent manner (Good and Nielsen , Goltermann et al. ). Although PNAs have a molecular weight (~3000), which far exceeds the nominal cut-off size for diffusion of soluble solutes across the porins of Gram-negative bacteria (Nikaido ), they nevertheless are able to enter into the bacterial cell. It has been suggested that the linear architecture of the PNA enables some entry through porins, albeit to a limited extent (Good and Nielsen ). Evidence that the antisense effect of PNAs is limited by the outer membrane (OM) barrier was shown in a number of ways. For example, PNA inhibition of target sequences is significantly more efficient in cell-free translation reactions than in vivo. In addition, Escherichia coli mutants that have a “leaky” OM are significantly more susceptible to antisense inhibition than E. coli with intact OMs (Good et al. ). Several strategies were explored to improve PNA membrane penetration and cellular uptake in order to increase their clinical applications including conjugation to CPPs (both natural and synthetically derived), conjugation to small molecules and vitamin B12, liposomal encapsulation, nanoparticle encapsulation, and association with tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDN) (MacLelland et al. , Siekierska et al. ).
 PMOs are single-stranded mimics of RNA in which the ribose is replaced by a six-atom morpholine ring, and the phosphodiester bond is replaced by a phosphorodiamidate linkage. The phosphate linkage between the morpholine rings is substituted with a dimethylamine for one of the oxygen atoms, which renders the PMO uncharged (Fig.  ). The neutral character reduces charge repulsion with RNA, and this enables the PMO to bind very tightly to its complementary RNA. These structural changes also make the PMO completely resistant to RNases and DNases, while retaining the steric architecture that enables binding to complementary RNA (Hudziak et al. ). PMOs do not bind to double stranded DNA (Summerton and Weller , Summerton ), and thus cannot interfere with transcription. The first utilization of a PMO as an antibacterial antibiotic was achieved by Geller et al. ( ), where a PMO was able to inhibit bacterial growth of an E. coli strain with a leaky OM.

## Designing asobiotics: target selection, design, and specificity 
ASOs are devised to modulate gene expression or translation by specifically binding to their cognate mRNA. The PNA and PMO mechanism of action is sequence-specific, and sterically interferes with ribosome function, by blocking assembly, initiation, or translocation of the ribosome past the initiation site (Good and Nielsen , ). In this context, the translation of target protein is inhibited due to the formation of an ASO/RNA heteroduplex, which blocks recruitment of 30S ribosomes to the mRNA (Fig.  ). Therefore, these ASOs are designed to bind to regions of regulatory sequences in the mRNA, such as the start codon or the Shine–Dalgarno (SD) sequence (the two most important elements of the ribosome-binding site), where initiation of translation occurs (Good and Nielsen , Good , Greenberg et al. , Geller et al. ).
 The availability of whole-genome sequences has facilitated the rapid design and development of ASOs that target essential genes, sensitize drug-resistant strains, or block expression of key virulence factors across Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria (Ayhan et al. , Sully et al. ). Importantly, algorithms have been developed to identify the most sensitive targets, such as the minimum transcript level (Goh et al. ), selection of gene targets from classes known to have synergistic inhibition (Good and Stach ), or the abundance or turnover rate of the targeted mRNA (Pedersen et al. , Hagedorn et al. ). Related to turnover rate, an RNA-seq based comparison of close to a dozen E. coli mRNAs that encode functionally diverse proteins has shown that ASO-induced target mRNA decay is not a universal phenomenon in antisense targeting of essential genes; and even where it occurs, it does not predict whether a given sequence would be a good target in terms of bacterial killing (Popella et al. ). Moreover, RNA-seq analysis of close to 70 phage genes showed induced mRNA decay to be the exception to the rule in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Gerovac et al. ). Generally, how ASOs induce target mRNA degradation, which nucleases are involved, and whether or not such induced mRNA decay increases the potency of an ASO of interest, remain questions that warrant more systematic investigation. At any rate, the seemingly obvious assumption that low-abundance mRNAs are better targets than high-abundance mRNAs, is easily invalidated by the fact that the most popular ASO target to date—the acpP mRNA—is one of the most highly expressed mRNAs in bacteria (Popella et al. , ).
We are unaware of any method that has been extensively tested on an essential genome to correctly and consistently identify the optimal targets across species. Recently, an unbiased screen of all essential genes in P. aeruginosa has been performed using peptide-PMOs to identify new gene targets (Greenberg laboratory, unpublished data). In a similar undertaking, close to 50% of all predicted essential genes in pathogenic and nonpathogenic E. coli have been targeted to better understand which features of antisense PNAs and their targets are conducive to effective killing (Vogel and Barquist laboratories, unpublished data). Such unbiased screens will be needed across bacterial space to help determine rules for optimal gene target selection. Early studies with PNAs and PMOs focused on identifying optimal positioning of the oligo for a given target gene. For both chemistries, the optimal positions were identified on or near the SD sequence and the region around the start codon (Dryselius et al. ); targeting sequences downstream of the start codon were shown to be ineffective (Goh et al. ). Even in this region of a particular gene, slight changes in positioning of the oligomer can have a substantial impact on in vitro activity and it remains poorly understood what the optimal position is for an antisense oligomer (Good and Nielsen , Greenberg et al. , Howard et al. , Sapkota et al. ). One important parameter that can be optimized is the number of bases in the oligomers. Both PNAs and PMOs reach a maximal inhibition of target expression at 9–12 bases in length (Good et al. , Goltermann et al. , Popella et al. ).
Computer programs such as MASON (Make Antisense Oligomers Now) and FAST (facile accelerated specific therapeutic platform) are available for designing a PNA or PMO against essential or nonessential genes of a particular bacterium (Eller et al. , Jung et al. ). MASON is available on a web server that allows the identification and analysis of off-target matches. Thorough analysis of off-target matches that target the ribosome-binding site (RBS) is very critical. Similarly, the FAST platform allows the design of PNAs that target nonessential genes. Furthermore, the FAST platform performs in situ synthesis, validation, and efficacy testing of selected PNAs. Both MASON and FAST are great tools to analyse off-target effects for selected PNA or other ASOs in antisense research.
Increasing the length beyond about 11 bases reduces the potency as measured by the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) in vitro. Importantly, the target site should be examined for uniqueness within the bacterial genome (Good ). In our own practice, we aim to design unique sequence-specific oligos in a particular bacterial pathogen that are not present across other species to make species-specific antisense PMOs (Greenberg laboratory designs). ASOs could target other unintended RNA (off target effects) that could be problematic. Proper antisense controls may validate the true antisense effect. For instance, use of scramble sequences that are similar but with some mismatches are an important control; even better are control ASOs with one or two mismatches in the central binding region. Alternatively, the target site could be altered, whenever possible (Good ). Furthermore, RNA-seq has been a great tool to investigate PNA specificity, off-target effects, and efficiency in antisense targeting by looking at the effects on global transcripts (Popella et al. ), although RNA-seq is limited by the fact that not all ASOs reduce the levels of their target mRNAs upon successful binding (see above). Since ASOs are easy to design, rapid to synthesize, changes in DNA sequences causing resistance could be retargeted with new antisense sequences. New chemistry schemes enable a low-cost synthesis of many ASOs in parallel, which could then be used to rapidly identify the most effective ASOs (sequence and length) for an mRNA of interest, as demonstrated with several different targets in E. coli (Danti et al. ). For these reasons, ASOs could be innovative therapeutics for the management of antibacterial-resistant pathogens.

## Uptake and delivery of asobiotics

## Overcoming barriers: the importance of delivery vehicles of antibacterial ASOs
The main challenge in harnessing ASOs as antibacterial agents is in achieving their efficient delivery into the bacterial cytoplasm (Vogel , Ghosh et al. ). Unlike traditional antibiotics, which are typically smaller and can more easily penetrate bacterial cell walls, ASOs are hindered by their larger size and negative charge. In Gram-negative bacteria, ASOs must traverse both an outer and an inner membrane to reach the cytosol. Gram-positive bacteria present a different challenge, where the thick peptidoglycan layer acts as an additional barrier beyond the cell membrane that ASOs need to overcome. Moreover, the negatively charged surface of both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria repels anionic ASOs through electrostatic interactions. Together, these physical and electrochemical barriers significantly limit ASO access to intracellular targets (Vogel ). An ideal vehicle for antibacterial ASOs should be stable, species-selective, highly efficient at cytosolic delivery, impervious to resistance development, nontoxic to both microbiome and host, and practical for clinical use. To address this, a variety of delivery systems have been developed to facilitate ASO uptake across bacterial cell walls and membranes to enhance intracellular delivery, target engagement and therapeutic efficacy (Fig.  ). The following sections highlight the most promising delivery strategies (Table  ), excluding isolated reports, which have been covered in other reviews (Moreira et al. , Pals et al. ).
 

## CPPs remain central to antibacterial ASO delivery
CPPs are short amino acid sequences, typically fewer than 30 residues in length and rich in positively charged residues, which facilitate the delivery of various molecular cargo into cells (Gori et al. ). Many CPPs are derived from protein transduction domains, such as the well-known peptide from the trans-activating transcriptional activator (Tat) protein of HIV-1 (Brooks et al. ), which have been widely used to transport a range of therapeutic and diagnostic molecules across the membranes of diverse mammalian cell types (Rizzuti et al. ). A subset of this group of peptides has been shown to transport ASOs into bacterial cells as exemplified by two CPPs: (KFF)₃K and (RXR)4XB (where X is 6-aminohexanoic acid and B is β-alanine) (Ghosal and Nielsen , Sully and Geller , Popella et al. , Schafer et al. , Tsylents et al. ). While the precise mechanisms of CPP-mediated delivery of ASOs across bacterial cells remain incompletely understood, they are generally thought to involve interactions with membrane lipids and structural components of the bacterial cell wall. In addition to their positive charge, many bacteria-specific CPPs contain hydrophobic residues that promote membrane insertion and facilitate cellular entry (Wojciechowska et al. , Tsylents et al. ). Their delivery efficiency is influenced by several variables, including the size and charge of the ASO, concentration, bacterial species/strains, and environmental conditions such as media, temperature, and aggregation properties. Given that most CPPs are cationic in nature, they are only efficient in delivering charge neutral ASOs, e.g. PNA and PMOs (Ghosh et al. ).
Among the most studied CPPs in bacteria, is the (KFF)₃K peptide. PNAs conjugated to (KFF)₃K have demonstrated antimicrobial activity (MIC: 0.2–12.5 μM) against a range of bacteria including Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens. A comparative study involving common CPPs like Tat and the (RXR)4XB peptide revealed that the (KFF)₃K peptide had a considerably smaller impact on the bacterial transcriptome (Popella et al. ). Even in mouse models of E. coli infection, treatment with PNAs delivered by (KFF)₃K showed improved survival rates (Tan et al. , Bai et al. ). However, susceptibility to proteases, resistance due to sbmA mutations and hemolytic activity at 40 µg/ml limit its therapeutic potential. To enhance CPP efficacy, researchers have explored the use of nonnatural amino acids and arginine-rich peptides such as (RFF)₃KXB, (RFR)4XB, (RX)6B, and (RXR)4XB (Tilley et al. , Mellbye et al. ). The (RXR)4XB peptide, in particular, has been shown to have broad-spectrum activity against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, Listeria monocytogenes, Acinetobacter spp., and Streptococcus pneumoniae, but its performance varies against S. pyogenes, members of Burkholderia cepacia complex (Bcc), and Bacillus anthracis. Studies indicate that arginine (R) is more effective than lysine (K) in bacterial permeabilization (Sturge et al. , Moustafa et al. , Barkowsky et al. , El-Fateh et al. ). In one study, it was demonstrated that two distinct arginine-based peptides were required to effectively treat Gram-negative coinfections (Bcc and P. aeruginosa) in vitro (Daly et al. ), underscoring the importance of peptide design in optimizing delivery across different bacterial species. There are also fascinating cases of high selectivity provided by the CPP: in P. aeruginosa, PNAs can be delivered very effectively by both the RXR and Tat CPPs, while no target activity at all is observed when KFF is used as a carrier (Gerovac et al. ).
Although isolated studies have explored the possibility of different CPPs for use in delivery in bacteria, the field lacks a study that systematically evaluates different CPPs; there is a need to develop toolkits that can assess for CPP delivery efficiency beyond antibacterial efficacy as antibacterial assays often overlook possible toxicity of the CPP to the bacteria. Moreover, CPP-PNA conjugates can also show sequence-independent antibacterial efficacy, as observed against Fusobacterium nucleatum (Cosi et al. ) where a nontargeting (RXR)4XB-PNA conjugate (FUS79) displayed potent antimicrobial activity against fusobacterial strains. This was an effect that seemed to be caused by the combination of the (RXR)4XB peptide and certain sequence elements of the conjugated ASO, rather than antisense inhibition of mRNA. To address this, a semiquantitative “switch-on” reporter assay to assess ASO uptake efficiency has been recently developed (Sarkar et al. ). This assay utilizes a synthetic RNA toehold switch linked to a fluorescent reporter protein mRNA that gets activated by a PNA-based ASO. Upon delivery into the bacterial cytosol by a CPP, the reporter gets activated in sequence specific binding of the PNA. Using this assay, various CPPs were screened as ASO delivery agents in E. coli and Salmonella enterica, observing up to a 60-fold activation depending on the CPP and bacterial strain. The high dynamic range and sensitivity of this assay makes it suitable for high-throughput screening of bacterial ASO carriers (Sarkar et al. ).
A key limitation of CPPs as ASO carriers is their potential toxicity and stability. Toxicity profiles vary among CPPs: for instance, (KFF)₃K-PNA exhibits cytotoxicity in A549 cells at concentrations above 5 μM, whereas (RFF)₃XB-PMO remains nontoxic up to 10 μM in Caco-2 cells (Tilley et al. , Mellbye et al. , Martinez-Guitian et al. ). Similarly, low toxicity was observed for certain CPP conjugates at concentrations up to 64 μM in human cystic fibrosis (CF) bronchial epithelial cells (Daly et al. ). In vivo, (RXR)4XB-PMO was well tolerated in animal models at intravenous doses up to 15 mg/kg (Amantana et al. , Geller et al. , Sturge et al. ), further supporting its favorable safety profile.
Another key challenge with CPPs is their susceptibility to proteolytic degradation. For example, it has been shown that loss of antibacterial activity was correlated with increasing (KFF)₃K fragment production due to proteolysis (Yavari et al. ). To address this general problem with stability, researchers have explored several strategies. Employing d-amino acid isomers, which retain the peptide’s activity while boosting resistance to degradation in serum, is one approach (Yavari et al. ). Additionally, modifications to peptide design, such as stapling or incorporating branched cationic structures (Fig.  ), have been explored to enhance stability against proteolytic enzymes (Iubatti et al. , Siekierska et al. , Lobka et al. ). Cationic dendrons, such as (Gbu)8-DAB (where Gbu: guanidinobutanoyl and DAB: diaminobutanoic acid dendron), resembling branched cationic CPPs, have been shown to effectively deliver PNA-based ASOs into E. coli and Klebiella pneumoniae (Iubatti et al. ). The conjugates also exhibited potent antibacterial activity against multidrug-resistant E. coli in a murine peritonitis model. Chen et al. developed dendritic polypeptide nanoparticles (DPPs) to deliver 2′-O-methyl (2′-OMe) modified ASOs into multidrug-resistant E. coli (Chen et al. ). Specifically, DPPs containing 8–10 positive charges, and two histidine residues (DP-AD7 and DP-AD8) effectively delivered ASOs, inhibiting bacterial growth and gene expression in vitro. DP-AD7, targeting acpP, significantly improved survival in septic mice infected with extended spectrum beta-lactamases producing (ESBL)-E. coli, demonstrating the potential of DPPs as ASO carriers for combating multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infections (Chen et al. ). In a follow-up study, the same group successfully optimized a dendritic polypeptide to deliver phosphorothioate-modified ASOs targeting ftsZ in Staphylococcus aureus (Li et al. ). Recently, the group of Trylska explored derivatives of anoplin and (KFF)3 K, including their stapled derivatives (e.g. (KFF)3K[2–6]-S-S-), as PNA carriers (Siekierska et al. ). They found that hydrocarbon stapling did not improve PNA delivery, and disulfide linkers reduced antimicrobial activity. However, anoplin conjugated to PNA via an ethylene glycol linker (eg1) effectively delivered PNA, targeting bacterial mRNA, and showed antibacterial activity against E. coli and Salmonella typhimurium at 2–4 μM, identifying anoplin as a potent PNA carrier. In a follow-up study, hydrocarbon-stapled lysine- and leucine-rich peptides as PNA carriers were investigated, and demonstrated that stapling at i and i + 4 positions enhanced peptide stability, antibacterial efficacy against resistant bacteria (MICs 2–4 μM), and resistance to enzymatic degradation, without hemolytic or cytotoxic effects at MICs (Lobka et al. ). Molecular dynamics simulations predicted increased membrane insertion and structural stability due to stapling, suggesting this strategy as a promising approach for developing stable and effective antibacterial ASO carriers.
Overall, CPPs are still the gold standard for delivering ASOs across bacterial membranes, but face hurdles in efficacy, resistance, and stability. Structural modifications, including d-peptides staples and branched CPPs, improve stability and uptake. However, challenges such as serum interference, resistance development, species-specific efficacy, and potential toxicity need addressing. Future directions should focus on targeted delivery, resistance mitigation, and stability optimization to advance CPP-mediated ASO therapeutics against resistant bacteria.

## Exploiting bacterial nutrient uptake pathways for targeted ASO delivery
Alternative delivery mechanisms exploiting bacterial nutritional requirements for ASO delivery leverage endogenous uptake systems for essential nutrients like iron or vitamins. Two prominent examples are siderophores and vitamin B₁₂, which hijack bacterial transport pathways for targeted delivery (Fig.  ).
Iron is essential for bacterial growth, survival, and virulence. To acquire it, many bacteria secrete siderophores, high-affinity iron-chelating molecules, that upon iron-binding are internalized. One key pathway involves TonB-dependent outer membrane transporters (TBDTs), which import ferric siderophore complexes into the periplasm (Schalk ). This natural transport system has been harnessed for a “Trojan horse” strategy, where siderophores serve as carrier moieties to smuggle antibiotics into bacterial cells. The clinical success of cefiderocol (Fetroja™), a siderophore-conjugated cephalosporin, validates the potential of this approach (Katsube et al. ). Recently, siderophores have gained attention as a targeted delivery strategy for antibacterial ASOs. This approach also takes advantage of TBDTs to shuttle ASOs across bacterial membranes, offering specificity for bacteria and minimizing off-target effects on human cells.
In one of two pioneering studies, the Velema lab utilized siderophore mimics, tris-catechol and mono-catechol, to deliver ASOs across bacterial cells. Specifically, siderophores conjugated to PNAs and PMOs targeting acpP inhibited the growth of E. coli and A. baumannii, at low micromolar concentrations (Pals et al. ). Siderophore-PNAs targeting the lacZ transcript led to a dose-dependent reduction in β-galactosidase production, confirming selective downregulation of the target protein. The authors also performed whole-genome sequencing of siderophore-PNA-resistant bacteria, to confirm that uptake occurred specifically through the siderophore-mediated iron transport pathway. Importantly, the conjugates showed no toxicity toward mammalian cells. In the other study, the Trylska lab developed linear and cyclic trihydroxamate-based-siderophore-PNA conjugates where the oligomers were designed to target the mRNA transcript and silence the expression of the red fluorescent protein (RFP) reporter gene in E. coli (Tsylents et al. ). Flow cytometry experiments demonstrated that these trihydroxamate-based siderophores successfully transported PNA oligomers into E. coli cells as revealed by the reduction of RFP expression.
Although initial results with siderophores as antibacterial ASO carriers are promising, several key questions remain to be addressed. These include validation in animal models of infection, comparing the delivery efficiency of different siderophore chemistries (e.g. catechol versus hydroxamate siderophores), elucidating their mechanisms of inner membrane translocation, quantifying the extent of target gene silencing, analyzing global transcriptional changes, and identifying the range of bacterial species susceptible to this approach. Major challenges for siderophore-mediated ASO delivery include resistance development through receptor loss or mutation, variability in siderophore uptake systems across strains, limited cytosolic delivery, and the synthetic complexity of designing stable, functional conjugates.
Despite this, siderophore-mediated ASO delivery represents a significant step forward, elegantly combining the targeted action of antisense therapeutics with the inherent bacterial mechanisms for iron uptake. However, it is still considerably distant from supplanting the established role and broad applicability of CPPs in ASO delivery.
Like siderophores, vitamin B₁₂, a nutrient actively imported by many bacteria, has also been explored as a carrier for delivering antibacterial ASOs (Giedyk et al. , Rownicki et al. , Pienko et al. ). Bacteria utilize the BtuB receptor for uptake of vitamin B12. Conjugates of vitamin B₁₂ with PNAs or 2′-OMe RNA targeting essential genes in E. coli inhibited bacterial growth at concentrations as low as 5 μM (Giedyk et al. , Rownicki et al. ). However, direct conjugation of vitamin B₁₂ with ASOs can hinder ASO-RNA binding, ultimately reducing therapeutic efficacy. Thus, the design of the linker between vitamin B₁₂ and the ASO plays a crucial role in therapeutic performance. Longer linkers, such as C12 chains, enhance hybridization and improve antisense activity (Wierzba et al. , , Rownicki et al. ). Despite acceptable results with PNAs, only about 6%–9% of the vitamin B₁₂-locked nucleic acid (B12-LNA) conjugates successfully reach the bacterial cytosol, which is insufficient for strong antibacterial effects (Pereira et al. ). Although, vitamin B₁₂ held potential as a carrier for ASOs due to its receptor-mediated uptake in bacteria, limitations reduce its therapeutic utility. These include poor cytosolic delivery, nutrient-dependent BtuB receptor expression, structural modification challenges that impair coenzyme function, competition with host and microbiota for uptake, and limited applicability to a few Gram-negative species.

## Alternative approaches
In addition to peptide- and nutrient-based methods, other delivery strategies such as lipids and metal nanoparticles (Fig.  ) have been explored for delivery of antibacterial ASOs. While some, like liposomes, are established in mammalian systems, their use in bacteria is still being optimized. Others, such as bolaamphiphiles, remain limited to isolated studies.
Lipid nanoparticles and liposomes show some promise for antibacterial ASO delivery, but their uptake is limited by the absence of efficient internalization mechanisms in bacteria and the barrier posed by multilayered cell walls. Still, notable examples such as fusogenic cationic liposomes and engineered lipid systems demonstrate improved uptake and ASO stability in bacterial environments (Kauss et al. , Moreira et al. ). Liposomes loaded with anionic ASOs (LipoNAMs) were evaluated for antibacterial activity in E. coli. While methoxyPEG-coated LipoNAMs exhibited low delivery efficiency, maleimide-functionalized PEG LipoNAMs achieved delivery to nearly half the bacterial population. Significant increase in delivery efficiency, along with enhanced selectivity and biocompatibility, were observed when antibodies were conjugated to maleimide-functionalized PEG LipoNAMs. In a different approach, lipid modifications were introduced onto nucleosides to generate lipid oligonucleotides (Nguyen et al. ). Among these, the nucleolipid carrier DOTAU ((N-[5'-(2',3'-dioleoyl)uridine]-N',N',N'-trimethylammonium tosylate) was shown to codeliver the antibiotic cefotaxime, resulting in the inhibition of ESBL-producing E. coli (Kauss et al. ). Overall, the application of lipid nanoparticles for antibacterial ASO delivery remains in its early stages.
In another approach, Liu et al. ( ) developed glucose polymer-modified fluorescent silicon nanoparticles (GP-SiNPs) (Fig.  ) conjugated to antisense PNAs (GP-SiNPs-asPNA). These nanoparticles (~5.6 nm) selectively enter drug-resistant bacteria via the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) sugar transporter, which is absent in mammalian cells. GP-SiNPs-asPNA effectively inhibited essential genes in multidrug-resistant E. coli and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) at low doses (MIC: 0.8 mM) while showing minimal uptake in human and mouse cell lines. In mouse models of bacterial keratitis and endophthalmitis, a 1 mM solution of GP-SiNPs-asPNA reduced bacterial loads by 98.6%–99% after 5 days, with no significant toxicity (Liu et al. ). Although promising, follow-up studies are needed to assess the applicability of this system to other bacteria.
Designs based on self-assembly have also been attempted to deliver ASOs in bacteria. Bolaamphiphiles, amphipathic molecules that self-assemble into stable complexes (Fig.  ), have shown potential for delivering ASOs into bacteria. Cationic bolaamphiphiles containing 25-mer 2′-OMe phosphorothioate ASOs successfully targeted Clostridium difficile genes, achieving strong antibacterial effects with minimal off-target toxicity (Hegarty et al. , Sharma et al. ). In another approach, self-assembled TDNs have been reported to induce PNA-mediated antibacterial activity in MRSA S. aureus (Zhang et al. , Hu et al. ). While these platforms are conceptually promising, they have yet to be broadly adopted beyond the initial studies and require additional development to assess their utility as delivery agents for antibacterial ASO.
In summary, CPPs remain the gold standard for ASO delivery in bacteria, particularly for PNAs and PMOs, though their clinical potential is limited by serum instability and toxicity. Advances such as peptide stapling, branching, and use of d-amino acids may help overcome these limitations. Siderophores offer an attractive alternative due to their structural diversity, bacterial specificity, and lack of mammalian homologues, potentially enabling species-targeted delivery. However, their application in vivo, resistance potential, compatibility with a broader range of ASO chemistries (e.g. LNA, RNA-MOE) and activity in Gram-positive pathogens remain to be fully explored. Nanoparticle-based systems, such as liposomes, have also shown promise but currently require further optimization and broader validation beyond isolated studies. Ultimately, a clinically viable ASO delivery strategy must combine bacterial specificity, high efficacy, and biocompatibility in humans to advance toward clinical use.

## ASO as antibacterial therapeutics
A major focus on successful development of antibacterial antisense treatment has been targeting genes necessary for bacterial survival, growth, or certain biological mechanisms, particularly those encoding structural proteins, metabolic enzymes, or antibiotic resistance (Brown and Wright ). In the following section, we highlight the steady and increasing success utilizing ASOs as potential therapeutics to combat several medically important bacterial pathogens, particularly those identified as critical MDR bacteria for which effective therapies are urgently needed. These studies present a proof-of-concept that gene and pathogen-specific targeting could usher in a highly promising era for antibacterial therapeutics.

## ASO targeting essential genes

## Gram-negative bacteria

Escherichia coli. The first proof-of-principle of ASO application in prokaryotes was demonstrated in E. coli using PNA designed to target genes encoding the nonessential β-galactosidase and β-lactamase mRNA in E. coli. In their work, Good and Nielsen ( ) demonstrated specific gene inhibition in vitro in a dose-dependent manner using nanomolar concentrations of the PNA. This inhibition was more efficient for a permeable E. coli strain compared to wild-type K-12 (Good and Nielsen ). The first bactericidal antisense application targeting acpP in E. coli was first shown by Good et al. using a PNA attached to (KFF)3 K peptide. acpP encodes the acyl carrier protein (ACP),a central enzyme in fatty acid synthesis that shuttles all fatty acyl intermediates, therefore playing a pivotal role in bacterial lipid metabolism. The peptide conjugated–PNA (PPNA) retained target specificity in cultures and within a cell culture system using HeLa cells (Good et al. ). A similar approach targeting acpP was also tested using PPNA. Tan et al. ( ) demonstrated the bactericidal ability of (KFF)3K-PNA in vitro and in vivo and showed that a PNA and PPNA significantly improved survival and reduced bacterial burden in a murine model of peritonitis. Additionally, Kulik et al. ( ) demonstrated that PNA which targeted H69 of E. coli 23S rRNA, a conserved and essential element involved in ribosomal functioning, was efficacious in blocking the translation leading to inhibition of E. coli growth in vitro. Importantly, they also noted a significant increase in the half maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) of PNA with (KFF)3 K, which is likely due to the charge of the conjugated peptide and high molecular weight compared with free PNA (Kulik et al. ). Geller et al. ( ) introduced the first antisense PMO targeting the essential gene, acpP. They demonstrated the antibacterial effects of the antisense PMO in pure cultures and in a murine model of peritonitis (Geller et al. ). Their work established, for the first time, the feasibility and efficacy of antisense PMO antibiotics in an animal model. In follow up studies, the same group was successful in significantly enhancing the potency and uptake of the peptide conjugated-PMO (PPMO) by (1) modifying the length and position of binding of the PMO on the target gene (Deere et al. , Tilley et al. ) and (2) improving the peptide composition using various cationic and nonpolar amino acid moieties. A (RXR)4XB-acpP PMO composed of 11 bases and targeting the region around the start codon of E. coli acpP was found to be the most potent in vitro and in vivo (Tilley et al. , Mellbye et al. , ). After these early studies, others started to explore the possibility of exploiting antisense technology as antibacterial therapeutics. Nearly a dozen mRNA targets have recently been tested in a comparative manner in uropathogenic E. coli (Popella et al. ). These reports provided clear evidence on the potential of targeting essential bacterial genes in E. coli as future therapeutics and encouraged exploring other targets in addition to essential genes.

Klebsiella pneumoniae. This pathogen is responsible for numerous hospital-acquired infections including pneumonia, bacteremia, meningitis, and soft tissue infections in almost all age groups including septicemia and meningitis in newborns. The increasing emergence and spread of carbapenem-resistant and β-lactamase-producing K. pneumoniae strains necessitates the need to develop new therapeutic strategies. Kurupati et al. ( ) described the effectiveness of PPNA targeting essential genes in Klebsiella. Antisense oligomers designed to target outer membrane protein A (OmpA) and gyrA gene encoding the DNA gyrase subunit A, a type II topoisomerase required for DNA replication and transcription, were found to inhibit the growth of K. pneumoniae at concentrations of 20 μM and 40 μM in vitro, respectively (Kurupati et al. ). Moreover, Bai et al. ( ) tested the antimicrobial activities of PPNAs targeting rpoD encoding RNA polymerase sigma factor 70, which is known to be essential for bacterial growth and is conserved across species of Gram-negative bacteria including K. pneumoniae. This feature implies that rpoD might be a potential broad spectrum target gene for an antisense approach. The (RXR)4XB- and (KFF)3K- conjugated PNAs were bactericidal against MDR-ESBL K. pneumoniae in a concentration-dependent and sequence-selective manner (Bai et al. ). Geller et al. ( ) designed several PPMOs that targeted various essential genes of K. pneumoniae including acpP, rpmB, and ftsZ. These PPMOs were shown to be efficacious in inhibiting bacterial growth and reducing the biomass and viable cells in established biofilms at MIC75 ~2 μM. Importantly, acpP-PPMO retained its activity and was effective against K. pneumonieae carbapenemase (KPC)-expressing strain in a murine pneumonia model, where delayed intranasal treatment improved survival by 89% and reduced bacterial burden in the lung by ~3 logs compared to control animals. This was the first gene-specific therapeutic shown to be effective against K. pneumoniae in vivo (Geller et al. ) and further emphasized that acpP-PPMOs are at least 20 times more potent than other peptide-conjugated nucleic acids targeting other genes (Kurupati et al. , Bai et al. ). Variations in PPMO potency may be attributed to several possible differences including target gene and the position of the oligomers relative to the start codons or ribosomal binding sites (RBSs), the membrane-penetrating peptides, or the strains and methods used to measure MIC.

Acinetobacter baumannii. An important nosocomial pathogen that has shown a great capacity for persistence in the hospital environment, and it is becoming increasingly challenging to manage infections caused by this bacterium owing to the ongoing rise in the frequency of MDR clinical isolates and the lack of treatment options. In 2013, Geller et al. ( ) demonstrated that PPMOs targeting essential genes can be utilized safely and efficiently to combat several MDR Acinetobacter isolates. His group reported the bactericidal activity of a (RXR)4XB-acpP PPMO against several clinical strains including A. lwoffii 17976, A. baumannii AYE, and A. baumannii AB0057 in pure culture and in a murine model of pneumonia. A dose as low as 250 µg/kg of (RXR)4XB-acpP was shown to be effective in improving survival and reduced bacterial burden following infection with either A. lwoffii or A. baumannii (Geller et al. ). Martínez-Guinian et al. ( ) evaluated the antimicrobial activity of a PPNA targeting lpxB, an essential gene for the development of an intact LPS and cell envelope. They showed that anti-lpxB PPNA decreased survival of the A. baumannii ATCC 17978 strain and increased the survival rate of infected A549 cells. When used in vivo, combination treatment of anti-lpxB PPNA and colistin was more effective than colistin monotherapy leading to increased survival rates in a Galleria mellonella infection model (Martinez-Guitian et al. ). Rose et al. confirmed the in vitro and in vivo antibacterial activity of a PNA compound that targets carA, a gene involved in pyrimidine metabolism that had previously been demonstrated to be an essential gene for A. baumannii (Rose et al. ).

Pseudomonas aeruginosa. This bacterium  represents a major challenge for new antimicrobial drug discovery due to its versatile lifestyle, ability to develop resistance to most antibiotic classes, and capacity to form robust biofilms on surfaces and in certain hosts such as those living with cystic fibrosis (CF). Increasing antibiotic resistance coupled with decreasing numbers of antibiotics in the developmental pipeline demands novel antibacterial approaches for this pathogen. Several attempts have aimed to develop nonconventional therapeutics for combating MDR P. aeruginosa infections. Greenberg et al. ( ) utilized PPMOs and developed antisense oligomers to specifically target P. aeruginosa acpP, lpxC (UDP-(3-O-acyl)-N-acetylglucosamine deacetylase), and rpsJ (30S ribosomal protein S10), essential genes involved in metabolism, LPS synthesis, and translation, respectively. They showed that lead PPMOs inhibited in vitro growth of several MDR clinical P. aeruginosa isolates at levels equivalent to those that were effective against sensitive strains. Biofilm is a significant barrier that hinders the delivery of therapeutics and enable P. aeruginosa to resist external adverse environments and enhance its colonization in the host. Lead PPMOs reduced established Pseudomonal biofilms alone or in combination with tobramycin or piperacillin-tazobactam (Howard et al. ). This same group further demonstrated the effectiveness of PPMOs in vivo against extensively drug-resistant P. aeruginosa in a murine model of acute pneumonia. Therapeutic dosing via aerosol delivery with lead PPMOs alone or combined with antibiotics reduced lung bacterial burden and resulted in superior survival compared to conventional antibiotics (Moustafa et al. ). The ability to modulate biofilm formation was also achieved using a (KFF)3K-PNA targeting motA. An PPNA that targets motA was effective in hindering biofilm formation in P. aeruginosa strain PAO1 in a dose-dependent manner. The sequence of motA is conserved in Pseudomonas and some other Gram-negative bacteria which suggests this gene could be a potential target beyond P. aeruginosa (Hu et al. ).

Salmonella typhimurium.  This pathogen is one of the most leading causes of "food poisoning" and exhibits a remarkable ability to evade the impact of antibiotics and killing by immune cells. Treating intracellular bacteria that reside inside host cells pose a greater clinical challenge, where host cells act as protective niche for such pathogens due to their cell membrane barrier, which results in poor intracellular accumulation of antibiotics. Tilley et al. ( ) demonstrated the efficacy of AcpP PPMOs against S. typhimurium in vitro with MIC lower than 1 µg/ml.  In addition, the reduction in colony-forming units (CFU) below the initial inoculum in pure culture and in infected Caco-2 cells validates the bactericidal impact of the PPMOs tested. They further showed that PPMOs can be internalized by mammalian cells, particularly Salmonella-containing vacuoles, and effectively deliver the PMO to its target in the bacterial cytoplasm where they can modulate gene expression (Tilley et al. ). Mitev et al. ( ) demonstrated that a cationic AcpP PPMO inhibited intracellular growth and viability of S. enterica in RAW264.7 murine macrophages. S. typhimurium has also served as a model organism for understanding optimal carriers, kinetics of killing, and specificity of acpP PNAs (Popella et al. ).

Burkholderia cepacia complex (Bcc). These bacteria cause opportunistic infections in people with CF and chronic granulomatous disease (CGD). In CF patients, they can contribute to both progressive pulmonary decline as well as the rapid deterioration in lung function known as “cepacia syndrome.” Bcc isolates from patients with CF are known to be notoriously resistant to frequently utilized antibiotics, such as minocycline, meropenem, and ceftazidime. Therefore, the need for new antimicrobials for Bcc infections is critically urgent. A study by Greenberg et al. ( ) in Bcc revealed that antisense oligomers designed to acpP were bactericidal against several clinical isolates of Bcc. The same PPMO not only augmented the killing of B. multivorans by human neutrophils from CGD patients ex vivo, but also provided a survival advantage and improved the overall pathology in a CGD murine model following B. multivorans challenge (Greenberg et al. ). The same group further explored additional PPMO sequences targeting acpP and identified the AUG start codon and SD sequence as optimal for efficacy (Daly et al. ). They explored the −35 to +44 region of the acpP and found new PPMOs that were more effective, including in artificial sputum media, an in vitro condition mimicking the CF lung. The potency of these PPMOs was still efficacious in vivo after delayed treatment and reduced the bacterial burden in the lungs of immunocompromised CGD mice (Daly et al. ).

Brucella spp. This is a family of bacteria known to cause human brucellosis, one of the five most common bacterial zoonoses in the world. Despite combination antibiotic therapy, relapse, and progression to chronic disease is very common due to the intracellular lifestyle of Brucella spp. which puts them out of reach of many bactericidal agents. Therefore, approaches are needed to ensure that therapeutic molecules can reach inside macrophages, the intracellular niche of Brucella. Rajasekaran et al. ( ) showed that some of the peptide-conjugated PNAs targeted to several Brucella essential genes involved in DNA (polA, dnaG, and gyrA), RNA (rpoB), cell envelope (asd), fatty acid (kdtA and acpP), and protein (tsf) synthesis can inhibit the growth of several Brucella spp. (B. melitensis 16 M, B. abortus 2308, and B. suis) in pure culture in a sequence-specific and dose-dependent manner at micromolar concentrations. In addition, PNAs targeting asd and dnaG were most efficient in inhibiting the growth and replication of B. suis in murine macrophages-like cells J774A.1 after 24 h of treatment (Rajasekaran et al. ).
Nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae (NTHi). This bacterium is a commensal of the human upper respiratory tract and a major respiratory pathogen that imposes a substantial disease burden due to its rapidly expanding spectrum and prevalence of antibiotic resistance. Biofilm formation plays a central role in the pathogenesis of otitis media caused by NTHi, where the bacterial biofilm population is nearly 1000 times more resistant to standard antibiotics than planktonic bacteria. In a strategy to target planktonic and biofilm forms to eradicate NTHi nasopharyngeal colonization, Otsuka et al. ( ) assessed the effect of different PPNAs that target acpP in vitro and demonstrated their antimicrobial effects on NTHi in planktonic and biofilm forms with MICs as low as 2.9 µg/ml. They also reported the critical factors affecting the activity of PPNAs against biofilm-formed NTH: their work not only emphasized the position of the PNA on the target gene as a critical factor in the design and the efficacy of the PNA, but also the design and position of the membrane penetrating peptide (Otsuka et al. ).

## Gram-positive and mycobacteria
Gram-positive bacteria cell walls are characterized by a thick layer of peptidoglycan. Pathogens such as L. monocytogenes (Pizarro-Cerda and Cossart ), S. aureus (Nikolic and Mudgil ), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Smith et al. ), can exhibit significant antibiotic resistance. To combat this, several studies have attempted to find alternative therapeutic approaches and demonstrated the efficiency of antisense therapy in combination with CPPs to eliminate even MDR strains.

Listeria monocytogenes. This is a psychrophilic Gram-positive bacterium known to cause severe foodborne listeriosis outbreaks (Quereda et al. ). L. monocytogenes survives within macrophages, so they can evade eradication by the host immune responses and conventional antibiotics which are incapable of passively penetrating host cells to kill intracellular pathogens. Previous studies focused on internalin (Inl) proteins which contribute to bacterial invasion. Kalani et al. ( ) designed a CPP-conjugated antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting the highly conserved regions of internalin (inl) genes and evaluated its inhibitory effect on L. monocytogenes survival and internalization in cell culture. Though CPP-antisense compounds did not prevent visible bacterial growth, they did decrease the expression of the inl genes and hence decreased bacterial invasion. These data suggest that CPP-antisense compounds could have synergistic effects in the host immune response. Other groups targeted the RNA polymerase A subunit (rpoA) in L. monocytogenes. Alajlouni and Seleem ( ) previously revealed the potential usefulness of antisense constructs targeting both rpoA and rpoD conjugated with (KFF)3 K. In a follow up study, the same group evaluated the antibacterial activity of PNAs conjugated to five different CPPs and found that (RXR)4XB was the most suitable peptide for maximizing the antisense effect, and CPPs conjugated to anti-rpoA constructs can successfully inhibit the expression of this gene and other virulence genes, such as hly, plcA, and plcB, and lead to direct killing L. monocytogenes both in vitro using J7741A macrophage like cell culture and in a Caenorhabditis elegans infection model (Abushahba et al. ). These findings emphasize that selecting an appropriate delivery peptide is critical for maximizing the antisense effect.

Staphylococcus aureus. This bacterium is a member of the microbiota of the body, frequently found in the upper respiratory tract and on the skin. It is also a formidable pathogen and the leading cause of a variety of infections, such as cutaneous abscesses, bacteremia and pneumonia. MRSA is resistant not only to methicillin but to almost all beta-lactam antibiotics (Tong et al. ). To develop ASOs against Staphylococci, Traykovska and Penchovsky ( ) targeted four riboswitches, including glmS, flavin mononucleotide, thiamine pyrophosphate, and S-adenosyl methionine (SAM)-I selected based on genome-wide bioinformatic analyses (Pavlova and Penchovsky , Traykovska and Penchovsky ). SAM-I controls gene expression involved in methionine or cysteine biosynthesis in Gram-positive bacteria by directly sensing the levels of SAM and other metabolites. It plays critical roles in modulating many bacterial cellular processes including metabolism and virulence. The authors demonstrated that a chimeric ASO conjugated to pVEC (pVEC-ASO) that targets SAM-I could inhibit S. aureus and L. monocytogenes growth in LB media (Traykovska and Penchovsky ). The pVEC is a short, cationic peptide that can pass through the cell via a receptor-independent mechanism (Elmquist et al. ). These pVEC-ASOs do not exhibit toxicity in human cell lines. These observations suggest that choosing a conservative region of the riboswitch sequence in S. aureus could be suitable targets for antibacterial drug development.

Streptococcus spp. This is a diverse genus of Gram-positive bacteria that can cause a wide range of infections in humans. S. pyogenes, also known as Group A Streptococcus (GAS), is a major human pathogen that causes a wide range of diseases such as pharyngitis, scarlet fever, impetigo, cellulitis, and erysipelas (Newberger and Hollingshead ). The coexistence of β-lactamase-producing bacteria, biofilm formation by GAS, and internalization of GAS into epithelial host cells contribute to beta-lactam resistance to penicillin and amoxicillin as well as alternative antibiotics like macrolides (Gergova et al. ). To develop novel therapeutic strategies, Patenge et al. ( ) focused on PPNAs by targeting gyrase A subunit (gyrA), a vital and essential gene for bacterial DNA replication, repair, and transcription. The HIV-1 Tat protein-derived arginine-rich recombinant peptide (Tat) was used as a CPP, which translocate by destabilizing phospholipid bilayers and thereby induces transient pores in the respective membrane (Herce et al. ). The authors demonstrated that Tat-conjugated-gyrA PNA treatment inhibited S. pyogenes growth in THY medium. Further Tat-anti-gyrA PNA showed synergy with the antibiotics levofloxacin, novobiocin, and spectinomycin (Patenge et al. ). Thus, these data suggest that antisense PNAs are promising candidates for combination therapy.
While GAS cause various infectious disease, S. mutans is associated with dental health. S. mutans can cause caries by adherence of tooth surfaces and biofilm formations (plaque), further contributing to the decay process. To combat chronic infections caused by S. mutans biofilm, groups designed the ASO sequence based on conserved sequences of the VicK protein-binding site (Zhang et al. ). VicK proteins bind to the gtfBCD, gbpB, and ftf promoter region, which regulate secretion of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) in the early stages of biofilm formation. This multitargeting antisense nucleotide sequence was carried by tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) into bacterial biofilms. Since each face is a complete triangle, tFNAs have strong structural stability. FNAs act as nanocarriers that are highly editable and can promote membrane destabilization (Guan et al. ). The authors showed an effective tFNA system for penetrating ASOs into the cell wall of S. mutans. Though ASOs-tFNA did not significantly alter the growth of planktonic S mutans cells in Brain-Heart Infusion (BHI) medium, it inhibited bacterial biofilm formation by targeting genes related to EPS synthesis.

Bacillus anthracis. Anthrax is a disease caused by the spore-forming bacterium B. anthracis, a zoonotic organism that primarily affects ruminants, but also major public health and bio-terrorism implications. The most effective antibiotics against B. anthracis are ciprofloxacin and doxycycline (Brooks et al. ). However, there is an increasing incidence of resistance to these standard antibiotics, and a perceived threat for genetically engineered strains that potentially pose a bioterrorist threat to national security (Dassanayake et al. ). Identifying countermeasures to MDR strains was a driving factor in developing an asobiotic against this biosecurity risk. Panchal et al. ( ) showed that PPMOs targeted to essential genes acpP, ftsZ, and gyrA were potent growth inhibitors in vitro in both ciprofloxacin sensitive and resistant strains. Moreover, AcpP- and GyrA-PPMOs each improved survival of mice infected with a lethal dose of the virulent Ames strain (Panchal et al. )

Mycobacterium species. This is a genus that comprises more than 70 species of a group of acid-fast, Gram-positive aerobic bacteria. The most famous species is M. tuberculosis (Mtb), the causative agent of tuberculosis. The complex cell wall of Mtb is a significant barrier to the effective delivery and uptake of antibiotics and common ASOs posing a challenge for therapeutic interventions (Buthelezi et al. ). Kulyté et al. ( ) demonstrated for the first time the application of antisense peptide-PNAs against M. smegmatis. In their work, they showed that PPNAs can efficiently and specifically inhibit both reporter and endogenous essential genes in mycobacteria (Kulyte et al. ). Skvortsova et. al. ( ), found that phosphoryl guanidine oligo-2′-O-methylribonucleotides (2′-OMe PGOs) were successful in penetrating M. smegmatis and inhibiting the growth of M. smegmatis downregulated the expression of bacterial alanine dehydrogenase (ald) and Ald protein levels. Thus, 2′-OMe PGOs were able to efficiently cross two cellular membranes and inhibit the growth of M. smegmatis (Skvortsova et al. ). Overcoming drug resistance in mycobacteria was achieved using PNA-mediated efflux inhibition as a therapeutic strategy by targeting LfrA, an efflux pump found in M. smegmatis, and is majorly involved in conferring innate drug resistance to this strain (Ghosh et al. ).

## Alternative targets
Bacteria can evolve to evade the effect of antibiotics through multiple different intrinsic and acquired mechanisms. Another successful approach utilizing ASOs was demonstrated through targeting highly conserved antibiotic-resistance genes. Although not bactericidal by themselves, using antisense oligomers in combination with antibiotics was assessed as a potential way for combatting multidrug-resistant bacteria. For example, ASOs can target bacterial efflux pumps, bacterial membrane transport proteins which are involved in extrusion of substrates, including multiple classes of clinically relevant antibiotics, from within the bacterial cells into the external environment (Du et al. ). In 2010, Wang et al. ( ) targeted a multidrug efflux pump in P. aeruginosa by developing an encapsulated phosphorothioate oligodeoxynucleotide (PS-ODN617) targeting oprM, a gene encoding an antibiotic-ejecting OM protein that plays a crucial role in exporting incoming chemotherapeutic agents across the membranes. MexAB-OprM-overproducing strains represent ~46% of P. aeruginosa isolates. Targeting the efflux pump components with PS-ODN617 was shown to restore susceptibility of multiple MDR P. aeruginosa clinical isolates to beta-lactam antibiotics and fluoroquinolones, and the MICs of six commonly used antibiotics (piperacillin, cefoperazone, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, imipenem, and amikacin) were significantly improved to a clinically sensitive breakpoint (Wang et al. ).
CmeABC is a multidrug resistance-nodulation-cell division-type efflux pump consisting of three protein components located in the inner membrane (CmeB), periplasm (CmeA) and OM (CmeC), which are encoded by the polycistronic cmeABC operon. Campylobacter jejuni is a common foodborne pathogen that expresses the multidrug efflux pump CmeABC, which confers resistance to a broad range of antibiotics including fluoroquinolones and macrolides, the drugs of choice for the treatment of human campylobacteriosis. Oh et al. ( ) reported the restoration of susceptibility to antibiotics by targeting various regions of the cmeABC operon, specifically PNAs targeting cmeA or cmeB, and these were effective in sensitizing C. jejuni to ciprofloxacin and erythromycin.
AcrAB-TolC efflux system is one of the major determinants of the intrinsic antibiotic resistance levels in Gram-negative bacteria and has drawn great attention as a strategic target for resensitizing bacteria to existing antibiotics (Jang ). Currently available efflux pump inhibitors are known to have significant toxicities and have limited use as therapeutic agents (Sun et al. ). Ayhan et al. ( ) designed a short antisense PPMO oligomer designed to inhibit the expression of acrA to restore antibiotic susceptibility in E. coli in vitro. In their work, they showed that treatment of bacteria with acrA-PPMO resulted in a 2–40-fold increase in antibiotic efficacy. They showed that acrA-PPMO was efficacious and enhanced activity of both synergistic and antagonistic antibiotic pairs when used together in vitro. Importantly, alignment of the genes coding for the AcrAB-TolC components demonstrated that the PPMO sequences designed for E. coli, are also complementary in other Gram-negative bacteria suggesting the broad-spectrum application of this antisense approach (Ayhan et al. ). Sturge at al. ( ) went on to demonstrate that an acrA-PPMO could restore activity to traditional antibiotics in in vivo infection models.
Beta-lactams are the most widely used antibacterial agents worldwide. However, bacteria have developed several resistance mechanisms to overcome all major classes of beta-lactam antibiotics. The New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase Ambler class B (NDM-1) is a plasmid-associated resistance gene that is rapidly spreading with an alarming rate, rendering many Gram-negative pathogens extremely resistant to the most potent antibiotics. Using gene specific antisense approach, Sully et al. ( ) were successful in restoring susceptibility to carbapenems using a PPMO designed to target blaNDM-1 gene. When tested in vitro using E. coli CVB-1(the first NDM-1 postive E. coli), inhibition of NDM-1 restored susceptibility and lowered the MIC of several carbapenems. Importantly, the therapeutic efficacy of the NDM-1 PPMO was evaluated in vivo in a murine model of sepsis. The PPMO coadministered with meropenem conferred a survival advantage and reduced bacterial burden indicating that the PPMO restores bacterial susceptibility to antibiotic killing in vivo (Sully et al. ).
Mobilized colistin resistance (MCR-1) is another transferable plasmid-mediated resistance mechanism and has been reported in many Gram-negative pathogens. Acquisition of MCR-1 resistance is clinically significant because polymyxins, like colistin, are often considered the last-line antibiotics used to treat extensively resistant organisms. Daly et al. ( ) successfully modulated colistin resistance by resensitizing five clinical mcr-1-positive E. coli strains to polymyxins using PPMOs targeting mcr-1. Importantly, therapeutic dosing with MCR-1 PPMO combined with colistin reduced bacterial burden and improved survival in a murine model of sepsis. This was the first practical in vitro and in vivo approach modulating colistin resistance using an antisense approach (Lopez et al. , Daly et al. ). Lopez and coworkers targeted aac(6′)-Ib, a gene that mediates the resistance to amikacin (AMK) and other aminoglycosides in several Gram-negative bacteria. In their work, they used CPPBD4, a hybrid oligomer composed of 2′,4′-bridged nucleic acid-NC (BNANC) residues and deoxynucleotides (BNANC-DNA) conjugated to the permeabilizing peptide (RXR)4XB, as an antisense inhibitor to AAC (6′)-Ib. When tested for its ability to reduce the levels of resistance to AMK, CPPBD4 in combination with AMK, showed sequence-specific inhibition of A. baumannii A155 cell growth in culture and a reduction of virulence in the G. mellonella infection model (Lopez et al. ).

## Bacteriophages
The most diverse biological entities on the planet are not the bacteria themselves, but the viruses that infect them, called bacteriophages (phages). Their enormous diversity and richness of unexplored genes have stimulated much interest in studying their genomes. However, while most existing tools for phage functional genomics require the bacterial host to be genetically tractable, the vast majority of bacteria are currently refractory to available gene engineering methods. This includes many newly discovered species and strains isolated from clinical samples. Moreover, many phages shield their genome by DNA modifications or enclose it in a protective compartment, which equally prevents genetic manipulation. Last but not least, essential phage genes cannot be permanently inactivated to begin with, since that phage would not produce progeny. To overcome these barriers, ASOs have recently been introduced as an alternative unbiased approach in the quest to explore phage gene functions in a generic manner.
In their study, Gerovac et al. ( ) used PNA-based ASOs to silence mRNAs in diverse phages in several different Gram-negative and Gram-positive host species, successfully targeting mRNAs of both DNA and RNA phages. They focused on discovering new essential genes in a so-called jumbo phage, the ΦKZ phage of P. aeruginosa, which is of particular interest for phage therapy of human lung infections. Operationally defined as phages with genomes >200 kb in length, jumbo phages encode hundreds of uncharacterized genes and thus represent a vast discovery space. A subset of jumbo phages protect their DNA by forming around its so-called phage nucleus, out of which phage mRNAs must be exported to the host cytosol and newly synthesized phage proteins imported to build phage particles. ASO-based phenotypic and transcriptome-based screening of ~70 ΦKZ mRNAs identified new proteins that act as decision points in the sophisticated replication cycle of this jumbo phage.
Successful infection by phage is a powerful readout for assessing ASO properties. For example, Gerovac et al. ( ) used it determine favorable features of PNA, through tiling with PNAs of the same length the 5′ region of the chmA mRNA, which encodes the major phage nucleus protein. They used these to pioneer the use of proteomics for the assessment of off-targeting on newly synthesized phage mRNAs (and thus, phage proteins), demonstrating high specificity of the ASOs; by comparing gene knockout at the DNA level with gene knockdown at the mRNA of the nonessential phage tubulin PhuZ, they revealed a great potency of the RNA-targeting ASO approach (Gerovac et al. ). Antiphage ASOs may not only be an important new tool for basic research, they might also find their way into industry applications, such as suppressing potential contaminating phages in bacterial starter cultures used for food production. Moreover, ASOs being a non-GMO approach could become important for phage therapy of MDR bacteria where genetic engineering requiring lengthy approval routines may not be an option.

## Off-target effects of asobiotics
During the development of antisense drugs, an important consideration is evaluating potential off-target hybridization events that may compromise drug efficacy or safety, herein called off-target effects (Fig.  ). While minimizing off-target binding is generally recommended when designing asobiotics (Good , Dryselius et al. ), intentionally targeting multiple bacterial genes might theoretically enhance antibacterial efficacy and reduce resistance development (Theuretzbacher et al. ), although this remains experimentally unexplored for ASOs. On the other hand, off-target hybridization may reduce the effective ASO concentration available for the intended target, a phenomenon analogous to the “sponge effect” observed with bacterial small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) (Papenfort and Melamed ). Moreover, unintended gene knockdown of essential genes in the commensal microbiota can lead to dysbiosis, comparable to broad-spectrum antibiotics (de Nies et al. ). Furthermore, off-target knockdown of nonessential genes could potentially induce resistance or tolerance, as observed by Aunins et al. ( ). Therefore, rigorous screening for off-target interactions is essential in asobiotic design.
 In addition, when targeting nonessential genes (Wang et al. , Otoupal et al. ), or phage genes (Gerovac et al. ), off-target effects to essential bacterial mRNAs could cause unwanted growth defects, underscoring the high importance of off-target screens in such cases.

## Off-target effects in target bacteria
Asobiotics typically consist of short (9–12 nucleobases) ASOs targeting the RBS of bacterial mRNAs. Their programmability allows targeting of any known bacterial gene. Due to the conserved nature of the RBS, particularly the AUG start codon and SD sequence (Shine and Dalgarno , Saito et al. , Wen et al. ), there is a considerable risk of unintended hybridization to other genes. For example, targeting the AUG start codon requires a “CAT” or “CAU” motif in the ASO sequence, significantly reducing sequence specificity. Similarly, the AG-rich SD region limits sequence diversity and increases the likelihood of off-target binding events.
The extent of off-target binding depends on ASO–mRNA hybridization strength and mismatch tolerance. PNAs exhibit high affinity and low mismatch-tolerance, especially at central positions (Jensen et al. , Weiler et al. , Igloi , Doyle et al. , Choi et al. , Kiliszek et al. ). Studies targeting bacterial RNAs with PNAs or PMOs consistently show that terminal mismatches minimally impact activity, whereas central mismatches strongly reduce efficacy (Good and Nielsen , Good et al. , Hatamoto et al. , Greenberg et al. , Jung et al. , Nanayakkara et al. ). For instance, Jung et al. ( ) demonstrated that inducing double-mismatches at either end of the binding sequence did not abolish the antibacterial activity of acpP-targeting PPNAs, and that off-target sequences with at least seven consecutive matching bases and no central mismatch were prone to off-target effects in Salmonella (Jung et al. ). Similarly, Nanayakkara et al. ( ) confirmed this positional dependence of mismatches in P. aeruginosa, both in vitro and in a mouse lung infection model.
A common misconception in the asobiotics field is that increasing ASO length prevents off-target binding because adding bases increases the diversity of the target sequence four-fold. While longer sequences reduce the likelihood of zero-mismatch off-targets, they increase the likelihood of partially complementary sites capable of stable hybridization (Fig.  ). This “length paradox,” previously described in eukaryotic ASOs (Hagedorn et al. ), necessitates careful length optimization to minimize off-target effects as well as self-binding.
 Hybridization-dependent off-targets are not limited to mRNA binding (Fig.  ). Asobiotics can also bind noncoding RNAs, e.g. rRNAs (Good and Nielsen , Górska et al. ), sRNAs (Tsai et al. ), tRNAs (Cui et al. ), and some specific ASO modalities (e.g. phosphorothioate oligonucleotides) are prone to protein binding (Liang et al. , Hyjek-Składanowska et al. ) highlighting the complexity of off-target assessment.

## Off-targets in the microbiota
An important yet understudied consideration in the development of asobiotics is their potential off-target effects within the host microbiota. Asobiotics offer promise in narrow-spectrum targeting, presumably leaving the beneficial microbiota untouched (Pifer and Greenberg , Vogel ). However, the use of delivery systems like CPPs may allow uptake into nontarget bacterial species. Additionally, the gene essentiality of target genes is often conserved across bacterial species (Ghomi et al. ), potentially broadening their antimicrobial spectrum. Although selective growth inhibition has been demonstrated in microbial consortia containing up to four species (Mondhe et al. , Hizume et al. ), comprehensive microbiome-wide studies, such as metagenomics or metatranscriptomics following asobiotic treatment are necessary to thoroughly assess potential off-target disruptions. Achieving selectivity thus requires careful consideration during the design of asobiotics, emphasizing the importance of rigorous off-target assessments as discussed below.

## Off-target effects in mammalian cells
While asobiotics primarily target prokaryotic transcripts, unintended hybridization within mammalian cells remains a concern, particularly for asobiotics capable of penetrating eukaryotic membranes. While these off-targets could, in theory, affect translation, splicing, or noncoding RNAs in mammalian cells, those ASOs active in eukaryotes typically require 18–30 nucleotides (Akpulat et al. , Roberts et al. , Egli and Manoharan ). Shorter ASOs (e.g. tiny LNAs) have been shown to modulate microRNAs but exhibit minimal proteomic or transcriptomic off-target effects (Obad et al. ).

## Assessing off-target effects
Several computational and experimental approaches exist to identify and quantify off-target effects of asobiotics. Computational predictions traditionally use sequence alignment tools such as BLAST to screen for potential off-target hybridization sites. Recently, specialized software has been developed explicitly for bacterial ASO design, including PNA Finder, a Python-based tool for designing PNAs while assessing off-targets within bacterial translation initiation regions (Aunins et al. ), and MASON, a web server that enables efficient bacterial ASO design and a comprehensive off-target prediction in bacteria, the human genome, and the human microbiome (Jung et al. ).
Experimental methods complement computational predictions, directly validating hybridization and functional off-target effects. Cell-free assays, such as electrophoretic mobility shift assays, thermal denaturation, and microscale thermophoresis can measure binding affinity between ASOs and target or off-target mRNAs (Goltermann et al. , Ghosh et al. , Story et al. ). Such assays allow precise characterization of mismatch tolerances, similar to previous studies on PNA–DNA duplex stability (Igloi ).
As mentioned, asobiotics primarily function by inhibiting translation. Therefore, methods measuring in vitro translation are valuable for experimentally validating target and off-target translation inhibition (Popella et al. , Ghosh et al. ). In addition, Ribo-RET is a method that allows for exact profiling of the translation initiation site by adding ribosome-stalling antibiotics and then ribosome-protected mRNAs fragments are isolated and sequenced (Meydan et al. ). However, traditional in vivo ribosome profiling can be challenging due to poor coverage of weakly expressed genes or difficulty culturing certain bacterial strains. Novel in vitro approaches like INRI-Seq (Hör et al. ) enable detailed, cell-free analysis of the translation initiation landscape across entire transcriptomes. These approaches enable comprehensive profiling of how ASOs influence translation initiation, determining both target-specific and off-target translation inhibition effects. Further efforts using these methods could improve understanding of translation-inhibiting mechanisms of asobiotics.
In addition to translation inhibition, asobiotics frequently trigger the decay of targeted mRNAs (Nikravesh et al. , Goh et al. , Bai et al. , Ghosal and Nielsen , Ghosal et al. , Patenge et al. , Popella et al. , , Ghosh et al. ). While traditional methods such as northern blotting confirm individual transcript depletion, RNA-seq provides a comprehensive, genome-wide view of hybridization-dependent and hybridization-independent off-target effects. RNA-seq is already a standard method to assess off-target impacts for mammalian ASOs (Chen et al. , Domenger et al. , El Boujnouni et al. , Kuijper et al. ) and has recently been successfully applied to bacterial asobiotics (Popella et al. , , Abt-Becker , Jung et al. , Cosi et al. ). Transcript depletion patterns vary depending on the targeted gene (Popella et al. ) and ASO modality (Ghosh et al. ). Yet, RNA-seq constitutes an efficient and low-cost method for hybridization-dependent and -independent off-target effect characterization.
Ultimately, PNAs affect the protein levels of their target gene. Western blot analysis can help detect single known proteins; however, assessing large numbers of proteins is not feasible. Thus, proteomics techniques can be used to determine of how proteomes are affected by asobiotic treatment. This has recently been demonstrated in jumbo phage-infected P. aeruginosa treated with PPNAs (Gerovac et al. ), paving the way for other studies in the future.
In summary, accurately assessing off-target effects is crucial for the safe and effective development of asobiotics. Computational tools such as PNA Finder and MASON, alongside experimental approaches like RNA-sequencing, ribosome profiling, and proteomics, provide powerful means to predict and quantify off-target effects. Emerging technologies, including single-cell sequencing, in vivo translation profiling, metagenomics and metatranscriptomics, and artificial intelligence-driven methods, offer promising avenues to refine off-target evaluations further and enhance asobiotic precision.

## Other bacterial responses to asobiotics
Hybridization-independent effects arise from cellular responses triggered by asobiotic treatment itself (Fig.  ). While early asobiotic studies focused on MICs and morphological effects (Nikravesh et al. , Goh et al. ), recent RNA-seq efforts have investigated bacterial stress responses induced by CPP-conjugated asobiotics. For instance, Popella et al. ( ) identified upregulation of the cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) resistance pathway, as well as the PhoP/Q and PmrA/B regulons in Salmonella upon PPNA exposure, promoting OM remodeling and resistance. Similar envelope stress-responses, including the CPX inner membrane stress response regulon, were upregulated in PPNA-treated uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) (Popella et al. ), which has been described before to induce tolerance to RXR-bound PNAs (Frimodt-Møller et al. ). In addition, the general outer-membrane stress response regulon sigma E (σE) was also triggered, in line with a recent study on F. nucleatum (Cosi et al. ). These stress responses were absent in PPNA-treated S. pyogenes and S. pneumoniae under sub-MIC conditions (Abt-Becker ). A comparative study on Salmonella further indicated conserved stress-responses across different ASO modalities, including PNAs, PMOs, and LNAs (Ghosh et al. ). These efforts underscore the power of simple bulk RNA-seq measurements to find bacterial stress responses and possible mechanisms of resistance or tolerance. Complementary serial passage experiments (Bai et al. , Ghosal et al. , Otsuka et al. , Mulkern et al. ) and single-cell (Homberger et al. ) analyses could further elucidate resistance development and cellular responses to asobiotics.

## Adverse effects and toxicity of asobiotics

## Toxicity of ASOs
Chemical modifications were critical to the enhancement of the ASOs’ performance including stability, protection from nucleases, and extending their half-life and therapeutic efficacy. Yet safety assessments of ASOs have already revealed that this class of antimicrobial drugs has shown some adverse reactions with varying levels of toxicity during clinical trials (Goyenvalle et al. ).
Hybridization-independent toxicities are adverse outcomes that do not rely on the specific binding between RNA sequences and an ASO through Watson–Crick base pairing. These toxicities are mainly attributed to various factors, including the accumulation of ASOs in high exposure organs, proinflammatory mechanisms (including immune complexes), which can lead to inflammation and tissue damage, or the binding of ASOs to proteins both inside and outside of cells, resulting in aptameric interactions (Goyenvalle et al. ). The highest concentrations of ASOs are generally found in the liver and kidney after systemic administration. In the kidney, they can accumulate in the lysosomes of proximal tubules, leading to damage and proteinuria. ASOs with higher affinity chemistry such as 2'-O-Methoxyethyl (2′-MOE), were shown to trigger kidney toxicity in preclinical species due high tissue concentrations. Additionally, high-affinity LNA have caused unexpected renal toxicity in humans (Ersoz and Demir-Dora ).
The hepatotoxicity of ASOs may be caused by binding to unintended RNA sequences, disrupting metabolism, and causing stress in hepatocytes leading to cell damage. Additionally, they could result from sequence motifs, such as TCC/TGC, that typically bind to hepatocellular proteins, increasing the activity of the p53 and nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) stress pathways. Other risks of hepatotoxicity are related to elevation of the liver enzymes, alanine transaminase and aspartate aminotransferase, and hepatic steatosis, which were reported with certain ASO drugs, mipomersen and inotersen, that target hepatic gene expression (Alhamadani et al. ).
Besides these hybridization-independent effects, other toxicities are independent of both hybridization and sequence. These toxicities are generally driven by the plasma concentration reaching above a threshold level and include prolonged coagulation time and activation of the alternative complement system. Acutely, activation of the alternative complement system can lead to significant drops in blood pressure. Repeated complement activation can result in exhaustion of C3 complement factor resulting in impaired complement-mediated clearance of antibody aggregates, which can lead to vascular inflammation (Engelhardt et al. ).
The immunomodulatory effects of oligonucleotides harboring so-called CpG motifs, have been an attractive feature and a useful tool to design nucleotide-based immunotherapies and vaccine adjuvants. Despite the fact that CpG like-motifs were avoided in the design phase, few therapeutic oligomers were reported to induce proinflammatory effects including injection site or infusion related reactions and flu-like symptoms. Thrombocytopenia is another reported adverse effect that has been reported in preclinical models and clinical trials following treatment with phosphorothioate (PS)-modified ASOs (volanesorsen, inotersen, and drisapersen). While the immune- and nonimmune-mediated hypotheses have been proposed for this adverse effect, the underlying mechanisms of thrombocytopenia are still being investigated (Flierl et al. ). Such effects are generally transient in nature and disappear when the ASO drug is cleared from plasma by tissue uptake, within hours from systemic administration. While the dosing regimens are often regulated under clinical supervision, these effects should be carefully considered in toxicity studies in which much higher doses are administered (Goyenvalle et al. ).
Overall, high-affinity chemistries on PS backbones have been reported to more likely trigger toxicity compared with chemistries with more binding affinity (such as MOE and Me modifications). In contrast, charge neutral PMOs have never been reported to induce such effects, but this may not hold true when the PMO is conjugated to a CPP to enhance their uptake (Haque et al. ). CPP-conjugated antisense oligomers have been increasingly used as one of the most useful tools for transmembrane delivery in different cell types and tissue that are hard to target. Their success is not only dependent on their versatility, but also on achieving therapeutic concentrations in cells and consequently improving their therapeutic outcome. They can be simply synthesized, modified, and improved (Xie et al. ). However, CPPs are a double-edged sword since they may induce significant adverse effects owing to toxicity and immunogenicity issues. Their polypeptide and cationic property may increase the likelihood of triggering the immune system in the host leading to undesired responses (Vedadghavami et al. ). Cytotoxicity often arising from cationic CPP conjugates remains a concern, as demonstrated by PMO-induced nephrotoxicity (Amantana et al. , Wu et al. , Sheikh and Yokota ) and CAMP toxicity (Hancock and Sahl ), highlighting the need for optimized delivery systems as discussed earlier. Preclinical studies, far too often, focus on assessing only the improved delivery and efficacy, neglecting the importance of thorough early toxicity evaluation. In this context, early preclinical assessment of safety aspects is crucial to ensure the future transition of ASO therapeutics to the clinic. However, it should be noted that the clinical data to date has been in the context of antisense for eukaryotic diseases and it remains unanswered whether a shorter-length ASO as an antibacterial would show similar potential safety liabilities.

## Bacterial resistance to asobiotics
Resistance to ASOs, such as PMOs conjugated to CPPs, can emerge in bacteria through multiple distinct mechanisms (Puckett et al. , Mulkern et al. ). Although direct studies on bacterial resistance remain limited, insights from viral resistance to antisense-based therapeutics (e.g. PPMOs and siRNAs) suggest that several common mechanisms may apply across microbial systems, offering a valuable framework for understanding and anticipating bacterial adaptation to ASOs (Mulamba et al. , Neuman et al. , Deas et al. , Kugelman et al. ). One well-supported mechanism is reduced uptake of asobiotics into bacterial cells. In E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and S. enterica, resistance to CPP-conjugated PNAs often involves mutations in the sbmA gene, which encodes a transporter essential for the internalization of PPNAs or PPMOs (Puckett et al. , Mulkern et al. ). Mutations in genes such as yaiW (OM protein) and ykfM (a predicted transmembrane protein) may also impair transport. The importance of uptake mechanisms is underscored by the observation that resistance varies depending on the CPP used; for instance, (RXR)4XB-conjugated PNAs exhibited lower frequencies of resistance compared to (KFF)₃K-conjugated PNAs, with resistance to the former primarily associated with mutations at the nucleotide binding site (Mulkern et al. ). Target site mutations represent another conserved resistance mechanism. In bacteria, mutations in the acpP mRNA sequence reduced the efficacy of acpP targeting PNA. Two central SNPs within the PNA binding site in E. coli were associated with modest increases in MIC (Mulkern et al. ). A study introducing mutations into the PPMO binding site in P. aeruginosa demonstrated that mismatches at the terminal ends of the PPMO-binding region have minimal impact on binding efficiency, whereas central mismatches substantially reduce binding affinity and therapeutic efficacy (Nanayakkara et al. ). However, these P. aeruginosa isolates harboring mutations in the PPMO-binding site exhibited no cross-resistance to conventional antibiotics and remained susceptible to alternative PPMOs—either targeting the mutated binding site or a different essential gene (Nanayakkara et al. ).
Most asobiotics rely on conjugation to cationic peptides for effective cellular uptake into bacterial cells (Good et al. , Geller et al. ). Cationic antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), which are generally short and positively charged, possess broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and have been extensively studied as potential alternatives to conventional antibiotics (Andersson et al. ). This overlap in structure and function means that resistance mechanisms evolved against AMPs may also compromise the efficacy of asobiotics. In Gram-negative bacteria, such resistance mechanisms often involve lipopolysaccharide (LPS) modifications that reduce the anionic charge of the OM—such as the addition of phosphoethanolamine or 4-aminoarabinose to lipid A—thus impairing electrostatic interactions with cationic peptides (Guan et al. ). However, this is likely not a universal finding as Daly et al. ( ) showed that PPMOs could enter polymyxin-resistant strains (Daly et al. ). In Gram-positive bacteria, resistance arises primarily through modifications of teichoic acids and membrane phospholipids, including d-alanylation and lysinylation, which reduce surface negative charge and peptide binding (Thedieck et al. , Li et al. , Brown et al. ). Additional intrinsic mechanisms include AMP efflux via transporters (e.g. VraFG) and proteolytic degradation by secreted proteases (Guina et al. , Li et al. ). Collectively, these mechanisms highlight the potential for bacterial resistance to asobiotics via conserved pathways of AMP evasion, emphasizing the need for careful design of delivery peptides and consideration of alternative uptake routes.
In conclusion, bacterial resistance studies on asobiotics highlight the importance of structural adaptations and target site mutations as key escape mechanisms. Furthermore, insights from viral resistance studies suggest that bacterial resistance may be broader and more complex than currently recognized, underscoring the need for proactive design strategies that anticipate both direct and indirect routes of resistance.

## Future scenarios and applications: asobiotics as pathogen specific therapy
The rise in worldwide antibacterial resistance is one of the leading public health crises to face our generation (Laxminarayan et al. ). This is particularly true for Gram-negative pathogens. In the 2019 updated Antibiotic Resistance Threat Report, the CDC continues to list pathogens, such as carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter as Urgent Threats. In addition, pathogens such as P. aeruginosa and Bcc are threats to both specific patient populations, such as those with CF, as well as hospitalized patients in general. These pathogens have become increasingly antibiotic resistant to multiple drugs leaving patients with few to no options for treatment.
The current pipeline for novel antimicrobials against these multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens remains narrow. The vast majority of traditional antibiotics fall within a few structural classes with a limited number of modes of action. Therefore, resistance mechanisms are often transferrable from older antibiotics to newer drugs. To limit the ease of cross-resistance evolution, the pipeline of new antimicrobials should favor those with unexploited mechanisms of action; asobiotics could be one way to rejuvenate the antibiotic space. Over the past 10 years several groups have made fundamental advances in the use of antisense molecules as pathogen-specific antibiotics. They contain numerous advantages over traditional small molecule antibiotics. These include: (1) their specificity allowing for both gene-specific and pathogen-specific design, (2) preclinical studies demonstrating activity both in vitro and in vivo, (3) similar potency in both drug-sensitive and MDR clinical strains, and (4) activity in biofilm and resistance mechanisms that are not related to the current main mechanisms of resistance seen in presently approved antibiotic classes. Indeed, a variety of the existing undruggable targets have been described demonstrating how asobiotics can simultaneously serve as a tool to discover new drugs targets but could ultimately be the drug themselves.
Although much work remains to get asobiotics into human clinical trials, there are a variety of clinical infections where pathogen-specific therapy would be a viable solution. This includes use in various chronic infections, such as pulmonary infections in those with CF or prosthetic implant infections (PJI). While potential toxicity with systemic administration requires further exploration, the possibility of targeted therapy, such as aerosolization to the lung, could be one viable delivery strategy, if systemic toxicity becomes a liability. There has been limited work on determining mechanisms of resistance, however, two likely possibilities include those related to entry into the bacterial cell as well as mutations in the gene target sequence. Both scenarios have potential solutions that could be successful circumventing strategies including combinations of asobiotics that either target multiple genes or in the case of entry defects, mixture of asobiotics that contain different delivery peptides. These both have drug-development challenges, however they also illustrate the power of antisense; namely the ability to rapidly design and synthesize compounds that are fundamentally the same chemical entity that differs only in the nucleotide composition of the oligomer. When asobiotics are compared to other pathogen-specific approaches, such as phage, several other differentiating factors emerge. This includes the observation that antisense design efforts usually succeed in finding gene targets that are completely homologous for any given species of pathogen. This means that a future asobiotic is likely to be active for any given genus and species that would be encountered clinically.
From a drug-development point of view, there remain some critical questions about how asobiotics will progress into the clinic. As one example, it remains unknown how regulatory agencies might view the development of follow on PPMOs against a specific pathogen after the first one has been approved. If the fundamental chemistry, oligomer length, and peptide do not change, what rigor of preclinical studies would be needed if only the nucleobases change? Although areas of study such as PK/PD of antibacterial PPMOs are currently ongoing, it is likely that PK/PD parameters for an 11-mer with the same peptide will not change because of nucleotide alterations. Other common questions surround the nature of developing pathogen-specific antibiotics and what diagnostic barriers might exist as we aim to rethink how we pursue antibiotic development. If we want to move to a world view where one would choose a pathogen-specific antibiotic over a broad-spectrum compound, what are the clinical infections where this would make sense? Are there diagnostic hurdles that must be overcome? It has become clear that as diagnostics become more rapid, there are now several clinical scenarios where pathogen-specific therapy could be deployed. We have already seen this for phage treatment in certain chronic infections (CF or PJI) where there is adequate time to identify the causal pathogen. Given the design features of PPMOs and the goal of finding targets that are universally conserved in a particular species, it is highly likely that asobiotics will be used in a variety of acute infections where rapid pathogen identification is already feasible. In addition, the recent approval of sulbactam–durlobactam for the treatment of documented A. baumannii pneumonia has paved the way for an approval pathway for pathogen-specific therapeutics. Given the rapidly changing landscape in both diagnostics and pathways to approve pathogen-specific antibiotics, the time has never been brighter for developing asobiotics for clinical use.
