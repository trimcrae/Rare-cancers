## Background
Super-enhancers (SEs), characterized by dense clusters of enhancer elements enriched with transcriptional activator binding sites, are involved in cell differentiation. However, little is known about SE-mediated regulation of adipogenic genes. The aim of this study was to elucidate the functional role of the KLF6-proximal SE during the adipogenesis of human adipose-derived stem cells (hADSCs).

## Methods
Adipogenic induction medium (AIM) was used for differentiation of hADSCs into adipocytes, which were evaluated for adipogenic gene expression and adipogenesis using quantitative PCR and Oil Red O (ORO) staining, respectively. The effects of SE inhibitors, locked nucleic acid-mediated enhancer RNA (eRNA) knockdown, and small interfering RNA-mediated knockdown of KLF6 on adipogenesis and adipogenic gene levels were evaluated. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were performed to identify transcriptional regulators binding to the promoter regions of KLF6 and the adipogenesis-inhibitory Delta-like non-canonical Notch ligand 1 (DLK1) gene during adipogenesis.

## Results
In silico screening identified KLF6 as an obesity-susceptibility gene associated with single-nucleotide polymorphisms and located within the domain of SE_00159, which was activated in adipocytes. AIM-cultured hADSCs exhibited time-dependent increases in KLF6 mRNA and protein expression. During adipogenesis, the transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) bound to the KLF6 promoter. Treatment with the SE inhibitor JQ1 resulted in a dose-dependent decrease in KLF6 mRNA expression and reduced ORO staining. Knockdown of eRNA expressed from the SE_00159 domain decreased KLF6 levels during adipogenesis. Consistently, KLF6 knockdown during hADSC adipogenesis downregulated the adipogenic genes PPARG and CEBPA, while upregulating DLK1. Additionally, KLF6 together with histone deacetylase (HDAC)3 bound to the DLK1 promoter and concomitantly caused dissociation of histone acetyltransferase p300 during adipogenesis.

## Conclusions
SE activation upregulates KLF6 through PPARγ/p300- and eRNA-mediated transcriptional induction during hADSC adipogenesis. KLF6, in turn, represses DLK1 expression through recruitment of HDAC3 to the promoter and p300 dissociation, thereby facilitating adipocyte differentiation. These findings support a working model in which the epigenetic regulation of KLF6 and DLK1 as a potential therapeutic axis in human obesity.

## Background
Age-related obesity is generally defined as a body mass index of 30 or higher, and currently affects over 2 billion people worldwide [ ]. Adipose tissue is classified into physiological and ectopic fat, both of which contribute to increased risk of insulin resistance and other metabolic disorders. Furthermore, adipocytes, which constitute the majority of adipose tissue, secrete adipokines that influence immune cell behavior. Metabolic inflammation reportedly involves a range of immune cell types, including dendritic cells, T cells, and B cells [ ].
Excessive fat accumulation primarily results from adipocyte hypertrophy due to overeating and epigenetic regulation driving the differentiation of progenitor adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) into adipocytes. The transcription factors PPARγ and CCAAT enhancer binding protein alpha (C/EBPα) play central roles in the latter process [ ], forming a cooperative feed-forward transcriptional circuit essential for adipocyte differentiation [ ]. By mutually reinforcing each other’s expression, they activate adipocyte-specific genes such as fatty acid transfer protein (FATP1), adiponectin (ADIPOQ), and fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4), which are crucial for lipid accumulation and adipocyte function. In PPARγ-deficient cells, C/EBPα alone cannot induce adipogenesis, whereas PPARγ activation can induce adipocyte formation even in the absence of C/EBPα [ ]. In vivo, PPARγ is indispensable for adipose tissue development; by contrast, C/EBPα-deficient mice still generate fat tissue, albeit with metabolic defects [ ]. Such findings indicate that PPARγ is the primary driver of adipogenesis, with C/EBPα mainly functioning to sustain PPARγ expression and maintain insulin sensitivity in mature adipocytes [ ]. Taken together, these factors orchestrate a transcriptional network [ ] governing adipogenic differentiation. Beyond PPARγ and C/EBPα, diverse regulatory mechanisms have emerged, including histone (de)methylation and (de)acetylation, DNA methylation, microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and super-enhancers (SEs) [ , , ].
SEs are DNA regions comprising clusters of multiple enhancers and are enriched with bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) and cyclin-dependent kinase 7 (CDK7), which are responsible for recruiting and activating RNA polymerase II via phosphorylation [ ]. Accumulating evidence points to the critical role that SEs play in cell type-specific development and differentiation, as well as in the development of various diseases [ ]. However, little is known about the SE-mediated regulation of adipogenic genes.
Under appropriate in vitro stimulation, ADSCs can differentiate not only into adipocytes but also into chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and myoblasts. Co-stimulation with 5‑Aza‑2′‑deoxycytidine and interleukin (IL)−6/soluble IL-6 receptor has been reported to induce differentiation into fast-twitch myoblasts [ ]. Notably, ADSCs possess the potential to be directed toward beneficial cell lineages that suppress adipogenesis, thereby potentially improving functional capacity and activities of daily living.
Krüppel-like factors (KLFs), a group of evolutionarily conserved zinc finger transcription factors, are important for pro- and anti-adipogenic pathways, as well as adipose tissue function and metabolic homeostasis [ ]. Eighteen KLF family members (KLF1–18) are present in mammals and exhibit diverse tissue-specific expression and functions [ ]. All KLFs possess three zinc finger DNA-binding domains at their carboxyl termini, along with transcriptional activation or repression domains at their amino termini [ ]. Although human KLF9 promotes adipogenesis of hADSCs by activating the CEBPA/B–PPARG pathway [ , ], little is known about the adipogenic role of human KLF6.
During embryogenesis and tissue morphogenesis, KLF6 is expressed in multiple organs (e.g., eyes, kidneys, and prostate), underscoring its pivotal role in developmental regulation [ ]. Notably, mice lacking KLF6 exhibit embryonic lethality by day 12.5 owing to severe impairments in hematopoiesis and yolk sac vascularization [ ]. KLF6 is also implicated in diverse physiological processes, including cell differentiation and proliferation, immune regulation, lipid homeostasis, and tissue repair [ – ], underscoring its functional relevance beyond embryonic development.
DLK1 is highly expressed during the proliferation phase of human ADSCs and declines during adipogenic differentiation. In contrast, knockdown of DLK1 during this process significantly upregulates the key adipogenic regulator PPARγ2 and the terminal adipogenic marker FABP4, indicating that DLK1 acts as a negative regulator of adipogenesis [ ]. Recent studies have shown that interferon-related developmental regulator 1 (Ifrd1) regulates Wnt signaling, a negative regulator of adipogenesis, thereby controlling Dlk1 transcription [ ].
In this study, we employed in silico analyses to identify characteristic target genes regulated by SEs among numerous factors implicated in obesity genetics. Furthermore, we aimed to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms governing their expression and functional roles during hADSC adipogenesis, with the ultimate goal of uncovering promising therapeutic targets.

## Materials and methods

## In silico analysis
The dbSUPER database (https://asntech.org/dbsuper/) contains 82,234 SEs across 102 human and 25 mouse tissue and cell types. A total of 743 obesity-susceptibility genes were identified through a genome-wide association study (GWAS) involving 6,059 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with high prevalence in individuals with obesity, as previously reported [ ].

## Cell culture
Three distinct lots of hADSCs, detailed in Supplementary Table S1 (Additional File 1), were purchased from Lonza (PT-5006, USA) and used within 3–4 passages for experiments. According to the manufacturer, the hADSCs were positive for CD13, CD29, CD44, CD73, CD90, CD105, and CD166, and negative for CD14, CD31, and CD45. The cells were cultured at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere using mesenchymal stem cell growth medium (GM; C-28009, Takara, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin–streptomycin, with slight modifications [ ]. Confluence was maintained at < 90% to prevent spontaneous differentiation. Adipogenesis of hADSCs was induced as previously described [ ]. Briefly, hADSCs at 90% confluence were trypsinized and seeded into Falcon 12 well-plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. The cells were then cultured in commercial adipogenic induction medium (AIM; C-28016, Takara, Japan) for 21 days, with medium replacement every 3 days. Cells were harvested at specified time points and centrifuged to collect pellets for subsequent analyses.

## Public ChIP-seq data analysis
Publicly available ChIP-seq datasets were obtained from the ENCODE project and used for visualization of regulatory regions. For analysis of the KLF6 locus, imputed H3K27ac signal tracks generated by the ENCODE ChromImpute pipeline (version 1.0.3) were used. These ENCODE-processed signal tracks were visualized using genome browser tools, and accession IDs and axis labels are provided in the corresponding figure panels.

## Reagents and PCR primers
Primers were purchased from Thermo Fisher (USA) for TaqMan-based quantitative PCR and from Eurofins (Luxembourg) for chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-PCR, and are listed in Supplementary Table S2 (Additional File 2). Details of the locked nucleic acid (LNA) GapmeR sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3 (Additional File 3). The other reagents are listed in Supplementary Table S4 (Additional File 4).

## Transfection of hADSCs with small interfering RNA (siRNA)
Four siRNAs (si Control, si KLF6#1, si KLF6#2, and si KLF12) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Japan) and transfected as previously described, with slight modifications [ ]. Briefly, hADSCs were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in GM, then treated with 25 pmol of siRNA and 4 μL Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778075, Invitrogen) diluted in 250 μL Opti-MEM (31985062, Invitrogen) for 24 h at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The siRNA transfection was repeated at 24-h intervals for two additional cycles, with replacement of fresh GM prior to each transfection. After three successive transfections, the medium was replaced with fresh AIM and the culture was continued under the same conditions.

## SE inhibitor treatment of hADSCs
BRD4 inhibitor JQ1 (A1910) and CDK7 inhibitor THZ1 (A8882) were purchased from APExBIO (USA). The hADSCs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in GM, and then treated with JQ1 (0.1–1.0µM) or THZ1 (0.1–1.0µM) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide for 24 h, as previously described [ ]. The cells were harvested for quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (RT-qPCR) analyses and stained with Oil Red O (ORO) at specified time points.

## ORO staining
ORO staining to evaluate adipogenesis was performed as previously described, with a slight modification [ ]. Briefly, hADSCs were cultured in AIM for 21 days to induce adipogenic differentiation, and then fixed with 1% formalin at room temperature for 20 min. After two rinses with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 1000 μL of 60% isopropyl alcohol was added to each well, incubated for 1 min, and then removed. ORO staining was performed on days 3, 10, and 21 of adipogenesis using ORG500 Solution (ScyTek, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were then rinsed twice with PBS and observed under a microscope. Lipid accumulation was quantified using ImageJ software [ ].

## RT-qPCR
RNA extraction and RT-qPCR were performed as previously described [ ]. Briefly, cultured cells were lysed in RLT buffer (Qiagen, Germany) and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was conducted on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative transcript levels were analyzed using the 2−△△Ct method and normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) expression.

## ChIP-PCR
ChIP-PCR was performed as previously described [ ]. Briefly, hADSCs were cultured in AIM for 4 days, starved for 1 day, and then stimulated with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) or AIM for 20 min. Chromatin was crosslinked by treatment with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min and fragmented (200–500 bp) by sonication. DNA was extracted using the EZ ChIP Kit (Merck Millipore, Germany), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. DNA was immunoprecipitated using a series of antibodies. PCR amplification was performed using specific primers listed in Supplementary Table S2 (Additional File 2).

## Western blotting
Western blotting was carried out as previously described [ ]. Briefly, hADSC pellets harvested at the indicated time points were sonicated (30 s on/30 s off, 5 cycles) in lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 1% Nonidet P-40, supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (A-0014–20, ITSI Biosciences). The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected as whole-cell lysates (WCLs). Equal amounts of WCL protein (10 μg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on a 4%–20% Tris–glycine gel and transferred to a 0.2-μm nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare). Immunoblotting was performed using primary antibodies followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. β-actin served as the loading control.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Following one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA, post-hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Dunnett’s multiple comparison test to compare each siRNA group (si KLF6#1, si KLF6#2, and si KLF12) with the si Control group at the corresponding time points. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## In silico mining for obesity-susceptibility genes proximal to SEs
To identify obesity susceptibility genes regulated by SEs, we retrieved SE-proximal genes from the dbSUPER database in two adipose contexts. This yielded 728 genes in adipose nuclei and 69 in adipose tissue, with 44 overlapping between datasets. Separately, mapping 6,059 obesity-associated GWAS sentinel SNPs to their nearest genes identified 743 candidate genes. Intersecting the 44 overlapping genes from dbSUPER with the GWAS-derived set identified two SE-proximal obesity susceptibility genes in human adipocytes: KLF6 and KLF9 (Fig. A). Because the adipogenic role of KLF6, but not KLF9, remains largely unexplored in humans, we focused on KLF6 for downstream validation. This gene is located within SE_00159 on chromosome 10 and positioned near the obesity-associated SNP rs12253976 (G/T) (Fig. B). 

## KLF6 expression dynamics during hADSC adipogenesis
KLF9, which served as a positive control for adipogenic gene expression, showed increased expression over time during the adipogenesis of hADSCs cultured in AIM (Fig. A). In contrast, expression of KLF6 gradually increased, peaked at day 10, and decreased thereafter (Fig. B), whereas expression of KLF12, which lacks a nearby SE and was used as a control, remained unchanged (Fig. C). Consistently, KLF6 protein levels also increased in a time-dependent manner under AIM culture conditions (Fig. D and Additional File 4). To examine whether KLF6 is also upregulated in other differentiation pathways, hADSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium (OIM) to induce osteogenesis. Expression of RUNX family transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) increased over time, confirming osteogenic differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S1A), while KLF6 expression remained unchanged from days 1–18 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). In contrast, hADSCs cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 5-azaC (10 μM) to induce myoblasts showed increased expression of MYOD at day 18, confirming myogenic differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S2A), while KLF6 expression remained unchanged from days 1–18 (Supplementary Fig. S2B). 

## KLF6 is involved in the regulation of adipogenic genes during adipogenesis of human ADSCs
Following transfection of hADSCs with KLF6-targeted siRNAs, the cells were cultured in AIM to induced adipogenesis and collected for RT-qPCR analysis of KLF6/12, PPARG, CEBPA, and FABP4 expression at days 1, 4, 10, and 18 post-transfection. Compared with the negative control siRNA, hADSCs treated with KLF6-targeted siRNA showed a significant decrease in KLF6 expression, but not KLF12 expression. Suppression of KLF6 expression to ~ 40%–50% of the control siRNA level persisted on days 1, 4, and 18 after transfection with KLF6-targeting siRNAs, whereas this suppression was not observed on day 10, suggesting incomplete or reversible gene silencing (Fig. A). KLF6 protein was downregulated during ADSCs adipogenesis in si KLF6 (#1 and #2) groups compared to si Control group (Supplementary Fig. S3). Furthermore, the expression levels of PPARG, its target gene FABP4, and CEBPA were significantly reduced under KLF6 knockdown on post-transfection days 1, 4, and 18, but not on day 10 (Fig. B). In contrast, while hADSCs transfected with KLF12-targeted siRNA exhibited KLF12 suppression relative to the control on post-transfection day 1 (Fig. A), the expression levels of PPARG, CEBPA, and FABP4 were unaffected (Fig. B). Next, hADSCs transfected with the same siRNAs followed by culture in AIM for 21 days were analyzed by ORO staining. Both the control and KLF12 knockdown groups exhibited strong ORO staining, whereas the KLF6#1 and KLF6#2 knockdown groups showed significantly reduced staining relative to the control group (Fig. C and D). 

## PPARγ binds the KLF6 promoter during hADSC adipogenesis
During adipogenic differentiation of hADSCs, PPARG expression increased in parallel with KLF6, suggesting that PPARγ may transactivate KLF6 transcription at the promoter level. Using the TRANSFAC database (http://www.gene-regulation.com), we identified a putative PPAR response element (PPRE) at position − 4780 to − 4655 within the KLF6 promoter region (Fig. A). To determine whether PPARγ directly binds to the predicted PPRE during human adipogenic differentiation, we performed ChIP assays. Under GM conditions, PPARγ exhibited weak binding to this region, whereas AIM stimulation markedly enhanced its occupancy (Fig. B and Additional File 5). Furthermore, under AIM stimulation for 20 min on day 4 of adipogenesis, the histone acetyltransferase p300, but not CREB-binding protein (CBP), was recruited to the same KLF6 promoter region. In parallel, histone 3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac)—a marker of active transcription—was elevated in this region, whereas H3K4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) remained unchanged. 

## SE inhibitors and enhancer RNA (eRNA)-targeted LNA downregulate KLF6 during hADSC adipogenesis
Given that SEs are often enriched with BRD4 and CDK7, we sought to evaluate the functional relevance of SE activation in human adipocytes (Fig. B), by stimulating hADSCs with the BRD4 inhibitor JQ1 or the CDK7 inhibitor THZ prior to adipogenic induction. Stimulation with JQ1 (1.0, 0.3, 0.1 μM) suppressed the expression of KLF6 and KLF9 in a dose-dependent manner, with no effect on KLF12 (Fig. A, C, and E). THZ1 downregulated all three genes in a dose-dependent manner, with particularly strong suppression of KLF6 and KLF9 (Fig. B, D, and F). For both inhibitors, no significant time-dependent changes in expression were observed at any time point across the KLF genes. 
Using the CAGE dataset, we identified a transcribed H3K27ac peak upstream of KLF6 in human adipocytes, which was present both in hADSCs and during ADSC adipogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Next, we demonstrated that eRNA expression from this peak gradually increased during hADSC adipogenesis, reached a peak at day 10, and then declined (Supplementary Fig. S4B). To assess the functional relevance of SE_00159 activation in human adipocytes, we transfected hADSCs with LNA targeting the sense (eRNA_S) and antisense (eRNA_AS) strands prior to adipogenic induction. Compared with negative control LNA, both eRNA_S- and eRNA_AS-targeted LNA significantly reduced expression of eRNA and KLF6, but not KLF9 and KLF12, at day 1, 4, 10, and 18 (Supplementary Fig. S5A, S5B, and S5C). The eRNA dynamics was unchanged in hADSCs cultured in OIM to induce osteogenesis and in DMEM + 5-azaC to induce myogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S1C and S2C).

## KLF6 downregulates Delta-like non-canonical Notch ligand (DLK1) by binding its promoter during hADSC adipogenesis
Using the same experimental conditions as Fig. A, RT-qPCR analysis revealed an inverse expression pattern between KLF6 and DLK1, an adipogenesis inhibitory gene, with DLK1 levels rising and KLF6 levels declining between day 1 and day 18, but not on day 10, during adipogenesis (Fig. A). Compared with negative control LNA, both eRNA_S- and eRNA_AS-targeted LNA significantly induced expression of DLK1 at day 1 and day 4 (Supplementary Fig. S5C). 
Complementing these findings, two putative KLF6 binding sites, namely GC-boxes (GGCGGGG), were identified in the proximal upstream region of the DLK1 promoter (Fig. B). In silico analysis suggested that KLF6 may regulate DLK1 transcription at the promoter level. Upon stimulation with AIM for 20 min, KLF6 and histone deacetylase HDAC3 were recruited to the GC-box region on day 4 during hADSC adipogenesis, whereas levels of the active mark histone 3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac) and p300 occupancy were reduced (Fig. C and Additional File 6).

## SE inhibitors suppress hADSC adipogenesis
Positive ORO staining was observed in hADSCs cultured in AIM, but not in GM, for 21 days (Fig. A and B). However, treatment of AIM-cultured cells with JQ1 reduced staining intensity at 0.3 μM, with a further significant decrease at 1.0 μM (Fig. C). The THZ1-treated group showed consistently weak ORO staining across the concentration range of 0.1 to 1.0 μM (Fig. D). Quantitative analysis using ImageJ confirmed that both JQ1 and THZ1 significantly suppressed lipid droplet formation during hADSC adipogenesis (Fig. E). The cytotoxicity of JQ1 and THZ1 was assessed as follows. JQ1 treatment (0.1–1.0 μM), but not THZ1 (0.1–1.0 μM), did not significantly change CCK-8 absorbance at either 24 h or 48 h compared with vehicle, indicating that JQ1, but not THZ1, had no detectable effect on cellular metabolic activity/viability under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. S6A and S6B). During non-adipogenic lineage commitment, we first analyzed the expression dynamics of the candidate eRNA. No significant temporal changes were observed under either osteogenic induction medium (OIM) or myogenic conditions (DMEM + 5-AzaC), indicating that this eRNA is not induced during osteogenic or myogenic differentiation (Supplementary Figures. S1C and S2C). We next assessed the impact of super-enhancer inhibition on lineage-marker expression. Under osteogenic conditions, JQ1 significantly suppressed RUNX2 mRNA at the indicated time points (0–24 h) in a dose-dependent manner, whereas KLF6 expression remained largely unaffected, suggesting that RUNX2 suppression by JQ1 is independent of KLF6. In myogenic cultures, JQ1 treatment did not elicit consistent dose-dependent changes in KLF6 or MyoD expression, with both genes showing similar temporal patterns in vehicle- and JQ1-treated cells. 

## Discussion
Using in silico mining, we identified KLF6, an obesity-susceptibility gene proximal to an SE activated in human adipocytes (Fig. ). During hADSC adipogenesis, KLF6 expression progressively increased (Fig. B and D), accompanied by PPARγ and p300 binding to the KLF6 promoter and enrichment of H3K27ac (Fig. B). Inhibition of SEs marked by H3K27ac and eRNA knockdown both lowered KLF6 expression (Fig. A, B, and Supplementary Fig. S4A, S4C, and S4D). In turn, knockdown of KLF6 upregulated DLK1, which encodes an anti-adipogenic factor (Fig. A), and both KLF6 and HDAC3 were recruited to the DLK1 promoter (Fig. C). SE inhibition also suppressed lipid droplet formation (Fig. C–E). Collectively, these findings support a pivotal role for the PPARγ–SE–KLF6–DLK1 axis in regulating hADSC adipogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S7).
Although the role of KLF6 in human adipogenesis remains unclear, our data demonstrated that KLF6 knockdown in hADSCs markedly reduces expression of PPARG, CEBPA, and the PPARγ target gene FABP4 (Fig. B). Given that PPARγ and C/EBPα are indispensable for adipogenesis, their downregulation likely accounts for the impaired adipocyte formation observed upon KLF6 silencing, with insufficient expression of these factors disrupting the transcriptional cascade required for differentiation (Fig. C, D). Furthermore, PPARγ was found to directly bind the human KLF6 promoter as part of a PPARγ/p300 complex (Fig. B), while KLF6 reportedly transactivates PPARγ and C/EBPα in mice [ ]. Collectively, these findings suggest KLF6 as well as PPARγ are indispensable for human ADSC adipogenesis. However, the hierarchical relationship between KLF6 and PPARγ during adipogenesis has not yet been fully elucidated. Further investigation will be necessary to determine whether KLF6 or PPARγ acts as an upstream regulator in this process.
Concurrently, KLF6 knockdown upregulated the anti-adipogenic gene DLK1 during adipogenic differentiation (Fig. A) and reduced lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. C and D). These findings suggest that KLF6 may serve as a promising target for obesity suppression. Interestingly, KLF6-mediated downregulation of Dlk1 has also been reported in mouse 3T3-L1 adipogenesis [ ], raising the possibility that the molecular mechanism elucidated here may be conserved across species.
Andreoli et al. demonstrated that mouse KLF6 targets the p21 gene, with accumulation of cells expressing high levels of p21 in adipose tissue from obese mice [ ]. Intermittent depletion of these cells was shown to both prevent and ameliorate insulin resistance. These pathological subsets observed in vivo may correspond to the adipocyte subpopulations identified in vitro in this study. Meanwhile, the possible involvement of human KLF6 in fat metabolism and mature adipocytes remains unclear. Raza et al. reported that suppressing KLF6 expression in bovine adipocytes reduced lipid synthesis, implicating KLF6 in intracellular lipid accumulation and adipocyte hypertrophy [ ]. Given that excessive lipid storage promotes insulin resistance and elevates blood glucose levels, targeting KLF6 in both differentiated adipocytes and preadipocytes such as ADSCs may represent a promising strategy for improving glycemic control. Taken together, these findings highlight the multifaceted role of KLF6 in obesity metabolism. Further investigation into the functions of KLF6 during in vitro ADSC adipogenesis and in vivo adipose tissue dynamics is warranted.
At the molecular level, both KLF6 and the transcriptional corepressor HDAC3 were recruited to the DLK1 promoter during human adipogenesis, coinciding with dissociation of co-activator p300 and reduced levels of the active histone mark H3K9ac (Fig. C). These findings suggest a novel molecular switch critical for adipogenesis, potentially driven by KLF6-mediated transcriptional regulation of target genes. Anticipating the therapeutic relevance of KLF6 and its regulatory factors in obesity, we subsequently investigated the upstream regulation of KLF6. Notably, KLF6 knockdown led to reduced PPARG expression, suggesting that KLF6 may enhance PPARG transcription. Conversely, the PPARγ/p300 complex was found to bind directly to the KLF6 promoter (Fig. B), indicating reciprocal regulation. Taken together, these findings indicate the existence of a positive feedback loop in which KLF6 and PPARγ mutually reinforce adipogenic differentiation of hADSCs.
In parallel, we identified an additional regulatory mechanism involving SE activity. p300 binds to the KLF6 promoter, which is enriched with H3K27ac, a hallmark of SEs (Fig. B). A key novelty of this work was the identification of SE_00159, which governs KLF6 expression in hADSCs. SEs are known to control genes that are crucial for cell identity [ ], and our data support the notion that KLF6 is one such gene in the context of adipose lineage commitment. The KLF6 locus lies within the SE_00159 domain—a defined adipocyte SE in dbSUPER (Fig. B)—and was downregulated by SE inhibitor treatment (Fig. A and B). In line with our findings, recent epigenomic studies have shown that SE landscapes are dynamically reconfigured during mesenchymal stem cell differentiation into adipocytes [ ]. Notably, both eRNA_S and eRNA_AS transcribed from SE_00159 were found to positively regulate KLF6 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Collectively, these findings indicate that KLF6 is tightly regulated by SEs, particularly SE_00159, which may represent a therapeutic target for suppressing adipogenesis. Taken together, our data highlight an epigenetic mechanism whereby an adipocyte-specific SE promotes adipogenesis by elevating KLF6 levels, reinforcing the pro-adipogenic transcriptional program (PPARγ/CEBPα) and repressing anti-adipogenic signals such as DLK1.
Ectopic fat deposition commonly manifests as hepatic steatosis (fatty liver), intramuscular fat (fatty infiltration of muscle), and lipomas. Intramuscular fat contains ADSCs, which serve as precursors for both muscle cells and adipocytes. Indeed, we previously demonstrated that IL-6 signaling promotes hADSC differentiation into myoblasts [ ]. In the present study, the expression dynamics of KLF6 remained unchanged during hADSC myogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S2), yet functional analysis revealed a potent adipogenic role for KLF6. These findings underscore the importance of integrating gene expression profiling with functional assays to elucidate molecular roles. Therefore, it would be of interest to explore whether targeting KLF6 influences myogenic lineage commitment in ADSCs.
One limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size of hADSCs (n = 3 independent lots), which may affect the generalizability of the findings. To more rigorously define the role of KLF6 in ADSC adipogenic differentiation, further studies employing a larger cohort will be required. Verification in animal models or human tissue will also be essential because the complex endocrine and mechanical cues present in vivo cannot be fully recapitulated in vitro. Additionally, in order to clarify the association between the KLF6 gene expression and the obesity-associated SNP rs12253976 (G/T), it will be necessary to perform reporter assays incorporating allele-specific regions, CRISPR–Cas9–mediated deletion of the SNP, and large-scale analyses of allele-specific KLF6 expression data.

## Conclusions
Our findings support a working model in which KLF6 expression is significantly upregulated during adipogenic differentiation by PPARγ and SE activity, including transcription of eRNA, acts as a key facilitator of hADSC adipogenesis by repressing the anti-adipogenic gene DLK1. These results highlight the PPARγ–SE–KLF6 axis as a promising therapeutic target for obesity intervention.
