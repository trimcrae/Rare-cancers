## Summary
Drug delivery vehicles increase cell permeability of their cargo, but otherwise are expected to be inert. In developing a potential strategy to treat myotonic dystrophy type 1, we developed a delivery vehicle that was designed to be a direct acting drug. The oligomeric delivery agent selectively targets the expanded DNA and RNA repeat sequences (d(CTG)exp and r(CUG)exp) that cause DM1. By forming polyplexes with a locked nucleic acid CAG16 gapmer, that targets r(CUG)exp, the polyplex facilitates cellular uptake of the LNA in HeLa cells, DM1 patient myoblasts, and the skeletal muscle of DM1 mice leading to phenotypic improvements in the in vitro DM1 models. Although the in vivo results are mixed, the overall results suggest the potential of a new strategy where both drug and delivery agent actively target a disease pathology.

## Introduction
Drug delivery vehicles offer an exciting strategy for increasing the amount of therapeutic agent reaching its target. This strategy has become increasingly important with the recent rise of DNA- and RNA-targeted therapies. In particular, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), siRNA, and CRISPR/Cas9 modalities are powerful ways to effect gene editing or silencing, but often suffer from low cellular uptake. To improve uptake and targeting, efforts have focused on delivery systems, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and nanocapsules. , , , However, even in the best circumstance where the excipient has no toxicity, the approach often involves low drug loading. More commonly, excipient toxicity is a significant issue. We describe a new strategy involving a combination therapy wherein a delivery vehicle to increase ASO cellular uptake is itself an active agent. In particular, we describe a series of oligomeric ligands that have been developed to serve both as drug molecules and as ASO delivery vehicles to treat myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1).
DM1 belongs to a class of currently incurable neuromuscular diseases that originate from unstable repeating sequences within the genome. In the case of DM1, it is the trinucleotide (CTG)·(CAG) repeat in the 3ʹ-untranslated region of the DMPK gene on chromosome 19 that undergoes progressive expansion, reaching thousands of added repeats in the most extreme cases. The transcription product, r(CUG)exp, is known to form secondary hairpin structures that can cause toxicity through multiple mechanisms. For instance, the r(CUG)exp can sequester splicing regulators, including the muscleblind-like (MBNL) proteins leading to the mis-splicing of hundreds of pre-mRNAs ( A). The toxicity of r(CUG)exp is further associated with repeat-associated non-ATG (RAN) translation and a loss of function of the Mef2 transcriptional network. 
RNA has emerged as an important therapeutic target for small molecules, , with DM1 efforts focusing on small molecule inhibition of MBNL1 sequestration by r(CUG)exp hairpin structures. Our group , and others , , , , have developed ligands that can bind to r(CUG)exp and inhibit protein sequestration rescuing splicing of the affected pre-mRNAs. Alongside the development of small molecules as well as gene editing technologies, several other therapeutic strategies for DM1 have been studied, including ASOs, , RNA interference (RNAi), RNA-targeting nuclease-dead Cas9, and AntagomiRs. The ASO approach was sufficiently promising that it advanced to human clinical trials but was ultimately halted because of low agent concentration in muscle cells and low efficacy. Generally development efforts toward improved delivery have focused on transfection reagents such as polyethylenimine (PEI) and Lipofectamine for in vitro delivery and lipid nanoparticles and bioconjugation to peptides or antibodies for in vivo delivery. , , , For example, Klein et al., recently reported ASO delivery using Pip6a peptide conjugate resulting in significant correction of DM1 phenotypes in HSA-LR mice. 
We recently reported an oligomeric mixture of cationic oligomeric ligands (see 3, A) that target d(CTG)exp to inhibit transcription and r(CUG)exp to release sequestered MBNL1 proteins. Here, we describe a set of multitarget agents that are structurally analogous, but capable of being prepared as discrete oligomers. In addition to testing the oligomers, we further explore whether they might, at higher concentrations, form polyplexes with a DM1-targeting ASO and deliver it inside cells. This strategy could be viewed as achieving 100% drug loading efficiency because one drug delivers another, and both target the same disease pathology. 

## Results

## Design of multitargeting discrete oligomer as active agent and ASO delivery agent
Our overall approach sought oligomeric ligands that would intervene in the DM1 pathogenic mechanism in three ways: (1) target d(CTG)exp to inhibit its transcription, (2) target r(CUG)exp to inhibit MBNL1 sequestration, and (3) deliver an ASO for RNase-mediated cleavage of r(CUG)exp ( B). Our group previously reported two classes of rationally designed ligands (e.g., 1 and 2) that can selectively bind to r(CUG)exp and inhibit MBNL1 protein sequestration ( A). , The triaminotriazine ring serves as a selective recognition moiety for U-U mismatches ( B). An oligomeric mixture of ligand 3 containing 4-mer to 8-mers was found to target both d(CTG)exp and r(CUG)exp in a multivalent fashion by virtue of its repetitive structure with triaminotriazine rings alternating with bisamidinium groove binders.
Efforts to separate the oligomeric mixture 3 or to prepare the corresponding discrete oligomers were unsuccessful so the more readily prepared oligomeric ligands 4–8 ( C and 2D) were designed with the same triaminotriazine ring as a recognition moiety, but alternating with two guanidinium groups separated by four methylene groups in place of the bisamidinium unit. The oligo-guanidinium structure was expected to afford high binding affinity toward d(CTG) and r(CUG) repeats through strong electrostatic interactions with the phosphate backbone and selective hydrogen bonding of the U-U mismatches. 

## Computational docking of oligomer 6 to r[(CUG)7]2
The crystal structure of r[(CUG)6]2 was obtained from Protein DataBank (code 3GM7) and extended to r[(CUG)7]2 using the Molecular Operating Environment’s “Build” feature to provide a sufficiently long binding site for 6 (see Methods section for details). Oligomer 6 was docked on r[(CUG)7]2 with triaminotriazine units on alternating U-U mismatches based on the results of previously reported 2. Oligomer 6 was docked in the major groove of the RNA duplex. Each triaminotriazine ring formed three hydrogen bonds with one U base with the other U base flipped out ( A–S1C). A single hydrogen bond between the triaminotriazine ring and the phosphate backbone of the flipped U base was observed. The guanidinium groups fit in the major groove of the helix and interact with the phosphate backbone. The length of the methylene chain was optimized through molecular docking studies in MOE so that the linker was long enough to span between U-U mismatches, but not so long that it required an unfavorable entropic cost. The computational docking of ligand 6 to d[(CTG)7]2 suggests a similar binding mode of ligand 6 to d(CTG)exp ( D–S1F).

## Synthesis of oligomeric ligands
Discrete oligomers 5–8 were prepared in an iterative fashion using a guanidinylation reaction of a primary amine and an isothiouronium group (see ). Monomer 4 was prepared by reaction of N2,N4-bis(4-aminobutyl)-1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triamine with 1H-pyrazole-1-carboximidamide to form the guanidinium groups. All ligands were purified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on a reversed phase C18 column and characterized by MALDI MS, 1H NMR and 13C NMR (see Methods section and and ).

## Binding affinity of oligomeric ligands to CTG and CUG repeats
To assess the binding affinity, multivalent effect, and selectivity of oligomeric ligands toward d(CTG)exp and r(CUG)exp, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments were performed with 4, 6, and 8. It was found that monomer 4 bound to d(CTG)16 with a high micromolar affinity (Kd = 900 ± 100 μM). Consistent with the notion that increasing the length of a multivalent ligand increases affinity, 6 featured a low micromolar Kd = 2.4 ± 0.7 μM and 8 exhibited a nanomolar Kd = 40 ± 20 nM ( A and ). The oligomeric ligands gave binding affinities toward r(CUG)16 that similarly followed this trend: monomer 4 bound to r(CUG)16 only weakly (Kd > 1 mM), whereas 6 (Kd = 3 ± 1 μM) and 8 (Kd = 40 ± 20 nM) had much stronger binding affinities ( ). The binding of these oligomeric ligands to random DNA duplex was very weak, with minimal heat generated as observed by ITC ( and ). Although these data suggest highly selective binding to the target, one would expect the polycationic nature of the ligands to provide some nonspecific electrostatic interactions. As described below, indeed, the oligomer is able under the appropriate salt conditions to form polyplexes with random sequence DNA.

## In vitro transcription inhibition of oligomeric ligands
To examine the potential of these oligomeric ligands to inhibit the transcription of d(CTG)exp, in vitro transcription assays were conducted with a T7 promoter located in the upstream region of a (CTG·CAG)90-containing plasmid. Ligand 8 strongly inhibited the production of (CUG)90 and did so in a dose-dependent manner ( B and 3C). Nearly 100% inhibition was achieved upon treatment with 8 at 500 nM. 
Control experiments with a plasmid containing a random sequence were performed to test the selectivity of ligand 8. No significant inhibition of the transcription was observed at working concentrations of 8 with the control plasmid ( B and 3C). The shorter oligomer 6 also showed selective in vitro transcription inhibition with dose-dependent inhibition seen with (CTG·CAG)90 but not the random sequence ( B). In this case, a higher concentration was needed with 10 μM of ligand 6 required for full inhibition. These results are consistent with the ITC data where the extended oligomer 8 has a higher affinity for the target compared to 6.

## Cell uptake of oligomer 8
Cell permeability of oligomeric ligand 8 was investigated with confocal microscopy to visualize the ligand inside live cells. Ligand 8 was fluorescently labeled with rhodamine B (RhB) and incubated with HeLa cells for 4 h at 37°C. Fluorescence was observed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, indicating cell permeability of the oligomeric ligands ( A). Lysotracker Green DND-26 (Invitrogen) was utilized to investigate the mechanism of cell penetration. The Lysotracker Green fluorescence showed both overlapping and non-overlapping regions of fluorescence with the RhB fluorescence from 8 ( B). The presence of 8 both inside and outside of lysosomes/endosomes is consistent with its design as a cell-penetrating mimic and ability to enter the cell by endocytosis. No disruption of the cell surface was observed in any of the experiments. The cellular uptake of ligand 8 was quantified through HPLC analysis of cell lysates. The cells were incubated with ligand 8 at 1 μM for 1 day before the cells were harvested and lysed. The cell lysate was analyzed by HPLC and the concentration of ligand 8 was determined to be 370 ± 90 nM by using the standard curve of ligand 8 ( ). This value suggests a significant amount of 8 was taken up by the cells.

## Bioactivity of oligomeric ligands in DM1 cell culture model
The bioactivity of oligomeric ligands 4–8 was evaluated in a standard DM1 model cell culture produced by first transfecting HeLa cells with a plasmid containing DMPK exons 11−15, with 960 CTG repeats in exon 15 (DT960) and then a plasmid containing the insulin receptor (IR) pre-mRNA. , A control cell culture was similarly produced with a DMPK-containing plasmid with 0 CTG repeats inserted (DT0) and the IR minigene. MBNL1 sequestration in the DT960 cells led to inclusion of exon 11 for IR pre-mRNA of around 30%, compared to 53% in DT0 cells. Ligands 4–8 were observed to rescue the IR pre-mRNA mis-splicing defect in DT960-transfected HeLa cells in a dose-dependent manner ( ). As the oligomeric ligands became longer, lower concentrations were able to achieve full rescue. For monomer 4, 47% of exon 11 inclusion was achieved at 100 μM, and the same degree of rescue was achieved with 40 μM of oligomer 5. Full rescue (53% of exon 11 inclusion) was observed with 10 μM of 6, 5 μM of 7, and 2 μM of 8.
To examine whether oligomeric ligands 5–8 could inhibit the formation of nuclear foci in standard DM1 model cell culture GFP-DT960 transfected HeLa cells were studied. The foci were readily visualized via immunofluorescent (IF) staining for MBNL1 and fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) with Cy3-labeled (CAG)10 as a probe for r(CUG)exp. To quantify the reduction of foci in treated DM1 model cells, the foci areas were measured with confocal microscopy and compared to the untreated model cells. Treatment with ligands 6–8 at 2–50 μM for 48 h significantly decreased the nuclear foci area. Increasing the concentration of oligomeric ligands led to total disappearance of ribonuclear foci in DM1 model cells ( D, 3E, and ).
Quantification of r(CUG)exp levels was performed with GFP-DT0 and GFP-DT960 transfected cells. , The DM1 model cells were treated with 6-8 for 72 h and harvested. The r(CUG)exp mRNA level was determined by measuring the level of exon 15 upstream of the r(CUG)exp relative to PABP mRNA as an internal control and normalized to the levels measured from untreated cells. As shown in , there was a strong dose-dependent reduction of cellular r(CUG)exp mRNA level with ligands 6–8. The longer discrete oligomer was more effective with the IC50 decreasing from around 20 μM for ligand 6 to 500 nM for ligand 8.

## Polyplex formation between oligomeric ligands and CAG16
Having observed the DM1 bioactivity of oligomeric ligands 4–8 and recognizing their polycationic nature as resembling transfection agents, we chose to study whether 8 might assist the delivery of a DM1 antisense agent for a new type of combination therapy. The antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) used in this study is a locked nucleic acid (LNA) CAG16 gapmer that contains 16 nucleotides, and can pair to form a duplex with r(CUG)exp and recruit RNase-H to cleave the r(CUG)exp. The LNA CAG16 gapmer was modified such that all the phosphate groups on the backbone were changed to phosphorothioate groups and so that three locked sugar ring nucleotides were positioned on both the 5′- and 3′-ends, to afford the LNA CAG16 gapmer with increased resistance to cellular nucleases (see for sequence). 
Before using the expensive LNA CAG16 gapmer, initial polyplex studies were undertaken using CAG16 oligonucleotides as a model for the gapmer. The polyplex size was determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). A commonly used polycationic transfection agent, PEI-2k (Mw ∼ 2000, similar to the Mw of 8) was also included for a head-to-head comparison. The ratio of polymer to CAG16 was varied and defined by the N/P ratio, which is the ratio of polymer amines that are likely protonated (N = nitrogen) groups to negatively charged nucleic acid phosphate (P) groups. For the oligomeric ligands reported here, there are three nitrogen atoms per guanidinium group and the N value represents the number of guanidinium groups. The diameter of the polyplex formed by ligand 8 and CAG16 was around 150 nm, similar to that of PEI-2k ( ). The sizes of the 8-CAG16 polyplexes determined by TEM were consistent with those obtained by DLS ( D). Further detailed studies on the polyplex structure and the mechanism of polyplex formation is shown in and . As seen in a 100–200 nm sized polyplex is formed between 8 and CAG16 or random duplex in 1× PBS buffer but higher salt concentrations inhibit its formation.

## Cell uptake of model polyplex
The ability of oligomeric ligands 6 and 8 to facilitate cell uptake of oligonucleotides was examined using flow cytometry and compared with the commonly used polymeric transfection agent PEI ( A and 4B). As above, a model substitute for the LNA CAG16 gapmer, studies were performed with Cy3–CAG16. Thus, Cy3–CAG16 was mixed with the oligomeric ligands 6 or 8, or PEI (Mw ∼ 2 kDa or 25 kDa) in 1× PBS buffer (pH 7.4) and incubated at room temperature for 5 min to form polyplexes. The polyplex solutions were added to HeLa cells in Opti-MEM medium and incubated for 4 h at 37°C. Cells were washed, detached, and subjected to flow cytometry to measure the intracellular fluorescence intensity. The results indicated that 8 was able to deliver significantly more Cy3 labeled oligonucleotides into cells in comparison to 6, PEI-2k, and the larger PEI-25k polymer ( A, 4B, and ). In addition, to quantify the cellular uptake of polyplex formed by ligand 8 and CAG16 oligonucleotide, cells were incubated for 1 day with polyplex with a final oligonucleotide concentration of 500 nM. Cells were harvested and lysed. The cell lysate was analyzed through HPLC and the concentration of CAG16 was determined to be 117 ± 23 nM by using a standard curve generated using the CAG16 oligonucleotide ( ). The results indicated that ligand 8 can form a polyplex with the model oligonucleotide CAG16 and facilitate its cellular uptake. 
Flow cytometry and HPLC quantification showed the overall cellular uptake, but not the target engagement. Indeed, endosomal escape is often regarded as a bottleneck in intracellular delivery. To examine the potential ability of polyplex to surmount the endosomal barrier, a liposome disruption assay was performed as a model study using the polyplex formed by ligand 8 and CAG16. RhB loaded liposomes in sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) and 1× PBS buffer (pH 7.4) were prepared. After measuring the initial fluorescence of liposome solutions for 500 s, the polyplex was introduced, and the fluorescence was measured for 40 min ( ). A 20% Triton X- solution was then introduced to lyse all liposomes, thereby releasing all encapsulated dye and allowing determination of the maximum fluorescence after rupturing. In pH 5.0 sodium acetate buffer solution, a 23% increase in fluorescence was observed when the polyplex was introduced, possibly due to the protonation of the triaminotriazine ring (pKa 5.1). However, minimal leakage was observed in pH 7.4 PBS buffer. Although this experiment uses a model membrane system, the results indicated the potential for the polyplex to disrupt the liposomes at pH 5.0, which may facilitate its endosomal escape.

## Cell uptake and multitarget activity of ASO polyplex
Encouraged by the ability of the model polyplex to enter DM1 model (HeLa) cells, the ability of a polyplex formed by 8 and an LNA CAG16 gapmer was evaluated first in DM1 model cells and then in DM1 patient-derived myoblasts. Cellular uptake and intracellular distribution were monitored using a fluorescein (FAM) labeled LNA CAG16 gapmer and confocal microscopy with Lysotracker Deep Red introduced to label lysosomes. After incubation for 1 day, a significant amount of the FAM-LNA CAG16 gapmer fluorescence was observed in HeLa cells treated with the 8·FAM-LNA CAG16 gapmer polyplex, whereas negligible uptake was seen in the absence of 8 ( C). The oligomer gave a higher level of uptake than did either PEI-25kD or Lipofectamine RNAiMAX, the latter being a particularly effective siRNA transfection reagent. Visualization of the FAM-LNA CAG16 gapmer fluorescence reveals numerous punctate foci with sizes ranging from that seen for the polyplex (150 nm) up to ca. 1 μm. The majority of FAM signal was observed in lysosomes, with some visible in the nucleus.
DM1 patient-derived myoblasts, differentiated from DM1 fibroblasts (500 CTG, GM03987, Coriell Institute) by using MyoD-expressing lentivirus were monitored for the uptake of the 8·FAM-LNA CAG16 gapmer polyplex. As seen in , the DM1 patient myoblasts exhibited a similar pattern to that in C indicating that 8 can successfully deliver the LNA CAG16 gapmer into DM1 patient myoblasts after a 1 day treatment.
To measure the degree to which the various agents can intervene in the disease pathobiology, the relative r(CUG)500 levels were measured after a 3-day treatment. The cells were harvested, total RNA collected, and subjected to real time RT-qPCR. The abundance of r(CUG)exp-containing DMPK was determined relative to GAPDH mRNA as an internal control and normalized to the level measured from untreated DM1 patient myoblasts. As shown in D, the oligomer 8·LNA gapmer polyplex showed the highest efficacy level, decreasing total toxic r(CUG)exp level by ∼30% relative to GAPDH mRNA in DM1 patient myoblasts. Neither the PEI-2kD (Mw ∼ 2,000) nor the PEI-25kD (Mw ∼ 25,000) polyplex significantly decreased the r(CUG)exp level.

## Transcriptome-wide assessment of polyplex in DM1 patient myoblasts via RNA sequencing
To assess the ability of oligomer 8 and its polyplex to rescue DM1-associated splicing defects transcriptome-wide, RNA seq analysis was performed on polyA-selected RNA isolated from normal myoblasts, patient myoblasts, and DM1 patient myoblasts treated with 8, LNA CAG16 gapmer or 8·LNA gapmer polyplex. Considerable splicing aberrations were observed in the DM1 patient myoblasts in comparison with normal myoblasts ( A and ). Upon treatment with the 8·LNA gapmer polyplex, around 45% of these mis-splicing events were partially rescued, bringing them closer to their normal PSI levels (percent spliced in) ( A–5D). In contrast, treatment with ligand 8 alone caused the reversion of around 36% of splicing aberrations, mostly with lower extents of rescue ( A–5D). Significant recovery of splicing aberrations was observed in genes, including LRRFIP2, MBNL1, MEG3, and several others ( B). 
Further, we also identified extensive gene expression changes by comparing patient-derived myoblasts to normal myoblasts (2192 genes). Treatment with 8 induced minimal gene expression changes in patient myoblasts, with no significant gene expression changes ( E). On the other hand, treatment with the 8·LNA gapmer polyplex induced changes in expression levels of 231 genes in patient myoblasts, several of which reinstate their expression closer to levels observed in normal myoblasts ( F and A). Interestingly, the levels of DMPK transcript, or other transcripts that contain CUG repeats were not affected by the polyplex treatment in patient myoblasts ( B).

## Delivery of ASO polyplex in HSA-LR transgenic mice model
To evaluate the ability of polyplex to deliver ASO in vivo, HSA-LR transgenic mice were administered ASO polyplex, ligand 8, or LNA CAG16 gapmer into their TA muscles ( A). HSA-LR mice express ca. 220 CTG repeats in the 3ʹ-UTR of truncated human skeletal muscle actin gene, leading to accumulation of pathogenic ribonuclear foci in the skeletal muscle nuclei. At the end of the treatment schedule, the total RNA from TA muscles for each treatment was isolated and the relative r(CUG)220 levels were analyzed by RT-qPCR, with Actb mRNA as an internal control and normalized to the level of PBS treated HSA-LR mice ( B). Treatment with LNA CAG16 gapmer alone reduced the r(CUG)220 RNA level to 60% compared to PBS treated controls (not significant), and treatment of ligand 8 was able to decrease the r(CUG)220 RNA level to ∼40% compared to PBS treated control mice. Mice treated with 8·LNA gapmer polyplex showed the most significant change with an 80% reduction in r(CUG)220 RNA load. Although the combination provided a statistically significant improvement relative to the individual treatments, we further tested an interaction between the two agents using a two-way ANOVA. As seen in , no statistically significant interaction was observed, indicating the effect of one agent did not depend on the presence of the other. 
We next examined whether the polyplex treatment could reduce the nuclear r(CUG)exp foci formation in vivo using FISH with Cy3-loaded (CAG)10 as a probe for r(CUG)220. The FISH assay was performed on 8 μm thick OCT embedded transverse cryosections from TA muscles, and nuclei were labeled using Hoechst 33342. Relative to PBS treated mice, the ASO polyplex treatment decreased the nuclear r(CUG)220 foci in the TA muscles of HSA-LR mice ( C and 6D). Ligand 8 showed a modest decrease in nuclear r(CUG)220 foci and LNA CAG16 gapmer alone did not show a statistically significant decrease in foci formation. As before, we performed a two-way ANOVA to test an interaction between 8 and the LNA CAG16 gapmer ( ). Here, a marginally significant interaction was observed suggesting that the effect of two agents, 8 and LNA CAG16 gapmer, depended on one another.
Despite the decrease in r(CUG)220 RNA levels and in number of nuclei showing foci, a significant rescue of splicing aberrations was not apparent. To investigate a potential origin of this lack of splicing rescue, we evaluated the tissue histology of control and treated HSA-LR mice. We noticed that administration of ligand 8 as well as with 8·LNA gapmer polyplex induced increased infiltration of immune cells into the muscle tissue ( ). This was also accompanied by muscle injury, as evident from the increased numbers of central nuclei in ligand 8 and 8·LNA gapmer polyplex treated mice.

## Discussion
Oligomeric ligands 4, 6, and 8 were shown to bind to both r(CUG)16 and d(CTG)16 selectively, suggesting their ability to serve as multi-targeting agents for DM1. In particular, by binding d(CTG)exp, the agents may act as transcription inhibitors suppressing the formation of the toxic expanded RNA transcripts. At the same time, if r(CUG)exp is produced, oligomers 4, 6, and 8 may inhibit MBNL1 sequestration. Both activities would serve to rescue the downstream mis-splicing.
Consistent with the multivalent strategy, the longer oligomer 8 gave the tightest binding to both target RNA and DNA oligonucleotides, followed by 6, and then 4 whose Kd values were in the millimolar range. The nanomolar affinity of 8 for d(CTG)16 and r(CUG)16 likely originates to a significant degree from its polycationic nature. Although this could compromise selectivity, the affinity of 8 for random sequence DNA appeared to be weak based on the minimal heat generated in the ITC. On the basis of these positive results, 8 was used in subsequent studies. In vitro studies demonstrated 8 to afford 100% inhibition of d(CTG)90 transcription at 600 nM whereas the same concentration of ligand showed negligible inhibition of a random sequence ( ), a result consistent with the selectivity seen in oligonucleotide binding experiments.
Interestingly, individual runs in the transcription inhibition studies revealed an unusually narrow concentration window over which inhibition rises to near 100% ( A and S6B). Although the origin of this effect has not been elucidated, it is consistent with some type of cooperativity, for example, the oligomer and RNA polymerase cooperatively forming a stabilized d(CTG)exp hairpin from the (CTG·CAG)90 sequence or the cooperative formation of a polyplex-like structure formed from multiple ligands and RNA strands.
Oligomeric mixture 3 was previously found to enter cells readily despite its significant molecular weight, charge, and large number of hydrogen bond donors, all violations of the Lipinski’s rule of five. The structural analogy between 3 and cell penetrating peptides was part of its molecular design intended to bring the agent into the cell and the nucleus. Indeed, fluorescently labeled ligand 8 entered cells being found in the cytoplasm and nucleus of HeLa cells. The overlap of the fluorescence with that from Lysotracker Green suggests endocytosis as a likely uptake mechanism, whereas additional nonoverlapping fluorescence is indicative of either ligand escape from the endosome or a direct penetration mechanism. Endosomal release remains a major obstacle to drug delivery and the cell uptake experiments here do indicate significant 8 residing in lysosomes. Nonetheless, observation of drug within the nucleus and cytosol indicates the ability of some 8 to escape the confines of lysosomes and to reach the intended targets.
To examine the ability of oligomeric ligands 4–8 to correct splicing defects, a standard DM1 cell culture model was used, namely DT960-transfected HeLa cells. The IR pre-mRNA was used in this assay because its mis-splicing is relatively difficult to rescue. Not only did these ligands exhibit dose-dependent mis-splicing rescue, but their activity correlated with their measured strength of binding. Furthermore, the longest oligomer, 8, proved to be superior to ligand 3, which is a mixture of oligomers ranging from 4-mer to 8-mer ( ).
One of the hallmarks of DM1 is the foci formed by the sequestration of MBNL proteins by r(CUG)exp in the nucleus, leading to the spliceopathy underlying many of the DM1 symptoms. Ligands 4–8 were effective in rescuing the mis-splicing of the IR pre-RNA and did so in a dose-dependent fashion with the activity paralleling their measured affinity. The latter observation suggests cellular uptake is efficient for all ligands. Inhibiting sequestration of MBNL proteins does not eliminate all of the r(CUG)exp toxicities described in the introduction, suggesting that agents able to target simultaneously d(CTG)exp and inhibit its transcription may have a therapeutic advantage. For this reason, we further tested the ability of oligomeric ligands to reduce the cellular levels of toxic r(CUG)exp in DM1 model cell culture. Again, 8 was found to be the most active agent, reducing in a dose-dependent fashion the CUG RNA level to <30% that of the untreated level at 1 μM.
The data presented above establish the ability of oligomers 5–8 to serve as multitarget therapeutic agents for DM1 by acting as a transcription inhibitor and reducing the formation of r(CUG)exp selectively and by inhibiting the sequestration of MBNL1 by r(CUG)exp. Importantly, none of the oligomeric ligands showed significant cytotoxicity at the working concentrations ( ).
Given the DM1 activity and acceptable cytotoxicity of 5-8 (see ) and their ability to complex some oligonucleotides, we studied whether they might complex and deliver antisense agents for DM1. Because the oligomeric ligands and CAG16 are oppositely charged polyelectrolytes, their interaction should be dependent on the salt concentration in buffer solution as well as concentration. Sizable polyplexes (100–200 nm) were observed by DLS ( ) and the observation of their formation in 1× PBS buffer, but not in the ITC buffer, which is consistent with the minimal heat observed with random DNA in the ITC experiments. Indeed, the nature of electrostatic interactions often involving entropically driven binding means that ITC is not always the best technique to determine the binding affinity of ligands targeting DNA or RNA through mostly electrostatic interactions. 
The 8·FAM-LNA CAG16 gapmer polyplex was taken up by HeLa cells with the former showing improved LNA uptake relative to known agents such as PEI-25kD or Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. The two active agents delivered together as a polyplex gave improved disease intervention as measured by two cellular markers in DM1 patient-derived myoblasts. In particular, the polyplex reduced both r(CUG)exp levels and in a transcriptome-wide analysis showed greater rescue of splicing aberrations than treatment with either agent alone. Direct evidence is lacking, but the reduction of r(CUG)exp levels and splicing rescue can be attributed both to 8 inhibiting the transcription of d(CTG)exp and to RNase H activation by the LNA CAG16 gapmer. Splicing recovery may also result from a reduction in MBNL sequestration.
In HSA-LR transgenic mice, the 8·LNA CAG16 gapmer polyplex reduced both nuclear r(CUG) foci formation and r(CUG)220 levels. Ligand 8 alone was active whereas LNA CAG16 gapmer alone did not decrease the foci formation. The latter observation was surprising because the reduction in r(CUG)220 levels observed ( B) were expected to lead to a reduction in foci. The overall mixed results, including lack of an interaction between ligand 8 and the LNA CAG16 gapmer seen in the two-way ANOVA of the r(CUG)220 RNA expression levels ( B) and marginally significant interaction observed in the foci data in D may be a result of a number of factors. For example, it may be that the sample size is under-powered to detect an interaction, there is too high a variance in the experimental measures, or possibly both. Nonetheless, the larger improvement directly observed in these measures hints at the potential utility of this new strategy in vivo.
Although robust splicing recovery was observed in the cellular models, it was not reproduced in the mouse studies. It is not known for certain why this downstream effect did not see improvement, however, tissue histology of control and treated HSA-LR mice reveals that ligand 8 and the 8·LNA gapmer polyplex caused muscle injury. Thus, we speculate that in vivo, tissue damage and increased inflammation associated with oligomer administration prevents the restoration of a normal muscle splicing program. Nonetheless, the improvement in the two up-stream measures of the DM1 pathology with the ASO polyplex in comparison to the LNA CAG16 gapmer or 8 alone demonstrate the potential application of our new strategy in vivo.
The collected data demonstrate that ligand 8 can serve both as a potent drug molecule and an ASO delivery vehicle, which indicated the robust potential of our new strategy. Antisense therapy is a powerful tool to regulate gene-associated diseases. In spite of this promise, the delivery of antisense agents to target cells has remained a major challenge due in large part to the difficulty of a transporting polyanionic structure across the cell membrane. Various polymeric systems have been reported, however, they have met with obstacles including toxicity and low drug loadings, often <10%. Such limitations have hindered their translation to the clinic. Here we have designed, prepared, and studied a series of discrete oligomers that can target d(CTG)exp and r(CUG)exp and lead to beneficial effects in DM1 model cells, DM1 patient myoblasts, and a DM1 mouse model. Their unique structure enables them to form polyplexes with DNA oligomers and thereby co-deliver an antisense warhead both in vitro and in vivo. Although specifically developed for DM1, the concept of an active drug serving as a delivery vehicle for an antisense or related agent should be generalizable to other DNA and RNA-mediated diseases.

## Limitations of the study
One possible challenge of this particular application is that multiple intervention mechanisms are occurring at the same time and may compete with one another. For example, whereas the LNA gapmer recruits RNase H, ligand 8 also binds to r(CUG) to displace MBNL proteins potentially making this bound RNA resistant to RNase H. In a broader context, polyvalent polycationic ligands such as 8 are ideally suited to complex antisense oligonucleotides and form polyplexes that are known for their enhanced cellular delivery. Thus, although this approach should be generalizable to other RNA and DNA targeted diseases, it may prove more challenging to apply this approach to other diseases not involving DNA or RNA targeting. Creative strategies would be needed for drug candidates with poor cellular uptake to have other active agents act as delivery vehicles. The biggest limitation of this study is seen in the failure to translate the success of the cellular studies to the animal model. Although the 8·LNA gapmer polyplex treated mice showed a significant reduction in both nuclear foci formation and CUG RNA expression relative to treatment with 8 or LNA separately, this was not accompanied by splicing defect rescue.

## Computational docking studies

## RNA docking study
The crystal structure of r[(CUG)6]2 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (code 3GM7) and extended to r[(CUG)7]2 using the Nucleic Acid Builder in Molecular Operating Environment (MOE, v2018.01). Three uracil (U) bases (from 2nd, 4th, and 6th U-U mismatches) were rotated out of the helix in the direction of the minor groove to simulate base flipping. The targeting triaminotriazine rings were docked on r[(CUG)7]2 at alternating U-U mismatches based on results of a previously reported ligand. Each triaminotriazine ring was docked with 3 hydrogen bonds to the Watson-Crick-Franklin face of the undisturbed (nonflipped) U and a single hydrogen bond with the phosphate backbone of the flipped U base. Bisguanidinium units were placed in the major groove of the helix and the structure was minimized. All minimizations were performed using the Assisted Model Building with Energy Refinement (AMBER) force field. Amber10 parameters were used for the nucleic acids, and the General AMBER Force Field (GAFF) parameter were used for the ligands. The cutoff distance was 10 Å, and the switching distance was 8 Å. Complexes were subjected to steepest descent minimization with a gradient threshold of 0.1 kcal/(mol·Å2). The triaminotriazine and bisguanidinium units were linked with 4 methylene groups and the structure was minimized. The terminal methylene groups and ammonium ions were added to yield the full docked structure of ligand 6 after final minimization ( ). The resulting docked structure of 6 was exported as a protein data bank (pdb) file. UCSF Chimera was utilized for image rendering and hydrogen bond quantification. 
Ligand 6 was docked in the major groove of the RNA duplex ( A). After minimization, ligand 6 maintained all 12 triaminotriazine hydrogen bonding interactions with uracil (4 per triaminotriazine). Two additional analogs of 6 were modeled in which each four-methylene linker group was replaced by either three methylene groups (analog 1) or five methylene groups (analog 2). These shorter and longer analogous ligands were docked to r[(CUG)6]2 but did not lead to the same well-formed complexes. These observations indicate that the 4 methylene-linker provides the best optimized contacts between the triaminotriazine recognition units and the U-bases as well as the guanidinium-phosphate contacts.

## DNA docking study
Because there is no reported crystal structure of d(CTG)exp currently, the DNA structure we utilized was mutated from crystal structure of r[(CUG)7]2 used in the RNA docking study (PDB: 3GM7) and then minimized. To mutate the r[(CUG)7]2 structure into d[(CTG)7]2, the 2’-hydroxyl group on each of the sugars along the backbone was deleted and each uracil was mutated to thymine by adding a methyl group on C5. The structure was minimized, and the ligand remained bound in the major groove with hydrogen bonds formed between triaminotriazine ring with thymine base and additional hydrogen bonds formed between guanidinium groups with the phosphate backbone.

## Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)
ITC studies were used to evaluate the binding affinity and multivalent effect across the discrete oligomer series. Ligand 4, 6, and 8 were studied by titrating ligand into d(CTG)16, r(CUG)16, or random duplex DNA to measure the dissociation constants (Kd) for these ligands. A TA Affinity ITC Auto, low volume (TA instruments, New Castle, DE) at Pennsylvania State University was utilized for these binding experiments. The instrument is kept under nitrogen atmosphere. The maximum volume of syringe is 250 μL and the reference and sample cells hold 190 μL each. To study CAG16 interaction with ligand 6 and 8, a TA Affinity ITC low volume instrument at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign was utilized with 1X PBS Buffer (pH 7.4). The maximum volume of the syringe is 250 μL and the reference and sample cells hold 190 μL each. The DNA and RNA oligonucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). All DNA/RNA solutions were freshly annealed before each ITC experiments by heating at 90-95 °C for 5 min and slowly cooling to 25 °C (over 1-2 h). DI water was used to fill the reference cell for all experiments. Between experiments, the autosampler, syringe, and test cell were with 2% Contrad solution. Each experiment was conducted at 25 °C and consisted of 20 injections of 2.00 ± 0.01 μL with 180-200 s delay and stirring at 125 rpm. The instrument was auto-equilibrated before each experiment. A master buffer solution was prepared and split amongst the DNA/RNA solution (cell) and the ligand solution (syringe). The buffer was made from stock solutions of NaCl (5 M, aqueous) and MOPS (1 M, pH 7.0 ± 0.2) to a final concentration of 300 mM NaCl and 20 mM MOPS (pH 7) in molecular biology grade water. Ligand stock solutions were 10 mM for ligand 4 and ligand 6, and 3 mM for ligand 8 in molecular biology grade water. Nucleic acid stock solutions were 2 mM in molecular biology grade water. For these experiments, the nucleic acid (cell) concentration was 10 μM and the ligand concentration (syringe) was 500 μM. The autosampler was used for loading cell and syringe as well as injecting titrant for the experiments with Auto ITC (Penn State) and the cell and syringe were manually loaded for the instrument at UIUC. NanoAnalyze software (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE) was used to analyze data. Because the reference cell contains DI water, the data is reported as raw data minus the background control (average across all points) from titration of ligand into buffer solution without nucleic acid. The automatically predicted baseline was used and auto-adjust feature was used to set integration regions. For ligand 4, the “independent” fitting algorithm (for one type of binding site) was utilized. For ligand 6 and 8, the “multiple site” algorithm was used because these oligomers are both expected to span several base pairs in binding and there are a few different sets of sites that could be spanned on the hairpin oligonucleotides. All models in the software package were considered before choosing the reported model. All experiments were run in triplicate and the Kd values reported as average of three independent experiments. Error was calculated and reported as standard error of the mean.

## In Vitro transcription inhibition assay
The assay was carried out following the previous report with modifications. The DNA template for transcription was prepared by linearization of (CTG)90-containing pSP72 plasmid by cleavage at BamH1 site. The control DNA template was prepared by PCR of a cloned plasmid, which has 38 bp of random sequences (5′-GCG TAG CTT ACA TAG TTC ACA TAG TCT GAT ACT TCC GA-3′) inserted to pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega). The ligands were pre-incubated with transcription reaction solution including 15 ng of DNA template, 0.5 mM each rATP, rUTP, rCTP, rGTP, 1X RNAPol reaction buffer (NEB, 1 mM DTT, 40 mM Tris-HCl, 6 mM MgCl2, 2 mM spermidine, pH 7.9) at room temperature for 3 h. After pre-incubation, 50 U of T7 RNA polymerase (NEB, M0251S) was added to each reaction tube and the solution was incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Reactions were quenched by adding 8 μL of quenching solution (7 μL of 8 M urea (aq), 1 μL of denaturing dye (95% formamide, 5 mM EDTA, 0.025% each xylene cyanol and bromophenol blue)). The solutions were heated at 95 °C for 5 min before gel loading. An aliquot of 15 μL was run on 8% denaturing PAGE in 1X TBE. The gel was post-stained with ethidium bromide and imaged with Gel Doc XR+ system (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was quantified with ImageJ software. The band intensity of (CUG)90 was normalized to that from the untreated transcription reaction.

## IR pre-mRNA mis-splicing rescue assay

## Dynamic light scattering (DLS) assay of model polyplex formation
The CAG16 oligonucleotide or random DNA duplex solution was prepared in 1X PBS buffer (pH 7.4) or matching ITC Buffer (300 mM NaCl, 20 mM MOPS pH 7, aqueous) at 500 nM. Ligand 6, ligand 8, or PEI-2k (Mw ∼ 2000) was added to the DNA solution to achieve different N/P ratios (N/p = 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 10) to form polyplexes. The total solution volume was 1.1. mL for each sample measured. The polyplex solution was incubated at room temperature for approximately 5 min before the measurement. The light scattering data was subsequently measured by using a Marvin Instrument Ltd. nanoZS Zetasizer with disposable cuvettes. The CAG16 and random duplex oligonucleotides were purchased from IDT. The sequence is 5′-AG CAG CAG CAG CAG CA-3′ and the Random Duplex sequence is 5′-CTG CAC TAG GCA CTA CCG-3’//5′-CGG TAG TGC CTA GTG CAG-3’.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of model polyplex
A polyplex solution was prepared by mixing ligand 8 and CAG16 oligonucleotides in 1X PBS buffer (pH 7.4) (N/P ratio = 5; c[CAG16] = 500 nM) was applied to a UC-A on a lacey gold TEM grid (Ted Pella) for 20 min. The polyplex solution was carefully removed using filter paper to absorb the solution. Ammonium molybdate (2 wt% in water, 8 μL) was added to the grid surface to negatively stain the polyplex. The staining process was conducted for 20 min and the solution was removed using filter paper. The TEM grid was air dried for 1 h. The TEM imaging was performed on a JEOL 2100 Cryo TEM with 200 keV, and images were processed using ImageJ.

## Flow cytometry assay of model polyplex cellular uptake
In a 6-well plate, 600,000 HeLa cells were added to each well with 2 mL of DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and the cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. The polyplex solutions were prepared by mixing ligands (ligand 6, 8, PEI-2k, or PEI-25k) with Cy3 labeled oligonucleotides (Cy3-CAG16) to achieve various N/P ratios (N/p = 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20) in 1X PBS buffer and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The polyplex solution was added to each well to reach the final concentration of Cy3 labeled oligonucleotides at 500 nM. The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 4 h. The cell medium was removed by centrifugation, and the cells were resuspended in 1 mL 1X PBS buffer (pH 7.4) by vortexing. The fluorescence of the cells was measured by flow cytometry under Cy3 channel. The Cy3-CAG16 oligonucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies Inc. ## HPLC analysis of model polyplex cellular uptake
In a 6-well plate, 600,000 HeLa cells were added to each well with 2 mL of DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and the cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. ## Liposome disruption assay
The experiments were carried out as reported with minor changes. The liposomes were prepared with vesicles made of egg-phosphatidylcholine and soy-phosphatidylserine (8/2; mol/mol). To a 20 mL evaporation flask kept on ice, was introduced 3.70 mL of 10 mg/mL phosphatidylcholine in chloroform and 0.96 mL of 10 mg/mL phosphatidylserine in chloroform. The solvent was gradually removed by increasing the vacuum to avoid solvent boiling. After 15 min, a white film of lipids was present on the flask wall and no chloroform odor was noticed. With the flask on ice, introduced 1.5 mL of diethyl ether in the evaporation flask to dissolve the lipids, and subsequently added 1.5 mL of Rho-B solution (20 μM). The flask was vortexed vigorously for 10 s and sonicated to produce a homogenous emulsion. During sonication, the flask was kept on ice. The vacuum was gently increased to remove solvent and generate a dark, fairly viscous and homogeneous solution. The solution was filtered with 0.4 μm nucleopore polycarbonate membrane 5 times and 10 times with 0.2 filter to generate large unilamellar vesicles. The free Rho-B molecules were washed off using Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter Units (MilliporeSigma). The vesicle solutions were prepared in either sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) or 1X PBS buffer (pH 7.4) to reach a final concentration of lipids of 1 mg/mL as quantified by a Stewart Assay. The polyplex solution was prepared by mixing ligand 8 and CAG16 oligonucleotides in sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) or 1X PBS buffer (pH 7.4) (N/P ratio = 5; [CAG16] = 500 nM). The fluorescence of Rhodamine B loaded liposome solution (1 mg/mL) was measured in kinetics mode for 500 s with excitation wavelength at 552 nm and emission wavelength at 575 nm. The polyplex solution was introduced while measuring fluorescence until 3000 s and 40 μL of the 20% Triton-X 100 solution was added to lyse all liposomes to measure the max fluorescence from full leakage of the encapsulated dye.

## Sulforhodamine B toxicity assay
The assay was carried out following the previous report with modifications. Approximately 10,000 HeLa cells or DM1 patient fibroblasts (GM03987, Coriell Institute) were plated in each well in a 96-well plate in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Ligand was added to the medium to reach the desired final concentration. Five replicates were performed for each concentration on each 96 well plate. The cells were incubated at 37°C for 72 h, after which, 100 μL of cold 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 4°C for 1 h. The cells were washed three times with tap water and air-dried. The cells were stained with 100 μL of 0.0057% (w/v) sulforhodamine B solution at room temperature for 30 min, and washed with 1% (v/v) acetic acid solution three times to remove the unbound dye. The plate was air-dried at room temperature and 200 μL of Tris base solution (pH 10.5) was added to each well. The plate was incubated at room temperature on a shaker for 5 min to solubilize the protein-bound dye. The optical intensity was measured at 510 nm using microplate reader (SPECTRAmax PLUS). Cell viability was calculated using the following formula:%cellviability=Mean(ODsample)−Mean(ODdeadcontrol)Mean(ODlivecontrol)−Mean(ODdeadcontrol)X100%
The data was plotted using Excel and the cytotoxicity experiments were replicated in triplicate for both cell lines.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Unless indicated the data collected represent a minimum of three independent runs, the values averaged and error bars representing standard error or error of the mean. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The details of each statistical analysis can be found in the figure legends and the method details.
For the RNA-seq data analysis, RNA-seq reads were filtered for quality and read length using Trimmomatic V0.38 and aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR (version 2.6.1d). Uniquely mapped reads and additional information are provided in the GEO database (see the section). HTseq package was used to obtain raw gene counts, which were then corrected using ComBat-seq method to remove any batch effects. DEseq2 package was used to perform differential gene expression analysis, where expression changes with FDR(q-value) < 0.05, |log2(fold change)| > 1 were deemed significant, unless specified otherwise. 
Differential splicing analysis was performed using rMATS v4.0.2(turbo), and significant events were identified using imposed cutoffs (FDR < 0.1, junction read counts ≥ 10, ΔPSI ≥ 10%). The most recovered events among the events that show the greatest difference between patient and normal myoblasts were identified as events with the highest value of R, calculated as:R= |P-N|-|T-N|
Where P, N and T are the average PSI values of the event in patient myoblasts, normal myoblasts and patient myoblasts treated with ligand 9 or its polyplex. Among the splicing events, the ones with R ≥ 0.05 were regarded as recovered, events with R ≤ -0.05 were regarded as worsened, and the remaining were considered unchanged.
