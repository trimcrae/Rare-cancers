## Abstract

## Introduction
Patients with metastatic oncogene-driven non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are experiencing longer and uncertain trajectories of life-limiting illness due to advances in precision medicine. These advanced cancer survivors face new challenges related to living with uncertainty and desire more support to maximize their health and quality of life. Therefore, we developed a population-specific, blended palliative and survivorship care intervention to address the supportive care needs of patients recently diagnosed with advanced lung cancer and who are receiving targeted therapy for NSCLC with EGFR, ALK, ROS1 or RET driver mutations.

## Methods and analysis
This study is a single-site, non-blinded pilot randomised controlled trial of an intervention for patients with metastatic oncogene-driven NSCLC, Patient-centred, Optimal Integration of Survivorship and palliative carE (POISE) versus usual care. POISE consists of a brief series of structured visits with a trained palliative care clinician to address coping with uncertainty, increase prognostic awareness and promote healthy lifestyle behaviours. We will recruit 60 patients from the Massachusetts General Hospital Cancer Center. Patients will be randomised into a 1:1 ratio to the intervention arm or the usual care arm. Patients randomised to the intervention arm will complete four 60 min virtual or in-person visits with a palliative care physician. The usual care arm will receive standard oncology care. Patients in both arms will complete survey assessments at enrolment, 12 weeks and 20 weeks after enrolment, and patients in the intervention group will complete an exit interview. The primary outcome measure of this trial is feasibility, which will be defined by ≥60% enrolment among eligible patients, ≥70% completion of all sessions for participants in the intervention arm and ≥70% completion of all surveys for all study participants. Exploratory outcomes include acceptability, emotional coping with prognosis, self-efficacy for chronic disease management, prognostic awareness, quality of life, anxiety, depression, intolerance of uncertainty and documentation of goals and values discussions in the electronic health record.

## Ethics and dissemination
This study was approved by the Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center’s institutional review board (protocol 20-722). The protocol is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines, and the study will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement for non-pharmacological trials.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
This is a non-blinded, randomised controlled trial to evaluate the feasibility of a blended survivorship and palliative care intervention for patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer receiving targeted therapy, Patient-centred, Optimal Integration of Survivorship and palliative carE (POISE), compared with usual care.
POISE is a population-specific, structured palliative care intervention that builds on the successful model of early palliative care integrated with oncology care and incorporates aspects of survivorship care to address the needs of an emerging population of advanced cancer survivors.
POISE was developed using theoretical frameworks and evidence regarding the supportive care needs of patients living with metastatic lung cancer and refined with input from stakeholders and participants in an open pilot study.
Potential limitations of the study are the non-blinded and single-site design, as well as the relatively homogenous sample of patients from an academic medical centre.

## Introduction
 Recent advances in precision medicine have markedly changed the experience of patients with lung cancer whose tumours harbour targetable genetic mutations. Targeted therapy extends survival, reduces disease-related symptoms and lacks the side effects of cytotoxic therapy. , However, despite these gains, metastatic lung cancer remains incurable and the duration of patients’ response to targeted therapy is variable and unpredictable. As patients with metastatic oncogene-driven non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) experience longer, uncertain trajectories of life-limiting illness, they also face unique challenges and their needs for support are frequently unmet.
Recent studies have illustrated a variety of challenges that metastatic oncogene-driven NSCLC patients experience, including distress related to uncertainty, lack of discussions about prognosis and end-of-life care, misperceptions of the curability of their disease, and high intensity of end-of-life care. , Palliative care, which is medical care focused on alleviating suffering from serious illness, has been shown to improve quality of life among people with advanced cancer. , Specifically, several studies demonstrate that integrating palliative care with oncology care early in the course of illness improves patients’ mood, understanding of illness and coping skills and improves patient–clinician communication about end-of-life preferences. , However, these studies predated modern therapies and thus participants experienced shorter survival and a greater symptom burden compared with patients with NSCLC receiving current cancer-directed therapies, including targeted therapy. Although there are clear benefits to early integration of palliative care with oncology care, how to optimally integrate early palliative care for patients with uncertain but potentially longer trajectories of advanced cancer is an evolving area of research. 
In addition to palliative care, another supportive care approach that may be beneficial for patients with advanced cancer is survivorship care. Survivorship care originated as care for patients who have completed definitive therapy for early-stage cancer and require long-term surveillance. Clinicians who deliver survivorship care focus on monitoring for cancer recurrence, screening for new or secondary malignancies, managing late or long-term effects of cancer treatment, promoting a healthy lifestyle and providing psychosocial support. , 
Thus, aspects of survivorship care may be relevant to people with metastatic oncogene-driven NSCLC, such as addressing the physical and psychosocial effects of living with cancer and supporting patients interested in health behaviour change. Yet, although the definition of ‘cancer survivor’ has broadened to include all people with cancer regardless of the stage of their illness, survivorship care has typically been limited in focus to people with early-stage cancer. Patients with metastatic oncogene-driven NSCLC represent an emerging category of advanced cancer survivors who warrant a new supportive care approach that incorporates elements of both palliative and survivorship care. 
Given this, we developed Patient-centred, Optimal Integration of Survivorship and palliative carE (POISE), a brief, population-specific blended palliative and survivorship care intervention to evaluate and address the supportive care needs of patients recently diagnosed with advanced lung cancer and receiving highly effective targeted therapy for tumours harbouring EGFR, ALK, ROS1 or RET mutations. POISE aims to improve patients’ emotional coping with their prognosis, increase their prognostic awareness and communication and improve patients’ ability to manage their long-term health through health behaviour change. In this paper, we describe the protocol for a randomised controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the feasibility of delivering the POISE intervention in comparison to usual care.

## Methods and analysis

## Study design
This is a single-site, non-blinded, parallel group RCT comparing the POISE intervention to usual care in 60 patients with metastatic oncogene-driven NSCLC receiving targeted therapy. All patients are recruited from the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) Cancer Center, an academic medical centre in the northeast USA and its satellite clinics. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram is shown in . We adhered to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials in drafting the protocol. 
 

## Participant selection
To be eligible, patients must be aged 18 or older, English or Spanish speaking, a patient of the MGH Cancer Center, within 6 months of diagnosis of metastatic NSCLC harbouring a targetable oncogene driver mutation (EGFR, ALK, ROS1 or RET), and receiving targeted therapy as first-line treatment. Though therapies targeting other mutations (eg, HER2 and MET) are available, we selected these molecular subtypes based on the high overall response rates, long duration of progression-free survival and favourable toxicity profile of the drugs that target them. We will exclude patients with a need for urgent palliative or hospice care and patients with cognitive impairment or serious mental illness that precludes their ability to provide informed consent or participate in study activities, at the discretion of the oncology team (see ).

## Study procedures

## Recruitment
Patients will be recruited from the MGH Cancer Center and affiliated satellite clinics, Mass General Brigham Healthcare Center (Danvers) and Mass General Cancer Center at Newton-Wellesley Hospital. There will be no direct advertising to patients for this study. We have obtained a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) waiver to allow a trained research assistant (RA) to perform a chart review of patients seen in the MGH Thoracic Oncology Clinics via the electronic health record (EHR). Based on oncology progress notes, the RA will identify patients within 6 months of diagnosis with NSCLC who are receiving targeted therapy. The RA will send an email to the treating oncologist to confirm patients’ eligibility, obtain oncologists’ permission to approach the patient in an opt-out method and ask them to mention the study to their eligible patients. Patients will be approached by telephone or in person, in which the RA will use a standard script to introduce and describe the study.

## Enrolment and randomisation
Eligible patients who choose to participate in the study will be asked to provide verbal informed consent by the trained RA during the telephone call or in-person approach. The consent form can be found in the . Participants will then complete a baseline survey that includes questions about demographic information, emotional coping with prognosis, prognostic awareness, self-efficacy for managing chronic illness, anxiety, depression, quality of life, tolerance of uncertainty and hope and goal setting (see ). Participants will then be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to receive either the POISE intervention or usual care using random permuted block randomisation. The allocation table will be generated by the statistician and uploaded to the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) randomisation module, concealed from the study team members who recruit and enrol patients. There will be no stratification of the randomisation.
 

## Assessments
In addition to the baseline survey, all participants will be asked to complete survey assessments at weeks 12 and 20 post enrolment, with a window of 1 week before and after each time point for survey completion (see ). Patients will be given the option to complete surveys over the phone, electronically via REDCap, or on paper by mail. After the completion of study activities, all participants in the intervention arm will be asked to participate in an exit interview by phone. Participants in the intervention group will also be asked four questions about the acceptability of POISE in the final survey. The survey questions can be found in the .

## POISE intervention
POISE is a brief, population-specific intervention consisting of four visits with a trained palliative care clinician over the course of the study. Visits will take place either in person or via secure video conferencing based on patients’ preferences and oncology visit schedules. Interpretation services will be provided for Spanish-speaking participants. The first visit will take place within 4 weeks of enrolment in the study, with the following three visits occurring every subsequent 4 weeks (±1 week). All visits will be 60 min in duration. Topics discussed in the visits are a blend of palliative and survivorship care and include topics such as rapport building, coping, lifestyle, prognostic understanding, and life goals and values (see ). Additional visits with the assigned palliative care clinicians may be scheduled at the discretion of the patients and providers after study completion. The scheduling of additional palliative care visits will be documented and accounted for when exploring the assessment outcomes.
 The content of POISE is informed by the theoretical frameworks, Mishel’s Uncertainty in Illness theory, Leventhal’s Common Sense Model and models of fear of cancer progression. , According to Mishel, uncertainty about chronic illness leads to uncertainty about other aspects of life and may be destabilising for patients. In some patients, abiding uncertainty leads to the development of maladaptive emotional, behavioural and cognitive responses, ultimately resulting in distress and fear of cancer progression. This distress and fear of cancer progression may also be perpetuated by physical symptoms, leading to avoidance of positive health behaviours. Lack of understanding about prognosis contributes to the sense of uncertainty. Therefore, POISE aims to ameliorate these multiple facets by targeting maladaptive cognitive processes, promoting healthy lifestyle habits through behaviour change and increasing prognostic awareness through patient–clinician communication.
Clinicians delivering POISE are trained with an evidence-based intervention manual informed by previously successful palliative care interventions, literature about survivorship care and fear of cancer recurrence, the theoretical frameworks stated above and clinical experience. The manual is designed for clinicians treating patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC and provides the clinicians with a foundational background on the patient population and the basis of the intervention, session format, objectives, and suggested content and phrasing for the visits. After the initial training, clinicians will use the manual to prepare for each visit and will be provided with an abbreviated, quick-reference version to use during the visits. In addition, the manual provides suggested language for communicating with the patients’ oncologists after each visit. Communication between the POISE interventionalists and the patients’ oncologists is critical for coordinated care and success of the intervention.
Similar to the manual for clinicians, patients receive a workbook to use before, during and after POISE sessions to promote adherence and maximise engagement. The workbook describes psychological concepts, coping strategies and resources for support. It also provides patients and their caregivers with the opportunity to preview questions the POISE clinician will ask in upcoming sessions and record their reflections and skills practice after sessions with the clinician.

## Usual care control
Participants randomized to the control group will receive standard oncology care, which may include specialist palliative care and other support services at the discretion of the oncology team and patient. Given that early palliative care utilisation among patients with oncogene-driven NSCLC is typically low, despite guideline recommendations to integrate palliative care with oncology care, we anticipate that the standard care group will remain distinct from the intervention group. We will extract data regarding palliative care utilisation by the usual care group from the EHR. The usual care group will complete survey assessments at baseline, 12 weeks and 20 weeks.

## Patient and public involvement
A stakeholder input phase was conducted to develop and refine POISE. We collected input on the manual from a diverse group of stakeholders, including thoracic oncologists, psychologists, palliative care clinicians and patient and caregiver advocates. We used stakeholder input to refine the intervention. Next, we conducted an open pilot study to further refine POISE based on patient and clinician feedback. Ten patient participants at MGH received POISE and completed all study procedures, including surveys and exit interviews, and we also conducted exit interviews with POISE clinicians. Based on the results of the open pilot, we further refined POISE and modified the study protocol.

## Outcomes

## Demographic and clinical characteristics
At baseline, patients will self-report age, gender, race, ethnicity, education, religion, income, relationship status, previous experience with mental health counselling or psychotherapy, and tobacco use. The RA will collect data regarding the patient’s date of diagnosis, tumour genotype, targeted therapy regimen, performance status and comorbidities from the EHR.

## Primary outcomes
The primary outcome for this study is feasibility. We define feasibility as ≥60% enrolment among eligible patients, ≥70% completion of all sessions for participants in the intervention group and ≥70% completion of all surveys for all study participants. We anticipate attrition due to advanced cancer progression and complications, consistent with prior studies in this population. 

## Exploratory outcomes
We will explore additional outcomes to assess the psychosocial effects of the intervention. To examine emotional coping with prognosis and prognostic awareness, we will use the Prognostic Awareness Impact Scale. This questionnaire includes questions regarding perceptions of the curability of disease and the effect of having knowledge about prognosis on attitudes and behaviours. For self-efficacy, we will use The Self-Efficacy of Management of Chronic Disease 6-Item Scale, which asks patients to rate their confidence in managing chronic disease. To assess tolerance of uncertainty, we will use The Intolerance of Uncertainty-Short Form, a 12-item questionnaire. For psychological distress, patients will complete the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale. Patients will also complete the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy—Lung as a measure of quality of life and the Adult Trait Hope Scale as a measure of hope and goal setting. Additionally, the Feeling Heard and Understood measure will be used to assess clinical communication in the 12-week and 20-week surveys. To determine the acceptability of POISE, a 4-item questionnaire regarding patients’ satisfaction with the delivery and helpfulness of POISE will be used for patients randomised to the POISE group. At last, at 24 weeks post enrolment, we will use a previously developed natural language processing/machine learning algorithm to identify documentation of goals of care conversations among patients and clinicians. 

## Safety and adverse effects
POISE is a low-risk behavioural intervention; therefore, we do not anticipate any harm to participants. However, the risks of participation include loss of confidentiality and patient distress during visits, surveys or the exit interview. We have used safeguards to avoid breach of confidentiality and maintain patient privacy, including requiring human subjects training for all study staff, using password-protected databases and storing data on secure, encrypted servers. At weekly meetings, the team will discuss summaries of the non-serious adverse events (non-SAEs) mentioned above, and the numbers and rates at which these occur by treatment group. Treatment retention and reasons for withdrawal in each group will also be discussed.
Due to the non-therapeutic nature of this trial, we do not anticipate any adverse events from the intervention to meet the Food and Drug Administration definition of an SAE. However, based on the study population, serious clinical events are expected to occur due to the incurable nature of the illness and high rates of symptoms from underlying disease and treatment side effects. Given that palliative care involvement does not have the capacity to affect the occurrence of SAEs and due to the high rate of SAEs that occur naturally in this population, we will not collect and report these events to the committee. This is consistent with previous and ongoing palliative care trials.

## Data collection and management
The study principal investigator (PI) will oversee all data collection and management performed by authorised study team members who have completed training in the protection of human subjects. Data will be stored and managed in password-protected, encrypted databases on secure servers. All participants will be assigned a study identification (ID), which will be linked to a single file containing patient identifiers and protected health information (PHI). However, all other files containing participant information will be linked to non-PHI study IDs. The final trial dataset will be available to the PI, statisticians and RA. Interview recordings will be destroyed once the study ends. Hard copies of consent forms and surveys will be placed in long-term storage or destroyed. All data will be stored for the study duration and then archived as de-identified files. A data safety and monitoring plan has been developed to ensure the protocol complies with Federal Regulations, HIPAA requirements and applicable Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center (DF/HCC) standard operating procedures.

## Statistical analysis
Prior to analysing primary and exploratory outcomes, we will assess the data for missingness and statistical assumptions, followed by sensitivity analyses and transforming variables as appropriate.
The primary outcome of the study is feasibility, which we define a priori as ≥60% enrolment among eligible patients, ≥70% completion of all sessions in the intervention group and ≥70% completion of all surveys in both groups. We will compute a sample proportion and corresponding 95% CI for each feasibility metric. The sample size of 60 will allow us to estimate feasibility estimates of ≥60% enrolment (95% CI within ±9.8% margin of error), 70% completion of all sessions (within ±16.4% margin of error) and >70% completion of study surveys (within ±11.6% margin of error).
To evaluate the preliminary effects of POISE on continuous exploratory outcomes of emotional coping with prognosis and self-efficacy, we will calculate means and 95% CIs of these scores within and between groups at baseline, 12 weeks and 20 weeks. We will use intention-to-treat analyses for all randomised patients. Additionally, we will compare change within and between groups over time using linear mixed models controlling for demographic and clinical factors as well as baseline values as necessitated by imbalances across study groups. We will analyse exploratory dichotomous outcomes of patient awareness of prognosis and documentation of goals and values by comparing proportions across groups at each time point, using binary regression models (eg, logistic regression) to adjust estimated proportions for baseline covariates. We will report summary and descriptive measures for acceptability scores and consider acceptability as ≥70% of patients reporting favourable responses to the acceptability questions (ie, agree or strongly agree with statements of acceptability). We will also compute standardised effect sizes (eg, Cohen’s d) to compare the magnitude of the intervention effect across exploratory preliminary efficacy outcomes. Effect sizes that we estimate in this study will serve as one metric to plan a future, adequately powered efficacy trial, along with measures of variability of outcome measures and any established estimates for detecting clinically meaningful differences in outcomes.

## Limitations
This pilot study has important limitations to note. First, the non-blinded study design introduces a risk of bias due to patient and clinician awareness of study assignment, which may affect how patients respond to outcome measurement questions. Blinding of this type of study is not possible; thus, this limitation must be considered in the interpretation of results. Limiting the study to a single site is also practically necessary at this stage of intervention development, though it affects the generalisability of the study. We will conduct future large-scale efficacy studies at multiple sites. Additionally, allowing the option for supportive services is ethically necessary, yet if patients receive early palliative care or mental health services, the study will be biased towards a null finding given possible contamination of the usual care group since palliative care clinicians may have received POISE training.

## Ethics and dissemination
This study was approved by the DF/HCC institutional review board (IRB) (protocol 20-722). The study will be reported in accordance with the CONSORT statement for non-pharmacological trials. All study measures and activities will be in accordance with the approved protocol. Any changes to the study protocol will be submitted as an amendment to the IRB and will be communicated to all study personnel if approved. Patients will provide consent for the study and will be informed if any major changes occur. If necessary, patients will be given the option to reconsent or withdraw from the study. This trial is low risk with no anticipated harm to the participants. The description of the study, status of the trial and results will be posted on ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04900935. Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals, presented at scientific meetings and disseminated to relevant organisations, including interested participants. Requests for data access via a data usage agreement with the DF/HCC IRB will be considered.

## Current trial status
Recruitment and enrolment for the RCT began on 21 November 2024. Any changes to the status of the trial will be updated on ClinicalTrials.gov (trial registration number: NCT04900935).
