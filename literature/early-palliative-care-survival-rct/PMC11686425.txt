## ABSTRACT

## Objectives
Individuals living with advanced cancer commonly experience death anxiety, which refers to the distressing thoughts or feelings associated with awareness of one's mortality. Deriving an overview of existing literature on the psychological and social factors linked to death anxiety may inform conceptual models, clinical screening, and intervention strategies in oncology and palliative care. Therefore, the present scoping review was conducted to summarize the current literature on the psychosocial correlates of death anxiety among individuals with advanced cancer.

## Methods
A comprehensive scoping review methodology was used following the Arksey and O'Malley framework. A literature search was conducted using four electronic databases: CINAHL, Embase, PsycInfo, and MEDLINE. Abstracts and full‐text articles were screened, and relevant data were extracted and summarized.

## Results
Sixteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Seventeen psychosocial correlates of death anxiety were identified, with depression, spiritual well‐being, and attachment security representing the most frequently investigated. Four previously tested death anxiety models were also identified, two of which were designed longitudinally.

## Conclusions
This review provides a current summary of psychosocial factors and established models related to death anxiety in advanced cancer. Multiple psychosocial correlates should be targeted concurrently in research and clinical practice to address death anxiety. Longitudinal studies designed to test new models are especially needed to identify unique pathways contributing to death anxiety across the disease trajectory of advanced cancer.

## Stage 5: Collating, Summarizing, and Reporting the Results
This final stage involved summarizing and reporting the results of the included studies. The first author (TLB) performed this process, which was then checked for accuracy by the last author (JJS). Scoping reviews aim to provide a descriptive account of the available research without evaluating it using a quality assessment tool [ ]. Therefore, a quality assessment was not conducted. Instead, the results below provide an overview of the included studies.

## Results
3.1

## Study Population
Sixteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Most were conducted in Canada (n = 8), with the rest conducted in Germany (n = 4), China (n = 2), Italy (n = 1), and the United States (n = 1). The sample size ranged from 21 to 386 participants, with the average study including 173 participants.

3.2

## Study Design
Ten of the 16 studies included conducted a secondary analysis of existing data [ , , , , , , , , , ]. Four studies conducted a secondary analysis of baseline data from intervention studies [ , , , ], three conducted a secondary analysis of baseline data from randomized control trials [ , , ], and three conducted a secondary analysis of longitudinal data from randomized control trials [ , , ]. The remaining six studies were all cross‐sectional [ , , , , , ].

3.3

## Model Tested
Four studies examined a conceptual model of death anxiety [ , , , ]. An and collegues [ ] used structural equation modeling to investigate protective and risk factors for demoralization and death anxiety, finding that higher symptom burden and lower social relatedness were significantly associated with demoralization, which in turn was linked to death anxiety. Moreover, Philipp and colleagues [ ] tested a longitudinal model and found that baseline attachment avoidance partially mediated the effects of baseline death anxiety on demoralization and anxiety scores at a six‐month follow‐up.
In addition, Scheffold and colleagues [ ] found that spiritual well‐being mediated the association between attachment security and death anxiety in fearful‐avoidant attached patients. Last, Willis and colleagues [ ] investigated a longitudinal model of terror management defenses (i.e., meaning in life, self‐esteem, and attachment security) and their buffering effect on death anxiety. They reported that individuals with a greater number of terror management defenses at higher levels showed lower death anxiety when experiencing more severe physical symptoms than those with one or no terror management defense at high levels.

3.4

## Participant Characteristics
All but one study [ ] used participants with mixed cancer types. All studies included participants with stage 4 cancer. Six studies used stage 3 and 4 participants without specifying the percentages for each stage [ , , , , , ]. Of the remaining 10 studies that did specify proportions for stage 3 and 4 [ , , , , , , , , , ], those in stage 3 ranged from 12.7% to 27.4% of the sample while those in stage 4 ranged from 62.0% to 100% of the sample. Two studies included participants with stage 1 and 2 cancer [ , ]. In the first study [ ], 20% of participants were in stages 1 and 2, while in the second study [ ], 5% were in stage 1.

3.5

## Identifying Psychosocial Correlates of Death Anxiety in Advanced Cancer
The included studies reported on empirical tests of relationships between 19 psychosocial factors proposed to correlate with death anxiety (see Table  ). Of these 19 psychosocial factors, 17 were significantly associated with death anxiety. However, two factors, social support (Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) [ ]; used in one study) and post‐traumatic growth (Post‐Traumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) [ ]; used in two studies), were not significantly related to death anxiety. The psychosocial correlates most commonly examined were depression (Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) [ ]; used in 7 studies), spiritual well‐being (Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy–Spiritual Well‐Being (FACIT‐SP‐12) [ ]; used in 7 studies), and attachment security (Modified Experiences in Close Relationships Scale (ECR‐M16) [ ]; used in 7 studies). Five studies investigated anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7; GAD‐7 [ ]), demoralization (Demoralization Scale; DS [ ]), and quality‐of‐life at end‐of‐life (Quality‐of‐Life at the End‐of‐Life Cancer; QUAL‐EC [ ]/Quality‐of‐Life at the End‐of‐Life; QUAL‐E [ ]), while three studies assessed self‐esteem (Rosenberg Self‐Esteem Scale; RSES [ ]). Two studies examined health‐related quality‐of‐life (Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–General; FACT‐G [ ]/Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy–Palliative Care; FACIT‐PALL‐46 [ ]). The following nine psychosocial correlates were utilized only in one study: combined depressive and anxiety symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire‐4; PHQ‐4 [ ]), existential distress (Existential Distress Scale; EDS [ ]), fear of cancer progression (Fear of Progression Questionnaire–Short Form; FOP‐Q‐SF [ ]), illness intrusion (Illness Intrusiveness Rating Scale; IIRS [ ]), medical coping style (Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire; MCMQ [ ]), perceived cognitive functioning (Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Cognitive Function; FACT‐COG‐V3 [ ]), personal dignity (Personal Dignity Inventory; PDI [ ]), religious and spiritual struggle (Religious and Spiritual Struggle Scale‐14; RRS‐14 [ ]), and resilience (Connor‐Davidson Resilience Scale; CD‐RISC [ ]).

3.6

## Death Anxiety Measures
The 16 studies in this scoping review used two different validated death anxiety measures. The DADDS [ , , ] was used in 15 studies, while the Templer‐Death Anxiety Scale (DAS) [ ] was used in 1 study.

3.7

## Assessing the Psychosocial Correlates of Death Anxiety
3.7.1

## Depression
Among the seven studies that investigated depression, results consistently showed a significant positive association between the PHQ‐9 total score and death anxiety [ , , , , , , ].

3.7.2

## Spiritual Well‐Being
Of the seven studies that assessed spiritual well‐being, five found a significant negative association between the FACIT‐SP‐12 total score and death anxiety [ , , , , ]. Moreover, the Meaning [ , , , ], Faith [ , ], and Comfort/Peace [ ] subscales were all found to be negatively associated with death anxiety. Still, one study found no significant relationship between the Faith subscale and death anxiety [ ].

3.7.3

## Attachment Security
Two of the seven studies that assessed attachment security reported that the ECR‐M16 total score positively correlated with death anxiety [ , ]. In addition, the Attachment Anxiety [ , , , ] and Attachment Avoidance [ , , , ] subscales were positively associated with death anxiety, with one study [ ] reporting no significant relationships between the Attachment Avoidance subscale and death anxiety. In another study [ ], cut‐off scores on the Attachment Anxiety and Attachment Avoidance subscales were used to create four attachment orientations: secure, dismissing, preoccupied, and fearful‐avoidant. According to this study, preoccupied and fearful‐avoidant attachment styles predicted higher death anxiety. Of note, preoccupied attachment was constructed based on scores above the Attachment Anxiety subscale mean cut‐off and below the Attachment Avoidance subscale mean cut‐off. In contrast, fearful‐avoidant attachment was defined as scores above both the Attachment Anxiety and Attachment Avoidance subscale cut‐offs.

3.7.4

## Anxiety
For anxiety, five studies consistently reported a significant positive relationship between the GAD‐7 total score and death anxiety [ , , , , ].

3.7.5

## Demoralization
The five studies that examined demoralization repeatedly found that the DS total score had a significantly positive correlation with death anxiety [ , , , , ]. In one of these studies [ ], death anxiety was significantly positively associated with all five demoralization subscales (i.e., Loss of Meaning, Dysphoria, Disheartenment, Helplessness, and Sense of Failure).

3.7.6

## Quality‐Of‐Life at End‐Of‐Life
Of the five studies that investigated quality‐of‐life at end‐of‐life, only one [ ] assessed and reported a significant negative association between the QUAL‐EC total score and death anxiety. Scores on the Life Completion [ , , ], Preparation for End‐of‐Life [ , , ], and Relationship with Healthcare Providers [ ] subscales were all negatively associated with death anxiety. One study [ ] found that all four subscales (i.e., Symptom Burden, Relationship with Healthcare Provider, Preparation for End‐of‐Life, and Life Completion) were associated with death anxiety during a univariate analysis. However, in a multivariate analysis including other risk factors for death anxiety, only the Preparation for End‐of‐Life and Relationship with Healthcare Providers subscales remained significantly associated with death anxiety.

3.7.7

## Self‐Esteem
The three studies that assessed self‐esteem consistently reported a significant negative association between self‐esteem and death anxiety [ , , ]. One of these studies [ ] found that after including self‐esteem in multivariate analysis with other risk factors for death anxiety, it was no longer predictive of death anxiety.

3.7.8

## Health‐Related Quality‐Of‐Life
One [ ] of the two studies examining the relationship between health‐related quality‐of‐life and death anxiety found a negative correlation between the FACT‐G total score and death anxiety, while the other [ ] reported a negative relationship between the FACIT‐PAL‐46 total score and death anxiety. Of note, these two health‐related quality‐of‐life measures (i.e., FACT‐G and FACIT‐PAL‐46) both have four subscales with the same names (i.e., Physical, Functional, Social/Family, and Emotional). In both studies [ , ], all four subscales were negatively associated with death anxiety.

3.7.9

## Other Psychosocial Correlates
The PHQ‐4 total score was positively related to death anxiety in one study [ ], with the Depression and Anxiety subscales also showing a positive linkage to death anxiety. In the one study [ ] that considered existential distress, the EDS total score was positively correlated with death anxiety. The one study [ ] that captured fear of cancer progression found that the FOP‐Q‐SF total score was positively associated with death anxiety. The one study [ ] considering illness intrusion reported a positive association between the IIRS total score and death anxiety. This significant association was documented in both univariate and multivariate analyses. In the one study [ ] addressing medical coping style, Resignation and Confrontation Coping Mode subscales were positively associated with death anxiety. The Avoidance Coping Mode subscale was not significantly related to death anxiety.
For perceived cognitive functioning, one study [ ] reported that greater perceived cognitive impairment, as indicated by the Perceived Cognitive Impairment subscale of the FACT‐COG‐V3, was significantly correlated with death anxiety scores. However, this association was not found in a multivariate analysis containing a broader range of death anxiety risk factors. One study [ ] looking at personal dignity reported a positive relationship between the PDI total score (i.e., with higher scores indicating more dignity‐related problems) and death anxiety. One study [ ] that assessed religious and spiritual struggle found a positive association between the RSR‐14 total score and death anxiety. Another study [ ] considering resilience found an inverse relationship between the CD‐RISC total score and death anxiety. Scores on all three subscales of the Chinese version of this scale [ ] (i.e., Tenacity, Self‐Improvement, and Optimism) were inversely correlated with death anxiety.

## Discussion
4.1

## Summary of Main Findings
The overarching goal of this review was to summarize the peer‐reviewed literature on the psychosocial correlates of death anxiety in advanced cancer. Only three of the 16 included studies employed a longitudinal design to detect participant changes over time. The remaining studies collected data at a single time point, providing a momentary rather than extended assessment of the associations between psychosocial factors and death anxiety. The review also found that only four of the 16 studies tested a conceptual model of death anxiety, with only two of these being longitudinal.
The review also identified 17 psychosocial factors significantly associated with death anxiety in advanced cancer. The most frequently reported factors were depression, spiritual well‐being, and attachment security, each highlighted in seven studies. Other factors mentioned in multiple studies include anxiety, demoralization, end‐of‐life quality, self‐esteem, and health‐related quality of life. Factors investigated in a single study include combined depressive and anxiety symptoms, existential distress, fear of cancer progression, illness intrusion, medical coping style, perceived cognitive functioning, personal dignity, religious and spiritual struggle, and resilience. These results offer alternative pathways for managing death anxiety and a foundation to guide future research.

4.2

## Directions for Future Research
Given the finding that only four studies [ , , , ] have attempted to validate a death anxiety model in advanced cancer, the development and testing of comprehensive conceptual models of death anxiety are crucial next steps in this field. Future research could focus on creating models that address the complex, multivariate pathways contributing to death anxiety, incorporating the psychosocial factors highlighted in this review. Additionally, as only two studies [ , ] have explored death anxiety models over time, there is a pressing need for longitudinal research to identify trajectory patterns throughout the progression of advanced cancer. In addition to providing an empirical basis for the development, assessment, and clinical treatment of death anxiety, these models could afford new conceptualizations of the underlying theories and processes involved in death anxiety [ ].
The development and testing of new models of death anxiety may also hold implications for decision‐making in advanced cancer by highlighting the role of death anxiety in healthcare choices and preferences. Drawing on the principles of TMT, the Terror Management Health Model (TMHM) [ ] postulates that health‐related behaviors and motivations can be profoundly affected by death anxiety. Perry and colleagues [ ] have extended this framework by proposing a model of serious illness and end‐of‐life care, which states that death anxiety can enhance emotional distress, which, in turn, can elicit avoidant coping responses, leading to inadequate preparedness, suboptimal advanced care planning, reduced utilization of palliative care, and an overreliance on aggressive, potentially unnecessary treatments. Future research could integrate the psychosocial correlates of death anxiety identified in this review with the thematic model proposed by Perry and colleagues. This model includes: (1) Mortality Prime—factors that heighten awareness of mortality (i.e., death anxiety); (2) Psychosocial Effects—the psychological and social responses shaped by mortality primes; (3) Emotional Regulation—psychological mechanisms that regulate or exacerbate psychological distress; and (4) Healthcare Implications—the consequences of psychosocial and emotional factors on healthcare decision‐making.
In addition to this framework, future research could examine spiritual dimensions of death anxiety in advanced cancer. Numerous studies [ , , , , , , ] in this review reported a link between spiritual well‐being and death anxiety, underscoring the need to investigate this relationship further. Such research may be particularly timely, as it has been suggested that spiritual care remains one of the most neglected elements of palliative care [ ]. One approach to prioritizing spiritual care could involve investigating meaning and meaning‐making processes in advanced cancer [ ]. Meaning in life is a critical component of spiritual well‐being and is often associated with positive mental health outcomes [ ], including lower psychological distress in cancer patients [ ]. It remains unclear, however, how individuals reconstruct their sources of meaning to accommodate the experience of advanced cancer and which kinds of meaning adjustments promote or hinder physical and psychological well‐being [ ]. Therefore, future research on death anxiety should focus on tailored strategies to promote meaning in life adaptability and optimal techniques to address barriers to meaning reconstruction.
Furthermore, the relational dimensions of death anxiety may also warrant further attention. Several studies [ , , , , , , ] in this review indicated a connection between attachment security and death anxiety. Accordingly, a potentially important area of future research involves examining death anxiety risk factors associated with the quality of the patient‐clinician relationship. For example, oncologists with more significant self‐reported death anxiety have been shown to experience more difficulties related to medical communication, such as early and open communication concerning palliative care and end‐of‐life issues in advanced cancer [ ]. These communication difficulties are likely to have differential effects on how individual patients navigate the clinical relationship and may leave some feeling more isolated and disconnected from their clinician [ , ]. In such instances, opportunities to support patient attachment needs are forfeited, which may serve as a risk factor for death anxiety rooted in one's interaction with the healthcare system. Hence, identifying patient attachment style‐specific strategies for patient‐clinician communication in advanced cancer may represent a vital area for future research.

4.3

## Clinical Implications
The routine assessment of death anxiety among individuals with advanced cancer may allow proactive interventions to be introduced to alleviate and prevent such distress [ ]. Moreover, interventions that explicitly target the above‐identified psychosocial correlates may improve existing and future psychological interventions for reducing death anxiety in advanced cancer. Consequently, the multiple psychosocial correlates identified in this review emphasize the need for a broader diffusion of psychological science and knowledge into clinical training, along with the application of psychotherapeutic interventions targeting death anxiety reduction. For example, substantial literature currently connects attachment theory to death anxiety [ ]. Understanding and applying this connection to clinical relationships with seriously ill patients can help clinicians meet care needs across divergent attachment styles [ ]. Incidentally, such an endeavor would also oblige oncology and palliative care providers to reflect on their own relationship to mortality and how it influences their provision of care and patient interactions [ , , , ]. The emerging concept of existential maturity [ , ] aligns with this clinical approach to death anxiety as it emphasizes—for patients and clinicians alike—accepting one's mortality, finding meaning in life despite its inherent limitations, and engaging in life‐affirming activities.
The recurrent association of spiritual well‐being and attachment security with death anxiety found in this review is consistent with TMT, which posits that individuals are fundamentally motivated to obtain a sense of meaning and value to alleviate death anxiety [ ]. Several existentially oriented interventions that recognize advanced cancer patients' need to maintain meaning and value in the face of heightened mortality salience are currently available. Such interventions include Breitbart and Poppito's Meaning‐Making Centered Therapy [ ], Chochinov's Dignity Therapy [ ], and Rodin and Hales' Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully [ ]. Thus, the findings of this review suggest a need for the broader implementation of these interventions to treat death anxiety. Furthermore, these findings may offer additional psychosocial themes that can be integrated into all three of these interventions to strengthen their efficacy in addressing death anxiety. Overall, these innovations in clinical practice may facilitate increased acceptance of imminent death and lessened resistance to specialist palliative care referrals and early palliative care [ ], a healthcare service shown to improve mood, quality‐of‐life, and median survival while lowering aggressive end‐of‐life care [ ].

4.4

## Study Strengths and Limitations
This review is the first to address psychosocial correlates of death anxiety, synthesizing evidence in an advanced cancer context. The focus of this review aligns with the recently expressed need for palliative care to incorporate a more psychologically sophisticated understanding of the patient experience in serious illness [ ]. It also comes at a time when existentially informed approaches to clinical psychology, which highlight death anxiety, are gaining traction [ ] and making promising inroads into palliative care [ ]. However, there are various limitations to this review. Despite an extensive search of multiple databases, some relevant studies may have been overlooked. For example, gray literature and studies in languages other than English were not included. Furthermore, except for two studies based in China, all included studies were conducted in Western countries. Therefore, results may be limited in their cross‐cultural application. In addition, the cross‐sectional correlations presented in this review do not allow for conclusions about causality to be drawn.

5

## Conclusion
This scoping review aimed to establish an overview of existing literature on the psychosocial correlates of death anxiety in advanced cancer. A total of 17 psychosocial correlates and four conceptual models of death anxiety were identified through 16 published studies. This review signifies a critical step in the process of moving toward death anxiety conceptual modeling and psychological interventions in palliative care to provide timely assessment and targeted support. Advances such as these will be essential as palliative care endeavors to deepen its understanding of psychological suffering in advanced cancer, explicitly concerning the existential wounds inflicted by impending death and acute mortality salience.
