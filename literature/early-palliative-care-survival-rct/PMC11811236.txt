## Background
Although the benefits of early palliative care have been established in advanced cancers, there remains a lack of access to and poor uptake of these services. Barriers include healthcare provider attitudes, lack of standardized referral approach, misaligned policy limited resources, and lack of palliative care expertise.

## Purpose
In consideration of these barriers, a description of the development and implementation of an early palliative care initiative at CancerCare Manitoba in Canada is presented. Explanations of innovative planning, processes, teams, and interventions are offered to facilitate insight for those intending to offer early palliative care.

## Discussion
Barriers and facilitators to integrating a nurse-led early palliative care initiative are discussed. Considerations to mitigate common barriers encountered and leveraging existing resources are offered.

## Conclusions
This information may be beneficial to those working to facilitate access to palliative care in oncology for patients with advanced cancers. This model has been successful at our institution with pre and post implementation data presented in an upcoming publication.

## Introduction
Cancer prevalence is expected to increase with many of these cancers being of an advanced and incurable stage [ , ]. During post COVID-19, many healthcare systems are struggling with human resources and health service reductions, and subsequent backlogs have led to a delay in the diagnosis and treatment of cancers [ , ]. Advanced cancers have been disproportionately affected, with 69% of patients with metastatic cancer reporting that their appointments had been cancelled, postponed, or rescheduled, compared to only 50% of patients with earlier-stage cancers [ ]. Waiting longer to be seen and receive care may be especially challenging for those with advanced cancers [ ], particularly for those dealing with a heavy symptom burden and rapid disease progression [ – ]. An early palliative care (EPC) intervention for those with advanced cancers can facilitate timely access to diagnostics to guide discussions around prognosis, treatment options, and goals of care.
Palliative care is a multidisciplinary approach to care, with a focus on patient and family-centred care and quality of life for those diagnosed with a life-limiting illness [ ], however, is typically only accessed in the final days or weeks of life in Canada [ ], after treatment options have been exhausted [ ]. Randomized studies have shown that earlier introduction of palliative care, EPC, improves the quality of life and symptom burden, reduces health care costs, and can improve overall survival [ – ]. The American Society of Clinical Oncology clinical practice guidelines recommend that for patients with advanced cancer, referral for specialist palliative care services should occur within 8 weeks of diagnosis and should be provided alongside standard oncologic care [ ]. However, several barriers exist to accessing palliative care early and uptake remains low [ , ].
In a survey of oncologists caring for patients with non-small cell lung cancer, < 20% of patients were referred for EPC due to a perceived lack of symptoms amenable to palliative care, and while most patients were unfamiliar with palliative care, 98% would have accepted a referral if recommended by their oncologist [ ]. However, even when attitudes and perceptions of EPC are not a barrier, a lack of time, clinic space, and access to specialist palliative care still exists [ ]. Challenges in standardization of EPC timing and enrolment criteria, time to discuss palliative care with patients and families, and biases or misinformation also contribute to low EPC uptake [ ]. Concern over palliative care interference with the oncology plan of care and oncology ownership of the patient care trajectory are also noted barriers to accessing EPC [ ]. Even policy-related barriers such as fragmented healthcare systems, a lack of funding for palliative care, inadequate reimbursement for palliative care, and regulatory barriers discourage the integration of EPC [ ].
To address these barriers at our provincial cancer institute, we developed a Clinical Nurse Specialist (CNS)-led initiative, providing EPC for patients with advanced and incurable pancreatic, cholangiocarcinoma, and hepatobiliary carcinoma. The scope of the CNS, an advanced practice master or doctoral prepared nurse, includes specialty complex clinical care, consultation, education, research, and leadership [ ]—well suited to the development and evaluation of innovative initiatives [ ].
This article reviews the implementation of a novel CNS-led model of EPC in the province of Manitoba, Canada. Informed consent was not sought as this manuscript reports on a model of care, not a clinical trial. Ethics approval was received by the University of Manitoba Health Research Ethics Board, HS2018:291, for ongoing research related to this described model of care. This research was deemed to be minimal risk by our ethics board, according to the Tri-Council Policy Statement 2: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans.

## Oncology and palliative services in Manitoba

## Oncology services
In Canada, healthcare is delivered provincially through a publicly funded system. Patients do not pay out of pocket for medically necessary healthcare services. In Manitoba, CancerCare Manitoba (CCMB) is responsible for the delivery of all oncology services to the provincial population of 1.369 million people. Solid tumour consultative services are provided primarily in ambulatory outpatient clinics in three tertiary care centres in Winnipeg, accounting for approximately 57% of the provincial population [ ], and one centre in Brandon, accounting for approximately 9% of the provincial population [ ]. Oncology care is shared between oncologists in the Winnipeg and Brandon centres and family practitioners in oncology throughout the province. Care is provided in person, by telehealth, phone, or secured video conferencing based on the need for in person assessment, physical location, and patient preference. Initial oncologist consultation is most often required to be in person at one of the Winnipeg or Brandon CCMB locations, with the ability to receive treatment closer to home within one of the 16 community cancer programs.

## Palliative care services
Access to home or community palliative care and hospice services is available through 5 regional service delivery organizations with varying accessibility, enrolment criteria, and services offered. The Winnipeg region offers comprehensive community-based and inpatient palliative care with enrolment criteria of a life expectancy of ≤ 6 months, for those not receiving active chemotherapy, and not wishing for resuscitative or life-prolonging measures. Services are generally more limited across rural and northern regions and enrolment criteria are variable but typically more inclusive. With a lack of integration of palliative and oncology services, and inconsistent and sometimes quite limited access to community-based palliative services, patients with advanced cancers have frequently been faced with deciding whether they wish to pursue palliative intent chemotherapy or community-based palliative care, a significant gap in the care we aimed to address.

## Early palliative care clinic

## Patients
This EPC clinic began with accepting patients with a high suspicion or diagnosis of unresectable or metastatic pancreatic cancer and has since expanded to now include patients with advanced cholangiocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma ineligible for local therapies. These advanced cancers were chosen due to high symptom burden and rapid disease progression. Patients can be referred to EPC at any time throughout the diagnostic process prior to seeing an oncologist.

## Setting
CCMB has a Central Referrals Office (CRO) where all new referrals are accepted/reviewed. When a patient presents to medical attention with suspicion of a cancer diagnosis, a referral is sent to the CRO and directed to the appropriate Disease Site Group (DSG). Within individual DSGs, each referral is assessed for completion of required diagnostic information prior to scheduling a consultation with an oncologist. This phase is overseen by an oncologist and is coordinated by a referral nurse and clerk. During this time, there is minimal contact between CCMB and the patient, and the patient has yet to meet an oncologist. As such, the primary care provider continues to be the patient’s primary contact and is responsible for ordering any outstanding diagnostic tests as requested by the CRO. Once the diagnostic workup is complete, the patient is scheduled to be seen by an oncologist. Cases can concurrently be referred to the Provincial Cancer Referral and Navigation (PCRN) Nurse Navigator service, which is seated within the CRO, and provides mostly telephone support and guidance for patients and healthcare providers navigating the cancer care system during the diagnostic phase; however, prior to the development of the EPC clinic, the CRO did not have an advanced practice nurse, a clinical space, or the expertise to assist in symptom management. Considering the pre-existing well-integrated and supported referrals system, alongside the gaps identified, we chose to situate the EPC initiative within the PCRN service.

## Referral process
When a patient is referred with a suspicion or diagnosis of one of the above-advanced cancers, the CRO standard operating procedure requires a referral for early palliative care consultation. EPC referrals are also received from hepatobiliary surgeons, primary care providers, nurse navigators, inpatient wards, emergency departments, or oncologists when cases require more complex palliative care input. The CNS evaluates the referral to ensure that it meets current EPC referral criteria. A letter is sent to the primary care provider and referrer acknowledging receipt and acceptance of the referral with the next steps or reasons for declining the referral. A copy of this letter is sent to all other relevant healthcare providers. The CNS aims to contact the patient within 24 to 48 business hours of receipt of the referral (see Fig.  for an overview of the EPC process). 

## Initial patient contact
The CNS contacts the patient, being mindful that not all patients have received a confirmed diagnosis. A brief introduction is made, offering support for symptom management and coping with uncertainty. Further discussion may occur regarding diagnosis, the cancer care system process, and potential treatment options, where appropriate and desired. A full consultation appointment is offered with a goal of seeing the patient within 7 days. A patient is free to decline the consult or invite additional support persons.

## EPC team
An initial consult is completed by a CNS with specialty certification in palliative care. The CNS will then refer to, or directly involve, other team members as needed. The primary care provider remains essential, particularly as no oncologist is yet assigned to the patient, but also throughout each patient’s time at CCMB and after. Appropriate referrals for internal collaborative care are sent to a registered dietitian with disease-specific expertise, the pain and symptom clinic for complex pain management, psychosocial oncology, nurse navigation, family practitioners in oncology, and radiation oncology. External referrals or consultations may be sent to hepatobiliary surgery, gastroenterology, and various community supports including community palliative care programs. If urgent assessment is required, both an oncologist with disease site-specific expertise and an oncologist with specialty palliative certification are available.

## Initial consultation
The initial consultation is modelled after the Canadian Hospice Palliative Care Association (CHPCA) guidelines for palliative care [ ] and the WHO definition of palliative care [ ]. Each consultation is tailored to the needs of the patient and family/caregivers and is cognizant that each patient may be in a different stage of diagnostic workup. The consult is structured to include a discussion of patient and family/caregiver understanding of diagnosis and prognosis, pain and symptom management, medication review, emotional wellbeing, coping, general review of disease-specific treatment options where appropriate, goals of care discussion, advance care planning, patient and family/caregiver education, and suggested referrals (see Table  ).
 
Prior to the initial EPC consult, the case has been reviewed by the DSG triage physician, who is a medical oncologist with expertise in pancreatic and liver cancers, and may have been reviewed in multidisciplinary rounds, by a hepatobiliary surgeon, a gastroenterologist, or other medical specialty. During the initial EPC consult, the advantages and disadvantages of relevant procedures (e.g. imaging-guided biopsy or endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy) and consults (e.g. medical oncology consultation to discuss systemic therapy options) are reviewed alongside a goals of care discussion. If a patient expresses a reasonable preference to forego upcoming diagnostic procedures, an oncology consultation, or cancer-directed therapy, along with a desire for referral to a community palliative care program, this is then reviewed with the DSG triage physician. Upon agreement, a referral is made to the local community palliative care program, and any unwanted upcoming appointments are cancelled. A plan for follow up and next steps is made at the end of the CNS consultation.
The CNS consult letter is sent to the primary care provider and copied to the referrer and all other relevant healthcare providers. This letter provides a summary of the consult, including symptom management recommendations, goals of care, advance care plan status, and substitute decision makers identified. It also identifies any additional referrals made as part of the CNS consult, the follow up plan and next steps, and any further requests required of the primary care provider.

## Follow up
Follow up is completed according to the plan identified in the initial consult or expedited if required by a change in patient status. Symptom management and any medication changes are reassessed and any incomplete discussions around goals of care are revisited. A new plan is formed for ongoing follow up based on patient and family/caregiver needs. If close monitoring is required, the patient can be seen again in 3–5 days. Patients and families/caregivers are encouraged to call back sooner if required. Regular follow up continues until the patient’s care is transitioned to either an oncologist or a community palliative care program. In the unusual case that a patient does not want to receive an oncology consultation, and does not qualify for enrolment in a community palliative care program, discussion with the primary care provider for ongoing follow up is initiated. Ongoing communication is maintained with other members of the healthcare team involved.

## Transition
A transition appointment is scheduled after the patient has met with either an oncologist or the community palliative care program to discuss any new plans made and to finalize any EPC follow up. At the transition appointment patients and family/caregivers are informed that their primary contact is now either their oncologist or their community palliative care team. No formal follow up is scheduled, but patients are encouraged to call back if requiring anything further from EPC. A summary transition letter is sent to the primary care provider.

## Characteristics of referred patients
The EPC clinic began in March 2022 with advanced pancreatic cancer consults and added hepatocellular and cholangiocarcinomas in March 2023. In the first 18 months, 269 referrals were received. Of these, 28 were declined as they did not fit within the EPC clinic criteria and four died before being seen. Of the remaining 241 referrals, 157 were referred for symptom management, 61 for goals of care discussion, 6 for further education and information, and 17 did not identify a specific need beyond addressing the general challenges associated with being diagnosed with an advanced hepatobiliary cancer. Of the 237 consults offered, two were declined by the patient and family/caregivers, and of note, three were found to have a different cancer type, and one individual was deemed to not have a cancer diagnosis. One hundred twenty-four were seen by EPC during the time of high suspicion of a cancer diagnosis, while 113 were seen by EPC after tissue diagnosis confirmation. At the initial consult for new or suspected diagnoses, 100 had goals of care aligned with chemotherapy, 90 had goals of care aligned with palliative care alone, including two expressions of desire for Medical Assistance in Dying (MAiD), and 45 were unsure. One hundred thirty-six went on to see the medical oncologist with 76 receiving chemotherapy. Of the 235 patients receiving an initial consult, 231 had confirmed or suspected pancreatic cancer, one had confirmed or suspected cholangiocarcinoma, and three had confirmed or suspected hepatocellular carcinoma (see Table  for a detailed description).
 

## Facilitators and barriers to implementation

## Stakeholder buy in
Essential to garnering support for both the EPC clinic, and the CNS role to lead it, was creating a strong connection and alliance with national, provincial, and institutional priorities. The EPC clinic aligns with the CCMB priorities of timely access to quality cancer care closer to home, providing evidence-based high-equality cancer services, improving the patient experience throughout the cancer continuum, and ensuring the sustainability of cancer services [ ]. It also aligns with the National Framework on Palliative Care in Canada [ ], the Canadian Model to Guide Palliative Care [ ], and upholds the national Palliative Care Competency Framework to ensure high-quality palliative care [ ]. Integral to the EPC clinic is the embedding of patient-centred care, including improving health equity for underserved populations, such as advanced cancers and First Nations, Metis, and Inuit populations.
Much of the buy in required stemmed from the pilot proxy IMPERATIVE trial [ ] which identified the benefits of EPC in advanced pancreatic cancer and developed strategic relationships with other healthcare providers. Lessons learned in the IMPERATIVE trial regarding resistance from other healthcare providers prompted an educational preemptive mitigation strategy on the role, expertise, and evidence-based benefits of a CNS and the improved patient outcomes achieved. Imperative to CCMB’s adoption of this new model of EPC for advanced cancers were the EPC champions, which included medical oncologists, radiation oncologists, palliative care specialists, researchers, the Director of Provincial Cancer Referral and Navigation Services, the Chief Medical Officer, Chief of Clinical Operations, the Chief Executive Officer, and the CancerCare Manitoba Foundation. Hepatobiliary surgeons also provided valuable support, as they saw this initiative as an opportunity to provide early symptom management for their patients. Navigation nurses in the PCRN received education and support for patients with symptom management, decision-making, and disease progression while completing diagnostic investigations. Oncologists saw benefits as patients came to oncology consultations with better symptom management and having considered their goals of care. Those wishing to proceed to community palliative care program enrolment were expedited instead of awaiting an oncology consultation before moving forward with their treatment goals. Referral nurses in the CRO received support in creating efficiencies in the referral process. Collaborations were created with primary care providers and with gastroenterologists and surgeons for expediting diagnostic and palliative procedures.

## The role of the clinical nurse specialist
Early in this initiative, we were met with the commonly encountered lack of understanding of the role of the CNS and the novel use of a nurse-led clinic, identifying an opportunity for education around the roles and expertise of different advanced practice nurses. The value and expertise specific to the CNS role is not commonly understood [ ] and stakeholders are likely to require clarity in the different nursing roles, such as a general duty nurse, a CNS, and a nurse practitioner. As a master or doctoral prepared nurse, a CNS was chosen for this seminal role due to the multifaceted nature of their expertise in (1) comprehensive direct complex clinical care in a specialty area, (2) consulting for all stakeholders to promote positive outcomes and system change, (3) education for patients and providers while championing evidence-informed practice, (4) research contribution both in the generation and application of new evidence, and (5) leadership at all levels, including nursing practice, advocacy and advancement, and program development and evaluation [ ]. Although formal evaluation and reporting are underway, informal surveys of healthcare providers after the first 6 months of EPC implementation showed improved patient symptom management and preparation for medical oncology consults. As stakeholders began to see the value of the CNS expertise, the positive impact on patients, and the potential cost savings to the system [ ], support and endorsement of the EPC clinic and the role of the CNS grew exponentially. Any additional hesitation by other healthcare providers was reduced over time with ongoing collaboration and through the advocacy of colleagues.
Educating stakeholders on the CNS role and using evidence of the outcomes of CNS practice to support this role may help overcome this barrier. Some evidence-based recommendations for the use of the CNS role in oncology include (1) CNS-led clinics for complex diagnostic workup, improving access to care and care coordination, (2) identifying, developing, and supporting complex clinical roles, programs, and teams around specialty practice, (3) providing mental health services, and (4) providing complex care within the Adolescent Young Adult population [ ]. The novel initiation of a nurse-led clinic may encounter hesitancy in a physician-centric organization due to entrenched cultural values, a lack of familiarity and acceptance of processes, roles and responsibilities, and insufficient knowledge of nurse-led clinic outcomes. Support for this model includes cost savings in reducing hospital readmissions and other outcomes, enhanced coordination of care and quality outcomes, improved patient outcomes, and patient flow [ ].
While a nurse practitioner, with prescribing abilities, would be a great addition for symptom management in a nurse-led EPC clinic, the program implementation and evaluation, systems perspective, education, and research abilities of the CNS were most suited to the development and initiation of this novel model of care. The addition of a nurse practitioner after establishing an EPC clinic may be a very valuable one with the role highly focused on the clinical aspect of the model [ ].

## Lack of understanding or acceptance of EPC
As EPC remains a relatively new subspecialty in oncology, a poor understanding of what EPC is, and is not, was an initial barrier. Confusion between EPC and a pain and symptom clinic or EPC and a community-based palliative care program was common. It was essential to repeatedly clarify the goals of EPC and the role of the CNS, ensuring that clinicians were aware that this new role was not intended to replace their own discussions with patients and families around symptom management and goals of care. Instead, the EPC CNS was an additional and complementary resource to guide patients through the gap between referral and oncology consultation and to provide additional palliative care supports. Knowledge translation has occurred with ongoing educational opportunities on the definition and purpose of EPC within CCMB, community palliative care programs, and primary care. Specific knowledge translation activities have included rounds of presentations, conference proceedings, and peer-reviewed publications.
Using the term “palliative care” within this initiative was intentional, although notably debated within the specialty [ ]. While traditionally associated with end-of-life, consistent education on the definition of palliative care as expert symptom management to support the best possible quality of life has been well received by both healthcare providers and patients, with many becoming advocates for more widespread education.

## Available resources
Another challenge in implementation was resource allocation. This model of EPC began with one CNS working with patients with pancreatic and hepatobiliary cancers and existing resources were leveraged. The IMPERATIVE study [ ] served as a pilot proxy, providing the foundational evidence needed to support an EPC clinic for the advanced pancreatic cancer population. The existing knowledge and expertise the CNS brought to and developed in the IMPERATIVE study, over 10 years of community palliative care, pain and symptom, and end-of-life expertise, alongside the developed pancreatic cancer expertise, was also leveraged for the EPC clinic. Without this preexisting expertise, additional training would be required in palliative care and the advanced cancers being served.
By placing the EPC CNS within the nursing navigation team in the CRO, existing structures, space, administrative and clerk staffing, and adapted frameworks and processes could be utilized. Therefore, the only additional upfront cost to initiate this clinic was the cost of one full-time CNS, which allows for all parts of the CNS role previously described and approximately 150 to 200 EPC patients per year, seen in four half-day clinics. Further nursing and potentially physician resources would be required to expand to additional disease sites. Although a CNS-led clinic was a new model of care within CCMB, the appropriate advanced practice nursing electronic documentation supports were available, such as the ability to schedule consultations and refer independently from a physician’s care, and it has led the way for other nurse-led clinics within CCMB.
While implementation leveraged existing resources to support the CNS-led EPC clinic, expansion to other advanced cancer types will require additional human resources, administrative supports, and office and clinic space, a significant challenge moving forward.
Our pilot has shown the great benefit of having a CNS in this role, from a clinical, education, and research perspective. As such, we would advocate for ensuring the CNS has protected time to devote towards research and educational activities. A full environmental scan, with a realistic assessment of available and required costs, space, expertise, human resources, technology, organizational culture, and stakeholder support is essential to building a successful model of care.

## Evaluation and future direction
A change/implementation framework was not formally created but closely followed Glasgow, Vogt, and Boles' (1999) “RE-AIM” framework [ ] consisting of a pilot evaluation of the extent to which the intended target population is reached, the impact on relevant outcomes, and willingness of the organization and individuals to implement EPC. A steering committee was formed to monitor and guide the intervention delivery, fidelity, quality, and consistency. This committee also monitors and guides its long-term sustainability, integration into routine practice, and future directions. EPC evaluation follows a logic model and includes specific outcomes related to symptom burden, quality of life, healthcare provider education, multidisciplinary collaboration, and patient-centred care.
Although not originally designed as a health equity intervention, through our pilot project, we believe that this model of EPC supports earlier access to specialist symptom management, treatment and goals of care discussions, and referrals to supportive oncology services that may otherwise not have occurred in rapidly progressive disease [ ]. As this model expands, the future inclusion of advanced esophagogastric cancer is strongly considered as another cancer with a high symptom burden and rapid disease progression. Significant interest has been noted from hematologic oncologists for the expansion of EPC locally. Additional future EPC team members may ideally include a nurse practitioner, who could support an increased number of patients seen and improved urgent access to medication for symptom management. Ongoing evaluation of our EPC intervention will continue and further contribution to the EPC literature is planned. A report of the patients in our first year is pending.

## Conclusion
Presented is the development and implementation of a novel CNS-led EPC initiative. While there are many established and emerging models of EPC we hope that by offering much-needed palliative supports at the time of diagnosis or high suspicion of cancer, we can improve many aspects of facing and living with an advanced cancer diagnosis. Sharing this detailed description of our innovative model may assist others to conceptualize how EPC may be implemented in their local institutions. We hope the ideas included in this low resource intense option may provide better access to earlier high-quality palliative care for patients with advanced cancers.

## Relevance
While there are many models of early palliative care emerging, our novel intervention at the time of referral to a cancer centre, during the diagnostic phase, and prior to oncology consultation, overcomes many cited barriers. It also allows for earlier palliative intervention for patients with advanced cancers associated with rapid disease progression. This information may assist other programs in providing better access to early palliative care.
