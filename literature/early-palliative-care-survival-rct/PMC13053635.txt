## ABSTRACT
Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality in Australia, with diagnoses projected to rise further following the introduction of the National Lung Cancer Screening Program in July 2025. Comprehensive molecular profiling has become central to the management of non‐small cell lung cancer, enabling tailored therapies such as chemoimmunotherapy, immunotherapy, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors in perioperative, adjuvant, and palliative settings. With the emergence of perioperative systemic therapies and novel agents for the management of metastatic disease, there is a need to ensure that equitable care is delivered across Australia. Further investment in oncology workforce expansion and planning is critical to meet growing demands. In this narrative review, we explore the rapidly evolving landscape of available therapeutics for managing patients with early and advanced NSCLC in the Australian context, highlight the emerging treatment options being investigated in ongoing clinical trials, and discuss future considerations for clinical practice.

Lung cancer remains Australia's leading cause of cancer death. With new screening and evolving therapies, equitable access and workforce readiness are essential. This review discusses current and emerging treatments for NSCLC in Australia, clinical trials underway, and future directions in delivering optimal, accessible care nationwide. 1.

## Background
Lung cancer is the fifth most common cancer in Australia, with over 14,000 new cases diagnosed in 2023, accounting for 9% of all cancer diagnoses [ ]. It is the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality, with over 8500 deaths annually. With the aging population in Australia, lung cancer diagnoses are expected to rise over the next decade [ ]. Currently, the 5‐year survival rate for patients with Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is reported to be between 20% and 30% [ ].
NSCLC accounts for the vast majority (80%–85%) of lung cancer diagnoses [ ]. There has been rapid advancements in staging, next‐generation sequencing, imaging, targeted therapies, immunotherapy, surgery, and radiation techniques for both early and advanced NSCLC globally in the last decade. In particular, the advent of immunotherapy and the rise of neoadjuvant (presurgery or radiation) and perioperative treatment strategies have been paramount to improved outcomes for patients with early‐ and late‐stage NSCLC.
There are multiple considerations to the delivery of optimal care to patients with lung cancer in an Australian setting, including variations in care and outcomes, rising healthcare costs, and the real‐world applicability of global trials to an Australian setting. Equitable access to these scientific advances remains a challenge for many Australians, with almost a third of the population living in regional and remote areas [ ]. Access to specialized diagnostics, radiation therapy, surgical, and systemic therapies in some regions can be limited.
Given the rapid evolution in clinical care, this narrative review article aims to describe recent advancements in the available approaches and therapies for NSCLC management and provide future perspectives for practice in Australia.

2.

## Screening for Lung Cancer
The National Lung Cancer Screening Program is due to commence in July 2025 across Australia. This program will target high‐risk individuals (age 50–70 years; 30 pack‐years of cigarette smoking or former smoking history who have ceased within the last 10 years), providing government‐funded low‐dose computed tomography (LDCT) imaging of the chest at regular intervals [ ]. LDCT screening is aimed at the early detection of lung cancer and has been shown to reduce mortality [ , ]. Importantly, the current criteria for screening excludes individuals without a history of smoking with a family history of lung cancer, identified as a high‐risk feature in the Taiwan Lung Cancer Screening in Never‐Smokers Trial (TALENT) [ ]. However, screening of individuals without a history of smoking has not been evaluated in a randomized setting, and expanding the screening program to include this population requires further research within the Australian context, given its diverse population.
Lung cancer screening also offers an opportunity for discussion regarding smoking cessation [ ]. Australian efforts to date have highlighted the high rates of sustained smoking cessation during screening visits [ ]. Discussion regarding smoking cessation during screening has the potential to decrease lung cancer mortality as well as mortality from other causes [ ].
The National Lung Cancer Screening Guidelines recommend that smoking cessation support be provided alongside the screening and assessment process [ ]. According to the Australian smoking cessation guidelines from the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners, initial support should include psychosocial interventions and nicotine replacement therapy, with pharmacotherapy options such as varenicline and bupropion available through the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) for individuals who require them [ ].

3.

## Diagnosis of NSCLC
The diagnosis of NSCLC is complex, and a multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach is crucial for the appropriate investigation and management of newly diagnosed NSCLC. Key components of diagnosis and workup include staging, tissue biopsy, molecular and PD‐L1 assessment alongside MDT discussion (Figure ). It is therefore unsurprising that lung cancer MDT discussions have been associated with improved adjusted survival in Australia [ ] and internationally [ ]. However, variations in lung cancer MDT models and representation do exist. A survey of Australian hospitals reported that different lung cancer MDTs held irregular meetings, lacked surgical representation and lung cancer nurse specialists, and had insufficient administrative or IT support [ ]. These inconsistencies may contribute to delays in timely referral and poorer patient outcomes and require improvement for the delivery of equitable care in Australia.
 Timely referral and investigation of suspected NSCLC is important to reduce the risk of cancer progression and tumor growth [ , ] and can mitigate patient anxiety and distress associated with uncertainty surrounding cancer diagnosis [ ]. As such, optimal care pathways for patients in Australia recommend that patients with suspected lung cancer are reviewed at a specialist thoracic oncology service within 14 days [ , ].
Accurate staging of NSCLC is critical to both the diagnostic workup and subsequent management, as it determines surgical resectability and the feasibility of definitive radiotherapy which can lead to cure for some patients. Staging should include comprehensive imaging such as CT scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis; an FDG‐PET scan when curative treatment is being considered; and brain imaging with either CT or MRI (Figure  ) [ ]. Acquisition of a tissue specimen through methods such as CT‐guided biopsy, endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) guided biopsy, excisional biopsy, surgical resection, or pleural drainage is required for diagnosis and further testing.
Adequate tissue sampling is crucial for molecular profiling in both early and advanced NSCLC, as the results guide targeted treatment decisions. Molecular alterations can be assessed using immunohistochemistry, fluorescence in situ hybridization, or multigene panel tests funded via the Australian Medicare Benefits Scheme (MBS). New consensus recommendations in Australia suggest that all patients with NSCLC should undergo PD‐L1 testing, those with non‐squamous disease undergo EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 testing, and those with advanced disease should also be tested for BRAF, KRAS, ERBB2 (HER2), MET, RET, and NTRK variants (Figure  ) [ ]. Reflex testing should occur on all specimens in patients with advanced disease to expedite targeted therapies, and whenever clinically appropriate, treatment should be deferred until these results are available.

4.

## Surgically Resectable Early‐Stage NSCLC
4.1.

## Surgery
For patients with early‐stage NSCLC, surgical resection is standard for medically operable patients with Stages I and II disease (Table , Figure  ). Staging in Australia is determined using the American Joint Committee on Cancer's (AJCC) ninth edition of the TNM (tumor, nodes, and metastasis) staging classification [ ] (Table ).
 The definition of surgical resectability, particularly for patients with Stage III NSCLC, has been brought into question with the influx of clinical trials in the perioperative sphere. The benefit of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy has been shown in multiple large randomized controlled trials [ , , , , ]. It is now routinely considered for Stage IIIA NSCLC prior to surgery and should be discussed following MDT presentation. In general, Stage III disease is heterogeneous, and patients with T1‐3 disease and N1–N2 single‐station nodal deposits are considered surgically resectable (Table ) [ ]. However, patients with N2 multi‐station/bulky disease or N3 involvement are considered unresectable. Larger T4 tumors require careful discussion regarding surgery and should be referred to specialized tertiary units and MDT discussion for evaluation.
Lymph node staging is crucial for determining resectability, and for patients in whom a curative approach is being considered, international guidelines recommend mediastinal nodal staging in patients with abnormal mediastinal and/or hilar lymph nodes on CT or FDG‐PET [ , ]. In patients without mediastinal involvement on imaging, EBUS biopsy is advised if there are enlarged or FDG‐avid ipsilateral hilar nodes, a non–FDG‐avid primary tumor, or a primary tumor measuring ≥ 3 cm. Mediastinoscopy should be considered for patients with suspected N1 disease or centrally located tumors when EBUS fails to confirm malignant nodal involvement.
These guidelines have not yet been updated with the use of neoadjuvant immunotherapy, and currently, there is no Australian‐specific guideline for mediastinal staging in NSCLC. Recent trials such as CheckMate‐816, CheckMate‐77T, and Neotorch, which investigated neoadjuvant immunotherapy, mandated preoperative sampling from at least three nodal stations in patients with mediastinal lymphadenopathy on FDG‐PET or CT [ , , ]. Staging was performed using mediastinoscopy, EBUS, or thoracoscopy. Patients with a clinically positive mediastinum (FDG‐PET and/or CT positive) but a negative EBUS biopsy were required to undergo mediastinoscopy to confirm nodal status. Current operative practice for staging should align with these trial protocols.
For patients deemed suitable for surgery, sublobar surgical resection (wedge resection or anatomical segmentectomy) for T1aN0 tumors (≤ 2 cm) has been shown to be non‐inferior to lobectomy [ , ]. This approach preserves pulmonary function at 6 months and whilst the clinical difference is modest, it may be most meaningful for patients with impaired pulmonary function at baseline or those with lower lobe disease, where lobectomy is associated with greater decline in pulmonary function. Additionally, lobectomy is preferred over pneumonectomy where there is appropriate anatomy and margin‐negative resection is achievable due to lower rates of long‐term morbidity [ , ].
Conventional thoracotomy, video‐assisted thoracic surgery (VATS), or robot‐assisted thoracic surgery procedures are currently utilized across different centers in Australia, and the choice of procedure is determined by the proceduralist, tumor anatomy, and available resources. Despite an increased risk of air leak and bleeding, VATS is associated with improved short‐term physical function, shorter hospital stays, reduced pain, and fewer post‐discharge complications [ ]. Other novel techniques, such as robot‐assisted thoracic surgery, appear non‐inferior to VATS with respect to short‐ and long‐term outcomes [ , ]. This technique is highly specialized, and larger trials are required for direct comparison.

4.2.

## Perioperative Therapy
Adjuvant therapy to treat residual micrometastatic disease with platinum‐doublet chemotherapy has been the standard of care following resection of primary NSCLC in patients with lymph node involvement or large tumors for over a decade. Adjuvant chemotherapy alone improves 5‐year survival by a modest 4% compared to placebo [ , ]. Recent oncology trends, including in NSCLC, have shifted more therapy into the neoadjuvant setting. Excitingly, many perioperative approaches in NSCLC, including combinations of chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and targeted therapies, have been shown to reduce tumor size, reduce recurrence rates, and improve pathological complete response (PCR) and survival (Table  ). Depending on pathological features and staging, perioperative systemic therapy is becoming routine for patients with early‐stage NSCLC across Australia, emphasizing the importance of MDT discussion prior to surgery.
 4.2.1.

## Adjuvant Systemic Therapy
Atezolizumab was the first PD‐L1 inhibitor available on the PBS in the adjuvant setting for resected Stages II–IIIA NSCLC with a PD‐L1 expression of ≥ 50% following surgery and adjuvant platinum‐based chemotherapy (Figure  ). The Phase III IMpower010 trial demonstrated a significant improvement in disease‐free survival (DFS) at 24 months of 71.4% with adjuvant atezolizumab compared with 63.6% receiving best supportive care in patients with Stages II–IIIA disease, with most benefit seen in those with PD‐L1 expression of ≥ 1% [ ]. Recently reported data demonstrated improved overall survival (OS) in patients with Stages II–IIIA disease and PD‐L1 expression ≥ 50% [ ]. However, no OS benefit was observed in patients with PD‐L1 expression ≥ 1%. Pembrolizumab has also demonstrated benefit in the adjuvant setting in the PEARLS/KEYNOTE‐091 study but is not yet subsidized in Australia for this indication [ ]. Other large RCTs investigating nivolumab (NCT02595944) and durvalumab (NCT04385368 and NCT04642469) in the adjuvant setting are underway.
Adjuvant systemic therapy has recently expanded to include oral tyrosine kinase inhibitors targeting EGFR and ALK‐positive NSCLC. The ADAURA trial demonstrated that adjuvant osimertinib significantly improved 5‐year OS rates to 88% compared to 78% with placebo (HR 0.49; 95% CI, 0.34–0.70) in EGFR‐mutated Stages IB–III NSCLC [ ]. Adjuvant osimertinib is available through the PBS for patients with resected Stages IB–IIIA NSCLC (Figure  ). The use of adjuvant chemotherapy prior to adjuvant osimertinib remains at the discretion of the treating physician and is consistent with the ADAURA trial protocol. Currently, adjuvant alectinib is not subsidized in Australia for resected NSCLC. However, the ALINA trial in ALK‐positive Stages IB–IIIA NSCLC is ongoing, with early results showing a significant improvement in the 3‐year DFS of 88.7% with adjuvant alectinib, compared to 54.0% with adjuvant chemotherapy [ ]. Several clinical trials are underway evaluating other neoadjuvant and adjuvant targeted therapies for resectable NSCLC with ALK (NCT02201992, NCT05015010), RET (NCT04819100, NCT03157128), MET (NCT04926831), and KRAS G12C (NCT05118854) oncogene driver mutations.

4.2.2.

## Neoadjuvant Systemic Therapy
In recent years, neoadjuvant therapy for NSCLC with combination chemotherapy and PD‐L1 inhibitors has been studied in multiple large Phase III randomized controlled trials. The CheckMate‐816 trial compared nivolumab and chemotherapy to chemotherapy alone for three cycles prior to resection of Stages IB–IIIA NSCLC [ ]. The nivolumab arm had a higher PCR rate of 24.0% compared to 2.2% in those receiving chemotherapy alone. The event‐free survival (EFS) was also prolonged in the nivolumab arm at 31.6 months compared to 20.8 months with chemotherapy (HR 0.63; 95% CI, 0.43–0.91) [ ]. Recently reported OS data have demonstrated an improved 5‐year OS with nivolumab plus chemotherapy at 65.4% and 55.0% with chemotherapy alone (HR 0.72; 95% CI, 0.5–1.00) [ ].
Similarly impressive results have been reported from other perioperative immunotherapy trials, including CheckMate‐77T [ ], KEYNOTE‐671 [ ], Neotorch [ ], and AEGEAN [ ]. Important differences between the trials include the stage of disease, with the KEYNOTE‐671 and AEGEAN studies recruiting Stages II–IIIB disease, while Neotorch reported on Stage III disease alone. All trials also allowed for adjuvant PD‐L1 inhibitor therapy, unlike the CheckMate‐816 trial [ ].
In Australia, neoadjuvant therapy is likely to be limited to large, high‐volume centers given a highly specialized patient workup and surgical approach is required, including biopsy sampling of FDG‐PET‐avid mediastinal lymph nodes with EBUS or mediastinoscopy. While nivolumab became available on the PBS in mid‐2024 for this indication (Figure  ), the use of anti‐PD‐L1 therapy for NSCLC is currently restricted to use once per lifetime. This raises questions about the optimal management for recurrence in the Australian setting following the use of immunotherapy in early‐stage disease.
Uncertainty also remains regarding the necessity of both neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapies in operable disease. Patient‐level analyses of the CheckMate‐77T trial (where patients received at least one dose of adjuvant nivolumab after neoadjuvant therapy) versus the CheckMate‐816 trial of neoadjuvant therapy, demonstrated a 40% reduction in disease recurrence or death with both neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy [ ]. The evidence is limited to a cross‐trial comparison, and currently, either neoadjuvant therapy alone (nivolumab) or adjuvant therapy alone (atezolizumab) is provided by the PBS as treatment for resectable disease. The ADOPT‐Lung trial (NCT06284317), now recruiting in Australia, aims to answer this question, assessing the benefit of adjuvant durvalumab following neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy for resectable NSCLC.

4.3.

## Radiotherapy
For patients deemed unsuitable for surgical resection (due to age, comorbidities, or preference), stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) provides high rates of local control and survival in patients with Stages I–IIA NSCLC (tumor size ≤ 5 cm) [ , , ] and minimal toxicity (Figure  ). This is well established for peripheral tumors, with increasing evidence supporting its safety for central mediastinal tumors, with radiation planning focused on minimizing toxicity [ ].
While there are no completed randomized trials of SABR versus surgery for early‐stage lung cancer, a pooled analysis from two randomized trials, both terminated due to inadequate accrual, suggests SABR may be an alternative option for patients with operable Stage I tumors [ ]. Trials are currently underway to further address this question [ ].
Current evidence regarding postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) following complete surgical resection is based on two large randomized studies, shows no improvement in survival outcomes [ , ]. However, a large observational study demonstrated improved OS in patients with > 1 lymph node involved [ ]. In the Australian setting, PORT should be considered in patients with high‐risk disease, including incomplete resection, extranodal extension, positive surgical margins, or inadequate adjuvant systemic therapy, supported by NCCN [ ], ASTRO [ ], and ESMO guidelines [ , ].
The role of radiotherapy with surgery in Australia is likely to evolve further with trimodal therapy (surgery, systemic therapy, and radiotherapy) currently under investigation. Phase II studies have demonstrated the feasibility of adding neoadjuvant radiotherapy to chemoimmunotherapy regimens, with the results of larger randomized trials awaited to determine safety and efficacy [ , ].

5.

## Unresectable Early‐Stage NSCLC
Combination chemoradiotherapy (60–66 Gy and platinum‐doublet chemotherapy) is an option for patients with unresectable Stage III NSCLC [ ]. For those with stable disease following chemoradiotherapy, consolidation immunotherapy with durvalumab is recommended (Figure  ). The phase III PACIFIC trial reported improved 5‐year survival at 42.9% with durvalumab versus 33.4% with placebo (HR 0.72; 95% CI, 0.59–0.89) [ ]. Real‐world cohort data, including patients from Australia, support this regimen outside of a clinical trial population [ ]. However, the efficacy of consolidation durvalumab in EGFR‐mutant or PD‐L1‐negative disease remains controversial, and prescribing practices vary in Australia for this population [ ]. The PACIFIC‐9 trial is currently underway internationally and across Australia, investigating the addition of novel immune checkpoint inhibitors, Oleclumab or Monalizumab, to Durvalumab following definitive concurrent chemoradiation therapy (NCT05221840).
For the population with an EGFR mutation, the use of osimertinib for unresectable Stage III NSCLC following sequential or concurrent chemoradiotherapy significantly improves median progression‐free survival (PFS) over placebo (HR 0.16; 95% CI, 0.10–0.24) [ ]. Its use for this indication has now been approved by the Therapeutic Goods Association but awaits PBS listing in Australia.

## Metastatic Disease
6.1.

## Targeted Therapy
Patients harboring clinically relevant EGFR, ALK, ROS1, MET, and RET mutations currently have oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapies available as first‐line management (Table  , Figure  ).
 
EGFR mutations occur in approximately 30%–50% of patients with metastatic lung adenocarcinoma [ ]. Osimertinib is first‐line management for untreated, advanced NSCLC with EGFR sensitizing mutations (exon‐19 deletions or exon‐21/L858R point mutations). The FLAURA phase III trial of osimertinib demonstrated improved median PFS and OS (38.6 vs. 31.8 months; HR 0.80; 95% CI, 0.64–1.00) compared to early‐generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors, erlotinib and gefitinib [ , ]. Osimertinib also reduced central nervous system (CNS) progression by 52% [ ]. Furthermore, the FLAURA‐2 trial recently reported improved median PFS with the addition of platinum‐based chemotherapy to osimertinib compared to osimertinib alone (25.5 vs. 16.7 months; HR 0.62; 95% CI, 0.49–0.79) [ ]. This regimen should be considered, especially for patients with CNS metastases or L858R mutations, who derived the most benefit, while accounting for higher toxicity in the osimertinib‐chemotherapy arm. However, osimertinib is not currently PBS‐listed in combination with platinum‐chemotherapy and requires funding by the individual. Erlotinib and gefitinib also remain options for those who derived benefit predating osimertinib or experienced toxicity.
Amivantamab, a bispecific monoclonal antibody targeting EGFR and MET, together with lazertinib, a third‐generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor, demonstrated improved median PFS compared to osimertinib (23.7 vs. 16.6 months; HR 0.70; 95% CI, 0.58–0.85) in the MARIPOSA trial [ ]. Although not PBS‐listed in Australia, this represents a promising alternative to osimertinib, despite high toxicity and discontinuation rates. Amivantamab in combination with chemotherapy has also shown efficacy in patients with EGFR exon 20 insertions [ ], in whom osimertinib is ineffective, but is yet to be available in Australia outside of self‐funding or compassionate access schemes.
For ALK‐positive NSCLC, accounting for up to 4% of patients with metastatic adenocarcinoma [ ], targeted ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors—alectinib, lorlatinib, crizotinib, and brigatinib—are PBS‐listed as first‐line options. Alectinib significantly prolonged PFS compared with crizotinib (34.8 vs 10.9 months; HR 0.43, 95% CI, 0.32–0.58) [ ] in the ALEX trial. The 5‐year OS was 62.5% with alectinib and 45.5% with crizotinib [ ]. Similar benefits were observed in the J‐ALEX trial with a lower dose of alectinib [ ]. Lorlatinib, in the Phase III CROWN study, improved median PFS (NR vs. 9.1 months; HR 0.19; 95% CI, 0.13–0.27) and delayed intracranial progression compared to crizotinib [ ]. Lorlatinib has the longest associated PFS duration reported for this population. However, its toxicity profile, including hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertension, should be a consideration when selecting first‐line therapy.
For patients with a ROS1 mutation, affecting up to 2% of patients with metastatic adenocarcinoma [ ], crizotinib and entrectinib are recommended. Crizotinib achieved a response rate of 72%, with improved median PFS and median OS of 51.4 months (95% CI, 29.3–NR) in an early‐phase clinical trial for this population [ ]. Pooled data from the ALKA 372‐001, STARTRK‐1, and STARTRK‐2 trials showed similar efficacy for entrectinib [ ].

MET exon 14 skipping mutations occur in 3%–4% of patients with NSCLC [ ]. The selective oral MET inhibitor, tepotinib is available for Australian patients on the PBS, demonstrated a median OS of 19.6 months (95% CI, 16.2–22.9) in the Phase II VISION trial [ ].
As of February 2025, selpercatinib is available for patients with RET fusion‐positive NSCLC on the PBS. Selpercatinib improved median PFS to 24.8 months compared to 11.2 months (HR 0.46; 95% CI, 0.31–0.70) with chemotherapy with or without pembrolizumab in the LIBRETTO‐431 trial [ ]. High response rates and intracranial efficacy were observed, highlighting the importance of comprehensive genomic testing for rare molecular fusions.
The Medicare‐funded multigene panel test of tumor tissue for NSCLC also tests for BRAF, KRAS, and NTRK mutations. While targeted therapy against BRAF (dabrafenib and trametinib) [ ], KRAS G12C (sotorasib) [ ], and NTRK (entrectinib and larotrectinib) [ , ] have shown promising results for management of metastatic NSCLC, access in Australia remains largely limited to clinical trials or compassionate programs. The ASPiRATION trial, currently ongoing in Australia, is assessing the impact of genomic profiling on metastatic NSCLC management, including substudies for HER2, NTRK, and BRAF V600E mutations [ ].

6.2.

## Immunotherapy
For patients without a targetable driver mutation, first‐line management of metastatic NSCLC is with immune checkpoint inhibitors, which have led to significant improvements in survival outcomes (Figure  ). Immune checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy is recommended for tumors with PD‐L1 expression ≥ 50% and combination chemoimmunotherapy for tumors with PD‐L1 expression < 50% (Table  ). However, clinicians may favor combination chemoimmunotherapy for high‐burden, symptomatic disease requiring rapid response regardless of PD‐L1 expression [ ].
 In the KEYNOTE‐024 trial, treatment‐naïve, advanced NSCLC patients with PD‐L1 expression on at least 50% of tumor cells had superior PFS and OS (26.3 vs. 13.4 months; HR 0.62; 95% CI, 0.62; 0.48–0.81) with pembrolizumab compared to platinum‐doublet chemotherapy [ , ]. The 5‐year OS was 31.9% in the pembrolizumab arm compared to 16.3% with chemotherapy. Similar benefits were also demonstrated in the EMPOWER‐1 study with cemiplimab monotherapy [ ].
The IMpower110 trial evaluated atezolizumab as first‐line treatment in patients with metastatic NSCLC and PD‐L1 expression of ≥ 1%. While patients with EGFR or ALK mutations were eligible, the greatest benefit was seen in EGFR or ALK wild‐type tumors with PD‐L1 ≥ 50%, where atezolizumab significantly improved median OS over chemotherapy (20.2 vs. 13.1 months; HR 0.59; 95% CI, 0.40–0.89) [ ]. Atezolizumab monotherapy is not currently PBS‐reimbursed for first‐line NSCLC but is available with platinum‐doublet chemotherapy and bevacizumab or following progression after chemotherapy or tepotinib.
In tumors with PD‐L1 expression < 50%, the combination of pembrolizumab with histology‐directed platinum‐doublet chemotherapy is superior to chemotherapy alone in squamous and non‐squamous disease. In the KEYNOTE‐407 trial, carboplatin, paclitaxel, and pembrolizumab improved PFS and OS (17.2 vs. 11.6 months; HR 0.71; 95% CI, 0.59–0.85) compared to chemotherapy alone for metastatic squamous cell carcinoma [ , ]. The 5‐year OS rates were 18.4% versus 9.7%, respectively. Similarly, in the KEYNOTE‐189 trial for metastatic non‐squamous carcinoma, carboplatin, pemetrexed, and pembrolizumab improved median PFS and OS (22.0 vs. 10.6 months; HR 0.60; 95% CI, 0.50–0.72) compared to chemotherapy [ , ]. The 5‐year OS rates were 19.4% versus 11.3%, respectively.
Although the aforementioned options are widely used in Australian practice, the IMpower150 regimen (carboplatin, paclitaxel, atezolizumab, and bevacizumab) is an alternative for non‐squamous NSCLC. Post hoc analyses demonstrated improved OS with additional atezolizumab compared to bevacizumab and chemotherapy alone in patients with baseline liver metastases and should be considered for this population [ , ]. Another treatment option comes from the CheckMate 9LA trial of nivolumab and ipilimumab plus two cycles of platinum‐doublet chemotherapy compared to four cycles of platinum‐doublet chemotherapy alone. This demonstrated superior OS in the immunotherapy arm (15.8 vs. 11.0 months; HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.62–0.85), with particular benefit seen in those with squamous cell carcinoma [ , ].
While the combination anti‐PD‐1 and ipilimumab, without chemotherapy, has yielded impressive results in metastatic melanoma [ ], results have varied in NSCLC and thus its use alone is not recommended or funded in an Australian setting. In the KEYNOTE‐598 trial, adding ipilimumab to pembrolizumab in PD‐L1 ≥ 50% NSCLC provided no additional benefit and led to increased toxicity compared to pembrolizumab alone [ ]. In contrast, the CheckMate‐227 trial demonstrated that nivolumab plus ipilimumab improved survival over chemotherapy, particularly in PD‐L1‐negative tumors [ ]. However, the clinical relevance of this finding remains uncertain, as the comparator arm did not include chemoimmunotherapy, which is now the standard of care.

6.3.

## Second‐Line Systemic Therapy
Chemotherapy remains the second‐line therapy with the most robust evidence for patients with or without targetable mutations following progression. For patients who have not received it in the first‐line, platinum‐doublet chemotherapy is recommended. For EGFR‐mutant disease, this can be considered in combination with the VEGF‐inhibitor bevacizumab, based on the benefit seen in the retrospective subgroup analysis of the IMpower150 trial [ ]. While the IMpower150 regimen saw benefit with atezolizumab, bevacizumab, and chemotherapy, the evidence continues to grow that there is little benefit to the use of immune‐checkpoint inhibitors in patients with EGFR‐mutant NSCLC, with disappointing results from the KEYNOTE‐789 [ ], CheckMate‐722 [ ], and IMpower151 [ ] trials.
Following platinum‐based chemotherapy, docetaxel chemotherapy remains the standard of care in the second‐ and third‐line settings. Docetaxel has a reported response rate of 7.1% and a median OS of 7.0 months compared to 4.6 months with the best supportive care [ ]. Despite modest response rates and significant toxicity, multiple studies have failed to demonstrate an alternative that significantly outperforms docetaxel in a clinically meaningful way. Immunotherapy is also an option for patients who did not receive it as first‐line treatment [ , , , ]. However, such patients are increasingly rare, as the majority of patients alive in 2025 received first‐line immunotherapy following its introduction to the PBS over 5 years ago.

6.4.

## Radiotherapy
Palliative radiation therapy should be considered for patients with metastatic NSCLC, either as an adjunct to systemic therapy, for those unsuitable for systemic therapy, or in the presence of focal symptoms.
Radiotherapy is highly effective for palliation, with evidence demonstrating it can reduce bone pain in patients with bone metastases and prevent motor or sensory deficits for spinal cord compression [ , , ].
Local therapy, with surgery or stereotactic radiosurgery, is generally recommended prior to systemic therapy for patients with NSCLC brain metastases [ ]. However, systemic therapies may take precedence in some settings, such as patients with oncogene mutations, due to high CNS penetration of agents such as osimertinib and lorlatinib [ , , ]. There is also increasing data to suggest that upfront chemoimmunotherapy combinations are effective in managing asymptomatic, untreated brain metastases in patients without oncogenic driver mutations [ , ]. Deferring brain radiotherapy may also postpone the onset of significant treatment‐related neurocognitive side effects and the risk of radionecrosis.
Radiotherapy has also been shown to be of benefit in patients with limited progression on systemic therapy (oligoprogression), and local ablative therapy should be considered to further extend the benefit of systemic therapy [ , ].
Palliative thoracic radiotherapy with concurrent chemotherapy is also of benefit in patients with incurable Stage III NSCLC, unsuitable for definitive radiation [ , , ]. However, all studies in this setting have demonstrated higher toxicity rates with combination chemoradiotherapy and require careful consideration when managing patients with palliative intent. Thoracic radiation alone should be considered for patients with symptomatic disease [ ] and can provide excellent palliation, particularly in the elderly or frail population.

7.

## Novel Drugs
Significant promise lies in the development of novel anticancer drugs for NSCLC, including antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs), cancer vaccines, tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, and bispecific T‐cell engager (BiTE) therapy. It is likely that these treatments will be available for management of NSCLC in Australian practice over the next few years.  ADCs are targeted monoclonal antibodies attached to cytotoxic drugs via a chemical linker. Trastuzumab deruxtecan, an ADC, received FDA approval for previously treated NSCLC with an activating HER2 mutation, following impressive PFS and OS results in the DESTINY‐Lung01 trial [ ]. While no ADCs are currently PBS‐listed for NSCLC, multiple studies of ADCs alone or combined with existing therapies are underway in Australia and internationally to assess their efficacy in the management of advanced NSCLC (Table ).

## Survivorship, Supportive, and Palliative Care
Although advances in the management of lung cancer have improved survival for patients, significant challenges persist for patients living with or following treatment for NSCLC. Australian NSCLC survivor populations describe impacts on employment, relationships, finances, and ongoing symptom burden from disease or treatment side effects [ , ]. Ongoing follow‐up and research are required to manage toxicity, facilitate psychological support, and understand the long‐term health impacts of NSCLC treatments [ ].
Early palliative care has also been shown to improve patient quality of life and survival in metastatic NSCLC [ ]. Thus, referral to palliative care should be considered the standard of care for these patients in Australia.
Recently, patients with advanced NSCLC and a prognosis of less than 6 months may be eligible for voluntary assisted dying (VAD) in the six major Australian states, and it is set to be introduced into the Australian Capital Territory in November 2025. Accessibility, for those who wish, to VAD allows patients autonomy in their end‐of‐life care. However, given that there remains some uncertainty surrounding prognostication in advanced cancer, including NSCLC, it has been suggested that improvements in the legislative wording are required to improve accessibility [ ].

## Considerations and Future Directions for Australian Practice
The successful integration of the described practice‐changing treatments in Australia demands careful consideration of local healthcare systems and population needs. Specifically, Australia comprises multiple diverse subpopulations, many of which experience poorer lung cancer outcomes. These populations include First Nations Australians, who comprise 3.8% of the population [ ], remote and regional patients, comprising 30% of the population [ ], as well as socioeconomically disadvantaged groups and migrant populations [ , , , , , ]. To combat this, the Australia and New Zealand Lung Cancer Clinical Quality Registry has received national ethics approval to monitor the safety and quality of lung cancer care across Australia and New Zealand [ ]. The assessment of national benchmarks under this registry will allow for improvements and equality in lung cancer care delivery in the coming years.
As the incidence of NSCLC continues to rise, innovations in diagnostics and therapies will pose increasing costs and pressure on the healthcare system and risk deepening these disparities. Currently, Australia's universal healthcare model ensures publicly funded access to healthcare services and pharmaceuticals via the MBS and the PBS. Higher lung cancer health care costs in Australia have been associated with younger age, distant metastatic disease at diagnosis, and longer survival [ ]. Costs of lung cancer care are expected to grow with the longer survival times afforded by the wider availability of new therapies.
Another important consideration for the Australian context is that the validity of international clinical trial findings in advanced NSCLC may have less applicability to Australia's unique healthcare landscape and population characteristics [ ]. The limited participation of regional centers in lung cancer trials further restricts the applicability of trial findings to broader Australian practice. The implementation of teletrials in oncology is emerging as a strategy to mitigate this disparity for regional patients. Evidence indicates that Australian patients in teletrials experience improved equity of access and reduced travel, time toxicity, and financial burdens [ ].
Although outcomes for Australians with NSCLC have improved significantly due to transformative advances in treatment, substantial challenges persist due to the rising incidence, longer survival times and rapid development of novel therapies. A sustained investment in Australia's oncology workforce and the expansion of clinical infrastructure—particularly in regional centers—is required to support timely and equitable access to emerging treatments.
