## ABSTRACT

## Background
Concomitant medications (CMs) influence outcomes in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), but their impact in sarcoma remains undefined. We assessed the association between CM use and ICI outcomes in patients with advanced or metastatic sarcoma.

## Methods
This pooled analysis included patients from seven investigator‐initiated phase II trials of ICI‐based therapy for sarcoma. CMs within 30 days of treatment were defined as baseline; on‐treatment exposure was captured longitudinally. The primary endpoint was progression‐free survival (PFS); secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), and immune‐related adverse events (irAEs). Multivariable Cox models adjusted for age, ECOG performance status, histological subtype, treatment regimen, and race.

## Results
Among 321 patients (median follow‐up: 47.4 months), in time‐dependent analyses, exposure to anti‐infective medications was associated with shorter PFS (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.54, 95% CI 1.05–2.27). Baseline use of vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products was associated with longer PFS (aHR 0.73, 95% CI 0.56–0.95) and OS (aHR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89). Baseline statin use was associated with longer PFS (aHR 0.64, 95% CI 0.47–0.87) but not OS. Baseline use of opioids was associated with shorter OS (aHR 1.71, 95% CI 1.12–2.61). No CM class was significantly associated with ORR or irAE occurrence.

## Conclusions
CM use is associated with differential efficacy outcomes in sarcoma patients receiving ICIs. These findings highlight the need for prospective studies to define the impact of commonly prescribed medications on ICI outcomes and to optimize clinical trial stratification.

## Introduction
Sarcomas are rare, heterogeneous mesenchymal malignancies comprising over 100 histological subtypes, resulting in substantial therapeutic challenges and variable treatment responses. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are an established treatment across multiple solid malignancies and have demonstrated an emerging role in sarcoma [ , ]. Concomitant medications (CMs) have been shown to influence outcomes in patients treated with ICIs [ , , , , , ]. However, the impact of CM use on outcomes in sarcoma patients receiving ICI‐based therapy remains poorly defined.
Current literature suggests that antibiotic, proton pump inhibitor (PPI), and systemic corticosteroid use is associated with inferior survival in patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly when administered at higher doses or in the peri‐treatment period [ , , , , , ]. In contrast, some CMs, including statins, aspirin, and metformin, have been reported to improve outcomes, although findings have been inconsistent [ , , ]. Other commonly prescribed agents, such as renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, beta‐blockers, and non‐aspirin non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, have demonstrated variable or inconclusive associations [ , , ]. Importantly, these data are retrospective and largely derived from common solid tumor types and have not been specifically validated in sarcoma populations.
Our study seeks to address these gaps by providing a comprehensive evaluation of CM use in patients with sarcoma treated with ICI‐based therapy. Unlike prior studies that have largely focused on common solid tumor types or relied on baseline exposure alone, we examine both baseline and time‐dependent CM exposure across a pooled cohort of patients enrolled in multiple prospective phase II ICI trials. By integrating time‐varying analyses with established clinical outcomes, including progression‐free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), duration of response (DoR), and immune‐related adverse events (irAEs), this study aims to more rigorously characterize the association between CM use and ICI outcomes in advanced or metastatic sarcoma.

2.

## Methods
2.1.

## Study Design
This post hoc pooled analysis included seven investigator‐initiated phase II ICI‐based trials (NCT03069378, NCT03414229, NCT03282344, NCT04242238, NCT04438824, NCT04577014 and NCT04784247) enrolling patients with metastatic sarcoma between April 1, 2017, and May 30, 2024. Trial details and primary outcomes have been published previously and are summarized in Table  [ , , , , , , ]. Briefly, all studies evaluated ICI‐based regimens in patients with advanced or metastatic sarcoma. Patients who received ≥ 1 dose of ICI were included in the pooled analysis. To account for inter‐trial heterogeneity, treatment regimen was included as an adjustment variable in multivariable analyses.

2.2.

## Concomitant Medications
CMs taken within 30 days prior to treatment initiation were defined as baseline; medications prescribed on an as‐needed (PRN) basis were excluded. Baseline medications included agents initiated prior to or overlapping treatment start. On‐trial medication was recorded from cycle 1, day 1 through treatment discontinuation using individual patient chart review and case report forms (CRFs). Medications initiated after documented disease progression were not considered exposed for time‐dependent analyses. Medications were grouped into 14 distinct classes: vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products; statins; beta‐blockers; PPI/H2 receptor antagonists; antidepressants; calcium channel blockers (CCB); non‐opioid analgesics/pain adjuncts; opioids; antihyperglycemic agents (excluding insulin); antihistamines; ACE inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers; systemic corticosteroids; and antiplatelet/anticoagulant agents. Anti‐infective agents included antibiotics, antivirals, and antifungals. Protocol‐directed oncolytic virus therapy, including talimogene laherparepvec, was classified as active study treatment and not as a concomitant medication; separately prescribed antiviral agents, including aciclovir, valaciclovir, and famciclovir, were included as concomitant anti‐infective exposures.

2.3.

## Endpoints
The primary endpoint was the association between CM classes and PFS. Exploratory analyses evaluated the association between baseline CM use and OS, ORR, DoR, and irAE. IrAEs were identified from the original trial AE datasets and included events graded according to CTCAE version 5.0 that were attributed by trial investigators as possibly, probably, or definitely related to ICI.

2.4.

## Statistical Analysis
Demographic and baseline clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Comparisons between groups were performed using χ
2 tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. OS was defined as the time from study entry to death from any cause. PFS was defined as the time from study entry to disease progression, as assessed by RECIST version 1.1, or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. DoR was defined as the time from first documented objective response to disease progression or death from any cause. Median follow‐up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.
Hazard ratios (HRs) for univariable and multivariable analyses were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Multivariable models were adjusted for age at study entry, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, histological subtype, treatment regimen, and race. For time‐varying analyses of concomitant medication exposure, start–stop (counting‐process) Cox models were used to mitigate immortal‐time bias, with medication exposure updated at each treatment cycle. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Associations between concomitant medications, ORR, and irAE were evaluated using univariable logistic regression, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the study, p‐values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1).

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Baseline Characteristics
A total of 321 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis, with a median follow‐up of 47.4 months. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table  . The median age at study entry was 58 years (IQR 46–68). Most patients were younger than 70 years (79%), had an ECOG performance status of 0 (65%), and had received three or more prior lines of systemic therapy (46%). ICI was administered in combination with cytokine/oncolytic therapies (43%), targeted agents (38%), or chemotherapy (19%).
 Histologic subtypes were heterogeneous, with liposarcoma being the most common (n = 75, 23%), comprising well‐differentiated/dedifferentiated (n = 68), pleomorphic (n = 3), and myxoid (n = 4) variants, followed by undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS)/myxofibrosarcoma (22%) and leiomyosarcoma (19%). Tables  and summarize baseline concomitant medication classes. Vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products were the most frequently used baseline medications (48%) (Table  and Table  ), followed by antiplatelet/anticoagulant agents (28%) and statins (25%). Systemic corticosteroids were uncommon at baseline (2%).
 When on‐trial concomitant medication use was considered, vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products remained the most common class (51%), followed by anti‐infective agents (47%) and proton pump inhibitor/H2 receptor antagonist use (39%) (Table  ).

3.2.

## Progression Free Survival
There were 285 PFS events in the study population, corresponding to a median PFS of 4.2 months (95% CI 3.22–5.49). Median PFS stratified by medication class is summarized in Table  . We evaluated PFS using two complementary exposure definitions: time‐dependent exposure, which incorporated on‐treatment medication use updated during follow‐up, and baseline exposure, which was restricted to medications used within 30 days before treatment initiation or overlapping with treatment start (cycle 1 day 1).

3.3.

## 
PFS Time‐Dependent Exposure Analysis
In univariate time‐dependent Cox regression analyses, use of vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products was associated with longer PFS (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.96; p = 0.02), whereas exposure to anti‐infective medications was associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.11–2.35; p = 0.01). No other concomitant medication classes demonstrated statistically significant associations with PFS in univariate analyses (Figure  ).
 In the multivariable time‐dependent Cox models, anti‐infective exposure remained independently associated with shorter PFS (adjusted HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.05–2.27; p = 0.027). No other medication classes were significantly associated with PFS after adjustment (Figure  ).

3.4.

## 
PFS Analysis Restricted to Only Baseline Concomitant Medication
In analyses restricted to baseline concomitant medication exposure, use of vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products was associated with longer PFS in both univariable (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.88, p‐value < 0.01) and multivariable models (adjusted HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.56–0.95, p‐value = 0.02). Baseline statin use was also associated with a longer PFS in both univariable (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54–0.93, p‐value = 0.02) and multivariable analysis (adjusted HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.47–0.87, p‐value < 0.01). No other baseline medication classes were significantly associated with PFS (Figures  and ). Effect estimates were directionally consistent with the time‐dependent analyses but were of smaller magnitude.
In sensitivity analyses evaluating vitamin D and multivitamin exposure, vitamin D use alone (n = 21) was not associated with longer PFS (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.56–1.46; p = 0.68). Vitamin D use in combination with other supplements (n = 63) showed a directionally similar but weaker association than that observed for the overall supplement/herbal category, with a non‐significant trend toward longer PFS compared with no vitamin D use (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.57–1.03; p = 0.08). When multivitamin and vitamin D exposure were analyzed together, neither use alone nor use in combination with other supplements was significantly associated with PFS (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.56–1.12; p = 0.18 and HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.57–1.06; p = 0.12, respectively).

3.5.

## Overall Survival
There were 234 deaths within the study population, corresponding to a median OS of 19.9 months (95% CI 17.5–22.9). For OS, analyses were restricted to baseline CM exposure only. Several medication classes were significantly associated with OS (Figure  ). In univariable Cox regression analyses, baseline use of vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products was associated with longer OS (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.53–0.90; p < 0.01). In contrast, baseline exposure to non‐opioid analgesics (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.10–2.16; p = 0.01) and opioids (HR 2.14, 95% CI 1.45–3.14; p < 0.01) was associated with shorter OS.
 In multivariable models, the association between supplement/herbal product use and longer OS remained significant (adjusted HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89; p < 0.01). Baseline use of non‐opioid analgesics (adjusted HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.11–2.26; p = 0.01) and opioids (adjusted HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.12–2.61; p = 0.01) also remained independently associated with shorter OS. No statistically significant associations with OS were observed for other baseline medication classes (Figure  ).
In sensitivity analyses evaluating overall survival and separating vitamin D exposure from other supplements, neither vitamin D use alone nor vitamin D in combination with other supplements was significantly associated with overall survival. Compared with patients not receiving vitamin D, vitamin D–only use was associated with a HR of 0.70 (95% CI 0.42–1.17; p = 0.17), while vitamin D use in combination with other supplements was associated with an HR of 0.82 (95% CI 0.60–1.12; p = 0.22). Similarly, when vitamin D and multivitamin exposures were modeled separately from the broader herbal/supplement category, no significant differences in overall survival were observed.

3.6.

## Sensitivity Analysis by Histology and Treatment Regimen
Given the biological and therapeutic heterogeneity of the pooled cohort, we performed exploratory sensitivity analyses stratified by major histologic subgroup and treatment‐regimen category (Tables  ). In histology‐stratified analyses, time‐dependent anti‐infective exposure generally trended toward shorter PFS across major histologic subgroups, with the strongest association observed in leiomyosarcoma. Baseline vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal product use generally trended toward longer OS across several histologic and regimen strata, whereas baseline opioid and non‐opioid analgesic exposure generally trended toward shorter OS, particularly across regimen‐defined subgroups.

3.7.

## Objective Response Rate and Duration of Response
Fourteen patients were not evaluable by RECIST. The ORR was 18%, comprising one complete response (CR) and 57 partial responses (PR). Stable disease was observed in 130 patients, while 119 patients experienced progressive disease (PD). No baseline concomitant medication class was significantly associated with ORR in univariable analyses, with all odds ratios crossing unity (Figure  ). The median DoR was 11.9 months (95% CI 9.2–17.6). Use of supplements or herbal products was the only concomitant medication category associated with a longer DoR, with a median DoR of 18.4 months (95% CI 16.9–NA) compared with 8.9 months (95% CI 5.5–NA) among patients not receiving these agents (HR 0.43; 95% CI 0.21–0.92; p = 0.03).

3.8.

## 
Immune‐Related Adverse Events

Overall, irAEs were common in this cohort, with most patients experiencing at least one irAE (Table  ). The most frequently observed irAEs involved general/constitutional (62.9%), dermatologic (42.7%), hepatic (37.7%), gastrointestinal (37.4%), and hematologic/coagulation (33.3%) systems. Grade ≥ 3 irAEs were less frequent and occurred most commonly in hematologic/coagulation (6.2%), hepatic (5.3%), and pancreatic (5.3%) categories. In univariable logistic regression analyses evaluating associations between baseline concomitant medication classes and the development of irAEs, no medication class was consistently associated with the occurrence of any‐grade or grade ≥ 3 irAEs across organ systems (Figures  ). A non‐significant trend toward increased gastrointestinal irAEs was observed among baseline antihistamine users (OR 2.37, 95% CI 0.97–5.98; p = 0.06); however, this finding was exploratory and limited by small event numbers.

4.

## Discussion
This pooled post hoc analysis evaluated associations between concomitant medication use and efficacy and toxicity outcomes among patients with metastatic sarcoma treated with ICI‐based regimens. The use of vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products was associated with longer PFS and OS, while baseline statin use was associated with longer PFS. In contrast, exposure to anti‐infective medications was associated with shorter PFS in our time‐dependent analyses, and baseline use of analgesic medications was associated with reduced OS. Given the retrospective nature of this analysis, these findings remain exploratory, and hypothesis‐generating and require further validation.
In this study, vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products were associated with longer PFS and OS. Our results are consistent with current literature with reports that selected vitamins, herbal products and supplements have been associated with ICI outcomes, although causality remains uncertain [ , , , ]. The supplement/herbal category in our cohort was heterogeneous and dominated by commonly used agents, including multivitamins and vitamin D. In sensitivity analyses separating vitamin D and multivitamin exposure from other supplements, vitamin D or multivitamin use alone was not independently associated with PFS, and observed associations were attenuated, suggesting that the primary signal is unlikely to be driven by a single supplement. Rather than representing a biologically coherent medication class, supplement/herbal product use may reflect a broader host or behavioral phenotype, including nutritional status, socioeconomic factors, care engagement, self‐directed wellness practices, or other unmeasured health‐related factors that are incompletely captured by conventional performance status measures. From the current literature, vitamin D supplementation has demonstrated the most consistent association with improved survival and response outcomes across multiple studies, while more limited evidence suggests potential benefit from vitamin K and probiotic use [ , , ]. Evidence for other specific vitamins and minerals remains limited; low dietary vitamin C intake has been reported as potentially detrimental, while omega‐3 fatty acids and certain amino acids have been suggested as potentially beneficial, although robust clinical data are lacking [ ]. Preliminary reports have explored vitamin C as a potential adjunct to immunotherapy in sarcoma, but data remain limited; we did not evaluate vitamin C separately because of low patient numbers, although this may warrant prospective study [ , , ].
Baseline statin use was also associated with longer PFS in both univariate and multivariable analyses but did not extend to overall survival. Similar associations have been reported in other studies [ , ]. Preclinical and translational studies suggest that statins have pleiotropic immunomodulatory effects, including modulation of antigen presentation, inhibition of immunosuppressive myeloid populations, and enhancement of T‐cell–mediated antitumor responses through cholesterol‐dependent signaling pathways [ ]. However, the modest magnitude and context‐dependent nature of this association warrant cautious interpretation. Nonetheless, the persistence of the association after adjustment for key clinical covariates supports further prospective investigation of statins as potential modifiers of ICI efficacy in sarcoma.
We reported that anti‐infective and analgesic medications were associated with inferior efficacy outcomes in patients receiving ICI‐based treatment for metastatic sarcoma. Anti‐infective exposure was associated with shorter PFS in the time‐dependent analysis, which captured on‐treatment exposure updated during follow‐up, but not in the baseline CM analysis. This distinction suggests that the timing of anti‐infective use relative to ICI treatment may be clinically relevant, although confounding by infection, disease status, or treatment complications cannot be excluded. This finding is consistent with prior studies associating antibiotic exposure near ICI initiation or during therapy with shorter survival outcomes, a relationship hypothesized to involve gut microbiome disruption and impaired antitumor immune responses [ , , ]. However, in our study, anti‐infective exposure may also reflect infection, treatment complications, hospitalization, worsening disease status, or other time‐varying clinical factors rather than a direct medication effect. In contrast, baseline use of analgesic medications, including opioids, was associated with shorter overall survival, a finding that likely reflects underlying disease burden, symptom severity, and advanced clinical status rather than a direct causal effect [ , ]. Patients requiring opioids at baseline may have more aggressive disease, higher tumor burden, or greater functional impairment, factors that are incompletely captured by conventional performance status measures. While immunomodulatory effects of opioids have been described, confounding by indication remains a substantial consideration in this context. Accordingly, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence that anti‐infective, opioid, corticosteroid, or supplement exposure directly modifies ICI efficacy, but rather as signals that require prospective evaluation with more granular data on indication, timing, dose, disease status, infection, hospitalization, symptom burden, nutritional status, and patient health behaviors.
In contrast to efficacy outcomes, we did not observe a consistent association between baseline concomitant medication use and the development of immune‐related adverse events in this cohort. This finding differs from prior pharmacovigilance and observational studies reporting medication‐specific associations with irAE phenotypes, including increased risks of nephritis, myositis, cutaneous toxicity, and hepatitis with agents such as PPI, statins, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and selected anti‐infectives [ ]. Conversely, several studies have reported reduced irAE incidence among patients receiving medications associated with impaired ICI efficacy, including antibiotics, corticosteroids, and PPI, suggesting a potential inverse relationship between antitumor immune activation and immune‐mediated toxicity [ ]. The absence of a clear irAE signal in our study may reflect limited power for organ‐specific toxicity analyses, heterogeneity in irAE definitions, or differences in exposure timing and indication, and underscores the complexity of disentangling medication effects on toxicity in ICI treated patients.
Our study had several strengths such as the use of prospectively collected clinical trial data minimized missing data and ensured systematic capture of outcomes, enhancing the reliability of the analyses. In addition, incorporation of time‐dependent concomitant medication exposure allowed for more accurate assessment of on‐treatment effects and mitigated immortal‐time bias, an important limitation of prior studies relying solely on baseline exposure. We also had several limitations including retrospective post hoc analysis that may be subject to residual confounding and potential confounding by indication. More specifically, residual confounding related to socioeconomic status, nutritional status, and health‐seeking behaviors could not be fully accounted for. Moreover, unmeasured confounding may arise from reporting bias as participants may underreport the use of dietary supplements. The relatively small number of irAE limited power to detect medication‐specific toxicity signals, particularly for organ‐specific irAEs. All included trials evaluated combination ICI‐based regimens rather than ICI monotherapy, which may limit generalizability to other treatment settings. Given the exploratory nature of this post hoc analysis, no formal adjustment for multiple hypothesis testing was performed across medication classes and endpoints. Therefore, the probability of false‐positive findings is increased, and associations with modest p‐values may not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. These results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory. Finally, the biological and clinical heterogeneity of sarcoma subtypes may have attenuated medication‐specific associations and limit the ability to draw histology‐specific conclusions.
Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and should incorporate baseline nutritional assessments, serum vitamin levels, and detailed supplement exposure data. Such data may help distinguish whether observed associations reflect biological effects of specific nutrients, underlying nutritional status, or patient behaviors, including health engagement and self‐directed wellness practices.

5.

## Conclusion
In this pooled analysis of ICI‐treated metastatic sarcoma, concomitant medication use was associated with differential efficacy outcomes. Use of vitamins/minerals/supplements/herbal products was associated with longer survival, while anti‐infective and analgesic use was associated with inferior outcomes; however, these associations may reflect a combination of medication effects and underlying host or disease‐related factors. Time‐dependent analyses highlighted the importance of exposure timing, particularly for anti‐infective medications, in relation to ICI efficacy. These findings are hypothesis‐generating and underscore the need for prospective studies to better define the impact of commonly prescribed concomitant medications on ICI outcomes in sarcoma.
