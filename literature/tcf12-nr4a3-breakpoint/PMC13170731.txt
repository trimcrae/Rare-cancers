## Purpose
To study a non-redundant role of Tcf12 in retinal health.

## Methods
A loss-of-function mutation in Tcf12 was identified by applying optical coherence tomography (OCT) to a forward genetic pipeline. CRISPR/Cas9-generated Tcf12ra/ra mice, expressing a replacement allele (“ra”) were used to validate the findings from the mutagenesis screen. Retinal morphology was assessed using OCT, fundus photography, histology, and immunohistochemistry. Retinal function was evaluated by electroretinography (ERG). Bulk RNA sequencing and proteomic analyses were performed, with select targets validated by RT-qPCR and immunoblotting.

## Results

Tcf12
ra/ra mice exhibited outer nuclear layer thinning on OCT, which was confirmed by histology. Fundus imaging revealed age-dependent accumulation of retinal fundus spots. Subretinal accumulation of Iba1⁺/Tmem119⁺ cells was observed, many of which stained positively for Gal3, suggestive of activated resident microglia. ERG deficits were noticed at 12 to 15 months of age in Tcf12ra/ra mice. Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling using two independent pathway analyses identified dysregulated pathways related to protein and RNA metabolism, mitochondrial and energy metabolism, cell cycle, signal transduction, cellular response to stimuli, and inflammation. RT-qPCR results showed upregulation of Tmem233 and downregulation of Doc2b, Alpk2, Agr2, and Apobec2 in Tcf12ra/ra retinas. Western blot analysis demonstrated upregulation of Selenbp1 and downregulation of Neurod1 and Faah in Tcf12ra/ra retinas.

## Conclusions
Deficiency in Tcf12 induces early-onset retinal structural alterations, and late-onset subretinal microglial activation and functional decline. Widespread dysregulation in metabolic and signaling pathways was documented in transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. These findings establish Tcf12 as a key contributor to retinal development and homeostasis.

Previous work has identified multiple pathogenic pathways, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, vascular dysregulation, and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) damage, as key drivers of disease onset and progression. – These pathways often involve the modulation of transcription factors. – Using a chemical mutagenesis pipeline in mice, we have been searching for genes essential for retinal health. – Here, we combined this forward genetic approach with optical coherence tomography (OCT)–based retinal screening, and identified transcription factor 12 (Tcf12, also known as HTF4 or HEB) as a gene of interest.

Tcf12 is a member of the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family of transcription factors. These proteins contain two distinct motifs, the HLH domain and adjacent basic regions, and are classified into two subclasses: class I (non-tissue-specific) and class II (tissue-specific). Tcf12 encodes a class I E-protein and is known to function as both a transcriptional repressor and an activator, particularly as a heterodimer with binding partners. – 
Tcf12 can form homodimers or heterodimers with other family members to regulate cell development and differentiation in various tissues. – For example, Tcf12 can form a heterodimer with Neurod1, helping regulate the induction of neuronal migration genes and the onset of neurogenesis during cortical development. 
Tcf12 has also been reported to be involved in the control of progenitor and proliferating adult neural stem cells and to play a role in sustaining their undifferentiated state. – However, to our knowledge, the relevance of Tcf12 in retinal physiology has not been explored.
The mutated Tcf12 allele identified in one of our pedigrees is predicted to lead to a null effect. It resulted in outer nuclear layer (ONL) thinning on retinal OCT. We then generated Tcf12ra/ra mice via CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis and carefully characterized their retinal phenotype, confirming the screening findings. Further studies revealed the accumulation of subretinal Iba1+, Tmem119+ cells, most of which were Gal3-positive. A mild functional deficit was also documented in electrophysiology (ERG) testing. Initial mechanistic evaluation using proteomic and transcriptomic analyses identified a set of molecules and pathways related to protein and RNA metabolism, mitochondrial and energy metabolism, cell cycle and signal transduction, and cellular responses to stimuli and inflammation that were altered in Tcf12ra/ra mice. Interestingly, despite reported effects of Tcf12 on skull and brain development, , no such abnormalities were detected in the CRISPR/Cas9-generated Tcf12ra/ra mice. Future studies will aim to evaluate early retinal developmental changes caused by Tcf12 deficiency.

## Material and Methods

## Animals
All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the UT Southwestern Medical Center (UTSW) and conducted in accordance with the NIH's guide for the care and use of Laboratory Animals, the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology's Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic Research, and other applicable international and national guidelines. For the forward genetics studies, germline mutations were randomly induced using N-ethyl-N-nitrosourea (ENU) treatment of C57BL/6J male mice (see  A). Tcf12ra/ra mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene targeting as described below. Mice were bred and maintained in the UTSW animal resource center under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad-libitum access to standard chow and water. Across all experiments, mice ranged in age from 3 weeks to 20 months.
 

## ENU Mutagenesis, Whole Exome Sequencing, and Determination of Candidate Genes
The forward genetic protocol ( ) involves the generation of mutations randomly throughout the genome of male C57BL/6J mice using ENU. , Germline mutations in G1 male founders were identified by whole-exome sequencing. Mutant G1 male mice were bred to generate G2 and G3 mice. The zygosity of each mutation in G2 dams and all G3 mice of each pedigree was determined prior to phenotypic screening by sequencing pedigree-specific coding/splice site mutations using Ion Torrent AmpliSeq custom primer panels. All G3 mice were phenotypically screened using OCT measurements ( C). The mice were 6 to 8 months old at the time of screening.
Automated meiotic mapping was performed by Linkage Analyzer software to detect statistically significant mutation-phenotype associations. In brief, Linkage Analyzer, an R-based analysis program, performs automated computations of single-locus linkage for every mutation in the pedigree. The output of this automated mapping is a Manhattan plot of genotype-phenotype associations for every mutation in the pedigree. In the Manhattan plot, −log10(P value) (y-axis) for non-linkage to a given phenotype were plotted against the chromosomal positions of the mutations (x-axis) that had been identified in the G1 founder of each pedigree. A gene was considered of interest if its Manhattan plot peak was above a horizontal line representing a threshold of P = 0.05 with Bonferroni correction. Candidate Explorer, a machine-learning algorithm, was then used to estimate the probability that the mutation was causative in independent gene-targeting experiments on a clean C57BL/6J background. 

## OCT Screening and Retinal Layer Analysis
Mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal (IP) injection using ketamine-xylazine cocktail (100 mg/kg–5 mg/kg) before OCT imaging. The pupils of the mice were then dilated with a 3:1 (v/v) mixture of 1% tropicamide ophthalmic solution and 2.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride ( ). Before imaging, 3% hypromellose gel ( ) was applied to both eyes to prevent corneal dehydration and to serve as a coupling medium. OCT images were acquired using a Micron V-OCT2 system (Phoenix-Micron Inc., Bend, OR, USA) with a half-size horizontal line scan located two disc-diameters superior to the optic nerve head (ONH). There were no differences in the ONH size between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice ( ). Images were then analyzed by a masked investigator to obtain measurements of the retinal layer thickness using the Freehand tool in ImageJ (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) as previously described. Three locations 200 µm apart and in the center of the image were measured for four parameters: total retinal thickness (TRT), ONL, inner retinal thickness (IRT), and ganglion cell complex (GCC). Using the Straight-Line tool in ImageJ, TRT was measured from the top of Bruch's membrane (BM) to the top of the internal limiting membrane (ILM). After sharpening the image to get better contrast, we measured the ONL from the top of the external limiting membrane to the bottom of the outer plexiform layer. IRT was measured from the top of the ONL to the top of the ILM. GCC was measured from the top of the inner nuclear layer (INL) to the top of the ILM. Finally, the three measurements for each parameter in an image were averaged before statistical analysis.

## Generation of Tcf12ra/ra Mouse Line
CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis was used to generate a Tcf12ra/ra mouse line according to protocols described before. , We recreated the same point mutation induced by ENU, a T-to-C substitution at position 71,922,918 bp on the minus strand of mouse chromosome 9 (GRCm39). We used the CRISPR/Cas9 target online predictor to select and evaluate possible targeting sites within 50 bp of the mutation site. Two candidate targeting sites were returned, and we selected the one closest to the mutation site (15 bp away). The target sequence is unique in the genome, and the next-highest off-target probability is an intergenic sequence on chromosome 13 that contains two mismatched DNA bases. Briefly, super-ovulated female C57BL/6N mice were mated overnight with C57BL/6N male mice, and fertilized eggs were collected the following day. In vitro transcribed Cas9 mRNA, Tcf12 CRISPR guide RNA (crRNA) (5′ - TACTCTAGATGAATCATAGG - 3′), transactivating crRNA, and HDR template DNA (tctgctttctttaggtatggatgagcgtggaggaacaacatcttggggaacaagtggtcaaccaagcccctcctatgattcatctagagCaagttttcttaagtcttggttacaactgtaatgatgat) were injected into the cytoplasm or pronucleus of the embryos. Two-cell stage embryos were transferred into the ampulla of the oviduct of pseudo-pregnant Hsd:ICR (CD-1) females. From the resulting mice, a founder male Tcf12 mutant mouse was used to start a colony by crossing with C57BL/6J female mice. Backcrossing to C57Bl/6J was performed for 3 generations to eliminate the rd8 mutation in the Crb1 gene (Crb1rd8), with genotyping at each step. After this, Tcf12ra/ra, Tcf12+/ra and Tcf12+/+ mice were generated by intercrossing heterozygotes.

## Genotyping Protocols for the Targeted Tcf12 Mutation and the Spontaneous Crb1rd8 Mutation
Genomic DNA was prepared from tail snips for genotyping of all experimental mice at weaning. Tail DNA samples prepared with Extracta DNA Prep for PCR ( ) were used for PCR genotyping of the Tcf12 target mutation and Crb1rd8 spontaneous mutation according to previously described protocols. – After cleanup with ExoSAP-IT PCR Product Cleanup Reagent ( ), the PCR products were sent to the UT Southwestern Sequencing Core facility for sequencing using the corresponding forward primers. Finally, the genotyping sequences were analyzed using FinchTV ( B). The primer sets used were as follows: Tcf12 Forward 5′-TGCTACATGCTACTGGGTCAAG-3′, Reverse 5′-GAACATCTGATGGGTACTCTGAGC-3′; Crb1rd8 Forward 5′-GCACAATAGAGATTGGAGGC-3′, Reverse 5′-TGTCTACATCCACCTCACAG-3′.

## Histological and Immunohistochemical Analysis of Brain and Retinal Sections
After anesthesia via IP injection of a ketamine-xylazine cocktail (100 mg/kg–5 mg/kg), followed by cardiac perfusion with PBS at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. Retinal sections were prepared from Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice using a modified freeze-substitution technique, as previously described. Eyes were enucleated and rapidly frozen in isopentane cooled with liquid nitrogen, then transferred to a freeze-substitution solution (methanol/acetic acid, 97:3) and stored at −80°C for at least 48 hours. Whole brains were carefully dissected from euthanized mice and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 48 hours. The brains and eyes were gradually warmed to room temperature, transferred to 70% and 100% ethanol respectively for paraffin embedding using routine techniques. Briefly, after appropriate fixation for each tissue, samples were paraffin-processed, embedded, and sectioned by members of the UT Southwestern Histo Pathology Core. Paraffin sections were prepared concomitantly and examined by dark-field microscopy.
Hematoylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on mouse parasagittal brain sections and retinal cross-sections using standard automated stainer procedures. Sections were stained for routine histopathologic evaluation by regressive H&E on a Sakura Prisma Plus x-y-z robotic-stainer using Leica Selectech reagents (Hematoxylin 560 and Alcoholic Eosin Y 515). Images of the H&E brain sections were taken at 4× magnification using a Nikon eclipse Ti2 E microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY, USA). Retinal H&E sections were imaged at 20× magnification on either side of the ONH using a Leica DM2000 upright compound microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The ONL thickness and number of nuclei per ONL column on the H&E images were measured at 500 µm intervals on both sides of the ONH (starting 250 µm from the margin of the ONH) using ImageJ. At each interval an average of three measurements 100 µm apart were used for statistical analyses.
For brain immunohistochemistry (IHC) labeling for microglia and activated Müller glia or astrocytes, additional consecutive parasagittal sections of mouse brains were deparaffinized and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95°C for 20 minutes. After blocking of endogenous peroxidases using 3% hydrogen peroxidase and 5% secondary antibody host-serum, sections were incubated at 4°C overnight with anti-Iba1 or anti-glial fibrillary acid protein (Gfap) antibody ( ). Iba1 staining was detected using an ImmPRESS Excel Amplified Polymer Kit, while Gfap staining was detected using a M.O.M. ImmPRESS HRP Polymer Kit, followed by DAB-based visualization ( ), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired using a Keyence BZ‑X1000 microscope (Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan) with a 10× magnification. H&E and IHC-stained parasagittal brain sections were examined by a board-certified neuropathologist.
For retinal IHC staining, retinal sections were deparaffinized, and antigen retrieval was performed using the heat-induced epitope retrieval method by heating in citrate buffer (pH 6) in a 95°C water bath for 25 minutes. The sections were then blocked and incubated overnight with anti-rhodopsin and anti-GS primary antibodies, followed by appropriate secondary antibodies ( ). DAPI was used for nuclear counterstaining. Rhodopsin staining was performed on retinal sections and the central field on either side of the optic disc was imaged. The area of rhodopsin staining was measured using ImageJ and divided by the length of the imaged section of retinal tissue (not the length of the field) to correct for the small differences in length associated with minor changes in the angle of the section in relation to the field (n = 10 Tcf12+/+ fields and n = 8 Tcf12ra/ra fields). Retinal sections from Tcf12+/+ (n = 6) and Tcf12ra/ra mice (n = 6) at 12 to 13 months of age were subjected to anti-GS and DAPI staining. All retinal sections were imaged at 25× magnification on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems).
The TUNEL assay was performed using the DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL system ( ) following the supplied and previously published protocols. Retinal sections from Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice (n = 5 per group at 3 months and n = 6 per group at 9 months) were stained and then imaged using a laser confocal microscope (see above). The TUNEL-positive cells were quantified as the total number of TUNEL+ cells per mouse.

## Fundus Photography and Fundus Spot Grading
Fundus photographs were captured using a Micron V retinal imaging microscope (Phoenix-Micron Inc., Bend, OR, USA) as previously described. The photos were scored, using a previously reported fundus spot scale, by investigators who were masked to the genotypic data of the mice. Briefly, scoring was based on the presence of white/yellow fundus spots as follows: 0: no spots present; 1: one to ten spots present; 2: spots occupy approximately one quadrant of the fundus; 3: spots occupy approximately two to three quadrants of the fundus; 4: spots occupy all four quadrants of the fundus. Each eye was scored independently, and the scores of both eyes were combined to generate a total score ranging from 0 to 8 for each mouse (9 potential values).

## Fundus Fluorescein Angiography (FFA) and Retinal Vascular Analysis
FFA was performed using a Micron IV retinal imaging system (Phoenix-Micron Inc.). Mice were anesthetized by IP injection of a ketamine-xylazine cocktail (100 mg/kg–5 mg/kg). The pupils were dilated, and hypromellose gel was applied to both eyes (see above). Prior to imaging, 10% fluorescein sodium (2 mg fluorescein per mouse; ) was administered intraperitoneally. Angiographic images were captured 4 minutes after fluorescein sodium injection. Vessel diameter was measured one optic disc diameter away from the center of the ONH, and the average diameter of all major vessels was calculated. The number of major vessels emerging from the ONH was recorded as the vessel count. Retinal vascular architecture, including vessel number and vessel diameter, was quantified using ImageJ. The parameters were compared between Tcf12+/+ (n = 4) and Tcf12ra/ra (n = 4) mice at 9 months of age.

## Flat Mounts and Analysis of Microglia
Age-matched 18- to 19-month-old (Tcf12+/+, n = 4, and Tcf12ra/ra, n = 4) mice were anesthetized via IP injection of a ketamine-xylazine cocktail (100 mg/kg–5 mg/kg), followed by humane euthanasia by cervical dislocation. Both eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% PFA at room temperature, following a previously described protocol. – In brief, enucleated intact eyes were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 minutes, followed by an additional 30 minutes after removing the cornea and lens. Then the retina was separated from the RPE-choroid-sclera. Retinal and RPE-choroid-sclera flat mounts (called RPE flat mounts hereafter) were generated by making 4 radial cuts and were fixed for an additional 1 hour. After blocking, flat mounts were stained with anti-Iba1, anti-Tmem119, and anti-Gal3 primary antibodies followed by appropriate secondary antibodies ( ). Phalloidin staining was used to assess RPE morphology. The RPE and retinal flat mounts were imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems) using a sequential scanning at 25× magnification. The flat mounts were divided into four quadrants (superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal) around the ONH, and each of those quadrants was divided into three regions (central, mid-periphery, and periphery). When two images were acquired between the central and peripheral regions (defined as the mid-periphery), their mean value was used to represent the mid-periphery measurement. In sum, a total of 12 fields were imaged for each mouse (4 quadrants × 3 anterior-posterior regions). For microglia quantification, fields of RPE flat mounts were grouped according to retinal region. Specifically, four peripheral fields were captured per eye (one per quadrant), and with four eyes per experimental group, a total of 16 peripheral fields were analyzed for each mouse line (similar for the central and midperipheral fields).

## Visual Function Analysis Using ERG
Scotopic and photopic ERG responses were recorded using a full-field ERG system (Celeris System; Diagnosys LLC, Lowell, MA, USA) in age-matched Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice. Scotopic ERG responses were obtained from Tcf12+/+ (n = 6, 5, 7) and Tcf12ra/ra (n = 5, 6, 6) mice at 3 to 4 weeks, 6 months, and 12 to 15 months of age. Photopic ERG responses were recorded from Tcf12+/+ (n = 6, 7) and Tcf12ra/ra (n = 6, 6) mice at 6 months and 12 to 15 months of age. All procedures were conducted under dim red-light illumination. Mice were dark-adapted for 12-16 hours before testing. After pupil dilation, scotopic and photopic ERG recordings were performed in deeply anesthetized mice (IP injection of a ketamine-xylazine cocktail, 100 mg/kg–5 mg/kg). A 0.3% hypromellose gel was applied to the eyes to prevent corneal dehydration. Each mouse was placed on the ERG console, ensuring contact between the full-field stimulators/electrodes and the corneal surface. For scotopic ERG, ten sweeps were recorded in response to flash stimuli of 0.01, 0.1, and 1.0 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2 (1 msec flash duration). For photopic ERG, mice were light-adapted for 10 minutes at 3 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2, after which 10 sweeps were recorded and averaged at flash intensities of 3 and 10 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2. Data were analyzed using Diagnosys Espion Software (Diagnosys, Inc.) to determine the a-wave and b-wave responses.

## Immunoblotting of Mouse Retinal Samples and Brain Samples
Neuroretina and brain samples from CRISPR/Cas9-generated Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice were harvested, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until use. Samples were processed similarly to previous studies. – Briefly, 100 µL of RIPA Lysis Buffer with 1× Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail ( ) was used to homogenize each sample for 1 minute on ice using a tissue grinder. After homogenization, samples were incubated on ice for 10 minutes and then spun at 19,090g for 10 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and used for a bicinchoninic acid protein assay ( ). After boiling in reducing SDS-PAGE buffer, 75–100 µg of total protein lysate/sample were loaded into a 4% to 20% Tris-Glycine mini gel and run for 1 hour at 140 V. Gels were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using an iBlot 2 gel semidry transfer device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Ponceau S ( ) was used to confirm uniform loading and protein transfer. Membranes were blocked in Intercept (TBS) Blocking Buffer ( ) for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were then washed in Tris-buffered saline solution supplemented with 0.1% Tween (TBS-T) and incubated in primary antibody overnight at 4°C on a shaker. Primary antibodies against Tcf12, Neurod1, Selenbp1, Faah, and β-actin ( ) were diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin in TBS containing 0.02% NaN₃, followed by incubation with the appropriate near-infrared-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in 5% nonfat milk in TBS-T for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were again washed with TBS-T and imaged on an Odyssey CLx (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA).

## Bulk RNA- and Single-Cell RNA-Sequencing for Retinal Transcriptome Analysis
For bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq), neuroretinas from 2- to 3-month-old Tcf12+/+ (n = 4) and Tcf12ra/ra (n = 4) mice were collected as previously described. In brief, after anesthesia via IP injection of a ketamine-xylazine cocktail (100 mg/kg–5 mg/kg), followed by cardiac perfusion with PBS at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. Eyes were enucleated, the anterior segment removed, and the neural retina isolated. The retina from both eyes of each mouse was pooled for analysis. Harvested retinal tissues were homogenized and spun in a centrifuge at 14,000 rpm for 10 minutes to obtain supernatant. Total RNA isolation was then performed using miRNeasy Tissue/Cells Advanced Micro Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol with on-column DNase digestion using RNAse-Free DNase Set ( ). Sample quality control was confirmed by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer ensuring RNA Integrity Number ≥ 7.5. Samples were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500 with desired number of reads per sample 25–35 M, read length 100 SR, single end reads. Pathway analysis (Reactome) was performed on a set of differentially expressed genes meeting statistical cutoffs of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, and log2FC > 1.
For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), single-cell suspensions were prepared from the retinas of C57BL/6J mice (4 months old, n = 4) using the papain dissociation system ( ), according to previously detailed protocols. , We also extracted Tcf12 expression in 90-day-old C57BL/6J retina from a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset deposited by Li et. al., under GEO accession no. GSE243413 (WT_P90 mouse retina, GEO accession no. GSM7785354). Cells expressing Tcf12 gene were categorized as being high Tcf12 expressors (“Tcf12-high,” expression > 2.5) or low expressors (“Tcf12-low,” expression > 0 and < 1.5). Pathway analysis (IPA) was performed by comparing “Tcf12-high versus “Tcf12-low” cells.

## RT-qPCR Validation of Retinal Bulk RNA-Seq Results
Neuroretinas from both eyes were collected from age-matched 2- to 3-month-old Tcf12+/+ (n = 4) and Tcf12ra/ra (n = 4) mice after perfusion and kept in RNAprotect Tissue Reagent ( ) at 4°C for up to 1 month before use. Total RNA isolation was then performed using miRNeasy Tissue/Cells Advanced Micro Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol with on-column DNase digestion using RNAse-Free DNase Set ( ). Quantity and purity of isolated total RNA samples were determined by NanoDrop OneC Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total RNA/sample 500 ng was then converted to cDNA using Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit for RT-qPCR ( ) according to manufacturer's protocol. Reactions were set up with 5 ng/sample cDNA, PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix ( ) and designed primers listed in for a total of 10 µL reaction volume in 384-well clear plate with barcode and ran using QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gapdh was used as a housekeeping gene for normalization, and the gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt formula with respect to the Tcf12+/+ samples.

## Proteomic Analysis of Retinal Samples
Neuroretinas from both eyes were collected from age-matched 2- to 3-month-old Tcf12+/+ (n = 5) and Tcf12ra/ra (n = 5) mice after perfusion. Neuroretinas were homogenized in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail using a pestle and a hand-held homogenizer. Homogenization was carried out for 1 minute on ice. Lysates were then incubated on ice for 10 minutes and spun in a centrifuge at 15,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were collected, and total protein concentrations were determined using BCA assay. 50 µL of 1 mg/mL tissue lysate/sample was submitted for proteomic analysis. After disulfide bond reduction and alkylation, samples were digested overnight with trypsin using an S-Trap micro columns (ProtiFi, Farmingdale, NY, USA). The peptide eluate from the S-Trap was dried and reconstituted in 100 mM TEAB buffer. The TMT10plex Isobaric Mass Tagging Kit ( ) was used to label the samples as per the manufacturer's instructions, quenched with 5% hydroxylamine, and the samples were combined. A reverse-phase high-pH fractionation spin column ( ) was used according to the manufacturer's directions to fractionate the sample into 8 fractions. The fractions were dried in a SpeedVac and reconstituted in a 2% acetonitrile, 0.1% TFA buffer and diluted such that ∼1 µg of peptides were injected. Peptides were analyzed on a Thermo Orbitrap Eclipse MS system coupled to an Ultimate 3000 RSLC-Nano liquid chromatography system. Protein identification and quantification were done using Proteome Discoverer v.3.0 SP1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Raw MS data files were analyzed against the mouse reviewed protein database from UniProt. Reporter ion intensities were reported, with further normalization performed using the total intensity in each channel to correct discrepancies in sample amount in each channel. The FDR cutoff was set to 1% for all peptides.

## Statistical Analysis
Graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1 and Microsoft Excel 16.63.1. Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. For all figures, groups of measurements were taken from distinct samples, rather than from repeated measurements. Sample sizes are included in each figure legend. Based on the assumption of normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were performed using a two‑tailed unpaired Student's t-test. For the quantification of fundus spots, which was based on a non‑continuous ordinal scale, differences between groups were analyzed using the non‑parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Survival data were analyzed using the χ2 test.

## Results

## Applying OCT Screening to a Forward Genetic Pipeline Identified an Essential Role of Tcf12 in the Retina
With the goal of identifying genes essential to retinal development and homeostasis, we screened 6,409 G3 mice (6 to 8 months old) from 212 pedigrees (C57BL/6J strain background) carrying heterozygous and homozygous mutations induced by ENU for retina alterations by OCT imaging ( ). We estimate that we have achieved 5.4% genome saturation with damaging mutations, each of which was tested in at least two homozygous animals. , , OCT images were analyzed for ONL thickness, and deviations from WT measurements were ascribed to mutations by automated meiotic mapping, as we previously described. – , Among the pedigrees analyzed, we identified a mutation in Tcf12 that was associated with thinning of the ONL (P = 8.56 × 10−7, additive model of inheritance;  D). The mutated Tcf12 allele contains a single-nucleotide substitution (A to G) at position 71,922,918 bp of mouse chromosome 9 (GRCm39), which damages the intron 4 splice donor site of the Tcf12 gene ( ). The mutation is predicted to cause skipping of exon 4 during splicing of the Tcf12 transcript, resulting in a 74-nucleotide deletion of exon 4 and a frame-shifted protein product: after amino acid 50 of the protein, there is inclusion of 8 aberrant amino acids, followed by early termination (total of 58 amino acids). The protein is normally 706 amino acids in length and contains two activation domains (AD1 and AD2), a leucine zipper domain, and a bHLH. Candidate Explorer software predicted the Tcf12 mutated allele to be an “excellent candidate” for causation.

## CRISPR/Cas9-Generated Tcf12ra/ra Mice Corroborate the ONL Thinning Found by OCT Screening in Forward Genetics
The forward genetic screened pedigree containing the mutated Tcf12 allele had 79 additional ENU-induced mutations ( D). Thus we decided to use CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis to generate transgenic mice with a “replacement allele” (which we will abbreviate as “ra” for the mouse genotype notation) identical to that detected in the ENU pedigree to confirm our findings ( ). We detected a substantial impact of Tcf12 deficiency on early survival consistent with prior reports. , Out of 111 pregnancies in the Tcf12+/ra x Tcf12+/ra crossing, only 87 litters survived. Of the 87 surviving litters, 54 had no Tcf12ra/ra pups. Finally, out of the total 426 pups, 30.5% were WT, 58% were Tcf12+/ra, and only 11.5% were Tcf12ra/ra. This demonstrated a highly significant decrease in the early survival of Tcf12ra/ra ( ; P < 0.0001). Yet the surviving Tcf12ra/ra mice appeared normal, including their eyes, except for displaying a kinked tail, which has been reported before. We did not observe any increase in mortality in Tcf12ra/ra mice after they had survived the first few weeks of life.
Western blot analysis confirmed a deficiency of Tcf12 in the Tcf12ra/ra mice ( ). As reported previously, , although the molecular weight of unmodified Tcf12 is around 70 kDa, it is a transcriptional factor and can undergo significant post-translational modifications, which can account for higher-molecular-weight bands. We detected bands at approximately 70 and 105 kDa in Tcf12+/+ retinas, which were greatly reduced in Tcf12ra/ra retinas ( B, C). Interestingly, we also detected a somewhat weaker 95 kDa band, which was not altered in Tcf12ra/ra compared to Tcf12+/+ retinas ( D, P = 0.46).
We began by obtaining OCT images at 3 to 4 months, 5 to 6 months, 7 to 8 months, and 13 to 16 months of age ( ). Analysis of OCT images confirmed a significant reduction in ONL thickness in Tcf12ra/ra retinas compared to Tcf12+/+ retinas. The average reduction in thickness was 8% and was statistically significant in all cohorts ( I; 3 to 4 months, P < 0.01; 5 to 6 months, P < 0.05; 7 to 8 months, P < 0.001; and 13 to 16 months, P < 0.05). Because we did not detect retinal thinning on OCT in heterozygous (Tcf12+/ra) mice at 5 to 6 months and 7 to 8 months old ( ), we restricted our analysis to comparisons between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice. Of interest, contrary to other models of retinal degeneration we have described, – the ONL thinning in Tcf12ra/ra mice showed no progression over the observation period. Thus, we added cohorts at 3 to 4 weeks of age and 17 to 20 months of age. These cohorts again showed a similar thinning (8% at 3 to 4 weeks, P < 0.05, and 12% at 17 to 20 months, P < 0.05). No significant differences were detected in GCC thickness ( J) or TRT ( K) at any age group. We did not detect any differences between male and female mice in retinal thinning across any of the measured parameters. OCT images from Tcf12ra/ra mice did not show any signs of focal retinal thinning, laminar disruption, or hyperreflective lesions, such as pseudo-rosettes, which can be observed in some retinal dystrophies, like the dystrophy seen in rd8 mutant mice. Analysis of choriocapillaris thickness in OCT images did not show any differences between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice ( ).
 

## Histological Examination of Tcf12ra/ra Retinas Confirms Photoreceptor Degeneration and INL Thickening
To confirm the OCT findings, we performed histological analysis of Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra retina sections. Images were obtained at 20x magnification ( A,  B). The ONL thickness and the number of nuclear layers within the ONL were quantified at predetermined intervals. At 12 to 13 months of age, Tcf12ra/ra mice exhibited a modest but significant reduction in ONL thickness and in the number of ONL nuclei per column compared to Tcf12+/+ mice ( C,  D). Photoreceptor outer segment (POS) thickness was determined by IHC using anti-rhodopsin antibody, which showed a significant thinning in Tcf12ra/ra retinas ( ).
 To investigate whether apoptosis contributes to the loss of photoreceptors in Tcf12ra/ra retinas, TUNEL staining was performed on retinal sections ( A, B). Quantitative analysis revealed low levels of TUNEL+ cells within the ONL of both Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra retinas, with no significant difference observed between the groups at either 3 or 9 months of age ( C, D).
Having shown ONL and POS thinning, we decided to screen for inner retinal pathology using an OCT screening parameter we have previously validated in our laboratory to reproducibly provide accurate measurements of this region, IRT. We chose our 3- to 4-month-old cohort because it was large and had shown highly significant ONL thinning by that age. Interestingly, we observed an increase in IRT in Tcf12ra/ra mice ( ), which we further explored in retinal H&E sections. Looking specifically at the INL, we again found a significant increase in thickness in Tcf12ra/ra retinas ( E). To determine whether this INL thickening is associated with Müller glia abnormalities, GS/DAPI double staining was performed on retinal sections ( ); however, no significant differences were detected between groups.
Of interest, our scRNA-seq data in C57BL/6J mice show that Tcf12 is expressed across a variety of retinal cell types, particularly photoreceptors, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, and Müller glia ( A). These results are generally confirmed by an analysis of the EyeIntegration database (https://eyeintegration.nei.nih.gov/; B). 

## FFA and Histological Analyses Reveal Normal Retinal Vasculature and Brain Structure, Respectively
Given the early onset of retinal anatomical changes in Tcf12ra/ra mice, we assessed whether there were associated developmental alterations affecting either the retinal vascular tree or the brain. Accordingly, FFA was used to assess retinal vascular architecture by quantifying vessel number and measuring vessel diameter. No significant differences were seen in either parameter between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice ( ). Additionally, H&E-stained parasagittal brain sections were examined by a board-certified neuropathologist using light microscopy. These analyses showed no abnormal histopathologic changes in any of the brain regions of Tcf12ra/ra mice compared to Tcf12+/+ mice. Of note, no changes in either the cortical or cerebellar layers were seen, nor were any defects observed within the hippocampal cornu ammonis system. Furthermore, there were no disruptions of the white matter tracts ( A–F). Interestingly, Iba1 staining revealed increased microglial numbers in the brain of Tcf12ra/ra mice compared to Tcf12+/+ mice, including in the hippocampus ( G). On the other hand, anti-Gfap immunohistochemical labeling of astrocytes showed no differences between the two groups ( H).
Western blot analysis of brain tissue revealed a very different band pattern compared to retinal tissue. Specifically, while we observed a mild reduction in the 70- and 105-kDa Tcf12 bands in Tcf12ra/ra brains ( E), the signal from these bands was much lower than that observed in retinal samples. Moreover, the ∼95 kDa band we had observed to be unchanged in Tcf12ra/ra retinas was, in the brain, much stronger than the 70 and 105 kDa bands. This 95 kDa band did not differ between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra brains.

## Tcf12ra/ra Mice Exhibit Accumulation of Fundus Spots and Activated Subretinal Microglia With Aging
Since previous studies have indicated that an increase in fundus spots is often associated with retinal degeneration, we assessed fundus photographs of Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice in different cohorts. Two masked investigators conducted a semiquantitative analysis of the images using a previously validated grading system. – We observed an increase in fundus spots (green arrows in  D) in Tcf12ra/ra mice compared to Tcf12+/+ mice, which became statistically significant at 5 to 8 months and remained elevated at 11 to 13 and 17 to 20 months of age ( A–E). Prior results from our group and others have demonstrated a correlation between fundus spots and activated subretinal microglia. , , – To determine if the fundus spots in Tcf12ra/ra mice were also associated with increased subretinal microglia, we prepared RPE flat mounts from 18- to 19-month-old Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra eyes and triple-stained them with anti-Iba1 (pan-microglial marker, green), anti-Tmem119 (resident microglia, white), and anti-Gal3 (activated microglia marker, red). Confocal microscopy was used to image the stained RPE flat mounts in three regions defined by the distance from the ONH as central (C), mid-peripheral (M), and peripheral (P) ( G). We found an increase in the number of subretinal microglia in Tcf12ra/ra mice. The difference was significant for all three microglia/macrophage markers and in all three retinal regions ( H–J). The vast majority of Iba1+ cells (99%) also stained positive for Tmem119. Meanwhile, 66% of Iba1+ cells also stained positive for Gal3. Although subretinal microglia were much more prevalent in Tcf12ra/ra mice compared to Tcf12+/+ mice, a few of these subretinal cells could be seen in Tcf12+/+ mice, the majority of which were in the peripheral region ( H). Analysis of retinal flat mounts using confocal microscopy showed that microglia in the inner retina (IPL and outer plexiform layer) appeared similar in number and morphology in Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice, with a homeostatic, highly ramified appearance ( A, B). On the contrary, microglia in the outer retina (POS) are seen in greater numbers in Tcf12ra/ra retinas and demonstrate a less ramified activated morphology ( C). Of note, phalloidin staining did not reveal obvious differences between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra RPE flat mounts ( ).
 

## ERG Reveals Decreased Retinal Function in 12- to 15-Month-Old Tcf12ra/ra Mice
Having confirmed that Tcf12 deficiency led to anatomical changes in the retinal outer nuclear layer, we decided to examine whether these alterations affected retinal function. Scotopic ERG was performed on Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice at 3 to 4 weeks, 6 months, and 12 to 15 months of age using three different intensities of light stimuli (0.01, 0.1, and 1 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2) ( ). At early time points (3 to 4 weeks and 6 months), no significant differences in a-wave or b-wave amplitudes were observed between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice across all flash intensities. However, aging Tcf12ra/ra mice exhibited significantly reduced a-wave amplitudes at 1.0 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2 (30% decrease at 12 to 15 months, P < 0.001). Similar reductions were observed in b-wave amplitudes at 12 to 15 months of age (24% reduction at the 0.1 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2 intensity, P < 0.05, and 35% reduction at the 1 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2 intensity, P < 0.01).
 Photopic ERG testing was performed, which showed indications of decreased a-wave amplitudes in the aging group of Tcf12ra/ra mice ( ). At 12 to 15 months, there was a statistically significant decrease in a-wave for both the 3 and 10 cd ∙ s ∙ m−2 light stimuli. However, the b-wave responses were not changed in Tcf12ra/ra mice.

## RNA Sequencing Identifies Alterations in Gene Expression and Biological Pathways
To better understand the cellular mechanisms behind the anatomic and functional abnormalities caused by Tcf12 deficiency, we performed bulk RNA-seq using neuroretinas from 2- to 3-month-old Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice. A volcano plot ( A) revealed 611 genes that were upregulated (log2FC > 0.58, equivalent to a fold change of > 1.5), whereas 1092 were downregulated (log2FC < −0.58) in Tcf12ra/ra mice at FDR < 0.05. A heat map of the top 100 genes was generated after ranking the genes based on a parameter that combines differential expression and significance (“Significance-FC rank” = −log10(FDR) × log2FC,  B).
 We then performed pathway analysis by applying the GSEA Reactome pathway enrichment tool to the bulk RNA-seq data. The top 40 upregulated pathways with FDR < 0.05 and |NES| ranking are shown in  C. Although none of the downregulated pathways passed the FDR < 0.05 cutoff, 29 pathways met a commonly used FDR cutoff of 0.1, including RND3 GTPase cycle and signaling by Hippo, cell-cell communication, and chromatin organization. Additionally, to evaluate the impact of Tcf12 by cell type, we used a well-characterized publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of mouse retinas (post-natal day 90) C57BL/6J, GEO: GSE243413/GSM7785354). We first categorized the rod photoreceptor cells as being Tcf12-high (>2.5 normalized expression) versus Tcf12-low (< 1.5 normalized expression, after eliminating zero values). IPA was performed on the scRNA-seq data. The top 40 up- or down-regulated pathways with P < 0.05 and |Z-score| ranking are shown in  D (only 5 downregulated pathways met this criteria). Interestingly, there was a significant overlap between the bulk-RNA seq and scRNA-seq pathway analysis results. Most of the top-ranked pathways were primarily associated with metabolism of proteins and RNA, mitochondrial and energy metabolism, cell cycle and signal transduction, and cellular response to stimuli and inflammatory pathways.
To look for effects of Tcf12 deficiency on individual genes, we performed an unbiased analysis of bulk RNA-seq data using an FDR < 1 × 10-5 and |log2FC| ≥ 2 as selection criteria. This identified 10 genes of interest: Tmem233, Doc2b, Alpk2, Agr2, Sned1, Nr4a3, Apobec2, Slco1a5, Arid3c and Papolb. To validate the transcriptional changes, from these candidates, we selected 5 genes distributed across the volcano plot. The RT-qPCR results demonstrated upregulation of Tmem233 and downregulation of Doc2b, Alpk2, Agr2, and Apobec2 in Tcf12ra/ra retinas ( ), consistent with the bulk RNA-seq dataset results. Tmem233 encodes a dispanin-family protein expressed in sensory neurons that interacts with the voltage-gated sodium channel NaV1.7 to modulate neuronal excitability. – 
Doc2b encodes a double C2 domain-containing protein that acts as a calcium-sensitive regulator of vesicle exocytosis in neurons and endocrine cells. , 
Alpk2 is a gene encoding a kinase involved in signal transduction and cellular stress responses. 
Agr2 encodes a secreted protein disulfide isomerase-like factor that functions in endoplasmic reticulum proteostasis. , 
Apobec2 is a gene encoding a cytidine deaminase involved in nucleic acid modification. – 

## Proteomic Analysis Reveals Differentially Expressed Proteins in Tcf12ra/ra Retinas
To characterize how Tcf12 deficiency affects retinal protein expression, we performed quantitative proteomic analysis on neuroretinas isolated from 2- to 3-month-old Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra mice. Differential expression analysis was performed using a threshold of P < 0.05 and ≥ 20% change in expression. Based on these criteria, 161 proteins were significantly upregulated, and 146 proteins were downregulated in Tcf12ra/ra neuroretinas compared to Tcf12+/+ neuroretinas. A volcano plot displays the distribution of differentially expressed proteins, with proteins showing |log2FC| > 1 and P ≤ 1 × 10−5 labeled individually ( A). Analysis of relative protein abundance revealed that Cpm, Fgf2, Gulo, Klhl41, and Selenbp1 were significantly upregulated, whereas Neurod1 and Faah were significantly downregulated in Tcf12ra/ra neuroretinas ( B). Notably, five of these molecules had also met differential expression criteria in bulk RNA-seq (labeled by double red asterisks in  B). To validate the protein changes, we chose Selenbp1, Neurod1, and Faah for immunoblotting. Western blot analysis demonstrated that Selenbp1 increased, while Neurod1 and Faah decreased in Tcf12ra/ra retinas, confirming the proteomic findings ( C,  D). Uncut membranes are shown in . Selenbp1 is a protein involved in selenium binding and redox regulation. , Neurod1 is a bHLH protein that drives neuronal differentiation and also regulates hormone gene expression in endocrine cells. , , Faah is a membrane-associated serine hydrolase that degrades endocannabinoids. Endocannabinoids are known to exert neuroprotective effects in models of retinal degeneration. – 
 

## Discussion
Understanding the fundamental mechanisms that support retinal development and homeostasis is crucial for generating novel therapeutic strategies for retinal diseases. In this work, we identified a previously undescribed essential role of Tcf12 in retinal health using a forward genetic pipeline combined with OCT imaging. Tcf12 is part of the bHLH protein family, specifically the class I sub-family (also known as E-proteins), which are non-tissue-specific and also include TCF3 and TCF4. , , 
Tcf12 can form either homodimers or heterodimers with other class I or class II (tissue-specific) bHLH proteins. These complexes bind to E-box sequences (5′-CANNTG-3′) in DNA to regulate multiple cell functions. , Only subtle retinal developmental deficits have been reported in association with the loss of individual bHLH genes. A notable exception is the atonal bHLH transcription factor 7 (Atoh7, also known as Math5) gene. However, in this case, the changes are primarily due to defects in the differentiation of the inner retina, particularly in retinal ganglion cells, rather than the photoreceptors. These changes lead to significant primary thinning of the inner retina in Atoh7-deficient mice, in contrast to the mild reactive thickening of the inner retina we describe below in Tcf12ra/ra eyes. Therefore this novel description of the essential role of Tcf12 in photoreceptor health will build on these previous findings about the importance of bHLH proteins in retinal physiology.
In our screening, we identified a mutated allele in Tcf12 predicted to result in a null effect. This mutation led to a mild but highly significant thinning of the ONL, which we later confirmed in a CRISPR/Cas9-generated Tcf12ra/ra mouse line using both OCT and histology. Of interest, we found an increased thickness of the INL in Tcf12ra/ra retinas. A similar thickening of the INL has been described in multiple models of retinal degeneration, including AMD and retinal dystrophies (such as Retinitis Pigmentosa, ABCA4-related retinopathy, and CDHR1-related retinal dystrophy). The decrease in ONL thickness in these diseases seems to be accompanied by a likely compensatory increase in INL thickness. It appears that as the photoreceptor layer (ONL) degenerates, the inner retinal cells (bipolar, horizontal, and Müller cells) rearrange, proliferate, or become inflamed, causing the INL to thicken. – In our study, despite significant ONL thinning and a modest increase in INL thickness in Tcf12ra/ra retinas, GS immunoreactivity in Müller glia was comparable to Tcf12+/+ retinas, suggesting that the observed INL thickening reflects subtle structural remodeling rather than overt Müller gliosis.
The retinal phenotype in heterozygous (Tcf12+/ra) mice was normal, indicating a recessive mode of inheritance in this model. Toward the end of our study, we discovered that ONL thinning could be detected as early as 3 to 4 weeks of age using OCT and then remained mainly stable, with minimal decrease until old age. This finding differs from other gene-phenotype associations we have identified in the past, which typically involve progressive retinal degeneration with age. – It suggests that at least part of the effects of Tcf12 on the retina occur during development. Analysis of Tcf12 expression across retinal cell types using eyeIntegration appears to support this concept, showing particularly high expression in early- and late-stage retinal progenitor cells in human retinas. Moreover, we did not observe an increase in TUNEL+ cells in the retinas of Tcf12ra/ra mice, suggesting that ongoing cell death is not a prominent feature of the mature Tcf12ra/ra retina. Yet, these low levels of apoptosis represent only snapshots in time, and the temporal window of apoptotic activity may not have been captured by our sampling. Alternatively, ONL thinning may result from chronic, low-grade cell loss below the detection threshold of TUNEL at any single time point, or from defects in retinal development or maturation that led to a reduced final number of photoreceptors. Early developmental analyses were not included in this study due to our late recognition of this feature of the clinical course, but will be the focus of our future investigations.
The retinal vasculature also appeared to develop normally. Although prior Tcf12 mutants (using either conventional knockouts or Cre-mediated mutations that disrupt different regions of Tcf12) showed craniofacial/cortical defects, , , our CRISPR/Cas9-generated Tcf12ra/ra mice lacked clinically obvious skull/brain abnormalities. IHC was suggestive of increased microglial activation in the brains of Tcf12ra/ra mice but showed no differences in activated Müller glia or astrocytes. There are a few potential explanations for the relatively mild brain phenotype in Tcf12ra/ra mice. In the canonical Tcf12 isoform, the exon 4 splice donor mutation is predicted to result in a 58-amino-acid-truncated protein, with the last 8 amino acids being aberrant. It is possible that some level of this truncated protein, or an alternative splicing isoform, may persist in the brain and preserve some of the functional properties needed for cranial neural crest migration and coronal suture formation. It is also possible that E-protein compensation (Tcf3/E2A, Tcf4/E2-2) may suppress non-ocular phenotypes. , A third potential explanation is that, although the reported defects had a low penetrance of 36% at postnatal stages, we did not specifically look for these abnormalities at E17 (when they showed the highest prevalence). Finally, it is possible that different Tcf12 isoforms are expressed in different tissues and that some brain isoforms are not affected by our mutation. Specifically, we noted a markedly different pattern of Western blot bands in the brain compared with the retina. In the retina, the two predominant bands were observed at 70 and 105 kDa and were greatly reduced in Tcf12ra/ra compared with Tcf12+/+ mice. Meanwhile, in the brain, a 95 kDa band was much stronger than the 70 and 105 kDa bands, and did not change in Tcf12ra/ra compared to Tcf12+/+ mice. Although it is not possible to categorically conclude that the 95 kDa band is another isoform of Tcf12 because it is not altered in Tcf12ra/ra mice, it is indeed possible that it is a Tcf12 isoform (or a modified isoform) that is unaffected by the mutation in question. If this is the case, this isoform appears to be insufficient to prevent retinal degeneration, possibly due to its relatively low expression in the retina. However, being the dominant band in the brain, it could explain the relatively minor findings in Tcf12ra/ra brains. Further studies will be needed to shine light on this issue.
Further characterization of Tcf12ra/ra eyes revealed accumulation of white/yellow fundus spots, which were associated with subretinal Iba1+, Tmem119+ cells. A very mild increase in these spots was first seen at 5 to 6 months of age, but the accumulation continued to increase up to one year of age. This delayed accumulation would suggest that there is a continued effect from Tcf12 deficiency beyond retinal development. We should note that, not only were over 99% of Iba1+ cells also Tmem119+, but virtually all Gal3+ cells were also Tmem119+. This suggests that the subretinal cells are activated resident microglia rather than infiltrating macrophages. Gal3 has been proposed as a marker of microglial activation and migration into the subretinal space, and may even be necessary for a protective phagocytic function. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have shown that in most degenerative conditions with an intact blood-retina barrier, subretinal immune cells are predominantly activated resident microglia rather than infiltrating macrophages. – An interesting finding in Tcf12-deficient mice was a preferential accumulation of subretinal microglia in the peripheral and mid-peripheral retinas, with few Iba1-staining subretinal microglia in the central retina.
 Although eyeIntegration data suggest that Tcf12 expression in RPE cells is lower than in the neural retina, Tcf12 deficiency could plausibly affect RPE structure or function. Phalloidin staining, which provides information on RPE morphology, cytoskeletal integrity, and general well-being, did not reveal obvious differences between Tcf12+/+ and Tcf12ra/ra RPE flat mounts. However, some subtle differences in either morphology or heterogeneity may require more sophisticated analysis, which we will address in our future mechanistic work using ZO-1-stained flat mounts and electron microscopy.
To investigate the impact of Tcf12 deficiency on retinal function, ERGs were performed in Tcf12ra/ra mice, and a mild functional deficit was detected. Although the effect was most consistent on scotopic ERG (both a-wave and b-wave), there was some signal for an effect on the photopic a-wave response, suggesting that further studies are needed to explore cone versus rod effects. Interestingly, similar to the pattern seen with microglial activation, the functional deficit also lagged behind the anatomical changes: we first documented functional loss in ERG at 12 to 15 months of age. We hypothesize that the chronic effects of Tcf12 deficiency could progressively lead to cellular dysfunction despite the absence of an obvious increase in cell death.
Although, to our knowledge, there is no available published work describing the role of Tcf12 in the retina, findings from its effects on other tissues and from class II bHLH proteins in the retina can give us some insight into some of its functions. Reports show that Tcf12 is involved in the control of progenitor and proliferating adult neural stem cells, playing a role in sustaining their undifferentiated state. – Downregulation of Tcf12 expression is crucial in osteoblast differentiation, partially by enhancing BMP and ERK1/2 signaling pathways. Multiple studies have also demonstrated that Tcf12 can function as both an oncogene and a tumor suppressor gene. Notably, just within the context of oncogenesis, Tcf12 has been shown to exert its effects by modulating multiple pathways, including activation of the Wnt/β-catenin, the repression of E-cadherin transcription, upregulation of CXCR4 expression, and activation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway. , , Meanwhile, Tcf12 has also been shown to suppress prostate cancer by inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, as well as tumor growth in vivo. 
We performed bulk RNA-seq and proteomics from Tcf12ra/ra versus Tcf12+/+ retinas to begin exploring the molecular mechanisms underpinning the effect of Tcf12 deficiency on retinal homeostasis. The top significantly affected pathways include protein and RNA metabolism, mitochondrial and energy metabolism, cell cycle and signal transduction, and cellular response to stimuli and immune system/inflammation. Notably, many of these pathways were again detected when we analyzed low-Tcf12 versus high-Tcf12 expressing photoreceptor cells using a published scRNA-seq dataset from C57BL/6J retina. This adds credibility to the findings because these are two completely different approaches to interrogate transcriptomic changes induced by low Tcf12 expression (different techniques and different mouse models). Both bulk and single-cell transcriptomic analyses consistently identified Wnt signaling, immune system-related pathways, and cell cycle pathways as some of the most prominently altered pathways in Tcf12ra/ra retinas.
Activation of the canonical Wnt pathway is known to be involved in retinal inflammation, oxidative stress, and retinal degeneration, as reported in AMD and DR. In our model, the activation of the Wnt pathway may partly be explained at the molecular level by the bulk RNA-seq finding of a downregulation of Wnt signaling inhibitors Doc2b, Alpk2, Axin, APC, Rnf43, and Znrf3. We confirmed decreased levels of Doc2b and Alpk2 using RT-qPCR. Doc2b suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signaling by enhancing the Ctnnb1 destruction complex and promoting β-catenin degradation in cervical cancer, while Alpk2 has been shown to negatively regulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling during cardiac development. Decreased levels of Axin and Dishevelled (DVL) may also contribute to Wnt pathway activation. DVL is known to inhibit the β-catenin destruction complex in canonical Wnt signaling or activate downstream effectors in non-canonical Wnt pathways. Finally, we found Fgf2 to be increased in our proteomics assay. Fgf2 is known to interact with the Wnt signaling pathway in complex ways, often to regulate neuronal progenitor cell proliferation versus differentiation. , 
It is still unclear whether the activation of the Wnt signaling pathway is a direct result of the Tcf12 deficiency or a secondary event. Tcf12 can heterodimerize with Neurod1 to regulate central nervous system neurogenesis. Neurod1 is a critical factor for photoreceptor survival and regeneration. , , In Tcf12ra/ra retinas, there is a concomitant reduction of Neurod1 and Tcf12. It is possible that upregulated Wnt signaling (and other cell cycle pathways regulating cell proliferation, growth, and survival) may be a homeostatic response to neurodegeneration resulting from Neurod1 and Tcf12 deficiency. These biological processes involved in cell signaling, differentiation, migration, and survival may be related to increased metabolic demand, as metabolism is necessary for cell survival and proper function. , We wonder whether the upregulation we observe in pathways related to RNA and protein metabolism and energy metabolism in our transcriptomic data results from these cellular changes or drives the alterations caused by Tcf12 deficiency. Of interest, the retina, especially the photoreceptor layer, exhibits one of the highest oxygen consumption rates in the body because of the energy demands of the dark current, phototransduction, and continuous renewal of outer segments. – Furthermore, growing evidence indicates that metabolic dysregulation contributes fundamentally to the pathophysiology of AMD and retinitis pigmentosa, underscoring the importance of maintaining retinal energy homeostasis. – The upregulation of metabolic pathways we see in this study specially protein translation, respiratory electron transport and Nonsense-Mediated Decay may be important under cellular stress conditions. Additionally, in our study, we overserved a significant increase in Tmem233, which encodes a dispanin-family protein that is highly expressed in sensory neurons. Reports show that Tmem233 interacts with the voltage-gated sodium channel NaV1.7, subtly modulating its inactivation and influencing neuronal excitability under both physiological and pathological conditions. – This may be important in the retina because Na+ transport by Na-K ATPase accounted for about half of the energy requirements of neurophysiological function. 
The upregulation of several immune pathways, including NF-kB signaling and antigen-processing-related pathways (e.g., ER-phagosome pathways), is consistent with the increased microglia activation and their migration to the subretinal space in Tcf12ra/ra retinas. Of note, the activation of the NF-kB signaling pathway has been reported to play variable roles depending on the type of affected cells and the retinal environment, leading to either cell survival or to inflammation and cellular damage. Interestingly, the above-mentioned Fgf2 increase may also be relevant to microglial activation. Fgf2 has been proposed to be secreted by degenerating neurons and to act as a “help- me” signal stimulating microglial activation, migration, and phagocytosis of unwanted debris. Another connection to the above-mentioned DEGs and microglial activation is Doc2b. Doc2b blocks cytokine-induced IKKβ-NF-κB signaling in human islets under inflammatory conditions, suggesting that its downregulation favors an inflammatory response, as would be needed for increased microglial activation. 
We identified additional inflammation-related molecular changes in Tcf12ra/ra retinas, including Agr2, Apobec2, Selenbp1, and Faah. Bulk RNA-seq revealed decreased expression of Agr2 and Apobec2, which we confirmed by RT-qPCR. Agr2 is required for proper protein folding and ER homeostasis and is known to modulate TGF-β/Smad and MAPK/ERK signaling involved in angiogenesis and inflammation. , 
Apobec2 maintains muscle mitochondrial integrity and is transcriptionally regulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines via NF-κB activation and supports retinal regeneration. – Concurrent reductions in Agr2 and Apobec2 point to disrupted ER stress responses, mitochondrial homeostasis, and inflammatory regulation. Proteomic results revealed increased Selenbp1 and decreased Faah, both of which were confirmed by Western blot. Selenbp1 is a redox-regulating methanethiol oxidase implicated in neurological, inflammatory, and ischemic processes, and thought to be protective against oxidative stress. , Faah is involved in regulating the endocannabinoid system in the retina, and decreased expression is predicted to be neuroprotective and to modulate inflammation. – 
One caveat of our study is that while NCBI and Ensembl predict a 706-aa Tcf12 canonical isoform, for which the effect of the replacement allele is predicted to be “probably null”, the same databases also predict additional potential splice variants that would not be affected by the mutation. If such variants do exist in the retina, their protein products may provide some Tcf12 functionality in Tcf12ra/ra mice. Nonetheless, the clear retinal phenotype, impact on perinatal survival, and decreased protein levels in Tcf12ra/ra mice indicate that the mutation is at least hypomorphic.
In summary, we have characterized the retinal phenotype resulting from Tcf12 deficiency and established a new mouse model for studying Tcf12-mediated mechanisms in retinal health. These data indicate that Tcf12 deficiency leads to broad transcriptional and proteomic reprogramming in the retina, particularly affecting pathways involved in cell cycle regulation, immune system modulation, and metabolism, all of which are critical for retinal cell homeostasis. However, it remains to be determined whether these changes are directly caused by Tcf12 deficiency or result from a response to Tcf12-deficiency-induced retinal degeneration. One limitation of this study is the lack of early developmental assessment. Future studies will focus on the effect of Tcf12 deficiency on the transcriptional and protein expression landscape of the retina during late fetal and early postnatal development.
