## Abstract
Nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F (Nr2f) proteins are essential for brain development in mice, but little is known about their precise roles and their evolutionary diversification. In the present study, the expression patterns of major nr2f genes (nr2f1a, nr2f1b, and nr2f2) during early brain development were investigated in zebrafish. Comparisons of their expression patterns revealed similar but temporally and spatially distinct patterns after early somite stages in the brain. Frameshift mutations in the three nr2f genes, achieved using the CRISPR/Cas9 method, resulted in a smaller telencephalon and smaller eyes in the nr2f1a mutants; milder forms of those defects were present in the nr2f1b and nr2f2 mutants. Acridine orange staining revealed enhanced cell death in the brain and/or eyes in all nr2f homozygous mutants. The expression of regional markers in the brain did not suggest global defects in brain regionalization; however, shha expression in the preoptic area and hypothalamus, as well as fgf8a expression in the anterior telencephalon, was disturbed in nr2f1a and nr2f1b mutants, potentially leading to a defective telencephalon. Specification of the retina and optic stalk was also significantly affected. The overexpression of nr2f1b by injection of mRNA disrupted the anterior brain at a high dose, and the expression of pax6a in the eyes and fgf8a in the telencephalon at a low dose. The results of these loss‐ and gain‐of‐function approaches showed that nr2f genes regulate the development of the telencephalon and eyes in zebrafish embryos.
Keywords: brain development, eye, Nr2f/COUP‐TF, telencephalon, zebrafish

We established zebrafish mutant lines for the three nr2f genes that are expressed in the telencephalon and examined their phenotypes. The data suggest that the nr2f genes play a role in telencephalon development via the formation of signaling centers.

## INTRODUCTION
During vertebrate development, the central nervous system (CNS) first appears as the neural plate, which subsequently forms the neural tube. Its anterior portion gives rise to three major brain vesicles: the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain. The dorsolateral forebrain later forms the telencephalic vesicles, which are then subdivided into the dorsal telencephalon (pallium) and ventral telencephalon (subpallium). The process of pallial development diverged among vertebrates; in mammals, the dorsolateral forebrain later evaginates, whereas in ray‐finned fish it everts, to generate the telencephalic vesicles (Folgueira et al.,  ; Mueller & Wullimann,  ). Although the telencephalon morphologies in mammals and ray‐finned fish appear to differ, developmental studies have shown that the basic structures are topologically similar and the developmental control mechanisms are conserved (Medina & Abellán,  ; Mueller & Wullimann,  ).
The pallium is responsible for processing various sensations and motor functions. During mammalian evolution, it dramatically expanded to form the cerebral cortex, with a six‐layered structure that includes the neocortex. The neocortex is tangentially organized into “areas,” defined as functionally unique subdivisions that differ in terms of cytoarchitecture and neuronal connections. The subpallium gives rise to the basal ganglia, in which the main components are the striatum and pallidum. In teleosts, cortex formation is not evident; instead, the pallium is anatomically divided into the central zone (Dc), medial zone (Dm), lateral zone (Dl), and posterior zone (Dp) (Mueller & Wullimann,  ). The ventral nucleus (Vv) and dorsal nucleus (Vd) are the two major brain nuclei that constitute the subpallium (Mueller & Wullimann,  ). The authors of a recent study proposed that the Dm, Dl/Dp, and Dc are the homologs of the mammalian amygdala, hippocampus, and neocortex, respectively (Diotel et al.,  ).
Thus far, the genetic mechanisms underlying telencephalon development have been extensively examined only in mice. Development of the pallium depends on the emx genes, whereas subpallium formation is governed by foxg1. Secreted factors play pivotal roles in the dorsoventral patterning of the telencephalon: ventral development is governed by Sonic hedgehog (Shh) and Fgf8, emanating from the ventroanterior portion, whereas dorsal patterning depends on Wnt and BMPs that emanate from the dorsal signaling centers (Hébert & Fishell,  ). In mammals, subsequent arealization of the cerebral cortex depends on similar signaling molecules. Two major patterning centers are the anterior neural ridge (ANR), which secretes Fgf8, and the cortical hem, which expresses Bmp and Wnt. Under the influence of these morphogens, four transcription factors—Emx2, Pax6, Nr2f1, and Sp8—exhibit graded expression patterns in the cortex, thereby specifying area identities (O'Leary & Sahara,  ). Sp8 and Pax6 are expressed in the rostral area, contributing to the identity of the anterior region, whereas Nr2f1 and Emx2 are expressed in the posterior region. ANR‐derived Fgf8 represses caudal genes, such as Nr2f1; this repression leads to suppression of FGF signaling. Nr2f1 then inhibits Sp8 and Pax6 expression to allow proper regionalized growth and the acquisition of the caudal identity (Bertacchi, Parisot, & Studer,  ; O'Leary & Sahara,  ).
Thus, nr2f1 genes are positioned at the top of the hierarchy that governs early cortical area patterning (Armentano et al.,  ; Bertacchi, Parisot, & Studer,  ), although detailed information about these genes remains limited. Nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F (Nr2f) proteins are transcription factors; nr2f genes are conserved in all metazoans, including vertebrates and invertebrates (Coppola & Waxman,  ). Four nr2fs (nr2f1, nr2f2, nr2f5, and nr2f6) have been identified in vertebrates, among which three are present in mammals (nr2f1, nr2f2, and nr2f6). Nr2f1 and Nr2f2 (previously known as COUP‐TF1 and COUP‐TF2, respectively) were initially implicated in the regulation of other nuclear receptor family transcription factors; they were subsequently implicated in several crucial biological processes, including neurogenesis, organogenesis, cell fate determination, and metabolic homeostasis (Mangelsdorf et al.,  ; Qiu et al.,  ; Qiu et al.,  ; Wang et al.,  ).
The expression of nr2f genes has been detected in the early embryos of many vertebrates, including zebrafish, in association with functions other than cortical arealization (Aranguren et al.,  ; Fjose et al.,  ; Love & Prince,  ; Lutz et al.,  ; Qiu et al.,  ). The general expression patterns are similar across species. Because nr2f genes are expressed in restricted regions of the CNS, their involvement in the regionalization of these brain structures is plausible. Several organs also show high levels of nr2f1 and nr2f2 expression, with overlapping but unique patterns (Love & Prince,  ; Pereira et al.,  ; Qiu et al.,  ). In mice, there is extensive overlap concerning the expression patterns of Nr2f1 and Nr2f2. Both genes are initially expressed in the brain around E8.5 and then in the telencephalon, preoptic area (POA), ganglionic eminences, and scattered cells in the subpallium at E13.5 (Qiu et al.,  ; Tripodi et al.,  ).
Extensive data regarding Nr2f proteins have been accumulated, although not fully understood, in terms of their biochemical activities, their cellular location, and the dynamics governing their functions. Nr2f proteins are considered orphan receptors (Alfano et al.,  ) that are able to repress or activate gene expression (Cooney et al.,  ; Kliewer et al.,  ; Tran et al.,  ). Transcriptional regulation by Nr2fs differs according to the DNA binding site of the target genes, the cellular context, and, most importantly, the presence of specific co‐activators or co‐repressors. In addition, numerous studies have demonstrated the interaction of nr2f genes with retinoids (Qiu et al.,  ; Zhuang & Gudas,  ), as well as with mitogen‐activated protein kinase/protein kinase C signaling pathways (Gay et al.,  ).
In mice with a conditional knockout (cKO) of Nr2f1, frontal/motor regions developed, whereas the development of primary sensory areas was significantly reduced (Armentano et al.,  ); these results indicated that Nr2f1 is required for arealization of the cerebral cortex, as noted above. Double conditional inactivation of Nr2f1 and Nr2f2 led to severe defects in the olfactory bulb, suggesting genetic compensation in this structure (Zhou et al.,  ). In humans, mutations in NR2F1 lead to Bosch–Boonstra–Schaaf optic atrophy (BBSOA) syndrome, an autosomal dominant disease (Bertacchi, Parisot, & Studer,  ). However, single KO of either Nr2f1 or Nr2f2 genes in mice does not affect early brain development despite the extensive expression of these genes in the neural tube (Pereira et al.,  ; Qiu et al.,  ).
Although considerable knowledge has been accumulated concerning the molecular mechanisms and roles of nr2fs in later brain development, the roles of these genes in early brain development have not been elucidated, probably due to species‐specific differences, the extensive gene duplication associated with resultant functional redundancy, and the distinct developmental contexts. In this study, these complications were avoided by using the zebrafish as the model organism; previous studies of the molecular mechanism of telencephalon development in this species confirmed the importance of the regulatory genes identified in mice. Zebrafish have six nr2f genes (nr2f1a, nr2f1b, nr2f2, nr2f5, nr2f6a, and nr2f6b), presumably because of the three whole‐genome duplication events that occurred in the teleost lineage, leading to two copies of nr2f1 and two of nr2f6 (Bertrand et al.,  ). The expression patterns of nr2f1a, nr2f1b, and nr2f2, and nr2f5 in the developing brain, including the telencephalon, after early somite stages have been described (Fjose et al.,  ; Fjose et al.,  ; Love & Prince,  ). However, in non‐mammalian vertebrates, the cerebral cortex is absent, and even its prototype has not been identified. Accordingly, the roles of nr2f genes in telencephalon development in teleosts remain to be elucidated.
The aim of this study was to obtain a better understanding of the regulatory network involving nr2f1a, nr2f1b, and nr2f2 in the early development of the zebrafish brain. The study utilized loss‐of‐function (LoF) experiments, in which nr2f genes were disrupted by the CRISPR/Cas9 method, and overexpression experiments involving nr2f1 mRNA injection. The results revealed that nr2f genes play roles in early forebrain regionalization and eye development, as was predicted from their expression patterns.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Fish stock and maintenance
The adult zebrafish (Danio rerio) were maintained at 27°C under a 14h/10 h light/dark photoperiod. Embryos were raised at 23°C, 25°C, or 28.5°C until they reached appropriate developmental stages. Morphological characteristics and the time after fertilization (given in hours postfertilization [hpf] or days postfertilization [dpf]) were used to stage embryos (Kimmel et al.,  ). To inhibit pigment synthesis, we used 0.2 mM 1‐phenyl‐2‐thiourea (Nacalai Tesque). The Committee for Animal Care and Use at Saitama University approved all study protocols using live fish.

2.2.

## Whole‐mount in situ hybridization
Digoxigenin (DIG)‐labeled RNA probes were synthesized using T3 and T7 RNA polymerases (Stratagene) with DIG RNA Labeling Mix (Roche Diagnostic) according to the manufacturers' protocols. Whole‐mount in situ hybridization (WISH) was performed essentially as described previously (Kikuta et al.,  ).

2.3.

## Disruption of 
nr2f
 genes by the CRISPR/Cas9 method
gRNAs against the N‐terminal region or functional domains  in nr2f gene products (cf. Figure  )   were designed using CRISPRdirect (https://crispr.dbcls.jp/) and synthesized using the CUGA7 gRNA Synthesis Kit (Nippon Gene) (Yuikawa et al., ). gRNAs were co‐injected with Cas9 protein (Nippon Gene) into fertilized eggs. The target sequences were disrupted and the mutants were genotyped by the heteroduplex mobility assay (HMA) method (Ota et al.,  ) (Table  ). The sequences at the target sites were analyzed to finally determine insertion/deletion (indel) structures.
 
2.4.

## Microscopic observation
A fluorescence stereomicroscope (Leica, MZFLIII) was used to observe live and stained embryos, and the images were captured by a cooled CCD camera (DFC 300 FX, Application Suite 3.3.1, Leica).

2.5.

## Image analysis
The sizes of the brain and eyes were measured using the line tool of ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Statistical analyses of the data, including two‐sample t‐tests, were conducted using Excel (Microsoft).

2.6.

## 
mRNA synthesis and microinjection into embryos
For synthesis of capped mRNA, the template plasmids were linearized with appropriate restriction enzymes and transcribed with SP6 RNA polymerase using the MEGAscript SP6 Kit (Ambion) according to the manufacturer's protocol (Kikuta et al.,  ). Synthesized mRNA was pressure‐injected into single blastomeres of one‐ to four‐cell stage embryos. egfp mRNA was used as a negative control.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## Expression patterns of 
nr2f1a
, 
nr2f1b
, and 
nr2f2
 during early brain development
Previous studies of zebrafish nr2f genes showed that nr2f1a, nr2f1b, and nr2f2 are expressed in the brain, including the telencephalon; in contrast, nr2f5 is expressed early in the neural plate, subsequently undergoing downregulation in the forebrain and midbrain. In addition, all of them are expressed in the eye primordium (Fjose et al.,  ; Fjose et al.,  ; Love & Prince,  ). These expression patterns of nr2f1a, nr2f1b, and nr2f2 were confirmed in the current study, in which the focus was on the telencephalon (Figure  ).
 Expression of all examined nr2f genes was initiated by 14 hpf in two broad domains of the neural plate: the anterior domain (i.e., forebrain and midbrain) and the posterior domain, which corresponds to the hindbrain; there was a gap around the midbrain–hindbrain boundary (MHB), as previously described (Love & Prince,  ). nr2f1a was expressed at 16 hpf in the diencephalon, anterior midbrain, and hindbrain, but not in the telencephalon. In the ventricular zone (VZ) of the telencephalon, nr2f1a expression was first detected faintly at 18 hpf but then intensely from 24 to 72 hpf. The expression of nr2f1b was initiated in the VZ of the telencephalon by 16 hpf, with a more distinct expression pattern by 18 hpf. By 24 hpf, nr2f1b expression was reduced in the telencephalon; it was no longer detectable after 2 dpf. nr2f2 expression in the forebrain, anterior midbrain, and hindbrain increased from 14 to 18 hpf and became visible in the VZ of the telencephalon at 24 hpf. Between 48 hpf and 72 hpf, nr2f2 expression in the VZ gradually decreased. These observations are consistent with previous observations (Love & Prince,  ) and further reveal similarities and differences in the expression patterns of the three nr2f genes in the telencephalon. Thus, in the VZ, nr2f1a is expressed after 18 hpf, nr2f1b is expressed from 16 to 24 hpf, and nr2f2 is expressed after 24 hpf (Figure  ). Notably, no caudally biased expression pattern was observed, in contrast to the Nr2f1 expression characteristics during cortical patterning in mice.
The expression patterns of nr2f genes in the eyes were subjected to further examination. At 18 hpf, nr2f1a and nr2f1b were weakly expressed throughout the optic vesicle (OV), whereas nr2f2 expression was detected in its posterior domain (Figure  ). At 24 hpf, nr2f1a was expressed in the posterior domain, nr2f1b was expressed in the anterior domain, and nr2f2 was strongly expressed in the posterior‐most domain. Therefore, in the OV, the three nr2f genes are expressed in a spatially complementary manner.

3.2.

## Establishment of 
nr2f
 mutant lines
Zebrafish carrying nr2f gene mutations were generated in previous studies (Barske et al.,  ; Dohn et al.,  ; Duong et al.,  ), although the respective mutations were not examined for their effects on brain development. In the present study, the three nr2f genes were disrupted using the CRISPR/Cas9 method (Figure  ). In the nr2f1a mutant (nr2f1a
∆4), a 4‐bp deletion was detected downstream of the first zinc finger domain, resulting in a truncated protein that lacked the second zinc finger and the ligand‐binding domain (Figure  ). In the nr2f1b mutant, a 14‐bp deletion in the first zinc finger domain resulted in a mutant gene (nr2f1b
Δ14) encoding the N‐terminal 14 amino acids (Figure  ). In the nr2f2 mutant, an octamer sequence (GAGCGGAG) was substituted by a 4‐mer (CCAA) (nr2f2
∆4; Figure  ); the product was expected to include 194 amino acids from the wild‐type (WT) sequence but not most of the ligand‐binding domain. Thus, all three mutations were expected to be null.

3.3.

## Morphological analyses of 
nr2f
 mutant embryos
Mutant embryos obtained by heterozygotic crosses were morphologically assessed, imaged, and genotyped (Figures  , , and ). Embryos obtained by crossing nr2f1a heterozygotes (nr2f1a

+/−
) were examined from 24 hpf to 5 dpf (Figure  ). At 24 hpf, the telencephalon was significantly thinner in homozygous mutants than in WT embryos (Figure  ). Thereafter, the telencephalon became progressively more hypomorphic and the eyes became smaller (Figure  , additional data not shown). The sizes of the brain and eye were measured from 1 to 5 dpf using ImageJ software (Figure  ; Figure  ). At 1 dpf, the telencephalon was significantly thinner in homozygous mutants (Figure  ). The length of the head from the anterior tip to the caudal margin of the OV was also measured (Figure  ), along with OV diameter (Figure  ). At 24 hpf, there were no significant differences in eye and brain sizes; both structures gradually became smaller in homozygous mutants from 3 dpf onward. In contrast, no brain anomalies were detected in heterozygous mutants. Notably, cardiac edema was substantial at all examined stages, consistent with the findings of knockdown (KD) studies (Figure  ) (Wu et al.,  ). Finally, homozygous mutants died after 7 dpf, whereas heterozygotes grew to maturity (Table  ).
 The morphologies of the nr2f1b mutants and the nr2f2 mutants were similarly analyzed (Figures  , , and ). At 24 hpf, embryos with these mutations showed no obvious differences in their telencephalon and head; at 3 and 4 dpf, homozygous mutants had a slightly smaller brain and smaller eyes compared with WT embryos. Homozygotes of these two mutant lines died after 12 dpf, whereas heterozygotes grew normally until the adult stage (Table  ).
To assess the effects of functional redundancy, zebrafish embryos obtained by crosses of double heterozygotes (nr2f1a

+/−
;nr2f1b

+/−
) were morphologically examined (Figure  ). At 24 hpf, single nr2f1a

−/−
 homozygotes had a thinner telencephalon, whereas no obvious defects were detected in single nr2f1b

−/−
 homozygotes, consistent with the above description (Figure  ). At this stage, the telencephalon of double homozygotes (nr2f1a

−/−
;nr2f1b

−/−
) was thin, resembling the telencephalon of the nr2f1a

−/−
 single mutants; these findings indicated no significant synergy of the two mutations (Figure  ). Similarly, no synergy was observed in double homozygous mutants at 3 dpf (Figure  ).

3.4.

## Cell death is enhanced in 
nr2f1/2
 mutant embryos
The potential contribution of cell death to morphological defects in nr2f mutants was explored by acridine orange (AO) staining. Offspring embryos from crosses of heterozygous nr2f mutants were stained from 24 hpf to 3 dpf. Dying cells were only sparsely observed in the heads of WT and heterozygous embryos at all stages (Figure  ). In nr2f1a homozygous mutants, in contrast, extensive cell death in the telencephalon, midbrain, hindbrain, and eyes was observed at 24 hpf (Figure  ). At 3 dpf, cell death remained readily detectable, but it was restricted to the eyes and optic tectum (Figure  ). In nr2f1b homozygous mutants, cell death was observed at 24 hpf in the hindbrain and eyes, but it was restricted to the eyes and optic tectum at 3 dpf (Figure  ). Therefore, abnormal cell death occurred in the brain and eyes of nr2f1b mutants, although the extent was reduced compared with nr2f1a homozygous mutants. In nr2f2 mutants, cell death was substantial only in the eyes, not in the brain (Figure  ).
 
3.5.

## Molecular phenotype in the brain and eyes of 
nr2f
 mutants
Offspring embryos obtained by heterozygotic mating were examined at 24 hpf for the expression of brain regional markers via WISH, followed by imaging of the staining patterns and genotyping. The focus was on the forebrain, including the telencephalon and eyes. In all cases, heterozygotes displayed normal expression patterns.
In nr2f1a homozygous mutants (Figure  , cf. Figure  ), the expression patterns of foxg1a and sp8b in the telencephalon (Kawakami et al.,  ) were nearly normal (Figure  ); emx3 expression in the pallium (Viktorin et al.,  ) was downregulated in the ventroanterior region and retreated dorsally; dlx2a expression in the subpallium and prethalamus (Akimenko et al.,  ) was expanded both dorsally and ventrally toward the POA; and nkx2.1 expression in the POA (Manoli & Driever,  ) was reduced (Figure  ). Thus, in nr2f1a mutants, global telencephalon regionalization was marginally affected, except in the POA‐related region, which was extensively affected.
 In nr2f1a homozygous mutants, the expression levels of pax6a in the telencephalon, diencephalon, and eyes (Puschel et al.,  ) and the expression level of pax2a in the optic stalk (Krauss et al.,  ) were significantly reduced (Figure  ). Downregulation of vax1 was observed in the POA, hypothalamus, and ventral retina; downregulation of vax2 was observed in the anterior telencephalon, POA, hypothalamus, retina, and optic stalk (Take‐uchi et al.,  ) (Figure  ). The expression of six3b in the anterior‐most telencephalon (Kobayashi et al.,  ) was slightly expanded in the ventral direction, resulting in ectopic expression in the POA (Figure  ). Thus, the development of the eyes, POA, and hypothalamus was altered in nr2f1a mutants.
The expression patterns of fgf8a and shha were also examined. fgf8a, a pivotal gene in vertebrate brain development (Reifers et al.,  ; Shinya et al.,  ), was expressed in the anterior‐most telencephalon, dorsal diencephalon, and MHB in WT embryos. In nr2f1a mutants, fgf8a expression was reduced in all these domains (Figure  ). shha regulates dorsoventral patterning of the forebrain; in the mutants, its expression was downregulated in the hypothalamus, zona limitans intrathalamica (ZLI), and floor plate, and it was not detectable in the POA (Figure  ). Therefore, the absence of nr2f1a negatively affected two important signaling centers in the zebrafish brain.
The brain defects in nr2f1b mutants were relatively mild (Figure  , cf. Figure  ). Their assessment at the molecular level revealed normal expression of foxg1a, sp8b, and emx3 in the forebrain of homozygous mutants (Figure  ); dlx2a and nkx2.1 were upregulated in the POA (Figure  ); pax6a expression was significantly decreased in the forebrain and eyes, similar to nr2f1a mutants, and pax2a expression in the optic stalk was weakly reduced (Figure  ). The expression of vax1 was almost intact, but the expression of vax2 in the anterior telencephalon was slightly expanded ventrally, resulting in ectopic expression in the POA (Figure  ); six3b expression was essentially normal (Figure  ). Thus, the forebrain (including the telencephalon) and eyes were affected in nr2f1b mutants. Although the extent of severity was weak in the forebrain compared with nr2f1a mutants, these results still suggest the roles of nr2f1b in the development of the forebrain and eyes. In terms of the signaling molecule genes, both fgf8a and shha showed almost normal expression patterns (Figure  ), showing that their expression does not strongly depend on nr2f1b.
 
nr2f2 mutant embryos were similarly assessed for the expression of regional markers (Figure  ). In homozygous mutants, the normal expression patterns of foxg1a, sp8b, emx3, and dlx2a implied that global telencephalon regionalization was not significantly affected, as expected from minor defects in morphology and cell survival. However, pax6a expression in the telencephalon, diencephalon, and eyes was downregulated; vax1 expression was upregulated, and vax2 expression was weakly downregulated. Thus, the anterior‐most forebrain and eyes were weakly affected, despite the lack of morphological defects, suggesting the involvement of nr2f2 in brain development. Of note, the expression patterns of fgf8a and shha in nr2f2 mutants were nearly normal.
Offspring embryos obtained by mating double heterozygotes (nr2f1a

+/−
;nr2f1b

+/−
) were evaluated for the expression of brain regional markers at 24 hpf. Consistent with the morphological observations, there was no clear synergy between the two mutations regarding the expression patterns of foxg1a, sp8b, emx3, pax6a, and fgf8a (Figure  ).

3.6.

## Overexpression of 
nr2f1
 in embryos by mRNA injection
Analyses of the mutants revealed relatively mild brain phenotypes at the end of somitogenesis, which could be at least partially due to gene redundancy. In an alternative approach to address the functions of nr2f genes in early brain formation, nr2f1b was overexpressed by the injection of its mRNA into embryos. This nr2f gene is a close co‐ortholog of mammalian Nr2f1, together with nr2f1a, and was expected to represent at least nr2f1 genes (Coppola & Waxman,  ); thus, in the following text, it is simply referred to as nr2f1.
Embryos injected with low‐dose nr2f1 mRNA (10 pg/embryo) exhibited a deformed forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain, whereas the trunk was relatively unaffected. However, the injection of high‐dose mRNA (50 pg/embryo) resulted in loss of the forebrain and midbrain, a residual hindbrain, and a deformed trunk (Figure  ). The effects of overexpression were further assessed using regional markers (Figure  ). Complete loss of the forebrain in embryos injected with high‐dose nr2f1 was confirmed by the lack of expression of forebrain markers (foxg1a, sp8b, emx3, dlx2a, fgf8a, pax6a). The expression of foxg1a and dlx2b in the pharyngeal arches and fgf8a expression at the MHB were detected at the anterior end of the deformed embryo, confirming specific abrogation of the forebrain and midbrain. In embryos injected with low‐dose nr2f1 mRNA (Figure  ), despite the weak morphological defects in the brain, the expression patterns of most brain markers were relatively normal, except for caudal expansion of fgf8a within the telencephalon and downregulation of pax6a in the telencephalon and OV.

## DISCUSSION
4.1.

## Expression patterns of 
nr2f
 genes in zebrafish embryos
Studies of the expression patterns of nr2f genes in vertebrates, such as mice, chicks, Xenopus, and zebrafish, have suggested their diverse functions during development. In the present study, the expression patterns of the three nr2f genes, previously reported in the telencephalon after mid‐somite stages (Love & Prince,  ), were re‐examined with a focus on the forebrain and eyes. Our analyses revealed similar expression patterns among the three genes within the neural plate. Expression was initially detected at early somite stages broadly both anteriorly and posteriorly to the MHB. Subsequently, the anterior expression was gradually restricted within the forebrain. At 24 hpf, the genes were strongly expressed in the VZ of the telencephalon, diencephalon, anterior midbrain, hindbrain, and eyes; no transcripts were detected in the posterior midbrain.
Nonetheless, several differences in the expression patterns of the three genes were observed. Expression in the VZ of the telencephalon occurred after 18 hpf for nr2f1a and after 24 hpf for nr2f2; nr2f1b expression was restricted to a short period, from 16 to 24 hpf. The expression of nr2f1b was significantly weaker than the expression of nr2f1a/2; it was extensively downregulated by 72 hpf.
As discussed below, the phenotypes of the three nr2f gene mutants differed: the most severely altered phenotypes were exhibited by the nr2f1a mutants, followed by the nr2f1b mutants; nr2f2 mutants displayed only minor defects. These disparities may be explained by differences in the expression patterns of these genes in the telencephalon. Thus, compared with nr2f1a expression, nr2f1b expression was weaker but initiated earlier, whereas nr2f2 expression was similarly intense but delayed. The distinct mutant phenotypes may be related to the molecular activities of the three Nr2f proteins, although no significant biochemical differences have been reported thus far. Alternatively, local expression in the brain, such as in signaling centers, may display sufficient spatial restriction that escaped our expression analysis. Further expression analyses are needed to examine these possibilities. The involvement of nr2f5 in telencephalon development can be excluded because its expression in the forebrain and midbrain was downregulated after 16 hpf (Love & Prince,  ).
Another feature of nr2f genes is their expression in the developing eyes, reported in mice (Qiu et al.,  ; Tang et al.,  ) and previously briefly mentioned in zebrafish for nr2f1a, nr2f1b, nr2f2, and nr2f5 (Love & Prince,  ). The present study showed that nr2f1a, nr2f1b, and nr2f2 were broadly expressed in OVs at 18 hpf, but they exhibited highly restricted and mutually complementary patterns of expression by 24 hpf. The implications of these results for eye development are discussed below.

4.2.

## Functions of 
nr2f
 genes revealed in previous mutant analyses
In humans, NR2F1 mutations lead to neurodevelopmental disorders, collectively referred to as BBSOA syndrome (Bertacchi, Gruart, et al.,  ). Many of the symptoms of this syndrome, such as optic atrophy, optic disc anomalies, and intellectual disability, were reproduced in a mouse model (Bertacchi, Gruart, et al.,  ). The defective development of glossopharyngeal nerves in Nr2f1 null mutants demonstrated that Nr2f1 is required for the proper fetal development of neural cells, as well as postnatal survival (Qiu et al.,  ). LoF experiments in mice, including cKO, implied Nr2f1 involvement in neocortex patterning (Armentano et al.,  ). A previous study showed that angiogenesis and vascular remodeling were defective in Nr2f2 null mutants (Pereira et al.,  ). These findings highlighted the distinct roles of Nr2f genes in later development, when the two genes differ in terms of expression pattern. However, the expression patterns of nr2f genes during early embryonic development exhibit strong similarity and overlap, especially in brain primordia (Jonk et al.,  ). Thus, although nr2f genes likely play pivotal roles in early brain development, functional redundancy complicates their further analysis.
Previous studies of zebrafish revealed that nr2f genes participate in the development of the upper jaw, cardiomyocytes, and pharyngeal muscle (Barske et al.,  ; Dohn et al.,  ; Duong et al.,  ). Additionally, KD experiments demonstrated the involvement of nr2f1a in cardiac development (Wu et al.,  ). However, the roles of nr2f genes during brain development have not been demonstrated, probably because the similar expression patterns in early brain primordia hinder such studies.

4.3.

## Morphological defects of 
nr2f
 mutants generated by the CRISPR/Cas9 method
In the present study, the CRISPR/Cas9 method was used to establish nr2f1a, nr2f1b, and nr2f2 mutant lines. In these mutants, indels were introduced into the N‐terminal site (nr2f1b) or functional domains (nr2f1a, nr2f2), such that the results were probably null mutations, not dominant‐negative, although persistence of partial functions cannot be excluded at present. Morphological studies showed that, among the nr2f genes, disruption of nr2f1a elicited severe defects in the telencephalon and eyes in homozygous mutants. Both structures were nearly normal at 24 hpf, but they gradually became smaller than in WT and heterozygous embryos after 3 dpf. Additionally, substantial cardiac edema was observed, consistent with previous findings (Wu et al.,  ). In contrast, the size reductions were marginal in the telencephalon and eyes of the nr2f1b and nr2f2 mutants. Thus, despite the highly similar expression patterns of nr2f genes in the brain, only a mutation in nr2f1a had a conspicuous impact.
To the best of our knowledge, these observations provide the first genetic evidence of the functions of nr2f genes, especially nr2f1a, in the early development of the telencephalon and eyes. Intriguingly, morphological defects in the brains of nr2f1a and nr2f1b double mutants were never more severe than the defects observed in single mutants, with no synergistic effects. However, considering the conserved expression patterns, functional redundancy among nr2f genes is still probable; more thorough analyses with multiple mutants will be required. In both humans and mice, haploinsufficiency is a feature of nr2f mutations (Bertacchi et al.,  ; Bertacchi, Gruart, et al.,  ); however, it was not observed in our zebrafish analyses, including the analyses of cell death and molecular defects. This issue should also be addressed in future studies.

4.4.

## Nr2fs contribute to neural cell survival in the embryonic zebrafish brain
In the present study, AO staining revealed extensive cell death at 24 hpf in the telencephalon, midbrain, hindbrain, and eyes of homozygous nr2f1a mutants. Cell death was subsequently restricted to the optic tectum and eyes. In nr2f1b mutants, cell death was more restricted; it was detected in the eyes and hindbrain at 24 hpf and in the eyes and optic tectum at 3 dpf. In nr2f2 mutants, there was no obvious cell death in the telencephalon, but the degree of cell death was substantial in the eyes. Therefore, consistent with the observed morphological defects, cell death was most extensive in nr2f1a mutants, followed by nr2f1b mutants and nr2f2 mutants.
The broad expression of nr2f genes in the zebrafish brain and eyes paralleled the broad distribution of dying cells in the embryos. As apoptotic cells were detected in the telencephalon and OVs of the nr2f1a mutants beginning at 1 dpf and in the OVs of nr2f1b mutants and nr2f2 mutants at 3 dpf, the hypoplasia of the telencephalon and eyes can be at least partially attributed to cell death. In the nr2f1b and nr2f2 mutants, extensive cell death occurred in the eyes only at 3 dpf, which was not accompanied by a significant size reduction. Therefore, the prolonged enhancement of apoptosis alone likely contributed to reduced OV size. Intriguingly, in Nr2f1 null mutant mice, excessive cell death was observed along the routes of developing cranial nerve migration, suggesting that excessive precursor cell death causes a decrease in neuronal number in the glossopharyngeal ganglia (Qiu et al.,  ) and that the roles of Nr2fs in the maintenance of neural cells are conserved among vertebrates.
It is unclear why the absence of nr2f results in cell death. One possibility is that nr2f genes enable cells in the embryonic brain and/or eyes to respond to differentiation and/or survival signals. Alternatively, nr2f genes may be required for the expression of the genes involved in the maintenance of neural cells. Another possible mechanism is that cell death occurs due to abnormal cell differentiation and proliferation caused by the failure of regionalization that is discussed below. The underlying mechanism merits exploration in future studies. The possible involvement of nr2f genes in cell growth should also be addressed. In this regard, it is important to note that the cell cycle of neural progenitors in Nr2f1 mutant mice was accelerated due to the shortened G1 phase, which is induced by downregulation of the cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor P21 (Bertacchi et al.,  ), suggesting that Nr2fs control the cell cycle and the progenitor ratio.

4.5.

## 

nr2f
 genes regulate the transcription factor genes required for telencephalon development
In mice and zebrafish, emx and foxg1/foxg1a encode the major regulatory transcription factors needed for development of the pallium and subpallium, respectively. Their expression is governed by two secreted factors: Fgf8/Fgf8a, emanating from the anterior telencephalon, and Shh, emanating from the subpallium/POA and hypothalamus (ZLI) (Hébert & Fishell,  ). Arealization of the pallium (cerebral cortex) in mice is regulated by interactions among Emx2, Pax6, Nr2f1, and Sp8. Fgf8 represses Nr2f1, which then suppresses FGF signaling and regulates Sp8, Pax6, and Emx2; overall, this process allows proper regionalized growth and the acquisition of caudal identity (Bertacchi, Parisot, & Studer,  ; O'Leary et al.,  ).
Although a cortex‐like structure is not present in non‐mammalian vertebrates, the basic structure of the telencephalon is shared among all vertebrates, with similar regulatory networks in mice and zebrafish. The development of the subpallium, hypothalamus, and prethalamus is regulated by dlx2, vax1, and vax2, whereas the formation of the entire forebrain depends on six3/six3b and pax6/pax6a, among other regulatory factors. In the POA and hypothalamus, nkx2.1 is expressed; the development of the retina and optic stalk depends on pax6/pax6a and pax2/pax2a. However, knowledge is lacking regarding the roles of nr2f genes in this regulatory network in zebrafish. Thus, to evaluate telencephalon development in nr2f mutants at the molecular level, we examined the expression patterns of these genes in the current study at 24 hpf, which is later than the onset of expression of all three nr2f genes in the telencephalon (Table  ). The expression patterns of fgf8a and shha, pivotal regulators of brain development in zebrafish, were examined in parallel.
 Unexpectedly, we found that the telencephalon was not globally affected, even in nr2f1a homozygous mutants. The expression patterns of foxg1a and sp8a were nearly normal, and emx3 expression in the pallium slightly retreated dorsally. In contrast, the subpallium, POA, and anterior hypothalamus were locally but significantly affected, as indicated by the downregulation of nkx2.1, pax6a, six3b, vax1, and vax2, as well as the upregulation of dlx2a and six3b in the POA. Conspicuous defects were observed in the eyes; pax6a and pax2a were downregulated in the retina and optic stalk, respectively. In the anterior telencephalon and POA/hypothalamus, fgf8a and shha were downregulated, respectively. In nr2f1b and nr2f2 mutants, consistent with the weaker morphological changes, similar but much weaker defects in the expression of transcription factor genes were observed; both fgf8a and shha were seemingly normally expressed in the anterior forebrain.
The absence of global defects in the brain of single nr2f mutants can be attributed to functional redundancy among the nr2f genes, which exhibit similar and overlapping expression in the neural tube. However, in the present study, even nr2f1a/nr2f1b double mutants did not show significantly more severe phenotypes than single mutants; the possible functional redundancy must be addressed through methods such as the examination of multiple KO mutants (discussed above).
In contrast to the absence of global phenotypes, very localized defects were observed, particularly in the POA region, hypothalamus, and anterior‐most telencephalon, along with altered expression of both fgf8a and shha. The absence of the spatial and/or temporal expression of the nr2f genes presumably disrupted signaling centers in the brain, strongly in nr2f1a mutants and mildly in nr2f1b/nr2f2 mutants. Such differences could be attributed to the distinct expression patterns among the three zebrafish nr2f genes as discussed above. In mouse embryos, Nr2f1 and Fgf8 form a regulatory loop during telencephalon development (Bertacchi, Parisot, & Studer,  ; Hébert & Fishell,  ). Thus, disrupted signaling centers may adequately explain the telencephalon hypotrophy associated with apoptosis in nr2f1a mutants. Accordingly, Nr2f may regulate telencephalon development through associated signaling centers. Considering the absence of a caudally biased expression pattern in the telencephalon, zebrafish nr2f is not involved in anterocaudal patterning, which differs from cortical development in mice.
Previous studies and the present study showed broad expression of nr2f genes in the developing brain, suggesting their involvement in diverse aspects of brain development besides telencephalon formation, which was the main focus of our current study. Indeed, nr2f mutants, especially nr2f1a mutants, showed abnormal diencephalon development, such as dorsal and ventral expansion of dlx2a expression and downregulation of pax6a and shha in the diencephalon. Taking into consideration the ventrally biased defects in the telencephalon, we assume that at least one of the possible roles of nr2f may be regionalization, or dorsal‐ventral patterning in the forebrain, which did not seem global but deserves further evaluation.

4.6.

## 

nr2f
 genes are necessary for eye development
As demonstrated in this work and previous studies (Fjose et al.,  ; Fjose et al.,  ; Love & Prince,  ), all three nr2f genes were expressed in the OV, suggesting their involvement in eye development. Indeed, the eyes were significantly smaller in nr2f1a mutants and slightly smaller in nr2f1b/nr2f2 mutants. Consistent with these defects, cell death was considerably enhanced in the developing eye, accompanied by substantial downregulation of pax6a in the retina and pax2a in the optic stalk. vax1/2 expression patterns were also disturbed in the retina and optic stalk. As in zebrafish embryos, both Nr2f1 and Nr2f2 are strongly expressed by progenitor cells in the developing murine eye (Tang et al.,  ). In double cKO mouse embryos, the expression patterns of Pax6, Pax2, and Vax1/2 were altered, whereas in vitro studies showed that Pax6 is directly regulated by Nr2fs. Therefore, the involvement of nr2f in eye development appears to be conserved among vertebrates. However, a single Nr2f mutation did not affect eye development in mice; in contrast, the alterations in zebrafish led to eye defects in all nr2f mutants.
In this regard, it should be mentioned that all three nr2f genes were expressed in differently restricted small portions in the retina, which contrasts with the broad defects identified through morphological and molecular analyses. The complementary expression of the three nr2f genes suggests that they have spatially segregated functions in eye development, which may explain the defects in all nr2f mutants. As each nr2f gene is locally expressed but governs the formation of the entire eye, nr2f‐mediated regulation of eye development may be indirect and thus non‐cell‐autonomous, implying the involvement of secreted factors. Indeed, Shh signaling maintains the optic stalk–retinal interface through vax activity (Take‐uchi et al.,  ) and is required for the specification of temporal identity in the ventral OV (Hernández‐Bejarano et al.,  ). Shh also regulates the formation of the ventral optic cup by limiting Bmp4 expression in mice (Zhao et al.,  ). Finally, retinoic acid has been implicated in the development of the ventral retina in zebrafish (Marsh‐Armstrong et al.,  ).

4.7.

## Analyses of the roles of 
nr2f
 genes by mRNA‐mediated overexpression
Mutant analyses are powerful approaches to reveal the endogenous functions of genes, although the observed phenotypes do not directly represent the activities of the gene products. Moreover, multiple paralogs with overlapping expression patterns can hinder the analyses because of functional redundancy, as we have proposed for nr2f genes during the early phase of brain regionalization. In the present study, overexpression analyses were performed to avoid these limitations.
Among embryos injected with nr2f1b mRNA, extensive dose‐dependent morphological defects were observed at the middle somite stage (18 hpf). At a high dose, the head anterior to the MHB was completely absent; the trunk region was deformed but recognizable. The expression of emx3, foxg1a, and dlx2a in the forebrain was lost, whereas fgf8a expression at the MHB and dlx2a/foxg1a expression in the anterior pharyngeal arches were detected at the anterior‐most position in the embryo. Although mRNA‐mediated overexpression could affect early embryogenesis before neural development, these results still suggested that the anterior brain is highly susceptible to Nr2f, consistent with their strong expression in this region. Intriguingly, the data also suggested that the hindbrain and trunk region are not regulated by Nr2fs. As the MHB is determined by the negative interaction between otx2 and gbx at early stages (Kikuta et al.,  ; Nakamura,  ; Nakayama et al.,  ; Rhinn & Brand,  ), susceptibility may be controlled by these homeobox genes.
Under a low dose of nr2f1 mRNA, the anterior brain was present and brain regionalization was relatively normal at the molecular level. Nonetheless, regionalization was morphologically affected because the brain vesicles were recognizable with indistinct boundaries. In this context, the expression patterns of fgf8a and pax6a were specifically disrupted; fgf8a expression was caudally expanded within the telencephalon and pax6a was downregulated in the telencephalon and OVs. Thus, the same genes were affected in nr2f mutants and in nr2f1‐overexpressing embryos. However, in both mutants and mRNA‐injected embryos, the effects could not be attributed to direct transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, Nr2fs are presumed to serve as both repressors and activators (Park et al.,  ), depending on the context. To avoid artifactual early effects of mRNA injection, we recently established a transgenic line in which nr2f1b could be induced by heat shock. In this model as well, the expression patterns of pax6a and fgf8a were affected by nr2f1b in the telencephalon and eyes (unpublished data). Overall, these results provide further evidence of interactions among nr2f, fgf8a, and pax6a during development of the telencephalon and eyes.
