## Abstract
The vast majority of cancer-related deaths are attributed to metastasis. The lung, being a common site for cancer metastasis, is highly prone to being a target for multiple cancer types and causes a heavy disease burden. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that tumor metastasis necessitates continuous interactions between tumor cells and distant metastatic niches. Nevertheless, a comprehensive elucidation of the underlying mechanisms governing lung-specific metastasis still poses a formidable challenge. In this review, we depict the lung susceptibility and the molecular profiles of tumors with the potential for lung metastasis. Under the conceptual framework of “Reciprocal Tumor-Lung Metastatic Symbiosis” (RTLMS), we mechanistically delineate the bidirectional regulatory dynamics and coevolutionary adaptation between tumor cells and distal pulmonary niches during lung-specific metastasis, including the induction of pre-metastatic-niches, positive responses of the lung, tumor colonization, dormancy, and reawakening. An enhanced understanding of the latest mechanisms is essential for developing targeted strategies to counteract lung-specific metastasis.
Keywords: Reciprocal Tumor-Lung Metastatic Symbiosis (RTLMS), Lung-specific metastasis, Coevolution, Pre-metastatic niche, Tumor colonization, Dormancy, Reawakening

## Introduction
Distant metastasis and relapse contribute significantly to high cancer-related mortality. Different cancer types display striking variability in organotropism. The lung is among the most common sites of metastasis for many malignancies, accounting for up to 54% of metastatic colonization events [ ]. The majority of lung metastases originate from extrathoracic primary tumors, including sarcomas, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), colorectal cancer (CRC), breast cancer (BC), melanoma, and prostate cancer (PCa) [ ]. Despite substantial efforts to investigate lung metastasis, clinically applicable prospective prediction models, tailored therapeutic approaches, and reliable prognostic evaluation systems remain to be developed. Therefore, conducting more in-depth, comprehensive, and rigorous synthesis and summarization of relevant research serves as a critical catalyst for accelerating the translation of scientific research findings into clinical practice.
The early hemodynamic or mechanical theory postulated that abundant blood flow and narrow capillaries are natural physical advantages facilitating lung metastasis [ ]. However, the non-random distribution of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) suggested that metastasis arises from mutual selection between tumor cells and distant organs instead of passive transport [ , ]. According to the "seed and soil" hypothesis, tumor cells (seeds) endowed with potent invasiveness and adaptability, along with a supportable organ microenvironment (fertile soil), are the prerequisites for distant metastasis [ ]. The mutual selection is achieved through intricate regulations, such as tumor cells inducing pre-metastatic niche (PMN) to create a favorable colonization environment [ ]. In recent years, in-depth studies have further unveiled the dynamic and continuous interaction between tumor cells and the lung microenvironment during metastasis. It cannot be ignored that the lung, as a receiver, is also actively involved in recruiting circulating tumor cells (CTCs), promoting tumor colonization, and inducing or reawakening dormant DTCs [ ]. The deepening understanding of crosstalk between tumor cells and lung microenvironment is conducive to developing more effective targeted therapies that can disrupt the metastatic cascade.
In this review, we delineate the disease burden and susceptibility of the lung as a metastatic destination, coupled with the genetic and molecular characteristics of tumor cells possessing lung metastatic potential. Subsequently, we focus on the latest advances regarding the intricate interplay between the distant environment and tumor cells during lung-specific metastasis. This includes the multiple regulatory mechanisms concerning how primary tumors induce the lung pre-metastatic niche (LPMN) and the corresponding active responses from the lung. We also systematically summarize how DTCs colonize, enter dormancy, and reactivate with the help of the lung microenvironment. Based on these studies, we propose a conceptual framework of "Reciprocal Tumor-Lung Metastatic Symbiosis" (RTLMS) that is analogous to the concept of ecology, namely tumor cells achieve "parasitic adaptation" by remodeling the LPMN, while lung-derived chemokines (e.g., CXCL12) actively recruit CTCs to form a mutually beneficial pathological synergy. In other words, we describe how tumor cells and distal lungs choose, recognize, and cooperate reciprocally from the perspective of coevolution.

## Lung as a metastatic destination

## The disease burden of lung metastasis
The lung represents a predominant metastatic target organ across diverse malignancies, serving as a prioritized site for distant colonization due to its unique microenvironmental niche. Retrospective cohort analyses show that among patients with metastatic disease, lung metastasis is diagnosed in 75–85% of osteosarcoma, 45.2% of RCC, 39.5% of HCC, 31.7% of CRC, 21–32% of BC, and 5% of melanoma patients (Fig.  ) [ – ]. An autopsy study of 1,589 PCa patients revealed that 46% had lung metastases in terminal-stage disease (Fig.  ) [ ]. While these epidemiological patterns highlight the metastatic vulnerability of the lung, molecular subtyping reveals different organotropism. For instance, luminal/human HER2 and HER2-enriched tumors were associated with a significantly higher rate of lung metastasis when compared with luminal A tumors [ ].
 Clinically, metastatic dissemination drives > 90% of cancer-related mortality, with pulmonary lesions contributing to 30% of these fatalities [ , ]. Compared with patients without lung involvement, the occurrence of lung metastasis significantly reduces the survival of patients and increases the risk of death. For example, CRC patients with pulmonary metastases demonstrate a median survival of merely 10 months, while osteosarcoma patients harboring lung lesions face a 2.34-fold elevated mortality risk versus non-metastatic counterparts [ , ]. These survival disparities, compounded by diagnostic challenges and limited therapeutic efficacy against established pulmonary metastases, underscore the urgent need for mechanistic investigations into lung-specific colonization pathways to inform next-generation interception strategies.

## Susceptibility of the lung
The lungs, while convoying for gas exchange and pathogen defense through robust immune surveillance, paradoxically exhibit obvious susceptibility to metastatic colonization. This vulnerability stems from their unique anatomical and physiological features: the thin blood-air barrier, though advantageous for respiratory efficiency, creates structural fragility that facilitates CTC infiltration. Abundant blood flow and intricate capillary networks further predispose pulmonary vasculature to CTC entrapment [ ]. Thus, the adaptations enable pulmonary homeostasis to become exploitable weaknesses in tumors.
Accumulating evidence implicates that pulmonary inflammation and immune dysregulation, driven synergistically by intrinsic and extrinsic vulnerabilities, are critical factors of lung metastasis [ , ]. In addition to the intrinsic physiological structure, the susceptibility is also reflected in its vulnerability to external damage, especially with a special genetic background. Direct environmental exposure renders it particularly susceptible to exogenous damage. The resultant inflammation and immune imbalance create opportunities for tumor cells. Inhalational toxins (e.g., cigarette-derived nicotine, tar, carbon monoxide, and heavy metals) elevate lung oxidative stress and permeability, promote ferroptosis and autophagy-dependent senescence of lung epithelial cells, elicit the recruitment of a large number of inflammatory cells, and ultimately establish a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, disrupt the normal structure and immune balance, and increase the risk of lung metastasis [ – ]. The smoking-associated chronic inflammation could induce squamous metaplasia and facilitate oncogenesis and metastasis based on the accumulation of genetic changes such as TP53 and AKR1B10 [ – ].
Pathogen-driven mechanisms similarly deteriorate pulmonary immunity. Bacterial infections trigger neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation via autophagy-dependent pathways and release inflammatory mediators that enhance CTC homing [ , ]. C-reactive protein (CRP) from the acute inflammatory response inhibits the activation of macrophages, thereby compromising immune surveillance [ ]. Allergic pulmonary inflammation-induced endothelial activation promotes CTC extravasation, a necessary event in lung metastasis [ ]. These pathological states recapitulate key features of the PMN—neutrophil infiltration, macrophage polarization toward immunosuppressive phenotypes, and vascular remodeling—that collectively prime the lung microenvironment for metastatic colonization [ ].
Systemic host factors further modulate pulmonary metastatic risk through microenvironmental reshaping. A high-fat diet, obesity, chronic psychological stress, and radiotherapy may also augment lung metastasis via neutrophil-mediated inflammatory cascades [ – ]. Intriguingly, the lower airway microbiome has emerged as a novel immunomodulator that correlates with progression and prognosis in lung cancer [ , ]. While preliminary evidence suggests microbiota may influence pulmonary susceptibility, mechanistic validation remains pending.
The overall status of distant metastatic organs is crucial for tumor cell dissemination and metastatic colonization. The multifactorial framework of pulmonary susceptibility integrates anatomical vulnerability, genetic predisposition, exogenous stimulations, and systemic modulation (Fig.  ). In view of these factors, it is reasonable that the lung has become the dominant site for distant colonization.
 

## The intrinsic determinants of lung metastatic potential in tumor cells
While microenvironmental factors influence metastatic organotropism, tumor cell-autonomous genomic and molecular features critically dictate lung colonization capacity. Key mediators include driver genes and surface receptor repertoires that confer invasive and adaptive phenotypes.

## Genes associated with lung metastasis
The clinical convergence of disparate cancers at common metastatic sites has catalyzed efforts to define the genomic alterations enabling organotropic colonization. Multi-region sequencing technologies now permit high-resolution mapping of intratumoral heterogeneity, uncovering metastatic subclones with distinct driver mutation profiles and evolutionary trajectories [ – ]. Nevertheless, a critical knowledge gap persists in identifying genomic determinants that fundamentally rewire cancer cell plasticity to confer organ-specific metastatic fitness.
Gene alterations driving lung metastasis have been identified across diverse cancer types. A panel of genes mediating breast cancer lung metastasis has been identified, including growth and survival factors (e.g., the HER/ErbB receptor ligand epiregulin), chemokines, cell adhesion receptors (e.g., ROBO1), and extracellular proteases (e.g., MMP1) [ ]. In addition, the KRAS and PIK3 CA mutations are associated with a higher cumulative incidence of CRC lung metastasis [ ]. PTEN deletion and conditional activation of the RAS/MAPK leads to lung macrometastasis with 100% penetrance in PCa [ ]. The chromosomal translocation-derived aberrant Rab22a drives lung-specific dissemination of osteosarcoma [ ]. To explore the tumor-gene-metastasis associations, Nguyen et al. analyzed a pan-cancer cohort of 25,775 patients with various primary and metastatic lesions, obtaining the tumor-gene-metastasis spectrum [ ]. Among them, they identified some significant positive associations between tumor genomic alterations and lung tropism, such as a higher frequency of APC mutations, CTNNB1 mutations, or WNT pathway alterations in PCa, a higher frequency of ERBB2 amplification in esophageal cancer, and KRAS mutations in microsatellite stable CRC.
In addition to the correlations between the genomic alterations and the occurrence of lung metastasis, elucidating functional genomics is crucial for designing accurate and effective targeted drugs to inhibit metastasis. TP53 is a well-known tumor suppressive gene and is associated with tumor metastasis [ ]. The p53-R172H cooperates with Brd4 to drive Csf- 1 transcription, facilitating esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) lung metastasis via epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) induction [ ]. PCSK9-V474I variant enhances metastatic outgrowth through low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1)-mediated repression of XAF1/USP18 tumor suppressors [ ]. Despite progress, the functional hierarchy and tumor-type specificity of identified lung-tropism genes remain unresolved.
The genes governing organ tropism of tumor cells to the lung could serve as predictors for the potential risk of lung metastasis. For example, a 6-gene classifier (DSC2, TFCP2L1, UGT8, ITGB8, ANP32E, FERMT1) stratifies breast cancer patients by pulmonary metastatic risk [ ]. Nevertheless, current oncology lacks definitive pan-cancer biomarkers predictive of organotropic metastasis. Pleiotropic genes like SNAI1, TWIST1 (EMT regulators), and MYC (proliferation amplifier) promote metastasis through generic mechanisms rather than organ-specific targeting [ , ]. Emerging evidence from large-scale next-generation sequencing (NGS) reveals that metastatic clones can co-opt host-derived transcriptional programs to develop site-specific phenotypic adaptations [ , ]. This paradigm-shifting discovery may prove foundational for developing tissue-selective therapeutic strategies targeting these acquired molecular vulnerabilities.
Metastasis consists of a stepwise sequence of events mediated by diverse categories of metastasis-associated genes [ ]. Genomic instability under microenvironmental pressure generates adaptive clones and stimulates tumor evolution, which exactly explains why the heterogeneity of CTCs affects the organotropism of tumor cells [ , ]. Organ-specific metastasis-related genes in primary tumors and CTCs could act as markers for predicting specific metastasis and recurrence. A better knowledge of the function of these genes could expedite the development of targeted therapeutics to prevent such poor prognosis. However, Notably, transient “metastasis-initiating” mutations may epigenetically reset upon niche colonization, complicating therapeutic target identification [ ]. Thus, it is necessary to: 1) establish temporal genomic mapping through longitudinal tracking of primary tumors, CTCs, and metastases to delineate phase-specific vulnerabilities; 2) resolve niche-specific gene expression programs in micrometastases; 3) identify mutations locked in metastatic lineages despite microenvironmental reprogramming. Then, the prognostic biomarkers of lung metastatic risk and precision interception targeting colonization checkpoints are expected to be achieved. Moreover, extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) is pervasive in human cancer and confers competitive advantages during cancer progression and metastasis [ , ]. Understanding and evaluating the role of dynamic mutations and ecDNA in organ-specific metastasis will be another research emphasis in future studies.

## Molecular determinants of lung metastatic tropism
The organ-specific dissemination of tumor cells represents a highly selective process, with surface molecule-mediated directional migration constituting a molecular basis. Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), encompassing transmembrane and extracellular matrix (ECM)-associated proteins, orchestrate critical intercellular and cell–matrix interactions, which is essential for metastatic progression [ ]. Substantial experimental evidence confirms their roles in facilitating tumor cell adhesion, motility, and intracellular signaling cascades that drive metastatic colonization [ ]. Heterogeneous molecular signatures predispose CTCs to be captured by organ-specific microvessels, establishing distinct metastatic patterns. Here, we summarize key lung metastasis-associated surface molecules, including endothelial cell adhesion molecules (ECAMs), selectins, integrins, immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF) adhesion proteins, and atypical membrane receptors (Table  ).

 As hematogenous transmission predominates in lung metastasis, vascular endothelial surface proteins critically mediate metastatic tropism [ , ]. Early in the 1990s, the lung endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (Lu-ECAM-1) was identified as a melanoma-specific pulmonary homing factor [ , ]. The anti-Lu-ECAM-1 monoclonal antibody led to a 90% reduction in lung colonies with no effect on the lung metastasis of other cancer types.
Selectins are a family of mammalian lectins that mediate cell-adhesion reactions among leukocytes, endothelial cells (ECs), and platelets. As anticipated, the selectin–ligand axes are involved in tumor metastasis [ ]. E-selectin expressed in LPMN can preferentially facilitate the shear-resistant adhesion and transendothelial migration of BC cells via CD44 binding [ ]. Moreover, chondroitin sulfate glycosaminoglycans on BC cells can function as P-selectin ligands, potentiating 4T1 cell pulmonary colonization [ ].
Integrins are another cell adhesion molecule family mediating cell-cell and cell-ECM interactions [ ]. Integrins typically consist of two subunits and are extensively involved in metastasis through various combinations, such as αvβ3, α5β1, α3β1, α4β1, α9β1, etc. A previous study has reviewed the role of integrins in organ-specific metastasis of human malignant melanoma, indicating that melanoma cells expressing integrin β3 tend to metastasize to the lungs [ ]. Integrin beta (1) endows breast cancer cells with the ability to trigger adhesion-related signaling events, thereby promoting the initial proliferation of diffuse micrometastatic cancer cells in the lungs [ ]. Integrins can be expressed not only in the lung endothelium but also in tumor cells to guide lung metastasis. For example, both the pulmonary endothelial α4β1 and BC cell α5β1 integrins support the lung colonization [ – ]. In addition, IgSF3, as a homophilic cell adhesion molecule, enhances the adhesion of B16F10 cells to vascular ECs [ ].
These findings illustrate that tumor cells make use of physiological adhesion systems for metastatic dissemination. Among that, adhesion molecules facilitating lung-specific metastasis predominantly localize on the ECs, underscoring the strategic significance of vascular-targeted therapies. Notably, atypical adhesion mechanisms have been identified to mediate lung-specific metastasis, including a yet-uncharacterized lung sialoadhesive receptor interacting with RCC disialosyl galactosylgloboside, and ephrinB2 on lung ECs interacting with melanoma tyrosine kinase receptor EphB4 [ , ].
The organotropic migration of tumor cells involves not only adhesive molecular interactions but also coordinated chemokine receptor signaling networks (refer to the “  ” section). Paradoxically, many pulmonary metastasis-associated molecules exhibit multi-organ expression, suggesting context-dependent regulation of ligand-receptor pairing during metastatic progression. This underscores the necessity to investigate spatiotemporal regulation of adhesion/chemokine systems and tumor cell phenotypic plasticity in establishing organ-specific metastatic niches. Future research should elucidate microenvironmental reprogramming events enabling tumor cells to exploit physiological molecules for metastatic advantages.

## Tacit cooperation between tumor and lung metastasis microenvironment
The tumor metastatic dissemination adheres to the Darwinian "survival of the fittest" principle, necessitating adaptive coevolution between tumor cells and metastatic niches to achieve successful colonization. As the RTLMS model shows, this reciprocal relationship involves tumor-induced PMN, subsequent niche-mediated directional migration of CTCs, and synergistic colonization (Fig.  ). Upon pulmonary infiltration, CTCs either proliferate within the LPMN or enter dormancy, determined by microenvironmental permissiveness. Such dynamic tumor-microenvironment interplay drives metastatic coevolution through bidirectional molecular signaling.
 

## Induce the lung pre-metastatic niche
The earliest interaction between tumor cells and distant microenvironment is the release of PMN signals. The tumor cells secrete tumor-derived secretory factors (TDSFs) and extracellular vesicles (EVs) to induce PMN formation, creating a more conducive environment for metastasis and colonization [ ]. Liu and Cao have summarized the six indispensable characteristics of PMN, including immunosuppression, inflammation, angiogenesis/vascular permeability, lymphangiogenesis, organotropism, and reprogramming [ ]. These characteristics have also been observed in lung-specific metastasis induced by the PMN signals.

## Extracellular vesicles
Extensive studies on PMN induction have focused on the contributions of EVs to the remodeling of distant metastatic niches. EVs are nanoscale membranous vesicular structures secreted by cells into the extracellular environment [ ]. EVs can be classified into three subtypes: microvesicles, apoptotic bodies or apoptosomes, and exosomes based on their size distribution and biogenesis mechanism [ ]. EVs contain diverse functional molecules, including proteins, lipids, and genetic material, and play a crucial role in intercellular communication [ ]. EVs, as messengers, are extensively involved in various physiological and pathological processes, including the cooperation and communication between the primary tumor sites and the secondary metastatic sites [ , ].
EVs encapsulate a variety of proteins, including integrins and carcinogenic proteins. The integrin repertoire of tumor-derived exosomes dictates the organ-specific adhesion and can redirect the metastatic destination of tumor cells [ ]. Among them, the exosomal integrins α6β4 and α6β1 direct pulmonary adhesion. In another study, salivary adenoid cystic carcinoma (SACC)-derived α2β1 integrin-containing EVs activate lung fibroblasts to remodel the LPMN [ ]. Exosomal LAP-TGF-β1 can reconfigure the pulmonary vascular niche at lower doses, facilitating the extravasation and colonization of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells in the lung. Moreover, EVs can deliver PD-L1 to the lung tissue-resident interstitial macrophages and upregulate the PD-L1 expression through metabolic reprogramming to induce an immunosuppressive LPMN [ ]. The uptake of tumor-derived microparticles triggers transcriptional reprogramming in macrophages within the early metastatic lung [ ]. This reprogramming renders spatiotemporal metabolic alterations, which in turn drive a functional transition of macrophages from an antitumor state to a protumor state.
Nucleic acids are another class of exosomal molecules with important regulatory functions in inducing LPMN. Lin28B derived from BC exosomes stimulates lung fibroblasts, neutrophils, and macrophages to release CXCLs, IL-6, and IL-10, respectively, resulting in neutrophil recruitment and N2 transformation, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment conducive to tumor metastasis [ ]. Small nuclear RNA (snRNA) derived from primary tumor exosomes can activate Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) and the downstream signaling pathways in lung epithelial cells, recruiting neutrophils and creating an inflammatory microenvironment [ ]. The long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) MRPL23 antisense RNA 1 (MRPL23-AS1) is highly expressed in SACC patients and correlated with lung metastasis [ ]. Further mechanism research revealed that MRPL23-AS1 accelerates lung metastasis by enhancing lung EC permeability. BC-secreted miR-122 inhibits glucose uptake of non-tumor cells in the PMN by downregulating the glycolytic enzyme pyruvate kinase, leading to systemic energy metabolism reprogramming and promoting metastasis [ ].
The emerging role of EVs in orchestrating the LPMN presents both challenges and opportunities for clinical and scientific advancements. Harnessing EVs as diagnostic tools and therapeutic vectors holds immense potential [ – ]. EV-based liquid biopsy platforms could detect LPMN-associated protein/RNA signatures for early metastasis monitoring. Engineered EVs loaded with anti-integrins, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or metabolic modulators may disrupt PMN formation, reverse immunosuppression, and prevent lung colonization. Critical knowledge gaps persist in EV heterogeneity, organotropic targeting mechanisms, and spatiotemporal regulation of PMN evolution. Advanced methodologies, such as single-vesicle proteomics, spatial transcriptomics, and multi-omics integration, are needed to decode EV subpopulation dynamics and their microenvironmental reprogramming cascades. Elucidating these mechanisms will enable precision interception of premeditated metastasis.

## Tumor-derived secretory factors
Tumor cells also establish LPMN through a repertoire of secreted bioactive molecules. Lysyl oxidase like 2 (LOXL2) secreted by HCC cells forms the LPMN through synergistic mechanisms [ ]. On the one hand, LOXL2 upregulates MMP9 and fibronectin in lung fibroblasts, driving ECM reorganization. Concurrently, it induces CXCL12 expression in fibroblasts, enhancing tumor cell migration, survival, and niche tropism. On the other hand, LOXL2 recruits bone marrow-derived cells (BMDCs) to the lung, where they collaborate with resident stromal cells to further modify the ECM architecture [ ]. Osteosarcoma-secreted EFEMP1 similarly primes the LPMN by activating fibroblast-mediated ECM remodeling and neutrophil-driven inflammatory conditioning [ ]. Tumor-secreted protease cathepsin C (CTSC) activates neutrophil membrane-bound proteinase 3 (PR3), leading to the promotion of IL-1β processing and NF-κB activation, thereby enhancing the secretion of IL-6 and CCL3 for neutrophil recruitment. Then, the CTSC-PR3-IL-1β axis induces the NET formation to degrade the metastasis-suppressive ECM protein thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) [ ]. BC-derived IL-6 activates STAT3 in lung lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), upregulating CCL5 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) to promote tumor cell recruitment, extravasation, and colonization [ ]. BC-secreted CCL2 recruits CCR2+ inflammatory monocytes to the LPMN, fostering metastatic progression [ ]. TNBC-produced CXCL1/2/8 induces lung fibroblast-derived CCL2/7, which in turn drives cholesterol synthesis in metastatic cells and enhances vascularization at lung lesions [ ]. Granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) from BC cells upregulates aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)-dependent PD-L1 expression in macrophages, promoting Treg differentiation and immunosuppressive microenvironment [ ]. Tumor-derived CXCL13 reprograms interstitial macrophages to secrete integrin αXβ2-enriched EVs, which interact with platelet-bound glycoprotein (GP)Ib to potentiate thrombosis-metastasis coupling [ ].
Beyond tumor-autonomous mechanisms, primary TME components contribute to LPMN formation. For example, CYP4A10high tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) at the primary tumor release VEGF, stromal-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), and TGF-β to precondition the lung for metastasis [ ]. Surprisingly, tumor cells even carry over fibroblasts from the primary lesion to the secondary organs, which may support the angiogenesis of metastatic sites [ ]. In summary, the primary tumors release TDSFs, EVs, or directly carry stromal components to establish LPMN that are conducive to tumor metastasis. Therapeutic targeting of these pathways may disrupt LPMN maturation and improve metastatic control. Therapeutic inhibition of LPMN induction may be achieved through combinatorial strategies: pharmacological blockade of EV biogenesis, neutralizing antibodies against LPMN-inducing molecules, and stromal remodeling agents synergized with immune checkpoint blockade. However, clinical translation requires meticulous consideration of the PMN spatiotemporal dynamics, particularly its temporal evolution and spatial heterogeneity, to optimize precision therapeutic timing and localization.

## The positive responses of the lung
The destination of tumor metastasis is not random but is determined by organotropism mediated by various mechanisms. Tumor cells produce a variety of signaling molecules, aiming to create a conducive microenvironment in the target organ before metastasis. However, these signals can travel with the circulatory system and reach different tissues, not only confined to the lungs. Thus, the release of PMN signals alone cannot guarantee organ-specific metastasis. It is noteworthy that the lung, frequently underappreciated, is also of significant importance in tumor organ-specific colonization as an active participant. After receiving the metastatic signals, the permissive lung, which has the potential to allow tumor cells to colonize and grow, will facilitate and support the directional migration of tumor cells as a response and feedback to the primary tumor.
When tumor cells enter the circulatory system and start their travels, the lung also actively attracts tumor cells. Homeostatic chemokines, as "cellular highways", navigate CTCs to specific organs [ ]. Lung-derived chemokines that establish chemokine gradients attracting CTCs are summarized in Table  . BMDCs recruited to LPMN enhance the MMP- 9 expression, which modifies the microenvironment and increases the secretion of SDF-1, creating a chemokine gradient to attract CXCR4+ CTC incorporation [ ]. Similarly, the lung-derived factors osteopontin and selectins guide CD44+ BC cells to migrate toward the LPMN through the interaction between soluble organ-derived factors and their receptors [ , ]. Furthermore, activated cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in LPMN secrete TGF-β, which augments the metastatic potential of osteosarcoma cells by inducing collagen type VI alpha 1 (COL6A1) and EMT [ ]. The primed LECs in LPMN accelerate tumor cell recruitment, extravasation, and colonization through the CCL5-CCR5 axis [ ]. The growth factors and chemokines from LPMN promote EMT and enhance the migratory ability as a response to primary tumor cells.

 Metabolic alterations and adaptations are manifested throughout the entire process of tumor metastasis, including CTCs [ ]. Elevated serum pyruvate and lactate concentrations exhibit significant predictive value in various metastatic cancers [ – ]. A study on key metabolic transcripts demonstrates that elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity serves as a critical predictor of metastasis to the lung rather than other organs, proposing that lactate production may represent a conserved feature of pulmonary metastasis [ ]. Pyruvate and lactate metabolism in CTCs has been demonstrated to confer resistance to reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced death in the circulation [ ]. In addition to supporting the survival of CTCs, these metabolites could promote tumor cell migration and metastasis as well. Bonuccelli et al. found that 3-hydroxy-butyrate and L-lactate can stimulate BC cell migration [ ]. More importantly, L-lactate increases the formation of lung metastases by about tenfold in nude mice. Furthermore, Christen et al. reported that the pyruvate levels in the lung interstitial fluid were higher than those in blood plasma [ ].
Based on the above studies, we raised several questions worth considering: Are these high concentrations of metabolites in the lungs and blood causative drivers or consequences of tumor metastasis? Do they induce adaptive metabolic rewiring in CTCs? If so, does the high concentration of pyruvate in the lung attract tumor cell colonization by triggering the advantage-seeking effect of tumor cells? Further exploration and research are needed to elucidate the effects and underlying mechanisms in the future.
Distant organs are not merely passive recipients but active responders subjected to selection pressure and undergoing specific modifications. Tumor cells induce the LPMN formation, in turn, the lung produces cytokines and chemoattractants to enhance the metastatic potential of tumor cells and guide their directional migration. Iterative signal exchange between tumor cells and the lung microenvironment ensures guided navigation and optimal niche selection. This reciprocal paradigm underscores the role of the lung as a participant in its own colonization. Continued investigation is required to disentangle microenvironmental instruction from tumor cell adaptation.

## Tumor colonization
Tumor cells undergo clonal selection under microenvironmental pressures, acquiring EMT phenotypes and cancer stemness properties—dual hallmarks of metastatic competence [ , ]. EMT facilitates detachment from primary lesions and circulatory intravasation, while stemness endows self-renewal capacity and resistance to therapeutic insults. These two capabilities complement each other and promote survival, metastasis, and distant colonization of tumors [ , ]. However, only a small fraction of CTCs survive to become DTCs capable of secondary organ colonization [ , ]. In this context, the coordination between the tumor cell adaptation and primed LPMN-derived support is a prerequisite for DTC colonization.

## Metastatic support from LPMN
ECs are the first cellular components that DTCs encounter within LPMN. The ECs have substantially lower expression of vein endothelial cadherin and higher expression of F-actin stress fibers. They are typical signs of weakened cell-cell junctions and impaired ECs that can enhance the paracellular permeability and facilitate the trans-endothelial migration of tumor cells [ ]. The dissociation of the cell-cell junctions in ECs is likely mediated by tumor-derived vascular permeability factors, such as SPARC and angiopoietin-like 4 [ – ]. The intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) on BC cells can facilitate the formation of CTC clusters and mediate the trans-endothelial migration of tumor cells, thereby motivating lung colonization [ ]. Moreover, perivascular macrophages activated by tenascin C (TNC) derived from BC cells can secrete nitric oxide (NO) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) to induce EC-mediated production of niche components, which in turn promote metastasis in mice by enhancing stem cell-like properties and the viability of cancer cells [ ]. Subsequently, nascent vessels at the metastatic lesion express vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), which recruits integrin α4β1 receptor positive bone marrow-derived endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) [ ]. The EPCs trigger an angiogenic switch to achieve the conversion from tumor micrometastases to macrometastases.
Neutrophils rank among the key contributors that are crucial in forming LPMN. The neutrophil-associated inflammatory microenvironment is a driving force of tumor initiation and progression [ ]. Recent investigations have identified the participation of neutrophils in pulmonary metastatic colonization. For example, neutrophils are recruited to form NETs, which degrade the metastasis-suppressive ECM protein TSP-1 and enable tumor cells to colonize [ ]. Furthermore, resident mesenchymal cells in LPMN induce neutrophils to substantially accumulate neutral lipids by inhibiting the activity of adipose triglyceride lipase. When metastatic colonization is established, neutrophils will supply the lipids to BC cells through a macropinocytosis-lysosome pathway and endow them with enhanced survival and proliferation capabilities [ ].
Macrophages are also one of the unique components that constitute the lung microenvironment. Alveolar macrophages (AMs) inhibit Th1 cell response by preventing the maturation of DC in the lung and regulating the secretion of immune cytokines while promoting Th2 cell response and down-regulating MHC II on tumor cells, thereby creating an immunosuppressive environment and assisting the lung metastasis of tumor cells [ ]. The reprogramming AMs upregulate genes involved in tissue remodeling and angiogenesis, which in turn promotes tumor cell invasion, angiogenesis, and colonization [ ]. Significantly, this process occurs independently of the adaptive immune system and is specific to the lung microenvironment. The VCAM-1/integrin α4β1-mediated adhesive interaction between TAMs and BC cells improves the survival capacity of metastatic BC cells during lung colonization, thereby facilitating tumor progression and metastatic dissemination [ ].
The tumor microenvironment harbors other key components that aid pulmonary metastasis and colonization in multiple ways. The metastatic HCC cell-derived EVs contain nidogen 1 and promote fibroblasts to secrete tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1), which further facilitates pulmonary colonization and enhances the growth and motility of HCC cells [ ]. Additionally, collagen-rich airway smooth muscle cells serve as a metastatic niche for bladder tumor cells expressing the collagen receptor CD167a, which activates the CD167a-HSP90-Stat3 axis, strengthens stemness, and promotes lung-specific colonization [ ]. Glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPX3)-positive AT2 cells secrete IL-10 to inhibit CD4+ T cell proliferation and enhance Treg generation, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment [ ].
In the LPMN, immune cells are predominantly in a suppressive state. High Chitinase-3-like-1 (Chi3l1) expression inhibits T cell activation, suppresses Th1 differentiation, and promotes Th2-related tumor-promoting effects, thus facilitating lung metastasis [ ]. It has been demonstrated that the activation of receptor DNAX accessory molecule-1 (DNAM- 1) by CD155 is involved in NK cell-mediated recognition and the effective initiation of cell-mediated tumor-specific immunity [ , ]. However, B16/BL6 melanoma cells produce soluble CD155 to suppress the DNAM-1-mediated degranulation of NK cells against tumor cells, creating an immunosuppressive environment that facilitates lung colonization [ ]. The recruited Treg kills NK cells through beta-galactoside-binding protein, thereby protecting tumor cells from elimination [ ]. Moreover, the Treg-derived IL-10 and GzmB also promote lung metastasis by regulating immunosuppression [ , ].
Nutrient availability represents a pivotal determinant of a permissive environment enabling metastasizing [ ]. The type 2 alveolar epithelial cells (AT2) secrete palmitate lipids into LPMN, increasing nutrient availability for metastatic cells [ ]. Then, metastatic cancer cells obtain acetyl-CoA by CPT1a-dependent palmitate oxidation. This acetyl-CoA, in turn, serves as a substrate for the acetylation of p65 by KAT2a, thereby promoting NF-κB transcriptional activation to support metastatic growth. In addition, nutrients can act as signaling molecules involved in lung colonization. High concentrations of aspartate in lung interstitial fluid activate the ionotropic N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor and promote CREB-dependent expression of deoxyhypusine hydroxylase, which triggers a translational program centered on TGF-β signaling, enhancing collagen synthesis in DTCs and facilitating their colonization and survival [ ].

## Adaptation of DTCs
The remarkable plasticity of tumor cells enables adaptive changes that facilitate the rooting of seeds in fertile soil. The unique lung microenvironment, characterized by elevated oxidative stress from high oxygen tension and cytotoxic compounds, poses significant challenges for metastatic colonization [ ]. Consequently, metastatic outcomes also depend on the capacity to resist oxidative damage and reprogram metabolic pathways.
Previous investigations demonstrated that metastatic cells increase peroxidase reductase to reduce hydrogen peroxide by transmitting electrons and up-regulate the PGC-1α expression to activate antioxidant gene networks and enhance systemic metabolic adaptability [ – ]. The activation of RGCC/PLK1/AMPKα2 signaling axis enables pulmonary TNBC cells to enhance energy production and NADPH generation via amplified mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation [ ]. Transfer RNA-derived fragments (tRFs) represent a recently characterized class of small non-coding RNAs processed from mature or precursor transfer RNAs (tRNAs). Liu et al. demonstrated that cysteine tRNA fragment (5’-tRFCys) directly interacts with nucleolin, facilitating its oligomerization with bound metabolic transcripts Mthfd1 l and Pafah1b1 to form a higher-order ribonucleoprotein complex that stabilizes transcripts [ ]. The regulation of phosphatidylcholine synthesis and one-carbon metabolism mediated by Pafah1b1 and Mthfd1 l is essential for successful lung colonization and metastatic BC cell survival. Basnet and colleagues developed fluorouracil-labeled RNA sequencing (Flura-seq) for high-sensitivity in situ analysis of transcriptionally active genes in micrometastases [ ]. Their findings confirmed that pulmonary micrometastatic cells upregulate antioxidant genes, potentially enabling clinical detection of occult lesions and the development of eradication strategies before progression to treatment-resistant macrometastases. These findings highlight the pivotal role of metabolic plasticity in fulfilling the specialized bioenergetic requirements of metastatic colonization.
The crosstalk between DTCs and the lung microenvironment not only recruits cells that remodel the niche to better satisfy the growth demands of tumor cells but also sustains the activation of survival signals, thereby enhancing the ectopic proliferation of tumor cells. This mutually beneficial interaction is achieved through the support of components within the lung microenvironment and the highly adaptable nature of DTCs. However, this dynamic reciprocity highlights therapeutic vulnerabilities in metastatic adaptation, particularly antioxidant pathways and metabolic adaption. Targeting these mechanisms may disrupt the colonization before irreversible macrometastasis.

## Tumor dormancy and reawakening
Despite favorable conditions, tumor colonization is not always unimpeded. The incompatibility of metastatic sites hinders the engraftment of DTCs with limited capacity to adapt to a new niche. Subsequently, some quiescent tumor cells, characterized by cell-cycle arrest yet possessing potential proliferative capacity, have been identified as dormant cells [ ]. It is widely accepted that the dormant state is a protective mechanism of tumor cells in harsh environments, and the metastatic microenvironment substantially determines whether DTCs enter or exit dormancy [ , ].

## Dormancy-associated regulatory networks
The identification of dormancy-associated gene regulatory networks suggests that dormancy is a pre-existing and inherent property of cancer cells [ , ]. Overexpression of KISS1 and DEC2 can maintain metastatic dormancy of human melanoma and SACC cells in the lung, respectively [ , ]. The B3GALT6-heparan sulfate/HS6ST1-heparan 6-O-sulfation/FGF1-FGFR2 signaling axis plays an important role in the survival and recurrence of dormant BC cells [ ]. A high p38/ERK ratio or a reduced Wnt pathway is associated with tumor growth arrest and dormancy [ , ]. The orphan nuclear receptor NR2F1 is a critical node in dormancy induction and maintenance, which can be epigenetically regulated and further regulate dormancy-related genes (e.g., SOX9, RARβ) to induce the dormancy of HNSCC and PCa cells [ ]. In a subsequent study, the NR2F1-specific agonist C26 suppresses lung metastasis by inducing an NR2F1hi/p27hi/Ki67lo/p-S6lo dormancy profile in solitary DTCs [ ]. In disseminated BC stem-like cells, lncRNA NR2F1-AS1 promotes NR2F1 translation, which inhibits the ΔNp63 transcription, resulting in EMT, reduced tumorigenicity, and enhanced dormancy [ ]. Recently, another dormancy-related signaling regulatory network involving transcription factor ZFP281 has been uncovered [ ]. In early disseminated BC cells, FGF2 signaling induces the ZFP281 expression, which then induces TWIST1. TWIST1, in turn, is required to maintain ZFP281 expression for mesenchymal-like phenotypes, making DTCs more invasive. Meanwhile, ZFP281 can regulate cell cycle- and dormancy-related genes to maintain a slow-cycling and growth-arrested dormant phenotype [ ].
Dormancy induction is achieved by the interplay of key regulators, epigenetic modulation, and lncRNA coordination. The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) results of distally disseminated BC dormant cells validate the multi-gene synergistic regulatory mechanisms in dormancy [ ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to establish a metastatic niche-induced dormancy signal library encompassing various primary tumor types and secondary organs to explore the commonalities in the mechanisms of reprogramming tumor cells into dormancy.

## Dormancy-regulating from lung microenvironment
The initiation and maintenance of the DTC dormancy are inextricably linked to the contributions of the specific lung niche. Direct contact with the type 1 alveolar epithelial cells (AT1) cells activates the anti-growth and pro-survival signals [ ]. Bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) proteins from the lung matrix can inhibit the proliferation of DTCs and induce a dormant state [ ]. Low matrix adhesion and degradability create a supportive environment for DTC dormancy [ ]. This may be attributed to the loss of survival signals from the matrix in the non-permissive ECM. Upon weak integrin-mediated cell-matrix adhesions, increased autophagy and decreased estrogen receptor-α expression result in the dormant state and resistance to tamoxifen in BC cells [ ]. Tumor cells can even produce and assemble a fibronectin matrix through α5β1 integrin-mediated adhesion and rho-associated kinase (ROCK)-mediated cell tension to create a sustainable environment and enter long-term dormancy [ ]. Furthermore, when angiogenesis is suppressed, DTCs are more likely to enter a dormant state characterized by balanced proliferation and apoptosis [ , ]. Mechanically, the dormancy of BC cells is induced by TSP-1 released from pulmonary stable microvessels, which is absent at sprouting endothelial tips [ ]. These phenomena indicate that mature microvasculature confers tissue quiescence while sprouting endothelium promotes tissue growth, consistent with the adverse and favorable survival conditions of DTCs.
In-depth mechanical investigations have revealed that immune pressures constitute a crucial part of dormancy modulation. Ten years ago, T and NK cells were discovered to participate in dormancy induction of lung-metastatic tumor cells [ , ]. Recently, the new roles of immune components from LPMN in dormancy regulation have been gradually revealed. For example, the primary BC cells could prime a systemic CD39+PD-1+CD8+ T cell response, which induces cell cycle arrest of DTCs by releasing IFN-γ and TNF-α [ ]. Besides, high abundances of pulmonary N1 neutrophils (antitumor phenotype) and their chemoattractants (CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL5) have been verified to be necessary for exerting survival pressure and driving metastatic dormancy in multiple BC models [ ]. AMs constitute an innate immune barrier and can maintain disseminated BC cells in a dormant state through the interaction between TGF-β2 and TGF-βRIII receptors [ ].
New evidence has shown that drug therapy can regulate the dormancy of DTCs. For example, the DNA methylation inhibitor 5-azacytidine (AZA) and the retinoic acid receptor ligands all-trans retinoic acid (atRA) augment TGF-β-SMAD4 signaling and induce a dormancy-like gene expression program, which is distinct from spontaneous dormancy [ ]. The AZA + atRA reprogramming protocol inhibits metastatic outgrowth in the lungs, confirming the feasibility of therapies targeting dormant cancer cells. The micellar formulation of retinoic acid derivative fenretinide was designed and developed to treat metastatic BC [ ]. It inhibits both metastasis initiation and expansion by simultaneously inducing antiproliferative signals and inhibiting the mTOR pathway, thus serving as a chemopreventive agent and a dormancy-inducing treatment for BC. These drugs may provide long-term survival benefits to patients by maintaining the persistent dormancy of DTCs.

## Reawakening
The reawakening of DTCs is the primary cause of cancer recurrence and high lethality. Dormancy occurs when DTCs are trapped in unfavorable microenvironments. Conversely, appropriate conditions can reawaken them. Changes in the lung microenvironment are the key factors promoting the awakening and proliferation of DTCs.
Many mechanisms that promote the awakening of DTC are the reverse regulation or blockade of the dormancy-related mechanisms. For example, previous research has identified Coco, a secreted antagonist of TGF-β ligands, which can promote self-renewal and cause recurrence by inhibiting lung-derived BMP dormancy signaling [ ]. Disseminated BC cells evade the dormancy-induction effect of tissue-resident AMs by avoiding interaction and downregulating the TGF-β2 receptor [ ]. RCC with high lysosomal protein transmembrane 5 (LAPTM5) expression maintains the self-renewal of stem cell-like characteristics by blocking the organ-specific inhibitory signals from BMPs [ ]. Moreover, lung-derived S100A8/A9 recruits N2-polarized neutrophils (pro-tumor phenotype) to create an immunosuppressive microenvironment that is conducive to tumor proliferation, indicating that facilitating the N1 to N2 transformation of neutrophils could alleviate the immune pressure and enable tumor cells to escape the dormancy program [ ].
Recent studies have further revealed the mechanisms by which the lung microenvironment and tumor cells overcome dormancy, including the regulation of dormancy-reactivation signals and metabolic reprogramming. In the aged lung, fibroblasts secrete more sFRP1 to suppress the classical WNT signal pathway, promoting the efficient reactivation of dormant cells by regulating the AXL-MER axis [ ]. Stress-activated neutrophils release proinflammatory S100A8/A9 proteins, which activate myeloperoxidase and result in the accumulation of modified lipids [ ]. Then, the released lipids upregulate the fibroblast growth factor pathway in tumor cells and awaken dormant cells. Downregulation of Her2 in BC cells disrupts redox homeostasis and compensatorily activates the antioxidant transcription factor NRF2 [ ]. Then, NRF2 reestablishes redox homeostasis and induces metabolic reprogramming, enabling tumor cells to rely on glutaminase to maintain metabolism and promote recurrence.
Figure  displays the regulatory mechanisms involved in the dormancy and reawakening of DTCs under the mutual adaptation between tumor cells and the lung microenvironment. When the lung microenvironment is inhospitable for survival, it induces DTC dormancy to conserve energy. Conversely, when the microenvironment is supportive of proliferation, the suppression is overcome, and DTCs reawaken. During the long latency period, DTCs may further evolve to adapt to harsh lung environments until they are awakened and undergo uncontrolled growth. In addition, macrophages within the primary tumor activate dissemination and dormancy programs in DTCs, and this activation is not limited to distant metastasis [ ]. There remain numerous mysteries regarding the interaction between tumor cells and lung-specific microenvironments that merit further investigation.
 

## Conclusion and perspective
Current research increasingly indicates that lung-specific metastasis is a pre-planned process. Susceptible epigenetic states predetermine the active responses of tumor cells to niche derived Wnt signaling upon reaching the pulmonary vessels [ ]. It appears to be a specific metastasis model between tumor cells and lung microenvironment, which is gradually achieved through a series of regulatory mechanisms.
Tumor cells determine their metastatic destination and possess metastatic potential before metastasis occurs. However, current research on the early initiation of organ-specific metastatic processes is limited. It remains unclear when these changes commence and what primes them. The establishment of a gene atlas from the onset of tumorigenesis to organ-specific colonization is essential for revealing the relationships between genetic alterations at different time scales. It is worth noting that the spontaneous metastasis model should be preferably chosen to avoid overlooking the impact of primary tumors on distant metastasis. Tumor cells with a metastatic tendency and the shared mechanisms of organ-specific metastasis can be identified by analyzing the genomes of primary tumors and metastatic foci throughout the metastatic process. The scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and snATAC-seq will help to unravel the spatiotemporal space-cell type-gene networks driving lung-specific tumor metastasis and decode the epigenetic regulatory landscapes underlying organotropic colonization at the niche level. This will provide a basis for metastatic risk assessment, organ-targeted monitoring, and the prophylactic use of drugs in clinical practice.
Surprisingly, the cunning tumor cells possess a camouflage ability to mimic normal cells by expressing host gene expression profiles. Early research found that aggressive melanoma cells express endothelium-associated genes and form an ECM-rich vasculogenic-like network called vasculogenic mimicry [ ]. Recent evidence shows that metastatic tumors are more transcriptomically like their target tissues, suggesting their ability to mimic organ-specific microenvironmental components to gain support at metastatic sites [ ]. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals that the P3 subpopulation of CRC exhibits cholangiocyte-like transcriptional signatures and is enriched in primary CRC and liver metastases, suggesting their potential role in organ-specific metastasis to the liver [ ]. Longitudinal proteogenomic analysis reveals increased neuronal state and acquired neuronal and synaptic functions at recurrent glioblastoma [ ]. Through this phenotypic mimicry, tumor cells establish functional connections with neurons and integrate into neural networks, promoting uncontrolled proliferation while evading immune surveillance. Similarly, metastatic BC cells establish pseudo-tripartite synapses with peripheral nerves in the brain to activate and sustain NMDAR-dependent invasive growth [ ]. This undoubtedly brings new opportunities for the prediction of organ-specific metastasis, but it also becomes a talisman for the immune escape of metastatic tumor cells.
Given that tumors need to make long-term preparations to induce LPMN and respond to lung chemokines for organ-directional migration before lung colonization, this provides a good opportunity for us to intervene. Valuable patient population samples are obtained by profiling paired EVs, secretomes, and metabolomes from patient-derived primary tumors, blood, and lung metastases (via organoid models or fresh biopsies). Integrate multi-omic profiling to identify the source and gradients of major metastasis-related chemotactic substances, validate key molecules that promote lung-specific metastasis, and establish a multi-dimensional liquid biopsy assessment model with high sensitivity and specificity. Due to the critical role of time in metastatic progression, longitudinal monitoring over prolonged durations is essential. Based on these results, precise inhibitor or targeted nanodelivery carriers that block the LPMN induction of the primary tumor and reverse the organ-specific immune microenvironment could be developed.
For patients with established lung metastases or asymptomatic high-risk individuals, the dynamic state of the lung microenvironment, particularly metastasis-associated niches, warrants prioritized attention. During lung colonization, dormancy, and reawakening, bidirectional crosstalk between lung microenvironment components (e.g., ECs and resident macrophages) and tumor cells influence metastatic outcomes. Clinically, spatial transcriptomic sequencing of fine needle aspiration biopsy samples from lung metastases enables the mapping of spatial coordinates and cellular states between resident lung cells and metastatic tumor cells and reveals niche-specific interactions. 3D cell co-culture platforms (for example, organ-on-a-chip models) provide in vitro systems that can model complex cellular interactions [ ]. These microphysiological systems become powerful and efficient tools for validating interactions within organ-specific settings. By integrating spatial location, gene expression profiles, and clinical data, novel multimodal deep-learning models could be developed to classify metastatic microenvironments into different subtypes with distinct prognostic risk levels. And then implement targeted interventions against key lung microenvironment components, such as disrupting NETs to reverse immunosuppression and promoting T cell-driven dormancy maintenance.
The newly emerging regulators of cancer progression indicate that tumor metastatic mechanisms are far more complex than we realize. The nervous system is widely involved in the initiation, invasion, extravasation, colonization, and immune evasion of tumor cells by releasing neurotransmitters and neuropeptides [ , ]. Tumor-resident intracellular bacteria promote CTC survival by enhancing their resistance to mechanical stress [ ]. However, the relatively insufficient recognition of neural regulation and intracellular microbes has hampered the thorough understanding of their roles in lung-specific metastasis. In the future, it is necessary to further investigate the interactions between tumor cells and distant lung niches to provide more evidence for the RTLMS model and develop targeted therapies to prevent metastasis.
