## Abstract
Overtreatment of patients with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is driven by a lack of a reliable prognostic biomarker. Widespread mammographic screening has resulted in a substantial increase in women diagnosed with DCIS. To improve patient risk stratification, we investigate circulating tumor cells (CTCs) as a biomarker for DCIS patients’ biological aggressiveness and as an indicator of early dissemination. We apply microfluidics to enrich CTCs from 34 patients with DCIS and find a significantly higher concentration of CTCs compared to in healthy controls. We profile CTCs and matched DCIS tissues using single-cell RNA sequencing. We find that CTCs express higher clonal aberrations when compared to white blood cells from the same samples, and clonal comparisons between matched tissue and CTC samples provide evidence for an evolutionary bottleneck model. mRNA expression in CTC reveals EMT/MET and immunoregulatory pathway regulation with a suggestion of racial differences. Last, we provide support for early dissemination in DCIS using a Mouse IntraDuctal model.

DCIS circulating tumor cells suggest early dissemination of cancerous cells that are probed for the risk of future breast cancer.

## INTRODUCTION
In 2022, more than 2.3 million women were diagnosed with breast cancer worldwide ( ), and it was the second-leading cause of cancer-related death for women in the United States ( ). Because of raised awareness of the importance of early detection in survival after a breast cancer diagnosis, routine screening mammography has been implemented. With improvements in mammography screening technology, a higher incidence rate of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is being detected ( ). DCIS is the nonobligate precursor to breast cancer that now comprises 20 to 25% of all breast cancer diagnoses ( , ). DCIS is regarded as a “stage 0” carcinoma by clinicians and widely labeled as precancerous. However, 10 to 53% of patients with DCIS progress from having an in situ carcinoma to invasive disease when left untreated ( , ).
Progression from in situ to distant/invasive carcinoma is associated with a poorer prognosis for patients with DCIS. The 5-year survival rate drops from 98% for patients with DCIS to 30% with the transition to distant breast cancer ( , , ). To improve patient outcomes, clinicians use a thorough treatment regime. Now, to prevent progression in patients with DCIS, 50% of patients receive lumpectomy with radiation, 20% receive unilateral mastectomy or lumpectomy without radiation, 5% opt for no surgery ( , ), and, in the case of hormone receptor–positive DCIS, patients receive additional antihormonal therapy. While more extensive treatments can reduce local recurrence, the additional radiotherapy and antihormonal therapy have not resulted in survival benefits in patients with DCIS ( ). Hence, the overarching question becomes, how do we identify patients that are at high risk of progression and in need of additional treatment?
Recent studies have found evidence of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in patients with DCIS which further complicates the timeline of carcinogenesis. Two seminal studies have found evidence of DTCs in patients with DCIS. In the first study, Sänger et al. ( ) analyzed the bone marrow aspirates of patients with DCIS taken at the time of primary surgery. In 4 of 19 cases of pure DCIS and 4 of 7 cases of DCIS with microinvasion, DTCs were detected using an immunoassay with established monoclonal anticytokeratin antibodies. In the second study, DTCs were detected via immunocytochemistry [anti–pan-cytokeratin (panCK)] in the bone marrow aspirates of 63 of 404 patients with DCIS ( ). These studies suggest an alternate hypothesis to early dissemination that this paper further examines: Subclones of cancerous cells can disseminate from the primary tumor site at earlier stages without clinically apparent invasion, priming a secondary tumor site while the primary site progresses toward localized invasion.
DTCs in patients with DCIS challenge the traditional definition of the preinvasive carcinoma but open the doorway to the analysis of the precursing cancerous cells, circulating tumor cells (CTCs). Early disseminated cancer cells are capable of intravasating into blood vessels from the primary tumor site and entering circulation as CTCs. A portion of CTCs can extravasate from the blood vessels to a secondary, distant site as DTCs ( ). Studying at a single-cell level both the primary DCIS lesion and the matched CTCs provides a unique opportunity to study the clonal origin of these cells and contribute to the growing bank of knowledge about breast cancer carcinogenesis. Furthermore, studying CTCs provides the opportunity to identify patients with DCIS at risk of future metastatic disease and poor survival who may benefit from systemic therapies such as adjuvant endocrine therapy. Ultimately, this assay could aid with patient risk stratification and targeting the overarching issues of improving patient outcomes while decreasing overtreatment.
Progression is inherently difficult to study in patients with DCIS, because patients can take a period of 10 or more years to progress to invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) ( ) or metastatic disease. Hence, it is difficult to monitor the outcomes of a patient cohort over a typical study period of a couple years. The Mouse-INtraDuctal (MIND) models innovated by Behbod’s group at the University of Kansas Medical Center (KUMC) created a platform for the study of progression of DCIS by injecting human-derived cancer cells into the mammary ducts of immunocompromised mice ( ). In a previous study, Behbod’s group reported that among 37 patient samples injected into 202 xenografts, 54% showed in vivo progression over a median timeline of 9 months, while 46% of samples remained noninvasive ( ).
Here, we report a study of 34 patients with DCIS assessing CTCs at diagnosis, evidence of early dissemination in the MIND models, as well as comparative clonal characteristics from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the CTCs and matched formalin fixation-paraffin embedding (FFPE) tissues. These comparisons have not previously been reported and provide a unique opportunity to explore CTCs as a prognostic biomarker in DCIS.

## RESULTS

## Unbiased CTC isolation using the Labyrinth from patients with DCIS
CTCs were enriched from a cohort of 34 patients with DCIS using the previously developed microfluidic Labyrinth technology ( ). The device exploits the size and rigidity differences of cancer cells and white blood cells to enrich CTCs into an outlet separate from 95 to 99% of white blood cells ( ). Enrichment based on physical characteristics is suitable for cancerous cells across a spectrum of phenotypes ( , ) as the technique is antigen independent. It should be noted, however, that some smaller CTCs part of the fraction not enriched may be missed from analysis. In accordance with our approved Institutional Review Board (IRB), in this study, blood samples were collected from patients with DCIS at the KUMC before they underwent surgery ( ). These blood samples were then shipped overnight to the University of Michigan in specialized CellRescue tubes for the preservation of DNA, RNA, and maximum stability of intact CTCs. Upon receipt, samples were processed on the Labyrinth. The enriched CTCs from each sample were enumerated using immunofluorescence labeling, and the remaining portion of the enriched CTCs was further characterized at the single-cell level using the 10x Genomics Flex protocol ( ).
 In parallel, a tissue sample was acquired from the patients during surgery. This tissue sample was processed for two downstream workflows. In the first, an FFPE tissue scroll of 25-μm thickness was acquired after a pathologist checked for sections with ≥5% tumor cells and was processed for single-cell analysis via the 10x Genomics Flex protocol. In the second, epithelial cells derived from the tissue sample were injected into the mammary ducts of mice to form the Mouse INtraDuctal (MIND) models ( ). Following implantation of cells, blood samples were collected from MIND models at 4, 6, and 12 months after the development of the model. These blood samples were shipped to the University of Michigan in 2.0-ml EDTA tubes and processed on the Labyrinth. Enriched samples underwent immunofluorescence labeling for CTC enumeration.
Our study involves 34 patients with DCIS with a median age of 61 years. The pathologist also determined the DCIS grade for each patient, and 18 patients were grade 2 or below while 16 patients were grades 2 and 3 or grade 3 ( ). Most patients were deemed to have pure DCIS, while five had simultaneous IDC, and three had simultaneous microinvasion. Patients also differed in terms of hormone receptor status with 30 patients being hormone-receptor positive, three human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-enriched, and four presenting HER2- DCIS. Racial demographics also differed between the patients with 6 Black patients, 1 Hispanic/Latino patient, and 27 white patients. Demographic information about the healthy controls is present in the Supplementary Materials (table S1).
 

## The presence and the frequency of CTCs per milliliter of blood differentiates between patients with DCIS and controls
CTCs were successfully enriched from blood samples from patients with DCIS and healthy controls ( ). The enriched CTCs were enumerated using an immunofluorescence staining panel. CTCs were characterized as cells with positive 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), panCK, and negative CD45 signals ( ). CTCs were present in 76% (26 of 34) of the patients with DCIS after accounting for false positives from the staining panel and only counting patients with counts one SD (±1.24 CTCs per milliliter of blood) above the mean of the healthy controls (1.20 CTCs per milliliter of blood). Previous studies have demonstrated the presence of epithelial cells in healthy controls ( – ), and a study has shown the presence of rare aneuploid epithelial cells in healthy breast tissue as a function of age ( ). In addition, patients with DCIS (n = 34, median of 6.1 CTCs per milliliter of blood) had significantly more (P < 0.0001) CTCs per milliliter of blood versus healthy controls (n = 10, median of 0.92 CTCs per milliliter of blood) ( ). Using a receiver operating curve, the metric of CTCs per milliliter of blood can differentiate between patients with DCIS and healthy controls with both high specificity and high sensitivity (area under the curve = 0.9097) ( ). Notably, when the number of CTCs was compared across racial demographics in the patient cohort, it was found that the Black patients (n = 6, median of 16 CTCs per milliliter of blood) had significantly more (P = 0.0263) CTCs per milliliter of blood than the white patients (n = 28, median = 5 CTCs per milliliter of blood) ( ).
 

## Single-cell analysis of matched tissue samples reveals distinct epithelial subtypes
To contextualize the plethora of information derived from hundreds of single cancerous cells in circulation, matched tissue samples from six patients (DCIS 4, DCIS 12, DCIS 14, DCIS 15, DCIS 17, and DCIS 25) were also processed for scRNA-seq ( ). When the single-cell transcriptomic data were integrated together using canonical correlation analysis (CCA) integration, many distinct stromal, epithelial, and immune cell populations were revealed ( ) across all the DCIS tissues. Using preestablished subtypes of epithelial cells in the adult human breast (Supplementary Text) ( ), two clusters of luminal hormone-responsive epithelial cells (LumHR), one cluster of basal epithelial cells, and one cluster of luminal secretory cells (LumSec) were identified. Differential gene expression analysis between the epithelial clusters revealed the top markers that defined each epithelial cluster within this dataset ( ). The contribution of each sequenced sample/patient to each cluster was also projected onto the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to visualize the primary subtype of epithelial cells for each patient ( ).
 Notably, when conducting differential gene expression analysis between the epithelial cell clusters, the second cluster denoted as “LumHR2” was the cluster of epithelial cells that expressed ERBB2, high BCL9, FOXA1, CEACAM6, NQO1, XBP1, SCL7A11, and ZNF281 with no expression of ESR1 and NR2F1. Many of these genes are implicated in DCIS progression and stemness, while ZNF281 is a dormant marker and SCL7A11 has been linked to the unfolded protein response pathway which confers resistance to chemotherapy in breast cancer ( ). Furthermore, NR2F1 regulates dissemination; however, it is down-regulated by BCL9 which has high expression in this cluster ( ). The top three patients with DCIS contributing to this cluster of cells had a HER2+ score.
Tumor cells were identified within the epithelial cell clusters by using an inferred copy number variation (CNV) identification program, InferCNV ( ). CNVs across the 22 chromosomes were inferred for each subtype of epithelial cell. The program was further able to identify subclones of epithelial cells within each subtype that shared profiles of CNVs. For example, DCIS 12 had one subclone of basal epithelial cells and three subclones of LumHR epithelial cells ( ). The subclones with the highest burden of aberrations were identified as tumor cells versus normal epithelial cells. In the integrated sample, grouping together all the tumor cells defined by these abundant aberrations and conducting differential gene expression analysis against the normal epithelial cells ( ) revealed many notable suppressed immune response pathways via gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) ( ) ( ). These pathways were also consistent across the two different racial demographic groups (fig. S1). The top 10 genes contributing to each of these suppressed immune response pathways were also mapped ( ).

## Single-cell analysis of CTCs unveils matched epithelial subtypes and regulated immune pathways
Enriched CTC samples were sequenced from the six patients from whom we obtained matched tissue samples, and the resultant transcriptomic data were integrated onto a UMAP using CCA ( ). Different blood cells including various immune cells and platelets present in the enriched CTC fraction were sequenced. Within these data, CTCs were characterized as cells with positive expression of epithelial genes [keratins and/or epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM)] and negative expression of canonical white blood cell genes (Supplementary Text). These CTCs were projected onto the UMAP (identified by the patient from which they originated) and clustered into two main groups: CTC cluster 1 and CTC cluster 2. CTC cluster 1 represented the group of CTCs that colocalized with platelets indicating the presence of CTC platelet clusters ( ), and CTC cluster 2 consisted of the remainder of the CTCs. Applying the top markers of the epithelial subtypes from the tissue data, CTCs were primarily classified into two of the four subtypes of epithelial cells ( ). Specifically, most of the CTCs were either of the basal subtype and/or the LumHR2, with CTCs from cluster 1 contributing more to the basal subtype than those from cluster 2.
 Differential gene expression analysis between all the CTCs and the rest of the blood cells unveiled many of the epithelial genes as differentiating characteristics between the two groups ( ). Furthermore, GSEA revealed important pathways associated with platelet aggregation activated in the CTCs and suppression of regulation of lymphocyte-mediated immunity ( ). In accordance with literature, platelet cloaking is one proposed mechanism via which CTCs survive in circulation ( , ). Closer analysis enabled by mapping the top keratin markers for basal epithelial cells (KRT5 and KRT14) and for luminal epithelial cells (KRT8 and KRT18) highlighted that most CTCs were either purely luminal or dominantly luminal ( ), with some patients such as DCIS 4 and DCIS 12 that had both basal and luminal CTCs. The heterogeneity of CTCs was also demonstrated by mapping in key genes related to the epithelial phenotype, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), metastasis, and cell proliferation ( ).
Our dataset enabled the direct comparison of sequenced CTCs and tissue tumor cells. Expression of KRT8 and VIM was compared across CTCs and tissue tumor cells to not only visualize the variation in expression across each cell but to also directly compare how the expression aligned on the epithelial to mesenchymal spectrum. Similarly, KRT5 and KRT8 expression was compared to probe the level of alignment with a more basal or luminal epithelial subtype, respectively ( ). These comparisons demonstrated a substantial population of CTCs with intermediate EMT characteristics. Differential gene expression analysis between the CTCs and tissue tumor cells also revealed key genes ( ) associated with extracellular matrix construction suppressed and platelet activation and aggregation activated in the CTC population compared to the tissue tumor cells ( ).
The role of racial demographics can also be further investigated through single-cell RNA analysis. Differential gene expression analysis between CTCs from Black patients versus all other cells ( ) revealed genes associated with suppression of lymphocyte immunity [suppression of natural killer (NK) cell–mediated cytotoxicity and immunity] ( ) and the associated top core enrichment genes. Prior studies in literature have commented on immune regulatory pathways in patients with DCIS versus controls ( ) but do not report race-based differences, particularly not in the circulating microenvironment.
Risk classifiers proposed in DCIS tissue studies can also be tested in CTCs from patients with DCIS. Strand et al. ( ) proposed an 812-gene classifier to predict ipsilateral recurrence in 5 years and overall risk of progression for a cohort of 542 patients with DCIS. Many of the genes from the 812-gene classifier were also up-regulated in the CTCs from patients with DCIS. DCIS 12 had the most genes that were significantly up-regulated, with 28 significantly up-regulated genes. DCIS 25 had the next most, followed by DCIS 4, DCIS 15, and DCIS 17 with three significantly up-regulated genes each (table S2). Monitoring this patient for progression could provide a useful indicator for whether, in CTCs, a greater burden of genes from this classifier also suggests a greater risk of progression.
The scRNA-seq data also enabled us to probe the phenotypic heterogeneity of CTCs with an EMT score. It has been posited that CTCs exist in an intermediate state ( – ) along the spectrum of epithelial to mesenchymal cell phenotypes. Using the scRNA-seq data from three patients with the highest number of CTCs in the dataset, an EMT score (scale 0 to 100 from epithelial-like to mesenchymal-like, respectively) was calculated (fig. S2) and assigned to each of the CTCs from the patients. The median scores (60 to 80) calculated for each patient provided further support for the intermediate phenotypic state of CTCs.

## Comparative clonal analysis between matched tissues and CTCs suggests evolution of metastasis
CNVs were inferred from the enriched CTC samples in the scRNA-seq data using InferCNV. The CNV score calculated by the program for each individual cell was projected across each chromosome to visualize the aberrations ( ), with gains in chromosome 8 and large losses in chromosomes 7, 12, 13, 14, 17, and 19.
 When matching the CNV profiles between the CTCs and tissue samples, the level of concordance was found to be patient dependent ( ). The degree of agreement was assessed on the basis of CNVs present in both the tissue tumor cells and the CTCs. DCIS 25 had the highest concordance between both the tissue and CTCs, whereas DCIS 12 had the least level of agreement, with many CNVs unique to the CTCs. The level of concordance could be indicative of the potential of CTCs to undergo transcriptomic changes upon dissemination and toward a state more suitable for survival in circulation. When we examined the subclones (cells with similar CNV profiles as determined by the program) within each tissue, DCIS 12 had the least number of subclones in its tissue (both basal and luminal), whereas DCIS 15 and DCIS 25 both had 10+ subclones of both basal and luminal epithelial cells. The opposite trend was true, however, when focusing on the CTCs, wherein DCIS 12’s CTCs had three subclones, and DCIS 15 and DCIS 25’s CTCs only had one subclone ( ). The vast difference, in some cases, of the number of subclones between tissue and circulatory tumor cells could be indicative of clonal selection as part of an evolutionary bottleneck model.
Within the CNVs unique to the CTCs, many of the genes with aberrations were important in the context of DCIS and breast cancer cell migration and proliferation. DCIS 12 had unique CNVs in genes related to chemoresistance, platelet cross-talk, metastasis regulation, and immune cell regulation (table S3). Common drivers and mutations in DCIS and breast cancer ( ) were also investigated within the tissue and CTCs ( ). In some cases, both the tissue and CTCs had similar CNVs, such as the gain in PIK3R1 in both of DCIS 25’s LumSec epithelial cells and CTCs and the loss in STK11 in both of DCIS 15’s basal epithelial cells and CTCs. In contrast, many of the CNVs in these driver genes were unique to the tissue cells and CTCs, such as in the case of DCIS 12’s loss of RUNX1 whereas DCIS 12’s LumHR group had a gain in the same gene.

## CellChat communication networks unveil common ligand-receptor interactions in CTCs and tissue epithelial cells
Intercellular communication pathways can be investigated to understand the interactions of CTCs with their microenvironment and how well those interactions are preserved within the tissue microenvironment. CellChat ( ) was used to predict the probability of ligand-receptor interactions between CTCs and other cells in the circulating microenvironment. Within the tissue microenvironment, the type and levels of interactions were dependent on the subtype of epithelial cells ( ). When comparing all intercellular interactions, basal epithelial cells had the highest incoming interaction strength of all cell types in the tissue. In comparison to all epithelial subtypes, basal cells also had the highest outgoing interaction strength. LumHR2 was the next strongest sender and recipient of interactions, followed by LumHR1, and lastly LumSec had the least number of interactions (fig. S3). In the circulating microenvironment, CTCs had the highest number of interactions with monocytes, NK cells, plasma cells, and one of the two clusters of platelets ( ).
 Comparing intercellular interactions between CTCs and epithelial cells from the tissues, some common ligand-receptor interactions were present ( ). The APP-CD74 ligand-receptor interaction was predicted to have high probability in both the LumHR2 tissue cells and the CTCs in circulation with other immune cells, with the highest probability of interaction with monocytes/macrophages. Other overlapping pairings included APP-SORL1, APP-CD74, CD99-CD99, CD99-Pilrb, THBS1-CD36, and LGALS9-CD44. THBS1 expression on tumor cells has been linked with glioblastoma expansion and invasion ( ). The THBS1-CD36 axis has also been highlighted for its role in establishing metastasis in the context of colorectal cancer ( ), suggesting potential oncogenic contributions in the context of DCIS. Two other interactions that were unique to the CTC group included the PF4-CXCR3 interaction with plasma cells and the CCL5-CCR3 interaction with granulocytes. In contrast, two of the interactions unique to the LumHR2 cells included the MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4) and THBS1-CD47 with most of the lymphocytes.

## CTCs are enriched from MIND models while the models were still preinvasive
CTCs were also identified in the MIND models while the models were still considered to be preinvasive. The cancerous cells were identified using a similar immunofluorescence staining panel with CTCs classified as DAPI+/CD45−/panCK+ cells ( ). In the longitudinal study over 12 months, CTCs were identified in circulation as early as 4 months after the development of the models ( ). Microfluidic isolation was also able to enrich these cells with high sensitivity and specificity ( ), as an increased presence of CTCs in blood samples from several MIND models (e.g., 2281) also correlated with a greater presence of epithelial cells indicated by EpCAM/panCK+ cells in the bone marrow as assessed by flow cytometry ( ). Furthermore, in the models wherein a high number of cells in circulation was observed, tissue staining was also positive for the panCK signal ( ).
 

## DISCUSSION
In this study, we have demonstrated the successful enrichment of CTCs from patients with DCIS using an antigen-independent microfluidic Labyrinth technology. Although circulating epithelial cells in patients with DCIS has previously been described ( – ), the molecular characterization of these cells as well as comparison to the primary DCIS, especially at the single-cell transcriptomic level, has been lacking. In the burgeoning era of personalized medicine, reliable markers are needed to identify the patients with DCIS who are at greater risk of progression to invasive disease and distant metastasis to guide treatment, decrease overtreatment, and improve outcomes. Liquid biopsies have the potential to identify these markers with the benefits of being minimally invasive ( ). The detection of CTCs in most patients with DCIS in this and previous studies calls into question the widely held belief that tissue invasion is an obligate step in tumor cell dissemination and distant metastasis. The malignant nature of CTCs in patients with DCIS was demonstrated by CNV analysis and their derivation from primary DCIS tumors by comparative genetic analysis. The ability of DCIS cells to enter the circulation before pathologic evidence of invasion was supported by the detection of CTCs in the preinvasive stages of DCIS in a MIND model. Long-term monitoring could help identify which patients with CTCs, and with what characteristics from the single-cell data, progress, which we expect to be in line with epidemiological studies. It would be of particular interest to monitor patients such as DCIS 12 whose CTCs presented CNVs in several genes of interest in the DCIS and breast cancer context as well as whose CTCs used several platelet activation and cloaking mechanisms for survival.
The lack of information about the natural evolution of DCIS leads to overtreatment of many patients, while the possibility of recurrence, progressive disease, and de novo tumors remains despite treatment ( ). A study based on the findings of the Sloane Project reported that despite a group of patients receiving lumpectomy with or without radiation, 5.3% developed invasive recurrence and 3.8% DCIS recurrence ( ). Similarly, the 15-year recurrence rate after surgery alone for DCIS is about 28% ( ). A potential advantage of CTC molecular analysis is that these cells may represent a subset of the primary DCIS cells that have the capability of intravasation and metastasis. Retrospective studies of breast cancer–related mortality of women with DCIS from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database ( – ) have demonstrated that while local therapies reduce local recurrence, there is no substantial difference in breast cancer–related mortality [at 20 years, 3.3% overall ( )] between women that underwent mastectomy, lumpectomy, or lumpectomy with radiation ( ) and women that underwent breast conserving surgery compared to radical mastectomy ( ). In a study tracing the origin of clones in invasive recurrent lesions, 18% of invasive recurrent cases were clonally independent of the original DCIS lesion ( ). These findings suggest the role of latent metastases in breast cancer–related mortality following DCIS ( ). If metastasis (and associated mortality) can be generated from the original DCIS and does not require local recurrence or invasive disease, then the characterization of CTCs provides an opportunity to study the potential of these cells to generate clinically substantial metastasis and the utility of systemic treatment such as adjuvant endocrine therapy for this population of patients with DCIS.
Large, tissue-based clinical studies of DCIS and breast cancer have proposed potential risk factors for progression that can be tested for the prognostic value in the CTCs of patients with DCIS. Hutten et al. ( ) determined HER2+ and solid histology along with a high copy number aberration burden as leading high-risk factors. Young age at diagnosis, multifocality, microinvasion, Black race, and tumor size greater than 25 mm have also been identified as risk factors for mortality ( , ). Racial demographics play a key role in breast cancer prognosis with Black women 40% more likely to die from breast cancer than white women and with the lowest 5-year survival rate of any of the racial demographic groups ( , ). While socioeconomic factors are also contributors to these disparities ( ), one-in-five Black women are diagnosed with triple-negative breast cancer, which is harder to treat and associated with quicker progression ( ). Testing racial demographics in patients with DCIS gives a unique opportunity to study triple-negative or basal-like subtypes of the carcinoma which are rarer ( ).
Our preliminary data also suggest the role of racial demographics in potentially worse outcomes for subgroups of patients with DCIS. We found that Black patients in our study had a significantly higher concentration of CTCs per milliliter of blood versus white patients. Comparatively, in this cohort, both solid histology (P = 0.2818) and HER2 status (P = 0.2670) did not create significant differences in CTC concentration (fig. S4). In the scRNA-seq data, on a tissue level, both racial demographic groups had suppressed immune regulation pathways when comparing the tumor cells to the normal epithelial cells. However, in CTCs, only Black patients had notable pathways associated with immune cell regulation suppression. Furthermore, when comparing CTCs from Black patients to those from white patients, Black patients had comparatively suppressed pathways of platelet aggregation and activation along with wound healing. Suppression of immune regulation could support the greater concentration of CTCs found in Black patients compared to white patients. Furthermore, these data could suggest the greater prognostic value of CTCs compared to primary tissues as they are able to capture the finer differences and implications of racial demographics in immune interactions and CTC concentrations in this dataset.
Our data further provide insight into the origin of CTCs from the matched tissues. Our data suggest that of the four epithelial subtypes present in tissues, CTCs are predominantly of the LumHR2 or basal subtypes. This aligns well with the gene signature found in the tissue LumHR2 cluster. In addition, clonal comparisons across matched tumor tissue cells and CTCs from patients with DCIS can help determine the origin of cancerous cells in circulation. A Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated for the dominant signature of the CTC CNV profile (or for each subclone’s profile as in the case of DCIS 12) against each tumor tissue subclone’s profile. In the case of DCIS 12 and DCIS 15, one to two dominant subclones were identified in the tissue with a mildly positive coefficient (0.3 to 0.4). For DCIS 25, several tissue subclones had a high Pearson coefficient (>0.9) and two were significant (r = 1, P = 0.0047; r = 0.9976, P = 0.0438) (fig. S5). This could provide further supporting evidence for the evolutionary bottleneck model in which there are many subclones present in the primary DCIS lesion, but only some clones are capable of escaping from the basement membrane ( ).
Last, the MIND models provide exciting preliminary data to support early dissemination of cancer cells during the carcinogenesis of breast cancer. CTCs were observed from these samples as early as 4 months after the development of the models and while they were still considered to be preinvasive. One limitation of the study is the absence of a corresponding MIND model for a sample also processed for single-cell sequencing. Tackling this limitation is of high priority and could help provide indicators for progression. We also aim to accrue more control samples, especially at repeated time points, such that a universal baseline for noise in the readouts can be established. Furthermore, the role of racial demographics can also be explored further in depth by creating cohorts of models from different racial demographic groups and comparing drivers for progression.
Overall, our study provides evidence for the alternate hypothesis of early dissemination during the breast cancer carcinogenesis timeline. Our study further bolsters the utility of liquid biopsies in probing sensitive differences in racial demographics and its implications for breast cancer carcinogenesis, suggesting that liquid biopsies could also be surrogates for tissue-based diagnostics and prognostics. Future studies can aim to specifically enroll more Black patients to enable targeted probing of genetic drivers behind worse prognoses and higher CTC burdens through the MIND models. Focusing the patient cohort on hormonal markers and HER2 could also enable more targeted comparisons between retention of these markers in CTCs compared to the primary lesion. The goal will also be to achieve a functional readout to assess the ability of CTCs to grow in the ductal space, invade, and metastasize. With more patient samples processed for single-cell sequencing, we aim to have a greater number of CTCs to investigate in the dataset (tables S4 and S5), increasing the robustness of the analysis.
Liquid biopsies enable the unique ability to longitudinally study these patients after surgery. In particular, with ongoing clinical trials assessing the utility of various modalities of treatment ( ), we envision liquid biopsy with a CTC assay as an additional monitoring arm, especially as patients undergo treatment, enabling a comparison of a baseline versus posttreatment sample. This approach can help probe for the presence of persistent CTCs and its implications for progression and recurrence. Ultimately, the goal is to assess the value of CTCs as a prognostic biomarker that could aid with patient stratification and tackle the issue of overtreatment in DCIS diagnosis and treatment.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Experimental design
Patients with DCIS consented for a blood draw at the time of their surgery appointment at the KUMC in the KUMC IRB-approved study (CR00020830). In this cohort, blood samples were collected successfully from 34 patients that presented either pure DCIS or DCIS with simultaneous IDC or microinvasion as denoted by the pathologist’s report. All patients with DCIS included in this study are female with a median age of 61 years. Weight was not a parameter included in the reports. The focus of the cohort was also treatment-naive patients with DCIS. Patients were not excluded on the basis of hormone status or racial demographics, and no randomization was performed as samples were based on availability. During surgery, a matched sample of tissue was also acquired for a selection of the patients. A portion of the tissue was preserved via FFPE and processed into 5- and 25-μm tissue scrolls. Epithelial and stromal cells were derived from the reminder of the tissue sample and were used for creation of the MIND models.
In conjunction with the patients with DCIS, 10 healthy controls donated a blood sample after informed consent. Seven of these patients were consented at the KUMC and were age-matched to the primary patient cohort. All the healthy controls included are also female with a median age of 43.5 years. As this was a pilot study to verify the prognostic value of CTCs in patients with DCIS and their downstream single-cell analysis, a formal power analysis was not required. Details about demographic information and information from the pathology report were initially blinded when DCIS samples were shipped to the University of Michigan for processing.

## Patient sample collection and shipment
A nurse drew a blood sample first into an EDTA tube to collect 1 to 2 ml of the sample to discard, and then the remainder of the blood was drawn into a CellRescue (Menarini Silicon Biosystems 84000186) tube. The tubes were inverted eight times after the sample was collected. CellRescue tubes were maintained and shipped at room temperature from the KUMC to the University of Michigan using overnight shipment.
Tissue scrolls were maintained at 4°C and shipped with cool packs to the University of Michigan using overnight shipment. These scrolls were kept at cool temperatures until they were ready for processing within a week of preparation and shipment.

## Microfluidic processing of patient blood samples
Blood samples were diluted 1:1 with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and layered with Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva, 17144002) for preprocessing. Layered samples were centrifuged at 400g for 40 min at 21°C. The plasma and buffy coat were collected into a separate tube and diluted to a total volume of 1:5 the original blood volume using PBS. The Labyrinth was primed with pluronic-F127 (Sigma-Aldrich, P2443) for 10 min to prevent sticking of cell material to the microfluidic device walls. Once the devices were primed, they were ready to be used for CTC enrichment. Samples were processed on the Labyrinth at 2500 μl/min until all the sample was passed through the device. Following the first round of processing, the output collected from outlets 2 and 3 of the device was combined. This combined output was then processed through the Labyrinth at 2500 μl/min for a second time.

## Immunofluorescent labeling of patient blood samples
From the Labyrinth output, aliquots were taken to prepare cytospin slides. Polylysine-coated slides (Epredia, P4981) were loaded into cytofunnels and placed inside the cytospin. Into each cytofunnel, 250 μl of the aliquoted output was pipetted. Slides were centrifuged at 800 rpm for 10 min. Following the first cycle, 250 μl of 4% paraformaldehyde was added to each slide to fix the cells, and the same centrifugation cycle was repeated. After the second cycle, slides were unloaded from the cytofunnels and placed in petri dishes. A hydrophobic pen was used to outline the perimeter of the cells, and within this perimeter, PBS was placed to keep the area hydrated.
A panel of four markers was used for immunofluorescent labeling of the samples. This panel consisted of DAPI (Invitrogen, P36931) and the following three primary antibodies: anti-CD45 (Bio-Rad, MCA87; RRID: AB_871979), anti-panCK (AE1/AE3, Cell Signaling, 67306S; RRID: AB_3662123), and anti-vimentin (Cell Signaling, 5741S; RRID: AB_10695459). The three primary antibodies were tagged with the following three secondary antibodies, respectively: goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G2a (IgG2a) Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21131), goat anti-mouse IgG1 Alexa Fluor 546 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21123), and goat anti-rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21245).
Immunofluorescent staining was performed using the following protocol: For primary staining, 0.2% trypsin was added on the slides for 3 min. Then, slides were washed three times with PBS with 5-min incubation times for each wash. Slides were then blocked with filtered 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, B4287) for 1 hour at room temperature. The BSA was then aspirated, and the antibodies (prepared in 1% BSA) were added to the slides. Slides were incubated overnight at 4°C.
The following day, for secondary staining, slides were first washed with PBS three times with 5-min incubation times each. Then, secondary antibodies (prepared in 1% BSA) were placed on the slides and incubated for 1.5 hours at room temperature and covered from direct light. Afterward, excess antibodies were washed off with PBS three times with 5-min incubation time for each wash. Last, DAPI was placed on each slide, and a glass coverslip was mounted. A Nikon Ti2 inverted microscope with a 20× objective and 100% laser power was used to image all immunofluorescent samples.

## Dissociation of FFPE tissues for single-cell processing
FFPE tissues were prepared in accordance with the 10x protocol for isolation of cells from FFPE tissue sections for chromium fixed RNA profiling. In summary, two intact 25-μm scrolls were transferred to a 1.5-ml Eppendorf tube. To the sections, 1 ml of xylene (Sigma-Aldrich, 534056) was added without direct contact with the tissue and incubated for 10 min. After incubation, the xylene was gently pipetted off. This cycle was repeated for a total of three xylene washes. Then, the scrolls were washed with decreasing concentrations of ethanol in water for 30 s each, starting with 1 ml of 100% ethanol, followed by 1 ml of 70% ethanol, and lastly 1 ml of 50% ethanol. Then, 1 ml of nuclease-free water was added to the scrolls and incubated for 30 s. Following the incubation, the water was removed and replaced with 1 ml of PBS. The scrolls in PBS were kept on ice.
We chose to use pestle dissociation for these tissue samples in accordance with the following protocol. The dissociation enzyme mix was prepared according to 10x’s guide and incubated for 10 min at 37°C. PBS was removed from the sample without breaking the scrolls and then 100 μl of the dissociation enzyme mix was added. Using a 1.5-ml pellet pestle (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12-141-364), the tissue scrolls were dissociated, breaking them to smaller pieces. Then, the remaining 900 μl of the dissociation enzyme mix was added and mixed with a pipette. This sample was incubated for 45 min at 37°C while mixing by inversion every 15 min. Using a 1000-μl pipette, the tissue pieces were triturated in the tube. Using a hemocytometer, it was ensured that sufficient cells were isolated. 500 μl of PBS was passed through the preseparation filter (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-041-407) and then the cell suspension was passed through the same filter while it was placed on a 15-ml conical. This suspension was then centrifuged at 850g at 4°C for 5 min. After, the supernatant was removed without disturbing the cell pellet. This cell pellet was resuspended in 0.5 ml of the Quenching Buffer (10x Genomics, 2000516), pipette mixed five times, and maintained on ice. Last, we stored our samples at −80°C while accumulating a sequencing cohort. To enable this, warmed Thaw Enhancer (10x Genomics, PN-2000482) and 50% glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich, G5516) were added to the suspension before it was stored in the freezer.

## MIND model generation
MIND models were generated according to previously published methods. Animal experiments were conducted following protocols approved by the University of Kansas School of Medicine Animal Care and Use and Human Subjects Committee (22-11-275).

## Blood draw from MIND models
Mice from MIND models were euthanized, and blood samples ranging from 0.7 to 1 ml were collected from MIND models via cardiac puncture. The blood was collected into 2-ml EDTA tubes (BD Vacutainer) and shipped overnight at cool temperatures, avoiding direct contact with cool packs.

## Microfluidic processing of MIND model blood samples
MIND model samples were diluted to a total volume of 4 ml using PBS and then layered onto 3 ml of Ficoll. Layered samples were centrifuged at 400g for 40 min at 21°C. The plasma and buffy coat were collected into a separate tube and diluted further if the total volume was not already 1:5 of the starting blood volume. Samples were processed once through the Labyrinth at 2500 μl/min. Cytospin slides were prepared for the samples following the same protocol as for the patient blood samples.

## Immunofluorescent labeling of MIND model blood samples
A similar immunofluorescent staining protocol was followed for the MIND models as for the patient panels, but the panel of antibodies was altered. The panel was adjusted to include anti-mouse CD45 (rat IgG2b κ, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 50-125-86) with goat anti-rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11006).

## Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism 10 was used to perform all statistical comparisons using an unpaired t test.
