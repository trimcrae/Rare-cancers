In this study, we used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to identify four EC subsets, including type L, type H, type R, and arterial ECs. Type H and type R ECs were primarily enriched in cell adhesion and angiogenesis pathways and represented key subsets that promote bone repair in NONFH. Their abundance increased during the mid-stage of NONFH but declined in the late stage. Stromal cells in the femoral head interacted with type H and type R ECs through multiple ligand–receptor axes, maintaining their function. Further screening identified PDK4 as a critical regulator of type H and type R EC abundance. PDK4 not only promotes endothelial fatty acid oxidation but also participates in iron homeostasis and angiogenesis. In vitro experiments showed that PDK4 knockdown significantly impaired the angiogenic phenotype of ECs, which was associated with suppressed fatty acid oxidation, highlighting PDK4’s role in sustaining the activity and function of type H and type R ECs under disease conditions. Peripheral blood transcriptome analysis revealed that the dynamic expression pattern of PDK4 across NONFH stages was consistent with the single-cell results, suggesting potential value for stage-specific diagnosis. By integrating single-cell and peripheral blood transcriptome analyses with in vitro experiments and metabolomic validation, this study elucidates the dynamic changes of specialized ECs and the PDK4-mediated metabolic regulatory mechanisms during NONFH progression. These findings provide new insights into vascular repair and potential therapeutic strategies for NONFH.

## Introduction
Non-traumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head (NONFH) is a common cause of hip pain and restricted mobility, with a high rate of disability [ ]. The number of ONFH patients worldwide is increasing annually, and it is estimated that there are 8.12 million NONFH cases in China alone [ ]. Common risk factors for NONFH include glucocorticoid use and chronic excessive alcohol consumption [ , ], but the underlying pathogenesis remains incompletely understood and may involve microcirculation damage, lipid metabolism disorders, coagulation abnormalities, and impaired bone repair capacity [ ]. Although these mechanisms differ, they all ultimately result in reduced or interrupted blood supply to the femoral head, leading to ischemic necrosis of bone cells and subsequent structural collapse [ , ]. Current clinical treatments mainly include bisphosphonates, core decompression, or total hip arthroplasty, but effective interventions targeting vascular regeneration are still lacking [ ]. Therefore, investigating vascular evolution and the mechanisms underlying impaired vascular repair in NONFH is of great significance for slowing or preventing disease progression.
The vascular network in the skeletal system not only mediates the transport of oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste, but also regulates the activity and differentiation of bone-resident cells through the secretion of regulatory factors, playing a crucial role in bone repair and the maintenance of bone homeostasis [ , ]. Bone vasculature is not a structurally or functionally uniform entity, but exhibits remarkable heterogeneity and diversity. These specialized endothelial cells (ECs) possess distinct molecular markers and functional characteristics [ ]. Type H vessels are a typical example, characterized by high expression of CD31 and Emcn, and play a key role in the coupling of angiogenesis and osteogenesis [ ]. During individual skeletal development, type H vessels gradually transition into type L vessels with low CD31 and Emcn expression; however, under bone injury conditions, the abundance of type H vessels increases and contributes to bone formation [ , ]. In addition, recent studies have identified a type of post-arterial capillary surrounding the trabeculae, termed type R vessels, which are characterized by Emcn and Cav1 [ ]. Type R vessels significantly increase during skeletal development from childhood to adulthood and exhibit close spatial and functional associations with osteoprogenitors and osteoclasts, contributing to bone maturation and remodeling. Previous studies have mainly relied on imaging or histopathological techniques to assess vascular changes or repair in ONFH [ , ], but these approaches cannot effectively identify these specialized EC subsets or their molecular states within the femoral head, limiting the understanding of vascular regeneration and repair mechanisms in NONFH.
The development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has provided a powerful tool for dissecting the microenvironment of skeletal diseases at single-cell resolution [ – ]. Compared with conventional bulk RNA sequencing, scRNA-seq can identify cellular subsets, reveal cellular heterogeneity, and infer cell state transitions as well as intercellular communication networks. Therefore, this study was based on scRNA-seq data to perform an in-depth analysis of ECs in the femoral head tissue of NONFH, aiming to identify EC subsets and their transcriptional features, and to explore potential molecular regulatory mechanisms, providing a theoretical basis for understanding impaired vascular repair in NONFH and for developing targeted intervention strategies.

## Methods

## Data obtaining
The single-cell RNA sequencing data used in this study were obtained from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (SRP361778, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). The data were generated using the 10x Genomics platform. This study included nine human femoral head samples, including three NONFH samples at Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO) stage 3a, three at ARCO stage 4, and three hip osteoarthritis (HOA) samples [ ]. In the original study, samples were collected by sectioning the entire coronal plane of the femoral head, which can therefore partially represent the overall microenvironment of the femoral head. In addition, peripheral blood transcriptome data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE123568, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds) were also included for analysis. These data were generated on the GPL15207 platform and consist of 40 peripheral blood samples, including 10 early-stage, 10 mid-stage, and 10 late-stage NONFH samples, as well as 10 control samples [ ].

## scRNA-seq data processing
The scRNA-seq FASTQ files were processed using Cell Ranger (v7.1.0), and subsequent analyses were performed with the Seurat R package (v4.4.0). The following quality control criteria were applied to select high-quality cells: cells expressing between 500 and 5,000 genes, with less than 10% mitochondrial gene content and less than 1% hemoglobin gene content. Cells not meeting these criteria were excluded. After data normalization, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to extract the top 15 principal components for dimensionality reduction. The Harmony algorithm was applied to correct for batch effects across samples. Cell clustering was performed using the FindClusters and FindNeighbors functions, and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was used for visualization. High-expression genes for each cell cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, and cell types were annotated based on classical marker genes.

## Transcription factor analysis
Transcription factor activity was evaluated using the DoRothEA R package (v1.18.0), which provides a curated resource of transcription factor–target gene regulatory interactions. High-confidence regulatory relationships at levels A, B, and C were selected for analysis. The VIPER algorithm was applied to convert single-cell gene expression profiles into transcription factor activity profiles, and the results were visualized using bubble plots.

## Gene enrichment analysis
To investigate the functional characteristics of each EC subset, enrichment analysis was performed using the ClusterProfiler R package (v4.14.4). Differentially expressed genes for each subset were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (p < 0.05 and |avg_log2FC| > 0.5), followed by Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The results were visualized using bubble plots.

## Pseudotime analysis
To investigate the differentiation trajectories and developmental potential of EC subsets, both CytoTRACE (v0.3.3) and Monocle2 (v2.26.0) algorithms were applied. CytoTRACE infers the differentiation state of cells by evaluating the number and characteristics of expressed genes. Its score is positively correlated with stemness, meaning that higher scores indicate lower differentiation and greater stem cell potential. In parallel, the Monocle2 R package was used to construct single-cell developmental trajectories. This algorithm applies the DDRTree method for dimensionality reduction and orders cells along a pseudotime axis to reconstruct cellular developmental trajectories.

## Cell–cell communication analysis
Cell–cell communication analysis was performed using the CellChat R package (v1.5.0) to explore the interaction patterns between EC subsets and other cell types. A CellChat object was created using the createCellChat function, and the built-in human ligand–receptor interaction database (CellChatDB.human) was used as the analytical reference. Communication networks were initially filtered using the filterCommunication function, followed by data normalization, identification of overexpressed genes and interactions, and computation of communication probabilities to construct a comprehensive intercellular communication network.

## Differential gene expression analysis
To identify key genes potentially co-regulating type H and type R ECs during NONFH progression, differential gene expression analysis and candidate gene selection were performed. First, the FindMarkers function in the Seurat package (p < 0.05 and |avg_log2FC| > 0.5) was used to perform differential expression analysis separately for type H and type R ECs, to extract genes that were upregulated in ONFH stage 3a compared with HOA and downregulated in ONFH stage 4 compared with stage 3a. Next, the two gene sets selected from each endothelial subset were intersected to obtain candidate key genes for type H and type R ECs, respectively. Finally, the intersection of candidate genes from both subsets was taken to generate a set of key genes that may jointly exert core regulatory functions during NONFH progression.

## Protein–protein interaction and co-expression network analysis
To explore the interaction network of key genes and identify hub genes, protein–protein interaction (PPI) and GeneMANIA gene functional association analyses were performed. First, the selected key gene set was submitted to the STRING database to construct and visualize the PPI network. Next, Cytoscape software was used to identify hub genes with the highest connectivity within the network. To further investigate the potential functional relationships of these key genes, the GeneMANIA database was used to construct a gene co-expression network, analyzing their associations in protein interactions, co-expression, pathway sharing, and other functional aspects, thereby evaluating their potential roles in regulating EC states.

## Gene set scoring
To assess the association between key genes and the functional states of EC subsets, the AddModuleScore function in the Seurat package was used to calculate the activity scores of specific functional gene sets at the single-cell level. Gene sets related to glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, lipid/fatty acid metabolism, and angiogenesis were derived from Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) terms, while the anti-ferroptosis gene set was obtained from the Suppressor category of the FerrDb V3 database. The scoring results were visualized as boxplots to compare the functional activity differences among EC subsets across different disease stages. These gene sets are summarized in Supplementary Table .

## Virtual knockout of PDK4
Virtual knockout of PDK4 was performed using scTenifoldKnk [ ] to evaluate its functional role in type H and type R ECs. Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) were reconstructed from the top 2,000 highly variable genes in the scRNA-seq data. PDK4 was then virtually removed, and manifold alignment between the perturbed and original GRNs was performed to identify differentially regulated genes. Genes with Z-score > 2 and adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered significantly perturbed.

## Peripheral blood transcriptome data analysis
To examine the expression patterns of key genes identified in the single-cell analysis within peripheral blood, NONFH peripheral blood transcriptome data were analyzed. The raw expression matrices were subjected to quality control, and batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm. Gene probes were then standardized and annotated to ensure consistent gene identifiers. Based on this, the expression levels of key genes were analyzed, and their distribution in peripheral blood was visualized using boxplots. Furthermore, the pROC R package (v1.18.5) was used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to compare the expression of these genes across different disease stages, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantitatively evaluate their diagnostic performance in distinguishing NONFH pathological stages.

## In vitro experiments

## Cell culture and siRNA transfection
Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (Wuhan, China) and maintained in endothelial cell medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sijiqing, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, China) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and, when reaching approximately 60% confluence, transfected with either negative control siRNA (si-NC) or PDK4-targeting siRNA (si-PDK4) using RNATransMate transfection reagent (Sangon Biotech, E607402) at a final concentration of 30 nM. Following 24 h of transfection, cells were exposed to hypoxic conditions for 6 h before subsequent experiments.

## RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells using an RNA extraction kit (R6834-01, Omega Bio-Tek), and cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse Transcription Quantitative Real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green dye (B690016, Sangon Biotech), and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences were as follows: PDK4: forward 5’-ATTGATCCTAACTGTGATGTGGTAGC-3’, reverse 5’-ACGATGTGAATTGGTTGGTCTGG-3’; CPT1A: forward 5’-CACTGAGCACGGCAAGATGAG-3’, reverse 5’-AGGCGAGGCAGCGATGTC-3’; VEGF-A: forward 5’-CAGAGCGGAGAAAGCATTTGTTTG-3’, reverse 5’-CTGCAAGTACGTTCGTTTAACTCAAG-3’.

## EdU assay
HUVECs (3 × 10³ cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates. Cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU assay kit (C0071S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. The proliferation status was quantified as the ratio of EdU-positive cells to Hoechst 33,342-positive cells.

## Transwell migration assay
Cell migration was evaluated using Transwell chambers (3428, Corning). HUVECs were suspended in serum-free medium at a concentration of 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL, and 100 µL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. After 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. Images were captured using an inverted microscope, and cell numbers were counted using ImageJ software.

## Tube formation assay
Tube formation assays were performed by coating 96-well plates with 50 µL Matrigel per well (BD356234, Corning). HUVECs transfected with si-NC or si-PDK4 were seeded onto the Matrigel at a density of 3 × 10⁴ cells per well. After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, images were captured using an optical microscope. The number of junctions was quantified using ImageJ software.

## Metabolomics analysis
Metabolomics analysis was performed on HUVECs transfected with si-NC (n = 6) and si-PDK4 (n = 6). Following BCA protein quantification, samples were precipitated with pre-cooled extraction solution (methanol: acetonitrile = 1:1, v/v) and centrifuged at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted with an internal standard-containing reconstitution solution (methanol: acetonitrile: water = 2:2:1, v/v/v). After a second centrifugation, the supernatant was subjected to LC-MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of A: water containing 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia, and B: acetonitrile. The injection volume was 2 µL. Mass spectrometry parameters were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 15 Arb; capillary temperature, 320 °C; full MS resolution, 60,000; MS/MS resolution, 15,000; collision energy, SNCE 20/30/40; spray voltage, 3.8 kV (positive) and − 3.4 kV (negative). Raw data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard software. Peak extraction, alignment, and identification were performed using R scripts against the BiotreeDB V3.0 database, yielding metabolite quantification data. OPLS-DA was applied to evaluate metabolic profile separation between groups. Box plots were used to visualize changes in metabolites of interest, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to characterize metabolic reprogramming at the pathway level. Metabolite extraction and LC–MS detection were conducted by Biotree (Shanghai, China).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses of single-cell and peripheral blood transcriptome data were performed using R software (v4.4.1). In vitro experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

## Results

## scRNA-seq reveals a gradual decrease in EC abundance in NONFH
After normalization and stringent quality control of the NONFH scRNA-seq data, a total of 69,013 high-quality single cells were retained. Based on classical cell marker genes, 14 major cell types were identified, including adipocytes, B cells, classical dendritic cells (cDCs), chondrocytes, ECs, macrophages, mast cells, monocytes, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), neutrophils, osteoblasts, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), plasma cells, and T/NK cells (Fig.  A–D). The top five differentially expressed genes for each cell type are shown in the heatmap (Fig.  E). Cell proportion analysis revealed notable changes in the abundance of different cell types across groups (Fig.  F). Notably, compared with the HOA group, both the overall proportion and the number of ECs gradually decreased in ONFH stage 3a and stage 4, consistent with the pathological feature of reduced blood supply in the femoral head of NONFH.

 

## Identification of specialized EC subsets in NONFH
To investigate the heterogeneity of ECs in NONFH, we further performed clustering analysis of ECs. Considering that previous studies have suggested the presence of lymphatic vessels in bone and their potential role in bone repair [ ], we examined the expression of classical lymphatic EC markers in the EC population. We found that PROX1, PDPN, LYVE1, and CCL21 were almost not expressed and did not form a distinct cell cluster, whereas pan-vascular endothelial markers such as PECAM1, EMCN, and CDH5 were highly expressed (Figure A-B). These results indicate that the EC population analyzed in this study is primarily composed of vascular endothelial cells. Subsequently, based on previously reported marker genes for specialized bone vasculature, four EC subsets were identified: type L, type H, type R, and arterial ECs (Fig. A–B). Type L ECs exhibited low expression of PECAM1 (CD31) and EMCN, but high expression of CD55, CTSL, and VCAM1; type H ECs specifically expressed RAMP3, SELP, and TSPAN12; type R ECs expressed AQP1, FMO2, and APLNR; arterial ECs expressed classical markers SEMA3G, GJA4, BMX, and VEGFC (Fig. C–D). Cell abundance analysis revealed that the number and proportion of type H and type R ECs were higher at the ONFH stage 3a but decreased at stage 4, showing a dynamic pattern (Fig. E). The top five differentially expressed genes for each EC subset are shown in the heatmap (Fig. F). Transcription factor regulatory network analysis revealed subset-specific transcriptional activity (Fig. G). Type L ECs were enriched for NR4A1, ATF3, FOSL1, RELB, and other transcription factors involved in stress response and immune regulation. Type H ECs specifically expressed NR2F2, NR2F1, PBX1, and other regulators associated with development and cell fate determination. Type R ECs were enriched in SMAD1, PPARG, NOTCH4, and other transcription factors regulating metabolism and bone remodeling. Arterial ECs expressed classical arterial identity factors, including SOX17, HEY1, and SOX13. GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses further revealed the functional characteristics of each EC subset (Fig. H–I). Type L ECs were significantly enriched in immune stress and inflammatory response-related processes, suggesting a role in NONFH-associated inflammation. Type H ECs were mainly involved in the regulation of cell adhesion, potentially contributing to tissue repair. Both type R and arterial ECs participated in angiogenesis and positive regulation of cell proliferation and migration, whereas arterial ECs were also significantly enriched in inhibitory pathways negatively regulating cell migration. These results suggest that the increase in type H and type R EC abundance during the mid-stage of NONFH may represent an important pathophysiological response, potentially mediating angiogenesis and bone repair in NONFH.

 

## Pseudotime analysis of EC subsets
We further investigated the developmental dynamics and differentiation relationships of EC subsets in NONFH. CytoTRACE analysis revealed that type H ECs exhibited the highest differentiation potential and were identified as the starting point of the differentiation trajectory, whereas arterial ECs, as specialized cells, displayed a relatively mature differentiation state (Fig.  A–B). Subsequently, a differentiation trajectory was constructed using Monocle2. The results showed that the developmental direction of type H ECs led toward type L ECs, consistent with previous studies (Fig.  C–F). Pseudotime gene expression heatmaps generated based on BEAM analysis highlighted key genes determining the distinct fates of each cell cluster (Fig.  G). Further analysis revealed that CD55, SEMA4A, and HIF1A were upregulated at later pseudotime, suggesting potential roles during the development of type L ECs; CDH5, DACH1, and VWF were upregulated at early pseudotime, likely corresponding to the developmental stages of type H and type R ECs; CXCL12 was upregulated at mid-early pseudotime, indicating a potential function during arterial EC development (Fig.  H). These findings indicate that each EC subset in NONFH follows a defined differentiation trajectory with time-dependent gene expression patterns, providing a molecular basis for understanding their roles in angiogenesis and bone repair.

 

## Cell–cell communication analysis of EC subsets
CellChat was used to analyze intercellular interaction patterns. The femoral head microenvironment in NONFH exhibited extensive communication among different cell types, with the highest number of interactions observed in ONFH stage 3a and the strongest interaction intensity in ONFH stage 4 (Fig.  A–B). Information flow analysis revealed that signaling pathways related to angiogenesis and endothelial function, including VEGF, SPP1, CXCL, LAMININ, PECAM1, THBS, and COLLAGEN, were highly active, suggesting that these molecules may mediate signaling among ECs and participate in angiogenesis, vascular homeostasis, and bone remodeling in NONFH (Fig.  C). When EC subsets were analyzed as signal senders, they interacted with all cell types in the NONFH microenvironment, but the strongest interactions occurred among the endothelial subsets themselves, indicating that these subsets broadly participate in intercellular regulation while predominantly communicating with each other (Fig.  D). Given the critical roles of type H and type R ECs in NONFH progression, we further focused on these two subsets to examine ligand–receptor interactions with other femoral head cells as signal senders (Fig.  E–F). Overall, compared with immune cells, stromal-derived cells such as adipocytes, chondrocytes, mesenchymal stem cells, and osteoblasts exhibited more active interactions with type H and type R ECs, primarily via extensive extracellular matrix–integrin signaling axes including COL1A2-(ITGA1 + ITGB1), FN1-(ITGA5 + ITGB1), and LAMA4-(ITGA2 + ITGB1). Notably, in ONFH stage 3a, adipocytes, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and pDCs showed significantly enhanced interactions with type H and type R ECs via the APP-CD74 axis, whereas these interactions markedly decreased in ONFH stage 4. This trend was consistent with the abundance changes of the two subsets, suggesting that APP-CD74 signaling may support EC survival and vascular stability. Further details of ligand–receptor pairs are shown in Fig.  E–F.

 

## Identification of key genes regulating type H and type R ECs
Type H and type R vessels are closely associated with angiogenesis and bone repair in NONFH, and their abundance increases in ONFH stage 3a but decreases in stage 4, which drew our attention. To identify key genes jointly regulating the dynamic changes of type H and type R ECs, differential expression analysis was first performed separately in the two subsets. Genes upregulated in “ONFH 3a vs HOA” and downregulated in “ONFH 4 vs ONFH 3a” were intersected to obtain candidate genes for each subset, and the intersection of these two candidate gene sets yielded 20 shared candidates (Fig.  A). Further screening for genes highly expressed in type H and type R ECs but lowly expressed in other endothelial subsets resulted in eight key genes: FKBP5, SLC27A4, TSC22D3, RNF115, KCTD3, SOCS2, PDK4, and ABCG2 (Fig.  B). Violin plots showed that the expression trends of these eight key genes across ONFH stages were consistent with the abundance changes of type H and type R ECs (Fig.  C). A PPI network was then constructed, in which PDK4 exhibited the highest connectivity and potential interactions with SLC27A4, TSC22D3, ABCG2, FKBP5, and SOCS2 (Fig.  D). Pseudotime analysis indicated that PDK4 was continuously highly expressed in both subsets during ONFH stage 3a (Fig.  E-F). Therefore, PDK4 was identified as a shared core gene of type H and type R ECs, potentially playing a crucial role in maintaining the state and function of these subsets.

 

## PDK4-mediated metabolic reprogramming of type H and type R ECs
PDK4 (Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Kinase 4) is a metabolic regulator that can inhibit glucose utilization in response to microenvironmental changes, shifting cellular energy metabolism toward fatty acid oxidation. SLC27A4 (Solute Carrier Family 27 Member 4) is a long-chain fatty acid transporter involved in lipid metabolism. Considering the potential cooperative function of these two key genes, we performed gene functional association analysis, which revealed their involvement in fatty acid transport, regulation of lipid metabolism, glucose metabolism, response to external stimuli, and acetyl-CoA synthesis (Fig.  A). To further assess the function of PDK4, we scored the relevant functional gene modules in type H and type R ECs during NONFH progression. Glycolysis scoring showed that glycolytic regulation in type H and type R ECs was significantly reduced at the ONFH stage 3a, whereas oxidative phosphorylation levels were markedly increased (Fig.  B). Fatty acid metabolism scoring indicated that lipid and fatty acid metabolic activity in both EC types was elevated at the ONFH stage 3a and decreased at stage 4 (Fig.  C). Given previous studies linking PDK4 to iron metabolism and angiogenesis, we further evaluated iron response and ferroptosis resistance, finding that both were significantly enhanced in type H and type R ECs at ONFH stage 3a, along with increased angiogenic activity (Fig.  D–E). Subsequently, we performed virtual knockout of PDK4 in type H and type R ECs using the scTenifoldKnk algorithm (Fig.  F–G). PDK4 knockout in type H ECs led to significant perturbation of four genes, including SLC27A4, ADIRF, RPS26, and FKBP5, which collectively are involved in fatty acid uptake, regulation of lipid metabolism, protein translation, and cellular stress response. In type R ECs, two genes, FKBP5 and TXNIP, were significantly perturbed, primarily associated with cellular stress response and energy metabolism regulation. These results suggest that PDK4 may maintain the functional state and dynamic abundance of type H and type R ECs through metabolic reprogramming.

 

## Expression analysis of key genes in the peripheral blood of NONFH
Although NONFH is a local necrotic disease of the femoral head, signaling molecules released from the local microenvironment may influence gene expression in peripheral blood. Based on the eight key genes identified in the single-cell analysis, seven genes, including FKBP5, TSC22D3, RNF115, ABCG2, KCTD3, SOCS2, and PDK4, were detected in NONFH peripheral blood transcriptome data (Fig.  A). Notably, the expression patterns of TSC22D3 and PDK4 were consistent with the single-cell data, showing a significant increase in the mid-stage of the disease, reaching a peak at this stage, and then decreasing in the late stage. Further evaluation of these key genes for NONFH stage discrimination showed that PDK4 had good performance in peripheral blood, with AUC values greater than 0.7 in early, mid, and late stages, indicating its potential as a peripheral molecular marker for NONFH staging (Fig.  B). The molecular expression patterns of PDK4 in femoral head ECs and peripheral blood were highly consistent, suggesting a link between local and systemic states in NONFH.

 

## PDK4 knockdown impairs EC function by disrupting fatty acid metabolism in vitro
To investigate the role of PDK4 in vascular ECs, PDK4 was knocked down in HUVECs using siRNA (Fig.  A). RT-qPCR analysis showed that PDK4 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of CPT1A, a gene involved in fatty acid metabolism, and VEGF-A, a gene associated with angiogenesis (Fig.  B). Given that si-PDK4_3 achieved the highest knockdown efficiency, this sequence was used for subsequent phenotypic experiments. EdU assays demonstrated that PDK4 knockdown markedly inhibited HUVEC proliferation (Fig.  C–D). Transwell assays indicated that PDK4 knockdown significantly impaired HUVEC migration (Fig.  E–F). Tube formation assays showed that PDK4 knockdown reduced the number of junctions (Fig.  G–H). To further validate the regulatory role of PDK4 in endothelial fatty acid metabolism, untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed. OPLS-DA analysis showed clear separation between si-PDK4 and si-NC groups, with the permutation test confirming the model’s robustness (Fig.  I–J). Compared with the si-NC group, multiple carnitine-related metabolites were significantly increased in the si-PDK4 group, including free carnitine (Carnitine) as well as acyl-carnitines such as valerylcarnitine and isovalerylcarnitine (Fig.  K–M). Acyl-carnitines serve as key transport forms for fatty acids entering mitochondria for β-oxidation, and their accumulation typically indicates impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation flux. Meanwhile, several long-chain fatty acids were significantly decreased in the si-PDK4 group, including arachidonic acid and stearic acid (Fig.  N–O), suggesting reduced intracellular fatty acid supply or altered metabolic flux. Consistently, GSEA indicated a downward trend in the fatty acid degradation, PPAR signaling, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids pathways in the si-PDK4 group, suggesting impaired fatty acid oxidation capacity (Figure A–D). Collectively, these results indicate that PDK4 knockdown suppresses fatty acid oxidation in HUVECs, compromises EC function, suggesting that its upregulation in the ONFH stage 3a may have a protective effect on type H and type R ECs.

 

## Discussion
NONFH is commonly associated with glucocorticoid use and alcohol abuse and is characterized by impaired blood supply and limited bone repair. During its pathological progression, disruption of the vascular network and functional decline directly affect osteocyte survival and bone remodeling, resulting in severely restricted self-repair and ultimately femoral head collapse. In this study, we analyzed endothelial cells in NONFH using scRNA-seq and found that the overall abundance of endothelial cells gradually decreased as the disease progressed from the mid to late stage. Further clustering analysis identified distinct EC subsets within the femoral head, including type L, type H, type R, and arterial ECs. Among these, type H and type R ECs are closely associated with angiogenesis and bone repair. Notably, the proportions of these two subsets increased during the mid-stage of the disease and declined at the late stage, suggesting a compensatory vascular response and attempted bone repair during disease progression. Molecular mechanistic analysis further indicated that PDK4-mediated metabolic reprogramming may promote the proliferation and sustain the function of type H and type R ECs in the mid-stage of NONFH. These findings provide a molecular basis for the dynamic changes of bone ECs during NONFH progression and suggest that maintaining the function of type H and type R ECs may have potential value in mitigating bone necrosis and promoting bone repair.
It is important to emphasize that, when studying the skeletal vascular system, its pronounced heterogeneity should be recognized. Specialized EC subsets within bone exhibit clear spatial and temporal differences, leading to distinct functional characteristics [ , ]. The earliest identified bone vascular subtype was the type H vessel, reported in mouse bone by Kusumbe et al. in 2014 [ ]. Type H vessels are named for their high expression of both CD31 and Emcn and are mainly located in the metaphysis and endosteal regions, forming columnar or arch-like structures. They are abundant in young individuals but markedly decrease with age and have the capacity to couple angiogenesis with osteogenesis. Subsequent studies using human femoral samples confirmed the presence of type H vessels in human bone and demonstrated an age-dependent decline, suggesting their potential as a biomarker for changes in bone mass [ – ]. In recent years, type H vessels have also been observed in the jaw, vertebrae, and calvaria, indicating that these vessels are widely distributed throughout the skeleton [ – ]. Type L vessels are distinguished from type H vessels by their low expression of CD31 and Emcn, and are primarily distributed in the diaphysis in a reticular pattern [ ]. During skeletal development, type H vessels gradually transition into type L vessels [ ]. Type E vessels, reported in 2017, are primarily present in early embryonic bone in mice and are named accordingly [ ]. They exhibit higher CD31 expression levels than type H vessels and promote osteogenic differentiation of bone progenitor cells during embryonic and early postnatal stages. As development proceeds, type E vessels gradually differentiate into type H vessels, continuing to mediate bone growth and development. Type S vessels, reported in 2023, were identified in the secondary ossification center of mouse long bones and are characterized by high expression of Ly6a, Ly6c1, and Col1a1, playing a key role in promoting epiphyseal growth and maintaining hematopoietic stem cell niche homeostasis [ ]. Type R vessels, reported in 2024, were named for their involvement in bone remodeling and represent a type of post-arterial capillary [ ]. They are characterized by high expression of Emcn and Cav1 but absence of Flt4, primarily located around trabecular bone in adult mice. Their abundance increases in aged mice and may be associated with improved osteoporosis outcomes. Notably, the discovery of both type S and type R vessels was enabled by single-cell sequencing technology’s fine resolution of murine skeletal vasculature, demonstrating its powerful capability in bone vascular research. Building on this, we identified type H, type L, type R, and arterial ECs in human NONFH femoral heads using scRNA-seq data, whereas type E and type S ECs were not detected, likely because they are predominantly present during early bone development. Additionally, in the original study of type R vessels and in a recent single-cell study of vascular subsets during fracture healing, a proliferative endothelial population expressing Top2a and Mki67 was identified [ , ]. In the present study, this proliferative endothelial population was not effectively clustered, which may indirectly reflect impaired endothelial function and loss of proliferative potential under NONFH pathological conditions.
Type H vessels have been extensively studied in skeletal disorders and are recognized as a classical pro-osteogenic vascular subset. Several studies have reported a marked reduction of type H vessels in patients and animal models of steroid-induced ONFH [ – ]. These studies highlight the important role of type H vessels in NONFH repair and suggest that therapies aimed at promoting type H vessel formation may enhance bone regeneration. However, most of these studies focus on overall differences between disease and control groups, and the dynamic changes of type H vessels across different pathological stages of NONFH remain poorly understood. In the present study, our single-cell analysis revealed that type H and type R ECs not only represent key pro-angiogenic and pro-osteogenic subsets in NONFH but also exhibit dynamic abundance changes, with a significant increase in ONFH stage 3a compared to controls and a marked decrease in ONFH stage 4 relative to stage 3a. These findings indicate that type H and type R ECs undergo dynamic changes during NONFH progression, suggesting their involvement in compensatory angiogenesis and bone repair during the mid-stage of the disease, followed by functional decline in the late stage. Cell-cell communication analysis showed that endothelial subsets engage in extensive interactions with other cell types in the femoral head. Stromal cells, including adipocytes, chondrocytes, and osteoblasts, act as signal senders and interact closely with type H and type R ECs through multiple ligand-receptor axes, including various extracellular matrix-integrin axes, APP-CD74, and PTPRM-PTPRM. Some of these interactions are particularly active in ONFH stage 3a, potentially supporting EC survival and angiogenesis. As the disease progresses to ONFH stage 4, these interactions weaken, suggesting that the limited vascular regeneration capacity may partially result from diminished intercellular signaling.
In further exploring key genes regulating the abundance changes of type H and type R vessels, our screening strategy identified PDK4 as a potential mediator. PDK4 is a mitochondrial enzyme that primarily functions by phosphorylating and inhibiting the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH), thereby reducing the entry of pyruvate into the tricarboxylic acid cycle, limiting glucose oxidation, and promoting reliance on alternative substrates for energy supply [ ]. Previous studies have shown that upregulation of PDK4 enhances cellular fatty acid utilization. In muscle cells, soy isoflavones can upregulate PDK4 expression through activation of the ERRα signaling pathway, promoting fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial respiration while reducing intracellular lipid accumulation [ ]. In human adipocytes, PDK4 shifts glucose metabolism from oxidative pathways to lipid synthesis and simultaneously increases fatty acid catabolism, providing an alternative energy source [ ]. Under normal conditions, ECs primarily rely on glycolysis to generate energy for angiogenesis [ ]. However, in NONFH, femoral head ECs are exposed to ischemic, hypoxic, or inflammatory stress, which may limit glucose availability or utilization and impair glycolytic metabolism. A previous single-cell analysis of NONFH bone marrow revealed that genes related to mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation were more highly expressed in patient samples than in controls [ ]. Our single-cell analysis, in vitro phenotypic experiments, and metabolomics data indicate that PDK4 drives type H and type R ECs to rely more on fatty acid oxidation for energy, thereby supporting their survival and function to a certain extent. Virtual knockout analysis revealed that genes such as SLC27A4, ADIRF, FKBP5, and TXNIP may act in coordination with PDK4 to collectively regulate fatty acid metabolism in these specialized ECs. In addition to mediating metabolic reprogramming, previous studies have identified PDK4 as a key gene conferring resistance to ferroptosis. PDK4 enhances cellular tolerance to ferroptosis by reducing ROS and lipid peroxidation, preserving mitochondrial structure and function, and regulating iron metabolism and ferroptosis-related genes [ – ]. In addition, the expression pattern of PDK4 in peripheral blood across different pathological stages of NONFH closely resembles that in Type H and Type R ECs of the femoral head. This concurrent increase in local tissue and peripheral blood may be driven by shared upstream factors, such as lipid metabolism dysregulation, metabolic stress, inflammation, or microvascular alterations. These findings suggest that PDK4 not only mediates local vascular metabolic adaptation at different pathological stages of NONFH but may also reflect systemic energy metabolic reprogramming. Therefore, peripheral blood PDK4 has the potential to serve as a biomarker for NONFH, providing indirect insight into the state of specialized ECs in the femoral head. However, its specificity and utility for assisting ONFH disease staging still require validation in larger cohorts.
This study has several limitations. First, the scRNA-seq data in this study only covered the intermediate and late stages of NONFH, which limits the comprehensive characterization of dynamic changes in EC subsets throughout the disease course. Future studies incorporating early-stage samples would help validate our findings and further refine the understanding of disease progression. Second, we selected HOA as the control because it primarily affects the region around the cartilage, whereas NONFH involves most of the internal femoral head. Moreover, type H and type R ECs in ONFH stage 3 showed a specific increase that clearly exceeds any potential pathological vascular expansion in HOA, suggesting that HOA can serve as a reference for analyzing the specialized EC changes in NONFH. Nevertheless, HOA does not fully represent healthy controls, and future studies should aim to obtain relatively healthy tissue as a control. Finally, single-cell RNA sequencing primarily reflects transcriptional changes; subsequent studies could incorporate proteomic analyses and validate key molecular mechanisms in in vivo animal models.
In conclusion, this study employed single-cell RNA sequencing to characterize the subset composition and dynamic changes of specialized ECs in NONFH. We found that type H and type R ECs exhibit compensatory angiogenesis during the mid-stage of the disease but undergo functional decline in the late stage. Notably, PDK4 was identified as a key regulator that may sustain the survival and function of these cells by modulating fatty acid oxidation. These findings provide new molecular evidence for understanding vascular metabolic adaptation and bone repair in NONFH and offer potential directions for therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving EC function or modulating their metabolic pathways.
