Therefore, the exploration of novel therapeutic strategies to eliminate these cells has become a hot topic in cancer research. Ferroptosis, a newly identified form of regulated cell death, has garnered considerable attention in the field of cancer therapy in recent years. As a novel form of regulated cell death, the core mechanism of ferroptosis lies in the accumulation of intracellular iron and the induction of lipid peroxidation. Oxidative stress, the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling pathway, autophagy, and lipid metabolism play dual roles in the survival of dormant cancer cells and the process of ferroptosis, influencing the response of dormant cancer cells to ferroptosis. These complex molecular mechanisms form a regulatory network between ferroptosis and dormant cancer cells, which holds significant implications for the development of future anti-tumor therapeutic strategies. This review synthesizes current evidence on targeting ferroptosis to eliminate dormant cancer cells, positions ferroptosis as a precision modality against dormant cancer cells, and discusses its therapeutic promise as a conceptual framework for developing next-generation anti-tumor strategies.

## Introduction
Global cancer statistics indicate that in 2020 an estimated 19.3 million new cancer cases and nearly 10 million cancer-related deaths occurred worldwide [ ]. As time progresses, the likelihood that cancer will become the leading cause of human death is increasing. Nevertheless, cancer therapy continues to face numerous challenges, among which the problem of dormant cancer cells is particularly prominent [ , ]. Current research on dormant cancer cells remains in its infancy, and their mechanisms and therapeutic approaches are still poorly defined and require further exploration.
Ferroptosis, a novel form of cell death distinct from apoptosis and necrosis, was coined in 2012 to describe erastin-induced cell death [ ]. It can still occur even when apoptosis and necrosis are inhibited [ – ]. Increasing evidence suggests that ferroptosis represents a promising strategy for targeting dormant cancer cells [ – ]. It can synergize with diverse therapies and offers potential for early intervention and overcoming tumor resistance, suggesting broad clinical applications. However, its underlying mechanisms require further elucidation to optimize therapeutic outcomes.
In this review, we elucidate the intricate connections between dormant cells and ferroptosis, focusing on four aspects: redox signaling, TGF-β signaling, lipid metabolism, and autophagy. We also delve into and explore innovative treatment strategies based on these connections, aiming to provide novel and feasible ideas for more effectively eradicating dormant cancer cells.

## The history of discovery and treatment of dormant cancer cells
Dormant cancer cells are cells that transiently reside in the G0 phase, do not undergo cell division, but can re-enter the cell cycle and proliferate upon stimulation by certain factors, ultimately leading to cancer recurrence or metastasis to other tissues [ , ]. At present, there is no reliable method to predict which cells will enter a dormant state. The unpredictable nature of dormancy has been recognized as one of the major hallmarks of cancer [ ]. Hypoxia and nutrient deprivation can drive a subset of cancer cells in solid tumors to transition from a proliferative state to dormancy; consequently, a proportion of cells in untreated tumors may already be dormant. Cancer therapies such as immunotherapy, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy can further remodel the cancer microenvironment and induce some proliferating cancer cells to enter dormancy. Dormant cancer cells can better adapt to new environments, prepare for metastasis, acquire resistance to anticancer treatments, and evade immune destruction. Currently, three main therapeutic strategies for dormant cells have been proposed: (1) maintaining cancer cells in a dormant state, (2) converting dormant cells to a proliferative state to increase their treatment sensitivity, and (3) directly killing dormant cells [ – ].
As early as 1934, Willis first described cancer dormancy by observing patterns of metastatic spread in patients with cancer. He defined it as a complex state in which cancer cells cease proliferation but retain the potential for malignant progression [ ]. In the following years, cancer dormancy attracted increasing attention from researchers. In 1954, Hadfield proposed the concept of “temporary mitotic arrest,” suggesting that a reversible block in mitosis could explain the long clinical latency of more than 5–6 years observed in some patients before late metastatic relapse, thereby directly linking dormant cancer cells to late-stage recurrence [ ]. Clinical follow-up data from 1945 to 1965 showed that 31% of breast-cancer-related deaths occurred 5–20 years after surgery. At that time, these patients were considered to be in a state of cancer dormancy [ ]. However, with the identification of cancer subtypes around 2010, this view was revised, and long-term recurrence is no longer attributed solely to the awakening of dormant cells [ ]. In 1959, Dr. Fisher and colleagues provided the first experimental evidence in animal models that laparotomy can activate dormant cancer cells and promote tumor development [ ]. In the years that followed, the phenomenon of tumor dormancy received increasing attention from researchers. In 1973, using a three-dimensional tumor spheroid model, Folkman and colleagues observed that cancer cells could enter a dormant state, characterized by continuous proliferation at the periphery, central necrosis, and overall stability in spheroid size. The onset of dormancy was attributed to a decreased surface-area-to-volume ratio, which limited nutrient diffusion and waste removal. This seminal work identified angiogenesis as a critical switch for escape from dormancy and resumption of tumor growth, thereby laying the theoretical foundation for anti-angiogenic therapies [ ]. In 1995, Heiss et al. reported that the persistent presence or increased number of dormant cancer cells after surgery is closely associated with tumor recurrence, whereas their elimination correlates with a favorable prognosis. Expression of the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) has been identified as a marker of dormant cells with high metastatic potential [ ]. Minimal/molecular residual disease (MRD) is a clinical monitoring concept that is considered to be strongly associated with dormant cancer cells. It refers to cancer cells that remain in the body after therapy, either at the primary site or at distant locations. MRD testing is therefore widely used to assess patient prognosis. However, MRD is not identical to cellular dormancy, as therapy-resistant cells and cancer stem cells also contribute to residual disease [ ]. With advances in immunocytochemistry and molecular diagnostics, the detection of disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in bone marrow and circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in blood has become an established approach for MRD assessment. At present, multiple MRD detection techniques play an indispensable role in clinical practice [ ]. In 2003, the Aguirre-Ghiso group demonstrated that the ERK/p38 signaling activity ratio could predict whether cancer cell lines derived from breast, prostate, melanoma, and fibrosarcoma would remain dormant or instead proliferate in vivo [ ]. Building on this, Dr. Aguirre-Ghiso was the first to systematically categorize dormancy into three mechanistic classes: cellular dormancy, tumor mass dormancy, and immunosurveillance-mediated dormancy [ ]. In 2010 and 2011, Essers and Sosa proposed three therapeutic strategies targeting dormant cancer cells: breaking dormancy, maintaining them in a dormant state, or eradicating them during dormancy [ , ]. By 2025, dormant cancer cells have become a prominent research focus, as evidenced by multiple publications in high-impact journals [ – ] (Fig.  ). 
Cancer dormancy can arise at multiple stages of cancer development and is a major contributor to cancer recurrence, even years after resection of the primary tumor [ ]. Many patients relapse years or even decades after apparently successful initial treatment, with residual dormant cancer cells that were not eradicated considered the primary culprits. Conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy predominantly target rapidly proliferating cells, whereas dormant cells, owing to their low proliferative and metabolic activity, can evade immune surveillance and drug- or radiation-induced killing. Effective identification and elimination of dormant cells would be expected to substantially prolong progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Thus, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing tumor dormancy is urgently needed to develop treatment strategies that more effectively eradicate these cells. Consistent with this, our group recently established a serum-starved HeLa cell dormancy model to directly compare the effects of X-ray and carbon ion irradiation. We demonstrated that reactivation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is crucial for driving dormant cells back into the cell cycle and promoting apoptosis, thereby sensitizing them to radiation-induced death [ ]. Furthermore, our unpublished data suggest that multiple regulatory networks, including ferroptosis, may also contribute to the clearance of dormant cancer cells. Growing evidence from recent studies has further confirmed ferroptosis as a promising therapeutic avenue for targeting dormancy, underscoring its potential as a key strategy for eliminating these treatment-resistant populations [ , – ].

## Ferroptosis has shown considerable potential as a therapeutic strategy for targeting dormant cells
Ferroptosis is a form of programmed cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxides within cells and by a requirement for substantial cellular iron to complete the death process; it also plays a role in the treatment of multiple cancers [ ]. In cancer, regulation of ferroptosis by the body may provide a means to combat tumors. Currently, various inhibitors targeting ferroptosis-related enzymes have shown therapeutic effects in multiple types of cancer, including liver, lung, breast, and ovarian cancer, as well as melanoma [ – ].
In recent years, ferroptosis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for targeting dormant cancer cells. Hangauer et al. systematically tested ferroptosis inducers in dormant models of breast, melanoma, lung, and ovarian cancers, and found that dormant cancer cells are highly sensitive to ferroptosis, whereas normal cells are largely spared, underscoring the therapeutic selectivity of this approach [ ]. Consistent with this, a melanoma study showed that dedifferentiated, drug-resistant subpopulations are also markedly vulnerable to ferroptotic cell death [ ]. In 2024, Hyemin Lee and colleagues extended this line of investigation to drug-tolerant persistent cells in colorectal, breast, and lung cancers. Their work revealed that inhibition of lipid metabolism further sensitized these slow-cycling cells to ferroptosis [ ]. Two additional studies focusing specifically on dormant colorectal cancer cells further support this concept. One reported that a quiescent subpopulation in colorectal tumors exhibits elevated levels of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and ferrous iron (Fe2+), and that targeting GPX4 significantly enhances ferroptotic responses [ ]. The other showed that fasting increases intracellular iron in dormant colorectal cancer cells, thereby promoting ferroptosis and amplifying the cytotoxic effects of ferroptosis inducers [ ]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which ferroptosis targets dormant cells remain incompletely understood. This review summarizes and analyzes how ferroptosis exerts cytotoxic effects on dormant cancer cells and discusses its future therapeutic prospects from four perspectives.

## Ferroptosis affects dormant cancer cells through redox-related mechanisms
Dormant cells, which heavily rely on antioxidant systems to maintain homeostasis, resist the effects of radiotherapy or chemotherapy by lowering reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels through high expression of antioxidant enzymes [ , ]. Not only that, but an increase in ROS levels can not only cause proliferating cancer cells to become dormant but also lead to the recurrence of cancer cells that were already in a dormant state [ , ]. The dormant transform process is related to the tumor microenvironment, such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress [ ]. Studies have shown that redox status influences the entry of cancer cells into dormancy by modulating key factors in intracellular signaling pathways and the tumor microenvironment. The factors in the microenvironment include hypoxia-inducible transcription factor (HIF), integrins, and TGF-β signaling. In addition, there are some redox-related factors involved in this process. Redox regulation is a critical component of how ferroptosis affects dormant cancer cells [ ]. In cancer cells, many redox factors (e.g., ROS, GPX4, HIF, and NRF2) are linked to dormancy and recurrence and act as key triggers of ferroptosis. Targeting these factors may therefore be crucial for exploiting ferroptosis to eliminate dormant cells.

## ROS
ROS are pivotal regulators of both entry into and exit from the dormant state in cancer cells. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy cause excessive oxidation and increase intracellular ROS levels in cancer cells. Excess ROS inactivates integrins on the cell surface, which are essential for maintaining cell-cycle progression, and activates TGF-β [ , , ]. Redox reactions activate glutathione peroxidase 7 (GPX7), thereby enhancing its ability to bind misfolded proteins, a process in which ROS play a central role. After BiP binds to misfolded proteins, it is released from the endoplasmic reticulum, activating endoplasmic reticulum stress signal transduction and binding to inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α), and then activating transcription factor 6α (ATF6α), and proline-rich receptor-like protein kinase (PERK) [ , ]. Activation of IRE1α and ATF6α may support the survival of dormant cancer cells, while activation of PERK can phosphorylate eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) and inhibit translation of cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK4), leading to cancer cell dormancy [ ] (Fig.  A). Our recent work demonstrates that PERK signaling regulates the sensitivity of liver cancer cells to high-linear energy transfer (LET) carbon ion irradiation by orchestrating autophagy, apoptosis, and ferroptosis, with TP53 status serving as a critical determinant of the radiation response [ ]. Interestingly, besides the ability of dormant cancer cells to re-enter the proliferative phase after accumulating sufficient nutrients, the hypoxia and microenvironmental oxidative stress that initially drove cancer cells into dormancy can also promote their exit from the dormant state [ , ]. 
An increase in ROS is closely associated with ferroptosis. The main source of intracellular ROS is the mitochondria, which produce H2O2, a substrate for the Fenton reaction. The NOX family constitutes one of the primary enzymatic sources of ROS and encompasses seven isoforms. Among them, NOX1, NOX2, and NOX4 show a positive correlation with ferroptosis. They promote ferroptosis by catalyzing the transfer of electrons from NADPH to O2− [ , ]. NOX1, an oncogene, can promote tumor growth in vivo and is also associated with the lipid metabolism of cancer cells [ , ]. A study on colorectal cancer cells showed that downregulation of NOX1 reduces mammalian target of rapamycin (mTORC1)-mediated inhibition of autophagy, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation and causing proliferating cancer stem cells to transform into slow-cycling cancer stem cells [ ]. The proliferation and apoptosis of endothelial cells are key angiogenic processes that contribute to carcinogenesis and tumor progression. It was found that although the expression levels of NOX2 in proliferating and quiescent endothelial cells are similar, NOX2 activity is crucial for cell proliferation, whereas its role in quiescent cells is mainly related to maintaining cytoskeletal structure and preventing apoptosis. Genetic silencing of NOX2 leads to a significant increase in caspase activity, indicating that NOX2 is necessary for promoting endothelial cell survival and supporting the cell proliferation process [ ]. Another study showed that NOX2 controls the metabolism and mitochondrial function of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cells by regulating the expression of NOX4, and that the metabolic changes caused by NOX2 silencing are partially mediated by upregulation of NOX4 [ ]. NOX4 can promote angiogenesis and tumor proliferation and is positively correlated with the activity of TGF-β1 [ ]. The H2O2 produced by NOX4 activates regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) and promotes the transition of quiescent endothelial cells to a proliferative state. The expression of NOX4 in proliferating cells is significantly higher than that in quiescent cells [ ]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) promote tumor growth and immune evasion, and NOX4 can promote the activation of CAFs in cancers. In one study, inhibition of NOX4 transformed differentiated CAFs into more quiescent fibroblast-like cells, indicating that the immune tolerance of CAFs can be effectively overcome [ ]. In a study on ovarian cancer (OC), it was demonstrated that NOX4 can enhance the therapeutic resistance of OC cells by activating human epidermal growth factor receptor 3 (HER3) [ ]. HER3 is a clinical marker for OC and is associated with poor prognosis, which may be related to an increase in cancer cell infiltration and metastasis [ , ]. In addition, high expression of NOX4 is associated with a poor prognosis in glioblastoma (GBM). Compared with differentiated GBM cells, the expression of NOX4 is higher in glioblastoma stem cells and transitional glioblastoma cells [ ]. Targeting ferroptosis through modulation of ROS, particularly via NOX-mediated pathways, therefore represents a highly promising strategy for eradicating dormant cancer cells.

## NRF2
Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) is a key transcription factor responsible for maintaining cellular redox homeostasis, especially under oxidative stress [ ]. In dormant cells, NRF2 is one of the main regulators that maintain low intracellular ROS levels [ ]. In ferroptosis-related pathways, NRF2 not only promotes transcription of SLC7A11 in system Xc− but also regulates the expression of glutamate cysteine ligase (GCL) and glutathione synthetase (GSS), thereby preventing lipid peroxidation [ , ] (Fig.  B). Studies have shown that NRF2 inhibitors can sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and head and neck cancer [ , ]. Recently, it has been reported that dormant breast cancer cells activate antioxidant defenses through NRF2 signaling, thereby facilitating tumor recurrence. Recurrent tumors exhibit NRF2 dependence, indicating that NRF2 is associated with poor prognosis [ ]. In addition, nanotechnology-based strategies targeting NRF2 have been developed to inhibit tumor dormancy and awaken the immune response against recurrent tumors [ ]. In lung cancer cells, NRF2 also shows features that promote resistance to targeted therapies [ ]. Collectively, these studies indicate that NRF2 suppresses ferroptosis in dormant cells, and that targeting NRF2 may be an important strategy to promote ferroptosis in this cell population.

## HIF
Hypoxic cancer cells induce dormancy by modulating HIF signaling. Under normoxic conditions, the HIF-1α inhibitors prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) and factor inhibiting HIF (FIH) suppress HIF-1α activity [ , ]. However, under hypoxic conditions, PHD and FIH, which are oxygen dependent, are inhibited. This allows HIF-1α to translocate to the nucleus and bind HIF-1β, leading to the transcription of dormancy-related genes [ – ]. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are an effective treatment for CML, but they cannot completely eliminate CML stem cells. It has been shown that combining HIF-1 inhibitors with TKIs enhances mitochondrial activity and ROS levels in CML stem cells. This disrupts their quiescence, promotes their entry into the cell cycle, and inhibits their self-renewal and regeneration. HIF-1-mediated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and ROS inhibition are key mechanisms by which CML stem cells adapt to TKI treatment, thereby maintaining their dormancy and repopulation capacity [ ].
HIF also plays a vital role in ferroptosis. HIF-1 can increase iron uptake by upregulating transferrin (TF) and divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), thereby enhancing cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [ ]. However, HIF-1 can also inhibit ferroptosis by upregulating the transcription of SLC7A11 and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and knockdown of HIF induces ferroptosis in cells [ – ]. Overactivation of HO-1 increases the labile iron pool (LIP) and induces ferroptosis, whereas moderate activation of HO-1 exerts antioxidant effects, reducing oxidative damage and protecting cells from ferroptosis [ – ] (Fig.  C). Fenretinide, an oxidative stress inducer in cancer cells, can effectively induce apoptosis in dormant CD34 cells of CML. It has been reported that after fenretinide treatment, antioxidant genes such as HO-1 exhibit antioxidant activity in CD34 cells [ ]. In oral cancer cells, HIF-1 is inhibited through negative feedback regulation, thereby promoting ferroptosis [ ]. In addition, HIF-2 can promote the production of ROS, leading to ferroptosis in colorectal cancer [ ]. In solid tumors, the combined activity of HIF-1 and HIF-2 is a major factor contributing to hypoxia-induced ferroptosis resistance [ ]. HIF plays pivotal roles in both dormant cancer cells and ferroptosis; therefore, manipulating HIF expression may potentiate ferroptosis-mediated eradication of dormant malignant cells.

## GPX4
GPX4, a key antioxidant enzyme markedly upregulated in dormant cancer cells, constitutes the primary defensive barrier against ferroptosis. Cells have evolved several antioxidant systems to counteract ferroptosis, the most important of which is the GPX4–GSH system mediated by GPX4 and reduced glutathione (GSH). This system converts lipid peroxides (L-OOH) into phospholipid (PL) alcohols [ ].
Currently, a variety of GPX4 inhibitors have been shown to eliminate dormancy in multiple tumor types, making them more sensitive to ferroptosis [ – ]. The important role of GPX4 in dormant cancer cells is undeniable. Therefore, inhibition of GPX4 and its related components (such as GSH) has become an important strategy for targeting dormant cells [ ]. GPX4 is a major antioxidant enzyme. In genome-wide short hairpin RNA (shRNA) data from 349 cancer cell lines, GPX4 is most strongly associated with the subgroup of compounds that induce ferroptosis [ ]. Moreover, the GPX4 inhibitors RSL3 and ML210 are among the most selectively lethal compounds for dormant cells. After treatment with GPX4 inhibitors, ferroptosis levels are highest in dormant cells and have the least impact on parental cells [ ]. In colorectal cancer, drug-resistant colorectal cancer cells are enriched within the residual tumor cell population after treatment and are mainly in a quiescent state. These cells exhibit reduced drug sensitivity and high expression of stem cell related genes and mesenchymal markers. In these cells, GPX4 and divalent iron are upregulated, making them more sensitive to GPX4 inhibitors and prone to undergo ferroptosis. Studies have confirmed that inhibition of GPX4 can limit tumor regeneration after anti-tumor drug treatment. In addition, GPX4 and ferritin heavy chain 1 were found to be upregulated in biopsies from patients with colorectal cancer who received neoadjuvant radiochemotherapy, and high GPX4 expression is associated with poor prognosis in these patients [ ]. Furthermore, some drugs targeting GSH have also shown similar effects [ , ]. With regard to the GPX4–GSH defense system, numerous studies have investigated various targeted agents, including recently developed nanosystem-based therapies, all of which provide hope that cancer cells heavily dependent on antioxidant defenses, such as dormant and drug-resistant cancer cells, can be eliminated through ferroptosis [ , , ].

## Ferroptosis affects dormant cancer cells through the TGF-β signaling pathway

## TGF-β affects cell entry and escape during the dormant phase
TGF-β, similar to integrins, is a membrane-associated protein that plays a crucial role in cell dormancy. In current research, TGF-β2 has been shown to induce dormancy in prostate cancer, breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and lung adenocarcinoma [ – ]. TGF-β2 can activate p38 and inhibit ERK1/2 signaling, leading to cancer cell dormancy [ , ]. A low p-ERK/p-p38 ratio is a marker of cancer cell dormancy, and when p38 is dominant, cancer cells are more likely to enter a dormant state [ ]. Activation of p38 is considered to be redox dependent, with the redox sensor tyrosine protein kinase Fyn regulating p-ERK1/2 and p-p38 [ ]. After p38 is activated, downstream oxidative stress signaling also contributes to cell dormancy [ , ]. In addition, nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F member 1 (NR2F1) is one of the downstream factors of p38. p38 upregulates NR2F1 expression, which induces active transcription of H3-PTM. In dormant cells, H3-PTM, in turn, activates NR2F1, whereas the opposite is observed in proliferating cells. In the lung and spleen, NR2F1 induces dormancy by upregulating SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) and RARβ. RNA interference (RNAi) of SOX9 completely blocks NR2F1-dependent growth inhibition. In the bone marrow, NR2F1 induces dormancy by regulating NANOG, which induces growth-arrest signals. In the bone marrow, NR2F1 induces dormancy by regulating NANOG, which can induce growth arrest signals [ ]. Interestingly, upon external stimulation, activation of TGF-β1 can lead to the escape of dormant cancer cells. Sprouting neovessels reactivate dormant cells and promote tumor growth by producing TGF-β1, and inhibition of TGF-β1 has been shown to induce cell dormancy in breast cancer [ , , ]. Exosomes from dormant lung cancer cells promote tumor recurrence and metastasis by delivering integrin beta 6 (ITGB6), activating the TGF-β signaling pathway, and remodeling the extracellular matrix through CAFs [ ]. Dormant cancer cells form a collagen-rich type III matrix to maintain their dormant state [ ]. In glioblastoma, TGF-β mediates the wrapping of surviving cells by perivascular fibroblast-like cells, promoting dormancy and inhibiting immune surveillance [ ].

## The relationship between ferroptosis, TGF-β, and cancer
In the previous section, we elucidated the impact of TGF-β on dormant cells. Interestingly, we have also found that ferroptosis is intricately linked to TGF-β (Fig.  C). In glioma stem cells (GSCs), TGF-β induces the expression of NOX4, which regulates the proliferation of GSCs. Silencing NOX4 reduces the frequency of stem cells and the expression of several stem-cell markers, causing more stem cells to enter a quiescent state [ ]. TGF-β2 can activate p38, leading to cell dormancy. We know that the p-ERK/p-p38 ratio can confirm whether cancer cells are dormant. In previous studies, ROS activated the p38 MAPK pathway, leading to ferroptosis [ ]. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells, inhibition of p38 reduces their sensitivity to ferroptosis [ ]. Interestingly, in lung cancer, inhibition of ERK protects lung cancer cells from ferroptosis [ ]. In EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer, TGF-β2 is highly associated with the survival of drug-resistant cells. Drug-induced upregulation of TGF-β2 drives epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), making cancer cells more sensitive to ferroptosis [ ]. Interestingly, TGF-β1 can also induce EMT in a cell-cycle-dependent manner. The more mesenchymal-like cancer cells become, the more pronounced their dormant-cell characteristics are [ ].
In addition, TGF-β1 plays a significant role in various tumor types. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), SLC7A11 is regulated by TGF-β1. TGF-β1 secreted by the tumor microenvironment inhibits SLC7A11, leading to GSH depletion and increased ROS levels, thereby making cancer cells more sensitive to ferroptosis [ ]. Exposure of HCC cells to a TGF-β1-rich environment before ferroptosis-inducing treatment can further enhance their sensitivity to ferroptosis [ ]. Recent studies have shown that the inhibitor of activated STAT3 protein (PIAS3) is one of the drivers of ferroptosis in HCC. The interaction between PIAS3 and SMAD2/3 activates TGF-β signaling and upregulates thioredoxin interacting protein (TXNIP), which further increases cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [ ]. In pancreatic cancer, gene set enrichment analysis has identified the TGF-β signaling pathway as one of the key ferroptosis-related pathways [ ]. SMAD4, a key molecule in the TGF-β pathway, can bind to the promoter of GPX4 and reduce its transcription in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), making PDAC cells more sensitive to ferroptosis upon TGF-β1 induction [ ]. A study using a combination of 5-azacytidine (AZA) and all-trans retinoic acid (atRA) to induce cancer cells into a dormant state and inhibit metastatic growth found that AZA + atRA-induced reprogramming leads to high SMAD4 expression in dormant cells, which plays an important role in maintaining cancer cell dormancy [ ]. The BECN1–SLC7A11 complex inhibits the activity of system Xc− and thereby promotes ferroptosis. In a recent study, TGF-β1 was found to increase erastin-induced BECN1–SLC7A11 complex formation in both in vitro and in vivo breast cancer models, revealing the potential for combined treatment with TGF-β1 and erastin [ ]. Interestingly, TGF-β1 exerted completely different effects in a study on triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). That study reported a TGF-β1-regulated factor, hepatoma leukemia factor (HLF), which activates γ-glutamyltransferase 1 (GGT1), promotes ferroptosis resistance, thereby supporting TNBC cell proliferation and metastasis [ ]. Currently, experimental data have shown that biomimetic magnetic nanoparticles composed of TGF-β inhibitors and other tumor-targeting components can induce ferroptosis in cancer cells [ ]. The TGF-β signaling pathway has been extensively studied in cancer biology and possesses a strong translational foundation, making it a highly promising target for ferroptosis-based therapeutic strategies against dormant cancer cells.

## Ferroptosis influences dormant cancer cells through lipid metabolism
Studies have shown that the escape of dormant cells is also linked to lipid metabolism. For instance, the fatty acid translocase CD36 can efficiently take up fatty acids and produce ATP, thereby promoting cancer cell proliferation [ – ]. In addition, cytochrome P450 epoxygenase can generate epoxyeicosatrienoic acids that facilitate tumor escape [ ]. Ferroptosis is also closely related to lipid metabolism. By modulating lipid metabolism pathways, ferroptosis can more effectively eliminate dormant cancer cells (Fig.  D).

## PUFA
PUFAs serve as both an essential substrate for ferroptosis and a lethal “poison” that selectively eradicates dormant cancer cells. PUFAs are converted into polyunsaturated phospholipids (PUFA-PLs) through the action of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) [ , ]. Iron oxidizes PUFA-PLs to L-OOH via both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms, and accumulation of L-OOH within cell membranes leads to ferroptosis [ ].
As lipid-storing organelles, lipid droplets play a crucial role in dormant cells by sequestering PUFAs and thereby neutralizing their ferroptotic toxicity. Lipid droplets can isolate and store excess lipids, preventing intracellular lipid peroxidation and lipotoxicity. They achieve this by esterifying free fatty acids and sequestering unsaturated fatty acids in their core [ ]. DGAT promotes the sequestration of PUFAs into lipid droplets, and dormant cells induce DGAT-dependent lipid droplet formation to sequester polyunsaturated fatty acids, thereby resisting lipid peroxidation [ ].
In some cancer cells with cell-cycle arrest and slow-cycling drug resistance, lipid droplets accumulate. After treatment with DGAT inhibitors (DGAT1 inhibitors: T863, A-922500; DGAT2 inhibitor: PF-06427878), PUFAs that would otherwise be stored in lipid droplets become dispersed in the cells and are converted into PUFA-PLs, making these cells more susceptible to ferroptosis. However, these inhibitors do not affect proliferating cells [ ], suggesting that they may be developed as drugs targeting dormant cells. Dormant breast cancer cells exhibit a higher rate of de novo lipogenesis. Compared with proliferating cells, the monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) content in their cell membranes is increased and incorporated into the membranes, thereby conferring resistance to lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. In addition, dormant cells resist ferroptosis by activating MUFAs through ACSL3 and incorporating them into cell membranes [ ].

## COX-2
Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), an enzyme upregulated in various tumors, also functions as a ferroptosis-inhibitory gene and is particularly associated with poor prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [ , ]. COX-2 is involved in lipid metabolism, and its activity is associated with tumor initiation, progression, and response to treatment. Overexpression of COX-2 enables cancer cells to survive in nutrient-deprived environments through metabolic reprogramming and its role in lipid metabolism. COX-2 inhibitors have been shown to enhance the antitumor effects of ferroptosis-inducing agents, indicating that COX-2 may play a role in regulating ferroptosis sensitivity. Inhibiting COX-2 expression can also block activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby inducing ferroptosis [ , ]. However, treatment with COX-2 inhibitors can promote cancer cell dormancy. The COX-2 selective inhibitor NS-398 significantly inhibits proliferation of the hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 in a dose-dependent manner, increasing the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and decreasing the proportion in the S phase [ ].

## NUPR1
Mucin protein 1 (NUPR1), also known as p8 or COM1, is a stress-induced transcription factor that is overexpressed in various cancers and is associated with cancer cell survival and drug resistance [ – ]. NUPR1 is a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, blocking this process by regulating iron metabolism and preventing iron accumulation and iron-induced oxidative damage [ ]. Studies have shown that NUPR1 induces resistance to ferroptosis by transcriptionally activating the expression of lipocalin 2 (LCN2), thereby reducing iron-induced oxidative damage [ ]. Downregulation of NUPR1 can promote cell-cycle arrest in H1299 cells, significantly inhibit their proliferation and colony formation, and induce apoptosis [ ].

## DPP4
DPP4 (dipeptidyl peptidase IV) is a ferroptosis-inhibiting gene that antagonizes ferroptosis in cancer cells [ ]. DPP4 is also one of the markers of CML stem cells. A study in CML demonstrated the therapeutic potential of targeting C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) in patients with ponatinib-resistant CML. Ponatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has limitations in eradicating dormant CML stem cells. Activation of CXCR2 increases DPP4 activity while inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway cascade [ ]. In addition to CML, DPP4 is also upregulated in dormant colorectal cancer stem cells [ ]. Interestingly, this feature has yielded different findings in gastric cancer: aggressive gastric cancer stem cells are DPP4 positive, whereas dormant gastric cancer stem cells are DPP4 negative [ ]. In summary, both direct and indirect evidence now link ferroptosis and lipid metabolism-targeting strategies to dormant cancer-cell eradication, underscoring their translational promise.

## Ferroptosis kills dormant cancer cells by promoting autophagy
Autophagy plays a crucial role in dormant cancer cells and is activated in dormant cells across multiple cancer types, including ovarian cancer, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), and osteosarcoma [ ]. Through multiple molecular mechanisms, it maintains the survival, metabolic homeostasis, and reactivation capacity of dormant cells. Studies have shown that dormant cancer cells exhibit significantly enhanced autophagic activity, characterized by an increased number of autophagosomes and autolysosomes, as well as upregulated expression of autophagy-related genes such as LC3, ATG4, ATG5, ATG7, and BECN1. Autophagy helps dormant cells survive in adverse environments such as nutrient deprivation and hypoxia by clearing damaged mitochondria, limiting the accumulation of ROS, and regulating metabolic pathways (e.g., by promoting glycolysis and glutaminolysis). In addition, autophagy plays a key role in the entry into, maintenance of, and transition of dormant cells back to a proliferative state. It can selectively degrade key proteins (such as Pfkfb3) and P-bodies to regulate the cell cycle and dormancy [ ]. Inhibiting autophagy by knocking out ATG5 or ATG7, or by deleting a single allele of Beclin 1, can suppress breast tumor recurrence, and autophagy inhibition leads to the death of dormant breast cancer cells in vivo [ ]. A similar phenomenon has been observed in pancreatic cancer cells, where uPAR can inhibit autophagy, promote pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, and prevent cell dormancy [ ]. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cells treated with cisplatin, mitophagy-induced autophagy leads to a dormant state, protecting cells from the toxic effects of cisplatin, whereas the combination of autophagy inhibitors and cisplatin can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy [ ]. The activation of autophagy is closely related to inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Redox can activate autophagy through the integrin/PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis and transcriptional control, thereby maintaining dormancy [ , , ]. Interestingly, oxidative stress can also activate multiple mechanisms that inhibit autophagy, such as weakening of autophagy related 4B (ATG4B) activity after redox-mediated modifications at Cys292 and Cys361, thereby promoting cells to exit dormancy [ , ].
Ferroptosis is also closely linked to autophagy. Multiple forms of autophagy, including ferritinophagy, mitophagy, lipophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), participate in ferroptosis [ ]. These two pathways share common regulatory networks that are critically involved in cancer pathogenesis and therapy. In CMA, the heat shock protein HSPA8 binds to GPX4 and recruits it to lysosomes for degradation via lysosome-associated membrane protein 2A (LAMP2A). Another heat shock protein, HSP90, stabilizes LAMP2A and further promotes GPX4 degradation [ ].
Beyond ferroptosis, the above-mentioned pathways (redox regulation, lipid metabolism, and TGF-β signaling) are also closely related to autophagy. TGF-β is a potent inducer of autophagy in cancer cells through both Smad-dependent and Smad-independent mechanisms. TGF-β-induced autophagy, in turn, feeds back to modulate TGF-β signaling and promotes EMT, angiogenesis, immune suppression, and metastasis [ ]. Redox imbalance and ROS are key regulators of autophagy and affect both its initiation and degradation. Stress-induced autophagy helps restore redox homeostasis in cancer cells and influences their survival and response to therapy [ ]. Lipid metabolism and autophagy are also tightly interconnected in cancer. Different fatty acid species can either activate or inhibit autophagy [ ]. In addition, lipophagy promotes ferroptosis by degrading lipid droplets and releasing free polyunsaturated fatty acids, which are prone to lipid peroxidation [ ].
Fasting triggers autophagy in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, and the concomitant induction of ferroptosis effectively eradicates the dormant subset. Under fasting conditions, activation of autophagy may promote ferroptosis by increasing intracellular iron and generating lipid ROS, which helps ferroptosis-inducing agents (such as erastin and RSL3) enhance their cytotoxic effects on CRC cells, including those in quiescent and drug-resistant states. Studies have shown that fasting can alter the tumor microenvironment, which may affect the metabolism and growth of cancer cells. Fasting was found to more effectively slow tumor growth in vitro and in vivo, but did not increase the sensitivity of CRC cells to 5-fluorouracil/oxaliplatin (5-FU/OXA)-induced apoptosis. During fasting, colorectal cancer cells transition from an active proliferative state to a quiescent state. mRNA sequencing indicated that fasting had the most pronounced impact on the ferroptosis pathway, with increased expression of ferroptosis-related genes [ ].

## Therapeutic strategies for targeting ferroptosis in dormant cancer cells
Studies have shown that strategies to eliminate dormant cells via ferroptosis can be achieved through different molecular pathways. Many proteins, enzymes, and small molecules associated with ferroptosis or dormancy may be functionally linked to both processes. Therefore, they may represent important targets for future ferroptosis-based therapies against dormant cells (Tables  and ). 

## Redox-targeting strategy
Collectively, disrupting the redox homeostasis of dormant cancer cells markedly sensitizes them to ferroptotic cell death. A well-validated armamentarium of inhibitors, ranging from GPX4 antagonists to NRF2 blockers, already provides a robust pharmacological scaffold for future therapeutic development. GPX4 plays a crucial role in ferroptosis by detoxifying lipid peroxides and thereby limiting lipid peroxidation-induced ferroptosis. Experiments have confirmed that the death of dormant cells caused by GPX4 inhibition is typical ferroptosis [ ]. Using GPX4 inhibitors to induce ferroptotic death of dormant cancer cells may represent an effective therapeutic strategy. Current preclinical studies have begun encapsulating agents such as RSL3 and ML210 into nanocarriers to enhance tumor uptake, reduce systemic exposure, and enable combination with radiotherapy, DNA-damaging agents, or immune checkpoint inhibitors [ – ]. In addition, combining GPX4 inhibitors with lipid metabolism regulators, such as DGAT inhibitors, may enhance therapeutic efficacy by multitargeted induction of ferroptosis [ ]. Withaferin A (WA), a natural anticancer agent, plays an important role in high-risk neuroblastoma and breast cancer, where different doses of WA either inactivate GPX4 or activate NRF2. NRF2 promotes the expression of HO-1, and excessive HO-1 increases intracellular labile iron, thereby leading to ferroptosis [ , ]. Therefore, using GPX4 inhibitors to kill dormant cancer cells through ferroptosis may be an effective therapeutic strategy. NRF2 promotes the production of GSH and serves as one of the main antioxidant defenses in dormant cells. In addition, NRF2 inhibitors can sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis. ML385 markedly enhances RSL3-induced ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction. By inhibiting the NRF2–SLC7A11 axis, it increases the sensitivity of esophageal squamous carcinoma cells to radiotherapy and ferroptosis, and thus shows promise for combination with ferroptosis-sensitizing agents [ , ]. Brusatol downregulates key regulators of glutathione synthesis and cystine transport, such as glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) and SLC7A11, in models of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, ovarian cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, osteosarcoma, and oral squamous cell carcinoma, thereby inducing ferroptosis and markedly enhancing the efficacy of cisplatin, doxorubicin, or targeted therapies when used in combination [ – ]. Luteolin can be combined with ferroptosis inducers to trigger ferroptosis in colorectal and other tumor models, and has already entered clinical testing in tongue cancer (NCT03288298) [ ]. Sapanisertib, a dual mTORC1/2 inhibitor, is in phase I/II clinical trials and appears particularly suitable for tumor subtypes with persistent mTOR/NRF2 activation and high resistance to conventional therapies [ , ]. We speculate that NRF2 inhibitors may also play an important role in the treatment of dormant cancer cells.

## TGF-β modulation
The TGF-β axis exerts bidirectional control over tumor-cell dormancy and ferroptosis, positioning it as a tractable therapeutic target. TGF-β1 agonists, such as fresolimumab, can disrupt cellular quiescence while simultaneously sensitizing malignant cells to ferroptotic stress. In hepatocellular carcinoma, TGF-β1 primes cancer cells for ferroptosis, and co-administration of ferroptosis inducers with TGF-β1 has shown preclinical efficacy in breast cancer models. On the inhibitory side of TGF-β regulation, fresolimumab and trabedersen, two TGF-β-targeting antibodies, have completed multiple phase I/II trials, showing acceptable safety and encouraging long-term survival signals [ ]. Glycoprotein A repetitions predominant (GARP) is a receptor on the cancer cell surface that binds latent TGF-β1, concentrates and activates TGF-β1, and thereby promotes immune evasion, fibrosis, and therapy resistance [ ]. JYB1907 (NCT05821595) and HLX6018 (NCT06310746) are monoclonal antibodies directed against the GARP–TGF-β1 complex that block the release of active TGF-β1 and are now in early-phase clinical trials. The TGF-β signaling pathway has a strong translational foundation and is a promising key target for designing ferroptosis-based strategies against dormant cancer cells [ , , ].

## Lipid-metabolic reprogramming
Dormant cancer cells display a lipid-metabolic profile that differs markedly from that of their proliferative counterparts, supporting adaptive changes that both sustain dormancy and increase resistance to ferroptosis. Precision targeting of key lipid regulators, including DGAT, COX-2, and NUPR1, offers a mechanistic route to resensitize dormant tumors to ferroptotic elimination. Pharmacological blockade of DGAT releases PUFAs from lipid droplet sequestration, leading to intracellular accumulation of these ferroptotic substrates and thereby lowering the death threshold of dormant cells.
PF-04620110, AZD7687, and pradigastat are highly selective DGAT1 inhibitors that have entered clinical development for metabolic diseases [ – ]. Although their early clinical application has been limited by gastrointestinal toxicity, recent cancer-related studies indicate that DGAT1 inhibition can remodel tumor lipid metabolism, reduce lipid droplet formation, and, in combination with genotoxic agents such as olaparib, promote free fatty acid accumulation and lipotoxicity, thereby potentially inducing ferroptosis-related cell death [ , ]. Ervogastat is a DGAT2 inhibitor currently in phase II clinical trials for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, where it markedly reduces hepatic fat deposition and circulating triglyceride levels. It may in the future be repurposed for cancer therapy to disrupt the lipid droplet-centered protective barrier against lipid peroxidation [ , ]. COX-2, frequently overexpressed across malignancies, endows cancer cells with metabolic plasticity under nutrient stress, and its inhibition synergistically enhances the activity of ferroptosis-inducing agents, underscoring COX-2 as a key regulator of ferroptosis susceptibility. Recent studies have shown that, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, celecoxib can enhance ferroptosis induced by GPX4 inhibitors, such as RSL3, or independently trigger ROS-dependent cell death [ , ]. Celecoxib is an approved selective COX-2 inhibitor and has long been investigated in multiple cancer-related clinical trials, for example, in colorectal cancer (NCT00005094). Conversely, NUPR1 operates as an endogenous brake on ferroptosis; genetic or pharmacologic suppression of NUPR1 not only enforces dormancy but also permits ferroptotic eradication of latent tumor clones.

## Autophagy-coupled activation
Autophagy acts as a double-edged regulator of dormant cancer cell survival and ferroptotic susceptibility. Its controlled activation can be harnessed to potentiate ferroptotic eradication of quiescent tumor clones. In colorectal cancer, fasting-induced autophagy increases intracellular iron pools and enhances lipid ROS, thereby sensitizing both cycling and therapy-refractory cells to ferroptosis inducers.
Ferritinophagy is a nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated form of autophagy that selectively degrades ferritin and represents a key targetable node for regulating ferroptosis in cancer cells [ ]. Upregulation of sirtuin 6 (SIRT6) and prostate apoptosis response 4 (Par-4), or downregulation of COPI coat complex subunit zeta 1 (COPZ1), promotes ferritinophagy and sensitizes tumors to ferroptosis [ – ]. 4-Octyl itaconate can also induce ferritinophagy and thereby kill drug-resistant retinoblastoma cells [ ]. Temozolomide, when combined with lysosomal/autophagy inhibitors, enhances ferroptosis in GSCs by disrupting iron metabolism and redox homeostasis [ ]. The NRF2–autophagy signaling axis has important therapeutic potential in cancer and is closely associated with tumor progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance. Various natural products and small-molecule compounds can upregulate or inhibit NRF2 to modulate ROS levels, autophagy, proliferative capacity, and drug sensitivity in cancer cells, often in combination with other agents. To date, more than 100 clinical studies have been registered targeting NRF2 and autophagy in cancer [ ]. HIF-1α is a major upstream regulator of autophagy in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment. It activates protective autophagy and mitophagy by increasing the expression of autophagy-related proteins such as BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3), Beclin-1, and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) [ ]. Different anticancer drugs can bidirectionally modulate the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway. Some agents block HIF-1α-driven protective autophagy to inhibit tumor growth, whereas others induce autophagy and apoptosis in cancer cells by enhancing BNIP3 expression and related signals [ ]. Consequently, pharmacologic modulation of autophagy, through either activation or calibrated inhibition, offers a tractable strategy to modulate ferroptosis sensitivity in dormant cancer cells and to expand the therapeutic repertoire against minimal residual disease.

## Conclusion and perspectives
Dormant cancer cells, characterized by robust antioxidant defenses and strong drug resistance, are a major cause of cancer recurrence and metastasis. Ferroptosis, a novel form of programmed cell death, shows unique potential in targeting cancer cells. Redox regulation, lipid metabolic reprogramming, autophagy activation, and TGF-β signaling all play key roles in both dormant cancer cells and ferroptosis, suggesting a close mechanistic link between them. This review systematically explores the connections between ferroptosis and dormant cancer cells, and future cancer therapies may leverage these pathways to selectively eliminate dormant cancer cells.
In clinical practice, leveraging these pathways to develop single-agent or combination therapies is likely to become an important strategy for inducing ferroptosis and eliminating dormant cells. However, there is currently a lack of literature directly exploring this approach, and basic research in this field remains limited. This review discusses the prospects of such strategies, but more in-depth studies are needed in several directions. In summary, ferroptosis offers a novel and promising perspective for eliminating dormant cancer cells. Through multidimensional mechanistic analysis, technological innovation, and clinical translation, ferroptosis-targeted therapies may help overcome the challenges of cancer recurrence and metastasis and provide patients with more durable survival benefits.
