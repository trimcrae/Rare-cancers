## Abstract
Quiescent cancer cells (QCCs) are considered an origin of cancer recurrence and present an ongoing challenge in cancer treatment. Following anti-cancer therapy, non-proliferating, therapy-resistant QCCs have been detected as subsets of residual cancer cells within patients. Clinicians and researchers widely believe that these minimal residual QCCs can eventually regain proliferative activity, acting as “seeds” for cancer recurrence. Despite the significance of QCCs, tracing and analyzing these microscopic residual cells in vivo models and patients remains extremely challenging, limiting our understanding. Consequently, reliable biomarkers for QCCs and the mechanisms underlying their ‘reversible’ reactivation are still poorly understood. This knowledge gap has hindered the development of diagnostics and targeted therapies for QCCs. The absence of diagnostic tools for QCCs also complicates predicting cancer relapse and determining the optimal duration of anti-cancer treatment. Moreover, without strategies to eradicate QCCs, preventing cancer recurrence remains elusive. This review aims to provide an overview of the current understanding of QCCs and related diagnostic efforts in both basic and clinical research. Additionally, potential strategies for the targeted elimination of QCCs are explored. Focused research and clinical attention to diagnosing and eradicating residual QCCs are essential for preventing cancer recurrence and ultimately conquering this deadly disease.
Keywords: Cancer recurrence, Diagnostics, Minimal residual disease (MRD), Quiescent cancer cells (QCCs), Targeted therapy

## INTRODUCTION
The development of anticancer chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy has significantly improved treatment outcomes and enhanced survival rates for several cancer types ( ). However, despite these advancements, increases in survival rates remain modest or minimal for certain cancers ( ). Notably, these incurable cancers often exhibit alarmingly high recurrence rates within 1 to 5 years: lung cancer (∼60%) ( , ), pancreatic cancer (∼50%) ( , ), liver cancer (50-80%) ( ), cholangiocarcinoma (60-70%) ( , ), acute myeloid leukemia (∼50%) ( ), gastric cancer (∼60%) ( ), colorectal cancer (∼25%) ( ), etc. ( ). Furthermore, some cancers, such as ovarian cancer (∼85%) ( , ) and glioblastoma (100%) ( , ), have an almost inevitable recurrence ( ). These findings underscore the urgent need for strategies to prevent cancer recurrence, which is essential for achieving curative treatment and improving patient survival.
 In some cases of recurrent cancer, the disease is even initially diagnosed as being in complete remission following first-line treatment, however, recurrence is eventually observed. These high recurrence rates, combined with incomplete diagnostics, can significantly diminish patients’ hopes for a cure. Furthermore, to mitigate the risk of relapse and enhance treatment efficacy, adjuvant therapies are often administered over extended periods. However, prolonged treatment can negatively impact patients’ quality of life and, in some cases, lead to overtreatment.
Given the persistently high recurrence rates, a fundamental cure requires the precise identification and elimination of small populations of residual cancer cells that survive initial treatment and later repopulate, leading to recurrence. A defining characteristic of these minimal residual cancer cells, formally named minimal residual disease (MRD) ( ), is their ability to enter a quiescent state, a phenomenon known as cancer cell quiescence ( - ). Many cancer researchers and clinicians believe that these small, residual quiescent cancer cells (QCCs) serve as the “real seeds” for cancer recurrence, making them a key target for strategies aimed at preventing relapse.

## QUIESCENT CANCER CELLS: PHANTOMS OF MENACE
The phenomenon of cancer cell quiescence, also referred to as cancer cell dormancy, is widely recognized as a key mechanism by which cancer cells evade hostile conditions such as anticancer therapies. By arresting or slowing down their cell cycle at the G0 or G1 phase, cancer cells are thought to survive and endure chemotherapy-induced genotoxic and metabolic stress ( - ). It is also believed that these specific slow-cycling or arrested cell populations are selectively enriched in such harsh environments. These QCCs have been identified through the absence of cell cycle markers, such as Ki67 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), in blood or tissue samples from patients following anticancer therapies. Numerous studies have consistently shown that, after therapy, remaining QCCs exhibit drug resistance ( - ), escape immune surveillance ( - ), and act as repopulating, stem-like cancer cells ( - ). Thus, these “phantoms of menace”—the invisible threats posed by QCCs—are now widely accepted as the origin of recurrent cancer.
However, tracing these hidden QCCs in in vivo animal models or patient samples remains exceptionally challenging due to their extremely small numbers and the lack of reliable QCC-specific markers that can be exclusively and easily traced in live animals and human bodies ( , ). Additionally, the limited availability of suitable in vivo cancer models has hindered progress in identifying biomarkers and understanding the reversible regulatory mechanisms governing QCCs ( , ). Importantly, current dignostics for MRD cannot detect QCCs. Existing methods either directly detect circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in the bloodstream ( ) or indirectly identify cancer cells through their shed traces, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) ( ), microRNA (miRNA) ( ), or extracellular vesicles (EVs) ( ). Furthermore, none of these approaches meet the accuracy required to reliably diagnose recurrent cancers.
Therefore, there remains an unmet need to develop advanced diagnostic tools capable of specifically identifying residual QCCs—the true seeds of cancer recurrence. Such advancements will enable precision strategies to effectively target and eliminate these remaining cancer cells while avoiding overtreatment and improving patient outcomes.

## CHARACTERISTICS OF QUIESCENT CANCER CELLS
QCCs exhibit several distinct and unique characteristics that differentiate them from other cancer cells. The first is their ability to enter a state of cellular quiescence (G0-G1 arrest in the cell cycle), allowing them to survive for prolonged periods with minimal cellular activity ( - ). Importantly, QCCs also retain the capacity to reactivate under specific conditions, enabling them to persist in a prolonged quiescent state ( - ). Another hallmark of QCCs is their resistance to conventional anticancer therapies. Most anticancer treatments primarily target actively dividing cells, whereas QCCs—characterized by low cellular activity and minimal proliferation—are able to evade these therapies and survive better than other cancer cells. Thus, entering a quiescent state may represent an adaptive survival mechanism that allows cancer cells to withstand treatment. Indeed, the therapy resistance of QCCs has been demonstrated in multiple in vitro and in vivo studies across various cancer types ( - ). The immune evasion capability of QCCs further contributes to their persistence. QCCs can remain undetected in the body for extended periods, evading destruction by immune cells ( - ). Recent studies have identified specific molecular mechanisms that validate how QCCs evade immune surveillance, shedding light on their ability to persist under immune pressure.
It is worth noting that QCCs have been referred to by various names in different studies. Due to their slow-cycling nature and long label-retention properties, QCCs are sometimes called slow-cycling cancer cells ( ), latency-competent cancer cells (LCCs) ( ), or label-retaining cells (LRCs) ( , ). When circulating in the bloodstream, they are referred to as CTCs ( ). When detaching from primary tumors and colonizing metastatic sites while remaining quiescent, they are described as disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) ( , ). Additionally, QCCs may overlap with or share characteristics of drug-tolerant persister cells (DTPs) ( ). While it remains challenging to clearly define and distinguish these populations, they all share, at least in part, the common feature of cancer cell quiescence. For example, CTCs in the bloodstream often exhibit a quiescent state. However, several critical questions arise: Do all circulating cancer cells possess the ability to transit between quiescent and active phases? What distinguishes the true “seed” of cancer recurrence among the numerous CTCs? In other words, can all circulating cancer cells cause recurrence under favorable conditions, or are only certain subsets capable of reactivation? It is well established that cancers of a certain size can release millions of cancer cells as CTCs, yet the majority of these cells perish, and only a small subset acts as the true “seeds” for recurrence ( ). Therefore, detecting CTCs alone in the bloodstream may be insufficient for identifying the hidden and rare population of QCCs responsible for cancer recurrence.

## CLASSIFICATION OF QCCs
QCCs have the potential to reawaken and act as repopulating seeds for recurrent or metastatic cancers. It is essential to distinguish QCCs from aged cancer cells, which remain in a state of permanent quiescence without the ability to reactivate, and from dying cancer cells (undergoing apoptosis or necrosis), which may exhibit temporary quiescence before being eliminated. While these other types of cancer cells may enter a quiescent state, they distinctly lack the reactivation capacity that defines QCCs. Therefore, developing precise definitions and exclusive biomarkers to identify the true “living seed” QCCs is crucial for distinguishing them from “dead seed” cancer cells ( ). Recently, insightful reviews have begun to classify QCCs ( , ); however, these distinctions remain imperfect and have yet to be experimentally validated. Further research involving QCC sorting and detailed analyses using in vivo QCC tracing models is required to refine the definition of QCCs and develop reliable, precise markers for their identification.
 

## Ture living seed for recurrent cancers 
The real, often unseen, danger lies in the reawakening of QCCs. Cancer cells in a quiescent state that remain permanently dormant—whether through aging, death, or functioning as non-dividing cells, akin to terminally differentiated cells in normal development—cannot serve as seeds for recurrent cancer. Therefore, the capacity for reactivation is likely the most critical criterion for defining true QCCs.
However, identifying the specific subset of QCCs with the ability to reactivate remains a challenge. Current research largely relies on retrospective analyses of recurrent cancers to infer the properties of QCCs following reactivation. To move forward, the development of advanced lineage tracing tools or models capable of capturing the precise moment of QCC reactivation will be essential ( , ). Additionally, these models should integrate single-cell sequencing techniques, genetic manipulation, and supplementary culture systems to identify the critical factors involved in the reactivation process.

## Senescent cancer cells 
Senescent cancer cells are characterized by a permanent state of cell cycle arrest, minimal cellular activity, and an inability to divide ( ). Despite their lack of proliferative activity, senescent cancer cells can still actively contribute to tumor malignancy by secreting senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors that promote the progression or resistance of surrounding cancer cells through paracrine mode ( ). These senescent cells also exhibit a G0 phase quiescent state and do not undergo cell proliferation, making them a potential type of QCCs ( , ).
Currently, no studies have clearly distinguished senescent cancer cells from QCC populations. Due to the small number of QCCs, these cells have often been analyzed through bulk sequencing. To accurately analyze and understand the mechanisms of QCC reactivation, it is essential to differentiate senescent cells from the QCC population. Established markers for senescent cells, such as SA-β-gal (Senescence-associated β-galactosidase) and p16, can be used for this purpose (SA-β-gal+, Ki67−: senescent cells; SA-β-gal−, Ki67−: QCCs) ( ). Additionally, advancements in single-cell analysis techniques will allow for the selective exclusion of senescent cells, enabling more precise investigations into the behavior and reactivation mechanisms of QCCs.

## Temporarily quiescent, but ultimately perising cells: apoptotic and necrotic cells 
Various dying cells, such as those undergoing apoptosis or necrosis, may sometimes be misclassified as QCCs. However, while these cells can enter a temporary quiescent phase, they do not have the capacity for reactivation and are ultimately eliminated. Apoptotic and necrotic cells can be distinguished from true QCCs using specific apoptosis markers ( ), such as cleaved caspase-3/7, cleaved PARP, or necroptosis markers ( ), including phosphorylated RIP, RIP3, and phosphorylated MLKL ( ).
Moreover, several recently identified cell death-related markers could potentially help distinguish QCCs, provided these markers can clearly identify cancer cells destined for elimination. Notably, some cells exhibiting weak expression of these death markers might evade elimination and retain the ability to reactivate if their stress or damage levels remain below a critical threshold. Therefore, it is not possible to completely rule out whether cells with weak expression of these markers could act as reactivated cells, and further experiments will be necessary.

## Limitations in distinguishing QCCs
Even after distinguishing senescent and dying cancer cells from the QCC population, it remains impossible to determine when and which QCCs will awaken. Additionally, cells in the intermediate stages of senescence or apoptosis—those expressing these markers at low levels without surpassing the threshold for aging or death—may still retain the ability to reactivate. Current single-cell analyses have primarily focused on CTCs, which are easier to collect compared to other conditions of QCCs. While CTCs are abundant, only a small fraction has demonstrated the capacity to reactivate. Moreover, it remains unclear whether capturing CTCs truly represents QCC cell clusters. The characteristics of quiescent cancer cells can also vary significantly depending on the cancer type and the genetic mutations involved. Therefore, more comprehensive lineage tracing tools, combined with single-cell analysis of QCCs, are needed to gain insights that address these challenges.
Another important consideration is that QCCs may not be the sole source of cancer recurrence; other cell types may also act as repopulating seeds. Drug-tolerant persistent cells ( ) and recently identified polyploid giant cancer cells ( , ) have also been implicated in recurrence. Further research is required to determine the extent of overlap between these cells and QCCs, as well as the shared characteristics they may possess.

## MOLECULAR MECHANISMS REGULATING QCCs
The regulatory mechanisms of QCCs have been studied for many years, leading to the identification of various factors, with many still being elucidated. Among the key pathways, p38 MAPK signaling, cytokines, morphogens, the DREAM complex, and the WNT/β-catenin pathway have been shown to play critical roles in maintaining and reactivating QCCs. These findings have been validated by multiple studies across diverse cancer contexts. Below is a brief summary of the key mechanisms underlying QCC regulation ( ).
 

## p38 MAPK signaling in QCCs
Early studies on QCCs using quiescent populations of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) revealed that p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) plays a critical role in maintaining cancer cell quiescence ( , ). The balance between p38 MAPK and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling is essential for both inducing quiescence and reactivating the cells. Specifically, high p38 MAPK and low ERK signaling are associated with cellular quiescence ( , ).
This key signaling modules is also linked to both external and intrinsic pathways. For example, activation of the urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) induces p38 MAPK activation, promoting cellular quiescence ( ). In contrast, activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and Src kinase induces ERK activation, promoting cancer cell proliferation ( , ). Importantly, the activation of uPAR and FAK/Src kinase is closely related to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling ( , ). Additionally, intrinsic pathways such as endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress/unfolded protein response (UPR) and autophagy are closely linked to p38 MAPK activation ( , ), suggesting that QCCs respond to both external and intrinsic changes through this master regulatory mechanism.
Addtionally, p38 MAPK activation is well-known for mediating cellular senescence, which halts the cell cycle in the G0 phase ( ). This implies that both QCCs and cellular senescence share conserved mechanisms for maintaining cellular quiescence.

## Interaction with the tumor microenvironment 
The tumor microenvironment (TME), including interactions with ECM, immune cells, and stromal cells, plays a key role in regulating cancer cell quiescence ( - ). For instance, integrin α5β1 and ECM fibronectin fibrils lead to ERK activation ( , ). Additionally, it has been reported that type I collagen deposition in fibrotic conditions induces awakening from the quiescent state ( , ).
Cytokines, morphogens, and growth factors are also closely related to the regulation of QCCs. TGF-β2 contributes to maintaining HNSCC cancer cell quiescence in the bone marrow (BM) through the activation of p38 MAPK and induction of p27 ( ). Similarly, stromal cell-secreted BMP-7 forms a quiescent niche in the BM, maintaining the quiescence of disseminated prostate cancer cells ( ). Additionally, interferon-γ (IFN-γ) plays a dual role in the activation or suppression of QCCs, depending on the context ( ). Immune cell interactions with QCCs are also crucial factors in regulating the ‘reversible’ activation of QCCs. For example, recent elegant study has shown maintains breast cancer cells in a quiescent state in the liver. When hepatic stellate cells secrete CXCL12, it inhibits NK cell activation, allowing breast QCCs to reawaken ( ). Another study demonstrated that cancer cells can block T-cell attacks by creating immune-suppressive hypoxic conditions ( ). These studies clearly show the active interaction between QCCs and their environment.
Additionally, the presence of a hostile microenvironment, such as hypoxia or nutrient deprivation, is well known to induce cellular quiescence ( - ).

## WNT signaling in QCCs
WNT signaling is known to regulate cell activity in various physiological and disease contexts as a gradient morphogen ( , ). In particular, WNT is recognized as an important niche factor that promotes cancer growth ( , ). As such, WNT signaling has been considered to play a significant role in the reactivation of QCCs.
For example, WNT signaling has been reported to be necessary for the dissemination of Her2+ breast cancer cells to the early lung ( ). Also, autocrine inhibition of disseminated latency-competent cancer cells occurs through the secretion of the WNT signaling inhibitor DKK1, which slows down the cell cycle and downregulates the immunoattractive ligand ULBP, thereby enabling the cancer cells to evade immune attacks ( ). Furthermore, inhibition of WNT signaling by non-canonical WNT5a/ROR/SIAH2 signaling also induces and maintains the quiescence of prostate cancer cells in bone ( ).
Although there is still limited research on the involvement of WNT signaling in QCC regulation, it is evident that this crucial pathway plays a key role in many cancer contexts. Further studies are expected to provide more insights into this important signaling pathway.

## The DREAM complex in QCCs
The DREAM complex (DP, RB-like, E2F, and MuvB complex) is a multiprotein complex that plays a crucial role in regulating the cell cycle, particularly in inducing and maintaining cell quiescence (G0 phase) ( , ). Similar to the well-known G1/S cycle regulation by RB-E2F, the DREAM complex, which includes the RB-like protein 2 (p130: RBL2) and the suppressive E2F4. Once the DREAM complex forms, it localizes to the promoters of nearly 1,000 target cell cycle genes, suppressing their transcription and resulting in the cells entering a quiescent phase ( , ). The major role of the DREAM complex in quiescence has led to the hypothesis that it may play a pivotal role in quiescent cancer cells. Indeed, it has been shown that the formation of the DREAM complex is essential for regulating cancer cell quiescence in various cancer contexts, including gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) ( ), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) ( ), and ovarian cancer ( ).
Importantly, similar to RB, the binding of p130 to E2Fs is regulated by CDK2 and CDK4/6 ( , ). This suggests that the efficacy of CDK4/6 inhibitors, a recently clinically approved anticancer agent, may be linked to the control of QCCs, as will be discussed later.

## Retinoic acid and NR2F1 signaling in QCCs
The RAR signaling pathway mediated by retinoic acid has long been known to induce cell quiescence ( , ). In fact, the effect of retinoic acid on RAR-mediated quiescence in quiescent cancer cells has been reported ( ). In a study using HNSCC quiescent cancer cells, it was found that quiescence induction by SOX9 and RAR occurred through retinoic acid-mediated upregulation of NR2F1.
Additionally, several studies have reported that NR2F1 activating agents can help maintain the quiescent network ( , ). Although it has not yet been fully elucidated whether this effect is significant in cancers other than SOX9-positive epidermal and lung cancers, the potential therapeutic applications of retinoic acid suggest that this mechanism will be importantly explored in the future.

## Other pathways are in regulating QCCs 
New signals regulating dormant cancer cells are continually being discovered and reported. Key processes such as epigenetic regulators ( - ), autophagy ( ), ER stress ( ), and, more recently, STING signaling ( ) have been identified as crucial regulators of QCCs. Due to space limitation, detailed discussions of these mechanisms are not included here.

## DIAGNOSTICS OF QCCS

## Current diagnostics markers for QCCs
Various reporters have been developed to specifically label QCCs. For instance, QCCs can be defined by the absence of cell proliferation markers such as Ki67 and PCNA, combined with the absence of cell death markers like TUNEL ( ). Additionally, tools like Pyronin Y, a cationic dye that intercalates with RNA, enable fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analyses to distinguish QCCs based on their low mRNA content ( ). Furthermore, live cell cycle-tracing genetic reporters such as DHB-mVenus ( ), mVenus-p27K(−) ( ), and Fucci2 ( ) have been developed and are actively used to label QCCs.
Despite these advancements, significant limitations persist in the application of these QCC markers for diagnostics. Current analyses often require supplementary methods, including marker staining, the creation of reporter cell lines, or specialized detection techniques. For example, identifying QCCs based on the absence of proliferation markers or low mRNA content requires fixed cells, which can only be processed using FACS. Similarly, cell cycle-tracing tools are not applicable to patient samples because they require additional genetic manipulation to induce reporters. These challenges limit the practicality and reliability of these systems for diagnosing QCCs.
Ideally, the identification of QCC-specific cell surface receptors or unique combinations of receptor repertoires would significantly improve QCC detection. However, while several markers for QCCs have been identified, their specificity remains limited. Moreover, most of these markers are not cell surface proteins, further complicating their use. Thus, no specific markers or reliable diagnostic tools currently exist for QCCs. The identification of QCCs primarily relies on histological analysis or indirect and sporadic detection among CTCs.

## Identification of QCCs in patient’s tissue and blood
In histological sections or blood samples, residual cancer cells can be identified using specific antibodies that highlight cancer-specific markers or by their atypical appearance or size compared to surrounding normal cells. Detecting QCCs in blood (by sorting CTCs), typically involves the use of epithelial markers such as EpCAM, as blood cells do not express E-cadherins, which could distinguish epithelial cancers from blood cells ( , ). Other cell-type specific markers, such as CK5/7/8/18/19, also used as markers for certain cancer types. Those cell type markers are sometimes combined with mesenchymal markers like Vimentin and Twist, as metastatic or circulating cancers often show coexpression of these markers. Additionally, specific cancer markers such as HER2, ER, and DLL3 can be used in combination with EpCAM or mesenchymal markers for detecting minimal residual cancer both in blood or patient tissues ( , ). Finally, co-staining with proliferation markers like Ki67 and PCNA can further aid in identifying QCCs in CTCs or patient tissues. Importantly, cancers continue to evolve and change their expression patterns, meaning these markers cannot be validated with complete certainty to detect QCCs in that tissue and blood samples.
Recently, with the advancement of single-cell spatial transcriptomics, it is now possible to obtain transcriptomic information from residual microscopic QCCs in patient samples. This approach may facilitate the analysis of biomarkers and characteristics specific to these microscopic residual QCCs.

## Current efforts in diagnosing recurrent cancers
Liquid biopsy provides an easy, efficient, and patient-friendly non-invasive method for monitoring minimal residual cancers ( ). This approach also offers potential for early metastasis detection, evaluation of treatment efficacy, and tracking of disease progression. Recently, CTC detection using liquid biopsy has begun to be introduced as a standard method for predicting recurrence after cancer treatment in the United States ( , ). The CellSearchⓇ system, developed by Veridex, was recently FDA-approved, and its clinical applications are expanding. It has become an important tool for assessing a patient’s risk of recurrence and guiding chemotherapy decisions. Currently, CellSearch recommends ongoing treatment until 1 to 5 CTCs are detected in the blood post-chemotherapy, which has significantly improved survivals of cancer patients. Given its accuracy and potential scalability, prognostic analysis and chemotherapy decision-making based on CTC detection are likely to become global cancer treatment standards.
However, CTC detection still has limitations in diagnosing recurrent cancer. There is a high likelihood that CTCs found in the bloodstream are fragments of larger residual cancerous lesions. As such, this method cannot reliably detect very small tumors or single QCCs. Even a single microscopic reactive tumor can lead to cancer recurrence. Thus, CTC detection in blood serves only as an indirect method for assessing residual microscopic disease, and it remains insufficient for identifying intact microscopic residual cancer.
Another potential method is the detection of ctDNA ( ), miRNA ( ), and extracellular vesicles ( ) secreted by residual cancer cells or possibly by QCCs via liquid biopsy. However, ctDNA detection remains limited to certain cancers and specific mutations (e.g., KRAS or EGFR), and its detection resolution has not been fully validated. While residual cancer-specific miRNAs and EVs show promise, clinical trials are still in the early stages, and significant advancements are needed.
Lastly, cancer-specific protein detection using antibodies or enzymes in blood or urine has been explored ( ). However, efficient and highly reliable methods are not yet available in the field.

## Future directions in diagnostics of QCCs
In the long term, a more direct method to label or signal the presence of microscopic QCCs will be essential. This approach would require ( ) specific biomarkers for detecting microscopic residual cancer, ( ) antibodies or nano-transporters that target these biomarkers, and ( ) enzymatic technologies capable of signaling the presence of microscopic cancer in the blood. Importantly, these markers should ideally be cell surface markers.
Given that cancer cells originate from the body’s own cells, identifying cancer-specific receptors that are highly distinct from those of normal cells is challenging, except when mutations occur in the receptors. Instead, cancers may present specific repertoires of receptor combinations. I believe that utilizing these specific receptor repertoires, which allow for highly sensitive detection, will be a key step in developing QCC-targeting biphasic and multi-phasic antibodies. If such a method can be developed, treatment could continue until all microscopic residual cancer is eliminated, or targeted therapies could be administered. Additionally, these antibodies could be employed in antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) strategies to target QCCs.

## THERAPUTIC TARGETING STRATEGIES OF QCCS
As interest in QCCs has grown, several recent reviews have proposed strategies for targeting these cells ( , , ). These strategies can be categorized into three primary approaches: ( ) maintaining cancer cells in a quiescent state to prevent their activation (sleeping strategy), ( ) reactivating QCCs to enhance their responsiveness to anticancer therapies (wake-up strategy), and ( ) exploiting vulnerabilities specific to quiescent cancer cells (targeting QCC-specific vulnerabilities). In addition to these three approaches, I propose a fourth strategy: ( ) preventing cancer cells from entering the quiescent phase during anticancer therapy (blocking the entry into quiescence). This fourth approach builds upon research into DTPs, which have been extensively studied for their role in cancer resistance ( ). provides an overview of these strategies.
 

## Induced sleep strategy: a method to continuously keep cancer cells quiescence 
The induced sleep strategy aims to prevent cancer cells from waking up from a quiescent state. This approach has been supported by clinical evidence showing that extending the adjuvant treatment period after cancer treatment increases the anticancer effect and reduces cancer recurrence ( ). For instance, it has been observed that long-term low-dose tamoxifen treatment significantly suppresses recurrence in patients with ER-positive breast cancer ( ). Therefore, if an adjuvant treatment can be continuously taken without affecting the patient’s health or daily life, it could be a highly effective strategy. As discussed above, it has been reported that activating retinoic acid signaling helps maintain the quiescent state of cancer cells. If a small, consistent dose of retinoic acid can suppress cancer activity over time, it could be a viable option for certain cancers. Additionally, Pitavastatin, a drug that promotes the formation of DREAM complex in lung cancer, was recently discovered through drug repurposing techniques ( ). In this study, Pitavastatin was shown to induce cell quiescence in lung cancer cells and inhibit their growth in both in vitro and in vivo models. Pitavastatin, a third-generation statin, is already used to treat hyperlipidemia and can be taken daily ( ). Furthermore, several studies already have reported that long-term use of Statins may reduce the incidence of several cancers, including lung cancer ( ). While more validation studies are to evaluate efficacy and toxicity and toxicity in clinical settings, this finding represents a promising strategy for suppressing cancer recurrence by maintaining cancer cells in a quiescent phase.
It is important to note that the strategy of maintaining microscopic residual cancer in a quiescent state has essential prerequisites: it must be feasible for continuous use without causing significant toxicity or side effects. However, this strategy has fundamental limitations, as it cannot completely prevent recurrence. The awakening of even a small number of exceptional QCCs could have serious consequences.

## Wake-up strategy: awakening cancer cells to enhance the secondary anticancer effect
The wake-up strategy focuses on increasing the responsiveness of QCCs to secondary anticancer treatments by reactivating them. A study using ovarian cancer cells demonstrated that pre-treatment with DYRK1A inhibitors, such as Harmine and Indy, which block the formation of the DREAM complex responsible for maintaining cancer cell quiescence, followed by cisplatin treatment, promoted cancer cell death ( ).
This strategy to awaken QCCs can vary depending on how and when the treatments are combined, and the outcomes may differ. Therefore, extensive research is needed to optimize these combination approaches for various cancer contexts. Moreover, awakening cancer cells can potentially reactivate various quiescent normal cells and fully differentiated cells throughout the body, which may lead to unknown side effects from first-line anticancer drugs. There is also a risk of awakening unknown cancer cells with unpredictable behaviors. Consequently, much more foundational research is required to address these concerns.

## Strategy to give specific vulnerability to QCCs
Ideally, specifically targeting QCCS may be the most effective strategy. This can be achieved by inhibiting specific mechanisms essential for the survival of QCCs or by direct targeting them by ADC using quiescent cancer-specific markers. For instance, studies have demonstrated that inhibiting IGF-1R or GPX4—both essential for residual cancer cell survival—can reduce the number of minimal residual cells ( , ). Furthermore, identifying unique cell surface protein signatures or receptor repertoires specific to QCCs could enable their targeting through ADCs, combining antibodies with toxin payloads. Although a comprehensive multi-omics analysis of QCCs is still lacking, uncovering mechanisms that specifically regulate QCCs could provide a highly effective and precise targeting strategy.

## Block the sleep strategy: preventing the entry into quiescence during anticancer therapy
Preventing cancer cells from entering a quiescent state during treatment represents another promising strategy. Recent studies have identified several epigenetic factors that regulate DTPs and QCCs ( , ), playing key roles in either promoting or maintaining their quiescence. By targeting these factors during anticancer therapy, it may be possible to suppress the formation of QCCs and enhance treatment efficacy.
For example, the anticancer-enhancing effects of CDK4/6 inhibitors, when used in combination with primary anticancer drugs, highlight this potential. CDK4/6 inhibitors, which have recently been FDA-approved for advanced breast cancer combination therapy ( ), likely exert their effects by inhibiting the RB and DREAM complexes, both critical for maintaining quiescence ( , ), thereby potentially reducing QCC populations. Additionally, co-administration of epigenetic inhibitors such as KDMs, HDACs, and BET inhibitors with chemotherapy has been shown to decrease DTPs across various cancer contexts ( - ). Similarly, blocking retinoic acid signaling,—an essential pathway for quiescent cancer formation—or using agents like 5-Azacytidine to inhibit epigenetic modifications has been reported to prevent QCCs formation in HNSCC and lung cancer models ( ). Furthermore, several epigenetic inhibitors have been approved for use in combination therapies in clinical trials, underscoring the importance of epigenetic modifications in facilitating cancer cell entry into the quiescent-resistant state. Future studies exploring the relationships between these inhibitors, QCCs, and cancer recurrence rates could provide essential evidence to support this hypothesis.
In conclusion, combining inhibitors that prevent QCC formation with primary anticancer therapies could give substantial potential to enhance clinical outcomes and reduce recurrence rates. Further research will be necessary to optimize these strategies and minimize potential side effects. Additionally, identifying QCC-specific activators or mechanisms will be vital for developing more targeted and effective therapies.

## CONCLUDING REMARKS
Detecting and understanding the characteristics of QCCs is critical unmet need for guiding cancer treatment decisions, monitoring recurrence, and developing effective strategies to prevent relapse. Currently, there are no specific diagnostic tools available for identifying QCCs, and targeting them remains a significant challenge in clinical practice.
Nevertheless, ongoing efforts are paving the way for progress in this area. Emerging strategies include liquid biopsies for detecting CTCs, MRD-specific ctDNA, miRNAs, and EVs. Additionally, therapeutic approaches targeting QCCs are evolving with advances in research. For instance, epigenetic inhibitors, such as HDAC inhibitors, are now being incorporated into combination therapies for various cancers ( ). These drugs, which target histone modifiers, hold promise in overcoming cancer resistance and recurrence by addressing QCCs. Furthermore, combining QCC-modulating agents, such as retinoic acid and Pitavastatin, with standard anticancer therapies may offer novel strategies for targeting these cells. Advances in single-cell analysis are also expected to uncover QCC lineages and identify intrinsic pathways specific to their survival.
With these continuous advancements, I am optimistic that, in the near future, we will see the development of effective diagnostic tools and targeted therapies specifically designed to address QCCs.
