## Introduction
Metastatic spread is a leading cause of mortality in breast cancer patients, posing a significant challenge in clinical treatment [ , ]. In the traditional views, metastasis was believed to occur in the late stages of tumor progression. However, accumulating evidence from experimental models and clinical specimens suggests that cancer cells can spread to distant organs at an early stage of the disease, often preceding the detection of the primary tumor [ – ]. For instance, a small percentage of breast cancer patients diagnosed with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), an early noninvasive lesion, die of metastasis without ever developing a primary tumor [ , , ]. Therefore, comprehending the mechanisms underlying early dissemination is crucial for devising effective therapeutic strategies and improving patient outcomes. Unfortunately, the mechanisms by which early-stage cancer cells (ECCs) acquire migratory and invasive features for dissemination and colonization in distant organs remain poorly understood.
Experimental studies have revealed the pivotal role of Her2 (also known as Erbb2, erb-b2 receptor tyrosine kinase), a member of the human epidermal growth factor receptor family, in facilitating the spread of early-stage breast cancer [ – ]. Mechanistically, Her2 turns off p38 signaling, thereby inducing the activation of β-catenin, a transcription factor in the Wnt signaling pathway. This activation leads to the dissociation of β-catenin from E-cadherin and its translocation into the nucleus, where it stimulates the transcription of pro-EMT (epithelial–mesenchymal transition) genes that suppress E-cadherin expression, leading to disruption of cell-cell contact and EMT. Consequently, ECCs acquire migratory and invasive properties, facilitating dissemination to distant organs and the formation of metastatic foci [ – ]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanism by which Her2 suppresses the activation of p38 signaling in ECCs is still unclear.
Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 4 (MAP3K4), also known as MEKK4, has been shown to induce p38 MAPK activation through MAP2Ks MKK3 and MKK6 [ – ]. Studies have indicated that MAP3K4 can modulate the p38 signaling cascade, influencing cellular events such as proliferation, survival, and migration of breast cancer cells [ – ], highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in breast cancer. However, its role in the dissemination of early-stage breast cancer remains unknown. In this study, we demonstrated that Her2 downregulates the expression of MAP3K4, inhibiting the activation of p38 and thereby promoting the early dissemination of ECCs. Mechanistically, Her2 attenuates the expression of HOXB13, a transcription factor belonging to the homeobox gene family [ ], leading to suppression of the transcription of MAP3K4 in ECCs of breast cancer. These findings shed light on the mechanisms driving early dissemination of breast cancer, potentially advancing the development of effective diagnostic methods and targeted therapies.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture and tissue sample
MCF-10A cells were obtained from ATCC, MCF10DCIS.com cells were purchased from Barbara Ann Karmanos Cancer Institute, and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium containing 5% horse serum,10 mg/ml insulin, 0.5 mg/mL hydrocortisone, 20ng/mL EGF and penicillin/streptomycin. HMEC cells were obtained from ATCC and cultured in Human Mammary Epithelial Cell Basal Medium containing Mammary Epithelial Growth Supplement. Breast cancer patient samples containing 21 (11 Her2- and 10 Her2+) cases of DCIS samples were obtained from Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region People’s Hospital. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committees at Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region People’s Hospital and Chongqing University Cancer Hospital. All patients provided informed consent according to the latest version of the Helsinki Declaration on human research ethics.

## Reagent
p38 inhibitor SB203580 and SB202190, Her2 inhibitor Lapatinib were purchased from Selleckchem. The antibodies used for Western blot analysis, immunostaining assays, chromatin immunoprecipitation assays are provided in Table in Supplemental Materials.

## Plasmids and lentivirus-based gene transduction
The coding sequences of human MAP3K4 and HOXB13 were chemically synthesized and cloned into the pLV-EF1-MCS-IRES-Bsd vector (Biosettia) by Genewiz. Oligos encoding shRNAs were designed, synthesized, and inserted into pLV-H1-EF1α-puro vector (Biosettia) according to manufacturer′s protocol. Oligo sequences are listed in Supplementary data (Table ). LXSN-Her2 was constructed by cloning Her2 cDNA into LXSN retroviral vector. Recombinant lentiviruses and retroviruses were packaged and transduced into cells as described previously [ ]. Infected cells were selected with 300 µg/ml of G418, 1 µg/ml of puromycin, 10 µg/ml blasticidin.

## Western blot assays
Protein lysate preparation and Western blotting was performed as previously described [ ]. More details are provided in Supplementary Methods. The appropriate primary antibodies were used as described in Supplementary Table .

## Transwell migration assay
Cell migration and invasion assays were performed in 24-well transwell plates with 8-µm Polyethylene Terephthalate inserts (Corning) separating lower and upper chambers. Cells were plated in the upper chamber without or with Matrigel (corning) at 5 × 104 cells/well in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium. The bottom chamber contained DMEM/F12 with 5% horse serum, Cells were allowed to migrate for 12 h. Filters were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 2 h and photographed. Number of migrated cells were quantified in triplicates by counting at least 6 random fields per replicate.

## 3D MCF-10A cell organoid culture
5.0 × 103 of MCF-10A cells were seeded in 400 µl assay medium (DMEM/F12, 5% horse serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 20ng/ml EGF and 2% Matrigel) in 8-well chamber slides (Falcon, 354108) with 40 µl of Matrigel (Coring, 356231). Cells were incubated for 8 or 16 days. Images were captured on microscope with BZ-X viewer software. Percentage of organoids with outward invading cells were quantified in triplicated wells by counting 40–60 organoids/well.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Immunofluorescence staining of 2D and 3D cultures were performed as previously described [ , , ]. More details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

## Quantitative real-time PCR and mRNA stability analyses
Total RNA was isolated using Trizol (Life Technologies). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed as described [ ]. All samples were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) expression and 2-ΔΔCt values were calculated as described [ ]. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

## Luciferase assay
Cells were transfected with wild-type or mutant human MAP3K4 promoters, followed by transfected with LXSN-Her2 and pLV-HOXB13-BSD in 24 − well plates. Lysates were prepared 36 h after transfection, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Luciferase activity was normalized to the values for Renilla luciferase.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
ChIP assays were performed using an EZ-ChIP kit (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at RT, and the formaldehyde was then inactivated by the addition of 125mM glycine. Chromatin extracts containing DNA fragments were immunoprecipitated using specific antibodies. The ChIP-enriched DNA was then uncrosslinked and subjected to quantitative PCR. The primers and antibodies used in these experiments are shown in Supplementary Table S3.

## Animal experiments
Animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing University Cancer Hospital and complied with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. A total of 1 × 10⁶ MCF10DCIS.com cells (as indicated) were injected into the fourth mammary fat pad of 6- to 8-week-old female BALB/c nude mice in a 1:1 (vol/vol) suspension of Matrigel (BD Biosciences) (n = 5/group). Primary tumor dimensions—length (a) and width (b)—were measured, and tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula: V = ab² × 0.52. Approximately 500µL of blood per mouse was collected via cardiac puncture. Bone marrow cells from the tibiae and femurs were flushed using a 26-gauge needle and 1mL syringe. Early disseminating cancer cells (eDCCs) in the blood and bone marrow were purified using a negative lineage cell-depletion kit (Miltenyi Biotec), deposited onto slides by cytospin, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and stained with anti-CK8 or anti-Her2 antibodies, as described in the immunofluorescence staining protocol. The number of eDCCs per mL of blood or per bone marrow sample was calculated by counting all CK8⁺ or Her2⁺ cells on the cytospin slides from each of the five mice.
Tumors and lungs were harvested at the end of the experiment. Tissues were inflated, fixed with 4% PFA, and embedded in paraffin. Tumor sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), while lung sections were subjected to IHC staining with anti-TRPS1 or anti-Her2 antibodies, following the same procedures outlined in the IHC staining section. The number of TRPS1⁺ or Her2⁺ eDCCs per field in lung sections was quantified by counting 50 randomly selected fields (40× magnification) across five mice per group (n = 5, with 150–200 total cells counted per field). Data, including the number of CK8⁺ or Her2⁺ eDCCs per ml of blood, per bone marrow sample, or per lung field, were compared between groups using unpaired, two-sample t-tests or one-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons.

## Immunohistochemical analysis
Immunohistochemical analysis of paraffin embedded sections was performed using the Envision Kit (Dako) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Sections were boiled in retrieval solutions to expose antigens. Specific antibodies were applied to the sections. Slides were counter stained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. The H-score method was used to evaluate the percentage of positively stained cells and staining intensity. Staining intensity was categorized as negative (score 0), low (score 1), medium (score 2), and high (score 3). The proportion of positively stained cells was scored as follows: 0% positive cells = score 0, 1–25% = score 1, 26–50% = score 2, and > 50% = score 3. Expression levels of the target genes were quantified using the H-score method, calculated with the following formula: H-score = 1 × (%cells with score 1) + 2 × (%cells with score 2) + 3 × (%cells with score 3). Samples with an H-score ≥ 5 were classified as high expression, while those with an H-score < 5 were categorized as low expression. Immunostaining was independently evaluated by two pathologists. The antibodies used in the experiment are shown in Supplementary Table .

## Statistical and bioinformatics analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and SPSS 22 software. All the data are presented as the mean ± SD and represent at least independent experiments. A chi-square test was used to analyze correlations in protein expression in tissue samples. A one-sided unpaired test was used to compare means between treatment groups. Statistical significance was determined by Student’s t test analysis of variance. p < 0.05 was considered as significant. The r-value test was used to evaluate the correlation analysis.
DCIS-Her2 + and normal tissue data were downloaded from the GEO database using the GEOquery R package. The data were processed through quality control, data cleaning, and expression profile data transformation. Boxplots were generated using ggplot2 to visualize the differences in Her2 and MAP3K4 expression between the two groups. Paired t-tests were used for statistical analysis, and an adjusted P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, the expression correlation between the target gene MAP3K4 and HOXB13 was calculated using Spearman correlation analysis, and the results were visualized using the ggplot2 R package. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## MAP3K4 is downregulated in Her2 + DCIS cells and tissues
In our previous study [ ], to investigate the mechanism by which Her2 inhibits the p38 signaling pathway to promote early dissemination of breast cancer, we assessed the expression levels of the upstream kinases of p38 MAPK in MCF-10A cells transduced Her2, an experimental model for early-stage breast cancer cell [ , ]. Remarkably, the forced expression of Her2 led to a significant reduction in both mRNA and protein levels of MAP3K4, which activates p38 through MAP2Ks MKK3 and MKK6 [ ]. This data suggests that Her2 may suppress the p38 signaling pathway by downregulating the expression of MAP3K4, thereby promoting the early dissemination of breast carcinoma in situ.
To validate this hypothesis, we conducted qPCR and Western blot analyses in MCF-10A and HMEC (an immortalized non-tumorigenic breast epithelial cell line) cells overexpressing Her2 and control plasmid, measuring the expression levels of MAP3K4, the activation status of p38, and the downstream epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) marker. As expected, the results revealed that Her2 overexpression in MCF-10A and HMEC cells markedly decreased MAP3K4 expression at both mRNA and protein levels. Concurrently, it reduced p38 phosphorylation, suppressed the expression of the epithelial cell marker E-cadherin, upregulated the expression of the mesenchymal cell marker Vimentin and the EMT mediator Snail (Fig.  A-B, Supplementary Fig.  A). These changes were accompanied by enhanced cell migration and invasion capabilities in cells overexpressing Her2 (Supplementary Fig.  B-E). Additionally, gene expression analysis of the breast carcinoma in situ database (GSE26304) further demonstrated a significant reduction in MAP3K4 expression in Her2 + DCIS tissues compared to normal control tissues (Fig.  C).

 

Furthermore, we collected 21 surgical specimens from patients diagnosed with noninvasive DCIS at the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region People’s Hospital. The specimens were categorized into Her2- and Her2 + groups based on pathological diagnosis. Immunohistochemical staining was conducted to assess the expression levels of MAP3K4, p-p38 and E-cadherin in both groups. Consistently, the results revealed a significant reduction in both MAP3K4 and E-cadherin expression in DCIS tissues of the Her2 + group compared to the Her2- group, accompanied by a notable decrease in p38 phosphorylation levels (Fig.  D-G). These collective findings indicate that, in early-stage breast cancer, Her2 significantly suppresses MAP3K4 expression and the activation of the downstream p38 signaling pathway, while concurrently promoting EMT features.

## MAP3K4 is indispensable for Her2-induced inhibition of p38 pathway and the early dissemination of DCIS
To investigate whether MAP3K4 is involved in Her2-mediated inhibition of the p38 signaling pathway and the promotion of early dissemination of DCIS cells, we restored the expression of MAP3K4 in MCF-10A and HMEC cells overexpressing Her2 using a lentivirus system. Western blot results revealed that restoring MAP3K4 expression significantly reversed the reduced levels of p38 phosphorylation and E-cadherin expression in Her2-overexpressing MCF-10A cells (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig.  A). Migration and invasion assay results indicated that MAP3K4 could hinder the enhanced cell migration and invasion capabilities induced by Her2 (Fig.  B-C, Supplementary Fig.  B). Additionally, in a 3D organotypic culture model derived from MCF-10A cells overexpressing Her2, MAP3K4 significantly reduced the percentage of organoids with outward invading cells (Fig.  D, Supplementary Fig.  C), suggesting that MAP3K4 attenuates the enhanced invasive potential induced by Her2. Moreover, these findings were supported by immunofluorescence staining for E-cadherin and β-catenin, commonly used EMT markers. Restoring the expression of MAP3K4 promoted the expression of E-cadherin and inhibited the nuclear translocation of β-catenin in MCF-10A cells overexpressing Her2 (Fig.  E-F, Supplementary Fig.  D-E). It is well established that cancer stem cells play a critical role in EMT and metastasis [ , ].

 

Therefore, we examined the effects of Her2 and MAP3K4 on the stemness of DCIS cells. Notably, Her2 overexpression significantly increased the expression levels of stemness markers Nanog, OCT4, and CD44, which could be effectively abolished by MAP3K4 overexpression (Supplementary Fig.  F). Additionally, neither Her2 nor MAP3K4 had an impact on the expression of dormancy markers NR2F1 and DEC2 or the apoptosis marker Cleaved-Caspase3, proliferation marker PCNA (Supplementary Fig.  F). Thus, these results indicate that MAP3K4 disrupts Her2-induced EMT in early-stage breast cancer cells.
To confirm these findings, we further knocked down MAP3K4 in MCF-10A-Her2 cells. Notably, the results demonstrated that knockdown of MAP3K4 led to further suppression of p38 phosphorylation and E-cadherin expression, while increasing the expression of the mesenchymal cell marker Vimentin (Supplementary Fig.  A). Consistently, migration, invasion, and 3D organoid culture experiments indicated that MAP3K4 knockdown further enhanced the migration and invasion capabilities of MCF-10A-Her2 cells (Supplementary Fig.  B-D). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining results also revealed that MAP3K4 knockdown further reduced E-cadherin expression and promoted the nuclear translocation of β-catenin (Supplementary Fig.  E-F), indicating that inhibiting MAP3K4 expression could augment the early dissemination capabilities of MCF-10A-Her2 cells. Together, our findings demonstrate that Her2 downregulates MAP3K4 expression, inhibits the p38 pathway, and thereby promotes the early dissemination of breast cancer cells.

## MAP3K4 inhibited the early dissemination of Her2 + DCIS cells through activating the p38 signaling pathway
To investigate whether MAP3K4 mediates the dissemination of Her2 + DCIS cells through regulating the p38 signaling pathway, we treated MCF-10A-Her2 and HMEC-Her2 cells overexpressing MAP3K4 with the specific p38 inhibitors SB203580 and SB202190 to block the p38 signaling pathway. Western blot results revealed that MAP3K4 overexpression significantly increased p38 phosphorylation and E-cadherin expression, while reducing Vimentin and Snail levels. These effects were reversed upon treatment with the p38 inhibitors SB203580 and SB202190 in both MCF-10A-Her2 and HMEC-Her2 cells (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig.  A-B). Migration, invasion, and 3D organoid culture experiments indicated that inhibition of the p38 pathway completely restored the decrease in cell migration and invasion abilities induced by MAP3K4 overexpression (Fig.  B-C, Supplementary Fig.  C-F). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining results demonstrated that p38 inhibitors effectively blocked the upregulation of E-cadherin expression induced by MAP3K4 overexpression and restored the nuclear localization of β-catenin (Fig.  E-F, Supplementary Fig.  G-H).

 

Furthermore, we ectopically expressed an inactive mutant of MKK6 (MKK6A), the direct upstream kinase of p38 [ ], in MCF-10A-Her2 cells overexpressing MAP3K4 to competitively block p38 activation. Consistently, blocking p38 activation in MCF-10A-Her2 cells completely restored the decrease in cell migration and invasion abilities induced by MAP3K4 overexpression, as evidenced by decreased E-cadherin expression levels, enhanced migration and invasion abilities, increased number of invasive organoids, and upregulation of nuclear localization of β-catenin (Supplementary Fig.  A-F). Taken together, our results demonstrate that MAP3K4 mediates the early dissemination of Her2 + DCIS cells by regulating p38 signaling pathway.

## Her2 reduces the expression of MAP3K4 through down-regulation of HOXB13
To investigate the downstream effectors and mechanisms through which Her2 regulates MAP3K4 expression in DCIS cells, we performed RNA-seq sequencing analysis on MCF-10A-LXSN and MCF-10A-Her2 cells, identifying a gene set regulated by Her2. By analyzing gene expression data from the breast carcinoma in situ database (GSE26304), we identified a gene set correlated with MAP3K4 expression. Additionally, using the JASPAR database, we predicted a transcription factor set capable of binding to the − 3 kb promoter region of the MAP3K4 gene. Intersection analysis of these three gene sets led to the identification of a transcription factor, HOXB13, which may mediate Her2 regulation of MAP3K4 expression in DCIS cells (Fig.  A). In the breast carcinoma in situ database, HOXB13 exhibited a significant positive correlation with MAP3K4 expression levels, with a correlation coefficient of 0.563 (Fig.  B). qPCR and WB experiments demonstrated that overexpression of Her2 in MCF-10A and HMEC cells markedly inhibited the mRNA and protein levels of HOXB13, as well as the expression levels of MAP3K4 and the phosphorylation levels of p38 (Fig.  C-D, Supplementary Fig.  A). Consistently, treatment of MCF-10A-Her2 cells with the Her2 small molecule inhibitor Lapatinib dramatically upregulated the expression levels of HOXB13, while also promoting MAP3K4 expression and p38 phosphorylation (Fig.  E).

 

These results were further supported by immunohistochemical staining of HOXB13, MAP3K4, p-p38 and E-cadherin in breast carcinoma in situ tissues. Compared to Her2- samples, Her2+ tissues showed obvious decreased expression of HOXB13, as well as decreased expression levels of MAP3K4, E-cadherin and phosphorylated p38 (Fig.  F-G). Moreover, in breast carcinoma in situ tissues, there was a significant positive association among the expression levels of HOXB13, MAP3K4 and E-cadherin, as well as the phosphorylation levels of p38 (Fig.  H). Thus, these findings suggest that Her2 may downregulate the expression of HOXB13, thereby inhibiting MAP3K4 transcription and suppressing the activity of the p38 signaling pathway.

## HOXB13 is implicated in Her2-induced early dissemination of DCIS cells
Next, we further explored whether HOXB13 is required for Her2-induced early dissemination of DCIS cells. As expected, overexpression of HOXB13 in MCF-10A-Her2 and HMEC-Her2 cells significantly increased the mRNA and protein levels of MAP3K4, promoted p38 phosphorylation, and consequently enhanced the expression of E-cadherin (Fig.  A-B, Supplementary Fig.  A). Moreover, forced expression of HOXB13 dramatically inhibited cell migration and invasion measured in transwell assays and 3D organoid models (Fig.  C-E, Supplementary Fig.  B-C), enhanced E-cadherin expression while decreasing β-catenin nuclear translocation detected in immunofluorescence staining (Fig.  F-G, Supplementary Fig.  D-E). Additionally, HOXB13 overexpression effectively inhibited Her2-induced stemness in MCF-10A cells, without affecting dormancy, apoptosis, or proliferation (Supplementary Fig.  F). Therefore, these results suggest that restoration of HOXB13 expression markedly impeded Her2-induced early dissemination of DCIS cells.

 

To validate these results, we further downregulated HOXB13 expression using shRNA in MCF-10A-Her2 cells (Supplementary Figure A). Notably, western blot analysis revealed that knockdown of HOXB13 further decreased the phosphorylation of p38 and the expression of the epithelial cell marker E-cadherin, while increasing the expression of the mesenchymal cell marker Vimentin in both MCF-10A-Her2 cells (Supplementary Fig.  B). Functional assay results indicated that downregulation of HOXB13 expression further promoted the invasion and migration of MCF-10A-Her2 cells, represented by enhanced migration and invasion abilities (Supplementary Fig.  C-D), an increased percentage of invasive organoids (Supplementary Fig.  E), reduced expression of E-cadherin, and increased nuclear translocation of β-catenin (Supplementary Fig.  F-G). Taken together, our results demonstrate that Her2 promotes the early dissemination of DCIS cells by downregulating the expression of HOXB13.

## HOXB13 facilitates the expression of MAP3K4 to inhibit early dissemination of Her2 + DCIS cells
To investigate whether HOXB13 contributes to Her2-induced early dissemination by regulating MAP3K4 expression, we initially overexpressed HOXB13 in MCF-10A-Her2 cells, followed by MAP3K4 knocked down. Western blot analysis revealed that knockdown of MAP3K4 significantly impeded the increase in p38 phosphorylation and E-cadherin expression induced by HOXB13 overexpression (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig.  A). Moreover, transwell assay results illustrated that knockdown of MAP3K4 effectively reversed the attenuation of migration and invasion capabilities caused by HOXB13 overexpression (Fig.  B-C, Supplementary Fig.  B). Consistently, MAP3K4 knockdown notably augmented the number of invasive organoids derived from HOXB13-overexpressing MCF-10A-Her2 and HMEC-Her2 cells (Fig.  D, Supplementary Fig.  C). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that downregulation of MAP3K4 expression markedly reduced E-cadherin expression and promoted β-catenin nuclear translocation in MCF-10A-Her2 and HMEC-Her2 cells with HOXB13 overexpression (Fig.  E-F, Supplementary Fig.  D-E), indicating that HOXB13 is involved in Her2-induced early dissemination of DCIS cells by inducing MAP3K4 expression.

 

## HOXB13 directly binds to the promoter of MAP3K4, activating its transcription
Next, we delved into the molecular mechanism through which HOXB13 regulates MAP3K4 expression. Given that HOXB13 functions as a sequence-specific transcription factor, we hypothesized that it might directly bind and activate the transcription of MAP3K4, thereby facilitating its expression. To test this hypothesis, we scrutinized the binding motif of HOXB13 in the JASPAR database and predicted its binding sites on the promoter of the MAP3K4 gene (Fig.  A). Our analysis unveiled four potential binding sites for HOXB13 on the − 3 kb promoter region of the MAP3K4 gene (Fig.  B). Subsequently, we performed ChIP (chromatin immunoprecipitation) analysis using specific antibodies against HOXB13 in both MCF-10A-LXSN and MCF-10A-Her2 cells. Interestingly, the results indicated that HOXB13 could indeed bind to the third site on the MAP3K4 promoter region, with the expression of Her2 markedly inhibiting this binding (Fig.  C-D).

 

To investigate the role of HOXB13 in regulating MAP3K4 transcription, we cloned the MAP3K4 promoter sequence and integrated it into the pGL3.0 vector, and then conducted dual-luciferase analysis to assess the promoter activity. As expected, the data revealed that HOXB13 significantly enhanced the promoter activity of MAP3K4, while Her2 attenuated it, suggesting that HOXB13 transcriptionally activates MAP3K4, whereas Her2 impedes HOXB13 expression to suppress MAP3K4 transcription (Fig.  E). Furthermore, we introduced mutations to the four potential HOXB13 binding sites on the MAP3K4 gene promoter region to disrupt HOXB13 binding (Fig.  F). Consistently, the results demonstrated that mutating the third site hindered HOXB13-mediated transcriptional activation of MAP3K4, while mutations in the other three sites remained inconsequential (Fig.  G), indicating that HOXB13 triggers the transcription of MAP3K4 by specifically binding to the third binding site on the promoter of the MAP3K4 gene.

## The Her2-HOXB13-MAP3K4 axis is essential for early dissemination of DCIS cells
To verify the role of the Her2-HOXB13-MAP3K4 axis in promoting early-stage breast cancer cell dissemination, we utilized a DCIS cell line, MCF10DCIS.COM, derived from MCF-10A cells. This cell line has been shown to form typical DCIS lesions in the mammary glands of female BALB/c mice [ , ]. As anticipated, exogenous overexpression of Her2 in MCF10DCIS.COM cells significantly decreased p38 phosphorylation and E-cadherin expression, while increasing the expression of Snail and the proportion of invasive organoids (Supplementary Fig.  A-B), suggesting that Her2 enhances the migratory and invasive capabilities of DCIS cells. Consistently, overexpression of either MAP3K4 or HOXB13 effectively blocked the Her2-induced enhancement of cell migration and invasion (Supplementary Fig.  A-D). Moreover, in MCF10DCIS.COM-Her2 cells overexpressing HOXB13, MAP3K4 knockdown effectively restored the migratory and invasive capacity, as evidenced by reduced p38 phosphorylation and E-cadherin expression, alongside increased Snail and Vimentin expression and a higher proportion of invasive organoids (Supplementary Fig.  E-F). These results indicate that Her2 promotes the dissemination of early-stage breast cancer cells by downregulating HOXB13, which in turn inhibits MAP3K4 transcription and suppresses the p38 pathway.
To further confirm the essential role of the Her2-HOXB13-MAP3K4 axis in the early dissemination of DCIS cells, we established xenograft tumor models by orthotopically injecting MCF10DCIS.COM cells into the mammary glands of BALB/c nude mice. Tumor size analysis revealed that Her2, MAP3K4 and HOXB13 have no significant effect on tumor growth (Fig.  A-B). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of xenograft tumors confirmed that MCF10DCIS.COM cells developed typical DCIS lesions (Fig.  C). Four weeks after injection, with the tumors still in the DCIS stage and without progression to invasive carcinoma, we collected blood, bone marrow, and lung tissue samples from the tumor-bearing mice. To evaluate early dissemination, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry staining were performed to quantify early disseminated cancer cells (eDCCs) in these samples. Notably, our findings indicated that Her2 overexpression significantly increased the number of CK8+ and Her2+ eDCCs in the blood and bone marrow, as well as TRPS1+ and Her2+ eDCCs in lung tissue. This dissemination effect was effectively abolished by the overexpression of MAP3K4 or HOXB13 (Fig.  D-H). Moreover, knockdown of MAP3K4 in HOXB13-overexpressing cells fully restored the Her2-induced early dissemination to levels comparable to the group with Her2 overexpression alone (8D-H). In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that the Her2-HOXB13-MAP3K4 axis is a critical driver of early dissemination in DCIS cells, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target for preventing early dissemination in breast cancer.

 

## Discussion
Emerging clinical and experimental evidence indicates that breast cancer, particularly Her2 + subtype, exhibits early dissemination, even during pre-cancerous stages, resulting in adverse outcomes and elevated mortality rates [ – , ]. Notably, approximately 2–3% of patients diagnosed with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) have already developed metastases without forming a primary tumor [ , , ]. This phenomenon underscores the aggressive nature of certain breast cancer subtypes and highlights the need for early detection and targeted interventions to impede disease progression and improve patient prognosis. Molecular and cellular studies have elucidated various mechanisms underlying early dissemination, such as EMT [ , , ], whereby cancer cells acquire migratory and invasive properties. Her2 is reported to be the key molecular players in this process, which inhibits the p38 signaling pathway to activate EMT, thereby promoting early dissemination of ECCs [ , ]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which Her2 downregulates the phosphorylation of p38 are not yet fully understood. In this study, we present compelling evidence that Her2 impedes p38 activation by suppressing the transcription of its upstream kinase, MAP3K4, during early dissemination. Moreover, Her2 exerts its suppressive effect on MAP3K4 transcription by dampening the expression of HOXB13, a pivotal transcription factor governing MAP3K4 expression in DCIS cells. Consequently, these findings offer insights into the mechanisms underlying breast cancer early dissemination, potentially facilitating the development of effective diagnostic methods and targeted therapies.
HOXB13, a member of the homeobox gene family, is a critical transcription factor with diverse functions in normal development and cancer progression [ ]. One of the fundamental functions of HOXB13 is its involvement in embryonic development, where it plays a crucial role in specifying cell fate, patterning, and organogenesis during embryogenesis [ , ]. This transcription factor regulates the expression of genes essential for proper embryonic development, influencing the formation of various tissues and structures [ ]. In cancer, dysregulation of HOXB13 has been implicated in tumor development and progression [ ]. In breast cancer, for instance, HOXB13 has emerged as a key regulator in tumorigenesis and metastasis [ , ]. Elevated levels of HOXB13 are associated with poorer prognosis in estrogen receptor (ER) + invasive ductal adenocarcinoma. It was reported that HOXB13 promotes breast cancer cell proliferation and invasion by transcriptionally upregulating IL-6, and contributes to tamoxifen resistance by directly inhibiting ERα expression [ , ]. Clinical research has highlighted that the HOXB13-to-IL17BR expression ratio is positively correlated with tumor aggressiveness and serves as an independent prognostic indicator for ER + breast cancer [ – ], underscoring its oncogenic significance in ER + invasive ductal adenocarcinoma. In this study, we found that HOXB13 is also implicated in Her2-induced early dissemination of DCIS cells. Intriguingly, HOXB13 significantly impedes the migration and invasion of DCIS cells through transcriptional activation of MAP3K4, suggesting its tumor-suppressive function in breast carcinoma in situ. The divergent roles of HOXB13 in breast carcinoma in situ and invasive ductal adenocarcinoma, similar to its downstream molecules MAP3K4 and p38, indicate their participation in a shared signaling pathway.
Moreover, HOXB13 has been reported to interact with hormone receptors in various cancers. In prostate cancer, for instance, HOXB13 interacts with androgen receptor (AR), influencing cancer progression [ ]. It serves as a co-factor for AR, modulating AR target gene expression and promoting tumor growth [ ]. Studies by Chaeyong Jung et al. have demonstrated that HOXB13 physically interacts with AR, functioning as an AR repressor to modulate the complex AR signaling and subsequent growth regulation of prostate cancer cells [ ]. In breast cancer, HOXB13 acts as a transcriptional regulator, modulating the expression of ER target genes, thus influencing tumorigenesis. Additionally, HOXB13 contributes to tamoxifen resistance by directly inhibiting ERα expression [ ]. Furthermore, research by Zuncai Wang and colleagues indicated that estrogen inhibits HOXB13 expression in an ER-dependent manner, which is reversed by tamoxifen [ ]. Consistently, our study revealed that the expression of HOXB13 is also inhibited by Her2, and implicated in Her2-induced early dissemination of breast carcinoma in situ. Thus, the intricate crosstalk between HOXB13 and hormone receptors highlights its diverse roles in cancer pathogenesis and potential as a therapeutic target. However, the precise molecular mechanism through which Her2 regulates HOXB13 expression in DCIS cells remains unclear and necessitates further experimental investigation.
In this study, we ectopically expressed Her2 in MCF-10A and HMEC cells to establish models simulating DCIS. Western blot analysis confirmed that Her2 expression levels in these modified cells did not significantly exceed those observed in Her2-amplified SK-BR-3 and BT474 cell lines (Supplementary Fig.  B), suggesting that our experimental system maintains physiologically relevant Her2 levels, thereby validating its suitability. Interestingly, a time-course analysis of HOXB13, MAP3K4, and p-p38 expression following lapatinib treatment revealed distinct regulatory dynamics. AKT activity began to decrease at 24 h post-treatment, coinciding with the upregulation of HOXB13 and MAP3K4, along with increased p38 phosphorylation. Simultaneously, we observed an increase in the epithelial marker E-cadherin and a decrease in the mesenchymal marker Vimentin. These changes became more pronounced over time, peaking at 72 h (Supplementary Fig.  C). These results highlight the temporal dynamics of the HOXB13-MAP3K4-p38 signaling axis. Notably, activation of this pathway appears to be part of a sustained regulatory process rather than an immediate response to Her2 inhibition. This temporal pattern underscores the complex and coordinated mechanisms by which Her2 inhibition influences downstream signaling, impacting the EMT in early-stage breast cancer cells.
In our previous study, we observed that Her2 induces the phosphorylation of Serine 72 of Skp2 (S-Phase Kinase Associated Protein 2) by PI3K/Akt, leading to increased Skp2 protein stability. Skp2, acting as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, forms an SCF complex with Cul-1 (Cullin-1) and Rbx1 (Ring-Box 1), which directly ubiquitinates Tpl2, an upstream kinase of p38, thereby inhibiting p38 activation and subsequently promoting early dissemination of breast carcinoma in situ [ ]. In this study, we demonstrate that Her2 suppresses the transcription of MAP3K4 by downregulating HOXB13 expression, thereby inhibiting the activation of p38 pathway and promoting early dissemination. These findings suggest that the mechanism by which Her2 regulates p38 pathway activation to induce early dissemination of breast carcinoma in situ is complex and multifaceted. The relative contribution of these pathways, or in other words, which pathway plays a predominant role, remains unclear and requires further investigation in the future.
In summary(Supplementary Fig.  A), we demonstrate that Her2 downregulates the expression of HOXB13, leading to inhibition of MAP3K4 transcription, subsequently suppressing the activity of the p38 signaling pathway, and thereby promoting the early dissemination of DCIS cells. However, additional animal experiments, including orthotopic transplantation models utilizing MCF-10A-HRas experimental DCIS cell lines or early dissemination models employing MMTV-Her2 mice with MAP3K4 or HOXB13 conditional knockout, are required to further validate the roles and regulatory mechanisms of MAP3K4 and HOXB13 in Her2-induced early dissemination. Nevertheless, our findings provide compelling evidence that any approach aimed at upregulating the expression or activity of HOXB13, MAP3K4, or p38 signaling could potentially serve as an effective therapeutic strategy for preventing the early dissemination of Her2 + breast cancer.
