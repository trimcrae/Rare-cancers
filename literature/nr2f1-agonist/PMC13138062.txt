## Summary
Estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancers often recur years after endocrine therapy, due to tumor cells escaping a dormant state. Mechanisms underlying dormancy and recurrence are poorly understood. We identify CREB1 as a key regulator of ER+ tumor dormancy and recurrence. Analysis of dormant tumor samples from aromatase inhibitor-treated patients revealed 1,057 dormancy-downregulated genes (DDGs) and 1,142 dormancy-upregulated genes (DUGs). Pathway analyses implicated CREB1, together with ER and E2F1, as central regulators of these gene programs. Similar gene changes were confirmed in ER+ cell and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumor models of estrogen deprivation-induced dormancy and reversed upon acquired estrogen independence and tumor recurrence. CREB1 inhibition suppressed DDG expression, induced DUGs, and reduced survival of endocrine- and CDK4/6 inhibitor-resistant ER+ breast cancer cells. Together, the findings establish CREB1 as a key regulator of transcriptional reprogramming during relapse and a promising therapeutic target to overcome endocrine resistance.

## Introduction
Estrogen receptor positive (ER+) breast cancer patients are typically treated with aromatase inhibitors (AIs) such as letrozole that reduce estrogen levels, or tamoxifen that directly inhibits ER. Despite these treatments ∼30%–50% of patients develop tumor recurrence, sometimes as long as 20 years after treatment. , Recurrent tumors are mostly nodal and distant metastases that are highly similar to their matched primary tumors, suggesting a “reawakening” of the original cancer after a period of dormancy. , , , Factors that control tumor dormancy arrest and recurrence/reawakening are ill-defined.
Standard care for recurrent and endocrine resistant ER+ tumors is CDK4/6 inhibitors with either AIs or the ER degrader fulvestrant (Faslodex). These treatments are not effective for all patients. In the MONARCH 2 trial, only 48% of patients who progressed on primary endocrine therapy showed an objective response to CDK4/6 inhibitor (abemaciclib) plus fulvestrant. , In MONARCH 3, 39% of patients failed to show an objective response to abemaciclib plus AI when the treatment was given up-front (no prior endocrine therapy). Moreover, all patients with metastasis will eventually progress on these combination treatments due to intrinsic or acquired resistance to the CDK4/6 inhibitor, the endocrine therapy, or both. , There are no standard treatments currently for patients whose cancers progress after CDK4/6 inhibitor plus endocrine therapy treatment.
CREB1 (cAMP response element binding protein 1) is an oncogenic transcription factor that binds the cAMP response element in target genes and promotes cell proliferation, growth, and survival. , Elevated levels of CREB1 have been observed in breast cancer tissues and are associated with poor prognosis, metastatic disease, and nodal involvement. , CREB1 has been implicated in endocrine therapy resistance. For example, CREB1 was reported to promote tamoxifen resistance in ER+ MCF7 cells by activating aromatase gene expression. Further, endocrine therapy resistance is commonly mediated by heightened E2F signaling, and CREB1 can functionally interact with E2F family proteins to co-regulate common genes. Lastly, CREB1 can interact with estrogen receptor alpha and promote ER target gene expression in an estrogen- or cAMP-dependent way. The ability of CREB1 to promote E2F-target genes and promote and maintain ER target genes in the absence of estrogen may contribute to the acquired resistance of ER+ breast cancers to anti-estrogen therapies.
In the current study, we compared tumor gene expressions from a clinical cohort of ER+ breast cancer patients that were treated with the AI letrozole and whose tumors experienced a prolonged dormancy arrest after treatment of up to 45 months or whose tumors experienced an initial arrest followed by tumor regrowth/recurrence. Our analysis revealed 1,057 “dormancy-downregulated genes (DDGs)” that are reduced during dormancy arrest and restored upon tumor recurrence, and 1,142 “dormancy-upregulated genes (DUGs)”. The majority of these genes are CREB1 targets and many are co-regulated by CREB1, ER, and E2F1. We went on to show that these same gene expression changes occur in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) and cell line models of ER+ tumor dormancy arrest and recurrence. Activated CREB1 protein levels were reduced during dormancy arrest in estrogen-deprived PDX tumors and increased upon estrogen-independent tumor recurrence. In ER+ cells and PDX tumors, both acquired resistance to estrogen deprivation (ED) and co-resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors coincided with increased levels of activated CREB1. Finally, inhibition of CREB1—either by small molecule inhibitor or gene knockdown—blocked CREB1, ER, and E2F1 target DDG gene expression while increasing DUGs and effectively suppressed growth of endocrine-resistant ER+ breast cancer cells, including those co-resistant to ED and CDK4/6 inhibitors. We conclude CREB1 controls oncogenic transcription programs in ER+ tumor dormancy and recurrence and suggest targeting CREB1 is a potential way to overcome endocrine and CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance.

## Results

## Identification of DDGs in clinical samples
Our initial objective was to investigate the mechanisms underlying tumor arrest and recurrence in ER+ breast cancers following endocrine therapy. Selli and Sims previously reported a cohort of 62 ER+ breast cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant AI letrozole for 4–45 months. All patients experienced >40% reduction in tumor size within the first 4 months. Subsequently, 20 patients progressed and were classified as “acquired resistant,” whereas 42 patients showed no progression and were classified as “dormant/arrested.”
Patient-matched tumor samples were collected at 3 time points: pre-treatment (≤0 days), early treatment (13–120 days), and long-term treatment (>120 days). Gene expression profiling was performed using GeneChip arrays (dataset GSE111563) to identify genes significantly regulated by treatment. We reanalyzed the GSE111563 dataset ( and on Selli et al. paper) to identify genes differentially regulated in dormant tumors but not in recurrent ones (see in the section). This analysis revealed 1,057 genes uniquely downregulated in dormant tumors. We termed these genes as “DDGs” ( ).
Because the GSE111563 dataset does not provide log fold-change (logFC) rankings of differentially expressed genes—a limitation of this dataset—we used the Ma’ayan Lab’s Enrichr tool to perform pathway enrichment analysis of the DDGs using the MSigDB Hallmark 2020 gene sets. , , This analysis revealed significant enrichment in the Estrogen Response, G2–M Checkpoint, and E2F Targets pathways ( A). In our preliminary studies, we found constitutive interaction between ERα and CREB1 in long-term estrogen-deprived (LTED) cells and E2-mediated interaction in parental MCF7 cells (data not shown). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the transcription factors ESR1, E2F1, and CREB1 regulate the expression of DDGs. To test this, we performed transcription factor enrichment analysis using hypergeometric testing with the combined ChEA and ENCODE target gene databases. This analysis showed that DDGs were significantly enriched for targets of ESR1 (ERα), CREB1, and E2F1, with CREB1 emerging as the predominant regulator, predicted to target 69.8% (738/1,058) of DDGs ( B, 1C, and ). In comparison, E2F1 and ESR1 were predicted to regulate 43.8% (463/1,057) and 21.6% (229/1057) of DDGs, respectively. Notably, 74.7% (171/229) of ESR1-target DDGs and 93.1% (431/463) of E2F1-target DDGs were also predicted in CREB1 targets, suggesting that CREB1 extensively co-regulates ESR1-and E2F1-controlled gene programs in these cells.
 

## Confirmation of DDG changes in ER+ cells
To validate DDG changes observed in clinical samples, we utilized estrogen-dependent ER+ MCF7 breast cancer cells. We selected MCF7 derivatives that are estrogen independent for growth by culturing parental MCF7 in the absence of estrogen for 13 months (cells called LTED). As expected, parental MCF7 proliferated in the presence of estradiol (E2), acute ED inhibits their proliferation, and LTED cells proliferate in the absence of E2 ( A).
 We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to compare gene expression profiles in MCF7 cells cultured with estrogen (+E2), LTED cells maintained under LTED (−E2), and MCF7 cells exposed to acute ED (−E2 for 1 month) ( ). This analysis identified 4,322 genes significantly downregulated under acute ED. Of these, 1,627 were upregulated (reversed) in LTED cells, which we termed MCF7 DDGs ( ). Transcription factor enrichment analysis of MCF7 DDGs, using the combined ChEA and ENCODE target gene databases, revealed that these genes are predominantly regulated by CREB1, ESR1, and E2F1. Specifically, CREB1 was predicted to regulate 54.9% (894/1,627) of the DDGs, compared with 15.4% (250/1,627) for ESR1 and 27.0% (440/1,627) for E2F1 ( B and ). Hypergeometric testing confirmed significant enrichment for CREB1-, ESR1-, and E2F1-regulated targets. These results suggest a reversal in CREB1-, ESR1-, and E2F1-mediated gene regulation between the dormancy-like ED state and the proliferative LTED state, consistent with patterns observed in clinical samples.
We next selected a panel of co-regulated CREB1- and E2F1-target genes known to be involved in cell-cycle progression and potentially endocrine therapy resistance—AURKA, AURKB, BUB1B, CCNB1, CCND1, CENPA, CENPF, CENPN, and MKI67—and assessed their expression by qPCR in MCF7 cells grown in the presence of estradiol (MCF7 E2), estrogen-deprived MCF7 (MCF7 ED), and LTED cells. These genes were significantly downregulated in MCF7 ED cells compared to MCF7 E2-controls, but their expression was partially restored in LTED cells, albeit at slightly lower levels than in MCF7 E2 conditions ( C). Notably, 92 genes overlapped between MCF7 DDGs and the tumor DDGs ( ); among them include AURKA, AURKB, BUB1B, CCNB1, CCND1, and CENPA, underscoring their importance as genes suppressed by ED and re-expressed upon acquired resistance.
The findings suggest that ED-induced dormancy in MCF7 cells is associated with downregulation of CREB1-, ESR1-, and/or E2F1-regulated cell-cycle genes and that reactivation of these transcription factors accompanies resistance to ED. CREB1 is activated by phosphorylation at serine 133 (S133). E2F1 is negatively regulated by RB1, and RB1 is inactivated by CDK4/6-mediated phosphorylation. We examined the phosphorylation status of RB1 and CREB1, as well as ERα protein levels in MCF7 grown in the presence of E2, MCF7 with acute ED, and in MCF7 LTED cells. Immunoblot analysis revealed decreased phosphorylation of RB1 in MCF7 ED cells, with phosphorylation restored in LTED cells ( D). This supports that RB1 is activated during dormancy and inhibiting E2F1 but is inactivated in resistant LTED cells allowing E2F1 activity to be restored. Similarly, CREB1 phosphorylation was reduced in ED cells but restored in LTED cells, consistent with CREB1 being inactivated in dormancy and reactivated in resistant cells ( E). Interestingly, ERα protein levels were increased in ED cells and further increased in LTED cells ( D), consistent with previous report that LTED cells increased ERα expression and basal function. , 

## Confirmation of DDG changes in PDX tumors
We previously characterized an ER+/HER2− breast cancer PDX model that exhibits estrogen-dependent tumor growth and sensitivity to tamoxifen. , In this model, ED induces a prolonged period of tumor growth arrest lasting approximately seven months—representing a dormancy phase—followed by tumor regrowth/recurrence ( A).
 To investigate gene expression dynamics during this progression, we collected sequential tumor biopsies from pre-ED, dormant (DT), and recurrent (RT) PDX tumors and analyzed them by qPCR. Expression of CREB1- and E2F1-target genes was significantly reduced in dormant tumors compared to pre-ED tumors, and this downregulation was reversed in recurrent tumors ( B). Consistent with these transcriptomic changes, immunoblot analysis of PDX tumor lysates revealed that levels of phosphorylated/activated CREB1 (pCREB1 S133) were reduced in dormant tumors and restored in recurrent tumors ( C). Similarly, levels of phosphorylated RB1 (pRB1 S807/811)—indicative of RB inactivation—were decreased in dormant tumors, suggesting RB activation, and subsequently re-elevated in recurrent tumors ( C), consistent with reactivation of E2F1 signaling. We think that ED reduces cyclin-dependent kinase activity, leading to RB hypophosphorylation and repression of E2F-target genes; conversely, in LTED cells, we detect restored CREB1 and E2F1 activity, up-regulation of cyclins and consequent RB hyper-phosphorylation. This sequence of events is consistent with RB inactivation leading to proliferation. Additionally, ERα protein levels were elevated in both dormant and recurrent tumors ( C), similar to that in MCF7 ED and LTED cells.
These findings parallel observations from MCF7 cell models, supporting the conclusion that ED induces a dormancy program via downregulation of CREB1-, ESR1, and E2F1-regulated genes. The reactivation of these pathways in recurrent tumors likely facilitates escape from dormancy and tumor regrowth. Notably, these CREB1- and E2F1-target genes—AURKA, AURKB, BUB1B, CCNB1, CCND1, CENPA, CENPF, CENPN, and MKI67 —highly expressed in breast tumors compared to normal breast tissues and their high expression correlates with worse outcome (reduced recurrence-free survival (RFS)) in ER+ breast cancer patients treated with endocrine therapy ( ). Increased expression of these genes in LTED cells reflects their re-entry into the cell cycle downstream of CREB1 and RB1 signaling, supporting these factors may contribute to endocrine therapy resistance.

## Confirmation of DUG changes in ER+ cells
Next, we identified 1,142 genes that were uniquely upregulated in dormant tumors in the GSE111563 dataset, which we termed “DUGs" ( ). Enrichr analysis of the DUGs revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), myogenesis, and TNF-α signaling via NF-κB ( A). Transcription factor enrichment analysis using hypergeometric testing further indicated that these genes were also significantly enriched for targets of CREB1, E2F1, and ESR1 ( B). EMT has been implicated in tumor dormancy by enabling disseminated cancer cells to enter a quiescent, therapy-resistant state, and prior studies by Aouad and Brisken and colleagues have shown that EMT is required for the establishment of a dormant state, whereas reacquisition of an epithelial phenotype is critical for recurrence in ER+ breast cancer. Notably, CREB1, ESR1, and E2F1 can function as both transcriptional activators and repressors. We therefore hypothesized that CREB1 may repress a subset of DUGs, including genes that promote dormancy. In this model, reduced CREB1 activity in ED cells would lead to increased expression of dormancy-promoting genes and maintenance of the dormant state, whereas reactivation of CREB1 in LTED cells would suppress these genes to facilitate exit from dormancy. Consistent with this hypothesis, analysis of RNA-seq data from MCF7 +E2, ED, and LTED cells identified several genes implicated in dormancy, including NR2F1, BTG2, FLRT2, GAS1, NR4A2, SPRY1, HOXA5, and STAT5B, , , , , , , , , that were upregulated in ED cells and subsequently downregulated in LTED cells; these expression patterns were confirmed by qPCR ( C). These genes are also annotated as direct CREB1 targets in the ChEA and ENCODE databases ( ). To further validate that these genes are repressed by CREB1, we transfected LTED cells with control or CREB1 siRNA and measured their expression by qPCR. CREB1 knockdown resulted in increased expression of all 8 genes ( D), supporting the conclusion that they are negatively regulated by CREB1.
 

## CREB1 inhibition downregulates E2F1-, ESR1-, and CREB1-target genes and effectively targets endocrine therapy-resistant ER+ breast cancer cells
Our data demonstrate that acquired resistance to ED in ER+ breast cancer (BC) is associated with upregulation of CREB1-, E2F1-, and ESR1-target genes. This suggests that resistant cells may be vulnerable to inhibition of these pathways using CREB1 inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors (to activate RB1 and suppress E2F1), and ER antagonists. To examine this, we treated LTED cells with the CREB1 inhibitor 666-15, the ER degrader fulvestrant, or the CDK4/6 inhibitor (and RB1 activator) abemaciclib. We monitored gene expression changes in response to these treatments and also measured cell viability.
CREB1 requires binding to the histone acetyltransferase CBP for activity. , The CREB1 inhibitor (CREBi) 666-15 blocks CREB1-CBP binding. First, we performed RNA-seq on MCF7 LTED cell treated with vehicle or 666-15 (1 μM) for 24 h ( ). A total of 2,445 genes were downregulated ( ), of which 38.7% (947/2445) were direct CREB1 targets, 13.5% (330/2445) ESR1 targets, and 22.9% (560/2,445) E2F1 targets ( and ). These results confirmed that CREB1 was inhibited by 666-15 and that some E2F1- and ESR1-target genes are CREB1 regulated. Notably, 21% (222/1,058, hypergeometric p = 3.05 × 10−91, 5.05-fold enrichment) of DDGs and 18.7% (304/1,672, hypergeometric p = 4.26 × 10−167, 6.91-fold enrichment) of MCF7 DDGs were downregulated by CREBi, further supporting the involvement of CREB1 in dormancy by regulating gene expression.
Next, LTED cells were treated with 666-15, abemaciclib, or fulvestrant, and target gene expression was examined by qPCR. Abemaciclib activated RB1 in LTED cells, indicated by reduced RB1 phosphorylation ( A). Fulvestrant reduced ERα levels as expected and Crebi slightly reduced ERα levels ( B). As expected, CREB1 and E2F1 co-target genes were reduced by both 666-15 (CREBi) and abemaciclib ( C). Fulvestrant had minimal effect on most of the CREB1 and E2F1 co-target genes, though cyclin D1, CENPN, and MKI67 were partially reduced by fulvestrant ( C), indicating they are also regulated by ERα. Interestingly, established ERα targets were reduced by fulvestrant and also reduced by 666-15 ( D), supporting that these genes may be ERα and CREB1 co-targets. Overall, the results indicate the CREB1 inhibition reduces CREB1-target gene expression while also reducing a subset of ERα- and E2F1-target genes. Notably, abemaciclib had no effect on ERα-target genes (data not shown).
 We then tested therapeutic efficacy of CREBi (666-15), abemaciclib, and fulvestrant. In colony formation assays, 0.5 μM CREBi plus abemaciclib led to complete loss of LTED cell survival, compared to ∼65% loss with abemaciclib or fulvestrant alone or in combination ( E). Even 0.1 μM CREBi reduced survival by ∼45%, and in combination with abemaciclib, caused nearly 90% reduction, exceeding the effect of abemaciclib plus fulvestrant. We used CREB1 depletion by siRNA to validate that these effects were CREB1 dependent. CREB1 depletion significantly impaired colony formation and enhanced abemaciclib efficacy ( F). Furthermore, consistent with the CREB1 KD results in D, CREB1 inhibition upregulated the 8 dormancy-associated genes ( G), supporting a model where CREB1 activity suppresses dormancy and its inhibition restores dormancy-associated gene expression.
Although CDK4/6 inhibitors like abemaciclib are approved for endocrine therapy-resistant ER+ breast cancer, resistance inevitably develops. To model this, we exposed LTED cells to chronic abemaciclib treatment (0.5 μM) for ∼8 months, generating double-resistant LTEDAR cells. These cells showed reduced RB1 expression ( A), consistent with known CDK4/6 resistance mechanisms.
 Surprisingly, LTEDAR cells exhibited elevated levels of S133 phosphorylated (activated) CREB1 and slightly increased ERα levels. RNA-seq revealed 6,925 genes upregulated in LTEDAR vs. LTED cells ( and ), with 74.7% (5,172/6,925) being CREB1 targets, 45.4% (3,145/6,925) E2F1, and 18.2% (1,260/6,925) ESR1 targets ( A), indicating hyperactive CREB1 signaling. Furthermore, although abemaciclib suppressed E2F1-target genes in LTED cells, its effect was blunted in LTEDAR cells ( B). Functionally, LTEDAR cells were resistant to abemaciclib but even more sensitive to CREBi than LTED cells ( B). CREB1 knockdown similarly reduced LTEDAR colony formation and enhanced sensitivity to abemaciclib ( C). CREB1 inhibition and knockdown also suppressed E2F1, CREB1, and ESR1 target genes in LTEDAR cells ( D and 6E), explaining its efficacy.

## Two CREB1-target gene signatures correlate with poor outcomes in AI-treated patients
The results described previously suggest that CREB1 plays a critical role in acquired resistance to endocrine therapy, particularly ED. As shown in , higher expression of several CREB1–E2F1 co-target genes was associated with reduced RFS in patients treated with endocrine therapy. However, because many of these genes are related to cell proliferation, their prognostic impact may primarily reflect intrinsic tumor aggressiveness in treatment-naive disease, making the observed association with poor outcome unsurprising. To more directly assess the contribution of CREB1 to endocrine therapy resistance in clinical settings, we examined whether CREB1-target gene signatures are associated with prognosis in AI-treated patient cohorts.
To derive a clinically relevant CREB1 signature, we intersected patient-derived DDGs with MCF7 DDGs that were annotated as CREB1 targets and downregulated by CREB1 inhibition. This analysis yielded 31 candidate genes ( ), of which 8 were significantly co-expressed in patient tumors in the TCGA pan-breast cancer dataset (cBioPortal) ( A). We defined these 8 genes as a CREB1-activated gene signature and evaluated its prognostic value in AI-treated patients. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high CREB1-activated signature scores had significantly shorter overall survival ( B and ; log rank test, p = 0.041).
 Interestingly, these 8 CREB1-activated signature genes were negatively correlated with NR2F1, whereas NR2F1 showed positive correlation with the seven CREB1-repressed DUGs ( C). We therefore tested whether a gene signature composed of the CREB1-repressed DUGs could also predict clinical outcome in the same AI-treated cohort. Notably, patients with high CREB1-repressed signature scores exhibited significantly prolonged overall survival ( D and ; log rank test, p = 0.040).
Together, these findings support a model in which CREB1 promotes resistance to AI therapy and indicate that CREB1-target gene signatures may serve as predictive biomarkers of response to endocrine therapy.

## Discussion
This study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning dormancy and acquired resistance in ER+ breast cancer, particularly in the context of ED therapy. By leveraging longitudinal clinical samples, in vitro models, and PDXs, we identified a conserved transcriptional program that is suppressed during tumor dormancy and reactivated upon recurrence. Central to this program are 3 key transcription factors—CREB1, E2F1, and ESR1—whose coordinated activity appears essential for the transition from growth arrest to endocrine therapy resistance.
Our reanalysis of clinical gene expression data from patients treated with neoadjuvant AIs revealed 2 distinct sets of genes—termed DDGs—that are suppressed specifically in tumors that remain dormant but not in those that develop resistance and recur and DUGs—that are upregulated in dormant tumors but not in acquired resistant tumors. Pathway and transcription factor enrichment analyses of these genes pointed to CREB1 as the dominant regulator, with E2F1 and ESR1 also playing prominent roles. These findings implicate a transcriptional axis involving CREB1, E2F1, and ESR1 in maintaining cell-cycle activity and tumor growth, which becomes attenuated during dormancy and reactivated during relapse.
In both cell-based and PDX models, we confirmed that ED induces a transient dormancy characterized by repression of CREB1-, E2F1-, and ESR1-target genes, reduced phosphorylation of CREB1 and RB1, and induction of the dormancy-associated gene including NR2F1. Reactivation of these pathways in LTED cells and recurrent tumors was accompanied by restored CREB1 and RB1 phosphorylation, consistent with transcriptional reawakening and a return to a proliferative state. These findings are in line with previous reports showing that RB1 activity enforces cell-cycle arrest and that CREB1 is activated by estrogen and multiple kinase pathways, including phosphorylation at serine 133 (S133) by PKA, MSK, AKT, and AMPK. , , We propose that increased CREB1 phosphorylation in LTED cells reflects heightened activity of these kinases, which are implicated in endocrine therapy resistance. , , 
The observed increase in ERα protein levels during both dormancy and resistance suggests a dual role for ESR1, contributing to dormant-state maintenance while also undergoing adaptive upregulation during resistance, as previously reported. , Notably, CREB1 stimulates and is necessary for optimal ERα activity, and high CREB1 expression has been correlated with improved prognosis in ER+ BC patients. We propose that CREB1 and ERα function in a co-dependent manner to coordinate estrogen-driven proliferation. High CREB1 expression may promote favorable endocrine responses by increasing ERα activity and dependence. While CREB1 and ERα activity are inhibited during ED dormancy, CREB1 reactivation may enhance the estrogen-independent reactivation of ERα thereby enabling dormancy exit.
The functional importance of this transcriptional axis was further demonstrated by pharmacologic and genetic inhibition studies. CREB1 inhibition using the small-molecule inhibitor 666-15 disrupted the expression of CREB1-, E2F1-, and ESR1-regulated genes and impaired the survival of LTED cells. In combination with the CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib, CREB1 inhibition exhibited synergistic effects, achieving complete suppression of colony formation in cells resistant to ED, outperforming the combination of abemaciclib and fulvestrant. Importantly, inhibition of CREB1 also reactivated dormancy-associated genes, including NR2F1, BTG2, and GAS1, among others, suggesting a shift toward a less proliferative, more dormant cellular state.
A particularly novel finding was that long-term treatment with abemaciclib led to the emergence of a dual-resistant phenotype (LTEDAR) marked by RB1 loss and hyperactivation of CREB1 signaling. These LTEDAR cells showed resistance to abemaciclib but remained highly sensitive to CREB1 inhibition. We hypothesized that RB1 loss in LTEDAR cells may contribute to CREB1 activation. To test this, we used shRNA to stably deplete RB1 in MCF7 cells. Immunoblotting showed that RB1 knockdown increased CREB1 phosphorylation (pS133) ( A). These cells were resistant to abemaciclib, maintained E2F1-target gene expression with abemaciclib treatment ( B and S3C) but became more sensitive to CREBi ( B). CREBi in RB1-depleted cells downregulated both E2F1- and ESR1-target genes ( D). These results suggest a feedback loop in which loss of RB1 drives compensatory CREB1 activation, driving continued proliferation via E2F1 and ESR1 targets and therapeutic escape. This not only underscores CREB1’s role as a central modulator in resistance but also identifies it as a critical vulnerability in tumors that have exhausted current endocrine and CDK4/6 inhibitor options. Consistent with the proposed role of CREB1 in promoting endocrine therapy resistance, we identified a CREB1 target gene signatures that are coexpressed in clinical tumor samples, with high CREB1-activated gene scores (GSs) significantly correlating with poor survival whereas high CREB1-repressed GSs significantly correlating with good survival in AI-treated patients.
Collectively, our data propose a model in which ED induces a dormancy state via RB1-mediated suppression of E2F1 and inactivation of CREB1 signaling. Escape from dormancy coincides with reactivation of CREB1 and E2F1 transcriptional programs, facilitated by RB1 inactivation and sustained ER signaling. As such, targeting CREB1 offers a mechanistically rational approach to both prevent recurrence and treat established resistance, including cases of primary endocrine resistance where RB1 is intact, and cases with co-resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors where RB1 function is compromised.

## Limitations of the study
It is important to acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, our conclusions regarding the role of CREB1 in promoting exit from dormancy are largely correlative, as we were unable to demonstrate that forced expression of an active form of CREB1 is sufficient to induce dormancy exit in ED cells. Second, the dataset from Selli et al. does not provide log fold-change values, which precluded the use of a pre-ranked GSEA analysis. Third, our RNA-seq dataset was generated from a single biological replicate; therefore, we used qPCR to independently validate key findings. Finally, transcription factor enrichment analysis based on hypergeometric testing does not encompass all possible regulators, and additional, as-yet-unidentified transcription factors may also contribute to the regulation of dormancy and acquired resistance, warranting further investigation in future studies.
Nonetheless, given that CREB1 is known to interact with ESR1 and E2F1 and regulates genes involved in proliferation, survival, and therapy resistance, these findings position CREB1 as a hub of convergent resistance pathways in ER+ breast cancer. Therapeutic targeting of CREB1, particularly in combination with CDK4/6 inhibitors, could represent a promising strategy to forestall or overcome resistance, warranting further preclinical development and eventual clinical evaluation.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Cell line culture
MCF7 breast cancer cell line (RRID:CVCL_0031) was obtained from ATCC (HTB-22) and used within ten passages of their acquisition and within two months in culture. The cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling performed by the Molecular Diagnostics Core at Rush. MCF7 cells were grown in DMEM medium, with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL). Testing with the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection kit (Lonza, catalog #LT07-118) confirmed the absence of mycoplasma contamination in these cell lines. For estrogen deprivation, cells were grown in phenol-red free DMED supplemented with Charcoal-striped FBS. Long term estrogen deprived (LTED) cells were obtained by continuously growing MCF7 cells in phenol-red free DMEM supplemented with Charcoal-striped FBS for more than 13 months.

## PDX mouse model
Female NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtmlWjl/Sz (NSG) (RRID:IMSR_JAX:005557) mice, aged 6-8 weeks, were used exclusively due to the predominantly female incidence of breast cancer.
Prior to xenografting, all mice were subcutaneously implanted with estrogen pellets (1.5 mg/pellet, 90-day release; Innovative Research of America). The ER+/PR+/ErbB2− patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumor line (TM00386) was obtained from The Jackson Laboratory and describe previously. Once the tumor reached a volume of 1 cm3, tumor-bearing mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested aseptically. Tumors were finely minced using sterile forceps and scalpels, then loaded into a 1 ml syringe and subcutaneously implanted into recipient NSG mice (100 μl per mouse) using a 14G needle.

## Method details

## siRNA-mediated transient knockdown
Pre-designed siRNAs against Creb1 (pooled siRNAs), and Control siRNA (universal negative control #1) were purchased from Horizondiscovery. Cells were transfected according to the manufacturer’s guidelines using DharmaFECT I reagent.

## RNA isolation, real-time quantitative PCR, and RNA-seq analyses
Total RNA was prepared using Total RNA Mini Kit (IBI Scientific, IA).
For PCR analysis, the first cDNA strand was synthesized using High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA). Manufacturers’ protocols were followed in each case. The PCR primers for the indicated genes are listed in . SYBR green PCR kit (Applied Biosystems) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. AB7300 system was used as follows: activation at 95°C; 2 minutes, 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C; 15 seconds and annealing/extension at 60°C; 60 seconds, followed by melt analysis ramping from 60°C to 95°C. Relative gene expression was determined by the ΔΔCt method using β-Actin to normalize. PCR reaction was conducted in technical triplicate and average CT values were used to calculate relative expression of genes.
RNA sequencing and primary bioinformatics analyses were performed by Novogene using their standard mRNA-seq pipeline (see detailed description in the Novogene report in Supplemental file 1). Libraries were prepared from poly(A)+ RNA and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. The human reference genome hg38 was used for all downstream analyses.
Raw reads in FASTQ format were subjected to quality control and filtering to generate clean reads. Reads were removed if they contained adapter contamination, if ambiguous nucleotides (N) constituted more than 10% of either read, or if low-quality bases (Phred score < 5) constituted more than 50% of the read. Quality metrics including error rate, Q20/Q30 scores, and GC content were assessed for each sample.
Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using the splice-aware aligner HISAT2, which supports accurate mapping of reads spanning exon–exon junctions. Only uniquely mapped reads were retained for downstream quantification; reads mapping to multiple genomic locations were excluded from gene-level counting.
Gene-level read counts were obtained using a feature-counting approach based on annotated gene models, and gene expression levels were reported as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) values. For downstream analyses and cross-sample comparisons, FPKM values were converted to TPM by normalizing each gene’s FPKM to the sum of FPKM values within each sample and scaling by 10ˆ6, such that the total expression per sample equals one million.
Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package, as implemented in the Novogene analysis pipeline. Raw read counts were used as input, and library size normalization was performed using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method. Gene-wise dispersion was estimated using edgeR’s empirical Bayes framework, which borrows information across genes to obtain stabilized dispersion estimates under a negative binomial model. Differential expression was assessed using likelihood-based statistical testing, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with an adjusted p-value (FDR) ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

## Immunoblotting
Immunoblotting was done as described. Whole-cell extracts were prepared by scraping cells into lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.5). Proteins were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The following primary antibodies were used: p-Creb1 (S133) (RRID:AB_2561044), Creb1 (RRID:AB_1903940), p-RB1 (Ser807/811) (RRID:AB_331472), RB1 (RRID:AB_823629), ERα (RRID:AB_2617128) (all from Cell Signaling Technology); and β-actin (RRID:AB_626632) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Primary antibodies were detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Life Technologies), followed by visualization with Clarity chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad). Each experiment shown is representative of at least two independent and reproducible experiments.

## Lentiviral infection
The lentiviral pLKO-control shRNA (Addgene plasmid #1864) and pLKO-RB1 shRNA purchased from Openbiosystems (clone ID TRCN0000040163 for RB1 shRNA) were cotransfected with the lentiviral packaging and envelop vectors psPAX2 and pMD2.G into 293FT cells as described. The lentiviral supernatants were collected 24 hrs after transfection and used to infect subconfluent MCF7 cell lines for 24 hours. The cells were then selected with puromycin (2 μM) for 72 hours to establish polyclonal lines.

## Identification of dormancy downregulated genes (DDGs) and dormancy upregulated genes (DUGs)
The GSE111563 dataset comprises sequential tumor samples from 62 patients undergoing extended neoadjuvant aromatase inhibitor therapy (4–45 months) with letrozole. Transcriptomic data were collected at three time points: baseline (≤ 0 days), early treatment (13–120 days), and long-term treatment (> 120 days). All patients experienced at least a 40% reduction in tumor size within the first four months of therapy. By the end of the follow-up period, 42 patients exhibited a “dormant” phenotype, while 20 developed “acquired resistance.”
Differential gene expression data comparing pre-treatment, short-term (early), long-term dormant, and long-term acquired resistant tumors were extracted from the supplemental tables of the original study. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with Rank Products. The significance of differences was evaluated by using unpaired Wilcoxon test for two groups and analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc Tukey HSD for multiple comparisons, as described in the original paper. We further defined DDGs (Dormancy-Downregulated Genes) as those uniquely downregulated in dormant tumors but not in acquired resistant tumors by identifying and excluding overlapping genes ( ). We also defined DUGs (Dormancy-Upregulated Genes) as those uniquely upregulated in dormant tumors but not in acquired resistant tumors by identifying and excluding overlapping genes ( ).

## Analysis of TCGA database
Coexpression analysis was performed using the Breast Invasive Carcinoma dataset (TCGA, PanCancer Atlas; 1,084 patients) accessed through cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org). To evaluate the association between the identified gene signature and clinical outcomes in patients treated with aromatase inhibitors, cases receiving treatment with anastrozole (n = 231), letrozole (n = 66), or exemestane (n = 44) were selected. Of these, 296 patients with both clinical outcome data and pre-treatment tumor RNAseq data were included in the analysis. mRNA expression levels (TPM) for AURKB, CDC20, CENPA, GGH, H2AC20, MND1, PRC1, and PSPH (CREB1-activated genes) as well as NR2F1, BTG2, FLRT2, SPRY1, NR4A2, GAS1, HOXA5, and STAT5B (CREB1-repressed genes) were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org). Expression of each gene was dichotomized into high or low groups based on the median expression across all patients, and assigned a score of 1 (high) or 0 (low). A composite gene score (GS) was calculated as the sum of individual gene scores, yielding values from 0 to 8. For CREB1-activated genes, the patients with GS scores 7–8 were defined as GS-high, and those with GS scores 0–6 as GS-low. For CREB1-repressed gene, the patients with GS scores 5–8 were defined as GS-high, and those with GS scores 0–4 as GS-low. the Overall survival was compared between GS-high and GS-low groups using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank testing in IBM SPSS software. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## In vivo ER+ PDX xenografting and estrogen deprivation
All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Rush University Medical Center (Protocol #18-071). The study adheres to the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal research. Female NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtmlWjl/Sz (NSG) (RRID:IMSR_JAX:005557) mice, aged 6-8 weeks, were used exclusively due to the predominantly female incidence of breast cancer. Mice were housed in pathogen-free conditions and monitored regularly for health and tumor burden, in line with humane endpoints.
Prior to xenografting, all mice were subcutaneously implanted with estrogen pellets (1.5 mg/pellet, 90-day release; Innovative Research of America). The ER+/PR+/ErbB2− patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumor line (TM00386) was obtained from The Jackson Laboratory and describe previously. Once the tumor reached a volume of 1 cm3, tumor-bearing mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested aseptically. Tumors were finely minced using sterile forceps and scalpels, then loaded into a 1 ml syringe and subcutaneously implanted into recipient NSG mice (100 μl per mouse) using a 14G needle.
When tumors reached approximately 600 mm3 (∼4 weeks post-implantation), biopsies were performed for protein and mRNA analysis. Estrogen pellets were subsequently removed, and tumors were monitored continuously. A second biopsy was performed around 16 weeks post-removal, with final tumor harvest conducted at approximately 36 weeks when tumor size had regressed to ∼1 mm3. At that point, mice were euthanized, and tumors were collected for further molecular analysis.

## Quantification and statistical analysis

## Quantification
Transcription Factor Enrichment Analysis: Target gene lists for CREB1, ESR1, and E2F1 were obtained from the Harmonizome database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Harmonizome). For each transcription factor, we generated a comprehensive list of targets by combining the ChEA Transcription Factor Targets and the ENCODE Transcription Factor Targets. The DDGs, DUGs, MCF7 DDGs, and CREB1-downregulated genes were then analyzed for overlap with these transcription factor target sets.
Enrichment analysis was performed using hypergeometric calculations via the online tool (https://systems.crump.ucla.edu/hypergeometric/index.php). Parameters were defined as follows: the number of successes (k) corresponded to the number of downregulated genes overlapping with transcription factor target genes; the sample size (s) was the total number of downregulated genes; the number of successes in the population (M) was the total number of transcription factor target genes; and the population size (N) was the total number of genes represented in the RNA-seq and GeneChip datasets. The analysis yielded fold enrichment values and hypergeometric p-values. Enrichment was considered significant if the fold enrichment was greater than 2 and the p-value was less than 0.001.

## Statistical analysis
One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student’s t-test were used to determine the statistical significance of differences among experimental groups. Prior to statistical analysis the normality of each dataset was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances with Levene’s test using the SPSS software. In all cases the data did not deviate significantly from a normal distribution and variances were comparable between groups, justifying the use of parametric tests. Student’s t-test was used to determine the statistical significance between control and experimental groups. A minimum of three biological replicates were used in each experiment to ensure statistical robustness.
