Communication between CAFs and cancer cells orchestrates key aspects of tumor dynamics, including the induction of dormancy. In response to adverse environmental stimuli, such as hypoxia, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy, cancer cells enter a dormant state, enabling them to evade environmental stimuli. The duration of this dormancy varies with the type, differentiation degree, and proliferation rate of primary cancer. The process is regulated by CAFs through various mediators, including growth factors, chemokines, inflammatory factors, exosomes, etc. Notably, environmental stimuli can induce the formation of polyploid giant cancer cells (PGCCs), a dormant cell type characterized by high drug resistance, tumorigenicity, and stemness. This review summarizes the relationship between CAFs and cancer cell dormancy, and explores the potential mechanisms through which CAFs regulate this process.

## Introduction
Normal fibroblasts (NFs) are typically quiescent stromal constituents embedded within the extracellular matrix (ECM), which become activated in response to tissue injury or other stimuli [ ]. Fibroblasts can originally perform a tumor-suppressive function but may be affected by the development of cancer and thus appear to promote cancer development and progression [ ]. Activated fibroblasts are characterized by enhanced proliferative and migratory capacity, metabolic reprogramming, and modified production of ECM components and growth factors production and are susceptible to epigenetic modifications [ , ]. When associated with cancer, these activated fibroblasts are designated cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [ ]. CAFs exhibit upregulated expression of specific surface markers, including alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibroblast activation protein, fibroblast-specific protein 1, and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β (PDGFR-β), etc [ ]. As critical components of the tumor microenvironment (TME), CAFs and the associated fibrotic reaction evolve dynamically throughout tumor progression. By secreting a diverse array of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix-degrading enzymes, CAFs orchestrate key malignant progression such as cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [ , ].
Cancer cells can disseminate to distant sites in disease progression, entering a dormant state that may persist for decades after primary tumor resection or chemoradiotherapy [ , ]. These disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) can subsequently reactivate, leading to the relapse of metastatic tumors. The hallmarks of this dormant state include cell cycle arrest, drug resistance, immune escape, metastatic relapse, and cancer reversibility [ ]. Stopping proliferation and entering cell cycle arrest are the premises and signs of the tumor entering dormancy. The development of clinical anti-tumor drug resistance is largely related to cancer cell dormancy, and dormant cancer cells are the root cause of tumor recurrence. The underlying mechanisms governing dormancy involve the quiescence of tumors, interactions with the extracellular and stromal microenvironments, autophagy, and epigenetic regulation [ ]. Furthermore, the maintenance of DTC dormancy is actively supported by the preservation of tissue homeostasis, particularly through immune and fibroblast-mediated equilibrium [ ].
Polyploid giant cancer cells (PGCCs) are produced by endoreduplication or cell fusion and have multiple or single giant nuclei [ , ]. PGCCs represent a population of cancer cells that evade treatment by entering a temporary dormant state, subsequently relapsing via diploidization to generate chemoresistant progeny [ , ]. PGCCs have the characteristics of cancer stem cells (CSCs) and generate daughter cells through asymmetric division [ ]. The formation of PGCCs is induced by various stimuli, including chemotherapeutic agents, radiation, and hypoxia, which dysregulate the expression of proteins involved in cell cycle control and cell fusion [ , , ]. The number of PGCCs is closely related to the malignancy degree of the tumor [ ].
As crucial components of the TME, CAFs play a central role in regulating cancer cell dormancy. This review is structured around the regulatory interplay between CAFs and dormant cancer cells. We summarized the bidirectional crosstalk wherein CAFs influence the formation, maintenance, and reactivation of dormant cancer cells, while the dormant cancer cells can in turn promote CAF reactivation. Based on this reciprocal relationship, we explore therapeutic strategies that target CAFs to suppress the induction and recurrence of dormancy. Furthermore, as a special population of dormant cancer cells, PGCCs and their specific interaction with CAFs were also discussed.

## CAFs: origins and heterogeneity
CAFs originate from a diverse spectrum of cell types, such as NFs, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) (another suggested precursor of CAFs), epithelial cells via epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), endothelial cells via endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition, adipocytes, pericytes, smooth muscle cells, and stellate cells [ , ]. This cellular diversity contributes significantly to the phenotypic and functional heterogeneity of CAFs. A summary of these cellular origins, alongside distinct CAF subsets and their key functions, is presented in Fig. .

 

CAFs comprise multiple fibroblast subsets, each with distinct functions, which exhibit dynamic plasticity and can interconvert under specific conditions (Table ) [ – ]. During early tumorigenesis, inflammatory stimuli drive fibroblasts to adopt pro-inflammatory and pro-tumorigenic phenotypes. Cytokines such as interleukin-1-beta (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) activate the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway, promoting the secretion of tumor-promoting factors and contributing to the maintenance of CAF phenotype [ ]. CAFs can also maintain their myofibroblastic characteristics independently of continuous interaction with cancer cells [ , ]. A hallmark of CAFs is the activation of the yes-associated protein (YAP) transcription factor, which causes stromal sclerosis to generate a positive feedback loop, resulting in stable and sustained activation of the YAP pathway observed in CAFs. This also explains the stability of the CAF phenotype in the absence of direct contact with cancer cells [ ].

 

CAFs orchestrate cancer progression by remodeling the ECM, inducing angiogenesis, and recruiting inflammatory cells [ ]. While the majority of evidence supports a tumor-promoting role for CAFs in the TME, some studies have paradoxically indicated that they can exert tumor-suppressive effects under specific conditions [ , ], which may be attributed to the existence of distinct CAF subset with anti-tumorigenic properties [ ]. For example, the tumor-suppressive function of inhibitor κB kinase β/NF-κB in CAFs has been demonstrated by using an autochthonous model of colitis-associated cancer and sporadic cancer [ ].

## CAFs are important components of the TME
The TME is a multicellular niche characterized by hypoxia and acidosis, wherein various stromal cells play critical roles in driving cancer progression [ ]. As a major stromal component, CAFs are the center of cross-communication between the tumor stroma and cancer cells and play an important role in cancer cell progression and the establishment of dormancy [ , ].
The TME acts as a reservoir of growth factors, cytokines, and other factors that transfer various signals to resident fibroblasts, thereby promoting their transformation into CAFs. TME-derived oxidative stress upregulates microRNA (miR)−200c through deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) demethylation, which reprograms fibroblasts to the mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition, inducing a senescent phenotype, activates metabolic and inflammatory reprogramming, fosters tumor growth, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, ultimately promotes cancer progression [ ]. The hypoxic TME activates IL-1β signal transduction to enhance CAF invasive phenotype [ ]. In turn, activated CAFs remodel the TME by secreting ECM proteins and angiogenic factors, thereby creating a pro-tumorigenic niche that facilitates angiogenesis and metastasis of cancer [ ]. Α−1,6-fucosyltransferase/core fucosylation in CAFs promotes the formation of aggressive TME both in vivo and in vitro [ ]. Dual inhibition of fibroblast growth factor (FGF) and colony stimulating factor 1 or vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling has been shown to enhance anti-tumor effects by targeting immune escape and angiogenesis in the TME [ ].

## The mechanism of CAFs promoting cancer cell dormancy and maintenance
The mechanisms by which CAFs regulate the dormancy of cancer cells are mainly associated with ERK/P38 and TGF-β signaling pathway [ – ] (Fig. ). In addition, autophagy, matrix stiffness, angiogenesis, and CAF-secreted cytokines were also involved in the dormancy of cancer cells [ – ] (Fig. ).

 

 

## Fibronectin (FN) secreted by CAFs regulates cancer cell dormancy through the extracellular regulatory kinase (ERK)/P38 signaling pathway
ERK is a key regulator of cell cycle and cytoskeletal recombination [ ]. The transition between active and dormant states is determined by a balance between two major mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways: mitogenic ERK and apoptosis/growth suppressor stress-activated protein kinase 2 (P38MAPK). High ERK/P38 ratios induce cell proliferation, whereas low ERK/P38 ratios induce cell dormancy. The regulation of ERK and P38 is mediated by the regulation of urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR), integrin α5β1, and FN [ ]. Inhibition of the ERK signaling pathway can induce cancer cell dormancy. The ERK signaling pathway is mainly produced by the fusion of two pathways: one is produced by the conduction of uPAR and integrin α5β1, and the other is related to FN and P38 activity [ ]. Downregulation of uPAR activity induces cancer cell dormancy, and the downregulation of uPAR leads to the abatement of uPA/uPAR/integrin α5β1-dependent signal transduction and decreases mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase/ERK pathway activation, thereby leading to dormancy [ ]. Integrin α5β1 adheres to FN and activates focal adhesion kinase (FAK), ERK, protein kinase B (AKT), and other signal transduction pathways [ ]. FAK inhibition decouples integrin α5β1 from the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), leading to tumor dormancy [ ]. EGFR-mediated activation of the uPAR/integrin/FN-induced growth pathway is ligand-independent. The expression of uPAR is important for the ERK signaling pathway, and the inhibition of EGFR signaling, uPAR, or its downstream FAK signaling can reduce the transduction of ERK signaling pathways, thereby inducing dormancy [ , ]. FN is the main ECM component secreted by CAFs, is crucial for maintaining long-term cancer cell dormancy. Dormant human breast cancer cells (BCCs) can also continue to produce and assemble a FN matrix through integrin α5β1-mediated adhesion and Rho-associated kinase-mediated cell tension [ ]. Integrin β1 serves as a key switch for reactivation from dormancy to metastatic growth. Integrin β1 activates the FAK/ERK pathway, and ERK induces myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) to phosphorylate MLC and translocate P27 to the cytoplasm. Inhibition of integrin β1 or its downstream signal transduction molecules, Src, p-FAK, p-ERK, and MLCK, ultimately leads to reduced MLC synthesis, maintaining the cancer cell dormancy [ ].

## CAFs can induce a quiescence-associated phenotype of cancer cells by the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling pathway
There are two methods of inducing cancer cell dormancy. One the one hand, TGF-β2 activates Smad1/5 via TGF-β receptor I (TGF-βRI) and TGF-βRIII, thereby inducing P27 expression. On the other hand, TGF-β2 activates the P38MAPK signaling pathway, leading to the induction of P27and the downregulation of cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) [ ]. TGF-βRIII enhances TGF-β-mediated dormancy through the TGF-βRI/Smad3-dependent and P38MAPK pathways [ ]. Both TGF-β2 and growth differentiation factor 10 can induce long-term dormancy of prostate cancer cells, and removal of either factors in vitro is insufficient to cause dormant prostate cancer cells to exit dormancy and resume proliferation [ ]. Osteoblast-secreted factors mediate dormancy in prostate cancer via activation of the TGF-βRIII-P38MAPK-pS249/T252RB pathway [ ].
TGF-β signaling in fibroblasts modulates the tumorigenicity of adjacent epithelial cells [ ]. In co-culture systems, TGF-β and reactive oxygen species (ROS) cooperatively induce metabolic reprogramming in both cancer cells and CAFs [ ]. As a depot for the TGF-β latent complex, FN makes TGF-β readily available to cells by coupling with the fibrillary FN matrix [ ]. Hepatic stellate cells can be converted into CAFs by TGF-β1 or FGF2. When co-cultured with cancer cells, hepatic stellate cells secrete IL-8, promoting a quiescent phenotype in pancreatic ductal epithelial cells and inducing a reversible dormancy in disseminated pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells [ ]. However, in a contrasting mechanism, activated hepatic stellate cells can secrete CXC motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), which induces NK cell quiescence via CXC chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) while simultaneously promoting the proliferation of DTCs [ ].

## CAFs regulate cancer cell dormancy by regulating autophagy
Low level of autophagy contributes to the survival of dormant cancer cells under nutrient-deprived conditions, whereas sustained and intense autophagic activation can lead to cancer cell death. Aplasia ras homolog member I (ARHI) can induce autophagic cell death by inhibiting the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways and can promote tumor dormancy under certain conditions [ , ]. Resveratrol can keep ovarian cancer cells dormant, which may be related to Beclin 1-dependent autophagy and ARHI [ ]. Overexpression of Wee1 can lead to G2/M arrest, and autophagy can degrade Wee1 and affect its function [ ]. Mitophagy can induce head and neck squamous cells to enter a dormant state [ ].
CAFs induce cancer cell autophagy and dormancy by secreting insulin-like growth factor 1/2 (IGF1/2), CXCL12, and other molecules, which elevate ROS and inhibit mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) to induce autophagy [ ]. IGF1 secreted by cancer cells can bind extracellular stress protein clusters to negatively regulate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [ ]. IL-6 inhibits autophagy in ovarian cancer by downregulating ARHI via miRNAs [ ]. ARHI induces autophagy in cancer cells, whereas VEGF, IGF-1, and IL-8 impair ARHI-induced autophagy and enhance signal transduction via the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways. Salvage experiments showed that VEGF, IGF-1, and IL-8 protect dormant ovarian cancer cells from ARHI-induced autophagy [ ]. ARHI-driven autophagy and growth arrest can be suppressed by TME-derived survival signals, which can result in cancer dormancy. The balance between the pro-autophagic and antiproliferation activity of ARHI and anti-autophagic and pro-proliferative activities of survival factors dictates the destiny of cancer cells: autophagic death, sustained dormancy, or proliferative recovery [ ].

## CAFs orchestrate cancer cell dormancy by regulating matrix stiffness
A primary mechanism by which CAFs regulate cancer cell dormancy is through the control of matrix stiffness. In hepatocellular carcinoma, a stiff matrix promotes proliferation and chemoresistance, whereas a soft matrix induces reversible dormancy and stem cell characteristics [ ]. The ECM, composed of various proteins, including FN, collagen, laminin, elastin, and proteoglycans [ , ]. CAFs secrete typical fibroblast products such as collagen, FN, or laminin, affecting the formation and remodeling of the ECM, which in turn affects cancer cell dormancy. By using a reversible dormancy model, Barney, L.E., et al. has established the ECM as a critical determinant for entry into and exit from dormancy, identifying it as a relevant therapeutic target to prevent the reactivation of dormant cell populations [ ].
Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) is activated in CAFs, whereas TGF-β and CXCL12 are produced and secreted by fibroblasts in an HSF1 dependent manner [ ]. HSF1 upregulates dickkopf-3 in the tumor stroma, enhancing wingless-related integration site (Wnt) signaling, and reducing YAP degradation, thereby promoting the generation of a stiff ECM and an aggressive TME [ , ]. The CAFs of Snail 1 (+) increase ECM stiffness through the Snail 1/ras homolog family member A/α-SMA pathway, and inhibition of Snail 1 function in CAFs suppresses tumor-driven ECM remodeling [ ]. Snail 1 promotes angiogenesis with platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) [ ]. Cell division cycle 42 (Cdc42) also involved the dormancy of cancer cells [ ]. Increased matrix stiffness translocates Cdc42 to the nucleus and activates P27 and P21 through epigenetic activation, while downregulating integrin β3, ultimately inducing a dormant state [ ].

## CAFs influence cancer cell dormancy by regulating angiogenesis
The angiogenesis and vascular maturation are also closely related to the dormancy of cancer cells [ ]. CAFs stimulate new blood vessel formation both directly and via macrophage recruitment, primarily through the secretion of pro-angiogenic growth factors such as VEGF A, FGF2, CXCL12, and PDGF C [ , ]. VEGF can maintain the viability of dormant autophagic cancer cells and promote the re-expression of ARHI, which can induce the dormancy of ovarian cancer cells [ ]. Similarly, the CXCL12-CXCR4-CXCR7 axis, driven in part by Twist1 in CAFs, is implicated in both angiogenesis and the regulation of dormancy-related genes [ – ]. Fibroblasts also secrete periostin (POSTN) [ ]. A stable microvascular system forms a “dormant niche.” The creation of new blood vessels reshapes the extracellular environment, producing POSTN and TGF-β1 to stimulate dormant cancer cells and induce tumor growth [ ]. The tip of the neovasculature is rich in tumor-promoting factors such as active TGF-β1 and POSTN, which promote the growth of BCCs. However, endothelium-derived thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) induces persistent BCC quiescence [ ]. As an inhibitor of angiogenesis, angiostatin can induce cancer cell dormancy [ ].

## Other cytokines secreted by CAFs affect cancer cell dormancy
Single-cell sequencing of clinical cohorts has identified nuclear receptor subfamily group 2 F member 1 (NR2F1) as a biomarker highly enriched in dormant cancer cells [ ]. NR2F1 is predominantly expressed in CAFs, particularly in the iCAF subset [ ]. In vitro studies indicate that NR2F1-induced dormancy depends on its direct target Sry-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) and retinoic acid receptor β, and is associated with altered expression of P27 and P16 proteins [ ]. Mechanistically, NR2F1 regulates cancer cell dormancy via the CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling pathway [ ]. NR2F1 also induces global chromatin inhibition and promotes expression of the pluripotency gene NANOG, thereby reinforcing DTC dormancy in the bone marrow [ ]. Therapeutically, NR2F1 agonists and DNA demethylating agents that upregulate NR2F1 can induce a long-term dormant state of cancer cells, suggesting a potential strategy to suppress metastasis [ , ]. However, existing evidence remains insufficient to conclusively establish that NR2F1-expressing CAFs directly regulate cancer cell dormancy.
CAF-derived interferon (IFN)-γ regulates stem cell markers in prostate cancer cells [ ]. Myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) in breast cancer CAFs contain IFN-α/β-myCAF subgroup associated with the IFN-α/β pathways [ ]. Persistent activation of the IFN-β/IFNAR/IFN regulatory factor 7 signaling axis in chemotherapy-treated ER− BCCs induces immune dormancy [ ]. In melanoma, both IFN-β and IFN-γ can activate indolamine 2,3-dioxygenase/kynurenine/aryl hydrocarbon receptor/P27 to mediate dormancy; however, IFN-β has a stronger ability to induce dormancy, which may be related to the fact that IFN-β can induce serine phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 [ ].
YAP is the core of the Hippo signaling pathway and is activated in dormant cancer cells [ ]. When cancer cells co-cultured with CAFs, the glycolysis of cancer cells increases, leading to an increased proliferation and invasion ability of cancer cells [ ]. Conversely, reduced glycolysis may contribute to dormancy by inhibiting YAP or modulating integrin expression to facilitate basement membrane adhesion [ ]. TSP-1, growth arrest-specific 6 (GAS6), bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7), TGF-β2, and osteopontin mediate cancer cell dormancy [ – ]. CAF-derived TSP-1 affects cancer cells [ ]. CXCL12 receptor binding of prostate cancer cells leads to an increased expression of integrin, CD164, and annexin A2, resulting in GAS6-induced quiescence of prostate cancer cells [ ]. As a way of intercellular communication, extracellular vesicles (EVs) include exosomes and microvesicles [ ]. CAFs and cancer cells communicate not only through classical paracrine signaling mechanisms but also through exosomes. miRNAs are the main components of exosomes. Dysregulation of exosomal miRNAs and miRNAs mediates CAF formation and activation. Exosomal miR-23b from bone marrow MSCs promotes dormancy in BCCs. Adult fibroblasts also contain miR-23b carrying exosomes [ ].

## Mechanism of CAF promoting reactivation of dormant cancer cells
The TME is indispensable for malignant tumor seed recurrence, especially for distant metastasis, with CAFs constituting the core of this pro-metastatic “soil”. Metastasis can be divided into three phases that overlap over time: dissemination, dormancy, and colonization [ ]. Cancer cells complete the metastatic cascade through invasion, intravasation, survival in the circulation, extravasation, and metastatic colonization [ ]. After cessation of external radiation and chemotherapy, dormant cancer can reactivate and begin to proliferate, leading to the formation of metastatic foci and tumor recurrence. CAFs-derived EVs and exosomes, inflammation, TGF-β signaling pathway, ECM, EMT, and cytokines secreted by CAFs are associated with the reactivation of dormant cancer cells [ , , – ] (Fig. ).

 

## EVs and exosomes secreted by CAFs regulate the proliferation and metastasis of dormant cancer cells
Cancer cells may experience dormancy due to the loss of mitochondrial DNA, and CAF-EVs induce metastasis by transferring the lost mitochondrial DNA into CSCs [ ]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), CAFs deliver exosomal miR-17-5p to cancer cells, which in turn sustains TGF-β1 secretion via the RUNX family transcription factor 3 (RUNX3)/MYC proto-oncogene (MYC)/TGF-β1 signaling axis. This process further activates CAFs, establishing a positive feedback loop that enhances exosome-mediated pro-tumor signaling and accelerates disease progression [ ]. CAF-derived exosomes package functional miR-196a and deliver it to cancer cells, thereby regulating head and neck cancer cell survival and proliferation [ ]. Exosomal miR-21 from cancer-associated adipocytes or CAFs suppresses apoptosis and confers chemoresistance [ ]. The exosome miR-500a-5p derived from CAFs regulates the proliferation and metastasis of BCCs by targeting ubiquitin-specific peptidase 28 [ , ].

## CAFs reactivate dormant cancer cells and promote metastasis via inflammatory signals
Neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)-mediated laminin is hydrolyzed by neutrophil elastase and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), activating integrin and FAK/ERK/MLCK/YAP signaling, and ultimately triggering the reactivation of dormant cancer cells [ ]. Activated fibroblasts produce high levels of pro-inflammatory factors that promote metastatic cancer growth [ ]. NF-κB is a key regulator of CAF-mediated tumor inflammation. In response to inflammatory signals, CAFs activate NF-κB signaling and drive macrophage recruitment, angiogenesis, and tumor growth. These effects are suppressed upon inhibition of the NF-κB pathway [ ]. Stellate cells are a source of CAFs, and activated stellate cells inhibit dormancy maintained by NK cells through CXCL12, thus mediating metastatic growth [ ]. Several inflammatory genes upregulated in CAFs are targets of known NF-κB transcription factors (cyclooxygenase-2, IL-6, CXCL1, CXCL2, and IL-1β) [ ]. IL-1β not only activates NF-κB but also operates in a dual capacity, as both an inducer and an effector in the CAF phenotype [ , ]. Result from a co-culture study show that cancer cells “educate” normal fibroblasts to express pro-inflammatory genes, a transformation potentially driven by IL-1β [ ]. Similarly, IL-17 drives the conversion of dormant gastric CSCs into invasive gastric CSCs [ ].

## CAFs enable dormant cancer cells to enter a proliferation state through the TGF-β signaling pathway
DTCs out of dormancy require high levels of TGF-β1 or TGF-βRIII downregulation [ ]. Versican, a key upregulated gene in CAFs, modulates fibroblasts function via TGF-βRII and Smad signaling. Overexpression of versican activates the NF-κB pathway and regulates the expression of CD44- and MMP9-mediated motor receptors, affecting the movement and invasion of cancer cells [ ]. Reduced FN activation of integrin β1 downregulates TGF-β2 and Cdc42, leading to MLCK activation, growth suppressor inactivation, and cell cycle regulator CDK6 activation, thereby enabling cells to exit dormancy and enter an active state [ ].

## CAFs regulate metastasis and recurrence of dormant cancer cells by regulating the ECM
The ECM and its crosstalk with dormant cancer cells function as a critical gatekeeper in regulating the reactivation of dormant cancer cells [ ]. In vitro model demonstrates that cancer cells interact with the ECM to change their cytoskeletal structure, thus affecting the transition from dormancy to reactivation [ ]. In 3D culture systems, cytoskeletal recombination and actin stress fiber formation interact with the ECM, affecting the metastatic growth of cancer cells from a quiescent state to an active state. The key determinant of cytoskeletal recombination may be the abundance of type I collagen (Col-I) at metastatic sites. Fibroblasts-derived Col-I activates PI3K/AKT pathway, stimulating an intracellular autocrine TGF-β3 that induces EMT in lung cancer cells [ , ]. Stromal matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) release angiogenic factors from the ECM, initiating angiogenic transformation required for dormancy and metastatic growth [ ]. Integrin β1 is a key regulator of the growth of cancer cells from dormancy to metastasis. The use of 3D culture system modeling dormancy also demonstrated that Col-I induces integrin β1 signaling to activate ERK, leading to the formation of pMLC, thus completing the transformation of cells from dormancy to proliferation [ , , ].

## CAFs affect cancer cell reactivation by regulating EMT
The activation of CAFs is often triggered by a pro-oxidative environment mediated by tumor-associated macrophages and hypoxia. Once activated, CAFs engage in paracrine interactions with cancer cells to drive EMT [ , ]. In prostate cancer, CAFs activate the monoamine oxidase A/mTOR/hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha signaling, upregulating CXCR4 and IL-6 receptors while inducing EMT [ ]. TGF-β serves as a strong inducer of EMT, and its effect is further amplified by IL-6, which may sustain a paracrine feedback loop to enhance TGF-β-driven EMT [ , ]. Whether EMT induces dormancy or affects reactivation after dormancy requires further investigation. Understanding the relationship between EMT and dormancy may provide new theoretical basis and therapeutic ideas for clinical cancer treatment.

## Other cytokines secreted by CAFs stimulate reactivation and metastatic growth of dormant cancer cells
Paired related homeobox 1 (Prrx1) in CAFs controls the structure and metastasis of tumors [ ]. Stimulator of interferon gene (STING) activation in cancer cells or fibroblasts induces IFN and associated CAF subsets production [ ]. STING signals helps modulate the interaction between dormant DTCs and immune microenvironment, preventing aggressive metastasis of cancer cells in the dormant state [ ].
Cancer cells enter a static state through inhibiting WNT signaling [ ]. Wnt family member 5 A (Wnt5A) serves as a key activator of the dormant DTCs. Wnt5A derived from osteoblastic niche can induce the dormancy of prostate cancer cells by repressing Wnt/β-catenin signaling mediated by the expression of siah E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2 [ ]. Wnt5A could promote initial dissemination of cancer cells, and then maintain cancer cells in a dormant state to allow survival and adaptation [ ]. Fane, M.E. et al. reported that aged lung fibroblasts could produce high level of secreted frizzled related protein 1 and decrease the expression of Wnt5A, which can promote the reactivation of dormant cells [ ]. Fibroblast-derived exosomes activate autocrine Wnt-PCP signaling through intercellular communication to drive BCC invasion [ ]. CAFs secrete exosomes containing long noncoding RNA CCAL to activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, and cancer cells close to stromal myofibroblasts have high Wnt signaling activity [ , ]. High Wnt signaling in a tumor is associated with poor prognosis [ ].
Enhanced BMP signaling mediated by CAF-specific genes (Grem1 and Islr) has been shown to suppress liver metastasis in CRC cells [ ]. The BMP7-BMPR2-P38-NDRG1 axis plays a key role in the dormancy and recurrence of prostate cancer stem-like cells [ ]. Coco, a BMP inhibitor, can reactivate BCCs at lung metastatic sites, promote CSC traits, and maintain the expression of stem cell transcription factors [ ]. In addition, CAFs generate high levels of ROS. Redox signaling is closely linked to the life cycle of dormant cancer cells. Primary cancer cells undergo oxidative stress, which increases ROS levels beyond the redox threshold required for tumor cell proliferation. Cancer cells undergo metabolic reprogramming, enabling survival at lower redox levels and entry into a dormant state. Upon reactivation, the redox levels in these cells may again exceed the threshold necessary for tumorigenic growth [ ].

## Dormant cancer cells regulate CAF formation and activation by secreting exosomes, TGF-β signaling pathway and ROS
Primary tumors activate fibroblasts by stimulating the NF-κB signaling pathway through EVs rich in integrin β-like 1 [ ]. Exosomes derived from dormant cancer cells promote pre-metastatic niche formation and support cancer cell survival in the bone marrow [ ]. The activation of CAFs by dormant cancer cells depends on factors released by cancer cells and tumor-infiltrating immune cells, such as TGF-β, PDGF, and FGF2 [ ]. EMT is the main source of CAFs [ ]. Exosomes derived from dormant cancer cells could be taken by fibroblasts. Exosomal ITGB6 transferred into fibroblasts could induce the activation of CAFs by activating the kruppel-like factor 10 positive feedback loop and TGF-β pathway [ ] (Fig. ). TGF-β secreted by cancer cells is a potential mediator of tumor desmoplastic responses [ ]. TGF-β induces CAF proliferation, expansion, myofibroblast differentiation, and EMT [ ]. The TGF-β/Smad pathway is a key driver of fibroblast activation. The Smad2/3-dependent signaling pathway mediates TGF-β1 to increase IL-6 mRNA expression and peptide secretion in activated pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) in an autocrine manner. IL-6 increases TGF-β1 expression and PSC secretion via an ERK-dependent pathway. These results suggest the existence of an autocrine ring between IL-6 and TGF-β1 in activated PSCs [ ]. After a large amount of TGF-β is released by cancer cells, the production of endogenous TGF-β and CXCL12 is increased through TGF-βR-Smad signaling, forming two positive feedback signal loops of TGF-β and CXCL12, thereby maintaining the myofibroblast phenotype [ ].
Both cancer cells and CAFs can produce high levels of ROS [ , ]. ROS functions as a pivotal signaling mediator that orchestrated the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts through the regulation of key cytokines and pathways, including TGF-β, hypoxia-inducible factor, CXCL12, PDGFR-β, and caveolin [ , ]. The oxidate stress environment further influences the proportions of fibroblasts subsets, CAF reprogramming, and cancer progression [ ]. In particular, ROS promote myofibroblast differentiation [ ], and high ROS level in activated myofibroblasts stimulates paracrine signaling, enhances ECM remodeling, and promotes angiogenesis, thereby creating a pro-tumorigenic feedback loop [ ]. External stressors can amplify this cycle; for example, radiation increases intracellular ROS, activating oxidative stress and TGF-β signaling to further promote CAF formation [ ]. Monoamine oxidase A/mTOR/hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha signaling regulates CAF-induced ROS production and CXCR4 and IL-6 receptor expression in cancer cells [ ]. Cellular oxidative stress can also trigger monocyte transdifferentiation into CAFs via the P38-MAPK pathway [ ].

## PGCCs can regulate the formation of CAFs
PGCCs represent a dormant form of cancer cells [ ], which manifests as abnormal expression of cyclin and proliferation arrest. PGCC formation is regulated by the P38MAPK-ERK-c-Jun N-terminal kinase-CDC25C signaling pathway with G2/M phase cell cycle arrest [ ]. PGCCs enter a dormant state under the stimulation of stress signals [ ]. PGCCs produce daughter cells to exit dormancy through asymmetric division [ , , ]. The abnormal cycle regulation of PGCCs causes them to enter a reversible dormant state, evade treatment, and then reawaken to produce more aggressive daughter cells [ ]. Paclitaxel-induced PGCCs have high surface expression of E-cadherin, N-cadherin, FN, and integrin α5 [ ]. Cyclin B1, CDC25C, and CDC25B are involved in the formation of PGCC via regulating cell cycle [ ], while dormancy is maintained by cell cycle-related proteins involving P21, ATM, ATR, CDK1, and cyclinB1 [ ]. Proteins involved in the formation of dormant PGCCs exhibit substantial overlap with those involved in the dormancy of conventional cancer cells. Therefore, the crosstalk between dormant conventional cancer cells and CAFs may offer valuable insights into the interactions between PGCCs and CAFs.
A new CAF subset marked by CD10 and GPR77 has been identified as a key component of CSC niche in breast and lung cancers [ ]. In vitro studies suggest that IL-6 derived from PGCCs can reprogram NFs into CAFs by enhancing embryonic stemness, promoting collagen synthesis, upregulating VEGF expression, and enriching CAFs and the GPR77+/CD10+ fibroblast subset, thereby contributing to cancer progression [ ]. In breast and lung cancer samples, the CD10+GPR77+ CAFs provide a niche for CSCs through IL-6 and IL-8 secretion, thereby promoting cancer progression [ ]. The reactivation of dormant PGCCs can secrete cytokines and induce the formation of CD10+GPR77+ CAFs (Fig. ). However, this proposed mechanism still needs validation in clinical settings.
Paclitaxel induces ovarian cancer epithelial cells to develop a benign fibroblast-like phenotype by dysregulating the pathways involved in EMT regulation [ ]. Daughter cells produced by budding PGCCs acquire mesenchymal phenotypes with higher expression of Twist, Slug, Snail, and vimentin [ , ]. PGCCs can be induced to differentiate into stromal cells, including fibroblasts, erythrocytes, chondrocytes and adipocytes [ , – ]. The stemness characteristics endow the plasticity of PGCCs, which have the capacity to differentiate into benign cells in vitro, including cells with a fibroblast-like phenotype [ ]. Mifepristone treatment of PGCCs markedly increased fibrotic features in cancer cells [ ]. Inducing the differentiation of PGCCs into benign fibroblasts may provide novel therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment.

## Therapeutic strategies
Owing to the stability of the CAF gene and the unlikely development of drug resistance, targeting CAFs could be a new cancer treatment strategy [ , ]. Therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs to inhibit dormant cancer cells are presented in Table .

 

## Targeting the active state of CAFs inhibits the reactivation of dormant cancer cells
Twist1 (a basic helix–loop–helix transcription factor) is a major inducer of EMT and a key regulator of CAFs and is expressed in both NFs and CAFs. Prrx1, which is essential for CAF activation [ ], is directly upregulated by Twist1. High expression level of Prrx1 upregulates tenascin-C (TNC), which in turn enhances the expression of Twist1, establishing a self-reinforcing Twist1-Prrx1-TNC positive feedback loop. This feedback loop acts as a bistable switch that regulates fibroblast activation and can be irreversibly activated under pathological conditions, resulting in sustained activation of fibroblasts [ ]. CAFs express molecules such as netrinG1 and signal the immunosuppressed population to further promote their conversion to CAFs [ ]. The bistable Twist1-Prrx1-TNC positive feedback loop is expected to provide new therapeutic targets for the treatment of fibrosis and cancer [ ]. Punicalin targets TNC and its downstream multidrug resistance associated membrane proteins and collagen associated proteins, thereby increasing the sensitivity of temozolomide resistant cells to chemotherapy drugs in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Furthermore, by modulating the activity of tumor stromal fibroblasts, the active vitamin D metabolite 1α,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 promotes the progression of CRC [ ]. The vitamin D receptor, the main regulator of PSCs, can transform PSCs from an active to a quiescent state and promote matrix remodeling [ ]. Retinoic acid induces the quiescent state of PSCs and reduces their motility, resulting in decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis of pancreatic cancer cells [ ].
CAFs promote the proliferation of cancer cells by generating nutrients through autophagy [ , ]. Inhibition of CAF autophagy could prevent it from providing nutrients to cancer cells, thereby inhibiting tumor growth. Autophagy inhibitors, particularly in combination with other therapeutic agents, may offer a promising clinical strategy. Autophagy defects in CAFs impede CAF activation by inhibiting proline biosynthesis and collagen production. Inhibition of mitochondrial autophagy in CAFs may be a stroma-focused anti-cancer strategy [ ]. CXCL12 inhibits P62-dependent autophagy degradation of programmed cell death 1 ligand 1, and targeting CAF-derived CXCL12 may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for bladder cancer [ ]. Chloroquine diphosphate-loaded MSC liposomes, by accurately targeting CAFs, inhibit CAF autophagy and improve the efficacy of immunochemotherapy in combating pancreatic cancer [ ].

P53 is the most commonly mutated gene in tumors, and it is very important for the formation and functional maintenance of CAFs. P53 can function in a non-cell-autonomous fashion [ ] and loss of P53 function promotes CAF activation [ – ]. Cancer cells suppress P53 activation in adjacent fibroblasts [ ]. Cancer cells with cellular tumor antigen P53 mutations secrete exosomes that inhibit the function of P53 “molecular police” in fibroblasts, leading to increased fibroblast proliferation [ ]. P53 in CAFs regulates the expression of CAF-specific genes and promotes the invasion and migration of cancer cells and tumor growth in vivo [ ]. CAF activation is also modulated by the downregulation of the Notch effector C-promoter binding protein-1, suppressor of hairless, Lag1 (CSL), which uses cell senescence as a fail-safe mechanism, and the downregulation of P53, which promotes cell proliferation [ ]. The epithelial cell cycle induces a paracrine P53 response through cell-specific inhibition of retinoblastoma protein function and inhibits fibroblast proliferation in the related stroma [ ]. P53 is a key regulator of activation and quiescence of cancer-associated PSCs (caPSCs), and P53 activation downregulates the expression of TGF-β and PDGF, while reprogramming caPSCs to a quiescent state, which may be related to the autocrine pathway of caPSCs [ ]. Given the established interplay among TGF-β, CAFs, and dormant cancer cells, targeting P53 (upstream of TGF-β) can be a new therapeutic approach.

## Precision therapy targeting tumors by regulating subset transformation of CAFs
Nicotinamide N-methyltransferase inhibitors can alleviate immune suppression in the TME by altering the H3K27 methylation of CAFs [ ]. Different CAF subsets have different specific markers and activate different signaling pathways. The identification of highly specific molecular markers is essential to accurately classify and target these subsets. Given the existence of pro-tumor and anti-tumor CAF subsets, specific markers are needed to distinguish between the two subsets and to selectively target the treatment of pro-tumor CAFs [ ].
Inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) and myCAFs can be transformed into each other, and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) can differentiate into myCAFs after culturing, indicating that apCAFs are in a dynamic cellular state [ ]. IL-1 induces iCAF formation through LIF expression and the Janus kinase (JAK)/STAT pathway, whereas TGF-β downregulates IL-1 receptor 1 to antagonize this process and induce myCAF formation [ ]. Inhibition of the Hedgehog signaling pathway impairs myCAF activity and tumor growth while promoting iCAF accumulation [ ]. Defining the functional roles of distinct CAF subsets in the tumor microenvironment, along with elucidating the mechanisms governing their formation and interconversion, will provide a foundation for developing therapies that selectively target specific CAF subsets.

## Targeting CAF-derived cytokines and miRNAs to treat dormant cancer cells
Anti-miRNA or mimetic oligonucleotides can be employed to normalize aberrantly expressed miRNAs in CAFs, thereby restoring a non-CAF phenotype [ ]. Additionally, targeting inflammatory cytokines to influence the formation, phenotype, and secretion of CAFs is novel. TNF-α, as a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is involved in inflammatory processes. Pirfenidone, an anti-pulmonary fibrosis drug, inhibits TNF-α synthesis and suppresses TGF-β-induced Col-I production [ , ]. TNF-α has the potential to induce iCAFs, and the use of TNF-α neutralizing antibodies and IL-1 receptor antagonists may be more effective in targeting iCAFs [ ]. IL-1 induces LIF expression and downstream JAK/STAT activation to generate iCAFs, while TGF-β induces myCAFs [ ]. NFs interact with FN-rich stromal ECM through TGF-β and integrin α5β1, leading to the development and activation of CAFs [ ]. The PDGF receptor signaling pathway plays an important role in CAFs and pericyte function in cervical cancer. The PDGF receptor inhibitor imatinib inhibits the production of FGF2 in CAFs, thereby impairing tumor angiogenesis and cancer cell proliferation [ ]. Fibroblast activation protein positive CAFs can suppress the function of T cells, thereby suppress anti-tumor immunity, and targeting fibroblast activation protein may affect tumor immunity [ ]. Fibulin-5, highly expressed in activated CAFs, is associated with radiation resistance in non-small cell lung cancer [ ]. The lactate derived from CAFs promotes the stability of anthrax toxin receptor 1, affects the resistance of CRC to oxaliplatin. Inhibition of lactate shuttle may improve the efficacy of oxaliplatin [ ].

## Inhibiting cancer-derived and CAF-derived exosomes to treat dormant cancer cells
Fibroblast differentiation leads to exosomes becoming an additional mechanism that promotes regulation of the stroma-ECM. Cancer exosomes regulate fibroblast phenotypes and functions. Exosomes can deliver TGF-β and promote NF differentiation into myofibroblasts [ ]. Cancer cell-derived exosomes can be secreted into the stroma to deliver miRNAs and proteins and activate related signaling pathways; exosomes can also induce a CAF-like phenotype in stromal cells [ ]. Rab27a is a key regulator of the exosome secretion pathway [ ]. Targeting Rab27a to block exosome release disrupts stromal support for tumor growth in vivo [ ]. It is reasonable to speculate that exosome inhibitors can reduce the chemotherapeutic resistance of CAFs and enhance their clinical efficacy. Hypoxic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cells promote CAF-like differentiation by secreting small EVs containing miR-192/215 family miRNAs, which are related to the inhibition of TGF/Smad signaling by caveolin-1 (the target gene of miR-192/215) [ ]. P53-deficient cancer cells exhibit intense fibroblast activation. Exosomal miRs (such as miR-375) promote CAF activation by decreasing P53 and RBPJ expression, the two key players in regulating fibroblast polarization [ , ].
Exosomes from CAFs are delivered to cancer cells to form a positive feedback loop during cancer progression [ ]. Exosome secretion was significantly increased in gemcitabine-treated CAFs, and a decrease in exosome release inhibited the chemotherapy resistance of CAFs [ ]. Exosomal miR-21-5p secreted by CAFs can inhibit ferroptosis, promote osteosarcoma proliferation and migration. Silencing exosomal miR-21-5p can suppress osteosarcoma progression [ ]. CAFs secrete miR-92a-3p-rich exosomes into the microenvironment and target CRC cells to promote cell migration, invasion, stemness, metastasis, and chemotherapeutic resistance [ ]. Disrupting the secretion of CAF-derived exosomes can inhibit CAF activation, thus severing the link between cancer cells and the stroma and providing ideas for stromal-based cancer therapy.

## Targeting the ECM to affect dormancy
As highlighted previously, CAFs play a central role in remodeling the ECM. The inhibition of key ECM components, such as FN, Col-I deposition, and cross-linking of lysyl oxidase, represents a strategy to create a microenvironment that induces to tumor dormancy or triggers excessive autophagy and cell death in dormant cancer cells, thereby preventing disease recurrence [ ]. Blocking MMP-mediated degradation of the FN matrix suppresses the reactivation of dormant cancer cells [ ]. Cancer cells interact with the ECM to change their cytoskeletal structure, and targeting the cytoskeleton may provide a new approach to inhibit the transformation of tumor cells from dormancy to clinical metastatic disease [ ]. Normalizing ECM by modulating CAF activity may contribute to tumor treatment [ ]. Understanding the mechanisms of crosstalk between cancer cells and the ECM and how the ECM regulates dormancy may provide new directions for inducing or maintaining cancer cells in a dormant state or for eradicating dormant cancer cells.

## Conclusions
As critical components of the TME, CAFs play a fundamental role in tumor initiation and progression. Dormant DTCs are the seeds of future tumor metastasis and recurrence, and elucidating the mechanisms underlying their transition from quiescence to reactivation may offer novel therapeutic opportunities. The phenotypic and functional heterogeneity of CAFs is closely linked to their diverse cellular origins. Various mechanisms can induce and maintain cancer cell dormancy; however, many activate dormant cancer cells. CAFs indirectly regulate cancer cell dormancy by influencing the ECM, angiogenesis, and exosome secretion. Dormant cancer cells regulate CAF production and phenotypic transformation by secreting cytokines, exosomes, and proteins. PGCCs not only have the characteristics of dormant cancer cells, which conform to the mechanism of dormancy of part of cancer cells, but also have the characteristics of CSCs, making them the “root” of tumor metastasis, treatment resistance, and relapse. Understanding the mechanisms of cancer cell dormancy entry and exit, as well as the role of the TME including CAFs in cancer cell dormancy, will provide critical insights for future therapies. Strategies that target specific CAF subsets or their secreted factors, revert CAFs to a non-activated state, or harness their intrinsic tumor-restraining functions may provide more effective treatment options.
