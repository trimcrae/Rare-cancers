## Abstract
Metastatic relapse is frequently driven by dormant disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) that previously evaded initial therapy, disseminated to distant tissues, entered into a non-proliferative state termed dormancy, and later reawakened to reinitiate active proliferation and the outgrowth of macroscopic metastases. Cancer dormancy manifests itself in two principal forms: cellular dormancy, characterized by the reversible, proliferative quiescence of individual cells, and tumor mass dormancy, defined by a balance between proliferation and compensating cell death. Dormant cells are notably resistant to conventional therapies and immune-mediated clearance, yet retain viability and the potential to re-enter the active cell cycle. The present review focuses on dormancy of DTCs residing in distant tissues and highlights recent advances in our understanding of both cell-intrinsic and -extrinsic regulators of cancer dormancy. Key cell-autonomous mechanisms include ERK/p38 signaling ratios, epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, and Wnt signaling. At the same time, signals received by dormant DTCs from the adjacent tissue microenvironment—such as TGF-β family cytokines, immune surveillance, and other stromal interactions—induce and sustain dormancy. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that microenvironmental conditions, including inflammation and aging, can trigger the awakening of dormant DTCs, leading to metastatic outgrowth. We review these evolving insights into the molecular and environmental control of cancer dormancy and awakening, underscoring their clinical relevance and therapeutic potential in preventing metastatic recurrence.
Keywords: Cancer dormancy and awakening, Disseminated tumor cells, Tumor microenvironment, Metastasis, Epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity

## Introduction
Despite remarkable progress in cancer therapy, metastasis and metastatic relapse remain leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. During the invasion–metastasis cascade, cancer cells disseminate from the primary tumor and infiltrate the parenchyma of distant organs. Clinically, patients are considered disease-free after treatment if no overt macrometastases are detectable by existing imaging techniques. However, due to the limited sensitivity of current clinical imaging procedures, many disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) may remain undetected at their sites of dissemination. These residual cells, often referred to as representatives of minimal residual disease (MRD), can persist in a ‘dormant’, non-proliferative state and, with some delay, become reactivated and actively proliferative, later giving rise to metastatic relapse. , 
Compelling clinical evidence supports the existence of DTCs. Notably, cancer transmission through organ transplantation has been documented in the recipients of tissues from donors with prior histories of melanoma, breast, or prostate cancer who were judged to be cancer-free when donating tissue. Following transplantation, these recipients suffered rapid tumor outgrowth, underscoring the ability of dormant cells to evade clinical detection in the organ donors and, in these cases, reinitiate tumor growth in immunosuppressed environments. , , Nonetheless, the precise cellular state in which dormant cancer cells reside remains unclear. They may persist as solitary, cell-cycle-arrested cells, small multicellular clusters, or microscopic lesions that remain indolent due to lack of vascularization or the actions of immune control. 
Of clinical importance, dormant cancer cells often exhibit markedly elevated resistance to conventional therapies, including chemotherapy and targeted treatments. Having eluded detection by standard imaging modalities and clinical biomarkers, they retain the capacity to exit dormancy and re-enter the active cell cycle in a process known as "awakening". Upon awakening, these cells regain proliferative and tumor-initiating capabilities, ultimately driving metastatic outgrowth and clinical relapse. The ability of dormant cancer cells to remain undetected and resistant to therapy, while maintaining viability and the potential to re-emerge and seed metastatic outgrowth, poses one of the greatest challenges to achieving durable cancer remission and cures.
Several comprehensive reviews have previously summarized mechanisms governing cancer cell dormancy, with particular emphasis on dormancy induction, maintenance, and tumor mass equilibrium. , , , , Here, we aim to advance this framework by explicitly integrating dormancy and awakening as a coupled, dynamic process, and by highlighting recent insights into the molecular and cellular mechanisms that regulate these states ( ). More specifically, we focus on three emerging conceptual advances: first, the role of epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity as a central axis linking dormancy and awakening; second, the integration of cell-intrinsic programs with microenvironment-derived cues that govern dormancy induction, maintenance, and subsequent awakening; and third, the recognition of inflammation, aging, and therapy-induced tissue remodeling as active triggers of dormancy escape. By positioning awakening as a biologically regulated and clinically actionable process, this review seeks to bridge mechanistically dormancy biology with metastatic relapse.
 
2.

## Definition of cancer dormancy
2.1.

## Cellular dormancy and tumor mass dormancy
Cellular dormancy and tumor mass dormancy are two distinct but related phenomena in cancer biology that describe how cancer cells can enter a non-proliferative or slow-growing state, allowing them to evade treatments and subsequently contribute to disease recurrence. Cellular dormancy refers to a state in which individual cancer cells enter a reversible quiescent phase (G0) characterized by cell cycle arrest, reduced metabolic activity, and a resistance to both chemotherapy and immune clearance. , Multiple preclinical studies have documented the existence of cellular dormancy and the ability of DTCs to enter a quiescent state and, with some delay, reactivate, thereby contributing to the eruption of metastatic disease; such reactivation is often termed “awakening”.
One study demonstrated that human tumor cells implanted in mice remained quiescent for months without significant growth until they were reactivated by activation of angiogenesis. Another study demonstrated that disseminated breast cancer cells can exit from a dormant state through the production of fibronectin and signaling via integrin β1, leading to actin cytoskeleton reorganization. Nonetheless, the existence of cellular dormancy in patients has yet to be conclusively demonstrated. In one clinical study, bone marrow aspirates were collected from patients with prostate, breast, colon, and kidney cancer and introduced into tissue culture, where researchers subsequently observed heterogeneity in the proliferative capacity of the cultured cells. Nonetheless, these studies have not demonstrated directly and definitively the presence of dormant cancer cells in patients.
Metastatic dormancy can also operate at the cell population level, where a dynamic balance of cancer cell proliferation and death is established. However, if the cells in such a population receive favorable, proliferation-inducing stimuli, this mass of cells can escape from their growth versus death equilibrium and spawn an aggressively growing tumor and thus clinical relapse. As some have proposed, tumor mass dormancy is regulated by three primary mechanisms—angiogenic dormancy, immunogenic dormancy, and tumor intrinsic dormancy. , , , 
In angiogenic dormancy, tumor growth is restricted due to inadequate microvessel formation, resulting in an insufficient nutrient and oxygen supply to the tumor and inadequate evacuation of metabolic wastes and carbon dioxide. A balance between the expression of pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF, FGF) and anti-angiogenic factors (e.g., thrombospondin-1, angiostatin, endostatin) determines whether a tumor grows or remains dormant and, similarly, whether previously dormant DTCs will initiate rapid cell proliferation. , 
In immunogenic dormancy, the immune system can hold tumors in a dormant state by eliminating highly immunogenic, highly proliferative cells while allowing poorly immunogenic, slowly proliferating ones to persist, once again establishing a state of equilibrium. Using an immunocompetent mouse model, Koebel et al. demonstrated that adaptive immune components, particularly CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, operating together with interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), can maintain tumors in a viable, non-growing state. However, following depletion of T cells or IFN-γ blockade, previously dormant tumors resumed active growth. 
Multiple lines of evidence suggest that tumor cell-intrinsic signaling pathways, independent of angiogenic or immunogenic influences, can also regulate tumor mass dormancy. Thus, some have reported that high levels of p38 MAPK (operating in a stress signaling pathway) and low levels of ERK1/2 (regulating a mitogenic signaling pathway) maintain a dormant state. , Additionally, p53-dependent pathways and/or epigenetic factors, such as histone modifications and DNA methylation, may contribute to maintaining tumor mass dormancy. , Overall, cellular dormancy is primarily responsible for delayed metastatic recurrences, while tumor mass dormancy contributes to prolonged periods of stable, often detectable disease characterized by minimal tumor growth.

2.2.

## Cell cycle regulation of dormant cancer cells
Dormant cancer cells are typically arrested in the G₀ phase of the cell cycle, a quiescent, non-proliferative state in which they remain metabolically active but do not undergo cell division. Additionally, these cells often express low levels of proliferative markers such as Ki-67 and PCNA, suggesting little if any active proliferation. In mouse models of spontaneous and experimental breast cancer metastasis, small clusters of carcinoma cells, which were consistently located adjacent to the microvascular endothelium in lung, bone marrow, and brain tissues, were found to be Ki-67 negative. This indicates that these tumor cells were in a quiescent/dormant state, likely arrested in the G₀ phase of the cell cycle. The presence of dormant tumor cells in the G₀–G₁ phase, as well as low expression of Ki-67 and PCNA, is also confirmed by a significant number of independent observations. , , , , , 
One of the defining features of cellular dormancy is its reversibility, distinguishing it from permanent senescence, in which cells irreversibly lose their ability to enter active cell cycle phases and thus are unable to proliferate under all conditions. An alternative is presented by dormant cancer cells that undergo reversible quiescence and temporarily withdraw from the cell cycle, often arresting in the G₀ or early G₁ phase while retaining the potential to re-enter into a state of active proliferation in response to environmental stimuli such as inflammation, hypoxia, extracellular matrix remodeling, or alterations in certain signaling pathways. This reversible, quiescent dormant state of cancer cells allows them to hibernate during periods of therapy-inflicted stress and later reactivate, thereafter contributing to tumor phenotypic heterogeneity, metastatic relapse, and resistance to conventional treatments.
Regulated intron retention has emerged as an important post-transcriptional mechanism distinguishing quiescent from senescent states. In dormant-like quiescent cells, selective retention of introns in transcripts encoding cell-cycle and proliferation-associated regulators suppresses their translation while preserving the transcripts in a poised state. Upon exit from quiescence, rapidly activated intron splicing enables swift reactivation of proliferative programs without the need for de novo transcription. In contrast, senescent cells lack this reversible intron retention program, consistent with their irreversible growth arrest. Dormant cancer cells are also mechanistically distinct from slow-cycling cancer cells, as the latter are defined as proliferating populations with extended cell-cycle duration. Such cells are not necessarily growth-arrested and may exhibit increased stress tolerance. 
The cell cycle machinery directly regulates the quiescent state of dormant cancer cells. Thus, the key cell cycle regulators p21Cip1 and p27Kip1, which inhibit cyclin-CDK complexes and thereby prevent the phosphorylation of retinoblastoma protein (Rb), can maintain dormancy in various cancers. , Certain kinases such as the dual specificity tyrosine-phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1B (DYRK1B) can actively induce quiescence. , In pancreatic and ovarian cancer cells, DYRK1B restrains the G0, G1 and S phase transition by regulating key cell cycle machinery proteins, including cyclin D1, CDK4 and p27. , , A related kinase, DYRK1A, can similarly promote quiescence by activating the DREAM complex, a multi protein transcriptional repressor composed of DP, RB family proteins, E2F and an MuvB core, which downregulates cell cycle gene expression to induce G0 arrest. Dormant cells often reside in specific microenvironmental niches, such as perivascular regions, in which endothelial cells secrete thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), thereby reinforcing dormancy through the upregulation of p21/p27 and the suppression of ERK signaling. 

3.

## Intrinsic signatures and determinators of cancer dormancy
3.1.

## Ratio of ERK/p38 activity
Several cell-intrinsic characteristics of DTCs have been identified as key factors regulating the behavior of these cells after they have successfully disseminated, extravasated, and thereby seeded the parenchyma of distant tissues. One well-established molecular governor of cell fate derives directly from the equilibrium between ERK and p38 kinase signaling. More specifically, a high ERK/p38 activity ratio promotes proliferation, whereas a low ratio sustains dormancy across various cancer types. , 
ERK signaling can be activated by cues from upstream receptors, including the urokinase receptor (uPAR), fibronectin, integrin α5β1, and EGFR. , , In contrast, p38 activity is enhanced by TGF-β receptor I signaling in epidermoid carcinoma and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Subsequent research has revealed that dormant cancer cells associated with various types of cancer and exhibiting high p38 activity also show elevated expression of the orphan nuclear receptor NR2F1, identifying it as a novel biomarker for cancer dormancy. , 

3.2.

## Epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity
One additional feature of dormant versus proliferative cancer cells is associated with residence in alternative epithelial versus mesenchymal phenotypic states. Thus, in the process of cancer metastasis, breast cancer (BrCa) cells from a primary tumor undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to acquire motility and invasive ability, enabling them to disseminate from primary tumors to distant anatomical sites. However, once cancer cells successfully seed the parenchyma of these distant tissues, the mechanisms by which alternative epithelial-mesenchymal states determine their subsequent behavior—such as dormancy and proliferation—remain poorly understood.
Recent research has shown that the transcriptional factor ZFP281 activates a mesenchymal-like dormancy state in early disseminated BrCa cells, suppressing metastatic growth in the lung parenchyma. , Other work has demonstrated that dormant disseminated estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) BrCa cells display mesenchymal characteristics, while forced expression of the key epithelial protein E-cadherin reverses the mesenchymal cell state and overcomes dormancy. Additional work has also shown that dormant HNSCC cells residing in the bone marrow exhibit elevated TGF-β2 expression, which sustains dormancy through an autocrine signaling mechanism. Given that TGF-β has been shown to induce a mesenchymal state in ras-transformed carcinoma cells, the autocrine TGF-β2 signaling in these cells may be critical to sustaining their mesenchymal-like phenotype.
The EMT program that regulates residence of cells in multiple phenotypic states does not operate as a binary switch but instead can direct cells into multiple alternative states arrayed along a spectrum of intermediate phenotypes. Between the extremely epithelial and mesenchymal states lies an array of intermediate phenotypic states that have been termed variously quasi-mesenchymal or hybrid epithelial/mesenchymal, in which carcinoma cells co-express multiple epithelial and mesenchymal markers. Of note, quasi-mesenchymal carcinoma cells exhibit elevated stemness (as gauged by their ability to initiate primary tumor formation or to seed metastatic colonies) relative to their extremely epithelial or mesenchymal counterparts. , , , , , 
Recent findings from our own research have indicated that BrCa DTCs residing in a highly mesenchymal state, in which they lack stemness and tumor-initiating ability, enter dormancy upon arrival in mouse lungs. In contrast, quasi-mesenchymal BrCa cells, which exhibit stemness and thus tumor-initiating capacity, rapidly form metastatic colonies upon seeding in the lungs. Consistent with previously cited research, we have found that the highly mesenchymal BrCa cells exhibit higher TGF-β2 expression compared to quasi-mesenchymal BrCa cells. The resulting autocrine signaling appears to help maintain such cells in a highly mesenchymal, non-proliferative state.

3.3.

## Wnt signaling
Wnt signaling has emerged as an additional critical regulator of stemness including cancer cell stemness and thus tumor-initiating ability. In latent competent cancer (LCC) cells derived from lung adenocarcinoma and HER2+ BrCa, reduced Wnt signaling does not permit post-extravasation metastatic colonization of distant sites of dissemination. , , Notably, these LCC cells exhibit elevated expression of DKK1, a secreted antagonist of canonical Wnt ligands. Mechanistically, autocrine DKK1 signaling induces a quiescent, dormant phenotype in lung LCCs by silencing Wnt-driven transcriptional programs, effectively suppressing their post-extravasation proliferation and thus their metastatic competence. 
In the context of epithelial-mesenchymal polarization, Wnt signaling helps to govern the quasi-mesenchymal stem-like state: canonical Wnt signaling is essential for maintaining stemness and tumor-initiating potential in quasi-mesenchymal BrCa cells, while a shift to noncanonical Wnt signaling occurs during their transition to a highly mesenchymal, non-stem-like phenotype. Supporting this thinking, our recent work has shown that highly mesenchymal, dormant BrCa cells express significantly higher levels of the DKK1 Wnt antagonist compared to their quasi-mesenchymal proliferative counterparts, suggesting a mechanistic link between Wnt suppression and dormancy. 

4.

## Microenvironment-induced cancer dormancy
The intrinsic properties of DTCs can be regulated by heterotypic signals received from the adjacent tissue microenvironment, both before and after their colonization of distant tissues. These interactions between DTCs and their surrounding microenvironment align with the classic "seed and soil" hypothesis, which proposes that the formation of metastases depends on the biological compatibility between specific types of disseminated cancer cells and specific tissue sites of dissemination. As examples, patients with aggressive malignancies—such as head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), gastric cancer, and BrCa—often harbor myriad DTCs in the bone marrow but rarely develop bone metastases or exhibit delayed metastatic outgrowth. , This observation suggests that the bone marrow microenvironment functions as an inhospitable site of dissemination, i.e., a "restrictive soil", imposing dormancy on DTCs that succeed in extravasating into this microenvironment.
A key driver of this dormancy is TGF-β2, a cytokine that is enriched in the bone marrow; this cytokine induces dormancy in DTCs arising from multiple distinct cancer types. , , More specifically, TGF-β2 secreted by bone marrow–resident NG2+/Nestin+ mesenchymal stem cells activates p38 signaling in disseminated BrCa cells in a manner dependent on TGF-β receptor type III (TGFBR3). Consequently, the resulting reduced ratio of ERK/p38 activity drives these DTCs to enter dormancy. A similar mechanism was also observed in the lungs, where TGF-β2 released by lung-resident alveolar macrophages induces dormancy of disseminated BrCa cells. 
Yet other members of the TGF-β family of cytokines have been implicated in inducing dormancy in DTCs after their arrival in distant tissues. For instance, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), a TGF-β family member secreted by lung tissue, induces dormancy in disseminated, extravasated BrCa cells. Similarly, BMP7 released from bone marrow stromal cells promotes dormancy in prostate cancer DTCs by enhancing p38 activity. Paradoxically, BMP is a known antagonist of TGF-β2, but both ligands appear nonetheless to trigger intracellular signals that converge on p38 activation, which in turn suppresses proliferation.
This inconsistency highlights unresolved questions about the interplay between BMPs and TGF-β2: How do these opposing ligands both succeed in driving dormancy? Do they act differently in different cellular contexts or do they act synergistically in such contexts? Further investigation is required in order to dissect their crosstalk, their pleiotropic effects on p38 signaling and, in turn, their context-dependent roles in dormancy regulation.
A critical unresolved question is how microenvironment-derived signals, such as those discussed above, induce cancer dormancy by modulating the epithelial-mesenchymal state of DTCs following their seeding in the parenchyma of distant tissues. As already described, the quasi-mesenchymal state, rather than the highly epithelial or mesenchymal states, harbors cells exhibiting stemness and tumor-initiating ability. This raises a possibility: TGF-β2, an EMT-inducing factor secreted by bone marrow and lung niches, could theoretically push quasi-mesenchymal DTCs (which are primed for proliferation) toward a highly mesenchymal, dormant state following dissemination and extravasation, in which they lose proliferative powers.
Another unresolved issue involves BMPs, which often operate as antagonists of TGF-β2 but may also act to impose cancer dormancy by regulating transitions between alternative epithelial-mesenchymal phenotypic states. One plausible reconciliation of these conflicting behaviors would propose that BMPs drive quasi-mesenchymal DTCs toward a highly epithelial state, which, similar to the highly mesenchymal state, harbors cells that lack stemness and tumor-initiating potential. This speculation surely requires experimental support to determine whether the highly epithelial state does indeed represent another phenotypic state of dormant cancer cells.

5.

## Immune surveillance and cancer dormancy
The host immune system clearly plays a pivotal role in inducing and maintaining cancer dormancy, contributing to both cellular dormancy and tumor mass dormancy. When inducing cellular dormancy, immune cells secrete cytostatic factors, such as TGF-β2 and BMPs, that act in a paracrine manner on cancer cells to drive these cells into a dormant state. , , , , , , , Thereafter, cancer cells can enter the resulting highly mesenchymal state and persist in a dormant, viable state for extended periods. Nonetheless, in order to persist over extended periods of time in immunocompetent hosts without being eliminated by the actions of the immune system, dormant cells must develop immune-evasive traits, such as downregulating major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and associated antigen-presentation machinery, as observed in both patients and mouse models of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and breast cancer , ; alternatively, they may acquire these traits by upregulating PD-L1 and other checkpoint proteins, as observed in esophageal cancer. As a result of these changes, the cancer cells that entered into cellular dormant state will not be cleared by cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, thereby escaping the attrition of disseminated dormant cancer cells often suffered in distant sites of dissemination. The downregulation of antigen presentation machinery is not unique to dormant cancer cells; similar mechanisms operate in quiescent tissue stem cells, enabling them to evade immune surveillance. 
By contrast, the dormancy of entire tumor masses depends on sustained immune surveillance, which exerts varying degrees of selective pressure on different cellular subpopulations within a tumor. As a result, while a subset of cancer cells is eliminated, others persist and may even expand, leading to a dynamic equilibrium in which the overall tumor mass remains constant. Such a dynamic equilibrium can permit ongoing immunoediting, in which the CD8+ T cells in an immunocompetent host continually eliminate more antigenic, proliferating cancer cells, while less immunogenic or dormant variants persist in a growth-restricted state. In fact, CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells have been found to preferentially clear proliferating cancer cells whereas non-proliferating dormant cancer cells survive due to acquisition of immune-evasive mechanisms. , , , Accordingly, the host immune system can sustain cancer dormancy through complementary mechanisms of cytokine-induced growth arrest and ongoing immune surveillance.

6.

## Microenvironment-driven awakening of cancer dormancy
The awakening of disseminated dormant cancer in distant tissues is a leading cause of clinical relapse due to the eruption of metastases in patients that previously lacked clear signs of metastatic burden. Nevertheless, the biological mechanisms governing this reactivation of dormant cell populations are largely unknown. In principle, unraveling the mechanisms underlying these processes could pave the way for the development of novel therapeutic strategies designed to prevent such clinical relapses.
Some emerging evidence indicates that the awakening of dormant cancer cells can be caused by shifts in the tissue microenvironment in which DTCs are embedded, rather than intrinsic, cancer cell-autonomous mechanisms operating entirely within these cells ( ). For example, pulmonary inflammation experimentally induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposure or tobacco smoke has been demonstrated to reactivate dormant BrCa cells lodged in the lungs. , More recently, our own studies and those of others revealed that lung inflammation triggered by chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin, cisplatin, or bleomycin can also awaken dormant BrCa cells seeded in the pulmonary microenvironment. , In addition, emerging evidence indicates that lung inflammation caused by respiratory virus infections, including SARS-CoV-2 and influenza, can similarly promote the reactivation of disseminated dormant BrCa cells. 
 In one experimental model of lung-specific awakening of disseminated BrCa cells, sustained LPS-induced inflammation promotes the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Subsequently, two NET-associated proteases, neutrophil elastase (NE) and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), degrade laminin, generating a remodeled extracellular matrix structure. This altered structure, in turn, stimulates dormant BrCa cell proliferation by activating integrin α3β1signaling pathways. In a recently described model of chemotherapy-induced awakening of dormant BrCa cells in the lungs, the formation of NETs is enhanced by fibroblasts that have become senescent in response to chemotherapy. Such awakening can be inhibited by senolytic agents, which selectively induce cell death in senescent cells, highlighting the potential of combining chemotherapy with senolytic drugs as an adjuvent therapeutic strategy. 
Our own recent work has demonstrated that inflammation induced in the lungs by the chemotherapeutic agent bleomycin or the presence of aggressive, lung-metastatic BrCa cells awakens dormant, highly mesenchymal BrCa cells. Mechanistically, during the early phase of bleomycin-induced inflammation, EGFR ligands secreted by M2-like macrophages drive the transition of highly mesenchymal, dormant cells into a quasi-mesenchymal state, an essential step for their acquisition of stemness, active proliferative powers, and thus awakening. These initial attempts at elucidating the mechanisms of metastatic awakening converge on the notion that awakening is triggered by changes in the tissue microenvironments surrounding DTCs rather than signaling processes operating entirely within the DTCs themselves.
In line with this microenvironment-centered view, accumulating evidence suggests that aging-associated stromal changes also play a pivotal role in awakening dormant DTCs and promoting metastatic outgrowth. , One study has demonstrated that an aged pulmonary tissue microenvironment contributes to the awakening of disseminated dormant melanoma cells. Fibroblasts in aged lungs were shown to secrete sFRP1, an inhibitor of non-canonical Wnt signaling. Together with the melanoma-derived factor PROS1, sFRP1 contributes to the awakening of dormant melanoma cells in aged pulmonary tissue, doing so by modulating the AXL/MER signaling axis operating within these cells. This indicates that aging can also be a parameter to be considered in studies of cancer dormancy and awakening.

7.

## Summary and prospects
Dormant DTCs pose a major obstacle to achieving durable cancer remissions and cures. These cells are difficult to detect, resistant to conventional therapies, and capable of reawakening to fuel metastatic relapse. Substantial progress has revealed both cell-intrinsic programs, including ERK/p38 signaling balance, epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity, and Wnt regulation, as well as extrinsic factors, such as stromal interactions, immune surveillance, and TGF-β family cytokines, that govern entry into and maintenance of dormancy. Importantly, perturbations in the microenvironment surrounding dormant DTCs, including inflammation, senescence, and aging, have emerged as critical triggers of awakening.
From a clinical perspective, a central challenge remains the detection and monitoring of dormant neoplastic disease in patients. Dormant DTCs are radiographically occult and reside within tissue-specific niches, rendering direct detection extremely challenging using current clinical modalities. However, host-derived biomarkers, including inflammatory mediators, senescence-associated secretory factors, or niche-derived extracellular matrix remodeling products, may provide indirect but actionable readouts of a pro-awakening microenvironment. Although current technologies are unlikely to comprehensively detect dormant DTCs across all organs, even partial evidence of a pro-awakening microenvironment could meaningfully inform patient risk stratification and guide surveillance intensity.
Equally critical is defining the therapeutic window during which intervention against dormancy exit is most likely to be effective. Lifelong therapy aimed at broadly enforcing dormancy is unlikely to be feasible. By contrast, the peri-treatment period may offer a relatively narrow and clinically actionable window, during which therapy-induced tissue damage, wound-healing responses, cytokine release (e.g., EGFR ligands), and stromal remodeling are transiently amplified and can actively drive the awakening of previously dormant DTCs. Targeting these pro-awakening signals during this adjuvant window may therefore provide a more rational strategy than chronically suppressing dormant cells over time ( A). However, even after successful treatment of the primary tumor and years of being considered clinically cancer-free, dormant DTCs may still be reawakened by diverse life-associated cues, including viral infection, smoking, and aging-associated stromal remodeling, ultimately leading to metastatic relapse. Because these triggers are often unpredictable and difficult to detect in real time, preventing dormancy exit in this setting remains a major unresolved challenge in metastasis prevention ( B).
 Importantly, not all patients are expected to benefit equally from dormancy-targeted approaches, underscoring the need for rational clinical prioritization. Patients with cancers characterized by long latency periods, such as hormone receptor positive breast cancer, melanoma, or certain prostate cancers, represent particularly compelling candidates, as their relapse patterns strongly implicate prolonged dormancy. Similarly, individuals with detectable minimal residual disease following curative-intent therapy, as well as patients experiencing strong therapy-induced inflammatory or senescent responses, and older patients with age-remodeled metastatic niches may be at heightened risk for dormancy escape and thus may derive disproportionate benefit from intervention. In contrast, in tumor types dominated by rapid metastatic progression, dormancy-directed strategies may play a more limited role.
Finally, integration of multi-omics profiling, including spatial transcriptomics and advanced in vivo lineage tracing, will provide unprecedented resolution of the molecular circuitry operating to govern dormancy and awakening. Such approaches promise to reveal therapeutic vulnerabilities that have thus far eluded discovery and, in turn, enable rational design of interventions that prevent metastatic relapse. By continuing to unravel the intricate balance between dormancy and awakening, we will move closer to the long-sought goal of preventing metastatic relapses, thereby transforming cancers into truly curable diseases.
