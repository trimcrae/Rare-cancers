Besides containing internal cell structures similar to eukaryotes ( ), S. cerevisiae has a special outer cell wall surrounding the cell membrane. The outer cell wall contains a high concentration of heavily glycosylated mannoprotein that allows for cell–cell recognition, while the inner cell wall is composed of a high concentration of β-glucans and some chitin ( ). Secondary metabolites from S. cerevisiae fermentation include alcohols, succinate, polyols, organic acids, esters, aldehydes, and vicinal diketones ( ). Thus, S. cerevisiae fermentation products (SCFP) fed to livestock likely contain S. cerevisiae cells, fermenter media, and bioactive secondary metabolites that could trigger cellular responses.
Some studies demonstrated that feeding SCFP allows cows to maintain higher milk production levels by enhancing feed efficiency ( ; ). Feeding SCFP also decreased concentrations of the stress hormone cortisol, the acute-phase protein serum amyloid A in blood, and somatic cell counts in milk, while it increased circulating white blood cells and neutrophil numbers during heat stress ( ). At the ruminal level, during subacute ruminal acidosis, feeding SCFP tended to reduce lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and concentrations of the pro-inflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor α in dairy cows suggesting a lower inflammatory response ( ; ; ). During feed restriction (FR), feeding SCFP increases granulocyte reactive oxygen species production ( ). Thus, these recent studies underscored the potential for SCFP supplementation to elicit benefits in the cow during periods of stress.
Dairy cows experience periods where voluntary feed intake is reduced, for instance, the transition into lactation ( ), periods of drought (as often occurs in grazing systems ( )), improper feed delivery, and overstocking ( ). Sustained periods of decreased feed intake in Holstein cows lead to physiological changes such as increased fat and muscle mobilization and decreased ileal villus height and crypt depth ( ; ). In Angus-Holstein calves, restricted feeding of concentrate increased intestinal permeability, as reflected by increased blood serum concentrations of the indigestible marker lactulose ( ). Thus, periods of FR can cause intestinal challenges in the animal.
The use of 40% FR to mimic reductions in voluntary DMI disrupted ileal morphology and was a cause of increased circulating LPS binding protein and serum amyloid A concentrations in dairy cows ( ). Induction of leaky gut (assessed via the paracellular transport marker Cr-EDTA) occurred with as little as 40% of FR ( ), underscoring the validation of the intestinal barrier challenge. The benefits of SCFP on mastitis ( ), digital dermatitis ( ), and lung damage ( ) led us to hypothesize that SCFP can affect ileal tissue integrity during FR partly through alterations in mRNA abundance. Thus, the specific objective was to assess the impact of a postbiotic SCFP on the ileal transcriptome in response to FR, a well-established model for reducing intestinal barrier function ( ; ).

## Materials and Methods

## Animal handling and experimental design
As described in a previous publication, the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Illinois approved the procedures (No. 17166) ( ). All cows were housed in a free-stall system equipped with individual feeding gates (American Calan Inc., Northwood, NH) and were fed once daily at ~0800 hours. Of the original cohort of 16 cows, 10 multiparous mid-lactating cows free of clinical disease from the University of Illinois Dairy Unit herd (97.1 ± 7.6 DIM) from which RNA of a suitable quality for RNA sequencing was isolated were used in the present work (see methods below). Cows were fed a basal diet of corn silage and alfalfa hay ( ) or the basal diet with dosage recommended supplementation of 19 g/d SCFP (NutriTek, Diamond V, Cedar Rapids, IA), top-dressed for 9 wk. Subsequently, all cows were subjected to an FR challenge for 5 d, during which they were fed 40% of their ad libitum intake from the prior 7 d ( ). After penetrating captive bolts at the Veterinary Diagnostics Laboratory, University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine, Urbana-Champaign, cows were exsanguinated, and within 10 min, the body cavity was opened to gain access to the gut tissues.

## Sample collection
Tissue samples from the ileum were harvested 20 min after slaughter. The ileal tissue was collected ~18 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction ( ). The 25 cm segments of ileum were cut into pieces measuring ~10 cm × 20 cm and washed with phosphate-buffered saline. Then, a sterile scalpel blade was used to scrape the epithelium. Samples were collected into cryovials and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. After transporting tissues to the laboratory, they were stored at −80 °C.

## RNA extraction
Approximately 50 mg of tissue was homogenized with 1 mL Qiazol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and RNA was extracted according to our published protocols ( ). Samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 × g at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and placed at room temperature for 5 min. Two hundred microliters of chloroform were added, shaken by hand, held at room temperature for 3 min, and centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 × g at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and mixed with 750 µL ethanol. Total RNA quantification was determined using a ­Nanodrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop Technologies, Rockland, DE). The RNA was diluted to 100 ng/μL with DNase/RNase-free water. The purity and integrity of extracted RNA were evaluated using an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Functional Genomics Unit, Roy J. Carver Biotechnology Center, University of Illinois, Urbana). The average RNA integrity number (RIN) was 7.19 ± 0.11 for those samples used for sequencing, i.e., 5 cows in each group. Other samples were deemed of low quality for analyses.

## RNA-sequencing
Sequencing was performed by the High-Throughput DNA Sequencing and Genotyping Unit of the W. M. Keck Biotechnology Center at University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign. The 10 RNAseq libraries were prepared with Illumina’s “TruSeq Stranded mRNAseq Sample Prep kit” (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries were pooled and quantitated by qPCR and sequenced on one SP lane from one end of the fragments on a NovaSeq 6000 with 100bp read length. Fastq files were generated and demultiplexed with the bcl2fastq v2.20 software (Illumina). The quality control of the raw sequences was assessed with FastQC software (v0.11.8), and low-quality reads were filtered using Trimmomatic v0.38. Single-end reads were first filtered using Trimmomatic 0.38 with a minimum quality score of 36 leading and trailing with a minimum length of 30 bp. All reads are shorter than 30 bp, and drop all reads where the average quality is below 30 bp. The sequence reads that passed quality filters were aligned to the Bos taurus reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2) using the software package STAR v2.7.6a, and the number of reads that mapped to each gene in each sample was calculated using FeatureCounts v2.0.0. Data are available at NCBI as BioProject PRJNA984341 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/?term=prjna984341).

## Identification of differentially expressed genes
The entire downstream analysis for the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEG) was conducted within R environment (v4.0.3) using the edgeR (v3.32.1) pipeline ( ). Non-expressed and weakly expressed genes were removed ( ). Trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) normalization was applied to all samples, and the data were log2-transformed. Once dispersion estimates were obtained and a negative binomial generalized linear model was fitted with a design matrix describing the treatment condition (SCFP and control groups), quasi-likelihood F-test was used to determine differential expression. Multiplicity correction was performed by applying the Benjamini–Hochberg method on the P values, to control the false discovery rate (FDR). The expression level was deemed to be significantly different between the two groups at an FDR ≤ 0.10 (DEG uncorrected P-value < 0.011).

## Functional analysis
The dynamic impact approach (DIA) software was used for functional analyses ( ). Briefly, DIA uses the KEGG systems information to rank biological pathways, calculating the overall impact (importance of a given pathway) and flux (direction of impact; e.g., upregulation, downregulation, or no change) based on gene fold-change (FC) values. For this purpose, the entire DEG data set (FDR ≤ 0.10) with associated statistical P values was imported into DIA, and the functional analysis was performed by interrogating all KEGG pathway databases. As in , a cutoff of ≥30% genes had to be present in our DEG list vs. the genome to be considered for analysis. For the analysis with KEGG pathways, the cutoff was ≥40%. Furthermore, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis ( ; ) was performed in DAVID (The Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery) (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) using default parameters to determine whether known biological functions, ontologies, or pathways were significantly over-represented in our list of DEG (P-value < 0.05; FDR < 0.10) and the figure was constructed with SigmaPlot 14.0. All annotated KEGG genes for Bos taurus genome were used as background for KEGG and DAVID analyses.

## Transcription regulator discovery
ChIP-X Enrichment Analysis 3 (ChEA3) tool ( ) was used to predict the transcriptional factors (TF) associated with the downstream DEG. ChEA3 is a transcription factor enrichment analysis software that ranks TF associated with user-submitted gene sets. The entire list of DEG (FDR ≤ 0.10) was uploaded on ChEA3, and core analysis was run using default settings. TF with FDR ≤ 0.05 was considered significantly associated with our DEG list.

## RT-qPCR validation
Primers were designed and tested by DNA gel and sequenced High-Throughput DNA Sequencing and Genotyping Laboratory, University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign. The cDNA synthesis and quantitative PCR (qPCR) were performed as reported in one of our previous papers ( ; ). Specific protocol details are reported in , and primer information is in (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/vb9wbkrdbr/1). Pearson correlation coefficients between RNA-seq Counts per million (CPM) and RNA qPCR were analyzed using PROC CORR with treatment as a partial factor and PROC MIXED in SAS OnDemand for Academics (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA; https://welcome.oda.sas.com/login). The PROC MIXED model tested the fixed effect of treatment and random effect of cows.

## Results and Discussion

## RNA sequencing
It is important to highlight that because of the nature of samples harvested and used for sequencing, i.e., ileal scrapings, the resulting sequences encompass signals from several cell types. Although enterocytes are the most predominant cell type, goblet cells (i.e., mucin-producing), Paneth cells (intestinal crypts, producing antimicrobial peptides/proteins), stem cells (intestinal crypts, regenerate the cells of the intestine), M cells (specialized cells overlying lymphoid follicles, Peyer’s patches), and may other unique cells likely compose the bovine ileal epithelium ( ).
Overall, on average, samples had ~30 million reads, of which 28 million (~ 94.68%) were uniquely mapped to the bovine genome. Approximately 20 million reads were assigned to specific genes in the bovine genome. The statistical analysis identified 15,704 uniquely annotated (EntrezID) genes. Of these, considering an FDR < 0.10, 1,696 genes (upregulated 451, downregulated 1,245) in response to SCFP were detected as differentially expressed ( ).
 We analyzed 14 genes by RT-qPCR to verify some of the major responses obtained via the RNA-seq approach ( ; https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/vb9wbkrdbr/1). These genes were among the top-regulated genes in the KEGG pathways pertinent to cell integrity, immunity, and health. The Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) between RNA-seq CPM and qPCR for all genes were greater than 0.75 (P-value < 0.01). Together, these data suggested that the RNA-seq approach was robust.

## Top upregulated and downregulated DEG
In , B4GALNT2 (Beta-1,4-N-Acetyl-Galactosaminyltransferase 2) mRNA abundance in the SCFP group had an FC of 1,252 greater abundance compared with the control group. It catalyzes the biosynthesis of the Sd(a) antigen and Cad antigen ( ) both of which are prominent in the colonic mucin glycan ( ). The upregulation of B4GALNT2 by feeding SCFP prior to and during FR was indicative of this postbiotic having promoted the synthesis of the numerous carbohydrate structures required for building Sd(a)/Cad-antigen-like structures confer protection to the ileal epithelium ( ). As such, those cows would have had a greater degree of protection against any noxious molecules or proteins, or noxious microbiota that might have been produced in the lumen of the ileum during FR. In fact, the marked upregulation of duodenase-1 (LOC508858) with SCFP, which in fish was driven by pathogenic bacteria in the gut ( ), likely contributed to the selective destruction of noxious bacteria through its trypsin-like and chymotrypsin-like activities ( ). This enzyme appears capable of activating chemokine receptors. Hence, it may contribute to the control of inflammation in the bovine gut ( ).
 The protein encoded by ONECUT2 (One Cut Homeobox 2) is a TF that regulates cell proliferation, migration, adhesion, differentiation, and cellular metabolism ( ). Its abundance was 97.9-fold greater with SCFP compared with the control. Knocking out ONECUT2 in mice led to reduced body weight gain during the early postnatal period and altered the abundance of several genes related to epithelial membrane and transport ( ). The abundance of GATA4 (GATA Binding Protein 4), a TF that regulates genes associated with cell growth and cell death ( ), was 69.7-FC greater due to feeding SCFP compared with the control. Without GATA4 expression, the intestinal length and width in mice decreased markedly, and a mutant GATA4 repressed intestinal cell proliferation ( ). The conditional deletion of GATA4 in the intestine increased claudin-2 expression ( ), which is a tight junction protein related to gut barrier permeability in mice ( ). Thus, the fact that feeding SCFP during FR upregulated GATA4 suggested the existence of a compensatory mechanism to help maintain normal morphology and barrier function of the ileum.
The expression of TFF2 (Trefoil factor 2) in the SCFP group was 1,817-fold lower compared with the control. This gene is primarily expressed in the gastric epithelium and ­duodenal Brunner’s glands, and together with the mucin isotype MUC6 is a component of the gastric mucus barrier ( ). One of the key functions of TFF2 is to help maintain and restore epithelial structural integrity via transducing key signaling pathways for epithelial cell migration, proliferation, and invasion. Because ileal microbes are able to consume mucus ( ) and mucin synthesis likely occurs during FR (albeit at a reduced rate) the upregulation of TFF2 mRNA abundance in the control cows would have activated the genes related to mucin synthesis. Thus, feeding SCFP during FR was beneficial for mucin homeostasis.
The protein encoded by FREM2 (FRAS1 Related Extracellular Matrix 2) is a membrane protein in the sublamina densa region of the extracellular matrix (ECM) ( ; ), and its expression was 201.9-FC lower with SCFP compared with the control. MFAP5 (Microfibrillar-associated protein 5) also encodes an ECM glycoprotein, and is related to ECM remodeling during inflammation ( ). Its abundance was 80.4-FC lower in SCFP compared with the control. In another study, SCFP reduced plasma concentrations of lipoteichoic acid, serum amyloid A, serum IL-1β, and ruminal LPS, indicating an improvement of epithelial integrity in gut tissue ( ). Taken together, the response in abundance for these 2 genes suggested that SCFP was able to dampen inflammatory or stress events that could compromise the integrity of the ECM. We observed similar responses in mammary epithelium from cows fed SCFP and challenged with a mastitis pathogen ( ). Overall, the responses observed with SCFP on the abundance of B4GALNT2 and TFF2 (related to mucin), ONECUT2 and GATA4 (related to cell proliferation), and FREM2 and MFAP5 (related to the ECM) suggested that SCFP elicited potentially protective effects on proper mucosal barrier and maintenance of tissue integrity during FR.

## GO analysis
The DEG from the RNA-seq data were used to determine their “enrichment” (or overrepresentation) in the GO database as a way to better understand the cellular location, cellular function, and the biological processes associated with the affected genes. In the GO analysis ( ), the top impacted cellular component (CC) terms were “extracellular exosome”, “extracellular space”, “focal adhesion”, “cell surface”, “proteinaceous extracellular matrix”, “cytoskeleton”, and “extracellular matrix”. Per the GO definition, “extracellular exosome” refers to vesicles released into the extracellular region by fusion of the limiting endosomal membrane of a multivesicular body with the plasma membrane. The “proteinaceous extracellular matrix” is a layer consisting mainly of proteins (especially collagen) and glycosaminoglycans (mostly proteoglycans) that form a sheet underlying or overlying cells such as endothelial and epithelial cells. “Focal adhesion” is a cell-substrate junction that anchors the cell to the ECM and forms a point of termination of actin filaments.
 The majority of DEG was associated with the ECM and the cell surface, which was likely due to the onset of leaky gut during FR and the protective effect of SCFP on the ileum. The ECM contains collagens, laminins, fibronectin, proteoglycans/glycosaminoglycans, elastin, and several other glycoproteins. The interstitial and pericellular matrices are two classes of ECM: interstitial matrices surround cells, while the pericellular matrices are closely in contact with cells such as the basement membrane ( ).
Top impacted molecular function (MF) GO terms were “calcium ion binding”, “heparin binding”, “actin binding”, “extracellular matrix structural constituent”, “extracellular matrix binding”, and “collagen binding”. Because collagen is the main structural protein in the ECM ( ) its appearance in the top MF is not surprising. Together, the MF terms support the interpretation of the CC terms in that molecular functions enriched among the DEG play important roles in the ECM.
The top impacted biological process (BP) GO terms were “cell adhesion”, “negative regulation of cell proliferation”, “regulation of cell shape”, “negative regulation of canonical Wnt signaling pathway”, “extracellular matrix organization”, “positive regulation of canonical Wnt signaling pathway”, “negative regulation of angiogenesis”, “embryonic digit morphogenesis”, “substrate adhesion-dependent cell spreading”, and “elastic fiber assembly”. According to the GO definition, cell adhesion pertains to the attachment of a cell to another cell or an underlying substrate such as the ECM via cell adhesion molecules. Furthermore, “substrate adhesion-dependent cell spreading” is the morphogenetic process that results in the flattening of a cell due to its adhesion to a substrate. The raw GO analysis data are provided in (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/vb9wbkrdbr/1). Overall, “cell adhesion”, “extracellular matrix organization”, and “substrate adhesion-dependent cell spreading” in the BP category agreed with the nature of the GO terms in the CC and MF underscoring a significant impact of SCFP on the ECM and cell surface structures during FR.

## KEGG pathways
The DEG were also analyzed using the KEGG pathway database to illustrate those pathways related to the beneficial effect of SCFP. The DIA analysis provides an overview of DEG in the context of KEGG pathways ( ). By using the DIA with the KEGG database, we can estimate the “impact” of the DEG on a given pathway (i.e., its biological “importance” during the investigated event) and also the “flux” (i.e., direction of the effect, “upregulated” or “downregulated”). The calculated impact of a given pathway indicates the magnitude of the effect on the pathway induced by the treatment (e.g., SCFP). As such, a larger number indicates a greater impact. With this in mind it was striking that most of the effects detected at the mRNA level with SCFP were associated with “downregulation” (green bars) of pathways ( ), i.e., those pathways were activated in the control cows. Among the 6 major KEGG categories, it was clear that “Metabolism” pathways were the most significantly impacted by feeding SCFP. Among pathways in “Metabolism” category that were markedly affected were “Glycan biosynthesis and metabolism”, “Metabolism of Cofactors and Vitamins”, and “Amino acid metabolism”.
 

## Gut integrity
The top impacted pathways, except for the human diseases category and unrelated pathways, are listed in and the complete dataset is available in (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/vb9wbkrdbr/1). The pathways related to gut integrity such as “Mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis” (impact = 763), “Other types of O-glycan biosynthesis” (impact = 475), “ECM-receptor interaction” (impact = 578), “Cell adhesion molecules” (impact = 427), “Focal adhesion” (impact = 402), and “Tight junction” (impact = 361) were markedly downregulated in response to feeding SCFP before the FR. The top DEG in “Mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis” and “ECM-receptor interaction” are listed in . In “mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis”, the fold-change of GCNT3 (glucosaminyl (N-acetyl) transferase 3), GALNT5 (polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 5), B3GNT3 (UDP-GlcNAc:betaGal beta-1,3-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase 3), GALNT18 (polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 18), and GALNT14 (polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 14) in the SCFP compared with the control group was −29.3, −13.10, −8.53, −6.17, and −2.93, respectively ( ). The mRNA abundance data from RT-qPCR also revealed a similar tendency for several of these genes ( ).
 The “ECM-receptor interaction” pathway was overall downregulated (Flux = −578; ), and all DEG in this pathway were downregulated (depicted in cyan color) in response to feeding SCFP. The fold-change in the SCFP compared with the control group for COL6A5 (collagen alpha-5(VI) chain isoform X1), ITGA8 (integrin alpha-8), COL4A1 (collagen alpha-1(IV) chain precursor), COL1A1 (collagen alpha-1(I) chain precursor), COL1A2 (collagen alpha-2(I) chain precursor), COL6A1 (collagen alpha-1(VI) chain precursor), LAMB2 (laminin subunit beta-2 precursor), ITGA2 (integrin alpha-2 precursor), and COL6A2 (collagen alpha-2(VI) chain precursor) was −8.4, −4.41, −4.02, −3.93, −3.63, −3.37, −3.33, −3.22, and −3.08, respectively ( ). These responses are in line with those observed for the pathways associated with gut integrity.
 In a previous study with SCFP, adding 0.5% and 1% SCFP in the starter of dairy calves improved the villus-to-crypt ratio (VCR) in the jejunum, ileum, and entire small intestine ( ). In addition, feeding SCFP increased the villi height of the entire small intestine and decreased the crypt depth in the jejunum and entire small intestine. Thus, feeding SCFP clearly has the potential to alter gut morphology ( ). In a study with broilers, feeding SCFP reduced the crypt depth and Tunica muscularis in the ileum, and the VCR was greater in the jejunum ( ). In a study with turkeys, feeding up to 0.375% SCFP did not alter villi height, crypt depth, VCR, or villi–crypt score ( ). However, during a coccidia challenge, the lesion score in the jejunum was significantly decreased by feeding a similar level of SCFP ( ). In dairy cows, previous studies confirmed damage of ileal morphology ( ; ) due to an FR or damage of the mammary epithelium due to a mastitis challenge ( ). The protection of digital epithelium and the better lung epithelium integrity in response to feeding SCFP ( ; ) are further support that this postbiotic may benefit the epithelium in various organs.
Yeast fermentate has been reported to restore intestinal villi height and mucosal thickness during heat stress ( ) to mitigate gastrointestinal discomfort and constipation in humans ( ), and to protect epithelial monolayer integrity from chemical damage in vitro ( ). In dairy cows, the reduction in plasma LPS when this postbiotic was fed ( ) underscored the protective effect of SCFP during FR. Thus, considering the marked downregulation of genes responsible for mucin synthesis and ECM in cows fed SCFP and the benefits of SCFP on intestinal health from previous studies, we speculate that FR in the control cows led to a compromised barrier function and upregulated these genes to restore the barrier.

## Immunity and health
The pathways related to immunity and health-related functions such as “Complement and coagulation cascades” (impact = 423), “Hematopoietic cell lineage” (impact = 402), “Viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor” (impact = 402), “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” (impact = 395), and “Glutathione metabolism” (impact = 368) were also highly impacted ( ). Together, these results underscored that SCFP had an important effect on the ileal immune system.
The “Complement and coagulation cascades” pathway was downregulated in cows fed SCFP compared with controls. The complement system is a proteolytic cascade in cells and a mediator of innate immunity, a nonspecific defense mechanism against pathogens ( ). Opsonization of pathogens, recruitment of inflammatory and immune cells, and the direct killing of pathogens are the main consequences of the activation of this pathway ( ). The CD59 protein (CD59 molecule; CD59 blood group) is the major inhibitor of the membrane attack complex ( ) that leads to the formation of cytotoxic pores on the surface of microbes and directly killing Gram-negative bacteria ( ). Thus, the downregulation of CD59 in cows fed SCFP could be a sign that this postbiotic offered greater protection to ileal tissue against microbial membrane attack complexes.
“Viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor” and “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” pathways were downregulated by SCFP compared with controls, with 10 chemokines or cytokines listed in . Chemokines attract specific populations of immune cells when the intestinal mucosa is undergoing an inflammatory event as a way to help repair cellular damage ( ). Among the affected genes by feeding SCFP, the abundance of CCL4 (C-C motif chemokine ligand 4), XCL1 (chemokine [C motif] ligand 1), and XCL2 (chemokine [C motif] ligand 2) was upregulated ( ). In contrast, the abundance of CCL16 (C–C motif chemokine ligand 16), CCL21 (C–C motif chemokine ligand 21), CCL14 (chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 14), CXCL12 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12), and CXCL14 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 14) was downregulated by SCFP ( and ). In nonruminants, both CCL4 and XCL1 attract monocytes, macrophages, T cells, Th1 T cells, immature dendritic cells, and natural killer cells ( ). CCL16, CCL21, CCL14, CXCL12, and CXCL14 also attract the same types of cells in addition to CD34 progenitor cells and most hematopoietic cell types ( ).
As reported in our previous paper, there was no statistical effect of SCFP during FR on metabolic and immune responses, plasma markers of inflammation (ceruloplasmin, haptoglobin, and myeloperoxidase), monocyte phagocytosis, or oxidative burst ( ). However, in vitro whole blood-isolated neutrophil phagocytosis and oxidative burst tended to be lower (P = 0.07, 0.15) due to feeding SCFP during FR ( ). Similarly, neutrophil oxidative burst and circulating neutrophil counts in lactating cows experiencing hyperpermeability to Cr-EDTA (indicator of leaky gut) due to FR were lower when Zn-hydroxychloride was supplemented ( ). Thus, the downregulation of chemokine mRNA abundance detected in ileum of cows fed SCFP is consistent with these neutrophil responses. In nonruminants, both CCL4 and XCL1 attract monocytes, macrophages, T cells, Th1 T cells, immature dendritic cells, and natural killer cells ( ). Proteins encoded by CCL16, CCL21, CCL14, CXCL12, and CXCL14 also attract the same types of cells in addition to CD34 progenitor cells and most hematopoietic cell types ( ). Overall, the lack of similar data for bovine highlights a need for immune cell and mRNA abundance-specific studies to better understand the complexity of chemokine regulation in the context of feeding SCFP.
The “Glutathione metabolism” pathway was upregulated overall in response to feeding SCFP, and the top DEG making up the pathway is listed in . The enzyme encoded by GSTM1 (glutathione S-transferase mu 1) and GSTA1 (glutathione S-transferase alpha-1) are among seven isotypes of the glutathione S-transferase superfamily. They conjugate glutathione to endogenous or exogenous electrophilic substrates for detoxification ( ). These two isotypes may have different functions, which could partly explain the up and downregulation of GSTA1 and GSTM1 mRNA abundance when SCFP was fed during the FR. For instance, the detoxification function of GSTA1 could explain the 10.25 FC greater mRNA abundance with SCFP during FR ( and ). The GSTA1 mRNA abundance was greater (P = 0.05), and AOX1 (aldehyde oxidase 1) and GPX8 mRNA abundance were lower (P < 0.01) in the SCFP than the control group ( ).
Five GPX isoforms are recorded in the Bos taurus NCBI database. The enzyme encoded by GPX8 (glutathione ­peroxidase 8) catalyzes the reduction of H2O2 or organic hydroperoxides using reduced glutathione, and although ROS concentration and GPX activity did not differ ( (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/vb9wbkrdbr/1; ), the downregulation of GPX8 mRNA abundance in the SCFP group suggested the ileum in those cows did not experience oxidative stress during FR. This agrees with data from an in vitro cell study reporting that the dried fermentate of S. cerevisiae reduced ROS production ( ; ). The enzyme AOX1 is categorized as a phase I enzyme within the drug metabolism pathway, and as an oxidase, it is a prominent source of reactive oxygen species ( ; ; ). Whether the marked downregulation of AOX1 translated into lower production of ROS in the ileum of cows fed SCFP, or greater production of ROS in the controls, is uncertain. Based on in vitro studies with nonruminant cells, it is tempting to speculate that SCFP had an antioxidant effect, i.e., it inhibited ROS synthesis in ileal tissue ( ). In dogs fed SCFP under transportation stress, blood cyclooxygenase-2, and malondialdehyde mRNA abundance increased, the serum malondialdehyde concentrations increased, and serum 8-isoprostane concentrations tended to increase suggesting potential benefits of dietary SCFP ( ). The few data available indicate that androgens, estrogens, adiponectin, and growth hormone exert transcriptional or translational control of the enzyme in the mouse ( ), but it is unclear how those factors could have been associated with the responses observed. Together, the data strongly suggested that SCFP did not alter glutathione production, but potentially increased the ability of ileal tissue to conjugate glutathione to toxic molecules.

## Transcription factor prediction
Forty-one of the potential 50 TF identified in the prediction analysis were detected in our RNA-seq data, and only 21 were affected (FDR P < 0.10) when SCFP was fed ( ). Among those, feeding SCFP downregulated the mRNA abundance of 9 homeobox TF in the ileum during FR. Furthermore, among the DEG detected, GLIS2, GLI2, GLI3 (glioma-associated oncogene 3), GLIS1 (GLIS family zinc finger 1), FOXS1 (forkhead box S1), FOXF1 (forkhead box F1), and FOXL1 (forkhead box L1) are related to the hedgehog signaling ­pathway, a well-studied pathway controlling cell differentiation ( ).
 The homeobox genes regulate tissue development, morphology, and differentiation and are classified into two classes: the hox genes and non-hox genes ( ; ; ). Most homeobox gene studies in the intestine have been focused on the remodeling of chromatin accessibility to close foregut enhancers and open intestine-specific enhancers, restricting the fate of the epithelium ( ; ). The high redundancy of homeobox genes may be one of the reasons why intestinal morphology is regulated by several genes ( ). Similar to dairy cows, the HOXB3 (Homeobox B3) gene is also highly expressed in the human ileum ( ), and was among the top predicted TF regulated by feeding SCFP ( ). Another top predicted TF was HOXC6 (homeobox C6), which promotes the metastasis (via WNT/β-Catenin signaling) of colorectal ( ) and gastric cancer ( ), and is overexpressed in gastrointestinal carcinoids ( ). The protein ZEB1 (zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1), is regulated by WNT/β-Catenin signaling and IRF6 (­interferon regulatory factor) ( ; ). Together, these data indicated that SCFP induced an ­interaction between HOXC6 and ZEB1 through the WNT signaling pathway in the ileum. Although the HOXC6 mRNA abundance was not altered, ZEB1 mRNA abundance was downregulated by SCFP suggesting some degree of interaction among the HOXC6-WNT-ZEB1 axis. The exact mechanisms regulating this interaction could not be discerned with the current data.
The top predicted TF with downregulation due to feeding SCFP was OSR1 (odd-skipped related transcription factor 1). Its isotype OSR2 (odd-skipped related transcription factor 2) was also in the list of the top 50 predicted TF. Although there is a lack of data on OSR genes in the gastrointestinal tract of mammals, available data in model organisms offer some clues as to their functional roles. For instance, OSR1 and OSR2 share 73% identity at the nucleotide level in Caenorhabditis elegans, and both seem to have unique functions during gut development ( ). This nematode survives on a diet of bacteria, and although knocking down OSR1 did not elicit phenotypic effects, knocking out OSR2 resulted in a loss of capacity for ingesting bacteria and movement of bacteria along the intestine ( ). Because at least 83% of the C. elegans proteome shares homology with human proteins, it has been proposed that impacts of microbes on the physiological processes of C. elegans can illuminate host–microbiome interactions that translate to the mammalian host. Both OSR1 and OSR2 are expressed in a variety of organs and structures in mice, including kidney, eye, branchial arches, and dermis ( ). The mRNA abundance of OSR2 was highly expressed in the uterus, testis, small intestine, and prostate in chick embryos ( ). Overall, the available data seem to suggest a potentially important role for OSR1 and OSR2 in the bovine ileum. Whether OSR1 is a link between intestinal bacteria, SCFP, or both with the intestinal epithelium should be studied further. Based on previous data with SCFP, it is likely that this postbiotic altered the luminal and mucosal-associated microbiota profiles ( ) both of which could contribute to the observed changes in DEG.
The GLI (glioma-associated oncogene) family, including GLI1 (glioma-associated oncogene 1), GLI2, and GLI3, is a key family of TF in the hedgehog signaling pathway ( ). The hedgehog protein interacts with the patched family cell receptors and regulates the GLI family of TF ( ). At least in nonruminants, the hedgehog signaling pathway controls many biological processes in the intestine. For instance, it maintains homeostasis of the adult small intestine and colon, regulates the size of the crypt compartment, controls epithelial proliferation, suppresses the lamina propria immune response ( ), and modulates gut development ( ). In humans, the downregulation of GLI1 is associated with inflammatory bowel disease and the immune response ( ). The TF in the GLIS (GLI-Similar) family is similar to the GLI family ( ). Given that the predicted TF analysis identified GLIS2, GLI2, GLI3, and GLIS1, and their mRNA abundance was downregulated by feeding SCFP, we speculated that SCFP could regulate the hedgehog signaling pathway in the intestine during FR. As such, this postbiotic can impact the regulation of inflammation in the ileum.
The protein FOXS1, downregulated by GLI1 ( ), inhibits proliferation, metastasis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in cancer cell lines ( ). DNA sites near FOXF1 and FOXL1 genes contain GLI binding consensus and both genes are targets of the GLI family, an effect that was confirmed when GLI2/GLI3 mutations reduced the expression of FOXF1 and FOXL1 ( ). FOXS1 upregulated the mRNA abundance of CXCL8 (C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 8; also known as IL-8), which is a key signal for neutrophil activation in colorectal cancer ( ). Overall, the known connections among the GLI/GLIS with the FOXF/FOXL reported in previous studies along with the present data suggest a potential functional link in the ileum.
One limitation of the TF prediction is that the results do not necessarily imply regulation of the TF, especially for those TF for which mRNA expression could not be detected in the RNA-seq data. Although it is well-established that TF networks can shape the cellular response to the environment ( ), the role that these networks play in the gastrointestinal tract of dairy cows or even humans and mice is largely unknown. Thus, further studies focused on TF and their response to postbiotics in the intestine will need to be performed.

## Conclusions
The large number of downregulated DEG due to feeding SCFP during FR that are associated with immune response, ECM remodeling, and gut integrity was indicative of a dampened immune response. Concomitantly, downregulation of genes associated with ECM remodeling and gut integrity suggested a decrease in structural protein synthesis. The large number of homeobox and hedgehog signaling pathway genes in the predicted TF list highlighted unique effects of SCFP on aspects of cell differentiation. Taking into account the benefits of SCFP on gut health reported in previous studies, it is possible that the downregulation of tissue integrity genes could be a mechanism to restore gut health during FR. Additional research is necessary to fully understand the functional outcomes of alterations in abundance of genes associated with the immune response, ECM, and mucin synthesis during intestinal challenges induced by stressors such as FR.
