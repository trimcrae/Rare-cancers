## Background
Tumor metastasis represents a stepwise progression and stands as a principal determinant of unfavorable prognoses among cancer patients. Consequently, an in-depth exploration of its mechanisms holds paramount clinical significance. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), constituting the most abundant stromal cell population within the tumor microenvironment (TME), have garnered robust evidence support for their pivotal regulatory roles in tumor metastasis.

## Aim of review
This review systematically explores the roles of CAFs at eight critical stages of tumorigenic dissemination: 1) extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, 2) epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), 3) angiogenesis, 4) tumor metabolism, 5) perivascular migration, 6) immune escape, 7) dormancy, and 8) premetastatic niche (PMN) formation. Additionally, we provide a compendium of extant strategies aimed at targeting CAFs in cancer therapy.

## Key scientific concepts of review
This review delineates a structured framework for the interplay between CAFs and tumor metastasis while furnishing insights for the potential therapeutic developments. It contributes to a deeper understanding of cancer metastasis within the TME, facilitating the utilization of CAF-targeting therapies in anti-metastatic approaches.

## Introduction
Malignant tumor metastasis encompasses the intricate process through which tumor cells evade immune surveillance, intravasate into the bloodstream or lymphatic system from the primary site, and are subsequently colonize in specific organ tissues. Distant metastasis is directly associated with poor prognosis and represents a challenging and urgent problem to be addressed , . Despite advancements in tumor therapy modalities such as surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, gene therapy, immunotherapy, and biologic therapy, the emergence of distant metastasis often presents a formidable challenge, limiting the efficacy of conventional treatments . The metastasis processes are influenced by various factors, including genetic alterations within tumor cells and reciprocal communications with various cells and cytokines in the tumor microenvironment (TME) . Therefore, gaining a deeper understanding of the intrinsic molecular mechanisms of tumor metastasis and exploring novel therapeutic strategies are crucial directions in cancer research.
The TME encompasses the intricate network of local tissue elements surrounding tumor cells, comprising an array of stromal cells such as fibroblasts, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), B cells, T cells, and others, along with the extracellular matrix (ECM) and vasculature . These diverse stromal components engage in complex paracrine signaling interactions with tumor cells, playing critical regulatory roles in tumor metastasis. In-depth exploration and clarification of the specific functions of each component within the tumor microenvironment in tumor metastasis hold promise in identifying potential targeted therapies.
Fibroblasts in the tumor stroma are considered cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which are heterogeneous populations of cells. These multifaceted cells originate from various cell types, including normal fibroblasts (NFs), cancer stem cells (CSCs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), stellate cells, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, adipocytes, pericytes, mesothelial cells, and macrophages ( ) , , , , , . While CAFs are characterized by molecular markers such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibroblast activation protein (FAP), vimentin, podoplanin (PDPN), and platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), their exact identification and classification remain areas of active investigation . 
In recent years, multiple studies have drawn attention to the regulatory role of CAFs in tumor metastasis, with findings indicating that CAF infiltration correlates with metastatic propensity through specific protein immunohistochemical staining . Coculture experiments with CAFs have demonstrated enhanced tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastatic phenotypes, underscoring the intricate interplay between CAFs and tumor progression . Interestingly, while most viewpoints currently suggest that CAFs promote tumor progression, some studies have indicated that certain subtypes of CAFs may have inhibitory effects on tumor metastasis. For instance, meflin-positive CAFs have been associated with reduced metastatic tendencies and improved prognosis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), indicating the complexity and heterogeneity of CAF functions in the metastatic context .
Considering the complex role of CAFs in tumor metastasis, this review comprehensively explores their involvement in eight major aspects: 1) ECM remodeling, 2) EMT, 3) angiogenesis, 4) tumor metabolism, 5) perivascular migration, 6) immune escape, 7) dormancy, and 8) premetastatic niche (PMN) formation. Additionally, potential antitumor strategies are discussed, offering valuable insights for both basic researches elucidating metastatic mechanisms and clinical efforts in developing targeted therapies

## Involvement of CAFs in eight major aspects of tumor metastasis

## ECM remodeling
Typically, the ECM is composed of collagens, proteoglycans, laminins, and fibronectin, with their proportions, spatial arrangements, and posttranslational modifications holding specific significance in physiological and pathological processes . Tumor stromal remodeling involves significant alterations in composition and physical properties, including increased matrix stiffness and tissue fluid pressure . In recent years, the role of ECM remodeling in promoting tumor metastasis has been the subject of extensive investigation , , which attracted our attention to the part of CAFs in tumor stromal remodeling.
The transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway plays a specific role in ECM remodeling altered by CAFs. Research conducted by Sapudom et al. indicates that CAFs, under TGF-β1 influence, exhibit enhanced capacity for ECM remodeling through increased production of hyaluronic acid (HA) . This HA-rich ECM not only promotes cancer metastasis but also induces CAF transformation, leading to increased drug resistance and metastasis . Additionally, in gastric cancer, the activation of TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling in CAFs upregulates hyaluronan and proteoglycan link protein 1 (HAPLN1), driving ECM remodeling and altering fiber physical properties . It appears that the TGF-β signaling pathway is primarily involved in altering ECM composition. However, further exploration is needed to determine whether these changes affect ECM physical properties and, if so, how they impact them.
Furthermore, besides TGF-β signaling, other mechanisms have been reported to play a role in promoting ECM remodeling by CAFs. The nuclear localization of yes-associated protein (YAP) was reported to influence matrix stiffness and collagen contractility, modulating tumor progression . Study conducted by Liu et al. highlight the role of YAP in ECM stiffness, cellular cytoskeleton modulation, and enhanced ECM deposition and remodeling by CAFs . In breast cancer, elevated ECM stiffness promotes EMT, cell invasion, and metastasis by activating the mechanosensitive ephrin type-A receptor 2 (EPHA2)/Lck/Yes-related novel protein tyrosine kinase (LYN protein complex) . Moreover, focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling activation in response to tissue rigidity promotes tumor progression, with FAK inhibition reducing CAF-induced ECM deposition and tumor cell invasion . Investigating the crosstalk between FAK signaling, ECM alterations, and tumor microenvironment dynamics could provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving tumor progression.
Taken together, CAFs play a crucial role in inducing changes in chemical composition and physical properties of the ECM through multiple pathways, thereby promoting tumor metastasis ( ). It is important to note that these studies are often based on in vitro or murine models, and in the complex tumor microenvironment within the human body, these mechanisms may be influenced by many other factors, leading to increased complexity and diversification. Furthermore, many mysteries remain unresolved regarding the exact mechanisms by which changes in ECM stiffness and chemical composition contribute to tumor metastasis, necessitating further research to elucidate these aspects. 

## EMT
EMT involves epithelial cells transitioning to a mesenchymal phenotype, a crucial step in tumor metastasis characterized by changes in cell adhesion molecules and acquisition of invasive properties. In this section, we explore key part of CAFs driving this process.
CAFs primarily accelerate the process of EMT by regulating a series of pathways related to β-catenin through cytokine secretion and extracellular vehicles (EVs). Related research has shown that CAFs secrete C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) and Gremlin-1 , which can activate β-catenin signaling in tumor cells, promoting EMT-related gene transcription. Furthermore, in the cytoplasm, there exists a β-catenin destruction complex consisting of adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β), tumor suppressors Axin, and casein kinase 1 alpha (CK1α). CAFs derived exosomal microRNA (miRNA)-1290 inhibits GSK3β, stabilizing β-catenin and enhancing EMT . Based on our understanding, activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathway phosphorylates GSK-3β. Activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway by CAF-secreted periostin (POSTN) and CXCL11 further contributes to EMT induction. CAFs can also downregulate PTEN expression via miR-148b-3p, activating PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, crucial for EMT progression . Moreover, the mRNA nuclear export and mRNA surveillance-related protein upframeshift 1 (UPF1) can bind to and downregulate the expression of PTEN mRNA. CAF-secreted exosomes contain downregulated exo-miR-210, which is associated with increased UPF1 expression in tumor cells, resulting in restricted PTEN expression and subsequent cascading promotion of EMT . Based on these studies, targeting β-catenin appears to be a promising therapeutic approach.
Besides β-catenin signaling, loss of caveolin-1 and galectin-1 overexpression by CAFs activate the TGF-β pathway, resulting EMT , . In addition, the role of the Janus kinase 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (JAK2/STAT3) signaling pathway in initiating the EMT process in tumor cells by CAFs cannot be overlooked . To recap, CAFs play an undeniable role in tumor metastasis by promoting the EMT process ( ). While exosomes derived from CAFs have been implicated in promoting EMT, the specificity and variability of their cargo contents warrant further investigation. Moreover, elucidating the hierarchy and interactions between different signaling pathways, such as PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, and JAK2/STAT3 could unveil potential vulnerabilities for targeted disruption.

## Angiogenesis
Angiogenesis is critical for tumor cell survival, immune evasion, and metastatic spread by providing oxygen, nutrients, and pathways for intravasation and extravasation. CAFs are actively involved in promoting angiogenesis processes within the tumor microenvironment.
On one hand, CAFs can secrete various cytokines to stimulate the formation of new blood vessels. Multiple studies have confirmed that under the stimulation of pro-cancer signals, CAFs can secrete vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and VEGFC, leading to neovascularization , . Hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), also play crucial roles in promoting angiogenesis and are secreted by CAFs with activated STAT3, engaging the PI3K/AKT and extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2 (ERK1/2) signaling pathways in endothelial cells , . CAFs can also activate vascular endothelial cells by producing interleukin (IL) and CXCL12 , stimulating the migration and formation of new blood vessels. MMPs released by CAFs are evidenced to degrade the extracellular matrix, facilitating vessel growth and migration , . However, the efficacy and relevance of these pro-angiogenic factors may vary depending on the tumor microenvironment, tumor type, and stage of angiogenesis. Future studies should address these contextual nuances to better delineate the contributions of CAFs to angiogenesis in different settings.
Noncoding RNAs, including miRNAs and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), derived from CAFs have been reported to promote angiogenesis. T-box transcription factor 5 (TBX5) is targeted by miR-10a-5p derived from CAFs, whose downregulation plays a role in activating the hedgehog (HH) signaling pathway and promoting blood vessel formation . CAFs can also secrete miR-135b-5p, which targets both forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) and thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) in tumor cells, indirectly facilitating angiogenesis , . LncRNA SNHG5 (small nucleolar RNA host gene 5) is upregulated in CAFs and can enhance the stability of zinc finger protein 281 (ZNF281) mRNA, thereby exerting transcriptional control over the expression of chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2) and CCL5 in CAFs. As a consequence, the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (P38 MAPK) signaling pathway is activated in endothelial cells, leading to enhanced angiogenesis . However, noncoding RNAs are known to exhibit varying degrees of stability, influenced by factors such as RNA-binding proteins, RNA modifications, and degradation pathways. Validation of the stability-dependent effects of noncoding RNAs in physiologically relevant models and clinical samples would strengthen the understanding of their role in CAF-mediated angiogenesis and inform the development of targeted therapies.
In summary, CAFs respond to the demands of tumor cells by aiding in the augmentation of vasculature within the stromal microenvironment ( ). The clinical application of anti-angiogenic drugs has also brought hope to cancer patients. However, tumor heterogeneity and the development of resistance mechanisms pose significant challenges in targeting CAF-mediated angiogenesis. Understanding how different tumor subtypes or stages respond to anti-angiogenic therapies in the presence of CAFs is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and overcoming resistance.

## Metabolism
Tumor cells undergo metabolic changes to thrive in harsh environments during metastasis, including increased glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, and lipid synthesis . Zhao et al.’s isotope tracing and intraexosomal metabolomic experiments revealed that CAF-derived exosomes transport various metabolites to support cancer cell proliferation and metabolic processes . Comprehending CAF involvement mechanisms can contribute to the development of novel targeted therapies for tumor metastasis.

## Glucose metabolism
Pavlides et al. (2009) proposed a metabolic partnership between cancer cells and CAFs, termed the “reverse Warburg effect” . Cancer cells secrete hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into the tumor environment, triggering oxidative stress and aerobic glycolysis in CAFs, leading to the secretion of lactate and other biomolecules. These energy-rich fuels, acting as paracrine tumor metabolites, promote oxidative mitochondrial metabolism (OXPHOS) in epithelial cancer cells, resulting in increased reactive oxygen species (ROS). CAF-secreted lactate serves not only supports cancer cell energy needs but also contributes to the acidic tumor microenvironment, promoting tumor invasion and metastasis. First, lactate accumulation in the tumor microenvironment enhances tumor cell proliferation by upregulating hyaluronan synthesis . Moreover, lactate-rich acidic tumor microenvironment, characterized by low oxygen, recruits all kinds of inflammation-related immune cells, and inhibits their activity or reprogram them to acquire procancer properties . In addition, renowned scholars have emphasized that angiogenesis can be drived by lactate under both aerobic and hypoxic conditions , . As we have known, cancer cells exhibit metabolic plasticity, dynamically adapting their glucose utilization strategies based on nutrient availability, oxygen levels, and metabolic demands. This metabolic flexibility poses challenges in targeting specific metabolic pathways for therapeutic intervention, as cancer cells can switch between glycolytic and oxidative metabolism to sustain their growth and survival under varying conditions.

## Amino acid metabolism
CAFs influence tumor metabolism beyond glycolysis by regulating amino acid metabolism, with glutamine being a key energy source for aggressive tumor cells. Yang et al. confirmed the significant upregulation of glutamine metabolism in CAFs using a mouse orthotopic ovarian cancer model, which continuously provides nutritional support to tumor cells and promotes tumor progression and metastasis under nutrient deprivation conditions . Similarly, CAF-derived exosomes carrying lncRNA LINCO1614 activate the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-kB) pathway in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), upregulating glutamine transport and enhancing glutamine metabolism . Interestingly, lactate signaling can also enhance glutamine uptake and metabolism in oxidative cancer cells, mediated by lactate uptake through monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) and the activation of the HIF-2α/c-Myc pathway , highlights the intricate cross-talk between glycolytic and glutaminolytic processes in cancer cells. Additionally, CAFs provide other nonessential amino acids, like alanine in PDAC, shaping a unique metabolic environment that reduces the tumor's reliance on glucose and serum nutrients .

## Lipid metabolism
In addition to enhanced glycolysis and amino acid metabolism, metastatic tumor cells exhibit increased lipid synthesis, fatty acid oxidation, and lipid storage, providing sufficient lipid supply to support cell growth and proliferation . Ippolito et al. observed that lactate from CAFs boosts lipid droplet storage in prostate cancer,influencing histone modifications . Additionally, CD10 on CAFs promotes tumor stemness and chemoresistance by degrading the antitumoral peptide opioid growth factor receptor binding protein (OGP), which inhibits lipid desaturation needed for cancer stem cells, making it a promising therapeutic target for breast cancer . While targeting lipid metabolism and its regulators holds promise for cancer therapy, challenges remain in translating these findings into clinically effective treatments. Identifying patient subsets with distinct lipid metabolic profiles and understanding the dynamic nature of lipid metabolism in response to therapy are critical considerations for advancing lipid-targeted therapies in cancer.
The investigation of tumor metabolism has perennially remained a focal and intricate issue. In this context, our primary focus resides in an analysis of the impact of CAFs on the metabolism of three major nutrients ( ). What cannot be overlooked is that the TME, including factors such as hypoxia, inflammation, and nutrient gradients, profoundly influences tumor metabolism. Future studies should explore how CAFs interact with these microenvironmental cues to modulate tumor metabolism. Understanding these interactions will provide insights into developing combination therapies that target both tumor cells and the stromal microenvironment.

## Immune escape
The suppressive immune microenvironment facilitates tumor metastasis by aiding tumor cells in evading immune surveillance. Investigations have revealed a strong association between FAP expression in CAFs and the presence of immunosuppressive cells like myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), exhausted T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) , , , suggesting CAFs' role in shaping the suppressive immune microenvironment. Therefore, we also focus on elucidating the interaction patterns between CAFs and various immune cells and their impact on tumor cell immune evasion.

## MDSCs
MDSCs, originating from immature myeloid cells, are increased in TME and contribute to immune suppression. CAFs secrete various substances that recruit MDSCs to tumor tissues, such as CD36+CAFs attracting CD33+ MDSCs through macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) and cluster of differentiation 74 (CD74), and FAP+CAFs recruiting MDSCs via fibroblastic STAT3-CCL2 signaling , . Complement signaling also plays a part. Monteran et al. discovered that treatment with doxorubicin in breast cancer activates complement signaling in lung fibroblasts, recruiting MDSCs to premetastatic lung tissue and facilitating tumor cell colonization .

## Macrophages
Macrophages in the TME exist in two distinctly opposite functional subtypes. Increased CAF activity has been shown to be closely associated with the prometastatic M2 subtype through IL-8 secretion, which inhibits phagocytosis and recruits them to the tumor microenvironment , . In addition to the commonly reported M2 subtype, further studies have also revealed associations between CAFs and some other subgroups that possess prometastatic characteristics. In TNBC, CAFs interact with monocytes via CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling, leading to monocyte transform into lipid-associated macrophage (LAM). LAM is closely related to immune checkpoint blockade resistance and tumor progression promotion . CD204+ TAMs secrete cytokines influencing T-cell responses and metastasis, with PDPN-expressing CAFs enhancing CD204+ TAM infiltration in lung squamous cell carcinoma .

## Neutrophils
Widespread infiltration of neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment is referred to as tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), which can facilitate the dissemination of cancer cells to distant organs . CAFs can modulate antitumor immunity and promote tumor metastasis through their influence on TANs. Interaction between CAFs and tumor cells recruits protumor N2-type neutrophils through the Notch2-Jagged1 interaction . According to Cheng et al.'s study, CAFs influence neutrophil behavior and function via IL-6-STAT3/PD-L1 signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma . In addition, neutrophils can also promote tumor progression through the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). NETs, first proposed in 2004, are web-like structures composed of DNA fibers, histones, and antimicrobial proteins secreted by activated neutrophils to capture and kill extracellular pathogens, playing a crucial role in tumor metastasis . FGF19-induced CAFs are reported to facilitate colorectal cancer liver metastasis by promoting NET formation . The research of Munir et al. suggests that CAFs depend on β-amyloid and ROS to mediate NET formation .

## Dcs
CAFs can also regulate the body's antitumor immune response by affecting DCs. In liver cancer, CAFs secrete IL-6, activating STAT3 in DCs, which generates regulatory DCs characterized by reduced expression of costimulatory molecules, increased production of inhibitory cytokines, and negative regulation of immune responses. This promotes Treg expansion . Additionally, CAF-derived Wnt signaling suppresses DCs. Shen et al. discovered that after chemotherapy or radiation, human ovarian cancer-associated fibroblasts secrete WNT16B, which lead to the accumulation of β-catenin in DCs and the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-ß. This contributes to the induction of Treg cell differentiation in the coculture environment .

## NK cells
The inhibition of NK cell activation by CAFs is also a potential promoting factor for tumor metastasis. Yang et al. confirmed that multiple mechanisms participate in this process. This includes reducing the proliferation rates of NK cells, diminishing their cytotoxic capacity, suppressing the extent of degranulation, downregulating stimulatory receptors, and upregulating inhibitory receptors . In addition, as we mentioned above, CAFs collaborate with TAMs in colorectal cancer, suppressing NK cells by promoting monocyte adhesion and M2 polarization of macrophages .

## T cells
The impact of CAFs on tumor metastasis through the modulation of T-cell activity has been extensively investigated . In the study by Mao et al., CAFs significantly impact cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs, CD8+ T cells) activity, contributing to the suppressive immune microenvironment in breast cancer lymph node metastases . CAFs, in comparison to NFs, can suppress T-cell proliferation and induce T-cell apoptosis by secreting ILs and NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) , . Numerous studies have reported the impact of CAFs on T-cell suppression through immune checkpoint regulation, which mainly includes upregulation of PD-1 expression in T cells and PD-L1 expression in tumor cells , . When PD-1 binds to PD-L1, inhibitory signals are generated.

## Treg cells
CAFs play a role in Treg infiltration into the TME. The study by Huang et al. suggests that antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) derived from mesothelial cells can induce the conversion of CD4+ T cells into Tregs . Jacobs et al. identified a CD70+ subset of CAFs that promote tumorigenesis by facilitating the accumulation of Tregs . Mechanically, CAFs can secrete IL-1β, activating NF-κB signaling, and upregulating the expression of CCL22 . Conversely, high Treg infiltration may suppress CAFs activity, suggesting a negative feedback loop between Tregs and CAFs . The interaction between them and their impact on tumor metastasis still requires further research and exploration.
These findings highlight the crucial role of CAFs in regulating immunoreaction, offering potential targets for therapeutic interventions in metastatic cancers ( ). Nevertheless, immune cells within the tumor microenvironment exhibit diverse functional states and responses influenced by CAFs. Understanding the heterogeneity of immune cell responses, such as the balance between pro- and anti-tumor activities of macrophages, neutrophils, DCs, NK cells, T cells, and Treg cells, is essential. Future studies should focus on dissecting the molecular mechanisms underlying these diverse immune responses and their impact on tumor progression and metastasis. The complex interactions among immune cells also remain to be further explored regarding the impact of CAFs on these interactions. 

## Perivascular migration
Within solid tumors, a physical barrier called the basement membrane separates epithelial cells from endothelial cells, primarily composed of laminin and type IV collagen secreted by CAFs . The process of tumor cells breaching this barrier to enter into blood vessels is known as intravasation. Subsequently, the tumor cells adhere to the vascular endothelial cells and traverse the vessel wall to enter new tissues, a process called extravasation, crucial for tumor metastasis. CAFs are believed to play essential roles in both intravasation and extravasation processes ( ).
First, CAF-secreted CXCL12 is considered pivotal in facilitating tumor cell intravasation by inducing their migration toward the vicinity of blood vessels in collaboration with macrophages . CXCL12 also recruits neovascular or preangiogenic endothelial cells, enhancing vascular permeability . Furthermore, under platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) stimulation, CAFs secrete VEGFC and VEGFA, favoring lymphangiogenesis and tumor cell intravasation . The extravasation of tumor cells from blood vessels is considered a rate-limiting step in metastasis. Research indicates that activation of the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway in tumor stromal cells, including CAFs, leads to the secretion of miR-214 in EVs, enhancing the extravasation and metastasis of cancer cells . Jiang et al. found that fibronectin secreted by tumor fibroblasts promotes the expression of intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1), increasing endothelial cells permeability and facilitating tumor cells transendothelial migration, thus aiding tumor cell extravasation . Combination strategies targeting multiple steps in the metastatic cascade, including intravasation, extravasation, and colonization, may improve therapeutic efficacy.

## Dormancy
Tumor dormancy refers to a state in which cancer cells, despite being present in the body, remain inactive or grow very slowly without causing detectable symptoms or progressing into clinically significant tumors. During dormancy, cancer cells may exist in a quiescent or non-proliferative state, often evading detection by the immune system and conventional diagnostic methods. However, these dormant cells can potentially reawaken, resume active growth, and lead to tumor recurrence or metastasis.
While research on the mechanisms of CAFs in tumor dormancy is limited, it provides valuable insights into both basic biology and clinical implications ( ). For tumor cells that have recently extravasated into secondary tissues, direct interaction with NFs may contribute to dormancy . Studies by Degeorges et al. suggest that IL-6 may be a stromal fibroblast-derived molecule that exerts an inhibitory effect on tumor cell proliferation . This contradicts our inherent understanding, as IL-6 generally plays a role in promoting cell proliferation in the body, and the validity of the research remains to be investigated. Additionally, CAFs may play a role in dormancy through markers like nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F member 1 (NR2F1), associated with lower mutation rates and reduced immune cell infiltration in tumors with high NR2F1 levels . Therefore, NR2F1 has the potential to serve as a dormancy marker, signaling the latent risk of tumor metastasis in patients. As one of the precursor cells of CAFs, mesenchymal stem cells are believed to secrete miR-23b, which can target and downregulate myristoylated alanine-rich C kinase substrate (MARCKS) in tumor cells, inducing dormancy by disrupting the cell proliferation cycle .
Apart from CAFs, other stromal components, such as immune cells, extracellular matrix components, cytokines, and growth factors, contribute to dormancy regulation. Understanding how CAFs collaborate with these microenvironmental factors to establish and maintain dormant niches is essential. Moreover, the impact of therapeutic interventions, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, on the dormancy-promoting or −reversing functions of CAFs requires further investigation.

## PMN (premetastatic niche) formation
Generally, due to the absence of essential cytokines and sufficient nutrients needed for tumor cell growth in the stroma, along with immune surveillance, the majority of tumor cells extravasated from blood vessels into the interstitial spaces cannot survive. Recent studies has revealed that primary tumor cells can release cytokines or EVs into the bloodstream, reaching specific tissues. These secretions regulates vascular permeability, angiogenesis, stromal cell activity, extracellular matrix structure, and immune cell recruitment . This process sets up a microenvironment known as the PMN formation, which supports the growth of metastatic tumor cells.
Numerous studies to date have confirmed the pivotal role of stromal fibroblasts as key mediators in the establishment of PMNs at distant sites by primary tumor tissues ( ). In breast cancer mouse models, activin A (ActA) from breast tumors triggers fibrotic changes and alters lung fibroblasts, leading to increased collagen in the lung PMN and promoting lung metastasis . Mo et al. found that ovarian cancer cells release exosomal miR-141, which targets and reduces YAP1 in specific tissue fibroblasts. This process results in increased granulocyte chemotactic protein 2 alpha (GROα) and CXCRs, converting fibroblasts into CAFs that contribute to PMN formation through cytokine release . However, the literature reviewed earlier suggests an increase in YAP within CAFs during the process of tumor metastasis and promotes ECM remodeling. Taking into account the findings here, we have reason to believe that the role of YAP in tumor metastasis is multifaceted and warrants further investigation.
While significant progress has been made in understanding PMNs and their role in metastasis, there are still many unanswered questions and challenges to address. Further research should investigate the dynamic changes in fibroblast phenotypes and functions during PMN formation, including the mechanisms driving their activation into CAFs. Understanding how these changes contribute to PMN remodeling and support metastatic cell survival will guide therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting PMNs.
As elucidated in these sections, fibroblasts are capable of responding to signaling cues from the primary tumor site, orchestrating the induction of tumor cell dormancy and the establishment of a PMN at secondary sites ( ). These processes collectively serve to foster a favorable environment for tumor cell colonization in distant secondary sites. 

## Potential antimetastatic strategies targeting CAFs
Given the significant role of CAFs throughout tumor metastasis, directing therapeutic efforts targeting CAFs holds immense potential in clinical settings. Diverse therapeutic strategies have been devised to counteract the pro-metastatic effects of CAFs within the tumor microenvironment. Broadly speaking, these strategies can be categorized into three main approaches: specific elimination of CAFs through surface marker-based therapies, conversion of activated CAFs into quiescent states, and inhibition of prometastatic signaling pathways within CAFs, as shown in . In this section of our study, we will delve into a detailed exploration of these therapeutic strategies. 

## Specific elimination of CAFs through marker-targeted therapy
FAP, an enzyme located on the cell membrane surface, is typically expressed in CAFs. Even more so, according to our recent research, in the TME of gastric cancer, FAP is the true specific marker of CAFs, compared to fibroblast-specific protein 1 (FSP1) and α-SMA . FAP has been extensively investigated as a therapeutic target against CAFs from various research perspectives.
One approach involves utilizing gene vaccines to selectively eliminate CAFs, thereby counteracting their pro-metastatic effects. This strategy entails selecting antigenic epitopes within the FAP protein, synthesizing gene sequences from these epitopes, and integrating them into carriers like adenoviruses or plasmids. Upon cellular uptake, these gene sequences prompt the expression of FAP protein fragments, stimulating the immune system to target CAFs harboring FAP epitopes. Multiple studies have demonstrated the efficacy of gene vaccines targeting FAP in inducing immune responses against CAFs and suppressing tumor progression , , , , . Another innovative strategy involves employing genetically modified chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells specifically targeting FAP. This approach has brought hope in reversing the immune-suppressive microenvironment orchestrated by CAFs, leading to enhanced CAR-T efficacy against various cancer types .
 , , . While gene vaccines and CAR-T cell therapies targeting FAP in CAFs show promise, challenges such as off-target effects, immune-related adverse events, and tumor escape mechanisms need thorough investigation. Future research should focus on enhancing the specificity and safety of these immunotherapies, possibly through engineered antigen recognition or combination therapies with checkpoint inhibitors.
Photodynamic therapy targeting FAP-expressing CAFs, utilizing photosensitizers and specific wavelengths of light, represents another therapeutic avenue. A typical architecture encompasses FAP-specific antibodies, chelators, and photosensitizers. In the study by Smeets et al., a FAP-binding minibody was employed , while Dorst et al. utilized the antibody 28H1 for FAP targeting . Parallel to photodynamic therapy, photodynamic immunotherapy targeting FAP capitalizes on photosensitizers generating ROS upon exposure to light, resulting in the demise of FAP-expressing CAFs . Near-infrared light, favored for its deeper tissue penetration, is also a pivotal component within photodynamic immunotherapy . Although photodynamic therapy targeting FAP-expressing CAFs has shown efficacy, optimizing the delivery of photosensitizers, ensuring specific FAP targeting, and addressing potential skin photosensitivity are critical areas for improvement.
Furthermore, molecular inhibitors like FAP inhibitors (FAPIs) have shown selective binding and inhibition of FAP activity, thus suppressing CAF functions. Remarkably, FAPI boasts a relative specificity for homing to tumor tissue and, in tandem with radiotracers, facilitates high-resolution quantitative imaging of FAP . Furthermore, FAPI serves as a potential drug carrier, enabling precision therapy for tumor tissues . The use of FAPIs combined with radiotracers for imaging and radiotherapeutic applications is a rapidly evolving field. Future studies should focus on refining FAPI variants, exploring novel radioactive isotopes, and determining optimal dosing and administration routes for effective CAF targeting while minimizing systemic toxicity.
Additional investigations have unveiled inhibitory effects on CAFs through FAP manipulation. Sitia et al. functionalized H-ferritin (HFn) nanocages with FAP antibody fragments and proficiently loaded navitoclax (Nav), an experimental Bcl-2 inhibitor pro-apoptotic drug. This cargo elicited specific cytotoxicity toward CAFs in a syngeneic mouse model of TNBC . Notably, Li et al. found a significant reduction in CAF accumulation and pronounced inhibition of xenograft tumor growth when oxaliplatin was combined with the small-molecule dipeptidase inhibitor PT-100 (targeting CAFs through FAP) . Recently, a nanodelivery system (PMNPs-D), loaded with doxorubicin, was shown to respond to CAF-overexpressing FAP-α to release integrin β1 (ITGB1), inhibiting CAF matrix production and improving the permeability of nanotherapeutics in tumors . Combining FAP-targeted therapies with conventional treatments like chemotherapy or small-molecule inhibitors can enhance therapeutic efficacy. However, the synergistic effects, optimal combinations, and potential resistance mechanisms need comprehensive investigation. Moreover, developing advanced nanodelivery systems for targeted drug delivery to CAFs requires further optimization and preclinical validation for clinical translation.
Moving forward, clinical trials evaluating the safety, efficacy, and long-term outcomes of FAP-targeted therapies in diverse cancer types and patient populations are essential. Additionally, identifying predictive biomarkers of response and resistance to these therapies will aid in patient selection and personalized treatment strategies.

## Induction of quiescent fibroblast states from activated CAFs
Several agents have emerged as potential candidates for deactivating CAFs, rendering them quiescent and halting their tumor-promoting activities. Notably, compounds such as artesunate (ARS), dihydroartemisinin (DHA) , and minnelide have shown promise in suppressing the TGF-β signaling pathway within CAFs, inducing a conspicuous reversal of their activated phenotype to a nonproliferative state. In the context of breast cancer, oroxylin A (OA) has exhibited the ability to selectively bind to alpha-actinin-1 (ACTN1), effectively reducing its expression and subsequently inhibiting α-SMA, fibronectin, vimentin, and MMP expression . A high-throughput screening platform identified dihydrorotenone (DHR) as a potent CAF deactivator. Treatment with DHR resulted in decreased expression of CAF-specific markers, reduced collagen gel contraction, and compromised support for tumor-promoting functions, including cancer cell proliferation and colonization .
Moreover, caffeine has also been found to provoke CAF deactivation through the upregulation of tumor suppressor proteins (p16, p21, p53, and Caveolin-1), the attenuation of cytokine expression/secretion (IL-6, TGF-β, SDF-1, MMP-2), downregulation of α-SMA, and inhibition of CAF migration/invasiveness . Furthermore, gold nanoparticles (GNPs) with a diameter of 20 nm exhibit the ability to hinder the activation of CAFs, as evidenced by alterations in cellular morphology, migratory behavior, and shifts in molecular markers . Stellate cells, crucial precursors of CAFs, can be deactivated by the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist exendin-4, which attenuates NF-kB-dependent SDF-1 secretion, leading to stellate cell deactivation and inhibiting cell proliferation and migration . Other potential candidates for inducing CAF deactivation include nanoparticles carrying FAP-α antibodies via cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), and traditional Chinese medicine like sipi soup (SPS) , .
While these agents show promise in deactivating CAFs, further research is needed to the precise molecular mechanisms through which these agents deactivate CAFs and inhibit their tumor-promoting functions, and evaluate their efficacy, safety profiles, and potential challenges in clinical applications. Exploring combination therapies and addressing issues of reproducibility and scalability will be essential for advancing CAF-targeted therapies in cancer treatment.

## Inhibition of prometastatic signaling pathways within CAFs
CAFs play a crucial role in creating a supportive microenvironment for tumor progression and metastasis through the secretion of various growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular matrix components. Targeting specific signaling pathways within CAFs that promote metastasis has emerged as a focal point in cancer research. Several inhibitors targeting the signaling pathways involved in CAFs participating in tumor metastasis, including Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT, TGF-β, JAK/STAT, HH, and NF-kB, have been demonstrated to effectively inhibit tumor progression and metastasis at the cellular or animal level , , , , , . While various antagonists targeting these pathways have shown promise in experimental models, their clinical translation faces substantial challenges due to the complex involvement in both physiological and pathological processes of these pathways.
All these approaches are currently undergoing extensive clinical trials to validate their effectiveness in slowing down the pace of tumor metastasis, presenting promising prospects for practical clinical application in the near future.

## Discussions
Due to its direct correlation with patients' shortened survival, tumor metastasis stands as a central focus within the realm of research. Within this comprehensive review, we conduct a synthesized analysis of CAFs, the most abundant stromal cell subset within the TME, across eight critical steps implicated in tumor metastasis. CAFs are highly heterogeneous, originating from various cell types such as NFs, tumor stem cells, and differentiated mature cells. apCAFs, derived from mesothelial cells, mainly exert immunomodulatory functions. However, whether CAFs derived from other cellular origins exhibit distinct biological functions remains largely unknown and requires further investigation. Recent studies have categorized CAFs into different functional subgroups based on single-cell and transcriptomic sequencing, shedding light on their diverse roles in tumorigenesis and metastasis. As our understanding of CAF functions deepens, precise targeting of CAFs in therapy holds greater translational potential, ultimately benefiting patients.
ECM remodeling plays a crucial role in cancer progression by influencing tumor cell invasion, migration, and the host's antitumor immune response. Here, we focus on alterations in matrix stiffness. Various stromal cells, including CAFs, respond to changes in matrix stiffness as well . Hupfer and colleagues observed that increased matrix stiffness can induce autophagy in CAFs, a process crucial for the activation of CAFs and nearby tumor cell growth . CAF autophagy also plays a crucial role in pancreatic tumor survival under glutamine deprivation by secreting nucleosides that enhance glucose utilization . These studies have led to clinical interest in drugs targeting CAF autophagy, such as the pharmacological inhibitor chloroquine . In colorectal studies, elevated matrix stiffness activated hepatic stellate cells to initiate lipid degradation. The released fatty acids serve as metabolic substrates taken up by tumor cells, leading to increased fatty acid oxidation within tumor cells , . In summary, the bidirectional interplay between CAFs and matrix physical properties plays a pivotal role in tumor initiation, development, and distant metastasis, which opens the door for future studies on making the ECM conducive to restoring a healthy state using emerging techniques.
Lactate, as the primary contributor to the acidic ECM, can promote tumor metastasis in multiple ways, as we previously demonstrated. Intercepting the pro-metastatic effects of CAF-secreted lactate represents an attractive research area. Shi et al. discovered that blocking monocarboxylate anion transporters 1 and 4 (MAT1 and MAT4) on CAFs in bladder cancer will eliminate the promoting effect of lactate secretion from CAF glycolysis on bladder cancer invasion and metastasis, suggesting that MAT1 and MAT4 are key effectors of the reverse Warburg effect . Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is the key enzyme responsible for the conversion of pyruvate to lactate. Suppressing both the LDHA and LDHB genes is necessary to fully inhibit lactate secretion . Moreover, GRP81 has been identified as a lactate receptor, and some antagonists targeting GRP81, such as AZ1and AZ2 , have been developed with the potential to counteract the pro-metastatic effects of lactate in tumors. As research on lactate in cancer becomes increasingly comprehensive and mature, we anticipate significant benefits for cancer patients in the near future. However, achieving precise targeting of lactate levels in CAFs while sparing surrounding cells requires further research.
The suppressive immune microenvironment, whether at the primary or metastatic sites, significantly contributes to tumor metastasis. Our work has unveiled intricate crosstalk between CAFs and various immune cells, establishing their paramount role in shaping the inhibitory immune microenvironment. Within the mechanisms orchestrated by CAFs to dampen T-cell functions, the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules has opened a new avenue for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, which is also a treatment path filled with obstacles. Abundant CAFs in fibrotic breast cancer hinder T-cell activity and ICB therapy , but a nanosystem was developed to reprogram CAFs into immune-activated antigen-presenting cells (APCs), leading to enhanced CD8+ T-cell activation, tumor suppression and prevention of autoimmune-like disorders. This innovative approach offers a promising strategy for improving response rates to ICB therapy . In conclusion, targeting CAFs to enhance immunotherapy effectiveness holds great promise for cancer patients.
Using single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data from the GEO and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases, researchers have been actively investigating the differential expression of CAF marker genes. This has led to the development of risk prediction models based on CAF markers, which have gained significant attention in recent research across various cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma . While predicting patient prognosis based on CAF marker genes has been explored, our focus shifts to directly predicting tumor metastasis. Zhang et al. predicted biochemical recurrence-free survival (BCRFS) and metastasis-free survival (MFS) after radiotherapy by constructing a CAF-related gene risk model . Advancing our ability to predict metastasis through CAF regulation holds immense potential for healthcare professionals to tailor more targeted treatment strategies, although further exploration in this domain is warranted given the limited extent of current research efforts.

## Conclusion and future directions
Collectively, CAFs exert multidimensional regulatory effects throughout the intricate process of distant tumor dissemination, thereby underscoring the profound clinical translational significance in targeting CAFs. Despite some progress being made, there are still many issues that require further exploration. Firstly, a deeper understanding of the cross-regulation mechanisms among different signaling pathways and their dynamic changes within the tumor microenvironment is needed. Secondly, for the discovered inhibitors, more in-depth and comprehensive clinical studies are necessary to evaluate their safety, efficacy, and potential side effects. Additionally, there is a need to develop more targeted and efficient novel inhibitors to address the challenges and issues in cancer treatment. Lastly, combining emerging treatment modalities such as immunotherapy, exploring the combined application of multiple treatment strategies holds promise for greater breakthroughs and advancements in cancer metastasis therapy.
