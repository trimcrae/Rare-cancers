## Abstract
Craniofacial bones, as essential components of the human head, play a crucial role in both physiological function and morphological evolution through their development and the maintenance of homeostasis. Unlike skeletal bones of the trunk, which originate from the mesoderm, craniofacial bones originate from the ectodermal neural crest, presenting challenges in directly applying findings from long bone studies. Their development occurs via intramembranous ossification, endochondral ossification, and specialized osteogenic patterns, and is co-regulated by both mechanical forces and molecular signalling pathways. The imbalances in these biomechanical regulatory mechanisms contribute to disruptions in craniofacial bone homeostasis, which can result in a range of developmental and acquired skeletal disorders. This review highlights recent advances in the molecular regulation of craniofacial bone development, examines the interplay between mechanical and genetic regulatory mechanisms, and discusses diseases associated with imbalances in craniofacial bone homeostasis, offering novel insights for the prevention and treatment of craniofacial bone disorders.
Keywords: Bone development, Facial bones, Osteogenesis, Mechanical signal transduction, Signal transduction, Craniofacial abnormalities

## Introduction
Craniofacial bones are parts of the cranial bones excluding the cerebral cranium, and primarily constitute the facial skeleton. In adults, the craniofacial bones typically consist of 14 bones: the paired maxilla, zygomatic bones, nasal bones, lacrimal bones, palatine bones, and inferior nasal concha bones, along with the isolated vomer and mandible. In vertebrate embryos, axial bones (e.g., vertebrae) and appendicular bones (e.g., long bones) develop from the mesoderm of the trunk. In contrast, craniofacial bones (e.g., maxilla and mandible) develop from cranial neural crest cells (CNCC). For cranial sutures, they separate bones of different embryonic origins while also originating from distinct tissues. Cranial sutures derived from CNCC include metopic and sagittal sutures, while coronal sutures are derived from the paraxial mesoderm. The developmental origin of lambdoid sutures remains unknown.
CNCCs are multipotent cells capable of differentiating into the majority of the skeletal, cartilaginous, and connective tissues of the head. They originate from the lateral edges of the anterior neural plate, migrate ventrally, and position themselves beneath the surface ectoderm. This process, regulated by epithelial-mesenchymal interactions, facilitates the formation of facial primordia, including the frontonasal prominence, maxillary arch, and mandibular arch. A small fraction of the facial bones derives from mesoderm. For example, within the mandibular arch, part of the mesodermal tissue differentiates into a cartilaginous scaffold. This cartilage gradually ossifies through endochondral or intramembranous processes, forming the primary structures of the maxilla and mandible.
For the craniofacial bones to develop normally, homeostatic maintenance is essential. Bone morphology and associated physiological functions may be impacted by any factor that disrupts normal development and causes homeostatic imbalance, such as genetic mutations, abnormal mechanical force, or endocrine disorders. Research on facial development requires a comprehensive understanding of normal morphology, cellular signalling, gene-gene interactions, and spatiotemporal transcriptional regulation, as craniofacial morphogenesis relies on ongoing and reciprocal tissue-tissue interactions. In order to provide new research directions for the early diagnosis and treatment of related disorders, this review will examine the regulatory mechanisms controlling the development of the craniofacial skeleton as well as the function of mechanical factors.
To deeply analyse the current research status and cutting-edge trends of craniofacial development, accurately understand its development stages and characteristics, the literature related to craniofacial development from 2000 to 2025 in the Web of Science core database was visually and quantitatively analysed from the perspectives of keywords and journals through the bibliometric tools CiteSpace and VOSviewer. Recent studies on craniofacial development have mainly focused on themes such as mesenchymal stem cells, mechanisms, cranial neural crest cells, osteogenic differentiation, tissue engineering, and bone regeneration. Disease-related topics including craniofacial abnormalities and obstructive sleep apnoea have also attracted increasing attention. These keywords suggest that the research focus in this field has gradually shifted from traditional morphological descriptions to investigations of cell lineage specification and molecular regulatory mechanisms, and further expanded toward regenerative medicine and translational research. Journal analysis further revealed that Development publishes the largest number of articles on craniofacial development, indicating that this field remains primarily rooted in developmental biology while increasingly integrating with interdisciplinary areas such as regenerative medicine and tissue engineering.
Because this is a narrative review (not a systematic review), we did not register a protocol but we recorded a transparent search strategy. Searches were run in PubMed and Web of Science using combined keywords and controlled terms. Searches were limited to the years 2000 to 2025 and English-language publications. Then import the retrieved records into EndNote for deduplication. In addition, we examined the reference list of the included articles to capture any studies that may have been overlooked (citation chain tracing) (as shown in , , , ).
 After deduplication, titles and abstracts were screened by two reviewers independently, excluding irrelevant records. Full texts of remaining articles were then assessed against criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion (third reviewer arbitrated if needed). Included studies met all of the following:
Research topic: Addresses craniofacial bone development or related pathology (e.g. sutural biology, facial dysostosis, cartilage development).
Subjects: Involves human data (clinical or genetic studies) or vertebrate animal models (e.g. mouse, zebrafish).
Methods: Contains experimental data (in vivo or in vitro) on molecular, cellular, or biomechanical aspects.
Publication: English-language, peer-reviewed original articles or reviews.
Excluded were:
Studies unrelated to developmental mechanisms (e.g. purely dental treatment protocols).
Case reports, small case series, editorials, or abstracts without sufficient data.
Non-English publications.
This review addresses three key aspects of craniofacial bone biology:
(1)Embryological origin and ossification mechanisms – how craniofacial bones derive from neural crest cells and the roles of intramembranous and endochondral ossification;
(2)Mechanical forces and gene regulation – the cooperative control of craniofacial bone growth and remodelling by biomechanical and signalling pathways;
(3)Developmental and acquired dyshomeostasis – including imbalances in craniofacial bone homeostasis arising from both congenital disorders and acquired factors.

## Initial development of craniofacial bones

## Embryology of craniofacial bone development
The process of craniofacial morphogenesis is intricate. The neural crest is where we begin. The ectodermal neural plate’s marginal region is the source of neural crest cells. They separate from the neural epithelium during the epithelial-mesenchymal transition that occurs during neural tube closure. After that, they move to the frontonasal prominence and pharyngeal arches, where they differentiate into chondrocytes and osteoblasts that support the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), maxilla, palatine bone, and the majority of the Meckel’s cartilage (MC). The coordinated action of several transcription factors (Sox9, Runx2, Msx, Hox, and Dlx) and signalling pathways (WNT, FGF, and TGF-β/BMP) controls this process. The facial primordia are made up of a mesodermal core encased in neural crest-derived mesenchyme and covered in epithelium that comes from either the ectoderm or both the ectoderm and endoderm. The face is the result of the growth and fusion of several facial primordia into a single, integrated structure. Morphogenetic events within each region are controlled by signalling interactions between adjacent tissues. For example, in the mandibular process and other pharyngeal arches, interactions among the endoderm, neural crest mesenchyme, paraxial mesoderm, and overlying surface ectoderm govern the patterned growth of arch derivatives. 

## Different ossification mechanisms of craniofacial bones
In the initial phases of embryonic development, bones consist of fibrous membranes and hyaline cartilage. Bones subsequently form and regenerate via two separate ossification mechanisms: intramembranous ossification and endochondral ossification. In both intramembranous and endochondral ossification, mesenchymal cells are sourced from either neural crest cells of ectodermal origin or from mesoderm. 
The preliminary phase of intramembranous ossification involves the mineralization of the collagenous FGFR2. Specific mesenchymal cells cluster and develop into osteoblasts, establishing ossification centres. These centres release osteoid, establishing an ossification centre, and then radiate to generate bone trabeculae, which interconnect to create a network. The periosteum envelops the surface layer, beneath which osteoclasts undergo differentiation. The skeleton attains its ideal morphology through ongoing bone production and resorption. This process is the primary mechanism of ossification in craniofacial bones and also transpires in flat bones across the body, including the cranial vault bones and clavicle. During cranium growth, the mesenchyme between cranial sutures remains unossified. It sits between the osteogenic fronts (OFs) of adjacent bone plates. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and osteoprogenitor cells along the OFs proliferate continuously. These cells then differentiate into osteoblasts. Osteoblasts secrete osteoid, a matrix that mineralizes and is deposited at the bone plate’s edge. New bone tissue gradually forms at the plate’s periphery, allowing the cranium to expand as the brain continues to grow. At the same time, the separation between bone plates is maintained so that the cranium can grow with the brain.
During endochondral ossification, bones develop by replacing hyaline cartilage. This process is not a direct transformation of cartilage into bone, but instead uses cartilage as a template. Initially, mesenchymal condensation occurs, forming a perichondrium at the periphery while the centre differentiates into chondroblasts. These cells secrete a cartilage matrix rich in type Ⅱ, Ⅸ, and Ⅺ collagen, along with specific proteoglycans such as aggrecan. The matrix surrounds and separates the chondroblast. As cells in the central region differentiate into hypertrophic chondrocytes, osteoblasts appear in the perichondrium to form a “bone ring”— osteoblasts deposit bone in the rings around the diaphysis. Simultaneously, blood vessels invade the cartilage, leading to matrix resorption. Osteoclasts also arrive with the vessels, thereby enabling skeletal growth and remodelling. Ultimately, hyaline cartilage is replaced by bone to form the epiphyseal line. This pattern governs the development of trunk bones, limb bones (except the clavicle), skull base bones, and the vertical plates of the ethmoid bone.
It is worth noting that among the craniofacial bones, TMJ holds a unique position as a defining feature of mammals. Formed by the junction of the dentary and squamosal dermal bones, it is known in humans as TMJ. In previous studies, it was discovered that the temporal bone squamous portion and mandibular ramus reach the future condylar position via intramembranous ossification. In contrast, the centre of the condylar oblasts undergoes cartilaginous ossification and becomes surrounded by membranous bone. The mandible originates from primary intramembranous ossification of fibrous mesenchymal tissue around MC. By the eighth week post-fertilization, the trabecular bone of the mandibular body rapidly grows, forming muscle attachments with the masseter, temporalis, and pterygoid muscles. Subsequently, the mandible rapidly separates from MC, and the condyle blastema forms at the posterior end of the linear mandibular trabeculae. The condyle blastema, attached to the upper portion of the pterygoid muscle, grows posteriorly and superiorly while undergoing endochondral ossification to form the condyle. In the epiphyseal plate of typical long bones, chondrocytes undergo a process of degeneration and death. However, in the mandibular condyle, chondrocytes remain viable throughout the entire cartilaginous region. The temporomandibular disc rarely undergoes fibrocartilaginous transformation with age. Current research on the mechanism of ossification of the condylar cartilage in TMJ remains insufficient, and the reasons for this discrepancy are not yet clear.
Coincidentally, both ossification mechanisms co-occurred in the MC of the mandible. Previously, the function of MC was understood to support and guide the growth of the mandibular process before mandibular ossification, facilitate the establishment of the mandibular-cranial joint, and subsequently guide and assist in the mandible’s anterior and lateral growth following ossification. However, Wang Songlin’s team employed single-cell RNA sequencing, scStereo-seq, and lineage-tracing mice to reveal spatial heterogeneity in mandibular MC ossification. The anterior MC differentiates into osteogenic lineage cells primarily through endochondral ossification, while the middle MC participates in membrane ossification via cell-to-cell communication. This discovery not only reshapes our understanding of the function of MC but also paves new pathways for future exploration into the molecular mechanisms regulating regional differentiation of the mandible and the mechanisms underlying oral and maxillofacial developmental abnormalities, such as craniofacial deformities. Studies indicate that MC-derived type Ⅱ collagen-positive cells (Col2+) exhibit significant migration and participation in the formation of the periosteum and subsequent endosteal bone formation within the jawbone. MC-derived Col2+ cells are primary constituents of periosteal and osteoblastic cells, and they play a part in the production of both chondrocytes and osteoblasts during the healing processes of bone injuries and sockets. These findings advance our understanding of mandibular development and regeneration.
This phenomenon also exists in the maxilla. Of the three bones that contribute to the palate, the palatal processes of the palatine and maxillary bones are both derived from intramembranous ossification rather than endochondral ossification. However, the region of the pterygoid process develops by secondary endochondral ossification. 

## Factors regulating craniofacial bone growth and remodelling
During the embryonic period, bones undergo gradual ossification through the selection of ossification mechanisms under genetic influence, thereby forming specific shapes. During the developmental stage, bone morphology is shaped not only by genetic factors but also by other influences such as mechanical transduction.

## Effects of mechanical signals on craniofacial bone growth and remodelling
Researchers have demonstrated that mechanical forces generate strains ranging from 2000 to 3500 microstrains, with no significant difference observed between long bones and craniofacial bones. It may be because the general function of bone tissue relies on intricate interactions among various cell types, a phenomenon commonly referred to as multicellular coupling effects. Long bones endure tensile, bending, compressive, shear, torsional, and combined forces, and exhibit high tensile and compressive strength but possess a relatively low elastic modulus. This implies they readily deform under external forces yet regain their original shape once stress is removed. Additionally, long bones demonstrate good toughness and plasticity, enabling them to resist bending and torsional loads to a certain extent.
Compared with long bones, craniofacial bones are flat or irregular bones composed of thin-walled bone tissue. Some contain air-containing cavities called sinuses (e.g., the maxillary sinus within the maxilla). Craniofacial bones connect to the cranial vault via cranial sutures. These craniofacial sutures offer greater flexibility than facial bones, resisting deformation from tensile, compressive, or combined force. The properties of sutures relate to their viscoelasticity, which results from the extracellular matrix composed of collagen fibers, proteoglycans, and water. During growth and development, the craniofacial region experiences mechanical forces from various directions. For instance, pressure applied during tooth eruption induces compressive strain, leading to bone thickening; tensile strain from facial muscle contraction lengthens the bone at the edge of the suture. Mechanical forces influence the elongation of chondrocyte columns within the tendon-bone junction, affecting extracellular matrix (ECM) production and thereby shaping the morphology of bony prominences. Changes in mechanical force induce a dynamic equilibrium between osteoblast and osteoclast activity—enhancing osteogenesis in areas requiring reinforcement and boosting osteoclast activity in regions needing reduction. When mechanical force exceeds skeletal load capacity, the homeostasis between osteogenesis and osteoclastism is disrupted, leading to bone injury. Therefore, it is essential to investigate how bones perceive, transmit, and respond to mechanical force.
ECM serves as the crucial structural and functional foundation of bone tissue. Pressure gradients induced by bone bending subsequently generate shear stress on the cell membrane. The bone matrix, rich in hydroxyapatite and collagen, exhibits piezoelectric properties. Piezoelectricity converts mechanical stimuli into electrical signals, which then modulate fluid flow by influencing fluid charge distribution, thereby promoting bone growth in response to mechanical loading. Osteocytes perceive mechanical changes in the ECM through harmful feedback mechanisms. Numerous proteins involved in transmitting mechanical signals within osteocytes exist; for instance, they can sense mechanical signals through interactions between integrin-mediated adhesion and the cytoskeleton. 
Among these, the piezoelectric channels are a mechanosensitive channels that converts mechanical stimuli into electrochemical signals. In vertebrates, they comprises two members: PIEZO1 and PIEZO2. These channels primarily transduce mechanical stimuli—such as tension, shear stress, and substrate stiffness—into intracellular signals via Ca²⁺ influx, thereby regulating various types of bone cells (including osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts, and mesenchymal stem cells) during craniofacial skeletal development. Existing studies indicate that PIEZO1/2-mediated Ca²⁺ flux initiates downstream pathways such as Calcineurin/NFAT, YAP/TAZ, and Wnt/β-catenin, which coordinately promote osteoblast differentiation. Additionally, these channels are involved in osteogenesis and bone remodelling through pathways including PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, and RhoA/ROCK.
PIEZO1 contributes to the response of the osteoblast cell line MLO-Y4 to fluid shear stress. Upon opening, PIEZO1/2 induces Ca²⁺ influx; the elevated Ca²⁺ levels subsequently activate downstream effectors such as calcineurin. PIEZO1 can couple with distinct downstream signalling networks in different cell types, exerting differential regulatory effects on bone metabolism. Zhou et al. demonstrated that the Ca²⁺/calcineurin signalling axis promotes the dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of NFATc1, facilitates the dephosphorylation and activation of YAP1 and CTNNB1, and enhances the physical interaction between NFATc1 and YAP1/CTNNB1, ultimately promoting osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. In another experiment, activation of PIEZO1 using its chemical agonist Yoda1 interfered with the RANK/TRAF6/PI3K/Akt signalling axis, suppressed the expression and nuclear translocation of NFATc1, and thereby inhibited osteoclast differentiation and function. Meanwhiler, loss of the mechanotransduction channel PIEZO1 in osteoblasts impairs osteoclast activity through impaired YAP signalling and collagen expression, leading to bone loss and spontaneous fractures. This phenomenon suggests that PIEZO1 signalling does not function merely as a pro-osteogenic or anti-osteogenic regulator, but rather acts as a mechanosensitive signal integrator, whose biological effects are contingent upon the nature of mechanical stimuli, cellular lineage, and the microenvironmental context. Accordingly, future studies should elucidate how PIEZO1-mediated Ca²⁺ signalling orchestrates the spatiotemporal selective activation of distinct downstream pathways.
Although accumulating evidence has established a central role for PIEZO1-mediated mechanotransduction in long bone remodelling, its function in craniofacial skeletal development remains comparatively underexplored. Notably, craniofacial bones are primarily formed through intramembranous ossification and are subjected to distinct mechanical environments arising from mastication, respiration, and cranial growth. These unique developmental and biomechanical contexts suggest that PIEZO1 signalling may exhibit spatially and temporally specific regulatory patterns in craniofacial tissues. PIEZO1 exhibits high levels of expression in the jawbone during the initial stages of mandibular arch mineralization, and its expression overlaps with that of the osteoblast marker alkaline phosphatase throughout embryonic development and the postnatal period. This indicates that PIEZO1 plays a crucial role in regulating jawbone formation and maintenance. Concurrently, PIEZO1 regulates Wnt1 expression via YAP and TAZ, activating the NFAT/YAP1/β-catenin transcription factor complex with PIEZO2 and the AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway, reducing cell proliferation while enhancing migration capacity. PIEZO1 is an indispensable mechanotransduction protein in mesenchymal stem cells, and its activity must be calibrated to maintain their proliferation, differentiation, and self-renewal capacity. YAP/TAZ and PIEZO1 serve as downstream effectors of Wnt5a-mediated actomyosin polarity and cytosolic calcium transients that orient and drive mesenchymal cell intercalations. PIEZO1 promotes mesenchymal stem cell differentiation into osteoblasts by upregulating BMP2. Under mechanical force, PIEZO1 increases intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration, upregulates osteogenic markers, and elevates the RANKL/OPG ratio, thereby facilitating mechanical force-induced osteoclastogenesis through the NF-κB signalling pathway. In chondrocytes, PIEZO1 activates apoptosis through the classical MAPK/ERK1/2 signalling pathway. Currently, studies directly targeting the cranial suture or cranium regarding Piezo are limited. Ye et al. identified the RhoA/ROCK-TAZ axis as critical for intramembranous cranial bone formation in a mouse model of cranial suture distraction osteogenesis ; Jiang et al., in an orthodontic experiment using a periodontal tissue model, showed that Piezo1 activation regulates Wnt/β-catenin and RANKL/OPG in alveolar bone. These findings indirectly suggest that PIEZO1 signalling also plays a role in craniofacial bone remodelling. However, direct evidence remains limited. Therefore, future studies should expand on several fronts. First, craniofacial-specific genetic models, such as neural crest–derived or osteoblast-lineage–specific PIEZO1/2 knockout or gain-of-function systems, should be established. Second, in vivo and ex vivo models should incorporate region-specific mechanical stimuli, including masticatory loading, orthodontic force, and suture tension. Third, single-cell multi-omics approaches could be employed to dissect PIEZO-mediated signalling and downstream pathway selection across distinct craniofacial cell populations. Finally, pharmacological modulation of PIEZO1/2, using selective agonists or inhibitors, may provide novel translational insights for the treatment of craniofacial disorders, including malocclusion, craniosynostosis, and temporomandibular joint diseases.
PIEZO2 is the primary mechanotransduction channel for proprioception and is expressed in the sensory terminals of proprioceptors innervating mouse muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs. It is also expressed in articular chondrocytes. Interestingly, selective ablation of Piezo2 in chondrocytes or osteoblasts does not overtly affect skeletal morphology, whereas mice lacking PIEZO2 in proprioceptive neurons develop scoliosis and hip dysplasia. This indicates that PIEZO2 primarily modulates skeletal development indirectly via the sensory nervous system, a mechanism potentially relevant to human skeletal disorders. Notably, a significant proportion of peripheral sensory neurons innervating bone (bone afferent neurons) express PIEZO2, and its activation contributes to their responses to noxious mechanical stimuli, which are crucial for alveolar bone remodelling during orthodontic tooth movement. Mechanial activation of PIEZO2 in nociceptive neurons promotes orthodontic tooth movement and associated alveolar bone remodelling. Besides, the chemical inhibition of Piezo channels has been shown to alleviate dentinal hypersensitivity, reduce the rate of orthodontic tooth movement, and slow the progression of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) in rat models. Moreover, the mandible is innervated by the trigeminal nerve and develops synchronously with the trigeminal nerve. PIEZO2 has been identified as the primary mechanosensitive ion channel in the trigeminal ganglion, where it is activated in dental mesenchyme during root development, triggering intracellular Ca²⁺ signalling, thereby regulating platelet-derived growth factor A (PDGFA) secretion. Collectively, these observations point to a largely unexplored role of PIEZO2 in craniofacial bone growth and remodelling, acting through neuroskeletal signalling axes. Future investigations should aim to elucidate the interactions between PIEZO1-mediated mechanotransduction in bone cells and PIEZO2-mediated neuronal input, and to assess the translational potential of targeting PIEZO2 for modulating craniofacial bone development, orthodontic remodelling, and repair of craniofacial skeletal defects.

## The influence of genes on craniofacial bone growth and development and bone remodelling
Genes serve as the primary factor governing skeletal shape changes, regulating bone development and homeostasis by controlling osteoblasts and osteoclasts (as shown in ). PIEZO1/2 channels convert external forces (e.g., muscle tension, traction) into Ca²⁺ signals, which are integrated into signalling pathways such as Wnt, TGF-β/BMP, FGF, and Notch, coordinating biological effects including MSC recruitment, osteoblast activation, and osteoclast inhibition (as shown in ).
 Specifically, mechanical force causes TGF-β1 release through PIEZO1, which subsequently stimulates bone mesenchymal stem cells migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation. Mechanical force also activates Ca²⁺ influx via PIEZO1, enhancing YAP/β-catenin signalling (amplifying Wnt pathway activity), reducing osteocyte-derived inhibitors (e.g., sclerostin, Dkk1) or activating Wnt activators (e.g., R-spondin), , , thereby promoting osteogenesis. The Notch pathway temporally restricts differentiation (e.g., Hey1 inhibits Runx2, and Hes1 blocks early osteocyte marker E11 ). FGF9 functions as a mechanically sensitive negative feedback factor; its reduced secretion in osteocytes under mechanical loading alleviates inhibitory effects and enhances osteogenesis. 
Recent single-nucleus and spatial transcriptomic analyses of human embryonic tooth germs have demonstrated that epithelial–mesenchymal interactions are orchestrated by canonical pathways, including WNT, TGF-β/BMP, FGF, and NOTCH, within spatially defined microenvironments. This provides direct human evidence of coordinated pathway activation. Evidence suggests that during the progression of overload-induced temporomandibular joint (TMJ) osteoarthritis, mechanical loading activates the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway through downregulation of sclerostin. This indicates that mechanical stimuli not only promote osteogenesis but may also drive pathological bone remodelling under excessive loading conditions. Therefore, future studies should elucidate the mechanisms by which mechanical cues regulate craniosynostosis, bone regeneration, and orthodontic interventions. Clinically, precise modulation of mechanical loading, combined with targeted regulation of signalling pathways, may enable more effective therapeutic strategies.

## Dyshomeostasis of craniofacial bones
Under normal physiological conditions, the development and maintenance of craniofacial skeletal homeostasis result from the combined effects of multiple factors, including hereditary factors, hormonal levels, and mechanical force. Any abnormality in these factors can disrupt craniofacial skeletal homeostasis, leading to various craniofacial deformities and bone disorders. Based on their ethology, these conditions can be classified as developmental disorders or acquired imbalances. Understanding these underlying mechanisms can guide early diagnosis and inform interventions in paediatric craniofacial surgery, orthodontics, and regenerative therapies (as shown in ).
 

## Developmental imbalance of craniofacial bones
Developmental disorders primarily refer to congenital conditions arising during embryonic development due to genetic mutations, abnormal signalling pathways, or environmental factors. These conditions result in imbalances in craniofacial suture tissue homeostasis or facial dysplasia. Such pathological states typically manifest at birth. Elucidating these mechanisms allows clinicians to anticipate craniofacial growth patterns and implement preventive or corrective interventions early in life.

## Aetiology and treatment of craniofacial suture tissue dyshomeostasis
Suture Mesenchymal Stem Cells (SMSCs) reside within cranial sutures and constitute a heterogeneous stem cell population capable of self-renewal and differentiation into multiple cell lineages, including osteoblasts and chondrocytes. SMSCs play a crucial role in craniofacial development, maintenance of sutural patency, and cranial bone repair and regeneration. Among the 17 sutures in the neonatal skull, more than half connect facial bones. Therefore, the homeostasis imbalance of sutures can seriously affect the development of the craniofacial bones. Facial sutures are crucial for maintaining facial structural integrity, absorbing mechanical force, and promoting postnatal bone growth. The theory that premature closure of facial sutures constitutes the primary pathological feature of craniosynostosis persisted into the 1990s. However, mouse model studies demonstrated that premature fusion of facial sutures alone—even without involvement of the cranial base—can produce significant facial deformities. Calandrelli et al. also hypothesize that premature fusion of the maxillary sutures leads to midface retraction and exacerbates airway hypoplasia. This finding provides a mechanistic explanation for the clinical association between midfacial hypoplasia and paediatric obstructive sleep apnoea, highlighting the importance of early orthopaedic or surgical correction.
Multiple signalling pathways—including FGF, Wnt, Hedgehog, and NOTCH—coordinate SMSC proliferation and differentiation. Dysregulation of these pathways disrupts suture homeostasis and contributes to craniosynostosis and related craniofacial malformations (as shown in ).Clinically, Rapid Maxillary Expansion has emerged as a viable treatment for skeletal Class Ⅲ malocclusion and has been demonstrated as the preferred orthodontic therapy for paediatric cases of Obstructive Sleep Apnoea Syndrome. This orthodontic procedure applies corrective and orthodontic forces through maxillary expansion, reopening the median palatal suture to widen the maxillary basal bone arch and dental arch. This addresses maxillary transverse deficiency, intercepting the onset and progression of malocclusion or mitigating its severity. This intervention exploits the biological plasticity of cranial sutures and demonstrates how mechanobiological insights can be translated into effective orthodontic therapies.
 

## Facial dysostosis
Facial dysostosis is a congenital disorder caused by abnormal development of the first and second pharyngeal arches and their derivatives, including the maxilla, mandible, and their hyoid-supported structures. It can be subdivided into mandibulofacial dysostosis (MFD) and acrofacial dysostosis (AFD). The most clinically characteristic form is Treacher Collins syndrome (TCS), featuring hypoplasia of the maxilla, mandible, and zygomatic complex, along with a lower lateral orbital fissure accompanied by lower eyelid defects and absence of the medial third of the lower eyelashes. TCS is considered a neural crest cell (NCC) disorder in which most patients have ribosome biogenesis defects : known causative genes are TCOF1, POLR1D, POLR1C, and POLR1B. 
A recent study found that the CCHC-type zinc finger nucleic acid binding protein (CNBP) binds to a G-quadruplex structure in the TCOF1 promoter, modulating its transcription; disrupting this G4 in zebrafish reduced TCOF1 expression and produced TCS-like facial anomalies. This suggests that TCOF1 expression is epigenetically regulated, which may help explain variable expressivity and offers a new target for intervention in TCS.
Clinically similar disorders include Miller syndrome, Nager syndrome, and mandibulofacial dysostosis with microcephaly (MFDM). Miller syndrome, a subtype of AFD, is characterized by micrognathia, orofacial clefts, hypoplasia of the zygomatic bones, and so on. It is caused by biallelic mutations in DHODH. Defining features of Nager syndrome include micrognathia, midface retraction, and cleft palate. Whole-exome sequencing positively identified mutations in SF3B4 as pathogenic variants causing Nager syndrome. MFDM is the first multiple malformation syndrome attributed to a major spliceosome defect, caused by variants in EFTUD2. Its characteristic features include midface hypoplasia, micrognathia, posterior nares atresia, cleft palate, and sensorineural hearing loss. A recent study using RNA sequencing revealed a novel EFTUD2 splicing variant, contributing to the understanding of the genotype-phenotype relationship in MFDM. 
A distinct category of craniofacial overgrowth is hemifacial hyperplasia, a rare congenital condition causing marked unilateral enlargement of all facial tissues (bone, muscle, fat, skin, etc.). This differs from hemimandibular hyperplasia caused by condylar hyperplasia and hemimandibular elongation. Its etiology is unclear, but an embryologic hypothesis by Pollock et al. proposes that a unilateral enlargement of the neural tube produces excess neural crest cells on one side, leading to persistent overgrowth of neural-crest–derived bone, cartilage, and soft tissues. Another hypothesis involves genetic alterations in the PI3K/PTEN/AKT1/mTOR pathway, and the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus partially reversed the abnormal cell overgrowth. 

## Craniofacial cartilage developmental defect
Mandibular condylar hypoplasia is associated with various craniofacial anomalies. Congenital condylar hypoplasia is characterized by unilateral or bilateral mandibular condylar underdevelopment, typically occurring as part of certain systemic disorders originating from the first and second pharyngeal arches, such as MFD and hemifacial microsomia. During development, MC contributes to the formation of the middle ear and mandible. The posterior portion of the MC contributes to the formation of the incus, malleus, the anterior ligament of the malleus, and the sphenomandibular ligament. Defects in their development can lead to hearing loss, as seen in Treacher Collins syndrome. The main body of the MC supports the development of the surrounding mandibular skeleton. Defects in MC result in the formation of a smaller, malformed dentary bone, leading to agnathia, micrognathia, or mandibular hypoplasia. 
FGFR3 gain-of-function mutations, which can cause craniosynostosis, may also result in structural abnormalities of the primary (Meckel’s) and secondary (condylar) cartilages of the mandible, leading to mandibular hypoplasia and deformities. Understanding condylar development and diseases can guide the design of molecular interventions to stimulate cartilage growth and mandibular correction.

## Acquired imbalance of craniofacial bones
Craniofacial acquired imbalance refers to abnormalities in the loss of homeostasis of craniofacial bones caused by external factors such as endocrine disorders, osteoporosis, or mechanical force. These issues typically emerge in adulthood and may be associated with lifestyle choices or underlying medical conditions.

## Endocrine and bone metabolism with facial bone development imbalance
Bone metabolism is tightly linked to the endocrine system, and age-related hormonal changes significantly impact craniofacial bone. Studies indicate that systemic osteoporosis correlates with lower alveolar bone density. The alveolar ridge has very high bone turnover, making it a sensitive indicator of systemic bone health. Thus, when occlusal loading decreases (e.g. tooth wear or loss), the lack of mechanical stimulus triggers alveolar bone resorption and height loss, clinically seen as tooth mobility and gingival recession. For example, in animal models occlusal unloading reduced periodontal mechanosensation and led to net alveolar bone loss (termed “alveolar bone disuse osteoporosis”), involving pathways like PIEZO1/Ca²⁺/HIF-1α/SLIT3. Clinically, early alveolar bone loss can serve as a warning sign of generalized bone loss.
The endocrine system plays a crucial role in bone growth and metabolism. After menopause, oestrogen levels fall and accelerate systemic osteoporosis and alveolar bone loss ; oestrogen replacement can slow down alveolar bone loss and increase tooth retention rate. Chronic glucocorticoid therapy (e.g. post-transplant immunosuppression) causes secondary osteoporosis. Vitamin D possesses extensive endocrine and autocrine functions, promoting osteoblast proliferation, osteoclast differentiation and activity, as well as bone formation, resorption, and coupling. Deficiencies in vitamin D and calcium adversely affect skeletal development and health. Vitamin D or calcium deficiency leads to rickets in children and worsened osteoporosis in adults, underscoring their vital role in skeletal health. The specific impacts of vitamin D and calcium on craniofacial bone development warrant further study.
Bone also acts as an endocrine organ. For example, osteocalcin, a bone matrix protein secreted by osteoblasts, not only participates in mineralization but also regulates glycometabolism. Research indicates a significant positive correlation between insulin sensitivity and osteocalcin, suggesting that reduced insulin sensitivity impairs bone formation and leads to bone loss. This means that impaired bone formation ability may also be related to metabolic disorders. Thyroid hormones and growth hormone have potent effects on craniofacial development: childhood hyperthyroidism accelerates bone growth. Congenital and adolescent acquired hypothyroidism causes delayed bone development accompanied by complex craniofacial dysmorphism, including a broad, flat nasal bridge, long face with wide distance between the eyes, broad face, and unclosed fontanelles. In adult hyperthyroidism, thyroid hormones potentiate the osteoclastic effects of parathyroid hormone, and the resulting worsening hypercalcemia leads to relative suppression of hormone secretion by abnormal parathyroid tissue. , Abnormal secretion of growth hormone severely impacts cranial development, causing alterations in facial morphology. Acromegaly, characterized by increased growth hormone release, typically presents with a rectangular face, enlarged nose and lips, and prominent anterior and frontal regions. The profound impact of hormonal regulation on bone metabolism suggests that future interventions may achieve comprehensive optimization of craniofacial bone health through precise modulation of hormonal signalling pathways.

## Mechanical loading induces bone remodelling
Wolff’s Law states that when bones are subjected to external force stimuli for a long time, they will enhance their hardness by adjusting bone density and structure; conversely, they will absorb and weaken. This implies that the morphological changes in jawbones can be caused by mechanical loads, including biting, chewing, and orthodontic forces. Unlike other bones, jawbones continuously face mechanical force and microbial challenges, maintaining bone homeostasis through dynamic adaptation. Mechanical forces generated during mastication not only directly influence jawbone remodelling but also promote bone homeostasis by regulating interactions between osteocytes and immune cells. For instance, mastication reduces sclerostin expression in osteocytes, thereby promoting bone formation; osteocytes produce IGF-1 in response to increased chewing, stimulating osteoblast generation. Under masticatory forces, periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) exhibit elevated calcium concentrations, upregulation of osteogenic markers, and increased ratios in key pathways such as the RANKL/RANK/OPG signalling axis. These alterations promote mechanically induced osteoclastogenesis via the NF-κB signalling pathway, influencing osteocyte activity and mesenchymal stem cell differentiation to coordinate resorption and formation. , Clinical studies indicate that malocclusion alters condylar movement trajectories. Abnormal occlusal positions and tooth wear lead to dental hypersensitivity, uneven bite forces, and occlusal trauma, imposing additional burdens and stresses on the TMJ. This may ultimately result in conditions such as facial asymmetry and temporomandibular disorders (TMD).
Respiratory function may also disrupt craniofacial bone homeostasis. Some scholars believe that the growth force of the maxilla and mandible is defined by the functional requirements of the oral, nasal, and pharyngeal cavities, that is, the airway maintenance mechanism. Consequently, respiratory function influences craniofacial development. Changes in breathing patterns caused by adenoid and tonsil hypertrophy, such as mouth breathing, may lead to abnormal maxillofacial development in children. Moreover, malocclusions like maxillary narrowing and mandibular retrusion often accompany airway narrowing, compelling patients to switch to mouth breathing. However, existing evidence at most confirms an association between severe nasal obstruction and craniofacial development, without establishing causality. Therefore, for patients with mandibular retrusion, prioritizing the exclusion and resolution of airway issues is a prerequisite for orthodontic treatment.
When mechanical forces are applied appropriately, they can yield positive outcomes. Orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) is a specific treatment for malocclusion and a process of alveolar bone remodelling. It relies on mechanically induced and locally inflammatory-regulated bone remodelling. During orthodontic tooth movement, mechanically induced physiological lactate promotes the proliferation and migration of alveolar bone marrow mesenchymal cells. Mechanical compression forces upregulate Adrb2 expression in primary cultured human PDLCs by elevating intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration, contributing to the sympathetic nervous system's regulation of OTM. , The mechanical sensor YAP mediates bone remodelling during OTM by inducing osteoclastogenesis through NF-κB p65. 

## Acquired condylar disease
Acquired (secondary) condylar hypoplasia may result from local or systemic factors and can be classified as condylar hypoplasia or condylar hyperplasia. If the condyle sustains mechanical injury during its active growth phase (e.g., trauma, joint or middle ear infections, or juvenile rheumatoid arthritis), development may cease, leading to mandibular deformity. Disruption of the expected growth pattern of the mandibular condyle during its developmental phase may result in condylar hyperplasia, causing progressive unilateral enlargement of the mandible. This is a self-limited disease primarily observed between the ages of 11 and 30, leading to facial asymmetry that ceases to progress after a specific period.
TMJOA is one of the most severe subtypes of TMD. It is a chronic degenerative joint disease whose pathogenesis involves articular cartilage degeneration, the emergence of subchondral bone osteophyte and lesions, synovial hyperplasia, and angiogenesis. The precise pathogenesis and progression remain incompletely understood, though numerous associated molecular mechanisms and mechanical signalling pathways have been identified during investigations. Experiments in mouse models revealed that Ectodysplasin-A deficiency exacerbates TMJOA by modulating the ATF4/Ihh pathway, while β-arrestin2 deficiency promotes inflammatory cytokine release, apoptosis, and chondrocyte autophagy during TMD progression. PIEZO1, a key mechanosensitive ion channel in cartilage, regulates condylar development and homeostasis. Under pathological conditions, PIEZO1 deficiency prevents TMJOA, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for osteoarthritis prevention and treatment. Recent studies have revealed a novel mechanically driven pathological axis in TMJOA: ECM stiffening activates the Plexin-B2/YAP/SLIT3 axis in chondrocytes, simultaneously driving H-type angiogenesis and cartilage degradation. This suggests a connection between mechanical stimulation, chondrocyte signalling, and vascular invasion.

## Conclusion and outlook—limitations of current research and suggestions for future research directions
This review systematically summarizes the development and homeostasis regulation of craniofacial bones under the combined influence of signalling pathways and mechanical factors. However, despite significant progress in elucidating the fundamental principles of craniofacial bone development, substantial research gaps remain. For instance, in signalling pathway research, studies directly elucidating the molecular regulation of craniofacial bone development remain scarce. Directly applying findings from long bone research to craniofacial bones is clearly inadvisable, as differences persist in their developmental patterns and regulatory mechanisms. New technologies are needed to provide fresh perspectives and data support. Furthermore, by investigating the spatiotemporal specificity of osteogenic patterns in MC, we may extend our understanding to other craniofacial bones. Finally, the interaction between mechanical forces and signalling molecules represents another crucial avenue for craniofacial bone development research. Mechanical forces such as masticatory pressure and muscle traction play vital roles in facial bone development. However, the mechanisms underlying the interaction between mechanical forces and molecular signalling remain incompletely understood. Specifically, the precise pathways through which mechanical load signals are transmitted via signalling molecules (e.g., TGF-β, PIEZO2) to regulate osteogenesis require further investigation. Future research integrating mechanical models with molecular biology techniques can elucidate how mechanical signals influence bone remodelling. Such studies not only reveal fundamental principles of craniofacial bone development but also offer novel approaches for early diagnosis and treatment of related developmental disorders.
