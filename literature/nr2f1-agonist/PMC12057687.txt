## Abstract
Triple-negative breast cancer cells must evade immune surveillance to metastasize to distant sites, yet this process is not well understood. The Eyes absent (EYA) family of proteins, which are crucial for embryonic development, become dysregulated in cancer, where they have been shown to mediate proliferation, migration, and invasion. Our study reveals an unusual mechanism by which EYA3 reduces the presence of cytotoxic natural killer (NK) cells in the premetastatic niche (PMN) to enhance metastasis, independent of its effects on the primary tumor. We find that EYA3 up-regulates nuclear factor κB signaling to enhance CCL2 expression, which, in contrast to previous findings, suppresses cytotoxic NK cell activation in vitro and their infiltration into the PMN in vivo. These findings uncover an unexpected role for CCL2 in inhibiting NK cell responses at the PMN and suggest that targeting EYA3 could be an effective strategy to reactivate antitumor immune responses to inhibit metastasis.

EYA3 promotes evasion of cytotoxic NK cells to enable metastasis, highlighting a therapeutic target in metastatic breast cancer.

## INTRODUCTION
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), marked by low/absent expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) ( – ), represents 12 to 20% of all breast cancers (BCs) ( , , ). TNBC has a predilection for women under the age of 40 ( ) and is a molecularly diverse disease that is often highly aggressive in nature ( , , ). Owing to the lack of targetable receptor expression, treatment is often limited to conventional surgery, radiation, and chemotherapeutic approaches ( ), leading to poor overall survival (OS) for patients with metastatic TNBC relative to other subtypes of BC ( ). With this in mind, further exploration and development of alternative treatment options such as antibody-drug conjugates, Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, or immune checkpoint inhibitors suggest targeted approaches may provide additional benefit in specific cases ( ). Unfortunately, stage IV TNBC remains nearly uniformly lethal ( ). Thus, a greater understanding of the metastatic processes used by TNBC cells is essential to improve outcomes in the future.
Embryonic programs are often reactivated in cancer. The vertebrate Eyes absent protein family, consisting of EYAs1 to 4, are critical for the development of multiple organ systems in mammals, including the kidney ( – ), ear ( , , , ), muscle ( , ), and craniofacial structures ( , , ). The developmental functions of EYAs are conferred through three distinct activities: (i) coactivation of transcription, mediated largely via an interaction with sine oculis homeobox (SIX) family proteins ( – ), (ii) tyrosine (Tyr) phosphatase activity, mediated through an intrinsic haloacid dehalogenase Tyr phosphatase domain in the C-terminal Eya domain (ED) ( , , ), and (iii) serine/threonine (Ser/Thr) phosphatase activity found in the N-terminal domain (ED2) ( ), and mediated via an interaction with the B55ɑ subunit of PP2A ( , ). While expression of EYA proteins is generally low or absent after development is complete ( ), increased expression or reexpression of EYAs is observed in many cancers, including breast ( , – ), lung ( , ), ovarian ( , ), and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors ( ), among others. In the context of development and cancer, EYAs promote many critical cellular processes, including epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition ( ), cell cycle progression and proliferation ( , ), DNA repair and cell survival ( , – ), and cellular migration, invasion ( ), and angiogenesis ( ). Through an interaction with PP2A-B55ɑ, EYAs stabilize the proto-oncogene c-MYC ( ), facilitating tumor progression ( ). We previously showed that EYA’s regulation of MYC promotes Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression in TNBC, leading to exhaustion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and enhanced primary tumor growth ( ). Of interest, EYAs have also been shown to regulate innate immune signaling in response to cytoplasmic DNA/RNA in both Drosophila ( ) and mouse embryonic fibroblasts ( ), suggesting that these functions may be conserved in the context of cancer. However, a role for EYA3 in the regulation of innate immune responses to tumors has never been demonstrated.
The NF-κB family of transcription factors (TFs) drives the expression of diverse target genes, including cytokines/chemokines, cell-surface receptors, prosurvival genes, stress response genes, and growth factors ( – ). The canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, which signals through the IκB kinase (IKK) complex ( ), is activated through phosphorylation of serine-177 and -181 on IKKβ ( ), leading to phosphorylation of IκB ( , ). IκB phosphorylation results in its degradation and subsequent release of the RELA proto-oncogene, NF-kB subunit (RELA) and p50 for nuclear translocation. Once nuclear, RELA/p50 activates numerous genes that regulate growth, survival, innate immunity, and inflammation ( ). While NF-κB can be antitumorigenic ( ), it is often protumorigenic and metastatic when activated in cancer cells through altering transcription of genes that lead to increases in tumor cell proliferation, survival, invasion, therapy resistance, inflammation, and changes in both adaptive and innate immune responses to tumors ( – ). These many functions of NF-κB initially made it an attractive cancer target, but unfortunately, its inhibition is associated with substantial toxicities ( , , , ).
During metastatic progression, cancer cells prime distant sites for metastasis, establishing a premetastatic niche that is favorable for cancer cell seeding ( – ). Formation of the premetastatic niche involves suppression of antitumor immune responses at secondary sites before the arrival of metastatic cells, requiring a complex cross-talk between tumor-derived factors, stromal cells, and immune cells ( – ). Numerous cytokines influence immune cells in the premetastatic niche ( ), and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), which can be regulated by NF-κB ( ), has been implicated in promoting the recruitment of both prometastatic inflammatory monocytes ( ) and antimetastatic neutrophils ( ) to the lung. While many immune cells regulate the premetastatic niche, cytotoxic natural killer (NK) cells are critical for the elimination of metastatic cells in the lung, as NK cell depletion results in increased metastatic outgrowth in mouse models of breast and lung cancers ( – ). Accumulating evidence indicates that NK cell activity is associated with a reduced risk of metastasis and that NK cells can be exploited therapeutically to treat metastatic disease ( ). However, NK cells can also be reeducated by cancer cells to promote tumor growth and metastasis ( , ). Thus, interactions in the immune premetastatic niche are complex, and a comprehensive understanding of the interaction between cancer cells and immune cells during the premetastatic window is lacking.
In this study, we demonstrate that EYA3 promotes NF-κB signaling in TNBC by enhancing nuclear localization of the RELA TF and that NF-κB signaling downstream of EYA3 is necessary for spontaneous metastasis of murine TNBC cells, but not for effects on primary tumor growth. We show that EYA3-mediated NF-κB signaling results in increased CCL2 expression, which limits cytotoxic NK cells in the premetastatic niche and inhibits cytotoxic NK cell activation potential in vitro. Last, we demonstrate that this EYA3–NF-κB–CCL2 axis extends to human BC, as these genes and signaling pathways strongly correlate in patient datasets. There remains a dire need for new therapeutic strategies to treat patients with metastatic TNBC. Thus, these findings suggest a possible future avenue for inhibiting EYA3 as a means to target both MYC and NF-κB signaling, thus inhibiting both primary and metastatic TNBC ( , – ).

## RESULTS

## EYA3 promotes spontaneous metastasis of murine TNBC cells
We previously demonstrated that EYA3 promotes primary tumor growth in BC via regulating a MYC–PDL1 axis and suppressing the adaptive immune response to the tumor ( ). However, whether EYA3 may also regulate spontaneous metastasis, which is the leading cause of BC-related deaths ( ), and thus substantially affect the prognosis of women with BC, remained unknown. To address this question, we first analyzed recurrence-free survival and OS in the METABRIC patient dataset for patients with high versus low EYA3 ( – ) and found that patients with high EYA3 expression (top quartile) exhibited decreased survival compared to those with low EYA3 expression (bottom quartile) ( and fig. S1A). We then stratified for patients with TNBC and observed a trend toward decreased recurrence-free survival in patients with high EYA3 expression (fig. S1B). However, this trend was not statistically significant, likely due to a smaller sample size when stratifying for only patients with TNBC.
 Given the association of EYA3 with worsened survival and that previous studies have only evaluated the effect of EYA3 on primary tumor growth ( ) and late-stage metastasis (the latter in immune compromised settings) ( ), we asked whether EYA3 could promote the entire metastatic cascade from the primary tumor in immune-competent models of TNBC. We focused specifically on TNBC, given that EYA3 is more highly expressed in this subset ( ) and there is a dire need for new therapies in this BC subgroup. We performed knockdown (KD) of Eya3 using two independent short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) in the 66cl4 murine TNBC cell line ( ) ( ), after which we injected luciferase-tagged 66cl4 cells with shRNA scramble control (SCR) or Eya3 KD into the mammary fat pad of immune-competent syngeneic BALB/c mice ( ). Primary tumor growth was tracked over time by caliper measurement and, in line with previous findings ( ), we observed a strong reduction in primary tumor growth with Eya3 KD ( ). In our first experiment, we removed the primary tumor when the largest tumor reached 1 cm3 and continued to image the mice weekly after tumor removal using the IVIS bioluminescent imager to detect metastases. We observed a strong decrease in bioluminescence in the lung window for mice injected with 66cl4 Eya3 KD cells compared to SCR cells ( and fig. S1C). As the 66cl4 SCR primary tumors were much larger than the Eya3 KD tumors at the time of removal ( ), and as tumor size is known to correlate with incidence of distant metastases in TNBC ( ), we further asked whether the increased tumor burden observed in the lungs of 66cl4 SCR mice after primary tumor removal was simply a consequence of increased overall tumor burden, or whether EYA3 was directly regulating a prometastatic phenotype. We thus repeated the metastasis experiment, but instead removed each tumor at the same size (1 cm3) rather than at the same time. After primary tumor removal, the mice were again imaged weekly and bioluminescence intensity was averaged across each group and plotted based on the number of days after tumor removal. Data from this second study revealed that EYA3 plays a distinct role in metastasis of TNBC, as a notable decrease in bioluminescence was still observed in the Eya3 KD versus SCR cells when tumors were removed at the same size ( and fig. S1D). To confirm that the measured luminescence correlated with pathologically demonstratable metastases, we performed hematoxylin and eosin staining on lungs from the mice shown in fig. S1B and observed clear metastatic lesions at the endpoint in the lungs of mice that harbored 66cl4 SCR and 66cl4 Eya3 KD primary tumors (fig. S1E).
To determine whether this finding holds true in a second immune-competent model of TNBC, and to further confirm that differences in primary tumor size are not contributing to EYA3’s effects on metastasis, we knocked down Eya3 in E0771 cells tagged with a luciferase–green fluorescent protein (GFP) construct, whereby luciferase and GFP are expressed together on the same plasmid (fig. S1, F and G). These cells are also considered TNBC ( ), though this designation is controversial ( ). We injected E0771 murine BC cells with or without Eya3 KD into the tail vein of syngeneic C57BL/6 mice (thus removing the effects of primary tumor size, but maintaining an immune-competent environment). After 21 days, we observed that several mice reached a humane endpoint without showing any signs of luciferase signal, leading us to speculate that the GFP-linked luciferase tag in this system had been either silenced or lost in these cells, as GFP is immunogenic and has been shown to promote increased immune clearance ( ). All E0771 cells additionally express a puromycin resistance gene as a part of the shRNA plasmid, which was transduced separately from the luciferase-GFP expression plasmid. Thus, we used this marker to identify tumor cells in the lungs of injected mice. We euthanized all mice at 23 days postinjection, isolated RNA from the lungs, and performed quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) on these samples compared to RNA isolated from lung tissue from a tumor-free mouse (used as a negative control). We additionally isolated genomic DNA from the lungs, and performed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) on lung genomic DNA samples. In line with our findings in the 66cl4 model, we detected reduced mRNA expression and reduced genomic incorporation of the puromycin resistance gene in the lungs of mice that were injected with E0771 Eya3 KD cells when compared to the SCR control cells (fig. S1H). Thus, our data demonstrate that in two immune-competent models, EYA3 mediates metastasis to the lungs.

## EYA3 promotes innate immune gene expression and NF-κB activation in murine TNBC cells
To determine how EYA3 may be promoting spontaneous metastasis, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on 66cl4 SCR and Eya3 KD cells. ShEya3 KD1 cells had a higher degree of Eya3 KD than shEya3 KD2 cells ( and fig. S2A), though Eya3 was knocked down in both groups. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that several of the top 15 down-regulated gene sets upon Eya3 KD relate to innate immune signaling ( ), including the Hallmark inflammatory response ( and fig. S2B) and Hallmark TNFα signaling via NF-κB ( ) gene sets. The heatmap of TNFα signaling via NF-κB genes demonstrates a dose-dependent change in expression of most genes in the pathway with Eya3 KD (fig. S2C), where a larger degree of Eya3 KD (shEya3 KD1) correlates with a greater change in the expression of genes in this gene set. Of note, MYC targets were also observed in our GSEA ( and fig. S2, D and E), corroborating previous findings that Myc mRNA and protein are regulated by EYA3 in TNBC ( , ). Further proteomic analysis confirmed that the top gene sets reduced in Eya3 KD cells include inflammatory and MYC signaling gene sets (fig. S2, F and G). These data demonstrate that in addition to regulating MYC, as previously reported ( , ), EYA3 controls another critical tumor promotional pathway in TNBC: NF-κB/inflammatory signaling. Because EYA3 has not been previously shown to control NF-κB/inflammatory signaling in TNBC, and because NF-κB has been implicated in metastasis ( – ), we decided to explore this finding further.
 We thus first asked whether EYA3 may be regulating a single TF within the NF-κB pathway that is responsible for the alterations to the inflammatory signatures. To answer this question, we performed TF enrichment analysis to determine whether genes altered by Eya3 KD were enriched within the target genes of a single TF. We found that genes regulated by Eya3 were significantly enriched within target genes of the RELA TF, which accounted for 9/10 of the top enriched TF motifs (with the remaining 1/10 being another NF-κB TF, c-Rel or Rel) (fig. S2H). RELA is the canonical TF for the NF-κB pathway; thus, alterations to genes that are bound by RELA would be expected to lead to changes in the Hallmark TNFα signaling via the NF-κB pathway ( ). Heatmaps of RELA target genes confirmed this finding, with dose-dependent changes observed in the expression of many RELA target genes in the shEya3 KD1 versus KD2 cells, when compared to the SCR cells ( ). These findings strongly suggest that EYA3 is regulating RELA transcriptional activity in murine TNBC cells.
To determine the mechanism by which EYA3 is regulating RELA target genes, we first examined whether Eya3 KD alters RELA protein levels. In the 66cl4 cell line and two additional murine TNBC cell lines, Met1 ( ) and E0771, we observed that Eya3 KD led to modest reductions in total RELA protein levels (fig. S2, I to K). Because RELA protein is expressed in an inactive state in the cytoplasm, and can only activate downstream gene transcription after translocating to the nucleus ( ), we further examined nuclear localization of RELA as a readout for NF-κB pathway activation. To this end, we performed fractionation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins in the 66cl4, Met1, and E0771 SCR and Eya3 KD cells and found that while cytoplasmic levels of RELA were unchanged, nuclear RELA levels were reduced with Eya3 KD in all three contexts ( ). Furthermore, we observed decreased phosphorylation of IκBα ( ), an event that occurs upstream of RELA nuclear localization. Using an NF-κB promoter luciferase reporter in the Met1 cell line ( ), we further corroborated our finding that Eya3 KD decreases NF-κB–mediated transcription in a second TNBC system (fig. S2L). Together, these data demonstrate that EYA3 promotes nuclear translocation of RELA, leading to an induction of RELA-mediated transcription in murine TNBC cells.

## EYA3-mediated NF-κB signaling promotes metastasis but not primary tumor growth in an immune-competent mouse model
To determine whether EYA3-mediated NF-κB activation is responsible for the prometastatic phenotype of EYA3, we transduced 66cl4, Met1, and E0771 SCR and Eya3 KD cells with either an empty vector (EV) plasmid or a plasmid driving expression of a phospho-mimetic form of the NF-κB activator kinase IKK2 (also referred to as IKKβ), which signals upstream of IκBα in the NF-κB pathway. The mutant protein (IKK2-SE), which has phospho-mimetic substitutions (S→E) of the activating residues Ser177 and Ser181, drives constitutively active NF-κB pathway activation ( ). We were unable to select Met1 Eya3 KD lines expressing this construct [likely due to growth arrest and apoptotic effects of constitutively active NF-κB signaling, which have previously been reported ( , )]. However, we were able to select both 66cl4 and E0771 Eya3 KD lines in which the IKK2-SE plasmid was introduced. Western blot (WB) analysis of fractionated cytoplasmic and nuclear protein in the 66cl4 and E0771 cell lines demonstrated that expression of IKK2-SE rescues nuclear RELA levels in both the 66cl4 and E0771 Eya3 KD cells ( and fig. S3A). When the IKK2-SE construct was expressed in shEya3 KD2 cells, nuclear RELA levels, a proxy for NF-κB activation, more accurately matched SCR + EV cells, as compared to nuclear RELA levels when IKK2-SE was expressed in the shEya3 KD1 cells (fig. S3B, determined in 66cl4 model). Thus, moving forward, we used the shEya3 KD2 IKK2-SE rescue system (rather than KD1). In addition, RNA-seq showed a strong enrichment for the Hallmark NF-κB signaling via TNFα and Hallmark inflammatory response gene sets with IKK2-SE addback in 66cl4 Eya3 KD cells ( ), a finding that was confirmed by performing qRT-PCR of NF-κB target genes in E0771 cells with Eya3 KD ± IKK2-SE addback (fig. S3C). Together, these data demonstrate that expression of IKK2-SE is sufficient to increase NF-κB activation in the context of Eya3 KD. While we have previously shown that EYA3 regulates MYC ( , ), MYC signatures were not rescued by restoration of NF-κB signaling as Hallmark MYC gene sets were not top enriched hits when comparing shEya3 KD2 + IKK2-SE to shEya3 KD2 + EV (fig. S3D), demonstrating that NF-κB signaling downstream of EYA3 is not responsible for EYA3-mediated activation of MYC. In line with this finding, IKK2-SE expression did not rescue the effects of EYA3 on proliferation (fig. S4, A and B), which may thus be mediated, at least in part, by MYC downstream of EYA3. In this way, we were able to separate the EYA3–NF-κB axis from the Eya3–MYC axis for subsequent studies.
 To determine whether EYA3 mediates metastasis via regulating NF-κB signaling, we injected 66cl4 SCR, Eya3 KD, and Eya3 KD + IKK2SE cells into the orthotopic site of immune-competent syngeneic BALB/c mice. Primary tumors were then removed (at day 36) and the mice were monitored over time for evidence of lung metastasis using bioluminescent imaging. Surprisingly, NF-κB reactivation in Eya3 KD cells did not rescue primary tumor growth in immune-competent mice ( ), but it did significantly rescue lung metastasis ( and fig. S4C). In line with the increased metastasis, restoration of NF-κB signaling downstream of Eya3 KD also significantly reduced survival ( ).
Given that NF-κB has been previously implicated in cell-autonomous phenotypes linked to metastasis, such as migration ( ), we asked whether IKK2-SE expression, downstream of Eya3 KD, alters migration in vitro, and metastasis in vivo in an immune-deficient NOD/SCIDγ (NSG) model. IKK2-SE rescued EYA3-mediated migration defects in vitro (fig. S5, A to D), and metastatic burden in NSG mice (fig. S5, E to H), demonstrating that tumor cell–autonomous effects contribute to EYA3–NF-κB–mediated metastasis. However, given the profound effects on metastasis observed in the immune-competent model ( ), and restoration of inflammatory signaling upon NF-κB reactivation in Eya3 KD cells ( ), we focused our attention moving forward on determining whether EYA3 may also be altering the immune tumor microenvironment (TME) to promote metastasis.

## TNBC-expressed EYA3 remodels the premetastatic lung immune microenvironment
We previously reported that Eya3 KD alters multiple adaptive and innate immune populations within the primary tumor ( ), and RNA-seq analysis of 66cl4 SCR versus Eya3 KD cells shows alterations in inflammatory signaling pathways, including multiple cytokines and cytokine receptors ( and fig. S2B). These data led us to hypothesize that EYA3 may be changing the TME not only in the primary tumor but also at the PMN, contributing to the large differences in metastasis observed with Eya3 KD in the immune-competent setting. As cancers progress to the metastatic stage, cells from the primary tumor prepare distant sites for colonization, in part, by altering the immune microenvironment in the premetastatic niche ( ). To determine whether EYA3 affects immune cells in the premetastatic niche, we injected 66cl4 cells with SCR or Eya3 KD into the mammary fat pad of syngeneic mice and allowed palpable tumors (>~50 mm3) to form ( ). In line with previous experiments ( ), cells with Eya3 KD grew at a slower rate at the orthotopic site ( ), and took longer to form palpable tumors ( ). Once tumors were palpable, mice were euthanized and lungs were processed into a single-cell suspension. A small portion of this single-cell suspension was used to isolate RNA, which was then probed for expression of luciferase mRNA by qRT-PCR to determine whether any cancer cells were detectable in the lungs at this early time point. Our assay was sensitive enough to detect 1 cancer cell in 105 lung cells (fig. S6A), and with this level of sensitivity, we did not detect any cancer cells in the lungs of mice with 50-mm3 palpable primary orthotopic tumors (fig. S6B). Thus, our data suggest that our experimental conditions could be used to assess the “premetastatic” phase before micro- or macro-metastases are detected.
 Next, we analyzed the lung single-cell suspensions from these mice by flow cytometry for the presence of various immune cell subsets (fig. S6C). We did not observe significant changes in CD45+ lymphocytes overall, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells, or neutrophils in the lungs of mice injected with SCR cells at the orthotopic site when compared to those injected with Eya3 KD cells (fig. S6D). However, mice harboring small Eya3 KD tumors demonstrated reduced levels of inflammatory monocytes ( ), interstitial macrophages ( ), and dendritic cells ( ) in the premetastatic lung compared to those with SCR tumors. In addition, we observed elevated levels of cytotoxic NK cells, marked by CD11b high/positive and CD27 low/negative ( ), in the lungs of mice injected orthotopically with Eya3 KD cells when compared to SCR control cells ( ). While we have previously observed changes to myeloid cells in the primary tumor with Eya3 KD ( ), we did not observe any changes to cytotoxic NK cell levels in early primary tumors by flow cytometry (fig. S7A) or Vectra staining (fig. S7B). These data demonstrate that Eya3 KD promotes the influx of antitumor cytotoxic NK cells to the premetastatic lung, while decreasing the infiltration of potential protumor innate immune cells.
To understand whether these immune cell alterations are maintained in the lung metastatic niche, we injected mice with 66cl4 SCR or Eya3 KD cells by tail vein and tracked metastasis by bioluminescence imaging. As expected, Eya3 KD cells developed metastases at a slower rate (fig. S7C). After metastases could be detected in all mice, we euthanized the mice and analyzed lung single-cell suspensions by flow cytometry for the presence of various immune cell subsets. Similar to the Eya3-regulated alterations observed in the premetastatic niche, we detected a decrease in inflammatory monocytes (fig. S7D), interstitial macrophages (fig. S7E), and dendritic cells (fig. S7F), and an increase in cytotoxic NK cells (fig. S7G) in mice that developed metastases from Eya3 KD cells compared to SCR ( ). However, distinct from changes observed in the premetastatic niche (fig. S6D), lungs containing Eya3-KD metastases exhibited an increase in CD45+ lymphocytes (fig. S7H), CD4+ T cells (fig. S7I), CD8+ T cells (fig. S7J), and B cells (fig. S7K), and a decrease in neutrophils (fig. S7L). These data demonstrate that several Eya3-regulated effects on immune cell infiltration to the premetastatic niche are maintained in the metastatic niche, and additional immune cell remodeling occurs as cancers progress to later stages of metastasis.

## EYA3 regulates secreted factors that inhibit cytotoxic NK cell infiltration and activation potential
Our data demonstrate that cancer cells in the primary tumor can influence immune cell residence in the premetastatic lung. Given that these effects are driven by the primary tumor in the absence of detectable micrometastases in the lung, we hypothesized that EYA3 must regulate secreted factors that mediate this effect. To test this hypothesis, we incubated phenol red and serum-free media with cancer cells for 48 hours, after which we injected this conditioned media (CM) into mice daily for 7 days ( ). After 7 days, we euthanized the mice and analyzed immune cell infiltration into the lung. In the absence of a primary tumor, we did not observe significant differences in the infiltration of inflammatory monocytes, interstitial macrophages, or dendritic cells to the lungs of mice that received CM from Eya3 KD cells compared to scramble cells ( ). Overall, there were fewer inflammatory monocytes and interstitial macrophages in the lungs of tumor-free mice receiving CM compared to the lungs of mice bearing primary tumors ( , and ), suggesting that the presence of the primary tumor is required for the changes we observed in these populations in our previous experiments. However, we did observe a statistically significant increase in cytotoxic NK cells in the lungs of mice that received CM from 66cl4 Eya3 KD cells when compared to mice that received media from 66cl4 SCR cells ( ). We tested this result in a second cell line, E0771, and did not observe notable differences in inflammatory monocytes, interstitial macrophages, or dendritic cells (fig. S8, A to C), but did observe a similar trend toward increased cytotoxic NK cells in the lungs of mice that received CM from E0771 Eya3 KD cells compared to E0771 SCR cells (fig. S8D). Together, these results suggest that EYA3-regulated changes in inflammatory monocytes and macrophages require the presence of a primary tumor, while EYA3-regulated secreted factors alone influence the presence of cytotoxic NK cells in the lung, even in the absence of a primary tumor.
 While these data demonstrate a role for EYA3 in altering cytotoxic NK cell numbers in the lung, they do not determine whether EYA3 can directly regulate NK cell function. To determine whether TNBC EYA3 can directly affect NK cell function, we enriched for NK cells from mouse splenocytes and cocultured them with BC cells with SCR or Eya3 KD. First, we directly cocultured NK and cancer cells and observed an increase in NK cell cytotoxic killing of 66cl4 cells with Eya3 KD (fig. S8E). To evaluate the effects of EYA3-regulated secreted factors on NK cell function, we used a transwell system that allows for the exchange of secreted factors but does not allow for direct interaction between cancer cells and NK cells. We then stimulated the NK cell–enriched splenocytes with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) and ionomycin to activate them and measured the ability of NK cells to produce the cytotoxic cytokines TNFα and IFN-γ by flow cytometry ( ). Compared to NK cells cultured in the absence of TNBC cells, NK cells cocultured with 66cl4 SCR cells showed reduced ability to produce TNFα and IFN-γ ( ). However, when NK cells were cultured with Eya3 KD cells, we observed an increase in the percentage of activated cytotoxic NK cells producing TNFα and IFN-γ ( ). We observed similar effects on cytokine production in a second BC model, E0771, whereby a higher percentage of NK cells cocultured with E0771 Eya3 KD cells were able to produce cytotoxic cytokines upon activation ( ). These data demonstrate that secreted factors from EYA3-expressing cells reduce the ability of NK cells to produce cytotoxic cytokines upon activation, highlighting a role for EYA3 in directly regulating NK cell activation potential.

## EYA3 promotes expression of the cytokine CCL2 through activation of NF-κB signaling
Immune cell recruitment and activation are largely driven by cytokine signaling, and NF-κB signaling has a well-established role in regulating the expression of various cytokines. Thus, we hypothesized that EYA3–NF-κB–mediated cytokine expression is responsible for changes to the immune premetastatic niche. To test this hypothesis, we first reevaluated our RNA-seq data from Eya3 KD cells with or without IKK2-SE addback ( and fig. S3C) to interrogate changes in cytokine expression by using the GSEA GO Molecular Function Cytokine Activity dataset ( ). We observed that only a subset of cytokines that were changed with Eya3 KD were also rescued with IKK2-SE addback ( ). We focused on cytokines that appeared to be positively regulated by EYA3–NF-κB activity (inset panel on ) and tested the effect of Eya3 KD on the expression of these cytokines in multiple TNBC cell lines by qRT-PCR, including additional cytokines previously implicated in premetastatic niche formation in our analysis ( ) ( and fig. S9, A and B). Using two independent Eya3 shRNA constructs in three different mouse mammary carcinoma cell lines, we found that Ccl2 was most consistently and statistically significantly changed with Eya3 KD ( , and fig. S9, A and B). We also observed a reduction in secreted CCL2 protein with Eya3 KD by performing WB analysis ( ), and/or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (fig. S9, C and D) on cancer cell CM from several mouse TNBC lines. To determine whether CCR2, the primary receptor for CCL2, is expressed on cytotoxic NK cells in the PMN, we reanalyzed frozen samples from the premetastatic lungs ( ). We observed that a higher percentage of cytotoxic NK cells from the premetastatic lungs of mice injected with SCR cells expressed CCR2, with a higher mean fluorescence intensity, compared to those injected with Eya3 KD cells (fig. S9, E and F). Furthermore, these effects were maintained in the metastatic niche, where we observed an increase in the percentage of cytotoxic NK cells expressing CCR2 in SCR lung metastases compared to Eya3 KD lung metastases (fig. S9G). However, CCR2+ cytotoxic NK cells showed no difference in mean fluorescence intensity between groups in the metastatic niche (fig. S9H). These data demonstrate that in the premetastatic and metastatic niches, Eya3 KD decreases CCR2 positivity on cytotoxic NK cells, suggesting that EYA3 regulation of CCL2-CCR2 may contribute to EYA3 remodeling of the PMN.
 In our RNA-seq dataset, Ccl2 mRNA was markedly decreased with Eya3 KD and rescued with IKK2-SE addback ( ), suggesting that Ccl2 is regulated by EYA3 downstream of NF-κB activation. This finding was confirmed by examining CCL2 protein levels with IKK2-SE addback in Eya3 KD cells by ELISA and WB ( and fig. S9I). In addition, treatment of 66cl4 cells with an IKK inhibitor, IKK inhibitor VII, led to a dose-dependent decrease in Ccl2 protein (fig. S9, J and K), again demonstrating that CCL2 is downstream of EYA3–NF-κB. We confirmed that the relationship between EYA3 and CCL2 is conserved in human TNBC, as EYA3 loss led to reduced CCL2 levels in human HCC70 cells ( , and fig. S9L). These data identify CCL2 as a target of EYA3–NF-κB signaling in both mouse and human TNBC.

## Ccl2 reexpression in Eya3-deficient cells rescues NK cell activation potential in vitro and cytotoxic NK cell infiltration to the lung
To determine whether CCL2 is responsible for EYA3 effects on cytotoxic NK cells, we used a retroviral approach to stably restore Ccl2 expression to 66cl4 Eya3-KD cells. Cancer cell CM was collected from SCR control and Eya3 KD cells ± Ccl2, and reexpression of CCL2 protein was verified by WB ( ) and ELISA ( ). To test whether CCL2 is responsible for EYA3 effects on NK cell activation potential, we cocultured splenocytes enriched for NK cells with 66cl4 Eya3 KD cells with or without Ccl2 reexpression, stimulated cells with PMA and ionomycin, and tested for NK cell production of cytotoxic cytokines by flow cytometry (as outlined in ). We observed that Ccl2 reexpression reduced the percentage of cytotoxic NK cells producing TNFα, IFN-γ, or both cytokines ( ). Next, we tested the effects of CCL2 on NK cell infiltration to the lung. To this end, CM from each condition was injected into mice daily for 7 days (as outlined in ), after which mice were euthanized and immune cell infiltration into the lungs was analyzed. Consistent with previous experiments ( ), we observed an increase in cytotoxic NK cells in the lungs of mice that received CM from Eya3 KD cells when compared to SCR cells ( ), and these effects were partially rescued with Ccl2 reexpression ( ). Ccl2 overexpression in SCR cells had no effect on cytotoxic NK cell residence in the lungs. Similar to previous experiments ( ), we did not observe notable changes in interstitial macrophages, inflammatory monocytes, or dendritic cells (fig. S10, A to C). These results demonstrate that CCL2 partially contributes to the effects of cancer cell–expressed EYA3 on NK cell cytokine production in vitro and NK cell infiltration in the lungs in vivo.
 To determine whether EYA3–NF-κB–CCL2 regulation of NK cells alters metastasis, we then injected cancer cell–CM from SCR control or Eya3 KD cells into mice daily for 7 days to prime the premetastatic lung ( ). During this time, NK cells were depleted from mice by administering anti-asialo GM1 antibody [used because BALB/c mice do not express the NK1.1 antigen ( )] or an IgG control, and depletion was verified by flow cytometry performed on blood drawn from treated mice (fig. S10D). While anti-asialo GM1 antibody is known to also deplete basophils ( ), we did not observe Eya3-dependent changes in basophils in either the premetastatic lung or in the lung metastatic niche (fig. S10E), suggesting that depletion of this population may not be a major contributor to the effects we observe. 66cl4 SCR cells were then injected via tail vein into mice from all groups: (i) SCR CM + IgG, (ii) shEya3 CM + IgG, and (iii) shEya3 CM + anti-asialo GM1 ( ). In this experiment, a modest decrease in metastatic colonization was observed in mice that were primed with shEya3 CM compared to those that received SCR CM ( ). Given that macro-metastases were detectable in the lungs after only 3 days and that 66cl4 SCR cells were injected (which are capable of producing their own CCL2), it is likely that this experimental setup gives low resolution for observing differences between the conditions treated with Eya3 KD versus SCR CM. Nonetheless, depletion of NK cells led to a significant increase in lung metastatic colonization, and OS was greatly reduced ( , and fig. S10F). We speculated that because SCR CM reduces cytotoxic NK cells in the lung, NK cell depletion would have a more modest effect on lung metastasis in mice treated with SCR CM. To address this question, we repeated this experiment and added an experimental group in which we treated mice receiving SCR CM with the anti-asialo GM1 antibody. While we observed that NK cell depletion enhanced lung metastastic colonization in mice that received shEya3 CM, this effect was ablated in mice that received SCR CM ( ). These data demonstrate that NK cells play a critical role in preventing TNBC colonization in the lung, downstream of EYA3.

## EYA3 is associated with NF-κB activity and CCL2 expression in human breast tumors
To determine whether our findings in mouse models are conserved in human BC, we performed pairwise correlation analysis between the expression of EYA3 and the expression of every other sequenced gene across more than 2500 patient tumors from the METABRIC dataset of invasive breast carcinomas ( ). From this pairwise correlation analysis, we were able to generate a ranked list of genes with the strongest correlation with EYA3 expression across all patients in this dataset. Using this list, we performed GSEA to identify gene sets exhibiting a strong correlation with EYA3 in these patient tumors. In support of our results in the mouse BC models, EYA3 expression in these tumors was strongly associated with Hallmark TNFα signaling via NF-κB and Hallmark inflammatory response gene sets ( and fig. S11, A and B), as well as with MYC target gene sets, aligning with the known function of EYA family members as regulators of MYC expression and stability ( , ). In the same dataset, we observed a positive correlation between CCL2 and EYA3 mRNA levels in human BC cell patient samples ( ). For each patient sample, we calculated a score for the Hallmark TNFα signaling via NF-κB signaling pathway and also observed a positive correlation between this pathway and CCL2 levels ( ). Both EYA3 and CCL2 levels were found to be highest in the ER−/HER2− gene classifier subtype, suggesting that EYA3–NF-κB regulation of CCL2 expression may be most relevant to TNBC ( ). We additionally observed a positive correlation between EYA3 and CCL2 mRNA in a second patient dataset from the Metastatic Breast Cancer project (fig. S11C). Together, these data demonstrate that EYA3, NF-κB pathway, and CCL2 are significantly correlated in samples from patients with BC, supporting the existence of an EYA3–NF-κB–CCL2 signaling axis in human patients.
 

## DISCUSSION
Understanding the mechanisms that drive metastatic progression is critical to developing new clinical strategies for patients with metastatic TNBC. Given that embryonic programs are co-opted by cancer cells during tumor progression, we interrogated the role of the embryonic protein EYA3 in TNBC metastasis, and show that loss of EYA3 reduces the ability of TNBC cells to complete the full metastatic cascade from a primary tumor. We discover that EYA3 promotes NF-κB activation and subsequent CCL2 expression in multiple models of TNBC and that EYA3–NF-κB–CCL2 signaling suppresses cytotoxic NK cell responses in the PMN, which are critical for metastasis. Altogether, this study elucidates tumor cell–autonomous and non–cell-autonomous mechanisms by which EYA3 promotes TNBC metastasis.
EYA proteins and NF-κB both drive key processes during embryonic development ( , ), and our data demonstrate that these two developmental pathways intersect in cancer to promote TNBC metastasis. We show that reactivation of NF-κB signaling is sufficient to restore metastasis in Eya3 KD cells, and also observe an increase in migration, which is in line with previous reports that tumor-intrinsic NF-κB signaling promotes invasive phenotypes ( ). EYA3 enhances primary tumor growth by regulating an MYC–PDL1 axis ( ), but, intriguingly, we find that neither MYC signaling nor primary tumor growth is restored with NF-κB reactivation. Thus, in this study, we discover that EYA3 differentially regulates MYC and NF-κB signaling to promote different stages of tumor progression. In mouse TNBC cells, we observe that EYA3 is predominantly in the cytoplasm ( ), suggesting that EYA3 may be regulating NF-κB signaling through its phosphatase activities rather than its transcriptional activities. In support of this idea, it has been demonstrated that in response to viral stimulation, EYA4 enhances innate immune signaling via Stimulator of interferon response cGAMP interactor 1 (STING) and/or NF-κB in mouse embryonic fibroblasts through its threonine phosphatase activity ( ). EYA3 regulates MYC stability through association with the serine/threonine phosphatase PP2A-B55α ( ), and future studies should evaluate whether this same phosphatase activity drives NF-κB regulation and metastasis, or whether EYA3 regulates these two pathways through completely independent functions.
Cancer cells remodel the PMN to allow for more efficient colonization and metastatic outgrowth at secondary sites. From the primary tumor, cancer cell–secreted factors alter the immune microenvironment in the PMN ( ) by influencing the recruitment, differentiation, and activity of various immune cells including monocytes ( ), macrophages ( ), neutrophils ( , ), and NK cells ( ). While all of these cell types contribute to immunosurveillance at metastatic sites, NK cells are critical for eliminating metastatic cancer cells ( , ), and cancer cell resistance to NK cell responses enables metastatic colonization and outgrowth ( ). CCL2 regulates monocyte migration to pulmonary metastases and retention of metastasis-associated macrophages, resulting in enhanced metastasis ( , ). Thus, it is intriguing that EYA3–CCL2 in the primary tumor alters monocyte and macrophage recruitment to the premetastatic lungs, but tumor cell CM alone did not affect these populations. It has been demonstrated that myeloid regulation of tumor cell metastasis begins in the primary tumor, where Tie2high macrophages associate with NR2F1+ cancer cells and enhance their intravasation, contributing to tumor cell extravasation and retention in the lung ( ). With this in mind, it is possible that a direct interaction between cancer cells and myeloid cells, either in the primary tumor or at sites of metastasis, is important for CCL2 effects on myeloid cell infiltration to the premetastatic lung, which may explain why these effects are lost when only CM was used.
The effect of CCL2 on NK cells in the PMN remains poorly understood, and in this study, we uncover a previously unappreciated role for CCL2 in negatively regulating cytotoxic NK cells in the premetastatic lung. In the context of pregnancy, stromal-derived CCL2 inhibits NK cell cytotoxicity through activation of SOCS3 ( ). In contrast, in other settings, CCL2 has been shown to directly promote NK cell migration and cytolysis. Given that NK cells adopt different phenotypes in different organs depending on local cues and interactions, it is not unexpected that CCL2 may have disparate effects on NK cells in distinct contexts. Thus, the specific effect of CCL2 on cytotoxic NK cells in the premetastatic lung warrants further investigation.
Increasingly, NK cells have been highlighted for their potential in cancer immunotherapy, with large-scale efforts to develop therapies that boost NK cell responses or create “off-the-shelf” NK cells for adoptive cell therapy ( ). Emerging evidence also suggests that NK cells positively contribute to the response to Programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 inhibitors ( ), which are already approved by the Food and Drug Administration for use in metastatic TNBC. Understanding how tumor-intrinsic mechanisms inhibit NK cell–mediated immunosurveillance is critical to the optimization of these therapeutic strategies. Because of its myriad functions in cancer cells, NF-κB is an attractive therapeutic target. However, the ubiquity of this signaling pathway and various antitumor activities of NF-κB make systemic therapeutic approaches challenging. In the same vein, CCL2 may be difficult to target given its diverse roles in immune cell signaling. In this study, we demonstrate that EYA3 is upstream of NF-κB–CCL2, presenting EYA3 as a promising target for inhibition of this pathway. EYA3 is highly expressed in TNBC, suggesting that EYA3 inhibition could more specifically reduce NF-κB activation in TNBC cells, likely with low side-effect profiles given that knockout of Eya3 in mice does not induce any major phenotypes ( ). EYA inhibitors are already in existence ( ), supporting the notion that EYA proteins can be pharmacologically targeted. However, many of these inhibitors target EYA tyrosine phosphatase activity, and new inhibitors may need to be developed if a different EYA3 phosphatase activity [such as that mediated through its interaction with PP2A ( )] is implicated in NF-κB–CCL2 regulation.
While this study focused primarily on TNBC, in part due to the paucity of therapeutic options available for metastatic disease of this subtype, one limitation of this study is the potential generalizability of this study to other subtypes of BC and other tumor types that express EYA3, which remains unclear. The human data analysis demonstrated in suggests that the EYA3–NF-κB–CCL2 axis may exist in other subtypes of BC, but because of the higher expression of EYA3 and CCL2 in TNBC compared to other types of BC ( ), this effect may be exacerbated in this context. Additional studies will be necessary to determine whether this effect is unique to TNBC or whether this persists in other tumor types. Our study also focused primarily on the lung microenvironment and TNBC metastasis to the lungs, a primary site of metastasis for our cell models. However, whether these effects are conserved at other metastatic sites is unknown, and will be an interesting future area of investigation. Furthermore, while we did not observe any Eya3-dependent changes in basophils in either the premetastatic lung or in the lung metastatic niche, the known off-target effects of anti-asialo GM1 antibody on basophils ( ) are a limitation of using this reagent to deplete NK cells. An additional limitation is that our studies were primarily performed in cell lines and xenograft models, and not in primary mouse tumors. Analyses were done in this manner to better facilitate the numerous genetic manipulations necessary for our study, but in the future, it will be of interest to examine the EYA3–NF-κB–CCL2 axis in additional models. Similarly, the immune studies demonstrated herein were performed in murine models, which may demonstrate functional differences from human immune regulation. Clinical application of EYA inhibitors will be necessary to fully determine whether this EYA–immune signaling axis can be therapeutically leveraged in humans.
In this study, we uncover the existence of a previously unappreciated EYA3–NF-κB–CCL2 signaling axis in TNBC metastasis and demonstrate that this pathway suppresses cytotoxic NK cells in the PMN to promote metastasis. Moreover, our past findings that EYA3 promotes MYC stabilization and suppresses CD8+ T cells in the primary tumor demonstrate that EYA3 plays numerous roles in TNBC progression. Our data suggest that EYA3 inhibition not only will affect primary tumor growth but also reduce metastasis and positively affect both adaptive and innate immune responses at both sites of cancer cell growth. Together, these data suggest that targeting EYA3, particularly in combination with immunotherapies, may be a powerful means to inhibit TNBC progression.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## METABRIC survival analysis
Gene expression and survival data for the METABRIC dataset ( – ) were downloaded from cBioPortal (30 May 2024). Gene expression for EYA3 was used to stratify the overall cohort into quartiles. Individuals from the top and bottom quartiles were compared for OS using the survival (v3.5-8) and survminer (v0.4.9) R packages. OS was analyzed with patients living the past 10 years right censored; the log-rank test was used to calculate statistical significance.

## Cell lines and culture conditions
Murine TNBC cell line 66cl4 (RRID:CVCL_9721) was obtained as a gift from F. Miller ( ), and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) high-glucose media supplemented with 10% calf serum, 1% l-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Pen/Strep), and 1% nonessential amino acids. The Met1 murine mammary carcinoma cell line (RRID:CVCL_U373) was obtained as a gift from J. Greg ( ), cultured in DMEM high-glucose media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% l-glutamine, and 1% Pen/Strep. E0771 gfp-luc murine BC cells (parental RRID:CVCL_GR23) were a gift from D. Cittelly ( ) and cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep. The human TNBC cell line HCC70 (purchased from the Cell Technologies Shared Resource on campus, STR profiled in April 2010 immediately before freezing down, and obtained originally from the American Type Culture Collection; RRID:CVCL_1270) was cultured in DMEM + F12 Ham’s media supplemented with 10% FBS + 0.5% Pen/Strep. All cell lines were grown at 37°C in 5% CO2. All cell lines are checked for mycoplasma every 3 to 6 months to ensure accuracy of our findings. Cell lines were passaged at least two times after thawing before use in experiments and were rethawed periodically to maintain low passage numbers.

## Generation of KD cell lines
Eya3 was knocked down in murine cell lines using two different shRNAs (shEya3 KD1—TRCN0000029858 and shEya3 KD2—TRCN0000029855, Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO) and control scramble shRNA (Addgene plasmid 1864, Cambridge, MA). shRNAs were lentivirally introduced into cells according to the PLKO.1 manufacturing protocol (Addgene, Cambridge, MA). All cells were maintained in puromycin (2.5 μg/ml) for selection of pooled populations and maintenance of KD. Parental cells were tagged with firefly luciferase using the MSCV Luciferase PGK-hygro vector (Addgene plasmid #18782, Cambridge, MA) and transduced cells were selected as a pooled population with hygromycin (200 μg/ml). E0771 cells were previously tagged with firefly luciferase using MSCV Luciferase PGK-hygro vector (Addgene plasmid #18782) and shEya3 KD cells were generated using the above Eya3 KD shRNAs.

## Animals and in vivo studies
All animal studies were performed according to protocols reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Colorado AMC. Only female mice were used in this study because BC predominantly affects females (>99%). Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Sample size was determined based on empiric data from preliminary experiments in the laboratory to elucidate differences between groups while minimizing the number of animals per experiment.
For spontaneous metastasis studies, 200,000 cells in Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Media (Gibco A4124801) were injected into the fourth mammary fat pad of 8- to 10-week-old syngeneic BALB/cJ mice (the Jackson Laboratory RRID:IMSR_JAX:000651) or 3- to 12-week-old male NOG/SCIDγ (NSG) mice (bred in-house). For IKK2-SE addback studies, 250,000 cells were used as these additionally carry an mCherry tag. Tumors were measured with calipers at the indicated intervals and volume was calculated as [long axis (cm) × short axis2 (cm)]/2. Once a tumor surpassed a volume of 1 cm3 (unless otherwise indicated), primary tumors were surgically removed. Mice that died secondary to surgical complications were excluded from survival analyses. One week after surgery, mice were imaged weekly under anesthesia on the IVIS200 bioluminescent imaging system 10 min after receiving an intraperitoneal injection of 100 μl of 100× luciferin (Gold Biotechnology, LUCK-1G). All mice were euthanized upon clinical signs of health deterioration, confirmed by a veterinarian. Bioluminescent images were analyzed using Living Image software. All images were normalized based on photon flux, and lung luciferase intensity was determined through selection of a lung window with Living Image software to capture luciferase signal around the lung.
For tail vein injection studies, 500,000 luciferase-gfp tagged E0771 cells were injected intravenously into 8- to 10-week-old syngeneic C57BL/6J mice (the Jackson Laboratory RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664). After 3 days, mice were imaged every 3 to 4 days with the IVIS200 bioluminescent imaging system as described above. Mice were monitored for labored breathing and weight loss and began reaching humane endpoint for sacrifice on day 21; all mice were euthanized by day 23. One mouse was excluded from KD1 due to degraded lung RNA sample, and three mice were excluded from KD2 due to premature death. Lungs were collected into RNAlater RNA preservation solution (Sigma-Aldrich) and frozen at −80°C. Lung tissue was subsequently minced and mixed, and RNA and genomic DNA were isolated for analysis of Puro and Actin expression by qRT-PCR and qPCR. In qRT-PCR analysis, the Robust regression followed by outlier identification (ROUT) method (Q = 5%) identified one outlier in KD1, one outlier in KD2, and one outlier in the tumor-free lung, which were removed. The ROUT method did not identify any outliers in qPCR analysis, so no mice were excluded. For metastatic niche analysis, 250,000 luciferase-tagged 66cl4 cells were intravenously injected into 8- to 10-week-old syngeneic BALB/c mice (the Jackson Laboratory). Mice were imaged every 3 to 4 days with the IVIS200 bioluminescent imaging system as described above, and imaging confirmed that all mice included in the study eventually developed metastases in the lungs. All mice were all euthanized 16 days after tumor cell injection.
For premetastatic niche studies, 200,000 cells were injected into the fourth mammary fat pad and tumor growth was tracked by caliper measurement. Tumors were considered palpable when they exceeded 50 mm3, at which point mice were euthanized for downstream analyses. In premetastatic niche experiments, if mice did not form tumors after 35 days, they were excluded from downstream flow cytometry analyses. For media injection studies, cancer cells were plated in six-well plates, and the following day, media was changed to 2 ml per well of phenol red–free, serum-free high-glucose DMEM supplemented with l-glutamine and nonessential amino acids. After 48 hours, media was collected, passed through a 0.2-μm filter, and frozen at −80°C. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 300 μl of CM each day for 7 days, then euthanized, and lungs were collected.
For NK cell depletion studies, animals were treated with 300 μl of CM daily for 7 days before injection of 200,000 66cl4 cells into the tail vein. CM was injected daily throughout the duration of the experiment. For depletion of NK cells, 50 μg of anti-asialo GM1 antibody (Invitrogen, #16-6507-39 RRID:AB_10718540) or 50 μg of polyclonal Rabbit IgG (BioXcell, #BE0095 RRID:AB_1107793) was injected intraperitoneally 4 days before tumor cell injection, 100 μg was injected 2 days before tumor cell injection, and then 50 μg was injected twice weekly for the duration of the experiment. Mice were imaged three times per week by IVIS200 bioluminescent imaging as above. Mice that had poor tumor injection (marked by tail vein burst or complete absence of metastatic cells throughout the course of the experiment), that died immediately upon tumor injection, or that died prematurely from non–cancer-related deaths were excluded.

## RNA expression analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells at 80% confluency using the RNeasy Plus Mini RNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, 74136) according to manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, 1708891) from 1 μg of mRNA. qRT-PCR assays were performed using ssoFast Evagreen Supermix (Bio-Rad, 1725205) and analyzed using the Bio-Rad CFX96 qPCR instrument. The primer sequences used are listed in table S1.

## WB analysis
Whole cell protein lysates (WCLs) were generated by lysis in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and lysed via sonication. For WB analysis of cancer cell media, serum-free CM was mixed at a 1:1 ratio with 2× Laemmeli buffer. Cytoplasmic/nuclear fractionated protein extracts were generated via the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78835) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein quantification was determined via Lowry assay. For WCLs and cytoplasmic/nuclear protein immunoblots, 25 to 50 μg of protein was electrophoresed on 5% stacking and 10% running gels or gradient gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were incubated for 1 hour with Ponceau stain and then imaged before blocking, followed by incubation overnight in primary antibody at 4°C and then 1 hour at room temperature (RT) in secondary antibody before imaging. Blots were developed using SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34080) and/or SuperSignal West Femto Chemiluminscent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34096) and imaged on the Licor Odyssey FC. For reimaging of different proteins at similar molecular weights, blots were stripped with Restore WB Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21059) for 5 min at RT and then blocked and reprobed as described above. Primary antibodies (all diluted in 5% BSA in TBST) are listed in table S2.

## RNA-seq analysis
RNA was extracted from samples using the RNeasy RNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen) in triplicate and sent to the University of Colorado Cancer Center Genomics Core Facility for sample preparation, sequencing, and generation of raw data. Library preparation was performed using the NuGEN Universal Plus mRNA-Seq kit. Samples were sequenced with paired-end reads (40 million reads per sample) on the Illumina NovaSEQ6000 sequencing platform. The quality of the fastq files was assessed using FastQC ( ) and MultiQC ( ). Illumina adapters and low-quality reads were filtered out using BBDuk (http://jgi.doe.gov/data-and-tools/bb-tools). Reads were aligned and quantified using STAR ( ) against the mm10 murine genome. Ensembl IDs were mapped to gene names and counts of genes with multiple IDs were aggregated. Low expression genes were removed if mean raw count <1 or mean counts per million (CPM) < 1 for the dataset. Reads were normalized to CPM using the edgeR R package (Bioconductor; Boston, MA) ( ). Differential expression was calculated using the voom function in the limma R package ( ). After processing, the University of Colorado Biostatistics and Bioinformatics Shared Resource RNA-seq analysis tool was used to perform pathway analysis, including overrepresentation analysis, functional GSEA, and TF motif enrichment analysis. A fold change cutoff of 2 was used for TF motif enrichment analysis. Heatmaps were generated with the ComplexHeatmap R package following z score transformation ( ). Raw data can be found in the Gene Expression Omnibus database under accession number GSE288338.

## Generation of 66cl4 NF-κB and Ccl2 rescue cell lines
Previously generated 66cl4 SCRs were transduced with a pLVX-EF1a-IRES-mCherry EV construct and 66cl4 shEya3 KD2 cells were transduced with either the EV or a pLVX-EF1a-IRES-mCherry IKK2-SE construct (both constructs donated by P. Ernst, PhD). After transduction, cells were sorted using flow cytometry for mCherry positivity. mCherry-positive cells were used for all subsequent experiments—cells were re-sorted every 2 to 4 weeks in culture to ensure high mCherry levels. For Ccl2 expression, mouse Ccl2 was amplified from mouse cDNA and cloned into pMSCVneo (Clonetech #631461, Mountain View, CA). The pMSCVneo-Ccl2 construct and retroviral packaging plasmid pcL-Eco were cotransfected into human embryonic kidney 293T cells using FuGENE transfection reagent (Promega, Madison, WI) to generate retroviral particles. Cells were transduced with viral particles and selected with neomycin (500 μg/ml).

## Flow cytometry
After mice were humanely euthanized, the lungs were perfused with 10 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and placed immediately into 1.5 ml of immune cell media (RPMI + 10% FBS + 1% Pen/Strep +50 μM β-mercaptoethanol) on ice. Lungs were then removed from media, minced, and incubated in digestion buffer [1× Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS), collagenase IA (0.1 mg/ml), and DNase I (60 U/ml)] at 37°C for 25 min with continuous shaking. Cells were washed with medium and filtered through a 70-μm nylon filter, then frozen in complete medium with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide at −80°C. Lung single-cell suspensions were thawed and resuspended in immune cell media and 1/10 of each lung was distributed into a round-bottom 96-well plate for each flow cytometry panel. For analysis of lymphocytes in the blood, 100 to 200 μl of blood was collected by submandibular bleed into 500 μl of 2 μM EDTA-HBSS, and red blood cells were lysed with 1× ACK buffer for 5 min before subsequent staining.
Cells were incubated with Zombie Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend #423101, San Diego, CA) for 10 min, washed and then stained for 30 min with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. Cells were then fixed using a BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences #554714, Franklin Lakes, NJ) or fixed and nuclear permeabilized using the eBioscience FOXP3/TF buffer staining set (eBioscience #00-5523-00, San Diego, CA). For analysis of T regulatory cells, cells were then incubated with an antibody specific for Foxp3 after permeabilization. For analysis of cytokine production, cells were incubated with antibodies specific for IFN-γ and TNFα after permeabilization. Stained cells were analyzed on a Bio-Rad ZE5 Cell Analyzer in the University of Colorado Cancer Center Flow Cytometry Shared Resource using Flowjo software (TreeStar, Ashland, OR). Antibodies used for these analyses are listed in table S3.

## NK cell coculture assay
Spleens from C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice were passed through a 70-μm nylon filter to make a single-cell suspension. The EasySep Mouse NK Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies #19855, Vancouver, Canada) was used to isolate mouse NK cells from spleen single-cell suspensions according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and NK cell enrichment was verified by flow cytometry staining. Cancer cells were plated into a 12-well plate, and the following day, the media was changed to serum-free cancer cell media. NK cells were then seeded in immune cell media [supplemented with fresh mouse IL-15 (10 ng/ml)] onto a 12-mm Transwell with 0.4 μm of Pore Polyester Membrane insert placed on top of the cancer cells. Cells were coincubated for 48 hours, and in the last 4 hours, PMA (50 ng/ml), ionomycin (1 μg/ml), and 1× Brefeldin A Golgi plugs (3 μg/ml) were added to NK cells. NK cells were then transferred to a round-bottom 96-well plate and stained for flow cytometry.

## NK cell cytotoxicity assay
The EasySep Mouse NK Cell Isolation Kit was used to isolate mouse NK cells from spleen single-cell suspensions according to the manufacturer’s instructions, as above. NK cells were immediately seeded at various effector:target ratios with cancer cells in a 96-well round-bottom plate in media supplemented with mouse IL-15 (10 ng/ml). After 24 hours of direct coculture, a cytotoxicity assay was performed using CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay from Promega. Cytotoxic killing was calculated using the formula % specific killing = (experimental cell death − spontaneous cell death)/(maximum cell death − spontaneous cell death) × 100.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays
Cancer cells were incubated with serum-free media for 48 hours. Media was collected and centrifuged to remove any floating cells. ELISA was performed on CM using Mouse or Human MCP1 ELISA kit (Abcam #ab100721 and #ab100586, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Correlation analyses from patient datasets
For pairwise-correlation GSEA, we accessed patient RNA expression data from the METABRIC patient cohort ( , ) via access through cBioPortal ( , ) as z scores of gene expression and performed a pairwise Pearson correlation between EYA3 and all other genes in the dataset using R (4.0.1) and R Studio (v.1.2.5033) and subsequently ranked genes based on their correlation with EYA3 expression. We used the ranked list as input for preranked GSEA (v4.0.3) using the Hallmarks Molecular Signature Database (h.all.v7.1) and identified pathways enriched with genes correlated with EYA3 expression in patient breast tumors. We plotted the significantly up- and down-regulated pathways using the ggplot2 package.
For EYA3 mRNA correlations, gene expression data for the METABRIC dataset ( – ) were downloaded from cBioPortal (30 May 2024). Using the GSVA (v1.48.3) R package, ssGSEA was used to calculate the NF-κB score using the Hallmarks gene set collection (v2023.2), HALLMARK_TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB ( , ). Patients were stratified by the 3-Gene classifier subtype ( , ) on cBioPortal. Data were plotted in GraphPad prism, and Spearman correlation was calculated for each correlation. EYA3 and CCL2 mRNA data from The Metastatic Breast Cancer Project were analyzed on cBioPortal.

## Vectra staining
We injected 66cl4 cells with or without Eya3 KD into the mammary fat pad of syngeneic mice and allowed palpable tumors (>~50 mm3) to form. Once tumors were palpable, mice were euthanized and tumors were fixed in formalin. SCR and shEYA3 KD1 tumor slices were stained with an NK-focused antibody panel by the Human Immune Monitoring Shared Resource at CU/AMC, imaged on the Akoya Biosciences’ Vectra Polaris, and scored for various cell populations using inForm image analysis software.

## Proteomics sample preparation, TMT labeling, and phosphoenrichment
Following procedures previously described in ( ), cells were washed with PBS and immediately harvested with 5% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate, 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethylphosphine), 40 mM 2-chloroacetamide, and 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.5, and boiled for 10 min. Cell lysates were probe sonicated and stored at −80°C. Extracted proteins were digested using the SP3 method ( ). Briefly, 500 μg of carboxylate-functionalized speedbeads (Cytiva Life Sciences) was added followed by the addition of acetonitrile (ACN) to 80% (v/v) inducing binding to the beads. The beads were washed twice with 80% (v/v) ethanol and twice with 100% ACN. Proteins were digested in 50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.5, with Lys-C/Trypsin (Promega) incubating at 37°C shaking at 1000 rpm overnight. Tryptic peptides were desalted using Waters HLB Oasis cartridge according to the manufacturer’s instructions and dried in a speedvac vacuum centrifuge and stored at −20°C. Peptides were labeled with the Tandem Mass Tags (TMT) 10-plex (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, peptides were suspended in 0.1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate and each TMT label was added in ACN and incubated for 1 hour at ambient temperature. TMT Labeling reactions were quenched with the addition of hydroxylamine and incubated for 15 min at ambient temperature then combined. The multiplexed samples were desalted using a Waters HLB Oasis cartridge according to the manufacturer’s instructions, dried in a speedvac vacuum centrifuge, and stored at −20°C. A small portion of the multiplexed samples was set aside for proteome analyses and the remainder was phosphoenriched using the High-Select TiO2 phosphoenrichment kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were suspended in the Binding/Equilibration buffer, loaded onto the TiO2 column, washed, and eluted. The elution and the unretained flow through fractions were dried immediately in a speedvac vacuum centrifuge. The TiO2 elution was stored at −20°C, while the dried unretained flow through fraction was subjected to a sequential phosphoenrichment with the High-Select Fe-NTA phosphoenrichment kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The dried TiO2 unretained fraction was suspended in the Fe-NTA Binding/Wash Buffer, loaded onto the Fe-NTA column, washed, and eluted. The TiO2 and the Fe-NTA elutions were combined and dried in a speedvac vacuum centrifuge. The proteome and phosphoenriched fractions were fractionated on an rpC18 column with high pH mobile phases (0.1% ammonium hydroxide) using a Waters M-class UPLC with a photo diode array (PDA) detector and a custom fabricated 0.5 mm by 150 mm UChrom rpC18 1.8-μm, 120-Å (nanolcms) column with a gradient from 2 to 40% ACN in 80 min for the proteome and from 2 to 25% ACN in 50 min for the phosphoproteome. Fractions were concatenated for a total of 24 proteome fractions and 12 phosphoproteome fractions. High pH fractions were immediately dried in a speedvac vacuum centrifuge and stored at −20°C until liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.

## Mass spectrometry and data analysis
Following procedures previously described in ( ), high-pH fractionated TMT-labeled peptides were suspended in 3% (v/v) ACN and 0.1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid and directly injected onto a reversed-phase CSH rpC18 1.7 μm, 130 Å, 75 mm by 250 mm M-class column (Waters), using an Ultimate 3000 nanoUPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the proteome analyses, peptides were eluted at 300 nl/min with a gradient from 2% to 20% ACN, 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in 40 min, and then to 40% ACN in 5 min and detected using a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the phosphoproteome analyses, peptides were eluted at 300 nl/min with a gradient from 2 to 20% ACN, 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in 120 min, and then to 40% ACN in 5 min and detected using a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Precursor mass spectra (MS1) were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 from 380 to 1580 mass/charge ratio (m/z) with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 x 106 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. Precursor peptide ion isolation width for MS2 fragment scans was 0.7 m/z with a 0.2 m/z isolation offset, and the top 12 most intense ions were sequenced. All MS2 spectra were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 with higher energy collision dissociation at 30% normalized collision energy. An AGC target of 1 x 105 and 100 ms maximum injection time were used. Dynamic exclusion was set for 25 s with a mass tolerance of ±10 parts per million. Raw files were searched against the UniProt Mus musculus database UP000000589 downloaded 13 November 2020 using MaxQuant v1.6.14.0. Cysteine carbamidomethylation and the TMT label were considered fixed modifications, while methionine oxidation, protein N-terminal acetylation, and phosphorylation at serine, threonine, or tyrosine were searched as variable modifications. All peptide and protein identifications were thresholded at a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). Proteome and phosphoproteome data were normalized using the cyclic loess method, then differential protein and phospho-occupancy were calculated using limma (Bioconductor.org) to generate a linear model for fold-change estimation and standard error then an Empirical Bayes implementation of an independent t test where the null distribution is inferred from the global data, and treatment distribution is Bayesian updated. FDRs (q values) were then generated using the Benjamini-Hochberg limma interpretation.

## NF-κB luciferase reporter assays
NF-κB 3kB Firefly luciferase expression and a 3kB mutant plasmid were obtained as a gift from R. Schweppe (originally obtained from Addgene 26699). For all lines tested (Met1 SCR, shEya3 KD1, and shEya2 KD2; Met1 SCR, shB55a KD1, and shB55a KD2), 20,000 cells per well were plated in 96-well plates and transfected 1 μg per well of either 3kB reporter (five wells per group) or 3kB mutant (3 wells per group) and 500 ng per well of Renilla control construct into indicated wells with Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, 11-668-019). After transfection, the plate was incubated overnight at 37°C. After 24 hours of incubation, cells were stimulated with 100 ng/ml of TNFα for 4 hours in an incubator followed by cell lysis using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega E1910) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The luciferase intensity per well and the relative intensity of Firefly:Renilla luciferase was measured and calculated using the Turner Biosystems Modulus microplate reader. Statistical analysis was performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.

## IncuCyte cell growth assays
Cell growth was measured using IncuCyte Zoom (Essen Biosciences) Live-Cell Analysis Platform. A total of 5000 66cl4 SCR + EV, 66cl4 shEya3 KD2 + EV, and 66cl4 shEya3 KD2 + IKK2-SE cells were plated in five replicates in a 96-well plate and were imaged every 2 hours with a 4× objective. The percent confluence of each well at each time point was calculated using IncuCyte Zoom image processing software. All five replicates per group were averaged to provide a single average confluence per time point, and each group was normalized to a consistent confluence percentage (as defined as being within 1% point) set to time point 0 hours for all groups. Cell growth curves were generated by graphing average confluence and SD across technical replicates for each group for each time point. Statistical differences between groups were calculated using a longitudinal mixed-effects model that compares groups over repeated measures.

## Cell migration assays
For transwell cell migration assays, 66cl4 SCR + EV, shEya3 KD2 + EV, and shEya3 KD2 + IKK2-SE cells were resuspended in 200 μl per insert of serum-free media and transferred into cell culture inserts with 8-μm pores (BD Falcon, 353097) (200,000 cells per triplicate inserts per group). Cell culture inserts were placed in 24-well companion dishes (Corning, 353504) containing 800 μl of full serum media per well and put in the incubator at 37°C (5% CO2) for 24 hours. After incubation, media inside the insert was aspirated, and the bottom of each insert was fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min at RT. After fixation, the bottom of each insert was stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 45 min at RT and then washed with ddH2O to remove excess stain. Inserts were dried at RT for 24 hours before imaging on an Olympus CKX41 microscope. Three representative images of each insert were taken at 10× magnification (imager was blinded to the treatment group of each insert). Images were analyzed and quantified using ImageJ.

## Statistical analysis
Prism software (v9.0; GraphPad) was used for all statistical analyses with the exception of longitudinal mixed-model analyses described below. In all in vitro experiments, the conditions were run with at least three replicates and repeated at least three times. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used when only a pair of conditions was compared. One-way or two-way ANOVA parametric test followed by Bonferroni or Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons between control and KD conditions. Experiments demonstrating changes over time (cell growth, tumor growth, luciferase intensity curves, and metastasis assays) were compared using a longitudinal mixed-effects model that compares groups over repeated measures. All survival curves were compared using log-rank (Mantel-Cox) analysis.
