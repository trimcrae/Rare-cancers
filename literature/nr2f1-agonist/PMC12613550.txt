## Introduction
Notwithstanding the impactful developments in innovative cancer therapies, ranging from targeted therapy to the latest immunotherapies and precision medicine approaches, the relapse of primary tumors and the emergence of metastases continue to be leading causes of cancer mortality. This underscores the exigent need for a more profound understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving disease progression, as well as the development of more effective therapeutic strategies for its prevention and treatment [ , ]. In some cases, it has been observed that metastatic disease arises in patients after a protracted period following treatment and surgical removal of primary tumor [ ]. Compelling evidence in solid neoplasms, multiple myeloma and leukemia suggests that the delayed onset of metastasis may be explained by the presence of a population of cells arrested at the G0/G1 phase of the cell cycle, commonly referred to as dormant cancer cells (DCCs), although a unique and standardized terminology for these cells is currently lacking [ , ]. After detaching from the primary tumor, these cells disseminate through the blood or lymphatic vessels and eventually colonize peripheral tissues. Once established, they can persist in a non-proliferative state for prolonged periods, evading immune detection and remaining clinically silent, sometimes for many years [ – ]. The maintenance of dormancy is sustained by complex interactions with the local and distant microenvironment, including stromal, immune, and endothelial cells, as well as soluble factors such as cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors [ , ]. DCCs also evade detection by standard imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and positron emission tomography (PET), which are routinely employed for disease monitoring [ ]. This limitation is primarily due to two factors: firstly, DCCs often rely on oxidative metabolism and exhibit a markedly reduced dependence on glycolysis and exogenous glucose uptake, making them poorly visible to imaging techniques that are based on metabolic uptake [ ]; secondarily, these cells are usually present in extremely low number within distant tissues, often as solitary cells or small clusters ranging from 2 to 25 cells, which fall below the detection threshold of conventional imaging systems [ ] (Fig. A). DCCs population remains undetectable and resistant to therapy, contributing to the establishment of minimal residual disease (MRD) [ ]. The clinical invisibility of MRD significantly complicates patient management, as it prevents early therapeutic intervention and often leads to the detection of metastases, raised by ‘awakened’ DCCs, only at an advanced stage [ ]. In this regard, the ability to detect DCCs is paramount, given that the dormancy phase could constitute a unique and suitable therapeutic window during which targeted strategies could be employed to eradicate these residual malignant cells, potentially preventing disease recurrence (Fig. B). In this review, we discuss a comprehensive and updated overview of our current understanding of DCCs, delving into the influence of the tumor microenvironment (TME), the mechanisms of immune evasion and the emerging therapeutic strategies specifically targeting DCCs to prevent relapse.

 

## Similarities and differences between dormant, senescent, persistent and polyploid cells. Are they faces of the same medal?
Cellular quiescence is an evolutionarily conserved survival strategy observed across a wide range of organisms. It is defined as a reversible state of cell cycle arrest, in which cells exit the cycle from G1 and enter G0, often in relation to physiological processes. In mammals, quiescence is essential for maintaining the functionality of embryonic and adult stem cells, as well as memory T cells, which enter a non-proliferative state to preserve their regenerative potential and protect themselves from hostile or inflammatory environments [ , ]. Particularly in pathological contexts, quiescence often overlaps with multiple cellular phenotypes, such as “dormant”, “senescent-like”, “cancer stem cell-like”, “slow-cycling”, “persistent” or “drug-tolerant” [ – ]. Exploring the similarities and differences among these terms can improve our understanding of dormancy and help identify the unique features of these cells, which could be exploited to prevent metastatic outgrowth following periods of proliferative inactivity (Table ).

## Dormancy
DCCs refer to a subgroup of cancer cells that assume an adaptive slow-cycling strategy, enabling them to remain latent, evade immune detection and resist therapeutic interventions [ , ]. This survival strategy is also exploited by pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis and HIV, which enter a dormant state within host cells to ensure their persistence and escape immune defenses. Extending this concept to cancer, tumor-related dormancy was firstly proposed in 1934 by pathologist Rupert Willis, who described the late onset of metastases in organs of transplanted patients without evidence of a primary tumor. This early description suggested that undetected malignant cells could persist in a dormant state in donors for prolonged periods, providing a foundational framework for understanding how cancer cells may remain latent before reactivating to drive relapse and metastatic progression [ ]. Twenty years later, Geoffrey Hadfield in 1954 hypothesized that the timespan between the removal of the tumor mass from the primary site and the occurrence of malignant growth in secondary sites, could be caused by cells exiting a “temporary mitotic arrest” state and reactivating proliferation [ ]. Particularly, Aguirre-Ghiso et al., described two types of dormancy, one referred to individual cancer cells entering a reversible, non-dividing state, and another related to tumor mass dormancy in which a whole tumor remains stable in size due to a balance between cell proliferation and cell death [ ]. Dormancy in the tumor context has been primarily associated with a group of cancer cells that remain undetectable for a prolonged time, years or decades, before re-entering the cell cycle, displaying a proliferative and metastatic state. DCCs were described as malignant cells able to maintain their multipotent capacity [ ]. Recently, the transcriptomic profile of DCCs has been characterized in human myeloma, revealing differentially expressed genes between DCCs and those within the primary tumor mass [ – ]. Dormancy is often linked to the behavior of cancer stem cells (CSCs), which exhibit extremely slow, or sometimes undetectable, proliferation rates, together with the ability to escape therapies and support tumor relapse [ – ]. Notably, the detection of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in the blood of cancer patients, years after clinical remission, supports the existence of a prolonged dormant state in the bloodstream, highlighting a potential mechanism for late relapse [ ]. As currently understood, DCCs are characterized by cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase, also referred to as the quiescent state [ ]. Dormancy can also be induced by factors such as the extracellular matrix (ECM), hypoxic conditions, immune responses, or therapy-induced stress [ , ]. Dormancy and quiescence have often been used interchangeably, both represent non-proliferative conditions that tumor cells adopt to survive under stress, enabling them to evade therapy and persist until favorable conditions for reactivation arise [ , ]. In a quiescent condition, cancer cells are not actively dividing but remain metabolically active and capable of re-entering the cell cycle under appropriate stimuli [ , ]. Proliferation arrest is often regulated by both intrinsic factors, such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKi) and extrinsic signals from the TME [ , ], including immune and stromal cells, soluble factors and ECM components, actively shape DCCs’ fate by providing signals that can induce, sustain or reawaken the cells [ ]. In particular, Mukherjee et al., demonstrated that the TME plays a critical role in regulating DCCs, specific ECM proteins such as laminins, collagen type XVII alpha 1 chain (COL17A1), heparan sulfate (HS) together with factors like fibroblast growth factor (FGF), transforming growth factor β 2 (TGFβ2) promote growth arrest by inducing key dormancy regulators, including p27 and the nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F member 1 (NR2F1) [ , ]. Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that COL17A1 upregulation in dormant colorectal CSCs, characterized by elevated p27 and leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 5 (Lgr5) expression, sustains chemoresistance and preserves their dormant state [ , ]. In this context, Sreekumar et al., showed that DCCs upregulate the enzyme Beta-1,3-Galactosyltransferase 6 (B3GALT6), which induces the synthesis of HS, leading to the activation of the FGF pathway [ ]. In addition, alterations in the microenvironmental architecture, driven by processes such as tissue aging, inflammation-driven fibrosis and the release of neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) and granules content elastase, cathepsin G and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) can modify the properties of metastatic tissue, ultimately leading to the awakening of dormant cells and enhancement of the proliferation [ , – ]. CDKi have been strictly correlated to dormancy/quiescence establishment, with increased levels of proteins like p27, p57 and p21. Balancing the expression of these molecules results in a reversible in-and-out transition in which alternatively CDK activation or inhibition prevails. In the phosphorylation of CDK4/6 complex, and consequently of the quiescent-proliferative cellular fate, the retinoblastoma protein (Rb) is implicated. In the absence of CDKi, cyclin D binds to CDK4/6 complex and cyclin E1 to CDK2 substrate, phosphorylating and thus inactivating Rb, which in turn is unable to bind early 2 factor (E2F) transcription factor, allowing the quiescence-exit state from G0 to G1 and expression of cell cycle-related genes. Conversely, increased levels of CDKi can activate Rb that binds to E2F, thereby preventing cell-cycle gene expression, thus favoring G1-G0 transition and quiescence entry [ ]. CDK-Rb-E2F cascade represents a reversible cellular switch able to address cells towards a static or proliferative path.
Quiescence appears to partially overlap with senescence, rather than being a completely distinct cellular state. While short-term quiescence is typically a reversible condition in which cells can readily re-enter the proliferative cycle upon receiving appropriate signals, prolonged quiescence tends to lead to a state that is more difficult to reverse [ ]. Over time, cells that remain in a quiescent state for extended periods are more likely to progress toward senescence [ ]. This transition, is not purely passive but seems to be actively regulated and depends on the phase of cell-cycle arrest, in particular, senescent-like cells arresting in G1 and quiescent cells in G0 [ , ]. The microenvironment influences DCC transition through the secretion of factors such as bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), interferon γ (IFNγ), and perforin. In combination with the prolonged activation of stress signaling, including the p38 pathway and sustained p21 expression, these signals further consolidate the transition of quiescent cells into the irreversible growth arrest characteristic of senescence [ ]. In this context, it has been observed that increased expression of p16 and p21, along with elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), slows the activation of the E2F–CDK axis, which is essential for re-entry into the cell cycle, effectively switching the balance toward senescence [ ]. In addition, impaired lysosomal activity can amplify this process by promoting the accumulation of ROS, known as senescence inducers [ – ].

## Senescence
Senescence is traditionally defined as an irreversible state of growth arrest. Nonetheless, accumulating evidence indicates that this state can, under certain conditions, be reversed. This phenomenon has been observed in particular conditions and cell types, suggesting that senescence reversal is a context-dependent process [ ].
Senescence in normal cells consists of a prolonged growth arrest state, in which the cells are metabolically active, but incapable of dividing. These cells are commonly characterized by telomere shortening and DNA damage, resulting in impaired cell division [ – ]. The phenotypic change of senescent cells lies in epigenetic and transcriptomic alterations in which key regulatory markers are modulated. The most significant ones are pathways related to DNA damage response (DDR) and cell-cycle arrest mediated by CDKs. Double-strand breaks (DSBs) are able to activate the DDR machinery for DNA repair, however, when repair fails, they can trigger senescence [ ]. Prolonged repair signaling supports p53 phosphorylation at serine residues, enhancing p53-related gene expression [ ]. Both phosphorylation of p53 and H2AX nuclear foci expansion could be considered as inductors of senescence. In addition, stressor events trigger the activation of p53, which in turn supports the expression of p21, acting as a senescence regulator [ ]. More specifically, the activation of p21 inhibits the activity of D1-CDK4/6 and E1-CDK2 cyclin complexes, which regulate the G1/S phase transition by hyperphosphorylating Rb. Thus, p21 prevents Rb hyperphosphorylation allowing the formation of the Rb-E2F complex, thereby blocking cell-cycle progression [ , ]. Recently, another protein has been associated with cell-cycle arrest and senescence such as p16, which has been shown to act similarly to p21 in inhibiting CDK, thereby maintaining Rb in a hypophosphorylated state. However, further paths need to be explored in the elucidation of its mechanism of action and the stimuli that promote its activation [ , ]. One paramount feature that characterizes senescent behaviour is the interaction with the microenvironment mediated by the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [ , ]. It consists of a plethora of secreted molecules like chemokines, growth factors, cytokines, extracellular vesicles and ECM remodelers that can be grouped as the secretome, which is essential to communicate with the neighboring cells depending on the physio-pathological context [ ]. Profiling a molecular and proteomic signature able to easily detect a senescent state is essential to select new biomarkers. To this end, recently Basisty et al., developed a database called ‘SASP Atlas’, which catalogs the proteomic components of SASP [ ]. However, some limitations emerge when trying to delineate a clearer senescent cellular profile, as up to date, an extended evaluation of the characteristic cell-types, stimuli-derived effects and molecular mechanisms lacks. Although senescence was initially considered a defense mechanism against tumors, by stopping the cell cycle through proteins like p53, Rb, p16 and p21 in response to stress or DNA damage, recent evidence highlights a potential pro-tumoral role of the SASP [ ]. A key regulator associated with senescent cellular fate is the lysosome. Extended studies have displayed how senescent cells possess a remarkable lysosomal compartment [ ] whose contents can be secreted into the extracellular space and are therefore considered part of the SASP. These lysosome-derived factors represent potential novel markers of cellular senescence [ ]. One significantly increased component in these cells is the Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase (SA-β-Gal), an enzyme recently investigated as a possible marker to detect and study the pathways implicated in the entrance into a stabilized senescent condition [ ]. Its increased expression has been observed both in vitro models and in aged human and animal specimens [ – ]. Notwithstanding, the exact mechanisms regulating the overexpression of this molecule in a senescent-dependent context are still unknown [ ]. Different studies highlight how increased SA-β-Gal activity may be detected in non-senescent cells, rendering its exclusive evaluation in senescence-related context challenging [ , ]. Future understandings of senescence and SASP involvement in tumorigenesis and cancer progression could open new scenarios in targeting molecular pathways and selecting potential biomarkers useful to address tumor recurrence and cancer growth. Conclusively, based on what has been audited until now, there are connections linking senescence and dormancy, such as the idea that senescent cells can awaken after a prolonged inactivity, depending on the insult received. Cells that undergo senescence are less efficiently detected and eliminated through therapeutic regimens, as many treatments target active proliferating cells, leaving arrested cells undisturbed and able to silently persist. The resistance to therapy-induced apoptosis is essential for these cells to remain inactive but alive for a consistent period, while enabling them to circulate and colonize secondary sites for metastasis establishment.

## Persistence
The term “persistent” cells or “persisters” was originally used to indicate a small population of bacteria resistant to antibiotic treatment. More in detail, Bigger et al. in 1944 discovered that even with high-penicillin doses those microorganisms could not be eradicated [ ]. In the last decades, the definition of persistent cells has been translated into cellular settings, describing a subpopulation of cells that could survive under anti-proliferative compounds. Drug tolerant persisters (DTPs) consist of a subgroup of tumoral cells that display a tolerant profile after exposure to anti-tumor therapy, thereby creating a small niche of cells able to survive and retain a resistant behaviour [ , ]. Given the intrinsic capacity of tumors to be heterogeneous and differently responsive to treatment, it has been hypothesized that DTPs may present a non-uniform phenotype, rendering their characterization challenging [ ]. The ongoing debate regarding whether DTPs and dormant cells can be comparable relies on their proliferative differences and cellular-inactivity similarities. Differently from dormant cells, which enter G0-G1 cell-cycle to maintain their silent-killing potential, DTPs consist of a slow-proliferating subset of malignant cells that can show different proliferation rates depending on the cancer type [ ]. DTPs have been recently characterized by the expression of markers that help identify these subpopulations, which survive treatment and contribute to drug resistance and relapse [ ]. The most recent include ABCB5, CD271 and KDM5B [ , ]. The ABCB5 gene encodes a protein belonging to the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family, which facilitates the efflux of chemotherapeutic agents, thereby contributing to drug resistance in malignancies such as colon, lung and skin cancers. In human colon cancer cells, ABCB5 has been implicated in conferring resistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), a process that is positively regulated by the oncogene MYC. Experimental silencing of ABCB5 in colon cells resulted in reduced survival following exposure to 5-FU. Furthermore, 5-FU-resistant colon cells exhibited significantly elevated ABCB5 expression, correlating with enhanced survival rates upon treatment. These findings propose ABCB5 as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming 5-FU resistance in colon cancer [ ].
The cell surface protein CD271, also referred to as the low-affinity nerve growth factor receptor (NGFR), is present in DTPs subpopulation within melanoma, esophageal, and hypopharyngeal carcinomas [ , ]. This subpopulation is characterized by stem cell-like properties, which suggest a role in cancer cell survival and resistance. CD271 functions as a neurotrophin receptor playing key roles in cell survival, differentiation, and migration [ ]. Elevated CD271 expression is associated with poorer clinical outcomes in oral squamous cell carcinoma, hypopharyngeal cancer, and esophageal cancer, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target [ , ].
KDM5B is a histone H3K4 demethylase that plays a crucial role in regulating gene transcription. It is implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple cancers, including melanoma, endometrial carcinoma, lung, breast and pancreatic [ ]. While not essential for tumor initiation, KDM5B is critical for sustained tumor growth and survival of DTPs. Targeting KDM5B demethylase activity with inhibitors like CPI-455 shows promise in eliminating these aggressive subpopulations, although several challenges in toxicity management and efficacious targeting need to be overcome [ ]. Future research aims to develop more specific and safe therapies against the KDM5B-expressing subpopulation in cancers.
To date, the mechanisms underlying DTPs survival to anti-cancer drugs are not entirely understood. Studies have investigated how transcriptional, epigenetic, and therapy-related mechanisms can foster both intrinsic and acquired resistance of DTPs, and how their interaction with the surrounding microenvironment further promotes their persistence by reshaping their metabolism accordingly [ , ]. Future investigations are warranted to better characterize persistent cells and provide additional support to the established knowledge, from the perspective of developing new therapies aimed at targeting these malignant cells and counteracting cancer recurrence.

## Polyploidy
Endoreplication is a phenomenon describing the replication of genomic DNA within cells without the separation of chromosomes during mitosis. In particular, it creates polyploid cells which retain multiple copies of each chromosome, upon unsuccessful separation of cell cytoplasm (also known as cytokinesis), partial nuclear membrane rupture or incomplete chromosome condensation [ ]. Endoreplication thereby results in the establishment of polyploid cells, also known as polyploid giant cells, that recently have been associated with tumoral setting [ ]. Recent evidence has shown how polyploid giant cancer cells (PGCCs) are more resistant to therapy than the non-polyploid sub-population, even though the mechanisms underlying this resistance are still unclear. Evidence indicates that dormant PGCCs employ endoreduplication and polyploidisation as adaptive survival strategies under harsh environmental conditions [ ]. Presumably, PGCCs under therapy-induced stimuli enter a reversible senescent state, thus avoiding being targeted by cytotoxic drugs and later re-enter the proliferative cycle [ ]. Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that PGCCs display a stem-like profile and are able to asymmetrically divide to generate a fraction of normal diploid cells, which will colonize secondary sites to form metastasis, and a small portion of polyploid cells to maintain this altered chromosome structure [ ]. Aurora kinase A/B (AURKA/B) dysfunction in the regulation of cytokinesis explains the functional divergence between normal mitosis and endoreplication in PGCCs [ , ]. Moreover, checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) and 2 (CHK2) are crucial checkpoint kinases that mediate the DNA damage response and regulate cell cycle progression in cancer cells, including polyploid variants. CHK1 is particularly vital for enabling cancer cells to withstand replication stress and DNA damage by orchestrating cell cycle arrest and promoting DNA repair mechanisms. Its activity is frequently elevated in highly proliferative and polyploid cancer cells. Inhibiting CHK1 can trigger apoptosis and induce polyploidization, highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target to eliminate polyploid cancer cells [ , ].
Donovan et al., discovered a population of polyploid tumoral cells in patients affected by glioblastoma resistant to chemotherapy and able to sustain tumor mass growth in vivo [ ]. They showed that this tumor subset is consistently enriched with quiescent cells compared to the bulk constituted by diploid cells. The collected data underscore how cells with increased chromosome quantity are more prone to undergo irregular cycling, thus acquiring cytotoxic-treatment resistance towards chemotherapeutic agents that target active proliferating cells [ ]. PGCCs undergo a dormant state upon stress-induced responses triggered by microenvironmental, hypoxia, chemo- and radio-therapy stimuli [ , ]. This leads to reduced proliferation and halt of cell-cycle, thereby rendering this subset of cells undetectable for proliferating cells targeted drugs, resulting in cancer recurrence by repopulation and expansion of tumoral niches. Studies of PGCCs and their characterization are only partially deepened, and further investigations are necessary. However, some remarkable features comparable between PGCCs and dormant cells are the regulation of cell-cycle, shaping of microenvironment-related signals, and adaptation to insults while maintaining a stall of molecular and cellular mechanisms [ , ]. Recent evidence showed that endocycling endows PGCCs to achieve a dormant state, by exploiting the same cellular pathways characteristic of dormant cells [ ]. Cell-cycle related molecules are in charge of balancing the dormant or active state of PGCCs, and as crucial regulators, they are able to allow their transition into both states, favoring resistance and survival according to the specific condition. Ultimately, PGCCs are considered dormant cells as they are easily adapting to surrounding conditions, thus existing in both dormant and arrested states, and upon the proper stimulation, existing the sub-proliferative dormant state and accessing a reactive and invasive state [ ]. Elucidating the differences and similarities among the several definitions used to characterize and describe dormancy is essential to trace a clearer profile when mentioning these terms, thus avoiding confusion or overlapping while providing an exhaustive overview.
In summary, cellular dormancy is characterized by a range of phenotypic features that allow cancer cells to adapt to multiple insults. Recognizing these differences is crucial, as each state contributes in a unique way to therapy resistance and long-term disease recurrence. A better understanding of cellular pathways and interactions with the tumor microenvironment will be essential to develop targeted approaches aimed at preventing metastatic progression and improving patient prognosis.

 

## Key regulators of cancer cell dormancy fate
The mechanisms and specific markers that govern the fate of DCCs remain largely unclear. Nevertheless, it is well established that these cells are characterized by reduced expression of proliferation markers such as proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and Ki67. This is maintained through tightly regulated cyclin-CDK activity, which modulates cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase [ , ]. As already mentioned, the activity of these CDKs is strongly modulated by key cell cycle inhibitors, like p27 and p21. As crucial mediators of the cell cycle G1-checkpoint, both p27 and p21 bind to cyclin-CDK complexes, effectively hindering their catalytic function and thereby promoting the essential cell cycle arrest that defines the dormant state [ , ]. In DCCs, the p21 gene promoter is epigenetically silenced through the combined action of COUP-TF-interacting protein 2 (CTIP2) and SUV39H1. Particularly, SUV39H1 is a crucial histone methyltransferase enzyme that plays a significant role in epigenetic gene silencing. Its primary function is to catalyze the trimethylation of lysine 9 on histone H3 (H3K9me3) at the p21 promoter, keeping it repressed. When SUV39H1 is inhibited, H3K9me3 levels decrease at this site, allowing p21 expression and cell cycle arrest [ ]. Expression of a dormancy-associated gene signature, including the transcription factors retinoic acid receptor β (RARβ) and SOX9, is induced by NR2F1 up-regulation [ , ]. Specifically, it has been demonstrated that in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), NR2F1, upon activation by p38, promotes dormancy through global chromatin repression, CDKi, RARβ, and SOX9 [ , ]. In addition to the dormancy-associated gene signature, cellular dormancy is controlled by several well-defined pathways involving the TGF-β family, the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) and downstream effectors p38α/β, as well as extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 mitogen-activated protein kinase (ERK1/2 MAPK), integrins, p21 and p27 [ , ]. Interestingly, among these factors, uPAR plays a central role as a key modulator in multiple cell lines, elevated uPAR expression and its activation and interaction with α5β1-integrin promoted the balance of high ERK to p38 ratio [ – ]. Recent findings underscore the critical involvement of TGF-β family signaling pathways in regulating cellular dormancy [ ]. Brown et al., have demonstrated that TGF-β regulates 1,130 signature genes, which in turn govern the quiescence in squamous cell carcinoma [ ]. For DCCs, the impact of TGF-β receptor signaling is highly specific to the specific cancer type cellular environment, acting as a pivotal factor in dictating dormancy fate [ – ]. The TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway plays a direct role in regulating quiescence by influencing the transcription of genes involved in the cell cycle, leading to a reversible arrest in the G1 phase that is fundamental for sustaining cellular dormancy [ ]. Beyond the influence of TGF-β signaling, the critical balance that governs cellular dormancy and the ultimate fate of DCCs is critically orchestrated by intracellular pathways. Specifically, the ratio between two MAPK pathways, p38 and ERK1/2 MAPK, determines either the balance between dormancy and active proliferation state. In particular, a higher p38 to ERK ratio encourages dormancy, whereas an elevated ERK to p38 ratio drives tumor growth [ , ] (Table ). Notwithstanding recent developments, dormancy research continues to have limitations, highlighting the ongoing need for the discovery of new markers, proteins, and gene expression profiles, as well as the deeper characterization and validation of those already identified.

 

## Immune evasion strategies of dormant cancer cells
The persistence of DCCs represents a significant obstacle to achieve sustained responses in cancer therapy. These quiescent or slowly dividing cells exhibit intrinsic resistance to conventional cytotoxic treatments and can evade immune surveillance, ultimately leading to late recurrences by exploiting mechanisms like “cloaking” or “camouflage” [ – ]. While the mechanisms governing the initial immune response to tumor cells have been extensively investigated, the specific interactions between DCCs and the anti-tumor immune response remain undefined. Understanding how DCCs withstand immune attack and potentially modulate the TME to their advantage is crucial for developing effective eradication strategies. Recent studies have unveiled this complex interplay, highlighting the potential for dormant cells to actively suppress immune cell function and establish an immunosuppressive niche [ ]. Through a sophisticated repertoire of evasive maneuvers, DCCs can effectively checkmate the anti-tumor immune response, ensuring their long-term survival and eventual relapse. The crosstalk between DCCs and the TME is a dynamic, bidirectional process in which the microenvironment exerts both passive and active regulatory roles over the induction and maintenance of dormancy, affecting both primary and metastatic sites [ ]. For instance, it has been demonstrated that macrophage depletion reduces the number of NR2F1-positive dormant DTCs, suggesting a key role for macrophages in sustaining the dormancy phenotype [ ]. DCCs can employ refined immune evasion strategies, notably including the downregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules on their surface. This reduction in MHC I expression hinders cytotoxic T lymphocytes from recognizing and killing these cells, thereby crippling immune mechanism for eliminating aberrant cells, allowing them to escape immune surveillance and persist in a non-proliferative state [ ]. Beyond the well-established mechanism of MHC I downregulation, DCCs exhibit a multifaceted arsenal for evading anti-tumor immunity. One key strategy involves the secretion of immunosuppressive factors in the TME. For instance, DCCs have been shown to release cytokines such as TGF-β and interleukin 10 (IL-10), which can directly prevent the cytotoxic T and natural killer (NK) cell functions, thereby fostering a favorable environment for their survival [ , – ]. Zippo and colleagues highlighted the role of retinoic acid (RA), a vitamin A derivative, in the TME and its influence on cancer cells. They observed that exposure to RA activates the expression of SOX9 in cancer cells, inducing a quiescent state that impairs NK cell-mediated immune surveillance [ ]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that specific cytokines, such as interleukin-15 (IL-15), can reinvigorate these NK cells, restoring their cytotoxic potential against DCCs. These findings suggest that therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing NK cell activity within the dormant microenvironment could be a promising avenue for preventing metastatic relapse. The intricate balance of activating and inhibitory signals, influenced by both dormant cells and their surrounding environment, is therefore crucial in determining the fate of these potentially dangerous cells [ ]. The persistent establishment and maintenance of an immunosuppressive microenvironment within the tumor niche is significantly underpinned by the remarkable capacity of DCCs to actively modulate the expression of immunosuppressive molecules. Among these, the upregulation of immune checkpoint proteins stands out as a paramount mechanism. In fact, a widely discovered phenomenon is the overexpression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), on the surface of aberrant cells, which represents a pivotal determinant in shaping a profoundly immunosuppressive milieu [ ]. Interaction between PD-L1 on the tumor cell surface with its receptor PD-1 on T cells leads to T cell exhaustion and functional impairment, effectively dampening the anti-tumor immune response [ , ]. By expressing PD-L1, dormant cells can create a localized immunosuppressive microenvironment, fostering their persistence and potentially contributing to future relapses. This active engagement of immune checkpoints represents a sophisticated mechanism of immune escape, highlighting the dynamic interplay between DCCs and the host immune system. This mechanism allows dormant cells to actively engage and suppress infiltrating immune cells, promoting their long-term survival. DCCs actively shape the TME to establish an immunosuppressive niche conducive to their survival (Fig. C, left panel). Within TME, hypoxia plays a critical role in inducing dormancy in tumor cells. Studies have shown that hypoxic microenvironments within primary tumors, such as in HNSCC and breast cancer (BC), are associated with the upregulation of key dormancy-related genes like NR2F1, p27, alongside hypoxia markers such as Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1 α (HIF1α) and Glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) [ ]. Significantly, DCCs exposed to constant hypoxia displayed elevated levels of NR2F1, p27, and TGF-β2, indicators of a dormant state [ ]. Moreover, in the tumoral niche DCCs establish a type III collagen-rich ECM to sustain their slow-growing state. As a critical ECM protein, type III collagen is essential for both inducing and maintaining dormancy in vivo. Proteomic analysis of the ECM confirmed that DCCs indeed create this specialized collagen-enriched niche. Furthermore, it activates a novel tumor cell dormancy signaling pathway, involving DDR1/STAT1, which in turn promotes the formation of type III collagen [ , ]. Their repertoire of capabilities within the TME also includes the ability to modulate the recruitment and reprogramming of diverse stromal and immune cell populations. By secreting specific chemokines and cytokines, DCCs influence the TME to promote the infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs). These chemoattractant signals direct MDSCs and Tregs towards the DCCs. Moreover, the release of soluble factors, such as TGF-β and IL-10, by DCCs actively supports the differentiation and expansion of these immunosuppressive populations. Consequently, the establishment of a local environment enriched with MDSCs and Tregs allows DCCs to effectively suppress anti-tumor immune responses, forming a protective shield that safeguards their long-term survival and contributes to the potential for future relapse [ ] (Fig. C, middle panel). Macrophages also play a pivotal role in the creation of innate immune barriers. In this context, Dalla et al.. observed that tissue-resident alveolar macrophages significantly influence the fate of breast DCCs in the lung [ ]. Their research indicates that the interaction between cancer cells and alveolar macrophages by the TGF-β2/TGF-βR3 axis induces a dormant state in the cancer cells. Conversely, the depletion of macrophages led to the reawakening of cancer cells and the formation of metastases [ ] (Fig. C, right panel). The intricate interplay between DCCs and the resultant immunosuppressive environment represents a critical barrier to successful immune surveillance and eradication of disseminated disease.

## Dissecting cancer dormancy: from genomic signatures to live-cell dynamics
Recent advancements in sequencing technologies have substantially deepened our understanding of cancer dormancy by enabling high-resolution molecular profiling of DCCs. Single-cell sequencing platforms, such as 10x Genomics and Smart-seq2, allow the dissection of cellular heterogeneity at an unprecedented level, revealing distinct genetic and epigenetic signatures associated with dormancy [ ]. Single-cell RNA sequencing studies have significantly advanced the knowledge of cancer dormancy by revealing distinct gene expression signatures and microenvironmental changes associated with dormant tumor cells. For instance, in BC, single-cell profiling of DCCs from bone and lung metastases identified genes such as CFH, GAS6, MME and OGN that are highly expressed in dormant cells. These genes correlate with recurrence-free survival in patients, suggesting their potential as predictive biomarkers [ ]. Additionally, single-cell analyses in immunocompetent transgenic mouse models uncovered remodeling of immune and stromal compartments during dormancy, highlighting pathways like Jagged-1/Notch signaling that regulate TME interactions and contribute to dormancy maintenance [ ]. Computational frameworks applied to single-cell transcriptomic data have further characterized dormancy-associated quiescence states, linking them to intact p53 function and specific mutational profiles. Together, these studies demonstrate that single-cell sequencing can dissect the cellular heterogeneity and molecular mechanisms underlying cancer dormancy, offering insights into therapeutic strategies to prevent relapse by targeting dormant cells and their niche [ – ]. Long-read sequencing technologies enhanced the detection of structural variations (SVs) in DCCs by generating continuous reads that span tens of kilobases, enabling precise characterization of complex and large-scale genomic rearrangements that are often missed by short-read sequencing [ ]. Unlike short-read methods, these platforms sequence native DNA molecules without amplification, reducing biases and allowing direct detection of epigenetic modifications alongside SVs. These tools are crucial for studying DCCs, where subtle structural variants such as large insertions, inversions, copy number alterations, and chromosomal rearrangements can influence gene regulation and contribute to the maintenance of either a dormant or active cellular state [ ]. For example, long-read sequencing has revealed complex gene fusions and repetitive element insertions that affect tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes, providing insights into the genomic heterogeneity underlying dormancy [ ]. Additionally, the ability to detect DNA methylation and other epigenetic marks directly on native DNA molecules helps elucidate epigenomic changes associated with dormancy states [ ]. Overall, the ultra-long reads and direct epigenetic profiling offered by innovative technologies provide a comprehensive view of the structural and regulatory genomic landscape in DCCs, facilitating the identification of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets that are inaccessible to traditional short-read sequencing approaches [ ]. Characterizing slow-proliferating, dormant, or quiescent cells in primary tumors and throughout tumor progression is a key objective in cancer research. Identifying and understanding the clonal populations that can evade immune control, resist chemotherapy, and metastasize to distant organs would represent a significant breakthrough in the fight against cancer [ , , , , , ] (Fig. A).

 

## Detection of dormant cancer cells through fluorescent dye labeling approaches
DCCs are characterized by their extremely low, or even absent, proliferative activity. They are typically identified by their negative staining for common proliferation markers like Ki67. Additionally, DCCs show no staining with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL), a marker for DNA fragmentation in cells undergoing programmed cell death [ ]. The absence of these markers highlights the quiescent, non-proliferative state of DCCs (Fig. B). Researchers also investigated mitotic index in dormant or slow-cycling cancer cells. The mitotic index measures cell proliferation by calculating the ratio of dividing cells to the total cell population. This can be assessed by observing the phosphorylation state of H3 histones or by measuring the incorporation of nucleotide analogs like BrdU, a thymidine analogue that serves as a “tag” for proliferating cells [ ]. To further differentiate between cycling, slow-cycling, and non-proliferating cells, scientists use dye-retaining assays, such as those employing carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) or PKH26. These methods leverage the ability of quiescent cells to retain fluorescent dyes over multiple cell divisions, thus distinguishing them from rapidly dividing cells that decrease the fluorescence of the dye [ , , ]. Another way to study dormant subpopulations inside heterogeneous cells is in vitro dormancy induction [ , ]. Rutecki et al., investigated the role of serum starvation for 72 h in the induction of a reversible dormant state in epithelial ovarian cancer cells, marked by G0/G1 arrest, resulting in reduced proliferation markers, and undetectable signs of apoptosis or senescence. This method reliably models dormancy and reactivation, making it suitable for mechanistic in vitro studies that can drive cells into the G1/G0 phase state of quiescence or dormancy [ ]. All these techniques, however, represent indirect, static and mostly endpoint methods to reveal cells residing in G0/G1 cell cycle state, limiting the ability to monitor dynamic changes or heterogeneity within cell populations [ ]. Conversely, biosensors tagged with cell cycle or dormancy-associated markers allow real-time and single-cell resolution tracking of cell state dynamics [ ]. These tools can be engineered to report specific molecular events, such as CDK activity or the expression of dormancy regulators like p27 and NR2F1, offering functional readouts of the regulatory networks that govern dormancy states [ , ] (Fig. C).

## Watermelon lentiviral vector system
To characterize the different subpopulations within a heterogeneous population of tumor cells and to track their lineage throughout various biological processes, Oren et al.. developed a lentiviral construct known as the Watermelon vector system [ ]. This high-complexity lentiviral library is constituted of two main domains: one that contains a semi-random sequence of barcodes and the other that allows the assessment of transcriptional and proliferative states at single-cell level. The proliferative state is indicated by the inducible expression of mCherry through doxycycline treatment. Following doxycycline induction, the cells that retain less mCherry are the most proliferative. These methods enable to discriminate live cells with a range of proliferation rate, facilitating their isolation through flow cytometry-based sorting. The presence of the barcode sequence allows tracking the maintenance or transition between a slow-cycling and a proliferative state, particularly in response to treatment regimens [ ] (Fig. D).

## Fucci system
The fluorescent ubiquitination-based cell cycle indicator (Fucci) is a fluorescent cell cycle reporter system to identify specific cell cycle phases in live cells at a single-cell level. It discriminates the arrest or progression by the cyclic degradation of key proteins through ubiquitination [ ]. This system leverages two important E3 ubiquitin ligase proteins APCCdh1 and SCFSkp2, which target geminin (Gem) and DNA replication factor 1 (Cdt1), respectively. APCCdh1 is predominantly active during G0/G1, promoting Gem degradation, while SCFSkp2 functions in S and G2 phases, targeting Cdt1. The interplay between these ligases regulates cell cycle fate: Cdt1 is highly expressed in G1, whereas Gem, expressed in S, G2, and M phases, inhibits Cdt1 to prevent re-replication. This regulation enables Fucci to effectively differentiate cell cycle phases [ , ]. In the original version, also referred to as Fucci(SA, Saturation/Arrest-optimized), the Fucci vector was synthesized by engineering two fluorescent proteins fused to Cdt1 and Gem. Fucci(SA) is optimized for detecting cell cycle arrest, making it particularly useful for identifying quiescent or dormant cells. The G1 phase is detected by red fluorescence, which is achieved by fusing the fast-folding red/orange fluorescent protein mKO2 with a truncated version of the protein Cdt1. The mKO2/hCdt1 fusion protein is ubiquitinated and degraded during the transition to the S phase. In the G2/M phase of cycle, a green fluorescence signal is observed, originating from a green fluorescent protein (monomeric Azami-Green, mAG) fused with human geminin (mAG1-hGem). This green fluorescent protein is visible during the G2/M phase and is rapidly degraded during G1. This construct, through the alternation between red and green fluorescent proteins, enables the tracking of specific cell transition throughout the cell cycle [ , ] (Fig. E (i)). Based on the original Fucci vector design many variants have been developed by modifying specific features while preserving the core of the technology. One of these is the Fucci(CA, Cell-cycle Activity–optimized), which allows to monitor full cycling activity and a precise distinction of interphase stages (G1, S, and G2) by substituting SCFSkp2 with CUL4Ddb1, thereby enabling monitoring of hCdt1 binding to PCNA, a key event marking the onset of S phase (Fig. E (ii)). The Fucci2 variant replaces the original fluorescent proteins with mCherry and mVenus, improving brightness and compatibility with other imaging tools, while the standard Fucci distinguishes mainly between G1 and S/G2/M phases [ , ] (Fig. E (iii)). Fucci4, compared with earlier Fucci systems, allows specific identification for S and M phases, the detection of S-phase arrest. The Fucci4 reporter system is designed to include four distinct fluorescent proteins, each engineered with different cell cycle regulators [ ]. The fusion protein mKO2-hCdt1 specifically marks the G1 phase, while the S phase is identified by the translation of a specific fusion protein derived from the cyan fluorescent protein, known as turquoise, with the stem-loop binding protein (SLBP). This protein is highly expressed at the beginning of the S phase and is rapidly degraded after S phase transition. During the G2 phase, cells express the fusion protein Clover-Geminin, while the M phase is marked by mMaroon1 and histone H1, which highlights mitotic chromatin condensation [ ] (Fig. E (iv)). Another variant of the Fucci vector is represented by the Fucci5 vector that incorporates coral-derived fluorescent proteins (h2-3 and AzaleaB5), selected for their enhanced brightness, stability, and spectral characteristics. These features enable more accurate and reliable visualization of the cell cycle. The proteins are fused in tandem constructs using a P2A sequence to ensure efficient co-expression while preserving their individual functionality [ ]. Two main variants of Fucci5 have been developed, Fucci(SA)5 and Fucci(CA)5. Fucci(SA)5 incorporates a Cdt1 fragment (amino acids 30–120, SA) that remains stable during late G1, enabling robust labeling of cells across the entire G1 phase. Fucci(CA)5, in contrast, uses a different Cdt1 fragment (amino acids 1–100, CA) that is specifically degraded in late G1, allowing a clearer separation between early G1 and the late G1/S transition (Fig. E (v-vi)). Fucci vectors have been widely used to track DCCs during tumor progression and therapy resistance, helping researchers to identify cancer cells that can enter a dormant state, to metastasize in distant organs and to survive upon treatment regimens. In vivo studies using Fucci showed that actively dividing cancer cells tend to cluster near the tumor surface and blood vessels, while quiescent, drug-resistant cells can promote new blood vessel formation, supporting tumor growth [ , ]. In melanoma the Fucci vector allowed to identify and characterize the therapy resistant- persistent slow cycling cancer cell due to the acquisition of a dormant phenotype, which failed to be eradicated by other targeted therapy [ – ]. In BC, Prunier et al.. used the Fucci system to identify a subpopulation of liver metastatic cancer cells capable of entering into a reversible quiescent state [ ].

## mVenus-p27K− lentiviral vector system
As previously described, p27 is a CDKi that, in the active form, resides in the nucleus, preventing the progression of the cell cycle by inhibiting CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6 complexes, arresting the cells in G0. In the transition from G0 to G1/S phases, it is phosphorylated by cyclin E/CDK2 complex, which in turn becomes unstable and is degraded [ ]. A new cell cycle sensor has been developed to identify the G0 phase by engineering a mutated p27 protein, known as p27K−, that lacks inhibitory activity on cyclin-CDK, fused with the fluorescent protein mVenus (green). Thus, cells that are quiescent and express p27 can be identified by the green fluorescence signal derived from the mVenus-p27K− reporter [ ]. Upon exit from the G0 phase, the p27K– protein undergoes the same degradation pathway as endogenous p27, resulting in the loss of fluorescence (Fig. E (vii)). The mVenus-p27K− vector has been widely used to study the role of dormant or quiescent cells in cancer progression. In glioblastoma, p27K− cells have been found at the tumor’s invading edge with a mesenchymal-like phenotype, indicating that DCCs in glioma acquire an invasive behavior [ ]. Michelatti et al. utilized the mVenus-p27K− vector to analyze the DCC’s role in BC dissemination and progression. In particular, this vector allowed for the identification of a subpopulation of dormant DTCs characterized by elevated expression of SOX9, which enables them to evade NK cell-mediated killing, colonize distant organs, and ultimately lead to metastasis [ ]. These findings align with literature linking DCCs to the main steps of tumor progression [ , , ].

## CDK2-venus sensor vector
Among the proteins of interest for tracking or modulating cell cycle progression, the CDK family stands out, as these kinases play a pivotal role in finely regulating the transitions between cell cycle phases. The CDK2-venus sensor vector is a genetically encoded fluorescent biosensor designed to dynamically monitor the activity of CDK2 at the G1/S transition. The sensor is constructed by fusing a fragment of human DNA helicase B (DHB, amino acids 994–1087), which contains four CDK consensus phosphorylation sites, a nuclear localization signal (NLS) and a nuclear export signal (NES), to the fluorescent protein mVenus. CDK2 is inactive during G0 or early G1, when the sensor predominantly localizes to the nucleus due to its NLS. As CDK2 becomes activated (during late G1 and S phase), it phosphorylates the DHB fragment at the consensus sites. This phosphorylation event masks the NLS and/or enhances the NES, causing the sensor to translocate from the nucleus to the cytoplasm [ , ]. Strong nuclear fluorescence indicates low CDK2 activity (cells are quiescent or in G0/G1). In contrast, loss of nuclear signal and increased cytoplasmic fluorescence indicate high CDK2 activity (cells entering S phase or actively cycling). This translocation is rapid and reversible, allowing for dynamic tracking of CDK2 activity at single-cell level [ ]. The application of this vector enabled the dynamic tracking of tumor cell cycle states in vivo, demonstrating that tumor-derived ECM, especially type III collagen, maintains dormancy by keeping cells in G0/G1. Furthermore, ECM remodeling correlates with tumoral reactivation [ ] (Fig. E (viii)).
Uncontrolled proliferation is a fundamental hallmark of cancer cells. However, the mechanisms driving tumor promotion and progression are not solely reliant on unchecked growth. Cell dormancy is considered one of the most important adaptive strategies in cancer evolution. It allows cells to survive in hostile environments, evade treatment, and potentially reactivate later to cause relapse. Overall, the in vitro biosensor systems described above represent live and direct techniques for identifying and characterizing subpopulations of DCCs involved in several biological processes. Many of these systems have expanded our understanding of these processes and facilitated the elucidation of the role of DCCs during various phases of therapy resistance, tumor progression, and metastasis formation.

## Targeting dormant cancer cells: clinical inhibitors and experimental therapeutic strategies
DCCs represent one of the greatest challenges in cancer therapy due to their extraordinary ability to evade detection, circumvent immune surveillance, and resist existing treatments. Their stealth and resilience critically hinder efforts to achieve true remission and a lasting cure. To date, therapeutic approaches have primarily focused on three strategies: (i) overcoming the downregulation of MHC in DCCs through chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy; (ii) reactivating dormant cells to sensitize them to cytotoxic therapies or (iii) maintaining their dormant state indefinitely to prevent metastatic relapses. Building on these concepts, recent research has developed and tested a range of targeted interventions in both preclinical and clinical settings. These interventions exploit unique signaling pathways, metabolic dependencies and epigenetic vulnerabilities of DCCs, aiming at effectively eliminate or control these elusive cells [ ]. As described above, DCCs can downregulate MHC molecules to evade immune surveillance. CAR T cells are engineered to recognize specific surface antigens on cancer cells in an MHC-independent manner. Bypassing the requirement for MHC class I presentation, CAR T cells are able to effectively target and eliminate DCCs [ ]. A study by Goddard et al. revealed the potent efficacy of CAR T cells engineered to target human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). Notably, in vitro assays revealed that HER2-specific CAR T cells robustly cleared both dormant and proliferative HER2+ BC cell lines. Additionally, evidence from in vivo studies conducted in immunocompromised mice, which carried HER2-expressing DCCs in their lungs, indicated that HER2-targeted CAR T cell therapy induced a considerable and statistically significant reduction in the number of dormant DTCs. These findings represent a pivotal step towards preventing metastatic recurrence, due to the strong therapeutic potential of HER2-directed CAR T cell therapy [ ]. However, the critical challenge lies in identifying a suitable target antigen that is specifically and consistently expressed on the surface of these DCCs. The principle of MHC-independent targeting by CARs underscores its potential as a therapeutic strategy against this immune evasion mechanism, contingent upon the discovery of a specific and targetable antigen on the dormant cell surface. Future research focusing on characterizing the unique surfaceome of DCCs is crucial for unlocking this therapeutic potential of CAR T cell therapy. One of the most extensively studied signaling pathways in the context of DCCs biology is the TGF-β cascade. This pathway plays a key role in maintaining cellular dormancy, dampening immune responses, and fostering a TME that supports a quiescent state. TGF-β initiates its action by binding to a receptor complex composed of type I and type II serine/threonine kinase receptors (TGF-βRI and TGF-βRII). Ligand binding activates TGF-βRII, which subsequently phosphorylates TGF-βRI, leading to intracellular signaling cascade activation [ ]. These downstream signals regulate gene expression programs that sustain cell cycle arrest, suppress immune activity, and contribute to the establishment of a dormancy-promoting niche within the TME [ ]. Galunisertib (LY2157299), a selective TGF-βRI kinase inhibitor, was evaluated in a phase Ib/II trial (NCT02734160) in combination with durvalumab, an anti-PD-L1 antibody, in patients with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). This combination aims at simultaneously lifting TGF-β–mediated immune suppression and facilitating immune-mediated clearance of residual DCCs, offering a rationale for this dual-pathway targeting [ ]. Another promising target to counteract DCCs is the AXL receptor tyrosine kinase, frequently upregulated during epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a phenotype associated with cellular dormancy and metastatic competence. Inhibition of AXL using Bemcentinib (BGB324) has demonstrated enhanced chemotherapy response in AXL-positive tumors and a reduction in metastatic burden in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and BC models [ ]. The mechanism involves both direct disruption of survival signaling in DCCs and interference with stromal and immune-mediated support systems. “Wake-and-kill” strategies exploit the concept of induced reactivation of dormant cells, followed by cytotoxic elimination. A preclinical study by Ganesan et al.. showed that docetaxel-induced stromal damage leads to interleukin 6 (IL-6) and granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) release, activating MEK/ERK signaling in DCCs and promoting their exit from dormancy [ ]. In a more recent study, the role of pulmonary inflammation following viral infection in the reawake of DCCs has been clarified. In particular, this mechanism is mediated by the recruitment of neutrophils and activation of IL-6/STAT3 signaling [ ]. Once proliferative, DCCs become vulnerable to subsequent rounds of chemotherapy, highlighting a window of therapeutic opportunity following controlled reawakening [ ]. Conversely, maintaining dormancy may be beneficial in cases where eradication is not feasible. “Sleep therapies” aim at pharmacologically reinforcing dormancy through inhibition of proliferation and autophagy [ ]. The PALAVY trial (NCT04841148) investigates the use of palbociclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor, combined with hydroxychloroquine, to block cell cycle progression and autophagy in ER+ BC patients with detectable DTCs. The rationale lies in sustaining quiescence and preventing re-entry into the cell cycle, thereby containing disease progression. Inhibitors of the Src family kinases have emerged as promising modulators of dormancy-associated signaling. Saracatinib (AZD0530), a selective oral Src inhibitor, has shown the ability to maintain dormancy in BC models by preventing collagen I-induced reactivation of quiescent cells. This occurs through the stabilization and nuclear accumulation of p27, suggesting that saracatinib may function as a “sleep therapy” approach aimed at sustaining the dormant state and preventing metastatic outgrowth. Notably, when combined with ERK1/2 inhibitors, saracatinib induces apoptotic death in DCCs, shifting its role toward a “wake-and-kill” strategy by exploiting a synergic disruption of survival and proliferative pathways. In this context, He et al., reported that chemotherapy can reawake DCCs through the action of neutrophils by NETs and subsequent remodeling of the microenvironment, thereby permitting their therapeutic targeting of these cells [ ]. These dual-context effects underscore its potential in both preventing dormancy escape and actively targeting the DCCs reservoir [ ]. Epigenetic reprogramming also contributes to dormant phenotypes. DCCs often exhibit a bivalent chromatin landscape that allows rapid switch between silent and active states. Inhibitors of histone demethylases, such as GSK-J4 (targeting JMJD3/UTX) and CPI-455 (targeting KDM5), have shown preclinical efficacy in modulating chromatin accessibility and reversing dormancy-associated gene expression patterns [ ]. These approaches may sensitize DCCs to immune recognition or induce cell death. In parallel, metabolic interventions such as ferroptosis induction offer a novel avenue for targeting dormant-like cancer cells. DCCs, often relying on antioxidant defenses, exhibit vulnerability to glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inhibition. Agents such as RSL3 and ML210 induce lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic death in DCC-enriched populations, bypassing traditional apoptosis resistance mechanisms [ ]. Collectively, these strategies represent a paradigm shift in cancer therapy, moving beyond proliferative tumor targets toward the covert, therapy-resistant reservoir of dormant cells (Table ). Future research should deepen the mentioned mechanism try to develop predictive biomarkers for DCCs identification and stratification, and to optimize combinatory therapies that target both proliferative and quiescent tumor compartments.

 

## Conclusion
This review provides a comprehensive overview of the hallmarks of DCCs, pinpointing key markers and commonly involved pathways. While conventional anti-cancer therapies effectively target the primary tumor bulk, the lingering presence of DCCs represents a silent threat directly responsible for metastatic recurrences. Recent research highlights how the intricate interaction between DCCs and various populations within the TME establishes a persistent immunosuppressive niche, which not only shields DCCs from immune detection but also contributes to their therapeutic resistance. Our review delves into the latest findings concerning DCCs immune evasion mechanisms and explores cutting-edge experimental laboratory techniques for DCCs detection. We also provide a current overview of pathways and protein inhibitors currently under investigation in clinical trials specifically targeting DCCs. The paramount challenge for the scientific community is to accelerate the discovery of novel therapeutic targets for DCCs. This necessitates the development of new drugs capable of either blocking DCCs survival pathways or, conversely, inducing their re-awakening to render them susceptible to eradication. The ultimate aim is to significantly enhance the efficacy of standard therapeutic approaches by integrating these new compounds. The advancements promise to offer a new perspective on the management of tumor recurrence and an improvement of life expectancy for oncological patients.
