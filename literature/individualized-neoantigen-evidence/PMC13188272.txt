## Introduction
Cancer is one of the leading causes of deaths globally [ ]. Solid tumors remain major contributors to cancer-related mortality, largely due to therapeutic resistance and the biological heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ , ]. In particular, the incidence of solid tumors continues to rise, while conventional treatment approaches using chemotherapy and surgery fail to achieve sufficient clinical benefits [ ]. Among those, colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers hold a distinct clinical and biological significance, accounting for more than 11–12% of all cancer cases [ ]. Specifically, colorectal cancer is expected to account for about 158,850 new cases and 55,230 deaths, making it the second most common cancer type in the United States, worryingly ranking highest in those under 50 years of age [ , ]. Colorectal cancers are classified into four molecular subtypes, CMS1-CMS4, with CMS4 tumors responding poorly to both chemo- and immunotherapy, showing the lowest overall survival and displaying a dense extracellular matrix (ECM) and an immunosuppressive TME [ ]. Pancreatic cancer, accounting for about 67,530 new cases and 52,740 deaths, is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths and is highly lethal due to its aggressive nature [ ]. Ovarian cancer is the seventh most common cancer in women. Similarly to colorectal and pancreatic cancers, diagnosis at late stage and resistance to chemotherapy contributes to its high mortality rates. Ovarian tumors are defined by a heterogenous TME, and patients with desmoplastic or mesenchymal tumors have a worse prognosis [ , ].
Beyond clinical and intrinsic factors, the success of anti-cancer treatment is impaired by the biology of solid tumors [ , ]. In general, colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancer cells are embedded within a complex TME, composed of multiple stromal cells and a dense ECM [ – ]. Through a process called ‘immunoediting’, the immune system evolves from a protective mechanism that destroys tumor cells to one that shapes and promotes tumor development [ ]. The immunosuppressive, so-called ‘cold’, TME facilitates immune evasion and resistance to treatment, in particular immunotherapy, promoting tumor growth [ , ]. In addition to conventional treatment approaches, targeted treatment strategies to interfere with the tumor immune microenvironment may improve patient outcomes. Immunotherapies are used as an anti-cancer approach to target elements of the ‘cold’ TME, as they boost tumor immunity to treat cancer by stimulating a patient’s own immune system [ ]. These include anti-cancer vaccines [ ], antibody drug conjugates [ ], monoclonal antibodies [ ], and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells [ ], all of which are developed to prevent, attenuate or destroy cancer cells [ – ]. Immunotherapies reduce the risk of tumor relapse and adverse side effects linked to conventional treatment approaches [ ].
Several cancer types, including melanoma, breast, bladder and lung cancers, have shown positive responses to immunotherapies [ , , ]. However, the wider implementation of immunotherapies to treat solid tumors with poor survival rates, late diagnosis and low surgical success remains challenging, with limited response rates in colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers [ , – ]. The lack of efficacy of immunotherapy is associated with the immunosuppressive TME which reduces drug diffusion and impairs immune cell infiltration [ , ]. Furthermore, immunotherapies induce off-target effects and systemic toxicity in these highly invasive and poorly penetrable tumors [ , ]. Assessing therapeutic response allows clinicians to tailor personalized treatment strategies and adjust strategies to improve quality of life. Thus, we need to better understand the mechanisms and therapeutic effects of immunotherapy-based strategies.
Many new anti-cancer therapies remain ineffective in clinical trials due to limited preclinical assessment and models that lack of key biological components and complexity of tumor tissues [ ]. Preclinical studies are mostly conducted using cell monolayers, organoids, or mini-replica of tissues, grown in animal-based matrices, murine and immunodeficient transplantation models, and do not capture the nature of the immunosuppressive TME of solid tumors. Animal models are widely used as the next best approach to improve the reliability for clinical testing. However, interspecies genetic differences alter tumor-immune cell interaction, leading to misinterpretation of immunotherapy responses in patients [ , ]. Advances in cell culture technologies, bioengineering approaches, and biomaterial fabrication helped bridge the gap between murine and human systems, creating more defined and physiologically relevant platforms to study tumor biology. Biomaterial-based platforms recreate the ECM with biochemical and biomechanical properties similar to those of patient-derived tissues [ – ]. By integrating multiple cell types, such as cancer, stromal and immune cells, and extracellular factors, biomaterial-based platforms enable real-time drug evaluation in 3D environments, offering opportunities to improve preclinical assessment and clinical outcomes [ ]. The growing need to replicate human-specific features, such as immune response, vascularisation and nutrient exchange, has driven the development of advanced 3D culture technologies, including hydrogels, microfluidic devices, 3D-printed scaffolds, polymeric structures and self-assembling biomolecules [ , , – ]. Despite challenges in maintaining immune cells long-term in vitro, advanced 3D cancer models support dynamic adaptation and integration of tumor and immune cells within the TME [ ], providing a powerful platform to study tumor-immune cell interactions and evaluate emerging immunotherapies (Fig. ).
Here, we highlight how the emerging discipline of tumor tissue engineering enables platforms to deconstruct tumor tissues and study immunological processes in disease progression and resistance to treatment including immunotherapy in immunosuppressed tumors. We summarize the central elements of the immunosuppressive TME, different types of immunotherapies and their limitations in treating these aggressive tumors. Then, we present how advanced 3D cancer models have been used to resemble tumor tissues and decipher cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions focusing on colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers. Lastly, we critically discuss the necessity of using advanced 3D cancer models to accelerate the development and screening of therapeutic strategies that target the immunosuppressive TME.

 

## Deconstructing the tumor immune microenvironment
Interactions between tumor, stromal and immune cell populations, vascular-like networks and the ECM dictate tumor growth, immune cell activation and response and resistance to therapy. Colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers exemplify the influence of the TME on disease progression and therapeutic failure. They share multiple similarities in their tumor immune microenvironment, including dynamic and co-evolving immunosuppressive features, diverse stromal cell populations, and mechanisms of immune evasion. Stromal cells and the ECM are key architects of the immunosuppressive TME, establishing a mechanically stiff and signaling-rich niche that restricts immune cell infiltration and dampens immune effectors. These biochemical and biomechanical cues not only shape cancer progression but also condition immune cell behavior, reinforcing local immune tolerance. The composition and organisation of these stromal compartments are highly tumor-type specific: colorectal cancers exhibit spatially resolved epithelial-stromal-immune architectures [ – ], pancreatic cancers are dominated by a stiff, desmoplastic, or scar-like, ECM [ ], and ovarian cancers exploit ascites-associated, mechanically permissive niches [ – ]. Within this context, specialised immune cell populations, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [ , ], act as principal mediators of immunosuppression by actively constraining anti-tumor immune responses. Recapitulating both stromal and immune components is therefore essential for the design of advanced 3D cancer models that accurately mimic the immunosuppressive TME and enable robust preclinical evaluation of immunotherapy-based strategies.

## Stromal cells
Stromal cell populations constitute a major immunosuppressive element of the TME and include endothelial cells and mesenchymal stromal cells that differentiate into chondrocytes, adipocytes, pericytes and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [ ]. These populations contribute to desmoplasia by producing ECM proteins that increase tissue stiffness, creating a biochemical and biophysical barrier that restricts immune cell infiltration. Among stromal subsets, CAFs are particularly influential, promoting cancer stemness, resistance to apoptosis, tumor growth and metastasis through bidirectional signaling with cancer cells, while simultaneously enforcing immune suppression via cytokines and chemokines such as IL‑10, TGF‑β, PGE₂ and CCL2. Stromal TGF‑β signaling suppresses T cell activation and drives Treg expansion, further reinforcing immune tolerance [ ]. CAFs exhibit functional heterogeneity, including inflammatory (iCAFs), myofibroblastic (myCAFs) and antigen-presenting (apCAFs), which differentially shape innate and adaptive immune responses [ ]. Spatially restricted interactions between CAFs and stem-like cancer cells at invasive fronts, as observed in colorectal cancer, promote immunosuppressive niches through recruitment of PD‑L1-expressing neutrophils, while senescent CAFs play analogous roles in pancreatic cancer [ , ]. Together, these observations highlight stromal cells particularly CAFs as dynamic regulators of tumor immunity and underscore the importance of incorporating stromal complexity into advanced 3D cancer models to better capture stromal-mediated immune suppression and therapeutic resistance.

## ECM
The ECM is a complex network of proteins and polysaccharides, or glycosaminoglycans, that provides structural support while actively modulating cell behavior [ ]. In solid tumors, elevated levels of proteins, such as collagen, vimentin, fibronectin and proteoglycans remodel the ECM, increasing rigidity and disrupting architecture, which supports cancer cell invasion, migration and metastasis [ , ]. CAFs in stromal-dense tumors, such as colorectal, pancreatic, and ovarian cancers promote the deposition of ECM proteins associated with tumor fibrosis [ , ]. Higher ECM stiffness is associated with higher tumor, lymph node and metastasis (TNM) staging and overall worse survival for colorectal cancer patients [ ]. In ovarian cancer, high ECM stiffness of is associated with worse prognosis, and a stiffer ECM leads to an altered macrophage phenotype [ ]. Increased matrix stiffness also promotes higher levels of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer [ ]. Advanced 3D cancer models that incorporate both stromal cells and ECM-like hydrogels resemble the immunosuppressive TME of solid tumors, creating a preclinical platform suitable for drug discovery and testing.

## TAMs
Macrophages are key regulators of anti-tumor immunity, but within the TME, TAMs frequently adopt tumor-promoting phenotypes [ ]. In colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers, TAMs arise largely from circulating monocytes [ ] and are recruited through chemokine and CSF‑1–CSF‑1R signaling, where they drive ECM remodeling, angiogenesis, metastasis and immunosuppression [ , ]. TAM-tumor cell crosstalk reinforces malignant behavior through cytokine-mediated programs, including JAK-STAT-dependent EMT, hypoxia-driven macrophage polarisation and TGF‑β-dependent cancer progression [ – ]. In parallel, TAM-derived immunosuppressive factors such as IL‑10 inhibit cytotoxic lymphocyte function, contributing to immune evasion and poor clinical outcomes [ ]. The ECM can educate TAMs towards an immunoregulatory phenotype in ovarian cancer-induced metastasis [ , ]. Together, these observations establish TAMs as central mediators of cancer progression and therapeutic resistance and highlight the importance of incorporating functional macrophage populations into advanced 3D cancer models.

## Tregs
This cell type is a subset of CD4+ FOXP3+ T cells that are involved in suppressing anti-tumor immunity. Increased levels of Tregs are associated with worse prognosis in pancreatic cancer. Enhanced levels of CCL5 recruit FOXP3+ Tregs to the TME, while inhibition of CCL5 inhibits cancer progression [ ]. Higher Treg scores also correlate with worse prognosis in ovarian cancer. In recurrent disease, levels of CD8+ cells are decreased, and Treg infiltration is evident [ ]. The CCR8-CCL1 and CCR8-CCL18 axes are important for the infiltration of CD4+ CCR8+ Tregs in ovarian cancer, and CCR8 + Tregs are associated with worse survival and disease progression [ ]. IL-10 derived from Tregs has been shown to promote metastasis of colorectal cancer to the lung [ ]. Blocking of CCR8 increases CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, leading to anti-tumor immunity, and emphasise the therapeutic potential of anti-CCR8 in targeting Tregs [ ]. Targeting Tregs is a major goal for the next-generation of immunotherapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors, for example cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) blockade is known to deplete Tregs. Biomaterials, such as alginate and gelatin methacryloyl, can be used to assess the immunosuppressive capacity of Tregs, providing a platform to test Treg-targeted therapies in a controlled 3D environment [ ].

## MDSCs
These cells are immature myeloid cells with potent immunosuppressive activity. They accumulate in colorectal, pancreatic and advanced ovarian cancers, promoting chemotherapy resistance and disease progression [ ]. Their recruitment is driven by chemokines such as CXCL1, CXCL8 and CCL2. In colorectal cancer, the accumulation of MDSCs is associated with advanced tumor stage and Treg expansion [ ]. Cancer cell-derived factors induce the recruitment of MDSCs and their immunosuppressive functions, for example via the neuronal guidance protein Netrin-1 [ ]. Depletion of MDSCs or targeting their immunosuppressive mechanisms [ , ] has shown efficacy in colorectal and ovarian cancers. Ovarian cancer cells dictate the function of MDSCs through regulation of glutamine metabolism [ ]. MDSC and TAM crosstalk has implications for immune evasion through CCL2- and GM-CSF-mediated signaling [ ]. Incorporating MDSCs into advanced 3D cancer models enables testing of therapeutics targeting these immunosuppressive populations.
Therapeutics that target multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms are currently in clinical trials (Table  ), and patient selection is an important factor determining the efficacy of single or combination immunotherapies. The design of advanced 3D cancer models is critical in testing and optimizing these targeted therapies preclinically (Table  ). To mimic tumor-immune cell interactions, the incorporation of multiple stromal and immune cell components, and a perfusable vasculature is required to accurately mimic the immunosuppressive TME. Combination strategies such as those to re-invigorate T cells, including immune checkpoint and TGF-β inhibitors, CAR T cells and CAF-targeting therapies, hold promise in modifying the immunosuppressive TME.

 

 

## ‘Hot’ versus ‘cold’ TMEs and their implications for immunotherapies
Immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, adoptive cell transfer therapy, cancer vaccines and engineered cytokine therapeutics, aim to re-activate the immune response to destroy cancer cells [ , ]. Immunotherapy became a main pillar of cancer treatment from 2011 onwards, when ipilimumab, an immune checkpoint inhibitor targeting CTLA-4, was approved by the FDA for the treatment of unresectable or metastatic melanoma [ ]. To date, immune checkpoint inhibitors are one of the most commonly used immunotherapies for the treatment of multiple forms of cancer, however, response rates vary between patients, with only 20–40% of patients responding positively to immunotherapy-based strategies [ ]. Response to immune checkpoint inhibitors highly depends on the TME, which influences how immune cells recognise and attack cancer cells.
TMEs are classified as ‘hot’ or ‘cold’ based on the infiltration, location and phenotype of immune cells [ ]. ‘Hot’ tumors have high levels of tumor antigens and are abundant in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells, resulting in an inflammatory TME. These tumors show a heightened response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [ , ]. Various types of cancer, such as melanoma and bladder cancer, are responsive to immunotherapies and often characterised as ‘hot’ tumors. For example, patients diagnosed with a rare subtype of melanoma, metastatic desmoplastic melanoma, had a 70% response rate to treatment with antibodies against programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or its ligand PD-L1 [ ]. This response was attributed to the ‘hot’ TME, as evidenced by a high mutational burden, due to ultraviolet light exposure, and infiltrating CD8+ T cells, which were limited by PD-L1 expression. In this setting, data suggests that discontinuation of immune checkpoint inhibitors in some melanoma patients is possible without disease progression [ ]. In bladder cancer, using the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin immunotherapy VPM1002BC resulted in recurrence-free survival in almost 50% of patients one year after treatment [ ]. Patients diagnosed with bladder cancer receiving a combined treatment of chemotherapy and immunotherapy had a 16% improved overall survival compared to chemotherapy alone, and improved progression-free survival by 22% [ ]. These studies emphasize the positive responses of tumors with a ‘hot’ TME to immunotherapy through the infiltration of immune cells.
On the other hand, colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers are ‘cold’ tumors characterised by a low immune cell infiltration, lack of immune response, and low levels of PD-L1 [ , ]. In ‘cold’ tumors, increased levels of TGF-β and IL-10 activate Tregs and MDSCs [ , , ]. Increased levels of Tregs are associated with a higher risk of relapse and lower survival [ ]. In colorectal cancer, increased IL-10 derived from FOXP3+ Tregs enhances the formation of lung metastasis. FOXP3+ Tregs and myeloid cells express high levels of IL-10Ra, and blocking of IL-10 decreased the occurrence of lung metastasis [ ]. Increased levels of immunosuppressive MDSCs in post-operative pancreatic cancer patients are related to worse survival and reduced NK cell function in the TME [ ]. In some ‘cold’ tumors, the number of stromal cells is directly correlated with an increased tissue stiffness, creating a barrier that prevents infiltration of immune cells into the tumor, including cytotoxic T cells [ ]. In pancreatic cancer, targeting FAP+ CAFs results in degradation of the desmoplastic matrix and loss of structural integrity. After targeting the FAP+ CAFs, a reduced tumor growth was observed using tumor antigen-targeting CAR T cells and the immune checkpoint inhibitor against PD-1 due to decreased MDSCs and induced infiltration of CD8+ and natural killer (NK) cells in the TME [ ]. This suggests that it is possible to skew ‘cold’ to ‘hot’ tumors.
Several ongoing clinical trials focus on combining immune checkpoint inhibitors with other therapies to alter ‘cold’ to ‘hot’ tumors (e.g. NCT04612309, NCT06098560, NCT05426005, Table ). Combining chemotherapy with CAR T cell therapy, cytokine therapy or cancer vaccines has yielded promising results in reactivating the immune system in ‘cold’ tumors, increasing the infiltration of immune cells and detection of cancer cells by the immune system. In colorectal cancer, most patients are resistant to immunotherapy [ ]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors are recommended for colorectal cancer patients with mismatch repair deficiency or microsatellite instability, only accounting for about 15% of patients. Treatment of these patients with ipilimumab and nivolumab achieved a 67% complete response [ ]. To overcome therapy resistance colorectal cancer, T cell and CAR T cell therapies are being administered [ , ]. These results emphasise the need for developing novel targeted therapies that reverse immunosuppression in patients and exploring methods for their combination and administration.
3D cell cultures, organoids and transplantation approaches are widely used to test novel therapies. For example, organoids were used to test the effects of targeting the JAK/STAT3 pathway in colorectal cancer. JAK/STAT3 is associated with high levels of stromal cells and worse prognosis for patients [ ]. Inhibition of the JAK/STAT3 pathway reduces the viability and proliferation of organoids [ ]. Using a transplantation model for ovarian cancer, combined treatment of JAK inhibitors and oncolytic vesicular stomatitis virus was tested [ ]. This combination treatment reduced tumor size [ ]. Using novel therapies such as oncolytic viruses is an important therapeutic strategy to target non-responsive solid tumors. In pancreatic cancer, the oncolytic adenovirus, VCN-01, was combined with chemotherapy, to disrupt the stromal cell barrier, resulting in disease stabilization. VCN-01, is a hyaluronidase-producing oncolytic virus that replicates in cancer cells with a dysfunctional RB1 pathway. This promising combination treatment decreases ECM stiffness and increases the expression of PD-L1 [ ]. Patient-derived organoids established from pancreatic tumors are used to screen the effects of combining chemotherapy with an oncolytic adenovirus, leading to an increased response compared to chemotherapy or oncolytic virus treatment alone [ ]. Establishing 3D cancer models that recapitulate the features of the complex TME has prognostic implications for patients and is critical for future developments in immuno-oncology (Table ).

## Tools for tumor tissue engineering
Advanced 3D cancer models are powerful tools that recapitulate the tumor immune microenvironment to study cell responses toward immunotherapy [ , ]. The 3D co-culture of cancer, stromal and immune cells in biomaterial-based platforms enables tumor-immune interactions that more closely resemble those observed in patients. Biomaterial-based platforms overcome the limitations of conventional 3D cell culture methods, for example floating spheroid or organoid cultures grown in animal-derived matrices, which are associated with inconsistent cell growth, variable uptake of nutrients, and lack of reproducible results between studies due to batch-to-batch variations of animal-derived matrices [ – ]. The limited physiological and biological relevance of murine or immunodeficient transplantation models hinders their predictive value for immunotherapy testing. Although humanized animal models have shown potential to educate cytotoxic T lymphocytes in KRAS-mutated colorectal and pancreatic cancers, they still fail to capture the tumor-immune heterogeneity observed in patients due to altered genetic and metabolic profiles associated with drug resistance [ ]. The associated costs, maintenance and required expertise make it challenging to conduct long-term studies using these models. These reasons emphasize again how advanced 3D cancer models that integrate the immunosuppressive TME represent innovative tools to develop more effective anti-cancer therapies. Advanced 3D cancer models provide defined experimental TME replicas with preclinical potential to screen targeted or personalized treatment strategies that integrate novel immunotherapies (Fig. ), in line with new approach methodologies as per the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Modernization Act 2.0 and 3.0 [ ]. This FDA Modernization Act allows non-animal models for safety and efficacy testing, supporting the federal drive to reduce animal testing and promote alternatives. Below we briefly introduce key bioengineering tools used to design physiologically relevant platforms.

## Biomaterial-based platforms
Biomaterials used in advanced 3D cancer models mimic the characteristic ECM of solid tumors and arecommonly classified as natural or synthetic [ ]. Natural biomaterials, including Matrigel and collagen, are sourced from animals and have batch-to-batch variations and an undefined composition, making them a limited option as TME replica. Specifically, the lack of structural stability and tunable matrix properties hinder their ability to truly model the TME [ ]. Thus, synthetic, such as poly(ethylene glycol), and composite polymer-based biomaterials have been developed offering controllable, defined, stable and biomimetic properties needed for a wide range of 3D cell culture applications. For example, poly(ethylene glycol) functionalized with maleimide groups are used as a 3D lymphoid model to study human B cell activity [ ]. Composite biomaterials combining suitable characteristics of natural and synthetic polymers displaying cell-instructive functionalities [ , ]. For example, gelatin methacryloyl has been used to mimic the ECM-specific features of the TME and metabolic activity of colorectal and pancreatic cancers [ , , , , ]. Likewise, gelatin methacryloyl-hyaluronic acid methacryloyl hydrogels mimic the stiffness of pancreatic tumor tissues and support the 3D co-culture of cancer and immune cells together with CAFs [ , ]. Synthetic and composite biomaterials have a broad range of physical properties that are further controlled through the functionalization with degradable peptide sequences allowing cell-specific matrix remodeling. For example, a hydrogel-based model made of matrix metalloproteinase-sensitive poly(ethylene glycol) functionalized with heparin biomolecules demonstrates the efficacy of combining chemotherapy and immunotherapy in targeting pancreatic cancer and immune cells [ ].

## Organoids
Organoids are self-assembled cell or tissue structures that serve as mini-replica of organs, typically grown in animal-derived matrices such as Matrigel or collagen [ ]. Patient-derived cancer organoids offer advantages over conventional cell line cultures, including improved long-term stability, preservation of genetic integrity, and retention of patient-specific features for assessing therapeutic response and biomarker selection [ , ]. The 3D co-culture of cancer organoids or spheroids with immune cells enables the study of cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions that facilitate tumor formation and metastatic spread in colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers [ ]. The analysis of ovarian cancer spheroids showed the interdependency of platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta and fibronectin expression in directing metastatic spread [ ]. Current efforts focus on incorporating multiple immune cell types into cancer organoids to enhance their translational potential for immunotherapy testing [ ].

## Microfluidic devices
The dynamic properties of tumor tissues are modelled using microfluidic or cancer-on-a-chip devices [ ] that incorporate trafficking of immune cells, nutrient flow, and vascular conditions for metastatic spread and drug delivery [ ]. Microfluidic devices are typically fabricated using polydimethylsiloxane that is cast in a custom-designed mould and contain inlets and outlets, enabling the controlled transfer, diffusion, and uptake of nutrients, secreted factors, and drugs [ , – ]. Hereby, vascular, stromal, and cancer compartments of the TME are integrated, promoting cellular interactions. A controlled perfusion allows for shear stress, interstitial flow, hydrostatic pressure, and gradients of soluble factors, forming a vascular-like network that supports microphysiological functions. Microfluidic devices have been used to track the transformation of immune cells including T cell activity and function in colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers [ – ]. Cancer-on-a-chip devices offer tuneable flow conditions that mimic tissue-level fluid transport, together with control over structural components, cell composition and matrix properties depending on the biomaterials used [ ]. They commonly integrate collagen, fibrin, hyaluronic acid, gelatin metacryloyl, and polyethylene glycol matrices, as well as organ-specific decellularized ECM blends, enabling a specific stiffness, viscoelasticity, ligand density, porosity, and degradability. These capabilities enable studies of how the TME shapes the tumor-immune dynamics, metastatic behavior or drug delivery under conditions that better reflect physiology than static 3D cell cultures [ ].

## Polymeric microgels
Microgel models support cell growth and adhesion independent of the biomaterial properties, such as matrix degradation, and are used to study the anti-cancer effects of CAR T cells [ , ]. Microgels are formed through visible-light crosslinking and offer tunable properties in terms of size, porosity, and stiffness [ ]. Advanced 3D cancer models can also be built from self-assembling biomaterials such as peptide amphiphiles that form nanofiber networks to resemble the architecture of the ECM present within the immunosuppressive TME or hydrogel-in-hydrogel appraches [ , , ]. Self-assembly of peptides and molecules is driven for example by ionic interactions or hydrogen bonds, enabling rapid hydrogel formation and control over cell functions [ ]. These next-generation bioengineering strategies coupled with real-time spatial imaging, single-cell transcriptomic and proteomic profiling of multicellular 3D cultures help to delineate the tumor-immune crosstalk [ ].

## 3D-printed scaffolds
3D printing technologies have been leveraged to generate spatially controlled models in terms of scaffold size, shape, and porosity for studying tumor-immune cell interactions. Functionalized 3D-printed scaffolds made of polycaprolactone harbor the chemical properties required for immune cell expansion [ , ]. Other scaffold biomaterials such as alginate, which maintains the structural integrity in aqueous environments, enable efficient T cell retrieval and cell viability for real-time analysis during cancer progression. 3D-printed gelatin-based hydrogels have demonstrated long-term stability for encapsulated immunosuppressive cells, highlighting their potential to maintain functional cell persistence and support extended studies for evaluating T cell therapies [ , ].

## Recreating the tumor immune landscape of solid tumors
The development of advanced 3D cancer models has surpassed conventional 3D cell culture methods, enabling improved resemblance of tumor tissue and immunosuppressive characteristics of colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers. For example, a cancer-on-a-chip device has been developed to recreate the vascular-like network in colorectal cancer using patient-derived tissues [ ]. Treatment with cytotoxic drugs and a TGF-ß inhibitor show heterogenous patient-specific responses that capture the tumor-stroma crosstalk [ ]. However, the lack of immunosuppressive cells limits its application for testing of immunotherapies or personalized treatment strategies. A different cancer-on-a-chip device was used to co-culture ovarian cancer and endothelial cells together with CAFs to investigate the tumor-stroma crosstalk [ ]. The microfluidic 3D co-culture supported the formation of a vascular-like network that promoted matrix remodeling, metastatic spread and drug resistance [ ]. A hydrogel-based 3D model was applied to study the biomechanical properties of the TME of colorectal and pancreatic cancers [ ]. Softer hydrogel matrices resulted in reduced cell proliferation and metabolic activity compared to the stiffer hydrogel matrices [ ]. Despite the benefits of using these dynamic 3D cancer models, immune cells and the immunosuppressive TME were absent.
The inclusion of immune cells is a pre-requisite for the tumor-immune crosstalk. A 3D co-culture model of metastatic colorectal cancer cells together with dendritic cells showed that dendritic cells drive pro-tumorigenic events and disease progression [ ]. However, other cancer-supporting cells found in colorectal cancers were omitted but play important roles in regulating cell fate. Multicellular cancer spheroids, formed by pancreatic cancer and stellate cells, endothelial cells and monocytes using the hanging drop method, demonstrated the immunosuppressive role of the cellular components in the pancreatic TME [ ]. Multicellular spheroid cultures showed an increase in tumor-promoting M2 macrophages and PD-L1 expression, as well as decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. Cancer organoids, that contained patient-derived pancreatic cancer cells, human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived mesenchymal and endothelial cells, as well as TAMs, were used to study the diversity of TAMs and modulation of cancer cell properties [ ]. TAMs differentiated into M1 (pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor) and M2 (anti-inflammatory and pro-tumor) macrophages and modulated angiogenic and hypoxic factors to support cancer cell proliferation and survival [ ]. Investigating the crosstalk between TAMs and tissue resident macrophages may provide further insight into the immunosuppressive TME and therapeutic response.
By increasing the complexity of 3D cancer models, the spatial confinement of immune cells helps to track their transformation during cancer progression. 3D-printed scaffolds seeded with colorectal cancer and immune cells induce M2 macrophage differentiation and drug resistance [ ]. The spatial properties of 3D-printed scaffolds enable cancer cells to secrete factors that drive immunosuppression. However, the absence of CAFs and ECM proteins limits the tumor-stroma crosstalk for immunotherapy testing. To study the tumor-immune crosstalk in the ovarian TME, a multicellular 3D model that integrates five different cell types, including adipocytes, fibroblasts, patient-derived cancer and mesothelial cells, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC)-derived monocytes, has been developed [ ]. Stromal cell-derived signals facilitated M2 macrophage differentiation without the need of cytokines, highlighting the role of direct tumor-immune cell interactions [ ].
To study immune cell migration in the pancreatic TME, a three-channel microfluidic device was used to establish vascular and stromal barriers [ ]. Both lateral channels were seeded with endothelial and pancreatic stellate cells respectively, and the central channel contained cancer cells embedded in a collagen matrix. Added T cells migrated towards the cancer cell-containing channel, whereas the presence of the vascular barrier reduced T cell infiltration. Activated T cells displayed a higher migratory behavior towards the cancer cell-containing channel without the vascular barrier compared to non-activated T cells. The stromal barrier also impaired T cell function and promoted cancer cell proliferation, highlighting their role in T cell activation [ ]. Overall, the versatility of advanced 3D cancer models enables detailed analysis of tumor-immune cell interaction within a physiologically relevant TME, making them powerful tools for future developments in immuno-oncology.

## Assessing cell responses to immunotherapies

## Engineered cell-based therapies
The approval of CAR T cell therapy to eradicate cancer cells independent of the major histocompatibility complex started a paradigm shift in T cells in cancer research [ ]. CAR T cells are engineered T cells and composed of an extracellular domain for antigen recognition, a transmembrane and co-stimulatory/intracellular domain for T cell activation [ , ]. Although CAR T cell therapy is used to treat patients diagnosed with haematological diseases and B cell malignancies, responses in solid tumors remain unsatisfactory [ , , ]. This is primarily caused by the immunosuppressive TME, which impedes the infiltration, activation and persistence of CAR T cells [ ].
Biomaterials have evolved to improve the efficacy of CAR T cells against solid tumors. Cell-instructive alginate scaffolds were used to produce highly functional CAR T cells in an immunodeficient transplantation model [ ]. Scaffolds were seeded with human PBMCs and retroviral particles encoding CAR genes to engineer CAR T cells in a lymphoma transplantation model, with greater persistence than conventional CAR T cell therapy [ ]. In another study, a cancer-on-a-chip device was used to validate the cytotoxic effects of CAR T cell therapy in ovarian cancer [ ]. Microfluidic 3D co-cultures of ovarian cancer and endothelial cells together with fibroblasts were established. The microfluidic device led to the formation of a vascular-like network that improved the migration of CAR T cells against an ovarian cancer-specific antigen, into the ovarian TME. The inhibition of the matrix-modifying factor TGF-β promoted a more efficient intake of CAR T cells in 3D cultures, leading to cancer cell death [ ]. Although the interaction between cancer and CAR T cells was studied in cell monolayers and spheroids and linked to cell death receptor signaling, the mechanism of T cell proliferation and retention was not further investigated in the microfluidic 3D co-cultures. Cell killing in microfluidic 3D co-cultures can be determined for example by cell viability and metabolic assays or analysis of fluorescently tagged cell types.
Targeted delivery systems have been developed to administer immunotherapies to specific tumor sites. Biodegradable capsules containing a hydrogel and engineered therapeutic cells, including stem cells, macrophages, NK cells and CAR T cells, showed potential to treat solid tumors [ ]. The proliferation and migration of the encapsulated cells towards cancer cells was confirmed by cell viability assays and bioluminescent imaging. Encapsulated CAR T cells had a slow but direct anti-cancer effect when used in a murine brain tumor resection model, indicating their potential for localized delivery and therapeutic efficacy [ ].
Despite providing significant insights on CAR T cell activity, many studies still rely on cell monolayer cultures, murine and transplantation models, which lack the dynamic properties of the TME as seen in patients. Advanced 3D cancer models mimic the interaction of CAR T cells and cellular elements in the TME. Microgel-embedded CAR T cells, generated against an ovarian cancer-specific antigen, showed enhanced persistence and expansion rate even after several days of cell encapsulation [ ]. However, the recovered CAR T cells revealed no evident cytotoxic effects on ovarian cancer spheroids over a short period of time [ ]. Thus, CAR T cell release and effects need to be studied over longer periods of time, and CAR T cells may need specific culture medium conditions to yield the desired cytotoxic response in vitro. These 3D cancer models lack immunosuppressive cell types which are enriched in the TME of colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers, restricting their application to investigate anti-cancer effects of CAR T cells. Advanced 3D cancer-immune models that resemble the immunosuppressive TME of solid tumors may progress the screening of cell-based immunotherapy beyond currently used experimental, murine and transplantation models.

## Monoclonal antibody-based immunotherapy
Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting CLTA-4 and PD-1 with monoclonal antibodies, are effective in treating for example melanoma, bladder and lung cancers [ , , ]. However, immune checkpoint inhibitors have limited therapeutic efficacy to provide sustained anti-tumor immune responses in immunosuppressed tumors like colorectal and pancreatic cancers [ ]. Thus, multicellular 3D cancer models that integrate immune cells are used to evaluate immune checkpoint blockade in combination with chemotherapy (Table ).
An advanced 3D cancer model based on a star-shaped poly(ethylene glycol)-heparin hydrogel matrix was used to co-culture patient-derived CAFs, PBMCs and pancreatic cancer cells mimicking the immunosuppressive TME [ ]. Anti-tumor responses were tested using the CD11b agonist ADH-503 in combination with anti-PD-1 treatment and the chemotherapeutic drugs gemcitabine and paclitaxel. As a result, IL-6 and IL-8, two cytokines that promote immunosuppression in the pancreatic TME, were downregulated [ ]. Spatial imaging and single-cell profiling of the crosstalk between cancer and cancer-supporting cells and the impact of functionalized heparin may be applied to determine the cell-specific cytokine secretion pattern.
Another advanced 3D cancer model integrated a gelatin methacryloyl-based hydrogel matrix to co-culture human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells, monocytes and colorectal cancer cells to study the immunomodulatory effects of the stromal-macrophage crosstalk [ ]. The stromal elements facilitated the secretion of CCL2 and CCL5 and pro-tumorigenic factors, which activated and recruited TAMs to remodel the tumor immune microenvironment. TAMs accumulated in the stromal-rich regions in the colorectal TME. The upregulation of the immunomodulating factors PD-L1 and CD47 activated a macrophage and T cell-mediated anti-cancer immunity, providing the rationale for anti-CLTA4 or anti-PD-1 therapy [ ]. This highlights the need to capture phenotypic changes of macrophages in a stromal-rich TME to address questions regarding therapy resistance [ ]. To further decipher the tumor-immune crosstalk and therapeutic response, a micro-immune response on-a-chip device was developed using 3D co-cultures of breast cancer cells, CAFs and PBMCs [ ]. The microfluidic device facilitated the migration of immune cells through the stromal region towards the tumor-stroma interface, which was hindered by the presence of CAFs and CAF-secreted ECM. Combination treatment using the monoclonal antibody trastuzumab, targeting HER2, and IL-2 mediated the migration of NK cells and PBMCs towards the cancer cells, inducing cytolytic activity and cytokine secretion. Time-lapse imaging showed cancer cell death within minutes as a response to treatment [ ]. No immune cell death or changes in ECM organisation and composition were observed upon treatment, which may be attributed to the lack of a vascular-like network in the microfluidic model limiting its physiological relevance.
Emerging developments in addressing the tumor-immune crosstalk resulted in a cancer-on-a-bead platform to integrate more than one immune cell type. Pancreatic cancer cells embedded in gelatin methacryloyl hydrogels form a tumor core that is surrounded by CAFs embedded in gelatin methacryloyl-hyaluronic acid methacryloyl hydrogels forming a rigid stromal compartment [ ]. The addition of dendritic cells, macrophages and T cells led to increased levels of immunosuppressive factors (e.g. CCL2, CXCL2, IL-6, IL-8, PD-L1, TGF-ß), driving M2 macrophage differentiation and downregulation of mature dendritic cells and T cell activation. The addition of lymphocytes and treatment with nivolumab restored T cell activation, demonstrating the platform’s relevance for testing immunotherapies and targeting T cell exhaustion [ ]. However, the impact of the biomechanical properties of both tumor and stromal compartments on T cell infiltration, a critical barrier for immunotherapy testing, has not been investigated.

## Next-generation immunotherapies
Nanomedicine approaches produced immunotherapies involving nanoparticles [ ] and antibody drug conjugates [ ], which have broadened our knowledge in targeted drug delivery. These biocompatible therapeutic agents combine chemical molecules leading to improved therapeutic response with fewer side effects. A novel antibody drug conjugate fused with two different antibodies targeting the tumor antigen CD30 and the T cell co-receptor CD3, directed against the major histocompatibility complex, induced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and anti-cancer effects in Hodgkin lymphoma [ ]. Lipid-based nanoparticles loaded with agonists of the stimulator of interferon genes STING and the key activator of the innate immune response TLR4 stimulated an anti-tumor response in pancreatic cancer murine and transplantation models [ ]. Combination treatment using nanoparticles and the KRAS inhibitor trametinib and CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib increased interferon signaling and antigen presentation. In addition, the combination treatment regimen activated NK cells and T cell immunity and reduced the number of immunosuppressive cells and tumor growth in murine and transplantation models [ ]. To test the effect of drug-loaded nanoparticles and vascular delivery, a colorectal cancer-on-a-chip device was developed [ ]. The microfluidic device, comprising a central cancer cell-seeded channel and perfused lateral channels, enabled endothelial cell migration and formation of a vascular-like network. Drug-loaded nanoparticles induced cancer cell death, with reduced expression of MMP-1 (metastasis marker), caspase-3 (apoptotic marker), and Ki-67 (proliferation marker) [ ]. The independent evaluation of nanoparticle delivery, drug release, and therapeutic response in this microfluidic device allows the testing of personalized treatment strategies. Despite the potential of novel combination approaches including immunotherapies, not much research and validation have been performed in advanced 3D cancer models to determine their efficacy in solid tumors [ ]. Major limitations include the presence of a dense ECM barrier that promotes immunosuppressive cell populations and drives cancer cell adaptation, leading to reduced anti-tumor immune recognition, thereby highlighting the need for further optimisation to improve their translational relevance. Addressing these gaps is critical to accurately recapitulate immunosuppressive mechanisms within advanced 3D cancer models and their application to immunotherapy testing (Fig. ).

 

## Outlook and future perspectives
Understanding the immunosuppressive TME in solid tumors will transform our treatment strategies for colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers. While conventional cell monolayer, animal and organoid models have provided fundamental insights into tumor-immune cell interaction, their limited physiological relevance has contributed to poor clinical translation. Immunodeficient transplantation models such as patient-derived xenografts offer an improved approach to replicate tumor biology. However, their utility is restricted by using an immunodeficient host, long implantation times, limited access to patient samples from late-stage disease and altered genetic profiles that may not fully capture patient-specific tumor-immune responses. The emerging discipline of tumor tissue engineering has resulted in the design of advanced 3D cancer models to deconstruct tumor tissues and study immunological processes in disease progression and resistance to treatment including immunotherapy for immunosuppressed tumors (Fig.  a). To apply advanced 3D cancer models to the testing of immunotherapies, several key components must be defined: the source of immune cells, delivery route, and delivery method (Fig.  b).
Many 3D cell culture models for colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers lack immunosuppressive factors and other important characteristics of the TME, such as varying oxygen conditions (e.g. hypoxia) or pH (e.g. acidosis). Although some 3D approaches integrate basic immune components like monocytes or PBMCs and other stromal cells, they still fail to integrate the complex tumor-immune crosstalk, omitting crucial immunosuppressive cell types, such as TAMs, Tregs and MDSCs. Donor- or patient-derived PBMCs are used as a general source of immune cells. Nevertheless, availability, inter-donor variability, patient accessibility, and ethical regulations hamper the process of developing preclinically relevant models. Alternatively, biobanks and standard differentiation protocols for innate immune cells using cord blood, hematopoietic and human-induced pluripotent stem cells are viable alternatives and suitable for long-term 3D cultures (Fig. b). However, matched patient-derived samples are difficult to acquire, and the suitability of non-matched samples needs to be carefully considered (Table ). Supplementing to cell culture medium with recombinant plasma components may facilitate the metabolic activity and growth of immune cells in 3D cultures. The addition of cytokines, for example IL-12, IL-15 and IL-18, or genetic modifications to produce NK cells are emerging strategies used to improve anti-cancer treatment [ ]. However, to date, most cytotoxicity studies using NK cells in colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers have been largely restricted to murine models.

 

Biomaterial-based 3D platforms offer a scalable and efficient approach for generating large quantities of immune cells for preclinical testing. Hydrogel-based 3D cancer models mimic the biochemical and biomechanical features of the immunosuppressive TME (Fig. a). Beyond structural support, these platforms can be engineered to improve immune cell expansion and function, enabling the development of personalized treatment strategies. Injectable hydrogels or beads (in vivo) and 3D-printed scaffolds (ex vivo), loaded with cytokines, immune cells, and drugs, have been designed to deliver sustained anti-tumor responses through controlled release of bioactive factors at tumor sites [ ] (Fig. b). During tumor development, the ECM undergoes continuous remodeling, altering its stiffness and viscoelastic properties to support tumor growth [ ]. These dynamic changes also regulate the trafficking and delivery of biomolecules, making the ECM a critical barrier for therapeutic efficacy. Advanced 3D cancer models can be designed to recreate the spatiotemporal ECM remodeling. Hydrogels made of synthetic or composite biomaterials that contain defined cell binding and degradable sites promote ECM remodeling and cell states in cancer organoids [ ]. Our still narrow knowledge about the multi-level role of matrix modifications in the TME hampers studies for targeted drug delivery systems. Immune resistance develops by constantly reprogramming the TME to escape anti-cancer effects. Immune cells interact with ECM components through adhesive or degradable domains or stimulatory receptors to regulate immunosuppression. Although the mechanisms driving this process are poorly understood, this underscores our need to capture the tumor-immune crosstalk to design novel combination strategies administering immunotherapies together with matrix-targeting compounds [ ] (Figure b).
Disrupting the dense ECM barrier using tumor antigen-specific immunotherapies enhances T cell infiltration in pancreatic cancer. Blocking FAP+ CAFs using target-specific CAR T cell infiltration into the TME loosens the dense ECM, synergistically enhancing T cell migration and killing of cancer cells. This example demonstrates how the endogenous activation of the adaptive immune response augments CAR T cell efficacy [ ]. CAFs attract immune cells by producing cytokines that activate signaling pathways to mask the presentation of tumor antigens. Targeting CAFs and activators of the innate immune system, such as TLRs, together with inducers of cell death improves responses in desmoplastic tumors. Similarly, conjugating PD-1 and CTLA-4 antibodies with matrix-targeting compounds enhances responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors in pancreatic cancer [ ]. However, the immunosuppressive role of other tumor checkpoint receptors, like LAG-3, TIM-3 and TIGIT, in colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers remains unclear. The secretion of factors (e.g. CCL2, CCL5, MCS-F, CXCL12, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNF) promotes signaling via TAMs and MDSCs to modify the ECM in colorectal and pancreatic cancers. In this context, administering therapeutic compounds specific to aberrant ECM molecules may improve the responses in solid tumors (Fig. b). Blocking the TAM receptors CCR2 and FRβ with a combination of immuno- and chemotherapy enhances the proliferation of cytotoxic T cells and expression of effector genes in pancreatic and ovarian cancers [ , ]. Loosening the ECM barrier by targeting key factors that contribute to the stiffness of tumor tissues, such as CAFs, TAMs and TGF-ß, may activate the NK and T cell-mediated immunity in solid tumors. High-resolution techniques, such as spatial imaging and single-cell profiling of patient-derived tissues, can locate ECM-modifying immunosuppressive cells [ ]. Single-cell spatial imaging of apCAFs revealed their association with mesothelial- and fibrocyte-like populations in colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian, cancers [ ]. Pancreatic cancers were enriched in fibrocyte-like apCAFs in lymphocyte-rich regions, with reduced expression of co-stimulatory markers (e.g. CD40, CD80, CD86) and increased secreted phosphoprotein 1 that is linked to disease progression and therapy resistance. On the other hand, mesothelial-like apCAFs were more abundant in colorectal and ovarian cancers and locate near tumor regions and involved in the tumor-immune crosstalk [ ].
One of the key challenges in immunotherapy testing is achieving sustained anti-tumor responses after ECM penetration without causing adverse side effects. Novel therapeutic strategies, including immune cell engagers, which are therapeutic molecules designed to redirect NK and T cells, ECM modifiers, hydrogel-encapsulated drugs, nanoparticles and dual-targeting CAR T cells, lead to better on-target effects (Fig. b). These are being developed to replace unsafe, toxic and expensive viral transfer techniques. Several studies elucidate the potential of multi-targeting NK and T cells encapsulated in hydrogels for non-toxic delivery to the TME [ – ]. Nevertheless, CAR T cell therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors still fail to provide sustained therapeutic responses in solid tumors. Key factors driving this effect include a low persistence and expression of non-target biomarkers on immunosuppressive cells, leading to off-target effects and systemic toxicity. Dual-targeting CAR T cell strategies may reduce the need for lymphodepletion by enhancing peripheral expansion and persistence [ ]. In immunocompetent murine models, CAR T cells targeting both CD19 on B cells and tumor antigens such as mesothelin improved systemic expansion and anti-cancer efficacy, resulting in increased survival [ ]. Despite tumor clearance, the mechanistic contribution of B cells to overcome the ECM barrier and tumor infiltration remains unclear. Another dual-targeting approach to reduce off-target effects and systemic toxicity are CAR T cells that have been modified to secrete bifunctional fusion proteins such as PD-L1-cytokine constructs (e.g. IL-12, IL-15, TGF-β) [ ]. PD-L1-IL-12 fused CAR T cells enhanced intratumoral IFNγ production and target engagement, promoting sustained anti-tumor responses and reduced levels of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages [ ]. However, the efficacy of this dual-targeting CAR T cell approach still needs to be validated using patient-derived samples with varying PD-L1 expression.
Predictive and computational tools to detect biomarkers and the mutational burden of tumors are still limited and will be vital to enhance the efficacy of novel immunotherapies and combination strategies [ ]. Patient-derived organoids from esophago-gastric adenocarcinoma have been used to predict therapeutic response through biomarker-based stratification, revealing that the lowest drug sensitivity corresponds to patients with poor pathological response [ ]. Although the study was limited by the relatively small cohort (n = 13), organoids were successfully established from most patient-derived tissues and generated within a clinically relevant timeframe of about three weeks, highlighting their suitability for therapeutic planning and organoid-guided clinical trials [ ].

 

To interpret the tumor dynamics, microfluidic or cancer-on-a-chip devices are most suited. In microphysiological systems with constant oxygen and nutrient supply, functional T cells are expanded and the efficacy of CAR T cells analyzed [ , ], enabling preclinical drug discovery [ ]. The complex fabrication of these devices often prioritizes vascularisation and cell migration over the modeling of immunosuppressive tumor-immune cell interactions. In addition, extended cell culture periods with continuous fluid exchange and shear stress reduce the fidelity of the spatial organisation of tumor and immune cells. To maintain the spatial distribution of TME components, 3D printing technologies have been leveraged using bioinks with improved biomechanical stability. 3D-printed scaffolds composed of multiple biomaterials help track the movement of T cells into the core tumor region [ ]. However, these scaffolds still lack essential immunosuppressive factors required to test the effect of CAR T cells and immune checkpoint inhibitors, reducing the translational aspects of the hostile TME. The requirement for regulated facilities, specialized expertise, and high production costs further delays the implementation of microfluidic and 3D-printed models as rapid diagnostic tools.
Overall, advanced 3D cancer models based natural and synthetic polymers continue to evolve for studying colorectal, pancreatic and ovarian cancers. Advanced 3D colorectal cancer models are based for example on gelatin methacryloyl hydrogels due to their tunable mechanical properties and ability to support cell growth and macrophage polarisation [ ]. Collagen scaffolds and microfluidic devices further enable the inclusion of dendritic cells, formation of a vascular-like network, and dynamics of drug delivery, although sill lack immune or stromal complexity [ , ]. Similarly, 3D-printed scaffolds induce macrophage differentiation but often fail to fully reproduce the ECM heterogeneity observed in patient tissues [ ]. Advanced 3D pancreatic cancer models are based for example on poly(ethylene glycol) hydrogels, and composite biomaterials such as star-shaped poly(ethylene glycol)-heparin and gelatin methacryloyl-hyaluronic acid methacryloyl hydrogel matrices [ , , ]. These systems aim to replicate the stiffness of tumor tissues, immunosuppression, and the ECM barrier while enabling evaluation of immunotherapies and drug diffusion. While microfluidic devices combined with fibrin and collagen matrices promote growth of cancer and immune cells, nanoparticle-based systems enhance drug delivery and immune cell trafficking [ , ]. ]. Norbornene-functionalised gelatin and microgel systems further support immune cell persistence and expansion, though they may not fully capture cytotoxic functionality or long-term tumor-immune cell interaction [ , ].
Despite providing physiological and biological insights, biomaterial-based platforms are still associated with technical bottlenecks. The diversity of biomaterial formulations and the lack of standardized protocols hinder manufacturing and scalability. Cell-laden hydrogels cannot always match the size and stiffness range of tumor tissues due to a low cell seeding density and growth. High-throughput techniques like cell- and tissue-selective or spatial proteomics are used to dissect molecular subtypes of cancer based on distinct properties of each cell in the TME. However, extraction and downstream processing lead to the loss of cellular material, protein and RNA required for high-throughput profiling. Gentle and non-destructive extraction methods like snap-freezing techniques are applied to address some of these drawbacks. While most hydrogel-based systems are biocompatible, their degradation and release of residual components may still alter immune cell behavior in unintended ways. When establishing multicellular 3D cancer models, the time required for cell growth and differentiation, functionalized or stimuli-responsive biomaterials, and adequate training, manufacturing and sterilization facilities need to be carefully considered (Table ).
In conclusion, advanced 3D cancer models are new approach methodologies to incorporate the tumor-immune compartment and evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of immunotherapies. Selecting relevant immunosuppressive cell types found in solid tumors will streamline the construction of next-generation 3D cell cultures. Their combination with predictive and computational tools, high-throughput omics, data science and bioreactors fast-tracks the screening oftherapeutic response. For example, a perfusion bioreactor has been developed to model lymphoid tissue responses to immunotherapies. Lymphoid tissue pieces grown in the perfusion bioreactor maintained their architecture and preserved T and B cell viability over several days [ ]. Treatment with immunotherapeutic agents reduced the viability of cancer cells by activating pro-inflammatory cytokines and T cells [ ]. Thus, integrating scalable approaches with ECM-mimicking biomaterials, that also protect cells from shear stress, for long-term 3D co-culture of cancer and immune cells allow for preclinical assessment. Exploring biomaterials responsive to external stimuli is advantageous to trace the infiltration and migration of immune cells in real-time [ ]. Next-generation custom-made 3D cancer models will be essential to achieve targeted therapeutic responses. Biofabrication techniques integrated with robotics and biotechnology tools enable us to design interdisciplinary concepts for engineering tumor tissues and accelerate immuno-oncology [ ].
