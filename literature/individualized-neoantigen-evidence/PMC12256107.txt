V(D)J recombination, along with N- and P-nucleotide insertions at the V(D)J junctions (also known as complementarity-determining regions (CDRs)) contributes to TCR sequence diversity, with estimates ranging from 1015 to 1016 different TCR sequences. The variety of TCR-pMHCs allows T cells to recognize numerous epitopes, and presents challenges in screening TCR-pMHC interactions.
During the past 3 years, machine learning computational methods have made great strides in predicting TCR-antigen binding specificity, thus complementing expensive, time-consuming, and low-throughput experimental procedures. These methods mainly address two predictions, the peptides which can be presented by a given MHC allele (identifying T cell epitopes) and the TCRs which can recognize a given pMHC complex or a given peptide (predicting TCR specificity). To identify T cell epitopes, allele-specific and pan-specific approaches are used. Allele-specific methods train a separate model for each MHC allele and limit predictions to individual MHC alleles. Pan-specific methods can predict binding affinities not only for the alleles that are present in the training data, but also new alleles not seen during training. In contrast, predicting TCR specificity involves peptide-specific model and pan-peptide methods. Peptide-specific methods predict TCR binding specificity for known peptides in the training data [ ], whereas pan-peptide methods predict TCR binding specificity for peptides that were not present in the training data [ ]. Predicting “unseen” epitopes and TCRs is challenging, as it requires creative strategies, such as meta-learning and transfer learning.
Building an accurate TCR-pMHC prediction model requires a large amounts of high-quality experimental data as inputs. Due to the complexity of TCR-pMHC interactions, experimental measurement data often contain noise and uncertainties. Improving the accuracy and consistency of experimental measurements is essential for enhancing data quality. Moreover, factors, such as data distribution bias, label accuracy, and feature extraction strategies, affect the predictive performance of the machine learning models. Data distribution bias, often caused by limitations in TCR-pMHC data collection, can result in poor predictive performance for certain types of TCR-pMHC pairs. Therefore, strategies, such as data augmentation, are needed to address this issue. Inaccurate labels can severely affect the model’s learning and generalization capabilities of models. Importantly, selecting the appropriate machine learning model architecture that completely captures the complex interaction mechanisms of TCR-pMHC binding is the key to improve the predictive performance. Given these reasons, it is necessary to systematically learn their specific impacts, and the corresponding strategies should be designed to further improve the model performance.
While MHC-I is more critical in the immune responses of CD8+ T cells [ ], the prediction of MHC-I binding requires more training data. Predicting the binding of MHC-I molecules is more successful than that of MHC-II molecules because MHC-I molecules could present the shorter antigens, are more well-defined binding affinity (BA) and specificity, and have a well-dissected closed peptide-binding groove at both ends. Several reviews have briefly summarized the TCR-pMHC binding prediction methods, including the prediction of TCR-antigen specificity by Hudson et al. [ ], and machine learning (ML) methods about immunogens identification by Bravi [ ]. Both reviews lack methods evaluations on external independent datasets. Buckley et al. performed methods evaluation on pMHC binding [ ], but they ignored the TCR specificity. In this review, we have primarily focused on machine learning methods for TCR-pMHC prediction limited to MHC-I. The effects of data size, quality, distribution, and feature extraction methods on TCR-pMHC prediction are also discussed in this review. Finally, we have elaborately reviewed the current limitations and possible directions of this methodology for predicting pMHC binding or TCR binding specificity in future and proposed a roadmap for TCR-pMHC binding prediction by machine learning.

## Key events involved in TCR-pMHC binding

## Antigen peptide processing and presentation
Human MHC molecules can present a wide variety of antigens due to their extreme polymorphism [ , ]. The processing and presentation of antigen peptides, mediated by MHC molecules, form the basis of T cell immune responses ( ). MHC-I molecules are expressed in all nucleated cells, and bind endogenous antigen peptides processed by proteasomes and present them to the TCRs of CD8+ T cells [ ]. Subsequently, these CD8+ T cells could recognize and kill exogenous pathogens via dendritic cell mediated cross-presentation process [ ]. MHC-I molecules typically present epitopes with 8–10 amino acids [ ].
 

## TCR-pMHC binding and T cell activation
TCRs recognize pMHCs presented on the antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and adopt a conserved canonical docking topology. CDR3 loops generally provide the most discriminative interactions with peptides, CDR2 loops interact mainly with the MHC molecules, and CDR1 loops tend to present soft interactions with both peptide and MHC molecules [ ]. According to the crystal structure data of the resolved TCR-pMHC ternary complexes, most of the V gene coding region in TCR β chain (Vβ) region is positioned over the α-helix in the α1 domain of the MHC-I heavy chain or of the MHC-II α chain, and the V gene coding region in TCR α chain (Vα) region is positioned over the α-helix in the α2 domain of the MHC-I heavy chain or the α-helix in the β1 domain of the MHC-II β chain [ ]. Thus, the TCR recognition process has MHC limitations.
The binding of the TCR to the pMHC complex, assisted by coreceptors (CD4 or CD8), is crucial for T cell activation ( ). This initiates a specific recognition signal through the CD3 signaling module. Among the three signaling subunits (CD3γε, CD3δε, and CD3ζζ) of CD3 molecules, CD3ε and ζ chains are inserted into the cell membrane [ ]. Binding of TCR and pMHC binding opens calcium channels, leading to a local influx of Ca2+. This triggers electrostatic interactions, dissociating the CD3ζζ from the cell membrane, exposing the immune receptor tyrosine activation motifs which can be phosphorylated by tyrosine kinases, transmitting signals for T cell activation [ ]. Another important signal for T cell activation is the interaction between co-stimulatory molecules on APC surfaces and their corresponding receptors on T cells. Both stimulatory and inhibitory signals were observed ( ). Inhibitory signals mediated by cell surface molecules, such as CTLA-4 and programmed death 1 (PD-1), play important roles in the induction and maintenance of T cell tolerance. Conversely, positive co-stimulatory signals mediated by CD28 and CD40L on the T cell surface could regulate the initial activation of naïve T cells and control their effector and memory functions [ , ]. These activated T cells exert specific cytotoxic effects on the target cells [ ].

## Available data resources to train TCR-pMHC models
Two scenarios have been reviewed according to the training data resources: the published articles with advanced experimental technologies and well-curated databases. The label accuracy and data size are distinct according to the resource.
Mass spectrometry data, referred to as eluted ligands (EL), have been extensively used to acquire paired pMHC. EL data typically consist of peptides with mixed specificities because the antibodies used to purify pMHC complexes are mostly pan- or locus-specific. This requires a prior user-biased pMHC annotation criteria [ ], resulting in large datasets, albeit with some mislabeling. In contrast to EL data, pMHC BA data only model a single event of pMHC binding, providing higher label accuracy but limiting prediction performance. With the known pMHC, multimer technology has been widely used to screen for antigen-specific TCRs [ ]. Multimer+ T cells are isolated and subjected to high-throughput TCR sequencing at either the single-cell or bulk level [ , ]. However, data retrieved from this technology often exhibit high background noise due to nonspecific binding of multimer binding. Ex vivo experimental verification ex vivo is another strategy to determine T cell specificity. In this method, peptide libraries are used to stimulate T cells ex vivo, followed by the measurement of fluorescence intensity and cytokine/interferon expression level [ ]. In such cases, TCR-epitope pairs are validated individually, thus producing smaller training datasets.
With the advancement of experimental and sequencing technologies, a considerable amount of TCR-pMHC data have been accumulated and recorded in several databases, including IEDB [ ], VDJdb [ ], IMGT [ ], and so on ( ). These databases displayed an incomplete TCR-pMHC information more or less. The researchers would clean the training datasets based on the meta information they collect and the input required by the machine learning models.
 

## Encoding TCR-pMHC molecules
Data encoding is a vital step in preparing data for machine learning tasks, serving as a bridge between human-readable data and machine-proccessable data. The TCR-pMHC data are typically decomposed into amino acid sequences of TCR, peptides, and MHC molecules, VDJ gene usage information, and spatial configuration ( ).

## Amino acid sequence encoding
Most of the machine learning methods use antigenic peptides, the MHC-I pseudosequence, and the CDR3 sequence of the TCR β chain as inputs. The peptides presented by MHC-I molecules are usually 8–10 amino acids in length [ ]. Classical MHC-I pseudosequences include 34 peptide-contacting residues that are within a distance of 4.0Å from the bound peptide [ ]. Other pseudosequences representing MHC-I molecules were also discussed in various studies [ ]. However, it remains unclear whether the pseudosequence is better at predicting TCR-pMHC binding. For TCR sequence encoding, in addition to the CDR3 region, evaluating the significance of CDR1 and CDR2 regions of the TCRβ and TCRα chains in predictive tasks is necessary. Notably, methods using entire TCR or MHC sequences show superior performance compared to those using partial sequences [ ]. This suggests that machine learning methods that incorporate more comprehensive sequence information perform better than those that rely on partial sequences.
Given an amino acid sequence, each amino acid is encoded into a vector embedding, providing a numerical representation of the sequence. Current encoding schemes often aim to capture prior domain knowledge of amino acids as similarities between vectors. While one-hot encoding assumes no prior knowledge, the BLOcks SUbstitution Matrix (BLOSUM) [ ] captures evolutionary relationships, and both AA-index [ ] and Atchley factors [ ] capture physicochemical properties.

## Structural encoding
The conformation of the TCR-pMHC determines its binding kinetics and affinity. Incorporating TCR-pMHC structural data can enhance the ability to capture crucial structural features, resulting in more accurate binding predictions [ ]. Contact map-based embedding and one-hot encoding of secondary structure angles are often used to convert the spatial arrangement of protein atoms into numerical representations [ , ]. The contact map encodes the spatial proximity between amino acid residues, considering physical interactions and folding patterns of the complex, thereby providing a more detailed representation than sequence-based embeddings. With the development of protein structure-resolving technologies, the prediction of the TCR-pMHC binding will widely utilize both sequence and structural data in future.

## Pretrained hidden embeddings in neural network
Advances in machine learning have partially shifted the focus from handcrafted features, such as those described above, to automatic representation learning. In this case, a complex neural network-based model is used to learn hidden features that contain intricate functional information regarding amino acid sequences.
A deep unsupervised model of protein sequences has recently emerged with the advent of large language models (LLMs) such as bidirectional encoder representation from transformers (BERT) and generative pretrained transformer (GPT) [ ]. These pretrained protein sequence models have proven effective in learning meaningful embeddings of amino acid sequences and secondary structures. And they have also been successfully used to predict contact sites between proteins. Pretrained protein language models include UniRep [ ], ESM [ ], ProtTrans [ ], Progen [ ], ProtGPT2 [ ], and xTrimoPGLM [ ]. TABR-BERT leverages BERT to pretrain a TCR embedding model and a pMHC embedding model separately. An MLP-based TCR-pMHC prediction model was trained based on these embeddings. ROBERTa [ ] was proposed to improve the BERT model in 2019. Based on pretrained ROBERTa model with label-agnostic protein sequences, MHCRoBERTa used transfer learning method to predict peptide–MHC class I binding. In addition to pretrained LLMs, the pretrained TCR-pMHCs models were also widely used. pMTNet [ ] uses hidden layer features from a pretrained auto-encoder to train the downstream neural network for predicting the TCR binding specificity of class I pMHCs. TCRBert [ ] leverages unlabeled TCR sequences to learn general, versatile representations of TCR sequences, enabling numerous downstream applications.
Hence, these pretrained models provide valuable resources to facilitate TCR-pMHC prediction performance, potentially achieving superior results. Inspired by the need for models designed for fine-tuned, we believe that integrating transfer learning (fine-tuning) with meta-learning algorithms could be a promising avenue for future exploration.

To encompass a comprehensive range of TCR-pMHC prediction methods, we conducted a literature review utilizing the PubMed Advanced Search Builder. The list of retrieved methods was manually filtered and supplemented, as illustrated in . Ultimately, we reviewed and compiled a total of 22 pMHC prediction methods and 30 TCR prediction algorithms, which are presented in and .
 

## Peptide–MHC binding prediction
The peptide-binding site of MHC-I molecules contains six pockets. The peptide extends from the A pocket to the deep F pocket, preferring some anchor sites and enabling amino acid selection [ ]. Most of the machine learning methods just predict the binding likelihood of pMHC pairs rather than the immunogenicity of a peptide. Both classification and continuous regression tasks have been performed, in which pMHC binding likelihood and BA were predicted, respectively ( , ).
Position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM)-based motif deconvolution and identification methods were early developed to score the pMHC binding likelihood. Examples include SYFPEITHI [ ], PSSMHCpan [ ], PickPocket ], and MixMHCpred2.2 [ ]. These methods can integrate the peptides of varying lengths. Anchor-restrained homology modeling methods, such as PANDORA [ ], use MHC’s high structural homology molecules and anchoring positions of bound peptides to constrain the conformational search space, and therefore effectively producing an ensemble of three-dimensional (3D) models. While this method can achieve high accuracy, it requires high computational resources ( ).
Machine learning-based models have been extensively developed over the past 3 years, such as NetMHCpan-4.1 [ ], NetMHC 4.0 [ ], MHCflurry 2.0 [ ], BigMHC [ ], and TranspHLA [ ]. These methods use peptide and HLA sequences as inputs and calculate their binding likelihood or BA. BERTMHC [ ] incorporates pretrained high-dimensional protein language models. The performance of using amino acid sequences or hidden features as model inputs remains to be evaluated. Pretrained LLMs with billions of parameters may be valuable resources for predicting new epitopes in future machine learning models. Utilizing structural information to predict pMHC binding is another challenge. Researchers have shown that AlphaFold2 holds great promise for providing structural insights into a wide range of peptide–protein complexes [ ], serving as a starting point for the detailed characterization and manipulation of these interactions, including pMHC interactions. TFold [ ] is an AlphaFold-based pipeline for pMHC structural modeling.

## Antigen-specific TCR prediction
Antigen-specific TCR prediction methods can be broadly divided into three categories: (1) supervised methods with known pMHC information; (2) unsupervised TCR sequence representations; and (3) protein docking and protein–protein interaction-based strategies. In this section, we will review and discuss these methods ( , ).

## Supervised antigen-specific TCR prediction algorithms
As we mentioned previously, the training data size, data integrity, the label accuracy, data distribution, and feature extraction strategy determine the model performance. Before single-cell technology occurrence, the training data were mainly obtained from bulk sequence of T cells captured by pMHC multimers. This sequence could produce several single-strand TCR sequences, particularly β chains that were of notable concern [ ]. Therefore, most methods could only predict peptide-TCR binding based on single-stranded TCR β chain sequence data. These methods utilize either the CDR3 region or the CDR1, CDR2 [ , ], and CDR2.5 [ ] regions paired with antigenic peptides. With the development of single-cell sequencing technologies, an explosive growth has been observed in paired TCR α and β chain data [ ]. Machine learning methods incorporating paired TCR chains have shown better performance than those using only a single chain [ , , , ]. Zero-shot, few-shot, and majority-shot machine learning frameworks have been developed based on the frequency of “seen” or “unseen” peptides.
The convolution neural network (CNN) [ ] was used to extract sequence features in the early stage; however, it did not consider the correlation among amino acids, and missed their mutually constrained relationships. Recurrent neural network (RNN)-based feature extraction methods, such as long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent unit (GRU) [ , ], make up for this deficiency. However, training based on RNN architectures can be slow due to their recurrent nature, particularly with long sequences. For a given TCR, distinct CDR regions or amino acid sites carry different weights in antigen peptide binding. The attention allows models to selectively focus on relevant parts of the input sequence and generate each element of the output sequence, therefore improving accuracy and capturing long-range dependencies in tasks. Context attention [ ], gate-based attention [ , ], multi-head attention[ , ], and cross-attention mechanisms [ ] have been widely applied.
Large amount of unseen epitopes exist, therefore the epitope-specific tasks cannot be readily extrapolated to that of the unseen epitope settings. Meta-learning or transfer learning fine-tuned models based on LLMs-derived BERT have significantly improved the generalization performance of models, such as TABR-BERT [ ], RandBert-Peptide [ ], and SC-AIR-BERT [ ]. These models are trained on hundreds of thousands to millions of antigen peptides and millions to billions of TCR sequences to extract high-dimensional potential features. The release of AlphaFold2 [ ] has provided millions of predicted protein structures predicted with high accuracy. A few models have integrated the TCR-pMHC structures as inputs, calculating the CDR3 structures using AlphaFold2, which has significantly improved TCR-pMHC binding prediction accuracy of TCR-pMHC binding [ ]. However, these models were trained in a peptide-specific manner, and their generalization performance needs to be further evaluated.
Model generalization is crucial in machine learning because a trained model may not perform well on new or unseen data. If a model fails to generalize, it becomes impractical and unreliable for practical applications. Here, we evaluated the generalization ability of six TCR-pMHC prediction methods, including ERGO-AE (auto-encoder), ERGO-LSTM (LSTM), Imrex (CNN), ATM-TCR (attention), BERTrand-peptide (BERT), and TABR-BERT (BERT) using self-curated unseen data. The TCR-pMHC pairs were retrieved from VDJdb, IEDB, McPAS, and ImmuneCODE databases and filtered using the following criteria: (1) Human source, (2) MHC-I restricted, (3) peptides length (8–11 aa), and (4) CDR3β length (10–20aa). A total of 154 483 unique pairs were identified. Several peptides contain thousands of paired TCRs, resulting in higher predictive values for TCRs paired with similar peptide sequences. To minimize this effect, downsampling was applied to limit each peptide (major shot) to a maximum of 100 TCR pairings [ ]. Moreover, the TCR-pMHC pairs found in the training sets of these six methods and when the HLA alleles were not included in any trained datasets were excluded. Finally, 8, 234 unseen pMHC-CDR3β pairs were kept to evaluate their generalization. In order to clarify which methods are most effective under specific conditions, we manually categorized and downsampled the antigens into three disease conditions including virus (7, 007 pairs), tumor (687 pairs), and antoimmune disease (117 pairs). Then six TCR-pMHC prediction methods were evaluated, separately ( and ). The negative control was produced by randomly assigning CDR3β of TCR repertoires from healthy donors to existed pMHC in the positive dataset [ ]. The ratio of positive and negative CDR3β-pMHC pairs was 1:1. As a result, the LSTM, Auto-Encoder, CNN, and attention embedding frameworks showed poor generalization, with an AUC value of ~0.5. By contrast, the BERT architecture performed better performance than the above methods, with an area under the curve of the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.758 and an AUC of precision recall curve (PR-AUC) of 0.771 ( and ). Although both BERTrand-peptide and TABR-BERT utilize the BERT framework, TABR-BERT demonstrated superior performance across the three disease conditions assessed, while the former model exhibited a significant decline in performance within the cancer and autoimmune disease subsets ( and ). This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in training data size ( ). Notably, in the context of autoimmune disease, the performances of the ImRex and ERGO-AE models are comparable to that of TABR-BERT. Thus, the BERT architecture shows considerable potential for extracting CDR3β-pMHC sequence features and embeddings, particularly when the training dataset is sufficiently large, thereby enhancing the model’s generalization ability. These analyses offer valuable guidance for selecting TCR-pMHC prediction methods across various disease conditions.
 

## Unsupervised TCR clustering method
In the absence of labels, clustering methods are often used to determine the similarity between a group of TCRs. TCRs within the same clusters are presumed to share antigen specificity, and clustering can help discover new epitope-specific TCRs by grouping them with TCRs of known specificity [ ]. This kind of method typically involves aligning TCR CDR sequences of TCR CDR regions, and calculating their similarity using substitution matrices, such as BLOSUM or Point Accepted Mutation amino acid substitution matrix, followed by graph clustering/hierarchical clustering [ ]. Hamming distance-based (ClusTCR) [ ], Smith–Waterman alignment-based (TCR dist) [ ], and motif-based clustering methods (GLIPH2) are often used to compute the TCR sequence similarity, considering factors, such as V gene family and CDR3 region length [ , ]. To improve clustering purity and handle large cohorts of TCR-seq datasets, GIANA [ ] converts the sequence alignment and clustering problem into a classic nearest-neighbor search in a high-dimensional Euclidean space.
Notably, TCR sequence similarity is not equivalent to their structural similarity. Single amino acid variations can result in significant structural changes, and amino acid sequences with substantial variations may share similar structures. Although the homology modeling, which was developed to predict the TCR structures of their CDR regions and define their structural similarity [ ], represents a novel approach, the accuracy requires further investigation.

## Protein–protein docking methods
Supervised or unsupervised antigen-specific TCR prediction methods only explore the binding ability and BA of peptide-TCR pairs. They do not, however, resolve the binding patterns or interacting residues in TCR-pMHC complexes. Protein docking methods provide novel insights to address these problems, including the universal docking tools RossettaDock [ ], ZDOCK [ ], HADDOCK [ , ], ClusPro [ ], and AlphaFold2 multimer [ ]. These methods have been applied to produce TCR-pMHC structural models, but they often show limited success with no more than 50% of prediction pairs displaying root mean square deviation≤1Å [ ]. Algorithms specifically developed for docking TCRs on pMHC, such as TCRFlexDock [ ], TCRpMHCmodels [ ], TCRmodel/TCRmodel2 [ , ], and TCRDock [ ] achieve better performance compared to general protein–protein docking methods.
The protein docking model facilitates the identification of small-molecule binding sites at protein–protein interfaces. For example, DeepInter [ ] exhibits excellent performance in predicting interaction sites in transmembrane proteins using stacked generalization ensembles of ultradeep residual neural networks. Similarly, PIsToN [ ] evaluates protein-binding interfaces with vision transformer model at atomic-level resolution. Protein language model-embedded geometric graphs provide valuable resources for improving the prediction accuracy of protein–protein interfaces and rigid body docking affinity. PLMGraph-Inter [ ] transforms multiple protein language models using graph encoders to predict inter-protein contacts, whereas TEIM-Res [ ] utilizes pretrained coarse-grained TCR–antigen interactions at the molecular level to predict fine-grained interactions after fine-tuning. Advances in machine learning, high-throughput sequencing, and single-cell sequencing, along with the accumulation of large-scale datasets and multidisciplinary collaborations, will drive remarkable progress in this field.

## Characterization of antigen-reactive T cells by gene expression profiles
T cell phenotypes that are activated through TCR-dependent mechanisms differ from those activated via cytokine-dependent pathways. This enables the characterization of antigen-reactive T cells using transcriptome data. T cell phenotypes are determined by antigens, TCR sequences, and immune strategies [ ], prompting to develop algorithms to identify antigen-specific T cells using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data with antigen specificity information. Identification of tumor-reactive T cells using individual or combined phenotypic markers has been well established [ ]. CXCL13 expression can effectively identify both exhausted and non-exhausted tumor-reactive CD8+ T cell clones by analyzing scRNA-seq datasets [ ]. Similarly, the combinations of cell surface markers have also been used to track COVID-19 vaccine-induced T cell responses [ ]. Most of the antigen-reactive T cell markers have been identified by analyzing differentially expressed genes in antigen-specific T cells. Machine learning algorithms provide an opportunity to identify antigen-reactive T cells more accurately. For example, Tan et al. trained a machine learning classifier (predicTCR) to identify tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells in an antigen-agnostic manner using scRNA-seq data [ ]. However, the model has shown poor performance in identifying TCRs for infectious diseases. Moreover, there is a potential relationship exists between antigen-specific T cell phenotypes and their TCR sequences. ScRNA-seq and single-cell index of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing have enabled the simultaneous characterization of TCRs interacting with specific epitopes and their transcriptional information [ , ], which potentially provides the valuable data resources to train models for antigen-specific T cells.

## Current challenges and limitations of machine learning methods

Lack of high-quality training data. The quality and size of the training data determine the model performance and generalization. The TCR-pMHC prediction accuracy is limited by several aspects. First, the insufficient paired TCR αβ chain data leads to the omission of TCR prediction that depends on α chain binding. Second, only a small number of epitopes were included in the training model ( ), which is much less than the real T cell epitopes [ , ]. Third, the training data with positive labels tend to be biased toward HLA alleles or antigens with high frequency generally lead to a higher rate of false positives when predicting unseen epitopes [ , ]. Forth, the reliability of true negative data sets will affect the model performance [ ], whereas most of the negative data sets are generated by random combinations rather than real unbinding data ( ).
 
Binding and immunogenicity. Although the binding of TCR-pMHC is extremely important, it does not always evoke an effective immune response [ ]. Most of the existing methods only consider the binding ability and the affinity of TCR-pMHC ( ). Meanwhile, the definition of immune response based on interferon (IFN)-γ production in the laboratory needs to be adjusted, because not all T cell responses will produce enough IFN-γ [ , ]. To identify epitopes that could evoke immunogenicity, more data associated with T cell function should be included. First, the structural or biomechanical features of TCR-pMHC were rarely included in the model because of the difficulty of obtaining them [ , ]. Second, single-cell multimodal data provide a new direction for defining immune responses, such as assessing the transcriptional changes in downstream TCR signaling pathways [ ]. Single-cell sequencing are generally accompanied by transcriptome, TCR, surface protein and other data, which can provide a more comprehensive characterization of cell cluster states and phenotypes ( ); however, only a limited number of studies have utilized these data [ , ].

Evaluation metrics bias. Given the aforementioned limitations, appropriate evaluation metrics should be considered to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model’s performance. Accuracy, precision, and recall are good methods for evaluating classification models for balanced datasets. However, if the data are imbalanced, other methods, such as ROC-AUC/PR-AUC, perform better in evaluating the model performance ( ).

Disease context should be considered. We and other studies have found that the prediction model is applicable only to specific scenarios or disease statuses [ ]. Since the antigens from tumors and autoimmune diseases are endogenous, while those from infectious diseases are exogenous, the captured TCR features exhibit distinct characteristics under these conditions. Furthermore, antigen-specific T cell phenotypes vary across different disease stages. This variability should be thoroughly considered when designing TCR-pMHC models using single-cell multimodal data.

## TCR-pMHC prediction facilitates the design of T-cell based vaccine
Screening for T cell immunogenic peptides can greatly benefit vaccine development and cancer immunotherapy. NEO-PV-01 is a personalized neoantigen vaccine comprising up to 20 peptides designed through bioinformatics analysis of a patient’s neoantigens, currently under investigation as a treatment for melanoma, lung cancer, and bladder cancer. These candidate peptides were preselected based on nonsynonymous mutations, high NetMHCpan 3.0 prediction scores, and significant proteasomal cleavage potential [ ]. Hadrup et al. utilized whole exon sequencing and RNA sequencing data to predict tumor neoantigens with HLA-I EL % rank scores <0.5 and transcripts per million values >0.1. Additionally, pMHC-multimer technology was employed to capture neoantigen-specific T cells for predicting the outcomes of adoptive cell therapy or PD-L1 blockade [ , ]. The design of these epitope vaccines is aided by immunopeptidomics and computational tools, which enable the optimization of MHC-BA and TCR specificity. Because Bravi discussed how machine learning guides rational vaccine design [ ], we briefly summarize the review here.
Proteasomal process contributes to the pool of MHC-I-presented epitopes. NetChop [ ] and TAPbinding [ ] tools were used to predict the cleavage sites of the human proteasome cleavage sites. pMHC binding predictors ( ) and the immunogenic prediction tools (PRIME2) [ , ], and DeepImmuno [ ]) can be used to screen out the peptides that are most likely to be presented as vaccine candidates. However, the immunogenicity prediction methods had a maximal ROC-AUC of around 0.7, partly because few methods take into account for T cell responses which are important for specificity. Prediction of T cell epitope immunogenicity is of particular interest in neoantigen discovery for designing T cell-based anticancer therapeutic vaccines. Hence, a more accurate prediction of TCR-pMHC interactions will help develop new immunotherapies and vaccines in future ( ).

## Roadmap for TCR-pMHC binding prediction by machine learning
Predicting TCR-pMHC binding is crucial for accurately estimating immune responses and holds a promise for revolutionizing immunotherapy development. However, there are still challenges in future. Improving the quality of both positive and negative data, incorporating structural features into the prediction model, and utilizing metrics relevant to T cell function are all crucially required to develop a more reliable model. Several strategies have been proposed to solve these problems. First, high-throughput methods (e.g. multimers) have produced large amounts of datasets; however, the training data produced by them have a high level of background noise. Second, AlphaFold2 can predict the 3D structure of a protein with high confidence and has revolutionized structural biology. However, its application to peptide-TCR and TCR-pMHC prediction remains systematically unevaluated. The structural differences between the 3D structures of TCR-pMHC and other protein complexes must be addressed before the widespread use of protein LLMs to accurately predict TCR-pMHC structures. Third, the T cell function-relevant metrics are difficult to determine. A panel of tumor-reactive T cell markers has been reported. However, they have poor generalizability in different types of tumors and infectious pathogen antigens. Thus, disease-, tissue-, and antigen-specific models must be built to achieve a higher accuracy. This review, for the first time, proposed a TCR-pMHC roadmap ( ). This roadmap calls for collaboration among computational biologists and immunologists worldwide to form a data alliance, which would enable the generation of high-quality training data with minimal noise, thereby enhancing the accuracy and reliability of TCR-pMHC predictions in future.

## Key Points
Our main contributions in TCR-pMHC binding prediction are as follows.
We evaluated the strengths and weaknesses of several state-of-the-art machine learning algorithms.
The critical challenges related to data quality, feature extraction, model training were discussed, and the general limitations and suggestions for overcoming these obstacles were highlighted.
We first proposed a roadmap for TCR-pMHC binding prediction by machine learning.
