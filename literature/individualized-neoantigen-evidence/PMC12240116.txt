## Highlights
•Nonsynonymous ESR1 mutations predominantly cluster at four critical hotspot residues (K303, E380, Y537, D538) in breast cancer, serving as promising neoantigen targets for precision immunotherapy.
•Eight HLA-A*0201-restricted neoepitopes derived from ESR1 hotspot mutations elicit robust cytotoxic T lymphocyte responses in vitro and in vivo, targeting mutant-specific tumor cells.
•Adoptive transfer of T cells primed with these neoepitopes suppresses tumor growth and enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration, offering a novel strategy for treating endocrine-resistant breast cancer via neoantigen vaccines or T cell therapy.
Keywords: ESR1, Hotspot mutations, Neoepitopes, HLA-A2, Immunotherapy

## Abstract
Endocrine therapy has shown significant clinical efficacy in estrogen receptor alpha (ERα)-positive breast cancer management, but the emergence of therapy-resistant mutations significantly undermines treatment outcomes, frequently leading to disease progression and metastasis. Among these resistance mechanisms, mutations in the ESR1 gene are particularly prevalent, detectable in 76% of endocrine therapy-resistant tumor specimens. The identification of immunogenic neoepitopes derived from mutant ESR1 offers a promising therapeutic avenue for patients with endocrine therapy-resistant breast cancer. In this study, we systematically investigated the mutational landscape of ESR1 across various cancer types, with particular emphasis on mutation frequency and spectrum analysis. Our findings revealed that non-synonymous ESR1 mutations predominantly occurred in breast cancer, clustering at four distinct hotspot sites: K303, E380, Y537 and D538. We further characterized the mutation prevalence at these hotspots across different breast cancer subtypes. Through comprehensive screening, we identified eight human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A*0201 restricted immunogenic neoepitopes derived from ESR1 hotspot mutations. These neoepitopes demonstrated the capacity to elicit specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) responses both in vitro and in vivo. The induced CTLs exhibited specific recognition and cytotoxic activity against both T2A2 cells loaded with mutant neoepitopes and HLA-A*0201-positive breast cancer cells transfected with minigene encoding mutant neoepitopes. Notably, adoptive transfer of T cells primed with a peptide pool containing these eight neoepitopes significantly suppressed tumor growth and enhanced CD8+ T cells infiltration within tumor tissue. These findings suggest that the identified neoepitopes represent promising candidates for the development of tumor shared neoantigen vaccines.

## Introduction
Breast cancer represents the most prevalent cause of morbidity and mortality among women worldwide, with classification into three major subtypes based on the expression status of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [ , ]. The ER-positive (ER+) subtype constitutes the most frequently diagnosed form, for which endocrine therapy serves as the primary treatment modality. Endocrine therapy functions through dual mechanisms: either by reducing systemic estrogen levels or by inhibiting estrogen synthesis, thereby preventing estrogen binding to cancer cells. This therapeutic approach has demonstrated significant efficacy in suppressing tumor progression while reducing recurrence risks and mortality in breast cancer patients [ , ]. Current clinical practice employs three major classes of endocrine therapeutic agents, aromatase inhibitors (AIs, e.g., exemestane and letrozole), selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs, e.g., tamoxifen), and estrogen receptor downregulators (SERDs, e.g., fulvestrant) [ , , ]. Despite these therapeutic advances, the development of endocrine therapy resistance remains a critical clinical challenge in ER+ breast cancer management, frequently culminating in disease recurrence and metastasis. The mechanisms underlying endocrine therapy resistance are multifactorial, with somatic mutations playing a significant role. Of particular importance are mutations in ESR1, the gene encoding the ER. Emerging evidence indicates that acquired somatic mutations in ESR1 represent a key molecular driver of endocrine therapy resistance in ER+ metastatic breast cancer (MBC) patients. These mutations confer constitutive transcriptional activity and diminished therapeutic sensitivity [ , , , ]. Notably, ESR1 mutations predominantly emerge under selective pressure from estrogen deprivation therapies and are rarely observed in primary breast cancer. The clinical impact of these mutations is substantial, with detection in up to 36% of MBC patients, predominantly clustering within the ligand-binding domain of the receptor [ ].
The landscape of ESR1 mutations in breast cancer has been progressively elucidated since the pioneering work of Zhang et al. in 1997, who first identified three missense mutations (S47T, K531E and Y537N) through screening of 30 metastatic breast cancer samples [ ]. Subsequent research employing molecular dynamics simulations has revealed that specific mutations, particularly Y537S and D538G, induce significant conformational changes in ESR1. These structural alterations result in sustained transcriptional activity even in the presence of tamoxifen and fulvestrant, necessitating substantially higher doses of therapeutics compared to cancer cells expressing wild-type ESR1 [ ]. At the molecular level, mutant ESR1 exhibits enhanced conformational stability and resistance to protein hydrolysis in the ligand-free state. This mutant form demonstrates reduced binding affinity for therapeutic inhibitors while showing increased recruitment of steroid receptor coactivator 3 (SRC3), collectively contributing to resistance against AIs, tamoxifen, and fulvestrant [ , ]. Among the clinically significant mutations, the K303R mutation, first characterized by Fuqua et al. in 2000, enhances ER activity through activating the IGF-1R/IRS/Akt pathway and altering the ligand binding affinities, thereby promoting the endocrine therapy resistance [ ]. The clinical relevance of ESR1 mutations has been further substantiated by comprehensive analyses of mutation frequencies in different disease states. Li et al. demonstrated that ESR1 mutations exhibit distinct patterns of occurrence, with significantly higher prevalence in distant metastatic tumors compared to locally recurrent lesions [ ].
Neoantigens, arising from tumor-specific mutations, have emerged as pivotal players in cancer immunotherapy due to their exceptional immunogenicity and tumor specificity [ , ]. The therapeutic potential of neoantigen-based vaccines has been progressively validated in clinical trials. Notably, iNeo-Vac-P01, a personalized neoantigen peptide vaccine, demonstrated significant clinical benefits in pancreatic cancer patients while maintaining an excellent safety profile, with no serious vaccine-related adverse events reported [ ]. Further supporting this approach, Keskin et al. observed durable T lymphocyte responses and enhanced tumor-infiltrating T cells in glioblastoma patients following administration of personalized neoantigen peptide vaccine [ ]. The clinical efficacy of neoantigen vaccines has been particularly evident in HLA-A*0201 or HLA-A*2402 positive glioblastoma patients, where vaccination successfully induced neoantigen-specific T cell responses. These activated T cells demonstrated remarkable tumor-homing capabilities, effectively modulating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and extending the median overall survival to 29 months [ , ]. However, a significant limitation of current neoantigen-based immunotherapies lies in their predominantly patient-specific nature, resulting in limited population coverage and hindering broader clinical application. This challenge has spurred interest in targeting tumor shared neoantigens, which could potentially benefit larger patient populations. Current clinical research is actively exploring shared neoantigen targets. TP53, the most frequently mutated tumor suppressor gene occurring in over 50% of human cancer, represents one such target [ ]. Similarly, KRAS driver mutations, prevalent in multiple malignancies (93% of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and 50% of colorectal cancer), have emerged as promising immunotherapeutic targets [ ]. The development of ELI-0022P, a lymph node-targeted neoantigen vaccine, exemplifies this approach. In a clinical study involving 25 patients (20 with pancreatic cancer and 5 with colorectal cancer), ELI-0022P elicited mutant KRAS-specific T cell responses in 84% of participants (21/25), demonstrating both safety and immunogenicity in KRAS-mutated cancers [ ]. These promising results provide a strong foundation for advancing the development of tumor shared neoantigen vaccines.
The rapid advancement of neoantigen-based tumor vaccines in recent years has ushered in a transformative era in cancer immunotherapy. This progress has been significantly propelled by breakthroughs in genomics and bioinformatics, which have accelerated both the development and clinical translation of neoantigen vaccines, establishing them as a forefront area in cancer research. The availability of comprehensive tumor mutation databases, including the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC), coupled with the development of sophisticated prediction algorithms, has greatly facilitated the identification of shared hotspot mutation-specific neoantigens [ ]. Particularly the HLA-A2 subtype is present in approximately 37.7% of individuals in Chinese population [ ]. This demographic characteristic underscores the importance of identifying HLA-A2-restricted neoepitopes, as such discoveries could provide both novel therapeutic candidates and innovative strategies for neoantigen-based immunotherapy with broad population coverage.
In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of ESR1 mutation patterns using COSMIC database, identifying four predominant hotspot mutations: K303R, E380Q, Y537S/N/C and D538G. Through systematic examination of whole genome sequencing data from tumor samples in the COSMIC database, we characterized the mutation frequency of ESR1 across various cancer types and mapped the distribution patterns of these four hotspot mutations. This analysis enabled us to determine the potential patient population that could benefit from ESR1 neoantigen-derived mutant peptides. Based on these findings, we employed computational prediction algorithms to identify potential HLA-A2-restricted CTL epitopes derived from these hotspot mutations. To validate the immunogenicity of these mutant peptides, we performed a series of immunological assays using both in vitro (peripheral blood mononuclear cells from HLA-A2-positive healthy donors) and in vivo (HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice) models. Furthermore, to evaluate the therapeutic potential of these epitopes, we adoptively transferred peptide pool-induced T lymphocytes from HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice into tumor-bearing NOD/SCID mice, allowing us to systematically assess their anti-tumor efficacy in vivo.

## Materials and methods

## Cell lines and animals
Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 (HLA-A*0201-positive) and MCF7 (HLA-A*0201-positive), along with the human lymphoblast cell line T2A2 (transporter associated with antigen processing deficient) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and maintained under standard culture conditions in our laboratory. Human embryonic kidney cell line HEK-293T was generously provided by Professor Shengdian Wang from the institute Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 complete medium (Corning Life Sciences, USA), while MCF7 and HEK-293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modifed Eagle's Medium (DMEM) complete medium (Corning Life Sciences, USA). T2A2 cells were cultured in Iscove's Modifed Dulbecco's Medium (IMDM) complete medium (Gibco, USA). All complete mediums were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biological Industries, Israel), 100 U/mL penicillin (Solarbio, China) and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Solarbio, China). For T lymphocytes culture, GT-T551 medium (#WK551T, Takara, Japan) supplemented with recombinant human interleukin 2 (rhIL-2, 100 U/mL, #200-02, PeproTech, USA) and rhIL-7 (10 ng/mL, #200-07, PeproTech, USA) was used. All cell cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.
HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice (aged 6-8 weeks) were purchased from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China), while female NOD/SCID mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd (Beijing, China). All animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at Zhengzhou University under controlled environmental conditions.

## Data obtained from the catalogue of somatic mutations in cancer (COSMIC)
Comprehensive mutation analysis of ESR1 mutation was performed using data obtained from the COSMIC database (GRCh38, March 15, 2023). The study encompassed a total of 2139 tumor samples across eight cancer types, including 18 penile cancer cases, 258 liver cancer cases, 237 pancreatic cancer cases, 998 breast cancer cases, 254 prostate cancer cases, 169 esophageal cancer cases, 73 endometrial cancer cases and 132 skin cancer cases harboring ESR1 mutations. We systematically characterized both the mutation spectrum distribution and frequency of ESR1 across these malignancies, with particular emphasis on four hotspot mutation sites: K303R, E380Q, Y537S/C/N, and D538G. The overall ESR1 mutation frequency in each tumor type was calculated by the formula = (number of ESR1-mutated samples) / (total number of sequenced samples). For breast cancer-specific analysis, the mutation type frequency was determined using the formula = (number of occurrences for each mutation type) / (total number of ESR1-mutated samples). Additionally, the hotspot site mutation rate in breast cancer subtypes was calculated by the formula = (number of mutations at each hotspot site) / (total number of missense substitutions).

## Prediction and synthesis of candidate neoepitopes from ESR1 hotspot mutations
NetMHC 4.0 Server and IEDB online epitope prediction softwares were used to predict HLA-A2-restricted CTL neoepitopes derived from neoantigen ESR1. Through integration of prediction results from these two softwares, we selected candidate neoepitopes (mutant peptides) based on their superior binding affinity scores. Selected peptides were synthesized using Fmoc solid-phase synthesis methodology. Following synthesis, all neoepitopes were purified by reversed-phase high performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC, Shimadzu, Japan) and verified by liquid chromatography mass spectrometer (LC-MS, Waters Corporation, USA).

## Construction of tumor cell lines stably expressing mutant or wild-type tandem minigene
Through comprehensive data analysis, we identified four predominant hotspot mutation sites K303R, E380Q, Y537S/C/N and D538G in ESR1. To establish stable cell lines expressing these mutations, we constructed both mutant and wild-type tandem minigenes encompassing these hotspot sites. These genetic constructs were subsequently cloned into pLVX-IRES-ZsGreen1 lentiviral vector system and transfected into HLA-A2-positive breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MCF7. Following rigorous selection and validation processes, we successfully generated stable isogenic cell lines MDA-MB-231-WT/MUT and MCF7-WT/MUT, which consistently express either wild-type or mutant epitopes, respectively.

## Induction of autologous DCs
PBMCs were isolated from HLA-A2-positive (HLA-A2+) healthy donors using density gradient centrifugation. The isolated cells were resuspended at a concentration of 5 × 106 cells/mL and seeded in 6-well culture plates. Following a 4-hour incubation, non-adherent cells (autologous peripheral blood lymphocytes, PBLs) were gently collected and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen for future use. The adherent cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS and differentiated into dendritic cells (DCs) through the addition of human granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (hGM-CSF, 500 U/mL, #300-03, PeproTech, USA) and interleukin-4 (hIL-4, 500 U/mL, #200-04, PeproTech, USA). The differentiation medium was replenished with fresh cytokine-containing medium at 50% volume every 48 hours to maintain optimal culture conditions. On day 5, DC maturation was induced by stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL, L8880, Solarbio, China) and specific peptides (20 μg/mL) for 24 hours.

## Generation of mutant peptides-specific T lymphocytes by peptides pulsed autologous DCs
Cryopreserved autologous PBLs were rapidly thawed and co-cultured with peptide-pulsed DCs at an optimal DC:PBL ratio of 1:10 in GT-T551 medium supplemented with rhIL-2 (100 U/mL) and rhIL-7 (10 ng/mL). This induction protocol was repeated weekly for three consecutive cycles to generate peptide-specific CD8+ T cells. Following the final induction cycle, the functional capacity of the induced CD8+ T cells was evaluated through intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assay by using flow cytometry and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay to quantify cytotoxicity activity against target cells.

## Generation mutant peptides-specific T lymphocytes from HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice
6 to 8 weeks HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice were randomly divided into Vehicle group and Peptide pool group (n = 5). The Vehicle control group was administered CpG ODN 1826 alone (30 μg/mouse), while the Peptide pool group received co-administration of CpG ODN 1826 (30 μg/mouse) with a peptide pool (100 μg/mouse). Immunization was performed weekly via multi-point subcutaneous injections at the base of tail. Following three consecutive immunizations, spleens and lymph nodes were obtained and processed into single cell suspensions. These cells were then stimulated with the peptide pool (10 μg/mL) and mIL-2 (100 U/mL, #212-12, PeproTech, USA) for 5 days in vitro.

## Adoptive T cell immunotherapy in tumor-bearing NOD/SCID mice
To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of these mutant peptides, we established a tumor-bearing NOD/SCID mouse model by subcutaneous implantation of MDA-MB-231-MUT cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse) into the right flank. When tumors reached 80-100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Normal saline control (NS group), (2) Non-induced T cell control (T Cell group, receiving 5 × 106 T lymphocytes from unimmunized HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice), and (3) Induced T cell treatment group (Induced T cell group, receiving 5 × 106 T lymphocytes from peptide pool-immunized HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice). Adoptive T cell transfer was performed via tail vein injection once weekly for three consecutive treatments. Tumor dimensions were measured regularly using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as follows V=1/2 × length × width × height. Seven days after the final treatment, mice were humanely euthanized for comprehensive analysis. Tumor tissues, spleens, lymph nodes and major organs were obtained for further examination. The infiltration of CD8+ T lymphocytes in tumor tissues was quantified using both immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry analysis. Potential treatment-related toxicity was assessed through histopathological examination of major organs using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

## Intracellular cytokine staining assay
To evaluate T lymphocyte functionality, induced T lymphocytes were collected and co-cultured with target cells, including MCF-7-WT/MUT, MDA-MB-231-WT/MUT, or peptides-pulsed T2A2 cells for 4 hours in the presence of protein transport inhibitor cocktail containing Brefeldin A (#555029, BD Biosciences, USA). Following incubation, cells were collected and surface-stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD3, CD8α and FasL for 30 minutes at 4°C in the dark. Cells were then washed and fixed using fixation buffer (#00-8222-49, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 minutes. After fixation, cells were permeabilized with permeabilization buffer (#00-8333-56, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and labeled with IFN-γ monoclonal antibody. Finally, samples were analyzed using flow cytometry.

## Cytotoxicity assay
To assess cytotoxic activity, induced T lymphocytes were co-cultured with target cells at varying effector-to-target (E/T) ratios for 4 hours. The experimental design included four groups: (1) experimental group, (2) volume correction group, (3) background control group, and (4) target cell maximum release group. Following the co-culture period, 10 μL of 10 × lysis buffer was added to both the maximum release group and volume correction group. Culture supernatants were then collected and incubated with the substrate mixture for 30 minutes at room temperature. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding the stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. All cytotoxicity assays were performed using the CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay kit (#G1780, Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Blood samples of NOD/SCID mice were collected and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes to separate the serum. The IFN-γ level in serum was measured using a mouse IFN-γ ELISA kit (#KMC4021, ThermoFisher Scientific).

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using one-tailed Student's t-test. Statistical significance was determined by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001, while P > 0.05 was considered not statistically significant (ns).

## Results

## Mutation distribution and frequency of neoantigen ESR1 across various cancer types and breast cancer subtypes
Somatic mutations represent crucial drivers in cancer pathogenesis and disease progression [ ]. Our analysis of drug resistance data in the COSMIC database revealed that 76.06% of endocrine therapy-resistant samples harbored ESR1 mutations, with the majority clustering at four key residues: K303, E380, Y537 and D538. Comprehensive profiling of ESR1 mutation frequencies across various malignancies demonstrated significant mutation rates in multiple tumor tissues, including penis (16.67%), liver (10.5%), pancreas (8.32%), breast (8.18%), prostate (7.79%), esophagus (7.25%), endometrium (6.41%), skin (5.66%) and so on ( A). To delineate potential patient populations harboring these four ESR1 hotspot mutations, we conducted detailed analyses of mutation frequencies, types, and distributions across different tumor subtypes. While ESR1 mutations were prevalent in various malignancies ( B-H), the majority represented nonsense mutations or undefined mutation types (Fig. S1). Notably, breast cancer exhibited a distinct mutation profile, with missense substitutions constituting the predominant mutation type. Non-synonymous mutations were detected in over 70% of breast cancer samples, highlighting the clinical significance of ESR1 alterations in this malignancy ( J).
 Further subtype-specific analysis identified ten breast cancer subtypes exhibiting ESR1 mutation frequencies exceeding 5% ( I). Notably, breast ductal epithelial hyperplasia demonstrated the highest ESR1 mutation frequency at 33.9%, with the K303R mutation present in all mutant samples ( K). In breast ductal carcinoma, ESR1 mutations were observed in 10.8% of cases, with the four hotspot mutations (K303R, E380Q, Y537N/S/C and D538G) covering all mutated samples, some of which harbored multiple concurrent mutations ( L). ER/PR-positive breast cancers showed an ESR1 mutation frequency of 8.65%, with three hotspot loci (E380Q, Y537N/S/C and D538G) representing 84% of mutations ( M). Similarly, breast lobular carcinoma exhibited an 8.38% mutation rate, with all mutations occurring at the four characteristic hotspot sites ( N). In ER-positive breast cancer, 7.68% of cases harbored ESR1 mutations, predominantly (91%) at the E380Q, Y537N/S/C and D538G loci ( O). Notably, breast cancer-NS demonstrated a 6.69% ESR1 mutation frequency, with K303R accounting for 74.7% of cases, while the four hotspot sites collectively represented 93% of mutations ( P). In breast ductolobular carcinoma, ESR1 mutations were present in 6.52% of samples, with K303R (58.3%) and Y537N/S/C mutations accounting for 83.3% of cases ( Q).
In summary, our comprehensive analysis of 998 ESR1-mutated breast cancer samples revealed that 704 cases (70.5%) harbored non-synonymous mutations. Notably, the four predominant hotspot mutations - K303R, E380Q, Y537S/N/C and D538G - accounted for over 80% of these non-synonymous mutations. Furthermore, a significant proportion of tumor samples exhibited concurrent mutations at multiple hotspot loci, suggesting potential synergistic effects in driving tumorigenesis and therapy resistance.

## Prediction and synthesis of HLA-A2-restricted neoepitopes derived from ESR1 hotspot mutations
Comprehensive analysis of ESR1 mutation patterns in breast cancer identified six predominant hotspot mutations: K303R, E380Q, Y537S, Y537N, Y537C and D538G. To identify potential HLA-A2-restricted CTL epitopes derived from these missense mutations, we employed two established epitope prediction platforms: NetMHC 4.0 Server and IEDB. Through integration of prediction results from both algorithms, we selected 14 candidate mutant peptides with optimal binding scores. These peptides were synthesized using Fmoc solid-phase synthesis methodology and subsequently purified by RP-HPLC. The molecular identity and purity of each peptide were confirmed by LC-MS, with detailed characteristics summarized in .
 

## Immunoreactivity of mutant peptides targeting T2A2 cells in vitro
To evaluate the immunogenicity of the 14 candidate mutant peptides, we isolated PBMCs from HLA-A2+ healthy donors. DCs, generated through GM-CSF and IL-4 induction, were loaded with respective peptides, and used to stimulate CTLs as effector cells. T2A2 cells loaded with either mutant or wild-type peptides served as target cells. Following co-culture of effector and target cells, we assessed antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses through intracellular cytokine staining for IFN-γ and surface staining for FasL expression. Notably, eight mutant peptides, E1, E3, E6, E8, E11, E12, E13, and E14, elicited significantly higher proportions of both IFN-γ+CD8+ and FasL+CD8+ T lymphocytes when co-cultured with mutant peptides-loaded T2A2 cells compared to their wild-type counterparts ( A-B), demonstrating their potent immunogenicity in vitro.
 To further characterize the cytotoxic potential of these CTLs, we conducted LDH release assays at E/T ratios of 12.5:1, 25:1 and 50:1. The results confirmed that CTLs induced by the eight peptides specifically recognized and lysed T2A2 cells loaded with mutant peptides, with cytotoxicity increasing in a dose-dependent manner ( C).

## Immunoreactivity of mutant peptides against breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 expressing wild-type or mutant tandem minigenes in vitro
To determine whether these eight immunogenic mutant peptides could be effectively presented by tumor cells and recognized by peptide specific T cells, we constructed isogenic human breast cancer cell lines stably expressing either wild-type or mutant tandem minigenes (MDA-MB-231-WT/MUT, HLA-A2+). Results showed that CTLs induced by mutant peptides specifically recognized MDA-MB-231-MUT cells, demonstrating significantly higher proportions of both IFN-γ+CD8+ T and FasL+CD8+ T lymphocytes compared to MDA-MB-231-WT cells (Fig. S2A-B). Furthermore, cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that CTLs specific for mutant peptides, E1, E3, E6, E8, E11, E12, E13, and E14 efficiently recognized and lysed MDA-MB-231-MUT target cells, while showing minimal activity against MDA-MB-231-WT cells (Fig. S3). These findings provide compelling evidence that the identified mutant peptides can be effectively processed and presented by tumor cells, eliciting specific recognition and cytotoxic responses against cells expressing the corresponding mutant epitopes.

## Immunoreactivity of mutant peptides against breast cancer cell lines MCF7-WT/MUT expressing wild-type or mutant tandem minigenes in vitro
To further validate the immunoreactivity of the identified mutant peptide, we established an additional isogenic breast cancer cell line pair (MCF7-WT/MUT) expressing either wild-type or mutant tandem minigenes. Consistent with our previous findings in the MDA-MB-231 system, CTLs induced by mutant peptides demonstrated significantly higher proportions of IFN-γ+CD8+ T and FasL+CD8+ T lymphocytes when co-cultured with MCF7-MUT cells compared to MCF7-WT controls (Fig. S4A-B). LDH cytotoxicity killing assays confirmed that CTLs specific for mutant peptides E1, E3, E6, E8, E11, E12, E13, and E14 exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against MCF7-MUT cells (Fig. S5). These consistent results across two distinct breast cancer cell line models provide robust evidence that the identified mutant peptides can elicit CTL responses capable of specifically recognizing and lysing both mutant peptides-pulsed T2A2 cells and tumor cells endogenously expressing the corresponding mutant epitopes.

## Immunoreactivity of mutant peptides in HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice
To investigate the in vivo immunogenicity of peptides E1, E3, E6, E8, E11, E12, E13, and E14, HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice were immunized with these peptides in the form of peptide pool. Following the three immunization cycles, splenocytes and lymph node cells were obtained and restimulated with the mutant peptide pool. The induced CTLs were then assessed for their functional capacity using two distinct target cells:T2A2 cells loaded with individual mutant peptides and the isogenic breast cancer cell line pair MDA-MB-231-WT/MUT. ICS results showed that lymph nodes-derived CTLs from immunized mice exhibited significantly enhanced IFN-γ production and elevated FasL expression when co-cultured with MDA-MB-231-MUT cells compared to MDA-MB-231-WT controls. Importantly, CTLs from vehicle-treated control mice showed no differential response to either target cell type ( A). Similar patterns of antigen-specific reactivity were observed in splenocyte-derived CTLs ( B).
 Complementary cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that peptide pool-induced CTLs specifically lysed MDA-MB-231-MUT cells in a dose-dependent manner across multiple E/T ratios, while showing minimal activity against MDA-MB-231-WT cells ( C). We also evaluated the cytotoxic activity of CTLs induced by the mutant peptide pool or Vehicle, derived from the lymph nodes and spleens, against both MCF7-MUT and MCF7-WT cells. The results showed that the mutant peptide pool-induced CTLs specifically lysed MCF7-MUT cells, with minimal activity observed against MCF7-WT controls. Notably, CTLs from the Vehicle control group demonstrated comparable cytotoxic activity across different target cell lines, indicating the absence of target-specific recognition (Fig. S6).
In addition, we found significantly higher proportions of IFN-γ+CD8+ T and FasL+CD8+ T cells in the peptide pool-immunized group when co-cultured with T2A2 cells loaded with the mutant peptide pool compared to those pulsed with the wild-type peptide pool. In contrast, CTLs from vehicle-treated mice showed no differential response to either target cell population ( A-B). Cytotoxic assays corroborated these findings, demonstrating that CTLs induced by the mutant peptide pool exhibited significantly higher lytic activity against T2A2 cells loaded with the mutant peptide pool compared to those T2A2 cells loaded with the wild-type peptide pool ( C). We also assessed the cytotoxic activity of mutant peptide-induced CTLs to T2A2 cells loaded with a single mutant peptide as well as the corresponding wild-type peptide. The results demonstrated that mutant peptide-induced CTLs specifically lysed T2A2 cells loaded with the corresponding mutant peptides, while showing minimal activity against cells loaded with wild-type peptides ( ).
 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the identified mutant peptides exhibit potent immunogenicity in HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice, eliciting robust CTL responses capable of specifically recognizing and targeting tumor cells presenting the corresponding mutant epitopes.

## Antitumor activity of mutant epitope peptide-specific T cells in NOD/SCID mouse model
To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the identified neoepitopes in vivo, we established a tumor model by subcutaneous implantation of MDA-MB-231-MUT cells in NOD/SCID mice. HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice were immunized with either normal saline plus CpG-ODN 1826 adjuvant or mutant peptide pools plus CpG-ODN 1826 adjuvant (Induced T cell group). After three immunization cycles and in vitro restimulation with the mutant peptide pool, 5 × 106 induced T cells were adoptively transferred via tail vein injection for three consecutive treatments ( A). Control group received either saline induced CTLs (T cell group) or normal saline group. On day 33, the induced T cell group exhibited significant tumor growth inhibition compared to the NS group (***P<0.001), while the T cells group showed partial tumor suppression compared to the induced T cell group (**P<0.01) ( B-D), accompanied by significantly higher serum IFN-γ levels compared to control groups ( E). ICS analysis revealed significantly enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumor tissues of the induced T cell group compared to both control groups (***P<0.001 and **P<0.01, respectively) ( F). Consistent patterns were observed in lymph nodes and spleens, with the induced T cell group showing elevated CD8+ T cell proportions ( G-H). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumor tissues of the induced T cell group ( I). In addition, histopathological H&E examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) revealed no treatment-related toxicity in any experimental group (Fig. S7). In summary, adoptive transfer of mutant peptide-induced CTLs significantly inhibits tumor growth while maintaining an excellent safety profile, highlighting the therapeutic potential of these neoepitopes for cancer immunotherapy.
 

## Discussion
The clinical efficacy of cancer immunotherapy is fundamentally determined by the frequency and functional capacity of tumor antigen-specific T lymphocytes. Neoantigens, arising from tumor-specific somatic mutations, offer distinct advantages in both tumor specificity and immunogenicity, making them ideal targets for cancer immunotherapy. Neoantigen-based cancer vaccines, which stimulate the activation and expansion of endogenous tumor-specific T cells, have shown promising results in clinical trails [ ]. Notably, Ott et al. demonstrated that the personalized neoantigen vaccine NEO-PV-01, when combined with anti-PD-1 checkpoint blockade, could induce durable neoantigen-specific T cell responses in patients with advanced melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer and bladder cancer. These neoantigen specific T cells exhibited both cytotoxic and memory phenotypes, with demonstrated tumor-homing capabilities [ ]. Similarly, the neoantigen-pulsed DC vaccine Neo-DCVac has shown feasibility, safety and efficacy in eliciting potent anti-tumor immunity in vivo [ ]. However, a significant limitation of current neoantigen-based immunotherapies lies in their predominantly patient-specific nature, which restricts their broader clinical application. This challenge underscores the critical need for identifying shared neoantigens with both high immunogenicity and broad population coverage, which would significantly enhance the clinical applicability and therapeutic potential of neoantigen-based cancer vaccines.
Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting women globally, accounting for 25% of all female cancer cases worldwide [ ]. The hormone receptor-positive subtype, characterized by ER and/or PR expression, represents the most common form, constituting approximately 80% of breast cancer diagnoses [ ]. Current therapeutic strategies for postmenopausal women with ER-positive breast cancer primarily include AIs, which block androgen-to-estrogen conversion, and SERMs, which inhibit ER activity by preventing cofactor recruitment [ ]. While these endocrine therapies have demonstrated significant clinical benefits, reducing the mortality rates by up to 40%, the development of treatment resistance remains a major clinical challenge, frequently leading to disease recurrence and metastatic progression [ ]. The presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in breast cancer lesions indicates inherent tumor immunogenicity [ , ], highlighting the potential of immunotherapeutic strategies that enhance immune cell infiltration and diversity within the tumor microenvironment. Recent advances in neoantigen-based immunotherapy have shown promising clinical outcomes. Researchers at the University of Washington developed a neoantigen DNA vaccine that demonstrated remarkable efficacy in a phase Ⅰ clinical trial for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), with 87.5% of patients achieving 3-year relapse-free survival [ ]. Furthermore, Rosenberg group reported the therapeutic potential of TIL therapy targeting P53 mutants in breast cancer patients [ ]. The field achieved a significant milestone in February 2024 when lifileucel (AMTAGVI) received FDA accelerated approval as the first commercially available TIL therapy for metastatic or unresectable melanoma [ ]. These developments underscore the tremendous clinical potential of neoantigen-based vaccines and adoptive T cell therapies in cancer treatment.
ESR1, an estrogen receptor highly expressed in ER+ breast cancer, represents a critical therapeutic target for endocrine therapy. Through analyzing the drug resistance patterns in the COSMIC database, we found that 76.06% of endocrine therapy-resistant patients harbored ESR1 mutations, with nearly all nonsynonymous mutations occurring specifically in breast cancer. Further investigation found that there were four predominant hotspot mutations (K303R, E380Q, Y537S/N/C and D538G) that collectively accounted for over 80% of ESR1 nonsynonymous mutations in breast cancer samples. Given that approximately 48% of the patients have the HLA-A2 subtype, we focused on identifying HLA-A2-restricted mutant peptides targeting these four hotspot mutations. We firstly evaluated the immunoreactivity of mutant peptide-induced T cells in vitro using PBMCs from HLA-A2+ healthy donors. Comprehensive functional assessment was performed through ICS and LDH release assays. To validate these findings in vivo, we immunized HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice with a peptide pool containing the eight identified mutant peptides and subsequently characterized the functional capacity of activated T lymphocytes from both spleen and lymph node. Notably, adoptive transfer of these peptide pool-induced T lymphocytes into tumor-bearing mice resulted in significant tumor growth inhibition and enhanced T lymphocyte infiltration in tumor tissues, spleens and lymph nodes.
Neoantigens, arising from tumor-specific somatic mutations, represent crucial targets for T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [ ]. Neoantigen-based personalized cancer vaccines (PCVs) offer the potential to elicit precise anti-tumor immune responses against cancer-specific epitopes, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target toxicity [ ]. Choueiri et al. developed a neoantigen vaccine for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) by predictive screening of up to 20 neoantigens through whole-exome sequencing (WES) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), combined with the adjuvant poly-ICLC. This approach demonstrated robust anti-tumor immune responses against driver gene mutations in high-risk RCC patients with low tumor mutational burden, showing excellent safety and immunogenicity profiles [ ]. The identification of eight HLA-A2-restricted neoantigenic epitopes in our study provides a foundation for developing shared neoepitope vaccines for patients with multiple breast cancer subtypes harboring these ESR1 hotspot mutations. Importantly, different breast cancer subtypes exhibit distinct mutation patterns: ductal epithelial hyperplasia predominantly features the K303R mutation, while breast ductal carcinoma presents all four hotspot mutations (K303R, E380Q, Y537S/C/N and D538G). This heterogeneity necessitates the development of tailored vaccine formulations, ranging from single-epitope vaccines to multi-epitope peptide pools, to address specific mutation profiles across breast cancer subtypes.
Recent studies by Goldberg et al. have experimentally validated the immunogenicity of peptides derived from ESR1 mutations, demonstrating their recognition and targeting by CTLs [ ]. They identified three 9-mer peptides originating from ESR1 mutations (Y537S, D538G and E380Q) and their corresponding wild-type sequences, which exhibit favorable binding affinity and complex stability with HLA-A*0201, including a peptide analogous to the one (E2) investigated in our study. Lopez et al. further corroborated the immunogenicity of HLA-A2-restricted ESR1 peptides in humanized HLA-A2/DR1 transgenic mice [ ]. Their analysis of treatment-resistant breast cancer patients revealed that ESR1 mutations correlate with downregulation of antigen-presenting machinery components and loss of HLA class I gene expression, resulting in reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration within tumors. Additionally, Shafer et al. independently confirmed the immunogenic potential of distinct ESR1 mutant peptides, though they were unable to validate the natural presentation of these epitopes [ ]. Although existing studies have investigated common hotspot mutations in ESR1, none have systematically explored the immunogenicity of Y537/D538 double-mutant peptides, or investigated their distinct immunogenic properties compared with single-mutant counterparts. These findings validate our experimental data, demonstrating potent immunogenicity of ESR1 mutant peptides, thus establishing that ESR1 mutations represent promising neoantigen targets for developing cancer peptide vaccines in hormone receptor-positive breast malignancies.
In conclusion, our study demonstrates that nonsynonymous mutations in ESR1 predominantly occur in breast cancer, with four major hotspot sites. We have characterized the patient populations likely to benefit from ESR1-targeted therapies and identified eight HLA-A2-restricted neoepitopes capable of eliciting potent mutant peptide-specific CD8+ T cell responses in both HLA-A2+ healthy donors and HLA-A2.1/Kb transgenic mice. Adoptive transfer of peptide pool-induced CD8+ T cells effectively suppressed tumor growth and enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumor tissues. These findings provide a novel therapeutic strategy for addressing endocrine therapy resistance and metastatic breast cancer, offering promising candidates for the clinical development of shared neoantigen-based vaccines.
