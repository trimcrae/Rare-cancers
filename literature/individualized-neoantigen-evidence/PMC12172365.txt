## Abstract
Chronic neuropathic pain (CNP) results from disease or damage within the somatosensory nervous system. It represents a prevalent and challenging form of chronic pain that exerts a severe impact on patients’ quality of life. This review aims to synthesize and highlight the current understanding of the mechanisms underlying CNP, as well as the evolving therapeutic landscape. With advancements in research, an increasing number of analgesic targets specific to various disease types are being developed, alongside the emergence of novel nanomaterials for treating chronic pain. In this review, we systematically summarize the applications and potential mechanisms of nanomaterials in treating CNP induced by various diseases, providing new insights for both fundamental research and clinical management of CNP.
Keywords: Chronic pain, CNP, Clinical disease models, Nanomaterials, Nanodrug delivery systems

## Background
Pain is regarded as the 5th vital sign in modern medicine. According to the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) in 2020, pain is “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage” [ ]. Based on duration, pain can be categorized into acute and chronic pain. Chronic pain ranks as the third most crucial health problem after cardiovascular diseases and tumors. About 25% of chronic pain is chronic neuropathic pain (CNP), which affects 6.9–10% of the population and results from damage to the somatosensory nervous system [ , ]. CNP is difficult to treat and often leads to anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life and creating socioeconomic burdens [ , ]. Current pharmacotherapy for CNP often provides insufficient analgesic effects while causing systemic side effects that reduce compliance [ ]. Therefore, it is essential to develop CNP therapies that exhibit better efficacy at lower doses with fewer adverse effects.
Nanotechnology is a swiftly developing field of science and technology. Since the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of Doxil (Doxorubicin Encapsulated in Stealth® Liposomes), the biomedical sector has increasingly implemented nanomaterials [ ]. Optimal nanomaterials should prevent the premature drug release and off-target accumulation, and enable controlled drug release at specific cells or tissues [ ]. The current state of nanomaterial development for treating CNP is nascent, owing to the physiological complexity of pain. Only a limited number of nanomedicines like Exparel® (liposomal bupivacaine) and Zynrelef® (bupivacaine/meloxicam) are approved for pain management. This review classified CNP and its potential mechanisms and summarized recent advances in nanomaterials treating various types of CNP and their associated mechanisms. In addition, it presented a prospective outlook on the application of nanotechnology in CNP treatment (Fig.  ).
 

## Present conditions of CNP

## Classification and clinical manifestations of CNP
In 2019, the IASP classified CNP into chronic peripheral and central neuropathic pain [ , ]. Caused by disease or damage to the peripheral somatosensory nervous system, chronic peripheral neuropathic pain includes trigeminal neuralgia (TN), chronic pain after peripheral nerve injury, postherpetic neuralgia, painful polyneuropathy, and radiculopathy. Chronic central neuropathic pain, resulting from disease or damage to the central somatosensory nervous system, covers pain related to spinal cord or brain injuries, post-stroke pain, and pain associated with multiple sclerosis [ ].
Unlike nociceptive stimuli, CNP involves positive (e.g., hyperalgesia or allodynia) and negative (e.g., hypesthesia or absence of sensation) sensory signs resulting from the somatosensory neural system [ ]. It may present as spontaneous or evoked pain, paresthesia, and other symptoms, with pain characteristics varying but often including traction, lightning, stabbing, tearing, or burning pain [ ]. TN, for instance, is triggered by innocuous stimulation and causes a lightning- or shooting-like pain. Painful radiculopathy results from damage to cervical, thoracic, or lumbar nerve roots, leading to persistent or recurrent pain that is often movement- or posture-dependent. Chronic central neuropathic pain may be spontaneous or evoked, like hyperalgesia (exaggerated pain response) or allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli).

## Clinical pharmacologic management strategies for treating CNP
Current clinical managements of CNP include pharmacotherapy and surgical intervention. Pharmacotherapy serves as the primary treatment approach, targeting pain relief and comorbid disorders such as depression, anxiety, and dyssomnia [ ].
Pharmacotherapy [ , ] consists of four categories of drugs: (1) First-line drugs like anticonvulsants (gabapentin, pregabalin, and carbamazepine) and antidepressants (amitriptyline, duloxetine, and venlafaxine); (2) Second-line drugs like opioids (morphine and oxycodone); (3) Local analgesics like lidocaine cream and capsaicin patches; (4) Adjuvant therapies like non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and bulleyaconitine A.
Nevertheless, both first- and second-line drugs have varying degrees of side effects (Table  ). Long-term opioid use may lead to dependence, tolerance, addiction, and abuse [ ]. While local anesthetics can effectively treat certain types of peripheral neuropathic pain, their therapeutic effects are of short duration [ ]. These limitations highlight the ongoing challenges in developing effective treatments for CNP.
 

## Potential mechanisms of CNP
CNP, a pervasive and incurable type of chronic pain, continues to have an incompletely elucidated pathogenesis. The widely accepted pathogenesis of CNP encompasses peripheral and central sensitization following nerve injury [ , ]. Ectopic discharges, sympathetic effects, transcription dysregulation of pain-related genes, conduction changes in pain signaling, aberrant activation of neuroglial cells, and alterations in the function of the central nervous system (CNS) after nerve injury are all potential contributors to develop peripheral and central sensitization [ , ] (Table  ).
 

## Peripheral sensitization
Peripheral sensitization occurs when inflammatory mediators from damaged tissues or abnormally activated nociceptors accumulate, lowering receptor thresholds and enhancing nociceptive response. Studies have shown that the primary factors generating peripheral sensitization include the release of inflammatory mediators triggered by the peripheral inflammatory response, changes in the expression of ion channels, and the phenotypic switching of nerve fibers [ ]. As shown in Fig.  Section A, tissue injury triggers immune cells (macrophages, mast cells) to release proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and mediators (prostaglandin E2, histamine, and bradykinin), directly increasing hyperalgesia [ , ]. Neuropeptides such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P (SP) further potentiate peripheral sensitization [ ]. 
 Figure  Section B illustrates that voltage-gated sodium channels (Nav1.3, Nav1.7) are closely associated with chronic pain. Their upregulation after nerve injury leads to neuronal depolarization and the generation of action potentials, which enhances neuronal excitability and results in ectopic discharges [ ]. Similarly, nerve injury can lead to the upregulation of voltage-gated potassium channels, T-type calcium channels, and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channels in DRG neurons, resulting in peripheral sensitization [ , ].
Peripheral nerve injury or the activation of injury receptors can alter the expression of genes associated with nerves. This, in turn, changes the characteristics of nerve fibers, which affects their ability to be stimulated and transmit signals. For example, neuromodulators (e.g., CGRP and SP) expression in class C nerve fibers may transform an otherwise innocuous stimulus into a noxious one when expressed in other fibers [ ]. In addition, collateral sprouting of nerve fibers and sympathetic abnormalities contribute to the development of CNP [ ].

## Central sensitization
Central sensitization refers to the enhanced responsiveness of CNS neurons to regular or subthreshold afferent inputs following injury [ ]. Depending on the composition of the CNS, it can be categorized into spinal and supraspinal mechanisms (Fig.  Section C).
At the spinal cord level, the mechanism of CNP is mainly related to the spatiotemporal summation plasticity of synapses, where neurons respond to repeated noxious stimuli with a superimposed effect in time and space [ ]. Increased synaptic activity frequency, ectopic discharges, enlarged receptive fields, and the increased excitability of neurons in upstream nociceptive conduction pathways can cause plasticity changes in the CNP. These changes occur at all levels of the spinal cord and involve nociceptive pathways in multiple brain regions [ ].
In addition, peripheral nerve injury enhances spinal neuronal excitability through glutamate receptor activation, coupled with downregulation of spinal glutamate transporter proteins and reduced activation thresholds, leading to increased neuronal excitability and neurotoxicity [ ]. Similar to peripheral sensitization, inflammatory mediators play an important role, particularly through microglial activation and proliferation in the DRG and spinal cord following nerve injury. These activated microglia release cytokines, chemokines, and cytotoxic substances that induce cellular responses, ultimately altering central excitability and neuroplasticity [ , ].

## Others
Supraspinal processes in individuals with CNP exhibit notable variations in brain region metabolism and neurotransmitter concentrations, particularly in areas like the thalamus and cingulate cortex, compared to those without pain. The extent of cortical reorganization in patients with CNP strongly correlates with the pain level experienced following nerve injury. Beyond that, dysfunction in the production and release of GABA and the apoptosis of inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord can result in hyperalgesia because of reduced central inhibitory control [ ]. Furthermore, structural modifications within the CNS are facilitated by central disinhibition, as demonstrated by the transformation of Aβ nerve fibers, which typically transmit non-painful stimuli, into pain-specific secondary neurons in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord [ ]. These interconnected pathological changes collectively drive CNP development and maintenance.

## Applications of nanomaterials in different types of CNP
Nanomedicine utilizes nanotechnology to improve pharmaceutical efficacy and safety through the development of advanced materials. These nanomaterials leverage physicochemical and biological properties to enable the sustained or controlled release and targeted delivery, thereby prolonging therapeutic effects while reducing toxicity [ , ]. Currently, these nanomaterials are being gradually applied to CNP caused by a variety of diseases and show great potential in pain management.

## Chronic peripheral neuropathic pain

## TN
TN, a classic cranial neuropathic pain disorder, affects 0.03–0.3% of the population with an annual incidence of 4–29 cases per 100,000 individuals [ ]. Typically, TN results from the compression of the trigeminal nerve root by adjacent blood vessels, which leads to demyelinating nerve changes. Demyelination increases the vulnerability of the exposed nerve fibers to aberrant electrical activity, which leads to subsequent modifications in the nerve center. Moreover, the persistent inflammation of the trigeminal nerve root might raise the sensitivity of central pain receptors [ ].
Hydrogen, a natural antioxidant, selectively scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) like hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and peroxynitrite anions (ONOO−) to decrease oxidative stress [ , ]. Moreover, hydrogen can suppress cellular pyroptosis by reducing the expression of the inflammatory vesicle NLR family pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3), which positively influences the prevention and treatment of neuropathic pain [ ]. Mu et al. produced a continuous release of hydrogen in the digestive system by giving inorganic nanosilica through the stomach (Fig.  A) [ ]. Subsequent research demonstrated that this therapeutic approach can hinder the production of inflammatory substances and the process of cell pyroptosis in microglial cells by obstructing the NLRP3-Caspase-1-Gasdermin D (GSDMD) signaling pathway. As a result, this intervention relieved the damage to the protective covering of the trigeminal nerve and alleviated pain symptoms (Fig.  B, C). Notably, these neuroprotective effects were hydrogen-dependent, as silicon dioxide (SiO2) alone showed no comparable activity.
 

## Chronic pain after peripheral nerve injury
Peripheral nerve injuries, caused by mechanical trauma (stretching, cutting, or crushing) or ischemia to nerve structures, often lead to motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunctions, potentially progressing to chronic pain after peripheral nerve injury [ , ]. In China, these injuries affect a range of 300,000 to 500,000 individuals annually, accounting for 2.8% of trauma cases [ ]. Owing to the regenerative and reparative capabilities of peripheral nerves, prompt intervention is crucial to impeding the advancement of pain after peripheral nerve damage [ , ]. At present, the most prevalent animal models of chronic peripheral nerve injury in basic research are chronic constriction injury (CCI), spared nerve injury (SNI), spinal nerve ligation (SNL), and partial sciatic nerve ligation (PSNL) models. In response to these models of CNP, different nanomaterials have been progressively developed for pain management.

## Polymeric micelles
As a type of nanoparticle possessing a core-shell structure, polymeric micelles are produced through the self-assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers in an aqueous solution. The hydrophilic shell stabilizes and supports the hydrophobic core, which thereby augments the solubility of drugs in water [ ]. In Fig.  A, Ramírez-García et al. developed a pH-responsive polymeric micelle. The pH-responsive polymeric micelle capitalizes on the mechanism through which SP in CNP can consistently communicate pain by redistributing it to acidified endosomes via neurokinin 1 receptor (NK1R) [ ]. It is composed of hydrophobic core (2-[N,N-(diisopropylamino) ethyl] methacrylate (DIPMA) and di(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (DEGMA) copolymer) and hydrophilic shell (poly (ethylene glycol) monomethyl ether methacrylate (PEGMA) and 2-[N,N-(dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) copolymer) encapsulated with the NK1R antagonist aprepitant (AP). In the SNI rat model, the formation of central sensitization was significantly suppressed by decreasing the reflex of C-fibers and the phenomenon of wind-up (Fig.  B, C). Moreover, a prolonged inhibition was detected in the excitation of spinal neurons generated by SP (Fig.  D). This reduction was achieved by triggering the collapse of the DIPMA structure through the protonation of its tertiary amine structure in an acidic endosomal environment. Furthermore, PFPA-AP nanostars were also prepared by chemically bonding pentafluorophenyl acrylic acid (PFPA) to aprepitant amine (AP-NH2) (Fig.  E, F) [ ], which successfully inhibited the signaling of NK1R endosomes generated by SP and the activation of G proteins and β-inhibin. These experimental studies demonstrate NK1R endosomal targeting as a promising CNP treatment strategy, though clinical translation requires further validation in higher-order models.
 

## Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid)
As a biodegradable polymer excipient, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) has received approval for drug delivery from the US FDA. Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) might accelerate the development of CNP by promoting the production of ROS and proinflammatory molecules (IL-1β, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), TNF-α). In addition to that, it can stimulate the activation of microglia [ ]. Hence, suppressing the expression of NF-κB may be a promising approach for managing CNP. Lee et al. administered small interfering ribonucleic acid (siRNA) targeting the NF-κB kinase β subunit inhibitor (IKBKB) into PLGA and injected it into the spinal cord of rats with an SNL model (Fig.  A) [ ]. This was done through intervertebral space administration to prevent the nuclear translocation of NF-κB. In rats with peripheral nerve damage, the messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and cyclooxygenase 2 (COX-2) (Fig.  B-D), and the protein expressions of IBA-1 and IKBKB in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, were decreased (Fig.  E, F). This inhibited the activation of spinal microglia and led to a reduction in mechanical hyperalgesia.
 

## Lipid nanoparticles
As a delivery system employing natural or synthetic lipids as carrier materials, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have garnered interest by virtue of their biocompatibility, high stability, low cost, and straightforward mass production [ ].
Yang et al. effectively produced morphine-loaded lipid nanoparticles (Tet1-LNPs) using the thin film hydration technique (Fig.  A) [ ]. In Figs.  B, C, the experimental results demonstrated that Tet1-LNPs exhibited an analgesic effect lasting up to 32 h in a CCI mice model. The concentration of morphine in the rat brain was only 1/8 of that in the free morphine group when Tet1-LNPs were used. This led to a considerable decrease in adverse effects on the CNS (Fig.  D). Joshi and his colleagues encapsulated carbon monoxide-releasing molecule-2 (CORM-2) within solid LNPs (SLNs) to enhance its water solubility and control the release of CO [ ]. This approach aims to harness the anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties of CO. Additionally, mechanical pain in rats with a CCI model was efficiently alleviated by boosting the expression of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 while reducing the levels of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) in the DRG and spinal cord, as well as related glial cell markers (IBA-1 and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)) (Fig.  E). Nevertheless, CO has both positive and negative effects. By modifying its surface, CORM-2 can gradually release CO as a systemic gas messenger. However, it is important to carefully evaluate the controlled release of CO and the possible harm it may cause by accumulating in tissues that are not the intended targets. Furthermore, Professor Hazal Eken and his research team [ ] at Anadolu University in Turkey utilized SLNs as a carrier by administering vitexin-containing SLNs to CCI rats via the abdominal cavity. The study found that activating 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) receptors, norepinephrine receptors, and GABA-A receptors may facilitate the antalgic activity of vitexin in this manner.
 

## Inorganic nanomaterials
Recently, the use of inorganic nanoparticles has received considerable attention from scholars in the field of CNP due to their notable benefits, including excellent stability, high drug loading, and straightforward customization. As illustrated in Fig.  A, Lu and his colleagues developed a microglia-targeting mesoporous silicon nanoparticle (A-MSN) to deliver the analgesic micro RNA (miRNA) miR-26a-5p, which exhibited 42-day long-lasting analgesia in SNI model mice (Fig.  B) [ ]. This strategy mitigates microglia activation and resulting inflammation by utilizing the non-classical wingless (Wnt) signaling pathway, which is mediated by Wnt5a. Liu et al. demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) could efficiently migrate to the targeted area and effectively fulfill their regenerative function by combining magnetic ferrosoferric oxide (Fe3O4) nanoparticles coated with polydopamine with MSCs and administering them intravenously to rats with CCI models, along with the application of magnetic fields to the spinal cord segments of L4-L6 (Fig.  C) [ ]. This resulted in the reduced demineralization of spinal neurons, the expression of inflammatory factors, and the activation of neuroglial cells, which led to rapid and long-lasting pain relief and therapeutic effects (Fig.  D-I). Similarly, Wan et al. integrated magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles and dexamethasone in a thermosensitive injectable thermo-crosslinked hydrogel made of chitosan and β-glycerophosphoric acid, which resulted in a hydrogel exhibiting excellent magnetic response and anti-inflammatory properties (Fig.  J) [ ]. The acupoint of the Zusanli (ST36) in rodents with CCI of the sciatic nerve was injected with hydrogel and physically stimulated by a magnetic field stretching the surrounding tissue. The administration of this treatment resulted in a notable decrease in the levels of serum inflammatory markers (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and a reduction in the expression of the P2X4 receptor and the phosphorylation of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) protein in the spinal dorsal horn of rats (Fig.  K-N). Consequently, this treatment effectively alleviated CNP produced by sciatic nerve damage. Nevertheless, this work requires additional investigation to determine the precise impact of magnetic strength on acupoints. 
 Moreover, Seres and his colleagues created cerium zirconium oxide nanoparticles (CZ NPs) specifically targeting microglia (Fig.  A) [ ]. These nanoparticles work by suppressing the release of intracellular inflammatory factors and ROS, which thereby inhibits microglia activation. As a result, hyperalgesia in a mouse model with spinal nerve transection (SNT) was significantly reduced (Fig.  B). Kuthatl et al. employed a hydrothermal method to synthesize manganese oxide nanoparticles (MONPs) with good biocompatibility and practical ROS scavenging ability [ ]. When injected into the spinal cord of rats with a condition called SNT, the nanoparticles could significantly reduce mechanical pain sensitivity and the expression of COX-2 in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord (Fig.  C). While inorganic nanomaterials exhibit unique physicochemical properties that are advantageous for drug delivery, their clinical translation for CNP management remains challenging due to unresolved concerns about long-term biodistribution, metabolic fate, and potential toxicity. 
 

## Composite nanohydrogels
Nanohydrogels are swellable polymer networks created through physical or chemical crosslinking. They possess a distinctive three-dimensional structure capable of accommodating diverse materials such as nanoparticles, polymers, and small molecules to form composite hydrogels. These hydrogels have significant potential for use in drug delivery and organ targeting processes [ , , ]. Khanal et al. developed a delivery system of injectable hydrogels/lipid nanocapsules (LNCs) by utilizing LNCs as a sustained-release vehicle to transport the local anesthetic ropivacaine, which was encapsulated in a chitosan hydrogel containing dexamethasone (Fig.  A) [ ]. The composite hydrogels exhibited significant analgesic effects in the CCI rat model (Fig.  B).
 Zhang et al. selected esketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartic acid (NMDA) receptor antagonist, as a therapeutic drug [ ]. As shown in Fig.  C, the antagonist was encapsulated with PLGA and inserted into the hyaluronic acid hydrogel to create a composite system. After injection through the spinal nerve root, the complex hydrogel was inhibited by the excitation of DRG neurons and the activation of astrocytes in SNL mouse models to a large extent (Fig.  D-F). Additionally, the reactions of hyperalgesia and neuroinflammation were effectively alleviated.
In addition, Sorenzoni and his colleagues developed a chitosan composite hydrogel containing capsaicin nanocapsules (CHNCCaps) and applied it in PSNL mouse model to evaluate its therapeutic effect [ ]. The findings of the study indicated that CHNCCaps contributed to the good tolerance of mice and could effectively reduce the occurrence of adverse reactions caused by capsaicin, such as local burning, edema and pruritis, compared with commercial capsaicin cream. Meanwhile, the analgesic time was also significantly prolonged, and the long-term anti-hypernociceptive effect was still maintained within 6 days after the withdrawal of the drug. 

## Painful polyneuropathy
Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), a prevalent chronic consequence of diabetes, affects 34.86% of individuals with diabetes in China. The main symptoms of DNP include pain, numbness, and sensory abnormalities. Painful DPN (PDPN) is a disorder characterized by pain as the main symptom and influences 57.2% of individuals with DPN. The pain usually affects the lower extremities first and extends upward through “hosiery-like” sensory abnormalities [ , ].
Ziconotide (ZIC), as an N-type calcium channel voltage-gated antagonist, is prescribed for the treatment of persistent severe pain. Due to its substantial molecular weight and hydrophilicity, however, ZIC faces formidable obstacles in traversing the blood-brain barrier, which restricts its administration to intrathecal injection. To enhance the accessibility of drug delivery, Song et al. synthesized a modified liposome containing ZIC, which was encapsulated in an ice tablet [ ]. It was combined with MSC exosomes, and cast and cured into a microneedle drug delivery system (Fig.  A). The method markedly enhanced the level of ZIC in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Fig.  B) and showed both safe and efficient pain-relieving effects in the rat PDPN model. Furthermore, Xie and his colleagues employed soluble microneedles to achieve the painless transdermal administration of a CGRP receptor antagonist (Fig.  C) [ ]. This method succeeds in addressing the limitations of low oral absorption, brief presence in the bloodstream, and elevated systemic toxicity associated with this medication (Fig.  D). Rana G. Eissa et al. enclosed the tricyclic antidepressant amitriptyline and the antidiabetic peptide liraglutide within nanovesicles and administered them orally [ ]. This method is effective in decreasing blood glucose levels, alleviating pain, diminishing oxidative stress and inflammation, and enhancing the structure and function of nerve fibers. Usman Abid et al. developed a keratolytic gel using ethanol body nanoparticles containing epalrestat (EPL), quercetin, 4-formyl benzene boronic acid (QB complex), and urea [ ]. This gel significantly enhanced the absorption of EPL and the antioxidant activity of the QB complex. It also considerably improved pain thresholds, blood glucose, and lipid levels in rats with PDPN while reducing skin damage (Fig.  E, F). These preclinical studies demonstrated that these novel therapeutic strategies may offer potential avenues for PDND treatment, pending confirmation of efficacy and safety in more clinical trials.
 
Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-related neuropathic pain (HNP) is a common complication of acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) and affects approximately 25–85% of AIDS patients [ ]. Research has indicated that Gp120, the envelope glycoprotein of HIV-1, plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of HNP [ , ]. This condition can lead to neuropathy and CNP through a variety of mechanisms. Such mechanisms include the stimulation of neuronal injury and apoptosis, the facilitation of inflammatory factor release from the spinal cord, the activation of chemokine receptors in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, and the promotion of neuroglia activation in the DRG. Mainly expressed in primary sensory neurons, adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-gated ion channels are additionally associated with neuropathy. Mainly expressed in primary sensory neurons, P2X3 receptors have a strong relationship with the progression of neuropathic pain as well [ ]. In an investigation of the gp120-induced HNP mouse model, Zhao et al. discovered a substantial elevation in the expression of the P2X3 receptor in this model [ ]. This increase was concurrent with the inhibition of anti-inflammatory agents and the release of inflammatory factors. To tackle this problem, the amphiphilic polymer micelles (PEGMA-DMAEMA-MAO) comprising 2-DMAEMA and PEGMA were synthesized through the polymerization of a reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT). Subsequently, PEGMA-DMAEMA-MAO was employed to encapsulate curcumin, an anti-inflammatory medication, to improve its bioavailability (Fig.  A). Following intravenous injection, the synthesized curcumin micelles notably reduced the expression of the P2X3 receptor and the phosphorylation of the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) protein in the rat DRG (Fig.  B). This substantially lessened CNP caused by HIV-Gp120 (Fig.  C, D).
 

## Painful radiculopathy
As a prevalent spinal disorder, lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is brought about by the physical injury or degeneration of the lumbar intervertebral disc. This damage typically results in the prolapse of the nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus. Subsequently, the annulus pulposus compresses adjacent neural structures and triggers a radicular compression lesion characterized by low back and leg pain, among other symptoms [ ]. Studies have shown that nearly 90% of sciatica is ascribed to LDH [ ]. The degeneration of the intervertebral disc (IDD) is a significant contributor to the development of LDH. Mechanical injury, inflammation, and other factors contribute to the degeneration of the intervertebral disc, the loss of type II collagen and proteoglycans, and the decrease of water absorption. These consequences include the reduced height of the intervertebral disc, the rupture of the annulus fibrosus, water loss in the nucleus pulposus, and the calcification of cartilaginous endplates [ ]. The nucleus pulposus breaches the annulus fibrosus and compresses the spinal nerve root as an additional consequence of mechanical injury. Then, this progress causes nerve root edema, which subsequently modifies the condition of ion channels and induces nociception sensitization. Besides, the protruding nucleus pulposus ruptures the blood-disk barrier, which liberates ROS and inflammatory factors causing an autoimmune response that intensifies the pain [ , ].
In Fig.  A, Yang et al. designed a nanomaterial (MnO2@TMNP) by enclosing manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanoparticles in macrophage cell membranes (TMNP) with excessive tyrosine kinase-A (TrkA) receptors. MnO2@TMNP can impede apoptosis, matrix degradation, and inflammation-induced nerve growth [ ]. It can specifically target macrophages to eliminate excessive ROS and prevent their transformation into M1-type polarization (Fig.  B, C). Hence, it effectively decreases disc degeneration, delays its progression, and mitigates pain occurrence.
 Kartha et al. directed their attention to the pain resulting from nerve root compression and specifically targeted the secondary oxidative stress process triggered by ROS. They successfully encapsulated superoxide dismutase (SOD) within porous polymer vesicles, which thereby eliminated ROS and preserved SOD activity (Fig.  D, E) [ ]. The findings demonstrated that the local delivery of porous polymersomes loaded with SOD to the nerve root was more effective than other groups in terms of the length of pain relief and the amount of medication required (Fig.  F). Aside from that, Kartha et al. also discovered that the spinal cord excessively produces secretory phospholipase A2 (sPLA2) in response to nerve root compression [ ]. This enzyme hydrolyzes glycerol phospholipids and releases free fatty acids contributing to tissue inflammation and neuronal damage. As shown in Fig.  G, they compounded a polymer micelle coated with the sPLA2 inhibitor thioether amide-PC (TEA-PC) containing superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) to achieve this objective. This micelle takes advantage of the elevated levels of sPLA2 at the location of pain to precisely deliver SPIONs to the pain area and serve as an imaging agent. Simultaneously, TEA-PC can potentially reduce SPLA2-mediated inflammatory responses, inhibit nerve excitability, and decrease nerve root and spinal cord pain sensitivity, regardless of whether it is administered locally through the nerve root or intravenously (Fig.  H, I).
More than that, Li et al. synthesized a carbon nanomaterial FT-C60 by chemically bonding fullerene (C60), which possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, with a particular peptide segment designed to bind to formylpeptide receptor-1 (FPR-1) [ ]. The substance has a strong affinity for macrophages expressing FPR-1 and effectively suppresses the generation of intracellular ROS and inflammatory molecules, including IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1, and COX-2 (Fig.  J, K). Experimental evidence from laboratory studies suggests that FT-C60 exhibits antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which may help alleviate intervertebral degeneration-associated low back pain in animal models. These mechanistic findings justify additional research, though efficacy and safety in human patients have not yet been established.

## Postherpetic neuralgia
Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) is a highly contagious neurotropic virus that mainly causes chickenpox. It can remain dormant in the DRG or trigeminal ganglion after the first infection. When reactivated, VZV can cause herpes zoster, a skin infection featuring excruciating pain and a frequency of two to four weeks [ , ]. Of note, a proportion ranging from 5 to 30% of individuals afflicted with shingles develop postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), which is a severe pain condition ensuing after the resolution of lesions [ ]. Vaccination plays an indispensable role in preventing herpes zoster and PHN. In Fig.  A, Cheng et al. successfully designed and developed a novel herpes zoster mRNA vaccination [ ]. The vaccine utilized an improved lipid nucleic acid nanoparticle formulation. Specifically, its effectiveness was increased by incorporating an ionized lipid, L319, which can escape from lysosomes, into a traditional four-component LNP. The formation of mgE@Syn-LNP adopted a microemulsion method and successfully enclosed the mRNA encoding VZV glycoprotein E (gE) within the LNP. The vaccine induced potent humoral and cellular immunity in the PHN mouse model. The immune responses generated by these vaccines were not only of similar potency to commercially available recombinant herpes zoster vaccines but also considerably more effective than conventional live attenuated vaccines (Fig. B, C). Additionally, humoral immunity can be maintained for over seven months without inducing notable adverse effects (Fig.  D). Liposomal vaccines have demonstrated efficacy in cancer therapy, with several candidates, including Lipo-MERIT and mRNA-4157, currently in clinical trials. Building on this foundation, these animal studies show that liposome-formulated mRNA vaccines are effective against herpes zoster and its complications. These collective findings suggest a potential strategy for preventing and treating VZV infections, though further validation in large animal models and human-relevant systems remains essential.
 

## Chronic central neuropathic pain

## Chronic central neuropathic pain associated with spinal cord injury

Spinal cord injury (SCI), a severe condition affecting the CNS, typically impairs sensory, motor, and autonomic functions. It occurs at 15–40 cases per million individuals every year. Central neuropathic pain is the most common complication of SCI and affects a large number of patients (up to 53%). This condition severely impacts individuals’ life quality and functional rehabilitation [ , ].
Central pain following SCI is strongly correlated with inflammatory mediator secretion by microglia and atypical immune cell stimulation. Saunders et al. utilized PLGA to reprogram peripheral immune cells (monocytes and neutrophils) with nanoparticles [ , ]. This reprogramming effectively decreased the expression of neuroimmune-related genes (e.g., Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP1), and transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily A member 1 (TRPA1)) in the DRG. Therefore, the frequency of inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in the spinal cord was reduced, which improved the symptoms of central pain after SCI. Liu et al. created a pH-responsive oral microsphere called αCT/Res-JNP using a double emulsification-solvent volatilization approach (Fig.  A) [ ]. The microspheres contained a combination of two analgesics: α-cobra venom (αCT) and resveratrol (Res). This design maximized the use of the fast pain-relieving effect of Res and the strong pain-relieving effect of αCT in the CNS. It also ensured that the drug was released in the alkaline environment of the small intestine in a model of central pain generated by SCI. This allowed the drug to achieve a synergistic pain-relieving effect through multiple modes of action while enhancing its concentration in the blood and its availability when taken orally (Fig.  B). Jee et al. created a core-shell nanoparticle made up of SPIONs and zinc oxide (ZnO) (Fig.  C) [ ]. This nanoparticle can efficiently enter the nucleus of human adipose tissue-derived stem cells (hATSCs) and enhance their growth and ability to regenerate and differentiate into multiple types of cells, particularly neuronal and B cells. Also, the nanoparticles may increase the expression of the Dicer protein involved in miRNA processing (Fig.  D), and modulate the expression of proteins associated with stem cell differentiation and antioxidant activity in hATSCs, including octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (Oct4), glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPX3), etc. (Fig.  E, F). In an experimental model of SCI in mice, applying nanoparticles to hATSCs led to their differentiation into neuronal cells secreting GABA. This process occurs in the living organism and has successfully alleviated the central neuropathic pain associated with SCI.
 

## Chronic central neuropathic pain associated with brain injury
As a CNP syndrome caused by stroke, central post-stroke pain (CPSP) manifests as spontaneous pain, mechanical allodynia and hyperalgesia, and is often accompanied by complications such as superficial and deep sensory deficits, anxiety, and depression [ ]. The incidence rate of stroke in China is approximately 10 per 2.01 million individuals, while the prevalence of post-stroke discomfort varies between 19% and 74% [ ]. Hence, the successful treatment of CPSP is a crucial factor in enhancing patients’ quality of life and rehabilitation management.
Bai et al. focused on the neuroinflammatory cascade response triggered by cerebral hemorrhage [ ]. It was discovered that the inflammatory reaction triggered by cerebral hemorrhage might result in the generation of ROS from microglia, which plays a crucial role in causing endoplasmic reticulum stress. Excessive ROS levels can cause neuronal death, disrupt the blood-brain barrier, trigger inflammatory response, and activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). MMPs, which play a dual role in breaking down and restructuring the extracellular matrix, can both facilitate neural repair and worsen neurological damage. Thus, an innovative nano-enzymatic system (ER-Ru MOF) was developed (Fig.  A). This system utilizes ruthenium-based (Ru) nano-enzymes and zeolitic-imidazolate-framework-8 (ZIF-8) as functional cores, along with p-dodecylbenzene sulfonamide (p-DBSN) modified liposomes as carriers. The main objective of p-DBSN is to target the endoplasmic reticulum specifically. The nanoenzyme system efficiently eliminates ROS in the endoplasmic reticulum of brain cells in mice with the CPSP model. It reduces the expression of MMPs, inhibits inflammatory response-induced damage, and consequently considerably alleviates post-stroke chronic pain. This system efficiently treats CPSP (Fig. B-D).
 

## Translational challenges of nanomedicine for CNP
As summarized in Table  , the primary nanomaterials investigated for chronic neuropathic pain include polymers, liposomes, inorganic nanomaterials, and hybrids combining these materials. These nanomaterials have been shown to efficiently deliver drugs to target sites through active targeting strategies, such as external stimuli-responsive systems or ligand-mediated targeting, thereby improving drug bioavailability. In animal models, these systems have demonstrated pain relief through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of cell death pathways, suppression of neuronal hyperexcitability, and anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. However, despite these encouraging laboratory results, substantial challenges remain in adapting nanomedicine approaches for clinical use in CNP management.
 

## Scaling up production
The industrial-scale manufacturing of nanomedicines presents unique challenges, requiring the careful selection of pharmaceutical-grade excipients, precise control over nanoparticle properties (size, charge, and structure), and rigorous quality assurance to ensure batch-to-batch consistency [ , ]. Each production step must be meticulously optimized to preserve the therapeutic properties of drugs while meeting regulatory standards for pharmaceutical manufacturing.

## Stability and pharmacokinetic challenges
A comprehensive evaluation of stability and pharmacokinetic properties is a critical component of nanomedicine preclinical development. Two key stability considerations must be addressed: (1) storage stability (including resistance to aggregation and chemical degradation during freeze-thaw cycles), and (2) systemic stability (maintenance of structural integrity in biological fluids). While promising in laboratory settings, some polymeric and liposomal formulations exhibit structural alterations and drug leakage during long-term storage, which can potentially compromise their targeting specificity and increase the risk of off-target effects. Furthermore, standardized protocols for assessing long-term stability remain underdeveloped [ ]. Inorganic nanomaterials generally exhibit better stability but face distinct pharmacokinetic challenges. In addition, prolonged circulation and stimulus-responsive release profiles of nanomedicine, while therapeutically advantageous, differ fundamentally from those of conventional small-molecule drugs, potentially leading to cumulative toxicity [ , ]. The current lack of robust pharmacokinetic data for novel nanomedicines poses significant barriers to clinical translation.

## Toxicology and safety
Current approaches for assessing the safety of nanomedicine primarily rely on in vitro cell-based assays and in vivo animal studies. However, these methods present several limitations for predicting human toxicity: (1) conventional cellular models often oversimplify the complex metabolic and compensatory pathways of human physiology, and (2) existing animal models fail to fully recapitulate human disease pathophysiology [ , ]. These fundamental gaps in predictive capability have largely constrained the development of nanomedicine to preclinical research, with substantial uncertainties remaining regarding its biological interactions and safety profiles in human patients.

## Administration
The clinical translation of nanomedicines faces significant regulatory hurdles. Current safety evaluation standards, primarily developed for conventional free drugs, fail to adequately address the unique pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic profiles of nanomedicine formulations [ ]. Establishing appropriate regulatory frameworks will require a comprehensive characterization of nano-specific biological interactions, the development of new safety and efficacy assessment criteria, and the generation of sufficient preclinical and clinical data to establish standardized industry guidelines for nanomedicine evaluation.

## Summary and outlook
As a form of complex chronic pain, neuropathic pain significantly affects the life quality of patients. Diverse treatment approaches have been implemented, but specific individuals continue to encounter challenges in attaining total alleviation. As the investigation into the mechanisms underlying CNP persists, novel therapeutic strategies and techniques have been introduced in the form of nanomaterials. This review comprehensively outlined the therapeutic approaches and potential mechanisms by which nanomaterials are utilized to treat CNP caused by various diseases. These include eliminating harmful stimuli from pathological pain environments, modulating and targeting pain-associated membrane receptors or signaling pathways, and constructing long-acting local anesthetics. While nanomaterials have significantly transformed the approach to managing CNP, the current advancements in nanomaterial design and development have zeroed solely in on specific common targets or mechanisms. The further exploration of nanomaterials addressing the possible mechanisms of CNP is still in its infancy.
Despite significant research efforts to develop nanomedicines for improved pain management, only a limited number of promising preclinical formulations have achieved clinical approval. Currently available nanomedicine-based treatments include polymer-based morphine (Avinza®), liposomal morphine (DepoDur™), liposomal bupivacaine (Exparel®), and the VZV glycoprotein E vaccine (Shingrix®). We further discussed the key challenges in the clinical translation of nanomedicines, ranging from scale-up production to clinical administration. It is imperative to acknowledge the limitations of nanomaterials, including their physiological stability, biocompatibility, potential for long-term toxicity, technological cost, and even administration. Expanding the indications of existing FDA-approved nanomedicines for pain therapy—by evaluating their efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and mechanisms in CNP—may accelerate the development of clinically viable treatments. Additionally, optimizing nanocarrier preparation methods to improve stability and scalability may offer new therapeutic strategies for CNP. Beyond material design and experimental validation, the advancement of more predictive in vitro cellular models and in vivo disease-mimicking animal models will enhance preclinical safety assessments. As robust preclinical safety data and expanded clinical evidence from repurposed nanomedicines accumulate, establishing refined regulatory standards will be crucial. These guidelines will not only provide clear evaluation criteria but also facilitate the safe, efficient, and clinically effective translation of nanomedicines.
These investigations require an interdisciplinary approach, integrating knowledge and methodologies from chemistry, materials science, biology, and clinical medicine. Proficiency in clinical medicine and medicinal chemistry will be indispensable in advancing nanotherapeutic options that are both more effective and safer. A deeper comprehension of pain mechanisms and ongoing advancements in nanotechnology are anticipated to bring about a paradigm shift in the management of CNP through nanomaterials. This transition will result in safer and more productive pain control solutions in clinical settings.
