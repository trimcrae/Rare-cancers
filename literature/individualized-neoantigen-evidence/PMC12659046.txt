Moreover, LNPs have emerged as a promising vehicle for delivering therapeutic agents due to their ability to encapsulate various molecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, and small-molecule drugs while protecting against degradation and facilitating targeted delivery [ – ]. Their success in delivering mRNA vaccines, as demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic, has sparked interest in exploring their potential for cancer immunotherapy [ – ].
Recent studies have explored the use of LNPs in mRNA-based cancer vaccines, such as BioNTech’s BNT111 [ , ] and Moderna’s mRNA-4157 [ , ] designed to deliver personalized neoantigens for treating solid tumors.
In cancer immunotherapy, mRNA vaccines delivered by LNPs have shown promise in inducing potent and specific immune responses against tumor-associated antigens [ , ]. This approach leverages the body’s machinery to produce these antigens in situ, leading to a more natural and sustained immune response. Moreover, LNPs have been engineered to enhance their stability, reduce off-target effects, and improve biodistribution, making them an ideal vehicle for mRNA delivery [ , ]. 
LNPs are composed of ionizable lipids, helper lipids, cholesterol and PEGylated lipids [ , ]. Among these components, ionizable lipids play a pivotal role in forming nanoparticles for mRNA delivery [ – ]. LNPs containing ionizable lipids are known to enter cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis or macropinocytosis, with an entry efficiency of approximately 40 ~ 50% [ – ]. To enhance the efficacy of mRNA delivery into cells, it is essential to increase the intracellular uptake of the nanoparticles. Consequently, the research interests have been elevated in developing new ionizable lipids to improve the intracellular efficiency of the LNPs for mRNA delivery. Therefore, we hypothesize that diversifying the cellular entry mechanism of nanoparticles could provide a promising approach to enhancing the mRNA expressions in cells.
Intranasal (IN) delivery of LNP vaccines was developed to address several challenges associated with traditional intramuscular (IM) administration. IM vaccines, while effective, often require invasive administration, which can lead to reduced patient compliance in large-scale immunization efforts [ , ]. This issue is particularly pronounced among individuals with needle phobia, a condition that affects a significant portion of the population and can result in vaccine hesitancy or outright refusal [ , ]. Additionally, IM vaccines primarily induce systemic immunity and may not optimally engage mucosal immune responses. To overcome these limitations, IN vaccines were designed to leverage the nasal mucosa’s unique immunological environment, facilitating both robust systemic immunity and localized mucosal responses [ – ]. The non-invasive nature of IN delivery not only enhances patient comfort and compliance but also addresses the barriers posed by needle phobia, making it particularly suitable for mass immunization campaigns. This is especially crucial during global pandemics, where rapid and widespread vaccination is necessary to control the spread of diseases on a large scale.
In this study, we developed a new type of ionizable lipid mimicking materials, an inorganic-organic hybrid lipidoid, named Cdoids, specifically induce mRNA expression in the nasal cavity. This lipidoid is composed of a carbon dot (Cdot) chemically conjugated with fatty acids for mimicking phospholipid structural properties in a highly biocompatible material. Uniquely, the Cdoids also possesses intrinsic fluorescence, allowing for real-time tracking of LNPs in biological systems, thus providing an invaluable tool for mRNA delivery studies. The distinctive characteristics of the Cdoids facilitate the assembly of nanoparticles that effectively encapsulate and deliver mRNA, leveraging the immunological landscape of the nasal mucosa to induce robust overall immune responses and antigen-specific immune responses. Notably, the Cdoids broaden the range of endocytic pathways compared to conventional LNPs, resulting in improved mRNA expression within target cells. Comparative analyses with commercially available ionizable lipid (SM-102) demonstrated that our lipid is more effective in delivering mRNA into the cytoplasm of target cells while also offering superior safety profiles. This was evidenced by enhanced mRNA expression and lower cytotoxicity in in vitro experiments. Furthermore, using an animal model, we confirmed that intranasal administration of LNPs formulated with lipid-like materials resulted in significant mRNA expression in vivo, showcasing the potential of this lipid for use in mRNA-based cancer vaccines.
Moreover, when applied as a cancer vaccine, this lipid-mediated mRNA delivery system exhibited robust tumor-therapeutic efficacy, highlighting its promise as a new candidate for LNP-based mRNA therapeutics. The results of this study suggest that newly designed ionizable lipid material not only enhances the efficiency and safety of mRNA delivery but is also specialized in IN administration with enhanced immunologic responses for vaccine development.
Figure describes the mechanism of an intranasal mRNA cancer vaccine delivery system utilizing hybrid lipidoids (Cdoids). The system is designed to induce a strong immune response against tumors by facilitating the efficient delivery of mRNA via the nasal route. The Cdoids structure consists of a hydrophilic head, made of a pH-sensitive carbon dot, and a hydrophobic tail composed of oleic acid. This amphiphilic design allows the Cdoids to form stable nanoparticles capable of encapsulating mRNA. Upon nasal delivery, the mRNA@Cdoids are absorbed into cells through an expanded endocytosis pathway. In contrast to conventional lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which primarily enter cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, Cdoids leverage both clathrin- and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, providing an alternative and potentially more efficient cellular uptake route. This dual mechanism allows for enhanced intracellular delivery of mRNA, particularly within the nasal tissues. The Cdoids-based formulation demonstrates high-dose biocompatibility, ensuring minimal toxicity in multiple tissues, including the nasal, lung, and liver. Following vaccination, the immune system is primed to recognize and attack tumor cells that express the corresponding antigen. At the tumor site, the activated CD8 + T cells release interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), leading to tumor cell death and preventing further tumor growth. Additionally, inducing a potent antigen-specific immune response, where the translated mRNA antigen triggers the activation of antigen-specific CD8 + T cells and the production of antibodies such as IgG and IgA. This response ensures targeted recognition and elimination of tumor cells expressing the specific antigen, contributing to long-term tumor suppression and immune memory.

## Results and discussion

## Preparation and characterization of carbon dots (Cdots) and carbon dot based lipidoids (Cdoids)
The carbon dots (Cdots) and carbon dot based lipidoids (Cdoids) were prepared using API and citric acid, following the methods from previous studies [ , ]. To confirm the carbon dot formation with preserving of functional group in precursors, we compared the 1H-NMR spectra with synthesized Cdot and the precursors, which were API and citric acid. Upon Cdot formation, the peak of the carboxyl group of citric acid disappeared by condensation during carbonization process (δ = 12.41; a, 1H, -COOH, Figure S1A). In addition, characteristic imidazole signals were retained in Cdots, and their integration supported the incorporation of imidazole moieties within the Cdot structure (Figure S1B. S1C). The morphology of the synthesized Cdots was characterized by TEM, revealing a size of approximately 3–5 nm with a lattice spacing of about 0.3 nm (Fig. A). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses identified the elemental peaks present in the Cdot. Notably, the presence of imidazole, which plays a crucial role in this carbon dot, was confirmed by the N 1 s peak (Fig. B, C).
To verify the structure of the oleic-acid–conjugated lipidoid, we performed 1H-NMR analysis. Cdoids exhibited imidazole peak patterns originating from the Cdot with the oleic-acid–specific hydrocarbon pattern (olefinic and aliphatic chain signals). The integral ratios of hydrocarbon pattern in Cdoids confirmed successful conjugation while preserving the long hydrocarbon chain of oleic acid (a, δ = 0.85; 3 H, -CH3; b, δ = 1.24; 2 H, -CH2-CH3; c, δ = 1.24; 2 H, -CH2-; d, δ = 5.32, 2 H, -CH = CH-; Figure S2B, S2C).
FT-IR spectrum analysis of Cdots, oleic acid, and the conjugate (Cdoids) was performed (Figure S3). The strong carboxylic C = O band of oleic acid at ~ 1710 cm⁻¹ and its broad hydrogen-bonded O–H envelope (3000–2500 cm⁻¹) disappeared in Cdoids, while characteristic amide bands emerged at ~ 1650 cm⁻¹ (amide I) and ~ 1540 cm⁻¹ (amide II). Concomitantly, intense CH₂ stretches at 2922/2852 cm⁻¹, bending modes at 1465/1377 cm⁻¹, and a rocking band at ~ 720 cm⁻¹ confirmed the presence of a long oleyl chain on the Cdot surface. The amide-pair (1650/1540) together with the loss of the ~ 1710 cm⁻¹ acid band indicates that oleic acid is covalently attached predominantly via amide rather than ester linkage, defining the Cdoid structure as oleyl-amidated carbon dots.
We conjugated oleic acid to Cdots to create an ionizable lipidoids (Cdoids) that could ionize depending on pH. The synthesis of Cdoids was verified using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-Vis), and its increased hydrophobicity was confirmed through a critical micelle concentration (CMC) (Fig. D, E). We confirmed that CMC was formed at approximately 1.1 mg/ml for Cdots and 0.78 mg/ml for Cdoids. This suggests that oleic acid was successfully conjugated to Cdots to form a lipidoid. When dispersed in water, the Cdoids formed nanoparticles of approximately 100 nm, as confirmed by Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Fig. F). Although Cdoids exhibited fluorescence similar to the original Cdots, a significant decrease in transmittance was observed upon particle formation in the water (Fig. G). Furthermore, we investigated the pH sensitivity of Cdoids due to the imidazole group (Fig. H). We confirmed a pKa of approximately 6.7, indicating the retention of key features of the Cdots (fluorescence and pH sensitivity). Consequently, Cdoids used in this study were confirmed to be an amphiphilic substance capable of self-assembling in aqueous solution, with surface charge changed according to pH. Given its properties similar to ionizable lipids used in lipid nanoparticle (LNP) fabrication, we compared the biocompatibility of Cdoids with commercially used ionizable lipids (SM-102, ALC-0315, DOTAP, MC3). Toxicity was assessed through MTT assays alongside oleic acid-based phospholipids (DOPE) at various concentrations (Fig. I). The results indicated that Cdoids exhibited superior biocompatibility compared to commercial ionizable lipids and showed toxicity levels comparable to non-toxic phospholipids. Therefore, this paper aims to explore using Cdoids to fabricate LNPs capable of encapsulating mRNA, with applications in cancer vaccine development.

 

## Preparation and characterization of mRNA@ Cdoids lipid nanoparticle
The purpose of this study was to deliver mRNA using Cdoids, we formulated lipid nanoparticles (LNPs). The LNPs were prepared using four conventional components of LNPs: ionizable lipid, helper lipid, cholesterol, and PEG-lipid. The synthesized Cdoids was used as the ionizable lipid, along with DOPE, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG to create Cdoids LNPs. (Fig. A). Various combinations of Cdoids LNP were produced by adjusting the ratio of Cdoids to DOPE, and their size and surface zeta potential were measured (Fig. B). Cdoids LNP 11 was selected for its size and PDI value to represent the characteristic features of Cdoids LNP.
For LNPs, it is crucial that the surface zeta potential changes at an appropriate pH to effectively release mRNA within the cytoplasm [ , , ]. Therefore, we determined the pKa of Cdoids and Cdoids LNP (Fig. C). The Cdoids LNP, which include DOPE, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG, exhibited a slightly increased pKa value (pKa = 7.1) compared to that of Cdoids. The fabricated Cdoids LNP had a size of approximately 141.2 nm, fitting the ideal nanoparticle size range (50–200 nm) for efficient cellular uptake [ – ], and exhibited a surface zeta potential of −25.2 mV at pH 7.4. (Fig. D). Additionally, we measured size and zeta potential of conventional LNP formulation (SM-102 LNP) (Figure S4). SM-102 LNP was approximately 131.8 nm, and the zeta potential was – 4.03 mV at pH 7.4. We confirmed that the physicochemical characteristics of the conventional LNP and the fabricated Cdoid LNP did not differ significantly. The particle morphology and approximate internal structure were observed using TEM and Cryo-TEM (Fig. E, F). The TEM images showed dark spots (Red circle) approximately 5 nm in size, corresponding to Cdots, and a visible lipid layer, indicating a structure similar to mRNA-encapsulated LNPs. When confirmed using cryo-TEM, it was confirmed that black dots exist within the lipid membrane structure. (Red circle) This means that c dots exist within the formulation encapsulating mRNA. Through the characterization of Cdoids LNP, we confirmed the potential of Cdoids as a novel ionizable lipidoid capable of encapsulating mRNA.
We formulated LNPs for mRNA delivery and quantified mRNA encapsulation efficiency by measuring contents of Cy5-labeled mRNA in formulations. LNPs prepared with the conventional ionizable lipid SM-102 showed an encapsulation efficiency of ~ 84%, whereas Cdoids-based LNPs achieved ~ 79%. Although Cdoids LNPs appeared marginally lower, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant, indicating comparable encapsulation performance (Figure S5).

 

## In vitro and in vivo mRNA delivering efficacy of mRNA@ Cdoids
The aim of this study was to determine whether the Cdoids LNP formulation utilizing Cdoids can deliver mRNA to cells. Using a Cdoids LNP composition with appropriate size and PDI values, we encapsulated mRNA coding for Firefly luciferase (mFLuc) and delivered it to HeLa cells. The expression of FLuc was quantified using a Firefly luciferase assay to measure luminescence in vitro (Fig. A). The highest luminescence was observed in Cdoids 11; thus, this composition was selected for further experiments. To compare the in vitro expression levels with the world’s widely used ionizable lipid SM-102 in COVID-19 LNP vaccines, we conducted in vitro experiments using HeLa cells. The experimental groups are as follows: G1:PBS, G2:Free mRNA, G3: Blank Cdoids, G4: mRNA@SM-102, G5: mRNA@Cdoids. The mRNA delivery efficiency of the group using SM-102 (G4) was similar to that of the using Cdoids group (G5) (Fig. B).
Endosomal fusion assays using prepared endosome-mimicking liposome revealed comparable fusion/escape potential between SM-102 LNPs and Cdoids LNPs (Figure S6). Both formulations showed higher fusion at pH 6.4 than at pH 7.4 as an endosomal condition, indicating no meaningful difference in endosomal escape efficiency. To describe the cellular entry routes, we profiled endocytosis mechanism using various endocytic inhibitors. Previous studies indicated that the uptake pattern of nanoparticles using Cdoids differed from that of other nanoparticles, prompting us to investigate the uptake mechanism of Cdoids LNP (Fig. C). We used 5-[N-ethyl-N-isopropyl] amiloride (EIPA), Pitstop, and nystatin as inhibitors of macropinocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and caveolin-mediated endocytosis, respectively. After pre-treating the cells with each inhibitor for one hour, Cdoids LNP were treated 4 h. Notably, the luminescent intensities were decreased by EIPA, Pitstop and nystatin treated in Cdoids LNP. In SM-102 LNP treated cells, luminescent intensities were decreased by EIPA and Pitstop. Thus, Cdoids LNPs exploit more diversified endocytic entry pathways, engaging macropinocytosis, clathrin- and caveolae-mediated routes to a greater extent than SM-102 LNPs. Therefore, the data establish that endosomal escape is similar between formulations, while Cdoids LNPs engage a broader repertoire of entry routes. This uptake-pathway diversification increases the effective internalization flux under the tested conditions and accounts for the superior cellular mRNA expression observed with Cdoids LNPs.
After that, we hypothesized that Cdoids LNP, once inside the cell, do not remain in the endosome but interact with the endosomal membrane to release the encapsulated mRNA into the cytoplasm. Because the Cdoids composing the LNP become increasingly positively surface charged as the endosomal pH decreases. To verify this, we tagged the mRNA with Cy5 for imaging. Given that Cdoids LNP inherently possess the fluorescence of Cdots, we compared this with Cy5 fluorescence. Four hours after treating the cells with Free mRNA (G2) and mRNA-loaded Cdoids LNP (G5), confocal microscopy images were analyzed (Fig. D). We observed the fluorescence of Cdots, labeled in yellow, and Cy5, labeled in red. Compared to the group where the same amount of mRNA was delivered without a carrier (G2), the Cy5 fluorescence was significantly increased. Additionally, the mRNA encapsulated within the Cdoids LNP (G5) was effectively released into the cytoplasm, as evidenced by the minimal overlap (orange) of Cdot (yellow) and Cy5 (red) fluorescence, with distinct yellow and red signals observed separately (Fig. E). Additionally, colocalization between Cdot and mRNA was quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficients (PCC) in Fiji/imageJ. The PCC was calculated as 0.29, which indicates weak global correlation despite occasional co-existence in confocal images (Fig. D–E).
We evaluated in vivo expression using Cdoids LNP for potential application in cancer vaccines to intranasal route. We evaluated the expression level by intranasal administration of the LNP group made of mRNA-encapsulated SM-102 (G4) and Cdoids LNP (G5) (Fig. F). We confirm that the intranasal luminescence of the G5 was significantly increased compared to that of commercially using G4 formulate. When quantified, it showed a difference of approximately 60-fold difference (Fig. G). Ex vivo experiments were performed to confirm effective delivery through intranasal administration and quantify the expression of mRNA (Fig. H, I). Luminescence was observed exclusively in the nasal region, confirming the feasibility of vaccination through intranasal. Additionally, some studies indicating that nanoparticles administered intranasally could cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and accumulate in the brain, we evaluated luciferase activity in the brain to determine mRNA expression. The absence of luminescence in the brain confirmed that Cdoids LNP did not migrate to the brain. This indicates that intranasal delivery of Cdoids LNP does not pose unforeseen toxicity issues related to brain accumulation. To confirm the biodistribution pattern of Cdoids and mRNA, organ fluorescence imaging was performed after intranasal administration of Cy5-labled mRNA (free mRNA), Cdoids (Blank Cdoids) and Cdoid LNPs (mRNA@Cdoids). Following administration of Cdoid LNPs, both the intrinsic Cdoids fluorescence and Cy5-mRNA signal were highest in the nasal cavity (Figure S7). Fluorescence intensity in lungs was relatively lower than that observed when Cdoid particles or free mRNA were administered alone. These results align with the reporter mRNA (FLuc mRNA) expression data. Thus, Cdoid LNPs predominantly localize to nasal tissues while enabling local delivery of mRNA after intranasal administration. Meanwhile, to assess the toxicity of Cdoids, an approximately 440-fold higher dose (200 µg/mice) was administered intranasally. After 24 h, tissues (Mouse nasal concha, liver, lung) were harvested to evaluate toxicity (Fig. J–L and Figure S8). No histological toxicity was observed, and several key biotoxicity markers in the blood chemistry analysis were within the normal range. These findings suggest that Cdoids exhibit high biocompatibility, as no signs of acute toxicity were detected even after high-dose administration.

 

## In vivo tumor growth inhibition test and confirm immune activity
The ultimate goal of our study was to evaluate the potential use of Cdoids LNP as a cancer vaccine platform. Therefore, we fabricated a cancer vaccine using mRNA encoding ovalbumin (mOVA), the most well-known antigenic protein. The anti-tumor activity was then assessed using B16-F10 melanoma cells, which overexpress ovalbumin on their surface (B16-F10_OVA). mOVA@ cdoids was administered intranasally for immunization in 5-week-old C57/BL6 mice with three doses (Fig. A). After inoculating the mice with B16-F10_OVA melanoma cells, tumor growth suppression was evaluated by measuring tumor size and body weight changes of each mouse over 21 days (Fig. B, C). G5 (mOVA@Cdoid LNP group) exhibited superior tumor suppression activity compared to G4 (formulated with SM-102). This effect was attributed to the mRNA delivered rather than the delivery vehicle itself (G3, Blank Cdoids). Body weight remained stable across all groups throughout the study period, indicating that Cdoids did not exhibit toxicity. When examining individual tumor size changes, all groups except the G5 showed significant tumor growth (Fig. D, E). G4 (mRNA@SM-102 group) displayed a mild tumor suppression effect, but it lacked statistical significance. In contrast, G5 group demonstrated sustained tumor growth inhibition over the 21-day period, suggesting an activated immune response to mOVA. 

## Confirmation of immunologic response via nasal administration
The antitumor effect induced by immune activation through intranasal delivery of the antigen (OVA) is mediated by CD8 + T cells recognizing the antigen. A statistically significant increase in the population of cytotoxic T cells was observed in the G5 group compared to the other groups (Figure S9). Subsequently, to verify the activity of these CD8 + T cells in vivo, we conducted experiments to isolate and assess their function. Splenocytes from mice that had undergone three immunizations were harvested, and CD8 + T cells were isolated using a MACS cell separation kit. (Fig. A). The activity of vaccinated immune cells was confirmed using isolated CD8 + T cells. To assess the cytotoxic activity of isolated immune cells against B16-F10 cells, we utilized B16-F10 melanoma cells overexpressing OVA (B16-F10_OVA)as the target, with wild-type B16-F10 cells (B16-F10_wt) serving as the control to ensure that the observed cytotoxicity was OVA-specific. A live/dead assay was performed, wherein dead cells were stained red with propidium iodide (PI), and viable cells were stained green with Calcein AM (Fig. B). Confocal microscopy analysis revealed significant cell death in the G4 and G5, exclusively within the B16-F10_OVA. This indicates that the immune cells were effectively activated by OVA-targeted vaccination. Notably, a higher degree of cytotoxicity was observed in the G5 group compared to G4, suggesting enhanced immune efficacy in this group.
We assessed whether the immune system exposed to OVA antigen via Cdoids LNP could produce immune factors. We evaluated immune activity by measuring representative antibodies, IgG and IgA (Fig. C–F). IgG is an antibody present in the blood that recognizes and eliminates antigens. IgA, a marker of mucosal immunity, is activated upon intranasal delivery and works alongside IgG to recognize and eliminate antigens. Thus, the activation of both IgG and IgA contributes to an enhanced immune response to OVA. We conducted three immunizations using G5 (Cdoids LNP group) and G1-4 (control groups) and collected blood and nasal fluid samples. The total amounts of IgG and IgA were quantified using an ELISA kit. To assess antigen-specific immune activity, we used an ELISA kit to detect OVA-specific IgG and IgA. G5 showed a significantly higher level of IgG (Fig. C), and IgA (Fig. D) compared to the G4, with a marked increase in antigen-specific secretion as well (Fig. E, F). These results indicate.
that the intranasal vaccine using Cdoids LNP was effective, and Cdoids LNP formulations utilizing Cdoids demonstrated superior performance compared to the commercially available SM-102 lipid in intranasal delivery.
We aimed to investigate the role of another critical immune factor, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). Recognized as a key player in tumor immunotherapy, IFN-γ is a cytokine predominantly secreted by CD8 + T cells and is instrumental in orchestrating the immune response against tumors. The purified CD8 + T cells co-incubated either the whole OVA protein or OVA peptide sequences recognizable by CD8 + T cells in an IFN-γ ELISpot plate for 48 h. After developing the plate to visualize the secreted IFN-γ, dark spots corresponding to IFN-γ production were observed (Fig. G and I). When the number of spots was quantified and graphed, G5 exhibited the highest IFN-γ secretion. Notably, the experiment using OVA peptides confirmed that the T cell immune response was OVA-specific, with the G5 (Cdoids LNP) showing a particularly strong immune response (Fig. H and J).

 

## Conclusion
In summary, this study focused on developing and applying a new type of ionizable lipid, Cdoids, synthesized from carbon dots and oleic acid, demonstrating promising potential for mRNA delivery and cancer immunotherapy. Cdoids were characterized as an amphiphilic substance with pH sensitivity, confirmed by a pKa of 6.7, and demonstrated fluorescence properties. Compared to commercially available ionizable lipids such as SM-102, Cdoids exhibited superior biocompatibility with low toxicity, verified through various assays. Lipid nanoparticles (LNP) were formulated using Cdoids along with helper lipids (DOPE), cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG, resulting in particles with an average size of 141.2 nm and a surface zeta potential of −25.2 mV (pH 7.4). The Cdoids LNP successfully encapsulated mRNA, and their internal structure, observed through TEM, resembled typical LNPs used in mRNA delivery. Importantly, Cdoids LNP demonstrated efficient mRNA release in the cytoplasm, with cellular uptake occurring primarily via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, confirmed through inhibitor assays. This differs from the predominant endocytic mechanisms of traditional LNPs. Through this, mRNA expression could be significantly increased through various endocytosis routes. In vitro studies showed that mRNA-loaded Cdoids LNP effectively delivered and expressed the mRNA encoding Firefly luciferase (mFLuc) in HeLa cells. Furthermore, in vivo tests using intranasal delivery in mice indicated that Cdoids LNP outperformed commercially used SM-102 in terms of gene expression and tumor suppression. Ex vivo experiments confirmed that Cdoids LNP is localized primarily in the nasal region. For cancer vaccine development, Cdoids LNP encapsulating mRNA encoding ovalbumin (mOVA) were tested in an OVA overexpressed-melanoma model. The intranasal administration of mOVA@Cdoids demonstrated significant tumor growth suppression, accompanied by an activated immune response, particularly through CD8 + T cells and enhanced production of antigen-specific IgG and IgA antibodies. These immune responses were stronger than those induced by vaccines using SM-102, highlighting the potential of Cdoids LNP for effective cancer immunotherapy. Overall, the study demonstrated that Cdoids LNP, formulated with Cdoids, could serve as a robust platform for mRNA delivery in cancer vaccines, offering advantages such as enhanced biocompatibility, efficient gene delivery, and strong immune activation, particularly via the intranasal route. We suggest that Cdoids LNP could be a valuable alternative to existing LNPs in mRNA-based therapies.

## Experimental section

## Preparation of carbon dots (Cdots)
First, 1 g of citric acid (CA, Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in 15 mL of distilled water (D.W) in a flask, and different amounts of 4 M 1-(3-aminopropyl) imidazole (API; Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA) were added with vigorous stirring. The solution was then placed in a 35 mL vial, which was heated in microwave synthesizer (Discover 2.0, CEM Corporation, NC, USA) at 300 W, 250 ℃ for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature (RT), the yellow-brown liquid obtained was dissolved in D.W. Then, it was dialyzed against D.W using a dialysis membrane (MWCO:100–500, Spectrum Laboratories, Rancho Dominguez, CA, USA) for 2 days (exchanged three times per day) to eliminate the remaining free CA and API, followed by lyophilization for use in further experiments. To characterize Cdots, UV-vis absorption, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-alpha, Thermo, U.K.) were performed using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (UV-2350, Shimadzu, Japan), and JMS 700 (Jeol, Japan), respectively. Synthesized Cdot was confirmed by 1H-NMR measurement (500 MHz, Bruker).

## Synthesis and purification of Cdoids
The conjugation of Cdots and oleic acids (OA) was performed via conventional N, N’-Dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC; Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA) and N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS; Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA,) coupling esterification. Briefly, 100 mg of OA (Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA; 10 mL) and DCC (94.8 mg, 460 µM_1.3-fold OA in moles), NHS (52.9 mg, 460 µM_1.3 fold OA in moles) and activated OA in 6 h at RT. After activation, dicyclohexyl urea (DCU) was removed using a 0.45 μm Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) syringe filter and added to Cdots (180 mg) each vial that were dissolved in DMSO. After 24 h of reaction, the solution was purified by precipitation in diethyl ether (200 mL, 3 times), and LH-20 hydrophobic chromatographic columns (mobile phase: pure methanol) were used. Then, the final solution was dried in vacuo to remove methanol. Cdoids syntheses were evaluated using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (UV-2350, Shimadzu, Japan). Synthesized Cdoids was confirmed by 1H-NMR spectrum analysis (500 MHz, Bruker, Germany) and FT-IR spectrometry (Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer, Agilent, USA).

## Measurement of critical micelle concentration (CMC)
The critical micelle concentration (CMC) of C-dots and Cdoids at varying molar ratios in water was determined at room temperature using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, VT, USA) with 1.4 µM Hoechst 33,342 as a fluorescent probe. Initially, C-dots and Cdoids were dissolved in water at different concentrations, followed by the addition of Hoechst 33,342 stock solution to form a 1:1 (v/v) mixture in a 5 mL glass vial. The solutions were incubated in a dark room for 1 h to ensure sufficient interaction, with vortexing every 5 min. After incubation, each sample was transferred to a black 96-well plate with a clear bottom to measure the fluorescence of Hoechst 33,342 (excitation at 350 nm, emission at 481 nm). The size and zeta potential were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Zetasizer Nano Zs (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK).

## Acid-base titrations for measuring the pKa
The solution was prepared by dissolving C-dots and Cdoids at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in distilled water (DW), then adjusting the pH to 3.0 using HCl. Afterward, 20 µL of 1 M NaOH was added to monitor the pH change. The equivalence point occurred during the titration when the amount of added base neutralized the acid, making the concentration of [H+] equal to that of [OH-]. This point was determined using the GraphPad Prism software.

## Preparation of mRNA@Cdoids
Prepare the synthesized cdoids by dissolving in pure ethanol at 1 mg/ml concentration. Prepare cholesterol, DOPE, and PEG-lipid in the same way. Afterward, mix the lipid and add a determined amount of lipid to a 1 ml low binding EP tube. Prepare mRNA (mFLuc, mOVA, etc.) at a concentration of 100 ug/ml using pure water. Add a determined amount of mRNA to the tube containing the lipid mix and perform mild voltexing using a voltexer. Centrifugate 3 times at 3500 rpm using a 3.5 kda pore size amicon and replace the solvent with PBS. The size, zeta potential, and morphology of cdoids and other samples were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano Zs, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). mRNA@ cdoids was observed using a field emission transmission electron microscope (FE-TEM; JEM-F200(TFEG), JEOL Ltd, Japan) and cryo-EM Transmission Electron Microscope (Cyro-EM; Talos L120C, FEI, Czech) after staining with a 2% solution of uranyl acetate to obtain external images with enhanced contrast.

## mRNA encapsulation efficiency of LNP formulations
mRNA encapsulation efficiency of LNP formulations was measured using Cy5-labeled mRNA. Cy5-labeled mRNA was prepared according to manufacturer’s protocols (Label IT® Nucleic Acid Labeling Kit-Cy5, Mirus Bio, USA). Unencapsulated mRNA amount was measured after LNP formulations dilution in TE buffer. To measure mRNA contents in formulations, LNPs were diluted in TE buffer containing 1% (v/v) Triton X-10 (30 min, 37 ℃). The fluorescence intensity of Cy5-labeled mRNA was detected using microplate reader (Excitation 640 nm, Emission 670 nm; SynergyH1, BioTek, USA). mRNA encapsulation efficiency (%) was calculated using following equation. < mRNA Encapsulation efficiency (%) = [(Total mRNA contents – Unencapsulated mRNA)/(Total mRNA contents)] x 100 >.

## Cell culture and incubation conditions
HeLa cells were received from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, HyClone, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone, USA) and 1% antibiotics (HyClone, USA) at 37 °C into 5% CO2 incubator.

## in vitro luciferase assay
Hela cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/well in a 96-well clear bottom plate. After 24 h, the cells were treated with cdoids LNP formulated with firefly luciferase mRNA (mFLuc) at various ratios. Afterward, the cells treated with sample incubated for 24 h in culture media. After incubation, cells were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) and added lysis buffer. The activity of luciferase protein was measured using a kit and following the manufacturer’s protocol (Luciferase assay system, Promega, USA). The luminescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (GloMax, Promega, USA).

## Endosomal fusion assay using endosome-mimicking liposome
Endosome-mimicking liposomes were prepared to determine the fusion abilities of LNPs by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay. Endosome-mimicking liposomes were fabricated using 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (DOPS), DOPC, DOPE and NBD-PE (donor) and Rhodamine-PE (acceptor) (Avanti polar lipids, USA). Lipids were mixed in chloroform to form a thin film by rotary evaporation (Molar ratio, 25:25:48:1:1). The lipid thin film was hydrated with PBS at pH 7.4 or pH 6.4 and sonicated for 20 min. To detect formulations fusion, pH 7.4 or pH 6.4 PBS was put into black 96-well plates (100 µL/well), and the endosome-mimicking liposomes (2 µL/well, 1 mM) was added. Endosome-mimicking liposomes served as the negative control (Fmin). Triton-X in PBS solutions (2%, v/v) was added to the liposome wells as a positive control (Fmax). 10 µL of LNP formulations were added to the wells and incubated at 37 ◦C for 1 h. Fluorescence intensity (F) was measured using a microplate reader (Excitation 465 nm, Emission 520 nm; SynergyH1, BioTek, USA). < The formulation fusion (%) was calculated as following equation. Fusion (%) = [(F-Fmin)/(Fmax-Fmin)] x 100 >.

## Confirmation of the cellular uptake and endocytosis pathway
To observe the cellular uptake and localization of mRNA@SM-102 and mRNA@Cdoids, HeLa cells were cultured in six-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) and incubated for 24 h in a cell incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with serum-free (SF) medium containing Cy5 tagging mRNA@Cdoids and the treated cells were incubated at pH 7.4. The cells were then washed thrice with DPBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM 710 Meta, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Fluorescence images were analyzed using the CLSM image software (Zen series, Carl Zeiss, Germany). For endocytosis inhibition experiments, the cells were preincubated for one hour at 37 ℃ with different endocytic inhibitors following concentrations: 5-(N-ethyl-N-isopropyl) amiloride (EIPA, 50 µM), wortmannin (100 nM), chlorpromazine (Pitstop, 10 µM), and nystatin (50 µM). All chemical inhibitors were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA. One hour later, FLuc@Cdoids was added in SF media for 24 h. After treatment, follow the above in vitro luciferase assay protocol.

## Colocalization analysis
To quantify the degree of colocalization between intrinsic fluorescent Cdot and Cy5-labled mRNA, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was determined from the corresponding confocal images using Coloc2 plugin in Fiji/ImageJ software. The fluorescent image of Cdot was set as channel 1 and the Cy5-labeled mRNA image was set as channel 2. PCC was calculated with Costes’ automatic thresholding and randomization (≥ 100 iterations; PSF block size 2–3 pixels) [ ]. 

## Comparison of cytotoxicity with conventional ionizable lipids
Cytotoxicity was assessed using the MTT colorimetric assay. HeLa cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well), and 24 h after seeding, Cdoids, SM-102, ALC-0315, DOTAP, MC3 and DOPE (SM-102, ALC-0315 and MC3 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA. DOPE was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids, Huntsville, AL) were added to each sample into well (0.1 mL). The wells were returned to the incubator for 12 h. After incubation, the plate was rinsed twice with DPBS phosphate-buffered saline (Gibco BRL, Invitrogen Corp., CA, USA) to remove samples that were not internalized into the cells. The cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h. The plate was rinsed twice with DPBS and an appropriate concentration of the MTT reagent (Sigma-Aldrich Co. St. Louis, MO, USA) for 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO. Absorbance intensity was measured at 571 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, VT, USA).

## Animal studies
All animal experiments were conducted with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Catholic University of Korea (The approval number is CUK-IACUC-2023-020). C57BL/6 mice were procured from Orient Bio (Korea) and maintained under controlled environmental conditions Prior to experimentation, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane via respiratory inhalation.

## In vivo luciferase assay
5-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (n = 3) were administered the formulation for each group (PBS; G1, free mRNA; G2, Blank cdoids; G3, mRNA@SM-102; G4, mRNA@cdoids; G5) to the intranasal route. Each formulation (40 µL/mouse) was delivered into the nasal cavity using a micropipette, with all samples dissolved in PBS buffer (pH 7.4). Six hours post-administration, mice were injected intraperitoneally with luciferin (VivoGlo, Promega, USA) at a 15 mg/mL concentration. Luminescence signals were captured and analyzed using the LUCI imaging system (CELLGENTEK Co. Ltd., Korea).

## Ex vivo luminescence imaging of organs
To assess FLuc mRNA expression in murine organs the formulation for each group (PBS; G1, free mRNA; G2, Blank cdoids; G3, mRNA@SM-102; G4, mRNA@cdoids; G5) to the intranasal route. After 6 h, organs were harvested, immersed in luciferin solution (300 µg/mL) for 2 min to ensure uniform tissue coverage, and subjected to luminescence imaging. Luminescence signals were captured and analyzed using the LUCI imaging system (CELLGENTEK Co. Ltd., Korea).

## In vivo fluorescent images of Cdoids and formulations
To detect the fluorescent signal of Cdoids and Cy5-labeled mRNA in mice, samples were administered intranasally (Cdoids, 1 mg/kg, Cy5-mRNA, 0.5 mg/kg). After 6 h, mice were euthanized by CO2 gas. Before imaging, transcardiac perfusion was performed with PBS at RT. After perfusion, organs were excised for fluorescent imaging. Fluorescence imaging and intensity were measured by using FOBI (NeoScience. Korea).

## Quantification of T cell populations in treated mice
To quantify T cell populations following treatment, spleens were harvested from mice in each group (5-week-old female C57BL/6 mice, n = 3 per group) that had undergone three times immunizations. Spleens were homogenized, treated with red blood cell lysis buffer, and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min to isolate cells. To identify CD4 + and CD8 + T cell populations, cells were stained with CD3-FITC, CD4-APC, and CD8-PE antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a FACS Canto II system (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software, version 10.

## Isolation of CD8 + T cells from the spleens of vaccinated mice
To isolate CD8 + T cell populations, we employed the MACS Cell Separation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) following a detailed protocol. Initially, cells were harvested from each group of vaccinated mice and prepared into a single-cell suspension by filtering through a 70 μm cell strainer to eliminate clumps. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of cold buffer (PBS, 0.5% BSA, 2 mM EDTA, pH 7.2). Next, cell concentration was adjusted to 10⁷ cells per milliliter. The cells were labeled with magnetic beads specific to the CD8 + cell population, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation, the cells were applied to a MACS Column placed in a MACS Separator. The column was washed with buffer to remove unbound cells, ensuring that only the magnetic beads-bound target cells remained in the column. Following the magnetic separation, the column was removed from the separator, and the bound cells were eluted with buffers. The eluted cells were collected and used in subsequent experiments.

## Quantification of OVA-specific T cells by ELISpot assay
The frequency of IFN-γ-secreting cells was determined using a mouse IFN-γ enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay, following the manufacturer’s protocol (ELISpot Plus, #3321-4APT-2, Mabtech, Sweden). Briefly, spleens were isolated from mice in each group. (C57BL/6 mice, n = 5 per group). Before culturing, the ELISpot plate was preconditioned with culture medium (RPMI 1640, 10% FBS, 1% antibiotics) for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, followed by washing with DPBS containing 0.5% FBS. Isolated splenocytes were cultured in 96-well ELISpot plates precoated with IFN-γ antibodies, in the presence of 10 µg/mL OVA whole protein (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) or 5 µg/mL OVA peptide. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h, followed by washing with DPBS containing 0.5% FBS. Biotin-conjugated detection antibodies were then added and incubated at room temperature (RT) for 2 h, followed by the addition of streptavidin-ALP and incubation at RT for 1 h. The substrate was added, and incubation continued until distinct spots developed in the wells. The reaction was terminated by washing with tap water, and after drying the plate, spot counts were automatically recorded.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences between groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s and Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests. Statistical significance was determined using GraphPad prism.
