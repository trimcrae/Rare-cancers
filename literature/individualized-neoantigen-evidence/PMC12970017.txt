## Abstract
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive malignant tumor affecting the central nervous system. Current standard of care provides only modest clinical benefits, most patients eventually develop tumor recurrence, at which point therapeutic options become markedly restricted and the prognosis remains dismal. In recent years, immunotherapy has achieved significant clinical success in several solid tumors and has become a key strategy for advanced cancers owing to its specificity and potent antitumor activity. Nevertheless, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of recurrent GBM (rGBM), along with its pronounced heterogeneity, and the presence of blood-brain barrier collectively undermine the efficacy of immunotherapy. With increasing insights into the tumor microenvironment and immune escape mechanisms, alongside refinements in immunotherapeutic strategies, immunotherapy may ultimately become one of the therapeutic options for patients with rGBM. Currently, principal immunotherapies under investigation for rGBM include immune checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T cell therapy, oncolytic virotherapy, and cancer vaccines. In this review, we comprehensively summarize the clinical evidence for these approaches, highlight their mechanisms of action, and critically evaluate the key challenges that limit clinical translation. By integrating recent therapeutic progress and unresolved obstacles, we aim to provide a timely perspective on the optimization of immunotherapy in rGBM and to inform the rational design of future clinical trials.
Keywords: recurrent glioblastoma, immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T cell therapy, oncolytic virotherapy, cancer vaccines, tumor microenvironment

## Introduction
Glioblastoma (GBM) accounts for approximately 61% of glioma diagnoses and has an annual incidence of about 3.27 per 100,000 population, with incidence peaking in older adults (aged ≥65 years). The current standard of care (SOC)—Stupp protocol, consisting of maximal safe surgical resection followed by concomitant radiotherapy (RT) and temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy, results in a median overall survival (mOS) of only 14.6 months, with a 5-year survival rate of <10%, underscoring the dismal prognosis. GBM exhibits diffuse infiltration and marked therapeutic resistance; most patients eventually experience recurrence. Furthermore, recurrent lesions frequently harbor distinct molecular alterations and phenotypic traits that differ from the primary tumor, contributing to treatment refractoriness and complicating management. After recurrence, even aggressive treatment confers an mOS of 6.5 months and a median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 5.5 months. 
Based on the latest research data, repeat surgical resection of recurrent GBM (rGBM) primarily serves to alleviate neurological symptoms or obtain updated histopathological information, rather than to significantly improve survival outcomes. Considering patient age, functional status, tumor pathology and lesion location, favorable responses to repeat surgery are observed in only a minority of patients with rGBM. With the refinement of precision RT approaches, studies have demonstrated improved survival in rGBM patients undergoing re-irradiation. Nevertheless, 90–95% of GBM recurrences arise within regions previously exposed to high-dose RT, and re-irradiation can induce significant neurotoxicity. Moreover, although RT combined with TMZ extends the survival of patients, this strategy may also trigger tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling, induce therapeutic resistance, and enhance tumor invasiveness. Thus, conventional salvage strategies offer only incremental benefit, and novel approaches are urgently needed.

## Current Immunotherapy in Recurrent Glioblastoma
In recent years, immunotherapy has become integral to the management of multiple solid tumors—including melanoma and lung cancer—where it has demonstrably improved clinical outcomes. , However, the clinical success of immunotherapy in rGBM remains limited, underscoring the importance of a timely and critical examination of barriers to clinical translation. In this review, we summarize the latest immunotherapeutic strategies under investigation for rGBM—including immune checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T cell therapy, oncolytic virotherapy, and cancer vaccines (literature search cutoff date: November 2025).

## Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors (ICIs)
Immune checkpoints are critical regulators governing immune homeostasis, curbing excessive T cell activation to avert the dysregulation of autoimmunity. However, cancer cells can suppress antitumor immune responses by upregulating immune checkpoint including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4). ICIs disrupt these inhibitory signals, thereby enhancing T cell–mediated tumor recognition and cytotoxicity, and have reshaped the treatment landscape in oncology ( ).

 

## PD-1 and PD-L1 Pathway
PD-1 is a co-inhibitory receptor residing on the membranes of certain immune cells, particularly T cells. Its ligand, PD-L1, is widely expressed on tumor cells and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Blocking PD-1/PD-L1 binding effectively reduces tumor immune evasion. Substantial clinical studies show that nivolumab (PD-1 inhibitor) extends OS in various malignant tumors. In human GBM specimens, PD-L1 expression was detected in 88.0% of newly diagnosed GBM (nGBM) and 72.2% of rGBM samples. Meta-analysis revealed that elevated PD-L1 expression in GBM tissue is significantly correlated with poor prognosis. These findings highlight the pivotal involvement of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in the pathobiology of rGBM.
CheckMate 143 (NCT02017717) was the first large, randomized phase III trial to assess PD-1 blockade in 369 patients with rGBM, comparing nivolumab with the antiangiogenic drug bevacizumab. Results showed that nivolumab did not improve outcomes compared to bevacizumab (mOS, 9.8 vs. 10.0 months; mPFS, 1.5 vs. 3.5 months). CheckMate 548 enrolled 716 nGBM patients with methylated O6-methylguanine–DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter, constituting the largest phase III study in this subgroup. Incorporating nivolumab into the SOC (RT + TMZ) did not significantly prolong mOS or mPFS (mOS, 28.9 vs. 32.1 months; mPFS, 10.6 vs. 10.3 months). Similarly, CheckMate 498 randomized 560 patients with GBM harboring an unmethylated MGMT promoter to receive RT plus nivolumab or RT plus TMZ in order to determine whether immunotherapy could offset the limitations of chemotherapy; survival was inferior in the nivolumab arm. Salvage nivolumab was examined in a small retrospective cohort of patients with bevacizumab-refractory rGBM, revealing an mOS of 3.5 months and a 6-month PFS rate (PFS-6) of 0%, suggesting limited clinical benefit. 
Although these studies failed to achieve their predefined primary endpoints, dissecting their limitations is critical for refining future immunotherapeutic strategies. In CheckMate 143, a post hoc exploratory analysis found that in the nivolumab arm, patients not receiving baseline corticosteroids had a longer mOS than those who received baseline corticosteroids (12.6 months vs. 7.0 months). This result may be attributed to the rapid progression of patients with cerebral edema requiring corticosteroid treatment, leaving an insufficient therapeutic window to realize the delayed benefit of ICIs, and the inhibitory effect of corticosteroids on T-cell function may weaken immune activation and limit the efficacy of ICIs. In contrast, bevacizumab can rapidly normalize vascular permeability and alleviate edema, inducing early radiographic responses, whereas ICIs require time to reinvigorate exhausted T cells, which makes it difficult to obtain sufficient benefit in rapidly progressive rGBM. In addition, nivolumab is a large-molecule antibody (146 kDa) with limited penetration across the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which restricts the translocation of molecules exceeding approximately 400–600 Da; even though the tumor, surgery, and RT/chemotherapy can cause local disruption of the BBB, the effective intratumoral exposure of the antibody may still be spatially heterogeneous, leading to insufficient blockade of lymphocytes expressing PD-1 within the tumor. Therefore, the effect of ICIs may depend primarily on peripheral immune activation and recruitment of T cells into the TME, rather than on high intratumoral antibody concentrations. These factors provide an explanation for the negative results of CheckMate 143 that is more consistent with trial design and tumor biology. Moreover, results from the CheckMate 548 and CheckMate 498 trials—highlight that MGMT promoter methylation alone is an insufficient predictor of benefit from PD-1 blockade, underscoring the need for composite selection criteria that integrate features of the tumor immune microenvironment and additional molecular markers to optimize immunotherapy outcomes in the recurrent setting.
Notably, neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade—administered preoperatively—has shown encouraging outcomes in clinical trials. A randomized trial assessed the immune responses and survival of 35 rGBM patients, administering another PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab in the neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting. Patients receiving neoadjuvant pembrolizumab showed significantly improved mOS and mPFS (417 vs. 228.5 days, p = 0.04; 99.5 vs. 72.5 days, p = 0.03) compared with patients receiving only adjuvant PD-1 blockade. Mechanistic studies revealed that neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade upregulated intratumoral expression of T cell and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) signature genes with concomitant downregulation of cell-cycle pathways. Additionally, a Phase II trial (NCT02550249) accrued 30 patients, including 27 with rGBM and 3 with nGBM, who received neoadjuvant nivolumab. Multi-omics analysis of pre- and post-surgical tumor samples confirmed that neoadjuvant nivolumab upregulated chemokine expression, which facilitated immune cell infiltration into tumors, and significantly increased T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire diversity in TILs. These studies suggest that neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade enhances antitumor activity through remodeling the TME and strengthening T cell–based immune responses, opening new avenues for the application of ICIs in rGBM. However, identifying optimal candidates for neoadjuvant immunotherapy remains a critical objective for future large-scale clinical studies.
PD-L1 inhibitors, including atezolizumab and durvalumab, have likewise been evaluated for preliminary efficacy in rGBM. , 

## CTLA-4 Pathway
Upon immune challenge, B7 ligands (B7-1/2) on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) engage the T-cell costimulatory molecule CD28, delivering signals promoting T cell differentiation, proliferation, and secretion of cytokines, thereby amplifying the immune response. CTLA-4 competes with CD28 for B7 binding with an affinity more than tenfold higher, thereby inhibiting T cell activation and expansion. Ipilimumab was the first monoclonal antibody targeting CTLA-4, has demonstrated therapeutic effectiveness across multiple cancer types, notably melanoma. In the murine GBM SB28 model, systemic CTLA-4 blockade elicits robust T-cell responses, reduces tumor burden, and prolongs survival. Additionally, studies have shown that ipilimumab elicits therapeutic responses among individuals with CNS-involved metastatic melanoma. These findings indicate the potential therapeutic activity of ipilimumab in the CNS tumors.
A phase II trial assessed the efficacy of ipilimumab plus TMZ versus TMZ alone in patients with nGBM resection and RT, the combined regimen failed to produce a clinically relevant benefit (mOS, 22.7 vs. 26.4 months; mPFS, 10.9 vs. 12.5 months). Although radiographic responses were observed in a small subset of patients treated with ipilimumab, the lack of reliable predictive biomarkers remains a challenge due to the intratumoral heterogeneity and immunosuppressive TME in GBM. Future studies should focus on identifying responder subgroups through comprehensive tumor and genomic biomarker analyses, rather than expecting overall clinical benefit in unselected patient populations. Another clinical study assessed the therapeutic impact and safety of ipilimumab plus bevacizumab in a small GBM patient cohort (n=20). A partial response was achieved in 31% of patients, with an additional 31% exhibiting stable disease and 38% experiencing disease progression. The investigators concluded the combined approach was safe, with manageable toxicity. However, given that 35% of patients experienced grade 3 toxicity, larger and more rigorously designed trials are warranted to validate the safety of ipilimumab.
Exhausted T cells commonly co-express multiple immune checkpoint receptors; therefore, blocking a single checkpoint is insufficient to effectively restore suppressed antitumor immunity. This limitation is particularly evident in rGBM, where the TME exerts profound immunosuppression. Therefore, concurrent blockade of multiple immune checkpoint pathways using combination ICIs represents an emerging therapeutic approach that may elicit more robust antitumor immune responses. In a phase I trial (NCT03233152), Duerinck team investigated the feasibility and safety of intraoperative intracranial administration of ipilimumab and nivolumab in 27 patients with rGBM, with the aim of improving both efficacy and safety. In the further study, they enrolled 42 patients with recurrent high-grade glioma (rHGG) and showed that direct intracerebral administration of nivolumab ± ipilimumab via intraoperative and postoperative delivery through an Ommaya reservoir was feasible and safe. The mOS reached 35 weeks, exceeding historical benchmarks despite no significant differences among cohorts. The two studies provide early clinical evidence that direct intracranial delivery of combination ICIs is a feasible and safe strategy, potentially enhancing local immune activation in the CNS and offering survival benefits in treatment-refractory tumors.
Beyond the well-established checkpoints, a second wave of immunoregulatory targets—most notably LAG-3, TIM-3, TIGIT and IDO1—is being explored for therapeutic modulation in rGBM, which will provide actionable targets for patients who are poorly responsive to PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 blockade, and enables the rational design of combination immunotherapies tailored to distinct immune phenotypes, potentially improving both efficacy and safety. LAG-3 represents an early indicator of T-cell exhaustion, suggesting that its blockade could confer therapeutic advantages. Accordingly, two phase I clinical trials (NCT02658981 and NCT03493932) are evaluating the LAG-3 inhibitor relatlimab in patients with rGBM. TIM-3 is another co-inhibitory receptor expressed on immune cells. One ongoing trial (NCT03961971) is assessing the TIM-3 inhibitor sabatolimab in combination with the PD-1 inhibitor spartalizumab and RT for rGBM. TIGIT, which is expressed on activated T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, has shown in preclinical models that its blockade can amplify antitumor activity, providing the rationale for the phase 0/I study (NCT04656535) of domvanalimab in combination with the PD-1 inhibitor zimberelimab in rGBM. In parallel, the combination of the IDO1 inhibitor BGB-5777 with PD-1 blockade has demonstrated significant and durable survival benefits in murine models, prompting ongoing phase I/II clinical studies in GBM. 
While most initial trials of ICIs have yielded negative results, substantial research avenues remain in rGBM, with numerous studies still underway ( ). Future studies should optimize patient selection by integrating biomarkers and molecular features, and improve drug delivery methods to overcome the limitations of the BBB, maximizing the exposure of ICIs in the CNS. Additionally, while concurrent blockade of multiple immune checkpoints has shown safety and activity in early trials, further exploration is required to fully optimize this approach, thereby improving survival outcomes in rGBM patients. , 
 

## CAR-T Cell Therapy
CAR-T cell therapy is an immunotherapeutic approach in which autologous T cells are genetically engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR), which recognizes tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) through its single-chain variable fragment (scFv) domain, mediating targeted cytotoxicity independent of endogenous T cell activation and directly recognizing specific antigens on the surface of cancer cells ( ). CAR-T cell therapy has achieved substantial clinical success in several hematologic malignancies including leukemias and lymphomas, since it was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2017. , Several studies have explored CAR-T cell therapy in patients with rGBM. The molecular targets include interleukin-13 receptor α2 (IL13Rα2), epidermal growth factor receptor variant III (EGFRvIII), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), with ongoing advancements focused on optimizing CAR constructs, discovering novel targets, and incorporating adjuvant enhancers to improve antitumor activity ( ).

 

## IL13Rα2
IL13Rα2 has been widely explored as a target antigen for CAR-T cell–based therapy in rGBM. Studies have shown that IL13Rα2 is overexpressed in more than 60% of GBM cases and in glioma stem cells (GSCs); correlates with higher malignancy and unfavorable prognosis. , 
The clinical trial (NCT00730613) evaluated the tolerability of IL13Rα2-CAR-T cell therapy in rGBM patients, demonstrating favorable tolerability and brief antitumor responses in two of three patients. Subsequent study incorporated the 4-1BB costimulatory domain and a mutated IgG4-Fc linker in the CAR-T cell, improved its specificity and safety. One patient showed significant regression of both intracranial and spinal lesions following intracavitary and intraventricular infusions, with disease control lasting 7.5 months before recurrence at new sites. , These findings underscore the clinical viability of IL13Rα2-CAR-T therapy in rGBM, highlighting the benefits of combined intracavitary and intraventricular delivery in controlling both local and distant CNS lesions, though durability of response remains limited. In a 2024 study, Brown’s team conducted the largest trial of IL13Rα2-CAR-T therapy among 65 patients with rGBM. Repeated local infusions (intracavitary and/or intraventricular) were feasible and safe, inducing IFN-γ-related cytokine responses in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and tumor cavity fluid. Patients receiving dual-route delivery achieved the greatest cytokine induction and showed a statistically significant OS improvement compared with other cohorts. Furthermore, given the limitations of personalized CAR-T cell therapy, notably its high treatment cost and prolonged cycle, the investigators developed a premanufactured glucocorticoid-resistant allogeneic IL13Rα2-CAR-T cell product. In a phase I clinical trial (NCT01082926) involving six GBM patients, the treatment was well tolerated and elicited transient antitumor activity in four patients, supporting the further development of this approach. 

## EGFRvIII
EGFRvIII is a ligand-independent, oncogenic EGFR variant that drives GBM progression and is detected in approximately 30% of tumors, making it a priority target for CAR-T cell therapy. 
Early clinical investigations of EGFRvIII-CAR-T cell therapy (NCT02209376 and NCT01454596) for rGBM have shown that intravenous delivery is feasible, with CAR-T cells successfully trafficking to tumor lesions and proliferating transiently without significant off-target toxicities; however, despite some patients achieving long-term survival, the clinical efficacy remained limited, with an mOS of approximately 8.3 months. , To address antigen heterogeneity and the immunosuppressive TME, Brian’s team developed CARv3-TEAM-E, an engineered EGFRvIII-targeted T cell product that secretes T cell-engaging antibody molecules (TEAMs) to recruit bystander T cells against wild-type EGFR. In vitro, these TEAM molecules effectively engaged regulatory T cells and redirected them toward the tumor site. A phase I trial (NCT05660369) of intraventricularly infused CARv3-TEAM-E T cells showed safety and significant tumor regression in three GBM patients, with one showing a response lasting over six months. Additionally, a phase I trial (NCT05168423) comprising 18 patients with rGBM who were intraventricularly administered dual-targeted CAR-T cells for EGFR and IL13Rα2 and showed the safety and biological activity of CART-EGFR-IL13Rα2 cells in rGBM. However, further confirmation in larger, longer-term studies and the identification of optimal patient subsets and predictive biomarkers are still required.
GBM cells frequently express high levels of immunosuppressive molecules such as PD- L1, which engage PD-1 on CAR-T cells and thereby drive functional exhaustion. This provides a biological rationale for combining CAR-T therapy with ICIs to counteract PD-1/PD-L1–mediated inhibition and sustain CAR-T cell cytotoxicity. However, a phase I clinical trial (NCT03726515) evaluating pembrolizumab in combination with EGFRvIII-CAR-T cells in GBM did not demonstrate meaningful clinical benefit. Mechanistic analyses suggest an expansion of glioma-associated macrophages (GAMs) and regulatory T cells in a subset of patients following CAR T-cell therapy. This observation may indicate additional immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME, suggesting that concurrent PD-1 blockade may be insufficient to reverse CAR-T cell exhaustion and restore effector function. Future studies should incorporate comprehensive TME profiling to delineate the underlying pathways and to develop rational combination strategies that mitigate GAM- and regulatory T cell-mediated immunosuppression. Despite a compelling biological rationale for combining ICIs with CAR-T cells, clinical trials to date have failed to translate this potential into a survival benefit, leaving these combination strategies in a strictly hypothesis-generating stage. In parallel, EGFR-CAR-T cells engineered to express antibodies against immune checkpoints such as CTLA-4 and PD-1 may offer an alternative avenue to overcome local immunosuppression, and this approach is currently under investigation in an ongoing clinical trial (NCT03182816).

## HER2
HER2 is a tyrosine kinase detected in ~80% of GBM cases while being absent in normal CNS tissue. , Dysregulation of HER2 expression promotes cell proliferation, malignant transformation, tumor invasion, and drug resistance. 
A phase I clinical trial (NCT01109095) involving 17 patients with GBM assessed the safety and antitumor activity of systemic administration of HER2-specific CAR-modified viral-specific T cells. Another phase I clinical trial (NCT03500991) assessed the local application of HER2-CAR-T cells in young patients with rGBM and detected local CNS immune activation. To mitigate antigen escape–driven recurrence after CAR-T therapy, investigators engineered a tandem CAR (TanCAR) that co-targets HER2 and IL13Rα2 by fusing a HER2-specific scFv with a mutant IL-13 ligand and incorporating a CD28–CD3ζ signaling module. In a murine GBM model, TanCAR-T cells curtailed antigen escape, elicited more durable antitumor responses, and prolonged survival. In another preclinical study, researchers developed a trivalent CAR-T cell targeting HER2, IL13Rα2, and Ephrin type-A receptor 2 (EphA2); at relatively low doses, these cells effectively suppressed autologous GBM growth in patient-derived xenograft models and significantly prolonged overall survival in treated mice. While trivalent CAR-T cells have demonstrated superior cytokine secretion and cytotoxicity to monospecific or bispecific versions, validation of their safety and efficacy is still required in clinical trials.
Overall, research on CAR-T cell therapy in rGBM remains at an early stage, with initial results showing feasibility, safety, and brief clinical benefits. Significant challenges persist in enhancing CAR-T cell tumor infiltration, improving immune responses, suppressing the TME, preventing antigen downregulation by rGBM, and avoiding adverse immune events such as cytokine storms and neurotoxicity. The optimal infusion dose and frequency still require validation in large-scale clinical trials. Optimizing CAR structures, discovering novel targets, developing bispecific CAR-T cells and combining with ICIs are key directions for future clinical translation. Beyond the three most commonly used targets mentioned above, other targets for rGBM CAR-T therapy, such as Ganglioside D2 (GD2), CD276, and CD70, as well as CAR-NK therapy targeting NK cells and CAR-M therapy targeting macrophages, , are gaining attention.

## Oncolytic Virotherapy
Oncolytic virotherapy represents a novel anticancer approach that selectively targets and destroys malignant cells while sparing normal tissues. During virus-mediated tumor cell lysis, released viral particles and TAAs activate the host immune system, promoting the production of pro-inflammatory factors and chemokines, creating a local immune microenvironment conducive to antitumor responses ( ). Among the most frequently used viral platforms in rGBM studies are herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1) and adenovirus, and other viruses also being continuously explored ( ).

 

## Herpes Simplex Virus Type 1 (HSV-1)
HSV-1 is an enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus characterized by neurotropism, preferentially infecting and replicating within neural tissue. The HSV genome contains several key genes, the modification of which enables selective viral replication within malignant cells while sparing healthy tissue. 
Early-generation vectors, such as HSV-1716 and G207, demonstrated a robust safety profile through γ34.5 deletions. However, clinical efficacy often diminished due to the attenuation of viral replication, particularly in the heterogeneous rGBM microenvironment and in GSCs. To address this limitation, the third-generation vector, G47Δ, incorporates an additional α47 deletion, which not only compensates for the replication deficits observed in earlier generations but also enhances the host’s antitumor immune response through upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). , A phase II trial demonstrating the therapeutic effectiveness of G47Δ in rGBM, showing a 1-year survival rate of 84.2%, with an mOS and mPFS of 20.2 months and 4.7 months. The results surpassed those of other therapies. Despite the successful application of G47Δ, comprehensive phase III studies are warranted to validate the therapeutic effectiveness of this emerging intervention. A phase I clinical trial (NCT03152318) evaluated 41 rGBM patients treated with CAN-3110. The study revealed that CAN-3110 persisted in replicating within tumors and was well tolerated. The treatment significantly increased TILs and, in some patients, enhanced the efficacy of ICIs, thereby improving OS. Additionally, patients seropositive for HSV-1 exhibited significantly longer survival than seronegative patients. Future research should investigate whether HSV-1 seropositivity has the potential to function as a predictive marker for therapeutic response and focus on combination therapy with ICIs. Other HSV-1-based viruses (M032 and C134) in clinical trials are actively being investigated. 

## Adenoviruses
Adenoviruses, which are non-enveloped icosahedral viruses with double-stranded DNA genomes, have been extensively studied as oncolytic platforms for rGBM. 
Clinical evaluations of the oncolytic adenovirus DNX-2401 in rGBM have demonstrated its safety and clinical activity across various delivery modalities, including direct intratumoral injection and convection-enhanced delivery (CED). , While the combination of DNX-2401 with TMZ or pembrolizumab is safe and has shown significant clinical benefit in certain patients, the addition of IFN-γ was poorly tolerated and did not enhance efficacy. This suggests that in the rGBM setting, intensified inflammatory stimulation alone is insufficient, potentially increasing the pro-inflammatory burden and leading to dose-limiting toxicity. DNX-2440, a derivative of DNX-2401, engineered to express the OX40 ligand, has demonstrated superior T-cell activation and CD8⁺ proliferation in murine models over DNX-2401; its clinical safety is currently being evaluated in a phase I trial (NCT03714334). CRAd-S-pk7, another GBM-targeted oncolytic adenovirus, can be delivered via neural stem cells (NSCs), whose intrinsic tumor-tropic properties enhance the targeting and infection of GSCs. The safety and preliminary clinical activity (mOS 18.4 months; mPFS 9.1 months) were demonstrated in a phase I GBM trial (NCT03072134). Future studies should validate these findings in larger cohorts, assess long-term safety, and delineate CRAd-S-pk7 interactions with GSCs to further improve therapeutic efficacy and clinical outcomes.
Adenoviruses are not only used as oncolytic agents for treating rGBM but can also be further engineered to deliver exogenous genes. Ad-RTS-hIL-12 is a genetically modified adenoviral vector that regulates IL-12 expression via the RTS promoter activated by oral veledimex. A phase I trial (NCT02026271) demonstrated the safety of peritumoral injection with an mOS of 12.7 months. Building on these results, a phase II trial (NCT04006119) is evaluating Ad-RTS-hIL-12 combined with veledimex and the PD-1 inhibitor cemiplimab in 40 rGBM patients, detailed data remain unpublished. VB-111 is a nonreplicating adenoviral vector engineered to deliver a Fas-fusion gene, thereby exerting antitumor effects through induction of tumor cell apoptosis and vascular disruption via Fas-mediated pathways. The combination of VB-111 and bevacizumab showed a marked survival benefit in a phase II trial (NCT01260506) (mOS, 13.8 vs. 7.4 months), but a phase III trial (NCT02511405) failed to demonstrate a therapeutic advantage (mOS, 6.8 vs. 7.9 months). , In contrast to the phase II study, which utilized a sequential regimen of VB-111 monotherapy priming followed by combination with bevacizumab upon progression, the phase III trial administered VB-111 and bevacizumab concomitantly from baseline. Consequently, the absence of VB-111 monotherapy priming may be the primary reason for the failure of the phase III study. ADV-TK is a nonreplicating adenoviral vector carrying the HSV-TK gene, which has demonstrated improved mPFS and mOS in rGBM patients in a randomized phase III study compared to SOC (mOS, 12.9 months vs. 8.6 months). Furthermore, a phase I study (NCT01811992) in HGG patients demonstrated that combining ADV-TK with an adenovirus expressing FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand resulted in an mOS of 21.3 months. This combinatorial adenoviral approach has exhibited favorable safety and feasibility profiles in patients with HGG and supports further phase II trials. ## Other Viruses

## Poliovirus
In a first-in-human trial (NCT01491893), intratumoral infusion of PVSRIPO via CED demonstrated favorable tolerability and significantly improved 24- and 36-month survival outcomes compared to historical controls in rGBM patients. A phase II trial expanded the patient cohort to 149 rGBM patients, with early results consistent with the phase I findings, showing increased long-term survival and mOS. Two ongoing trials are evaluating PVSRIPO combines with atezolizumab (NCT03973879) and pembrolizumab (NCT04479241) for rGBM.

## Retrovirus
The most extensively explored retrovirus for rGBM is vocimagene amiretrorepvec (Toca 511). In three open-label, dose-escalation phase I trials initiated in 2010, 126 patients with rHGG were enrolled to assess the safety, viral kinetics, immune response, and preliminary efficacy of gene therapy using Toca 511 in combination with Toca fluorocytosine (FC). The regimen was well tolerated, with no persistent viremia. Importantly, mOS was significantly prolonged compared to historical controls, irrespective of delivery route. However, a phase II/III trial involving 403 patients with rGBM (NCT02414165) showed no significant survival benefit of Toca 511/FC over SOC (mOS, 11.1 months vs. 12.2 months). This discrepancy likely stems from the rapid disease progression inherent to the rGBM population, which limited Toca 511/FC treatment to a median of two cycles—a duration insufficient for full immune activation. Additionally, unmonitored multi-center variability may have contributed to suboptimal virus delivery. Consequently, more rigorous trials are warranted to substantiate the efficacy of Toca 511/FC in rGBM.
Over the past two decades, oncolytic viruses have made significant strides in the treatment of rGBM, with numerous studies confirming their safety and efficacy. Nevertheless, extensive clinical validation is still required to establish whether oncolytic viruses can become part of the standard therapeutic regimen for rGBM. Furthermore, to enhance the therapeutic outcomes of oncolytic virus therapy, ongoing research should focus on identifying the most efficacious viral strains, optimizing delivery methods, genetically engineering viral molecular structures to improve tumor cell specificity and antitumor immune responses, and exploring combination strategies with other immunotherapies.

## Cancer Vaccines
Cancer vaccines are being actively investigated as an approach to elicit antitumor immunity by enhancing local immune responses against tumor cells. Tumor antigens are central to vaccine development; therefore, selecting appropriate antigenic targets is critical for eliciting effective antitumor responses. Both TAAs and tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) serve as immunological targets for vaccines to stimulate adaptive immunity ( ). The most extensively studied vaccine platforms include peptide vaccines, dendritic cell (DC) vaccines, and tumor neoantigen vaccines ( ).

 

## Peptide Vaccines
Peptide vaccines consist of short peptides, ranging from 8 to 25 amino acids, that contain antigenic epitopes, often conjugated to carrier proteins such as keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH) to enhance immunogenicity. These peptides are inherently non-immunostimulatory and require formulation with immune adjuvants for effective activation of innate immunity, such as Toll-like receptor agonists or aluminum salts, to enhance APC activation and co-stimulatory signaling for a stronger adaptive immune response. 

## Rindopepimut (CDX-110)
Rindopepimut is the most prominent peptide vaccine targeting EGFRvIII. Multiple phase II studies have reported favorable safety profiles and therapeutic benefits of this vaccine in GBM patients. In a pivotal phase III trial (NCT01480479) evaluating Rindopepimut combined with SOC in nGBM patients, although well tolerated, the vaccine failed to significantly improve mOS. Further studies revealed that EGFRvIII expression was lost in approximately 60% of patients upon tumor recurrence, a phenomenon more pronounced in the Rindopepimut-treated group. This highlights the necessity of confirming target antigen expression via biopsy before enrolling rGBM patients in EGFRvIII-targeted clinical trials. The dynamic expression of EGFRvIII underscores the challenges posed by tumor antigen heterogeneity, suggesting that future immunotherapeutic strategies for rGBM may require multi-target combination peptide vaccines.

## SurVaxM
SurVaxM is a peptide vaccine targeting Survivin, an inhibitor of apoptosis protein family member highly expressed in GBM but absent in normal brain tissue, making it a key prognostic biomarker. A phase I trial (NCT01250470) in nine Survivin-positive rGBM patients confirmed the safety of SurVaxM. A subsequent phase IIa study (NCT02455557) showed that SurVaxM combined with TMZ significantly improved mPFS (11.4 months) and mOS (25.9 months) in nGBM patients, demonstrating clinical benefits. However, it should be noted that the safety and efficacy of this combination therapy in rGBM remain to be validated. A phase II trial (NCT04013672) is evaluating the synergistic effects of SurVaxM combined with pembrolizumab in rGBM patients. Preliminary data show a PFS-6 of 34.5%, significantly surpassing historical monotherapy data, indicating potential synergy. 

## HSPPC-96
Heat shock proteins (HSPs), frequently upregulated in various malignancies, facilitate tumor growth, invasive behavior, and metastatic progression. Among HSP subtypes, HSP96 is most closely associated with GBM, regulating multiple signaling pathways. The safety and efficacy of HSPPC-96 vaccination have been confirmed in rGBM patients in phase I and II trials. , However, pretreatment lymphopenia reduced immunotherapy effectiveness, suggesting that screening for lymphopenia and stringent patient stratification based on immune competence could help prevent the premature erosion of vaccine-induced responses. Evidence from the two trails (NCT02122822 and NCT00905060) demonstrated that robust tumor-specific immune response or low myeloid PD-L1 expression could predict superior outcomes in nGBM patients receiving HSPPC-96 vaccination. , These findings have limited translational relevance to rGBM, as the enhanced immunosuppressive microenvironment and T-cell exhaustion in recurrent tumors often reduce the relevance of these biomarkers to actual clinical survival. Additionally, a randomized phase II study (NCT01814813) found that HSPPC-96 vaccination, alone or with bevacizumab, did not improve survival compared to bevacizumab monotherapy in patients with rGBM. 

## Wilms Tumor 1(WT1)
The WT1 gene, overexpressed in various cancers including GBM, plays a key role in GBM cell proliferation. Izumoto team performed a phase II study in 21 WT1/HLA-A2402-positive rGBM patients. The vaccine demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and achieved a PFS-6 of 33.3%. A phase I trial of a WT1 HLA class I and II peptide cocktail vaccine in rGBM patients showed favorable safety, achieving an mOS of 24.7 weeks and a 1-year OS rate of 36%. A phase III trial evaluated the combination of the WT1 protein-derived peptide vaccine DSP-7888 and bevacizumab in rGBM patients. The results showed that the combination therapy significantly improved OS (mOS, 16.1 months vs. 9.3 months) compared with bevacizumab alone. 

## Dendritic Cell Vaccines
DCs possess potent antigen-presenting capabilities and can induce antitumor immune responses, making them a cornerstone in tumor vaccine development. DC vaccine preparation involves isolating CD14⁺ monocytes from the patient’s peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and differentiating them into immature DCs with GM-CSF and IL-4. These immature DCs can be loaded with various tumor antigens (peptides, lysates, nucleic acids) followed by cytokine-induced maturation. Multiple DC vaccines, including DCVax-L, ICT107, and cytomegalovirus (CMV)-DC, have been clinically evaluated in rGBM.

## DCVax-L
DCVax-L, an autologous tumor lysate-pulsed DC vaccine, represents the most extensively investigated DC-based immunotherapy to date. A phase III trial (NCT00045968) enrolled 331 nGBM patients, who were randomized 2:1 to receive either TMZ plus DCVax-L or TMZ plus placebo after surgery and chemoradiotherapy. Upon recurrence, all patients were offered DCVax-L treatment. Intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis revealed an overall mOS of 23.1 months; in the MGMT-methylated subgroup, mOS was 34.7 months with a 3-year survival rate of 46.4%. These data indicate that adding DCVax-L to SOC for GBM patients is feasible, safe, and may improve survival outcomes. After incorporating an external control, the research team published a phase III prospective cohort study, which showed that DCVax-L combined with TMZ improves mOS in both nGBM and rGBM cohorts (19.3 vs. 16.5 months; 13.2 vs. 7.8 months). While suggestive for DCVax-L development, data interpretation warrants caution due to the high crossover rate and concerns regarding the appropriateness of the external control selection. While promising for DCVax-L development, data interpretation warrants caution due to the high crossover rate and concerns regarding the appropriateness of the external control selection. An Italian phase I/II trial (NCT04002804) of 20 IDH1-mutant rGBM patients demonstrated superior survival with DCVax-L plus tetanus toxoid (TT) preconditioning compared to TMZ. A 2024 US phase II trial (NCT01204684) demonstrated that DCVax-L plus Poly-ICLC induced polarized interferon activation in circulating monocytes and CD8+ T cells, suggesting their utility as blood biomarkers for immunotherapy in HGG. These findings provide new directions for optimizing DCVax-L vaccine therapy in the future.

## ICT-107
ICT-107 is another DC vaccine composed of autologous monocyte-derived DCs pulsed with six known GBM-associated TAAs. An early phase I study evaluated intradermal ICT-107 in GBM and observed preliminary clinical activity. The phase II trial (NCT01280552) enrolled nGBM patients carrying HLA-A1 and/or HLA-A2 alleles. Patients treated with ICT-107 had a 2.2-month prolongation in PFS while maintaining OS compared to unpulsed DC. Notably, HLA-A2–positive patients mounted stronger immune responses and derived clinically meaningful benefit from ICT-107 in both MGMT-methylated and unmethylated groups, whereas an OS advantage was observed only in HLA-A1–positive patients with MGMT methylation. These findings underscore the necessity for further investigation in HLA-A2 positive patient population and provide rationale for the ongoing phase III trial (NCT02546102).

## CMV-DC Vaccine
The CMV-DC vaccine is composed of autologous DCs loaded with mRNA encoding the CMV matrix protein pp65, aiming to elicit CMV-specific T cell responses against GBM. Studies have shown that CMV-DC vaccination significantly prolongs mPFS and mOS in nGBM patients, with nearly one-third remaining tumor-free for five years post-diagnosis. Given that CMV-DC induces robust CMV-specific CD8+ T cell responses, further studies need focus on combining CMV-DC vaccines with CMV-specific T cells. In a clinical study (NCT00693095), nGBM patients receiving both CMV pp65–specific T cells and CMV-DC vaccination exhibited enhanced activation of CMV-specific CD8⁺ T cells, which was positively associated with prolonged OS. These data support evaluation of this combination in larger randomized trials. Nevertheless, the efficacy of CMV-DC vaccine in patients with rGBM remains uncertain.

## Personalized Neoantigen Vaccine
Personalized neoantigen vaccine is an emerging cancer immunotherapy that activates host immunity against tumor-specific neoantigens—peptides derived from somatic mutations or aberrantly expressed genes absent in normal tissues, rendering them highly immunogenic and ideal precision immunotherapy targets. Drawing on the results from the European GAPVAC and US NEOVAX trials, neoantigen vaccines significantly activate memory phenotype CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, inducing robust immune responses, with GAPVAC showing mPFS of 14.2 months and mOS of 29.0 months in nGBM patients, and NEOVAX reporting mPFS of 7.6 months and mOS of 16.8 months in MGMT unmethylated nGBM patients. , However, Narita team performed a randomized phase III trial in 88 rGBM patients comparing personalized neoantigen vaccine treatment with best supportive care, revealing no significant survival benefit (mOS, 8.4 months vs. 8.0 months). Subgroup analysis revealed that patients with negligible baseline cytotoxic T lymphocyte levels who received the SART2-93 peptide exhibited a significantly shorter mOS compared to the corresponding placebo group (HR=15.98), suggesting that therapeutic vaccination in the absence of substantial preexisting immunity may trigger inconvenient antitumor responses or precipitate immune exhaustion. Consequently, future strategies for personalized vaccine selection must incorporate a comprehensive evaluation of the patients’ baseline immune microenvironment, shifting beyond a solitary reliance on humoral markers (IgG) to integrate the baseline status of cytotoxic T lymphocyte.
Current cancer vaccines for rGBM have demonstrated safety and initial immune activation in multiple phase I/II clinical trials. However, the failures of Rindopepimut and personalized neoantigen vaccines in phase III trials highlight significant challenges in treating rGBM: the high heterogeneity and dynamic evolution of tumor antigens, impaired host immune function, and a highly suppressive TME. Future research should focus on integrating multi-omics data to precisely map the dynamic antigen landscape, incorporating appropriate adjuvants to enhance immune responses, and improving the migration and homing efficiency of DCs to the draining lymph nodes.

## Discussion and Future Perspectives
Despite the increasing number of clinical trials investigating immunotherapy for rGBM, clinical outcomes remain disappointing, underscoring the significant biological differences between rGBM and immune-responsive “hot” tumors, such as melanoma and lung cancer. rGBM is characterized by greater invasiveness and faster progression compared to other solid tumors, often resulting in tumor advancement before immunotherapy can effectively induce an antitumor immune response. Compared to primary GBM, rGBM has a more immunosuppressive TME, characterized by an increased proportion of GAMs, GSCs, and terminally exhausted T cells. This “cold” TME is notably deficient in the high tumor mutational burden and neoantigen load, which are crucial to the success of immunotherapy in melanoma and lung cancer. Additionally, the pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity of rGBM complicates immunotherapy by hindering the targeting of all tumor subpopulations, as different tumor cell populations may respond differently to treatment. Moreover, the BBB not only impedes the efficient delivery of drugs but also restricts the infiltration of activated immune cells into the tumor. Collectively, these challenges severely hinder the successful clinical translation of immunotherapy in rGBM. To address these challenges, future research should focus on strategies to cross the BBB, overcome tumor heterogeneity, and remodel the immunosuppressive TME, while leveraging combination immunotherapies to reinvigorate the anti-tumor immune response in rGBM patients.

## Crossing the Blood-Brain Barrier and Improving Drug Delivery Efficiency
The blood–brain barrier is a highly selective, semipermeable barrier that isolates the CNS from the peripheral circulation. While it effectively prevents toxins, pathogens, and certain drugs in the bloodstream from entering the brain, the BBB also limits the permeability of immune-based therapies to the brain parenchyma. Currently, strategies to bypass the BBB in the context of rGBM include CSF delivery, intracranial administration, and nanotherapy. CSF delivery and intracranial administration have exhibited therapeutic benefits in clinical evaluations; future improvements in delivery devices may further enhance these outcomes. , , Preclinical studies have demonstrated that self-assembled nanocomposites are capable of crossing the BBB to facilitate delivery of therapeutic agents, significantly enhancing the accumulation of ICIs in the brain. With the aid of nanoparticles, CAR-T cells can overcome the BBB, improving targeting specificity, cytotoxicity, and treatment efficacy. , Although nanobiotechnology-based strategies to improve immunotherapy have not yet been validated in clinical settings for rGBM, they could provide valuable avenues for future research and application.

## Deciphering Cellular Heterogeneity in rGBM to Overcome Immunotherapy Resistance
The pronounced heterogeneity of rGBM is a hallmark feature that plays an essential role in mediating resistance to immunotherapy. This heterogeneity manifests as differences in genetic mutations, diversity of tumor cell subpopulations, and variability within the TME. Such complexity enables rGBM cells to evade immune-mediated destruction, thereby contributing to therapeutic resistance and tumor recurrence. Current strategies addressing this challenge include the design of bispecific CAR-T cells, genetically modified T cells engineered to secrete therapeutic molecules, and combination immunotherapies, which have shown preliminary activity and safety in early clinical trials. , , Recent advances in scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics have uncovered immune resistance mechanisms across distinct tumor subpopulations and their microenvironments. These findings facilitate the discovery of potent immunotherapeutic targets and pave the way for more precise immunotherapy approaches. , Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that the activation of senescence-associated pathways and reprogramming in amino acid metabolism within cancer cells significantly promote the cellular heterogeneity of rGBM by generating distinct transcriptional and metabolic states across tumor subpopulations, causing resistance to immunotherapy. , Future research aiming to overcome resistance will likely focus on these underlying mechanisms.

## In-Depth Understanding of the TME in rGBM to Overcome Tumor Immune Evasion
The profoundly immunosuppressive TME characteristic of rGBM represents a major challenge to effective immunotherapy. Ongoing bidirectional signaling between tumor cells and the TME substantially contributes to tumor progression and malignancy ( ). 

 GAMs represent one of the predominant immune cell subsets in the TME, and their proportion significantly increases after GBM recurrence, markedly promoting tumor growth and treatment resistance. GAMs are recruited to the TME through interactions with the metabolic and signaling pathways of tumor cells. Upon recruitment, they predominantly polarize into an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, secreting VEGF and immunosuppressive factors, including TGF-β and IL-10, and even expressing immune checkpoints. Such processes facilitate tumor progression and dissemination, contribute to immune escape, and compromise immunotherapeutic efficacy. , Recent studies have demonstrated that within the GBM TME, GAMs present antigens via MHC class I molecules, thereby driving the differentiation of CD8⁺ T cells from a progenitor exhausted (Tex_prog) state to a terminally exhausted (Tex_term) phenotype. Notably, it has been well established that PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade preferentially reinvigorates Tex_prog cells, triggering a robust proliferative burst, while exerting minimal effects on Tex_term cells. This phenotypic transition, induced by GAM-mediated antigen presentation, may consequently limit the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors by reducing the proportion of PD-1-responsive Tex_prog cells. Furthermore, GAMs release cytokines, including IL-6 and CCL2, that activate key signaling pathways in GSCs, thereby promoting their survival and stemness, and contributing to tumor malignancy. The further elucidation of the functions and roles of GAMs through advanced technologies such as scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic holds promise for uncovering new immunotherapeutic targets to modulate the TME in rGBM.
NK cells represent an important population of innate immune effectors besides T cells. However, the immunosuppressive TME of rGBM severely impairs NK cell function, hampering their antitumor activity. NKG2D, an essential activating receptor expressed on NK cells, detects stress-induced ligands presented by tumor cells, thereby triggering cytotoxic activity. Yet, rGBM tumor cells evade NK-mediated immunity by downregulating NKG2D expression and secreting immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β, IL-10, and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which further impair NK cell proliferation and cytotoxicity. Moreover, most rGBM cells highly express MHC-I molecules that engage inhibitory killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) on NK cells, suppressing their activation and enhancing immune escape. Strategies aimed at enhancing the infiltration capacity of NK cells into rGBM, as well as blocking signaling pathways related to NK cell exhaustion or suppression, are crucial for augmenting NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity within the TME. In the future, immunotherapeutic strategies targeting NK cells to restore NK cell–mediated antitumor immunity may represent an emerging area for improving efficacy in rGBM, though further research is needed to assess its feasibility.

## Enhancing Antitumor Immunity Through Combination Immunotherapy
A paucity of TILs in rGBM’s TME limits the efficacy of immunotherapies. Recently, combination immunotherapy strategies aimed at improving the abundance of TILs, eliciting robust immune responses and converting “cold” TMEs into “hot” TMEs have attracted growing attention and support across clinical studies ( ). CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated the capacity to target antigens specific to rGBM. However, pronounced immunoediting—characterized by antigen loss and adaptive resistance—has curtailed the clinical efficacy of CAR-T cells, yielding only transient responses. Combining CAR-T cell therapy with ICIs is an emerging therapeutic strategy; however, its clinical efficacy remains to be established and requires further validation. Moreover, immune checkpoint expression is frequently upregulated in GBM patients after cancer vaccine therapy, which may facilitate immune evasion. On this biological basis, combining cancer vaccines with ICIs has shown clinical benefit in GBM. , Oncolytic virotherapy significantly alleviates immunosuppression in the TME by promoting immune-cell infiltration and upregulating PD-L1 in cancer cells, thereby improving responsiveness to ICIs. Clinical studies in rGBM have shown clear therapeutic efficacy of combining oncolytic viruses with ICIs, including significant prolongation of mOS and, in some patients, long-term remission. , 
 

## Conclusions
Over recent years, driven by the remarkable success of immunotherapy in various solid tumors, multiple immunotherapeutic strategies targeting rGBM—including ICIs, CAR-T cell therapy, oncolytic virotherapy and cancer vaccines—have been extensively investigated extensively in both preclinical and clinical studies. However, most phase III clinical trials have not provided evidence of meaningful clinical benefits, highlighting the considerable challenges that remain in rGBM immunotherapy. These limitations emphasize the pressing necessity for further comprehensive investigation and optimization of immunotherapeutic strategies to ultimately establish viable treatment options for patients with rGBM.
