Accordingly, combining NCVs with ICIs offers a synergistic approach that boosts neoantigen-specific T-cell responses and enhances antitumor immunity.
Various platforms have been developed to deliver NCVs, including peptides, messenger RNA (mRNA)-lipid nanoparticles (mRNA-LNPs), and viral vectors, all of which have shown promising results in preclinical tumor models and are advancing through clinical development. Notably, formulated mRNA-LNP neoantigen vaccines have demonstrated strong immunogenicity and clinical activity in multiple settings, including randomized clinical studies. DNA-based vaccines, in particular plasmid DNA (pDNA), have been extensively explored and have demonstrated comparable therapeutic potential in preclinical models. However, traditional pDNA production relies on large-scale, cell-based fermentation, which presents significant manufacturing and logistical challenges for the rapid, small-batch requirements of personalized cancer immunotherapy. These processes are not only time-consuming and costly but are also difficult to scale down, making them impractical for individualized clinical applications.
To accelerate production—a critical medical need in oncology—synthetic and enzymatic approaches have been explored for DNA vaccines. A PCR-based method was initially developed for pandemic preparedness and has more recently been adapted for cancer and NCVs. An alternative method based on rolling circle amplification (RCA) has also been compared with pDNA for cancer vaccines. Unlike pDNA, enzymatically synthesized DNA is produced in a cell-free environment, eliminating the need for bacterial hosts and their associated impurities, such as endotoxins and residual genomic DNA. These properties reduce timelines and cost of goods, enhance safety, and permit dose sparing due to the absence of bacterial backbone sequences. These constructs are well-suited for rapid delivery via electroporation (EP), which significantly improves cellular uptake and immunogenicity. EP has been successfully employed in clinical settings for both COVID-19 vaccines and NCV delivery, reinforcing its role as a reliable and scalable method for DNA vaccine administration. However, the mechanism of action (MOA) of pDNA or linear DNA delivered by EP has not been investigated in depth. Most of the previous studies have used STING knockout mice to prove a correlation between cytoplasmic DNA and induction of inflammation in the muscle or lymphoid organs. In our study, to more precisely define the mechanism of action, we extend this approach by directly comparing hairpin DNA (hpDNA) with pseudouridine-modified mRNA delivered by EP, a delivery context that allows transgene expression but may not provide the innate immune activation typically induced by clinically validated formulated mRNA.
Here, we present a novel, clinically scalable NCV platform based on enzymatically synthesized linear hpDNA delivered by EP. We directly compare the immune responses elicited by our hpDNA platform to those of its pDNA and, notably, its mRNA counterparts. Our findings reveal that while mRNA delivered by EP is a valid tool for protein expression, it did not elicit detectable antigen-specific T-cell responses under the conditions tested. We demonstrate that the hpDNA-EP platform is highly immunogenic and effective, and we provide new insights into its MOA by comparing the local gene expression profile induced by hpDNA versus mRNA vaccination. The results strongly support the hpDNA-EP platform as a viable and potent tool for personalized cancer immunotherapy.

## Methods

## Preparation of vaccines
For MC38-specific and CT26-specific vaccines, named M20 and C20, respectively, neoantigens are listed in . When assembling the final polypeptide, the position of each neoantigen was carefully chosen to avoid the creation of immunogenic junctions (BA junctions >500 nM for the H-2-Kd, H-2-Dd, and H-2-Ld MHC I molecules). Briefly, the hpDNA vaccines were synthesized starting from a backbone-free, sequence-verified master template, produced via a proprietary process, containing the neoantigen cassette of interest by an isothermal RCA method using a proprietary version of Phi29 DNA polymerase in combination with primers or a DNA primase. The process incorporates deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs) to produce concatemeric DNA at a concentration of 1 g/L. The amplified DNA was then processed into discrete DNA expression cassettes using a combination of enzymes and adaptor molecules to produce the target linear DNA molecule. The DNA was then treated with a combination of enzymes to degrade any non-target DNA material. Purification of the linear DNA was performed, involving enzymatic degradation of process enzymes by a chromatography step, and final concentration/diafiltration via tangential flow filtration (TFF). Batch sizes ranged from 1 mg of research-grade DNA to 500 mg of GMP-like hpDNA product. Sizing and purity of the final hpDNA vaccine was assessed using capillary gel electrophoresis (CGE, Fragment Analyzer, Agilent) using the Agilent dsDNA 920 Reagent kit, for analysis of large dsDNA fragments up to 15,000 bp, as per manufacturer’s instructions.
M20 and firefly luciferase mRNA was prepared by In Vitro Transcription (IVT) of linear synthetic DNA templates using Cellscript T7 Flashscribe kit with modifications, using modified N1-methylpseudo-UTP and CleanCap AG capping reagent. IVT RNA was precipitated with LiCl and resuspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5. dsRNA content was assessed via dot blot assay (J2 anti-dsRNA antibody) and mRNA integrity was confirmed with a Fragment Analyzer 5200 system (Agilent) with FA 12-Capillary Array Short using the DNF-471 reagent kit as per manufacturer’s instructions.

## Immuno-precipitation and western blot
HEK293 cells were cultivated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) + 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) + 1% Pen/strep. 1×106 cells were transfected with 4 µg M20_HA-pDNA and 8 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen; 11668019). 24 hours after transfection, cells were lysed in 100 µL of lysis buffer (Invitrogen; FNN0011) supplemented with protease inhibitors cocktail (Roche; 11836153001). 300 µg of total protein were immunoprecipitated with 4 µg of rabbit-αHA antibody (Sigma; H6908) by using Dynabeads protein G immune-precipitation kit (Invitrogen; 10007D). For western blot, immunoprecipitated samples were incubated at 4°C overnight with mouse-αHA antibody (Sigma; H6363) diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer (5% milk+0.1% Tween-20 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)) and Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibody (Invitrogen; G21040) diluted 1:10,000 in blocking buffer for 1 hour at room temperature. Part of crude lysate (10 µg) was used as loading control and incubated at 4°C overnight with αVinculin (Sigma; V9131) diluted 1:5,000 in blocking buffer and then with HRP-conjugated antibody (Sigma; A9044) diluted 1:5,000 1 hour room temperature.

## RNA extraction and quantitative PCR
106 cells were transfected with different quantities of M20-hpDNA (4–1-0.25 µg) and 8 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen; 11668019). After 24 hours RNA was extracted with RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN; 74104) with DNase digestion (following the guidelines of the product). The complementary DNA (cDNA) was obtained by using iScript gDNA clear cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad; 1725035). The target region, as for β-actin housekeeping gene, used for loading control was amplified with iTaq Universal Probe Supermix (Bio-Rad; 1725131). For quantitative measure of the copy number of transcript, the standard curve harboring the target region was built with M20-hpDNA spanning from 107 genomes/µL to 102 genomes/µL. The primers used for the amplification were the following: tPA FW 5’ CGCGCGCGCCACCAGACA 3’; tPA REV 5’ AGCCCTCTCTTCATTGCATCC 3’; tPA probe 5’6FAM CCATGGGTCTTTTCTGCAGTCACCGTCC 3’Tamra; β-Actin FW 5’ CAGGCACCAGGGCGT 3’; β-Actin REV 5’ CCAGATTTTCTCCATGTCGTC 3’; β-Actin probe 5’Texas Red GTGACGATGCCGTGCTCGATGGGG 3’BBQ650.

## Cell line and in vivo protocols
MC38 and CT26 colon carcinoma cell lines were used as previously described. Analysis of luciferase expression in vivo was carried out as previously described using the IVIS 200. 
A monoclonal population of MC-38 Luciferase (Luc) cell line was established by infecting MC38 wild-type (WT) cell line with lentivirus containing luciferase gene and selected with puromycin (Invitrogen cat. J67236.XF). The engineered cell line was maintained in culture using DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS+1% Pen/strep+2 µg/mL of puromycin at 37°C with 5% CO2. CT26 luciferase cell line was purchased from VitroBiotech cat. VLU5B080 and was maintained in culture with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium supplemented with 10% FBS+1% Pen/strep+1 µg/mL of puromycin at 37°C with 5% CO2.
Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with national legislation and institutional guidelines. All procedures were authorized by the Italian Ministry of Health (authorization no. 155/2023-PR). Female 6–7 weeks old C57BL/6 and Balb/c mice (Envigo) were used for experiment involving M20 and C20 vaccines, respectively. Animals were housed in the Plaisant animal house and kept in standard condition according to national legislation. Mice were injected in the tibialis and quadriceps, alternately, bi-weekly or tri-weekly, with different doses of DNA in a volume of 50 µL, as described in each figure legend and electrically stimulated using a Cliniporator Device EPS01 with the following electrical conditions: 8 pulses 20 ms each at 110 V, 8 Hz, 120 ms interval. α-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) (9D9) monoclonal antibody (100 µg) (Bio X Cell cat. BP0164) and/or α-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) (B7-H1) monoclonal antibody (100 µg) (Bio X Cell cat. BE0101) were injected intraperitoneally in 200 µL of PBS. Tumor monitoring was determined using calipers as previously described. Mice were euthanized when the tumor volume reached 1,000 mm3. Lung metastasis staining was performed as previously described. India ink (cat. SKU#: STIIN25 EA, StatLab) was diluted at 15% into water. After mice sacrifice, the 25g×5/8’ needle (cat. 303179, BD plastic) was inserted intratracheal, then 3–4 mL of diluted India ink was inflated by manual syringe into lung. After complete perfusion, the lungs were removed, washed briefly in water and collected in fixative solution: ethanol 70% (cat. 1.00983.1000 Supelco), 4% glacial acetic (cat. 033252.AK, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 3.2% formaldehyde (cat. F8775, Sigma Aldrich). The charge repulsion between India ink and metastasis allows to visually detect the metastasis as white spots. The metastatic area was measured with ImageJ software. For MC38-Luciferase cell line and CT26-Luciferase cell line, the metastasis spreading was measured with In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) as photon/second after subcutaneous administration of Luciferin (Revvity cat. 122799), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Immunological assays
Interferon (IFN)-γ-enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Mouse IFN-γ ELISpot BASIC ALP, Mabtech) and intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) was performed as previously described. For the flow cytometry (FC) cells were incubated with the anti-Fcγ receptor (2.4G2) followed by staining with the following antibodies: Fixable viability dye −700 (BD Bioscience cat. 564997), Fixable viability stain NIR (Invitrogen cat. L349756), CD45-BV605 (BD-horizon cat. 563053 clone 30-F11), CD3-AF488 (Invitrogen cat. 53-0031-82 clone 145–2 C11), CD4-PerCP-eFluor 710 (Invitrogen cat. 46-0042-82 clone RM4-5), CD8-APC-eFluor780 (Invitrogen cat. 470081-82 clone 53–6.7), IFN-γ-PE (Invitrogen cat. 12-7311-82 clone XMG1.2), TNFα-PE-Cy7 (Invitrogen cat. 25-7321-82 clone MP6-XT22), CD62L-eFluor 450 (Invitrogen cat. 48-061-82 clone MEL-14), CD44-BV480 (BD Bioscience cat. 566200 clone IM7), Adpgk (Immudex, JA03803-PE). Samples were acquired through a Northern Lights flow cytometer (Cytek), and the data were analyzed using Cytek software. Cytokine expression in the presence of only DMSO (with no peptides) was considered background and subtracted from the values measured with stimuli.

## Bulk RNA sequencing
RNA was extracted by homogenizing tissue with TissueRuptor II (QIAGEN cat. 9002756) directly in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen cat. 15596026) followed by RNA isolation as manufacturer’s guidelines. The quality of FASTQ files was assessed using FastQC. The mouse reference genome (mm39) and the corresponding GTF file (mm39.ncbiRefSeq.gtf) were retrieved from University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Genome Browser. The reference genome index was generated with STAR using the “--sjdbOverhang” argument set to 99. Reads were then mapped to the reference genome using STAR with the options “--quantMode GeneCounts” and “--twopassMode Basic”. Gene-level read counts were extracted from the “ReadsPerGene.out.tab” file. For differential gene expression analysis, we used the R package DESeq2, defining differentially expressed genes as those with an adjusted p value (false discovery rate) <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change >1.

## Single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing analysis
CD3+ CD4− CD8+ Adpgk+ T cells were sorted from whole spleen tissue using Aurora CS equipped with 405, 488, and 633 nm laser, using an 85 µm nozzle (Cytek Biosciences) with the antibodies described above and: Live dead 575 (BD Horizon cat. 565694 used 1:1000), Adpgk dextramer-PE (Immudex cat. JA03803 PE 50 used 1:50). Total 10,000 CD8+Adpgk+ T cells were collected and incubated with (BioLegend cat. 155871 used 1:100). The cells were loaded into Chromium single-cell 5’ chip (10x Genomics) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Single cells were partitioned into droplets with gel beads to form emulsions, after which cellular lysis and barcoded reverse transcription of mRNA was performed. Paired 5’ gene expression, hashtag barcode and V(D)J libraries were prepared using the following kits from 10x Genomics and protocols provided by the manufacturer: Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Library and Gel Bead Kit V.2; Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Amplification Kit, Mouse T Cell.
FASTQ files of gene expression data were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using the Cell Ranger software suite (Cell Ranger count), with the “--expect-cells” parameter set to 10,000. For the single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) experiment, FASTQ files were processed using the Cell Ranger V(D)J pipeline with the mm10 V(D)J reference transcriptome. The R packages Seurat and scRepertoire were used to integrate gene expression and T-cell receptor (TCR) data. Cells lacking at least one detected CDR3 region from either TRA or TRB were excluded. Additionally, cells with fewer than 200 or more than 2,500 detected genes, or with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 5%, were discarded. Only cells with CD8a expression >0 were retained for subsequent analyses. Following the Seurat pipeline for gene expression analysis, the data were log-normalized, and the 2,000 most variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis. Clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, with the first 10 principal components and a resolution of 0.5, resulting in four clusters. The runUMAP function was applied to visualize cells in a low-dimensional space. To assign functional phenotypes to clusters, we employed the reference-based packages ProjecTILs and SingleR. The mouse Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (TIL) atlas (ref_TILAtlas_mouse_v1.rds) served as the reference for ProjecTILs, while SingleR used ImmGen data from the celldex package. Finally, specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers and FindMarkers functions, selecting significant markers with an adjusted p value≤0.1 to refine phenotype assignment.

## Statistics
GraphPad Prism software (V.8.0.2) was used to perform all statistical analyses. Depending on the experiment, comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney test, one-way analysis of variance with appropriate post hoc testing, or log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test for survival analysis. P value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as mean±SEM.

## Results

## Design and in vitro characterization of the M20 hpDNA vaccine
Consistent with the principles of polyepitope vaccine design, we generated the M20 polypeptide to encode 20 neoantigens from the murine MC38 colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line ( ). We selected nine neoantigens (Adpgk, Dpagt1, Reps1, Tmem135, Spire1, Wbp7, Hace1, Nle1, and Zbtb40) based on their established immunogenicity in previous studies. The remaining 11 (Hnrnpl, Pop1, Pam, Actr1b, Yipf4, Huwe1, Slc35e4, Gtf2i, Lats1, Ndfip2, and Atg9a) were selected from a panel characterized by Capietto et al and were consistent with previous data. For each neoantigen, a 28-mer sequence was generated, positioning the mutated amino acid at the 14th position whenever feasible. To confirm protein expression, we generated a plasmid vector containing the expression cassette ( ) encoding the M20 polypeptide, fused with a C-terminus HA tag. We then transfected the M20_HA-pDNA into the HEK293 cells. Western blot analysis of whole-cell lysates following immunoprecipitation with an anti-HA antibody detected a specific polypeptide signal exclusively in the transfected cells ( ), confirming expression of the polyepitope protein.
 To circumvent the manufacturing and logistical limitations of conventional plasmid-based vaccines, we used an enzymatically synthesized hpDNA platform provided by 4basebio. The rolling circle amplification-based method yielded highly pure DNA at 1 g/L without bacterial backbone sequences, which is a major advantage for safety and regulatory compliance, as well as enabling dose reduction ( ). The final product, M20-hpDNA, is a linear double-stranded DNA molecule closed at each end with a single-stranded hairpin, which may contribute to structural integrity ( ). The M20-hpDNA vaccine construct was designed without the hemagglutinin (HA)-tag to mimic the final product for clinical use. CGE confirmed identity of a single defined hpDNA population of 3,776 bp within ±10% sensitivity of the methodology (expected size, 3,826 bp) and with a purity of 100% ( ). To confirm the suitability of the platform to synthesize longer sequences, an hpDNA comprising 40 different neoantigens (4,566 bp) was produced at comparable yields, again with a homogenous hpDNA product and a purity of 99.3% ( ).
To quantify the in vitro expression of the hpDNA construct, we developed a universal mRNA-based quantitative PCR assay. The M20 polyepitope is encoded as a polypeptide fused to a Tissue Plasminogen Activator (tPA) signal, which directs protein secretion into the medium ( ). We leveraged the tPA and Cytomegalovirus (CMV) intron sequences to develop a method for quantifying mRNA expression from our hpDNA vectors. In transfected cells, we observed a dose-dependent expression of the M20 construct ( ), confirming that the hpDNA is actively transcribed into mRNA. These results supported the use of the M20-hpDNA platform for subsequent in vivo studies.
To confirm storage stability of the hpDNA vaccine, mRNA expression in transfected cells was determined following storage at −20°C over a 12-month time course. Comparable mRNA expression was observed at each time point demonstrating vaccine efficacy was maintained ( ). An accelerated stability study was also performed with hpDNA stored at +50°C for 2 days still demonstrating comparable expression to that stored at −20°C, although storage at +65°C for 20 days eventually led to non-detectable levels of M20 mRNA in transfected cells ( ).

## M20-hpDNA-EP elicits potent immunogenicity and protects mice from tumor challenge
We first evaluated the immunogenicity of the M20-hpDNA vaccine in a prophylactic setting. Mice were immunized with three doses of M20-hpDNA via intramuscular (i.m.) injection followed by EP on a 3-week interval protocol ( ). We measured the antigen-specific immune response in splenocytes 1 week after the final vaccination. Ex vivo IFN-γ ELISpot assays confirmed cellular responses against 13 of the 137 individual 15-mer peptides, which mapped to 7 of the 20 predicted neoantigens ( ). We further analyzed the T-cell phenotype by ICS and FC, which detected the presence of neoantigen-specific IFN-γ producing cells within both the CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell subsets ( ). Analysis of cross-reactivity to WT sequences confirmed the lack of reactivity for some neoantigens (Adpgk, Kmt2a, Spire1) and reduced cross-reactivity for others (Reps1 and Huwe1), supporting the neoantigen specificity of the induced responses ( ).
 Having demonstrated hpDNA immunogenicity, we next performed a head-to-head comparison with a benchmark pDNA vaccine. Using a luciferase reporter, we first confirmed that equimolar doses of hpDNA and pDNA delivered by EP yielded comparable gene expression in vivo ( ). We then directly compared the immune responses induced by M20-hpDNA-EP and M20-pDNA-EP using the same three-dose vaccination protocol ( ). Blood was collected 1 week after the final immunization to measure T-cell cytokine profiles in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by ICS. Both constructs induced robust and comparable percentages of neoantigen-specific, Th1-type T-cells. Specifically, we observed a clear dose-response effect in the production of neoantigen-specific IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α in CD8+ T cells ( ). While percentages of cytokine-producing T cells were comparable between the hpDNA and pDNA groups, both were significantly higher than those in naïve mice.
 To determine whether these responses conferred protection 1 month after the final vaccination, mice were inoculated subcutaneous (s.c.) with 5×10⁵ MC38 cells. All vaccinated animals exhibited significantly reduced tumor growth ( ) and improved survival ( ) versus naïve controls, in a clear dose-dependent manner. Notably, the highest doses of both vaccines provided complete tumor protection in a subset of animals (M20-hpDNA-EP: 80% protection at 6.7 µg; M20-pDNA-EP: 100% protection at 10 µg). Long-term efficacy was confirmed by rechallenge: surviving mice were re-inoculated on day 160; all M20-hpDNA-EP vaccinated mice were completely protected, whereas all naïve mice developed tumors ( ). In a separate dose-escalation experiment, increasing the hpDNA dose to 20 µg did not enhance immune responses or protective efficacy compared with 6.7 µg ( ). In this independent cohort, the magnitude of protection observed at 6.7 µg was lower than that shown in and was accompanied by reduced CD8+ T-cell responses, consistent with a correlation between protective efficacy and the magnitude of the induced cellular immune response. These findings demonstrate that hpDNA and pDNA vaccines were equally effective at inducing protection in the MC38 prophylactic model.
We then validated the platform in CT26 colon adenocarcinoma. We generated C20-hpDNA-EP containing 20 CT26 neoantigens ( ). Using the same vaccination schedule ( ), 6 of the 20 neoantigens were immunogenic by ELISpot assay ( ). Similar to M20, C20-hpDNA-EP and C20-pDNA-EP triggered comparable neoantigen-specific IFN-γ and TNF-α responses in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells ( ). Although initial prophylactic efficacy was slightly lower for hpDNA, both vaccines provided complete protection on rechallenge ( ). Thus, hpDNA-EP is as immunogenic as pDNA-EP at equimolar doses across models.

## M20-hpDNA vaccination induces a durable T-cell memory compartment and a unique TCR repertoire
Adpgk neoantigen was the most immunogenic component, exhibiting the highest T-cell response and a low half maximal effective concentration (EC50) value ( ). To characterize the Adpgk-specific CD8+ T-cell population, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and targeted scTCR-seq on CD8+Adpgk+T cells sorted from the spleens ( ). After quality control, we analyzed the transcriptomic and paired TCR profiles of 311 cells.
 Graph-based clustering identified four subsets ( ). We combined reference-based annotation with manual curation to classify these clusters ( ) and found the following cellular composition: effector memory cells (T_EM, 37%), central memory cells (T_CM, 31%), precursor exhausted cells (T_pex, 21%), and effector cells (T_eff, 11%). Key marker genes ( ) , such as Cd247, Prkcq, and Nlrc5 for T_eff cells and Pdcd1lg2, Nr4a3, and Irf4 for the T_pex cluster, supported our functional assignments ( ). Stat4, Arhgap15 and Sp100 further distinguish the effector memory from the central memory. The presence of a strong memory T-cell compartment (T_EM and T_CM) highlights that the M20-hpDNA vaccination protocol is highly effective at establishing long-lasting immune responses.
To characterize the receptor landscape of these cells, we analyzed the single-cell TCR sequences. This analysis identified 15 unique TCR sequences, 4 hyperexpanded and 4 non-hyperexpanded clonotypes ( ). The hyperexpanded clones were predominantly found within the central and effector memory compartments, while the unique, non-hyperexpanded clones were mainly present in the effector and precursor-exhausted cell populations ( ). The most hyperexpanded clonotype was “CAMRSNTGYQNFYF CASSERDRGRNSPLYF,” which was shared across multiple T-cell clusters.
We further investigated whether the TCR repertoire induced by our vaccine was similar to publicly available sequences for the Adpgk neoantigen. We compared our data to a publicly available dataset from a peptide-based vaccine study by Liu et al. No shared TCR sequences were found, which suggests that our hpDNA vaccine induces a unique and specific immune response. This unique immune response is maintained and sustained through the hyperexpanded TCR sequences found in the long-lived memory T-cell compartment.

## M20-hpDNA-EP demonstrates efficacy in therapeutic settings and prevents metastasis
We evaluated the efficacy of M20-hpDNA-EP in an early therapeutic setting, implanting s.c. MC38 in C57BL/6 mice. Vaccination began 3 days post-inoculation ( ), and was evaluated both as a monotherapy and combined with αCTLA-4. While untreated mice showed rapid tumor growth, M20-hpDNA-EP or αCTLA-4 showed modest activity ( ). However, the combination significantly reduced tumor growth and prolonged survival ( ). Consistent with the reduced tumor growth, we observed a significant increase in neoantigen-specific CD8+IFN-γ+T cells in tumors of combination-treated mice ( ).
 Next, we investigated the anti-metastatic properties of the M20-hpDNA-EP by inoculating MC38 tumor cells via tail vein injection to induce lung metastasis ( ). On day 14, M20-hpDNA-EP vaccination alone significantly reduced metastatic foci; αCTLA-4 or αPD-L1 monotherapy was not effective indicating the vaccine as the primary driver ( ), consistent with lung-weight analysis ( ). M20-hpDNA-EP vaccination significantly prolonged mouse survival ( ). To follow-up the tumor growth we used an MC38 expressing luciferase ( ). On day 3 vaccinated mice showed a significantly reduced signal which is consistent with the day 10 measurement ( ) and survival ( ), suggesting that vaccine-induced immune responses are effective limiting tumor establishment as well as during the tumor expansion. Immunoprofiling on day 14 showed significantly higher Adpgk-specific CD8+ T-cell frequency in vaccinated mice compared with untreated mice, while they were almost absent in control lungs, confirming again the correlation between immune response and antitumor activity ( ). Similar anti-metastatic effects were observed with C20-hpDNA vaccine in CT26-Luc ( ), reinforcing hpDNA-EP versatility in aggressive disease.

## M20-hpDNA-EP, but not M20-RNA-EP, induces a productive local inflammation and sustained immune response
We observed that pseudouridine-modified mRNA delivered by EP did not elicit detectable neoantigen-specific T-cell responses under our experimental conditions. We exploited this observation to better characterize the mechanism of action of hpDNA, which functions as the minimal form of DNA capable of inducing robust immunogenicity. We therefore directly compared the immune responses and gene-expression profiles triggered by hpDNA-EP and mRNA-EP.
In vivo comparison of hpDNA and mRNA encoded luciferase signals revealed comparable levels up to 48 hours post-delivery followed by a faster decline in mRNA-Luc-EP-treated mice ( ). We then compared immune responses over time with M20-hpDNA-EP versus M20-mRNA-EP. M20-hpDNA-EP response was detected after the first vaccination, peaked after the third, and declined thereafter ( ). In contrast, M20-mRNA-EP did not elicit an immune response at any time point indicating that gene expression alone was not sufficient to induce immunogenicity under these conditions ( ). Analysis of memory at peak showed an effector memory response ( ) consistent with the single cells data ( ). Thus, under our experimental conditions, EP was not compatible with non-formulated mRNA in this model, whereas hpDNA plus EP elicited an immune response.
 To identify the genes modulated by the immunogenic M20-hpDNA-EP, we leveraged M20-mRNA-EP, which induces comparable gene expression yet without a T-cell immune response. In this RNA-seq experiment the transcriptomic profiles showed a clear separation between the different groups of samples with M20-hpDNA-EP treated muscles grouped distinctly from non-immunogenic M20-mRNA-EP and controls ( ). Although M20-mRNA-EP compared with control revealed hundreds of deregulated genes, the direct comparison of hpDNA versus mRNA-EP revealed far fewer differentially expressed genes (19 up and 47 downregulated) ( ). Despite this, the hpDNA-associated immunogenic response is characterized by a strong shift toward pro-inflammatory signaling and immune cell activation, while the non-immunogenic mRNA treatment appears to suppress or lack these pathways. A deep investigation of the differentially expressed genes provides a clear molecular signature for why hpDNA is immunogenic and mRNA is not. The genes upregulated in the hpDNA group are hallmarks of a coordinated immune response, from initial inflammation to the activation of adaptive immunity. The upregulation of Il6 and chemokines like Ccl4 and Cxcl2 indicates a strong pro-inflammatory response ( ). These signaling molecules recruit immune cells like T cells and macrophages to the vaccination site, a critical first step in building an immune response. The presence of Cd80 is particularly significant. This gene encodes a co-stimulatory molecule on Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs) that is essential for activating T cells. Its upregulation confirms that the innate immune system is being activated and is effectively priming the adaptive immune system for a specific, targeted response. Finally, Spp1 is a cytokine involved in cell-mediated immunity and inflammation. Collectively, these transcriptional changes are suggestive of activation of cytosolic DNA sensing pathways, potentially involving STING-associated signaling, which are known to mediate innate immune recognition of foreign DNA and trigger a full-scale immune response.
These data suggest that M20-hpDNA-EP induces an early inflammatory response in the muscle at early time points and immune activation in secondary lymphatic organs, which are not observed following mRNA-EP vaccination. This divergence in the induction of inflammation provides a plausible mechanistic basis for the different immunogenicity profiles observed between the two platforms under electroporation-based delivery.

## Discussion
In this study, we demonstrate for the first time the use of clinically scalable synthetic DNA to deliver personalized NCVs via EP. The immunogenicity and antitumor efficacy of the hpDNA-EP platform were evaluated in two widely used preclinical tumor models, supporting its further characterization and potential clinical translation. Importantly, comparison with EP-delivered, pseudouridine-modified mRNA revealed a distinct MOA.
To validate the expression cassette encoding a multiepitope NCV, we conducted in vitro studies confirming that the assembly of 20 neoantigens in the form of 28-mer peptides was detectable at the protein level ( ). Detection of the terminal tag served as a surrogate for translation of the upstream neoantigen sequences. While this approach established proof-of-concept protein expression, it necessitates modification of the native construct and relies on protein-based analytical procedures that are not readily scalable across individualized vaccine variants, as previously noted in constructs encoding a smaller number of neoantigens and employing alternative gene architectures. We therefore adopted a transcript-based assay applicable to all constructs sharing the conserved tPA leader and CMV intron elements. As we previously demonstrated for the pDNA in vitro measurement of gene expression indicates DNA integrity. Indeed, we further validated this method by keeping M20-hpDNA in harsh storage conditions, which resulted in proportional reduction of gene expression as well as immune responses ( and ).
Benchmarking against pDNA showed that hpDNA-EP delivery in muscle, at an equimolar ratio, resulted in similar levels of gene expression ( ). These findings are consistent with previous side-by-side in vivo comparisons of linear DNA and plasmid DNA, including the study by Walters et al (2014), which reported similar expression kinetics up to 7 days following electroporation. Comparable functional outcomes have also been described for alternative linear DNA platforms, such as doggybone DNA and linear DNA amplicons, although these DNA vectors were synthesized by different methodologies to the hpDNA described here. While we cannot exclude the influence of DNA quality on gene expression, our data extend these observations to enzymatically produced hpDNA constructs within a multiepitope vaccine context, supporting their functional equivalence to pDNA under the conditions tested. Notably, the absence of bacterial backbone, including antibiotic resistance genes, in the hpDNA constructs enabled a dose reduction compared with pDNA with functional equivalency observed at equimolar ratios (33% reduction in DNA mass for M20 vaccine). For a clinical vaccine, this may improve patient safety and reduce manufacturing costs of the drug product.
Immune responses to neoantigens induced by M20-hpDNA-EP revealed that Adpgk and Reps1 elicited the strongest CD8+ T-cell responses, consistent with previous reports with Adpgk generating the strongest response, followed by Reps1 ( ). From a safety perspective, the M20-hpDNA-EP vaccine induced strong neoantigen-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell responses with minimal cross-reactivity to WT epitopes. This suggests that the platform induces a tumor-specific immune response with a favorable safety profile. In line with previous findings, Reps1 showed low cross-reactivity with its WT sequence. Whether this is mediated by the higher avidity (0.001 µM) relative to Adpgk (0.07 µM) is not clear. Phenotypic analysis of Adpgk-specific CD8+ T cells demonstrated a memory phenotype, and TCR sequencing identified a distinct repertoire with respect to peptide vaccination. This suggests a complementary role for different vaccine platforms and the potential for expanding TCR diversity by combining hpDNA-EP vaccination with mRNA-LNP or peptide-based vaccines.
The M20-hpDNA-EP dose-finding ( ) showed that 6.7 µg was effective for both immunogenic response and tumor protection. This dose aligns with preclinical and clinical data with a COVID-19 vaccine using the same expression cassette ( ) and delivered i.m. by EP. This suggests that a 1 mg dose—corresponding to 10 µg of pDNA in the mouse model—should be effective in eliciting potent T-cell responses against neoantigens in patients with cancer. The observation that hpDNA encoding 20 neoantigen constructs is effective in the CT26 tumor model generalizes the use of hpDNA as a vaccination platform ( ). In this model, we also confirmed immunogenic neoantigens previously reported with pDNA-EP and other delivery methods. 
For personalized vaccines, the DNA manufacturing platform must not only scale to clinically relevant quantities but also scale out to support the simultaneous production of multiple patient-specific batches. RCA-based enzymatic approaches are particularly well suited to this requirement, as they allow flexible batch sizes and can be performed in single-use plastic tubes or bags. In contrast, pDNA production is constrained by bioreactor geometry and availability. Other enzymatic methods for DNA production, including PCR-based, have been reported but are not currently available at GMP-grade, present challenges with scalability and fidelity and the unprotected ends are susceptible to degradation. mRNA LNPs have already showcased translation promise in the clinic; however, their manufacture is a multistep process involving DNA template production, IVT mRNA synthesis and LNP encapsulation. The developed hpDNA vaccine bypasses the latter two steps with high potential to reduce needle-to-needle time in clinical practice.
The hpDNA production method described was scaled to 500 mg for the M20-hpDNA drug substance with multigram batches reduced to practice for other constructs, meeting the clinical requirements for NCVs. The M20-hpDNA product demonstrated high purity and was stable for 12 months at −20°C or 2 days at +50°C without loss of efficacy. This platform is not limited by size or sequence, demonstrated by the production of both 20 and 40 neoantigen constructs ( ). Ongoing studies aim to explore an NCV encoding 40 neoantigens, including both CD4 and CD8 epitopes. Previous research has shown that universal CD4 epitopes enhance CD8 responses, and recent investigations have focused on neoantigen-specific CD4 responses. Future iterations of hpDNA-based NCVs will incorporate CD4 neoantigens to further optimize immunogenicity.
Interestingly, only the combination of DNA (hpDNA or pDNA) with EP was immunogenic in our study, whereas mRNA-EP was not. This observation is specific to the delivery conditions tested here, namely electroporation of non-formulated, pseudouridine-modified mRNA, and should not be generalized to formulated mRNA vaccines. Clinically validated mRNA-LNP platforms are known to be immunogenic and clinically active; thus, our comparison is intended to inform delivery-specific and platform-specific mechanisms rather than to evaluate mRNA vaccines as a class. Despite similar levels of gene expression between hpDNA-Luc-EP and mRNA-Luc-EP, immunogenicity was not equivalent. These findings challenge the reliance on gene expression as a sole predictor of immunogenicity, emphasizing the importance of local inflammation at the injection site. Previous studies have proposed multiple Mechanisms of action (MOAs) for pDNA-EP. Here, we compared the gene expression profile of the new hpDNA format at immunogenic dose with the pDNA and mRNA vaccine at matched doses. We leveraged the observation of lack of immunogenicity of the mRNA-EP to dissect the MOA of hpDNA-EP. Gene expression changes were consistent with activation of cytosolic DNA sensing pathways, potentially including STING-associated signaling, although this was not directly tested.
Although the lack of immunogenicity of non-formulated mRNA has been described for a long time, we suggest that this is not due to degradation, as protein expression persisted for several days, but rather reflects an absence of a productive innate inflammatory program at the injection site under electroporated conditions. Analysis of gene expression in the muscle at an early point reveals that the inflammation is different from that caused by hpDNA, a minimal form of DNA administered with the same method. We cannot exclude that the administration site or the presence of pseudouridine in the mRNA may play a key role in the absence of RNA immunogenicity. Our data align with previous work that showed how naked mRNA is not immunogenic per se and requires formulation in LNPs and extends this observation to administration via EP. Further studies are needed to define which immune cells are specifically modulated by DNA but not by mRNA in the muscle and in primary and secondary lymphoid organs.
As oncology advances toward increasingly personalized interventions, synthetic hpDNA delivered by EP represents a safe, scalable, and adaptable approach for individualized neoantigen vaccination. The timely adoption of innovative delivery and manufacturing solutions like these will be critical to enable successful clinical translation.

## Limitations and translational considerations
Our efficacy studies were performed in MC38 and CT26, two widely used murine colon cancer models with relatively high tumor mutational burden and neoantigen load. These models are well-established benchmarks for evaluating neoantigen vaccine design, immunogenicity, and mechanism of action, making them appropriate for initial platform characterization. However, their favorable immunogenic features may overestimate therapeutic performance relative to human tumors with lower antigenicity, greater heterogeneity, or immune-excluded microenvironments. Accordingly, while these models are suitable for defining platform activity and mechanism, future studies in poorly immunogenic and/or immune-excluded tumor models will be important to further define the breadth and translational robustness of hpDNA-EP activity across diverse cancer contexts.
