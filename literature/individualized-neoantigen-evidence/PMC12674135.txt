## Abstract
B cell involvement in neoantigen-driven antitumor immunity remains largely unexplored because of challenges in predicting B cell responses. Here, we developed a method to identify B cell epitopes by characterizing >437,000 peptides tested for IgG binding and >370 million B cell receptor (BCR) clones. Our single-cell BCR sequencing of pre– and post–severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 vaccination validates the performance of this method. Mouse vaccination experiments demonstrate that B cell neoepitopes enhance immune responses, driving BCR expansion and tumor regression. Genomic analysis of >8000 The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) samples reveals an inverse correlation between predicted B cell reactivity and mutation allele frequencies, indicating B cell–mediated neoantigen elimination. Applying our multiomics model to checkpoint blockade responses in 2074 patients highlights the clinical relevance of B cell neoepitope prediction. A meta-analysis of 11 personalized vaccine trials involving 1739 neoantigens suggests that incorporating B cell neoepitopes may improve vaccination efficacy. These results underscore the significance of B cell–reactive neoantigens in antitumor immunity.

Artificial intelligence may help design neoantigen cancer vaccines that work better by engaging B cells.

## INTRODUCTION
While the focus of antitumor immunity has predominantly been on T cells, recent findings suggest a significant and versatile role for B cells, particularly in light of the discovery of tertiary lymphoid structures ( – ). Following chronic inflammation and intricate interplay between immune cells including T cells, B cells, and dendritic cells (DCs), localized cell clusters develop that resemble lymph nodes. These cellular aggregates are recognized as crucial in generating and sustaining a robust local immune response. Within tertiary lymphoid structures, B cells contribute to the reinvigoration of T cells through antigen recognition and interaction. Recently, Bod et al. ( ) demonstrated that B cell–specific checkpoint molecules regulate the activity of tumor-specific effector T cells through antigen presentation and costimulation.
Neoantigens may also be involved in this process. In a murine cancer model, Cui et al. ( ) demonstrated the capability of neoantigens to activate tumor-specific B cells and CD4+ T cells, leading to the generation of IL-21 (interleukin-21)–producing T follicular helper cells. T follicular helper cells then contribute to the activation of antitumor effector CD8+ T cells and the production of granzyme B. However, it remains unclear whether B cells in general contribute to neoantigen-induced immune responses in various types of human cancers. This is in part due to the lack of reliable methods for identifying CD4+ T cell– and B cell–reactive neoepitopes. Recently, our group has developed a computational model to predict CD4+ T cell–reactive neoantigens ( ).
As for B cell epitopes, there have been efforts to predict linear (continuous) and conformational (discontinuous) epitopes. First-generation prediction methods were based on amino acid properties such as hydrophilicity, flexibility, and turns ( – ). More recent approaches were based on machine learning models, such as hidden Markov model, neural network, and support vector machine ( – ). Nevertheless, benchmark evaluations indicate that the performance of these algorithms may be still unreliable, calling for more accurate prediction methods ( ). The prediction of immunogenic peptides has been a challenge because of the variability of immune receptors ( ). Furthermore, the frequent occurrence of somatic hypermutations (SHMs) in the antigen-binding site of the B cell receptor (BCR) compounds this difficulty in epitope prediction.
In this work, we present a reliable method for predicting linear B cell epitopes. Leveraging the accomplishments of our DeepNeo model ( , , ), this method characterizes the spatial interaction between BCR and epitope sequences using preferential binding affinity between amino acids while also considering variable BCR alleles and SHMs. To confirm the effectiveness of our approach, we scrutinize our single-cell transcriptome and BCR sequencing from 24 individuals who underwent COVID-19 vaccination, specifically observing changes in BCR clonality according to predicted severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) epitope reactivity.
We then investigate the role of neoantigen-mediated B cell immunity in cancer. Our vaccination trials on three mouse cancer models and analyses of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) pan-cancer mutanomes indicated that neoantigens likely to elicit B cell compartments of the adaptive immune system show a tendency for greater antitumor effects. We construct a multiomics model to enhance the prediction of the clinical benefit of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. As a result, neoantigen-stimulated B cell response, along with T cell immunity, demonstrated explanatory power in predicting the survival outcome. Our analysis of human clinical trials on neoantigen vaccines also shows that the inclusion of neoepitopes capable of engaging the B cell immune system contributes to overall responses to vaccination. Together, this work demonstrates the effect of B cell immunity on neoantigen-driven anticancer immunity.

## RESULTS

## Deep learning prediction of linear B cell epitopes
Modeling B cell epitope binding has been challenging because of the complexity of the immune repertoire including the presence of SHMs and the lack of data for binding properties between BCR and epitope sequences ( , ). Here, we developed a convolution neural network (CNN) model, named DeepNeo-BCR, to capture distinct local properties of amino acid interactions between peptides and BCRs by using our previous DeepNeo model architecture ( , ). To address the variability of the BCR repertoire, we used sequence information embedded in >370 million clones retrieved from 2152 immunoglobulin G (IgG) sequencing samples ( ).
For model training, we collected 437,396 IgG binders and nonbinders of fixed length (12-, 15-, and 16-nucleotide oligomers) tested in human or mouse from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) (table S1) ( ). Specifically, this dataset is composed of experimental results measuring the reactivity of peptides against a general B cell repertoire pool based on structural or chemical methods. The data were randomly split into a set for training and validation and a separate set for testing with a 7:3 ratio before model construction. Because the vast majority of this dataset was based on tests against a nonspecific pool of BCR repertoires, we designed an ensemble model that aggregates 63 CNN models, each representing an immunoglobulin heavy chain variable (IGHV) allele of human or mouse ( ). The frequency of SHMs per IGHV residue was also incorporated into the model ( ).
 First, each peptide was tested against every individual IGHV allele to construct single-allele models. Ensemble models were then constructed on the basis of linear regression with prediction values from single-allele models ( ). To evaluate the contribution of incorporating multiple alleles, we constructed an ensemble model of less than 20 alleles and that of more than 20 alleles. When applied to the separated test set, the ensemble model built using more than 20 alleles outperformed the single-allele model and smaller-scale ensemble model in terms of the receiver operatic characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC), precision-recall AUC (PR-AUC), F1 score, and accuracy score ( ). The validation and test performance of the full ensemble model composed of all 63 alleles showed reliable prediction accuracy ( ). The models using only human alleles or mouse alleles achieved similar levels of accuracy to the full ensemble model on the test dataset (fig. S1). The performance of the ensemble DeepNeo-BCR model was substantially higher than the reported performance of previous methods ( ), including other linear epitope prediction tools analyzing fixed-length peptides, LBtope ( ), LBEEP ( ), and IgPred ( ), as well as BepiPred-3.0 ( ), which functions for both linear and conformational epitopes.

## Analysis of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination data
B cells with BCR sequences that recognize immunogenic epitopes undergo clonal expansion. Accordingly, B cell clonal expansion in response to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination has been reported by multiple studies ( – ). Therefore, we sought to test whether BCRs with clonal expansion in the course of COVID-19 vaccination have high DeepNeo-BCR scores for SARS-CoV-2 epitopes by examining the BCR sequences of individuals who received the vaccine.
To identify BCR clones undergoing expansion, we generated single-cell BCR sequencing data for 24 participants without previous SARS-CoV-2 infection (table S2). The blood samples were acquired before and after vaccination to investigate vaccination-induced changes in the B cell landscape. The IGHV sequences of individual B cells from each sample were clustered into clones on the basis of sequence similarity, and the expansion of the clones was determined ( ). All the retrieved IGHV sequences were estimated for average affinity toward all peptides encompassing the BNT162b2 SARS-CoV-2 vaccine by DeepNeo-BCR. As a result, BCR clones with higher average affinity for the vaccine sequences were more likely to experience clonal expansion during vaccination than those with lower affinity ( ).
 A similar test was performed with postvaccination samples only. When the sequences were divided into larger clones (greater than the mean read fraction) and smaller clones (lower than the mean read fraction), the larger clones had higher odds of having higher affinity with the vaccine sequences as predicted by DeepNeo-BCR ( ). A high level of consensus was found among the clonally expanded BCRs ( ).
We also generated single-cell transcriptome data for the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of the 24 vaccinated samples. A total of 65,542 immune cells, including 5446 memory B cells and 5827 naïve B cells, were collected from the 24 samples ( and fig. S2). The DeepNeo-BCR prediction scores against the vaccine sequences were greater for memory B cells than those for naïve B cells ( ), leading to overrepresentation of the high-affinity BCRs in memory B cells relative to naïve B cells ( ).
For further validation, we used bulk BCR sequencing data of nine vaccinated participants from another study ( ). The IGHV sequences were extracted and clustered into clones, which were divided into the groups of large (greater than the mean read fraction) and small (lower than the mean read fraction) clones (fig. S3A). Similar to our single-cell BCR data, the average DeepNeo-BCR scores of the large clones were greater than those of the small clones (fig. S3B). Moreover, the odds of high-affinity clones (greater than the mean DeepNeo-BCR score) being one of the large clones per sample was 1.5 (fig. S3C), suggesting that BCR sequences with high predicted affinity toward the vaccine sequences may be prone to clonal expansion.
To validate our method at the individual epitope level, we used the experimental results of epitope mapping for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein ( ). Briefly, the spike protein was split into short epitopes, and each epitope was tested for IgG response by adding sera of patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 ( ). As a result, 34 hotspot epitopes with high reactivity to IgG and 158 epitopes with low IgG response levels were identified. All epitope sequences were subjected to DeepNeo-BCR for estimating affinity to the clonally expanded and nonexpanded BCR sequences obtained from our single-cell data. Against the clonally expanded BCRs, the hotspot epitopes had higher prediction scores than the nonreactive epitopes (D statistics = 0.66) ( ). This trend was less evident against the BCRs without clonal expansion (D statistics = 0.46), supporting DeepNeo-BCR’s capability of predicting immunogenic epitope-BCR interactions.

## Analysis of mouse cancer vaccination data
We previously developed an algorithm to assess the T cell response of a given peptide-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) complex ( ). This methodology, referred to as DeepNeo-TCR (where TCR stands for the T cell receptor), was used to identify TCR(+) epitopes (TCEs) from three murine cancer models (EMT6 breast cancer, LLC1 lung cancer, and B16F10 melanoma). On the basis of the prediction, 46 synthetic long peptides were manufactured and administered three times to cancer-free mice. The splenocytes were then harvested to measure IFN-γ secretion using the ELISpot assay. To ensure reproducibility, each peptide was tested on three mice, with each mouse undergoing two assays, leading to a total of six measurements per peptide. Consequently, a total of 276 measurements were made for the 46 candidate neoantigens. IFN-γ signals were stronger for most of the neoantigens than the negative control. Specifically, 15 of the 15 B16F10, 15 of the 19 EMT6, and 10 of the 11 LLC1 neoantigens had IFN-γ signals stronger than the negative control.
Here, we reanalyzed this dataset by running DeepNeo-BCR for all potential neoepitopes within each of 44 synthetic long peptides after filtering out two outliers. Given that the wild-type sequences were available for all neoantigens, we obtained a differential DeepNeo-BCR score for each neoepitope by subtracting the wild-type score from the mutant score. The maximum of the differential DeepNeo-BCR scores of all potential neoepitopes within a vaccine was obtained. According to this score, the 44 vaccine constructs were divided into two groups: TCR(+)BCR(high) indicating those with the DeepNeo-BCR score greater than the mean versus TCR(+)BCR(low) indicating those with the DeepNeo-BCR score lower than the mean (table S3).
As a result, the TCR(+)BCR(high) vaccines displayed stronger IFN-γ signals than the TCR(+)BCR(low) vaccines ( and fig. S4, A and B), suggesting that the B cell reactivity of T cell–immunogenic neoantigens is capable of inducing a more robust immune response. A similar pattern was observed when the same analysis was repeated, considering only the mutant score (fig. S4, C to E). These findings suggest that neoantigens that stimulate B cells as well as T cells may provoke more potent immune responses than those that do not engage B cells.
 We then sought to evaluate whether vaccines identified by DeepNeo-BCR could enhance antitumor cytotoxicity by promoting B cell expansion. To this end, we generated two groups of TCR(+)BCR(high) vaccines (B16F10-1, n = 5; B16F10-2, n = 5) for testing in B16F10 melanoma models (table S4). C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously implanted with tumor cells on day 0 and treated with either B16F10-1, B16F10-2, or polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly I:C) alone (vehicle; negative control) on days 5 and 12. The group sizes were as follows: vehicle, n = 17; B16F10-1, n = 18; B16F10-2, n = 18.
We first examined whether the vaccines could induce tumor regression. Longitudinal analysis of tumor volumes revealed significant tumor regression with both B16F10-1 and B16F10-2 ( ). To determine whether B cells are actually required for the antitumor response, we performed in vivo B cell depletion using anti-CD20 before vaccination (vehicle, n = 9; B16F10-2, n = 10; B16F10-2 + anti-CD20, n = 10). Flow cytometry confirmed depletion of circulating B cells (B220+CD19+ cells: 33.2% in controls versus 0.3% in the depleted group). Tumor volumes were significantly larger in B cell–depleted mice compared with vaccine-only mice (P = 0.024), providing direct evidence that B cells are required for the enhanced antitumor effects induced by our TCR(+)BCR(high) vaccines ( ).
To investigate whether neoantigen vaccination reshapes the BCR repertoire, we performed BCR sequencing for 34 mice in total (vehicle, n = 11; B16F10-1, n = 13; B16F10-2, n = 10). The Shannon diversity of the BCR repertoire showed a strong correlation with tumor size (R = 0.52, P = 0.0017; ). Vaccine-treated mice were further divided into regressive and nonregressive groups, with regression defined as tumor size falling more than one standard deviation below the mean of the vehicle group (table S5). Regressive tumors exhibited significantly lower BCR diversity than nonregressive tumors (B16F10-1, P = 0.071; B16F10-2, P = 0.018; fig. S5A), suggesting that effective tumor control involves a more clonal B cell response.
IGHV gene usage revealed treatment-specific patterns of germline segment utilization, suggesting vaccine-driven selection of B cell clones ( ). Analysis using the Morisita-Horn similarity index further demonstrated treatment-specific clustering of IGHV sequences, indicating differential clonal populations (fig. S5B). Notably, treatment-specific clonal similarity was more pronounced among mice with tumor regression, indicating that antitumor immunity was driven by vaccine-induced BCR remodeling ( ). Furthermore, alignment of representative CDR3 regions revealed unique sequence motifs enriched in each treatment group’s regressive tumors ( ). Together, these findings validate the predictive capacity of DeepNeo-BCR and highlight the critical role of B cell–reactive neoantigens in shaping the B cell repertoire and enhancing antitumor immunity.

## Analysis of treatment-naïve human cancer data
Our mouse vaccination data showed that neoantigens with BCR reactivity have higher potential for stimulating immune responses. To recapitulate this finding in human cancers, we collected and processed the mutanomes of TCGA pan-cancer data encompassing 8385 samples across 26 cancer types. In our previous work ( ), we illustrated the signals of TCR(+) neoantigen depletion in accordance with varying immune microenvironments as estimated by 74 immune phenotype indexes (table S6) ( ). Our rationale was that the variant allele frequency (VAF) of immunogenic mutations should be specifically lower in samples with higher immunity than those with lower immunity.
To test whether the addition of B cell immunity can strengthen neoantigen depletion, we further divided TCR(+) mutations into TCR(+)BCR(+) and TCR(+)BCR(−) epitopes, as predicted by DeepNeo-BCR. The magnitude of neoantigen depletion was measured by the difference in the average VAF between high-immune and low-immune samples. In most of the 74 immune phenotypes examined, high-immune samples typically maintained lower VAFs for TCR(+) mutations, as indicated by negative values directed to the left in . This pattern of neoantigen depletion was more pronounced for TCR(+)BCR(+) epitopes (red bars) than TCR(+)BCR(−) epitopes (blue bars). This difference indicates neoantigen depletion attributed to B cell responses, suggesting that the B cell component of the adaptive immune system can bolster negative immune selection against neoantigens. The same pattern was observed when BCR(+) and BCR(−) mutations were compared without regard to T cell reactivity (fig. S6). These results highlight the often-underappreciated role of natural B cell immunity in recognizing and selectively eliminating neoepitopes expressed on tumor cells.
 Next, we investigated whether our prediction of B cell neoepitopes could be supported by BCR repertoire properties by using the exome and matched transcriptome of 4435 TCGA samples. BCR(+) epitope (BCE) load was determined for each sample by using DeepNeo-BCR. BCR repertoire properties were calculated in terms of clonality and entropy. For comparison, we used DeepNeo-TCR ( ) to compute TCE load and also determined the clonality and entropy of the TCR repertoire using the transcriptome data.
Consequently, grouping of samples by cancer type clarified the relationship between BCE load and the BCR repertoire, with a positive correlation with BCR clonality (R = 0.44; P = 0.023) and a negative correlation with BCR entropy (R = −0.37; P = 0.058) ( ). Furthermore, these correlations exhibited cell type specificity. Specifically, BCR clonality and entropy were more closely associated with BCE load than with TCE load ( ). Therefore, our predictions of BCR(+) neoantigens appear to be biologically significant in relation to the BCR repertoire.
To further investigate this relationship, we analyzed how the composition of the B cell population might influence these correlations. We stratified samples into memory B cell–high and memory B cell–low groups to examine tumors with varying levels of antigen-experienced B cells. This classification revealed distinct patterns of correlation between BCE load and BCR repertoire metrics (fig. S7, A and B). In a higher proportion of memory B cell group, BCE load showed a significant positive correlation with BCR clonality (R = 0.4; P = 0.037). This correlation was weaker and not statistically significant in the low group (R = 0.33; P = 0.096) (fig. S7A). Similar patterns were observed for correlations with BCR entropy (fig. S7B). Notably, correlations between TCE load and TCR repertoire metrics showed less variation between these groups, suggesting that the B cell population composition specifically influences BCE-related metrics (fig. S7C).

## Multiomics prediction of clinical responses to ICB treatment
Next, to elucidate the role of B cell neoepitopes within the context of ICB therapy, we gathered genomic and transcriptomic data of 18 ICB cohorts ( , – ) (total n = 2074) across four different types of cancer, including lung cancer (n = 956, 42%), melanoma (n = 536, 28%), bladder cancer (n = 347, 18%), and kidney cancer (n = 235, 12%) ( and table S7). Our three lung cancer datasets ( , , ) were combined to generate the Samsung Medical Center (SMC) cohort (total n = 586). Most of the patients received programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) agents (n = 1620, 78%), followed by cytotoxic T lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA4) agents (n = 181, 9%), and combinatory treatment (n = 273, 13%). Moreover, the samples were predominantly collected before ICB initiation (pretreatment, n = 2023, 98%). Among the patients, 45% had a durable clinical response, defined as a complete response, partial response, stable disease with progression-free survival >6 months, or overall survival >1 year, according to the RECIST version 1.1.
 To assess the importance of B cell neoepitopes in predicting the clinical response to ICB, we sought to develop robust machine learning models encompassing multiomics features selected on the basis of insights from both previous studies and our current research. More specifically, the feature set included the load of neoepitopes predicted to be capable of engaging CD8+ T cells (TCE class 1 load) ( ), CD4+ T cells (TCE class 2 load) ( ), and B cells (BCE load). Also, we integrated the expression of genes (CXCL9 and CD274) ( , ) and the fraction of immune cells (CD4/8+ T cells, germinal center B cells, and plasma cells) ( – , , , ), along with immune repertoire measurements (TCR/BCR clonality and entropy) ( – ). The collected ICB cohort dataset was further divided into the training set (80%) and the test set (20%) with consideration of both clinical response and cohort ratios. To overcome the potential issues from unavailable data points, we implemented advanced algorithms, such as Extreme Gradient Boost model (XGBoost), histogram-based gradient boosting classification tree model (HistGBM), and LightGBM, known for their effective handling of missing data ( ). We also built a single-factor model on the basis of tumor mutation burden (TMB), which is a Food and Drug Administration–approved biomarker for guiding ICB treatment ( ).
We then compared the performance of the constructed models. The XGBoost model achieved the highest level of performance (training AUC = 0.93 and test AUC = 0.71), substantially outperforming the TMB-only model (training AUC = 0.66 and test AUC = 0.58) ( ). Also, the calculated predictive probabilities ( ) and evaluation of confusion matrices ( ) of each model indicated that the predictive models that consider multiple immune-related features achieved higher discriminatory power for stratifying the ICB response (fig. S8). According to patient stratification by the XGBoost model, the patient group predicted as responders showed a significantly better survival rate than that as nonresponders ( ). Similar performance in survival prediction was achieved with the HistGBM and LightGBM models, showing better discrimination than the TMB-only model, especially on the test dataset (fig. S9). This suggests that multimodal machine learning models leveraging multiomics data can accurately identify individuals who are more likely to respond to the treatment, thereby indicating its potential clinical utility in prognosticating patient outcomes.

## Analysis of the ICB multiomics model
The contribution of each feature in predicting the ICB response was evaluated by interpreting each trained model ( ). The fraction of CD8+ T cells, one of the most well-known markers for ICB responsiveness, emerged as the most influential feature in XGBoost. Notably, BCE load ranked second in the magnitude of contribution in the XGBoost model and ranked first in the HistGBM model. Following these features, the germinal center B cell score, TCE (class 1) load, expression of the CD274 gene, and TCE (class 2) load were identified as important contributors ( ).
 To specifically estimate the survival benefit of neoepitope load in comparison to TMB, a bootstrapping analysis with 5000 iterations for survival outcome was performed ( ). As a result, TCE (class 2) load emerged as the most significant factor in predicting the ICB response, surpassing the explanatory power of TMB. Also, BCE load demonstrated superior discriminative power compared to TMB and TCE (class 1) load, with TCE (class 2) load median P = 4.2 × 10−15, BCE load median P = 1.9 × 10−10, and TCE (class 1) load median P = 1.7 × 10−7 in comparison to TMB median P = 3.3 × 10−7. This finding suggests the importance of cross-talk between CD4 T cells and B cells ( ).
To investigate the statistical association between each individual feature and therapeutic response (benefit versus no benefit), a meta-analysis was conducted using logistic regression across the multiple ICB cohorts ( and table S8). The analysis revealed the significant association of BCE load with the ICB response [odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.46 (1.21 to 1.77), P = 9.6 × 10−5]. Simultaneously, to evaluate the influence of features on the overall survival, a similar meta-analysis was conducted using the Cox proportional hazard model ( and table S9). Here again, BCE load demonstrated its significance, presenting a significant association with patient survival [hazard ratio with 95% CI: 0.78 (0.66 to 0.93), P = 5.2 × 10−3].
Last, we investigated the relationship between BCE load and the BCR immune repertoire in the ICB cohort samples, similarly to how it was done with TCGA samples (fig. S7). Initially, analysis of the complete cohort showed modest correlations between BCE load and BCR repertoire metrics (fig. S10A). However, when we stratified samples on the basis of B cell maturity, a more evident pattern emerged (fig. S10B). In the memory B cell–high group, BCE load demonstrated strong correlations with BCR repertoire characteristics, whereas these correlations were substantially weaker in the memory B cell–low group. Notably, the correlations between TCE load and the TCR repertoire were similar between the two groups. This stratified analysis reveals that the relationship between BCE load and BCR repertoire features is particularly pronounced in samples with a higher proportion of memory B cells, consistent with our observations in the TCGA cohort. In conclusion, through our extensive analysis, the load of B cell neoepitopes emerged as a crucial predictive feature in predicting response and survival outcomes of patients who received ICB treatment, highlighting the importance of B cell immunity in therapeutic settings.

## Analysis of human clinical trial data
To ascertain the influence of the B cell reactivity of neoantigen vaccines in human clinical trials, we collected data from 11 clinical trials ( – ) involving 1739 vaccines administered to 123 patients in the form of mRNA constructs, synthetic long peptides, or peptide-loaded DCs (table S10). Each patient was treated with 5 to 30 vaccines, producing 14- to 52-nucleotide oligomer peptides. All potential neoepitopes from each vaccine were analyzed by DeepNeo-BCR to identify BCEs ( and table S11).
 Activated B cells can potentially interact with CD4+ T cells recognizing other antigens or other epitopes of the same antigen. As such, B cell epitopes might have the capability to enhance not only the cognate vaccines in which they are present but also other vaccines introduced concurrently into the same patient with differing epitope compositions. To test this possibility, we assessed each patient’s overall immune response to vaccination, gauged by the percentage of IFN(+) vaccines, in relation to the overall B cell reactivity of all delivered neoantigens.
We first used the maximum DeepNeo-BCR score among all neoepitopes per patient to determine the patient-level B cell reactivity of vaccines. This index showed a strong positive correlation with the patient-level immune response (R = 0.32; P = 1.9 × 10−4) ( ). For instance, the patient labeled as “Sahin P04” received a vaccine with high DeepNeo-BCR epitope scores, “vac1,” and showed an IFN(+) response for 8 of 10 vaccines ( , upper). Another patient “Chen P04” was responsive to 3 of 13 vaccines without any BCEs across all vaccines ( , lower). In a notable contrast, the DeepNeo-TCR score did not show such a correlation with the patient-level immune response (fig. S11A). The fraction of BCEs measured by DeepNeo-BCR per patient had a positive correlation (fig. S11B). Other B cell epitope prediction tools, including LBtope ( ), LBEEP ( ), IgPred ( ), and BepiPred3.0 ( ), also failed to result in this pattern (fig. S11, C to F).
We then used the percentage of BCEs across all vaccines per patient as the measure of the collective B cell reactivity of vaccines. This measure also showed a positive correlation with the percentage of IFN(+) vaccines (R = 0.25; P = 9.6 × 10−3) (fig. S10B). For instance, when considering >70% IFN(+) vaccines as a sufficient immune response, the inclusion of >20% BCEs leads to a 4.72-fold increase in the odds of prompting a patient-level response to vaccination (P = 4.11 × 10−3; ). When the same analyses were repeated with other B cell epitope prediction tools, the contribution of B cell neoepitopes to patients’ overall vaccination response was not observed (fig. S12). Collectively, these results indicate that patients who receive vaccine formulations favoring B cell reactivity are more likely to manifest robust T cell responses and that DeepNeo-BCR can reliably forecast B cell reactivity, not only for individual neoantigens but also when considering the collective effect of all administered vaccines.

## DISCUSSION
There has been a remarkable increase in research efforts and clinical trials related to personalized cancer vaccines. However, a significant portion of neoantigens, predicted via in silico techniques, falls short in stimulating an immune response in vivo. In the clinical trial data analyzed in this study, a mere 43% of neoantigen vaccines succeeded in prompting a T cell reaction, with response rates varying between 7.7 and 66.7% across different cohorts. In 45% of the total patient pool, fewer than half of the vaccines administered incited a response. This reality underscores the urgent need for enhanced precision in identifying neoantigens with potential immunogenic properties.
Emerging research is highlighting the pivotal role that B cells play in antitumor immunity, with a particular emphasis on aspects such as tertiary lymphoid structures and B cell–specific checkpoint molecules ( – ). Silva-Cayetano et al. ( ) reported that localization of T follicular helper cells and subsequent interaction with B cells are pivotal in forming a robust vaccine response. A recent cancer vaccine trial reported a robust neoantigen-driven IgG response, providing direct clinical evidence of B cell involvement ( ). However, despite these research advancements, the potential reactivity of B cells is not being factored into the selection process of neoantigens. This is mainly attributed to challenges in accurately identifying B cell epitopes. Nonetheless, our algorithm for B cell epitope prediction, DeepNeo-BCR, shows reliable accuracy and achieves validation with COVID-19 vaccination data.
We proceeded to validate DeepNeo-BCR in the context of cancer neoantigens. Initially, we used the mouse vaccination dataset generated in our previous work to validate DeepNeo-TCR ( ). Our reanalysis of this dataset showed that neoantigens with BCR reactivity had a higher potential for stimulating immune responses. Further investigations were conducted to characterize changes in tumor-infiltrating B cells following neoantigen vaccination in tumor-bearing mice. Neoantigen vaccines developed on the basis of DeepNeo-BCR induced notable tumor regression and exhibited dependence on B cells, as evidenced by diminished therapeutic efficacy after B cell depletion. Comprehensive analysis of the intratumoral BCR repertoire revealed the formation of distinct, treatment-specific BCR clonotypes. These preclinical data provide mechanistic insights into the role of B cells in neoantigen-mediated antitumor immunity.
To recapitulate these findings in human cancers, we analyzed the mutanomes of TCGA pan-cancer data encompassing >7000 samples across 26 cancer types, similarly to how it was done with in our previous work ( ). Briefly, we illustrated the signals of BCR(+) neoantigen depletion in accordance with varying immune microenvironments by examining the VAF of neoantigens detected by DeepNeo-BCR. Our multiomics model, applied to the genomic and transcriptomic data of >2000 ICB-treated samples, provided another layer of validation in human cancer. The load of B cell neoepitopes stood out as a crucial predictive feature in predicting response and survival outcomes of patients who received ICB treatment. The specific correlation of B cell neoepitope load with memory B cell features, consistently observed in TCGA and the ICB cohort samples, offered an additional line of evidence that our method can identify B cell–reactive neoantigens.
Our in vivo characterization of BCR(+) neoantigen vaccination using murine tumor models, along with the retrospective analysis of the human trial data, provides compelling evidence that neoantigens boost B cell–mediated immunity. Notably, our findings underscore the pivotal role of memory B cells and their synergistic interaction with T cells in forming robust antitumor immunity. These results emphasize the potential of our approach in personalized neoantigen design to enhance treatment responses.
However, there exist certain limitations to this study. The ROC-AUC of DeepNeo-BCR, when evaluated on a test set, was found to be less than 0.9. In our analysis of the clinical trial data, we were constrained to selecting BCEs from the sequences encompassing neoepitopes preselected with a focus on MHC binding, instead of identifying potent B cell–engaging neoepitopes ab initio from the entire pool of mutations. In addition, unlike the mouse data, the wild-type sequences of the human vaccines were only partially available and were particularly limited in the case of frameshift mutations. Therefore, it is likely that our findings may still underestimate the influence of B cell–recognized neoepitopes on vaccination efficacy.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Data collection for DeepNeo-BCR
We gathered a list of linear epitopes with in vitro binding test from the IEDB ( ) on or before 17th January 2023. From the gathered data, we applied several filters: (i) The peptide must be in desired length (12-, 15-, or 16-nucleotide oligomer), (ii) the B cell and peptide binding experiment should have been tested in human or mouse, (iii) the peptide must be linear and not conformational, and (iv) the peptide must be tested on IgG. After applying the filters, a total of 437,396 data entries that measured B cell and peptide binding either through qualitative measure, cytokine secretion, direct measure, or x-ray crystallography–based binding assay were obtained. If contradictory results of the same peptide were collected, the peptide was labeled as a binding epitope, given that the experimental results can differ depending on experimental conditions. Because the peptides are of different lengths, they were padded to match the longest (16-nucleotide oligomer) peptide. More specifically, padding was applied to the first and last two residues for 12-nucleotide oligomers and to the last one residue for 15-nucleotide oligomers. The collected data were randomly split into 7:3 ratios into the train/validation and test sets for an accurate assessment of the model performance. The train/validation sets were subjected to threefold cross-validation for robust model construction.
Because the B cell-epitope binding was measured as a general reactivity, not of a particular BCR sequence, we designed our model to collectively predict the binding of a peptide to multiple BCR sequences. More specifically, we gathered 48 human and 203 mouse IGHV alleles from the IMGT database ( ). Human alleles were used as is. For mouse alleles, one representative allele per superfamily was used to reduce computational burden. As a result, 63 representative alleles, including 48 human and 15 mouse alleles, and the corresponding sequences were collected.
Prediction of B cell epitopes has been more challenging than that of T cell epitopes, partly due to SHMs occurring on IGHV sequences. To account for SHMs, we obtained the previously calculated frequency of SHMs per residue of IGHV allele superfamilies ( ). The rate was calculated as the average SHM rate per nucleotide position from 249,972 clones. To translate into the protein dimension, the SHM rate per amino acid position was calculated as the mean of every three nucleotide sequences. If the superfamily of the query allele was provided, it was used as is, and if the superfamily was not provided, the SHM rates of all alleles were used.

## Development of the DeepNeo-BCR algorithm
Our CNN architecture aimed to capture the preferential binding of BCRs and epitopes at the amino acid level. Two-dimensional matrices containing interaction strengths between the amino acids of BCRs and epitopes were created on the basis of previously calculated Cα-Cα distances ( ). To briefly summarize, the preferential binding matrix for each protein was created by considering the Cα-Cα distance between amino acids. A cutoff distance of 6.5 Å was used, excluding the nearest neighbors in the sequence. In addition, atom-atom contacts between the amino acid residues were considered. Residues i and j were considered to be in contact if any atom of residue i was within a distance of 4.5 Å from any atom of residue j.
CNN model input was generated using this matrix of preferential binding strength between amino acids. Moreover, the SHM rate was implemented as an additional weight per residue to account for IGHV diversity. The SHM rate calculated by previous publication was first normalized per superfamily from the scale of zero to one ( ). Then, the normalized SHM frequency per residue was scaled down to 1/10 as the SHM rate was used as an auxiliary weight to amino acid preferential binding. The input value per matrix cell is defined as
Pij=(Iij)×(1+SHMi)where i and j represent the positions of the IGHV and peptide sequence, respectively. Iij represents preferential binding interaction between IGHV i and peptide j, and the SHM frequency is given as additional weight according to the value of IGHV i.
Because the IEDB ( ) only contained information on B cell binding but the not full sequence or genotype of IGHV alleles, we created the input matrix of preferential amino acid binding between 63 representative IGHV alleles and peptides. The CNN model was created for each allele, resulting in 63 independent models that predict the binding of the corresponding allele and peptide. To yield the final prediction score, the individual CNN models were subjected to linear regression. Because predicting all 63 models per peptide is computationally demanding, the linear regression models were built to be appropriate for usage: using the entire set (63 alleles), human set (48 alleles), human subset (25 alleles), or mouse set (15 alleles). The subset of human alleles was defined as the representative alleles that have less than 80% sequence similarity as calculated by CD-HIT ( ).
The CNN model of individual alleles was constructed with two convolution layers, a fully connected layer, and a sigmoid layer. The convolution layers were designed to capture spatial interactions between IGHV and peptide sequences. Because of protein folding, the contact residue of BCR may not be consecutive. To account for this feature, the first kernel encompasses a full IGHV sequence. The convolution layers use the rectified linear activation function (ReLU) without pooling to extract the feature of both contact and distal interactions. Two hundred epochs were used to optimize the model, and different sets of hyperparameters were used to ensure the best performance per CNN model. Specifically, combinations of different parameters including learning rate (0.001,0.01,0.1), momentum (0.1,0.5,0.9), the number of kernels of the first and second layers (10,30,50), and regularization parameter (0.0001,0.001,0.01) were tested for optimal prediction accuracy. The model was tested with Keras ( ) on TITAN XP GPU, and statistical analysis was performed with the Python scikit-learn package ( ).

## Evaluation of DeepNeo-BCR performance
To investigate whether ensemble models outperform single-allele models, we constructed models of three categories, namely single-allele models, ensemble models composed of less than 20 alleles, and those composed of more than 20 alleles. Then, models were subjected to accuracy metrics including ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, F1 score, and accuracy score. The statistics were calculated with the Python scikit-learn package ( ). The validation curve was calculated as the mean of threefold cross-validation. For comparison with other B cell epitope prediction tools, we gathered reported AUC for each tool from the respective publication. Ensemble models using all 63 alleles were used as the AUC of DeepNeo-BCR.

## Data generation for the SARS-CoV-2 vaccination cohort
A total of 161 individuals with no previous history of COVID-19 infection were recruited from Chungnam National University Hospital (CNUH), Seoul Medical Center (SEOUL), and SMC. From the entire dataset, the data used in this study were filtered with the following criteria. First, single-cell BCR sequencing data of both pre- and postvaccination of the first dose must be available. Second, matching single-cell transcriptome data must be available. Last, the samples must have been vaccinated with mRNA vaccines. As a result, 24 samples met these criteria. The study protocols were approved by each medical center and the Institutional Review Board of Korea National Institute of Health under the following approval codes: CNUH 2020-12-002-008, SEOUL 2021-02-016, SMC 2021-03-160, and 2020-09-03-C-A. Informed written consent was obtained from all samples.
All samples were collected and processed in a biosafety level 3 facility. The blood samples were retrieved at each time point and allocated in EDTA tubes and cell preparation (CP) tubes (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). To prevent contamination, the biospecimens were processed within 2 hours after sample collection. The blood samples in the EDTA tubes were inverted 8 to 10 times, and those in the serum separator (SS) and CP tubes were centrifuged (SS tubes: 1300g; 10 min; 20° to 25°C; CP tubes: 1500g to 1800g; 15 min; 20° to 25°C after inverting 8 to 10 times). The EDTA and SS tubes were transported upright and refrigerated; the CP tubes were transported at room temperature. From the CP tubes, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated to be subjected for single-cell sequencing.
Using the 10X Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ Library kit, multiplexed single-cell libraries (four-sample pooled) were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol (10x Genomics, CA). The target cell count was 3000 cells per sample. The DNA samples extracted from the remaining cells were subjected to an single-nucleotide polymorphism array using Illumina Infinium Asian Screening Array-24 version 10 kit for demultiplexing. The libraries were sequenced by the Illumina NextSeq6000 platform, aiming 200 million reads per library. Using the cellranger ( ) mkfastq function, the raw sequence BCL files were demultiplexed and processed into FASTQ files. Then, the raw data were aligned to the GRCh38-2020-A transcriptome reference, as provided by 10x Genomics.
To generate single-cell BCR sequencing data, the libraries were generated using the Chromium Single Cell Human BCR Amplification Kit in combination with the Chromium Single Cell 5’ Library Kit. Then, the libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq6000 platform to ensure a minimum of 6000 reads per library. The data that passed quality control were used in the downstream analysis.

## Analysis of BCR clonal expansion after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination
Bulk BCR sequencing of postvaccination samples (n = 9) was downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information website [PRJNA839082 ( )]. In the analysis, BCR sequencing data after vaccination were used to assess the impact of clone size with high or low affinity with the vaccination sequence. The IGHV sequences were aligned and clustered using the –write-alignments option in MiXCR ( ) to map the full-length sequences including SHMs. To rule out the effect of very small clones, clones with a substantial size, or those that belong to the second to fourth quartiles, were selected. The clones were then divided into large and small clones according to the mean read fraction. The odds ratio of large clones having high affinity with the vaccine as predicted with DeepNeo-BCR was calculated per sample, and the mean and 95% CI were then derived. The differential distribution of the DeepNeo-BCR prediction score between the large and small clones was calculated using Kolmogorov’s D statistic.
The single-cell BCR sequencing data were processed with the following steps. The full amino acid sequence of immunoglobulin heavy chain compartment per cell was mapped with cellranger ( ). To match the format of DeepNeo-BCR input, the IGHV sequences were further parsed. If the full amino acid sequence of IGHVs was mapped, the sequence was subjected to local alignment using BioPython ( ) against the genotyped IGHV alleles to detect gap sequences and was converted into 99-nucleotide oligomer sequences. For those missing FR1 (framework 1) alignment, the FR1 segment of mapped IGHV alleles was used.
The sequences per sample were then clustered into clones. Because traditional methods of considering V(D)J genotypes and the CDR3 sequence only yielded clones with fewer than five cells, we defined a cluster as cells with 90% or higher sequence similarity as calculated by CD-HIT ( ). Because the total number of cells in time points differs within the same sample, the raw cell count is an inaccurate method to compare clonality before and after vaccination. Thus, the clonality change was calculated as the difference in cell fraction or

The sequence logo of expanded clone with the highest affinity against the vaccination sequence was plotted with WebLogo3 ( ).
The postvaccination single-cell RNA sequencing data of 24 samples were processed with the following procedures. Using scanpy ( ), low-quality cells were filtered. Specifically, those with less than 200 genes, three cells, and more than 5% mitochondrial genes were filtered. The data were then normalized and batch corrected with scanorama ( ) with samples as the batch key. Then, the data were clustered using UMAP (uniform manifold approximation and projection) with a resolution of 1.2. The clusters were annotated with well-known markers. More specifically, naïve CD8+ T cells (CD3D, CD8A, TCF7, and CCR7), naïve CD4+ T cells (CD3D, CD4, TCF7, and CCR7), memory CD4+ T cells (CD3D, CD4, and IL7R), memory CD8+ T cells (CD3D, CD8A, and CCL5), NK cells (natural killer cells; NKG7 and GLNY), naïve B cells (MS4A1 and IGHD), memory B cells (MS4A1 and CD27), plasma cells (MZB1 and XBP1), monocytes (CD14, LYZ, and FCGR3A), DCs (CST3 and IL3RA), plasmacytoid DCs (CD74 and FCER1A), platelets (PPBP), and erythrocytes (HBD and HBM) were used as identification. As a result, we annotated 5827 naïve B cells and 5446 memory B cells. The barcodes of single-cell BCR and RNA sequencing were matched to compare predicted B cell affinity against SARS-CoV-2 vaccination between naïve and memory B cells.

## Analysis of hotspot B cell epitopes
From Li et al.’s study ( ), we obtained 34 hotspot and 158 nonhotspot epitopes. They first tested for IgM and IgG responses against SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and observed that only the IgG response was distinct between healthy and SARS-CoV-2–infected samples. Thus, Li et al. constructed a microarray by using peptides derived from SARS-CoV-2 spike protein to measure the IgG response. Then, sera of 55 SARS-CoV-2–infected samples were added to measure the IgG response. Those with a high IgG response were labeled as hotspot epitopes, and those that do not elicit a B cell reaction were labeled as nonhotspot epitopes. Peptides that were insoluble or failed to synthesize were excluded from the analysis. Expanded and nonexpanded BCR clones were obtained from single-cell BCR sequencing of our vaccination cohort samples.

## Immunogenicity analysis of vaccine responses in cancer-free mice
The utility of DeepNeo-BCR in the context of neoantigen cancer vaccination was tested using mouse experiment data from our previous publication ( ). Three experiment sets, labeled as EMT6-1, LLC1, and B16F10, were used in this study because these three trials used synthetic long peptides unlike the EMT6 experiments, which used the epitope only. In essence, peptides predicted to stimulate T cell responses were administered to BALB/c or C57BL6 cancer-free mice. Splenocytes were subsequently harvested and subjected to ELISpot experiments. Vaccination was conducted once a week for 3 weeks. A total of 46 TCR(+) vaccines were tested across 276 measurements for three mouse models: EMT6 breast cancer (n = 19), LLC1 lung cancer (n = 12), and B16F10 melanoma (n = 15). Each vaccine was tested on three mice and two assays each, resulting in six measurements per peptide.
In this study, this vaccination dataset was retrospectively evaluated with DeepNeo-BCR to test whether B cell–reactive vaccines had stimulated stronger immune responses than nonreactive vaccines. Two outlier neoantigens with >500 IFN-γ spots, one from EMT6 and one from LLC1, were excluded from the analysis. Thus, a total of 264 experiments on 44 vaccines were used in the analysis. Because the experiment was conducted on mice, DeepNeo-BCR prediction was conducted on the “mouse” mode. We obtained a differential DeepNeo-BCR score for each potential neoepitope by subtracting the wild-type score from the mutant score. The maximum of the differential DeepNeo-BCR scores of all potential neoepitopes within a vaccine was obtained. According to this score, the 44 vaccines were divided into the BCR-high and BCR-low groups according to the mean of the 44 constructs. The statistical difference of IFN-γ count between the BCR-high and BCR-low groups was measured with the scikit-learn package ( ). All plots were generated with the R ggplot2 package ( ).

## Murine cancer treatment with B cell neoantigens
TCR(+)BCR(high) neoantigens were selected using the following steps. From our previous publication, we retrieved somatic mutations and putative neoantigen sequences. The B cell reactivity was predicted with DeepNeo-BCR using mouse alleles, and TCR(+) was determined using prediction scores of DeepNeo-TCR. The putative neoantigens were prioritized on the basis of the prediction scores. Then, we filtered peptides on the basis of features, including hydrophobicity (<1) and instability (<60), to facilitate peptide synthesis. The peptide characteristic was calculated using the Peptides R package (version 2.4.4), and hydrophobicity was measured using the Kyte-Dolittle method.
All animal studies were conducted at the Asan Institute for Life Sciences in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Asan Institute for Life Sciences. Female C57BL6 mice, aged 7 weeks, were obtained from JA Bio and housed under specific pathogen–free conditions. B16F10 melanoma cells (1 × 106 cells in 100 μl of phosphate-buffered saline) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of the mice. Tumor growth was monitored using a digital caliper, and mice were euthanized when the tumor volume reached approximately <500 mm3. The tumor volume was calculated using the formula volume = 1/2 × length × width. Before each injection, 50 μg of B cell–reactive neoantigen vaccine was mixed with 40 μg of poly I:C (Invivogen) as an adjuvant. For the vehicle group, 80 μg of poly I:C was used. The vaccine mixture was injected subcutaneously into both footpads of each mouse. Tumors were harvested from euthanized mice following CO2 inhalation. For the B cell depletion experiment, 250 μg of anti-CD20 was administered. B cell depletion was confirmed 48 hours later by measuring the proportion of B220+CD19+ cells in peripheral blood.
For bulk BCR sequencing, the following steps were used. Tumors were enzymatically digested with collagenase IV (1 to 2 mg/ml) for 1 hour at 37°C. The digested tissue suspension was passed through a 100-μm cell strainer to remove debris. Isolated cells were treated with red blood cell lysis buffer to remove red blood cells. After centrifugation, cell pellets were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline. From cell suspensions obtained from tumors, B cells were enriched from tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and B cells were enriched using anti-mouse CD45R (B220) microbeads (Miltenyi Biotech) and LS MACS columns (Miltenyi Biotech) according to manufacturers’ instructions. Total RNA was extracted from enriched B cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was verified for quality using Qubit and Agilent Tapestation 4150 before proceeding with library preparation. Mouse BCR libraries were prepared using the SMARTer Mouse BCR IgG H/K/L Profiling Kit (Takara Bio USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, first-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA with an oligo(dT) primer. Then, two rounds of polymerase chain reaction were performed to amplify the variable region of heavy chain of IgG. Amplified products were purified and converted into sequencing libraries with Illumina-compatible adaptors. The concentration and quality of DNA libraries were determined by Qubit and Agilent Tapestation 4150. BCR libraries were pooled and sequenced by the Illumina MiSeq Platform using MiSeq Reagent Kit version 3 (2 × 300 base pairs, 600 cycles).
The bulk BCR sequencing data were aligned and processed using MiXCR (version 4.6.0) ( ) using the takara-mouse-rna-bcr-smarter preset. We first filtered out low-quality alignments, namely those with a low read count (<3) and those unmapped to reference sequences. The clones were called using the immunoglobulin heavy chain, and Shannon diversity was calculated using the postanalysis function in MiXCR with no downsampling. Regression between tumor size and Shannon diversity was calculated with the cor.test function in R. Antitumor regression was defined as one standard deviation below the mean of vehicle group’s tumor size.
The clones were called with Change-O ( ) with a Hamming distance of 0.15 using AIRR (Adaptive Immune Receptor Repertoire)–formatted aligned files. IGHV usage was first calculated per mouse and then averaged across mice within the treatment group. Top 18 IGHV genes across three groups were used to visualize preferential variable gene usage. To visualize the similarity of vaccine-induced clonotypes, the Morisita-Horn index was calculated using clonally expanded sequences (read fraction >0.005) and those not shared with vehicle samples using the Vegan R package ( ) and visualized with the pheatmap R package ( ). Representative CDR3 sequences from regressed tumors were aligned with MAFFT and plotted using the ggmsa R package ( ) using default options. The plots were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 or the R ggplot2 ( ) package.

## Neoantigen depletion analysis using TCGA samples
MC3 mutation generated by the Pan-Cancer Atlas consortium was downloaded from the publication page (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas). To examine the impact of neoantigen-mediated spontaneous immunity by B cells along with T cells, we selected 7392 TCGA samples with both MHC I and MHC II genotypes. Because calculating B cell neoepitope load does not require MHC genotypes, we were able to profile neoepitope load for 9595 samples. The samples were from different cancer types, including adrenocortical carcinoma, bladder urothelial carcinoma, breast adenocarcinoma, cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, colon adenocarcinoma, lymphoid neoplasm diffuse large B cell lymphoma, esophageal carcinoma, glioblastoma multiforme, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma, acute myeloid leukemia, lower grade glioma, liver hepatocellular carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, mesothelioma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma, prostate adenocarcinoma, rectum adenocarcinoma, sarcoma, skin cutaneous melanoma, stomach adenocarcinoma, ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, thymoma, and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma.
Variant effect predictor ( ) was applied to the obtained MC3 mutation to annotate and find the protein sequence adjacent to the mutation. Then, neighboring amino acids were extracted using pVAC-seq ( ). In-house script was used to slide through the sequence to generate putative neoantigen candidates. To evaluate the reactivity of neoepitopes to both B cells and T cells, neoantigen candidates were restricted to 15-nucleotide oligomers. For the prediction of T cell reactivity, DeepNeo-TCR was used with a suggested threshold of 0.5. Peptides that bind to the MHC complex and stimulate the T cell response were considered as neoepitopes. For the prediction of B cell reactivity, DeepNeo-BCR with human alleles was used. We evaluated immunogenicity on the mutation level rather than on the neoepitope level to observe the effect of immunogenicity on individual mutations. Thus, the mean prediction value of B cell binding and CD4+ T cell binding was used for each mutation.
We retrieved 74 immune-related phenotypes (table S6) ( ). For each signature score, the samples were divided into high- and low-score groups on the basis of the mean value while excluding samples with scores of NA (not applicable). TCR(+)BCR(+) mutations were defined as mutations predicted to have both T cell and B cell reactivity, while only T cell responses were predicted for TCR(+)BCR(−) mutations. The magnitude of immune pressure by neoantigens was calculated as the difference of VAFs between immune phenotype–high and immune phenotype–low groups. The magnitude of negative value indicates the relative strength of neoantigen depletion signal in the immune phenotype–high group than the immune phenotype–low group.

## TCGA immune repertoire analysis
We gathered raw transcriptome files of 4435 samples. The raw transcriptome files in FASTQ format generated from the tumor sample were collected from the TCGA data portal (n = 4435). To remove adapter sequences, Trimmomatic (version 0.39) ( ) was used, and ribosomal RNA was filtered out by SortMeRNA (version 2.1b) ( ). We then aligned the remaining reads to the GRCh38 (hg38) build provided by the 1000 Genomes Project and assigned genes on the basis of the gene structure information from gencode (version 40) using STAR (version 2.7.10a) ( ) in two-pass mapping. Then, de novo assembly on the V, J, and C genes including CDR3 sequences was conducted. The TCR and BCR sequences were further mapped using TRUST4 ( ) in conjunction with the IMGT database ( ). The associations with genomic features, including genes, exons, promoters, and genomic bins, were calculated with the alignment data to calculate the clonality and entropy of the BCR and TCR repertoire. TCEs were defined as positive for DeepNeo-TCR MHC I or MHC II. TCE load was calculated for 3392 samples with MHC genotype information. The correlation was measured across cancer types by the Pearson correlation. The fractions of memory B cells and naïve B cells were calculated by CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) ( ). The maturity of B cell composition was calculated by the ratio of memory to naïve B cell fractions, and groups were classified by the median value of maturity per cancer type.

## Immunotherapy-treated lung cancer cohort
Patients with histologically confirmed lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma, including previously reported cases ( ), treated with either PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors were recruited ( ). Clinical information of this cohort was collected from electronic medical records, and tumor response was evaluated with Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (version 1.1). Progression-free survival was defined as the time from the initiation of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors until the date of documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Overall survival was defined as the time from the initiation of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to death from any cause. The AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, US) was used to purify RNA from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded or fresh tumor samples. Subsequently, NanoDrop and Bioanalyzer (Agilent, US) were used to measure the RNA concentration and purity. Library preparation was performed with either TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit version 2 (Illumina, US) or the TruSeq RNA Access Library Prep Kit (Illumina, US), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Processing genome and transcriptomic sequencing data of ICB cohorts
For processing whole-genome sequencing data, the raw FASTQ files generated from the tumor and matched normal sample were collected. The adapter sequences were removed from the raw FASTQ files using Trim Galore (version 0.6.7) (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). The reads were aligned to the GRCh37 (hg19) build of the 1000 Genomes Project using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.17-r1188) with default parameters. The aligned reads were sorted with HTSlib (version 1.7-2) in SAMtools (version 1.7) ( ), and duplications were marked with Picard (version 2.25.0-5-ga2f44ae-SNAPSHOT) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). Last, the analysis-ready BAM file was obtained by performing base quality score recalibration using ApplyBQSR in the Genome Analysis Toolkit (version 4.2.6.0) ( ). The mutation was called by Mutect2 in the Genome Analysis Toolkit ( ), and the calculation of neoepitopes followed the description above. For the cohorts by Hellmann et al. ( ) and Anagnostou et al. ( , ), only VCF files with mutation profiles were available and were processed with the same pipeline as other cohorts for BCR(+) neoepitope calculation.
For RNA sequencing, the raw FASTQ files generated from the tumor sample were collected. To remove adapter sequences, Trimmomatic (version 0.39) ( ) (TruSeq3-PE-2.fa:2:30:10:2:keepBothReads LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 MINLEN:36) was used, and ribosomal RNA was filtered out by SortMeRNA (version 2.1b) ( ) (silva-euk-18 s-id95.fasta, silva-euk-28 s-id98.fasta). We aligned the remaining reads to the GRCh38 (hg38) build provided by the 1000 Genomes Project and assigned genes on the basis of the gencode.v35.annotation.gtf using STAR (version 2.7.6a) ( ) in two-pass mapping. The aligned reads were sorted by SAMtools (version 1.7) ( ), and the read counts were calculated using HTSeq (version 0.12.4) ( ). To quantify gene expression, the read counts were normalized in the transcripts-per-million value. For the melanoma cohort [Newell et al. ( )], the BAM files provided from EGA were used.

## Calculation of immune cell fraction and immune repertoires
ImmuCellAI (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/ImmuCellAI/, analysis from the website) ( ) was implemented to deconvolute the immune cell fraction of patients on the basis of the gene expression profile of the tumor sample. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis with gseapy (version 0.10.8) (https://github.com/zqfang/GSEApy) ( ) was performed to calculate B cell module scores (germinal center B cells and plasma cells) for each sample ( ). The normalized enrichment module score was used in the analysis. TRUST4 ( ) (version 1.0.7) was used to compute the immune repertoire. For the calculation, the input included a reference genome-mapped sorted BAM file, while the reference FASTA file containing TCR and BCR sequences was used.

## Construction and evaluation of machine learning models in the ICB cohort
First, the dataset was split into training and test sets (8:2 ratio), stratified by the response rate and cohort information using the “train_test_split” function in the “sklearn” package (version 1.2.2) ( ). Subsequently, each feature was normalized per each cohort and each set using the “MinMaxScaler” function. To construct the machine learning model, we used the “XGBClassifier” function in the “xgboost” package (version 1.7.5), the “HistGradientBoostClassifier” function from the “sklearn” package, and the “LGBMClassifier” function in the “lightgbm” package (version 4.0.0). Hyperparameter tuning was performed using the “GridSearchCV” function from the “sklearn” package with a fivefold cross-validation. The best model was selected on the basis of cross-validation performance in the metric of AUC. The prediction probabilities were calculated using the “predict_proba” function from the “sklearn” package. To generate the confusion matrix, patients with prediction probabilities greater than 0.5 were classified as predicted responders. For survival analysis, the overall survival of patients was fitted to the predicted class using the “Surv” and “survfit” functions in the “survival” package (version 3.4.0) to assess the discriminative power of the calculated prediction probability.

## Bootstrapping analysis
To estimate the robustness of features for classifying the response to ICB therapy, we performed the bootstrapping analysis for 5000 iterations with resampling allowed. For each iteration, resampled patients were classified into the “high” group and “low” group by the mean value. Then, the overall survival with events was fitted into the group using the “survfit” function in the “survival” package. The P value for each iteration was obtained with the “surv_pvalue” function in the “survminer” package (version 0.4.9).

## Meta-analysis for clinical response and survival
First, we conducted a meta-analysis to investigate the association between multiple features and the response of ICB in several cohorts. For each cohort, the scaled value of each feature was fitted to the clinical response using logistic regression by the “glm” function. Next, the estimated values and standard errors across cohorts were pooled using the “metagen” function in the meta package (version 6.0.0). To account for heterogeneity between studies, we established a random effect model to estimate the effect of each feature. From the random effect model, we obtained the overall estimates, standard errors, and P values.
Similarly, to assess the impact of features on survival across ICB cohorts, we implemented the Cox proportional hazard model in meta-analysis. The scaled value of each feature was fitted to the overall survival of the patients using the “coxph” function in the “survival” package. The estimated effects and standard across cohorts were pooled using the “rma.uni” function in the “metafor” package (version 3.8.1). As a result, the overall effect size and standard error with the P value for each feature were obtained. The analysis was performed under R (version 4.2.0), and all plots were generated with ggplot2 ( ) and ggpubr (version 0.4.0) R packages.

## Collection and analysis of human vaccination trial data
To investigate the role of B cells in the efficacy of human cancer vaccine, we collected data from 11 studies ( – ) that used personalized neoantigen vaccines. Studies that administered a single driver mutation vaccine were excluded. From the individual research, we collected vaccine sequences and results of the corresponding immunogenicity assay. Because not all studies provided an immunogenicity assay for all patients, only samples with an immune assay on individual vaccines were collected and are listed in table S10. For example, Awad et al. ( ) started the clinical trial with 38 enrolled patients; 16 of them finished vaccination, and the immunogenicity of peptides was measured for 9 patients. Thus, the neoantigen sequence and immune assay of the 9 patients were included in this study. Of the 11 studies, 8 ( , – ) used peptides, 3 ( , , ) used mRNA constructs, and 1 ( ) used peptide-loaded DCs to administer a personalized neoantigen vaccine. In total, 123 samples and 1739 vaccine sequences were retrieved. Fifty entries for the immunogenicity assay were marked as NA or not determined, leaving 1689 vaccines with valid immune assays.
The immunogenicity of each tested vaccine was assigned as IFN(+) or IFN-γ(−) according to the results provided from each study. If binary immune assay results were given, it was used as is. If the immunogenicity was provided separately for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, we labeled vaccine IFN(+) if either subtype of T cells elicited an immune response. For Cai et al.’s study ( ), we interpreted the images of ELISpot results and determined immunogenicity as the number of IFN-γ spots in comparison to the negative control. If immune assay results were provided at multiple time points, vaccines inducing an IFN-γ response at any time point were regarded as immunogenic.
To evaluate the B cell reactivity of each vaccine, all putative neoepitopes were retrieved by sliding ( ). Each putative neoepitope was evaluated by running DeepNeo-BCR to identify BCEs.
To analyze the effect of BCEs at the patient level, we first divided samples into two groups according to the reported immunogenicity of vaccines. Each patient was administered with up to 20 vaccines, and the patient-level response was calculated as the fraction of IFN(+) vaccines. For each sample, the fraction of BCEs or the highest DeepNeo-BCR score was obtained depending on the analysis. The significance of the odds ratio from different analyses was assessed with the Fisher exact test. The correlation between the patient-level B cell epitope score and the fraction of IFN(+) vaccines was measured with the Pearson correlation. The patient-level score was calculated as the highest B cell epitope prediction score of all possible peptides derived from the personalized vaccines. Two samples, namely P04 of Sahin et al.’s study ( ) and P04 of Chen et al.’s study ( ), were taken for example. The B cell epitope scores per vaccine were plotted, and the colors were added to express the immunogenicity of the vaccine. All plots were generated with the ggplot2 R package ( ).
The same analysis was conducted using BCEs as defined by LBtope ( ), LBEEP ( ), IgPred ( ), and BepiPred3.0 ( ). Because BepiPred3.0 ( ) does not provide the prediction for a fixed-length peptide, the mean prediction value of subsets derived from the peptide was used as the representative prediction score. For IgPred ( ), the prediction value of IgG was used. Thresholds of 60 for LBtope ( ) and 0.6 for LBEEP ( ) were used. LBtope ( ) and IgPred ( ) prediction was performed on its web page (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/lbtope/index.php and https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/igpred, respectively).
