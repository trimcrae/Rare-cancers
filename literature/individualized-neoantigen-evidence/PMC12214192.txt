## Abstract
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks among the most prevalent malignant tumors globally. However, current frontline treatment modalities fail to achieve satisfactory therapeutic outcomes. In recent years, T-cell receptor (TCR)-T therapy has garnered increasing attention as a promising therapeutic approach. This review provides an overview of the epidemiology of HCC, evaluates the efficacy of current first-line treatment options, and introduces adoptive cellular therapies (ACTs), with a specific focus on TCR-T therapy. Notably, we categorize existing TCR-T research targeting different antigens for the treatment of HCC, covering both published literature and registered clinical trials. Furthermore, we discuss the limitations of current TCR-T research and applications, while offering insights into future research directions and development prospects. In summary, this review comprehensively examines the research progress on TCR-T therapy for HCC from the perspective of antigen targeting. Although several challenges remain to be addressed, we believe that TCR-T therapy represents a promising approach for the treatment of HCC.

## Introduction
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) stands as one of the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide [ , ], ranking sixth in incidence among all cancers with a mortality rate that ranks third [ . Notably, 70.1% of HCC cases are reported in Asia, with East Asia bearing the highest burden. Mongolia exhibits alarming figures, with incidence rates reaching 26.9 and 8.9 per 100,000 men and women, respectively. While China has the largest number of observed cases due to its large population and high-incidence rate [ – ]. Approximately, 80% of HCC cases in China are attributable to hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. The relative risk of developing HCC increases by 15–20-fold in patients with HBV compared to uninfected individuals [ – ]. In areas with high HBV vaccination rates, the incidence of HCC has declined [ , ].
In Asia, the availability of screening and surveillance programs is limited, with less than 25% of HCC patients diagnosed through monitoring, in over 70% of cases, the disease is detected at an advanced stage [ , ]. In the USA, while the incidence rate of HCC is not notably high, the estimated mortality rates for male and female HCC patients in 2024 rank fifth and seventh, respectively [ ]. The low rate of early diagnosis, along with high rates of recurrence and mortality associated with current treatment modalities, are urgent issues that require immediate attention in the management of HCC.
In this review, we explore the current frontline treatments for HCC, evaluate their efficacy and consider their limitations. Then, we introduce adoptive cellular therapies (ACTs), with a specific focus on T-cell receptor (TCR)-T therapy. In particular, we categorize existing TCR-T researches targeting different antigens for the treatment of HCC, covering published literatures and registered clinical trials. Lastly, we address the ongoing challenges in implementing TCR-T therapy and offer insights into its future potential in HCC treatment.

## The current frontline treatment modalities for HCC
The treatment strategy for HCC patients is currently determined based on tumor burden, liver function, and cancer-related symptoms [ , ]. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) algorithm continues to be the primary staging system for HCC patients. Commonly utilized frontline treatment modalities, which have shown survival benefits in clinical practice, include surgical resection, transplantation, ablation, transarterial chemoembolization, and systemic therapies such as atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, tremelimumab plus durvalumab, lenvatinib, and regorafenib [ , ]. The treatment algorithm for HCC patients from early to advanced stages is shown in Supplementary Figure . Partial research findings regarding these treatment modalities and their efficacy are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.
 Surgical resection is widely recognized as the preferred treatment modality for early-stage HCC patients, while eligibility criteria for resection vary by region. The criteria recommended by European and American guidelines are more restrictive compared to Asian guidelines [ – ]. Therefore, the reported outcomes vary significantly across regions, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 50% in Chinese cohorts (2020–2023) to 70% in European studies (2018–2022) [ , ]. However, the recurrence rates remain high, ranging from 76.5 to 87.09% , , and some patients may require multiple resections.
In theory, liver transplantation represents the optimal treatment choice as it offers the potential to cure both the tumor and liver cirrhosis, providing the strongest survival rates for selected HCC patients. The selection of liver transplant recipients typically relies on strict criteria considering tumor size and quantity . Nevertheless, at the current transplantation rate, less than 10% of the global demand for organ transplants is fulfilled [ ]. Despite these, the recurrence rates of HCC patients post-transplantation still range between 12.8% and 29% [ – ]. Post-transplantation HCC recurrence often manifests as multifocal and invasive, leading to inevitable adverse outcomes [ ].
Local treatment methods encompass image-guided tumor ablation and transcatheter tumor therapy. Image-guided tumor ablation is a widely accepted treatment option for early-stage HCC patients. It offers the advantage of minimal invasiveness [ , ], making it a first-line treatment for small HCCs in patients with impaired liver function or related serious diseases. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is the primary ablative technique, and clinical studies have demonstrated similar survival rates for post-ablation compared to surgical resection [ ]. In patients over 65 years old, the treatment efficacy of RFA for tumors over 3 cm is superior to surgical resection [ ]. While image-guided transcatheter tumor therapy aims to induce tumor necrosis and is based on the arterial vascularization of HCC compared to the surrounding liver parenchyma [ ]. Taking transarterial chemoembolization as an example, the current median survival duration achievable with this treatment approach exceeds 30–40 months [ – ]. Compensated liver function patients with asymptomatic multifocal or large HCC being unsuitable for resection are optimal candidates for transarterial chemoembolization. However, in these local treatment methods, the high recurrence and metastasis rates remain significant threats to HCC patients [ , , ].
For patients diagnosed with advanced HCC or progressing to advanced stages after the failure of other treatments, effective treatment options were lacking. Until 2020, first-line treatment for unresectable HCC (uHCC) was limited to tyrosine kinase inhibitors like sorafenib [ , ], which demonstrated an improvement in overall survival (OS) compared to placebo [ – ]. However, the clinical efficacy of sorafenib is not satisfactory [ , ]. Moreover, the frequent occurrence of adverse events (AEs) such as diarrhea, fatigue, and hand–foot skin reactions with sorafenib often necessitates discontinuation or dose reduction [ , , ]. In recent years, studies have demonstrated that combination regimens based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including STRIDE (tremelimumab administered at regular intervals with durvalumab), atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, and camrelizumab plus rivoceranib, significantly improved OS compared to sorafenib [ – ]. The frequency of treatment-related AEs with STRIDE and durvalumab monotherapy is lower than that with sorafenib [ ]. Despite these efforts, the incidence of AEs still fails to meet satisfactory levels, and the OS duration for HCC patients is still limited [ ].
Current therapeutic approaches are inadequate for meeting the needs of HCC patients. To effectively improve the survival rate of HCC patients and their quality of life, there is an urgent need for more effective treatment modalities.

## The ACTs for treating HCC
For patients with advanced-stage HCC, ICIs represent a crucial therapeutic approach in their treatment. By blocking immune checkpoints such as PD-1/PD-L1, ICIs can relieve tumor immune escape, activate and enhance the immune response, enable the immune system to recognize, and eliminate tumor cells. However, despite some clinical progress, both primary and acquired resistance to ICIs remain common, the treatment outcomes and AEs are still unsatisfactory. Moreover, given the unique immune characteristics of HCC, the effectiveness of ICIs in restoring T-cell function remains suboptimal in clinical practice [ , ].
ACTs, utilizing autologous or allogeneic immune effector cells activated and amplified in vitro to inject into patients, represent another form of immunotherapy. In contrast to ICIs, which target inhibitory T-cell receptors, ACTs rely on the ex vivo expansion of T cells, potentially bypassing certain inhibitory immune regulatory factors. Some forms of ACTs involve genetic modification or reengineering of immune cells, enhancing their specificity. ACTs have the potential to address the limitations of ICIs by increasing the quantity, specificity, and reactivity of immune cells. Currently, there are three primary forms of ACTs: tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T, and TCR-T [ ].
TILs are lymphocytes that have migrated from the bloodstream into the tumor microenvironment. They constitute a heterogeneous population of cells, with broad antigen-recognition capabilities [ ]. Among them, both CD8+ and CD4+ T cell subsets play crucial role in tumor control through various mechanisms. Activated CD8+ T cells can produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and directly kill tumor cells, while CD4+ T cells can promote the generation of antibody-producing plasma cells and facilitate the activation of CD8+ T cells [ , ]. Abundant T cells infiltration in tumor tissues are associated with a favorable prognosis [ – ]. However, the existing TILs can become ineffective due to the inhibitory tumor microenvironment, which is characterized by the presence of suppressive regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [ ]. As a therapeutic strategy, TILs are isolated lymphocytes from the immunosuppressive environment. This process involves collecting and isolating TILs from resected tumor tissues, expanding them ex vivo, and reinfusing them back into the patient's body. This therapy is well tolerated, with most toxicities related to lymphodepleting chemotherapy and the use of IL-2 [ ]. However, it also has several limitations. TILs require a large number of lymphocytes, and some tumor tissues may not yield a sufficient quantity of TILs for therapy. Some patients with rapidly progressing diseases may not have the luxury of waiting for TILs to grow ex vivo. Additionally, TILs may become depleted and exhibit poor cytotoxicity and persistence due to prolonged ex vivo expansion [ ].
CAR is a synthetic molecule composed of an antigen-recognition domain typically derived from extracellular single-chain variable fragments (scFv), an intervening spacer domain, and one or more intracellular signaling domains [ ]. CAR-T therapy utilizes gene transfer technology (retrovirus, lentivirus, non-viral vectors, etc.) to introduce antigen-specific CAR molecules into T cells, thereby endowing them with the precise ability to attack tumors. CARs recognize their targets like antibodies, by conformation-dependent binding, without the need for intracellular antigen processing and presentation through the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) pathway [ ]. This feature renders them unrestricted by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) limitations, but it also brings some drawbacks: the use of CARs is limited to molecules present on the cell surface, covering only about 30% of all known proteins [ , ]. Furthermore, the lack of HLA restriction makes CAR-T cells more susceptible to interference from soluble antigens in peripheral blood. In addition, CAR-T cells are typically ineffective against target cells with low antigen density, so a small population of tumor cells with low antigen density often escapes the effects of CAR-T therapy, leading to relapse [ , ]. Most importantly, the artificially synthesized single-chain structure results in uncontrolled excessive reactions, potentially leading to cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity [ ].
The TCR-T represents a natural extension of TILs while retaining more physiological characteristics compared to CAR-T therapy. TCR is a lineage-defining, membrane-anchored, clonotypic immune receptor that plays a crucial role in ligand-dependent activation of T lymphocytes. Due to the unique rearrangement mechanism of the TCR variable regions during T-cell maturation, the recognition between TCRs and antigens exhibit high specificity and sensitivity [ , ]. Although the process of manufacturing TCR-T is somewhat similar to CAR-T, they exhibit distinct differences. TCRs recognize not only cell surface antigens but also intracellular antigens, thus enabling responses to antigens from the entire cancer cell proteome, including approximately 88% of proteins that are exclusively present in intracellular compartments such as the cytoplasm, nucleus, and mitochondria, significantly expanding the range of actionable immunotherapeutic targets [ ]. In solid tumors, most target antigens are primarily intracellular, providing TCR-T therapy with a significant advantage over CAR-T. However, the use of antigen-specific TCR-T in immunotherapy depends on the specific type and quantity of HLA molecules expressed on target cells, particularly since MHC-like molecules can be downregulated in the context of viral infections and the tumor microenvironment. Despite this, in recent years, an increasing number of researchers have focused on TCR-T in the study of solid tumors, including HCC. It can be considered a promising therapeutic approach for HCC.

## Viral antigens
Viral antigens represent the sole class of exogenous tumor antigens that can be targeted by TCRs. They are not expressed by normal cells but are not restricted to tumor cells either, cells invaded by virus may express viral antigens. Due to their distinct nature, viral antigens are classified separately. Some viral infections have been proven to be associated with the occurrence of tumors, including human papilloma virus (HPV) [ ], hepatitis viruses [ ], and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) [ , ]. In many cases, virus-driven cancers are mediated by the expression of viral oncogenes, which drive cellular transformation and cancer progression [ – ]. And they have become attractive targets for tumor antigen-directed therapies [ ].
For patients with HBV- or HCV-related HCC, antibody-based treatments are unlikely to be effective due to the presence of circulating antigens, such as hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) [ , ], which can interfere with antibody-mediated recognition of infected cells. In contrast, HLA-restricted TCR-T cells do not recognize soluble antigens, allowing them to bind specifically to HCC cells with integrated viral DNA post-infection [ ]. Currently, most TCRs targeting HCC-associated viral antigens focus on HBV and HCV.

## HBV-specific antigens
Through in-depth analysis of T cells isolated from human HCC tumor tissues, researchers have identified the presence of distinct subsets of HBV-specific CD8+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells, which are associated with prolonged recurrence-free survival (RFS) [ ], provides robust evidence for research on HBV-specific TCR-T cells. HBV-specific antigens are the most studied types in virus-related HCC. They can be further classified according to the specific regions they target.
HBsAg, especially HBs183, has been extensively studied. Gehring et al. demonstrated that engineered T cells expressing HBs183-specific TCR through lentivirus-mediated transduction can recognize HBV-infected cells and HCC tumor cells expressing viral antigens derived from naturally integrated HBV DNA [ ]. The immunotherapy of redirected T cells generated through retroviral transduction tends to favor the introduction of stable TCR-T cells. These cells exhibit long-term persistence and yield high treatment efficacy [ , ]. However, the introduction of stable expressing TCRs may lead to chronic autoimmune reactions due to chain error matrix reactivity [ ]. In cases of HCC, long-term immunization against HBV-infected hepatocytes might be counterproductive, potentially inducing or exacerbating hepatitis. Comparative study demonstrated that mRNA electroporation achieves higher 24-h expression level of HBs183-specific TCR compared to retroviral transduction [ ]. This non-integrating approach eliminates insertional mutagenesis risks associated with viral vectors [ – ], thereby reducing the possibility of exogenous antigens introduction and subsequent autoimmune responses. While therapeutic efficacy remains comparable between both methods [ ], the transient nature of mRNA-mediated expression necessitates repeated administrations to maintain optimal therapeutic outcomes.
Considering the impact of immunosuppressive drugs on TCR-T cells post liver transplantation, researchers have developed tacrolimus (TAC) and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) resistant HBs183-specific TCR-T cells, which can serve as a potential anti-recurrence strategy [ ]. Recognizing the limited tumor infiltration of conventional CD4+/CD8+ T cells, another research group addressed the challenge by engineering mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells to express HBs183-specific TCR. This alternative approach leveraged MAIT cells' natural liver tropism, resulting in engineered cells with enhanced polyfunctionality, improved HBV antigen sensitivity, and preserved liver-homing chemokine receptor expression compared to traditional CD4+/CD8+ T-cell platforms [ ].
Furthermore, researchers have designed TCRs with enhanced affinity targeting HBs370-379, which exhibit a certain degree of universality and can recognize corresponding partial derivative antigens. Preclinical studies using xenograft mouse models have shown remarkable results, with the full-dose treatment group achieving complete tumor elimination for 28 days, and no recurrence observed during an additional 17 days of monitoring [ ].
Despite the therapeutic potential of HBV-specific TCR-T cells, their clinical application requires careful consideration due to the risk of on-target, off-tumor effects against HBV-infected hepatocytes [ ]. Clinical study of a post-transplantation HBV-HCC patient with chemoresistant metastases, TCR-T cell infusion reduced HBsAg levels without inducing hepatic inflammation or significant toxicity [ , ]. A phase I trial (NCT03899415) involving 10 postoperative HBV-HCC patients (including transplant recipients, hepatectomy, and RFA patients) demonstrated reduced/stabilized HBV DNA levels, with three patients showing tumor destruction or disease stabilization. The cohort achieved a median OS of 33.1 months with no severe hepatotoxicity [ – ]. These findings support TCR-T cell safety in adjuvant settings and suggest efficacy beyond transplantation recipients. However, recent data from a larger cohort study of SGC101 (NCT05417932) in 46 advanced HBV-HCC patients revealed a 21.7% tumor reduction rate but a 13% incidence of grade 3 CRS, underscoring the need for dose optimization in future clinical applications [ ].
As for the research on TCRs targeting hepatitis B core antigen (HBcAg), researchers have demonstrated the natural presence of HBc-specific TCR-T cells in patient TRMs [ ]. However, in another study, TCRs targeting HBc18-27 showed no cytotoxicity against HLA-A*02 ± cell lines [ ], limiting further investigation. Nevertheless, researchers administered mRNA-electroporated HBc-specific TCR-T cells in 6 HBV-positive patients with recurrent HCC post-transplantation (NCT02719782), and no severe AEs occurred, underscoring the favorable tolerability [ ].
There has been no published research on TCRs targeting hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) in basic research so far. However, a clinical study was conducted on two patients with metastatic HBV-HCC. After the infusion of HBe-specific TCR-T cells, no AEs affecting liver function were observed. The study also demonstrated decreases in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, one patient experienced significant reductions in five out of six pulmonary metastases. Additionally, there is evidence that this treatment partially restores the patient’s immune response. It promotes the proliferation and division of CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood and enhances viral clearance capacity [ ].

## HCV-specific antigens
There have been some studies targeting HCV-specific antigens. One study verified that both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells transduced with HCV-specific TCRs recognized cells loaded with the HCV NS3:1073–1081 peptide [ ]. Another study confirmed that engineered HCV-specific TCR-T cells may represent a feasible immunotherapy approach for the treatment of HCV-associated malignancies [ ].
However, it is important to note that HCV can now be cured through antiviral drug treatment, and the integration of HCV into hepatocytes is not as widespread as in some other chronic viral infections. As a result, the research on TCR-T therapies targeting HCV-specific antigens remains relatively limited.

## Host antigens
TCR-T cells targeting host antigens in HCC research fall into three categories. Tumor-specific antigens (TSAs), which arise from somatic mutations and are exclusively expressed in tumor cells, present minimal off-target toxicity risks. Tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) are expressed in both malignant and certain normal tissues, encompassing differentiation antigens shared with specific differentiated normal cells, and overexpressed antigens that demonstrate significantly elevated expression levels in tumor cells. Cancer-germline antigens (CGAs) are uniquely expressed in tumor cells while being physiologically restricted to immune-privileged germ cells, thereby minimizing off-target risks due to the absence of HLA class I expression in normal germline tissues , [ – ].

## TSAs
TSAs have gained substantial research interest due to their precise tumor-targeting capabilities and reduced treatment-related toxicity. Neoantigens, a subset of TSAs, exhibit remarkable tumor specificity and potent immunogenicity [ , ]. Clinical observations reveal that patients demonstrating favorable responses to TIL-based therapies typically harbor accumulated genetic mutations that elicit T-cell-mediated immunity against neoantigens [ – ]. Although the patient-specific nature of neoantigens limits their utility as universal targets for cancer vaccines, researchers have successfully identified specific TCRs capable of recognizing these individualized neoantigens [ ].
While neoantigens are typically patient-specific, recent advances have identified shared mutations across individuals, expanding their therapeutic potential. The most studied mutations in TCR-T therapy are the RAS gene family (including KRAS, HRAS, and NRAS), which are found in approximately 25% of cancer patients [ ]. Published researches on HCC have focused on KRAS G12V [ ], [ ] and G12D [ ], with TCR-T cells targeting these antigens demonstrating strong affinity and specificity [ ] in vitro and reducing tumor volume in mouse models [ ]. Moreover, researchers have conducted in-depth validation of their recognition and binding through crystal structures [ ].
Despite their theoretical promise and supportive in vitro data, the lack of robust clinical evidence significantly limits their immediate translation into clinical practice, neoantigen-targeted therapies remain in early developmental stages.

## TAAs
TCR-T therapies targeting TAAs, particularly overexpressed antigens like AFP and GPC-3, have shown promise in HCC treatment [ ].
AFP, a fetal liver protein re-expressed in 50–80% of HCC cases, serves as both a diagnostic marker and therapeutic target [ ]. Several TCR-T therapies targeting AFP and its derivatives have been explored, predominantly focusing on AFP158. Preclinical investigations of AFP-specific TCR-T cells have demonstrated robust tumor cytotoxicity in murine models, with multiple studies confirming their safety profile through careful optimization of antigen selection and expression regulation [ – ]. Recent mechanistic studies have revealed innovative combination strategies: c-Jun co-delivery enhances T-cell activation and antitumor efficacy [ ], while arsenic trioxide combination therapy induces ferroptosis susceptibility in tumor cells [ ]. These findings have spurred clinical translation, with several ongoing trials systematically evaluating AFP-targeted TCR-T therapies, detailed in Table 4.
GPC3, an oncofetal protein absent in healthy hepatocytes but expressed in HCC, correlates with poor prognosis [ – ]. Cellular immunotherapy targeting GPC3 has predominantly been studied in CAR-T cell researches. In vitro experiments have demonstrated specific cytotoxic effects, while in vivo infusion of GPC3-specific CAR-T cells in mouse models of Huh-7 xenografts significantly prolong the OS [ , ]. Investigations into GPC3-specific TCR-T therapies have also yielded promising results, successfully generating TCR-T cells capable of effectively killing tumor cells and demonstrating tumor growth inhibition in murine models [ ].

## CGAs
CGAs have emerged as a critical focus in TCR-based immunotherapy, with NY-ESO-1 standing out as one of the most extensively investigated targets due to its distinctive expression pattern and clinical relevance. NY-ESO-1 demonstrates elevated expression in 20–40% of liver cancer cases and other solid tumors [ – ], provides a favorable therapeutic window. Clinical trials have consistently demonstrated NY-ESO-1’s capacity to elicit specific T-cell responses, coupled with an excellent safety profile [ ].
However, the magnitude of these immune responses often proves insufficient for complete tumor eradication. To enhance therapeutic efficacy, clinical investigations have explored high-dose adoptive transfer of NY-ESO-1-specific TCR-T cells, demonstrating the capacity to generate robust antitumor responses and achieve tumor elimination in selected cases. Current research strategies focus on developing multi-antigen targeting TCRs to address tumor heterogeneity and enhance response breadth. Additionally, combination approaches integrating TCR-T therapy with ICIs are being actively investigated to overcome tumor immune evasion mechanisms. These innovative strategies leverage NY-ESO-1’s inherent safety profile while potentially overcoming the limitations of single-target approaches [ – ].

## Challenges and future perspectives
Significant progress has been made in reducing HCC incidence through effective control of risk factors like HBV. However, mortality remains high due to limitations in early diagnosis and treatment. TCR-T therapy, which targets tumor cells, reverses immune exhaustion, and shows favorable safety, has emerged as a promising breakthrough in HCC treatment. While notable achievements have been made in both preclinical and clinical studies, significant challenges must be addressed before TCR-T therapy can be widely adopted.

## HLA mismatch: application limitations in high-incidence regions
Currently, most studied TCR-T cells are derived from HLA-A*02:01 antigen libraries. However, this allele is not the most prevalent in East Asia, where HCC incidence is highest. For instance, in China, HLA-A*11:01 and A*24:02 are the dominant alleles [ ]. This mismatch between the HLA-A*02:01-based TCR-T designs and the HLA distribution in high-incidence regions poses a significant challenge to the broad application of TCR-T therapy. As a result, targeted antigens and TCRs optimized for HLA-A*02:01 may not be effective for individuals carrying other prevalent HLA alleles in these areas. To address this challenge, efforts should be made to actively establish antigen libraries based on the HLA genotypes prevalent in high-incidence regions. Future research should focus on identifying and developing TCR-T cells targeting antigens that are compatible with a broader range of HLA alleles. Additionally, exploring universal TCR strategies or combining multiple HLA-restricted TCRs could offer a more inclusive approach, enhancing the applicability of TCR-T therapy across diverse genetic backgrounds. These advancements could significantly expand the clinical utility of TCR-T cells for HCC patients worldwide.

## Antigen identification dilemma: personalized or universal
Currently, the targeted antigens studied in the field of TCR therapy for HCC are still relatively limited. Next-generation sequencing technologies enable high-throughput detection of antigen sequences, significantly advancing the construction of antigen libraries. Recent research on TSAs has focused on two main strategies. The first is a personalized approach, which involves analyzing individual genomes to identify patient-specific neoantigens for therapy. Current studies often compare antigen libraries from patients and healthy cohorts [ ]. Research has shown that patients with chronic HBV infection exhibit reduced TCR richness and diversity compared to those with acute infection, likely due to immune exhaustion [ , ]. Establishing longitudinal antigen libraries tracking disease progression in individual patients could help identify more precise and personalized targets. However, this approach demands substantial resources, making it challenging for large-scale clinical application in the near term.
The second strategy aims to identify common mutation types. Although mutations are typically patient-specific, certain sites and types show consistent patterns. For instance, HBV integration sites and RAS family neoantigens have been targeted in TCR-T therapy for HCC. By screening antigen libraries from relevant populations and selecting prevalent mutations, TCR-T products can be designed with greater convenience and feasibility while maintaining specificity and safety. Other common mutations associated with HCC, such as TP53, PIK3CA, CTNNB1, AXIN1, TERT, and ARID1A, also hold potential as therapeutic targets. For example, TP53 mutations, found in 40–50% of cancer patients, including HCC, influence genomic instability, proliferation, and metastasis [ – ]. Structural studies reveal that TCRs targeting the TP53 R175H mutation exhibit 60-fold higher binding affinity than wild-type , and in vitro experiments confirm its immunogenicity [ ]. Engineered TCR-T targeting this mutation has shown sustained objective responses in refractory breast cancer patients, with effects lasting up to 6 months [ , ]. Similarly, TCR-T targeting PIK3CA has demonstrated antitumor effects in breast cancer [ – ]. However, these therapies have not yet been explored in HCC, and their safety and efficacy in vivo remain under investigation. For other common mutations, TCR-T research is even more limited. Key questions remain: Are these mutations hindered by low transcription or translation efficiency in cancer cells? Is the immunogenicity of the resulting antigen peptides insufficient? How can we better study and utilize these common mutations? Addressing these challenges is critical for advancing TCR-T therapy in HCC.

## Enhancing affinity: the utilization of this double-edged sword
Despite the superior safety profile of TCR-T therapy [ ], which stems from its compatibility with physiological signaling pathways and diminished potential for T-cell hyperactivation, the occurrence of AEs has been reported in clinical investigations. For example, TCR-T targeting AFP (NCT03132792) was halted because of off-target toxicity, highlighting the need for improved TCR screening platforms to minimize cross-reactivity. Similarly, TCR-T targeting NY-ESO-1 (NCT01967823) achieved a complete response rate of less than 10%, likely due to a combination of factors, including epitope loss, and potentially insufficient TCR affinity. These challenges have limited the broader application of TCR-T therapy.
To address these issues, researchers have developed in vitro simulation experiments and antigen screening platforms to reduce cross-reactions and identify highly reactive antigen peptides while minimizing AEs [ , ]. In vitro studies have demonstrated that TCRs exhibit remarkable specificity and sensitivity, enabling antigen recognition even at low peptide concentrations. However, in cases like HBV-HCC, reduced viral antigen expression can lead to tumor immune evasion. To overcome this, strategies such as increasing TCR-T infusion doses within safe limits and enhancing TCR affinity for target antigens have been explored. For instance, researchers have engineered TCRs with affinity increases exceeding one million-fold compared to baseline TCRs [ , ]. In summary, optimizing TCR affinity and infusion doses, while addressing challenges such as antigen expression variability and epitope loss, is key to advancing TCR-T therapy for HCC.

## Differentiation and homing properties: limitations or opportunities
The therapeutic efficacy of TCR-T cells in vivo is influenced by both their differentiation type and homing capacity. Among the most widely studied are TCR αβ T cells, which offer high specificity by targeting MHC-presented antigens. However, their application is constrained by HLA compatibility requirements and the risk of CRS, which can compromise therapeutic outcomes. In contrast, γδ T cells and NK T cells have gained attention for their ability to recognize tumor cells independently of MHC, enabling broader tumor targeting. Despite this advantage, their high functional heterogeneity and poor persistence in vivo have limited their clinical potential [ , ].
Beyond these cell types, researchers are exploring T cells with enhanced homing capabilities, particularly for targeting specific tissues like the liver. For example, MAIT cells, which naturally accumulate in the liver, have demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy in vitro compared to conventional T cells, leveraging their intrinsic homing properties [ ]. Similarly, TRMs show promise due to their ability to persist at tumor sites, though their expansion in vitro remains a significant challenge [ , ]. Another promising subset is stem cell-like memory T cells (Tscm), which exhibit robust self-renewal and sustained antitumor activity in preclinical models [ , ]. However, their full therapeutic potential requires further research and clinical validation.

## Beyond end-stage treatment: broader possibilities
Currently, the clinical application criteria for TCR-T therapy remain poorly defined. Most clinical studies have focused on advanced HCC, particularly HBV-associated HCC, as well as cases of recurrence or metastasis following liver transplantation or resection. In these settings, TCR-T therapy is primarily used as a last-line treatment when conventional therapies fail. However, research on its efficacy in earlier disease stages, such as acute or chronic hepatitis, or as an adjuvant therapy in the early post-transplant or post-resection period, is limited. Furthermore, comparative studies evaluating TCR-T therapy against traditional treatment modalities in these contexts are scarce. Addressing these gaps is critical to establishing a robust foundation for defining the future indications and optimizing the clinical use of TCR-T therapy.
In the context of liver transplantation, TCR-T therapy shows potential for reducing recurrence. However, its efficacy may be compromised by the concurrent use of immunosuppressive therapy, which is essential to prevent transplant rejection. This creates a significant challenge in balancing immunosuppression with optimal TCR-T function. Researchers have explored this issue by studying the effects of clinically relevant concentrations of immunosuppressive agents on TCR-T activity. For example, in vitro experiments with TAC and MMF demonstrated that their suppressive effects on multifunctional TCR-T cells are reversible. This reversal typically occurs within 3–5 days, providing valuable insights for optimizing the clinical application of TCR-T therapy in post-transplant settings .

## Multimodal combination therapies: benefits or risks
Currently, the majority of TCR-T therapy models remain focused on single TCR-T infusion. The potential benefits of combining TCR-T therapy with other immunotherapies to enhance treatment efficacy, as well as whether such combinations yield more advantages than disadvantages, remains an area of active investigation. In this context, we will discuss and analyze the available studies on this topic.
Theoretically, combining PD-1 inhibitors with TCR-T therapy may enhance antitumor efficacy by reversing T cell exhaustion through blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, thereby improving the persistence and cytotoxic capacity of TCR-T cells. Preclinical studies in murine models have demonstrated that co-administration of PD-1 inhibitors with adoptive cell therapies, including TCR-T and CAR-T, can amplify T-cell-mediated tumor clearance, prolong functional durability, and reduce the proportion of immunosuppressive cells within the tumor microenvironment [ – ]. However, a clinical trial evaluating PD-1 blockade combined with NY-ESO-1-specific TCR-T cells and peptide-pulsed DC vaccines reported transient enhancement of antitumor activity and a lower frequency of terminally differentiated T cells compared to monotherapy. Nevertheless, disease progression eventually occurred, potentially mediated by antigen loss escape mechanisms [ ]. Another clinical study investigating PD-1 inhibitor-TCR-T combinations (NCT03970382) was suspended due to safety concerns, with two participants experiencing severe AEs of unclear causality [ ].
Notably, small-molecule drugs such as arsenic trioxide (ATO) may offer complementary mechanisms to augment TCR-T therapy. ATO has been shown to enhance TCR-T efficacy by inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells, thereby promoting antigen release and immune activation [ ].
Tumor vaccines activate the host immune system by delivering tumor antigens, such as neoantigens or viral antigens, and naturally complement TCR-T therapy. The GPC3 peptide vaccine has demonstrated safety in advanced HCC patients and induces antigen-specific T-cell responses, suggesting its potential for combination with GPC3-targeted TCR-T therapy. It induced antigen-specific T-cell responses in advanced HCC patients, however, the objective response rate was below 10% [ ]. Furthermore, personalized neoantigen vaccines, such as mRNA-4157, have entered Phase III clinical trials in solid tumors (NCT03897881), and their combination with TCR-T therapy holds promise for overcoming the immunotolerant microenvironment [ ]. Future studies should focus on exploring the temporal relationship between vaccine-induced antigen presentation and TCR-T-targeted killing, and optimizing combination strategies to maximize therapeutic efficacy.
Some researchers have proposed the simultaneous targeting of surface and intracellular antigens by combining CAR-T and TCR-T therapies, leveraging the unique strengths of each approach: CAR-T cells recognize membrane-bound antigens, while TCR-T cells target intracellular antigen-derived peptides presented by MHC molecules [ ]. However, this dual-targeting strategy remains largely theoretical, with almost no experimental studies to date exploring its feasibility or efficacy. Significant challenges, including the coordination of cell manufacturing processes, optimization of infusion protocols, and potential synergistic toxicities, must be systematically addressed before clinical translation. Further research is essential to carefully design and validate this approach, moving it from conceptual speculation to practical application.

## Operational complexity and high costs: improving accessibility
Despite significant advancements in TCR-T research over recent years, including improvements in screening platforms and operational techniques, the development process remains highly complex. Currently, the mainstream screening approach involves identifying meaningful mutations or aberrantly expressed antigenic peptides by comparing sequencing data (e.g., whole-exome sequencing [WES] or RNA-seq) from individual patients or patient cohorts with healthy controls. TCRs can then be obtained through two primary pathways: isolation from TILs or generation from PBMCs. The TIL-based approach involves isolating tumor-specific T cells directly from patient tumor tissues, followed by stimulation and sorting to identify antigen-specific T cells. However, this method faces challenges such as the difficulty of obtaining sufficient tumor tissue and the potential functional impairment of TCRs due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. On the other hand, the PBMC-based approach involves co-culturing PBMCs from patients or healthy donors with matching genotypes alongside target peptides to expand and sort antigen-specific T cells, as shown in Fig.  . While PBMCs are more accessible than tumor tissues, this method places higher demands on the immunogenicity of the target peptides. If the peptides exhibit weak immunogenicity, they may fail to stimulate the expansion of pre-existing antigen-specific T cells or induce the generation of new ones, resulting in a low yield or even absence of antigen-specific T cells. This, in turn, significantly reduces the likelihood of obtaining TCRs with specific recognition and cytotoxic capabilities through VDJ sequencing. Furthermore, TCRs are heterodimeric structures, and sequencing yields separate sequences and proportions for the α and β chains. Accurately pairing these chains remains a significant challenge, as current bioinformatic prediction methods lack sufficient reliability. Even after successfully completing these steps, the expression of TCRs in T cells via lentiviral transduction or electroporation presents additional hurdles. Due to the relatively large molecular size of TCRs, achieving successful expression is inherently difficult, and even when the TCR-encoding sequences are successfully introduced, the proportion of T cells expressing the TCRs remains low. Finally, the challenges do not end with the generation of TCR-T cells. Validating their antitumor functionality and ensuring their safety in vivo require extensive additional work, with each step carrying the risk of nullifying all prior efforts.
Certainly, the exorbitant cost of TCR-T therapy poses a significant barrier to its widespread adoption and advancement. The intricate and labor-intensive process of developing TCR-T cells demands extensive human expertise, specialized equipment, and costly reagents. Each step, including high-throughput sequencing, peptide synthesis, T-cell isolation, and lentiviral transduction, contributes to the overall expense. Moreover, the low efficiency of TCR expression and the need for rigorous quality control further escalate production costs, often resulting in a prohibitively high price tag for therapeutic TCR-T cells. As a result, TCR-T therapy is frequently deprioritized in favor of more cost-effective treatments, particularly in resource-limited settings. To address these challenges, there is an urgent need to develop more efficient and scalable manufacturing methods, such as optimized gene delivery techniques, and standardized quality assessment protocols. Only through improved accessibility can this innovative treatment modality fulfill its promise as a transformative approach in cancer immunotherapy.

## Conclusions
This article reviews the current therapeutic landscape of HCC, introduces the theoretical basis of ACTs, with a specific focus on TCR-T therapy. Additionally, it summarizes and discusses existing preclinical and clinical studies on TCR-T therapy targeting various antigen specificities for HCC. It is evident that TCR-T therapy for HCC possesses specificity, tumor cell killing capability, and safety, with promising results observed in clinical patients. However, the limited researches available for large-scale clinical application of TCR-T therapy underscores the need for further investigation and resolution of numerous outstanding questions. Nonetheless, we believe that TCR-T therapy holds significant promise for further exploration in the treatment of HCC.
