## Abstract
The majority of patients who are diagnosed with cutaneous melanoma are candidates for surgical resection and thus curable from their disease. However, the risk for a recurrence is high for many patients, including those with lymph node-negative melanoma, thus necessitating additional therapies beyond surgery. With the advent of anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-based immunotherapies, which are vastly more effective compared to previous standard-of-care treatments in the advanced setting, the landscape of adjuvant therapy has fundamentally changed in recent years. Anti-PD-1-based immune checkpoint inhibition therapy is now the standard of care for many patients with stage IIB or higher melanoma. Neoadjuvant approaches have demonstrated superior outcomes compared to adjuvant-alone therapy. However, a number of questions remain including treatment combinations such as combined anti-PD-1 + lymphocyte activation gene-3, optimal sequencing of therapies, and the use of predictive markers to further improve outcomes for patients with high-risk melanoma.
Key words: melanoma, PD-1 inhibition, T cells, adjuvant, neoadjuvant, immunotherapy

## Highlights
•The immunotherapeutic treatment landscape for patients with high-risk melanoma has increased in complexity at a rapid pace.
•Neoadjuvant immunotherapy has an increasingly important role in patients with high-risk surgically resectable melanoma.
•Identifying the most effective therapeutic regimen in the adjuvant setting will require additional randomized studies.
•Novel checkpoint inhibitors, personalized vaccines, and better predictive tools are on the horizon.

## Introduction and historical perspective
Most patients with early-stage melanoma are treated effectively with surgical resection. However, for patients with higher-risk disease, limited systemic treatment options with the intent to improve outcomes from surgery alone were available in the past. Historically, interferon-α was the only US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved therapy for adjuvant treatment of patients with high-risk resected stage III melanoma. , The robust antitumor activity of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), initially demonstrated in patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma, led to their testing in the adjuvant setting. Ipilimumab was the first ICI studied in patients with stage III melanoma in a randomized phase III trial (EORTC18071). The trial showed superior recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with placebo, , resulting in its approval by the US FDA in 2015. However, given its high toxicity, ipilimumab monotherapy was not widely adopted in the adjuvant setting and has been replaced by anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-based regimens, which we discuss and analyze here.

## Adjuvant immunotherapy for stage III or IV melanoma

## Nivolumab versus ipilimumab (CheckMate 238)
A randomized phase III study compared nivolumab and ipilimumab in 906 patients with resected stage IIIB, IIIC, or IV melanoma. Based on initial results demonstrating a 12-month RFS of 70.5% in the nivolumab group and 60.8% in the ipilimumab group [hazard ratio (HR) 0.65], nivolumab was approved by the US FDA in 2017. In the 5-year update, with a median follow-up of about 61 months, the 5-year RFS was 50% in patients receiving nivolumab and 39% in those receiving ipilimumab (HR 0.72); the 5-year OS was not different between the two groups ( ). An explanation for the lack of OS difference between the two arms could be the robust antitumor activity of anti-PD-1-based therapy in patients with advanced melanoma: patients with anti-PD-1-sensitive melanoma who were randomized to ipilimumab and experience unresectable melanoma recurrence could still achieve long-lasting disease control.
 

## Pembrolizumab versus placebo (EORTC 1325/KEYNOTE-054)
In another phase III trial, 1019 patients with resected stage III melanoma were randomized to receive pembrolizumab or placebo for 1 year. RFS was found to be superior in patients who receive pembrolizumab compared to patients who received placebo (12-month RFS 75.4% versus 61.0%; HR 0.57) leading to US FDA approval in 2019. In the 5-year update, the superior RFS in the pembrolizumab cohort was maintained [55.4% compared to 38.3% with placebo (HR 0.61)]. Notably, patients with American Joint Committee on Cancer seventh edition stage IIIA with lymph node metastases >1 mm were included in this study and a trend in prolonged RFS in patients who received pembrolizumab (HR 0.66) was observed. OS from the study has not been reported to date.

## Pembrolizumab versus interferon-α versus ipilimumab (SWOG 1404)
In the intergroup trial SWOG 1404, patients with surgically resected stage III melanoma were randomly assigned to receive adjuvant pembrolizumab versus therapies that had previously been standard-of-care systemic adjuvant therapies. At a median follow-up of 47.5 months, patients in the pembrolizumab arm had a longer RFS compared to patients who received either interferon-α or ipilimumab ; however, no OS improvement was observed. The lack of OS benefit may be due to limited OS events (15% in each arm) in the analysis, and about 47% of patients in the control arm went on to receive anti-PD-1-based therapy at time of recurrence. The higher efficacy and more beneficial toxicity profile observed in this study further support the role of pembrolizumab in the adjuvant setting compared to ipilimumab and interferon-α.

## Ipilimumab plus nivolumab (IMMUNED)
Combined treatment with ipilimumab plus nivolumab is widely considered the most effective therapy in the first-line setting for patients with metastatic melanoma, providing a rationale to test this regimen in the adjuvant setting. IMMUNED is a three-arm, randomized phase II trial comparing adjuvant ipilimumab 3 mg/kg plus nivolumab 1 mg/kg versus nivolumab versus placebo in 167 patients with resected stage IV melanoma. , Patients were randomized 1 : 1 : 1 and stratified by tumor programmed death-ligand 1 status. Patients treated in the combination arm had a substantially prolonged RFS compared to patients who received placebo (HR 0.25), indicating that this regimen might be an attractive adjuvant treatment option for patients with resected stage IV melanoma. Caveats are the relatively small size of the trial and the potential heterogeneous population of patients with resected stage IV disease. The data interestingly contrast with the lack of RFS benefit seen in the Checkmate 915 study, in which 1833 patients with resected stage III/IV melanoma were randomized to receive ipilimumab at 1 mg/kg (every 6 weeks) and nivolumab 240 mg (every 2 weeks) or nivolumab alone. Notably, the substantially lower dose of ipilimumab utilized in Checkmate 915 may account for the drastically different results between the two trials.

## Overall survival benefit with PD-1 inhibition in stage III melanoma?
Given the lack of OS data from the only adjuvant phase III study that directly compared PD-1 inhibition to placebo (Keynote-054), indirect treatment comparison studies were conducted to address this question. In one such effort, intention-to-treat population data from CheckMate 238 and EORTC 18071 were analyzed (each study with ∼4 years of follow-up) and nivolumab was found to confer both prolonged RFS and OS. , The OS improvement of ipilimumab over placebo in EORTC18071 and the RFS prolongation of nivolumab compared to ipilimumab in CheckMate 238 further support the hypothesis that nivolumab may result in superior OS over placebo despite the absence of direct evidence in direct comparison to date.

## Adjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy for patients with stage II melanoma
In two separate placebo-controlled, randomized, phase III trials (Keynote 716, Checkmate 76K), pembrolizumab and nivolumab were also tested in patients with high-risk stage II melanoma. Of note, stage IIB/C melanoma is associated with a 5-year recurrence risk of 35%-43% and a 5-year melanoma-specific survival of 77%-87%—a risk profile that is comparable to stage IIIA/B disease. 

## Pembrolizumab (Keynote 716) and nivolumab (CheckMate 76)
In KEYNOTE-716, 976 patients with resected stage IIB and IIC melanoma were randomized 1 : 1 to receive either pembrolizumab or placebo for up to 1 year. At a median follow-up of about 14 months and 15 months at the first and second interim analyses, absolute differences in RFS of 6% and 9% and HR of 0.65 and 0.61 for RFS were seen, respectively. , In the Checkmate 76K study, 790 patients with resected stage IIB or IIC melanoma were randomized 2 : 1 to receive up to 1 year of adjuvant nivolumab. At a median follow-up of 16 months, the 12 months RFS was 89% in patients who were treated with nivolumab versus 79% in patients who received placebo (HR 0.42). This superior outcome associated with nivolumab was seen across pre-specified patient subgroups including T category and stage. Based on the results from these two studies, both pembrolizumab and nivolumab were approved by the FDA and the European Medicines Agency for the treatment of patients with stage IIB/C melanoma.

## Adjuvant therapy: Considerations for clinical practice
Although the FDA has approved adjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy for patients with all stage III, limited data are available for the sub-stage IIIA. Patients with stage IIIA disease were not included in CheckMate 238 and only about 80 patients with IIIA disease with a sentinel lymph node tumor deposit of at least 1 mm were included in KEYNOTE 054 in the pembrolizumab and placebo groups, respectively. In a subgroup analysis, the HR for RFS was 0.38 (0.11-1.31); although for stage IIIA patients the RFS was numerically higher with anti-PD-1 in the adjuvant setting, the difference did not reach statistical significance. Moreover, patients with <1 mm lymph node involvement were not included in these trials.
An important consideration for discussion with patients in clinical practice is the lack of direct evidence from randomized prospective trials that prolonged RFS achieved with adjuvant PD-1 inhibition translates into an OS benefit—with evidence of improved anti-PD-1-mediated OS benefit currently being limited to indirect treatment comparisons. Given the risk of immune-related toxicities, including occasional long-term morbidity, and the lack of a directly demonstrated OS benefit, physicians should carefully weigh the risk and benefits of these therapies for each patient under consideration for adjuvant therapy, particularly for patients with stage IIIA melanoma (who have high RFS rates even without adjuvant therapy). It should be clear to patients (and providers) that the efficacy of adjuvant therapy cannot be reliably assessed in individual patients given that there is no measurable disease to follow, and a specific biomarker to identify benefits from adjuvant therapy is lacking. The absolute risk reduction of recurrence with adjuvant pembrolizumab when compared to placebo is about 15% for patients with stage IIIB disease and 18% for patients with stage IIIC disease, respectively. This benefit may seem modest for some patients, especially when taking into consideration an individual patient’s comorbidities, social support, and potential uncertainty to recognize and address immune-related toxicities quickly. To date, no reliable biomarkers are available in the clinic that could allow more accurate prediction of risk or expected benefit from adjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy. In an effort to maximize clinical efficacy and minimize toxicity, biomarker-informed (‘personalized’) patient selection and tailoring of the most suitable treatment agents and dosing schedules are currently explored in clinical trials. The array of biomarkers under investigation is broad including genomic markers derived from blood and tumor such as circulating tumor DNA and tumor mutations, immune cells, cytokines, chemokines, cell surface expression molecules, and many others. Given the consistent availability of post-treatment surgical tumor tissue, the neoadjuvant setting is a particularly fruitful testing ground for such efforts.

## Neoadjuvant therapy for resectable melanoma
Long established in other solid tumor settings, the clinical investigation of neoadjuvant therapy for resectable high-risk melanoma has gained considerable momentum in recent years. It is worthwhile to note that before nivolumab and pembrolizumab were demonstrated to be effective in the adjuvant setting of melanoma, there was initially some hesitation in the field whether PD-1-directed therapies could be effective in the absence of a ‘physical’ tumor microenvironment that results in the interaction between the endogenous tumor-specific T-cell response and other tumor resident cell populations. Moreover, for optimal priming and activation of a melanoma-specific antitumor T-cell response, tumor antigens in sufficient quantity may be necessary. , Consequently, the presence of a tumor at the time of treatment initiation provides a strong rationale for neoadjuvant therapy and thus improve efficacy over post-surgical adjuvant therapy alone. From a clinical and therapeutic perspective, another advantage of neoadjuvant therapy is the prognostic information provided by the pathological assessment of the post-treatment surgical tumor. 

## Neoadjuvant combination ICI

## OPACIN-NEO
In a randomized phase II trial, three neoadjuvant dosing schedules for ipilimumab plus nivolumab were assessed in patients with resectable stage III disease ( ). Ipilimumab 1 mg/kg and nivolumab 3 mg/kg was identified as the best-tolerated dosing combination and resulted in at least equivalent radiologic and pathologic response rate compared to the more toxic regimens. The 2-year RFS was 84% for the total population, 97% for patients with pathologic response, and 36% for patients without pathologic response (P < 0.001). Notably, patients did not receive adjuvant therapy after surgical resection in this trial.

## PRADO
In a subsequent study, 99 patients with resectable stage III melanoma with lymph node-only disease (no in-transit metastases were allowed) received two cycles of neoadjuvant treatment with nivolumab 3 mg/kg + ipilimumab 1 mg/kg. All patients then underwent surgical biopsy of an ‘index lymph node’ (ILN) and were assigned to different treatment groups based on the pathological response to neoadjuvant therapy observed in the ILN ( ). The rate of major pathologic response (MPR) was 61%. The majority of patients with MPR (59 of 60 patients) did not undergo a completion lymph node dissection, resulting in superior quality of life and the overall and distant recurrence rates at 24 months were low. However, the recurrence rate of patients with pathologic partial response or pathologic non-response was between 24% and 36% at 3 years.

## SWOG S1801
SWOG S1801 is the first randomized trial reported comparing neoadjuvant + adjuvant versus adjuvant alone in patients with high-risk melanoma ( ). The primary endpoint of this phase II study was event-free survival. Two-year event-free survival was 72% with neoadjuvant pembrolizumab compared to 49% with adjuvant therapy (HR 0.58). In an exploratory analysis of surgical tissue samples from patients, 105 patients treated on the perioperative arm (out of 135 patients treated overall, 78%), the pathologic complete response and MPR were 38% and 53%, respectively. 

## Perspective and remaining questions in the field
Anti-PD-1 inhibition has been firmly established as therapy for patients with resected stage III or IV melanoma. However, only about one in five patients benefit when PD-1-directed monoclonal antibodies are given as monotherapy in the adjuvant setting and an OS benefit has not been established with this therapy to date. The striking observation from SWOG S1801 that simply shifting the year-long course pembrolizumab ahead in time by three doses (so that a patient receives both neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy) decreases the absolute risk of melanoma recurrence by >20% is remarkable. These results have already led to a practice change by many oncologists for patients with clinically apparent stage III disease—despite the fact that regulatory agencies have not (yet) approved this regimen and the data stem from a phase II, rather than a phase III, trial. An open question in the field is currently whether combination neoadjuvant therapies followed by adjuvant therapy (either for all patients or risk stratified for patients without an MPR) might improve outcomes even further ( ). Higher radiographic and pathologic response rates have already been demonstrated with anti-PD-1-based combination therapies including anti-PD-1 + anti-cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 and anti-PD-1 + lymphocyte activation gene-3. The ongoing NADINA randomized phase III trial compares neoadjuvant ipilimumab + nivolumab followed by adjuvant nivolumab or dabrafenib/trametinib (if BRAFV600+) versus adjuvant nivolumab and will therefore not provide information on the most effective neoadjuvant + adjuvant therapy regimen ( ). Randomized studies comparing neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 inhibition as monotherapy (as per the SWOG1801 study) versus anti-PD-1-based combination therapy would therefore be informative.
 In the absence of randomized trials, some oncologists currently recommend neoadjuvant anti-PD-1-based combination therapy (mostly ipilimumab + nivolumab) for patients with high tumor burden or borderline resectable disease based on the higher overall response rate of this combination compared to anti-PD-1 monotherapy. The higher efficacy of combination therapy should be carefully weighed against the substantially higher risk of high-grade immune-related toxicities associated with ipilimumab + nivolumab. However, similar to the first-line advanced disease setting, reliable predictive markers to stratify patients before neoadjuvant therapy are currently lacking. Furthermore, while results from the relatively small trials conducted to date suggest that patients with MPRs after neoadjuvant anti-PD-1-based therapy have excellent outcomes, it is currently unclear whether these patients derive benefit from adjuvant anti-PD1 monotherapy. Conversely, it is currently unclear whether (or to what extent) patients without MPR after neoadjuvant anti-PD-1-based therapy benefit from adjuvant therapy. Clinical trials stratifying patients based on the pathologic response and allocating different adjuvant therapies based on this information could provide further clarity ( ).
In clinical practice, the discussion of whether to proceed with adjuvant therapy for each patient should include shared decision making to address the risk of relapse or death from melanoma with the benefit of RFS or potential OS benefit, consideration of toxicities, some of which could be permanent, in addition to comorbidities or history of autoimmune conditions. Treatment decisions should be individualized for careful consideration of adjuvant therapy, especially for patients with lower risk (stage IIIA disease). Active surveillance remains a reasonable option for some patients.
Beyond improving patient stratification and delineating the impact of neoadjuvant versus adjuvant therapy more clearly in randomized clinical trials, additional immunotherapies have recently shown benefit in patients with resected disease, including anti-TIGIT antibodies and personalized neoantigen vaccines. , Inducing durable tumor-specific T-cell responses by vaccinating against tumor mutations has recently emerged as an attractive treatment approach and may be particularly suitable for the adjuvant setting. , , The prolonged RFS observed with a personalized RNA vaccine combined with pembrolizumab compared to pembrolizumab alone in patients with resected high-risk stage III and IV melanoma is an important milestone in the field. Incorporating these therapies into future (neo)adjuvant strategies promises to further improve outcomes for patients with stage III melanoma in the future.
