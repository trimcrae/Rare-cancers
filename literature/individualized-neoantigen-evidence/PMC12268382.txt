These developments are important because the prognosis for patients at stage IIB–IV, even after complete surgical resection, is highly variable and associated with a substantial risk of disease recurrence ( - ).
Weber et al. presented an interesting analysis from one of the pivotal trials with adjuvant systemic therapy in melanoma CheckMate 238 demonstrating that patients who progressed to the unresectable disease on/after postoperative treatment have relatively poorer prognosis with shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) than reported in clinical trials for treatment-naïve patients, especially in the subgroup of early recurrences (up to 12 months from initiation therapy). The most significant factor impacting the survival of patients after recurrence on adjuvant treatment was the time of recurrence occurred. PFS and OS on next-line systemic therapy were longer for patients who recurred late. For patients treated with nivolumab, the difference in 24-month rates for patients who recurred late versus early was more evident for OS (36% difference) than PFS (15% difference) ( ). Moreover, the rechallenge to nivolumab was more successful in patients who recurred late; in early recurrences, the better rescue option was ipilimumab-based regimens or targeted therapy.
Unfortunately, for adjuvant immunotherapy, about 2/3 of recurrences occur early during the planned 12 months of adjuvant treatment. In the study best responses were seen in patients receiving subsequent BRAF/MEK inhibition and a combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab. It is in line with the recommendation of European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) consensus and real-world evidence (RWE) observation that, after progression on adjuvant immunotherapy, alternative agents should therefore be given (as combined anti-PD-1/anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy or BRAF + MEK inhibitors in BRAF-mutated cases) ( , ). Recent data confirm that the majority of recurrences following adjuvant anti-PD-1-based therapy occur early, usually when still on the drug, and the outcomes are poor, regardless of subsequent treatment ( , ).
Regardless of the used adjuvant therapy, most recurrences are distant. In the CheckMate 238 study, the survival outcomes for patients with different recurrence types revealed a compelling finding: those experiencing locoregional recurrences exhibited survival outcomes comparable to those facing distant recurrences. This observation underscores the critical importance of locoregional recurrences as significant events within the broader context of the overall recurrence-free survival (RFS) endpoint, highlighting their relevance in patient prognosis ( ). It is worth noticing that patients receiving targeted adjuvant treatment seem to have a higher risk of metastatic spread to the brain as the first recurrence location ( ).
Currently, there are several dilemmas in systemic perioperative therapy, especially in stage III melanoma, and the most challenging is the choice of the first-line adjuvant treatment in BRAF-mutated melanomas and treatment of metastatic relapse during or after adjuvant therapy ( - ). These scenarios have not been tested in randomized clinical trials. Recent data from RWE studies showed that the risk of recurrence was reduced more on targeted therapy than anti-PD-1 adjuvant treatment in BRAF-mutated high-risk melanomas with median relapse-free survival (RFS) significantly longer in targeted therapy-treated patients: 31 vs. 17 months, P<0.001 ( ). Another study from the German group shows that PD-1-treated patients had more and earlier recurrences than targeted treatment patients. RFS at 12 months was 63.0% and at 24 months 50.4% [95% confidence interval (CI): 46.0–55.2] for patients treated with adjuvant PD-1 inhibitors and 90.8% and 66.9% (95% CI: 58.1–77.1) for patients receiving targeted treatment ( ). Similarly, another multicenter study showed better median RFS (51.0 months) in the group treated with adjuvant dabrafenib + trametinib, which was significantly longer than in the anti-PD-1 cohort [44.8 months; univariate: hazard ratio (HR) =0.66, P=0.003; multivariate: HR =0.58, P=0.007]. Nevertheless, OS between adjuvant targeted therapy and immunotherapy groups was similar, which was probably related to a higher proportion of distant metastases in the dabrafenib/trametinib group among patients who experienced recurrence ( ).
Responses to single-agent immunotherapy administered after prior adjuvant targeted therapy remain comparable to those achieved with first-line immunotherapy in the metastatic setting. Furthermore, these responses are significantly greater than those reported with second-line immunotherapy in the same context ( ). Patients who recurred at distant sites after adjuvant targeted therapy had favorable response rates after switching to 1st line ipilimumab + nivolumab as compared to those who were treated with adjuvant anti-PD-1 (42.9% vs. 35.3%, respectively). Moreover, median PFS was also significantly longer in patients who switched to ipilimumab and nivolumab for stage IV disease after adjuvant targeted therapy, which may imply that sensitivity to combined immunotherapy is preserved after BRAF/MEK inhibitors ( ). Studies show that targeted therapy has a higher incidence of treatment modification due to adverse events (AEs) than anti-PD-1 treatment, but fewer persistent AEs ( ). Nevertheless, the disease relapse during or after adjuvant therapy is combination of aggressive tumor biology and primary or secondary resistance ( ), so we need additional research and tools guiding subsequent systemic treatment which may be the most effective at early or late failure of perioperative therapy and based on different modalities as imaging, liquid biopsy [as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) testing] or molecular tumor profiling.
It is necessary to underline that treatment in the adjuvant setting is with curative intent. The time to relapse after failure of adjuvant treatment may reflect different mechanisms of resistance; treatment with the same therapy for first-line metastatic disease as that received in the adjuvant setting is unlikely to be curative ( ). Retrospective observational data involving patients with BRAF mutant advanced or metastatic disease indicate a limited therapeutic benefit associated with this immunotherapy retreatment approach. These findings suggest the presence of treatment resistance in this patient population ( ).
The situation in perioperative therapy in melanoma may be on the brink of significant change, thanks to new approaches. The introduction of adjuvant treatment with a combination of the V940 mRNA vaccine and anti-PD-1 has shown promising results in the phase IIb trial, significantly prolonging RFS compared to monotherapy with pembrolizumab (HR =0.56) ( ). Another potential game-changer is the introduction of neoadjuvant treatment in macroscopic stage III melanoma disease, with the dramatic improvement of event-free survival and distant metastasis-free survival over standard adjuvant anti-PD-1 monotherapy reported in the Neoadjuvant Ipilimumab Plus Nivolumab Versus Standard Adjuvant Nivolumab in Macroscopic Stage III Melanoma (NADINA) trial ( ). With the neoadjuvant approach using combined immunotherapy, the recently presented data demonstrated a benefit in terms of distant metastases-free survival of more than 20% over adjuvant nivolumab.
Despite these advancements, adjuvant anti-PD-1 remains the gold standard strategy in patients with BRAF-negative high-risk melanoma. In the context of resected stages III and IV, the impact of adjuvant therapy on OS in high-risk melanoma patients is still unclear. Still, the data on the strategy for management of distant relapses after adjuvant therapy are scarce ( , ) and algorithm is based mostly on published consensus ( ). Definitively, such a cohort of patients should be analyzed in the current clinical trials. Continued dedication and contributions to the field are crucial in furthering our understanding and improving the outcomes of melanoma treatment.
