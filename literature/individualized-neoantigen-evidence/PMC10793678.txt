## ABSTRACT
The personalized neoantigen nanovaccine (PNVAC) platform for patients with gastric cancer we established previously exhibited promising anti-tumor immunoreaction. However, limited by the ability of traditional neoantigen prediction tools, a portion of epitopes failed to induce specific immune response. In order to filter out more neoantigens to optimize our PNVAC platform, we develop a novel neoantigen prediction model, NUCC. This prediction tool trained through a deep learning approach exhibits better neoantigen prediction performance than other prediction tools, not only in two independent epitope datasets, but also in a totally new epitope dataset we construct from scratch, including 25 patients with advance gastric cancer and 150 candidate mutant peptides, 13 of which prove to be neoantigen by immunogenicity test in vitro. Our work lay the foundation for the improvement of our PNVAC platform for gastric cancer in the future.
KEYWORDS: Cancer vaccine, neoantigen prediction, deep learning, gastric cancer

## Introduction
Personalized therapeutic tumor vaccine, targeting tumor neoantigens instead of tumor-associated antigens, has become a new strategy for tumor immunotherapy from 2017, when Nature reported two clinical trials of individualized neoantigen vaccine for the treatment of melanoma patients. , However, despite preliminary clinical trial data showing strong immunogenicity and evidence of targeted tumor cell killing for some neoantigen vaccines, a larger proportion of neoantigen vaccines do not induce specific anti-tumor immune responses in patients. The accurate selection of “high-quality” (high immunogenicity) neoantigens has become crucial for personalized neoantigen vaccine therapy.
Neoantigens are generated from tumor cells through various mechanisms, such as genomic mutations, abnormal transcription variants, post-translational modifications, and viral open reading frames, and they can be presented by the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) of antigen-presenting cells. Through the comparison of WES sequencing data of tumor tissue and normal tissue DNA, tumor-specific mutant genes are obtained. Combined with RNA-seq data to determine whether the mutant gene is expressed in the tumor cell, suitable mutations could be screened out to make neoantigen prediction in the computer through the neoantigen prediction tool, and peptides with higher ranking are synthesized for vaccine preparation.
Machine learning is well-known and has been widely employed across various domains in biomedical research. , Early neoantigen prediction tools, such as Pickpocket, typically use the peptide-MHC (p-MHC) binding affinity data from experiments and positional weight matrix algorithm for model training. In recent years, mass spectral elution data have been added, and the training algorithm has also transitioned from positional weight matrix algorithm to artificial neural network, leading to an improving prediction performance of neoantigen prediction tools. 
Recent benchmarking studies use receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis as the performance metric to assess prediction tools. Taking the most widely used model, NetMHCpan, as an example, although it achieves the best area under the ROC curve (ROC-AUC), statistics show that less than 5% of the antigens predicted can be presented to the cell surface, and less than 1% can be bound and recognized by MHC molecule.
With the widespread application of cell immune response-related detection technologies such as cytometric bead array (CBA), enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISPOT), tetramer, more new antigen prediction models like DeepHLApan, IEDB immunogenicity, DeepImmuno-CNN, Seq2Neo-CNN etc., have been developed. The scores generated by these models for antigen peptides not only cover aspects like peptide transport and cleavage efficiency, pMHC affinity or pMHC stability but also include the immunogenicity of antigen peptides. These new models have been developed in a relatively short period of time. While they have shown good predictive performance on some datasets, they are still limited by the insufficient number of immunogenic peptides required for supervised learning and the uncertainty in feature selection. As a result, these models still carry the risk of overfitting and cannot be widely applied.
Resected stage IIIB – IVA gastric cancer is highly incurable, with high risks of recurrence and metastasis. The three‐year overall survival rate is only 33% for stage IIIB gastric cancer. In addition to the standard treatment of surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, we have developed a personalized neoantigen nanovaccine (PNVAC) platform in our previous work. The PNVAC platform not only showed an improved LN-targeting capacity and a significant CD4+ and CD8+ T cells mediating immune response in the preclinical studies, but also clearly prolonged DFS in patients with high-risk G/GEJ cancer. In this platform, we used NetMHCpan as the prediction tool. However, we found that a portion of predicted antigenic epitopes fail to induce specific immune responses, which may be related to the efficacy of the prediction tool. Given the uncertainty and inaccessibility of various existing tools for neoantigen prediction, it is an urgent clinical need to develop a novel neoantigen prediction model, with better prediction accuracy, to improve our existing PNVAC platform for gastric cancer.

## Materials and methods

## Data preparation
We obtained a large amount of epitope data from IEDB and applied multiple filters and screenings to obtain a high-quality dataset with reliable immunogenicity labels. As the premise for antigen peptides to stimulate an immune response in T cells Is that the pMHC complexes can be stably and persistently expressed on the surface of antigen-presenting cells, we have chosen presenting score, binding affinity, and binding stability as the three most important features, which are calculated based on the input of antigen peptide amino acid sequences and HLA typing into the corresponding prediction tools. In addition to antigen peptide amino acid and HLA typing sequences, there may be some currently unknown associations with the peptide immunogenicity. To incorporate these considerations as much as possible, we also include the strings of antigen peptide amino acid and HLA typing sequences as features in the model training. (See supplementary materials)

## Construction and performance evaluation of five prediction models
Five mainstream machine learning algorithms, including generalized linear model(GLM), random forest(RF), extreme gradient boosting(Xgboost), gradient learning machine(GBM), and fully connected neural network(FCNN), were used to construct a tumor neoantigen prediction model. In the selection of machine learning algorithms, given the relatively small size of the training dataset and to prevent overfitting, a compromise is made by opting for algorithms with moderate complexity. GLM, DRF, Xgboost, and GBM are decision tree-based methods, known for their fast training and inference speed and mature practical applications. FCNN, on the other hand, is based on neural networks and exhibits strong fitting capabilities. If there is a substantial increase in the dataset size in the future, it would be advantageous to derive more complex models based on this foundation. The above models were all trained using default hyperparameters. Detailed explanations of the crucial hyperparameters for each model are provided in the supplementary materials. The H2O.AI platform divides the Train.csv into training and validation sets in a 4:1 ratio. After setting the default hyperparameters, the optimal model is trained on the training set. Subsequently, the model parameters are fine-tuned based on the validation set to obtain the final model. The primary evaluation metrics for the final model are accuracy and AUC. (1) If these metrics perform poorly on both the training and validation sets, it indicates a relatively weak fitting ability of the model. (2) If there is a significant gap in these metrics between the training and validation sets, it suggests a relatively weak generalization ability of the model.-

## Construction and performance evaluation of NUCC prediction model
We adopted the TensorFlow v1.12.0 framework, with Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN) to redevelop a novel neoantigen prediction tool called NUCC. The performance and generalization ability of NUCC were preliminarily evaluated, Similarly. (See supplementary materials for detailed steps)

## Comparison of NUCC model and other prediction models on third-party test sets
In current clinical application, the purpose of the neoantigen prediction model is to select the most likely immunogenic antigen peptides from numerous tumor-specific mutations in a patient, in order to narrow down the selection range of mutated peptides. The ultimate determination of whether a mutated peptide is truly with immunogenicity is based on in vitro specific immune response experiments. In other words, a better neoantigen prediction model may have a higher number of neoantigens included in the top-ranked peptides. The current neoantigen prediction model mostly provides numerical scores as results. Therefore, in order to compare the performance of these models, the most important indicator is their ability to discover neoantigens. Therefore, we select the number of neoantigens discovered in the top 5, top 10 and top 20 ranking scores as the evaluation criterion for the neoantigen prediction model. We define it as TOPx（TOP5, TOP10, TOP20）.
The data labels of the test set should be accurate and recognized. The TESLA dataset is derived from diverse neoantigen predictions on shared genomic data from a global consortium. They use pMHC multimer assays to identify neoantigens, and 37 out of tested peptide-MHCs are bound by patient-matched T cells. The Rosenberg dataset consists of 5 subjects and contains a total of 246 mutant peptides, out of which 4% (11 peptides) are neoantigens. They stimulate patient T cells with antigenic peptides in vitro and identify neoantigens by comparing the levels of interferon release after stimulation with wild-type peptides and mutant peptides. In both of these test sets, the in vitro identification of neoantigens was carried out by reliable experimental teams and recognized experimental methods.

TESLA dataset:
DeepHLApan, IEDB immunogenicity, DeepImmuno-CNN, and Seq2Neo-CNN are the latest developed models for predicting tumor neoantigens. Using an independent third-party TESLA antigen peptide dataset (6 subjects with a total of 599 mutated peptides, including 6% or 37 neoantigen peptides), the ranking performance of the NUCC was compared to other four new models in terms of identifying neoantigens. We inputted all epitopes from the TESLA dataset into the NUCC prediction model, sorted from high to low based on their immunogenicity scores generated by NUCC, and calculated how many neoantigens were present in the top 20 and top 50 ranked epitopes. We then compared these results to those predicted by the four models mentioned above in order to determine which model could identify more neoantigens at higher ranks.

(2) Rosenberg dataset:
NetMHCpan, NetMHCstablepan, and MixMHCpred are well-established traditional prediction models that have been developed for a long time. We compared the performance of the NUCC prediction model with these traditional models in terms of ranking new antigens using an independent third-party dataset called the Rosenberg antigen peptide dataset. This dataset consists of 5 subjects and contains a total of 246 mutant peptides, out of which 4% (11 peptides) are neoantigens. All epitopes from the Rosenberg dataset were inputted into the NUCC prediction model. Based on its output immunogenicity scores, peptides were ranked from high to low and the number of neoantigens present among the top 5, top 10, and top 20 ranked peptides was calculated. We then compared these results with those obtained from predictions made by the above traditional models to determine which model can identify more new antigens at higher ranks.”

## Building the DrumTower dataset from scratch
25 patients with stage III gastric cancer who underwent curative surgery in our center was included. Tumor tissue specimens and peripheral blood were collected from these patients for whole exome sequencing (WES), RNA-seq, and HLA typing. We selected non-synonymous mutations with RNA expression levels and used the NetMHCpan prediction model for neoantigen prediction. We included candidate peptides with affinity greater than 500 nM or percentile rank < 2.0% for peptide lengths less than 15 amino acids, while excluding strongly hydrophobic peptides, as they may encounter significant difficulties during dissolution and purification processes.
All candidate peptides were synthesized in a laboratory equipped with GMP conditions(See supplementary materials for method used for laboratory synthesis). Each synthesized candidate peptide was evaluated for specific cellular immune response using CBA assay and ELISPOT assay in vitro to identify immunogenic neoantigen peptides. Finally, a new epitope dataset called DrumTower dataset was constructed from scratch. (See supplementary materials for detailed steps)

## Comparison of NUCC model and other prediction models on DrumTower test set
NetMHCpan is a widely recognized and extensively used neoantigen prediction model that has been developed for a long time. In order to further compare the predictive ranking performance of the NUCC and NetMHCpan, We used the DrumTower dataset as a benchmark. Firstly, peptides from DrumTower dataset was inputted into the NUCC prediction model. Based on the immunogenicity score, we ranked all peptides from high to low and calculated the number of neoantigens in TOP5, TOP10, and TOP20 positions. These results were compared with the rankings obtained from NetMHCpan. Furthermore, in order to compare the ranking ability of NetMHCpan and NUCC for neoantigen peptides, we obtained the Binding Affinity and Immunogenicity Score of 150 antigen peptides using NetMHCpan and NUCC, respectively. Based on the values of Binding Affinity and Immunogenicity Score, we obtained the original ranking position (Rank1) and secondary ranking position (Rank2) of the neoantigen peptides in each patient’s antigen peptide repertoire, respectively. Rank1 and Rank2 represent the ranking ability of NetMHCpan and NUCC models for neoantigens, with smaller values indicating better performance. Due to the small sample size (n = 13) and the inability to determine whether the sample data comes from a normal distribution population, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was used for statistical analysis to determine whether there is a significant difference between two related groups of Rank1 and Rank2.

## Results

## Evaluation of neoantigen prediction models based on five machine learning algorithms
Five mainstream machine learning algorithms were selected to build prediction models, including Generalized Linear Model (GLM), Distributed Random Forest (DRF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Gradient Boosting Machine GBM), Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN). The AUC value and other important metrics of these models were obtained ( ).

Accuracy and AUC value on training set
 Xgboost and GBM model obtained a prediction accuracy of more than 0.7 on training set, while GLM, DRF and FCNN model obtained a low prediction accuracy. For AUC value, Xgboost and GBM model exceeded 0.85, while GLM, DRF and FCNN model had a low AUC value.

(2) Generalization Ability
The validation set is commonly used to adjust the parameters of a model in order to make it fit the test data better. Therefore, the difference in performance between models built using different algorithms on the training set and validation set can reflect the generalization ability of the model and whether there is overfitting. Thus, we compared the prediction accuracy of these models on the training set and validation set ( ). Xgboost and GBM model obtained high prediction accuracy on the training set, but low on the validation set, indicating a poor generalization ability. These two models clearly cannot meet the requirements for subsequent applications. Therefore, we reconstructed a novel tumor neoantigen prediction model called NUCC to solve the problem.

## Evaluation of NUCC prediction model
This prediction tool is like a “black box,” and its inner working flows are difficult to understand. For ease of understanding, we provided a simplified model diagram ( ) and a detailed model diagram ( ).

 We further evaluated the predicting accuracy of NUCC on training set and validation set ( ). This model showed a consistently high accuracy on both the training and validation sets, indicating no significant overfitting. This suggests that it has better generalization ability and the potential to be used in subsequent production applications. Therefore, we selected this model for subsequent work.

## Comparison of NUCC model and other prediction models on third-party test sets
On TESLA dataset, the TOP20 and TOP50 predicted by NUCC were 5 and 11, compared to 3 and 4 by DeepHLApan, 1 and 3 by IEDB immunogenicity, 3 and 5 by DeepImmuno-CNN, 4 and 6 by Seq2Neo-CNN, 4 and 9 by Prime, respectively ( ).

 On Rosenberg dataset, the TOP5, TOP10 and TOP20 predicted by NUCC were 2, 3, 4, compared to 0, 2, 3 by NetMHCpan, 2, 2, 3 by NetMHCstablepan, 1, 2, 3 by MixMHCpred, 1,1,3 by Prime. The TOP5 by NUCC was not lower than others, and TOP10 and TOP20 were higher than others ( ). It is particularly noteworthy that no neoantigen was found in TOP5 by NetMHCpan, while NUCC found 2.

## Building the DrumTower dataset from scratch
As mentioned earlier, we performed neoantigen prediction for 25 patients with gastric cancer. Finally, we synthesized 150 candidate peptides. In order to determine whether a peptide was a neoantigen or not, we used CBA method to identify each one in vitro, and 2 times more than control group was defined as positive ( ). Some of the candidate peptides showing weak positive by CBA method were subsequently reconfirmed by ELISPOT method ( ).

 We obtained the distribution of neoantigens in each patient ( ). Neoantigens were found in 12/25 (48%) patients ( ). One patient found 2 neoantigens. We numbered these 13 neoantigens with ID 1–13 and built a completely new epitope dataset from scratch, called DrumTower dataset. This dataset included a total number of 150 candidate mutant epitopes in 25 gastric cancer patients, of which 13/150 (8%) neoantigens were found in 12/25 (48%) patients. Identification results for all peptides were included in supplementary materials.

## Comparison of NUCC model and other prediction models on DrumTower test set
We used the DrumTower dataset as a benchmark to compare NUCC with NetMHCpan. We found that TOP5, TOP10 and TOP20 predicted by NUCC were 1, 2, 6, respectively, significantly higher than 0, 1, 3 by NetMHCpan prediction tool ( ). Then NetMHCpan was used to obtain the original ranking position of each neoantigen(Rank1) in the corresponding patient, and the secondary ranking position of each neoantigen(Rank2) in the corresponding patient was obtained by NUCC. We listed two ranking positions of 13 neoantigen peptides in detail ( ) and found that 11/13 neoantigen peptides (84.6%) had significant improvement in the secondary ranking position compared to the original one ( ).

 The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was used for statistical analysis to determine whether there is a significant difference between two related groups of Rank1 and Rank2.The test statistic W(Rank2−Rank1) was 80.5, which corresponds to the sum of ranks for the differences. Due to a sample size of N = 13 and a sum of ranks equal to 80.5, the two-sided p-value was determined to be .01 by consulting the critical values table for the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test. The Z value was −2.469 and the p value was .013 > .01, for Exact Significance (Two-Tailed). Although strictly speaking, no statistical difference was observed, it is very close to the significance threshold. There is a potentially inconsistent trends between Rank1 and Rank2, with Rank2 having relatively smaller ranking values.

## Discussion
Personalized therapeutic tumor vaccine strategy, targeting tumor neoantigens instead of tumor-associated antigens, has become the most promising treatment for malignant tumors. , In addition to the standard treatment of surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy for patients with advanced gastric cancer, we have developed a personalized neoantigen nanovaccine (PNVAC) platform previously. The PNVAC platform not only shows an improved LN‐targeting capacity and a significant CD4+ and CD8+ T cells mediating immune response in the preclinical studies, but also clearly prolongs DFS in patients with high‐risk G/GEJ cancer. NetMHCpan is chosen for neoantigen prediction in our PVNAC platform. However, the results shows that there are still a number of epitopes cannot induce specific immune response. A novel tool is urgently needed to improve the neoantigen predicting accuracy.
Several critical steps are required for neoantigens to activate antigen-specific T cells: (1) peptides undergo proteasomal cleavage processing and TAP-mediated transport; (2) peptide-MHC molecules form strong, stable and long-lasting binding; (3) TCR recognition and generation of specific immune response. Among them, step 2 is particularly crucial and most traditional prediction models also focus on this aspect. However, they only focus on limited factor and do not take immunogenic results in vitro as label of training set, using relatively simple machine learning algorithm for model training, resulting in relatively poor neoantigen prediction accuracy. Newly developed models have added various features with advanced machine learning algorithm. Despite their good predictive performance on some datasets, they are still limited by the insufficient number of immunogenic peptides required for training supervised learning model. As a result, these models still carry the risk of overfitting. Moreover, they lack comprehensive consideration, leading to the uncertainty in feature selection. Even more troublesome is that some models are protected by patents and are not authorized for open use. Therefore, we need to rebuild a prediction model to replace NetMHCpan as the new antigen prediction tool for our PVNAC platform.
We searched the IEDB database for epitope data with immunogenicity labels. After multiple filtering and screening processes, a high-quality and reliable dataset was obtained, which was further divided into training and validation sets to construct neoantigen prediction models. Considering the fact that the strong and stable binding of peptide-MHC complexes, as well as their presentation on the surface of antigen-presenting cells, is a prerequisite for generating a specific immune response, presentation score, , pMHC binding affinity and pMHC binding stability were incorporated as data features. Characters of peptide sequence and MHC typing were encoded and Convolutional Neural Networks and Fully Connected Neural Networks were adopted for model training to develop a new neoantigen prediction tool, NUCC. For the first time, we integrated these features above into a deep learning approach and generated a novel neoantigen prediction tool. It showed better neoantigen prediction performance than other tools as we known, not only in two independent epitope datasets, but also in a totally new epitope dataset we constructed from scratch, especially proving its effectiveness in gastric cancer patients cohort. Furthermore, we found the ranking position of neoantigens applied by NUCC rises potentially compared to NetMHCpan.
For personalized peptide vaccines design, the reduction in peptide quantity can reduce the cost and difficulty of vaccine preparation, while the improvement in peptide quality can make vaccines more effective. , Overall, our work lay the foundation for the future optimization of our self-developed PNVAC platform. We will continue to improve this tool in the future, adding prediction functions for MHC class II antigen peptides and integrating other bioinformatics tools to provide a one-stop solution for neoantigen prediction from raw sequencing data.
Given the limited size of the training dataset, the presence of disease-specific variations in gene mutations, and an imbalanced distribution of HLA types within the training set, the model may exhibit some degree of uncertainty when applied to other test datasets. Therefore, in the future, expanding the training dataset is essential to enhance the model’s generalization capability and adaptability to a broader range of test sets.
