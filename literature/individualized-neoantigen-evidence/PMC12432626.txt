## Background and Objective
With increasing understanding of tumor immune tolerance mechanisms, a series of immune checkpoint molecules have been identified. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, which was developed based on these discoveries, has emerged as a promising strategy for the clinical treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and has shown notable therapeutic benefits. However, the limited response rate remains a major barrier to further improving ICB efficacy and poses a significant challenge to the clinical management of NSCLC. This review summarizes recent advances in clinical immunotherapy for NSCLC and discusses future prospects in the field.

## Methods
A systematic literature search was performed using the official websites of organizations and databases, including PubMed, the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA), China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), the National Cancer Institute (NCI), the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO), the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) The inclusion criteria were: (I) English-language articles published between 2004 and 2025; and (II) clinical studies specifically addressing immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy in NSCLC populations.

## Key Content and Findings
Researchers have explored various approaches from drug development and treatment response evaluation to combination strategies, and have made substantial progress in enhancing the effectiveness of ICB therapy.

## Conclusions
ICB therapy for NSCLC has evolved from monotherapy to multimodal combination approaches, and significant breakthroughs have been achieved; however, three major challenges persist; that is, acquired drug resistance, a lack of reliable biomarkers, and the suboptimal management of special patient populations. To address these issues, future research should focus on: (I) elucidating resistance mechanisms through multi-omics approaches; (II) developing novel biomarkers and next-generation targeted agents; and (III) refining combination therapy regimens. These coordinated efforts will ultimately enable personalized precision immunotherapy tailored to individual patients’ molecular profiles and clinical characteristics.

## Introduction
Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide ( ).
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80–85% of all lung cancer cases ( ).
Early stage NSCLC can be cured; however, metastatic NSCLC has a 5-year survival rate of less than 20% ( ). Currently, surgery is the gold standard local treatment for early stage NSCLC. For patients with locally advanced NSCLC who are not eligible for surgery—due to impaired general or respiratory condition, or because of extensive local tumor involvement—chemoradiotherapy is the preferred local treatment approach ( ).
For patients with distant metastases, systemic therapies are the cornerstone of treatment, such as targeted agents—when a driver gene mutation is present—or chemotherapy ( ).
Despite comprehensive treatment strategies, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and small-molecule targeted therapies, the overall prognosis of NSCLC patients remains unsatisfactory. Thus, new and effective treatment strategies urgently need to be developed for clinical NSCLC management. With the deepening understanding of tumor immune escape mechanisms, immunotherapy has emerged as a significant clinical breakthrough, and is now considered the fifth major cancer treatment modality, alongside surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and radiotherapy. Immunotherapy activates anti-tumor immune responses by modulating the immune system to overcome immune evasion. Among these, immune checkpoint blockades (ICB) enhance anti-tumor responses by blocking inhibitory signals in T-cell activation, which can substantially improve clinical symptoms and provide long-term benefits for cancer patients ( ). They have emerged as a central component in the clinical management of NSCLC ( ).
In the context of non-oncogene-driven NSCLC, ICB particularly programmed death-1 (PD-1) or programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) blockers, have significantly improved overall survival (OS) across disease stages. Initially, their benefit was demonstrated in metastatic disease, first as second-line therapy after chemotherapy ( ), then as first-line treatment—either as monotherapy in patients with positive PD-L1 expression ( , ) or in combination with chemotherapy ( , ). Their use then expanded to earlier stages. In unresectable stage III NSCLC, consolidation immunotherapy after chemoradiotherapy significantly improved OS, becoming a new standard of care ( ). More recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown benefit in the perioperative setting for resectable stage II and III disease, used either alone or combined with chemotherapy ( - ). This stepwise integration of ICIs into NSCLC treatment reflects their growing impact across all stages.
Although the 5-year OS rate of advanced-stage NSCLC patients with high PD-L1 expression who undergo ICB is up to 40% ( ). Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients still fail to achieve effective disease control, and most eventually develop either primary or acquired resistance to ICB. It is estimated that on average only about 25% of solid tumor patients respond to current PD-1/PD-L1 blockers ( ). To address this issue, researchers have identified numerous biomarkers to predict ICB responses, and have conducted extensive experimental and clinical studies combining immunotherapy with various treatment approaches, leading to a series of important advances.
This review summarizes the current status of immunotherapy for NSCLC in clinical practice, focusing on therapeutic agents, treatment response, response evaluation, and synergistic treatment strategies. It systematically discusses the challenges faced in NSCLC immunotherapy and explores potential solutions, aiming to improve the efficacy of ICB by addressing the key issues in NSCLC treatment. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2025-754/rc).

## Methods
A systematic literature search was performed across the PubMed database, and the official websites of the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA), China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), the National Cancer Institute (NCI), the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO), the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO). The search included English-language articles published between July 15, 2004 and May 5, 2025. The search strategy incorporated the following key terms: “non-small cell lung cancer”, “immune checkpoint inhibitor”, “immune resistance”, “tumor microenvironment”, “combination strategies”, and “immune checkpoint therapy biomarkers”, with the inclusion criteria restricted to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, basic science studies, and randomized controlled trials. Relevant publications were critically evaluated to synthesize evidence on the current status, clinical challenges, and future directions of ICB therapy in NSCLC. All selected publications are comprehensively cited in the reference section. The complete search methodology is delineated in .
 

## Tumor immune checkpoint mechanisms and ICB drugs

## Immune checkpoint molecules in tumors
The human immune system is capable of recognizing, responding to, and eliminating tumor cells (TCs). However, during tumor progression, a variety of cellular and molecular mechanisms emerge—notably including adaptive changes in the immune microenvironment at the tumor site—that allow TCs to evade immune attack. TCs adopt various strategies to adapt and to resist to immune surveillance, a phenomenon collectively referred as adaptive immune resistance (AIR). AIR results from the long-term adaptation and diverse responses of TCs, contributing to significant heterogeneity among tumors, even in the same pathological subtype. This heterogeneity presents a major challenge in cancer treatment ( ).
A key mechanism of tumor immune evasion involves the activation of immune checkpoint pathways. Immune checkpoints are immunosuppressive molecules that regulate immune responses and prevent damage to normal tissues. During tumor initiation and progression, immune checkpoints become one of the main contributors to immune tolerance.
The immune system maintains a dynamic balance, and to more effectively eliminate TCs, it activates a series of anti-tumor responses known as the cancer-immunity cycle ( ). A critical step in this cycle is the activation of T cells, which requires two major processes: (I) T-cell receptors (TCRs) recognizing and binding to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules loaded with neoepitopes on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) or other cells (MHC class I molecules are expressed on almost all cells, while MHC class II molecules are mainly expressed on APCs. TCs presenting neoepitopes via MHC-I to CD8+ T cells, while APCs display tumor neoepitopes on MHC-II to CD4+), triggering the TCR signaling pathway; and (II) the co-stimulatory receptor CD28 binding to B7 ligands (CD80: B7.1; CD86: B7.2) on the APCs. T-cell activation subsequently promotes proliferation, enhances effector functions, and supports the generation of memory T cells ( ). Meanwhile, to prevent excessive T-cell activation and off-target effects, checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, CTLA-4 (also known as CD152), LAG-3 (or CD223), TIM-3, TIGIT, and VISTA are upregulated to serve as immune “brakes” ( ) ( ).
 PD-1 is highly expressed on tumor-infiltrating T cells, particularly exhausted T cells, and has two ligands: PD-L1 and PD-L2. PD-L1 is expressed by TCs and various intratumoral cells, including endothelial, epithelial, and myeloid cells, while PD-L2 expression is more restricted, and is primarily found on dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and B cells ( ). TCR signaling is tightly regulated, and its dysregulation can lead to autoimmune diseases (AIDs). On binding to its ligands, PD-1 recruits Src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 1 (SHP-1) and 2 (SHP-2) tyrosine phosphatases to dephosphorylate key components of the TCR signaling pathway, such as CD3ε and zeta-chain-associated protein kinase 70 (ZAP-70), thereby inhibiting T-cell activity ( , ). CTLA-4 and PD-1 suppress T-cell activation via different mechanisms. CTLA-4 is rapidly upregulated after TCR engagement and competes with CD28 for B7 ligands, weakening CD28-mediated co-stimulation. Conversely, PD-1 is induced later during T-cell activation and inhibits TCR signaling primarily through tyrosine phosphatase recruitment on ligand binding ( ). LAG-3 is not expressed on naïve T cells but is induced on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells on antigen stimulation. Its expression is closely correlated with its inhibitory function. In chronic infections, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells continue to express high levels of LAG-3 and other inhibitory receptors, losing their effector capabilities and becoming exhausted Similarly, tumor-infiltrating T cells persistently exposed to tumor antigens express high levels of LAG-3 and other inhibitory molecules ( ).
TIM-3 is expressed on various immune cells (ICs), including CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and is notably elevated on dysfunctional or exhausted T cells. It marks the most terminally dysfunctional subset of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Although the precise mechanism by which TIM-3 induces terminal dysfunction is not fully understood, it is hypothesized that TIM-3 impairs the stemness of CD8+ T cells by antagonizing T-cell factor 1 (TCF-1), a transcription factor critical for maintaining stemness and inhibiting terminal differentiation ( ). TIGIT is expressed on activated CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and follicular helper T cells, but is more lowly expressed on in naïve T cells. The TIGIT pathway modulates tumor recognition via both T cells and NK cells in vivo and in vitro ( ). VISTA, which is a member of the B7 family and considered a homolog of PD-1, exerts immunosuppressive effects through non-redundant signaling pathways ( ).

## Development of ICB drugs
Negative immune checkpoint molecules play a crucial role in maintaining T-cell viability and facilitating tumor immune tolerance. Targeting these molecules has emerged as a promising strategy for cancer immunotherapy, leading to the development of ICB drugs. These therapies primarily utilize inhibitory monoclonal antibodies to disrupt immune checkpoint signaling and restore anti-tumor immune responses.
Among them, therapies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis have garnered global attention, and are revolutionizing the field of oncology ( ). Monoclonal antibodies targeting PD-1 include nivolumab, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, toripalimab, camrelizumab, penpulimab, zimberelimab, serplulimab, cemiplimab, and dostarlimab. PD-L1 inhibitors include atezolizumab, durvalumab, avelumab, envafolimab, sugemalimab, adebrelimab, and cosibelimab.
In addition, CTLA-4 inhibitors have been approved globally, including humanized monoclonal antibodies such as ipilimumab, and tremelimumab. Notably, cadonilimab and QL1706 represent dual-specificity antibodies targeting both PD-1 and CTLA-4.
While monotherapies targeting a single immune checkpoint molecule have shown promising results, their efficacy remains limited in many cases. A study indicates that PD-1 inhibitors predominantly expand exhausted-like CD8+ T-cell subsets, while CTLA-4 blockade facilitates the proliferation of inducible T cell costimulator+ (ICOS) type 1 T helper-like CD4+ effector T cells and contributes to CD8+ T-cell reprogramming. These distinct yet complementary mechanisms provide a strong rationale for combination therapies ( ). Dual blockade strategies, especially those targeting PD-1 and CTLA-4 simultaneously, have shown superior efficacy in multiple cancer types. For example, cadonilimab (AK104), developed by Akeso Biopharma (Zhongshan, China), became the first PD-1/CTLA-4 bispecific antibody approved in China in 2022 for recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer following failure of platinum-based chemotherapy ( ). Another innovative agent, ivonescimab [a PD-1/vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) bispecific antibody], also shows promising anti-tumor potential ( ). AstraZeneca’s MEDI5752 (London, UK), a PD-1/CTLA-4 bispecific antibody, has demonstrated tumor-targeting enrichment and enhanced efficacy in preclinical models ( ).
For LAG-3, monotherapy has shown limited benefit ( ); therefore, its combination with PD-1 inhibitors is currently the dominant research direction. On March 18, 2022, the US FDA approved the combination of relatlimab (anti-LAG-3) with nivolumab for the treatment of metastatic melanoma, highlighting the therapeutic potential of dual immune checkpoint inhibition ( ). Additionally, PD-1/LAG-3 bispecific antibodies are also under active development ( ). Conversely, no approved therapies currently exist for other immune checkpoints such as TIM-3, TIGIT, and VISTA, though preclinical and early phase clinical trials are ongoing.
Multiple PD-1/PD-L1-targeting drugs have been approved and have demonstrated promising clinical efficacy; however, considerable limitations associated with monoclonal antibody therapies remain. These include high production costs, stringent storage and transportation requirements, immunogenicity, prolonged half-life, and sustained target binding, which can contribute to serious immune-related adverse events (irAEs) ( ). In contrast, small-molecule compounds offer several pharmacological advantages. They are typically capable of penetrating cell membranes and other biological barriers more easily, allowing them to access intracellular targets ( ). Further, small molecules can be structurally modified to enhance efficacy, selectivity, and safety profiles ( ). Consequently, small-molecule inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint are emerging as attractive alternatives or complementary agents to existing monoclonal antibodies, and the development of such inhibitors has become a focus in current immuno-oncology drug research.
The first peptide-based PD-1 inhibitor, AUNP-12, was reported in 2014 ( ). Compared to monoclonal antibodies, AUNP-12 has a shorter half-life, which may help reduce the risk and the severity of irAEs ( - ). Non-peptide small-molecule inhibitors are also under development. Bristol-Myers Squibb (BMS) has conducted in-depth exploration of small-molecule inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway and published the first patent for biphenyl-type immunomodulators in 2015 ( ). These small molecules aim to overcome immune-related diseases caused by PD-1 and PD-L1 activation, such as tumors and hepatitis C virus (HCV), by blocking PD-1/PD-L1 binding ( ). BMS-202 binds to two dimeric PD-L1 cylinders, forming hydrophobic cavities. The interaction surface of dimerized PD-L1/PD-L1 is highly similar to that of PD-1/PD-L1, thereby this interaction surface prevents the normal interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1, and ultimately blocks the signaling pathway ( , , ). In 2018, a class of aromatic acetylene and aromatic vinyl compounds was discovered which can significantly inhibit the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction ( , ). CA-170, an oral small-molecule dual inhibitor targeting both VISTA and PD-1, was jointly developed by Curis Pharmaceuticals (Cambridge, MA, USA) and Aurigene Pharmaceuticals (Hyderabad, India) ( ). To some extent, it mimics FDA-approved monoclonal antibodies and overcomes their limitations, such as high production costs and adverse reactions ( ).
Although small-molecule inhibitors offer many advantages, their binding affinity for targets is generally lower than that of antibody drugs. They are also prone to off-target effects, which can significantly reduce therapeutic efficacy and cause unpredictable toxicities. Moreover, the uncertainty of PD-L1 expression in cells and the short half-life of small-molecule drugs further contribute to their limited in vivo activity ( ).

## ICB therapies for NSCLC (monotherapy)
There are many anti-tumor drugs that target PD-1/PD-L1 in NSCLC. PD-1 monoclonal antibodies include nivolumab, pembrolizumab, camrelizumab, tislelizumab, sintilimab, and cemiplimab. PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies include durvalumab, atezolizumab, avelumab, and sugemalimab. Both types are used as monotherapy or in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy, and are currently approved for the treatment of NSCLC. Compared with PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies, CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody drugs, although developed and clinically applied earlier, have relatively limited options and are often used in combination with other ICIs. For example, Ipilimumab has been approved by the US FDA for the clinical treatment of tumors and is the first drug proven to prolong OS in patients with advanced melanoma ( ). Over the past decade, with a deeper understanding of the tumor immune microenvironment and immune escape pathways, the application of ICIs in NSCLC treatment has developed rapidly ( ).
 The CheckMate 017/057 study was a randomized phase III clinical study targeting patients with second-line squamous and non-squamous NSCLC respectively, establishing the standard position of nivolumab in the second-line treatment of advanced non-small cell lung cancer ( ), and in 2016, the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab was officially approved by the US FDA for the first-line treatment of NSCLC, marking the true beginning of the immunotherapy era for lung cancer. The KEYNOTE-024 study showed that the 5-year OS rate of advanced NSCLC patients with high PD-L1 expression (≥50% of TCs) treated with pembrolizumab was twice that of those treated with chemotherapy (31.9% vs. 16.3%). The median OS was nearly doubled (26.3 vs. 13.4 months), and the risk of death was significantly reduced by 38% ( ).
In 2020, the US FDA approved atezolizumab as first-line monotherapy for metastatic NSCLC with high PD-L1 expression (TCs ≥50% or ICs ≥10%) and no epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) mutations, based on improved OS and progression-free survival (PFS) compared to chemotherapy ( ). Platinum-based chemotherapy was for decades the primary treatment option for NSCLC patients. However, more than 40% of patients with advanced NSCLC have a poor physical condition, and most are elderly with serious complications, making them ineligible for platinum-based chemotherapy. A phase III clinical trial [Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (PS) 0–1] included patients with stage IIIB or IV NSCLC who were either not suitable for platinum-based chemotherapy, were aged 70 years or older, had a poor physical condition, or had serious complications or contraindications. These patients were randomly assigned to receive atezolizumab immunotherapy or chemotherapy. The results showed that compared with chemotherapy, atezolizumab improved the OS rate of NSCLC patients, doubling the two-year survival rate, and confirming its good efficacy and safety as a first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC patients not suitable for platinum-based chemotherapy ( ). A study of neoadjuvant atezolizumab in a large cohort of resectable NSCLC patients demonstrated its safety and met its primary endpoint of a major pathological response (MPR). Data from this single-arm, non-randomized trial suggest that the profile of innate ICs in pre-treatment peripheral blood may predict pathological response after neoadjuvant atezolizumab ( ).
In February 2021, the US FDA approved cemiplimab-rwlc as a first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC patients with high PD-L1 expression. The EMPOWER-Lung1 study showed that compared with chemotherapy, cemiplimab monotherapy significantly improved the OS and PFS of advanced NSCLC patients. Patients receiving monoclonal antibody therapy had significantly fewer grade 3–4 treatment-emergent adverse events than those receiving chemotherapy, thus, monoclonal antibody therapy represents a potential new treatment option for this patient population ( ). Cemiplimab is the only second anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agent to show efficacy in advanced NSCLC as both a monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy for both squamous and non-squamous histologies ( ).
In the second-line treatment setting, NSCLC patients face more complex disease status and therapeutic challenges. The RATIONALE 303 study, serving as a significant milestone for second-line immunotherapy, demonstrated the marked advantages of tislelizumab in the second- or third-line treatment of advanced NSCLC ( ). Nivolumab and durvalumab have been approved by the US FDA as second-line treatments for advanced NSCLC. CheckMate 078 was a randomized, open-label, phase III clinical trial conducted primarily in a Chinese population with previously treated advanced NSCLC patients. Compared with docetaxel, nivolumab improved OS and demonstrated a survival benefit ( , ). Durvalumab has been proved that its use after radiotherapy and chemotherapy in patients with locally advanced NSCLC can improve prognosis ( ).
ICIs have achieved significant breakthroughs in first-line and second-line monotherapy for NSCLC, profoundly reshaping the treatment paradigm for advanced NSCLC. However, ICI monotherapy still faces the challenge of limited response rates.

## Factors affecting the efficacy of NSCLC ICB treatment
Although the application of ICIs in the treatment of NSCLC is becoming increasingly widespread, only about 20% of unselected patients respond to treatment, and not all responders can sustain their response. Most patients receiving ICI treatment will experience various irAEs, which may serve as prognostic markers in NSCLC ( ). Therefore, it is crucial to identify NSCLC patients who are likely to benefit from ICI therapy.
The expression level of PD-L1 on the TC surface remains the most accurate and widely used biomarker for predicting which patients will benefit from anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment. However, current imaging methods for evaluating treatment response are not completely accurate because PD-L1 expression is influenced by many factors. At present, the US FDA has approved four immunohistochemical methods to detect PD-L1 expression and guide immunotherapy. These methods can be used as biomarker to predict the therapeutic efficacy of nivolumab in NSCLC or melanoma, pembrolizumab in NSCLC, atezolizumab in urothelial carcinoma or NSCLC, and durvalumab in urothelial carcinoma. Indeed, in numerous studies evaluating anti-PD-1 therapies in NSCLC patients, PD-L1 expression has been correlated with treatment efficacy, as shown in the KEYNOTE-042 trial in the metastatic setting, the IMpower010 trial in the adjuvant setting, and the CheckMate 816 trial in the neoadjuvant setting ( , , ). But this biomarker appears to be imperfect, as illustrated by the KEYNOTE-189 trial, in which a subgroup analysis showed similar survival benefits across all PD-L1 expression levels ( ). Another noteworthy point is that PD-L1 expression on TCs does not appear to correlate with the efficacy of dual immune checkpoint blockade (anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4), as demonstrated in the CheckMate 227 and CheckMate 9LA trials, suggesting that this strategy could be a valuable treatment option for patients with PD-L1-negative metastatic NSCLC ( , ).
The efficacy of ICIs varies significantly among patients with different molecular subtypes of NSCLC. EGFR mutations and ALK fusion are classic “oncogene addiction” driving variations in NSCLC. These tumors generally show resistance to ICI treatment, and this resistance is closely related to its unique immunosuppressive microenvironment. Clinical studies have consistently confirmed that even with high expression of PD-L1, the objective response rate (ORR) of patients with EGFR mutations or ALK fusion to ICI monotherapy was generally lower than 10% ( , ). Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations account for 25–30% of non-squamous NSCLC, different mutant subtypes of KRAS have unique immune characteristics ( ). G12C mutations are more common in smokers and are associated with high tumor mutational burden (TMB) and increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, conferring the best response to ICIs. In contrast, the G12D subtype is often linked to low TMB and low PD-L1 expression, presenting a relatively immune-excluded phenotype ( ). Serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11, encoding LKB1 kinase), and Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) are key tumor suppressor genes in NSCLC. Such mutations independently or collaboratively induce strong ICI resistance and have become the focus of research in recent years. The landmark study published in Nature in 2024 confirmed for the first time that dual ICB (anti-CTLA-4 + anti-PD-L1) can effectively overcome STK11/KEAP1-related drug resistance ( ). This study integrated data from clinical cohorts, prospective trials (POSEIDON), and preclinical models, providing a complete chain of evidence from mechanism to efficacy. From the “immune desert” of EGFR/ALK to the heterogeneous response of KRAS, and then to the breakthrough of STK11/KEAP1 dual ICB, stratified treatment under molecular guidance is profoundly changing the immunotherapy landscape of NSCLC.
The tumor mutation burden (TMB) refers to the number of non-synonymous mutations in somatic cells within a specific genomic region. In 2014, a correlation between the TMB and immunotherapy efficacy was first identified in melanoma. In 2018, the NCCN guidelines first included the TMB as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy efficacy in NSCLC patients. In NSCLC, high TMB levels have been shown to be associated with better prognosis following PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment ( ). The TMB in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has also shown promise in predicting benefits from PD-L1/PD-1 inhibitors. The B-F1RST study, an open-label phase II trial, evaluated the blood TMB as a predictive biomarker for first-line atezolizumab monotherapy in locally advanced or metastatic stage IIIB–IVB NSCLC ( ). Previous studies have shown that anti-PD-1 therapy can enhance the responsiveness of neoantigen-specific T cells, and that the genomic landscape of lung cancers shapes the response to anti-PD-1 therapy ( , ).
However, the TMB is not an optimal indicator for a number of reasons. First, its predictive effectiveness varies among different tumor types, and contradictory results have been observed in different clinical trials. Second, the TMB can be influenced by many factors, including tumor type, intrinsic genetic and molecular characteristics, changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME), external carcinogens, differences in detection methods, and inconsistent threshold values. These factors may contribute to heterogeneity in the TMB, limiting its accuracy as a predictive biomarker.
CtDNA is a key stratification biomarker for the use of ICIs and can be detected in peripheral blood using liquid biopsy. It provides a non-invasive alternative to tumor tissue sampling. BR.36 is a two-stage, ctDNA-directed, molecular response-adaptive clinical trial for NSCLC patients, which confirmed that the ctDNA response can be used to accurately identify newly diagnosed NSCLC patients who respond poorly to PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy. The trial met its primary endpoint, and had a sensitivity of 82% and a specificity of 75% for the ctDNA response in terms of the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors. The ctDNA response was found to be significantly correlated with the patients’ PFS and OS ( ). A proof-of-concept study has also shown that quantitative changes in ctDNA levels early during treatment are a promising tool for assessing responsiveness to ICIs. However, the analysis of ctDNA alone does not allow for the assessment of the TME or IC dynamics, and current technologies cannot detect ctDNA in all patients with metastatic cancer ( ).
The TME, the niche where malignant cells reside, comprises heterogeneous cellular components (including ICs and stromal cells), extracellular matrix structures, and soluble signaling molecules. This complex ecosystem has emerged as a focal point in cancer immunotherapy research, providing both mechanistic insights for therapeutic development and potential predictive biomarkers for ICI responsiveness. Immunologically active (“hot”) TMEs, characterized by abundant TILs, antigen-presenting DCs, and anti-tumoral macrophages, demonstrate heightened inflammatory signaling through elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and effector molecules. Such immunologically active microenvironments are generally associated with favorable responses to immunotherapies ( ). Conversely, immune-“cold” TMEs exhibiting sparse immune infiltration not only indicate compromised anti-tumor immunity but also create permissive conditions for tumor immune evasion mechanisms ( ). While TME profiling holds promise as a predictive biomarker, current methodological limitations in comprehensive microenvironment characterization hinder its clinical application. Key challenges include spatial heterogeneity quantification, dynamic cellular interaction mapping, and the establishment of standardized quantification protocols for microenvironmental components ( ).
Multiple new static, dynamic, and patient-specific biomarkers are currently under investigation, including tumor genetic signatures, antigen presentation profiles, TC-IC interactions, blood-based biomarkers, radiomics, and the gut microbiome. Optimize ideal multimodal biomarker models may enable the longitudinal assessment of tumor response or progression, allowing for the more accurate prediction of ICB efficacy ( ). However, these biomarkers currently have limited utility and/or require standardized testing approaches for broader pan-cancer application. Biomarker development strategies should be based on tumor pathogenesis and mechanisms of resistance to ICB therapy to maximize disease control, reduce adverse events, optimize costs, and enable widespread implementation in clinical practice, thereby offering new opportunities for improved clinical outcomes.

## Collaborative therapeutic approaches in ICB treatment
For most NSCLC patients with negative driver genes who cannot receive targeted therapy, the standard regimen for the first-line treatment of NSCLC is dual-drug chemotherapy ( ). Currently, immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy has been widely used in advanced or metastatic NSCLC patients without EGFR, ALK, or other driver gene mutations ( ).
 In previously untreated metastatic non-squamous NSCLC patients without EGFR or ALK mutations, the addition of pembrolizumab to platinum-based chemotherapy can significantly prolong OS and PFS ( ). In 2021, the US FDA approved the combination of cemiplimab (anti-PD-1) with platinum-based chemotherapy for the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC patients with high tumor expression of PD-L1 [a tumor proportion score (TPS) ≥50%]. In 2022, the FDA expanded the indication and approved cemiplimab combined with platinum-based chemotherapy as a first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC in adult patients, regardless of PD-L1 expression levels or tumor histology. The EMPOWER-Lung 3 study showed that compared with chemotherapy alone, the combination of cemiplimab with platinum-based chemotherapy significantly improved treatment outcomes in NSCLC, prolonging median survival (22 vs. 13 months) and significantly improving the 1-year OS rate (66% vs. 56%). PFS was also significantly prolonged (median PFS: 8 vs. 5 months), and the 1-year PFS rate more than doubled (38% vs. 16%) ( , ).
Neoadjuvant or adjuvant immunotherapy can improve outcomes for resectable NSCLC patients, and combining the benefits of both can enhance long-term prognosis during in perioperative treatment. The CheckMate 816 study showed that the addition of nivolumab to neoadjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved event-free survival (EFS) in patients with stage IB to IIIA NSCLC, and resulted in a higher percentage of patients with pathological complete remission compared to chemotherapy alone, without increasing the incidence of adverse events or compromising surgical feasibility ( ).
Compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone, perioperative durvalumab plus neoadjuvant chemotherapy resulted in greater EFS and pathological complete remission, and its safety was consistent with that of individual agents ( ). KEYNOTE-671 is a clinical study evaluating the efficacy of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy followed by resection and adjuvant pembrolizumab in early stage NSCLC patients. The results showed that compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone followed by surgery, the addition of perioperative pembrolizumab significantly improved the EFS, MPRs, and pathological complete remission of the resectable early stage NSCLC patients ( ).
The NEOTORCH study is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase III clinical trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of perioperative toripalimab combined with platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with resectable stage II/III NSCLC. The results showed that the addition of toripalimab to perioperative chemotherapy significantly improved the EFS of the resectable stage II or III NSCLC patients compared with chemotherapy alone, and this treatment strategy had a manageable safety profile ( ). Similarly, in resectable stage IIA or IIIB NSCLC patients, neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery and adjuvant therapy resulted in significantly longer EFS compared to chemotherapy alone ( ). However, the optimal strategy for combining checkpoint blockers with chemotherapy during perioperative therapy, as well as the optimal treatment duration, remain unclear.
The PACIFIC study is an international, multicenter, placebo-controlled, double-blind phase III clinical trial that compared durvalumab with placebo in patients with unresectable, stage III NSCLC whose disease had not progressed after platinum-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT). The results showed significant benefits in OS (42.9% versus 33.4%) and PFS (33.1% versus 19.0%), and no new safety signals were identified, establishing consolidation durvalumab after CRT (the PACIFIC regimen) as a new benchmark standard of care for patients with unresectable, stage III NSCLC ( , ).
First-line nivolumab plus ipilimumab has led to improved OS in patients with advanced-stage NSCLC. The CheckMate 9LA trial demonstrated that nivolumab + ipilimumab combined with two cycles of chemotherapy had an increased clinical benefit compared to chemotherapy alone, and had a favorable risk-benefit profile ( ).
Almost all patients eventually develop treatment resistance to EGFR tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (TKIs). The ORIENT31 study is the world’s first prospective phase III study to report significant benefits in EGFR-TKI-resistant non-squamous NSCLC patients. Specifically, it showed that compared to those treated with chemotherapy alone, the EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients who experienced disease progression during TKI treatment and were treated with Sintilimab plus bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305 plus chemotherapy (pemetrexed and cisplatin), and sintilimab combined with pemetrexed and cisplatin showed a significant and clinically meaningful improvement in terms of PFS, confirming that immunotherapy combined with anti-angiogenic therapy and chemotherapy was more efficacious ( , ).
New immunotherapy combination strategies need to be established for NSCLC patients who are resistant to PD-(L)1 targeted therapy, or those with PD-L1-low or PD-L1-negative tumors who are less likely to respond to anti-PD-(L)1 therapy. The combined inhibition of PD-(L)1/CTLA-4 has shown synergistic effects in preclinical models. In May 2020, the US FDA approved Opdivo (nivolumab) + Yervoy (ipilimumab) dual immunotherapy for the first-line treatment of advanced-stage NSCLC patients without EGFR or ALK gene driver mutations and with PD-L1 positivity (expression level ≥1%). This is the first FDA-approved dual immunotherapy. The CheckMate-227 research results showed that regardless of the PD-L1 expression levels, the first-line combination therapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab had better efficacy in NSCLC patients than chemotherapy ( , ). Conversely, in NSCLC with high PD-L1 expression, adding an anti-CTLA-4 agent to anti-PD-1 therapy does not improve clinical outcomes and is associated with increased toxicity ( ).
POSEIDON is a phase III, global, randomized, open-label study in which a limited course of anti-CTLA-4 (tremelimumab) therapy was administered in addition to anti-PD-L1 (durvalumab) therapy and chemotherapy. It provides insights into both the long-term efficacy and tolerability of this treatment in patients with first-line metastatic NSCLC ( ). PD-L1 plus CTLA-4 therapy could be an interesting treatment option for NSCLC patients with PD-L1 negative expression and/or specific molecular profile (STK11/KEAP1 mutated) ( ). Although radiotherapy can enhance anti-tumor immunity, it did not increase inhibition responses to combined PD-L1 (durvalumab) and CTLA-4 (tremelimumab) therapy in NSCLC patients resistant to PD-(L)1 therapy ( ). In conclusion, the combination of CTLA-4/PD-1 blockers and chemotherapy has a synergistic effect on activating immune responses against TCs.
Cytokines exert critical immunomodulatory effects by regulating the TME to overcome resistance to ICB. When analyzing the gene expression patterns of non-responsive T cells, researchers noted that these T cells had lower receptor expression for certain cytokines ( ). Additional cytokine treatment in mouse models of lung tumors enabled previously non-responsive T cells to fight tumors, suggesting that cytokine therapy may prevent or even reverse T-cell dysfunction ( ). Large clinical trials have shown that when combined with anti-PD-1 or PD-L1 antibodies (atezolizumab, nivolumab, or ipilimumab), the anti-interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) antibody tocilizumab, which is the most commonly used cytokine-targeting drug, boosts ICB efficacy and reduces irAEs ( ). However, cytokine therapy for humans is currently unsafe, as cytokines can cause serious side effects and trigger “cytokine storm” reactions, leading to severe fever, inflammation, fatigue, and nausea.
Some small-molecule inhibitors can also synergize with immunotherapy in NSCLC. APG-2575 (a BCL-2 inhibitor) activates NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) to promote antitumor polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), thereby enhancing the efficacy of NSCLC immunotherapy. Research results provide reliable evidence that combining APG-2575 with PD-1 monoclonal antibodies enhances NSCLC immunotherapy sensitivity ( ). Stimulator of interferon genes (STING) proteins play a critical role in anti-viral innate immunity, and type I interferon—the main downstream effector molecule in the STING signaling pathway—is a key cytokine that promotes anti-tumor immune responses ( ). In a mouse model, interferon (IFN)αR knockout accelerated tumor metastasis and dissemination ( ). Given their role in promoting immune activation, mobilization, and differentiation, novel STING agonists have been widely studied in the immunotherapy of various solid tumors ( - ). However, single-agent STING agonists have shown limited efficacy. Studies have found that STING pathway activation induces massive interferon secretion, leading to PD-L1 upregulation, which may promote immune evasion and metastasis. Therefore, clinical tumor research typically combines PD-(L)1 antibodies with STING agonists, achieving promising therapeutic effects ( - ). However, to date, no STING agonists have successfully entered phase III clinical trials. The mechanisms underlying resistance to STING agonist-based immunotherapy in cancer patients remain unclear and require further investigation. In addition, Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) inhibition seem to restore responsiveness to ICB in resistant tumors. Indeed, the JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib was shown to reduce neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios and immunosuppressive myeloid cell content, thereby enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in animal models of both solid and hematologic tumors ( ). In a phase II study of treatment-naive metastatic NSCLC patients with PD-L1 ≥50%, sequential administration of the JAK1 inhibitor itacitinib following two cycles of pembrolizumab achieved a best overall response rate of 67%, exceeding expectations for anti-PD-1 monotherapy ( ).
Another promising therapeutic approach is the combination of ICB with personalized anti-tumor vaccines, such as V940 (mRNA-4157). This strategy has improved EFS in the adjuvant treatment of melanoma, as shown in the INTERpath-002 study ( ), and is currently being evaluated in a phase III trial in the adjuvant setting for resected NSCLC.

## Discussion
To further understand the challenges and future prospects of ICB therapy, it is essential to explore the mechanisms underlying immune resistance and accumulate clinical experience in real-world settings. Most cases of resistance to ICIs are associated with disruptions in the T-cell activation process, such as insufficient antigen recognition, impaired T-cell trafficking and infiltration, and reduced cytotoxicity ( ). For example, TCs may lose the expression of MHC class I (MHC-I) molecules or acquire mutations in MHC-I components, leading to immune evasion and ICI resistance ( , ). It is speculated that resistance due to impaired interferon-gamma signaling may be primarily attributable to the downregulation of MHC-I expression ( ).
The TME is characteristically hypoxic and glucose-deprived. Glucose deficiency impairs T-cell activation and limits anti-tumor immunity ( ). Further, lactate accumulation in the hypoglycemic TME has been shown to upregulate PD-1 expression in Tregs, thereby promoting immune suppression and contributing to ICI resistance ( ). In addition, several immunosuppressive cells, including Tregs, TAMs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and cancer-associated fibroblasts, as well as certain immunosuppressive cytokines, also mediate resistance to ICIs. While anti-PD-1 therapy may inadvertently enhance Treg-mediated immunosuppression, CTLA-4 inhibitors have been shown to deplete Tregs ( ). Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) contributes to immune suppression by promoting Treg expansion and inhibiting the function of effector T cells and antigen-presenting DCs ( , ). VEGF-A further exacerbates immune exhaustion by increasing PD-1 and other inhibitory checkpoint expression on CD8+ T cells ( ). The combined inhibition of VEGF and ICI pathways has demonstrated superior efficacy ( , ), and the dual blockade of TGF-β and VEGF signaling has been found to reduce Treg infiltration and restore sensitivity to anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapies ( ).
PD-L1 is not only expressed on the TC membrane but can also be found in the cytoplasm, exosomes, and even the nucleus. Further, the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression in tumors remains one of the main reasons for suboptimal responses to anti-PD-L1 therapy ( ).
Understanding both primary and acquired resistance mechanisms is key to the development of predictive biomarkers and novel therapeutic strategies with improved efficacy. Numerous small-molecule agents targeting immune checkpoints, cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways, metabolic regulators, oncogenic signals, immune-related kinases, and pattern recognition receptors are currently being explored to overcome resistance in patients who do not respond to or have progressed after ICI treatment ( ).
In clinical practice, the use of ICIs in NSCLC faces several challenges. First, due to the lack of comprehensive predictive indicators and standardized criteria for treatment response, it remains difficult to accurately determine the optimal initiation and discontinuation time points for ICB therapy. Second, early stage radiologic assessments cannot reliably differentiate between true progression, pseudoprogression, and hyperprogression, which could potentially lead to premature or delayed discontinuation of treatment. Third, managing the large proportion of patients who develop drug resistance, along with mitigating irAEs, remains a pressing clinical need. Additionally, special patient groups need to be taken into consideration. For the elderly, who constitute an increasing proportion of the NSCLC population with the aging of society, multiple studies have found that those in good physical condition can benefit from immunotherapy. This provides evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of immunotherapy in fit elderly lung cancer patients ( , ). Conversely, immunotherapy efficacy appears diminished in frail patients ( ), with some deriving no benefit from ICIs and experiencing significantly shorter survival compared to those with better PS ( ). Approximately 14% of NSCLC patients have concurrent AIDs, most commonly rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and polyarthritis ( ). In this population, the efficacy of ICIs is similar to that in patients without AIDs, and irAEs can be effectively managed, permanent discontinuation due to drug toxicity is uncommon ( - ). Immunotherapy also demonstrates similar safety and efficacy in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive NSCLC patients compared to the non-HIV population, without increasing the incidence of irAEs ( - ). Managing immunotherapy for NSCLC in these special populations is a complex and highly challenging task, requiring individualized, meticulous care and multidisciplinary collaboration. The paramount principle is a thorough assessment of the risk-benefit ratio, aiming to maximize anti-tumor efficacy while minimizing additional treatment-related risks.
Over the past decade, the clinical indications for ICIs have gradually become more precise. However, long-term irAEs present unique challenges that require further investigation. Management of irAEs is particularly important. Currently, the severity of irAEs is classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) into grades 1 to 5. Grade 1 represents mild toxicity, while grade 3 and above are considered “severe” toxicities; grade 5 denotes death ( ). During ICI treatment, the possibility of an irAE should be considered whenever a patient develops new symptoms. Although there is currently no standard diagnostic test for irAEs, diagnosis typically involves a detailed patient history, symptom assessment, comprehensive physical examination, and laboratory and imaging studies to rule out other conditions with similar presentations. Therefore, diagnosis is usually achieved through exclusion ( ). Generally, for grade 1 irAEs, immunotherapy can continue; for grade 2, treat with topical or systemic (oral) low-dose corticosteroids; ICI therapy may be paused; for grade 3, intravenous or oral corticosteroid treatment is required, and if the condition does not improve, other immunosuppressants can be used. The risk/benefit ratio should be evaluated to determine whether the patient can resume treatment. For grade 4, systemic intravenous corticosteroid treatment is necessary. For patients whose condition does not improve, immunosuppressants can be used, and immunotherapy should be permanently discontinued. Most irAEs can be controlled and reversed by temporarily suspending the drug administration ± corticosteroids ( , ). Corticosteroids are a cornerstone of irAE treatment. Systemic corticosteroids are typically the first-line therapy for irAEs. For patients who do not respond to corticosteroids or cannot tolerate them, immunosuppressive agents such as infliximab, methotrexate, or cyclophosphamide may be considered ( ). In cases of steroid-refractory myocarditis, immunosuppressants including mycophenolate, infliximab, antithymocyte globulin, abatacept, and alemtuzumab have been used ( , ). Predictive biomarkers are critical for patient stratification and therapeutic efficacy evaluation, representing a vital link between basic research and clinical application. These biomarkers reflect various biological dimensions of TME heterogeneity and are closely correlated with treatment response. Moreover, the local immune contexture evolves dynamically, transitioning from a baseline immune state to a treatment-induced immune state. Postoperative immune profiling thus offers valuable insights into prognosis prediction, treatment responsiveness, and the discovery of novel therapeutic targets.

## Conclusions
ICBs play an irreplaceable role in the treatment of advanced NSCLC. Current research is focused on optimizing their therapeutic efficacy, minimizing toxicities, accurately assessing treatment response, and rationally designing combination regimens. However, the intricate immune equilibrium and the high degree of tumor heterogeneity remain substantial obstacles to immunotherapy for NSCLC. To improve outcomes in NSCLC, it is imperative to gain deeper insights into the mechanisms of ICB resistance, identify robust predictive biomarkers for novel treatments, and stratify patients accordingly. The ultimate goal is to develop precise immunotherapeutic strategies that maximize the clinical benefit while minimizing the adverse effects, thus transforming the management and prognosis of NSCLC patients.
