## Abstract

## Background
Enhancing antigen cross-presentation is essential for the development of a tumor neoantigen vaccine. One approach is to stimulate antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to uptake neoantigens. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTb) contains pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) recognized by APCs and adhesion molecules that facilitate MTb invasion of APCs. Therefore, we suggest using MTb as a carrier to enhance APC phagocytosis of neoantigens, thereby promoting antigen cross-presentation.

## Methods
The successful preparation of the MTb carrier (phMTb) was confirmed through electron and confocal microscopy. Fluorescence microscopy was used to detect PAMPs and adhesion molecules on phMTb as well as to observe its role in aiding dendritic cells (DCs) in antigen uptake into endosomes or lysosomes. Flow cytometry was used to assess the retention of PAMPs and adhesion molecules on phMTb, investigate antigen uptake by DCs, evaluate their activation and maturation status, examine the presentation of tumor neoantigens, and analyze immune cells in draining lymph nodes and tumor tissues. The efficacy of phMTb vaccine formulations in combination with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody therapy was assessed using the MC38 mouse tumor models. Adverse effects were evaluated through H&E staining of major organs, assessment of reproductive capability and detection of biochemical indices.

## Results
The engineered porous hollow phMTb carrier successfully encapsulated model tumor neoantigens, with or without the adjuvant CpG. The phMTb retained PAMPs and adhesion molecules on its surface, similar to the parental MTb, thereby enhancing DC uptake of phMTb and its formulations containing tumor neoantigens and CpG. Vaccines formulated with phMTb facilitated DC maturation, activation, cross-presentation of tumor neoantigens, and promoted migration of phMTb-laden DCs to lymph nodes, enhancing effector and memory CD8+ T lymphocyte function. In murine tumor models, immunization with phMTb-formulated neoantigen vaccines elicited a robust tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocyte immune response with minimal adverse effects. Additionally, vaccination with phMTb-formulated neoantigen vaccines effectively reversed the tumor’s immune-suppressive microenvironment. Concurrent administration of the PD-1 antibody with the phMTb-formulated neoantigen vaccine exhibited significant synergistic therapeutic effects.

## Conclusions
The results of our study highlight the potential clinical translation of personalized tumor neoantigen vaccines using the phMTb carrier.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Tumor neoantigen vaccines have demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials; however, their therapeutic potential is constrained by the rapid degradation of short peptides and limited cross-presentation efficiency, which hinders the advancement of personalized tumor immunotherapy.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
This study introduces an innovative tumor vaccine carrier that enhances DC uptake of tumor neoantigens, promoting DC maturation, activation, and cross-presentation of these antigens. This cascade triggers the development of tumor neoantigen-specific CD8+ effector cells, counteracting their exhaustion and the immune-suppressive tumor microenvironment, ultimately improving anti-tumor efficacy, especially when combined with immune checkpoint inhibition therapy.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE, OR POLICY
This study presents a novel approach for future clinical trials of personalized tumor neoantigen vaccines, aiming to enhance the outcomes of cancer immunotherapy.

## Introduction
 Tumor neoantigens, derived from genetic mutations in tumor cells, represent promising targets for personalized cancer immunotherapy. Advances in gene sequencing have facilitated the precise identification of these antigens, enabling the development of tailored cancer vaccines that elicit specific immune responses against cancer while minimizing autoimmune reactions. , A significant challenge lies in the rapid degradation of short peptides containing these neoantigens, limiting their capacity to provoke robust immune responses. Therefore, the transformative potential of tumor neoantigen vaccines underscores the necessity for further research to optimize their integration with other immunotherapies in cancer treatment. Adaptive immunity relies on the interaction between dendritic cells (DCs) and T lymphocytes. DCs are pivotal in antigen presentation via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. MHC I presents intracellular antigens from viruses or tumors, activating CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), while MHC II presents extracellular antigens to engage CD4+ T helper cells. Recent studies have underscored the significance of antigen cross-presentation, facilitating the stimulation of CD8+ T lymphocytes via MHC I by specific extracellular antigens. This process is crucial for eliciting immune responses against viruses that do not infect antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and tumor antigens that are not synthesized by DCs. Therefore, enhancing tumor neoantigen cross-presentation is vital for the effective development of tumor neoantigen vaccines, as these vaccines consist of extracellular neoantigens that require uptake and processing by DCs for cross-presentation.
Pathogenic bacteria have retained evolutionarily conserved molecules, referred to as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), to ensure their survival, which are typically absent in host cells. The majority of immune cells in the human body express pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to detect PAMPs and recognize invading pathogens. On recognition of PAMPs, PRRs activate immune cells, initiating inflammation and specific as well as nonspecific immune responses. Therefore, the utilization of engineered porous hollow bacteria, particularly rod-shaped bacteria containing tumor antigens, may enhance the processing and presentation of these antigens by DCs. 
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTb), the causative agent of tuberculosis, is an intracellular bacterium with adhesion molecules that bind to host immune cell receptors, facilitating phagocytosis. , Recent studies have identified membrane-bound proteins, such as Erp and FadD, that specifically interact with macrophage receptors. Since macrophages and DCs originate from bone marrow monocytes and MTb infects DCs similarly to macrophages, we hypothesize that leveraging MTb’s PAMPs and adhesion molecules could enhance DC recognition and phagocytosis, potentially improving tumor vaccine efficacy by eliciting more robust tumor-specific immune responses through conventional DCs.
In this study, we engineered MTb as a porous hollow vaccine carrier (phMTb) that effectively retained PAMPs and adhesion molecules. The carrier was loaded with tumor neoantigens and CpG adjuvant, demonstrating enhanced neoantigen-specific immunity in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistic investigations revealed that phMTb promotes DCs maturation, antigen cross-presentation, and migration, and modulates immune microenvironments. This approach offers a promising strategy for tumor neoantigen vaccine development.

## Cell lines and cell culture
The 4T1 cell line, a murine breast cancer line expressing OVA and luciferase, and the MC38 colorectal cancer line were procured from ATCC (MD, USA) and cultured for less than 10 generations. They were sustained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere, and subjected to specific reagents as required for research purposes.

## Construction and validation of engineered phMTb for loading tumor neoantigens and adjuvant CpG
The phMTb was prepared with tumor neoantigens, with or without a CpG adjuvant. The MTb strain H37Ra (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA) was irradiated with 10 Gy and treated with 95% ethanol for 1 hour, followed by 1.5% Triton X-100 for 5 hours. After filtering through a 0.22-μm membrane and dialyzing in ultrapure water at 4°C for 5 hours, the bacteria were electrophoresed at 100 V for 2 hours. The cells were rinsed twice with ultrapure water, and tumor neoantigens (H-2Kb OVA SIINFEKL or H-2Db ADPGK) were loaded into phMTb with or without CpG1826, forming phMTb/Cp, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe formulations. These were co-incubated with phMTb under vacuum for 12 hours, and any unbound components were washed out. The morphologies of the formulations were examined using scanning and transmission electron microscopy. The phMTb, neoantigens, and CpG were labeled with red, green, and blue fluorescent colors, respectively, and analyzed for spatial relationships and co-localization using confocal microscopy (FV1000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## Isolation of single cells from lymph nodes and tumor tissues
Cells were isolated from lymph nodes or tumor tissues using established protocols. Tissues were homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using a tissue homogenizer and filtered through gauze to remove large fragments. The homogenate was digested with pancreatin at 37°C for 4 hours, with DNase I added to prevent cell aggregation. After digestion, culture medium supplemented with FBS was added, and the suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and cells were washed with PBS to remove residual enzymes, yielding a preparation suitable for subsequent analyses such as flow cytometry.

## Flow cytometry analysis
Flow cytometry was employed to examine cancer cell growth and the characteristics of bone marrow–derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) and lymphocytes from lymph nodes and tumors. Cells were collected from cultured BMDCs or isolated tissues and labeled with fluorescently conjugated antibodies at dilutions of 1:200–1:500. Fluorescent dyes 7-AAD and CFSE were used to mark specific cell populations. After a 30-min incubation at 4°C, cells were filtered, centrifuged at 1000 rpm, and resuspended in PBS for analysis using the CyFlow Cube 6 system. FlowJo V.10 was used to analyze flow cytometry data, with gating strategies to exclude debris and select cell types based on forward and side scatter. Dead cells were eliminated via 7-AAD staining. For tumor analysis, CD45-negative cells were selected, while CD45-positive cells were used for immune cell analysis, with detailed gating procedures outlined in the related experimental methods.

## Detection of peptidoglycan or PstS-1 on the surface of phMTb
The presence of representative PAMPs, peptidoglycan, and the adhesion molecule PstS-1 on phMTb and parental MTb surfaces was examined using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-peptidoglycan (1:500) or anti-PstS-1 antibodies (1:400), washed with PBS, and subsequently stained with FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:300) or Texas Red-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:300) for 1 hour at 4°C. Fluorescence images were captured using confocal microscopy and analyzed with FlowJo software to obtain quantitative data and images.

## Generation of bone marrow–derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) and characterization of their maturation, activation, and antigen presentation
BMDCs were generated from 8 to 10 week-old female C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice. Bone marrow cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with IL-4 and GM-CSF to promote differentiation. Erythrocytes were lysed, and non-adherent cells were discarded on days 2 and 4. Adherent cell clusters were transferred on day 6, and after 12 days, non-adherent cells with characteristic DC morphology were collected. BMDCs were then exposed to various vaccine formulations for 24 hours. Following exposure, cells were harvested and labeled with antibodies against DC maturation and antigen presentation markers (CD86, CD80, CD40, SIINFEKL-MHC I, MHC II). Flow cytometry was used to analyze marker expression levels, and ELISA kits measured the secretion of activation markers TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6.

## Development of anti-serum for Escherichia coli and Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Anti-Escherichia coli (Ecoli) and anti-MTb serum were prepared using established protocols. Non-pregnant female rabbits were immunized with Ecoli (strain Seattle 1946; ATCC, MD, USA) or MTb mixed with complete (CFA) or incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (FIA) in a 1:1 ratio. They received subcutaneous injections of 2.0 mL (CFA with 1 mg of antigen), 2.0 mL (FIA with 1 mg of antigen), and 1 mL (FIA with 0.5 mg of antigen) on days 1, 14, and 21, respectively. Blood was collected 7 days after the third immunization, allowed to clot at 4℃, and serum was separated via centrifugation. It was stored at 4℃ overnight, preserved with 0.01% thiomersal, divided into sterile bottles, and stored at −20℃. The anti-Ecoli serum served as a blocker for PAMPs, while the anti-MTb serum blocked both PAMPs and MTb adhesion molecules in experiments exploring their relationships with vaccine components, DCs, and types of immunization.

## Analysis of phagocytosis uptake of different vaccine formulations by bone marrow–derived dendritic cells (BMDCs)
The capacity of BMDCs to internalize various vaccine formulations was evaluated using a standard protocol. To investigate direct vaccine uptake, the formulations (CpNe, MTb, phMTb, phMTb/Cp, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe) were labeled with CFSE-green and co-cultured with mouse-isolated BMDCs for 24 hours. Flow cytometry measured the green fluorescence intensity of CD45-positive BMDCs, indicating vaccine internalization. To assess neoantigen SIINFEKL engulfment, SIINFEKL was labeled with FITC (green) and the formulations with CFSE-red. After a 24-hour incubation, flow cytometry identified CD45-positive BMDCs that were double-positive for both labels. The impact of PAMPs and MTb adhesion molecules on phMTb/CpNe phagocytosis was analyzed by co-incubating BMDCs with anti-Ecoli serum to inhibit PAMPs or anti-MTb serum to inhibit both PAMPs and adhesion molecules.

## Immunofluorescence observation
Immunofluorescence microscopy was used to examine neoantigen uptake and processing in vaccine formulations within endosomes and lysosomes and to identify immune-suppressive cells in tumor tissues. The model neoantigen SIINFEKL, conjugated with FITC, was co-cultured with BMDCs for 5 hours. BMDCs were stained with anti-EEA1 (1:500) and Texas Red-goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500). Lysosomes were labeled with LysoTracker Red (1:500) for 30 min at 4℃. Tumor sections from vaccinated mice were immunochemically stained with anti-CD11b (1:400) and anti-Gr-1 (1:500) for myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and anti-CD4 (1:300) and anti-Foxp3 (1:500) for regulatory T cells (Tregs).

## Evaluation of the maturity, activation, and antigen presentation capabilities of bone marrow–derived dendritic cells (BMDCs)
The maturation, activation, and antigen presentation of BMDCs were evaluated using flow cytometry and ELISA. BMDCs were treated with various vaccine formulations for 24 hours, and the maturation markers CD86, CD80, and CD40 were analyzed. The cells were labeled with fluorescently-tagged antibodies at 4°C for 30 min, and CD45+ cells were isolated for fluorescence intensity measurement. The BMDC culture supernatants were collected to quantify IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels using ELISA kits. For antigen presentation assessment, the BMDCs were exposed to vaccine formulations and treated with anti-SIINFEKL and anti-MHC II antibodies. The CD45+ BMDCs were then analyzed for MHC I and MHC II fluorescence intensity. The FlowJo software was used to extract specific fluorescence values and images from FCS files.

## Detection of dendritic cell (DC) migration into localized draining lymph nodes
The vaccine formulations were labeled with CFSE-green. They were then administered individually to syngeneic mice through subcutaneous injection on their backs. After 24 hours, the lymph nodes near the injection site were collected and processed into single cells. These single cells were stained with a PE-conjugated anti-CD11c antibody at a concentration of 1:400 (CD11c is a marker for DCs). Subsequently, flow cytometry was used to analyze the percentage of 7-AAD-negative live CD45+CD11c+CFSE+ DCs. These cells are believed to have taken up various vaccine components labeled with CFSE and migrated to the local lymph nodes.

## Evaluation of immune cell subtypes in draining lymph nodes and tumor tissues
Individual cells from draining lymph nodes (DLNs) and tumor tissues were analyzed by flow cytometry. CD8+ T lymphocytes specific to neoantigens SIINFEKL and ADPGK tetramers, along with exhausted T cells expressing TIM-3 and PD-1, were identified. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in the suppressive microenvironment, including MDSCs (CD11b+Gr-1+), Tregs (CD4+Foxp3+CD25+), and M2-type tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs; CD11b+F4/80+CD206+), were also examined. Fluorescent-labeled antibodies were diluted 1:200–500 and incubated with cells for 30 min at 4°C. Flow cytometry analysis gated 7-AAD-negative live cells and CD45-positive immune cells, distinguishing between cell types and subtypes. Cell percentages were quantitatively analyzed using FlowJo software.

## Establishment of murine tumor models, vaccination, and observation
Murine tumor models were established using MC38 and 4T1-OVA tumor cells in 6–8 week-old female C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice. For therapeutic models, 5×106 MC38 or 4T1-OVA cells were injected subcutaneously into the right flank, while 1×106 4T1-luciferase cells were administered intravenously for metastatic models. Mice were allocated to five groups of ten, receiving subcutaneous vaccine formulations at a consistent dose of 15.5 nmoL of ADPGK or SIINFEKL, and optionally 2.3 nmoL of CpG, on days 1 and 8 post-tumor inoculation. In the metastatic model, BALB/c mice received identical vaccines on days −7 and 0, preceding 4T1-luciferase injection on day 0. Tumor progression was monitored triennially using a portable measuring device, while metastatic fluorescence was evaluated via D-fluorescein potassium injection and captured through an IVIS Lumina II system. All experimental procedures were approved by Hainan Medical University’s Ethics Committee (HYLL-2022–086) and adhered to Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments guidelines.

## Assessment of adverse side effects
C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were used to study potential side effects of the vaccine over 180 days. The mice were not injected with tumor cells and received various vaccine formulations following a preventive metastasis protocol. Monthly records were kept on body weight, lifespan, well-being (fur condition, eating habits, behavior), and fertility that was assessed by breeding female mice after the second vaccination, observing litter size and birth timing. After the observation period, tissues from key organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected, fixed in formalin, and analyzed microscopically after H&E staining. Blood samples were taken from the tail vein and tested using a small animal biochemical analyzer. Bone marrow toxicity was evaluated through white and red blood cell levels, and major organ toxicity was assessed based on creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels.

## Evaluation of cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) immunity
To assess the efficacy of CTLs in eliminating tumor cells, splenocytes (effector cells) were examined against MC38 or 4T1 tumor cells (target cells) using a CytoTox 96 cytotoxicity assay. The experiment was carried out in 96-well plates with effector to target ratios ranging from 5:1 to 40:1 and incubated for 6 hours at 37°C in 5% CO2. Following incubation, the samples were transferred to a fresh plate and further incubated for 50 min at 4°C in darkness. The reaction was halted with a solution, and the optical density at 492 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The percentage of target cell mortality at each ratio was calculated following the established protocol. An in vivo method was used to evaluate CTL response by injecting CFSE-labeled MC38 or 4T1 tumor cells into C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice treated with different vaccine formulations. Following euthanasia of the mice after 24 hours, proliferated tumor and immune cells from the abdominal cavity were isolated and stained with an anti-CD45 antibody. Analysis of MC38 or 4T1 cell proliferation and proportions was conducted, with CD45-positive immune cells excluded. Flow cytometry was employed to assess the percentages of CD45-CFSE+ MC38 or 4T1 cells. Additionally, we analyzed neoantigen-specific SIINFEKL or ADPGK tetramers in CD8+ T cells derived from tumor tissues of diverse vaccinated mice. Using flow cytometry, we determined the live CD45+CD8+Tetramer+ cell population within the tumor tissue, with exclusion of 7-AAD positive cells. The scarcity of CD45+CD8+Tetramer+ cells in phMTb and phMTb/Cp-immunized tissues suggested potential non-specific staining. Consequently, flow cytometry was used to evaluate the CD45+CD8+Tetramer+ cell subset that produced IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 in tissues from mice immunized with CpNe, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe.

## Examination of the antitumor effectiveness of combining phMTb/Ne immunization with anti-PD-1 antibody therapy
In this study, vaccination with phMTb/CpNe significantly reduced tumors, influencing the combination therapy results. We opted to use phMTb/Ne to assess the effectiveness of combining phMTb/Ne immunization with anti-PD-1 treatment. C57BL/6 mice were divided into four groups: normal saline (NS) only, phMTb/Ne (immunized on days −7, 0, and 7), anti-PD-1 (received antibody six times starting on day 2), and phMTb/Ne+anti-PD-1 (received both treatments). All groups received subcutaneous injections of 5×106 MC38 cells on day 1, with tumor volumes measured using a TM900 device.

## Calculation of synergistic indexes
The synergistic index (SI) was used to evaluate the efficacy of combination therapy in inhibiting tumor growth, as reported in a previous study. Tumor volume data from the NS, phMTb/Ne, anti-PD-1, and phMTb/Ne+anti-PD-1 groups were analyzed to calculate the SI values. The relative ratio of tumor volume (RRTV) for the phMTb/Ne, anti-PD-1, and phMTb/Ne+anti-PD-1 groups was determined by dividing their mean values by the mean value of the NS group. The expected RRTV for the phMTb/Ne+anti-PD-1 group was calculated by multiplying the RRTV of the phMTb/Ne and anti-PD-1 groups. The SI value was then calculated by dividing the expected RRTV of the phMTb/Ne+anti-PD-1 group by the observed RRTV. An SI value greater than 1 indicates a synergistic anti-tumor effect.

## Heatmap construction and statistical analysis
The heatmap graphs in this study were created using the pheatmap function from the R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap). Experimental data were presented as mean±SD. Statistical analysis involved unpaired t-tests for two-group comparisons and one- or two-way ANOVA with Tukey correction for multiple groups, conducted in GraphPad Prism 10. Significance was set at p values<0.05. Lowercase letters above columns indicated significant differences among groups, with identical letters showing no statistical difference.

## Results

## Development and validation of porous hollow Mycobacterium tuberculosis for encapsulating tumor neoantigens and CpG adjuvant
The preparation process ( ) of phMTb with tumor neoantigens and CpG adjuvant involves perforating MTb, removing intracellular contents while preserving the scaffold, and integrating the antigens and adjuvant per established protocols. The formulations are designated phMTb/Cp for CpG, phMTb/Ne for neoantigens, and phMTb/CpNe for both. Scanning electron microscopy revealed noticeable pores in phMTb compared with wMTb ( ). Transmission electron microscopy indicated that phMTb had cell wall discontinuities, fewer organelles, and reduced cytoplasm ( , left and middle panels). Loading phMTb with CpG and neoantigens increased cytoplasmic content but still lacked distinct organelles ( , right panel). Successful loading was confirmed by colocalization using confocal microscopy ( ). Retention of PAMPs and adhesion molecules on phMTb was validated through labeling peptidoglycan ( ) and PstS-1 ( ), showing similar fluorescence intensities in phMTb and wMTb. However, flow cytometry showed decreased fluorescence for peptidoglycan ( ) and PstS-1 ( ) in phMTb compared with wMTb, but no significant differences were found between phMTb and wMTb. These results confirm the effective fabrication of phMTb carriers, integrating tumor neoantigens and CpG while preserving key PAMPs and adhesion molecules.
 

## Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and adhesion molecules on phMTb help dendritic cells (DCs) take up tumor neoantigens
Challenges arose in fully blocking PAMPs and adhesion molecules on MTb due to their diversity. To tackle this, serum against Ecoli were developed for PAMP blockade, while serum against MTb targeted both PAMPs and adhesion molecules. Mouse BMDCs were differentiated and cultured for 24 hours with various combinations of CpG and neoantigen SIINFEKL (CpNe), MTb, phMTb, different phMTb formulations, and anti-serum. Flow cytometry showed a significant increase in CD45-positive and CFSE-positive BMDCs internalizing MTb and all phMTb formulations compared with CpNe ( and ). There were no clear distinctions in internalization rates among MTb, phMTb, and phMTb/CpNe ( and ). Co-incubation with anti-Ecoli or anti-MTb serum notably reduced internalized cells, especially with anti-MTb ( and ). BMDCs treated with phMTb or phMTb/CpNe exhibited similar fluorescence intensity in microscopy ( ) and flow cytometry analyses ( ). Furthermore, FITC-labeled SIINFEKL and CFSE-labeled CpNe and phMTb/CpNe were co-cultured with BMDCs, indicating that FITC+CFSE+ BMDCs with phMTb/CpNe were about twice as abundant as those with CpNe alone ( ), with significant reductions when co-incubated with anti- serum ( ). Efficient neoantigen uptake was confirmed by co-localization of FITC-labeled SIINFEKL with endosomes (EEA1, and ) and lysosomes (Lyso, and ) in cells incubated with phMTb/Ne or phMTb/CpNe, but not with phMTb. In conclusion, these findings suggest that phMTb enhances the uptake of encapsulated tumor neoantigens by BMDCs through interactions with PAMPs and adhesion molecules.
 Some Mycobacteria strains can downregulate MHC I molecules on epithelial and cancer cells. The effect of phMTb formulations on MHC I expression in 4T1 and MC38 cells was studied. Co-culturing 4T1 ( ) or MC38 cells ( ) with CpNe, phMTb, phMTb/Cp, phMTb/Ne, or phMTb/CpNe did not alter MHC-I levels in either cell line.

## PAMPs and adhesion molecules on phMTb drive dendritic cell (DC) maturation, activation, and cross-presentation of tumor neoantigens
Mature and activated DCs express costimulatory molecules CD86, CD80, and CD40 and secrete TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6. Flow cytometry showed that co-culturing BMDCs with phMTb/Cp, phMTb/Ne, or phMTb/CpNe increased CD86 ( and ), CD80 ( and ), and CD40 ( and ) levels compared with CpNe, with the highest expression in those treated with phMTb/Cp or phMTb/CpNe ( and ). Blocking with anti-MTb serum significantly reduced these markers in BMDCs co-incubated with phMTb/CpNe ( and ). ELISA showed increased TNF-α ( ), IFN-γ ( ), and IL-6 ( ) secretion in BMDCs co-cultured with phMTb/Cp, phMTb/Ne, or phMTb/CpNe compared with CpNe, with peak levels in cells treated with phMTb/Cp or phMTb/CpNe. Anti-MTb serum blockage lowered cytokine secretion in BMDCs co-cultured with phMTb/CpNe ( ). These findings highlight the crucial role of PAMPs and adhesion molecules on phMTb in DC maturation and activation, especially in combination with CpG.
After processing neoantigens, DCs present epitopes with MHC I molecules to CD8+ T lymphocytes, leading to CTL generation. Flow cytometry showed significantly higher levels of SIINFEKL-MHC I complexes in BMDCs co-cultured with CpNe, phMTb/Ne, or phMTb/CpNe compared with those with phMTb or phMTb/Cp ( and ). BMDCs with phMTb/Ne or phMTb/CpNe had higher expression than those with CpNe, peaking in phMTb/CpNe ( and ). Blocking PAMPs and adhesion molecules with anti-MTb serum reduced SIINFEKL-MHC I expression ( ). Additionally, MHC II expression increased in BMDCs co-cultured with phMTb formulations compared with CpNe, particularly in phMTb/Cp or phMTb/CpNe ( and ). Blocking PAMPs on phMTb/CpNe also decreased MHC II expression ( ). Taken together, these data indicate that phMTb enhances MHC I cross-presentation and MHC II antigen presentation in BMDCs.

## Vaccination with PhMTb/CpNe boosts dendritic cell (DC) migration and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) production in draining lymph nodes
In cross-presentation, DCs in DLNs process exogenous antigens from vaccines to activate CTLs against tumor antigens. To study this, various phMTb formulations were labeled with CFSE dye and injected subcutaneously into mice. After 24 hours, single cells from DLNs were isolated for flow cytometry to quantify CD11c+CFSE+ cells ( and ). The results showed a significant increase in CD11c+CFSE+ DCs in DLNs after administering all four phMTb formulations compared with CpNe ( ). Formulations with the adjuvant Cp, such as phMTb/Cp and phMTb/CpNe, exhibited higher levels of CD11c+CFSE+ DCs than those without Cp ( ). These findings indicate that phMTb formulations enhance DC migration, transporting the vaccine to the DLNs.
 To study the impact of phMTb on tumor neoantigen presentation to CD8+ T cells by DCs, flow cytometry analysis of CD45+ cells in DLNs was performed ( and ). Formulations without neoantigens, such as phMTb and phMTb/Cp, showed significantly low levels of CD8+H-2Kb+ cells, while those with neoantigens, including CpNe, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe, had significantly higher levels ( ). Immunization with phMTb/CpNe resulted in the highest CD8+H-2Kb+ level, followed by phMTb/Ne, compared with CpNe ( ). Statistical analysis indicated significant differences among these formulations ( ). Treatment with FTY720 to block CD8+ T cell egress increased CD8+Tetramer+ cells in lymph nodes for phMTb/Ne and phMTb/CpNe, but not for phMTb alone ( ). Furthermore, FTY720 led to further enhancement but also significant decreases in CD8+Tetramer+ levels when co-administered with phMTb/Ne and phMTb/CpNe ( ). Taken together, these data indicate that immunization with phMTb/CpNe was the most effective in enhancing CD8+ T cell cross-presentation.
Exhaustion was noted in tumor-specific CD8+ T cells. Flow cytometry assessed stemness markers in CD8+H-2Kb+ and CD44+CD8+H-2Kb+ memory T cells to evaluate the effectiveness of a phMTb-based tumor neoantigen vaccine against this exhaustion ( ). The results showed distinct profiles in cells vaccinated with phMTb or phMTb/Cp lacking the tumor neoantigen compared with those with neoantigen ( ). The lower detection in the former is likely due to the absence of Ne-specific CD8+H-2Kb+ T cells ( ). Exhausted CD8+TIM-3+PD-1+ cells were mainly found in formulations containing the neoantigen, while TIM-3+PD-1+ T cells decreased in phMTb/Ne and phMTb/CpNe compared with CpNe vaccinations ( ). Immunization with phMTb/CpNe significantly reduced exhausted TIM-3+PD-1+CD8+H-2Kb+ T cells ( ) and increased exhausted CD8+H-2Kb+CD44+TCF1+ memory T cells ( ) compared with CpNe and phMTb/Ne. These results indicate that phMTb/CpNe immunization boosts cross-presentation of tumor neoantigens to CD8+ T cells, thereby reducing their exhaustion.

## Vaccination with phMTb/CpNe shows strong anti-tumor effects with minimal side effects
Tumor models were created in BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice using OVA-4T1 breast cancer cells that express the neoantigen SIINFEKL and MC38 colorectal cells with ADPGK, as shown in . Anti-tumor responses were evaluated by immunizing mice with CpNe and various phMTb formulations. Tumor volume was measured every 3 days, with euthanizing occurring when tumors neared 3000 mm³. On day 18, tumor sizes were recorded in , with a heatmap in . Formulations lacking neoantigens, such as phMTb and phMTb/Cp, had little effect on tumor volume ( ). In contrast, formulations containing neoantigens (CpNe, phMTb/Ne, phMTb/CpNe) led to varying tumor reductions, with phMTb/CpNe resulting in the smallest tumors and notable inter-group differences ( ). Additionally, a metastatic model using luciferase-modified 4T1 cells assessed the effectiveness of phMTb formulations against metastasis ( ). Results showed that mice immunized with phMTb or phMTb/Cp had increased lung metastasis rates, while those receiving CpNe, phMTb/Ne, or phMTb/CpNe showed lower rates, especially phMTb/CpNe ( ). In conclusion, vaccination with phMTb/CpNe was significantly more effective than CpNe or phMTb/Ne, highlighting its clinical potential.
 This study examined the adverse reactions of different phMTb formulations in female BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice over a 180-day postimmunization period. Parameters assessed included lifespan, fur condition, behavior, body weight, fertility, histological analysis of organ samples, and blood analysis for white and red blood cell levels, creatinine, AST, ALT, and LDH. Results showed no significant differences in these parameters among groups immunized with CpNe, phMTb, phMTb/Cp, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe ( ). In conclusion, the phMTb/CpNe vaccine did not induce detectable adverse reactions.

## Vaccination with phMTb/CpNe boosts tumor neoantigen-specific cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) immunity
Cellular immunity, especially CTLs, is crucial in the immune response against tumors. The LDH release assay was first used to measure neoantigen-specific CTL responses with splenocytes as effector cells and tumor cells as target cells. Splenocytes from mice immunized with formulations containing neoantigens, such as CpNe, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe, showed significantly higher cytotoxicity against MC38 or 4T1 cells than those immunized with phMTb and phMTb/Cp ( ). Notably, mice vaccinated with phMTb/Ne and phMTb/CpNe exhibited superior cytotoxicity compared with the CpNe group, with phMTb/CpNe showing the highest activity ( ). The cytotoxic effects of phMTb formulations on tumor cells were also evaluated in vivo by injecting CFSE-labeled MC38 or 4T1 cells into immunized mice. After 24 hours, peritoneal cells were analyzed by flow cytometry ( ). The results revealed that mice immunized with phMTb had higher counts of CD45-CFSE+ tumor cells compared with those with CpNe, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe. Mice with phMTb/Ne and phMTb/CpNe had fewer CD45-CFSE+ tumor cells than those receiving CpNe, especially in the phMTb/CpNe group ( and ). Additionally, CD45-CFSE+ tumor cells in phMTb and phMTb/CpNe underwent four divisions, while those in CpNe and phMTb/Ne underwent three and only two in phMTb/CpNe ( , ). Furthermore, flow cytometry was used to analyze CD45+CD8+ cells in tumor tissues from mice receiving various vaccine formulations. These CD45+CD8+ cells were evaluated for their expression of ADPKG or SIINFEKL tetramers and their production of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 ( )). The results showed limited infiltration of CD45+CD8+Tetramer+ cells in phMTb and phMTb/Cp-vaccinated mice, while CpNe, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe groups had significant increases ( and ). Specifically, CpNe had the lowest proportion, followed by phMTb/Ne, with phMTb/CpNe showing the highest ( and ). Due to the low number of CD45+CD8+Tetramer+ cells in phMTb and phMTb/Cp tumors, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 cytokine-producing cells were only evaluated in CD45+CD8+Tetramer+ cells from mice immunized with CpNe, phMTb/Ne, and phMTb/CpNe. As expected, vaccination with phMTb/Ne and phMTb/CpNe displayed higher frequencies of cytokine-producing cells than CpNe, with phMTb/CpNe having the highest proportions ( and ). Overall, these findings suggest that neoantigen-containing phMTb formulations effectively stimulate the eradication of tumor cells, with the phMTb/CpNe formulation showing enhanced efficacy.
 

## Vaccination with phMTb/CpNe reverses the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment
The tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment is predominantly characterized by the presence of MDSCs, Tregs, and M2-type TAMs. Thus, MC38 tumor tissues were immunohistochemically stained to detect whether these tissues contained MDSCs and Tregs (schematic representation in ). The results showed that tumors from mice vaccinated with vaccine formulations lacking neoantigen showed higher levels of CD11b+Gr-1+ MDSCs and CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs, while those vaccinated with neoantigen formulations exhibited lower levels, with the lowest observed in phMTb/CpNe ( ). Flow cytometry was used to analyze live (7AAD-) CD45+ immune cells for MDSCs ( ), Tregs ( ), and CD11b+F4/80+CD206+ M2-type TAMs ( ), respectively. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in MDSCs ( ), Tregs ( ), and M2-type TAMs ( ) in tumors immunized with phMTb/CpNe compared with other formulations, with minor differences in MDSCs ( ), Tregs ( ), and M2-type TAMs ( ) between phMTb/CpNe and phMTb/Ne groups. However, Tregs and M2-type TAMs were significantly lower in phMTb/CpNe than in tumors immunized with CpNe, phMTb, and phMTb/Cp ( and ). Taken together, these findings suggest that the phMTb/CpNe vaccine may alter the tumor immune-suppressive environment, potentially enhancing vaccine effectiveness.
 

## Vaccination with phMTb/Ne in combination with anti-PD-1 antibody therapy synergistically enhances the anti-tumor effects
This investigation demonstrated that the phMTb/CpNe formulation had better anti-tumor efficacy than phMTb/Ne. However, using phMTb/CpNe for combination therapy may not yield accurate synergistic effects due to small tumor size. Thus, we chose phMTb/Ne to assess whether the combination of phMTb/Ne vaccination with anti-PD-1 antibody therapy could produce synergistic anti-tumor effects. The experimental schedule is shown in , involving four groups: NS control, phMTb/Ne immunization, anti-PD-1 treatment, and combination therapy (phMTb/Ne+anti-PD-1). The results showed significant tumor growth inhibition in both the phMTb/Ne and anti-PD-1 groups compared with the NS controls, with the combination group exhibiting the highest efficacy ( ). Statistical analysis indicated significant differences between each group (p<0.001), showing that the NS group had the largest tumors, while the combination group had the smallest ( ). The synergy index calculated from mean tumor volumes indicated values above one at all time points except day 9, peaking at 1.831 on day 21 ( ). In conclusion, our findings highlight the synergistic anti-tumor effects of combination therapy with phMTb/Ne vaccination and anti-PD-1 therapy.
 

## Discussion
Tumor neoantigens have become recognized as potential tumor antigens in cancer immunotherapy due to their individual specificity and strong immunogenicity, as well as their lack of expression in normal tissues. More and more studies have demonstrated that tumor neoantigen-based vaccines have shown promising results in clinical trials. Currently, the key factor affecting the efficacy of tumor neoantigen vaccines lies in whether the antigens can be effectively engulfed by antigen-presenting cells and delivered to the lymph nodes for specific cross-presentation to CD8+ T lymphocytes. This ultimately induces a tumor neoantigen-specific cellular immune response, mainly through the generation of specific CD8+ T cell responses. However, tumor neoantigens are typically short peptides of around 10 amino acids, which are easily degraded when administered alone or directly as vaccine antigens. Therefore, selecting an appropriate carrier for delivering tumor neoantigens is crucial for the effective development of a tumor neoantigen vaccine. Bacterial surfaces contain pathogen-associated molecular patterns and adhesion molecules that can be recognized by antigen-presenting cells for engulfment. Therefore, in this study, MTb, an intracellular bacterium with unique surface adhesive molecules, was used as a carrier to develop a porous hollow vaccine carrier called phMTb. Tumor neoantigens were loaded into this carrier, along with or without the adjuvant CpG, to create a tumor neoantigen vaccine. Our findings indicate that phMTb is an ideal tumor neoantigen vaccine vector that was used to prepare two major tumor neoantigen model vaccines: phMTb/CpNe and phMTb/Ne. The former contains both tumor neoantigens, while the latter contains only tumor neoantigens. Our in vitro experiments demonstrate that the phMTb/CpNe vaccine, prepared using the phMTb vector, can be effectively engulfed by DCs and can efficiently process tumor neoantigens through both the cytoplasmic-nuclear and lysosomal pathways. This effectively induces the maturation and activation of DCs, promoting cross-presentation of tumor neoantigens by DCs. In vivo experiments show that after immunization with the phMTb/CpNe vaccine, there is a significant increase in tumor neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cells, which effectively inhibit tumor growth in tumor-bearing mice and prevent tumor metastasis. These results suggest that tumor neoantigen vaccines prepared using phMTb as a vector can be effectively engulfed by DCs and promote efficient antigen cross-presentation, leading to the induction of more effective cellular immune responses. Additionally, they inhibit exhaustion of tumor-specific CD8+ T lymphocytes, significantly improving the tumor immune-suppressive microenvironment. This novel tumor neoantigen vaccine has potential clinical translational value and warrants further research to promote its clinical application.
CD8+ T lymphocytes are a crucial part of TILs and play a significant role in tumor immunity. However, within the tumor microenvironment, CD8+ T lymphocytes are often exposed to antigens, which can lead to the activation of inhibitory receptors and a loss of effector functions. This results in impaired cytotoxic function, known as T cell exhaustion. Exhausted T cells express high levels of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, and TIM-3, with PD-1 being the main regulator of T cell exhaustion. T cell exhaustion is commonly associated with tumor immune evasion, making the targeting of exhausted T cells a potential therapeutic strategy. Moreover, there is a population of TCF-1+ CD44+ CD8+ T cells in lymphoid tissues that act as precursors to terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells in the tumor state. These precursor cells have self-renewal and differentiation capabilities. TCF-1, which is encoded by TCF-7, is a key transcription factor in T cell exhaustion and is closely linked to the generation and maintenance of memory CD8+ cells. Following blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, these cells can proliferate and mature into effector cells, thereby enhancing anti-tumor responses. Based on our current knowledge, there is limited research on the connection between tumor vaccines and T cell exhaustion. Our experimental results suggest that tumor neoantigen model vaccines phMTb/CpNe and phMTb/Ne may have the ability to prevent the development of exhausted T cells and restore their full functionality. Our findings also indicate that these vaccines can reduce the number of exhausted T cells and increase the number of exhausted progenitor cells, suggesting they could effectively alleviate T cell exhaustion. Additionally, when phMTb/Ne was combined with anti-PD-1 blockade, we observed synergistic anti-tumor effects, although it remains unclear whether this was due to the reactivation of exhausted T cells. Our study does not explore the specific molecular mechanisms behind this phenomenon, which should be further investigated by researchers interested in tumor vaccines.
Throughout the process of tumor development, tumors have evolved to create an immune-suppressive environment that helps them evade the immune system and avoid attacks. This environment is characterized by the presence of certain immune-suppressive cells, such as MDSCs, Tregs, and TAMs, which play a key role in suppressing the immune response. These cells work in different ways to inhibit the function of CTLs and promote tumor growth. , Their ability to evade the immune system is a major factor in the ineffectiveness of many tumor treatments, including immunotherapy. Therefore, targeting these immune-suppressive cells to improve the tumor microenvironment could lead to more successful anti-tumor outcomes. In this study, we discovered that tumor neoantigen vaccines created with phMTb as a vector, such as phMTb/CpNe and phMTb/Ne, were able to decrease the presence of immune-suppressive cells in the tumor microenvironment, including MDSCs, Tregs, and M2-type TAM cells, to varying degrees. Among these, phMTb/CpNe exhibited the most notable inhibitory effect, indicating that tumor neoantigen vaccines made with phMTb as a vector can effectively enhance the tumor immune-suppressive microenvironment and reduce immune suppression within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, the vaccine adjuvant CpG contributed to this effect to some extent. These findings suggest that in addition to the choice of vaccine vectors, the selection of adjuvants also plays a role in improving the tumor immune-suppressive microenvironment. Therefore, further investigation and exploration into various vaccine formulations and the use of different adjuvants in combination are warranted.
Currently, most of the tumor vaccines in clinical practice are unable to completely eradicate tumors, which may be related to tumor immune escape. In the state of tumors, immune cells may become depleted or suppressed, leading to a limited number of immune cells fighting against the tumor, ultimately resulting in tumor immune escape. Therefore, combining therapies can further enhance the anti-tumor activity of tumor vaccines. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has the ability to reshape the immune microenvironment, transitioning it from an immune-suppressed state to an immune-activated state, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immune responses and showing great potential in clinical applications. Given its unique ability to reshape the immune-suppressive environment, combining tumor neoantigen vaccines with ICB therapy, using neoantigen-induced neoantigen-specific CTL infiltration, and reactivating exhausted effector immune cells through immune checkpoint inhibitors to reshape the immune microenvironment may be a key breakthrough in further improving the efficacy of tumor neoantigen vaccines. Numerous related clinical and basic experiments have been conducted, for example, the personalized neoantigen vaccine NEO-PV-01 combined with PD-1 blockade in phase I clinical trials in patients with advanced melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, or bladder cancer. The results showed that all patients exhibited neoantigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell immune responses after vaccination. The vaccine-induced T cells displayed a cytotoxic phenotype, were able to migrate to tumor tissue, and exerted cytotoxic effects on tumor cells. No treatment-related severe adverse events were observed. Additionally, Ni et al developed a dual-adjuvant neoantigen nanovaccine that, in combination with a PD-1 antibody, significantly induced therapeutic responses and led to complete regression of 70% of neoantigen-specific tumors without recurrence. Similarly, as predicted, this study yielded similar results, as the tumor neoantigen vaccine phMTb/Ne prepared with the phMTb vector in combination with a PD-1 antibody significantly inhibited the growth of MC38 tumors, providing important scientific evidence for enhancing the efficacy of tumor neoantigen vaccines.

## Conclusion
The study developed a porous hollow carrier, phMTb, derived from MTb, to enhance tumor neoantigen vaccine efficacy. Leveraging PAMPs and bacterial surface adhesion molecules, phMTb effectively encapsulates tumor neoantigens and adjuvants, promoting DC phagocytosis and eliciting neoantigen-specific CTL responses. In murine models, the vaccine demonstrated significant tumor suppression, modulated the tumor microenvironment, and exhibited synergistic effects when combined with an anti-PD-1 antibody, suggesting potential for personalized cancer immunotherapy.
