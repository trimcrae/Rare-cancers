## ABSTRACT
For successful clinical translation of therapeutic human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines, optimizing antigen selection is key to enhancing efficacy. Traditional E6/E7 targets are limited by heterogeneous tumor expression. This review advocates expanding the antigen repertoire to include tumor-specific neoantigens in next-generation vaccines. We systematically evaluate established HPV therapeutic antigens and vaccine platforms, and comprehensively explore the frontier of novel antigens, advanced screening technologies, and recent vaccine innovations. Central is a bioinformatics-guided antigen selection framework spanning multi-omics analysis, immunogenicity prediction, and vaccine design. Key selection parameters – including antigen clonality, expression level, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) binding affinity and stability, and immunogenic potential – are critically assessed alongside state-of-the-art computational tools. By proposing standardized selection criteria and optimization strategies, this review aims to provide a roadmap for developing more potent and broadly effective therapeutic vaccines, ultimately bridging the gap between the molecular etiology of HPV-associated cancers and successful therapeutic discovery.

## Introduction
The development of therapeutic HPV vaccines constitutes a critical and urgent biomedical imperative. Distinct from prophylactic vaccines, these agents are designed to induce cell-mediated immunity targeting cells harboring persistent HPV infection and expressing viral oncoproteins (notably E6/E7). This strategy aims to eliminate established infections and treat associated HPV-induced diseases, specifically high-grade precancerous lesions (e.g., cervical intraepithelial neoplasia2/3, anal intraepithelial neoplasia, vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia) and HPV-positive cancers. Cancers induced by HPV infection are called HPV-associated cancers, including the majority of cervical cancers (CCs) and related to anal, penile, vaginal, and oropharyngeal cancers. According to reports, CC still ranks as the fourth most prevalent cancer in women. In 2020, approximately 604,000 women were diagnosed with CC globally with an incidence rate of 15.6/100 thousand, and around 342,000 of them succumbed to the disease with a mortality rate of 5.52/100 thousand. It is worth noting that CC is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in 23 countries and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in 36 countries. By addressing the fundamental limitation of prevention-focused strategies, therapeutic vaccines fulfill a significant unmet medical need for the vast global population already infected. Their potential to clear the underlying viral infection, promote lesion regression, reduce recurrence rates following conventional therapy, and impede carcinogenic progression represents a transformative approach, thereby addressing not only CCs but also a significant proportion of other HPV-associated tumors, such as head and neck cancers and oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC). Consequently, advancing effective therapeutic HPV vaccines is paramount for comprehensive global cancer control efforts, particularly in reducing the burden of HPV-associated malignancies.
HPV infection is common worldwide. , Researchers have detected more than 200 types of HPV, which are categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups according to their oncogenic potential. According to the comprehensive cancer information of the National Cancer Institute, high-risk HPVs are highly correlated with HPV-associated cancers. HPV 16 and 18 are responsible for the majority of HPV-associated cancers and approximately 70% of CCs and pre-cancerous cervical lesions. It is estimated that around 80% of individuals, regardless of gender, will contract HPV infections at some point in their lifetime. Most HPV infections are transient, but if the virus persists in a latent form, this may trigger precancerous lesions and even cancerous transformation. HPV-associated viral proteins as foreign proteins, such as E6 and E7, are always specifically expressed in the tumor cells and stimulate antigen-specific cellular immune responses to kill tumor cells, becoming an ideal antigenic protein candidate for HPV-associated tumors. However, a critical oversight is that not all HPV-associated tumor cells consistently express the HPV protein, and HPV-associated tumors also have general tumor properties, that is, the tumor cells carry the neoantigens. Puram, S.V., et al. based on single-cell ribonucleic acid sequencing (RNA-seq) technology have found significant heterogeneity in HPV-associated cancers, with some tumor cells lacking HPV protein expression. Interestingly, recent immune landscape analysis has shown that neoantigens of HPV-associated cancers are detectable and potentially immunogenic. How to utilize HPV-associated proteins expressed in tumors to design more efficient therapeutic HPV vaccines and address the issue of some tumor cells losing HPV-associated proteins and thus not responding to the vaccines is the key to improving the effectiveness of therapeutic HPV vaccines in the future.
We systematically review conventional HPV therapeutic vaccine antigens (viral proteins) and their current vaccine landscape, expanding our focus to explore emerging HPV therapeutic vaccine antigens (screened viral epitopes and neoantigens). Besides, we have developed a comprehensive set of strategy outlining the necessary conditions and precautions during the screening process for both viral protein antigens and tumor neoantigens.

## Methods

## Identification of studies and selection
This review identified relevant literature through a systematic search of databases including PubMed and Web of Science (2000–2024), utilizing a combination of keywords such as “Human Papillomavirus,” “therapeutic vaccine,” “E6/E7,” “tumor neoantigen,” and “personalized vaccine.” The search focused on studies elucidating antigen screening strategies and clinical translation challenges.

## A framework for the discovery of neoantigen and viral target antigens ( )
 
Sample acquisition: Samples are collected in cancer patients for tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues (or blood and saliva).
Identify tumor-specific HPV proteins and nonsynonymous somatic mutations: Tumor-specific HPV-related proteins and nonsynonymous somatic mutations were verified through rapid sequencing and comparative analysis of the collected samples.
Antigen prediction: bioinformatic algorithms are conducted to predict the most attractive viral epitopes and neoantigens for immune targeting based on their ability to elicit a T-cell response through some features such as tumor heterogeneity, expression, MHC binding prediction, and T cell receptors (TCRs) recognition.
Vaccine design and preparation, vaccination, and safety and efficacy evaluation. This process is designed to screen for the optimal antigen and to perform the validation.

## Anti-tumor mechanisms of therapeutic vaccines
Tumor antigens are molecules that enable the immune system to distinguish between healthy cells and cancer cells. They can be classified into tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and tumor-specific antigens (TSAs). TSAs are newly formed aberrant special proteins generated by tumor cells. Compared to traditional TAAs, which are overexpressed in tumor cells and also expressed in normal cells, TSAs are exclusively expressed by tumor cells, which can elicit truly tumor-specific T-cell responses without off-target damage to normal tissues. Besides, as de novo epitopes, they can help bypass the possibility of circumventing T cell central tolerance of self-epitopes, potentially boosting immune responses against cancers. , Furthermore, TSAs are distinct from normal proteins. The mutant amino acid sequences show greater divergence from original sequences compared to TAAs, giving TSAs higher affinity toward MHC molecules and making them more likely to be recognized by T cells.
The mechanism of tumor therapeutic vaccines is mainly to induce or enhance the specific active immune response against tumor antigens and recruit more tumor-specific T cells, to achieve the control and killing of tumor cells, remove small residual lesions, and establish lasting anti-tumor memory ( ). Exposed antigens enter antigen-presenting cells (APCs) through multiple mechanisms, such as endocytosis, phagocytosis, pinocytosis, and receptor-mediated uptake. In the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), MHC-II dimers are first assembled and bound to the invariant chain. When the invariant chain is destroyed by a series of endosomal proteases, the MHC-II site that can bind to antigens is exposed for binding the specific epitope derived from a mutant protein broken down in the endosomal pathway. These peptide-MHCs will then traffic to the cell surface where they are recognized by CD4 T cells, , which are crucial in promoting the cross-presentation of endocytosis proteins by MHC class I molecules to initiate cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), and they will also release a series of cytokines to regulate the proliferation and differentiation of immune cells. Cross-presented antigens are ubiquitinylated and transported to cytoplasmic proteasomes where they are processed into peptide fragments. The fragments, called epitopes, are transported into ER through the transporter of antigen processing (TAP) complex and loaded onto MHC-I with high affinity. After traffic to the cell surface through Golgi, the peptide-MHC complex is recognized by CD8 T cells through TCRs, and CD8 T cells differentiate into CTLs and induce tumor cell apoptosis through the Perforin/granzyme pathway or Death receptor pathway. Subsequently, more tumor antigens are released, further activating the immune system and forming a virtuous cycle.

 

## Established antigens: From discovery to current clinical hurdles
TSAs inducing specific anti-tumor immune responses in HPV-associated cancers can be subdivided into two categories: viral antigens derived from viral-integrated mutations and neoantigens resulting from non-synonymous somatic mutations. Current therapeutic vaccines for HPV-associated cancers primarily focus on viral antigens.

## HPV-Driven oncogenesis and the emerging role of viral proteins as tumor antigens
HPV is a small deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) virus that mainly infects epithelial tissues and mucosa in humans. The HPV genome comprises circular, double-stranded DNA of approximately 8000 base pairs, consisting of three regions: 1. Long control region (LCR): regulating HPV transcription and replication. 2. Early region (E): encoding viral proteins (E5, E6, E7), regulatory proteins (E1, E2, E4). 3. Late region (L): encoding structure proteins (L1, L2). The E1 and E2 proteins of HPV are responsible for recognizing the origin of replication, while E4 is thought to play a role in the life cycles of the virus. E5 can enhance growth factor signaling and promote cell cycle transition from the quiescent (G0) phase to the postmitotic (G1) phase. E6 and E7 target cell cycle regulators such as p53 and retinoblastoma (Rb) proteins to affect the cell cycle and stimulate cell proliferation. The action of p53 would normally lead to apoptosis in unscheduled S-phase. However, in HPV-infected cells, E6 targets p53 for proteolytic degradation and activates telomerase, thereby extending cell lifespan. E7 binds to Rb and activates the E2F transcription factor, causing DNA replication. 
The life cycle of HPV is closely related to the differentiation of host epithelial cells. HPV infection typically occurs at easily accessible anatomical sites, where the virus first invades basal epithelial cells, initiating the process of infection and inducing excessive proliferation. Over time, the gene copy number of the virus significantly increases and self-assembles into viral particles in the upper layer of the epidermis. Finally, infectious virus particles are released. The viral protein of high-risk HPV will induce malignant proliferation and transformation of infected cells, subsequently leading to the formation of HPV-associated cancers.
HPV viral proteins are not only tumor-specific but also shared among most patients, making them popular therapeutic targets for a while. The E6 and E7 oncoproteins are required for the initiation of HPV-associated cancers and sustained high expression. They drive carcinogenesis through multiple pathways: suppressing cell growth, disrupting normal cell cycle progression, promoting tumor cell proliferation and differentiation, and conferring resistance to cell death. Consequently, E6 and E7 have become the two most important target antigens for HPV therapeutic vaccines, with numerous clinical trials conducted to evaluate them. , However, many HPV therapeutic vaccines that achieved satisfactory preclinical results do not work well enough in clinics, , and there are still no licensed products in the market.

## Current therapeutic vaccines: Effectiveness and seeking improvement
For treating HPV-associated cancers, therapeutic vaccines can serve as both mainstream and adjuvant treatments to improve treatment outcomes. These vaccines primarily target HPV viral proteins that possess inherent advantages, mainly focusing on E6 and E7 oncoproteins ( ). Among therapeutic HPV vaccines, precancerous lesions represent the furthest-advanced indication, with a lesion regression rate of about 54% and an HPV clearance rate of about 42%. Similarly, a meta-analysis indicated that in vaccine groups, the overall regression rate of high-grade lesions reached 62.48%, with an HPV clearance rate of 48.59%. Despite near 100% objective response rate (ORR) in preclinical studies, TG4001 and ADXS11-001 showed limited clinical efficacy in cancers: 22% ORR for TG4001 + Avelumab in recurrent CC, and 17.1% (monotherapy) to 14.7% (with cisplatin) for ADXS11-001 in advanced CC. The preclinical efficacy of HPV therapeutic vaccines relies on optimal immune-activating conditions, whereas clinical tumors often evade immunity through mechanisms like an immunosuppressive microenvironment and antigen loss. In some HPV-associated tumors, viral antigen release is limited, failing to initiate a potent immune response. Moreover, sustained immune pressure can lead to loss of HPV antigen expression (e.g., E6/E7 gene silencing), rendering vaccine-induced T cells unable to recognize tumor cells. Actually, Puram, S.V., et al. have identified significant heterogeneity in HPV-associated cancers, with some tumor cells lacking HPV protein expression, which are associated with a decrease in HPV-associated cell cycle phenotypes, decreased response to treatment, increased invasion, and poor prognosis. , 
 

## Expanding the antigen repertoire beyond E6/E7
Looking forward, the underexplored E2 and E5 proteins represent promising therapeutic targets. Specifically, E2 functions as a negative regulator of the oncoproteins E6 and E7; consequently, its deletion leads to dysregulated E6/E7 expression. Notably, E2 expression predominates over E6/E7 in precancerous lesions, positioning it as a compelling candidate for future HPV therapeutic vaccine development. Moreover, the E5 protein, ubiquitous in high-risk HPV types, modulates cell cycle progression, tumor proliferation, apoptosis, and survival pathways, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target. 
It is worth noting that during tumorigenesis and progression, tumor cells undergo thousands of mutations. Neoantigens are the abnormal proteins produced by non-synonymous somatic mutations, which refer to base mutations that change the sequence of amino acids, including single nucleotide variations (SNVs), insertions-deletions (indels), gene fusions, and frameshifts. Besides, neoantigens can also be derived from dysregulated RNA splicing, disordered post-translational modification, and mutations in noncoding genome regions. The most frequently studied neoantigens are antigens resulting from SNVs and small indels. Therefore, neoantigens also generate an effective immune response and represent an additional and distinct immunotherapy strategy against HPV-associated cancers beyond HPV viral-associated antigens. Research has demonstrated that HPV-associated cancers harbor immunogenic neoantigens resulting from their distinct mutational landscape. This landscape is primarily driven by viral somatic mutations and specific mutagenic processes, notably apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic subunit 3 (APOBEC3)-mediated mutagenesis, which is potently activated by HPV infection and significantly contributes to tumor mutational burden (TMB). In CCs, deep sequencing has further confirmed that APOBEC3-associated driver mutations are significantly enriched, and such mutations can be detected up to six years before clinical diagnosis, with their variant allele fraction increasing as lesions progress. Beyond APOBEC, deficiencies in DNA damage repair pathways and recurrent driver mutations further diversify the mutational profiles. Furthermore, molecular profiling reveals that alterations in genes like TERT, NOTCH1, and NFE2L2 are recurrent and common to tumors driven by multiple HPV types. It is worth noting that, the molecular landscape of cutaneous HPV-associated squamous cell carcinomas is shaped not merely by the virus, but by a unique mutational signature resulting from the interplay of HPV and ultraviolet radiation as co-carcinogens. Thus, the combination of shared HPV-driven and unique tumor-specific mutational profiles in HPV-associated cancers provides strong candidate tumor antigens, making neoantigen vaccines a highly promising strategy. Its advancement will require the discovery of novel, high-potential targets via comprehensive identification, precise epitope selection, and optimized delivery systems.

## Comprehensive identification of high-potential target antigens
The optimal antigen requires the following conditions: 1. Antigens are homogeneous and clonal; 2. Antigens are highly and correctly expressed within tumors at the protein level, and can be processed by APCs into peptides suitable for specific MHC presentation; 3. High-affinity binding between antigens and MHCs with good stability; 4. Strong immunogenicity. To this end, a wide array of bioinformatics tools has been developed to evaluate and prioritize candidate antigens against these criteria, as summarized in .
 

## Homogeneity and clonality (neoantigens)
As HPV-associated cancer progresses, the integrated viral genome remains stable while simultaneously inducing the accumulation of mutations in the host cellular genome. When certain cells that have a fitness advantage are selected, clonal selection occurs, leading to a variety of tumor cell populations and resulting in tumor heterogeneity. For neoantigens derived from mutations, one effective solution is to opt for multi-region sequencing on representative biopsy samples. This technique involves selecting samples with a high concentration of tumor cells to ensure comprehensive coverage of all tumor cells. By doing so, it helps to minimize any sampling bias that could affect the identification of neoantigens from a single biopsy.
Next, we can identify clonal neoantigens by calculating the percentage of tumors that have the variants of interest using PyClone and FastClone. Clonal mutations, also known as truncal mutations that occur early in tumor evolution and are present in most tumor clones, are increasingly targeted. In contrast, targeting sub-clonal or branched neoantigens has lower clinical benefits. To note, variant allele frequency (VAF) is an alternative approach to measuring clonality, which is more readily available from sequencing pipelines, although it may not take into account certain factors such as copy number variation, contamination from germline tissue, or sample purity.

## Highly and correctly processed into peptides (both neoantigens and viral antigens)
Antigens need to be expressed within the cell to elicit an immune response. Millions of mutation peptides can be identified through genomic data, yet a majority of them are left unanalyzed in peptide-MHC (pMHC). Spontaneous neoantigen-specific T-cell responses are only triggered by 1–2% of mutations found in a single tumor. The sequencing of patients with mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) showed that the majority of nonsynonymous mutations were not expressed. Therefore, the high expression of target antigens at the protein level is the foundation for both neoantigens and viral antigens.
Firstly, neoantigens should be highly expressed, which can be broken down broadly into mRNA expression, protein expression, and active translation. Whole exome sequencing (WES) is capable of supplying information on TMB. mRNA expression can be evaluated through RNA-seq data (transcript per million, TPM), targeted sequencing, or microarray data, while protein expression can be assessed through various array-based methods and mass spectrometry (MS). If there are issues with the loading of mutated peptides and incorrect translation, it can lead to invalid predictions. One of the first steps in MHC-I restricted antigen processing is proteasomal cleavage of proteins in the cytoplasm. There are tools available that use in vitro protease digestion data or in vivo MHC-ligand elution data to predict different proteasomal cleavage sites, including NetChop20S, NetChopCterm, ProteaSMM for pMHC-I, with NetChop-3.0 Cterm showing the best performance, and PepClearCD4, MHC NP II for pMHC-II. Additionally, another crucial step in processing is the transport of peptides by TAP for loading onto MHC and predicting the specificity of TAP for specific peptide motifs can improve the prioritization of neoantigens. Tools such as PREDTAP and TAPREG , can estimate the efficiency of peptide transport by analyzing TAP binding affinity. To note, due to the feature of non-self, allergy (e.g., AllerTOP 2.0 ) and toxicity (e.g., ToxinPred , analysis are necessary to ensure the safety of antigens.
Furthermore, it has been noted that neoantigens contribute to signaling pathways that promote the growth of tumors associated with HPV, and they are typically found in a higher abundance within tumor cells. Consequently, software capable of predicting protein structure and protein-protein interaction can be utilized to carry out functional analysis and confirm the potential biological significance of such neoantigens, e.g., Alphafold 2, Phyre2, AlloMAPS 2, QAUST, PANNZER, Wei2GO, MMSMAPlus. Viral muti-epitope vaccines undergo several analyses, including molecular docking with toll-like receptor 4 (e.g., ClusPro 2.0 ), and molecular dynamics simulation (e.g., GROMACS ) to assess the stability of vaccines.
The evaluation of presentation potential relies on the integrity of the antigen presentation mechanism itself. Tools such as LOHHLA can be utilized to detect loss of heterozygosity in human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles, while tools like pVACtools can analyze mutations and expression of key genes in the antigen presentation pathway, thereby avoiding invalid predictions caused by defects in the presentation mechanism.

## High affinity and stability of antigen-MHC (both neoantigens and viral antigens)
MHC molecule alleles exhibit high levels of polymorphism in the population. Certain types of MHC molecules present certain antigenic peptides to T-cells through specific TCR. As a result, the strong binding of pMHC is a crucial feature in identifying optimal antigens for clinical immunotherapy.
Multiple studies have demonstrated the substantial role of MHC class I binding affinity in predicting the immunogenicity of neoantigens. Various models have been developed to anticipate pMHC binding affinity, employing HLA-eluted ligands that are identified through MS or binding affinity data gathered from publicly accessible immunopeptidomic datasets. Initially, stabilized matrix-based models assumed that each amino acid residue has a linear impact on the binding affinity, which is rarely the case since the correlation between neighboring residues may also contribute to the HLA binding. Thus, machine learning algorithms-based models have been developed to identify the non-linear binding affinities between the peptide sequence and MHC, but require more training data. Moreover, consensus-based models integrate different peptide-binding predictors, improving the prediction accuracy ( ). A comprehensive assessment and comparison of tools for HLA I peptide-binding prediction in 2023 show that MHCflurry can perform better on more alleles, but there is no universally best predictor for all time. 
 In addition to the intrinsic affinity of pMHC, the restriction of MHC molecules, i.e., TCR only binds to its homologous epitopes present in specific MHC molecules, also greatly affects the subsequent binding of TCR-MHC-peptide complexes. Therefore, identifying the antigenic epitope peptides with different MHC class molecular limitations is a key node for smoothly entering the subsequent screening process. To account for HLA diversity, two main strategies have emerged. Allele-specific models are trained on data corresponding to a single HLA type, while pan-HLA models are trained on data obtained from multiple HLA types, thus they can learn cross-HLA type features important for neoantigen binding and perform well in predicting previously unseen HLA types ( ).
Besides, another aspect of binding that has received less attention is its stability. The binding affinity between two molecules is an instantaneous indicator, but in the immune response, neoantigens should remain bound to MHC until T-cells recognize them. Better stability can increase the possibility of pMHC being recognized by T cells through NetMHCstabpan and thermostability profiling. 

## Strong immunogenicity of antigens (both neoantigens and viral antigens)
A multitude of neoantigen candidates can be obtained by predicting the binding affinity of pMHC. However, the majority of antigens that are expected to bind to MHC do not elicit effective immune responses. Their capacity to elicit an immune response from T cells requires additional in vitro experimental validations. There are multiple methods for validating immunogenic neoantigens: 1) T-cell based assays: ELISA, ELISPOT, flow cytometry, MSD U-plex assay, triplex fluoroSpot assay; 2) multicolor-labeled MHC tetramers ; 3) Mutation Associated Neoantigen Functional Expansion of Specific T cells ; 4) TCR profile-based assays.
Moreover, due to the manufacturing cycle of vaccines and the limitations of patient disease development, many clinical studies targeting tumor neoantigens find it difficult to have sufficient time to experimentally validate candidate neoantigens. Consequently, it is feasible to consider their potential immunogenicity during the screening process of neoantigens. It is crucial to take TCR recognition of pMHC complexes into account. With the ever-growing amount of available TCR-pMHC specificity data, there are several methods to forecast neoantigen-specific T-cell recognition. The commonly used method is NetCTL/NetCTLpan, which does not directly predict TCR recognition, but integrates proteasome cleavage, TAP, and MHC I binding affinity prediction into the overall prediction of CTL epitopes. In general, structural prediction of TCR-pMHC binding is better, but it is limited by the degree of TCR diversity and conformational flexibility of the TCR-pMHC interface, and few attempts have been pursued up to now, e.g., TCRFlexDock, GLIPH, DynaDom, TCR-pMHCmodels, and the model created by Dhusia et al., Timothy et al. Therefore, at first, models were developed to predict pMHC-TCR binding based on the primary protein, MHC, and TCR sequences or characteristics, e.g., NetTCR, ERGO, pMTnet, Repitope, PRIME, ATM-TCR, PiTE, TPBTE. Besides, manually planned TCR database annotations allow for training TCR-specific predictive factors and matching, e.g., VDJdb, McPAS-TCR, and CEDAR. 
The Tumor Neoantigen Selection Alliance (TESLA) reported that low agretopicity or high foreignness contributes to immunogenicity in the most likely presented peptides. The greater the difference between the neoantigen sequence and the original coding sequence, and the lesser the difference from the pathogen sequence, the higher the immunogenicity. To determine the strength of immunogenicity, it is useful to analyze sequence homology and foreignness using tools like BLAST and FASTA. 

## Rational design of optimized antigenic peptide cocktails (both neoantigens and viral antigens)
In contrast to malignancies like melanoma, the advancement of neoantigen-targeted therapeutics for HPV-associated tumors is still in its nascent phase. Notably, in a pivotal 2017 study employing adoptive T-cell therapy for metastatic CCs, Stevanović et al. found that the dominant immune responses were directed against neoantigens derived from somatic mutations and a cancer-testis antigen, rather than against HPV viral antigens, though no clear clinical efficacy was achieved with this approach, which provides compelling evidence supporting the potential of neoantigen-targeted therapeutic vaccines for HPV-associated cancers. Currently, initial attempts have begun to identify and combine optimal antigenic peptides. The Institute of Medical Biology at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences has screened conserved CTL epitopes from HPV16 proteins that can bind to high-frequency HLA class I molecules, and has developed multi-epitope vaccines incorporating these epitopes. , A Phase I PANDA-VAC study (NCT04266730) investigated adjusted 10–20 neoantigen peptides, and a Phase I trial (NCT03552718) assessed the yeast-based YE-NEO-001 vaccine’s(5–10 epitopes/patient) safety and recommended phase 2 dose determination in head-neck carcinomas. A 2019 study demonstrated the safety and efficacy of VB10.NEO (DIRECT-01), a personalized DNA plasmid vaccine holding up to 20 unique neoantigens, in combination with checkpoint blockade therapy for up to 40 patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors. In conclusion, the development of therapeutic vaccines should encompass two critical steps: first, precise selection of antigenic peptides based on bioinformatic predictions and experimental validation, followed by scientific formulation of the selected optimal peptides to maximize the elicitation of specific immune responses and achieve ideal therapeutic outcomes.
Scientific antigen combination requires rational proportionalization. Currently, the matrix screening approach serves as the primary method, wherein candidate antigens are combined at varying molar ratios (e.g., 1:1:1, 10:1:1, etc.). These formulations are subsequently evaluated through in vitro and in vivo assays to assess the potency, breadth, and functional profiles of T-cell immune responses elicited by each ratio. Comprehensive analysis via multiparameter flow cytometry and multiplex cytokine detection assays is employed to characterize CD8+/CD4+ T cell activation levels, antigen coverage breadth, and memory phenotype development. This integrated strategy ultimately identifies an optimal formulation that synergistically enhances immune responses while mitigating inter-antigen inhibitory effects.

## Strategic selection of promising delivery systems (both neoantigens and viral antigens)
Recent years have witnessed remarkable progress in cancer vaccine delivery platforms, with three major technological pathways emerging: biologically derived carriers, viral vectors, and engineered nanosystems. These platforms demonstrate distinct advantages in antigen presentation, immune activation, and clinical applicability ( ). For instance, pyroptotic vesicles leverage tumor cell-derived extracellular vesicles from programmed cell death, achieving over 90% lymph node targeting efficiency while enhancing dendritic cell activation threefold through damage-associated molecular patterns. This “bioinspired delivery” strategy exemplifies cutting-edge approaches that harness innate immune mechanisms. Viral vectors exhibit unique clinical potential due to their intrinsic immunostimulatory properties. A phase II trial showed that neoantigen-expressing vaccinia viruses achieved a 42% objective response rate in triple-negative breast cancer, surpassing peptide vaccines (15–20%). This success stems from three synergistic mechanisms: Toll-like receptor pathway activation via viral pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), inflammatory microenvironment remodeling, and fusion antigen formation. Meanwhile, bacteriophage platforms (e.g., M13) enable lymph node-specific delivery while stimulating CD40, showcasing the versatility of viral systems. Engineered nanosystems address critical challenges in personalized manufacturing. A thermosensitive hydrogel reduced production time from 4 weeks to 7 days while boosting CD8+ T-cell infiltration tenfold in colorectal cancer models. An engineered pH-responsive bacteriophage vector achieves precise antigen release in the acidic tumor micro-environment (pH ~ 6.5), enhancing targeted immunotherapy efficacy. 
 

## Challenges and prospects
In recent years, personalized neoantigen vaccines for non-HPV-associated tumors have achieved remarkable efficacy through the combination of antigenic peptides. For example, in melanoma, the NeoVax/mRNA-4157 vaccine – comprising 20–34 individualized long peptides combined with pembrolizumab – significantly prolonged relapse-free survival in a Phase II trial. Administration of 6–20 individualized synthetic long peptide vaccines achieved a comparable survival benefit in hepatocellular carcinoma. Keskin et al. reported in their phase I/Ib trial that personalized multi-epitope neoantigen vaccines could induce multifunctional CD4+/CD8+ T-cell responses in glioblastoma patients. For microsatellite stable colorectal cancer, personalized neoantigen vaccines comprising 7–19 selected peptides successfully induced antigen-specific immune responses in 66.7% of vaccinated individuals. Chen et al. designed a 20-peptide neoantigen vaccine for advanced pancreatic cancer. The mean overall survival (OS), OS associated with vaccine treatment and progression-free survival (PFS) were reported to be 24.1, 8.3 and 3.1 months, respectively. Despite these significant advances across multiple cancer types, the development of neoantigen-targeted therapeutics for HPV-associated tumors remains in its nascent phase.
Based on the current status of existing HPV therapeutic vaccines and the development of tumor vaccines, this review summarizes potential improvement strategies for future HPV therapeutic vaccines, including novel antigen discovery, precise screening of antigenic peptides, optimal antigen combinations. To achieve these goals, there are still some restricting factors, and addressing these issues is the key to the widespread popularization. The mechanism by which antigens are processed into antigenic polypeptides and transported remains largely unknown. The quality of antigens is crucial for cancer vaccines to trigger anti-tumor immune responses. Most prediction methods currently rely on machine learning, but their success is restricted by insufficient training data, leading to potential sampling bias. Additionally, different algorithms have varying standards that result in inconsistent predictions and uneven accuracy. To enhance prediction accuracy and effectiveness, further experimental and theoretical improvements are essential to optimize sequencing technology and neoantigen prediction algorithms, expand the database, and establish an optimal threshold for all platforms and each HLA allele. Furthermore, addressing obstacles such as tumor heterogeneity, tumor microenvironment, immune escape, high treatment costs, and lengthy manufacturing periods are pressing challenges that demand urgent solutions. While current neoantigen therapies emphasize cellular immunity, the subcellular localization of antigens – particularly membrane-bound and secreted forms – directly enables humoral immune activation and thereby initiates innate immune responses. Research on HPV-associated cancers should therefore extend to these neoantigens. Localization-based classification tools can aid in identifying candidates that engage this dual-pathway immunology, opening avenues for therapies that concurrently mobilize both innate and adaptive immunity. Beyond their direct application in vaccine design, neoantigen discovery platforms also hold significant potential for enhancing immunomonitoring in HPV-associated cancers. The dynamic profiling of neoantigen-specific T-cell responses can serve as a sensitive biomarker to track immune activation, response durability, and early signs of relapse following vaccination or immunotherapy. Moreover, monitoring epitope spreading provides a critical indicator of robust and sustained anti-tumor immunity. Together, these approaches not only optimize vaccine strategies but also enable functional assessment and personalization of immunotherapy in HPV-driven malignancies.
We also outline the optimization of delivery systems and platforms, including biologically derived carriers, viral vectors, and engineered nanosystems, to enhance lymph node targeting, APC activation, and personalized manufacturing efficiency. Adjuvants can enhance vaccine efficacy by increasing antigen immunogenicity, modulating the type of immune response, and prolonging immune memory; therefore, their selection and optimization are equally important. However, because this review focuses primarily on antigen screening, adjuvants are not discussed here. Future studies could integrate adjuvant design with antigen selection to further improve therapeutic outcomes.
In this review, computer-based prediction is currently the most mainstream, highest-throughput, and relatively low-cost preliminary screening method, though it correspondingly suffers from a high false positive rate. In contrast, MS-based immunopeptidomics, as an experimental identification approach, can select peptides that are genuinely presented and is a more reliable means for discovering “real” neoantigens. However, due to cost and technical barriers, its application is often limited to validating computer-predicted results. For example, MS-based immunopeptidomics provides guidance for screening genuinely presented immunogenic targets. End-to-end pipelines represented by NeoDisc enable systematic identification and prioritization of tumor-specific antigens through integration of multi-omics data. Its extended platform, NeoDiscMS, further increases the detection rate of tumor antigenic peptides by up to 20%. This method is also a key direction for improving the accuracy and success rate of neoantigen screening.
The therapeutic vaccine for HPV-associated cancer attracted the attention of experts in immunotherapy and is a significant development direction in the future. Through the rapid development of sequencing technology and artificial intelligence deep learning and the accumulation of validation databases, the algorithm for predicting optimal antigens is in full bloom at present. This ongoing optimization is leading to the development of therapeutic vaccines that target the optimal antigens, including epitopes from viral proteins and neoantigens. These are great news for the numerous cancer patients who will benefit from these advancements.
