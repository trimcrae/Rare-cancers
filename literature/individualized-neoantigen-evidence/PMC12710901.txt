## ABSTRACT
Checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1/PD-L1 have revolutionized cancer immunotherapy, yet their efficacy in prostate cancer has been limited. We previously reported a clinical trial (NCT02499835) evaluating PD-1 blockade combined with an anti-tumor DNA vaccine, pTVG-HP (encoding prostatic acid phosphatase), in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. Decreases in serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels were observed in 35% of patients, and 42% of patients developed immune-related adverse events (irAE). In long-term follow-up, any decrease in PSA or the development of an irAE was associated with prolonged overall survival (p = 0.009 or p = 0.006, respectively), suggesting a potential association between irAE and anti-tumor immunity. To evaluate this, tumor biopsies obtained pre- and post-treatment from 12 patients were evaluated by gene expression profiling and spatial protein analysis. Clinical responders (those with any decrease in PSA) presented with reduced checkpoint marker expression and increased expression of markers associated with dendritic cells, antigen presentation, and T-cell activation. These gene expression signatures highly overlapped with signatures from patients who experienced an irAE. Conversely, non-responders showed enrichment of immunosuppressive pathways, including elevated expression of VISTA and myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC)-associated markers. Patients without irAE had increased expression of PARP. These findings suggest that patients experiencing irAEs can have immune responses to tumor irrespective of obvious anti-tumor efficacy, at least with these treatments, and underscore the importance of tumor-infiltrating professional antigen presenting cells and T-cell activation for successful immunotherapy. Moreover, our findings suggest that combining vaccines and PD-1 blockade with MDSC-targeting therapies, anti-VISTA, and/or anti-PARP therapies might be further explored.

National Institutes of Health - U.S. (P30 CA014520, P50 CA269011, R01 CA129154) To analyze the tumor microenvironment (TME) of these tumor biopsies, and to discern differences in the immune landscape of responders and non-responders or those who developed irAE, we performed Nanostring nCounter analysis on bulk RNA collected from pre- and post-treatment biopsies. Additionally, we performed GeoMx spatial proteomics analysis on the pre- and post-treatment biopsies. Morphology scans from GeoMx analysis demonstrated the presence of CD8 T cell-rich regions in post-treatment biopsy specimens as well as in the pre-treatment biopsy specimens, regardless of the clinical responder status. Based on the staining, regions with and without CD8 T cells were selected (Supplemental Figure 2), and differential gene expression analysis was performed on the selected regions of interest (ROIs) after grouping them, as shown in Supplemental Figure 3.
Using nCounter analysis, we demonstrated that post-treatment biopsies were generally characterized by increased expression of MDSC-related markers, including CD163 (M2 macrophages), CXCL9 and CXCL10 ( ), compared to pre-treatment samples, regardless of the clinical response status. We also observed increased expression of MNDA (myeloid nuclear differentiation antigen), a gene associated with M2-macrophage polarization, in pretreatment biopsy samples. Additionally, genes related to antigen presentation, such as HLA-DRB1, HLA-B, and HLA-C (human leukocyte antigen subtypes), had increased expression in posttreatment samples ( ). Moreover, we observed increased expression of NBL1 (Neuroblastoma suppressor of tumorigenicity 1), a tumor suppressor gene, and increased expression of TGFB1 (Transforming growth factor beta 1), which is associated with tumor progression in late-stage prostate cancers ( ).
 When GeoMx proteomics analysis was performed selectively on CD8 T cell-rich regions from all pre- and post-treatment biopsy specimens, we observed heightened expression of checkpoint-related markers, such as CTLA-4, PD-1, PD-L2, PD-L1, and LAG3, along with the expression of some immune activation related markers such as CD80, 4-1BB, CD27, ICOS, CD40, Granzyme-A (GRZ-A), STING and FOXP3 ( ), in the pre-treatment biopsies. Interestingly, we also observed increased expression of BCL6 (B-cell lymphoma 6), which plays a role in the formation of germinal centers (GCs), and increased expression of BIM (Bcl-2 interacting mediator of cell death), a gene that regulates apoptosis and prevents autoimmunity ( ). Similarly, upon comparison of the gene expression levels between pre- and post- treatment biopsies from all selected regions (with and without CD8 T cells), we observed increased expression of checkpoint and exhaustion-related markers, such as CTLA-4, PD-L1, and LAG3, along with the expression of immune activation-related markers, such as CD80, ICOS, and 4-1BB, in pre-treatment biopsy samples. Interestingly, we also observed increased expression of p53 and BIM in pre-treatment specimens (Supplemental Figure 4A).
To identify differences in the CD8 T cell-rich regions in pre- and post- treatment biopsies, and to identify other immune cell populations in close proximity to CD8 T cells, we performed differential expression analysis between the “CD8 T cell-rich regions” and “CD8 T cell-excluded regions” from pre- and post-treatment biopsies. We observed increased expression of checkpoint- and exhaustion-related markers, such as PD−1 and PD-L2, exclusively in CD8 T cell rich regions of pre-treatment samples ( ), whereas antigen presentation-related markers, such as CD40, CD27 and HLA-DR, were present in CD8 T cell rich regions from both pre- and post-treatment biopsy specimens ( ). Moreover, we observed the presence of other cell types of CD4 T cells, CD20 (B cells), CD14 (monocytes) and CD68 (tumor-associated macrophages, TAMs), in CD8 T cell-rich regions from both pre- and post-treatment biopsies ( ). Additionally, increased expression of CD39 was observed in CD8 T cell rich regions from pre- and post-treatment biopsies ( ). CD39 is an ATPase that is expressed on stromal cells as well as multiple immune cell populations, including regulatory T cells (Tregs) and MDSCs, and is associated primarily with immune suppression ( ). Similarly, when pre- and post- treatment samples were pooled, our analysis showed elevated expressions of T cell activation (CD27, ICOS and STING) and antigen presentation (HLA-DR, CD40, and β-2-microglobulin)-related markers in CD8 T cell-rich regions, with infiltration of CD4 T cells, B cells (CD20), monocytes (CD14), TAMs (CD68) and M2 macrophages/MDSCs (CD163) (Supplemental Figure 4B).
Overall, these analyses demonstrated that CD8 T cells were present at tumor sites before treatment and that these sites presented increased expression of activation- and antigen presentation-related markers but also expressed more checkpoint and exhaustion-related markers. However, upon treatment with the vaccine with or without anti-PD-1, while the expression of checkpoint markers was reduced, this was accompanied by gene changes suggesting MDSC recruitment and the expression of other immune suppressive factors in the TME.

## Markers associated with dendritic cells and antigen presentation were associated with clinical response
Following our analysis that compared pre- and post-treatment biopsies, we wanted to evaluate gene expression differences more specifically between responding and non-responding patient samples. Using nCounter analysis, we detected increased expression of ITGB8 (integrin subunit beta−8), ITGB3 (integrin subunit beta-3), PFKFB3 (6-phosphofructo−2-kinase/fructose-2,6-biphosphatase 3), NCF2 (neutrophil cytosolic factor 2), FZD7 (frizzled class receptor 7), FLT4 (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 3) and SIRPA (signal regulatory protein alpha) in pretreatment biopsies from nonresponding patients, whereas SPHK2 (sphingosine kinase 2) expression was increased in pretreatment biopsies from responding patients ( ). All the genes reported to be differentially expressed in pretreatment biopsies were indicative of more advanced disease and were associated with promoting tumor progression by either promoting cell growth and metastasis or by resulting in an immune-suppressive microenvironment. More specifically, ITGB8 and ITGB3 are expressed primarily on Tregs, and SIRPA is known to be an immune checkpoint marker.
 In contrast, when similar nCounter analysis was performed on post-treatment biopsies, we observed increased expression of immune activation-related markers, such as CD27, NKG7 (natural killer cell granule protein 7), IRF7 (interferon regulatory factor 7), IRF1 (interferon regulatory factor 1), BCL2 (B-cell lymphoma 2), STAT1 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 1), and LTB (lymphotoxin beta), along with immune cell recruitment markers, such as DOCK2 (dedicator of cytokines 2), CCL5 (CC motif chemokine ligand 5) and CXCL10 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10), in biopsies from responding patients. We also observed increased expression of antigen presentation-related markers such as TAP2 (transporter associated with antigen processing 2), HLA molecules (A, B, C, and E), β-2-microglobulin, and PSMB9 (proteasome subunit beta type−9) in post-treatment biopsies from responding patients ( ). Moreover, genes associated with promoting tumor growth were also identified to have increased expression in these samples, namely, TGFB2 (transforming growth factor beta 2), JAG1 (Jagged 1), NFKB2 (nuclear factor kappa B subunit 2), and NCF2 ( ), along with increased expression of the tumor suppressor gene ATM (ataxia-telangiectasia mutated) in biopsies collected from responding patients ( ).
From GeoMx proteomics analysis, we observed increased expression of VISTA in non-responding patient samples when post-treatment biopsies were analyzed for all selected ROIs ( ) or when analysis focused on CD8 T cell-rich regions in post-treatment biopsy samples (Supplemental Figure 5B). Interestingly, we also observed increased expression of markers associated with dendritic cells (CD11c) and increased expression of the antigen presentation-related marker β-2-microglobulin in the biopsy samples from responding patients when analysis was performed on post-treatment biopsies only ( ), CD8 T cell rich regions only ( ), pooled pre- and post-treatment biopsies (Supplemental Figure 5A), and CD8 T cell rich regions from post-treatment biopsies (Supplemental Figure 5B). We also observed increased expression of B7-H3 in biopsies from responding patients when differential expression analysis was performed on pre- and post-treatment samples pooled together, regardless of the presence of CD8 T cells ( and Supplemental Figure 5A).
Overall, these analyses demonstrated that the biopsies from responding patients were characterized by increased expression of genes associated with antigen presentation, T-cell activation, and T-cell recruitment. On the other hand, non-responders were characterized by increased expression of VISTA and other tumor growth-promoting factors.

## Patients that developed irAEs showed increased T cell activation; PARP expression was elevated in patients that did not develop an irAE
As previously noted, the development of irAEs was strongly correlated with delayed time to progression and improved overall survival ( and Supplemental Figure 1A). We compared the biopsy specimens from patients who developed irAEs with biopsies from patients who did not develop irAEs to study the differences in their gene expression patterns. nCounter analysis data showed that patients with irAEs presented significantly different patterns of gene expression, which included increased expression of several genes related to immune activation. Specifically, IFNGR2 (Interferon gamma receptor 2), LAT (Linker for Activation of T cells), GZMK (Granzyme-K), and NKG7 – all associated with T-cell activation; CCL5 – associated with T-cell recruitment; LTB, CD84, and PLCG2 (phospholipase C gamma 2) – proteins associated with B-cell activation and GC formation; and increased expression of PTCH1 (patched 1) – a protein that acts as a tumor suppressor ( ). In contrast, the same subset of patients also presented increased expression of genes that promote tumor growth and metastasis, such as KAT6A (Lysine Acetyltransferase 6A), SDC1 (Syndecan-1), PRKCA (protein kinase C alpha), SKIL (Ski-like proto-oncogene), SH3PXD2A (SH3 And PX Domains 2A), PDGFRA (platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha), GSR (glutathione reductase), LAMA4 (laminin subunit alpha 4), LTBP1 (Latent-transforming growth factor beta-binding protein 1), SMAD7 (mothers against decapentaplegic homolog 7), FLT4, PGF (Placental Growth Factor), AXL (AXL receptor tyrosine kinase), and ITGB2 (Integrin subunit beta-2) ( ).
 GeoMx proteomics analysis showed that patients who developed irAEs presented increased expression of CD27 and BAD when differential expression analysis was performed on post-treatment biopsies ( ). Notably, PARP expression was increased in the post-treatment biopsies from patients who did not develop irAEs ( ). Moreover, infiltration of TAMs (CD68) was observed in patients who developed irAEs when pre- and post- treatment biopsy samples were pooled together (Supplemental Figure 6A, B). Additionally, we observed increased expression of pro-apoptotic factors BAD (BCL2-associated agonist of cell death), the antiapoptotic factor BCLXL (B-cell lymphoma-extra large), and CD39 in CD8-rich regions of patients who developed an irAE (Supplemental Figure 6B).
When unsupervised clustering analysis was performed based on the protein expression data collected from Nanostring GeoMx on all the ROIs collected from post-treatment samples, we observed that the responding and non-responding patient ROIs clustered together, but with some exceptions ( ). The same was true for these ROIs when clustering was performed based on the development of an irAE ( ). Interestingly, there was significant overlap in the clustering analysis between the PSA response and the development of an irAE ( ), indicating similar gene expression signatures between responders and patients who developed an irAE. Of note, patients who experienced any PSA decline and developed irAEs had the best long-term clinical outcome ( ), which was associated with increased expression of markers associated with dendritic cells, B cells, activated T cells and antigen presentation.
 Overall, these data showed that patients who developed an irAE had similar inflammatory or tumor-associated changes as those observed in patients who were defined as clinical responders. Both subsets presented increased expression of T cell activation-related genes and antigen presentation-related genes, along with some pro-tumorigenic genes. However, patients who do not develop an irAE had increased PARP expression, specifically in post-treatment samples.

## Discussion
PD-1 blockade therapy has had a major impact on the treatment of cancer and is used for multiple cancer types. However, PD-1 blockade therapy has been mostly ineffective in the treatment of immunologically “cold” tumors such as prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer and certain types of breast cancers. Because PD-1/PD-L1 blockade acts primarily on T cells, newer combination approaches have demonstrated activity when delivered with T-cell-activating therapies, including combinations with vaccines. , In a clinical trial combining a DNA vaccine and PD-1 blockade therapy, we reported that responses in terms of PSA decline and prolonged time to progression, which were detectable in approximately 35% of patients with advanced prostate cancer. , , Because the majority did not have a decrease in PSA, and half of patients had evidence of radiographic progression at 6 months, we wished to identify key differences within the tumor microenvironment that might be associated with mechanisms of response or acquired treatment resistance. In addition, 42% of patients developed irAEs, which was also associated with prolonged time to progression and overall survival. Hence, we reasoned that the development of irAEs might be associated with successful anti-tumor immunity. To evaluate this phenomenon and to identify markers of treatment response and resistance, we performed a comparative analysis between responders and non-responders, defined by the percentage changes in PSA at the time of biopsy, and in patients who experienced an irAE, using biopsies collected from patients enrolled in the NCT02499835 trial.
Even though prostate cancer is classified as one of the immunologically “cold” tumors, we observed the presence of CD8 T cells in both pre- and post-treatment biopsy samples. This indicates that the immune system can effectively infiltrate prostate tumors with potentially cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. As expected, the CD8 T cells in pre-treatment samples presented increased expression of checkpoint- and exhaustion-related markers. Moreover, upon analysis of post-treatment biopsies of non-responding patients, we identified several mechanisms of resistance, such as increased expression of markers related to immuno-suppressive function (Tregs and MDSCs), increased expression of the checkpoint marker VISTA, and increased expression of PARP, an enzyme involved in DNA damage repair.
We observed increased infiltration of M2 macrophages and TAMs and increased expression of the chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10 in post-treatment biopsies, suggesting that these factors were related to treatment and may also represent adaptive mechanisms of resistance. Both chemokines are known for attracting effector cells to the TME; however, this pathway can also be exploited by MDSCs to infiltrate tumor sites. The presence of M2 macrophages and TAMs is typically associated with poor prognosis, as they have anti-inflammatory functions and contribute to cancer progression. , Interestingly, VISTA, an immune checkpoint molecule primarily expressed primarily on myeloid cells and known to inhibit T cell activation, was also found to be overexpressed in post-treatment biopsies from non-responding patients. Xu et al. reported that blocking VISTA abrogated the suppressive function of MDSCs and demonstrated a connection between expression of VISTA and effector function of MDSCs. Notably, PARP was also found to have increased expression in the post-treatment biopsies of patients who did not develop an irAE. Its expression in tumors has been associated with poor prognosis, since it can be used by tumor cells to evade cell death. Given the fact that gene signatures of non-responders closely clustered with those that did not experience an irAE, this could be a primary mechanism of resistance acquired by prostate tumor cells. These observations, if supported by additional validation studies, suggest that MDSC depletion, anti-VISTA therapies or PARP inhibitors might be evaluated in combination with anti-tumor vaccines and PD-1 blockade to determine whether they can improve the efficacy of this approach.
In contrast, post-treatment biopsies from responding patients showed reductions in the expression of checkpoint markers, increased expression of T cell activation-related markers, and increased expression of genes associated with DCs, B cells and antigen presentation. These findings suggest that the administration of the DNA vaccine with or without anti-PD-1 treatment was able to reshape the immune cell composition in the TME. We have previously shown that the administration of a DNA vaccine can result in increased CD8 T cell infiltration and detectable circulating T cell immunity. , Theoretically, this could result in improved T-cell trafficking to the tumor leading to tumor cell death, the release of other tumor-associated antigens, the recruitment of professional antigen-presenting cells, and ultimately increased antigen presentation and polyclonal CD8 T-cell activation, leading to an improved overall response to therapy.
Another finding of our analysis was the increased expression of genes related to B-cell activation and GC formation in biopsies of patients who were classified as responders or who developed an irAE. B cells were found to be present in CD8 T cell rich regions. This could be indicative of the development of early tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS). TLS have been noted to form in tumors that respond to therapy and have been reported to be associated with favorable prognosis. TLS have been shown to exist in multiple maturation stages, ranging from basic lymphocyte aggregates (early stage) to T and B cell compartmentalization (intermediate stage) to mature TLS with GC formation (late stage). This implication needs to be explored further to determine how to promote the formation of mature TLSs and to further understand the role of B cells as antigen-presenting cells. 
As reported here, the development of irAEs was strongly correlated with improved overall survival, especially in those individuals who also had any decrease in PSA during treatment. Our clustering analysis of the post-treatment samples revealed similar gene expression signatures between those classified as responders based on serum PSA and those patients who developed irAEs. This strongly suggests that the development of an irAE might be an indicator of a concurrent tumor-specific immune response to the therapy and provides a mechanistic link between improved long-term overall survival of patients and the development of an irAE. The development of irAEs, or the detection of any decrease in PSA from pre-treatment, could be developed as on-treatment biomarkers for the further clinical evaluation of vaccines and PD−1 blockade for prostate cancer.
A major limitation of our study was the small sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings and limit the statistical power to detect subtle immunological differences. A larger cohort would allow for more robust conclusions and better characterization of variability among patients. In particular, we subsequently identified one patient who underwent biopsy collection to have a tumor with an MSIHI phenotype. Conceivably, MSIHI tumors could have different baseline or treatment-induced changes compared to MSS tumors. As previously noted, tumors that are characterized as MSIHI typically have an increased mutational burden and are more responsive to checkpoint blockade therapy. Therefore, the inclusion of this patient biopsy in our analysis may have confounded the association of irAE with the immune cell changes observed within tumors. In addition, while metastatic lesions in prostate cancer tend to be relatively small and while the same metastatic lesions were biopsied before and after treatment, it is possible that intratumoral heterogeneity could explain some differences observed at different time points. It is also unclear whether our findings are generalizable to other immunotherapy treatment approaches. For example, trials of combined PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade in patients with advanced prostate cancer have demonstrated higher frequencies of irAEs and no obvious associations with improved clinical outcomes. However, despite these limitations, our study revealed rational patterns in responders and non-responders following treatment, demonstrated a possible association of irAEs with an anti-tumor treatment response following this specific treatment approach, and suggested several mechanisms of resistance that could be validated by other methods and further evaluated in preclinical models.
Taken together, our findings highlight the major differences observed between responding and non-responding patients who were treated with a combination of PD-1 blockade and a DNA vaccine. Cytotoxic CD8 T cells pre-existed with increased expression of exhaustion-related markers, but vaccination with or without PD-1 blockade resulted in tumor-infiltrating CD8 T cells with reduced expression of these exhaustion-related markers and increased expression of markers associated with antigen presentation. The increased infiltration of DCs and B cells in CD8 T cell-rich zones suggests that DNA vaccination with PD-1 blockade can potentially reshape the tumor immune microenvironment, possibly leading to the formation of early TLS-like foci. Because non-responders demonstrated increased expression of VISTA, increased MDSC infiltration, and increased expression of PARP, these features likely serve as mechanisms of resistance. Targeting these components in combination with vaccination and PD-1 blockade will be a focus of future studies.
